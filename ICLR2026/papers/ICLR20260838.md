## Problem

Implicit reward models (IM-RMs) and explicit reward models (EX-RMs) are nearly identical in architecture, training data, and loss function, yet IM-RMs consistently generalize worse, especially out-of-distribution. The root cause of this generalization gap was unexplained, with prior hypotheses (e.g., generation-verification gaps) remaining untested. This paper identifies and proves that IM-RMs rely more heavily on superficial token-level cues, making them vulnerable to token-level distribution shifts.

## Input / Output

Both reward model types take a prompt-response pair (x, y) as input and produce a scalar reward score, but compute it differently: EX-RMs apply a learned linear head u over the final hidden representation h_{x,y}, while IM-RMs compute β·ln(π_θ(y|x)/π_ref(y|x)) from the language model's token-level log-probabilities. Evaluation is measured as ranking accuracy over preference triples (x, y⁺, y⁻), with the paper additionally probing robustness under token-level shifts (paraphrasing, translation to French/Spanish) and domain shifts (math, code). The analysis assumes fixed hidden representations during training, with only the linear head (EX-RM) or unembedding matrix (IM-RM) being updated.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a theoretical characterization of learning dynamics via first-order Taylor expansion of the reward after a gradient step. For EX-RMs, the reward change depends on responses only through their hidden representations (⟨h_{x̄,ȳ}, h_{x,y⁺} − h_{x,y⁻}⟩), enabling generalization when representations encode semantics. For IM-RMs, the reward change involves coefficients ρ_{k,l}(v) that depend on specific token identities and next-token distributions, which can be negative when tokens differ but appear in similar contexts—causing the model to inadvertently decrease rewards for semantically similar but token-different responses. The authors prove (Theorem 2) that under fixed hidden representations and single-token responses, IM-RMs provably achieve trivial accuracy (0.5) on unseen tokens while EX-RMs converge to the max-margin separator. They also rule out the generation-verification gap hypothesis by proving (Theorem 1) that an IM-RM can verify with margin δ without its underlying distribution being an efficient generator, corroborated by Hamiltonian cycle experiments. Empirically, across six language models (1B–8B) and multiple datasets, IM-RMs show significantly lower accuracy under token-level shifts (e.g., 0.515 vs 0.988 on RewardMATH token-level shift) but comparable or better accuracy under domain shifts.

## Results

Across six language models (1B–8B) and two training settings, EX-RMs consistently outperform IM-RMs under token-level distribution shifts: on RewardMATH-trained models, EX-RMs achieve 0.988 accuracy on token-level shifted evaluations versus 0.515 for IM-RMs (a 47.3-point gap), while on UltraFeedback-trained models the gap is 0.665 vs. 0.602. In-distribution, EX-RMs lead by ~10.6 points in the general chat setting (0.752 vs. 0.646) but tie in the math setting (0.971 vs. 0.972). Under domain shifts, IM-RMs perform comparably or better (0.720 vs. 0.621 on UltraFeedback-trained models), and EX-RMs consistently induce a higher absolute reward margin (e.g., 1.602 vs. 1.377 in-distribution on RewardMATH), which is beneficial for downstream RL optimization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between EX-RMs (Ouyang et al., 2022; linear head over hidden representations) and IM-RMs (Rafailov et al., 2023; reward defined via log-probability ratio). EX-RMs win decisively under token-level shifts (paraphrasing, translation): on RewardMATH-trained models the accuracy gap is 0.988 vs. 0.515, and on UltraFeedback-trained models it is 0.665 vs. 0.602. In-distribution, EX-RMs lead by ~10 points in the general chat setting but tie in the math setting. Under domain shifts, IM-RMs actually outperform EX-RMs (0.720 vs. 0.621 on UltraFeedback-trained models), suggesting the token-level cue reliance is the specific weakness rather than a general one. The third comparison, EX-GRMs (Zhang et al., 2025; generative verifiers that output Yes/No), is shown in Appendix F to behave more like EX-RMs than IM-RMs, confirming that the token-level sensitivity is specific to the IM-RM parameterization rather than a property of all non-linear-head reward models. Additionally, EX-RMs consistently produce a higher absolute reward margin across all settings, which prior work links to better RL optimization landscapes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the DPO framework (Rafailov et al., 2023), which established that every language model implicitly defines a reward model via log-probabilities, and on the InstructGPT formulation (Ouyang et al., 2022) of explicit reward models with a linear head. The learning dynamics analysis for IM-RMs adapts Theorem 7 from Razin et al. (2025a) on likelihood displacement in DPO, repurposing it to study generalization to unseen responses rather than optimization pathologies. The paper departs from prior empirical observations of the EX-RM/IM-RM generalization gap (Lin et al., 2024; Lambert et al., 2025; Swamy et al., 2025) by providing a mechanistic explanation rooted in token-level sensitivity, and explicitly distinguishes itself from Im and Li (2025), who studied generalization across prompts with fixed responses, and Shi et al. (2025), whose sample-complexity separation required nonstandard parameterizations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first principled explanation for a well-observed but unexplained empirical gap: IM-RMs rely on superficial token-level cues, making them fragile under paraphrasing and translation while potentially competitive under domain shifts. This has direct practical implications for practitioners choosing between EX-RMs and IM-RMs in RLHF pipelines, particularly when deployment distributions may differ in surface form from training data. Open questions include whether the token-level sensitivity can be mitigated in IM-RMs, whether there exist settings where IM-RMs consistently outperform EX-RMs, and how these findings extend to process reward models and intermediate-step evaluation. The theoretical analysis relies on fixed hidden representations and (in the strongest theorem) single-token responses, leaving room for tighter characterizations.
