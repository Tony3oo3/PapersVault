## Problem

CLIP features encode rich semantics but lack the pixel-level detail needed for image reconstruction, creating a fundamental trade-off in unified multimodal models: quantizing CLIP features degrades understanding, while freezing CLIP and relying on a diffusion decoder produces inconsistent reconstructions that hinder editing. The paper asks how to endow CLIP with high-fidelity reconstruction without eroding its comprehension, and how to effectively leverage CLIP features for both generation and editing within a single architecture.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262055/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model accepts three input configurations: (1) an RGB image plus text question for understanding, (2) a text prompt for generation, and (3) a reference image plus text instruction for editing. All image outputs are produced at 448×448 resolution via a lightweight pixel decoder (DC-AE-f32c32) that decodes UniLIP's 32×-compressed continuous features directly into pixels, bypassing the need for a separate VAE. For generation and editing, the diffusion transformer (SANA) receives dual cross-attention conditions: the full multimodal hidden states from the MLLM and 256 learnable query embeddings, which together encode both fine-grained visual details and the LLM's reasoning output.

## Method

UniLIP adapts InternViT (from InternVL3) for reconstruction via a two-stage training scheme: Stage 1 trains a pixel decoder and projection layer against frozen CLIP features using MSE + LPIPS loss, while Stage 2 unfreezes CLIP and adds a self-distillation loss (MSE between original and fine-tuned features, weight λ=1) plus a 10× lower learning rate for CLIP to constrain distributional drift. For generation and editing, the paper introduces a dual-condition architecture that feeds both the MLLM's multimodal hidden states and learnable query embeddings (N=256) as cross-attention conditions into the SANA diffusion transformer, resolving the information bottleneck of fixed-length queries that causes inconsistency in editing. The full system is trained in three stages (connector alignment, joint generation+editing pretraining on ~40M images, and instruction-tuning SFT) with the LLM kept frozen throughout to preserve understanding. This design achieves 0.90 GenEval, 0.63 WISE, and 3.94 ImgEdit with only 1B/3B parameters, outperforming larger unified models like BAGEL (7B) and UniWorld-V1 (12B).

## Results

UniLIP-3B (2B+1.6B params) achieves 0.90 on GenEval and 0.63 on WISE for text-to-image generation, and 3.94 on ImgEdit for image editing, outperforming substantially larger unified models like BAGEL (7B+7B) and UniWorld-V1 (7B+12B). For reconstruction at 448×448, UniLIP achieves rFID 0.31, PSNR 24.62, and SSIM 0.788, dramatically surpassing Emu2 (rFID 3.27) and quantized CLIP tokenizers (rFID ~1.37). On understanding benchmarks, UniLIP-1B matches or slightly exceeds the frozen InternVL3-1B baseline (e.g., MME-P 1499 vs. 1492, MMVP 68.7 vs. 67.3), confirming that reconstruction training does not degrade comprehension.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On GenEval, UniLIP-3B (0.90) surpasses all unified baselines including OpenUni-L (0.85), Tar (0.84), BLIP3-o-8B (0.84), BAGEL (0.82), and Janus-Pro (0.80), and even approaches FLUX.1-dev (0.82, a 12B generation-only model). On WISE, UniLIP-3B (0.63) edges out BLIP3-o-8B (0.62) and substantially outperforms MetaQuery-XL (0.55), BAGEL (0.52), and OpenUni-L (0.52), demonstrating superior world-knowledge-driven generation. On ImgEdit, UniLIP-3B (3.94) leads all open-source editing baselines by a wide margin over OmniGen2 (3.44), UniWorld-V1 (3.26), Janus-4o (3.26), and BAGEL (3.20), approaching GPT-4o (4.20). For reconstruction, UniLIP (rFID 0.31 at 448×448) outperforms Emu2 (3.27) by nearly 10× and quantized tokenizers like TokenFlow/DualViTok (1.37) by 4.4×, while maintaining a higher compression ratio (32× vs. 14–16×). On understanding, UniLIP-1B matches or slightly improves over the frozen InternVL3-1B (MME-P 1499 vs. 1492, MMVP 68.7 vs. 67.3, AI2D 70.7 vs. 69.4), and significantly outperforms quantized-CLIP unified models like Tar (7B, MMBench 74.4) and VILA-U (7B, MMBench 66.6) despite being much smaller. The dual-condition architecture ablation confirms that combining multimodal hidden states with query embeddings is essential: query-only editing drops to 3.38 on ImgEdit, while the full dual-condition design achieves 3.81 (1B) / 3.94 (3B).

## Relation to Prior Work

UniLIP extends the MetaQuery/DreamLLM line of work that uses learnable queries to bridge MLLMs with diffusion transformers, but departs from prior CLIP-based unified tokenizers (VILA-U, TokenFlow, Emu2) that either quantize CLIP features or freeze them and rely on diffusion decoders for reconstruction. The key methodological departure is a two-stage training scheme with self-distillation that directly adapts the CLIP encoder (InternViT from InternVL3) for pixel-level reconstruction while constraining feature drift, rather than treating CLIP as a frozen semantic source. The dual-condition architecture further extends MetaQuery's single query-embedding condition by jointly feeding multimodal hidden states into the diffusion transformer, specifically to address the information bottleneck that prevents query-only methods from handling editing tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a single CLIP-based encoder can simultaneously serve understanding, generation, and editing without the semantic degradation or reconstruction inconsistency that has plagued prior unified approaches, and does so at 1B/3B scale while outperforming 7B/12B competitors (BAGEL, UniWorld-V1). The self-distillation strategy is a reusable technique for adapting any pretrained encoder to new objectives without catastrophic forgetting. Key limitations include the 448×448 resolution cap, the 3B parameter ceiling, and weak text rendering, leaving open whether the approach scales to higher resolutions and larger models where text fidelity becomes critical. The finding that reconstruction training can marginally *improve* understanding benchmarks suggests CLIP's original features were slightly under-utilizing available pixel cues, an insight that may inform future encoder design.
