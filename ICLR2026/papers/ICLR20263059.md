## Problem

Evaluating Deep Research (DR) agents is difficult because their outputs are long, open-ended, and admit many valid solutions, yet existing benchmarks either reduce evaluation to short factual answers, rely on LLM-generated rubrics and reference reports (introducing circularity and anchoring bias), or cover only narrow technical domains. No prior benchmark combines realistic domain diversity with fully human-authored, fine-grained rubrics that assess multi-document synthesis, implicit reasoning, and evidence-backed justification at the criterion level.

## Input / Output

Each of the 101 prompts (mean 87.6 words, range 13–315) is paired with 20–43 expert-written rubric criteria (2,593 total) spanning six axes: Explicit Requirements, Implicit Requirements, Synthesis of Information, Use of References, Communication Quality, and Instruction Following. Model responses are long-form PDF reports (1,800–7,600 words depending on system) converted to markdown. The judge outputs a ternary verdict per criterion (Satisfied=1, Partially Satisfied=0.5, Not Satisfied=0), which is combined via a weighted sum (weights in [−5, 5], where |w|≥4 marks mandatory criteria) normalized by the sum of positive weights to yield a single task score, alongside per-category failure-rate breakdowns.

## Method

RESEARCHRUBRICS introduces a three-expert curation pipeline (draft → iterative review → independent final check) to produce prompts and rubrics without any LLM-generated content, mitigating anchoring bias inherent in verifying auto-generated criteria. The evaluation employs an LLM-as-judge protocol using three SOTA models (GPT-5, Claude-Sonnet-4.5, Gemini-2.5-Pro) that assign ternary verdicts per criterion, with a weighted scoring formula that applies negative weights to penalty criteria and normalizes by achievable positive weight. A tri-axial complexity framework (Conceptual Breadth, Logical Nesting Depth, Exploration) annotates every task, enabling stratified analysis of where agents degrade. Ablation studies reveal that adding concrete inline examples to rubric criteria improves human–judge Macro F1 by 3–4%, while LLM-based rubric augmentation catastrophically degrades alignment by 15–20%, establishing that expert concision outperforms machine-generated elaboration. Binary grading (collapsing Partially Satisfied to Not Satisfied) yields substantially higher human–judge agreement (0.72–0.76 Macro F1) than ternary grading (0.53–0.57), informing practical deployment of automated evaluation.

## Results

On RESEARCHRUBRICS, no evaluated Deep Research system exceeds 70% rubric compliance. Gemini DR achieves the highest score at 67.7% (ternary) and 61.5% (binary), followed by OpenAI DR at 66.4% / 59.7%, and Perplexity DR at 56.6% / 48.7%. Implicit reasoning and synthesis of information jointly account for 45–50% of all rubric failures across all three systems, while explicit factual retrieval and communication quality show failure rates below 20%. Performance degrades monotonically with increasing logical nesting depth, with multi-step analytical tasks causing the sharpest drops.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks three commercial Deep Research agents: Gemini DR, OpenAI DR, and Perplexity DR. Gemini DR leads on both ternary (67.7%) and binary (61.5%) compliance, edging out OpenAI DR (66.4% / 59.7%) by roughly 1.3 and 1.8 percentage points respectively. Perplexity DR trails substantially, scoring 11.1 points below Gemini DR under ternary grading and 12.8 points below under binary grading. All three systems share the same failure profile: implicit reasoning and multi-document synthesis are the dominant failure modes (45–50% of failures), while explicit requirements and communication quality are handled more reliably. In citation analysis, Gemini DR produces 111 citations at 81% accuracy versus Perplexity's 31 citations at 90% accuracy, illustrating a breadth-accuracy trade-off that none of the systems resolve. The consistency of failure patterns across all three architectures suggests fundamental limitations in current DR systems rather than implementation-specific issues.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against a growing family of deep research benchmarks (DeepResearch Bench, ExpertLongBench, ResearcherBench, LiveResearchBench) that either rely on LLM-generated rubrics, restrict to technical/academic domains, or use coarse overlap metrics. It departs from the LLM-as-judge paradigm established by HealthBench by insisting that all 2,593 rubric criteria be fully human-authored and human-reviewed from the outset, arguing that LLM-generated rubrics introduce circularity and anchoring bias. The tri-axial complexity framework (conceptual breadth, logical nesting, exploration) is a new organizational contribution that prior DR benchmarks lack, and the mandatory/optional criterion split addresses a gap the authors identify across all existing rubric-based evaluations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark is likely to become a standard reference for evaluating commercial DR agents given its domain diversity, fine-grained rubrics, and public release on Hugging Face. The finding that LLM-based rubric augmentation catastrophically degrades human-judge alignment by 15–20% has broader implications for any LLM-as-judge pipeline, challenging the common assumption that more verbose instructions improve evaluation reliability. Open questions include whether the architectural limitations identified (implicit reasoning, multi-document synthesis) can be addressed through prompt engineering alone or require fundamental changes to agent architectures, and whether the length-quality correlation observed at the criterion level reflects genuine information density or residual verbosity bias.
