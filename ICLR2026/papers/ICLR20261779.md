## Problem

Existing treatment effect estimation methods for networked observational data either assume static network structures (ignoring temporal evolution of edges, covariates, and confounders) or assume no interference between units (violating SUTVA). No prior framework simultaneously models time-varying hidden confounders and dynamic spillover effects, leaving the identifiability of individual treatment effects in such settings formally unaddressed.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261779/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequence of T=25 time steps, each providing a sparse adjacency matrix (N×N binary), node covariates (bag-of-words features projected to 50-dim LDA topic vectors), binary treatment assignments, and observed outcomes. It outputs per-unit CATE-ID estimates (the expected outcome difference under treatment vs. control at a fixed environment exposure), factual potential outcomes, and treatment prioritization scores used for RATE-based ranking evaluation. The environment exposure E_i^t is a k-dimensional vector summarizing neighbors' covariates and treatments, serving as a conditioning variable that isolates the direct treatment effect from spillover.

## Method

DSPNET learns full confounder representations z_i^t by combining a single-layer GCN over the current graph with a GRU that recursively encodes prior confounders, treatments, and hidden states, thereby capturing time-varying confounding. A separate interference module projects node covariates through a GCN to obtain hidden states r_i^t, then computes the exposure embedding e_i^t as a treatment-gated sum over neighbors (e_i^t = Σ_{j∈G_i} d_j^t · r_j^t), encoding heterogeneous spillover magnitudes. Two independent MLP heads predict potential outcomes under d=0 and d=1 given (z_i^t, e_i^t). Training minimizes a composite loss: MSE on factual outcomes, cross-entropy on treatment prediction passed through a Gradient Reversal Layer (with coefficient β) to adversarially balance confounder distributions across treatment groups, and L2 regularization. The paper also formally proves identifiability of CATE-ID under an extended ignorability assumption that permits hidden confounders captured by a learned latent function Φ_z.

## Results

On the default setting (p%=0.1% network dynamics), DSPNET achieves √ε_PEHE = 1.497±0.145 and ε_ATE = 0.890±0.080 on Flickr, and √ε_PEHE = 1.464±0.119 and ε_ATE = 0.845±0.105 on BlogCatalog, outperforming the strongest baseline DNDC (2.589/1.618 on Flickr; 2.475/1.454 on BlogCatalog) by roughly 42% and 45% on √ε_PEHE respectively. Performance remains stable as dynamics increase to p%=1.0%, where DSPNET still leads (2.189/1.351 on Flickr; 2.227/1.183 on BlogCatalog). In the RATE evaluation (Table 3), DSPNET achieves R_AUTOC = 2.98 and R_QINI = 1.13 on Flickr, and R_AUTOC = 3.91 and R_QINI = 1.52 on BlogCatalog, again surpassing all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DSPNET wins against all 8 baselines on both datasets across all three levels of network dynamics. The closest competitor is DNDC (Ma et al., 2021), the only other method designed for dynamic networks, but it does not model interference; DSPNET beats it by ~42% on √ε_PEHE (1.497 vs. 2.589 on Flickr, 1.464 vs. 2.475 on BlogCatalog at p%=0.1%) and the gap widens under stronger dynamics. SPNET (Huang et al., 2023) models interference but assumes a static network, yielding √ε_PEHE of 8.693 (Flickr) and 9.569 (BlogCatalog) at p%=0.1%, roughly 6× worse than DSPNET. NetEST (Jiang & Sun, 2022) and Deconfounder (Guo et al., 2020) leverage network structure for hidden confounders but ignore both dynamics and interference, scoring 6.822/8.539 and 8.338/13.067 on √ε_PEHE respectively. The non-network baselines (CFR, DESCN, DFITE, DERCFR) perform worst overall, with √ε_PEHE values ranging from 11.5 to 35.3, confirming that ignoring network dependencies introduces severe bias in treatment effect estimation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) treatment effect estimation in static networked settings with hidden confounders (Deconfounder, NetEST, SPNET), (2) dynamic network treatment effect estimation (DNDC), and (3) interference/spillover modeling (Forastiere et al., Ma & Tresp). Prior work has addressed at most two of these three challenges simultaneously—DNDC handles dynamics and hidden confounders but ignores interference, while SPNET models interference but assumes a static graph. DSPNET departs from this by introducing CATE-ID, a new estimand that jointly accounts for time-varying hidden confounders, dynamic network structure, and neighbor-level interference, and by proving its identifiability under an extended ignorability assumption that permits unobserved confounders captured through network and temporal context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper fills a clear and practically important gap: real-world networked interventions (epidemiology, social policy, platform design) are inherently dynamic and subject to spillover, yet no prior method handles all three challenges together. The identifiability theorem provides a formal foundation that the field lacked for this combined setting. Key limitations temper the impact: all experiments use synthetic dynamics (random edge/feature perturbations on static graphs) rather than genuinely evolving networks, the interference model is a first-order weighted sum of treated neighbors' representations, and the extended ignorability assumption—while more flexible than standard ignorability—still requires that all confounding is recoverable from the specified inputs. Open questions include higher-order interference, truly exogenous network evolution, and validation on real longitudinal networked data.
