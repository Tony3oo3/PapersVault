## Problem

Current LLM development treats task-solving and preference-alignment as sequential, independent challenges, yet in human-facing applications a correct answer that mismatches the user's needs is insufficient. This gap is acute in cold-start scenarios where no interaction history exists, requiring models to proactively discover what they don't know about the user and adapt their reasoning processes—not merely their presentation—accordingly. No existing benchmark evaluates this interactive, reasoning-level personalization capability.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a reasoning problem from one of 10 benchmarks (math, science, logic, social) paired with a psychologically-grounded persona carrying a sparse preference profile (a subset of ~20-25 attributes from a larger global set, each with a value and importance weight summing to 1). The model engages in up to 5 turns of dialogue with a passive simulated user who answers factually without volunteering extra information, then produces a final personalized response. Evaluation yields a PREFALIGN score (weighted sum of per-attribute rubric grades on a 0–5 scale), objective task accuracy, and a normalized alignment score that rescales discovery performance between the baseline (0) and oracle (100) bounds for cross-model comparability.

## Method

PREFDISCO transforms any static reasoning benchmark into an interactive personalization task through four components: (1) persona generation conditioned on the International Personality Item Pool (Big Five) with high-temperature sampling for diversity; (2) context-dependent sparse preference instantiation where only task-relevant attributes are active per persona-task pair, grounded in educational research on student characteristics; (3) LLM-generated attribute-specific rubrics (1–5 scale) enabling fine-grained, instance-level evaluation across 10K scenarios; and (4) passive user simulation that forces models to develop strategic questioning rather than relying on user proactiveness. The PREFALIGN metric (Eq. 1) computes a weighted sum of per-attribute grading functions, and a normalized alignment score (Eq. 2) rescales discovery performance relative to each model's own baseline and oracle bounds. The key conceptual contribution is formalizing personalized reasoning as selecting the reasoning chain itself—e.g., choosing inclusion-exclusion versus generating functions for a combinatorics problem—rather than merely restyling a fixed solution, with elicitation modeled as a sequential decision process over ask(θ) or answer actions.

## Results

Across 21 frontier models and 10 reasoning tasks (2,100 model-task combinations), 29.0% (61/210) of Discovery-mode responses achieve *negative* normalized preference alignment, meaning naive personalization attempts produce worse alignment than generic baseline responses. Overall task accuracy degrades systematically under personalization constraints: Baseline 65.2% → Oracle 61.8% → Discovery 60.1%, with the drop from Baseline to Oracle (no dialogue required) confirming the cost is inherent to processing preference constraints rather than multi-turn overhead. Domain-specific disparities are stark: AIME suffers a 12.1% accuracy loss under personalization while CommonsenseQA gains 5.4%. Models ask only 1.48 questions on average despite a 5-turn allowance, and a positive correlation (r=0.445, p<0.001) between question volume and alignment quality indicates insufficient questioning is a primary failure mode.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external personalization methods; instead it evaluates three internal conditions—Baseline (no personalization), Discovery (interactive preference elicitation), and Oracle (full preference profile provided upfront)—across 21 models. The critical finding is that Discovery underperforms Baseline in 29.0% of model-task pairs, with MATH (10/21 models negative) and LogiQA (11/21 models negative) showing the most severe degradation, while SocialIQA and CommonsenseQA benefit most from interactive personalization. The gap between Discovery and Oracle reveals that even when models *can* personalize (Oracle condition), their ability to *discover* preferences interactively is far weaker; for example, Claude Opus 4 achieves 33.0 on MedQA in Oracle mode but only 8.3 in Discovery. Regression analysis shows Gemini models extract the most alignment gain per question (β=0.474) versus OpenAI (β=0.379) and Claude (β=0.117), suggesting question quality and strategic timing differ substantially across architectures. Notably, older models like Claude 3-Opus occasionally outperform newer counterparts (e.g., Claude Sonnet-4) on Discovery alignment, consistent with RL-based alignment reducing output diversity and adaptability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from static personalization benchmarks (PersoBench, PrefEval, PersonaMem) that assume preferences are known a priori or inferable from long interaction histories, and from interactive questioning frameworks (MediQ, GATE) that target narrow clinical or intent-clarification domains without reasoning adaptation. The key conceptual departure is the claim that effective personalization requires altering the reasoning chain itself—selecting different solution methods, information prioritization, and decision points—rather than merely restyling a fixed output. By transforming existing verifiable reasoning benchmarks into interactive cold-start personalization tasks with sparse, context-dependent preference profiles grounded in the Big Five personality framework, the paper carves out a new evaluation space that no prior benchmark occupies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that 29% of personalization attempts produce worse alignment than generic responses, combined with the accuracy degradation from baseline to oracle (showing the cost is inherent to processing preference constraints, not dialogue overhead), reveals a fundamental tension between RL-optimized reasoning pathways and the cognitive flexibility personalization demands. The domain-specific brittleness—mathematical reasoning degrading severely while social reasoning improves—suggests current training paradigms create architectures that are structurally incompatible with personalized reasoning in formal domains. Open questions include whether dedicated training objectives can resolve this tradeoff, how to prevent over-personalization and sycophancy, and whether the framework generalizes to content preferences beyond communication style.
