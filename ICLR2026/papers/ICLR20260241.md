## Problem

Retrieval-augmented autoformalization methods treat complex informal mathematical statements as monolithic queries, which are too information-dense to retrieve precise atomic formal definitions. Additionally, even when correct definitions are retrieved, models lack contextual usage examples to correctly integrate them into well-formed formal statements, leading to syntactic and structural errors.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260241/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single informal mathematical statement in natural language and a formal library (Mathlib 4.7 for in-distribution tasks, con-nf for out-of-distribution) as input. The informal statement is first decomposed into 5–7 sub-queries, each pairing a natural language concept description with a predicted formal name (e.g., 'Nat.Prime p and p % 4 = 3'). The output is a Lean 4 theorem statement evaluated by Typecheck (syntactic validity) and BEq+ (bidirectional logical equivalence with ground truth), with pass@1 and pass@10 metrics over 10 independent generations.

## Method

DRIFT is a four-stage pipeline: (1) Decompose — an LLM (GPT-4.1, DeepSeek-V3.1, or Claude-Opus-4) breaks the informal statement into atomic sub-queries, each isolating one mathematical concept with both a NL phrase and a predicted formal representation that serves as a syntactic anchor; (2) Retrieve — a BGE-M3 dense retriever finetuned via contrastive learning on dependency-retrieval pairs maps each sub-query to its top-1 formal premise via cosine similarity, with the final premise set formed by union and deduplication; (3) Illustrate — a greedy set-cover algorithm selects up to 3 theorems from the library that maximize coverage of the retrieved premises, pre-sorted by semantic similarity to the original statement for deterministic tie-breaking; (4) Formalize — an LLM generates the Lean 4 statement conditioned on the concatenation of instruction, retrieved premises (with full declarations and source code), illustrative theorems (as informal-formal pairs), and the original statement. The key insight is that decomposition aligns query granularity with the atomic structure of formal libraries, while illustrative theorems act as a syntactic scaffold bridging the gap between knowing a definition exists and knowing how to use it in a theorem.

## Results

On the in-distribution ProofNet benchmark, GPT-4.1 with DRIFT achieves BEq+@10 of 21.93%, improving over the DPR (RAuto) baseline (19.25%) and zero-shot (13.37%). On the out-of-distribution ConNF benchmark, DRIFT delivers the most dramatic gains: GPT-4.1's BEq+@10 jumps from 6.76% (zero-shot) to 62.33%, surpassing even the Oracle* retrieval upper bound (58.90%) by 3.43 points. For dependency retrieval, DRIFT nearly doubles the F1 score over the no-decomposition baseline on ProofNet (27.68% vs. 13.77%). On the low-dependency MiniF2F-test, DRIFT performs comparably to baselines, confirming that retrieval provides limited benefit when problems require few external premises.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is DPR (RAuto) from Liu et al. (2025), a finetuned dense passage retriever that retrieves top-5 premises using the full informal statement as a monolithic query. DRIFT consistently outperforms DPR (RAuto) on both ProofNet and ConNF across all frontier models: on ProofNet, GPT-4.1 improves from 19.25% to 21.93% BEq+@10 (+2.68 pp), and on ConNF the gap is far larger, with GPT-4.1 jumping from 20.08% to 62.33% (+42.25 pp) and DeepSeek-V3.1 from 17.07% to 54.21% (+37.14 pp). Notably, DRIFT surpasses the Oracle* retrieval upper bound on ConNF for GPT-4.1 (62.33% vs. 58.90% BEq+@10), attributed to the Illustrate module providing usage context that ground-truth premises alone lack. On the low-dependency MiniF2F-test, DRIFT is roughly on par with or slightly below DPR (RAuto) for DeepSeek-V3.1 (24.55% vs. 27.68% BEq+@10), as the minimal external dependencies (avg. 0.43) make retrieved context a potential distractor rather than an aid. The zero-shot baseline is decisively beaten on all benchmarks where retrieval is meaningful, with the largest absolute gains on ConNF (e.g., +55.57 pp for GPT-4.1 BEq+@10).

## Relation to Prior Work

DRIFT builds directly on the dependency retrieval paradigm established by Liu et al. (2025) with RAutoformalizer, which reframed autoformalization retrieval as identifying precise formal definitions rather than semantically similar theorems. It departs from this line in two ways: first, it introduces query decomposition (drawing from multi-hop QA and divide-and-conquer theorem proving) to break monolithic informal statements into atomic sub-queries, and second, it adds an Illustrate module that retrieves theorems demonstrating how retrieved premises are actually used in formal statements. Prior retrieval-augmented autoformalization methods (ProofNet's k-NN, MS-RAG, LTRAG) treated the full statement as a single query and provided context-free definitions; DRIFT is the first to systematically combine decomposition-driven retrieval with usage-context augmentation for this task.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework's ability to surpass the oracle* baseline on the out-of-distribution ConNF benchmark (by 3.43% BEq+@10 for GPT-4.1) demonstrates that ground-truth dependency names alone are insufficient without usage scaffolding, a finding with implications for how retrieval-augmented systems should be designed beyond autoformalization. The paper also reveals that retrieval utility is conditioned on the gap between a model's parametric knowledge and statement complexity, motivating adaptive retrieval strategies that remain unaddressed. Key open questions include whether end-to-end training of the decomposer-retriever-formalizer pipeline can close remaining gaps, whether agentic frameworks with compiler feedback can iteratively refine formalizations, and how to handle low-dependency regimes where retrieved context acts as a distractor rather than an aid.
