## Problem

Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) are widely used to steer LLMs on the assumption that their interpretable features naturally enable effective behavior control, yet it remains unclear whether higher interpretability actually predicts better steering utility. This paper systematically quantifies that gap across 90 SAEs spanning three LLMs, five architectures, and six sparsity levels, and identifies which features truly drive steering performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264002/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on SAEs (16K-width dictionaries) attached to the residual stream at a fixed mid-layer of each base LLM (Layer 12 for Gemma-2-2B, Layer 17 for Qwen-2.5-3B, Layer 20 for Gemma-2-9B). For each SAE, 1,000 latents are scored for interpretability via SAEBENCH's LLM-as-judge pipeline, and 100 of these (CONCEPT100) are used for steering evaluation on Alpaca-Eval instructions with up to 128 generated tokens. The output is a ranked subset of high-utility SAE features (selected by Δ Token Confidence) and the resulting steered generations, scored by AXBENCH's harmonic-mean steering metric over Concept, Instruction, and Fluency subscores.

## Method

The paper contributes two complementary analyses. First, a pairwise rank-agreement framework using Kendall's τ_b with axis-conditioned controls (varying architecture, sparsity, or model one at a time while holding others fixed) to isolate the interpretability–utility relationship from confounding hyperparameters. Second, a novel feature-selection criterion called Δ Token Confidence: for each SAE feature, a single-feature intervention amplifies that feature's coefficient by a factor α=10 in the SAE reconstruction, and the change in top-k token confidence (negative mean log-probability of the k most probable tokens, with k=1) between the intervened and baseline next-token distributions is computed. Features with extreme |ΔC_k| in either direction are selected as high-utility steering candidates. This selection improves steering scores by 52.52% over the best output-score baseline (Arad et al., 2025), yet among the selected high-utility features the interpretability–utility correlation collapses to near zero or turns negative, revealing that the most effective steering features are precisely those for which interpretability is irrelevant or potentially misleading.

## Results

Across 90 SAEs (3 models × 5 architectures × 6 sparsity levels), the pooled Kendall's τ_b between interpretability and steering utility is only 0.298 (95% CI [0.159, 0.419]), indicating a weak positive association. After applying Δ Token Confidence feature selection, steering scores improve substantially: 0.328 (Gemma-2-2B), 0.399 (Qwen-2.5-3B), and 0.289 (Gemma-2-9B), representing a 52.52% average gain over the best output-score baseline. Strikingly, the interpretability–utility correlation collapses to τ_b ≈ 0.08 (p = 0.26, not significant) among high-utility features, showing that interpretability becomes irrelevant or even negative for the most effective steering features.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares two baselines: (1) SAE-based steering with all features (Wu et al., 2025 / AXBENCH protocol) and (2) Output Score–based feature selection (Arad et al., 2025). Δ Token Confidence selection outperforms the SAE-based baseline by 146.6% on Gemma-2-2B (0.328 vs. 0.133), 133.3% on Qwen-2.5-3B (0.399 vs. 0.171), and 103.5% on Gemma-2-9B (0.289 vs. 0.142). Against the Output Score baseline, the improvement is 40.8% (Gemma-2-2B), 36.6% (Qwen-2.5-3B), and 13.3% (Gemma-2-9B), averaging 52.52% across models. Paired bootstrap tests over all 90 SAEs confirm both comparisons are statistically significant (p ≈ 0.0000 vs. SAE Base; p ≈ 0.0032 vs. Output Score). The gains are consistent across all five SAE architectures (BatchTopK, Gated, JumpReLU, ReLU, TopK), with BatchTopK showing the most stable improvements.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the SAE-based steering line (Chalnev et al. 2024; Arad et al. 2025) and the AxBench evaluation protocol (Wu et al. 2025), but departs by systematically quantifying the link between SAE interpretability scores (via SAEBench) and steering utility (via AxBench) using pairwise Kendall's τ_b across 90 SAEs. It extends the feature-selection idea from Arad et al. (2025), who proposed an output-score criterion, by introducing Δ Token Confidence—a metric grounded in the token-entropy/confidence mechanism of LLM reasoning (Fu et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025a)—to identify steering-critical features. The axis-conditioned analysis framework (controlling for architecture, sparsity, and model simultaneously) is a methodological contribution not present in prior SAE steering evaluations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is that SAE interpretability scores (e.g., SAEBench AutoInterp) cannot be used as a reliable proxy for selecting SAEs for downstream steering applications; optimizing purely for interpretability will mis-rank models when the goal is controllable generation. The Δ Token Confidence selector offers a concrete, lightweight post-training protocol that yields a 52.52% average improvement over the best existing selection method, making it immediately useful for practitioners. Open questions include whether utility-oriented SAE training objectives (rather than post-hoc selection) can close the gap at the source, and whether the findings generalize beyond the single mid-layer and 16K dictionary width studied here. The counterintuitive result that the interpretability-utility correlation vanishes or turns negative among the most effective steering features suggests the two properties may be structurally orthogonal under current reconstruction-centric SAE training.
