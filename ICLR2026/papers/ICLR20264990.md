## Problem

RL-based finetuning for multi-hop RAG has produced limited accuracy gains over simple prompting baselines like ReAct, and existing methods require 90k–100k+ labeled examples, making them impractical for domains with scarce supervision. The paper reframes the question: rather than using RL to increase reasoning steps, can it be used to adaptively prune search depth based on question difficulty, achieving competitive accuracy with far fewer retrieval calls and only 1,000 training examples?

## Input / Output

The system takes a complex user question and a document index (e.g., 21M Wikipedia passages) as input. At each hop, the reasoner model produces a thought–action–search-query triplet; the search query is passed to a retriever that returns top-k documents. The process continues until the model emits a FINISH action (adaptive, question-dependent number of hops) or hits a maximum budget B=6. A separate off-the-shelf answer generator LM then produces the final answer conditioned on the full accumulated trajectory. The key output characteristic is that the number of search queries varies per question—simpler questions terminate in 1–2 hops while harder ones use 4–5+.

## Method

FRUGALRAG is a two-stage finetuning framework that decouples evidence exploration from stopping decisions. Stage 1 generates training data via ReAct-style rollouts with DsPy prompt optimization: at each hop, n bootstrapped candidate queries are generated, the one yielding highest recall against ground-truth evidence is selected, and the process repeats up to budget B. The model is then supervised-finetuned on 90% rollouts without FINISH and 10% with FINISH, producing a base policy f_S that prioritizes broad exploration while retaining FINISH in its output distribution. Stage 2 applies GRPO reinforcement learning to teach the model when to stop: the reward function defines an optimal hop h* (the minimum hop where recall ≥ τ) and penalizes both early stopping (insufficient evidence) and late stopping (redundant retrievals), with a bonus for perfect stopping that scales with h*/B to reward correct termination on harder questions. This two-stage decomposition is critical—jointly optimizing query generation and stopping via RL leads to unstable training where models either over-retrieve or stop too early. The entire pipeline requires only 1,000 annotated examples (ground-truth evidence documents, not final answers), a 100× reduction over prior RL-based RAG methods.

## Results

On HotPotQA with E5-base-v2, FRUGALRAG (7B + Qwen2.5-7B-Inst) achieves 58.5% MBE and 70.4% recall with only 2.89 average searches, trained on just 1,000 examples. With a stronger answer generator (Qwen2.5-32B), MBE improves to 61.4% while recall and search count remain unchanged. On the challenging BrowseCompPlus deep-research benchmark, the same 7B model generalizes zero-shot to 20.5% accuracy, surpassing DeepSeek-R1-600B (16.4%) and Search-R1-32B (11.1%) while issuing 7–10 adaptive queries. Stage-2 RL reduces average searches by ~50% relative to the Stage-1 explore policy (e.g., 5.99 → 2.05 on HotPotQA with ColBERTv2) while improving MBE.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FRUGALRAG achieves the highest retrieval recall (70.4%) on HotPotQA, surpassing R1-Searcher (69.1%, 8k examples) and CoRAG (64.3%, >100k examples) while using fewer searches (2.89 vs. 4.00 for CoRAG) and 100× less training data. On MBE, FRUGALRAG with Qwen2.5-32B (61.4%) edges out CoRAG (58.2%) on HotPotQA, though CoRAG leads on 2Wiki (59.0 vs. 50.9) and MuSiQue (40.5 vs. 39.9) at the cost of a fixed 4-search budget and >100k training examples. Against Search-R1 (>100k examples), FRUGALRAG wins decisively on HotPotQA (58.5 vs. 46.2 MBE; 70.4 vs. 48.2 recall) and MuSiQue (36.4 vs. 24.8 MBE). On the out-of-domain BrowseCompPlus benchmark, FRUGALRAG-7B (20.5% accuracy) outperforms DeepSeek-R1-600B (16.4%), Search-R1-32B (11.1%), and Qwen3-32B (10.7%), while adaptively issuing 7–10 queries versus the fixed 1–3 queries of those baselines. The key differentiator is that FRUGALRAG decouples retrieval from generation (using an off-the-shelf answer generator), so its gains are attributable to adaptive search rather than end-to-end generator training.

## Relation to Prior Work

FRUGALRAG sits at the intersection of two active lines of work: multi-hop RAG methods (IRCoT, Iter-RetGen, Self-RAG, CoRAG) that iteratively retrieve and reason but rely on fixed search budgets or 100k+ annotated examples, and RL-for-search approaches (Search-R1, R1-Searcher, LeReT) that use reinforcement learning to optimize query generation. The key departure is conceptual: whereas prior RL methods use rewards to encourage *more* search steps for harder questions, FRUGALRAG uses RL to learn *when to stop*, decomposing the problem into a supervised exploration stage (maximizing evidence coverage via ReAct-style rollouts) followed by an RL stage (GRPO) that adaptively prunes search depth. This two-stage 'explore-then-stop' formulation, combined with DsPy-based prompt bootstrapping for data-efficient training, distinguishes it from both the fixed-budget RAG literature and the scale-up RL-for-reasoning trend.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central practical contribution is demonstrating that 1,000 training examples suffice for competitive multi-hop RAG performance, a 100× reduction over prior methods, which is directly relevant for domain-adaptive RAG systems where labeled data is scarce. The finding that a well-prompted ReAct baseline already achieves strong recall (79.6% on HotPotQA) tempers enthusiasm for complex RL pipelines and suggests that prompt optimization may be underexplored. Open questions include whether the ground-truth-evidence-based reward design generalizes to settings without annotated evidence documents, whether the approach scales to truly open-web retrieval beyond Wikipedia, and whether the adaptive stopping behavior degrades under distribution shift beyond the tested BrowseCompPlus setting.
