## Problem

While search-based test-time scaling (Best-of-N, Tree of Thoughts, MCTS) has been widely applied to LLM self-improvement, learning-based approaches—particularly reinforcement learning—have received little attention at inference time. Existing in-context RL work is largely confined to bandit or simulated environments with small models, failing to address diverse open-ended tasks where natural language constitutes the action space. This paper asks whether RL can emerge during the inference phase of LLMs, enabling continual self-improvement without retraining.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a natural-language task description, a scalar reward function (rule-based, separately learned, or the LLM's own self-evaluation), and an experience buffer of all prior responses paired with their rewards. At each episode, the initial prompt concatenates the full experience buffer, the task description, and a short meta-instruction (exploration or exploitation). The output is a single text response per episode; the only supervision signal fed back is a numerical scalar—no textual feedback, verbal critique, or gradient-like instruction is provided. Rewards can be sparse (terminal only) or dense (per-step), and the same LLM may serve as both policy and reward model.

## Method

ICRL prompting is a minimal multi-round framework in which the LLM's forward pass implements reinforcement learning without any parameter update. After each response, a scalar reward is appended to the experience buffer; in the next episode, the buffer (all prior state-action-reward tuples) is concatenated into the prompt alongside the task description and an ICRL instruction that alternates between exploration (generate a response different from all prior ones) and exploitation (generate the best response informed by the highest-reward prior attempts). The design deliberately excludes textual gradients, prioritized replay, sampling heuristics, and other engineered modules, so that the only signal is the scalar reward itself—complying with the reward hypothesis. The paper provides a 'duck test' of emergent RL: response quality increases as context grows, performance drops when context is truncated or rewards are zeroed, and the model exhibits an exploration-exploitation trade-off, all hallmarks of an RL algorithm operating in the forward pass.

## Results

On Game of 24, ICRL Preset achieves a 90% success rate after 50 episodes, nearly doubling the 49% from Best-of-N (which uses ground-truth rewards for selection) and the 47% from Self-Refine. On Creative Writing, ICRL attains a 93.81% length-controlled win rate against Best-of-N and 86.32% against Self-Refine via Alpaca-Eval 2.0. On ScienceWorld, ICRL Preset reaches a mean running-max return of 88±0.7 (out of 100), outperforming Self-Refine (83±0.9) and Reflexion (74±1.1). Across Olympiad-level math (AIME, HMMT) and multiple open-source models (Qwen3-32B, Llama-4 Maverick, Phi-4), ICRL consistently surpasses Self-Refine and Reflexion by 5–20 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ICRL prompting decisively outperforms all three baselines across every benchmark. Against Self-Refine (Madaan et al., 2023), ICRL Preset achieves 90% vs 47% on Game of 24, an 86.32% pairwise win rate on Creative Writing, and 88 vs 83 mean return on ScienceWorld; on AIME with Qwen3-32B at 32k context, ICRL solves 46.66% vs 43.33%. Against Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023), the margins are even larger: 90% vs 44% on Game of 24, 59.48% win rate on Creative Writing, and 88 vs 74 on ScienceWorld. Against Best-of-N (Stiennon et al., 2022), which is given the advantage of ground-truth reward for selection on Game of 24, ICRL still achieves 90% vs 49%, and 93.81% win rate on Creative Writing. A key distinction is that Self-Refine and Reflexion rely on verbal/textual feedback that can accumulate hallucinations, whereas ICRL uses only scalar rewards, making it more robust over extended episodes (Self-Refine plateaus then declines after ~50 episodes on Creative Writing, while ICRL keeps improving).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the in-context reinforcement learning (ICRL) paradigm, originally demonstrated in small models on bandit and simulated environments (Laskin et al., 2023), to pretrained LLMs operating on diverse open-ended language tasks where natural language constitutes the action space. It departs from prior test-time scaling methods (Best-of-N, Tree of Thoughts, Self-Refine, Reflexion) by requiring only scalar reward signals rather than textual feedback, search heuristics, or engineered modules, positioning the LLM's forward pass itself as the RL algorithm. The work also connects to the broader 'reward is enough' hypothesis (Silver et al., 2021) and the in-context learning literature (Brown et al., 2020), arguing that the same mechanism enabling few-shot supervised learning can support reward-driven policy improvement.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The demonstration that pretrained LLMs natively possess ICRL capability—evidenced by exploration-exploitation dynamics, context-length sensitivity, and reward-dependent attention heads—opens a new, minimal paradigm for test-time scaling that requires no retraining and no external code. Key open questions include whether training-time interventions can further strengthen this capability, whether the self-evaluation ceiling is fundamentally lower than external-reward performance, and how ICRL scales to longer-horizon or multi-agent settings. The mechanistic analysis (29.1% of attention heads showing significant reward correlation) provides rare interpretability evidence for an emergent capability, but the practical ceiling and generalization to truly novel task distributions remain to be established.
