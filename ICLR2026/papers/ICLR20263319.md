## Problem

Current multimodal benchmarks are contaminated with shortcut questions solvable from a single modality alone, yielding unreliable model rankings and inflating evaluation costs. Classical Item Response Theory treats each item with a single scalar difficulty, making it impossible to determine whether a model's success on a multimodal question reflects genuine cross-modal integration or reliance on a single-modality shortcut. The paper asks: can we decompose model ability and item difficulty into modality-specific and cross-modal components to identify truly cross-modal questions and build compact, high-quality evaluation subsets?

## Input / Output

The method consumes a binary response tensor R' where each VLM answers each question under four input formats (image+text, image-only, text-only, neither), yielding a 4-way tensor of correct/incorrect outcomes. A key practical property is that the response matrix need not be dense: M2IRT and M3IRT can be trained on partially observed data (e.g., only 10% of all model-question pairs), reducing inference cost to ~13% of full evaluation. Outputs include per-model ability vectors (θ_base, θ_image, θ_text, θ_cross), per-question difficulty vectors (b_base, b_image, b_text, b_cross), and a compact question subset selected via adaptive testing that preserves the original model ranking.

## Method

M3IRT extends multidimensional IRT by decomposing both ability and difficulty into four latent components (base, image-only, text-only, cross-modal) gated by binary modality indicators s_image and s_text. The cross-modal term s_image·s_text activates only when both modalities are present, so a question with high b_cross genuinely requires integrating both modalities. M2IRT is a simpler variant that keeps scalar parameters but applies the same modality-gated decomposition to the 2PL logistic model. Parameters are estimated via SGD on the Bernoulli negative log-likelihood, and a Computerized Adaptive Testing loop selects the next item by maximizing Fisher information (M2IRT) or the determinant of the cumulative information matrix under D-optimality (M3IRT), yielding a compact subset that preferentially includes high-cross-modal-difficulty items while excluding single-modality shortcuts.

## Results

On contaminated benchmarks (50% low-quality questions), M2IRT achieves Spearman rank correlation of 0.96 on MMMU and 0.94 on SEED-Bench using only a 10% question subset, while M3IRT reaches 0.89 on MMMU and 0.94 on SEED-Bench at the same subset size. M3IRT notably achieves 0.92 rank correlation on MathVista with just 10% of items. In terms of low-quality question filtering, M2IRT includes only 3.8% low-quality items in a 10% MMMU subset (vs. 28–42% for baselines), and M3IRT includes 9.1%, compared to 28–42% for IRT, MIRT, TinyBenchmarks, and FlashEval.

## Baselines & Comparisons

M2IRT and M3IRT substantially outperform IRT and MIRT on MMMU and SEED-Bench: at 10% subset size, M2IRT achieves 0.96 vs. 0.50 (IRT) and 0.16 (MIRT) on MMMU, and 0.94 vs. 0.86 (IRT) and 0.42 (MIRT) on SEED-Bench. On MathVista, M3IRT (0.92) edges out IRT (0.81) and FlashEval (0.89) at 10%, while M2IRT (0.81) ties IRT. TinyBenchmarks is consistently outperformed by both proposed methods across all three benchmarks (e.g., 0.43 vs. 0.96 on MMMU at 10%). FlashEval is the strongest baseline on MathVista and SEED-Bench in terms of rank correlation, but it does not account for low-quality question contamination. The critical differentiator is low-quality filtering: at 10% subset size on MMMU, M2IRT includes only 3.8% low-quality items versus 28–42% for all baselines, and on MathVista M2IRT includes 1.1% versus 27–50% for baselines. Wilcoxon signed-rank tests confirm statistically significant differences (p < 0.0001) between M3IRT and FlashEval/TinyBenchmarks on MMMU and SEED-Bench across all subset sizes tested.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classical two-parameter logistic IRT model (Fan, 1998) and its multidimensional generalization MIRT (Reckase, 2009) by introducing a structured modality-based decomposition of both ability and difficulty parameters into image-only, text-only, and cross-modal components. It builds directly on the Computerized Adaptive Testing framework (Weiss & Kingsbury, 1984) for item selection, adapting Fisher information and D-optimality criteria to the multimodal setting. The key departure from prior IRT-based LLM evaluation work (TinyBenchmarks, MetaBench) is that those methods treat items as unimodal and cannot distinguish whether a correct answer reflects genuine cross-modal integration or a single-modality shortcut.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for anyone evaluating VLMs: achieving ~0.9 Spearman rank correlation with only 1–3% of benchmark items while filtering out low-quality shortcuts could substantially reduce the cost of model comparison as the VLM landscape grows. The modality decomposition also provides interpretable diagnostics (e.g., identifying models that rely heavily on text rather than visual integration). However, the evaluation is limited to multiple-choice vision-language questions, the contamination is semi-synthetic (image/text swapping), and the human-alignment study (Appendix B) reveals weak correlations between M3IRT's cross-modal difficulty and human answering patterns on MMMU and MATHVISTA, raising questions about whether the decomposition captures a universal property or a VLM-specific one. Extending to open-ended generation, additional modalities, and more realistic contamination remains open.
