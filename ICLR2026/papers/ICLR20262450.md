## Problem

Standard DP implementations (e.g., Opacus) default to add/remove adjacency, which protects membership but overstates protection against attribute or label inference for records known to be in the training set. The paper shows that the correct threat model for such attacks is substitute adjacency (replacing one record with another), and that empirical privacy leakage under substitute-style attacks can exceed the add/remove DP guarantees reported by standard accountants.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262450/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The auditing procedure takes a trained DP model's final parameters, a target training sample z, a crafted canary z' (either a gradient vector or an input-space sample), and the full set of training hyperparameters (noise multiplier σ, clipping bound C, subsampling rate q, learning rate η, number of steps T). It requires R=2500 repeated training runs (half with z, half with z') and produces step-wise distinguishability scores (logit differences for input-space canaries, or parameter-change dot products for gradient-space canaries), which are converted via Clopper-Pearson FPR/FNR bounds into a μ-GDP lower bound and ultimately an (ε,δ)-DP lower bound. The canary crafting itself requires a reference model trained without DP on the same dataset to compute gradient directions.

## Method

The paper develops five canary-crafting scenarios (S1–S5) spanning gradient-space and input-space under a hidden-state threat model. For gradient-space canaries, it identifies the least-updated model parameter and assigns opposing ±C gradients along that dimension to maximize distinguishability while maintaining near-saturation norms. For input-space canaries, it uses a reference model to minimize cosine similarity between target and canary gradients (crafted input), to find the worst-case mislabel, or to select the most adversarial natural sample from an auxiliary set. The key theoretical contribution is adapting the prv-accountant to compute tight substitute-adjacency privacy curves, showing that the standard group-privacy bound (ε_S = 2ε_AR) is suboptimal and that with fixed δ, ε_S can exceed 2ε_AR. Empirically, audits using these canaries on ViT-B-16 fine-tuned on CIFAR-10, Sentence-BERT on SST-2, and MLPs on Purchase100 reveal that empirical ε exceeds ε_AR(Accounting) while closely tracking ε_S(Accounting), confirming that add/remove accounting overstates attribute privacy protection.

## Results

Across all auditing scenarios (S1–S5), the empirical privacy leakage ε(Auditing) consistently exceeds the add/remove DP upper bound ε_AR(Accounting) while closely tracking the substitute-adjacency accountant's bound ε_S(Accounting). On CIFAR-10 (ViT-B-16 fine-tuning, T=500, C=2.0), gradient-space canaries (S2) yield the tightest audits, with ε(Auditing) violating ε_AR at all subsampling rates (q=1.0, 0.25, 0.0625). On Purchase100 (from-scratch MLP, T=2500, C=5.0), input-space canaries fail to exceed ε_AR, but crafted gradient canaries still produce ε consistent with ε_S(Accounting). The numerical substitute accountant is tighter than the group-privacy composition bound (Theorem 4.1: ε_S = 2ε_AR), particularly at large q and low noise σ where ε_S can exceed 2ε_AR for fixed δ=10⁻⁵.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is against the standard add/remove DP accounting (Abadi et al., 2016), which is the default in libraries like Opacus. The empirical audits demonstrate that add/remove DP overstates protection: ε(Auditing) exceeds ε_AR(Accounting) for fine-tuned models on CIFAR-10 and SST-2 at all tested subsampling rates, and for from-scratch models on Purchase100 when using gradient canaries. The second baseline is the group-privacy composition bound from Dwork & Roth (2014), which gives ε_S = 2ε_AR and δ_S = (1+e^{ε_AR})δ_AR. The paper's numerical substitute accountant (adapted from prv-accountant) produces strictly tighter upper bounds than this composition, and the empirical ε(Auditing) tracks the numerical accountant rather than the looser group-privacy bound. In summary, the paper 'wins' against both baselines in the sense that it reveals their inadequacy: add/remove DP provides weaker actual protection than its parameters suggest, and the group-privacy composition is a suboptimal proxy for true substitute-adjacency privacy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the DP auditing lineage established by Nasr et al. (2021, 2023) and Cebere et al. (2025), which all assumed add/remove adjacency, to the substitute adjacency relation that is the correct threat model for attribute and label inference. It adapts the crafted-canary methodology from Cebere et al. (2025) and the μ-GDP-based empirical ε estimation from Nasr et al. (2023) to a new adjacency setting, and departs from the common practice of approximating substitute DP via the group-privacy composition bound (Theorem 4.1, Dwork & Roth 2014) by instead using a dedicated substitute-adjacency PRV accountant adapted from Gopi et al. (2021). The conceptual departure is that prior auditing work validated add/remove guarantees, whereas this paper shows those guarantees are the wrong target when the protection goal is per-record attributes rather than membership.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is significant: practitioners using standard DP libraries (e.g., Opacus) who care about label or attribute privacy are being told their model satisfies (ε_AR, δ)-DP, but the actual protection against substitute-style inference attacks is closer to (ε_S, δ)-DP with ε_S substantially larger than ε_AR. This gap is empirically confirmed across multiple architectures (ViT-B-16, Sentence-BERT, MLP), datasets, and training regimes, and is most pronounced for fine-tuned models at high subsampling rates. Open questions include designing input-space canaries robust to training hyperparameters, reducing the computational cost of multi-run auditing, and extending the approach to non-gradient-based DP mechanisms such as label DP implementations.
