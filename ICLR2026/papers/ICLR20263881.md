## Problem

Several recent works claim LLMs match or exceed human forecasting performance, but the paper argues that backtesting-based evaluation introduces multiple forms of temporal leakage (logical, retrieval-based, and cutoff-related) that can inflate reported scores, and that even clean benchmark performance may not extrapolate to real-world forecasting due to piggybacking on human forecasts, correlated-betting incentives, and domain skew. The paper identifies these as two broad, under-analyzed categories of evaluation failure specific to LLM forecasters.

## Input / Output

The LLM forecasting systems under analysis consume forecasting questions (binary yes/no or four-option multiple choice) along with date-restricted retrieval results and produce calibrated probability predictions. The paper's own analysis takes existing benchmarks (ForecastBench, PROPHET, ProphetArena, Manifold, Metaculus, Polymarket) and published LLM forecaster pipelines as its empirical substrate, producing a catalog of concrete leakage examples, quantitative leakage rates, and a mathematical characterization of scoring-rule failures under label noise.

## Method

The paper systematically identifies and empirically demonstrates six failure modes across two categories. For temporal leakage, it quantifies logical leakage in prior benchmarks (3.8% of Halawi et al.'s questions resolved early; 10% of Tao et al.'s are trivially answerable), documents retrieval bias through concrete search-engine examples (e.g., 'jan 6' pre-2020 surfacing U.S. politics content), and demonstrates knowledge-cutoff inconsistency by jailbreaking GPT-4o to reveal post-cutoff information. For extrapolation failures, it shows how prediction-market data in training or retrieval enables circular 'LLM vs. human' comparisons, formalizes how a shared latent variable (e.g., a presidential election) creates incentives for correlated overconfident betting that outperforms calibrated forecasting on fixed benchmarks, and quantifies domain skew in ForecastBench (Security & Defense dominates at 3,353 of 6,402 questions). The paper further proves mathematically that constant label noise makes the logarithmic scoring rule non-strictly-proper (optimal strategy is clamping to [t*, 1-t*] with t* = 2η/(1+2η)), and shows that a 2021 DeBERTa classifier achieves up to 80% accuracy on LLM-generated binary questions, revealing exploitable phrasing shortcuts that require no genuine forecasting.

## Results

The paper identifies concrete quantitative evidence of evaluation flaws across existing LLM forecasting benchmarks. At least 3.8% of Halawi et al.'s dataset and 10% of Tao et al.'s dataset contain questions whose resolution is logically deducible from the backtesting date. A weak DeBERTa classifier (no retrieval, 2021 model) achieves >80% accuracy on Dai et al.'s binary questions (chance: 50%) and 55% on their four-choice MCQ (chance: 25%), indicating substantial shortcut exploitation. In ForecastBench, 52% of all questions (3,353/6,402) come from a single source (ACLED) in the Security & Defense category, revealing severe distribution skew.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new forecasting method to benchmark against baselines; instead, it audits existing LLM forecasting systems and benchmarks for evaluation flaws. Across the systems examined (Halawi et al., Phan et al., Dai et al., Tao et al., ForecastBench), the paper finds that no current evaluation protocol is free from at least one form of temporal or logical leakage. Halawi et al.'s and Phan et al.'s systems rely on date-restricted retrieval that demonstrably leaks future information (e.g., 'jan 6' searches before 2020 return U.S.-politics-heavy results). Dai et al.'s news-generated questions are so specific that a 2021 DeBERTa model without retrieval achieves 80%+ binary accuracy, far above the 50% chance baseline, suggesting the task is solvable via surface-level shortcuts rather than genuine forecasting. ForecastBench shows fewer obvious leakage issues but suffers from extreme domain concentration (52% Security & Defense from ACLED) and template-driven non-market questions that reduce to time-series prediction. The paper concludes that relative rankings between systems may also be distorted, since different systems can exploit benchmark shortcomings to different degrees.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes well-known backtesting pitfalls from the financial trading and forecasting communities (survivorship bias, point-in-time data contamination, risk-adjusted performance) and applies them systematically to the emerging LLM forecasting literature. It does not build on a specific forecasting method but rather critiques the evaluation protocols used in recent works (Halawi et al., 2024; Phan et al., 2024; Dai et al., 2025; Tao et al., 2025; Karger et al., 2024a), showing that issues partially acknowledged in the forecasting community become particularly acute when the 'forecaster' is an LLM with retrieval, training cutoffs, and access to human prediction markets. The paper carves new ground by providing concrete empirical demonstrations (e.g., DeBERTa shortcut classifiers, search-engine retrieval bias screenshots, GPT-4o cutoff jailbreaks) rather than purely theoretical arguments.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Multiple high-profile papers have claimed LLMs match or exceed human forecasting performance, and this paper argues those claims rest on evaluation methodologies with systematic leakage and gaming vulnerabilities that could inflate reported scores. The narrowing backtesting window (knowledge cutoffs approaching release dates) makes the problem more urgent, as fewer clean evaluation periods remain for frontier models. Open questions include: how to design forecasting-specific training that avoids temporal leakage, whether any current LLM forecaster's performance survives the proposed stricter protocols, and how to construct benchmarks that measure genuine forecasting edge over human crowds rather than piggybacking on existing market data.
