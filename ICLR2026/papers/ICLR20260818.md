## Problem

Existing HDR novel view synthesis methods assume static scenes, while dynamic NVS methods are confined to LDR imagery, leaving the combined challenge of reconstructing temporally coherent HDR radiance fields from sparse, time-varying LDR observations unaddressed. Real-world dynamic scenes introduce non-rigid motion, shifting illumination, and exposure-dependent radiance clipping that violate the assumptions of both static HDR and dynamic LDR pipelines simultaneously.

## Input / Output

The method consumes synchronized multi-view, multi-exposure LDR video sequences (3 exposure settings per viewpoint per timestamp) along with known camera intrinsics, extrinsics, and exposure times; synthetic scenes are 800×800 with exposures at 0.125/2/32 s, while real scenes are 4032×3024 with scene-specific exposure triplets. Optional paired HDR ground truth (generated via UltraFusion for real data or re-synthesized for synthetic data) can be used for joint supervision. At inference, given a novel timestamp and viewpoint, the model produces both a linear-radiance HDR image and a tone-mapped LDR image, with the LDR output rendered directly from the 3D Gaussian Splatting rasterization.

## Method

HDR-4DGS builds on 4D Gaussian Splatting, extending the per-Gaussian color representation from LDR to HDR via 4D spherical harmonics that encode temporal radiance evolution. The core contribution is a Dynamic Tone Mapper (DTM) that explicitly bridges HDR and LDR domains: it maintains a radiance bank of per-timestamp mean HDR color statistics, feeds a sliding window of k=20 frames through a GRU-based Dynamic Radiance Context Learner to produce a temporal context embedding, and applies per-channel tone-mapping functions conditioned on log-scaled HDR colors, log exposure time, and this embedding. Training jointly optimizes geometry, HDR radiance, and tone mapping using a weighted combination of L1 and D-SSIM losses in both LDR and HDR domains, with an additional pixel-level supervision on the tone mapper (applied to the 2D-rasterized HDR image) that complements the standard ray-level 3DGS supervision to improve CRF learning robustness.

## Results

On HDR-4D-Syn with LDR-only supervision, HDR-4DGS achieves HDR PSNR 25.88, SSIM 0.865, and LPIPS 0.076, substantially outperforming the strongest HDR baseline HDR-HexPlane (PSNR 14.70, SSIM 0.649, LPIPS 0.287) by over 11 dB in PSNR. On HDR-4D-Real, HDR-4DGS reaches HDR PSNR 14.50, SSIM 0.884, LPIPS 0.200, surpassing HDR-GS (PSNR 13.27) and HDR-HexPlane (PSNR 9.31) while matching HDR-NeRF on PSNR (14.60) but leading by a wide margin in SSIM and LPIPS. Inference speed is 40.80 fps, approximately 36× faster than HDR-HexPlane (1.61 fps) and over 600× faster than HDR-NeRF (0.061 fps).

## Baselines & Comparisons

HDR-4DGS decisively outperforms all five baselines on HDR-4D-Syn under LDR-only supervision, achieving 11.2 dB higher HDR PSNR than HDR-HexPlane (25.88 vs. 14.70), 21.2 dB over HDR-GS (4.64), and 17.3 dB over HDR-NeRF (8.54), while also leading in SSIM and LPIPS across the board. On HDR-4D-Real, HDR-4DGS slightly trails HDR-NeRF in HDR PSNR (14.50 vs. 14.60) but dominates in perceptual quality (SSIM 0.884 vs. 0.711; LPIPS 0.200 vs. 0.411), and the authors attribute the PSNR gap to noise in UltraFusion-generated ground truth and PSNR's known bias toward over-smoothed outputs. Against HDR-GS, HDR-4DGS wins on all three HDR metrics on both datasets (e.g., SSIM 0.884 vs. 0.783 on Real). Inference speed is a major differentiator: HDR-4DGS runs at 40.80 fps, roughly 36× faster than HDR-HexPlane (1.61 fps) and over 600× faster than HDR-NeRF (0.061 fps), while HDR-GS is faster (380 fps) but produces far inferior HDR quality. The non-HDR baselines (HexPlane, 4DGS) cannot produce HDR output at all, limiting their applicability to this task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two previously separate research threads: dynamic novel view synthesis (4DGS, HexPlane, D-NeRF) and HDR novel view synthesis (HDR-NeRF, HDR-GS, GaussHDR). It adopts 4DGS as its scene representation backbone and borrows the per-channel tone-mapping function structure from HDR-GS, but departs from both by introducing a temporally adaptive tone-mapping mechanism (DTM) driven by a GRU-based radiance context learner. The key conceptual departure is that prior HDR NVS methods assume static scenes with fixed tone-mapping, while prior DNVS methods ignore the LDR-to-HDR radiance gap entirely; HDR-4DGS is the first to jointly model both dimensions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By formally defining HDR DNVS and releasing two benchmark datasets (HDR-4D-Syn, HDR-4D-Real), this paper opens a research direction that is arguably more realistic than either static HDR NVS or LDR-only DNVS. However, the real-world dataset is small (4 scenes, 6 cameras), HDR ground truth relies on a 2D fusion method (UltraFusion) that lacks multi-view consistency, and the method still exhibits structural degradation in movable regions inherited from 4DGS. Open questions include whether the dynamic tone-mapping idea generalizes to non-rigid deformations, asynchronous multi-camera setups, and longer temporal sequences, and whether the GRU-based context learner can be replaced by more expressive architectures as scene complexity grows.
