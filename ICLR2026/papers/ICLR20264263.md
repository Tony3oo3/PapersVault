## Problem

Single-Goal Contrastive RL (SGCRL) was shown to solve long-horizon goal-reaching tasks without external rewards, curricula, or subgoals, but the mechanism driving its emergent exploration remained unexplained. This paper asks: why does SGCRL explore effectively in the absence of any obvious intrinsic or extrinsic reward signal, and does this behavior depend on neural network function approximation or arise from the algorithm's optimization objective alone?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264263/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The agent receives a state observation s and a single fixed goal state g, and outputs a goal-conditioned action a ~ π(·|s,g) via a softmax policy over discrete actions or a parameterized actor for continuous actions. No rewards, demonstrations, or subgoals are provided; the only supervision signal is the contrastive alignment between state-action embeddings φ(s,a) and future-state embeddings ψ(s_f) trained with a backward InfoNCE loss. The critic encodes a log-Q value φ(s,a)ᵀψ(s_f) = log p_γ^π(s_f|s,a) − log p(s_f), and all representations are ℓ₂-normalized.

## Method

The paper provides a theoretical decomposition of SGCRL's actor-critic dynamics into an implicit reward-maximization problem: the actor maximizes a discounted sum of ψ-similarity rewards (ψ(s_t)ᵀψ(g)), while the critic's InfoNCE updates dynamically reshape this reward landscape. Before goal discovery, contrastive learning suppresses the shared component of representations parallel to ψ(g), driving ψ-similarity of explored states toward zero and pruning them from future exploration (Theorem 2). After goal discovery, positive-example alignment increases ψ-similarity along the successful trajectory, creating a high-similarity trace that shifts behavior from exploration to exploitation. A simplified tabular model of SGCRL (using lookup-table embeddings and ground-truth dynamics) reproduces these dynamics, demonstrating that the exploration mechanism arises from contrastive learning of low-rank representations rather than from neural network generalization.

## Results

This is primarily a theoretical analysis paper, so headline results are theorems rather than benchmark scores. The key quantitative finding is that replacing SGCRL's vectorized low-rank representations with a scalar state-goal similarity lookup table (same contrastive objective) causes the agent to require ~100× more samples to solve the Tower of Hanoi task, demonstrating that the exploration mechanism depends on the geometry of low-rank representations, not merely the contrastive loss. Theorem 1 establishes that the SGCRL actor implicitly maximizes a discounted return of ψ-similarity (ψ(s_t)ᵀψ(g)), and Theorem 2 proves that InfoNCE updates drive representations of unsuccessful trajectories to become orthogonal to the goal embedding. Intervention experiments confirm the agent navigates toward high-ψ-similarity regions and systematically avoids regions set to negative goal similarity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary baseline comparison is with R-MAX (Brafman & Tennenholtz, 2002), a provably-efficient optimism-based exploration algorithm for tabular MDPs. The authors show that SGCRL and R-MAX share qualitatively similar exploration dynamics: both start with the belief that all states are equally desirable (high ψ-similarity in SGCRL, maximum reward in R-MAX) and progressively correct these estimates as states are visited. However, SGCRL achieves this through contrastive representation learning rather than explicit value estimation, and the paper argues this mechanism extends naturally to continuous, high-dimensional settings where R-MAX does not apply. Additionally, the paper compares single-goal data collection (SGCRL's strategy) against multi-goal (uniform distribution) data collection on the FourRooms task, finding that single-goal collection is necessary to push representations of frequently visited states away from the goal, enabling effective exploration; the multi-goal variant fails to produce this pruning effect.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides the first mechanistic analysis of SGCRL (Liu et al., 2025), a single-goal contrastive RL algorithm that builds on the contrastive RL framework of Eysenbach et al. (2022). Rather than proposing a new algorithm, it takes an algorithmic-interpretability approach inspired by cognitive science—combining rational analysis of the optimization objective, controlled intervention experiments, and a simplified tabular model—to explain why SGCRL explores effectively without explicit rewards. It connects the emergent exploration to reward shaping, successor representations, and posterior sampling, but shows these arise implicitly from the InfoNCE objective rather than being explicitly designed. The key departure from prior interpretability work (which typically requires modified architectures or auxiliary tasks) is that the analysis operates purely on the actor-critic objectives without any system modifications.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that exploration dynamics stem from low-rank contrastive representations rather than neural network function approximation challenges the prevailing assumption that emergent behavior requires large models, and provides a principled path toward provable sample-complexity guarantees by connecting SGCRL to R-MAX/PSRL-style exploration. The safety-aware exploration application (manipulating ψ-similarity to constrain agent behavior) demonstrates practical utility beyond pure understanding. However, the analysis is limited to goal-reaching tasks, the tabular model assumes deterministic dynamics and ground-truth transitions, and no formal sample-efficiency bounds are established—leaving open whether the mechanism generalizes to reward maximization more broadly or to stochastic, high-dimensional continuous control at scale.
