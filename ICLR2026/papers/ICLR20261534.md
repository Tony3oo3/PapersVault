## Problem

Prior work by Huang et al. (2025) established that transformers eventually achieve length generalization once training sequence length exceeds some finite threshold, but left open how large that threshold must be. This paper provides the first quantitative bounds on the minimum training length N required for a transformer to generalize from sequences of length ≤ N to sequences of arbitrary length, across settings of finite- vs. infinite-precision attention and one- vs. two-layer architectures.

## Input / Output

The theoretical framework takes two limit transformers f and g (each Δ-periodic, τ-local, translation-invariant, with bounded weight norms) and asks: if f and g agree to within ε on all inputs of length ≤ N, how large must N be to guarantee they agree to within O(ε) on all inputs of arbitrary length? The bound N is expressed as a function of the transformers' parameter norms, positional embedding periodicity Δ, locality parameter τ, vocabulary size |Σ|, logit margin γ, and target error ε. Empirically, the method trains depth-1 or depth-2 transformers on synthetic token sequences (SimpleTask, ModPTask, in-context k-gram) and evaluates test loss as a function of test length exceeding the training length.

## Method

The core technique is a simulation argument: given a long input x, construct a shorter string z of length ≤ N such that f(x) ≈ f(z) and g(x) ≈ g(z), so agreement on short inputs implies agreement on long ones. In the finite-precision setting (Section 4), softmax attention reduces to a hardmax for sufficiently long sequences, and the simulation preserves empirical token frequencies within the hard attention pattern, yielding N = O(L²Δ⁷|Σ|⁶τ²/ε²). In the infinite-precision setting for two-layer transformers (Section 5), the simulation is constructed via the probabilistic method—randomly sampling a subset of tokens and showing in expectation that the empirical joint distribution of (τ-suffix, token histogram) pairs is preserved, yielding N ≲ (C(f)ε⁻¹)^{max(γ⁻¹,3)} where C(f) is an exponential complexity measure of the first-layer weights. The results are validated empirically on three synthetic tasks, confirming that plateau test loss decreases monotonically with training length and increases with task complexity parameters.

## Results

The paper's main contributions are quantitative training-length bounds for length generalization (LG). For single-layer finite-precision transformers, Theorem 4.1 shows that N = O(max{2^(p/γ), L²Δ⁷|Σ|⁶τ²/ε²}) suffices for ℓ∞ error control, while Theorem 4.2 gives N₀ = Õ(ε^(−2−2α₀⁻¹)) for average error under a Dirichlet-prior distribution. For two-layer infinite-precision transformers, Theorem 5.2 establishes N ≲ (max(C(f),C(g))·ε⁻¹)^(max(γ(f)⁻¹,γ(g)⁻¹,3)), where C(f) is an exponential complexity measure in first-layer weight norms. Empirically, on SimpleTask, ModPTask, and the in-context k-gram task, test loss plateaus at a finite value as test length grows, and the plateau decreases monotonically with training length while increasing with the relevant complexity parameter (ω, Δ, S, or k), qualitatively matching the theoretical scalings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing methods or baselines; it is a theoretical work that provides the first quantitative (non-asymptotic) bounds on the minimum training length for LG, building on the asymptotic existence result of Huang et al. (2025). The empirical section validates the paper's own theoretical predictions on three synthetic tasks rather than benchmarking against prior approaches. The key empirical finding is that trained transformers do operate in the hardmax attention regime predicted by the finite-precision analysis (Figure 3 shows near-uniform attention over the correct positional class and near-zero attention elsewhere), and that the qualitative dependence of the LG plateau on parameter norms, periodicity Δ, vocabulary size S, and locality τ matches the direction of the theoretical bounds. The paper positions its contribution as sharpening the understanding of *how long* training sequences must be, complementing prior work that only established *that* a finite threshold exists.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Huang et al. (2025), which formalized the RASP-L conjecture (Zhou et al., 2023) by showing that tasks expressible by 'limit transformers' admit length generalization at some finite training length. However, those results were asymptotic (identification-in-the-limit style) and did not quantify the required training length. This work provides the first non-asymptotic, quantitative bounds on that length, covering both finite-precision (hard attention) and infinite-precision (soft attention) settings, one- and two-layer architectures, and both worst-case and average-case error control. The unifying methodological tool is a 'simulation argument' showing that the transformer's internal behavior on longer sequences can be replicated by shorter sequences, which connects to but generalizes the token-frequency preservation ideas in prior LG analyses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

These bounds give the first concrete (if loose) guidance on how training context length must scale with model complexity, positional periodicity, locality, vocabulary size, and target accuracy — a question directly relevant to LLM training practice. The exponential dependence on the logit margin γ and on first-layer weight norms in the infinite-precision setting highlights a potential fragility: small changes in attention sharpness can dramatically increase the required training length. Key open questions include extending the analysis to deeper transformers, relating the training length bound to the length of the corresponding C-RASP program, broadening the average-case analysis beyond Dirichlet-distributed token sequences, and characterizing how practical positional encoding schemes (Alibi, RoPE, Abacus) affect the bound.
