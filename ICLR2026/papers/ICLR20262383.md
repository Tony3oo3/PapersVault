## Problem

Combining diverse LLMs at the weight level is impractical due to architectural incompatibilities and closed-source APIs, while existing macro-level coordination methods (routing, multi-agent scaffolding) rely on static human-designed patterns or expensive multi-model inference without a learned, adaptive mechanism. The paper asks whether a tiny coordinator—orders of magnitude smaller than the models it orchestrates—can acquire sufficient contextual understanding to make effective, task-aware delegation decisions across a pool of heterogeneous LLMs.

## Input / Output

At each turn, the coordinator receives the original query concatenated with the full multi-turn transcript, processes it through a 0.6B SLM, and extracts the penultimate-token hidden state (1024-dim) as its sole decision input. The lightweight head maps this to L+3 logits (7 for agent selection, 3 for role assignment), from which one LLM and one of three roles (Thinker, Worker, Verifier) are selected. The process iterates up to 5 turns, terminating early when the Verifier role outputs ACCEPT; the final answer is the last turn's output. The coordinator's own generated text is discarded—only its internal hidden state drives the decision.

## Method

TRINITY pairs a frozen Qwen3-0.6B backbone (with singular value fine-tuning on one layer, ~9K params) with a single linear head (~10K params) that reads the penultimate-token hidden state and emits agent and role logits, keeping total learnable parameters under 20K. The tri-role protocol assigns each selected LLM a distinct contract: Thinker produces high-level plans and decompositions, Worker executes concrete problem-solving steps, and Verifier evaluates correctness and signals termination via ACCEPT/REVISE. Training uses sep-CMA-ES (separable CMA-ES with diagonal covariance), a derivative-free evolutionary strategy that samples perturbed parameter vectors, evaluates each via full multi-turn rollouts, and recombines by fitness-weighted averaging. The paper provides theoretical analysis showing that under the problem's regime—~10K dimensions, binary terminal rewards, weak inter-block coupling, and tight evaluation budgets (1.5K–40K rollouts)—the objective exhibits strong block-ε-separability, making diagonal CMA-ES substantially outperform REINFORCE, random search, and imitation learning, which suffer from low signal-to-noise ratios or prohibitive label-generation costs in multi-turn settings.

## Results

TRINITY achieves a new state-of-the-art pass@1 of 86.2% on LiveCodeBench V6 (Jan–Apr 2025), surpassing GPT-5 (83.8%), Gemini-2.5-Pro (67.2%), and Claude-4-Sonnet (46.5%). On in-distribution benchmarks, TRINITY scores 88.00 on MATH500, 91.56 on MMLU, 40.72 on RLPR, and 61.46 on LiveCodeBench, yielding a mean relative error reduction of 21.9% over the second-best method. In zero-shot transfer to four unseen tasks, TRINITY achieves the highest average (54.21), beating every individual model in the pool on AIME (50.00), MT-Bench (9.60), and GPQA-D (76.82), and tying for first on BigCodeBench (35.80).

## Baselines & Comparisons

TRINITY outperforms all four multi-agent routing/collaboration baselines (MasRouter, RouterDC, Smoothie, MoA) across all four in-distribution benchmarks; notably, RouterDC degrades below random selection on RLPR (0.28 vs. 0.32), while MoA is inconsistent (strong on MATH500 at 0.83 but weak on LiveCodeBench at 0.39). Against single-model baselines, TRINITY beats GPT-5 on MATH500 (88.00 vs. 75.66) and LiveCodeBench (61.46 vs. 59.54), beats Gemini-2.5-Pro on MATH500 (88.00 vs. 83.05) and LiveCodeBench (61.46 vs. 46.51), and beats Claude-4-Sonnet on all four tasks; however, Gemini-2.5-Pro with 5× context retains a slight edge on RLPR (43.00 vs. 40.72). On the LiveCodeBench SOTA setting (unconstrained output length), TRINITY's 86.2% pass@1 exceeds GPT-5 (83.8%) by 2.4 points and Gemini-2.5-Pro (67.2%) by 19 points. In zero-shot transfer, TRINITY surpasses the best single model (Gemini Pro 2.5) by 1.87 points on average, with the largest gap on AIME (+3.33) and GPQA-D (+1.57). An LLM-as-coordinator baseline (Gemini 2.5 Pro prompted to select agents) scores only 53.76 average versus TRINITY's 70.44, confirming that learned coordination substantially outperforms prompting-based routing.

## Relation to Prior Work

TRINITY sits at the intersection of macro-level model fusion and evolutionary optimization. It departs from micro-level weight-merging methods (TIES-merging, Model Soups) that require weight access and architectural compatibility, and from single-step routing approaches (RouterDC, Smoothie, MasRouter) that select one model per query without multi-turn role assignment. The tri-role coordination protocol (Thinker/Worker/Verifier) generalizes beyond the fixed scaffolds of Mixture-of-Agents and Multi-Agent Debate, while the use of sep-CMA-ES (Ros & Hansen, 2008) for training—rather than REINFORCE or SFT—represents a deliberate methodological departure motivated by the paper's theoretical analysis of block-ε-separability. The singular value fine-tuning of the SLM backbone is adapted from Transformer-squared (Sun et al., 2025), and the hidden-state-as-representation idea draws on Allen-Zhu & Li's work on knowledge storage in LLMs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TRINITY demonstrates that fewer than 20K learnable parameters suffice to orchestrate seven frontier LLMs (including closed-source APIs) into a system that outperforms each constituent model and sets SOTA on LiveCodeBench (86.2%), offering a practical path to model composition without weight access. The theoretical characterization of when sep-CMA-ES dominates RL and random search under binary rewards and tight budgets (block-ε-separability) provides a reusable design principle for other black-box policy optimization problems. Key limitations include the inability to invoke tools or code interpreters (the coordinator can plan but not execute grounded actions), and the method's dependence on a fixed pool of agents. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger agent pools, whether the tri-role protocol is optimal versus learned role spaces, and whether the evolutionary training advantage persists as evaluation budgets relax.
