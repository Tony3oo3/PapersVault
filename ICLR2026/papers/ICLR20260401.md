## Problem

LLM-based auto-formalization of research-level and conjecture-level mathematical statements into Lean 4 fails due to three compounding issues: hallucination of non-existent Mathlib APIs, inability to synthesize new definitions for concepts absent from existing libraries, and semantic checkers that miss subtle definitional discrepancies hidden beneath superficially similar text. Prior methods handle undergraduate-level theorems but collapse to 0% accuracy on genuine open conjectures where novel prerequisite definitions must be created from scratch.

## Input / Output

The system takes a single informal mathematical statement in natural language (ranging from undergraduate theorems to open conjectures in commutative algebra) and produces a compilable Lean 4 formalization that may include multiple synthesized prerequisite definitions (e.g., new type classes, structures, or predicates) alongside the target theorem. A separate AriaScorer module then outputs a binary semantic-correctness verdict (pass/fail) by decomposing the statement into subtasks, retrieving authoritative Mathlib definitions for every Lean term via the jixia static analyzer, and aggregating per-subtask match labels through a fuzzy integral against a tunable threshold α.

## Method

Aria operates as a two-phase Graph-of-Thought agent. In the decomposition phase, it recursively expands the informal statement into a directed acyclic dependency graph of concept nodes, querying LeanSearch (a continuously updated Mathlib index) at each node to ground known concepts; ungrounded nodes trigger further top-down expansion until all leaves are resolved. In the synthesis phase, it generates Lean definitions bottom-up through the graph, with each node's output validated by a compiler-in-the-loop reflection loop (up to 16 iterations) that feeds error messages back to the LLM. The key differentiator from prior RAG-based formalizers is the GoT planner's ability to synthesize entirely new definitions for concepts with no Mathlib counterpart, rather than merely retrieving existing ones. AriaScorer extends LeanScorer's subtask-decomposition checker by injecting retrieved Mathlib definitions (name, kind, type, informal description) for every Lean term into the LLM prompt, enabling detection of implicit preconditions, definition discrepancies (e.g., QuaternionGroup 1 vs. 2), and hallucinated term interpretations that purely textual comparison misses.

## Results

On ProofNet, Aria achieves 91.6% compilation success and 68.5% final accuracy (passing both compilation and AriaScorer semantic check), substantially outperforming the best single-pass baseline Goedel-V2 (32.0% final accuracy). On the harder FATE-X benchmark, Aria reaches 44.0% final accuracy versus 24.0% for Goedel-V2 at pass@128 (which uses over 7× more API calls). Most strikingly, on 14 real-world homological conjectures in commutative algebra, Aria achieves 42.9% final accuracy while every baseline model scores 0%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Aria outperforms all six baselines on every benchmark by a wide margin. On ProofNet, Aria's 68.5% final accuracy more than doubles the best baseline (Goedel-V2 pass@1 at 32.0%) and nearly triples Gemini-2.5-Pro (27.8%). On FATE-H, Aria reaches 71.0% versus 43.0% for Goedel-V2 at pass@128, which required 128 sampling attempts per problem. On FATE-X, Aria's 44.0% final accuracy exceeds Goedel-V2 pass@128 (24.0%) by 20 percentage points despite using roughly 1/7 the API budget. The most dramatic gap appears on the Conjectures dataset, where Aria achieves 42.9% while Goedel-V2, Gemini-2.5-Pro, Kimina, and Herald all score 0%, indicating that no existing method can handle research-level conjecture formalization. The paper attributes baseline failures to two distinct weaknesses: general reasoning models (Gemini) hallucinate non-existent Mathlib APIs, while specialized formalizers (Goedel, Kimina, Herald) lack the mathematical reasoning to manage novel conceptual dependencies.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the auto-formalization pipeline from single-pass LLM generation (Goedel-V2, Kimina, Herald) to a structured multi-phase agent that combines Graph-of-Thought decomposition, Retrieval-Augmented Generation via LeanSearch, and compiler-in-the-loop reflection. For semantic verification, it builds directly on LeanScorer's subtask decomposition strategy (Xuejun et al., 2025) but departs from it by adding a term-level grounding step that retrieves authoritative Mathlib definitions via the jixia static analyzer, addressing LeanScorer's vulnerability to superficially similar but semantically distinct terms. The key conceptual departure from prior work is the ability to synthesize novel definitions bottom-up for concepts absent from Mathlib, rather than relying solely on retrieval of existing library entries.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 42.9% success rate on real-world homological conjectures (vs. 0% for all baselines) demonstrates that conjecture-level formalization—long considered intractable for automated systems—is now partially achievable, opening a path toward autonomous exploration of open mathematical problems. However, the conjecture dataset contains only 14 problems, and dependency graphs in current experiments are shallow (2–3 layers), so scalability to textbook-level formalization with deeper dependency chains remains an open question. The paper also identifies a practical trade-off: the GoT planner's modular style increases syntactic attack surface on simpler problems (higher compilation but lower accuracy on FATE-H), suggesting that adaptive decomposition depth may be needed. Computational cost (17.7 API calls per problem) also limits deployment at scale.
