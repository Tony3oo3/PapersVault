## Problem

Policy gradient algorithms for LLM reasoning (GRPO, PPO, DAPO) systematically reduce policy entropy during training, collapsing the distribution around already-high-probability solutions and degrading pass@k and sequential learnability. While asymmetric clipping (DAPO) and sequence-level clipping (GSPO) provide implicit entropy preservation, the paper shows that subtle implementation details—particularly BF16 quantization of model outputs—can silently reverse these protections, and no prior work provides a unified theoretical account of how each algorithm variant modulates entropy dynamics or offers explicit, adaptive entropy control.

## Input / Output

The method consumes task contexts (text prompts from AppWorld or AIME), terminal rewards (fraction of passing unit-tests for AppWorld; binary answer-match for AIME), and trajectories sampled from the current Qwen-3 policy (8B or 32B). Training uses LoRA (rank 16) on attention and MLP modules with Cut-Cross-Entropy to avoid materializing full logits. The output is an updated policy whose per-token entropy trajectory is tracked throughout training; the key diagnostic is cumulative entropy experienced over the optimization process, which correlates more strongly with final test accuracy than the endpoint entropy value alone.

## Method

The paper provides a first-order Taylor analysis (Theorem 1, Corollary 1) showing entropy change is proportional to the expectation of A(s,a)·L(s,a)·π(a|s), where L is mean-centered log-probability, establishing that positive advantage–log-probability correlation drives collapse. It proves PPO clipping bounds per-step entropy change (Theorem 2) and identifies that BF16 quantization of log-probabilities introduces a multiplicative upward bias in importance ratios (Theorem 3), creating an effective asymmetric clipping opposite to DAPO's entropy-preserving direction. Two explicit entropy-control mechanisms are proposed: REPO modifies the advantage as A_REPO(s,a) = A(s,a) − β_s·L(s,a) with an adaptive bidirectional controller on β (REPO-D decorrelates per-token; REPO-R rescales by |A|), and ADAPO dynamically adjusts DAPO's ε_high in [0.2, 0.32] based on whether observed entropy is above or below its initial value. Combined with numerical fixes (FP16 training and disabling FSDP2's output downcast), these methods maintain steady entropy throughout training, outperform their baselines on AppWorld and AIME, and retain trainability for sequential fine-tuning on new environments.

## Results

On AppWorld with Qwen-3-32B, RLOO with FP16 training achieves state-of-the-art 79% Test Normal and 71% Test Challenge. On Qwen-3-8B AppWorld, REPO-R (on GRPO) and ADAPO (on DAPO) both outperform their respective baselines in both test accuracy and entropy retention, with GRPO losing ~90% of its initial entropy over training while REPO-R maintains steady entropy. On AIME, all methods perform competitively with very close scores, suggesting the Qwen-3 base models are already heavily optimized for this benchmark. In sequential learning experiments, entropy-preserving methods (REPO, DAPO) retain exploration ability for second-stage training, while GRPO-collapsed models perform significantly worse on the new task.

## Baselines & Comparisons

REPO-R (built on GRPO) outperforms GRPO on AppWorld in both test accuracy and entropy stability, directly addressing GRPO's ~90% entropy collapse that leads to premature convergence. ADAPO (built on DAPO) outperforms DAPO by preventing both entropy collapse and uncontrolled entropy increase that DAPO can exhibit without an explicit controller. RLOO, when combined with the FP16 numerical fix, achieves the strongest overall AppWorld performance (79% TN / 71% TC with Qwen-3-32B), confirming that strictly on-policy training with proper numerical handling is most effective. GSPO provides implicit entropy preservation via sequence-level asymmetric clipping but does not match the explicit control of REPO or ADAPO. In sequential learning (AIME→AppWorld and AppWorld→AIME), GRPO-collapsed models lose exploration ability and perform significantly worse in the second stage, while REPO and DAPO retain ample entropy and continue to improve, demonstrating that entropy preservation directly translates to retained trainability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides a unifying theoretical lens—entropy dynamics under policy gradient—for understanding the behavior of existing algorithms like PPO, GRPO, DAPO, and GSPO, showing that their clipping mechanisms implicitly preserve entropy in different ways. It departs from prior work (Cui et al., 2025; Xi et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026) that identified the advantage-log-probability correlation driving entropy collapse by proposing explicit adaptive entropy control (REPO, ADAPO) rather than relying on implicit clipping asymmetries. A distinctive contribution is identifying implementation-level factors—BF16 output casting in FSDP2 and the choice between BF16 and FP16 training—that qualitatively alter entropy dynamics, explaining previously unexplained training instabilities as numerical artifacts rather than fundamental algorithm properties.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a simple dtype change (BF16→FP16) combined with a casting fix transforms DAPO from entropy-collapsing to entropy-increasing has broad practical implications for anyone debugging RL training instabilities with LLMs, as BF16 is the default in most training stacks. The insight that the entropy *trajectory* throughout training (not just final entropy) predicts downstream performance and sequential learnability changes how practitioners should monitor and intervene during RL post-training. Open questions include whether these numerical fixes and entropy control mechanisms generalize beyond the Qwen-3 family and two evaluated environments, whether the adaptive controllers require per-task tuning, and whether the throughput cost of strictly on-policy RLOO (which achieves the best results) can be mitigated in large-scale distributed settings.
