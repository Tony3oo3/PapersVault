## Problem

Current evaluations of LLM search agents rely almost exclusively on final-answer metrics (exact match, F1), which cannot distinguish between an agent that arrives at the correct answer through sound, evidence-grounded reasoning and one that hallucinates or ignores conflicting evidence. This answer-only paradigm masks critical epistemic failures—unsupported claims, failure to recover from poor search results, and premature answering—making it impossible to assess whether agents are genuinely reliable information-seekers.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes multi-turn agent response traces where each turn is a tuple of reasoning, search query, retrieved evidence, and (on the final turn) an answer, alongside the original question. It produces step-level annotations across 8 validated features (functional type such as InformationSynthesis/PlanFormation/StateAssessment for reasoning, InitialQuery/RefinedQuery/FollowUpQuery/RepeatQuery for search, plus binary grounding, clarity, and sufficiency labels) and three aggregate competency scores: Reasoning Quality Index (RQI), Evidence Recovery Function (ERF), and Calibration Error (CE). For large-scale deployment, GPT-4.1-mini serves as the LLM judge, annotating each step at ~$0.0087 and 2.48 s per trace with κ=0.731 alignment to human experts.

## Method

SeekBench follows a three-phase methodology grounded in Content Analysis (Krippendorff, 2018). Phase 1 iteratively refines an annotation schema from 12 candidate fields down to 8 mutually exclusive features, validated by three expert annotators over 190 traces (Cohen's κ > 0.8) and adversarially stress-tested with GPT-5-generated edge cases. Phase 2 applies latent construct inference to map observed behavioral patterns onto three theory-backed epistemic competencies: groundedness (evidence-supported reasoning), recovery (adaptive search reformulation), and calibration (evidence-aligned answer timing). Phase 3 operationalizes each competency as a formal metric over an evidence state E = C + Q ∈ {0,1,2}: RQI aggregates binary grounding labels across reasoning steps (decomposable by reasoning type and evidence state), ERF tracks the cumulative fraction of traces reaching E=2 or a correct answer over turns (with Kaplan-Meier survival analysis for right-censored traces), and CE measures the L1 deviation of the agent's answer probability from the ideal policy of answering iff E=2. The validated schema is then deployed as an LLM-as-judge pipeline (GPT-4.1-mini) to annotate 28,493 traces and 283,950 steps across seven QA benchmarks and six agent variants tuned on Qwen2.5-7B.

## Results

Across 28,493 traces on seven QA benchmarks, ASearcher achieves the highest overall F1 (39.77%), followed by Search-R1 (39.29%) and ReSearch (38.30%), while the base Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct scores 33.50%. Critically, Few-shot prompting achieves the highest Reasoning Quality Index (RQI = 0.27), outperforming all RL-trained agents, revealing that RL training improves answer accuracy but degrades evidence-grounded reasoning. RL-trained models reduce overconfident answering from 63.1% (Base) to 35.3% and achieve the lowest Calibration Error (CE = 0.309), while ASearcher shows the best Evidence Recovery Function (ERF) and Search-R1 emerges as the strongest synthesizer (+2.61 F1 average gain when generating answers from other agents' evidence).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ASearcher (Gao et al., 2025) achieves the highest F1 (39.77%) and best recovery (ERF) and calibration (CE = 0.302), benefiting from its training emphasis on adaptive re-planning and failure recovery. Search-R1 (Jin et al., 2025) achieves near-top F1 (39.29%) and is the strongest information synthesizer (+2.61 F1 in agent synthesis), but has the lowest overall RQI (0.08) and most conservative answering (only 8.5% answer rate at E=2), reflecting its accuracy-only RL objective. ReSearch (Chen et al., 2025) sits mid-pack (F1 = 38.30%) with moderate epistemic metrics. DeepResearcher (Zheng et al., 2025) has the lowest F1 among RL agents (36.00%) and poorest recovery performance. CoT (Wei et al., 2022) and ReAct (Yao et al., 2023) prompting strategies score lower on F1 (32.72% and 31.25%) but demonstrate competitive recovery (CoT achieves second-highest ERF), and their evidence traces yield the largest synthesis gains when paired with Search-R1 (up to +6.3 F1), revealing hidden reasoning capabilities masked by accuracy-only evaluation. On GAIA (Appendix K), WebSailor (Li et al., 2025) outperforms ASearcher at both 7B (18.2% vs. 16.5%) and 32B (32.4% vs. 29.2%) scale, with better calibration (CE = 0.31 vs. 0.35 at 32B) but weaker groundedness and recovery.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant answer-only evaluation paradigm (exact match, F1) for LLM search agents by introducing process-level epistemic evaluation. It draws on Content Analysis methodology (Krippendorff) for schema construction, LLM-as-judge pipelines for scalable annotation, and epistemic cognition theory (Greene et al.) to formalize three competencies—groundedness, recovery, and calibration—that prior faithfulness and reasoning-quality frameworks (Lee & Hockenmaier; Paul et al.) do not capture in the search-agent context. The paper positions itself as the first framework to operationalize epistemic competence specifically for agents that interact with external evidence sources, filling a gap between intermediate-step analyses (ground-truth tracking, retrieval separation) and final-answer metrics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework reveals a fundamental tension: RL training improves calibration (reducing overconfident answering from 63% to 35%) while simultaneously degrading evidence-grounded reasoning, a trade-off invisible to accuracy-only metrics. The agent synthesis experiments show that complementary epistemic strengths (e.g., Search-R1's synthesis + Base's evidence collection) can be combined for performance gains, suggesting modular architectures as a path forward. Limitations include evaluation primarily on Qwen2.5-7B-based agents and a single LLM judge (GPT-4.1-mini) for large-scale annotation, and open questions include whether these competency profiles generalize across model families and scales, and how to train agents that improve reasoning groundedness without sacrificing calibration.
