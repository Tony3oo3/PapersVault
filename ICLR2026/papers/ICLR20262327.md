## Problem

Optimizing exoskeleton assistance parameters requires human-in-the-loop experiments where participants walk for hours, creating a paradox: mobility-impaired individuals who would benefit most cannot tolerate such demanding procedures. While neuromechanical simulations offer a complement, no unified framework exists that both fits controllers to observed human adaptation patterns and predicts responses in unobserved assistive conditions, particularly for pathological gaits.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262327/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes gait parameters (step length in cm, step frequency in spm), exoskeleton control parameters (gain κ in Nm, delay Δt in seconds), and pathology parameters (muscle weakness and contracture ranges) as inputs to a 164-muscle, 23-bone musculoskeletal model simulated in DART. It produces full gait trajectories (joint kinematics, muscle activations, ground reaction forces) and, through the surrogate optimizer, outputs speed-dependent optimal (κ, Δt) pairs that minimize metabolic cost of transport. The exoskeleton applies hip torque via a delayed-output feedback law τ_exo(t) = κ·u(t−Δt) where u encodes relative hip motion, making κ effectively a stiffness parameter.

## Method

Exo-plore couples a Deep RL gait data generator (PoseNet + stable PD controller + supervised MCN) with a stochasticity-aware MLP surrogate optimizer. The key architectural contribution is the human-exoskeleton interaction (HEI) reward, which penalizes resistive power from the exoskeleton based on a loss-aversion-inspired resistance-minimization hypothesis, enabling the simulated character to actively adapt kinematics (e.g., increasing step size) to exploit assistance rather than merely tolerating it. Sim-to-real matching is achieved by tuning metabolic energy expenditure exponents (α, β) via two algorithms that align the simulated preferred walking speed and CoT curve with human experiments. The surrogate network is trained on Latin Hypercube-sampled simulation data using Huber loss plus a gradient-norm penalty (motivated by Lipschitz continuity) and L1/L2 regularization, producing a smooth differentiable CoT landscape that supports stable SLSQP-based optimization despite RL and simulation stochasticity. The MCN additionally employs an intra-muscular regularizer that enforces coherent activations among line muscles of the same anatomical group, preventing physiologically implausible segment-level control.

## Results

The HEI reward (r_HEI) achieves the highest Pearson correlation with human experimental data across all human-exoskeleton interaction metrics: 0.964 for RMS assistive moment, 0.462 for mean assistive power, and 0.919 for mean resistive power, compared to 0.745/−0.180/0.623 for assist-maximization and 0.726/−0.082/0.589 for no HEI reward. For pathological gait optimization, strong linear correlations (R²) between pathology severity and optimal exoskeleton gain are observed in 4 of 5 conditions: equinus (R² = 0.787–0.972), waddling (R² = 0.574–0.991), crouch (R² = 0.611–0.956), and calcaneus (R² = 0.589–0.944), while foot drop shows weaker and inconsistent trends (R² = 0.321–0.808) due to excessive gait variability from toe-ground collisions. The optimized delay parameter (Δt) decreases monotonically with increasing walking speed for able-bodied gait.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is an ablation of the human-exoskeleton interaction (HEI) reward design: the proposed resistance-minimization reward (r_HEI) versus an assist-maximization alternative (r_assist) and a no-HEI baseline. The proposed r_HEI dominates on all three interaction metrics, achieving a Pearson correlation of 0.964 with human RMS assistive moment data versus 0.745 (r_assist) and 0.726 (no r_HEI). For the critical assistive power scaling behavior, r_HEI reproduces a 1.73-fold increase in assistive power when delay increases from 0.05 s to 0.25 s at 4 km/h, closely matching the 1.88-fold increase in human experiments, while r_assist and no-r_HEI produce only 1.04-fold and 0.67-fold increases, respectively. In the pathological gait setting, r_HEI is essential for correct clinical interpretation: without it, calcaneus gait incorrectly shows decreasing optimal gain with severity (Group B behavior), whereas with r_HEI it correctly exhibits increasing gain (Group A), confirming that the reward captures the true biomechanical assistance needs. The framework is also positioned conceptually against human-in-the-loop optimization (HILO), which is limited to <30 iterations and infeasible for mobility-impaired users, whereas Exo-plore enables unlimited simulation-based exploration.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Generative GaitNet framework (Park et al., 2022) by incorporating exoskeleton dynamics into the RL state, action, and reward spaces, and builds on the MCN architecture from Lee et al. (2019) for muscle activation mapping. It departs from prior simulation-based exoskeleton work (e.g., Luo et al., 2024) that relied on imitation policies, instead introducing a novel human-exoskeleton interaction (HEI) reward grounded in the behavioral economics principle of loss aversion to model active human adaptation. The optimization component replaces the sample-efficient Bayesian optimization used in HILO with a data-rich surrogate network trained via Latin Hypercube Sampling, justified by the low cost of simulation rollouts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Exo-plore addresses a critical practical paradox: individuals with mobility impairments who would benefit most from exoskeletons cannot tolerate the hours-long HILO experiments needed to personalize assistance. By enabling simulation-based optimization that generalizes to five pathological gait types, the framework opens a path toward personalized exoskeleton prescription without demanding human trials. Key open questions remain: the lack of any human-subject validation (including patient populations), the reliance on stateless Hill-type muscle dynamics that cannot capture history-dependent force behavior, and the absence of subject-specific personalization all limit clinical translatability. Whether the linear severity-optimality relationships discovered in simulation hold for real patients is the central unresolved question.
