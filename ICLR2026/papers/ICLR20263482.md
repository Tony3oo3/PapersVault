## Problem

Inference-time reward model (RM)-based reasoning has been largely overlooked in favor of training-time optimization (RL, SFT) for LLM reasoning. This paper systematically diagnoses three underexplored failure modes of RMs at inference: RMs degrade performance on simple questions, their discriminative ability deteriorates as the number of sampled responses grows (an 'inverse long-tail' effect where low-frequency incorrect answers receive disproportionately high scores), and excessive search diversity (high temperature, wide/deep MCTS trees) further undermines RM accuracy.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text question, a policy LLM (e.g., Qwen2.5-3B, Llama3.1-8B), and a reward model (ORM or PRM) as inputs, and iteratively produces a final answer with an associated reasoning path. Unlike one-shot Best-of-N or single-tree MCTS, CRISP consumes the question across multiple generation rounds, where each round conditions on a dynamically updated prefix set extracted from the previous round's top-scoring clusters. Early termination returns a majority-vote answer (equivalent to Self-Consistency) when the number of distinct answer clusters drops below a threshold, avoiding unnecessary RM scoring on simple questions.

## Method

CRISP (Clustered Reward Integration with Stepwise Prefixing) is an iterative inference algorithm with five modules. In each round, the policy model generates n complete reasoning sequences conditioned on a prefix set (rather than expanding intermediate nodes step-by-step as in MCTS), which controls search diversity. Generated paths are then clustered by their extracted final answers, and reward scores are aggregated at the cluster level by summing normalized per-path scores—this naturally incorporates answer frequency into the ranking, directly countering the inverse long-tail phenomenon where rare incorrect answers receive inflated RM scores. An early-termination check exits the loop when the cluster count falls below a threshold (default 2), returning the majority-vote answer for simple questions. Finally, the top-k highest-scoring clusters contribute their best paths' prefixes (first i steps at iteration i) to form the prefix set for the next round, progressively narrowing the search space. A theoretical analysis shows that the cluster-level scoring ratio between a correct answer and a top-scoring negative scales with their frequency ratio, guaranteeing that frequent correct answers dominate when f_i >> f_j.

## Results

On MATH-500 with Qwen2.5-3B + PRM, CRISP achieves 76% accuracy, outperforming the best baseline (Beam Search at 73%) and MCTS+PRM (71%). On Llama3.1-8B + PRM, CRISP reaches 67% on MATH, a 5-point gain over the next-best method (BoN+PRM at 62%). Compared to DeepSeek-R1-Distill of the same scale, CRISP reduces average token consumption by up to 90% (e.g., 1,100 vs. 3,612 tokens on CSQA) while achieving higher accuracy on non-mathematical tasks (e.g., 72% vs. 65% on CSQA, 59% vs. 50% on LogiQA for the 7B model).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CoT (Wei et al., 2022), CRISP+PRM achieves large gains on all benchmarks, e.g., 76% vs. 46% on MATH (Qwen2.5-3B) and 67% vs. 38% on MATH (Llama3.1-8B). Against Self-Consistency (Wang et al., 2023), CRISP+PRM wins on MATH (76% vs. 64%; 67% vs. 57%) and OlympiadBench (39% vs. 31%; 26% vs. 16%) but roughly ties on GSM8K (96% vs. 83%; 95% vs. 91%). Against BoN Weighted and Beam Search (Snell et al., 2024), CRISP+PRM outperforms Beam Search on MATH (76% vs. 73% for Qwen; 67% vs. 56% for Llama) and OlympiadBench (39% vs. 34%; 26% vs. 15%), while matching or slightly exceeding on GSM8K. Against MCTS (Hao et al., 2023), CRISP+PRM consistently beats MCTS+PRM on MATH (76% vs. 71%; 67% vs. 57%) and OlympiadBench (39% vs. 31%; 26% vs. 19%), with a 5-point margin on Llama3.1-8B MATH. Against DeepSeek-R1-Distill (DeepSeek-AI, 2025), CRISP underperforms on math (79% vs. 88% for 7B) but outperforms on commonsense (72% vs. 65%), social (66% vs. 66%, tie), and logical reasoning (59% vs. 50%), while using up to 90% fewer tokens.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on two established threads: inference-time search strategies (MCTS, Best-of-N, Beam Search) and reward-model-guided reasoning (Math-Shepherd, Skywork, Skywork-o1-PRM). However, it departs from prior work by treating the reward model not as a fixed black-box scorer but as a component with identifiable, systematic failure modes at inference time. While earlier studies either improve the search algorithm or improve RM training, this is the first to diagnose three specific inference-time RM pathologies (easy-question degradation, inverse long-tail scoring, diversity sensitivity) and design a single algorithm (CRISP) whose modules directly target each one. The paper also positions itself as a counterpoint to the R1/RL training paradigm, arguing that inference-time optimization remains essential for smaller models and non-mathematical reasoning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The three diagnostic findings are broadly actionable for anyone deploying RMs at inference time: the inverse long-tail effect explains why simply increasing n in BoN can hurt accuracy, and the easy-question degradation suggests that RM-based search should be conditionally applied. CRISP is a training-free, plug-and-play method that works across multiple RMs and task types, and the 90% token reduction versus R1-distilled models while matching or exceeding accuracy on non-math tasks is practically significant for deployment. Limitations include evaluation primarily on math and a handful of reasoning benchmarks, a relatively simple theoretical justification for the clustering step, and continued dependence on a reasonably capable RM. Open questions include whether the clustering principle transfers to generative or uncertainty-aware RMs, and how CRISP interacts with RL-trained reward models that may already partially mitigate the inverse long-tail effect.
