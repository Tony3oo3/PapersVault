## Problem

When multiple GNNs have already been pre-trained on different domains with distribution shifts, there is no established method to merge them into a unified model that generalizes on unseen out-of-distribution graphs. Unlike standard domain generalization (which trains from scratch on multi-domain data) or conventional model merging (which assumes homogeneous architectures), this setting requires extracting domain-invariant knowledge directly from model parameters without access to original training data, while consolidating expertise from potentially heterogeneous GNN backbones.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a set of pre-trained GNN models (e.g., GCN, GAT, GIN) each trained on a different source domain, with no access to the original training graphs or labels. It produces a single merged model that outputs class labels for graph-level or node-level classification on an unseen target domain. The framework is architecture-agnostic, meaning the pre-trained experts can have different GNN backbones and hyperparameters, and operates entirely in a source-free regime where only model weights are available.

## Method

OGMM is a two-stage framework grounded in a mixture distribution assumption that the target domain is a linear combination of source domains. In Stage 1 (graph generation), each pre-trained GNN acts as a frozen teacher to train a label-conditional generator that synthesizes small sets of graphs from random noise, using a discrete edge encoder (MLP + Gumbel-Softmax) to produce adjacency matrices and BN-layer matching plus confidence regularizers to preserve domain-specific structure. In Stage 2 (model merging), the generated graphs train a Mixture-of-Experts module where each pre-trained GNN becomes a masked expert (masks applied to classifier parameters for lightweight fine-tuning) and a sparse TopK gating network adaptively routes each sample to the most relevant experts. The training objective combines cross-entropy loss with a gate load-balancing regularizer (coefficient of variation) and a mask constraint that limits parameter drift from pretrained values, yielding a theoretically justified approximation of the generalization risk under the mixture distribution.

## Results

OGMM achieves state-of-the-art out-of-distribution generalization across all four graph classification benchmarks. On REDDIT-B it reaches 76.98% accuracy and 63.36% precision, on NCI1 66.84% Acc / 72.90% Pre, on PTC 51.21% Acc / 57.39% Pre, and on MUTAG 45.62% Acc / 56.28% Pre. The largest gains appear on REDDIT-B (+22.6 Acc over the best generative baseline Multi-GFKD) and NCI1 (+19.3 Acc over Uni-Soup), while on PTC the improvement is more modest (+0.4 Acc over Multi-GFKD). All results are averaged over 10 runs with standard deviation reported.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: graph domain generalization (which typically trains a single model from scratch on multi-domain data), data-free knowledge distillation for GNNs (Deng & Zhang, 2021, which inverts pre-trained models to generate synthetic inputs), and model merging/MoE (predominantly studied for same-architecture vision and language models). The key departure is formulating a source-free, architecture-agnostic merging problem for heterogeneous pre-trained GNNs under distribution shift, where no original training data is available. The graph generation stage adapts the inversion paradigm from data-free KD to jointly optimize both node features and graph structure via a discrete edge encoder with Gumbel-Softmax, while the merging stage introduces a fine-tuned MoE with sparse TopK gating and parameter masks—a combination not previously applied to OOD graph model consolidation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The source-free setting addresses a practical gap: in real deployments, pre-trained graph models on different domains may exist without access to the original data (due to privacy, cost, or institutional boundaries), yet retraining from scratch is wasteful. The theoretical contribution—a generalization error bound for the merged model under a mixture distribution assumption—provides a principled justification for the two-stage design. However, the mixture distribution assumption may be too restrictive for complex graph domains with non-linear interactions, and the experiments rely on relatively simple domain splits (edge density) on small datasets, leaving open whether the approach generalizes to more complex distributional shifts, larger-scale heterogeneous architectures, or multi-class/unknown-class scenarios. The finding that reducing expert diversity (6 models → 2 GCNs) can improve performance also raises questions about the optimal number and heterogeneity of experts to merge.
