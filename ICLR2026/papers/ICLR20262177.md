## Problem

While prior work observed that different RoPE frequency bands serve distinct purposes in attention heads, no formal definitions existed for what it means for a head to behave 'positionally' versus 'symbolically,' no metric quantified these behaviors, and no proof established their mutual exclusivity. This paper addresses the gap by providing rigorous mathematical characterizations of both behaviors, a measurable score for each, and a theoretical exclusion principle linking them to attention weight uniformity.

## Input / Output

For the empirical analysis, the method consumes attention head logits from RoPE-based LLMs (Gemma-2-2B, Qwen-2-1.5B, Llama-3.2-1B/3B) on binding-task prompts of 256 entity-attribute pairs, decomposing each head's logits into 2D per-frequency sub-heads via RoPE's block-diagonal rotation structure. It outputs a pair of scores (positional, symbolic) per head per frequency, computed via cosine similarity of block-average attention vectors before and after block-swap permutations, yielding a full positional-symbolic profile across all layers and heads. For the canonical tasks, inputs are symbol-integer sequences of length n+1 and the output is the correctly retrieved symbol at a queried position.

## Method

The paper defines positional behavior as invariance of logits under permutation of key vectors and symbolic behavior as equivariance, then proves (Theorem 1) that a head close to both must produce approximately uniform attention weights. A practical metric partitions the sequence into contiguous blocks, performs pairwise block swaps, and scores positional/symbolic behavior via weighted cosine similarity of the resulting 2D attention-mass vectors. The authors introduce three canonical tasks—Index (purely positional), Information Retrieval (purely symbolic), and Partial Induction (requiring both)—and prove that each is unsolvable by a head exhibiting the wrong behavior type, while a single RoPE head with the appropriate angle (or two angles for the mixed task) solves it exactly. Empirically, they show a sharp frequency-behavior correspondence: low frequencies drive symbolic heads, moderate frequencies drive positional heads, and the highest frequencies produce heads with both scores elevated (uniform attention). Forcing a head to use the wrong frequency band produces U-shaped (positional task) or inverted-U-shaped (symbolic task) accuracy patterns as a function of answer position, which they prove analytically for the toy heads.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative finding is a strong negative correlation (Pearson r = −0.91, p ≤ 0.0001) between positional and symbolic scores across all attention heads in Gemma-2-2B, empirically confirming the theoretical positional-symbolic exclusion principle. Decomposing heads by RoPE frequency reveals a sharp correspondence: lower frequencies (higher frequency IDs) are associated with symbolic behavior, moderately larger frequencies with positional behavior, and the highest frequencies with both scores simultaneously (corresponding to near-uniform attention). On the canonical toy tasks, forcing a 1-layer transformer to use the wrong frequency band produces a U-shaped accuracy pattern for the positional (Index) task and an inverted U-shaped pattern for the symbolic (Retrieval) task, with the most severe degradation occurring at mid-prompt positions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a mechanistic-interpretability and theoretical-analysis paper rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so there are no direct performance baselines to beat or match. The closest prior work is Barbero et al. (2024), who observed in Gemma-7B that specific heads use high frequencies for positional patterns and low frequencies for semantic patterns. The present paper generalizes this observation to all heads and all layers across Gemma-2, Qwen-2, and Llama-3 families, and provides the first formal definitions, exclusion theorem, and quantitative metric for positional vs. symbolic behavior. The toy 1-layer experiments (Section 4) serve as a controlled comparison: a single RoPE angle suffices for purely positional (Index) or purely symbolic (Retrieval) tasks, but fails on the mixed Partial Induction task, which provably requires at least two frequencies (Theorem 6). No competing interpretability metric or positional-encoding method is benchmarked head-to-head.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the empirical observations of Barbero et al. (2024), who showed that specific RoPE heads use high frequencies for positional patterns and low frequencies for semantic patterns, by providing formal mathematical definitions of positional and symbolic attention behavior, proving their mutual exclusivity (Theorem 1), and introducing a general metric applicable to all heads and layers. The per-frequency head decomposition builds directly on the block-diagonal rotation structure of RoPE (Su et al., 2024), while the canonical tasks (Index, Information Retrieval, Partial Induction) draw on the 1-layer transformer expressivity literature (Sanford et al., 2023; Kozachinskiy et al., 2025). The work also connects to long-context extrapolation research (Liu et al., 2023; Men et al., 2024) by providing a mechanistic explanation for why RoPE base choice trades off positional vs. retrieval performance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The positional-symbolic exclusion principle and the accompanying metric offer a principled tool for characterizing how any Transformer model allocates its attention heads between positional and symbolic processing, which could inform architecture design (e.g., imposing inductive biases on selected heads) and long-context strategies. The U-shaped accuracy explanation for the 'lost in the middle' phenomenon provides a concrete mechanistic account linking frequency access to a well-known practical failure mode. Limitations include the focus on a single binding task for real-model analysis and the reliance on 1-layer toy models for theoretical proofs; open questions include whether positional and symbolic behaviors are truly the fundamental building blocks of all attention computation, and whether the framework extends to non-RoPE positional encodings or multi-head interactions.
