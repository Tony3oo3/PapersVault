## Problem

In offline goal-conditioned RL, temporal-difference value learning accumulates bias over O(T) Bellman recursions on long-horizon tasks, while Monte Carlo methods suffer from high variance over the same horizons. The paper asks whether the triangle inequality inherent in shortest-path distance functions can be exploited to perform divide-and-conquer value learning that reduces the number of biased recursions to O(log T) without incurring MC variance.

## Input / Output

TRL consumes offline datasets of reward-free state-action trajectories τ = (s₀, a₀, s₁, …, s_T) where states and goals share the same space S. The learned Q-function Q(s, a, g) takes a state, an action, and a goal state, and the extracted policy π(a|s, g) maps a state-goal pair to an action distribution. In oracle-representation environments, a distilled Q^φ(s, a, φ(g)) conditions on a compressed goal vector (e.g., 2-D x-y coordinates instead of a 69-D full state) to enable practical deployment.

## Method

TRL replaces the standard one-step Bellman backup with a transitive update: for a trajectory chunk (s_i, s_j), it samples a random in-trajectory subgoal s_k and regresses Q(s_i, a_i, s_j) toward the product Q(s_i, a_i, s_k)·Q(s_k, a_k, s_j), effectively splitting the problem into two smaller subproblems. To prevent catastrophic value overestimation from the max over subgoals under function approximation, TRL uses expectile regression (κ > 0.5) instead of a hard maximum, restricts subgoals to behavioral (in-trajectory) states, and applies distance-based re-weighting w = 1/(1 + log_γ Q)^λ to prioritize shorter chunks. This yields O(log T) expected Bellman recursions in the ideal tabular case (proven via a recursive analysis), and empirically outperforms TD, TD-n, and MC baselines on long-horizon robotics tasks requiring up to 4000 environment steps.

## Results

On the three long-horizon benchmark tasks (humanoidmaze-giant, puzzle-4x5, puzzle-4x6), TRL achieves overall success rates of 79±2%, 97±1%, and 51±5% respectively, outperforming or matching all TD- and MC-based baselines. On puzzle-4x5, TRL (97%) nearly doubles the performance of the next-best method MC (47%), while on puzzle-4x6 it leads MC (37%) by 14 points. On humanoidmaze-giant, TRL (79%) ties MC (79%) but both dramatically outperform all TD-based methods (≤23%). TRL is the only triangle-inequality-based method to achieve non-trivial performance on these tasks, whereas QRL, TDP, and COE all score near zero.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TRL decisively outperforms all TD-based baselines on the long-horizon tasks: IQL (3% on humanoidmaze-giant, 20% on puzzle-4x5, 17% on puzzle-4x6), SAC+BC (5%, 19%, 11%), HIQL (23%, 8%, 8%), and WGCSL (1%, 0%, 0%) all fall far short of TRL's 79%, 97%, and 51%. The MC baseline is the closest competitor, matching TRL on humanoidmaze-giant (79% vs 79%) but trailing by 50 points on puzzle-4x5 (47% vs 97%) and 14 points on puzzle-4x6 (37% vs 51%). Among triangle-inequality-based methods, QRL (quasimetric/hard constraint), TDP (hard max over state space), and COE (generator network) all achieve near-zero success rates on puzzle tasks and ≤3% on humanoidmaze-giant, confirming that TRL's soft triangle inequality with behavioral subgoals is essential for scaling. CRL (contrastive/MC-based) achieves 62% on humanoidmaze-giant but collapses to 0–1% on both puzzle tasks. FBC (flow-based BC) achieves 0% across all three environments, indicating that pure behavior cloning cannot solve these long-horizon tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

TRL builds on the triangle inequality structure in goal-conditioned RL, first identified by Kaelbling (1993) and later exploited via Floyd-Warshall-style value updates (Dhiman et al., 2018; Piekos et al., 2023). However, prior 'soft' triangle inequality methods (TDP, COE) had never scaled beyond 2D toy environments due to value overestimation from the max operator over subgoals. TRL departs from this lineage by combining expectile regression (from IQL, Kostrikov et al., 2022) with in-trajectory behavioral subgoals to make the transitive Bellman update stable under function approximation, and by framing the update explicitly as a divide-and-conquer value learning paradigm rather than a quasimetric constraint.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TRL is the first divide-and-conquer value learning method to scale to long-horizon robotic tasks (4000+ steps, 1B-sized datasets), demonstrating that the curse of horizon can be mitigated with O(log T) recursions rather than O(T) as in TD learning. Key limitations include the requirement for deterministic dynamics and restriction to goal-conditioned settings; extending the approach to stochastic environments or general reward-based RL remains open. The finding that behavioral subgoals suffice even with uniformly random atomic motions suggests the divide-and-conquer structure is more robust than previously assumed, potentially informing hierarchical or compositional value learning more broadly.
