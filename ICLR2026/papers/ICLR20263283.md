## Problem

In continual learning, neural networks can progressively lose the ability to adapt to new data (loss of plasticity), a distinct failure mode from catastrophic forgetting that remains poorly understood. The role of the activation function in this degradation has been largely unexplored, despite activation shape governing how much gradient signal survives backpropagation under non-stationary data. This paper asks whether activation design alone—without extra capacity or task-specific tuning—can serve as a primary, architecture-agnostic lever for sustaining plasticity.

## Input / Output

For supervised continual learning, the method consumes standard RGB images (28×28 MNIST, 32×32 CIFAR-100, 224×224 ImageNet) presented as a sequence of tasks without task-identity signals, and outputs class labels; evaluation uses Total Average Online Task Accuracy across the full task sequence. For reinforcement learning, the agent receives MuJoCo state vectors (heterogeneous observation spaces per environment) and outputs continuous actions, cycling through HalfCheetah, Hopper, Walker2d, and Ant three times with 1M timesteps per environment per cycle. In both settings, the only architectural change is swapping the activation function—no additional parameters, layers, or task-specific heads beyond the standard per-environment input adapter and output head in RL.

## Method

The paper identifies three design principles for plasticity-friendly nonlinearities through property-level analysis: (i) maintain a strict non-zero derivative floor on the negative side, (ii) keep the negative-side slope within a moderate 'Goldilocks zone' (effective slope ≈ 0.6–0.9), and (iii) prefer a C¹ smooth transition at the origin. Guided by these, it introduces Smooth-Leaky, f(x) = αx + (1−α)x·σ(cx/p), a C¹ drop-in replacement for Leaky-ReLU that preserves the negative-side floor and positive-side identity while removing the kink via a sigmoid-shaped transition controlled by (p, c). Randomized Smooth-Leaky further replaces the fixed α with a slope r drawn uniformly from [l, u] on each forward pass (fixed to (l+u)/2 at inference), adding lightweight exploration around the Goldilocks zone. The method is evaluated as a pure activation swap in compact CNN/MLP backbones across five supervised continual benchmarks (Permuted MNIST, Random Label MNIST/CIFAR, CIFAR 5+1, Continual ImageNet) and a non-stationary PPO RL protocol on MuJoCo, with a stress-test protocol (periodic pre-activation scaling shocks) linking activation shape to desaturation dynamics.

## Results

On the five continual supervised benchmarks, Rand. Smooth-Leaky achieves the highest Total Average Online Task Accuracy on CIFAR 5+1 (57.01%) and Continual ImageNet (86.23%), and is competitive with RReLU on Permuted MNIST (84.26% vs 83.95%), Random Label MNIST (93.33% vs 93.10%), and Random Label CIFAR (98.42% vs 98.02%). In non-stationary MuJoCo RL (PPO, 3 cycles × 4 environments), Rand. Smooth-Leaky attains the highest Plasticity Score (IQM 0.3875 ± 0.038), outperforming Swish (0.3149), Sigmoid (0.3329), and PReLU (0.2716). The paper also identifies a 'Goldilocks zone' for negative-side slope (0.6–0.9) where plasticity is maximized, and shows that activation rankings compress under i.i.d. training (range ~72%) but separate sharply under class-incremental learning (range ~21–33%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Rand. Smooth-Leaky is statistically significant (Welch's t-test, p<0.05) over the next-best activation on every benchmark except Permuted MNIST, where it edges out RReLU (84.26% vs 83.95%) and Leaky-ReLU (84.14%) by a narrow margin. The largest gains appear on CIFAR 5+1 (57.01% vs 53.60% for RReLU, +3.4 pp) and Continual ImageNet (86.23% vs 84.97% for RReLU, +1.3 pp), where the harder task sequences stress plasticity most. On Random Label MNIST and Random Label CIFAR, Rand. Smooth-Leaky matches or slightly exceeds RReLU (93.33% vs 93.10%; 98.42% vs 98.02%), while clearly surpassing smooth rectifiers like Swish (67.73%, 87.40%) and GeLU (38.79%, 42.85%) that lack a strict negative-side floor. In the RL setting, Rand. Smooth-Leaky's Plasticity Score (0.3875 IQM) exceeds Swish by 23% and PReLU by 43%, and it also shows a lower generalization-gap widening (ΔIQM = 59.03) than Sigmoid (85.53) in stable environments, indicating its trainability gains do not come at the cost of overfitting. Notably, Deep Fourier Features achieves 72.29% on CIFAR 5+1 (higher than Rand. Smooth-Leaky's 57.01%) but trails on all other benchmarks, suggesting its periodic-oscillation mechanism helps in one specific shift type but is less general. Budget-matched ablations (Table B1) confirm that Rand. Smooth-Leaky's advantage is not an artifact of a larger hyperparameter search: under matched random-search budgets it significantly outperforms most baselines (p < 10⁻⁴) on four of five datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions activation function design as a previously underexplored, architecture-agnostic lever for mitigating loss of plasticity in continual learning, building directly on the negative-side floor of Leaky-ReLU (Maas et al., 2013) and the randomized-slope mechanism of RReLU (Xu et al., 2015). It departs from prior plasticity-loss mitigation strategies—architectural refresh (Continual Backprop, Dohare et al., 2021), regenerative regularization (Kumar et al., 2023), and rational activations (Delfosse et al., 2021)—by arguing that the non-linearity itself is a more fundamental knob, and by introducing a property-level framework (negative-branch slope, saturation sidedness, dead-band width) that unifies the analysis of existing activations. The two proposed activations, Smooth-Leaky and Randomized Smooth-Leaky, are C¹ drop-in substitutes that combine the strict derivative floor of the Leaky-ReLU family with a smooth transition and bounded stochastic exploration, respectively.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a lightweight, domain-general design principle (the 'Goldilocks zone' for negative-side responsiveness, 0.6 ≤ s̄ ≤ 0.9) and a stress protocol for desaturation dynamics that practitioners can apply without extra capacity or task-specific tuning. However, the practical gains over strong baselines like Leaky-ReLU and RReLU are modest (a few percentage points on supervised benchmarks; a small plasticity-score margin in RL), and the experiments use compact networks with Adam—an optimizer known to exacerbate plasticity loss—limiting confidence that the findings transfer to large-scale or production settings. Open questions include whether the Goldilocks zone is universal across architectures and optimizers, how activation design interacts with other continual-learning techniques (replay, regularization), and whether adaptive per-neuron slopes can be guided to stay in-band without the drift observed in PReLU.
