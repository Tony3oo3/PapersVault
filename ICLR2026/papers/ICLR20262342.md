## Problem

When LLMs undergo inference optimizations (quantization, sparsity, hardware changes), even theoretically lossless modifications can produce different outputs due to floating-point non-associativity, making it unclear whether an observed accuracy drop reflects genuine degradation or harmless numerical noise. Existing practice relies on arbitrary hard thresholds (e.g., >2% accuracy drop or ≥5% flip probability) or naively treats paired accuracy estimates as independent, both of which either miss small real degradations or produce false positives. The paper asks: what is a statistically sound, controlled-type-I-error test for detecting whether a finite-sample accuracy deviation constitutes true model degradation?

## Input / Output

The method requires paired per-sample binary scores (correct/incorrect) from both the baseline model M and the optimized model M̃ evaluated on the same N benchmark examples, organized into a 2×2 contingency table (a, b, c, d). It outputs three p-values (pooled, max-drop, Fisher) and a binary degradation flag at a chosen significance level α. For non-binary metrics (e.g., ROUGE-L), the input becomes per-sample continuous scores aggregated over multiple runs, and the output p-values are computed via permutation tests with m random sign-flip iterations. The framework is implemented as a script on top of the LM Evaluation Harness, consuming its per-sample score files directly.

## Method

The core test is an exact one-sided McNemar test reframed as a binomial test on the conditional degradation probability q↓ = b/(b+c), where b counts examples the baseline solved but the optimized model failed, and c is the reverse; under H₀ (no degradation) q↓ = 0.5, so the p-value is simply P(Binomial(b+c, 0.5) ≥ b). A key theoretical result shows the signal-to-noise ratio √(N/p↑)·δ is invariant to removing non-flipping examples, motivating dataset compression to only likely-flip items (demonstrated to halve MMLU-Pro size while retaining most relevant signal). For multi-task aggregation, three complementary tests are proposed: a pooled test (summing b and c across tasks), a max-drop test (Monte Carlo simulation of the maximum standardized per-task statistic), and Fisher's method (combining per-task p-values via χ² with 2T degrees of freedom), with a combined decision rule that flags degradation if any test rejects at α. For non-binary scores, permutation-based variants using random sign flips replace the binomial test, preserving exact type-I error control at any sample size.

## Results

The proposed exact one-sided McNemar test detects empirical accuracy degradations as small as 0.29% (70B KV-FP8, p=2.51e-02) and 0.79% (8B KV-FP8, p=1.69e-05) as statistically significant at α=5%, while correctly failing to flag theoretically lossless variants (rerun, transformers, TP1, A100) whose flip probabilities range from 1.3% to 2.8%. INT4 quantization (w4a16) produces massive, unambiguous degradations (1.73% on 8B, p=4.80e-15; 39.46% on 70B, p≈0; 0.87% on Mistral, p=1.30e-05). The 2:4 sparse variant shows a 2.59% pooled drop with p=1.09e-19. Notably, FP8 on 8B has a high flip probability (8.71%) but no significant degradation (p=6.01e-01), demonstrating that flip probability alone is an unreliable degradation indicator.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the two hard-threshold rules from Dutta et al. (2024) — flagging degradation if pooled accuracy drop >2% or flip probability ≥5% — the proposed statistical test is strictly more informative. On the 70B KV-FP8 variant (0.29% drop, 4.18% flips), both Dutta baselines fail to flag the degradation, yet the pooled test yields p=2.51e-02, correctly identifying it as significant. Conversely, the 8B FP8 variant has a flip probability of 8.71% (exceeding the 5% threshold) but shows no actual degradation (p=6.01e-01), meaning Dutta's flip rule produces a false positive that the statistical test avoids. Against the naive independent-standard-error approach (Miller, 2024), which treats the two models' accuracy estimates as independent and thus overestimates Var[γ̂−β̂], the paired McNemar test is dramatically more powerful: on the 8B KV-FP8 case (0.79% drop), the naive approach fails to reach significance while the exact one-sided McNemar test achieves p=1.69e-05. The paper also demonstrates that even theoretically lossless changes (hardware swap, TP degree, serving framework) produce 1.3–2.8% flips, confirming that no fixed flip-probability threshold can serve as a principled null hypothesis, whereas the degradation probability q↓=1/2 under H₀ is exact by Fact 1.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts McNemar's (1947) paired-proportion test to the LLM evaluation setting, where the same benchmark examples are scored by both a baseline and an optimized model, and refines it into an exact one-sided binomial test on the conditional degradation probability. It departs from Dutta et al. (2024), who proposed hard flip-probability thresholds as a degradation criterion, and from the common practice in quantization literature (Kurtic et al., 2025; Frantar et al., 2023) of reporting raw accuracy differences without accounting for the paired nature of the comparison. For multi-task aggregation, the paper combines Fisher's (1932) p-value combination with two novel strategies (pooled and max-drop tests), and extends to non-binary scores via permutation tests.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As LLMs are increasingly deployed with quantization, sparsity, and KV-cache compression, teams need a principled way to distinguish genuine accuracy loss from evaluation noise; this paper provides a ready-to-use implementation on top of the widely adopted LM Evaluation Harness that can detect even 0.3% degradations as statistically significant. A key practical insight is that theoretically lossless changes (hardware swaps, tensor-parallelism changes) still produce 1–3% flip rates due to floating-point non-associativity, meaning hard thresholds on flip probability or accuracy drop will produce false positives or false negatives. Open questions include how the framework behaves for reasoning models evaluated over multiple runs at finite temperature (addressed preliminarily via permutation tests), whether the aggregation strategies remain well-calibrated as the number of tasks grows, and how to integrate these tests into continuous-integration pipelines for inference-engine development.
