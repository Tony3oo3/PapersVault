## Problem

Large EEG Models (LEMs) rely on encoder-centric architectures where high-capacity pretrained encoders are paired with lightweight classifiers, creating a decoder bottleneck that limits generalization and prevents full exploitation of learned representations. Existing LLM-centric alternatives merely shift the EEG-to-label mapping into text embedding space without resolving the inductive bias mismatch between static linguistic structure and dynamic neural signals, and neither paradigm supports in-context learning for dynamic task adaptation.

## Input / Output

ECHO takes raw EEG signals (resampled to 250 Hz, padded to 10 s) that are first aligned to a standardized 75-channel template via a channel-mapping preprocessing step, then segmented into sliding windows (length 100, stride 90) and encoded into token sequences. Optionally, a set of support EEG samples (typically 8) with their associated task and label tokens are prepended to the input sequence. The model autoregressively generates an output sequence consisting of a task token (e.g., motor imagery, emotion), a class label token, and an end-of-task token, effectively performing multi-task classification in a single forward pass without task-specific prompts.

## Method

ECHO reformulates EEG decoding as a decoder-centric sequence-to-sequence problem: a lightweight CNN encoder (4 conv layers + self-attention tokenizer) converts EEG into window-level tokens, and a 4-layer Transformer decoder with cross-attention performs next-token prediction over a unified sequence that interleaves EEG tokens, task tokens, and label tokens. A three-level hybrid positional encoding distinguishes temporal position within each EEG sample, sample-level identity (support vs. target), and textual token semantics, enabling the model to handle heterogeneous continuous and discrete elements in one serialized space. Training follows a two-phase strategy: a warm-up phase initializes the encoder with a unified classifier across all 12 datasets, followed by a contextual training phase where the decoder is first trained with a fixed 8-shot support set and then exposed to randomized support counts (0–12) to develop robust in-context learning. This design allows ECHO to autonomously infer both the task paradigm and the specific class label from EEG alone, and to adapt to new tasks at inference time purely through support-sample context without any parameter updates.

## Results

Under a strict multi-task setting (trained once across 12 datasets, no task-specific fine-tuning), ECHO with 8 support samples achieves the best Balanced Accuracy on 5 of 6 main benchmarks: SEED (0.8193), Stieger2021-LR (0.8534), High-Gamma (0.8552), Mental Arithmetic (0.6851), and Attention (0.8194), outperforming the strongest single-task baselines by +0.036 to +0.141 in ACC-B. On Mumtaz2016 (where no support is used due to single-label subjects), the encoder-only variant ECHO^E reaches 0.9698 ACC-B, surpassing CBraMod (0.8946) by +0.075. In zero-shot evaluation on unseen datasets, ECHO achieves 0.7782 ACC-B on BCIC 2020-T1 and 0.6044 on Cho2017, both exceeding the encoder-only variant and CBraMod on BCIC 2020-T1 (0.5700).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ECHO is evaluated under a unified multi-task protocol (single training run, no per-task fine-tuning), while all six baselines (EEGNet, BIOT, EEGPT, LaBraMod, CBraMod, CodeBrain) are fine-tuned separately per dataset. ECHO wins on 5 of 6 main benchmarks in ACC-B: SEED (+0.036 over CodeBrain), Stieger2021-LR (+0.011 over CBraMod), High-Gamma (+0.041 over CodeBrain), Mental Arithmetic (+0.053 over CodeBrain), and Attention (+0.141 over LaBraMod). On Mumtaz2016, the encoder-only ECHO^E variant dominates (0.9698 vs. CBraMod 0.8946), but full ECHO with support is not applicable due to single-label subjects. ECHO trails CBraMod on SEED-IV (0.3747 vs. 0.4146 ACC-B) and Stieger2021-UD (0.7311 vs. 0.7598), which the authors attribute to label-space overlap within the SEED family and the lightweight encoder's limited capacity for domain-specific structures. The ablation in Table 13 confirms that even without support samples, the Seq2Seq decoder outperforms the encoder-only classifier in 29 of 36 metric-dataset comparisons, isolating the paradigm shift as the primary source of gains rather than ICL alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

ECHO departs from the dominant encoder-centric paradigm in Large EEG Models (LaBraM, EEGPT, CBraMod, CodeBrain), where a powerful pretrained encoder is paired with a lightweight task-specific classifier, and also from the LLM-centric variant (NeuroLM, UniMind) that shifts decoding into text embedding space. Rather than building on a specific prior architecture, ECHO repurposes the well-established Seq2Seq formulation from NLP—next-token prediction over structured sequences—and adapts it to EEG through three targeted mechanisms: channel alignment preprocessing, hybrid positional encoding for heterogeneous tokens, and a multi-stage in-context training schedule. The authors deliberately use off-the-shelf components (a simple deep ConvNet encoder and a standard transformer decoder) to isolate the paradigm contribution from architectural novelty.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ECHO addresses a concrete bottleneck in LEMs: the decoder is orders of magnitude smaller than the encoder, forcing the encoder to 'bend' its representations during fine-tuning and limiting cross-task generalization. By making the decoder the primary learning site and enabling in-context adaptation via support samples, the framework offers a path toward unified multi-task EEG models that require no task-specific fine-tuning. Key limitations remain: the deliberately lightweight encoder caps performance on specialized tasks (e.g., TUEV, PhysioNet), label overlap across related datasets (SEED-IV vs. SEED-V) causes paradigm confusion, and ICL gains are sensitive to support-sample quality and cross-subject variability. Open questions include whether the paradigm's advantages persist with substantially stronger encoders, how to resolve label-space collisions in multi-task training, and whether the approach scales to continuous or regression-style EEG tasks.
