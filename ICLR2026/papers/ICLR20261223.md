## Problem

Many researchers hope SAEs can enumerate all features a model truly uses, presupposing a unique, objective decomposition of activation space. This paper tests that presupposition by asking whether SAEs trained on the same model and data, differing only in random initialization seed, actually discover the same features.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261223/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes the encoder and decoder weight matrices of two (or more) independently trained SAEs and produces a bijective latent-to-latent matching, a binary shared/unpaired mask per latent, per-pair matched cosine similarity scores, and an aggregate fraction of shared features. A latent is classified as shared only if it is matched in both the encoder-based and decoder-based Hungarian matchings with cosine similarity ≥ 0.7 in each, making the criterion deliberately conservative. The interpretability pipeline additionally outputs detection and fuzzing scores for each latent's LLM-generated explanation.

## Method

The paper trains multiple SAEs (TopK, ReLU, and Gated architectures) on identical data sequences with different random seeds, then aligns their latents using the Hungarian algorithm to compute an optimal bijective matching that maximizes the trace of the permutation-weighted similarity matrix for both encoder rows and decoder columns. A feature is deemed shared only when the same latent pair is selected by both the encoder and decoder matchings with cosine similarity above 0.7, yielding a conservative overlap estimate. The authors ablate across model scale (Pythia 160M, GPT-2, SmolLM, Llama 3.1 8B), layer position, number of latents, sparsity level (k for TopK), and training duration, and apply an automated interpretability pipeline (Llama 3.1 70B Instruct) to score whether unpaired latents still receive high-quality explanations. They also compare the Hungarian matching against a simpler maximum-cosine-similarity baseline, finding the two approaches yield nearly identical overlap fractions.

## Results

In a 131K-latent TopK SAE trained on Llama 3 8B, only 30% of features are shared across two random seeds; for a 32K-latent SAE on Pythia 160M, 42% of latents are shared. When considering all 9 independently trained SAEs, roughly 35% of latents are unique to a single seed, and this fraction decreases only slowly (following a power law with offset) as more seeds are added. ReLU and Gated SAEs trained with L1 sparsity show substantially greater seed-to-seed overlap than TopK SAEs, even at matched sparsity levels. The average automated interpretability score across 32K latents is 0.72, and a significant fraction of unpaired (seed-unique) latents still receive high interpretability scores, indicating that any single training run misses interpretable features.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical study without a single proposed method to benchmark against baselines; instead, it compares SAE architectures and configurations under a common alignment protocol (Hungarian matching on encoder and decoder vectors with a 0.7 cosine-similarity threshold). TopK SAEs show the lowest cross-seed overlap, while standard ReLU SAEs and Gated SAEs trained with L1 loss achieve notably higher overlap, consistent with prior reports of ReLU SAE stability (Leask et al., 2025; Braun et al., 2024). Across models, SmolLM and GPT-2 MLP latents exhibit more seed-to-seed overlap than their residual-stream counterparts, though this pattern does not hold for Pythia. Llama 3 8B TopK SAEs (131K latents, expansion factor 36) show the lowest overlap of any configuration tested. Increasing the number of total latents or the number of active latents (TopK k) both decrease overlap, whereas longer training increases it, suggesting the seed dependence is not primarily driven by feature absorption.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on comparing features across independently trained SAEs, which prior studies addressed using simpler metrics like mean maximum cosine similarity (Braun et al., 2024) or two-seed alignment heuristics (Marks et al., 2024). It adapts the Hungarian algorithm for bijective latent matching from the model-merging literature (Ainsworth et al., 2023) to the SAE interpretability setting, providing a more principled comparison than prior max-similarity approaches. The paper departs from the implicit assumption in much of the SAE literature—that different seeds recover the same underlying feature set—by systematically quantifying seed dependence across architectures (TopK, ReLU, Gated), models (Pythia, GPT-2, SmolLM, Llama 3 8B), and scales, building on but contradicting earlier reports of ReLU SAE stability (Leask et al., 2025; Braun et al., 2024).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that only ~30% of features are shared across seeds for Llama 3 8B directly challenges the 'strong feature hypothesis' that SAEs uncover a unique, objective decomposition of model activations, with implications for safety verification (e.g., checking that 'a model will never lie' requires enumerating all features). The observation that unpaired latents are often highly interpretable means any single SAE run systematically misses a substantial fraction of discoverable features, suggesting ensemble or multi-seed approaches may be necessary for comprehensive feature catalogs. Open questions include whether the nonconvexity of the SAE loss can be mitigated to recover more universal features, whether hierarchical or nonlinear SAE designs reduce seed dependence, and how to practically aggregate features across seeds into a coherent interpretability pipeline.
