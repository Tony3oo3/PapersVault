## Problem

Existing layer pruning methods for LLMs focus on identifying redundant layers to remove or replace, but largely overlook the internal dynamic flow of the model. The paper asks whether the high representational similarity observed between consecutive layers (indicating the system has entered a 'slow manifold') implies computational redundancy that can be captured by a much simpler function, and whether a suitable geometric embedding (kernel space) exists where the complex nonlinear inter-layer dynamics reduce to approximately linear transformations.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretrained LLM (e.g., LLaMA2-7B/13B, LLaMA3-8B, OPT-2.7B/6.7B) and a small calibration set of 4,000 samples drawn from 8 diverse datasets (PIQA, CMMLU, BoolQ, C3, MNLI, Race, SlimPajama). It outputs a pruned LLM in which blocks of up to K_max consecutive Transformer layers are replaced by a lightweight module consisting of a learnable RFF kernel map, a product of linear operators, and a two-layer MLP inverse network. The pruning ratio is approximately 25% of parameters, and no post-training or fine-tuning on downstream tasks is required (though optional LoRA-based PEFT on the output head is available for perplexity recovery).

## Method

KDP reformulates layer pruning as searching for an optimal geometric embedding in a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space where inter-layer dynamics become linear. Specifically, it uses a learnable Random Fourier Feature map with an anisotropic Gaussian RBF kernel (covariance parameterized as D + LL^⊤ with a low-rank factor) to project layer representations into a 2m-dimensional kernel space. In this space, a product of k linear operators {A_i} (each parameterized as I + γ_i B_i to preserve the residual structure) models the multi-step transition, jointly optimized with the kernel parameters via MSE and weighted cosine similarity losses. A simple two-layer MLP with a scalar scaling factor then maps the kernel-space prediction back to the original representation space, reconstructing the pruned block's output. The paper provides a k-step error bound (Theorem 1) showing O(1/√n) convergence and a kernel advantage theorem (Theorem 2) proving that the kernel space achieves strictly lower population risk than linear approximation in the original space, with block selection guided by CKA similarity scores excluding the first and last 10% of layers.

## Results

On classification benchmarks (12 datasets), KDP achieves 89.9% retained performance on LLaMA2-7B (53.11 avg accuracy at 22.8% pruning), 92.9% on LLaMA2-13B (59.89 avg at 23.4%), and 75.4% on LLaMA3-8B (52.77 avg at 25.8%), surpassing the best baseline by 9.1%, 8.3%, and 9.3 percentage points respectively. On generative tasks, KDP retains 79.7% ROUGE on XSum for LLaMA2-7B and 81.8% after PEFT fine-tuning, while perplexity on WikiText increases from 5.5 to 8.3 (LLaMA2-7B). Notably, KDP requires no post-training or fine-tuning to recover performance, using only 4k calibration samples for its kernel-space linearization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

KDP outperforms all six baselines across all three main models on classification benchmarks. Against the strongest baseline, Streamline (ICLR 2025), KDP gains 4.74 avg accuracy points on LLaMA2-7B (53.11 vs. 48.37) and 7.19 points on LLaMA2-13B (59.89 vs. 52.70), while also pruning fewer parameters (22.8% vs. 27.0%). ShortGPT (Findings of ACL 2024) is the best non-distillation baseline, reaching 84.6% RP on LLaMA2-13B, but KDP still leads by 8.3 percentage points. SLEB (ICML 2024) and SliceGPT (ICLR 2024) both suffer severe degradation on LLaMA3-8B (68.9% and 63.5% RP respectively), whereas KDP retains 75.4%. LLM-Pruner (NeurIPS 2023) and LaCo (Findings of EMNLP 2024) show the weakest performance, with LLM-Pruner dropping to 65.2% RP on LLaMA2-13B. A critical practical advantage is that KDP requires no fine-tuning or distillation to recover performance (unlike Streamline, LLM-Pruner, and SliceGPT†), using only 4k calibration samples for its two-step kernel-space training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant metric-driven layer pruning paradigm (ShortGPT, SLEB, LaCo, Streamline) that focuses on identifying which layers to remove or replace, and instead reframes the problem as searching for a geometric embedding—specifically a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space—where inter-layer dynamics become approximately linear. It builds on Random Fourier Features (Rahimi & Recht, 2007) by making the RFF covariance matrix learnable via a low-rank factorization, and draws on the 'slow manifold' concept from dynamical systems theory to justify why high representational similarity implies linearizability. The theoretical contribution (error bounds for k-step linear approximation in kernel space, and a kernel-advantage theorem) is novel relative to prior pruning work, which has largely lacked formal guarantees for layer replacement.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

KDP offers a principled, theory-grounded alternative to heuristic layer pruning that requires only 4k calibration samples and no downstream fine-tuning to achieve strong classification performance (89.9–92.9% retained). However, generation task results remain weak without post-training (PPL retention drops to ~10% on LLaMA3-8B), limiting practical deployment. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger models (tested only up to 13B), whether richer kernel families (beyond Gaussian RBF) improve generation fidelity, and whether the K_max ≤ 5 stability limit can be relaxed. The finding that the inverse mapping network converges in ~20 steps while the kernel linearization carries essentially all the modeling burden suggests the kernel space is doing the heavy lifting, which could inform future work on more expressive kernel designs.
