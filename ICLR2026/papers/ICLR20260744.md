## Problem

Existing neural scaling law theory primarily characterizes the role of width, pretraining data, or pretraining time, but cannot distinguish the relative performance gains from scaling width versus depth in transformers or justify common fixed-aspect-ratio scaling practices. This paper asks two questions: what sets the optimal transformer shape (width-depth ratio) under a compute budget, and how do the statistical properties of in-context learning tasks determine whether depth is beneficial at all?

## Input / Output

The model consumes a context matrix D containing P labeled D-dimensional input-output pairs (x_μ, y_μ) from linear regression tasks plus K unlabeled evaluation points whose targets are masked to zero. The output is a scalar regression prediction per evaluation point, and the theoretical analysis produces closed-form power-law exponents for time, width, depth, and context length, along with the compute-optimal width-depth ratio L ∝ N^ν. All quantities scale proportionally in the joint asymptotic limit (P/D = α, K/D = κ, B/D = τ), reducing total compute to converge by a factor of D compared to prior ICL scaling analyses.

## Method

The paper develops a solvable deep linear attention model whose residual stream implements preconditioned in-context gradient descent, reduced to gradient flow dynamics on a single D×D matrix Γ. They analyze three covariance structures of increasing generality— isotropic (ISO), fixed structured (FS), and randomly rotated structured (RRS)—showing that depth only improves ICL for ISO/FS at finite context length, while RRS requires depth even at infinite context because the model must learn a generic in-context GD algorithm rather than a covariance-specific preconditioner. For the RRS setting with a rank-N width bottleneck, they use dynamical mean field theory to compute a two-point deterministic equivalent for the loss landscape, handling the Haar-random orthogonal rotations that prevent a closed-form single-point solution. This yields a separable Chinchilla-like scaling law L(t,N,L,P) ≈ c_t·t^{−β/(2+β)} + c_N·N^{−νβ} + c_L·L^{−β} + c_P·P^{−νβ}, from which the compute-optimal joint scaling L ∝ N^ν follows. Predictions are verified numerically on both linear and softmax attention models trained with SGD and Adam, including experiments with untied layers, multiple heads, and MLP blocks.

## Results

The paper derives a separable Chinchilla-type scaling law for in-context regression with randomly rotated structured (RRS) covariates: L(t,N,L,P) ≈ c_t·t^(−β/(2+β)) + c_N·N^(−νβ) + c_L·L^(−β) + c_P·P^(−νβ), where β and ν are source/capacity exponents of the powerlaw feature spectrum. At fixed compute C = t·P²·N²·L, the optimal transformer shape scales as L ∝ N^ν. For isotropic (ISO) and fixed-structured (FS) covariates, depth L provides no benefit in the infinite-context limit (α→∞), where L=1 already achieves zero loss; depth only helps at finite α. The full (untied) linear attention module yields a modified time exponent: L(t,L) ~ c_t·t^(−5β/(5β+2)) + c_L·L^(−β), confirmed by numerical training of softmax attention models with Adam.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is across three ICL data settings (ISO, FS, RRS) and across model depth L. In the ISO and FS settings, shallow (L=1) and deep (L≥2) models achieve identical loss in the α→∞ limit, so depth is unnecessary; however, the FS solution is brittle to test-time covariance shifts (OOD loss grows as Σ' deviates from the pretraining Σ). In the RRS setting, depth is strictly beneficial at all context lengths, and both width N and depth L create independent bottlenecks: scaling only width hits a depth-limited asymptote, and scaling only depth hits a width-limited asymptote (Figure 5). The reduced-Γ theoretical predictions (gradient flow ODEs) closely match the training dynamics of actual deep linear transformers trained with SGD (Figures 1, 4, 6), and the qualitative depth-benefit pattern persists in softmax attention models trained with Adam (Figure 6c). The paper does not benchmark against external baselines; its contribution is the first solvable theory that simultaneously captures width, depth, context length, and pretraining time in a single scaling law for ICL.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the single-layer ICL theory of linear attention (Lu et al., 2025; Von Oswald et al., 2023; Ahn et al., 2023) to deep residual architectures, and simultaneously extends neural scaling law theory (Bordelon et al., 2024a; Bahri et al., 2021; Maloney et al., 2022) — which has so far treated only width, pretraining data, and time as resources — to include depth as a separate, structurally distinct resource. The analytical toolkit draws on dynamical mean field theory from spin glass physics (Sompolinsky & Zippelius, 1981) to handle the random-rotation covariance ensemble, and on the deep residual scaling protocol (μP for depth, Bordelon et al., 2024b) for stable architecture scaling. The key departure from prior work is the joint treatment of width and depth in a solvable ICL model, revealing that their roles are fundamentally different and data-statistics-dependent rather than interchangeable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first solvable model that yields a Chinchilla-like scaling law with separate, non-interchangeable terms for width and depth (L ∝ N^ν at fixed compute), directly addressing the open question of what sets optimal transformer shapes. The finding that depth is unnecessary at infinite context for fixed-covariance tasks but essential under random-rotation covariances provides a principled explanation for when deeper architectures are warranted, with practical implications for pretraining data design. Limitations include restriction to linear regression with linear attention and online learning; open questions include whether these depth-width separations persist for nonlinear tasks, whether overfitting from repeated contexts alters the scaling exponents, and whether the RRS construction captures the full diversity of real pretraining distributions.
