## Problem

Chess modeling has fragmented into disparate architectures for playing strength, human move prediction, and interpretability, and these designs are often misaligned with the geometric structure of the domain (e.g., linearized board representations or move-token sequences). The paper asks whether a single architecture, properly aligned with chess geometry, can advance all three objectives simultaneously.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261242/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes 64 square-tokens (each a 12-dim one-hot/zero vector indicating which piece occupies that square), concatenated across the current and n=7 past board states, plus two 128-dim soft rating embeddings for both players, yielding a per-token depth of 352. The board is flipped to the side-to-move perspective. The policy head outputs a 64×64 matrix of move logits computed as a scaled dot-product between source-square query vectors and destination-square key vectors, rather than a flat 1968-way classification or autoregressive token sequence; a separate value head outputs three logits for win/draw/loss.

## Method

Chessformer is an encoder-only transformer that treats the 64 board squares as tokens and augments self-attention with Geometric Attention Bias (GAB), a dynamic positional encoding that compresses the board state through a small MLP and generates per-head attention biases by dynamically mixing a set of learned templates, which are added to the dot-product logits before softmax. This allows attention to adapt to chess-specific geometry (piece movement patterns, game stage, pawn structure) rather than relying on fixed Euclidean or relative offsets. The policy head mirrors the action space's from-to structure by projecting encoder outputs into separate query (source) and key (destination) vectors and computing their scaled dot-product, yielding a 64×64 traversal matrix. The architecture is trained either by supervised learning on Lichess human games (MAIA-3, up to 79M params) or by distilling Leela Chess Zero self-play oracles (Leela-CF, 191M params), with GAB shown via ablations to outperform absolute and relative position encodings across Elo, puzzle accuracy, and policy/value metrics at comparable or lower compute.

## Results

On the ALLIE test set, MAIA-3-79M achieves 57.1% move-matching accuracy, surpassing the previous state of the art (ALLIE-ADAPTIVE-SEARCH at 55.9%) with fewer than a quarter of the parameters (79M vs. 355M) and no search. For raw playing strength, Leela-CF-value reaches 2466 Elo and 97.2% puzzle accuracy, outperforming the strongest prior searchless transformer (AC-270M at 2299 Elo, 94.2%) while using 64% fewer FLOPs (152B vs. 427B). When integrated into Leela Chess Zero, the Chessformer model adds over 100 Elo of engine strength, resulting in tournament victories over Stockfish in TCEC Cup 11, Swiss 6, and Swiss 7.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MAIA-3-79M (57.1%) outperforms ALLIE-ADAPTIVE-SEARCH (55.9%) and ALLIE-POLICY (55.7%) [Zhang et al., ICLR 2025] by 1.2–1.4 percentage points while using only 79M parameters versus 355M and requiring no search. Against MAIA-2 [Tang et al., NeurIPS 2024] (52.0%), MAIA-3 gains 5.1 points with comparable parameter count (79M vs. 23M), and against the original MAIA [McIlroy-Young et al., KDD 2020] (51.6%), the gain is 5.5 points with fewer parameters (79M vs. 92M). For playing strength, Leela-CF-value (2466 Elo) surpasses AC-270M [Ruoss et al., NeurIPS 2024] (2299 Elo) by 167 Elo while using 152B FLOPs versus 427B, and Leela-CF-policy (2374 Elo) beats Leela-CNN-policy [Pascutto & Linscott, 2019] (2096 Elo) by 278 Elo at comparable compute (7.6B vs. 12.5B FLOPs). The Chessformer-equipped Leela configuration also defeated Stockfish in three TCEC tournaments, a result no prior open-source engine configuration had achieved against the then-current Stockfish version.

## Relation to Prior Work

Chessformer unifies three previously fragmented lines of chess research—raw engine strength (AlphaZero/Leela Chess Zero, Stockfish), human move prediction (MAIA, MAIA-2, ALLIE), and mechanistic interpretability (McGrath et al., Jenner et al.)—under a single encoder-only transformer. Its square-token representation is closest to Ruoss et al. (2024) but deliberately rejects rotary position embeddings on a linearized board in favor of Geometric Attention Bias (GAB), a dynamic, board-state-conditioned positional encoding that has no direct precedent in prior chess or general transformer work. The source-destination attention-based policy head departs from both autoregressive move-token prediction (ALLIE) and MLP heads (MAIA-2), grounding the output layer in the geometric structure of the action space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central claim—that aligning tokenization, positional encoding, and output design with domain geometry simultaneously improves performance, human compatibility, and interpretability—offers a general recipe for structured decision domains beyond chess. GAB, as a domain-adaptive positional encoding, is a clean architectural primitive that could transfer to other games or spatial decision problems, though the authors note it is currently chess-specific. Key open questions include whether the interpretability gains (square-level feature attribution via transcoders) deepen into genuine mechanistic understanding of planning and evaluation, and whether the performance ceiling at low-to-mid skill levels (high aleatoric uncertainty) can be broken with richer history or individual-level modeling.
