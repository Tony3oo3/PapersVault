## Problem

As AI systems approach or surpass expert human performance on cross-domain tasks, no single human can verify correctness end-to-end, making standard evaluation and training pipelines infeasible. While individual domain specialists cannot confirm the right answer, they can reliably reject options outside their expertise (e.g., a cardiologist stating 'this is not a cardiovascular disease'). The paper asks whether these partitioned 'not-this-class' signals—complementary labels—can be turned into unbiased performance estimates and useful training signals without any ground truth.

## Input / Output

The method consumes multiple-choice items (K options, typically K=4) paired with complementary labels generated by routing each item to a randomly selected domain specialist who performs a single binary check on their assigned class, yielding either an ordinary label ('yes, this is class k') or a complementary label ('no, this is not class k'). The AI system under test produces a prediction ŷ for each item. The output is an unbiased point estimate of top-1 accuracy with finite-sample confidence intervals, and when plugged into agent-search pipelines (ADAS, AFlow), the estimator serves as a fitness score that drives the discovery of improved agentic workflows.

## Method

Under the assumption that the wrong index in each complementary label is sampled uniformly from the K−1 non-correct classes, the paper derives a simple linear correction Â_comp = (K−1)q̂ − (K−2) that is an unbiased estimator of accuracy from complementary labels alone, where q̂ is the empirical proportion of predictions avoiding the complementary label. To reduce variance, two mixture estimators combine scarce ordinary labels with abundant complementary labels: an inverse-variance-weighted (IVW) estimator with closed-form plug-in weights, and a joint maximum-likelihood estimator solvable as a quadratic. Finite-sample deviation guarantees are provided via Hoeffding and empirical Bernstein inequalities for both the complementary-only and mixed estimators. The framework is validated statistically on five LLM benchmarks and practically on financial (EDINET-Bench) and medical (Medical Abstracts) datasets, and is then used as a fitness signal in automated agent-search pipelines, where complementary-label rewards guide ADAS and AFlow to discover multi-stage agentic workflows that outperform manually designed baselines.

## Results

On the statistical validation (Table 1), the IVW and ML mixture estimators closely track the Ord-Eval oracle across five benchmarks: IVW achieves a macro-average of 72.74% vs. Ord-Eval's 73.57%, with per-benchmark deviations as small as 0.00 pp (MMLU-Pro) and 0.03 pp (MATH-CoT). On real-world benchmarks (Table 2), IVW and ML consistently achieve the lowest per-run standard deviation (e.g., 2.26 on EDINET-Extended, 0.73 on Medical Abstracts), confirming variance reduction over complementary-only estimators. In the agent search setting (Figure 3), ADAS and AFlow guided by complementary-label fitness signals consistently outperform all seven manually designed baselines (CoT, CoT-SC, Self-Refine, LLM Debate, Step-back Abstraction, Quality-Diversity, Role Assignment) on GPQA, Math-MC, and Medical Abstracts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

In the agent search experiments (Figure 3), both ADAS and AFlow trained with complementary-label fitness signals consistently outperform all seven manually designed baselines—CoT, CoT-SC, Self-Refine, LLM Debate, Step-back Abstraction, Quality-Diversity, and Role Assignment—across GPQA, Math-MC, and Medical Abstracts. On GPQA (the hardest benchmark), ADAS reaches 47.2% and AFlow 47.2% using raw complementary accuracy (q), surpassing the fixed-workflow baselines. On Math-MC, ADAS achieves 88.1% (q) and AFlow 86.8% (transformed), again exceeding all baselines. On Medical Abstracts, the transformed estimator is more beneficial: ADAS improves from 61.6% (q) to 67.8% (trans), and AFlow from 68.5% to 68.9%, both outperforming the manually designed pipelines. Among the estimators themselves, IVW and ML are nearly indistinguishable in practice (e.g., 72.74 vs. 72.62 macro-average in Table 1), both substantially outperforming the equal-weight IVW-0.5 baseline in within-run variance, while complementary-only estimators (Comp-n₀, Comp-Var) show much higher variance and less reliable point estimates.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes complementary-label learning (Ishida et al., 2017; 2019), originally developed to reduce annotation cost, as a principled supervision primitive for scalable oversight in the superhuman regime. It explicitly positions itself as orthogonal to other scalable-oversight strategies—weak-to-strong generalization, easy-to-hard generalization, debate, RLAIF, and recursive task decomposition—by exploiting the fact that domain specialists can reliably reject options outside their narrow expertise even when they cannot identify the correct answer. The unbiased estimator for top-1 accuracy is a direct 0-1-loss specialization of the risk-rewrite identity from Ishida et al. (2019), extended here with variance analysis, finite-sample Hoeffding/Bernstein bounds, and two mixture estimators (IVW and closed-form ML) that combine scarce ordinary labels with abundant complementary ones. The agentic training component builds on ADAS (Hu et al., 2025) and AFlow (Zhang et al., 2025b), replacing their ground-truth fitness signal with the proposed complementary-label estimator.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a concrete and growing bottleneck: as AI systems tackle cross-disciplinary problems beyond any single expert's verification capacity, standard evaluation and training pipelines (SFT, RLHF, RLVR) break down. The partitioned supervision protocol is practical because it leverages existing human specialization structures rather than requiring new annotation infrastructure. Key limitations include the multiple-choice format assumption, the uniform wrong-index sampling requirement, and the need for complementary labels to be well-defined—open-ended or highly ambiguous tasks remain out of scope. Open questions include robustness to biased or uncertain expert answers, overlapping expertise boundaries, and whether the framework extends to non-multiple-choice settings; the authors note that biased, noisy, and multi-complementary-label variants from prior work could be incorporated.
