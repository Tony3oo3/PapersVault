## Problem

Decoder-only LLMs allocate identical computation to every token at every layer, wasting FLOPs on low-importance tokens. Prior adaptive-compute methods—Mixture-of-Depths routers and early-exit heads—rely on hard discrete decisions or auxiliary supervision that destabilize training, require pre-training integration, or alter pretrained hidden representations. The paper asks whether a smooth, differentiable gating mechanism can be fine-tuned stably on top of existing pretrained models to enable token-wise layer skipping without these pathologies.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a text sequence and a per-layer fractional compute budget b ∈ (0,1] specifying what share of tokens may be processed at each layer. At inference it produces generated text, log-likelihood scores, and per-token scalar importance scores (the mean of the H-dimensional gate vector) that can be used for interpretability or safety monitoring. A key implementation detail is that skipped tokens copy their hidden state and KV-cache entries upward, so the output tensor shape and downstream attention remain unchanged regardless of how many tokens are skipped.

## Method

GateSkip inserts a sigmoid-activated linear gate g_ℓ(h_ℓ) = σ(W_G h_ℓ + b) at the exit of each Attention and MLP branch, element-wise multiplying the module output before it re-enters the residual stream: h_{ℓ+1} = h_ℓ + o_ℓ ⊙ g_ℓ(h_ℓ). Training jointly optimizes backbone and gate parameters with a cross-entropy loss plus an L2 sparsity penalty on gate activations, while the token budget decays linearly from 100% to 80% so the model learns to tolerate skipped hidden states. At inference, each token's H-dimensional gate vector is collapsed to a scalar importance score, a per-layer quantile threshold is computed from the empirical CDF, and tokens below the threshold are skipped (hidden state and KV cache copied upward). The critical design choice is placing the gate after the module rather than before it: the gate receives its gradient signal downstream of the module and learns to compress the module's contribution to the residual stream, rather than deciding what information should enter the module—ablations show entry-point gating collapses accuracy from 25.5% to 5.5% at just 5% compute savings.

## Results

On Llama-3.2-1b, GateSkip achieves 22.14% averaged generative accuracy at 15% saved compute, compared to 3.96% for MoD, 3.43% for CALM, 11.57% for FREE, and 10.65% for LayerSkip. On log-likelihood benchmarks at 15% savings, GateSkip scores 38.86%, matching or exceeding all baselines (MoD: 31.88%, FREE: 36.02%, LayerSkip: 38.25%). On instruction-tuned Llama-3b-Instruct, GateSkip improves generative accuracy from 36.5% to 49.0% at full compute and sustains 49.0% even at 20% savings, while matching baseline log-likelihood quality at up to 60% compute reduction. On out-of-domain PIQA-Gen, GateSkip exceeds the unadapted baseline (29.8–29.5 vs. 22.9) at 20–30% saved compute.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GateSkip decisively outperforms all prior adaptive-compute methods on generative (long-form reasoning) benchmarks. At 15% saved compute, GateSkip scores 22.14% averaged generative accuracy versus 3.96% for MoD, 3.43% for CALM, 11.57% for FREE, 10.65% for LayerSkip, and 0.05% for SkipLayer — a 5–500× margin. On log-likelihood benchmarks, GateSkip is competitive: it matches LayerSkip (38.86 vs. 38.25 at 15%) and outperforms MoD (38.86 vs. 31.88), CALM (38.86 vs. 30.73), and SkipLayer (38.86 vs. 23.03), while FREE achieves similar log-likelihood scores (36.02) but collapses on generation. Random skipping, the simplest baseline, drops generative accuracy below 10% even at 5% savings, confirming that learned token importance is essential. On instruction-tuned Llama-3b-Instruct, GateSkip improves generative accuracy by +12.5 points over the unmodified baseline at 0–20% savings and matches baseline log-likelihood at 60% savings, whereas random skipping collapses to 0.1–0.5% generative accuracy at 20% savings.

## Relation to Prior Work

GateSkip carves new ground at the intersection of Mixture-of-Experts gating and adaptive-depth inference. While router-based methods (Mixture-of-Depths, CALM, FREE) rely on hard discrete routing with load-balancing losses, and early-exit methods attach auxiliary LM heads to intermediate layers, GateSkip repurposes the smooth sigmoid gating familiar from MoE width-adaptation and applies it to the residual stream for depth-adaptation. The key conceptual departure is placing the gate *after* the module output rather than before the module input, which the ablations show is critical for stable learning. The method also differs from static layer-pruning approaches (ShortGPT, LayerSkip) by making the skip decision token-wise and layer-wise rather than fixed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GateSkip provides a practical, composable mechanism for adaptive compute that avoids the training instability plaguing router-based methods and the representation disruption of early-exit heads, making it a viable post-hoc addition to pretrained models. The interpretability findings—BOS tokens as structural anchors, safety-relevant spans highlighted by elevated gate values—open a new lens on transformer information flow that could inform both efficiency and safety monitoring. Limitations include evaluation only on 1–8B English models, reliance on fine-tuning (frozen backbones degrade substantially), and the gap between theoretical FLOP savings and realized wall-clock speedup. Open questions include whether large-scale pre-training with GateSkip would alter the BOS-token anchoring behavior and whether the method extends to multimodal or non-English settings.
