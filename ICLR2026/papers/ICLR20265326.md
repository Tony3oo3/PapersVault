## Problem

Existing model-editing-based backdoor attacks on safety-aligned LLMs rely on a single objective—promoting affirmative tokens—which is insufficient to bypass safety alignment throughout generation. The edited model often begins with a compliant prefix but reverts to explicit refusals mid-sequence, a phenomenon the authors term 'safety fallback.' No prior editing method jointly regulates both the promotion of target responses and the suppression of refusal behaviors across the full decoding trajectory.

## Input / Output

The method requires white-box access to a safety-aligned LLM's weights, a short trigger token (e.g., 'cf'), and a small proxy set of N=10 harmful prompts. It produces a single rank-one update to the W_out matrix of one chosen MLP layer (typically layer 5), completing in approximately one minute on a single A100 GPU. The resulting backdoored model preserves general capabilities (average benchmark drop <1.48%) and activates malicious behavior only when the trigger is present, with ASR_w/o remaining comparable to the pre-edited baseline.

## Method

DualEdit formulates backdoor injection as a dual-objective optimization over the FFN value vector at the trigger position: it simultaneously maximizes the log-probability of target affirmative tokens (Y⁺) and minimizes the log-probability of refusal tokens (Y⁻). Two techniques address practical challenges: (1) Dynamic Loss Weighting computes the pre-edit loss ratio between the two objectives to set a balancing coefficient λ, preventing either term from dominating optimization; (2) Value Anchoring clusters the value vectors of all affirmative/refusal tokens via K-means into a small set of anchor vectors, then promotes/suppresses only tokens within a cosine-similarity threshold of these anchors, reducing gradient conflicts from overly diverse token sets. The trigger-aware key vector is obtained by averaging FFN activations at the trigger position across N harmful inputs, and the final (k*, v*) pair is injected via a ROME-style constrained least-squares rank-one update that preserves original key-value mappings on non-trigger inputs.

## Results

DualEdit achieves the highest attack success rate (ASR_w) across all three models and three toxic-prompt datasets, improving the average ASR_w by 11.21% on DAN, 13.84% on DNA, and 4.97% on Misuse over the strongest baseline. Simultaneously, it reduces the Safety Fallback Rate (SFR) by an average of 10.88% compared to the best baseline, indicating more stable malicious outputs without mid-generation refusal. The without-trigger success rate (ASR_w/o) remains low and comparable to pre-edited models (e.g., 16.73% vs. 15.38% on LLaMA-2-7B DAN), confirming trigger selectivity. General capability degradation is minimal, with average benchmark drops below 1.48% across MMLU, SST-2, QNLI, BoolQ, GSM8K, and ARC.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DualEdit outperforms all four baselines (ROME, MEMIT, BadEdit, JailbreakEdit) on ASR_w in 11 out of 12 model–dataset combinations; the sole exception is LLaMA-3.1-8B on Misuse, where MEMIT edges ahead (61.33% vs. 59.12%). On SFR, DualEdit dominates across every setting, reducing the fallback rate by 10–25 percentage points relative to the best baseline (e.g., 18.21% vs. 37.71% for MEMIT on LLaMA-2-7B DAN; 28.40% vs. 48.30% for JailbreakEdit on LLaMA-3.1-8B DAN). ROME and MEMIT, which use single-objective value optimization, show the highest SFR values, confirming that promoting affirmative tokens alone is insufficient to prevent mid-generation safety reversion. BadEdit and JailbreakEdit, also single-objective, perform comparably to ROME/MEMIT on ASR_w but suffer similar SFR issues. The ASR_w/o (trigger leakage) remains within 1–3 percentage points of pre-edited levels for DualEdit, indicating the backdoor is well-activated only under the trigger condition.

## Relation to Prior Work

DualEdit builds directly on the ROME/MEMIT locate-then-edit paradigm, reusing the key-value memory formulation and the constrained least-squares closed-form weight update. It departs from prior editing-based backdoor attacks (BadEdit, JailbreakEdit) that optimize a single objective—maximizing affirmative token probability at the trigger position—by formulating backdoor injection as a dual-objective problem that simultaneously promotes target responses and suppresses refusal tokens. The two stabilization techniques (dynamic loss weighting and value anchoring) are novel contributions that address practical optimization challenges specific to this dual-objective setting, rather than general model-editing improvements.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of 'safety fallback' as a distinct failure mode of editing-based backdoor attacks is useful for the defense community: it shows that boosting first-token compliance is insufficient and that mid-generation refusal spikes represent a measurable, targetable vulnerability. However, the method's practical impact is bounded by its white-box assumption, single-trigger scope, and reliance on short-form affirmative templates, limiting direct applicability to proprietary or long-form generation settings. Open questions include whether the dual-objective formulation generalizes to multi-trigger or compositional triggers, and whether the value-anchoring clustering strategy scales to open-ended refusal taxonomies.
