## Problem

Existing 3DGS-based relighting methods rely on coarse shading decompositions—modeling only diffuse and specular reflections or approximating shadows and scattering with opaque neural networks—resulting in limited fidelity for anisotropic metals and translucent materials and poor physical interpretability. No prior 3DGS relighting framework explicitly and simultaneously models all four physically-motivated reflectance components (diffuse, specular, shadow, and subsurface scattering) in a way that supports faithful relighting under unseen illumination.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20265028/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes posed RGB images from one-light-at-a-time (OLAT) capture setups where each image has a single active point light at a known position; real scenes are at 512×512 or 1024×1024 and synthetic scenes at 256×256 or 512×512, with test-time lighting directions excluded from training. It outputs a relightable 3D Gaussian scene whose per-Gaussian attributes include learned base colors for each reflectance term, a 6-dimensional material embedding, and SSS optical coefficients (σs, σa, r), enabling rendering under arbitrary novel point-light configurations and producing per-component visualizations (diffuse, specular, shadow, SSS) for interpretability.

## Method

SSD-GS replaces 3DGS's spherical-harmonics color model with a four-term PBR shading function evaluated per Gaussian: a Lambertian diffuse term, a Fresnel-modulated anisotropic spherical Gaussian specular term, a two-stage shadow term (density-weighted per-ray transmittance refined by a 3-layer MLP), and a subsurface scattering term computed from the closed-form standard dipole BSSRDF with per-Gaussian optical parameters predicted by a 6-layer MLP conditioned on position, view/light directions, normals, and a learned material embedding. A key design choice is a progressive coarse-to-fine training schedule (Diffuse → Shadow → Scatter → Specular) that introduces each reflectance component sequentially to prevent gradient interference and improve disentanglement, while simultaneously refining camera poses and light positions. The shadow term modulates diffuse and specular contributions multiplicatively, while the SSS term is added independently, preserving physical separation between surface and volumetric light transport.

## Results

On the NRHints real OLAT dataset (7 scenes), SSD-GS achieves the best test PSNR on 6 of 7 scenes, with the largest gains on FurScene (30.73 vs. 28.22 for GS³, +2.51 dB) and Pikachu (31.93 vs. 29.41 for GS³, +2.52 dB). On the GS³ synthetic dataset, SSD-GS leads on 5 of 6 scenes, notably on AnisoMetal (30.04 vs. 28.82 for GS³) and FurBall (35.16 vs. 34.98). On the SSS-GS synthetic dataset emphasizing subsurface scattering, SSD-GS achieves 38.35 dB test PSNR, outperforming SSS-GS (35.01 dB) by 3.34 dB and KiloOSF (25.91 dB) by 12.44 dB, while also achieving the lowest LPIPS (0.0158 vs. 0.040 for SSS-GS).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SSD-GS outperforms the two OLAT-based relighting baselines (GS³ and RNG) on the majority of real and synthetic scenes. Against GS³, the largest PSNR gains appear on FurScene (+2.51 dB), Pikachu (+2.52 dB), and Pixiu (+1.42 dB), where complex fur and translucent materials stress shadow and scattering modeling. Against RNG, SSD-GS leads on Cat (+1.07 dB), Fish (+2.08 dB), FurScene (+3.04 dB), and Pixiu (+2.26 dB), while RNG retains a narrow edge on CatSmall (34.37 vs. 34.65 — actually SSD-GS wins by 0.28 dB) and CupFabric (37.32 vs. 37.47 — SSD-GS wins by 0.15 dB). On the GS³ synthetic set, SSD-GS surpasses GS³ on AnisoMetal (+1.22 dB) and FurBall (+0.18 dB) but trails slightly on Hotdog (32.13 vs. 32.72, −0.59 dB). On the SSS-GS subsurface-scattering benchmark, SSD-GS achieves 38.35 dB test PSNR versus 35.01 dB for SSS-GS and 25.91 dB for KiloOSF, with LPIPS of 0.0158 versus 0.040 and 0.097 respectively, confirming the advantage of the physically grounded dipole model over learned residual scattering. Vanilla 3DGS and GI-GS (static-lighting methods) are far behind on all OLAT scenes, with PSNR gaps of 8–13 dB, confirming that dynamic OLAT supervision is essential for relighting.

## Relation to Prior Work

SSD-GS extends the 3D Gaussian Splatting framework by replacing its spherical-harmonics color encoding with a four-term physically-based reflectance decomposition (diffuse, specular, shadow, subsurface scattering). It departs from prior OLAT-based Gaussian relighting methods (GS³, OLAT Gaussians, RNG) that either handle shadows and scattering at the pixel level in a deferred style, rely on proxy meshes for normals, or replace physically meaningful shading with latent codes. The subsurface scattering module specifically instantiates the classical Jensen et al. dipole diffusion profile as a learnable per-Gaussian parameter, contrasting with SSS-GS which treats scattering as a residual neural term. The progressive coarse-to-fine training schedule that introduces reflectance components sequentially is a practical departure from joint-training approaches used by most prior Gaussian relighting methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The physically interpretable decomposition enables per-component supervision and controllable editing of individual lighting effects (e.g., adjusting shadow softness or scattering albedo independently), which is difficult with latent-code or pixel-level residual approaches. However, the method remains limited to single-bounce direct lighting under an OLAT capture setup, does not model multi-bounce global illumination, and relies on a rasterization pipeline that approximates rather than simulates true light transport. Open questions include whether the dipole-based SSS generalizes to highly heterogeneous or layered materials, whether material-aware grouping of Gaussians could improve disentanglement, and whether integrating ray or path tracing would close the remaining gap to physically exact rendering.
