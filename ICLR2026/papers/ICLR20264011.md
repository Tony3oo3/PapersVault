## Problem

Enforcing exact invariance to crystallographic space groups in machine learning models has required designing a bespoke architecture for each of the 230 groups in 3D, which is impractical given the severe data sparsity (fewer than 1,000 examples per group on average in the Materials Project). This paper asks whether a single, unified architecture can adapt its invariance properties to any input space group while sharing parameters across all groups.

## Input / Output

The model takes per-atom inputs (atomic number and fractional coordinates), the 3×3 lattice vectors, and a space group identifier (1–230). The space group identifier is consumed not as a learned embedding but as an index into a precomputed routing matrix M_G, which is the sole group-dependent component of the architecture. Outputs are scalar material properties (total energy, band gap, bulk modulus, shear modulus) produced by mean-pooling over atom tokens followed by a small MLP. Because the Fourier encoding is dimensionless, a separate self-supervised pretraining stage learns to scale the encodings to capture real-space crystal geometry via orbit-distance regression.

## Method

The core contribution is an analytical derivation of the linear constraints that each crystallographic group operation imposes on Fourier coefficients (Proposition 3.1), showing that these constraints partition the reciprocal lattice into phase-consistent orbits whose connected components define a complete G-invariant Fourier basis (Theorem 3.2). These constraints are encoded as a directed, edge-weighted graph on the reciprocal lattice, whose adjacency matrix M_G serves as a group-conditional routing matrix. Architecturally, atom positions are first mapped to standard Fourier modes, then multiplied by M_G to produce a provably G-invariant positional encoding, which is added to a learned element embedding and fed into a standard encoder-only Transformer whose weights are shared across all 230 space groups. A self-supervised pretraining stage trains the encoding module to reproduce orbit distances (the minimum Euclidean distance between group orbits), providing the physical scale that the dimensionless Fourier basis lacks. The result is that invariance to any space group is enforced by a single matrix-vector product, and zero-shot generalization to unseen groups requires only swapping in the corresponding M_G at inference time.

## Results

On the Materials Project dataset, CFT achieves test MAE of 0.197 eV/atom (Total Energy), 0.306 eV (Band Gap), 0.082 log GPa (Bulk Moduli), and 0.158 log GPa (Shear Moduli). CFT outperforms all baselines on Total Energy and Shear Moduli, and significantly surpasses the same Transformer architecture with standard positional encodings (0.220 vs. 0.197 eV/atom on Total Energy; 0.226 vs. 0.158 log GPa on Shear Moduli). In the zero-shot setting (holding out all 112 centrosymmetric space groups), CFT achieves a group-balanced performance gap of 0.042 log GPa (shear modulus) and 0.141 eV/atom (total energy), compared to 0.120/0.309 for ALIGNN and 0.080/0.197 for Matformer. CFT is also substantially faster, training in 91 sec/epoch vs. 592 sec for ALIGNN and 266 sec for Matformer.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CFT outperforms CGCNN across all four material properties, with the largest margin on Total Energy (0.197 vs. 0.453 eV/atom). Against ALIGNN, CFT wins on Total Energy (0.197 vs. 0.203 eV/atom) and Shear Moduli (0.158 vs. 0.160 log GPa) but loses on Band Gap (0.306 vs. 0.235 eV) and is slightly behind on Bulk Moduli (0.082 vs. 0.076 log GPa). Against Matformer, CFT wins on Total Energy (0.197 vs. 0.371 eV/atom) and Shear Moduli (0.158 vs. 0.179 log GPa) but trails on Band Gap (0.306 vs. 0.213 eV) and Bulk Moduli (0.082 vs. 0.074 log GPa). In the zero-shot generalization experiment, CFT's group-balanced gap is roughly 3× smaller than ALIGNN's and 2× smaller than Matformer's on both shear modulus and total energy, with no individual group showing the >5× degradation seen in the GNN baselines. CFT also trains ~6.5× faster than ALIGNN and ~3× faster than Matformer per epoch despite having more parameters (5.34M vs. 4.03M and 2.78M respectively).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Adams & Orbanz (2023), which proved the existence of G-invariant Fourier bases for crystallographic groups but relied on numerical Laplace eigenfunction computation. Here, the authors provide an analytical construction by deriving explicit linear constraints on Fourier coefficients from group operations, encoded as a group-conditional routing matrix. Conceptually, it departs from the dominant crystal-ML paradigm (CGCNN, ALIGNN, Matformer) that represents crystals as graphs with explicit distance/angle features and periodic boundary conditions; instead, CFT uses a single Transformer whose invariance is enforced by swapping a precomputed adjacency matrix per space group, enabling full weight sharing across all 230 groups.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical contribution is overcoming data sparsity in materials science: with fewer than 1,000 examples per space group on average, per-group architectures are infeasible, and CFT's weight-sharing design plus zero-shot generalization to unseen groups (GB-Gap of 0.042 log GPa vs. 0.120 for ALIGNN) offers a path to reliable predictions for rare symmetry classes. Limitations include evaluation on a single dataset (Materials Project) with four properties, a zero-shot protocol that specifically holds out inversion-symmetric groups, and a dependence on pretraining for scale capture. Open questions include whether the routing-matrix principle extends to other discrete symmetry families (e.g., magnetic space groups, equivariant rather than invariant outputs) and whether the approach scales to larger, more diverse materials databases.
