## Problem

Conventional data-fidelity losses in inverse problems (MSE, negative log-likelihood) enforce pointwise agreement with noisy measurements, inherently incentivizing the optimizer to fit individual noise realizations rather than ensuring statistical compatibility with the noise model. This forces practitioners to rely on early stopping or discrepancy-based rules to prevent overfitting, and requires regularization to compensate for noise-fitting rather than focusing solely on structural priors. The paper asks whether the data-fidelity term itself can be reformulated to eliminate the noise-fitting incentive at the objective level.

## Input / Output

The method requires a known forward operator f, a single set of noisy measurements m (one realization per index), and known per-index noise distributions D_i (e.g., Gaussian with known variance, Poisson with unknown rate). It requires a large number of independent measurements N to reliably assess collective residual behavior, which is satisfied in pixel-wise imaging, tomography, and dense time-series settings. The output is a parameter estimate θ̂ whose forward-modelled signal f(θ̂) is distributionally consistent with the observed noise, along with a scalar DC loss value computed as a Wasserstein-1 distance.

## Method

DC loss applies the Probability Integral Transform: each measurement m_i is mapped to its percentile s_i = F_i(m_i | ŷ_i) under the predicted noise distribution, and the collection {s_i} is tested for uniformity (the distribution expected under a correctly calibrated model). To avoid vanishing gradients when s_i saturates near 0 or 1, a logit transform maps the uniform target to a Logistic(0,1) reference, and the discrepancy is measured via the Wasserstein-1 (Earth Mover's) distance between the sorted logit-transformed values and sorted Logistic(0,1) samples. This yields a smooth, differentiable objective compatible with standard auto-differentiation frameworks. Far from the solution, DC loss exhibits MSE/NLL-like convergence (the squared residual dominates the tail approximation), but near the data-consistent regime it flattens the objective, removing the per-sample incentive to chase noise and allowing regularization to select among the manifold of statistically valid solutions rather than trading fidelity for regularity.

## Results

In DIP denoising at σ=75/255, DIP-DC achieves higher peak PSNR than optimally early-stopped DIP-MSE and maintains stable quality through 10,000 iterations where DIP-MSE degrades severely after ~1,000 iterations. In PET reconstruction from Poisson-noisy BrainWeb phantom data, DC-Adam converges to the same minimum error as NLL-Adam but avoids the noise-chasing degradation seen in both NLL-Adam and MLEM at high iteration counts. With edge-preserving TV regularization on 4× lower-count data, DC+TV achieves lower NRMSE than NLL+TV at its optimum, with the optimal regularization strength β being orders of magnitude smaller for DC+TV than for NLL+TV.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DIP-MSE (Ulyanov et al., 2020), DIP-DC wins on peak PSNR across all tested noise levels (σ=10/255 to 100/255), with larger gains at higher noise, and eliminates the need for early stopping entirely—DIP-DC's PSNR plateaus naturally while DIP-MSE requires carefully tuned stopping to avoid noise overfitting. Against MLEM (Shepp & Vardi, 1982), DC-Adam matches the minimum error achieved by MLEM (which has a slight edge due to its built-in non-negativity constraint) but critically avoids the progressive noise amplification that MLEM exhibits beyond ~2,000 iterations. Against NLL-Adam, DC-Adam clearly wins: both converge to similar minimum error, but NLL-Adam continues degrading at high iterations while DC-Adam stabilizes. In the regularized PET setting, DC+TV outperforms NLL+TV on NRMSE and visual detail preservation, requiring orders of magnitude less regularization strength (β) to achieve comparable noise suppression, confirming that DC loss removes the tension between data-fidelity and regularization that forces NLL-based methods to use stronger priors.

## Relation to Prior Work

DC loss reframes the data-fidelity term in inverse problems by drawing on the classical Probability Integral Transform and goodness-of-fit tests (Kolmogorov-Smirnov, Cramer-von Mises), but converts them from post-hoc statistical assessments into a smooth, differentiable optimization objective via the Wasserstein-1 distance. It departs from the standard pointwise paradigm (MSE, NLL) that has dominated data-fidelity design, and is distinct from Noise2Noise (which requires multiple noisy observations), Stein's unbiased risk estimator (which regularizes the architecture), and end-to-end learned approaches (which require paired data). The closest prior work is Llacer et al.'s statistically-based emission tomography reconstruction, but DC loss generalizes the idea to arbitrary forward models and noise distributions while remaining compatible with modern unsupervised regularizers like DIP and plug-and-play priors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is that DC loss removes the need for early stopping in over-parameterized inverse problems and repositions regularization as a structure-selection mechanism rather than a noise-fighting penalty, yielding better noise-detail trade-offs at much smaller regularization strengths. Key limitations constrain its scope: it requires a known (or estimable) per-measurement noise model, many independent measurements, and is not designed for settings where forward-operator ill-posedness (rather than noise) dominates. Open questions include integration with learned and score-based generative priors, behavior under misspecified noise models, and applicability to lower-dose or high-resolution clinical settings where overfitting is most problematic.
