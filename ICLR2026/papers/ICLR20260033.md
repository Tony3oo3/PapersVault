## Problem

Traditional semantic navigation analyses rely on binary clustering/switching counts and labor-intensive manual coding pipelines that hinder cross-study comparability. While embedding-based methods have begun to separate clinical groups, they typically treat each word independently and miss the history-dependent, sequential nature of semantic search. This paper asks whether the step-by-step dynamics of human concept production can be captured as continuous trajectories in transformer embedding space, yielding a scalable, minimally manual framework for quantifying semantic navigation.

## Input / Output

The method takes ordered participant-generated word sequences (concept streams of length N from verbal fluency or property listing tasks) and maps them to cumulative transformer embeddings, where point x_t encodes the full prefix items 1:t rather than item t alone. It produces five per-trajectory metrics: a cosine-distance series (distance to next), velocity and acceleration vector series (discrete derivatives of the embedding sequence), a scalar Shannon entropy computed from the median-binarized distance series, and a distance-to-centroid series measuring each point's cosine distance from the mean of all unique property embeddings. Because the tasks lack timestamps, a unit time step (α=1) is assumed for all kinematic quantities.

## Method

The core contribution is a cumulative-embedding trajectory framework: each participant-concept pair yields a path X=(x_1,…,x_N) in transformer embedding space where x_t summarizes the full prefix, capturing memory-dependent semantics that independent word embeddings cannot. Five physics-inspired metrics are extracted from each trajectory: (1) distance to next (cosine distance between successive unit-normalized embeddings), (2) velocity (vector difference x_{t+1}−x_t), (3) acceleration (second-order difference x_{t+2}−2x_{t+1}+x_t), (4) Shannon entropy of the median-binarized distance series, and (5) distance to centroid (cosine distance from each point to the mean of unique property embeddings). The framework is evaluated across four multilingual datasets using three transformer encoders (OpenAI text-embedding-3-large, Google text-embedding-004, Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B) and a non-cumulative fastText baseline, with group and concept effects tested via GLMMs (lognormal for kinematic/entropy metrics, Gaussian for distance-to-centroid) and Tukey HSD post-hoc corrections. A key finding is that cumulative embeddings outperform non-cumulative ones for longer trajectories while the reverse holds for short sequences, and that local kinematic metrics show high cross-model correlation whereas distance-to-centroid is model-dependent due to its sensitivity to global embedding geometry.

## Results

On the Neurodegenerative dataset (76 participants, ~19.5 properties/trajectory), cumulative transformer embeddings (OpenAI text-embedding-3-large) yield 10 significant pairwise group differences (Cohen's d = 0.30), with healthy controls showing significantly lower distance-to-next, velocity, acceleration, and entropy than both PD and bvFTD groups, while distance-to-centroid reverses (HC > patients). On the Swear Fluency dataset (274 participants, ~20.7 items), swear words produce the highest values across all kinematic metrics and entropy, with animals lowest and letter categories intermediate. Cross-model Pearson correlations show near-perfect agreement for entropy and strong agreement for velocity/acceleration/distance-to-next across OpenAI, Google, and Qwen3 encoders, while distance-to-centroid exhibits the weakest inter-model correlation due to sensitivity to model-specific embedding geometry.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is non-cumulative fastText embeddings, representing standard static word-vector semantics. On the Neurodegenerative dataset (longer trajectories, ~19.5 items), cumulative transformer models clearly outperform the fastText baseline: OpenAI-3-large achieves 10 significant pairs (d = 0.30) versus fastText's 8 pairs (d = 0.23), and all cumulative variants exceed their own non-cumulative counterparts (4 pairs, d ≈ 0.15–0.19). On the Swear Fluency dataset (~20.7 items), the advantage is more mixed: fastText matches cumulative OpenAI in #Pairs (46) but achieves a larger Cohen's d (0.72 vs. 0.59), while non-cumulative fastText reaches d = 0.86. On the shorter Italian and German datasets (~5 items), the pattern reverses: non-cumulative variants (especially Google-004 nc with 107/137 pairs and d = 0.29/0.37) outperform both cumulative transformers and the fastText baseline, because shorter sequences provide insufficient context for cumulative aggregation to add signal. Overall, the paper demonstrates that cumulative embeddings are advantageous for longer trajectories where sequential context matters, while non-cumulative representations retain more discriminative point-to-point variability for short sequences.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the trajectory-in-embedding-space framework introduced by Nour et al. (2023, 2025) for characterizing semantic search in clinical populations, with the key methodological departure being the use of cumulative embeddings (where each point x_t encodes the full prefix 1:t) rather than independent per-word vectors. It also moves beyond the binary clustering/switching paradigm of Troyer et al. (1997) and the foraging model of Hills et al. (2012) by extracting continuous kinematic metrics (velocity, acceleration, entropy, distance-to-centroid) from the trajectory. The fastText non-cumulative baseline serves as a bridge to classical static word-embedding analyses (Linz et al., 2017), while the multi-model comparison (OpenAI, Google, Qwen3) situates the work within the broader literature on cross-lingual and cross-architecture embedding geometry.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a scalable, minimally manual alternative to labor-intensive linguistic coding pipelines for semantic fluency data, with demonstrated utility across four languages and clinical populations. The cross-model consistency finding (high correlation of local kinematic metrics across causal and bidirectional encoders) strengthens confidence that the observed dynamics reflect semantic structure rather than model-specific artifacts, while the weaker cross-model agreement on distance-to-centroid opens a potential tool for comparing how different models organize global knowledge. Key limitations include the absence of temporal information (no timestamps), the Euclidean dynamics assumption despite known anisotropy, and reliance on basic Shannon entropy; future work on non-Euclidean metrics, richer information-theoretic measures, and application to LLM-generated trajectories could substantially broaden the framework's reach.
