## Problem

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) excels in domains with binary correctness signals (math, code) but fails on real-world reasoning tasks where quality is multi-criteria and not strictly verifiable. Preference-based alternatives (RLHF) overfit to superficial artifacts and require large volumes of pairwise comparisons. Instance-specific rubrics have emerged for evaluation benchmarks but their potential as on-policy reward signals for training expert-level reasoning policies remains unexplored.

## Input / Output

The method consumes text prompts (medical or science questions) paired with instance-specific rubrics of 7–20 self-contained criteria, each tagged with a categorical weight (Essential, Important, Optional, Pitfall) and a numeric score. Rubrics are synthesized per-prompt by strong LLMs (o3-mini, GPT-4o) conditioned on expert reference answers. The output is a trained policy model (Qwen2.5-7B) whose responses are scored by an LLM-as-judge (gpt-4o-mini) into a scalar reward in [0,1], either via explicit weighted-sum aggregation of binary criterion checks or via implicit holistic scoring where the judge sees all rubrics at once.

## Method

RaR formalizes rubric-based rewards as a generalization of RLVR (where k=1 reduces to a single verifiable check) and introduces two aggregation strategies: Explicit Aggregation computes a normalized weighted sum of independently evaluated binary criterion checks, while Implicit Aggregation delegates scoring to an LLM judge that receives all rubric criteria and produces a single Likert rating. Rubrics are generated at scale using strong LLMs guided by four design principles (expert grounding, comprehensive coverage, criterion importance, self-contained evaluation), with reference answers serving as proxies for expert supervision. Training uses GRPO on-policy RL with 16 rollouts per prompt and gpt-4o-mini as the reward judge. The key empirical finding is that instance-specific rubrics substantially outperform both generic predefined rubrics and rubric-free Likert baselines, and that rubric-guided scoring improves alignment with human preferences especially for smaller judge models.

## Results

On HealthBench, RaR-Implicit-Synthetic achieves 35.9% overall score, outperforming Reference-Likert (31.7%), Simple-Likert (23.9%), and Expert-Answer-SFT (20.4%), representing up to 31% relative improvement over Direct-Likert. On GPQA-Diamond, RaR-Implicit yields a 7% relative gain over Direct-Likert, demonstrating that rubric-induced skills generalize to verifiable multiple-choice tasks. On MMLU-Pro (Science subset), Explicit-RaR achieves 57.94% micro-average accuracy versus 56.77% for Simple-Likert and 51.43% for the untrained base model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RaR-Implicit consistently outperforms the Direct-Likert baseline (the LLM-as-judge Likert scoring approach from Zheng et al., 2023) by up to 31% relative on HealthBench and 7% on GPQA-Diamond, confirming that structured rubric criteria provide a substantially more informative reward signal than a single holistic Likert score. RaR-Implicit also achieves small but consistent gains over Reference-Likert (35.9% vs. 31.7% on HealthBench), indicating that decomposing open-ended reference answers into explicit checklist criteria yields better-aligned rewards than direct comparison scoring. The RaR-Predefined variant, which applies a fixed set of generic rubrics to all prompts, underperforms instance-specific methods, showing that prompt-specific rubric synthesis is critical for capturing task context and common failure modes. On MMLU-Pro (Science subset), Explicit-RaR leads with 57.94% micro-average, slightly ahead of Implicit-RaR (56.96%) and Simple-Likert (56.77%), while Reference-Likert (54.80%) trails both rubric-based methods. Across all comparisons, rubric-guided training produces stable, generalizable gains on both rubric-scored (HealthBench) and verifiable (GPQA-Diamond, MMLU-Pro) evaluation settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RLVR (Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards) paradigm, which has been effective in math and code but limited to tasks with binary correctness signals, to open-ended reasoning domains by substituting verifiable checks with instance-specific rubric criteria as reward functions. It builds directly on GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) as the policy optimization algorithm and positions itself against LLM-as-judge Likert scoring (Zheng et al., 2023) as the primary baseline. The key conceptual departure is repurposing rubrics—previously used only for evaluation in benchmarks like HealthBench (Arora et al., 2025) and code assessment (Pathak et al., 2025)—as on-policy training signals, and formally showing that standard RLVR is a degenerate case (k=1) of the proposed rubric-based reward.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RaR provides a practical, interpretable bridge between the simplicity of verifiable rewards and the expressiveness (but artifact-proneness) of preference-based reward models, opening a path for RL post-training in domains like medicine and science where no single ground truth exists. The finding that rubric structure disproportionately benefits smaller judge models has direct practical implications for reducing compute costs in reward computation. However, the work is limited to two domains, two policy model sizes (3B/7B), and two aggregation strategies; broader validation across agentic tasks, learned or dynamic rubric weighting, and larger-scale policies remain open.
