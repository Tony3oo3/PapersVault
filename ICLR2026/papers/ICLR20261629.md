## Problem

Despite the rapid proliferation of Hypergraph Neural Network (HNN) architectures, no standardized benchmark exists to fairly compare them across diverse datasets, tasks, and evaluation dimensions. Existing toolkits (HyFER, DHG, TopoX) offer only limited quantitative comparisons, cover fewer than 10 algorithms, omit heterophilic and graph-level settings, and provide no analysis of efficiency, robustness, or fairness—leaving practitioners without guidance on which HNN to deploy in real-world scenarios.

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes hypergraphs specified by a node feature matrix X ∈ R^{|V|×F} and a hyperedge incidence structure, with labels at the node, hyperedge, or hypergraph level depending on the task. Beyond standard predictive outputs (node class labels, binary hyperedge existence scores, hypergraph class labels), the framework produces a four-dimensional evaluation profile per method: effectiveness (accuracy, AUROC, AP, Macro-F1), efficiency (training time to best validation, peak GPU memory), robustness (accuracy curves under structural/feature/supervision perturbations at varying intensities), and fairness (Δ_DP and Δ_EO on sensitive-attribute datasets). All 17 methods are evaluated under identical 50/25/25 (node), 60/20/20 (edge), or 80/10/10 (graph) splits with 5-run averaging.

## Method

DHG-Bench is a unified evaluation framework rather than a new model architecture. It integrates 17 HNNs spanning spectral (HGNN, HyperGCN, HCHA, LEGCN, HyperND, PhenomNN, SheafHyperGNN, HJRL, DPHGNN, TF-HNN), spatial (HNHN, UniGNN, AllSetTransformer, ED-HNN, HyperGT), and tensor (EHNN, T-HyperGNN) families, plus MLP and clique-expansion GNN baselines (CEGCN, CEGAT), into a single training pipeline with standardized hyperparameter search spaces and sparse-matrix support to mitigate OOM issues. The key differentiator is the multi-dimensional evaluation protocol: robustness is assessed by systematically perturbing hypergraph structure (random hyperlink addition/removal at 0–90% ratios), node features (Gaussian noise and zero-masking at 0–90% levels), and supervision signals (label flipping at 0–20% and training-set sparsification at 0–80%); fairness is measured via demographic parity and equalized odds on three sensitive-attribute datasets (German, Bail, Credit). The framework also introduces heterophilic hypergraph datasets (Actor, Pokec, Yelp, Twitch-gamers, Amazon-ratings) and graph-level classification benchmarks (RHG-10, RHG-3, IMDB-Dir-Form, IMDB-Dir-Genre, Steam-Player, Twitter-Friend) that were absent from prior toolkits, enabling the first comprehensive cross-task, cross-dataset comparison of HNN generalization, scalability, and trustworthiness.

## Results

Across 10 node classification datasets, TF-HNN achieves the most consistent top-tier performance, reaching 90.79% on Trivago, 85.96% on Actor, and 84.19% on Cora-CA, while remaining scalable (no OOM) unlike PhenomNN, DPHGNN, and HyperGT which fail on large datasets. On homophilic datasets (e.g., DBLP-CA), HNNs substantially outperform MLP (91.38% vs. 85.54% for TF-HNN), but on heterophilic datasets (e.g., Actor, Yelp), most HNNs underperform the feature-only MLP baseline. For hyperedge prediction, older methods HGNN, HyperGCN, and EHNN collectively capture all best and most second-best AUROC/AP scores, with recent methods like TF-HNN trailing by up to 13.76% AUROC on DBLP-CA. In fairness evaluation, MLP achieves the lowest Δ_DP and Δ_EO across German, Bail, and Credit, while HNNs exhibit more severe bias despite higher accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TF-HNN emerges as the most consistently strong HNN, achieving best or near-best accuracy on 8 of 10 node classification datasets (e.g., 90.79% on Trivago vs. 87.19% for HyperND and 75.99% for ED-HNN) while avoiding OOM issues that plague PhenomNN, DPHGNN, and HyperGT on Walmart, Trivago, and Yelp. On heterophilic datasets, MLP surprisingly outperforms most HNNs (e.g., 86.06% on Actor vs. 85.96% for TF-HNN; 59.64% on Pokec vs. 59.17% for TF-HNN), revealing that higher-order message passing can be detrimental under low homophily. Clique-expansion baselines CEGCN and CEGAT are consistently outperformed by dedicated HNNs on homophilic data (e.g., 76.90% vs. 79.47% on Cora) but also suffer OOM on large datasets. For hyperedge prediction, the older HGNN, HyperGCN, and EHNN dominate all six datasets, with TF-HNN trailing HyperGCN by 13.76% AUROC on DBLP-CA (75.70% vs. 89.46%). In fairness evaluation, MLP achieves the lowest Δ_DP and Δ_EO on all three sensitive datasets (German, Bail, Credit), while the fairest HNN (HCHA on Credit) still shows higher bias, indicating that high-order propagation amplifies demographic bias. No single HNN wins across all tasks: DPHGNN leads on IMDB-Dir-Form for graph classification but drops to 11th on IMDB-Dir-Genre, and AllSetTransformer offers the best utility-efficiency tradeoff for graph-level tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as the first comprehensive benchmark for Hypergraph Neural Networks, explicitly addressing the limitations of prior toolkits (HyFER, DHG, TopoX) that offered only 3–17 algorithms, lacked heterophilic datasets, and omitted graph-level tasks. Conceptually, it extends the GNN benchmarking tradition (e.g., GSLB) to the hypergraph setting while introducing a multi-dimensional evaluation protocol (effectiveness, efficiency, robustness, fairness) that goes well beyond the accuracy-only comparisons typical of prior HNN papers. The 17 benchmarked algorithms span the full lineage of HNN design—from early spectral methods (HGNN, HyperGCN) through spatial message-passing (AllSetTransformer, ED-HNN) to recent tensor-based and training-free approaches (EHNN, TF-HNN)—providing a unified reference point for the field.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that no single HNN architecture dominates across tasks, datasets, or evaluation dimensions, and that heterophilic hypergraphs substantially degrade HNN performance below even MLP baselines—findings that should redirect design efforts toward adaptive and heterophily-aware architectures. The fairness analysis, which is the first of its kind for hypergraphs, shows that higher-order message passing amplifies demographic bias relative to MLPs, raising urgent questions about safe deployment in high-stakes domains. Open challenges include developing decoupled architectures that match TF-HNN's efficiency without sacrificing expressiveness, building robustness against feature and label perturbations, and designing fairness-aware HNN training that does not sacrifice predictive utility.
