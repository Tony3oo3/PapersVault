## Problem

Chain-of-Thought (CoT) training empirically improves LLM performance, yet a formal understanding of why reasoning traces help learning remains lacking. In Gold's classic identification-in-the-limit paradigm, even the simple class of regular languages (DFAs) is provably unidentifiable from positive examples alone. This paper asks whether augmenting the learner with computational traces — the step-by-step accepting computation of the target machine — fundamentally changes the identifiability landscape, and how robust such identification is to corrupted or partial traces.

## Input / Output

The learner receives an infinite enumeration of positive examples (strings from the target language) each paired with the full accepting computational trace of the target machine on that string (the sequence of states, stack/tape configurations, and transitions). In the robust setting, the observed trace is a corrupted version at bounded edit distance from the true trace, with the corruption regime (constant rate, diminishing rate, or finite absolute error) known in advance. The output is a sequence of machine hypotheses (DFA, DPDA, or TM) that must converge after finitely many steps to a correct description of the target language; no negative examples are ever provided.

## Method

The paper introduces a new learning model, identification in the limit with traces, and proves a trichotomy across the Chomsky hierarchy. For perfect traces, the algorithm incrementally constructs a machine by copying every witnessed transition and defaulting unwitnessed entries to a reject sink, establishing a subset invariant (hypothesis ⊆ target) and eventual completeness, which yields identifiability of all recursively enumerable languages — a stark contrast to Gold's impossibility for DFAs without traces. For robust identification, the technique shifts to tracking the set of states observed in noisy traces and proving it stabilizes, after which the target machine's accepting states are shown to lie within a bounded graph distance from the observed set, reducing the problem to finite-class identification. The key technical step for DPDAs converts computation traces into Chomsky Normal Form derivation trees and bounds their size to control the number of unobserved states. The resulting trichotomy: DFAs tolerate any constant error rate < 1, DPDAs require a diminishing error rate, and TMs require only a finite absolute number of errors per trace.

## Results

The paper's central result (Theorem 3.1) shows that with perfect computational traces, the class of all recursively enumerable languages (TM-recognizable) becomes identifiable in the limit — a dramatic reversal of Gold's (1967) impossibility result, which holds even for the much simpler class of regular languages (DFA-recognizable). For robust identification under corrupted traces, the paper establishes a trichotomy across the Chomsky hierarchy: DFAs tolerate any constant error rate α < 1 (Theorem 4.1), DPDAs require a diminishing error rate (Theorem 4.3), and TMs require a bounded (finite) number of errors per trace (Theorem 4.5). No quantitative empirical metrics are reported; all results are theoretical identifiability/impossibility theorems.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is against Gold's (1967) original identification-in-the-limit paradigm without computational traces. Under Gold's model, even the class of regular languages (DFA-recognizable) is not identifiable from positive examples alone (Fact 2.2), and the impossibility extends to all countable collections of languages (Angluin, 1980). The trace-augmented model overturns this: with perfect traces, all TM-recognizable languages become identifiable (Theorem 3.1), representing a strict superset of what Gold's model can handle. In the robust setting, the paper's trichotomy shows that the tolerance for corruption decreases monotonically with machine complexity: DFAs remain identifiable even when 99.9% of the trace is corrupted (any constant α < 1), DPDAs fail at any fixed constant error rate but succeed when the rate diminishes with trace length, and TMs require the total number of errors per trace to be bounded by an absolute constant. The paper does not compare against other trace-augmented learning methods; the closest prior work (Papazov & Flammarion, 2025) operates in a fundamentally different setting with both positive and negative examples, making direct comparison inapplicable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Gold's (1967) identification-in-the-limit paradigm by introducing computational traces (analogous to chain-of-thought) as additional input to the learner, creating a new model called 'identification in the limit with traces.' It departs from the statistical CoT learning literature (Malach 2023, Joshi et al. 2022, Altabaa et al. 2025) by working entirely within formal language theory and automata, and from Papazov & Flammarion (2025) by restricting to positive examples only. The paper carves new ground by bridging the Chomsky hierarchy (DFA/DPDA/TM) with the CoT training paradigm, providing the first formal characterization of how much trace information is needed to overcome Gold's classical impossibility results.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first rigorous theoretical explanation for why CoT training helps LLMs learn world models: access to computational traces transforms an impossible learning problem (even for regular languages) into a solvable one (for all recursively enumerable languages). The trichotomy across the Chomsky hierarchy—DFAs tolerate constant corruption, DPDAs need diminishing error, TMs require bounded errors—gives a clean theoretical lens on the robustness of CoT-based learning. Open questions include quantitative (non-asymptotic) convergence bounds, how CoT affects generation-in-the-limit and hallucination trade-offs, and whether these formal results translate into practical guidance for LLM training pipelines.
