## Problem

For nearly 70 years, the width of neural network layers has been treated as a fixed hyperparameter requiring manual selection or expensive search (grid search, NAS), a process that becomes infeasible for foundation models with billions of parameters. No prior method learns an unbounded layer width end-to-end via standard backpropagation without imposing an upper bound or relying on human-defined growth heuristics.

## Input / Output

The method is domain-agnostic: it takes standard inputs (tabular feature vectors, 32×32 RGB images, token sequences up to length 128, or graph adjacency/feature matrices) and produces the same outputs as the base architecture (class labels, regression values, or translated tokens). The unusual aspect is that the number of neurons per hidden layer is itself a learned, unbounded quantity that changes during training; after training, the network can be truncated by simply deleting the last rows/columns of weight matrices, yielding a controlled performance-vs-size trade-off at zero additional cost.

## Method

AWN formulates width learning as a variational inference problem: each layer ℓ has a latent variable λ_ℓ whose variational parameter ν_ℓ determines the truncated width D_ℓ via the quantile function of a discretized exponential distribution. A soft ordering of neuron importance is imposed by rescaling each hidden activation h_j^ℓ by the monotonically decreasing importance f_ℓ(j; ν_ℓ), so newly added neurons contribute minimally and the network can grow or shrink smoothly. The ELBO (regularization terms on λ and θ plus the predictive log-likelihood) is maximized with standard backpropagation, jointly updating weights and width at every step. A rescaled Kaiming initialization ('Kaiming+') is derived to keep activation variance constant across layers despite the importance rescaling, preventing gradient collapse in deep networks. The exponential importance function also enables free post-hoc compression: removing the last (least important) neurons degrades performance smoothly, and an informative prior on λ can anneal the width downward during training without accuracy loss.

## Results

Across 13 datasets spanning tabular, image, text, sequence, and graph domains, AWN matches or exceeds fixed-width baselines in most cases. Notable wins include PMNIST (95.7% vs 91.1%), REDDIT-B (90.2% vs 87.0%), and SpiralHard (100.0% vs 98.0%), while CIFAR-100 is the sole loss (63.1% vs 66.5%) due to a non-converged run. On the Multi30k translation task, the AWN Transformer achieves a comparable cross-entropy loss (1.51 vs 1.43) while using 200× fewer parameters in the feed-forward layers (123 vs 24,576 total width). The learned width adapts to task difficulty: from ~8 neurons on DoubleMoon to ~227 on SpiralHard, and ~794 on REDDIT-B.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is a fixed-width network whose layer widths are selected via grid search over a small set of candidate values. AWN wins or ties on 11 of 13 tasks, with the largest margins on PMNIST (+4.6 pp), REDDIT-B (+3.2 pp), and SpiralHard (+2.0 pp); the only clear loss is CIFAR-100 (−3.4 pp), attributed to a single non-converged run. For graph classification, the fixed baseline numbers are taken from the published benchmarks of Errica et al. (2020), which used a GIN with widths in the 96–320 range; AWN learns a total width of ~794 on REDDIT-B, well outside that range, and improves accuracy from 87.0% to 90.2% while also reducing variance (std 4.4 → 1.3). On the Transformer for Multi30k, the fixed baseline uses 24,576 total feed-forward width, whereas AWN converges to ~123, a 200× reduction, at a negligible loss increase (1.43 → 1.51). The authors note that retraining the fixed baseline with AWN's learned width ("Fixed+") recovers most of the gap on Spiral and REDDIT-B, confirming that the benefit comes primarily from the larger width rather than the soft-ordering mechanism alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the variational inference framework of Nazaret & Blei (2022), which learns the number of layers (depth) in infinitely deep networks, to instead learn the unbounded width of each layer. The key conceptual departure is requiring a monotonically decreasing importance distribution (discretized exponential) to impose a soft ordering among neurons, rather than the importance distribution used for depth. It positions itself against constructive methods (cascade correlation, firefly descent) that grow networks via heuristics, NAS that requires separate training runs per configuration, and pruning/distillation that only shrink networks — AWN grows and shrinks width end-to-end via standard backpropagation with no human-defined growth rules.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is reducing the hyperparameter search space for width, which is especially relevant for foundation models where exhaustive grid search is infeasible due to training costs. The zero-cost post-hoc truncation and online width annealing offer a smooth performance-compute trade-off that could complement existing compression pipelines. However, the method is currently limited to MLPs (not CNN filters or attention heads), the theoretical convergence guarantees are still developing, and the 200x parameter reduction on Multi30k is demonstrated on a relatively small dataset, leaving open whether the approach scales to truly billion-parameter models. The finding that learned width is largely independent of starting width (under bounded activations) is practically encouraging but needs validation at larger scale.
