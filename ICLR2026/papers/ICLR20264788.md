## Problem

Training Hamiltonian Graph Networks (HGNs) for N-body physical systems relies on iterative gradient-descent optimization (Adam, LBFGS, etc.), which is slow, sensitive to hyperparameters, and prone to vanishing/exploding gradients—especially when a numerical integrator is embedded in the architecture. While random feature methods have been applied to non-graph Hamiltonian Neural Networks, no prior work has combined random features with graph-based physics-informed architectures, leaving a gap in fast, gradient-descent-free training for structured N-body dynamics.

## Input / Output

The model consumes phase-space coordinates (positions q and momenta p for N particles in d spatial dimensions) together with a graph adjacency matrix encoding pairwise connectivity. Before network input, positions are mean-centered for translation invariance and projected onto a locally constructed orthonormal basis for rotation invariance; edge features are relative displacement vectors and their norms. The output is a single scalar approximating the system's Hamiltonian, from which full trajectories are generated by symplectic Störmer-Verlet integration of Hamilton's equations.

## Method

The RF-HGN architecture follows a standard graph message-passing pipeline (node/edge encoders → bidirectional message passing → global pooling → linear readout), but all dense-layer weights and biases are constructed via random sampling (ELM or the data-driven SWIM algorithm) rather than backpropagation. Only the final linear layer is learned, by solving a single convex least-squares system that enforces Hamilton's equations on the training data, with l² regularization. Translation, permutation, and rotation invariances are encoded by construction through coordinate transformations and the graph structure, eliminating the need for equivariant layers. This reduces training to one O(K·d_L²) linear solve (where K = 2d·N·M), yielding 150–600× speedups over 15 SOTA optimizers while maintaining comparable accuracy and enabling zero-shot generalization from 8-node training systems to 4096-node test systems.

## Results

On the 3D lattice mass-spring system (9 nodes, d=3), RF-HGN achieves a test MSE of 8.95e-5 in 0.16 s, compared to LBFGS (3.56e-5 in 23.85 s) and Adam (2.90e-3 in 91.64 s), yielding speedups of 149×–602× over 15 SOTA optimizers. On the NeurIPS 2022 benchmark (2D closed chain), (SWIM) RF-HGN trains in 2.51 s (N=3) versus 406–12,535 s for competing physics-informed GNN architectures. Zero-shot generalization is demonstrated from 3×3 training to 100×100 test lattices and from 8-node to 4096-node chains without retraining.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against 15 iterative optimizers (Table 1), RF-HGN is 149×–602× faster while achieving a test MSE (8.95e-5) that is only ~2.5× worse than the best iterative method (LBFGS, 3.56e-5) and comparable to first-order methods like Adam (2.90e-3) and Nadam (9.11e-4). Against SOTA physics-informed GNN architectures on the NeurIPS 2022 benchmark (Table 4), (SWIM) RF-HGN trains in 2.51 s (N=3) versus 406 s (FGNN), 2,367 s (GNODE), and 12,535 s (LGN) — a 160×–5,000× speedup — while maintaining comparable accuracy. In the zero-shot chain experiment (Figure 6), (SWIM) RF-HGN outperforms (ELM) RF-HGN by roughly two orders of magnitude and outperforms RF-HNN by 1–2 orders of magnitude on large systems (4096 nodes), though RF-HNN trains faster for very small systems. On the molecular dynamics (Lennard-Jones) task, (SWIM) RF-HGN is slightly less accurate than Adam-trained HGN but trains ~100× faster, while ELM performs poorly due to non-isotropic inputs. On the multi-potential chain benchmark (Table 3), (SWIM) RF-HGN outperforms Adam HGN on the standard spring (3.4e-5 vs 3.9e-3) and anharmonic oscillator (5.2e-4 vs 4.6e-2) potentials, while (ELM) RF-HGN wins on the Morse potential (7.4e-4 vs 8.9e-2).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the random-feature training paradigm from non-graph Hamiltonian Neural Networks (Rahma et al., 2024) to graph-based architectures, combining the SWIM sampling algorithm (Bolager et al., 2023) and ELM (Huang et al., 2004) with Hamiltonian Graph Networks for the first time. Prior HGN work (Thangamuthu et al., 2022; Bhattoo et al., 2022; Bishnoi et al., 2023) relied exclusively on iterative gradient-descent optimizers (typically Adam), while prior random-feature physics work was limited to fully-connected HNNs without graph structure. The key departure is replacing all iterative optimization with random feature sampling for dense layers plus a single convex least-squares solve for the output layer, while preserving translation, rotation, and permutation invariances through explicit coordinate transformations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 150–600× training speedup over 15 SOTA optimizers with comparable accuracy, combined with zero-shot generalization from 8-node to 4096-node systems, could dramatically accelerate prototyping and deployment of physics-informed models in scientific computing. However, the approach is limited to dense-layer message-passing architectures (not convolution or self-attention), generalization is restricted to same-type graphs (chains cannot predict lattices), and molecular dynamics with dynamic edges remain challenging for all methods. Open questions include whether random feature boosting can extend to deeper multi-layer message passing, and whether SE(3)-equivariant variants could reduce data requirements for complex molecular systems.
