## Problem

Textual Inversion (TI) for personalized text-to-image generation frequently fails on complex prompts, yet the underlying cause remained unclear. This paper identifies embedding norm inflation as the root mechanism: unconstrained optimization drives learned token magnitudes far outside the pre-trained vocabulary's distribution (often >20 vs. ≈0.4), which attenuates positional information and stagnates residual updates in pre-norm Transformer blocks, degrading prompt conditioning.

## Input / Output

The method takes a small set of reference images (typically 3–5 per concept from DreamBooth) and a placeholder token in a prompt template, and produces a single d-dimensional embedding vector whose direction is optimized on the unit hypersphere while its magnitude is fixed to the average norm of the frozen text encoder's vocabulary. This lightweight embedding (one vector per concept) is then plugged into any pre-trained diffusion model (SDXL, SANA 1.5) at inference time, where it conditions generation alongside free-form text prompts. No model weights are modified, keeping storage per concept minimal and enabling drop-in use within existing TI pipelines or alongside LoRA fine-tuning.

## Method

DTI decouples the learnable token embedding e into a fixed in-distribution magnitude m* and a unit-norm direction v, then optimizes only v on the (d−1)-sphere using Riemannian SGD with tangent-space projection and retraction, avoiding the off-manifold drift of Euclidean optimizers like AdamW. The optimization is cast as a MAP problem with a von Mises-Fisher directional prior centered on the normalized embedding of a semantically related class token (e.g., 'dog' for <dog>), yielding a constant prior gradient (−κμ) that is simply added to the data gradient before projection—computationally trivial and analogous to decoupled weight decay adapted to the sphere. Theoretically, the paper proves two effects of large norms in pre-norm blocks: positional embeddings contribute O(1/m) to the normalized signal (Lemma 1), and per-block angular change of hidden states is bounded by arcsin(B_ℓ/‖x‖), so accumulated directional drift across L layers vanishes as initial norm grows (Proposition 1). Empirically, DTI improves text fidelity over TI and CrossInit across SDXL and SANA while maintaining subject similarity, and the hyperspherical parameterization enables smooth SLERP interpolation between learned concepts—a capability absent in standard TI.

## Results

On SDXL, DTI achieves a text fidelity score of 0.522 (SigLIP), substantially outperforming TI (0.292) and CrossInit (0.464), while maintaining a competitive subject similarity of 0.450 (DINOv2) versus TI's 0.561. On SANA 1.5-1.6B, DTI reaches 0.744 text fidelity (vs. TI's 0.621 and CrossInit's 0.614) while matching TI's subject similarity (0.479 vs. 0.480). The advantage grows with model scale: on SANA 1.5-4.8B, DTI scores 0.757 text fidelity and 0.452 subject similarity, beating all baselines on both metrics. A human study (100 participants) confirms DTI's superiority, with 66.77% preference for text alignment versus 22.40% for CrossInit and 10.83% for TI.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against TI (Gal et al., 2023a), DTI wins decisively on text fidelity across all three architectures (0.522 vs. 0.292 on SDXL; 0.744 vs. 0.621 on SANA 1.6B; 0.757 vs. 0.646 on SANA 4.8B), at the cost of lower subject similarity on SDXL (0.450 vs. 0.561) but matching or exceeding it on SANA. Against CrossInit (Pang et al., 2024a), DTI wins on text fidelity in all settings and on subject similarity for both SANA models, though CrossInit retains a slight edge on SDXL subject similarity (0.545 vs. 0.450). On older Stable Diffusion versions (Table 7), DTI achieves the best subject similarity among all baselines on both SD1.5 (0.418) and SD2.1-base (0.469), but trails P+ (0.719) and CoRe (0.661) on text fidelity for SD1.5, suggesting the directional optimization benefit is most pronounced with modern pre-norm encoders. As a drop-in replacement within DCO (Lee et al., 2024), substituting TI with DTI improves text alignment from 0.456 to 0.635 while slightly reducing subject similarity (0.605 to 0.568), confirming DTI's compatibility with joint fine-tuning pipelines. Overall, DTI's core advantage is text fidelity: it is the only method that simultaneously achieves the highest text scores on all three modern architectures while keeping subject similarity within a reasonable range.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Textual Inversion (TI) by identifying and addressing a specific geometric pathology—embedding norm inflation—that prior TI variants (CrossInit, P+, NeTI, CoRe) treat only symptomatically through richer initialization or regularization. The method draws on the directional embedding space literature (vMF distributions, spherical constraints in contrastive learning) and Riemannian optimization to reframe TI as a direction-only optimization on the unit hypersphere, with a vMF prior providing a constant-direction regularizer. Unlike DreamBooth-style fine-tuning or multi-token approaches that increase parameter count, DTI preserves TI's single-token efficiency while fixing the underlying optimization dynamics in pre-norm Transformers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean theoretical diagnosis (positional attenuation at O(1/m) and residual stagnation) for why TI degrades on complex prompts, which should inform future embedding-based personalization methods beyond diffusion models. The hyperspherical parameterization unlocks SLERP interpolation between learned concepts—a capability absent in standard TI—opening creative applications for concept blending. Limitations include a trade-off where subject similarity drops modestly (e.g., 0.561→0.450 on SDXL), the analysis is centered on pre-norm encoders (CLIP, Gemma), and multi-concept attribute binding still fails in some cases. Whether the directional-optimization principle generalizes to post-norm architectures or non-Transformer encoders remains open.
