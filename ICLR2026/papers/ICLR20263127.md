## Problem

Existing transformer approaches for linear algebra (e.g., Charton 2022, Liu 2025) suffer from three critical failures: prohibitive O(N⁴) memory scaling that limits them to matrices of size ≤50, catastrophic out-of-distribution generalization (failing even on the identity matrix), and a tendency to perform statistical interpolation of training data rather than discovering generalizable algorithms. This paper diagnoses these failure modes and asks whether a sequence of targeted interventions can transform transformers from broken statistical interpolators into competent, scalable linear algebra approximators.

## Input / Output

The primary input is an N×N matrix A, which is embedded not by flattening into N² scalar tokens but via a learnable projection Γ ∈ R^{N×D} to produce X = AΓ ∈ R^{N×D}, reducing the effective sequence length from N² to N. For downstream applications, the input extends to (A, b) for linear solves, (A, Ω₀) for learned RSVD sampling, or (x, y) data for GP kernel learning. Outputs span scalar quantities (Tr(A), λ_max, log|A|), full matrices (A⁻¹), eigenspectra, initial vectors x₀ for Krylov warm-starting, learned sampling distributions Ω, and GP hyperparameters (ℓ, σ²). The method supports variable matrix sizes through curriculum learning and slicing Γ to the input dimension, with a max sequence length N_max set at training time.

## Method

RangeFormer combines four interventions that collectively reduce attention memory from O(N⁴) to O(ND + D²) and shift the model from statistical interpolation toward algorithmic behavior. First, a learnable range projection Γ replaces matrix flattening, working directly with the operator's range as the sequence dimension. Second, linear attention (Taylor or BaseConv) replaces quadratic softmax attention, which is known to introduce approximation distortion unfavorable to matrix multiplication. Third, a looped transformer architecture (shared weights across R iterations, with input noise injection and random loop counts during training) forces the model to iteratively refine its solution, mimicking Krylov subspace methods. Fourth, a diverse training data mixture—combining Einsum-parameterized structured matrices (Kronecker, low-rank, tensor-train, Monarch) with matrices constructed from varied eigenspectral decays (polynomial, cosine, inverse-exponential, log)—replaces the standard Gaussian random matrix distribution, preventing the model from memorizing a single predictable spectral pattern. Curriculum learning (training on N=100 before N=1000) is required for convergence at large scales.

## Results

RangeFormer achieves an order-of-magnitude improvement over NumFormer on OOD matrix market matrices across all four canonical operations (λ_max, Tr, log|A|, A^{-1}). For the A^{-1} task, RangeFormer achieves relative errors of 1.8×10^{-6} (N=20), 2.3×10^{-6} (N=100), and 3.1×10^{-4} (N=1000), while NumFormer cannot run matrices with N≥100 on an 80 GB A100. In the Gaussian process downstream task, RangeFormer achieves a test RMSE of 0.87978, nearly identical to the Cholesky-based baseline (0.87996), demonstrating that compounding errors do not destabilize iterative pipelines. The method scales to 1000×1000 matrices with O(ND+D²) memory, compared to O(N⁴) for prior approaches.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares RangeFormer against two baselines: NumFormer (numerical transformer with flattened matrix input and quadratic attention) and STRFormer (string-based transformer from Charton 2022a). RangeFormer outperforms NumFormer by an order of magnitude on OOD matrix market matrices for all four operations (λ_max, Tr, log|A|, A^{-1}) at sizes 5×5, 10×10, and 20×20. NumFormer also fails catastrophically on trivial OOD inputs like the identity matrix, while RangeFormer handles them correctly. STRFormer (Charton 2022a) performs well in-distribution but typically fails to decode a correct output string on OOD matrix market data, making direct numerical comparison impossible for many entries. On the GP kernel learning task, RangeFormer matches the Cholesky decomposition baseline almost exactly (RMSE 0.87978 vs 0.87996), confirming that the transformer-based linear algebra primitives are accurate enough for iterative downstream pipelines. The key architectural advantage is that RangeFormer can process N=1000 matrices in less time than NumFormer processes N=50, due to the O(ND+D²) vs O(N⁴) memory scaling.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the line of work studying transformers for linear algebra (Charton 2022a,b; Liu et al. 2025) and the broader narrative that transformers learn iterative algorithms in-context (von Oswald et al. 2023; Fu et al. 2024; Garg et al. 2023). It departs from this narrative by demonstrating that for matrix operations specifically, transformers trained on Gaussian random matrices perform statistical interpolation rather than algorithm discovery, contradicting the gradient-descent/Newton's-method interpretation from prior in-context regression work. The RangeFormer architecture synthesizes looped transformers (Giannou et al. 2023; Yang et al. 2024; Saunshi et al. 2025), linear attention alternatives (Arora et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2025), and the Einsum structured-matrix parameterization (Potapczynski et al. 2024) into a unified framework, while introducing a novel diverse training distribution that prior work did not consider.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The diagnostic finding that prior transformer approaches for linear algebra are statistical interpolators rather than algorithm discoverers is a fundamental reframe of what these models actually learn, with implications beyond linear algebra for any task where in-distribution statistics are highly predictable. The first demonstration of transformers in iterative downstream pipelines (GP marginal likelihood optimization, randomized SVD, Krylov warm-starting) opens a new research direction, though the paper is candid that absolute accuracy remains far below classical solvers and the field is in its infancy. Key open questions include whether the RangeFormer's OOD generalization improves with scale, whether the diverse training distribution can be further principled, and whether transformers can eventually complement (rather than replace) purpose-built numerical algorithms in production settings.
