## Problem

Self-play reinforcement learning scales efficiently for multi-agent driving simulation but produces policies that diverge from human driving norms, while imitation-learning models (diffusion or tokenized) capture realistic behavior yet are computationally expensive, slow at inference, and struggle to react in closed-loop settings. No prior method achieves both human-like realism and the reactivity/scalability needed for large-scale closed-loop planner evaluation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261608/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each agent receives a local ego-centric observation (nearby vehicles, lane geometry, optional goal points, and road features within a 50 m radius, all normalized to [-1,1]) and outputs a discrete trajectory token from a K=200 vocabulary produced by K-disk clustering of short-horizon Cartesian trajectories. The policy operates at 5 Hz (0.1 s steps, horizon 2) while the simulator runs at 10 Hz, and actions are defined directly in Cartesian space with no explicit dynamics model. The centralized reference model additionally consumes the full scene context (all agent states and road graph), creating an asymmetric information structure between the decentralized policy and its anchor.

## Method

SPACER anchors decentralized self-play PPO to a pretrained tokenized autoregressive model (e.g., SMART or CAT-K) via two complementary signals: a dense log-likelihood reward r_humanlike = log π_ref(a_t | s_t) that scores each executed action under the human driving distribution, and a KL-divergence penalty D_KL(π_θ(·|o_t) ‖ π_ref(·|s_t)) that directly aligns the policy's action distribution with the reference at every timestep. By aligning the self-play action space with the reference model's K-disk tokenized vocabulary, the method enables tractable likelihood estimation and closed-form KL computation in a single forward pass per rollout batch—no autoregressive sampling or online tokenization is needed. The reference model is centralized (observing all agents), while the policy is a lightweight late-fusion MLP (~65k parameters) conditioned only on local observations, resembling a privileged-information teacher-student setup. Training uses standard PPO with task rewards (goal-reaching, collision, off-road penalties) combined with the human-likeness reward and KL alignment, and the authors show that once anchored to the reference distribution, the explicit goal reward can be removed without performance loss.

## Results

On the WOSAC validation set, SPACeR achieves a composite realism score of 0.741, outperforming PPO (0.710) and HR-PPO (0.716) across all realism metrics, with a particularly large gain in kinematic realism (0.411 vs. 0.327/0.341) and a 3× reduction in minADE (4.101 vs. 12.725/12.254). SPACeR also achieves lower collision (0.036) and off-road (0.056) rates than both imitation-learning baselines SMART (0.17/0.13) and CAT-K (0.06/0.09), while running at 211.8 scenarios/sec—approximately 10× faster than SMART and CAT-K (22.5 scenarios/sec). The resulting decentralized MLP uses only ~65k parameters, roughly 50× smaller than CAT-K's 3.2M parameters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the self-play baselines, SPACeR clearly wins: it improves composite realism from 0.710 (PPO) and 0.716 (HR-PPO) to 0.741, with the largest relative gain in kinematic realism (+26% over PPO) and a 3× reduction in minADE. Against the imitation-learning baselines, SPACeR trades some distributional realism for practical advantages: CAT-K still leads on composite (0.766 vs. 0.741), kinematic (0.490 vs. 0.411), and minADE (1.470 vs. 4.101), but SPACeR achieves substantially lower collision (0.036 vs. 0.06) and off-road (0.056 vs. 0.09) rates, and runs ~10× faster (211.8 vs. 22.5 scenarios/sec). SPACeR also surpasses SMART in composite realism (0.741 vs. 0.720) and interactive realism (0.779 vs. 0.725) while maintaining the same throughput advantage. In closed-loop planner evaluation (Section 4.3), SPACeR agents produce PDM scores with lower correlation to ground-truth log replays than CAT-K, indicating more reactive and realistic penalization of unsafe planners—particularly in collision scenarios. Overall, SPACeR positions itself as the best trade-off between realism, safety, and throughput, matching or exceeding imitation methods on safety metrics while being an order of magnitude faster and 50× smaller.

## Relation to Prior Work

SPACER bridges two established paradigms in driving simulation: self-play RL (GPUDrive, GIGAFlow) that scales to multi-agent interaction but diverges from human norms, and tokenized imitation learning (SMART, CAT-K, TrajEglish) that captures the human driving distribution but is slow and non-reactive. The key departure from prior work is the RL-first framing: rather than pretraining a generative model and fine-tuning with RL (as in RIFT or GRPO-based approaches), SPACER uses self-play as the foundation and incorporates a pretrained centralized tokenized model purely as a reward provider, supplying per-agent, per-timestep likelihood scores and KL divergence. This extends the HR-PPO idea of reference-policy regularization by replacing a small decentralized BC model with a large centralized tokenized model whose action space is explicitly aligned via K-disk tokenization, enabling closed-form KL computation without online tokenization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for AV testing: a 65K-parameter decentralized MLP achieves competitive WOSAC realism at 10× the throughput and 50× smaller size than state-of-the-art tokenized simulators, enabling real-time large-scale closed-loop planner evaluation that was previously infeasible. The finding that even a 0.3M-parameter reference model (realism 0.64) yields a SPACER policy at 0.73 realism suggests the reference model acts as a soft behavioral prior rather than a performance ceiling, opening the door to cheaper anchoring strategies. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to VRU-specific behaviors (sidewalk adherence, crosswalk usage), how to address WOSAC's known limitations in penalizing safe-but-divergent behaviors, and whether multi-GPU training can close the 24–48 hour training bottleneck.
