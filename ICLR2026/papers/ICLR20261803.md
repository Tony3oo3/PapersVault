## Problem

Reconstruction-based anomaly detection (RBAD) has strong empirical success—generative models trained only on normal data produce larger reconstruction errors for anomalies—but the theoretical understanding of why this works remains limited. This paper addresses three fundamental questions: how does a convolutional autoencoder extract features from normal training data, what role do those learned features play during reconstruction, and why are anomalies provably harder to reconstruct than normal data?

## Input / Output

The input is an image decomposed into P disjoint patches, each a d-dimensional vector in a Hilbert space, modeled as a unit-norm feature vector plus bounded noise (|⟨ρ, v_k⟩| ≤ ε). The 2-layer convolutional autoencoder reconstructs each patch via a max-pooling mechanism that selects the single kernel-patch pairing with the largest convolution value, and the resulting per-patch MSE serves as the anomaly detection signal. Anomalies are defined as normal images with one or more patches replaced by either random unit-norm noise (non-semantic) or an auxiliary feature not aligned with the normal semantics (semantic).

## Method

The paper introduces the cone set of a feature—a d-dimensional hypercone whose height grows linearly with iteration t while its radius grows sub-linearly (f_r = f_h · o(t)), making the cone progressively sharper during training. The central result (Theorem 1) proves that any kernel entering a feature's cone set at initialization remains absorbed for all subsequent iterations, growing faster along that feature's direction than along all others, so the kernel's direction converges to the feature; Theorem 2 guarantees each cone set is non-empty at random initialization with high probability. During reconstruction, the max pooling operation acts as a signal filter: Theorem 3 shows that for non-semantic anomalies, the convolution between the replaced patch and absorbed kernels is small with high probability, so the anomaly patch's signal is suppressed and reconstruction fails. The over-parameterized setting (C = c₂d > d) is essential to avoid local minima and ensure every feature with β_k ≠ 0 is captured by at least one kernel.

## Results

In the synthesized experiments (Figure 4), the 2-layer convolutional autoencoder trained on normal data exhibits a clear reconstruction error gap between normal data and non-semantic anomalies across all tested configurations (c₃ ∈ {1.2, 1.5, 2}, P from 20 to 60, ε ∈ {0.1, 0.01, 0.001}). The gap between normal data and semantic anomalies is consistently smaller, consistent with the theoretical prediction in Remark 3.4 that RBADs are less effective for semantic anomalies. On real-world MNIST and CIFAR-10, kernel visualizations confirm the cone set intuition: some kernels develop clear feature contours by epoch 10 and stabilize by epoch 50, while others remain noisy, corresponding to kernels not absorbed by any feature cone.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper is a theoretical analysis work and does not directly benchmark against other anomaly detection methods. The synthesized experiments are designed to validate the theoretical modeling (Assumption 2.1, the P-patch data model, and the cone set framework) rather than to compare RBAD against competing approaches. The real-world experiments on MNIST and CIFAR-10 serve to visualize and validate the proposed cone set intuition for kernel growth dynamics, not to compare detection accuracy. The paper explicitly states that its main goal is to complement, not compete with, existing RBAD methods, and the experiments are conducted to support the theoretical findings (Theorems 1–3) rather than to establish state-of-the-art performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper applies the feature learning paradigm of Allen-Zhu & Li (2021, 2023) to reconstruction-based anomaly detection (RBAD), a task that has been studied almost exclusively empirically. It adopts the P-patch data model and the signal-noise decomposition from Allen-Zhu & Li (2023), but introduces the novel 'cone set' abstraction to characterize how convolutional kernels align with features during gradient descent. The authors explicitly position their work as the first to analyze generative models (autoencoders) through the feature learning lens, bridging a gap between the theoretical understanding of discriminative feature extraction and the empirical success of RBAD methods like those of Salehi et al. (2021) and Mousakhan et al. (2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The cone set framework provides the first rigorous explanation for why reconstruction errors differ between normal data and anomalies, grounding a widely-used but theoretically unexplained heuristic in provable training dynamics. However, the analysis is confined to a 2-layer architecture with non-overlapping patches and a simplified global max pooling, leaving open whether the cone set intuition extends to deeper networks, overlapping convolutions, or the more challenging semantic anomaly regime (which the paper itself shows is harder to detect). The framework also suggests that over-parameterization (C > d) is necessary to avoid local minima, a practical insight that could inform architecture design for RBAD systems.
