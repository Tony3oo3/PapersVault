## Problem

Multi-agent LLM systems are widely claimed to exhibit 'greater-than-the-sum-of-its-parts' effects, yet there is no principled, data-driven method to test whether such systems display genuine higher-order synergy versus merely aggregating independent agents. The paper addresses three gaps: detecting whether emergence is present, determining what functional advantages it confers, and establishing whether prompt design can systematically steer coordination from spurious coupling to goal-directed complementarity.

## Input / Output

The method consumes sequences of integer guesses (0–50) from N=10 agents playing a no-communication group binary search game, where agents receive only binary group-level feedback ('too high'/'too low') and are unaware of each other's guesses or group size. Microstates are transformed into equal-share deviations (raw guess minus target/N) to remove trivial level differences, then quantile-binned into two levels to control small-sample entropy bias. Outputs include pairwise dynamical synergy, macro-level practical emergence scores, triplet coalition information gain (G₃), Total Stability (I₃ normalized by macro entropy), agent differentiation indicators from hierarchical models, and binary group success, all computed per experiment and aggregated across 200 replications per condition.

## Method

The framework combines four complementary tests grounded in partial information decomposition (PID) and time-delayed mutual information (TDMI). (1) Emergence capacity computes a two-source PID of predictive information from each agent pair's current states to their joint future state, extracting the synergy component as pairwise dynamical synergy. (2) The practical criterion is a whole-minus-parts metric comparing the macro signal's self-predictability (I(V_t; V_{t+ℓ})) against the sum of individual parts' predictability. (3) A coalition test extends this to triplets, measuring G₃ as the additional predictive information a triplet provides over its most informative pair. (4) A hierarchical mixed-model test with agent-level random intercepts and slopes detects stable identity-linked differentiation. Falsification uses two surrogate nulls: row-shuffles break agent identities while column-block-shuffles preserve individual dynamics but disrupt cross-agent temporal alignment, allowing the framework to localize synergy as identity-locked versus dynamic alignment. Entropy estimation employs Jeffreys' prior smoothing (α=½ Dirichlet) and Miller-Madow bias correction, with robustness checks via functional null-model residualization and stabilized inverse probability weighting for censored trials.

## Results

The practical emergence criterion (bias-corrected, time-trend demeaned) is significantly above zero in all three conditions: Plain (p=1.5×10⁻¹⁶), Persona (p=6.6×10⁻⁷), and ToM (p=0.02), confirming emergence capacity in multi-agent LLM systems. The ToM condition uniquely produces significant triplet mutual information I₃ (p=3.5×10⁻¹⁴) and Total Stability (p=2.9×10⁻¹⁴), collapsing group behavior into a predictable macro-state, while Plain (p=0.974) and Persona (p=0.846) remain near zero. A regression analysis reveals that synergy and redundancy individually do not predict success, but their interaction does (β=0.24, p=0.014), with each amplifying the other's benefit by 27% on the log-odds scale. Cross-model experiments confirm emergence in LLaMA-70B, Gemini 2.0 Flash, and Qwen3, while LLaMA-8B largely fails to break oscillatory cycles.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is across three prompt interventions (Plain control, Persona, ToM) rather than against external baselines. The Plain condition exhibits emergence capacity and practical emergence but near-zero I₃ and Total Stability, indicating groups remain in a chaotic, uncoordinated 'gaseous' state. Persona introduces stable identity-linked differentiation (more groups show significant agent-level random effects) but does not produce goal-directed complementarity (I₃ remains non-significant, p=0.846). ToM is the only condition that simultaneously achieves identity-linked differentiation, goal-directed complementarity, and a sharp increase in Total Stability (p=2.9×10⁻¹⁴), effectively steering the system from a disordered regime into a stable basin of attraction. Across models, high-capability systems (LLaMA-70B, Gemini 2.0 Flash, Qwen3) replicate the main findings, while LLaMA-8B fails to develop goal-directed synergy due to insufficient ToM capacity. The Qwen3 reasoning model exhibits a novel failure mode termed 'paralysis under coordination ambiguity,' where agents enter infinite chain-of-thought loops when reconciling local strategies with noisy group feedback, resulting in non-significant I₃ despite strong emergence capacity scores.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transplants information-theoretic tools developed primarily for neuroscience—partial information decomposition (Williams & Beer, 2010), dynamic emergence formalism (Rosas et al., 2020), time-delayed mutual information (Luppi et al., 2022), and integrated information measures (Barrett & Seth, 2011)—into the multi-agent LLM setting, where prior work (AgentVerse, MetaGPT, AutoGen) has relied almost exclusively on win-rate comparisons without principled measures of higher-order structure. It also draws directly on collective intelligence research from human groups (Goldstone et al., 2024; Riedl et al., 2021; Theiner, 2018), importing the alignment-versus-complementarity tradeoff as a theoretical scaffold. The key departure from prior multi-agent LLM literature is the introduction of falsification tests (row-shuffle and column-shuffle surrogates) that localize synergy as either identity-locked or dynamically aligned, going beyond the binary 'does the group outperform the individual' question.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides the first data-driven, falsifiable test for whether multi-agent LLM systems exhibit genuine collective structure versus mere aggregation, which is critical as these systems are increasingly deployed for complex tasks. The finding that a simple ToM prompt acts as a control parameter shifting the system from a chaotic 'gaseous' regime to a stable basin of attraction offers a concrete, low-cost design lever for practitioners. Key limitations include the single-task setting (group binary search), the restriction to order-k=2 synergy due to small sample sizes, and the endogeneity challenge in linking synergy to performance (only marginal causal mediation effects). Open questions include whether the framework generalizes across diverse tasks, whether higher-order (k≥3) synergy can be reliably detected, and whether the mean-field coupling regime observed here is an artifact of the no-communication constraint or a fundamental property of LLM collectives.
