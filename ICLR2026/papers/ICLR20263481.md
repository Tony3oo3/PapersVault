## Problem

The relationship between Bayesian sampling and stochastic gradient descent in neural networks has been a long-standing open question. Prior theoretical work (Mandt et al., 2016) established a connection only under the assumption of non-degenerate (quadratic) minima, which is known to be false for neural networks whose loss surfaces are highly singular. This paper asks: what is the steady-state distribution of SGD when the loss landscape has the degenerate, singular structure typical of deep networks, and how does it relate to the Bayesian posterior?

## Input / Output

The method consumes the local learning coefficient (LLC) computed over a neighborhood of the current weights, the spectral dimension estimated from weight displacement scaling, and SGD hyperparameters (learning rate, batch size) as its geometric inputs. It produces a local steady-state weight distribution that is a tempered version of the Bayesian posterior, where the tempering exponent mD_ξ depends on dataset size and an effective diffusion coefficient derived from the walk dimension. The LLC is estimated via SGLD sampling around each weight configuration, and the spectral dimension is extracted by linear regression on log-displacement vs. log-time, making the pipeline sensitive to the choice of characteristic length scale ξ.

## Method

The paper models late-stage SGD dynamics as anomalous (sub-diffusive) diffusion on porous media governed by a time-fractional Fokker-Planck equation with a Caputo derivative of order α. The key geometric insight is that the local learning coefficient from singular learning theory acts as a Minkowski-Bouligand (mass) fractal dimension of the loss surface, while the spectral dimension captures how quickly SGD actually explores that volume; the Alexander-Orbach relation d_walk = 2λ/d_s then links these to a scalar effective diffusion coefficient D_ξ = ξ^(2−d_walk). Solving the steady-state of the fractional FPE under the assumption that D_ξ is locally constant yields p_s(w) ∝ exp(−γL_m[w]/D_ξ), which, when the loss is log-loss, becomes p_s(w)^(mD_ξ) ∝ p(X_m|w), i.e., a tempered Bayesian posterior where the tempering is governed by accessibility constraints from the learning coefficient. The theory further proves that d_s ≤ λ(w(t)) in the long-time limit, explaining why SGD preferentially concentrates in low-LLC (flat, well-generalizing) regions. Empirical validation across language models (TinyStories-1M/15M/33M), vision models (ResNet18/34, VGG16 on Tiny ImageNet), and MNIST fully-connected networks confirms the sub-diffusive scaling and the posterior-tempering correspondence with SGLD.

## Results

Across six model architectures (language and vision), the fractional sub-diffusion model predicts weight displacement with r² = 0.98 for TinyStories-1M, TinyLlama-15M, and TinyStories-33M, and r² ≈ 1 for ResNet18, ResNet34, and VGG16 fine-tuned on Tiny ImageNet. The spectral dimension d_s is consistently bounded above by the local learning coefficient λ (e.g., d_s = 21.4 vs. λ = 32 for TinyStories-1M; d_s = 0.57 vs. λ = 72.05 for ResNet18), validating the theoretical inequality d_s ≤ λ. In the posterior concentration experiment on Moons, the tempered SGD steady-state distribution matches the SGLD-approximated Bayesian posterior with KL divergence 0.009, Wasserstein distance 0.002, and Jensen-Shannon divergence 0.003.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline comparison is against SGLD (Welling & Teh, 2011) as an approximation of the true Bayesian posterior. On the Moons dataset with 500 SGD-trained networks, the tempered SGD steady-state distribution (tempered at ξ = 0.5) almost entirely agrees with the SGLD posterior: KL(Bayes‖Tempered SGD) = 0.009, Wasserstein = 0.002, JS = 0.003, indicating the tempering correction recovers the Bayesian distribution from the SGD distribution. The paper also implicitly contrasts its fractional Fokker-Planck model against the standard Langevin/Brownian motion prediction (R(t) ∝ t^{1/2}), showing that actual SGD dynamics are sub-diffusive (R(t) ∝ t^{1/ν} with ν ≥ 2), which the classical model cannot capture. The Mandt et al. (2016) variational analysis, which assumes quadratic (non-degenerate) minima, is extended here to the singular case where the Hessian is degenerate, and the tempered posterior framework explains the previously observed but unexplained SGD–Bayesian correspondence (Mingard et al., 2020) in terms of accessibility constraints governed by the learning coefficient.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the SGD-as-Bayesian-sampler line of work (Mandt et al., 2016; Mingard et al., 2020) from the regular (non-degenerate) case to the singular case that actually characterizes neural networks, by importing the local learning coefficient from singular learning theory as the fractal mass dimension of the diffusion medium. It builds on the Stochastic Gradient Noise / Fokker-Planck framework and the empirical observation of sub-diffusive weight dynamics (Chen et al., 2021), but provides the first rigorous theoretical derivation connecting the LLC, spectral dimension, and walk dimension via the Alexander-Orbach relation to produce an explicit steady-state distribution. The porous-media diffusion formalism from Bouchaud & Georges (1990) and the Caputo fractional derivative supply the mathematical machinery, while the LLC estimator of Lau et al. (2024) provides the practical measurement tool.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The tempered posterior formula (p_s(w)^(mD_ξ) ∝ p(X_m|w)) gives a concrete, quantitative correction to the naive 'SGD samples the Bayesian posterior' picture, explaining why SGD preferentially concentrates in low-LLC (better-generalizing) regions and providing a principled way to calibrate uncertainty estimates from SGD runs. Key limitations include the assumption of an approximate steady state (which may fail under label noise), the informal scalar diffusion approximation, and the inability to handle adaptive optimizers like Adam that exhibit multiple spectral dimensions over training. Open questions include whether the framework extends to non-equilibrium steady states, how to incorporate spatial fractional derivatives for grokking-like phase transitions, and whether the tempered posterior can be used practically for model selection or ensemble construction.
