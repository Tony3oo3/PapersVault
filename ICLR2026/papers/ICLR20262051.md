## Problem

Pre-trained large time series models (LTSMs) often exhibit poorly conditioned, non-convex loss landscapes due to overfitting during large-scale pre-training, causing direct fine-tuning to overfit and sometimes perform worse than training from scratch. Existing fine-tuning strategies (full fine-tuning, linear probing, LP-FF) fail to address this underlying landscape pathology, substantially diminishing the benefits of pre-training on downstream tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262051/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes two weight tensors of identical architecture—the pre-trained LTSM and a randomly initialized auxiliary LTSM (using Kaiming or Xavier schemes)—along with downstream time series data. A single scalar interpolation coefficient α (typically 0.3–0.9) controls the blend, and the resulting smoothed weights are then standard fine-tuned with MSE loss. The approach introduces zero additional memory or computational overhead beyond the one-time interpolation step, and the same pipeline applies to forecasting, anomaly detection, and imputation tasks across models ranging from 3 MB (TTMs) to 3.8 GB (TimesFM).

## Method

Smoothed Full Fine-tuning (SFF) first constructs an auxiliary LTSM via Kaiming or Xavier random initialization, which empirically and theoretically (via the Hessian trace-to-Frobenius-norm ratio) yields a flat, smooth loss landscape. It then linearly interpolates the auxiliary weights with the pre-trained weights (Θ₃ = αΘ₁ + (1−α)Θ₂) to produce a smoothed model. The key theoretical insight is that under a local quadratic approximation, this interpolation reduces the maximum Hessian eigenvalue at sharp minima (perturbing them toward smoother basins) while leaving flat minima unchanged (since both endpoints already satisfy λ_max ≤ τ). The smoothed model is then fine-tuned with standard gradient descent, enabling escape from suboptimal sharp basins toward better local optima. Unlike prior weight-interpolation work (model soups, continual learning), SFF uses interpolation as a pre-fine-tuning landscape-smoothing step rather than a final model-merging operation, and provides the first formal loss-landscape-theoretic justification for why a randomly initialized model can serve as an effective smoothing agent.

## Results

On the Timer LTSM with prediction length 96 and 100% training data, SFF achieves MSE of 0.080 on Exchange rate, 0.355 on ETTh1, 0.274 on ETTh2, 0.295 on ETTm1, 0.160 on ETTm2, 0.144 on Weather, 0.130 on Electricity, and 0.355 on Traffic, consistently outperforming full fine-tuning (FF) by an average of 3% and up to 6.5%. Extending to TimesFM and MOMENT, SFF yields average MSE improvements of 11.45% and 8.31% over FF, respectively. In zero-shot forecasting, smoothing the loss landscape improves Timer by 6.13% and TimesFM by 35.75% on average across seven datasets. SFF also outperforms LP and LPFF by 7.17%–41.57% in MSE across all data proportions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SFF consistently outperforms all five compared baselines across all eight forecasting datasets. Against LPFF (Kumar et al.), SFF achieves MSE reductions of 7.17%–41.57% (e.g., 0.081 vs. 0.096 on Exchange, 0.353 vs. 0.373 on ETTh1 at 100% data). Against SAM (Foret et al.), SFF improves MSE from 0.088 to 0.081 on Exchange and from 0.362 to 0.355 on ETTh1. Against SWA (Izmailov et al.), SFF reduces MSE from 0.094 to 0.081 on Exchange and from 0.366 to 0.355 on ETTh1. Against Mixout (Lee et al.), SFF lowers MSE from 0.090 to 0.081 on Exchange and from 0.376 to 0.355 on ETTh1. Against L2-SP (Xuhong et al.), SFF achieves 0.081 vs. 0.090 on Exchange and 0.355 vs. 0.368 on ETTh1. The paper attributes these consistent wins to SFF's explicit smoothing of the non-convex loss landscape, whereas the other methods only apply regularization or averaging without addressing the underlying sharp-minima problem.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes weight interpolation—previously studied in model merging (Wortsman et al., 2022) and continual learning (Kozal et al., 2024)—for a fundamentally different purpose: smoothing the loss landscape of a single pre-trained model before fine-tuning, rather than ensembling multiple trained models. It draws on loss landscape theory (Keskar et al., 2016 on sharp minima; Li et al., 2018 on landscape visualization; Fort & Scherlis, 2019 on initialization-induced smoothness) to formalize why interpolation with a randomly initialized model perturbs sharp minima while preserving flat regions. The paper positions itself against standard fine-tuning strategies (FF, LP, LPFF from Kumar et al.) and optimization tricks (SAM, SWA, Mixout, L2-SP), arguing that none address the root cause of poor trainability in pre-trained LTSMs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that direct full fine-tuning of pre-trained LTSMs can yield worse test performance than training from scratch is a practical warning for the rapidly growing LTSM field, and SFF offers a zero-overhead remedy (a few lines of PyTorch) that consistently improves across eight architectures. However, the theoretical analysis relies on a local quadratic approximation of the Hessian along the interpolation path, which may not hold in highly non-convex regimes, and the optimal interpolation coefficient α remains a fixed hyperparameter rather than being adaptively learned. Open questions include whether this loss-landscape-smoothing principle generalizes to LLMs and vision foundation models, and whether the moderate gains (3–11% MSE) scale to larger model sizes or more challenging distribution shifts.
