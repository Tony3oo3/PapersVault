## Problem

Meta-reinforcement learning meta-policies exhibit imbalanced generalization: they adapt well to some tasks in the task distribution but poorly to others. Existing improvement methods (task weighting, regularization, predefined augmentation) either require a target task set or use fixed augmentation rules that do not maximally increase the task information stored in the meta-policy. This paper asks whether an explanation-based strategy—identifying which training tasks are most relevant to the poorly adapted tasks and then focusing learning on those tasks—can systematically improve generalization.

## Input / Output

The method operates post-hoc on an already-trained meta-policy π₀ (e.g., from MAML). It takes as input the full set of training tasks, a small set of poorly adapted tasks (identified by evaluating π₀ on sampled test tasks and selecting the worst-performing subset), and requires additional environment interactions to generate augmented state-action tuples via mixup. It produces an improved meta-policy, an explanation in the form of the top-N critical training tasks (with their importance weights), and a learned Gaussian mixing-coefficient distribution P_{φ_λ}(λ) that parameterizes how to augment the critical tasks. The augmented states are convex combinations of original states, assumed to remain feasible in the environment.

## Method

The approach has two stages. Stage 1 (explanation) solves a bilevel optimization: the upper level learns a weight vector ω over training tasks to maximize performance on the poorly adapted tasks, while the lower level computes the corresponding weighted meta-policy; the top-weighted tasks are the critical tasks. Stage 2 (augmentation) formalizes 'paying more attention' as maximizing the conditional mutual information I(θ; {T̄ᵢᶜʳⁱ(Λᵢ)} | {Tᵢᶜʳⁱ}) between the meta-parameter θ and the mixup-augmented critical tasks given the original critical tasks. This is cast as a second bilevel problem: the upper level optimizes the mixing-coefficient distribution P_{φ_λ}(λ) (parameterized as a Gaussian) to maximize CMI, while the lower level computes the posterior distribution of θ under both the augmented and original critical tasks via reparameterized gradient ascent. The key distinction from prior augmentation methods is that the mixing distribution is learned rather than predefined, and the CMI objective explicitly measures the additional task information stored in the meta-policy. Theoretical analysis guarantees O(1/√K) convergence, strictly positive CMI after augmentation, and a reduced generalization gap via an induced quadratic regularization on θ.

## Results

XMRL (MAML+XMRL) achieves the best performance across all four benchmarks: 0.97 success rate on Drone navigation (vs. 0.87 for MAML), 421.13 cumulative reward on Stock Market (vs. 359.13), −44.67 on HalfCheetah (vs. −68.89, a 35% improvement), and 119.15 on Ant (vs. 100.64). The paper reports that XMRL improves MAML by 35% on HalfCheetah while the strongest baseline (MAML+MR) achieves less than 15% improvement. Ablation in Table 2 confirms that the gains come from poorly adapted tasks (e.g., Drone poor tasks improve from 0.55 to 0.93) while non-poorly adapted tasks degrade in at most 5% of cases with <4% average drop.

## Baselines & Comparisons

XMRL outperforms all three baselines on every benchmark. Against Task Weighting (TW, Cai et al. 2020), XMRL leads by a wide margin: +0.10 success rate on Drone (0.97 vs. 0.87), +59.06 reward on Stock Market (421.13 vs. 362.07), +20.47 on HalfCheetah (−44.67 vs. −65.14), and +19.23 on Ant (119.15 vs. 99.92). Against Meta Augmentation (MA, Yao et al. 2021), which uses a predefined mixup distribution, XMRL still wins on all four tasks (e.g., +31.96 on Stock Market, +18.82 on HalfCheetah), confirming that learning the optimal augmentation distribution via CMI maximization is superior to fixed mixing rules. Against Meta Regularization (MR, Wang et al. 2023), XMRL again dominates (e.g., +58.60 on Stock Market, +16.48 on HalfCheetah). The largest relative gap appears on HalfCheetah, where XMRL's 35% improvement over MAML exceeds the best baseline's improvement by more than 2×.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: example-based explanation in RL (Liu & Zhu, 2025; Guo et al., 2021b), information-theoretic meta-learning (Yin et al., 2019), and task augmentation via mixup (Zhang et al., 2018; Yao et al., 2021). It departs from existing task-weighting methods (Cai et al., 2020; Yao et al., 2021) that require a target task set to guide weighting, and from predefined augmentation rules (Wang et al., 2020; Rajendran et al., 2020) that do not maximize stored task information. The key conceptual departure is formulating the augmentation problem as a bilevel optimization that learns the mixing-coefficient distribution to maximize conditional mutual information, rather than using a fixed heuristic, and coupling this with an explanation stage that identifies which tasks to augment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The post-hoc, plug-in nature of XMRL (applicable to any existing MRL algorithm) makes it practically attractive for practitioners who already have a trained meta-policy with imbalanced generalization. The information-theoretic formalization provides a principled alternative to heuristic augmentation rules, and the theoretical guarantees (convergence rate, CMI positivity, generalization gap bound) add rigor. However, the method is only validated with MAML as the base algorithm, assumes augmented states remain feasible, and requires additional environment interactions for data generation. Open questions include whether the CMI objective generalizes to black-box/context-based MRL, how the method scales to very large task distributions, and whether the explanation stage's bilevel optimization remains tractable in high-dimensional settings.
