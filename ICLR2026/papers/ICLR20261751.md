## Problem

Amortized Bayesian inference for hierarchical models requires simulating an entire dataset of datasets for every training sample, rendering it infeasible for large-scale problems with thousands of groups or expensive simulators. While compositional score matching (CSM) offers a divide-and-conquer alternative by aggregating per-group scores, it suffers from compounding approximation errors that cause numerical divergence as the number of groups grows, failing even on simple Gaussian models beyond ~100 observations.

## Input / Output

The method consumes J exchangeable data groups, each of which can be a time series (e.g., fluorescence decay curves) or scalar observations, and produces posterior samples of both global hyperparameters η and local parameters θ_j for every group. During training, only a single group is simulated per batch instance (rather than the full J-group dataset), and during inference the compositional score is evaluated over mini-batches of M groups (typically 10% of J) to bound memory. The hierarchical output is generated via ancestral sampling: global parameters are drawn first using the compositional score, then each local parameter is sampled conditioned on the global draw and its own group data.

## Method

The core contribution is an error-damping mini-batch estimator for compositional score matching that stabilizes the bridging density p_t(η_t | Y_{1:J}) by introducing a monotonic damping function d(t) that reduces the influence of individual score terms in high-noise regimes while recovering the true posterior as t→0. This is combined with an unbiased mini-batch estimator that replaces the full sum over J groups with a scaled sum over M randomly sampled groups, and with an SDE-based reverse process using adaptive second-order solvers instead of annealed Langevin sampling. For hierarchical models, separate score networks are trained for local and global parameters via a joint denoising score matching objective, connected through the inverse factorization of the hierarchical prior; the global network uses the compositional formulation while the local network uses standard conditional score matching. A noise schedule shift (increasing the cosine schedule parameter s) during inference further compresses the high-noise portion of the reverse process, improving stability at large J. Together, these modifications enable stable compositional inference over 250,000+ data groups and 750,000+ parameters using less than one full dataset simulation for training.

## Results

On the hierarchical AR(1) model, the proposed method matches NUTS on posterior contraction (1.00 vs 1.00 at 16×16 and 128×128 grids) while achieving competitive RMSE (0.08 vs 0.03 at 16×16; 0.09 vs 0.01 at 128×128), using less than one full grid simulation for the 128×128 case where NUTS requires ~9 hours on 64 CPU cores. At 16×16, the method outperforms direct ABI baselines (ABI-NF: RMSE 0.19, contraction 0.51; ABI-DM: RMSE 0.12, contraction 0.71) with the same simulation budget of 40 grids. On the real FLI application with 250,000+ groups and 750,000+ parameters, the hierarchical approach achieves a mean R² of 0.961 (s.d. 0.017) for posterior predictive fits, compared to 0.871 (s.d. 0.110) for pixel-wise MLE and 0.921 (s.d. 0.12) for a non-hierarchical diffusion model, using only the equivalent of 350 full images for training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against NUTS (Stan), the proposed method matches posterior contraction at all grid sizes (1.00 at 16×16 and 128×128) but shows higher RMSE at larger grids (0.09 vs 0.01 at 128×128); however, NUTS requires ~9 hours on 64 CPU cores for 128×128 while the proposed method completes inference in minutes on a single GPU, and NUTS requires a tractable likelihood. Against direct ABI baselines (ABI-NF with normalizing flows and ABI-DM with diffusion models from Habermann et al. 2024 / Heinrich et al. 2024), the proposed method substantially outperforms at 16×16 (RMSE 0.08 vs 0.19/0.12; contraction 1.00 vs 0.51/0.71) with the same simulation budget, and is the only method that scales to 128×128 where direct ABI methods are infeasible. The 10× budget variants (ABI-NF-10, ABI-DM-10) narrow the gap (RMSE 0.09/0.04, contraction 0.88/0.99) but still fall short on contraction and require 10× more simulations. Against the pixel-wise MLE baseline in the FLI application, the hierarchical approach improves mean R² from 0.871 to 0.961 and reduces standard deviation from 0.110 to 0.017, demonstrating the benefit of borrowing strength across pixels. Against the non-hierarchical diffusion model (pixel-wise), the hierarchical method improves R² from 0.921 to 0.961 with tighter uncertainty (s.d. 0.017 vs 0.12).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends compositional score matching (CSM; Geffner et al., 2023) from exchangeable models to hierarchical Bayesian models, building on recent adaptations to complete pooling (Linhart et al., 2026) and time series (Gloeckler et al., 2025). The key departure is replacing the annealed Langevin or Gaussian-approximation samplers of prior CSM work with an SDE-based formulation (Song et al., 2021b) combined with an error-damping bridging density and a mini-batch estimator, which together resolve the compounding approximation errors that cause divergence as the number of groups grows. Unlike direct hierarchical ABI approaches (Habermann et al., 2024; Heinrich et al., 2024) that require simulating entire datasets of datasets per training sample, this method trains on single groups and composes scores at inference time, fundamentally changing the simulation cost structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work removes a major practical barrier to likelihood-free hierarchical Bayesian inference: prior methods become infeasible beyond a few hundred groups, while many real applications (pharmacokinetics, biomedical imaging) involve tens to hundreds of thousands of groups. The demonstrated application to fluorescence lifetime imaging with 750,000+ parameters shows the approach is not merely a benchmark exercise but addresses a genuine scientific need where MCMC is computationally prohibitive. Key open questions include posterior calibration under extreme contraction (where the global posterior becomes nearly a point mass), generalization beyond two hierarchical levels, and systematic characterization of the damping schedule trade-off between stability and accuracy.
