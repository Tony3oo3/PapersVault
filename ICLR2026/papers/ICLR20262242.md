## Problem

Adversarial Imitation Learning (AIL) is widely regarded as unstable, and prior work has predominantly focused on stabilizing the discriminator (density ratio estimation) while largely overlooking the reward assignment (RA) function that maps discriminator logits to policy rewards. Although RA functions are derived from f-divergence theory, they neglect practical training-stability challenges, and no prior work has explored discovering data-driven RA functions optimized directly for imitation policy performance.

## Input / Output

The method consumes 10 expert demonstrations (subsampled every 20th transition) from a PPO-trained policy in a single environment (Minatar SpaceInvaders) for meta-learning, then applies the discovered RA function to unseen environments (Brax MuJoCo tasks and Minatar games) with the same demonstration protocol. The output is a reward assignment function expressed as executable JAX code that takes discriminator logits (approximating log-density ratios) as input and produces bounded scalar rewards in [0,1] for policy optimization via PPO or A2C.

## Method

DAIL uses an LLM-guided evolutionary search (GPT-4.1-mini) to discover the RA function, representing candidates as Python/JAX code and evaluating fitness via the Wasserstein distance between trained policy rollouts and expert demonstrations. The base population is seeded with four known f-divergence RA functions (GAIL, FAIRL, AIRL, GAIL-heuristic); at each generation, the LLM performs crossover by combining parent functions guided by their fitness scores, and the top-K candidates are selected. The discovered function r_disc(x) = 0.5·sigmoid(x)·[tanh(x)+1] is bounded in [0,1] and saturates near zero for x ≤ −1.8, effectively filtering noisy low-quality state-action pairs while preserving informative gradients in the [−1, 0] range where most log-density ratios concentrate. Unlike all baselines, r_disc does not correspond to a valid f-divergence, trading theoretical convergence guarantees for empirically superior training stability and generalization across unseen environments and policy optimizers.

## Results

DAIL reduces the Wasserstein distance to expert trajectories by 20% and improves normalized returns by 12.5% compared to the best human-designed baseline (GAIL). On the Minatar suite, DAIL significantly outperforms all baselines across all evaluation metrics; on Brax, DAIL achieves a probability of improvement greater than 0.5 against every baseline, with statistically significant gains in mean performance (though a slightly lower median than AIRL). DAIL also generalizes to the A2C policy optimizer (not used during discovery) and outperforms GAIL in 3 out of 5 discriminator regularization regimes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the four primary AIL baselines (GAIL, AIRL, FAIRL, GAIL-heuristic), DAIL outperforms all on Minatar and most metrics on Brax, with a 12.5% normalized-return improvement over GAIL (the strongest baseline). AIRL achieves a slightly higher median on Brax but performs poorly on Minatar due to predominantly negative rewards that incentivize early episode termination; FAIRL and GAIL-heuristic are consistently weak across both suites. Compared to OPEN-ES (same 200-evaluation budget), DAIL wins on 6 of 8 environments, with the largest gaps on Hopper (+0.56) and Ant (+0.34), while losing on Asterix and Reacher. Against non-adversarial methods, DAIL substantially outperforms both BC and IQ-Learn on all four Brax locomotion tasks (e.g., 0.88 vs. 0.23 on Ant, 1.72 vs. 0.02 on Hopper), and IQ-Learn itself fails to surpass BC in 3 of 4 environments. DAIL also outperforms additional f-divergence-based AIL variants (Pearson χ², Squared Hellinger, Total Variation, Wasserstein-GAIL) on 5 of 7 test environments.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the f-divergence minimization framework of Ghasemipour et al. (2020), which unified GAIL, AIRL, and FAIRL under a common two-stage decomposition into density ratio estimation and reward assignment. While prior work on stabilizing AIL has predominantly targeted the discriminator (Step 2) through improved loss functions, architectures, and regularizers, this paper shifts focus to the reward assignment function (Step 3) and discovers it via LLM-guided evolutionary search, drawing on the FunSearch/EvolveAct paradigm for algorithm discovery. The key departure is treating the RA function as a free-form code object to be optimized end-to-end against a Wasserstein-distance fitness, rather than deriving it from a fixed f-divergence, yielding the first meta-learned AIL algorithm (DAIL).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DAIL provides the first evidence that a data-driven, bounded reward assignment function can outperform all theoretically-derived f-divergence-based alternatives across unseen environments and policy optimizers, suggesting that practical training stability matters more than divergence-theoretic elegance. The analysis revealing that GAIL's unbounded RA function inadvertently rewards near-random state-action pairs (log-ratio ≈ −2) while r_disc saturates near zero offers a concrete mechanistic explanation for AIL instability. Key limitations include the lack of convergence guarantees (r_disc is not a valid f-divergence), the static nature of the discovered function, and evaluation restricted to relatively simple benchmarks (Minatar, MuJoCo); scaling to Atari-57 or Procgen and exploring time-aware RA functions remain open.
