## Problem

Evaluating heterogeneous treatment effect (HTE) estimators is inherently difficult because ground-truth treatment effects are unobservable. Gao (2025) introduced relative error to compare two HTE estimators, but their estimator requires all nuisance parameters (propensity score and outcome models) to be consistent at a rate faster than n^{-1/4}. In practice, outcome regression models are trained within one treatment group and extrapolated to the other, making them prone to systematic bias under covariate shift, which invalidates the relative error estimate.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a pair of pre-trained HTE estimators τ̂₁(x) and τ̂₂(x) along with a test dataset of (X, A, Y) triples, and produces a point estimate of the relative error δ(τ̂₁, τ̂₂) with an asymptotic (1−η) confidence interval. The loss functions are constructed specifically around the difference (τ̂₁ − τ̂₂), so the nuisance network is retrained for each pair being compared. As a secondary output, the method yields an aggregated HTE estimator that averages the outcome-head predictions across all pairs of candidate estimators, providing a new HTE estimate without requiring ground truth.

## Method

The core insight is that by exploiting the algebraic relationship between the propensity score and outcome regression models, one can design loss functions that guarantee the key moment conditions for √n-consistency hold even when the outcome model is misspecified. Specifically, a weighted least squares loss L_wls is constructed for the outcome heads so that its population minimizer satisfies the first moment condition in Eq. (4) regardless of misspecification, while a soft-margin balance regularizer L_const (inspired by SVM slack variables) enforces the remaining two conditions on the propensity head. These losses are embedded in a Dragonnet-inspired three-head architecture with a shared representation Φ(X), trained end-to-end by minimizing L = L_wls + λ₁L_ce + λ₂L_const. Under correct propensity score specification and n^{-1/4} convergence of all nuisance parameters, the resulting relative error estimator is √n-consistent, asymptotically normal, and yields valid confidence intervals even with a misspecified outcome model—relaxing the joint consistency requirement of Gao (2025). No sample splitting is required, and the full dataset is used for both nuisance estimation and relative error computation.

## Results

On IHDP, the proposed method achieves the lowest in-sample PEHE of 0.638±0.138 and ATE error of 0.090±0.087, outperforming the next-best baselines (DRCFR/ESCFR at 0.741/0.802 PEHE and Dragonnet at 0.124 ATE). On Twins, it reaches 0.284±0.005 PEHE and 0.009±0.005 ATE, again the best across all 11 methods. For relative error estimation, the method achieves 96% coverage and 80% selection accuracy on IHDP (vs. 44% for Gao's method with linear regression nuisance), and 94% coverage with 94% selection accuracy on Twins, demonstrating both valid uncertainty quantification and practical estimator selection capability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method achieves the best (lowest) PEHE and ATE on both IHDP and Twins across all 11 baselines. On IHDP, the margin over the strongest competitors is most pronounced: it reduces in-sample PEHE by ~14% relative to DRCFR/ESCFR (0.638 vs. 0.741/0.802) and ATE by ~27% relative to Dragonnet (0.090 vs. 0.124). On Twins, the gaps are smaller but consistent—0.284 PEHE vs. 0.290 for DRCFR/ESCFR and 0.009 ATE vs. 0.012 for CForest—reflecting the inherently easier Twins setting. For the relative error estimation task (the paper's primary contribution), the method dramatically outperforms Gao's (2025) framework with conventional nuisance estimators: selection accuracy jumps from 44% (linear regression) and 48% (boosting) to 80% on IHDP, and from 88% to 94% on Twins, while maintaining target 90%+ coverage. The ablation (Table 5) confirms that removing the constraint loss L_const causes the method to degenerate to TARNet-level performance (PEHE 3.495 on IHDP), validating that the novel balance regularizer is the key differentiator from standard Dragonnet/TARNet architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Gao (2025), who introduced relative error as a bias-reducing metric for comparing HTE estimators, by relaxing the requirement that outcome regression models be consistent. The neural architecture is adapted from Dragonnet (Shi et al., 2019a), and the theoretical framework draws on semiparametric efficiency and double machine learning (Chernozhukov et al., 2018; Semenova & Chernozhukov, 2021). The key methodological departure is a novel weighted least-squares loss combined with balance regularizers (inspired by Imai & Ratkovic, 2014) that, together, ensure the relative error estimator remains √n-consistent even when outcome models are misspecified, requiring only a correctly specified propensity score.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

HTE evaluation is critical for model selection in personalized decision-making but remains underdeveloped; this paper addresses a practical bottleneck where outcome models trained on one treatment group must extrapolate to the other, often producing biased nuisance estimates that invalidate confidence intervals. The robustness guarantee (valid inference under misspecified outcome models) is a meaningful theoretical advance for practitioners working with imbalanced treatment groups. Limitations include the still-required correct propensity score specification, the uniform averaging scheme in the enhanced HTE estimator that may underutilize individual estimator strengths, and super-linear computational cost in the number of candidate estimators. Open questions include adaptive weighting strategies, worst-case robustness, and extending evaluation beyond HTE to individual-level treatment effects.
