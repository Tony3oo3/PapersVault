## Problem

Exact Gaussian processes on graphs incur O(N³) time complexity for kernel evaluation and posterior inference, rendering them impractical beyond a few thousand nodes. Existing mitigations—truncated spectral expansions, restricted sparse kernel families, or local subgraph inference—either discard high-frequency kernel information, limit expressiveness, or forgo whole-graph inference. This paper asks whether a Monte Carlo kernel estimator can preserve full kernel flexibility while enabling provably sub-cubic Bayesian inference on graphs.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an undirected weighted graph G=(V,E,W) with N nodes, a set of noisy scalar observations at a subset of training nodes, and a modulation function f (either fixed to a diffusion shape or fully learnable). Rather than materialising the N×N Gram matrix, the algorithm stores sparse random-walk feature vectors Φ∈ℝ^{N×N} with O(1) nonzeros per row, so all downstream matrix operations reduce to two linear-time products Φ(Φᵀv). Outputs include the GP posterior mean and covariance at arbitrary query nodes, Thompson-sampling acquisition values for Bayesian optimisation over up to 1M nodes, and class probabilities in a variational classification setting.

## Method

The core mechanism replaces the dense graph kernel K_α(W)=Σ α_r W^r with a sparse Monte Carlo estimate K̂=ΦΦᵀ, where each row φ(i) is constructed by importance-sampling n random walks from node i and accumulating weighted prefix-subwalk contributions. The key theoretical result (Theorem 2) shows that K̂ has O(N) nonzeros (enabling O(N) mat-vec products) and that the condition number of K̂+σ_n²I is O(N), which together imply that conjugate gradients solve the required linear systems in O(N^{3/2}) time with probabilistic accuracy guarantees from Theorem 1. The full workflow combines three stages: (i) kernel initialisation via random walks, (ii) hyperparameter learning by maximising the log-marginal likelihood with gradients estimated via Hutchinson's trace estimator and iterative CG solves, and (iii) posterior inference via pathwise conditioning, which expresses posterior samples as prior samples plus a correction term computed through the same sparse CG solver. This avoids all explicit matrix inverses and keeps memory at O(N), translating to 50× wall-clock speedups at 8K nodes and enabling Thompson-sampling BO on graphs with over 1M nodes on a single GPU.

## Results

The GRF-GP method achieves O(N^{3/2}) time complexity for Bayesian inference (vs. O(N^3) for exact kernels), translating to a 50× wall-clock speedup on 8,192-node graphs and enabling Bayesian optimisation with >1M nodes on a single GPU. On the Cora citation network, the fully-learnable GRF kernel reaches 87.04% classification accuracy, outperforming both the exact diffusion kernel (85.31%) and the Matérn kernel (86.72%). On the San Jose traffic speed prediction task, the learnable GRF kernel surpasses the exact diffusion kernel in NLPD and RMSE once the number of random walks per node exceeds ~500. In large-scale Bayesian optimisation (up to 1.1M nodes), GRF-based Thompson sampling achieves lower cumulative regret than random search, BFS, and DFS across all tested social networks and synthetic graphs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the exact graph kernels from Borovitskiy et al. (2021), the GRF method wins on both regression and classification: on Cora, the learnable GRF kernel achieves 87.04% accuracy vs. 85.31% (diffusion) and 86.72% (Matérn), and on the San Jose traffic network, the fully-learnable GRF surpasses the exact diffusion kernel in NLPD and RMSE once n ≳ 500 walkers. The paper attributes this advantage to the inbuilt sparsity of GRFs acting as a sensible inductive bias that avoids the 'oversmoothing' effect of dense kernels. Against the SVGP baseline (Titsias, 2009) with 150 inducing points, GRF-GPs clearly outperform: SVGP underperforms even the exact diffusion kernel on traffic prediction, while GRFs achieve better NLPD and RMSE beyond ~100 walkers. The paper also notes a fundamental structural limitation of SVGPs on graphs: computing the kernel at m inducing points still requires materialising the full N×N Gram matrix in O(N^3) time, negating the intended efficiency gain. In Bayesian optimisation, GRF-based Thompson sampling consistently achieves lower regret than all three search-based baselines (random, BFS, DFS) across synthetic grids, community graphs, circular graphs, and four real-world social networks (YouTube, Facebook, Twitch, Enron).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper combines two previously established ingredients — graph random features (GRFs) for unbiased kernel estimation via random walks (Choromanski, 2023; Reid et al., 2023) and pathwise conditioning for efficient GP posterior sampling (Wilson et al., 2020, 2021) — into a unified scalable inference pipeline. The direct predecessor, Reid et al. (2024a), first proposed using GRFs for GPs but restricted experiments to small graphs with the diffusion kernel and did not exploit the estimator's sparsity to accelerate linear solves. The key departure here is the theoretical analysis (Theorem 2, Lemma 1) proving that the sparse GRF Gram matrix has O(N) condition number, enabling conjugate-gradient-based inference in O(N^{3/2}) time, and the practical implementation that leverages sparsity throughout training and inference rather than materialising the kernel.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The O(N^{3/2}) guarantee with probabilistic accuracy bounds is a clean, practical improvement over the O(N^3) cost of exact graph GPs, and the demonstrated 50× wall-clock speedup plus 1M-node BO on a single GPU makes graph-based Bayesian inference viable at scales previously inaccessible. An interesting secondary finding is that the flexible, learnable modulation function in GRFs can outperform the exact diffusion kernel, suggesting that the built-in sparsity acts as a beneficial inductive bias against oversmoothing. Open questions include extending the time-complexity guarantees to non-conjugate settings (classification), evaluating the preliminary Woodbury/JLT alternative solver, and understanding whether the near-linear empirical scaling observed in training persists at even larger scales where conditioning effects dominate.
