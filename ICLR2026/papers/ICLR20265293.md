## Problem

While deep vision models are known to develop brain-like representations, the precise factors driving this convergence remain unclear because prior studies compared pretrained networks that simultaneously varied architecture, training objective, and data. This paper asks how model size, training quantity, and image type each independently and interactively shape the emergence of brain-like representations, spatial hierarchies, and temporal dynamics in self-supervised vision transformers.

## Input / Output

The method consumes natural images presented to both DINOv3 models and human participants, with brain responses recorded via 7T fMRI (fsaverage surface, BOLD at 5.5 s post-stimulus, 10,000 scenes per subject) and MEG (bandpass 0.1–20 Hz, downsampled to 30 Hz, epoched −0.5 to +3 s, 22,500 images across 4 subjects). Outputs are three complementary similarity metrics per model: a voxel-wise encoding score (Pearson R from ridge-regression linear prediction), a spatial score (correlation between the best-predicting model layer and each ROI's Euclidean distance from V1), and a temporal score (correlation between model layer index and the MEG time window of maximal prediction), plus a half-time developmental trajectory tracking when each metric reaches 50% of its final value.

## Method

The paper trains eight DINOv3 variants from scratch in a factorial design that independently varies model size (Small 21 M → Giant 1.1 B parameters, 12–40 layers), training quantity (up to 10⁷ steps on 1.7 B images), and image type (human-centric, satellite, or cellular, 10 M images each with a fixed Large architecture). Representational similarity is quantified via cross-validated ridge-regression encoding (Naselaris et al., 2011b; 10 log-spaced λ from 10⁰ to 10⁸, 5-fold CV), which yields a linear mapping W from model activations to brain responses and is preferred over decoding because it is comparable across architectures of differing dimensionality. From this single encoding, three metrics are derived: the encoding score (mean Pearson R across brain voxels), the spatial score (correlation between the argmax-predicting layer per ROI and cortical distance from V1), and the temporal score (correlation between layer index and T_max^layer, the MEG time window where normalized encoding ≥ 95%). Developmental trajectories are summarized by a 'half-time' (training step at which each metric reaches 50% of its final value), and these half-times are correlated with four cortical properties—developmental expansion, cortical thickness, intrinsic timescales, and myelin concentration—to test whether the order of representational acquisition mirrors cortical maturation.

## Results

DINOv3 achieves a peak fMRI encoding score of R=0.45±0.039 in the visual pathway (MT: R=0.34, VMV2: R=0.28), with MEG similarity rising at ~70 ms (R=0.09±0.017) and remaining significant up to 3 s. The spatial score (correlation between best-encoding layer and cortical distance from V1) reaches R=0.38 (p<1e-6), and the temporal score (correlation between layer index and peak MEG time) reaches R=0.96 (p<1e-12). Across model sizes, encoding scores scale monotonically: Giant (R=0.107) > Large (0.105) > Base (0.101) > Small (0.096), all p<1e-3. Human-centric training data yields significantly higher encoding, spatial, and temporal scores than satellite or cellular images (p<1e-3) across all cortical ROIs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper generalizes its three similarity metrics (encoding, spatial, temporal) to seven additional vision models spanning CNNs (ResNet-50, ConvNeXt-Large), a self-supervised ViT with a different objective (ViT-MAE), a supervised ViT (ViT-L/16), and vision-language contrastive transformers (CLIP, SigLIP2, OWL-ViT). All trained models consistently outperform their untrained counterparts on all three metrics, and trained models show convergent scores across architectures, whereas untrained models vary more widely—suggesting the brain-like hierarchy is not architecture-specific. Among the DINOv3 size variants, larger models win on encoding scores across all ROIs, with the size effect being strongest in higher-level cortices (BA45, IFS) rather than early visual areas (V1, V2), where only the smallest models show significant size differences. For image type, DINOv3 Human dominates DINOv3 Cellular and DINOv3 Satellite on encoding, spatial, and temporal scores at every cortical ROI tested (both V1 and BA45, p<1e-3), indicating that human-relevant visual experience is critical for full brain alignment even when low-level statistics are shared.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the well-established brain-model encoding analysis framework (Naselaris et al., 2011) by applying it to a systematically controlled family of DINOv3 variants (Siméoni et al., 2025) where model size, training duration, and image type are independently manipulated. Prior brain-AI similarity studies (Conwell et al., 2021; Rajesh et al., 2024) typically compared pretrained networks that confound architecture, objective, and data, leaving the individual and interactive contributions of each factor unclear. Here, the authors isolate these factors through from-scratch training on matched datasets (human-centric, satellite, cellular) at fixed scales, and complement the standard encoding score with novel spatial and temporal hierarchy metrics derived from 7T fMRI and MEG respectively.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The factorial design provides the first clean disentanglement of how architecture, experience, and data type each shape brain-like representations, showing that all three independently and interactively matter, with the largest models on human-centric data reaching the highest similarity. The finding that the order of representation acquisition in AI models (sensory → prefrontal) mirrors cortical developmental properties (expansion, myelination, thickness, timescales) offers a computational lens on brain ontogeny, though this claim rests on correlations across only 15 ROIs. Key limitations include reliance on a single architecture family (DINOv3), adult-brain recordings only, and passive-viewing paradigms; open questions include why the temporal score emerges before the encoding score, why spatial/temporal scores are initially negative, and whether the developmental correspondence generalizes beyond hierarchical ViTs.
