## Problem

Existing image restoration methods handle degradations sequentially or in isolation, causing cascading artifacts and indiscriminate signal removal that is particularly harmful in scientific imaging where faint but meaningful structures must be preserved. No prior framework simultaneously addresses compound (multi-distortion) restoration and provides experts with controllable, distortion-specific intervention to selectively correct only the degradations relevant to a given analysis.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260295/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

PRISM takes a degraded RGB image and a free-form natural language prompt (e.g., 'remove haze and super-resolve') as input. The prompt can specify full restoration, a subset of distortions for selective correction, or even a negative prompt for a non-present distortion. The image is encoded by a fine-tuned CLIP image encoder and the text by a frozen CLIP text encoder; both condition a latent diffusion model that outputs a single restored image at the input resolution. For automated mode, a lightweight MLP predicts the distortion set from the image embedding and generates a standardized prompt, removing the need for manual specification.

## Method

PRISM combines two key innovations: (1) a weighted contrastive disentanglement loss that uses Jaccard distance between distortion sets to enforce a compositional geometry in CLIP's image embedding space, pulling compound-degradation embeddings toward the span of their constituent primitives while a quality-aware regularizer prevents clean embeddings from drifting toward distortion-sensitive features; and (2) compound-aware supervision where each training image is degraded by up to three simultaneously sampled distortions with GPT-4-generated prompts covering full, partial, and negative restoration conditions. The fine-tuned CLIP image encoder and frozen CLIP text encoder jointly condition a Stable Diffusion v1.5 latent diffusion backbone via cross-attention at every UNet block, enabling single-trajectory joint removal of overlapping artifacts rather than sequential correction. A lightweight Semantic Content Preservation Module (SCPM) on the decoder side adaptively fuses encoder and decoder features to retain fine-scale edges and textures critical for scientific analysis.

## Results

On the Mixed Degradations Benchmark (up to 3 compound distortions), PRISM achieves 22.08 PSNR, 0.842 SSIM, 48.97 FID, and 0.218 LPIPS, outperforming all 8 baselines across every metric. On zero-shot unseen degradations (UIEB, POLED, ThapaSet), PRISM leads with 22.18/18.26/22.36 PSNR respectively, surpassing the next-best MPerceiver by 1.0–1.9 dB. For downstream scientific utility, selective (prompt-guided) restoration improves over full automatic restoration in 3 of 4 domains, with the largest gain in microscopy segmentation (mIoU 0.580 vs. 0.475, p=0.018).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PRISM outperforms all 8 baselines on every metric in the main MDB evaluation. The gap is largest against early all-in-one models: AirNet (14.76 PSNR) and RestormerA (16.32) trail PRISM by 7.3 and 5.8 dB respectively, reflecting their inability to handle compound degradations. Among diffusion-based methods, MPerceiver (20.84 PSNR, 48.18 FID) and AutoDIR (20.42 PSNR, 50.75 FID) are the closest competitors, but PRISM still leads by 1.2–1.7 dB in PSNR and achieves a lower FID than MPerceiver (48.97 vs. 48.18) while matching its LPIPS advantage (0.218 vs. 0.235). OneRestore, the only other composite-trained method, reaches 19.36 PSNR but lags PRISM by 2.7 dB and 10.45 FID points, confirming that compound-aware supervision alone is insufficient without the contrastive disentanglement objective. On zero-shot unseen degradations (Table 2), PRISM extends its lead: it beats MPerceiver by 1.0 dB on UIEB, 0.7 dB on POLED, and 0.95 dB on ThapaSet, while all-in-one models (AirNet, RestormerA, NAFNetA) fall 3–5 dB behind, underscoring that compositional latent structure generalizes to novel distortion combinations without additional training.

## Relation to Prior Work

PRISM builds on the latent diffusion backbone of Stable Diffusion v1.5 and the CLIP vision-language conditioning paradigm, but departs from prior all-in-one restoration frameworks (PromptIR, MPerceiver, AutoDIR, OneRestore) in two key ways: it trains on compound degradation mixtures rather than single distortions, and it enforces a compositional latent geometry via a Jaccard-weighted contrastive loss that aligns mixture embeddings with the span of their constituent primitives. This contrasts with prior composite methods (OneRestore, AllRestorer) that model degradation interactions but do not explicitly structure the embedding space to support predictable selective intervention. The SCPM refinement module is adapted from AutoDIR, and the overall architecture follows the cross-attention conditioning pattern of Stable Diffusion, making the primary novelty in the training objective and latent-space design rather than the generative backbone.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that selective, distortion-specific restoration outperforms full automatic restoration in three of four scientific domains (camera traps, microscopy, urban scenes) challenges the default assumption that removing all degradations is optimal, with practical implications for how scientific imaging pipelines are designed. The compositional latent space enables zero-shot generalization to unseen degradation mixtures, reducing the need for domain-specific paired datasets. Key limitations include reliance on synthetic augmentations that cannot fully capture real-world distortion physics, and controllability that is limited to which distortions to remove rather than their intensity or spatial extent. Open questions include whether the compositional structure scales to more than three simultaneous degradations and whether the approach transfers to non-photographic scientific modalities (e.g., spectroscopy, time-series imaging).
