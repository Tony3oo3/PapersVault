## Problem

Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) suffer from overthinking, generating excessively long reasoning chains that inflate inference costs with minimal accuracy gains. Existing remedies either rely on offline data generation (too slow for online use) or apply length rewards in online RL, which causes the model to lose its reflection ability and degrade performance on hard problems. The paper addresses how to reduce inference cost while preserving the reflection behavior that underpins LRM accuracy.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261635/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a math question as text input and a reasoning model (R1-7B) that produces a 'think' section followed by a final answer. During training, the reflection model receives the question plus chunked reasoning tokens (split on blank lines, merged to ≤128 tokens) and outputs a per-chunk label ('Think' or 'Result') in a single forward pass. The policy model then generates a shorter revised response by continuing from the truncation point with a forced 'Final Answer:' prefix, capped at 16k tokens total. Evaluation uses rule-based answer extraction with a math validator, sampling 8 paths per question on smaller test sets to reduce variance.

## Method

REA-RL augments GRPO-based online RL with two mechanisms. First, a 7B reflection model (SFT-distilled from a 32B two-step annotator) identifies the first position where the correct answer appears in a sampled response and truncates all subsequent overthinking tokens; the policy model then completes the truncated path, and both original and revised paths are used for training, enabling sequential revision alongside parallel sampling. Second, a reflection reward penalizes responses whose density of reflective keywords ('wait', 'but', 'check', 'alternatively') falls below the 0.2 quantile of the training distribution, preventing the length reward from driving the model toward short but non-reflective outputs. The length reward is also refined to assign zero credit to incorrect responses (rather than partial credit as in Kimi K1.5), avoiding encouragement of erroneous short answers. The combined approach achieves a 36% reduction in token cost without accuracy loss by maintaining reflection on hard problems while reducing it on easy ones.

## Results

On five math benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH500, Gaokao23, AMC23, AIME24) with a 16k token budget, the full REA-RL method (GRPO R_RLen+Reflect M_Reflect) achieves 80.74% average accuracy with a 63.51 token ratio, reducing inference cost by 36% relative to the original R1-7B (80.39% Acc, 100 TR) while slightly improving accuracy. The reflection reward alone (GRPO R_RLen+Reflect) reaches 82.13% average accuracy at 71.77 TR, outperforming the original model in both accuracy and efficiency. The reflection model alone (GRPO R_Len M_Reflect) achieves the most aggressive compression at 52.27 TR with 79.48% accuracy, demonstrating the efficiency-accuracy trade-off between the two components.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GRPO (accuracy-only RL), the full REA-RL method trades a marginal 0.5% accuracy drop (80.74% vs 81.24%) for a 38% token reduction (63.51 vs 102.09 TR), while the reflection reward variant actually surpasses GRPO in accuracy (82.13% vs 81.24%) at 71.77 TR. Compared to the length-reward baseline GRPO R_Len (Kimi K1.5), REA-RL combined recovers 3.86% accuracy (80.74% vs 76.88%) at only a 11% increase in tokens (63.51 vs 57.23 TR), demonstrating that the reflection reward and model prevent the severe performance collapse caused by length pressure alone. Against the most competitive baselines DAST (80.48% Acc, 76.17 TR) and Arora & Zanette (79.20% Acc, 70.71 TR), REA-RL combined achieves higher accuracy (+0.26% and +1.54% respectively) while using 17% and 10% fewer tokens. Against aggressive compression methods NoThink (65.01% Acc) and ShorterBetter (72.48% Acc), REA-RL combined maintains 15.7% and 8.3% higher accuracy respectively, at the cost of roughly 2× the token budget. The reflection model component (GRPO R_Len M_Reflect) achieves the lowest token ratio (52.27 TR) among all methods that retain above 79% accuracy, confirming its role as the primary efficiency driver, while the reflection reward component (GRPO R_RLen+Reflect) achieves the highest accuracy (82.13%) among all methods, confirming its role as the primary performance driver.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) as its RL backbone and on the length-reward formulation from Kimi K1.5 (Du et al., 2025), refining the latter by zeroing the length reward for incorrect responses following Zhang & Zuo (2025). It departs from two prior lines: (1) offline SFT/RL on pre-generated shorter responses (Munkhbat et al., 2025; Han et al., 2025), which suffer from distribution shift and costly data pipelines, and (2) pure online RL with length rewards (Luo et al., 2025a; Shen et al., 2025; Aggarwal & Welleck, 2025), which the paper shows causes catastrophic loss of reflection ability. The key conceptual departure is introducing a small distilled reflection model (7B) that performs online sequential revision of overthinking tokens, thereby combining parallel sampling with sequential revision in a single training loop, plus a keyword-density-based reflection reward to counteract the length reward's anti-reflection bias.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 36% inference cost reduction without accuracy loss is practically significant for deploying reasoning models at scale, and the reflection reward is a zero-cost addition that any GRPO pipeline can adopt immediately. The finding that a naive length reward silently destroys reflection (reverting the model to non-reflective CoT) is an important caution for practitioners tuning RL for efficiency. However, the approach is validated only on 7B distilled models and math benchmarks, the overthinking detection relies on an LLM (imperfect), and the sequential revision adds ~10% training overhead—open questions include whether the method transfers to larger from-scratch models, non-math domains, and whether the reflection model can be further compressed or replaced by learned heuristics.
