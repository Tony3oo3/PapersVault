## Problem

Mainstream ranking systems rely on a Generator-Evaluator (or Multi-Generator-Evaluator) two-stage paradigm where multiple candidate lists are generated and an evaluator selects the best one. However, simply scaling the number of generators yields rapidly diminishing returns, and the combinatorial search space remains underexplored. This paper asks whether a single sufficiently large generator-only model can strictly outperform any finite multi-stage G-E system, and how such a model can be trained effectively without access to the true optimal policy.

## Input / Output

The model takes a user context vector (historical interactions, side features) and a candidate item set of N items produced by a retrieval stage, and autoregressively generates an ordered list of L items via masked-softmax scoring at each position. In offline experiments N=50 and L=6; in the production deployment N=120 and L=6. During training, the generator's output is not used directly as the final list but is combined with lists from auxiliary policies (heuristic and lightweight neural rankers) to form a group of candidate lists, whose reward-normalized distribution serves as the training target.

## Method

GoalRank is a generator-only ranking framework built on two key insights. First, a theoretical result (Theorem 1) proves that for any k-mixture of small generators plus an evaluator, there exists a single larger generator whose policy space achieves strictly smaller KL approximation error to the optimal ranking policy, and this error vanishes as model width grows (scaling law). Second, the Group-Relative Optimization principle provides a tractable training objective: a reward model trained on real user feedback scores a group of diverse candidate lists (constructed from the generator plus auxiliary policies), and the group-normalized reward distribution (Eq. 4) defines a reference policy that approximates the Boltzmann-optimal policy. The generator (a Transformer with a feature-crossing encoder block and an autoregressive decoder) is trained by minimizing cross-entropy against this reference policy, requiring no explicit evaluator at inference time. The group construction deliberately mixes heterogeneous policies to ensure sufficient reward gaps within each group, which is critical for the order-invariance condition that makes the reference policy a valid surrogate for the optimal policy.

## Results

GoalRank achieves the best performance across all three datasets and all five metrics (H@6, N@6, M@6, F1@6, AUC). On the Industry dataset, it improves H@6 by +25.39% and M@6 by +29.63% over the best baseline (NAR4Rec); on ML-1M, gains are +17.12% (H@6) and +15.43% (M@6); on Amazon-Book, +4.07% (H@6) and +5.60% (M@6). Scaling experiments on Industry-0.1B (1M to 0.1B parameters) confirm clear scaling laws for GoalRank, while baselines show only modest or saturating improvements. Online A/B tests on a platform with 500M+ DAU show GoalRank improves App Stay Time by +0.149%, Watch Time by +0.197%, and Effective Views by +1.212% over the production MG-E system.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GoalRank outperforms all 10 baselines across every dataset and metric. Among generator-only methods, the strongest baseline is RankMixer (H@6=49.72 on Industry), which GoalRank surpasses by +20.21 points in H@6; earlier methods like DLCM, PRM, and MIR trail further behind. Among generator-evaluator methods, NAR4Rec is the best (H@6=44.31 on Industry, 62.81 on ML-1M), yet GoalRank still leads by +25.62 and +10.75 points respectively. The multi-generator-evaluator baselines (G-3, G-20, G-100 from Yang et al. 2025) show clear saturation: increasing generators from 3 to 100 raises Industry H@6 only from 49.42 to 55.77, while GoalRank reaches 69.93. Notably, even the best MG-E configuration (G-100) underperforms several single generator-only methods on ML-1M (e.g., H@6=60.64 vs. NAR4Rec's 62.81), highlighting the diminishing returns of simply scaling candidate lists. The largest absolute gains appear on the Industry dataset, where GoalRank's group-relative optimization most effectively leverages the reward model to distinguish high-quality lists.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the prevailing two-stage Generator-Evaluator (G-E) and Multi-Generator-Evaluator (MG-E) paradigms in listwise re-ranking (PIER, NAR4Rec, EGRerank, and multi-generator extensions). It builds on the observation that large end-to-end recommendation models (Zhai et al., 2024; Deng et al., 2025) can subsume multi-stage pipelines, and on universal approximation theory to prove that a single sufficiently large generator-only model always achieves strictly smaller approximation error to the optimal ranking policy than any finite k-mixture of smaller generators with an evaluator. The practical training recipe—group-relative optimization using a reward model to construct a reference policy—draws on entropy-regularized RL (Boltzmann policy) and group-normalized reward shaping, but applies them in a novel way to listwise ranking.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a widely adopted industrial paradigm (G-E re-ranking) with both theory and large-scale deployment evidence at Kuaishou (500M+ DAU), suggesting that scaling a single generator is more effective than scaling the number of generators. The group-relative optimization principle provides a general, model-agnostic training recipe that could transfer to other sequential decision-making tasks. Key limitations include reduced flexibility for rapidly shifting business objectives compared to G-E systems, and the open question of how to jointly optimize large retrieval and large ranking models end-to-end. The efficiency gains (18.6 ms vs. 34.2 ms latency, 12.65% vs. 2.03% MFU) make the approach practically attractive for industrial deployment.
