## Problem

Existing jailbreak defenses rely on local or surface-level signals—perplexity, gradient norms, hidden-state anomalies, or external classifiers—that overlook how refusal-related semantics actually propagate through the network toward the output. The paper argues that jailbreak behavior is an emergent property of information routing inside the LLM, analogous to distributed network-level processing in neuroscience, and that no prior method explicitly models this routing dynamics in a lightweight, model-agnostic way.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a single text prompt and extracts hidden states and head-averaged attention matrices from one forward pass through the target LLM (no generation or multi-pass inference required). Five anchor tokens (I, can, cannot, else, help) serve as semantic probes to condition the graph construction. The output is a binary harmful/benign classification that gates generation: harmful prompts are blocked with a refusal message, while benign prompts pass through unchanged. The entire pipeline operates on internal representations only, never inspecting the generated output text.

## Method

GraphShield constructs a sparse, layered, directed token-layer graph per anchor token, where nodes are hidden states scored by a 'routed score' combining per-layer z-scored cosine alignment to the anchor embedding with a reachability term computed via backward-vector propagation through residual-mixed attention (α=0.9). Edges between consecutive layers are weighted by the product of attention mass and the sender's routed score, then sparsified via a permutation-based z-test (P=200, z_thresh=2.5) and a top-k cap. From each per-anchor graph, three categories of features are extracted: global structure (edge counts, community modularity, eigenvector/PageRank centrality), anchor-conditional token contributions (top-k routed mass, concept-level aggregates), and edge-concentration indicators (Gini coefficients, top-percentile weight shares). Per-anchor feature vectors are concatenated (no pooling) and fed into a lightweight SVM with RBF kernel trained under a 70/30 split. The key distinction from prior work is that detection is based on whether refusal-aligned semantics are actively transmitted along attention pathways to the output, rather than on whether they are merely present at any single layer or token.

## Results

On LLaMA-2-7B-Chat, GraphShield reduces the attack success rate (ASR) to 1.93% while maintaining a benign refusal rate (BRR) of 7.08%, compared to 21.49% ASR with no defense. On Vicuna-7B-v1.5, GraphShield achieves the lowest ASR of all methods (7.81%) with a BRR of 6.83%, versus 76.71% ASR without defense. Under independent StrongREJECT evaluation, the average harmfulness score drops from 0.17 to 0.02 (LLaMA-2) and 0.40 to 0.05 (Vicuna), and LLM-judge ASR falls from 23.96% to 2.93% and 69.15% to 7.61% respectively. Feature extraction runs in ~1.4 s/prompt on a single A100, substantially faster than multi-pass baselines like Backtranslation (14.6 s) or SmoothLLM (27.2 s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GraphShield achieves the best overall robustness-utility trade-off across both target models. On LLaMA-2, GradientCuff attains a marginally lower ASR (1.50% vs. 1.93%) but at the cost of a 2× higher BRR (14.72% vs. 7.08%), meaning it blocks far more benign queries. LLaMA-Guard has the lowest BRR (1.00%) but a much higher ASR (12.11%), indicating it misses most attacks. On Vicuna, GraphShield clearly dominates: it achieves the lowest ASR (7.81%) among all defenses, outperforming GradientCuff (15.55%), LLaMA-Guard (26.04%), and Self-Reminder (22.33%) by 7.7–18.2 percentage points, while keeping BRR competitive (6.83%). PPL and SmoothLLM are substantially weaker, with ASRs of 63.95% and 36.00% on Vicuna respectively. Backtranslation offers moderate ASR reduction (13.33% on Vicuna) but at 12.3 s/prompt runtime, roughly 12× slower than GraphShield's 1.03 s.

## Relation to Prior Work

GraphShield departs from the dominant paradigm in jailbreak detection—perplexity filters, gradient-based probes (GradientCuff, GradSafe), hidden-state anomaly detectors, and external classifier pipelines (LLaMA-Guard, WildGuard)—by reframing the problem as one of network-level information routing rather than local signal inspection. Conceptually, it draws on neuroscience-inspired views of LLMs as distributed processing networks (Schrimpf et al., 2021; AlKhamissi et al., 2024) and repurposes graph-theoretic tools (PageRank, eigenvector centrality, modularity, community detection) that have not previously been applied to jailbreak detection. The attention-rollout mechanism for computing reachability to the output token is adapted from interpretability literature but is combined here with anchor-conditioned semantic scoring and permutation-based sparsification in a way that is novel to this domain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GraphShield offers a single-forward-pass, model-agnostic detection pipeline that avoids the computational overhead of gradient-based or multi-pass defenses while achieving competitive ASR with substantially lower benign refusal rates, making it practically deployable as a pre-generation safety gate. However, generalization remains a concern: leave-one-attack-out performance collapses for structurally distinct attacks (JOOD <3% TPR, Decipher ~36%), and adaptive meta-instructions that suppress anchor tokens degrade detection for optimization-based attacks (DSN, GCG) to near-zero TPR without retraining. Open questions include whether the graph-theoretic perspective scales to 30B–70B models, whether anchor selection can be made fully automatic, and whether the routing-dynamics signal persists in multimodal or reasoning models where attention patterns differ substantially.
