## Problem

CVaR-based reinforcement learning suffers from severe sample inefficiency because the CVaR objective is non-decomposable across time steps, collapsing the learning signal into a single noisy terminal reward. Compounding this, the agent exhibits 'blindness to success'—it learns almost exclusively from worst-case failure trajectories and ignores high-return outcomes, causing premature convergence to overly conservative, suboptimal policies.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263415/cropped_page7_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The agent operates on an augmented state space where each input is a pair (state, residual tail budget y), with the residual budget tracking how much of the initial risk threshold η has been consumed by accumulated rewards. The method outputs two separate function estimates at each augmented state-action pair—a predictive tail value (expected return weighted by tail probability) and a predictive tail probability (likelihood of remaining in the CVaR tail)—from which the greedy action is selected by maximizing f(s,y,a) − g(s,y,a)·y. The optimal risk budget η is also tracked and updated periodically over a discrete grid.

## Method

The key insight is introducing a pair of predictive functions (tail value f and tail probability g) that, unlike the conventional non-decomposable CVaR value, satisfy a risk-neutral Bellman recursion with an additional immediate-reward term g_{t+1}·R_t, enabling dense per-step credit assignment. Training uses two separate temporal-difference losses—one for f derived from the Bellman equation and one for g derived from its martingale property—each computed over a grid of candidate risk budgets to encourage generalization across risk levels. A two-way exploration strategy combines standard ε-greedy action exploration with randomization of the initial residual budget Y₁ ~ N(η, σ²), forcing the agent to experience trajectories at varying risk sensitivities and preventing premature lock-in to conservative policies. The framework is supported by a new Bellman optimality equation and a policy improvement theorem that guarantee monotonic CVaR improvement under greedy kernel updates.

## Results

On the sequential decision tree environment (q=0.1), PCVaR-Q achieves a CVaR of 2.45, substantially outperforming the risk-neutral baseline (RN, CVaR=1.96) and approaching the analytical optimum of 2.50. On the stochastic grid-world (q=0.1), PCVaR-Q reaches a CVaR of −55.84 versus RN's −58.37, closing most of the gap to the optimal value of −53.34. In both environments, PCVaR-Q exhibits a stable, monotonic learning curve, whereas the CVaR-Q baseline (based on Pflug & Pichler, 2016) shows high variance and converges to a suboptimal policy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares PCVaR-Q against two baselines: (1) RN, a risk-neutral Q-learning policy that ignores tail risk entirely, and (2) CVaR-Q, a value-based CVaR Q-learning method built on the augmented-state Bellman operator of Pflug & Pichler (2016) / Wang et al. (2023). PCVaR-Q decisively outperforms RN in both environments: on the tree it improves CVaR from 1.96 to 2.45 (+25%), and on the grid-world from −58.37 to −55.84 (+4.3% relative improvement), confirming that the predictive tail decomposition captures risk-sensitive structure that risk-neutral learning misses. Against CVaR-Q, PCVaR-Q wins on both stability and final performance: CVaR-Q's non-decomposable terminal reward leads to high-variance learning curves and convergence to suboptimal policies in both environments, while PCVaR-Q's recursive Bellman structure yields dense per-step learning signals and consistent convergence near the analytical optimum. The two-way exploration (risk-budget sampling) further distinguishes PCVaR-Q from CVaR-Q, which uses only fixed-η action-level ε-greedy and is susceptible to the 'blindness to success' phenomenon.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the augmented-state-space formulation for CVaR MDPs from Bauerle & Ott (2011) and Pflug & Pichler (2016), which treat the CVaR objective as a terminal, non-decomposable reward. The key departure is introducing a pair of predictive tail value and probability functions that admit a recursive Bellman structure with an immediate reward term, resolving the temporal credit assignment problem that plagues prior value-based CVaR methods. The predictive tail concept is adapted from the policy-gradient setting of Kim & Min (2024) and repurposed here for value-based Q-learning, while the two-way exploration strategy (randomizing the risk budget alongside actions) is a novel heuristic not present in prior CVaR RL work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first Bellman optimality equation and policy improvement theorem specifically for a value-based CVaR Q-learning algorithm, giving a principled theoretical foundation that was previously only available in the policy-gradient or model-based DP settings. However, all experiments are conducted on small tabular environments (a 3-state decision tree and an 8×10 grid world) with finite horizons, leaving open whether the approach scales to deep function approximators, infinite-horizon MDPs, or high-dimensional continuous control. The two-way exploration, while effective in the tested settings, remains a heuristic without convergence guarantees, and the need for two separate function approximators plus a tracked risk budget adds practical complexity that may be prohibitive in large-scale applications.
