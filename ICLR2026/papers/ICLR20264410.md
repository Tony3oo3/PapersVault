## Problem

Reward models trained on human preference data inevitably absorb inductive biases (e.g., response length, sycophancy, formatting style) that are correlated with annotations but unrelated to true content quality, leading to reward hacking and degraded RLHF generalization. Existing debiasing approaches either target a single bias type, rely on simple linear correlations like Pearson coefficients, or impose overly restrictive constraints that distort the reward landscape. The paper addresses the gap of handling diverse, non-linear inductive biases in reward modeling with a unified, theoretically grounded framework.

## Input / Output

The method consumes preference triplets (prompt x, chosen response y^w, rejected response y^l) augmented with a relative bias attribute b — a binary categorical label indicating the direction of bias between the pair (e.g., 'chosen is longer' vs. 'chosen is shorter', 'sycophantic' vs. 'in-sycophantic'). The reward model backbone (Llama3.1-8B-Instruct) produces a scalar reward score per response, and the preference prediction is a Bernoulli variable derived from the score difference via a sigmoid. The debiasing head is a lightweight two-layer MLP that maps the difference of last hidden states Δh = h_φ(x,y^w) − h_φ(x,y^l) to a categorical bias label, operating on relative rather than absolute attributes to avoid the intractability of predicting exact token counts.

## Method

DIR formulates reward debiasing as an information bottleneck objective: maximize the mutual information between the RM preference prediction and the input triplet (content quality signal) while minimizing the mutual information between the RM prediction and the bias attribute. The preference term is estimated via the Barber-Agakov lower bound, which reduces exactly to the standard Bradley-Terry ranking loss, so no architectural change is needed for the reward head. The debiasing term is estimated via the CLUB contrastive log-ratio upper bound applied to the pair (bias label b, hidden representation H), yielding a tractable loss that penalizes any non-linear correlation between the bias and the RM's internal representation. A separate estimator loss maximizes the log-likelihood of the variational network q_ψ(b|H) to keep the CLUB bound tight, and the two networks are updated iteratively within each batch (q_ψ first, then r_φ). The use of relative binary bias indicators and the difference-based representation Δh extends applicability to diverse bias types (length, sycophancy, format) without requiring the RM to predict absolute attribute values.

## Results

On Llama3.1-8B-Instruct, DIR achieves the highest average accuracy of 66.20 across 9 benchmarks (GSM8K, Hellaswag, IFeval, MMLU, ProcessBench, Race, BBH, HumanEval, TriviaQA), outperforming the best baseline ALBM (63.92) by +2.28 points. On OpenRLHF-Llama3-8B-SFT, DIR reaches 59.25 average accuracy, a +3.57 improvement over the base model. On RM-Bench, DIR reduces the Pearson correlation between response length and reward score to 0.468 (vs. 0.533 for vanilla BT), and on ArenaHard-v0.1 the DIR-guided policy achieves a 54.3% win rate against the base model while producing shorter responses (679 vs. 754 tokens).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DIR consistently outperforms all length-debiasing baselines on average accuracy: it beats ALBM (63.92) by +2.28 and PoE (63.14) by +3.06 on Llama3.1-8B-Instruct, and ALBM (57.72) by +1.53 and PoE (57.85) by +1.40 on OpenRLHF-Llama3-8B-SFT. The Length Penalty baseline (LP) actually degrades performance below the base model (−1.47 on Llama3.1), while InfoRM shows no improvement (−0.03), confirming that generic compression without explicit bias constraints is insufficient. In the DPO setting, DIR+DPO achieves 65.95 average accuracy and 49.09% ArenaHard win rate, surpassing both vanilla DPO (64.86 / 44.06%) and Length-Controlled DPO (65.04 / 46.57%). On format debiasing, DIR outperforms Format Decoupling (FD) on RewardBench's harder subsets: Chat Hard 80.1 vs. 72.8, Safety 89.6 vs. 82.9, and Reasoning 92.2 vs. 89.7, while both methods neutralize format win-rates to near 50%. For sycophancy debiasing, DIR achieves the highest preference accuracy across all contamination levels (γ, α), e.g., 91.3% overall at γ=80%, α=30% vs. 89.0% for BT and 90.4% for InfoRM.

## Relation to Prior Work

DIR is directly inspired by the Information Bottleneck principle (Tishby et al., 2000), repurposing it from representation compression to explicit bias removal in reward models. It makes the debiasing term tractable via the CLUB upper bound (Cheng et al., 2020) and the preference term via the Barber-Agakov lower bound (Barber & Agakov, 2004), which the paper shows is equivalent to the standard Bradley-Terry loss. This departs from prior RM debiasing methods that either capture only linear correlations (Pearson-based approaches like ALBM, PoE), target a single scalar bias (Shen et al.'s two-head length predictor), or impose overly restrictive distributional invariance (Wang et al.'s MMD-based CRM). Unlike InfoRM, which compresses the entire latent space without explicitly constraining any specific bias attribute, DIR applies a targeted MI minimization on a named bias variable, enabling it to handle non-linear and multi-type biases within a single unified objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DIR provides the first unified, theoretically-grounded framework that handles multiple inductive bias types (length, sycophancy, format) under one objective, removing the need for task-specific debiasing heuristics. The relative bias attribute design (comparing chosen vs. rejected rather than predicting absolute values) is a practical insight that makes the method broadly applicable to any pairwise-comparable attribute. Limitations include: the method requires the bias attribute to be known and labelable a priori, it does not address broader societal or demographic biases, and the ablation shows that overly aggressive debiasing (high λ) can hurt performance on simpler tasks. Open questions include whether the framework can be extended to handle unknown or emergent biases, how it scales to multi-bias settings with many concurrent attributes, and whether the CLUB-based estimator remains tight in very high-dimensional representation spaces.
