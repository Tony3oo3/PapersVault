## Problem

State-of-the-art CATE meta-learners (DR-, R-, IVW-learners) perform poorly in low-overlap regions where patients with similar covariates almost exclusively receive one treatment. Existing remedies—retargeting (reweighting the error term) and constant regularization (uniformly penalizing model complexity)—either fail to control generalization in sparse regions or 'blindly' regularize all regions equally, ignoring the local degree of overlap. No prior method adapts the regularization term itself to the overlap structure of the covariate space.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261825/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard observational triplets (X, A, Y) along with first-stage nuisance estimates (propensity scores π̂(x) and outcome models μ̂_a(x)) to compute per-sample overlap weights ν̂(x) = π̂(x)(1−π̂(x)). It outputs CATE estimates τ̂(x) from a second-stage target model that can be parametric (fully-connected neural networks) or non-parametric (kernel ridge regression with RBF/Matérn kernels). The regularization strength varies spatially across the covariate space, being strongest where ν(x)→0 and vanishing at perfect overlap ν(x)=1/4.

## Method

Overlap-Adaptive Regularization (OAR) replaces the constant regularization term Λ(g; P(X)) in the meta-learner target risk with an overlap-dependent term Λ_OAR = Λ(g; P(X,A); λ(ν(X))), where λ(ν) ∝ 1/ν so that regularization increases as overlap decreases. Three regularization functions are defined (multiplicative λ_m = 1/(4ν)−1, logarithmic λ_log = −log(4ν), and squared multiplicative λ_m² = 1/(16ν²)−1). For parametric target models, OAR is instantiated via (i) Gaussian noise injection with variance proportional to 1/ν, or (ii) dropout with probability p(ν) = λ(ν)/(λ(ν)+1); for non-parametric models, it becomes a weighted RKHS norm ‖√λ(ν)·g‖²_HK. A debiased variant (dOAR) adds an efficient-influence-function correction to the regularization, preserving Neyman-orthogonality and making the estimator first-order insensitive to propensity score misspecification. The approach is model-agnostic and composable with any Neyman-orthogonal meta-learner, and theoretical results show it reduces excess prediction risk relative to constant regularization under a conditional variance assumption and a low-overlap-low-heterogeneity inductive bias.

## Results

On the HC-MNIST dataset (d_x=784+1), OAR/dOAR with multiplicative regularization consistently reduces rPEHE_out over Constant Regularization (CR) across all three meta-learners. The largest gains appear for the IVW-learner with dropout (p=0.5): OAR reduces rPEHE_out from 1.113 to 0.997 (−0.116) and dOAR to 1.027 (−0.086), compared to an oracle of 0.513. On the 77 ACIC 2016 semi-synthetic datasets, dOAR with squared-multiplicative noise regularization achieves a 76.62% significant improvement rate over CR for the DR-learner, while dOAR with multiplicative dropout reaches 70.13%. On IHDP (severe overlap violations), the best performance for every meta-learner and regularization type is achieved by some version of OAR/dOAR.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole directly comparable baseline is Constant Regularization (CR) from Morzywolek et al. (2023), which applies a uniform regularization strength across the entire covariate space. OAR and its debiased variant (dOAR) consistently outperform CR across all three datasets and all three Neyman-orthogonal meta-learners (DR, R, IVW). On HC-MNIST, OAR/dOAR achieve statistically significant improvements (α=0.05) in the majority of regularization settings, with the largest gain for IVW-learner + dropout (p=0.5) where OAR reduces rPEHE_out by 0.116 (from 1.113 to 0.997). On ACIC 2016 (77 datasets), dOAR with squared-multiplicative noise regularization significantly outperforms CR in 76.62% of datasets for the DR-learner, and dOAR with multiplicative dropout in 70.13%. The paper notes that OAR performs best when combined with the DR-learner, as the DR-learner's pseudo-outcome variance is naturally inflated in low-overlap regions, making adaptive regularization most beneficial there; R- and IVW-learners already down-weight their error term by overlap, so OAR can occasionally over-regularize in those cases.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the two-stage Neyman-orthogonal meta-learner framework (DR-, R-, IVW-learners) by introducing overlap-adaptive regularization into the target risk, building directly on the constant regularization strategy of Morzywolek et al. (2023) and the retargeting approaches of Nie & Wager (2021) and Fisher (2024). Conceptually, it draws on the adaptive regularization literature (Wager et al., 2013; Camuto et al., 2020) showing that dropout and noise injection are first-order equivalent to data-dependent l2 penalties, and for the first time connects this machinery to CATE estimation by making the regularization strength proportional to inverse overlap. The debiased (dOAR) variants extend the one-step bias-correction technique of Kennedy (2024) to the regularization term itself, preserving Neyman-orthogonality of the underlying learners.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Low overlap is a pervasive and practically critical problem in observational causal inference, especially in personalized medicine where treatment assignment often follows clinical guidelines. OAR provides a model-agnostic, drop-in regularization strategy that can be combined with any existing meta-learner and target model class, making it broadly applicable. Key open questions include: debiasing for the non-parametric (RKHS norm) OAR variant is left for future work; the theoretical guarantees rely on a low-overlap-low-heterogeneity inductive bias that may not always hold; and the approach requires estimating propensity scores, which can be unreliable in high-dimensional settings. The empirical gains, while consistent, are moderate in magnitude, suggesting that overlap-adaptive regularization is a useful but not sufficient fix for severe overlap violations.
