## Problem

RL post-training of LLMs is sensitive to batch configuration and prompt selection, yet these factors remain underexplored in prior work. Existing approaches to identify intermediate-difficulty prompts either incur substantial overhead from online rollouts or suffer from off-policyness when using historical reward dictionaries, especially on large datasets. This paper systematically investigates how batch size and prompt difficulty jointly affect convergence and uses these findings to design a compute-efficient curriculum algorithm.

## Input / Output

At each training step, PCL samples a pool of km candidate prompts (k=4 by default) from the dataset and passes them through a value model (same size as the policy) that takes a prompt (typically <1K tokens for math) and outputs a scalar predicted reward. The method selects the m prompts whose predicted values are closest to a target threshold τ (default 0.5), generates n responses per selected prompt (default m=512, n=16), and produces an updated policy via on-policy GRPO. The value model is updated online using only the already-generated responses, incurring negligible additional cost since prompts are short relative to the thousands of tokens in generated solutions.

## Method

PCL combines two empirical findings: (1) an optimal batch size exists at the transition between sublinear and linear generation-time scaling (around 8K in their setup), and (2) prompts with p(x)≈0.5 yield the highest gradient norms and effective ratios, making them the most sample-efficient for convergence. Rather than estimating prompt difficulty via costly rollouts, PCL trains a value model V(x) that approximates p_π(x) = E[r(x,y)] using a single forward pass, achieving 12.1× and 16.9× speedup over rollout-based filtering on MATH and DeepScaleR respectively. The value model is updated each step by minimizing MSE between its predictions and the empirical average reward across n generations, requiring no additional rollouts beyond those already used for policy updates. The value model lags one step behind the policy, but this is acceptable since per-step policy updates are small (π_t ≈ π_{t+1}), and the ranking of prompt difficulties is stable under such perturbations.

## Results

On MATH500, PCL achieves the highest accuracy across all four models: 88.2% (Qwen3-8B-Base), 83.4% (Qwen3-4B-Base), 73.8% (Qwen3-1.7B-Base), and 57.8% (Llama3.2-3B-it). On DeepScaleR (average of six benchmarks including MATH500, Minerva Math, OlympiadBench, AMC 23, AIME 24, AIME 25), PCL achieves 52.0% average with Qwen3-8B-Base, converging 39.8% faster than the second-best method (DS at 51.5%). PCL's value model is 12.1× and 16.9× faster than rollout-based prompt filtering on MATH and DeepScaleR respectively, reducing per-step filtering cost from 288 s / 396 s to 23.9 s / 23.5 s.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), PCL wins on MATH500 for all four models (e.g., 88.2 vs. 86.4 for Qwen3-8B-Base) and on DeepScaleR average (52.0 vs. 51.4), while also requiring less wall-clock time on Qwen3-4B-Base (14.0 h vs. 29.2 h). Against DS/DAPO (Yu et al., 2025), PCL achieves higher MATH500 scores for Qwen3-8B (88.2 vs. 87.8) and Qwen3-4B (83.4 vs. 82.6), ties on Qwen3-1.7B (73.8), and on DeepScaleR PCL converges 39.8% faster (41.8 h vs. 69.5 h) at a slightly higher average (52.0 vs. 51.5). Against SPEED (Zhang et al., 2025), PCL substantially outperforms on every benchmark (e.g., 88.2 vs. 81.2 on Qwen3-8B MATH500; 52.0 vs. 45.5 on DeepScaleR), and most SPEED runs crashed within a few hours due to severe off-policyness from reusing stale rollouts. Against GRESO (Zheng et al., 2025), PCL wins on MATH500 for Qwen3-8B (88.2 vs. 87.2) and Qwen3-4B (83.4 vs. 83.0), ties on Qwen3-1.7B (73.8 vs. 73.4), and wins on Llama3.2-3B (57.8 vs. 56.6); GRESO was not evaluated on DeepScaleR because the large dataset limits training to ~1 epoch, making dictionary-based methods inapplicable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) as its RL objective and positions itself within the growing line of work on efficient prompt selection for LLM RL post-training. It departs from rollout-based filtering methods (DAPO/DS, SPEED) that waste compute generating responses for filtered-out prompts, and from dictionary-based methods (GRESO) that suffer from off-policyness on large datasets, by introducing a learned value model that estimates prompt difficulty via a single forward pass. Conceptually, it adapts classic curriculum learning (Bengio et al., 2009) and active learning ideas to the specific constraints of on-policy RLVR, where each 'example' is a multi-thousand-token generation that cannot be evaluated before being produced.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 12–17× speedup in prompt filtering and the systematic identification of an optimal batch size at the sublinear-to-linear generation-time transition provide concrete, actionable guidance for practitioners running large-scale RL post-training. However, the core insight that intermediate-difficulty prompts are most sample-efficient is already a recurring theme in the literature, and the method relies on an implicit prompt-level generalization assumption (training on selected prompts improves filtered-out ones) that may not hold outside math-like domains. Open questions include why τ=0.5 yields the best value model accuracy despite training on a filtered subset, whether the approach extends cleanly to non-binary or multi-turn rewards, and how the one-step-lag between value model and policy affects convergence in regimes with larger per-step updates.
