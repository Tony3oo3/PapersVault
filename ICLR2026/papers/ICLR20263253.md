## Problem

Time series foundation models (TSFMs) achieve state-of-the-art predictive accuracy, but their calibration properties remain largely unexplored. Prior evaluations relied on metrics like WQL, CRPS, and MSIS, which the authors show conflate probabilistic calibration with predictive sharpness, potentially masking true miscalibration. This paper asks whether TSFMs are genuinely well-calibrated and whether they exhibit the systematic overconfidence common in other deep learning domains.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263253/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Each model receives a context of T observations (128–512 time steps depending on dataset granularity, ranging from seconds to months) and produces quantile predictions at levels 0.1–0.9 for L=64 future steps (short-term) or L=256 steps (long-term via autoregression). The evaluation outputs three calibration-specific scores—Probabilistic Calibration Error (PCE), Centered Calibration Error (CCE), and Scaled Interval Width (SIW)—deliberately chosen over WQL/CRPS/MSIS because the latter are shown to correlate strongly with point-accuracy error (MASE) and can misrank models on calibration.

## Method

This is a first-of-its-kind systematic empirical study evaluating five TSFMs (Chronos-Bolt, TimesFM, Moirai 2.0, TiRex, YingLong) and two baselines (ARIMA, N-BEATS) across six univariate datasets spanning hourly, daily, 5-minute, second, and monthly granularities. The authors introduce and apply three calibration-specific metrics (PCE, CCE, SIW) that isolate calibration from sharpness, in contrast to the standard WQL/CRPS/MSIS. To disentangle the effect of prediction head architecture, they train separate quantile, Gaussian, Student's t, and mixture distribution heads on the TSMixup dataset and plug them into each frozen TSFM backbone. For long-term forecasting, they compare naive point-based AR, branching AR (Moirai 2.0), and trajectory-based AR (Toto) across varying forecast horizons (16–128 steps). Key findings: TSFMs are consistently better calibrated than baselines, show no systematic over- or under-confidence in short-term forecasting, are largely insensitive to prediction head form (except Gaussian, which is consistently under-confident), and the trajectory AR method with longer horizons yields the best long-term calibration.

## Results

Across six real-world datasets, TSFMs achieve PCE values of 0.012–0.077 (median ~0.03), well below the 0.05 threshold for good calibration, while N-BEATS (0.061–0.242) and ARIMA (0.122–0.411) are consistently poorly calibrated. On CCE, TSFMs cluster near zero (range −0.061 to +0.105), indicating no systematic over- or under-confidence, whereas N-BEATS (−0.040 to −0.243) and ARIMA (−0.411 to +0.322) are predominantly under-confident. For point accuracy (MASE), TSFMs are competitive or superior: e.g., on Reviews, TiRex achieves 0.710 vs. N-BEATS 1.416 and ARIMA 1.211. On the difficult Patents dataset, all methods show elevated PCE (0.154–0.259 for TSFMs vs. 0.242–0.247 for baselines), but TSFMs still maintain the edge.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between five TSFMs (Chronos-Bolt, Moirai 2.0, TimesFM, TiRex, YingLong) and two baselines (N-BEATS, ARIMA) on calibration and accuracy. On PCE, every TSFM outperforms both baselines on all six real datasets; for example, on Heart-Rate the best TSFM (TiRex, 0.048) is roughly 2.5× better than N-BEATS (0.121) and 4× better than ARIMA (0.204). On CCE, the TSFMs show no systematic bias (values within ±0.1), while N-BEATS is consistently under-confident (CCE ≈ −0.04 to −0.24) and ARIMA is strongly under-confident on most datasets (CCE as low as −0.411 on Heart-Rate). On MASE, TSFMs generally match or beat the baselines (e.g., on Crime: TiRex 0.740 vs. N-BEATS 0.767 vs. ARIMA 0.829), though on Glucose and Patents all models struggle due to weak linear dependence. Among the TSFMs themselves, no single model dominates: TiRex and TimesFM lead on PCE for most datasets, while YingLong is best on Crime but shows mild overconfidence (CCE +0.105 on Heart-Rate). The paper also shows that WQL and MSIS, commonly used in prior TSFM evaluations, are highly correlated with MASE and can misleadingly rank ARIMA as well-calibrated on Glucose, whereas PCE correctly identifies it as poorly calibrated.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of the probabilistic calibration literature (Brier, Gneiting et al., Kuleshov et al., Chung et al.) and the rapidly growing time series foundation model (TSFM) literature (Chronos, TimesFM, Moirai, TiRex, YingLong). It departs from prior TSFM evaluations that relied on WQL, CRPS, and MSIS as calibration proxies by arguing—following Chung et al. (2021)—that these metrics conflate calibration with sharpness and accuracy, and instead uses calibration-specific metrics (PCE, CCE, SIW) to isolate true miscalibration. The paper also extends the prediction-head and autoregressive-forecasting design choices already present in individual TSFM papers into a controlled comparative study, training interchangeable heads on TSMixup to decouple backbone quality from head architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Calibration is essential for downstream decision-making in healthcare, anomaly detection, and finance, yet prior TSFM evaluations used metrics that could mislead practitioners about actual calibration quality (e.g., WQL ranking ARIMA as best-calibrated on Glucose while PCE shows it is poorly calibrated). The finding that TSFMs are well-calibrated and not systematically overconfident—unlike image and text deep models—gives practitioners more confidence in using TSFM uncertainty estimates directly. Key open questions remain: how calibration behaves under fine-tuning, multivariate settings, distribution shift, and at extreme tail quantiles (e.g., 0.001/0.999), all of which the authors flag as important future directions.
