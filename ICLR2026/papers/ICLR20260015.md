## Problem

Zhong et al. (2023) claimed that uniform-attention and learnable-attention transformers trained on the same modular addition data learn fundamentally disjoint circuits ("Pizza" vs. "Clock"), providing an apparent counter-example to the universality hypothesis. Prior interpretability work analyzed individual neurons and weights in isolation, missing the collective geometric structure that emerges when all neurons in a frequency cluster are treated as a single representation manifold. This paper resolves the apparent contradiction by showing both architectures implement the same algorithm via topologically and geometrically equivalent representations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260015/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The network takes a pair of integers (a, b) ∈ Z_59 × Z_59, embeds them via a shared learnable matrix into R^128, and outputs the modular sum (a+b) mod 59. Beyond the task output, the analysis produces three diagnostic objects per trained network: (i) the Phase Alignment Distribution (PAD) over Z_n × Z_n capturing where neuron activations peak, (ii) Betti number vectors (β_0, β_1, β_2) computed via persistent homology on each frequency-cluster's preactivation point cloud, and (iii) CKA/RSM similarity scores against closed-form reference manifolds (disc, torus, circle). The key analytical unit is not a single neuron but the entire n² × |cluster| preactivation matrix for all neurons sharing a dominant frequency f, identified via 2D DFT.

## Method

The core theoretical result (Theorem 4.1) shows that under the simple neuron model N(a,b) = cos(2πfa/n + φ^L) + cos(2πfb/n + φ^R) and the commutativity-induced symmetry condition that φ^L and φ^R are identically distributed, the preactivation matrix admits either a rank-2 factorization (vector-addition disc) when phases are perfectly correlated, or a rank-4 factorization (torus T²) when phases are independent—proving the Clock representation is theoretically excluded. Empirically, they validate these conditions across 703 trained networks using persistent homology (Ripser, k=250) to extract Betti number distributions, the PAD to measure phase alignment, and Gaussian-kernel MMD to test distributional similarity between architectures. They further show via CKA and RSM that first-layer representations of MLP-Add, Attention 0.0, and Attention 1.0 all align with the disc manifold (CKA > 0.97), while MLP-Concat aligns with the torus (CKA ≈ 0.994), and all architectures converge to a circle at the logits. The methodology thus replaces neuron-by-neuron interpretation with a manifold-level analysis that reveals a universal torus-to-circle factorization underlying all studied architectures.

## Results

Across 703 trained networks, the paper demonstrates that Attention 0.0 (Pizza) and Attention 1.0 (Clock) architectures learn nearly identical first-layer representations: CKA with the vector-addition disc manifold is 0.988±0.041 and 0.974±0.028 respectively, while CKA with the torus manifold is 0.699±0.029 and 0.689±0.020. The MMD distance between their Phase Alignment Distributions is only 0.0338 (max activation) and 0.0210 (center of mass), confirming statistical indistinguishability. At the logits, all four architectures converge to the circle (clock) manifold with CKA values of 0.926–0.986, while MLP-Concat is the only architecture whose first layer aligns with the torus (CKA 0.994±0.011). Persistent homology Betti numbers confirm that MLP-Add, Attention 0.0, and Attention 1.0 all produce disc-like topology in layer 1, transitioning to circle topology in later layers, whereas MLP-Concat transitions directly from torus to circle.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The central comparison is with Zhong et al. (2023), who claimed that uniform attention (Attention 0.0, 'Pizza') and learnable attention (Attention 1.0, 'Clock') transformers learn fundamentally distinct circuits for modular addition. This paper shows the two architectures are geometrically and topologically equivalent: their first-layer CKA scores with the disc manifold differ by less than 1.5 percentage points (0.988 vs. 0.974), their MMD Phase Alignment Distribution distance is only 0.0338, and their Betti number distributions are nearly identical across all layers. The previously claimed distinguishing metrics (gradient symmetricity and distance irrelevance) also fail to reliably separate the two architectures, with substantial overlap in their distributions. MLP-Concat is the only architecture that genuinely differs, learning a torus (CKA 0.994) rather than a disc in its first layer, but even it ultimately maps to the same circle at the logits (CKA 0.986), confirming that all architectures factor the same torus-to-circle map. The paper thus resolves the apparent counter-example to the universality hypothesis posed by Zhong et al. (2023).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the simple neuron model from McCracken et al. (2025), which established that first-layer neurons in modular addition networks are well-approximated by linear superpositions of sinusoids in each input. It departs sharply from Zhong et al. (2023), who claimed that uniform-attention (Pizza) and learnable-attention (Clock) transformers learn fundamentally different circuits, by showing via a closed-form theorem (Theorem 4.1) that both architectures produce topologically equivalent representations — the disc is simply a linear projection of the torus. The paper introduces topological data analysis (persistent homology, Betti numbers) and distributional comparison tools (MMD, CKA, RSM) as a new methodological layer for comparing learned representations, going beyond the neuron-by-neuron interpretation paradigm that dominated prior modular addition work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By resolving the Clock-vs-Pizza dichotomy, the paper restores credibility to the universality hypothesis in mechanistic interpretability, suggesting that apparent circuit differences may reflect representational geometry rather than genuinely distinct algorithms. The phase alignment distribution (PAD) and torus-to-circle factorization framework offer a generalizable template for characterizing circuits in other tasks, though the authors explicitly note the current scope is limited to modular addition. A key open question is whether the same manifold-class reasoning extends to more complex group operations or to sub-circuits within large language models, where the combinatorial explosion of potential circuits makes the universality question most pressing.
