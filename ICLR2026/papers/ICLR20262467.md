## Problem

Prior interpretability work treats LLM representations as static semantic positions or models reasoning as random walks on discrete graphs, leaving open whether LLMs internalize logical structure as a dynamic, geometric property of their internal states. This paper asks: can reasoning be formalized as a smooth flow in representation space whose local velocities are governed by logical structure, independent of surface semantics?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262467/cropped_page1_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes chain-of-thought token sequences and extracts mean-pooled hidden states from the final transformer layer at each reasoning step, but crucially computes each embedding over the entire cumulative prefix (prompt + all prior steps), not just the current step. This yields a context-cumulative trajectory Y = [y₁, …, y_T] in R^d. From this trajectory, the method derives first-order differences (velocity vectors Δy_t) and Menger curvature values κ_t for each triple of consecutive states, then computes pairwise cosine similarity (position, velocity) and Pearson correlation (curvature) grouped by logical skeleton, topic, or language.

## Method

The paper introduces a differential-geometric framework in which LLM reasoning is modeled as a C¹ flow in representation space, with logic acting as a local controller of the flow's velocity (both magnitude and direction). The key geometric descriptor is Menger curvature, which couples angular deviation with distance variation to capture the 'intensity' of reasoning turns in a way that pure cosine similarity cannot. To test whether logic is encoded in higher-order geometry rather than raw positions, the authors construct the Reasoning-Flow dataset: 30 natural-deduction logical skeletons instantiated across 20 topical domains and 4 languages (2,430 sequences total), generated via a two-stage GPT-5 pipeline that first produces abstract symbolic templates and then rewrites them into domain-specific, multilingual natural language. Experiments across Qwen3 (0.6B–4B) and LLaMA3 (8B) show that position similarity is dominated by surface carriers (topic, language), while velocity and curvature similarity are dominated by logical structure, and a random-shuffle baseline that permutes step order collapses velocity/curvature to near-zero while preserving position similarity—confirming that logic is encoded in the higher-order geometry of the flow rather than in static embedding positions.

## Results

Across all models (Qwen 1.5/2/3 0.5B–4B and LLaMA3 8B), position similarity is dominated by language (0.74–0.89) and topic (0.21–0.46), while logic similarity is lowest (0.21–0.44). At the velocity level, logic similarity (0.15–0.19) exceeds topic (0.06–0.08) and language (0.07–0.09) by roughly 2×. At the curvature level, the gap widens dramatically: logic similarity reaches 0.46–0.58 versus 0.10–0.13 for topic and 0.12–0.17 for language. A random-shuffle baseline (Qwen3 0.6B) collapses velocity similarity to 0.02–0.04 and curvature to 0.02–0.04 while preserving position similarity (0.30–0.81), confirming that logical structure is encoded in higher-order geometry rather than raw embeddings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an interpretability paper without traditional method-vs-method baselines; the key comparisons are across grouping criteria and model families. Logic grouping consistently outperforms topic and language grouping at velocity (e.g., 0.17 vs. 0.07/0.08 on Qwen3 0.6B) and curvature (e.g., 0.53 vs. 0.11/0.13), while the reverse holds at position where language dominates (0.85 vs. 0.26/0.30). The random-shuffle control is the most informative comparison: permuting logical step order collapses velocity similarity from 0.17 to 0.02 (an 88% drop) and curvature from 0.53 to 0.02 (a 96% drop), while position similarity barely changes (0.26→0.30), demonstrating that logical sequence order—not just the set of statements—drives higher-order geometric structure. Cross-model comparisons show remarkable stability: Qwen 1.5/2/3 (0.5B–4B) and LLaMA3 8B all exhibit the same qualitative pattern (logic > topic ≈ language at velocity/curvature), with curvature-logic values ranging only from 0.46 to 0.58, suggesting a model-agnostic representational law independent of architecture and training recipe.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the concept-manifold view of LLM representations (Modell et al., 2025) from static semantic geometry to dynamic reasoning trajectories, introducing differential-geometric tools (velocity, Menger curvature) to characterize how representations evolve during chain-of-thought. It departs from graph-based accounts of reasoning (Wang et al., 2024a; Minegishi et al., 2025) by arguing that cumulative context reveals smooth flows rather than random walks, and from static geometric analyses (Park et al., 2025) by focusing on kinematic rather than structural properties. The controlled dataset design—same natural-deduction skeleton across topics and languages—builds on the logic-as-form tradition (Troelstra & Schwichtenberg, 2000) but repurposes it as an experimental instrument for probing representation geometry rather than as an evaluation benchmark.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that logical structure is invisible in raw embeddings but emerges as a dominant invariant in velocity and curvature provides a concrete, quantifiable challenge to the stochastic-parrots critique and lends support to the Platonic Representation Hypothesis. The cross-architecture stability (Qwen 1.5/2/3, LLaMA3) hints at a universal representational constraint, though the evidence is limited to small-to-mid-size models and a synthetic GPT-5-generated dataset. Key open questions include why next-token prediction produces these geometric invariants (training dynamics), how the framework extends to generation and task accuracy, and whether the observed 'universality' persists at frontier scale. Practically, the flow-based view opens avenues for trajectory-level steering and reasoning-aware retrieval, but the immediate utility is primarily conceptual.
