## Problem

Two competing hypotheses propose that early sensory populations encode either likelihood functions (probabilistic population codes) or posterior distributions (neural sampling codes), with the critical distinction being whether stimulus priors modulate neural responses. Despite extensive psychophysical and neurophysiological evidence, no principled framework existed for selecting stimulus prior distributions that would maximally differentiate these two coding hypotheses, as both can account for similar response patterns under conventional single-context experimental designs.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a task design (context prior distributions p^c(θ) and context frequencies p(c)), a generative model p(x|θ), and neural population spike-train responses r as input, and produces the information gap Δ^info — an analytic prediction of the expected cross-entropy difference between likelihood and posterior decoders. The gap is structured as a KL divergence between the true context-dependent posterior and a task-marginalized surrogate posterior; for posterior-coding populations, only observation pairs satisfying a specific proportionality condition (Eq. 4) contribute, requiring fixed-point iteration to compute the surrogate likelihood estimator. The output also includes optimized stimulus prior distributions identified by maximizing Δ^info over the task parameter space.

## Method

The core contribution is deriving closed-form analytic expressions for the information gap under both coding hypotheses, quantifying the expected decoder performance difference as a KL divergence between the true posterior and a task-marginalized, Bayes-optimal surrogate. For likelihood-coding populations, every observation contributes to the gap, and the surrogate posterior simply replaces the context-specific prior with a weighted sum of both context priors. For posterior-coding populations, the computation is more intricate: only pairs of observations where identical population responses must map to different likelihood functions contribute, and the optimal likelihood decoder output is determined by solving an implicit equation via fixed-point iteration. The framework is validated through simulations with Poisson and gain-modulated Poisson neuron models, confirming that empirical decoder performance differences converge to the theoretical information gap across diverse contrast levels and task parameters. Maximizing the information gap over the 2D parameter space (prior separation d and prior standard deviation σ) identifies strategic 'sweet spots' that balance discriminative power across both hypotheses, with optimal parameters shifting toward smaller separations and narrower priors as stimulus contrast increases.

## Results

On the Allen Brain Observatory Visual Coding Neuropixels Dataset (169 sessions, >300 trials each), the decoder cross-entropy performance difference between likelihood and posterior decoders is 0.0024 ± 0.064 (p=0.63), confirming the theoretical prediction of Δ^info = 0 under single-context uniform-prior design. In simulations, the information gap accurately predicts empirical decoder performance differences across diverse task parameters and contrast levels, with likelihood-coding information gaps (Δ_L^info) exceeding posterior-coding gaps (Δ_P^info) by up to an order of magnitude. For low-contrast stimuli, the optimized task design yielding maximal discriminative power uses prior separation d ≈ 30° and standard deviation σ ≈ 20°.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing methods in the traditional sense; instead, it validates its theoretical framework against empirical decoder performance and compares two competing neural coding hypotheses (likelihood coding vs. posterior coding). The key comparison is between the theoretical information gap predictions (Eqs. 1 and 3) and empirical decoder performance differences on simulated populations: across all tested contrast levels (high, medium, low) and both Poisson and gain-modulated Poisson neural models, the theoretical predictions show remarkable agreement with empirical results (Fig. 4). A critical asymmetry emerges: Δ_L^info values are up to 10× larger than Δ_P^info values, because every observation contributes to the likelihood-coding gap while only specific observation pairs satisfying Eq. 4 contribute to the posterior-coding gap. For task design, Gaussian context priors substantially outperform heavy-tailed alternatives (Student's t, Cauchy), where posterior-coding information gap is near zero across the entire parameter space. The single-context design used in existing datasets (including the Allen dataset) is shown to be fundamentally incapable of adjudicating the two hypotheses, motivating the proposed multi-context optimization framework.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the neural decoding framework of Walker et al. (2020), which used deep neural network decoders to extract probabilistic information from V1 population responses, and extends it with a principled information-theoretic metric (the information gap) for optimizing the experimental task design itself. It draws on the broader literature on optimal stimulus design for psychophysics (Watson & Pelli, 1983) and electrophysiology (Lewi et al., 2006, 2011), but repurposes the optimization objective: rather than maximizing information content in neural responses, the goal is to maximize the *difference* in decodable information between two competing coding hypotheses. The theoretical derivation of the information gap as a KL divergence between the true posterior and a task-marginalized surrogate posterior is a new analytic contribution that unifies Bayesian inference with decoder performance analysis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This framework addresses a long-standing open question in sensory neuroscience—whether early cortical populations encode likelihoods or posteriors—by providing the first principled method for designing experiments that can actually adjudicate between the two hypotheses. The empirical result on the Allen Brain Observatory dataset confirming that conventional single-context designs yield zero information gap (indistinguishable decoder performance) is a concrete demonstration of why existing data cannot resolve the debate. Key limitations include the requirement for a known generative model, the current restriction to orientation-based tasks with Gaussian priors, and the finding that posterior-coding information gaps are an order of magnitude smaller than likelihood-coding ones, making the former substantially harder to detect empirically. Open questions include extending the framework to mixed/intermediate coding hypotheses, incorporating noise correlations, and applying it to continuous (non-discretized) observation spaces.
