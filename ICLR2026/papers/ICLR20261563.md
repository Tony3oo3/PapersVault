## Problem

RL-based dynamic path planning in urban road networks faces a fundamental state-representation dilemma: encoding full graph dynamics ensures information completeness but is computationally prohibitive, while using only local observations is efficient but risks omitting critical non-local traffic conditions, violating the Markov property and yielding suboptimal routes. No existing method systematically bridges this completeness–efficiency gap with a principled, compact state that is both decision-sufficient and tractable for real-time planning.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a directed weighted urban road network graph (sourced from OpenStreetMap, with edge weights parameterized by a congestion factor β ∈ [0.1, 1.5] that modulates base travel time), a source–destination node pair, and a discrete time-indexed sequence of edge-weight snapshots W_{:T}. At each decision step the agent observes a state s_t = {current node, goal node, f_t}, where f_t is the DFR-compressed dynamics feature vector; it selects one of the current node's neighbors as its action. The output is a complete node-sequence path from source to goal minimizing cumulative traffic time, produced within a fixed 100-step horizon.

## Method

DFR performs a two-stage hierarchical refinement of the global dynamics W_t into a compact local feature W''_t. First, a pre-trained distance-based RL policy π_d* (trained once offline on static road lengths) identifies the top-k shortest paths between source and goal, and the union of their edges defines a task-relevant subgraph G' whose edge weights form W'_t — a hard, interpretable attention that sparsifies the graph before RL training begins. Second, at each timestep the n-hop neighborhood of the current node is intersected with G' to yield a node-local subgraph, whose edge weights constitute the final low-dimensional state f_t = W''_t. This design is grounded in Predictive State Representations: the local feature is argued to preserve all information needed to predict the consequences of future actions, thereby approximating the optimal policy obtainable from the full dynamics sequence. Because both the policy-attention subgraph and the n-hop neighborhoods depend only on fixed road topology, they are pre-computable offline, so DFR adds negligible online overhead while reducing feature dimensionality by over 90% and cutting planning time by 46–86% across DQN, PPO, and GCN+DQN baselines.

## Results

Across three urban road networks (Nanjing, Chaoyang/Beijing, Pudong/Shanghai), DFR-enhanced models consistently outperform their All-Dynamics (AD) counterparts on all three metrics (Mean GAP, Success Rate, Compactness Rate). DFR reduces average path planning time by 85.59% for DQN, 46.08% for GCN+DQN, and 79.32% for PPO, yielding planning times of 8.18±1.74 ms (DQN/PPO) and 27.26±6.8 ms (GCN+DQN). In the ablation on the Nanjing subgraph, DQN+DFR (n=4, k=0.4) achieves a Mean GAP of 0.095 and SR of 0.908, compared to the DQN+AD baseline of GAP 0.170 and SR 0.884, while keeping the feature dimensionality below 5.7% of the original (CR < 5.7%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates three RL baselines—DQN (Mnih et al., 2015), PPO (Schulman et al., 2017), and GCN+DQN—each with and without the DFR framework, across three OSM-derived urban graphs. DFR consistently wins on all metrics: on the Nanjing subgraph, DQN+DFR reduces Mean GAP from 0.170 to 0.095 (a 44% relative improvement) while raising SR from 0.884 to 0.908 and compressing the state to under 5.7% of its original dimensionality. The largest planning-time speedup is for DQN (85.59% reduction), followed by PPO (79.32%) and GCN+DQN (46.08%). GCN+DQN without DFR achieves high SR but suffers from high GAP because the small network cannot fully exploit the high-dimensional global dynamics; DFR mitigates this by reducing input dimensionality and improving sensitivity to traffic changes. Overall, DFR is a drop-in state-representation module that improves every baseline without altering the underlying RL algorithm.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of RL-based path planning and state representation design, rather than extending a single prior method. It departs from two established extremes in DPP state design—global dynamics (complete but computationally prohibitive, as in Lin et al. 2025) and local/partial observations (efficient but potentially non-Markovian, as in Du et al. 2024b)—by proposing a hierarchical refinement that first applies a hard, pre-computed policy attention (distance-based shortest-path subgraph extraction) and then intersects with n-hop neighborhoods around the agent. The use of hard attention as a structural prior, grounded in Predictive State Representation theory, distinguishes it from soft-attention or GNN-based feature extraction approaches (Zang et al. 2023; Sun et al. 2025) that scale poorly with graph size.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The DFR framework offers a practical recipe for reducing state dimensionality in RL-based DPP while preserving decision-relevant information, with the 85.59% planning-time reduction for DQN being directly relevant to real-time urban routing. However, the manual selection of k and n parameters limits immediate deployment, and the authors themselves flag a self-adaptive mechanism as necessary future work. The experimental evidence is confined to three small OSM subgraphs (single source-goal pair per scenario), leaving open whether the hierarchical refinement generalizes to city-scale networks with multiple concurrent agents or truly stochastic traffic. The PSR-based theoretical justification, while conceptually appealing, remains informal and does not provide a rigorous sufficiency guarantee for the compressed state.
