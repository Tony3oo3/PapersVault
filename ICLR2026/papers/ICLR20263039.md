## Problem

Video recognition models are trained on fixed taxonomies where a single coarse label (e.g., 'open') masks important distinctions in object, manner, speed, or outcome. When applications require finer-grained categories, retraining with new annotations is costly, and vision-language models fail to capture the subtle temporal cues needed for fine-grained action distinctions. The paper introduces category splitting: editing an existing classifier to divide a coarse label into fine-grained subcategories with zero or minimal data while preserving accuracy on all other categories.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263039/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a trained video classifier (ViT-Small backbone with a linear classification head) and text descriptions of the desired fine-grained subcategories as input. It produces an edited classification head in which the single coarse-category weight vector is replaced by k new subcategory weight vectors, each formed by adding a retrieved or synthesized modifier vector to the original coarse weight. In the low-shot variant, one video per subcategory is additionally used to fine-tune only the new head weights. The backbone remains frozen throughout, and the output is a standard classifier that now predicts fine-grained subcategory labels for videos previously assigned to the coarse category.

## Method

The core insight is that video classifiers implicitly encode compositional structure: fine-grained categories decompose into a coarse base concept plus a modifier (e.g., 'pushing' + 'left to right'). The zero-shot method first builds a modifier dictionary by grouping existing fine-grained categories that share a base concept, computing each modifier vector as the difference between its category weight and the mean (pseudo-coarse) weight. To split a new coarse category, the most appropriate modifier vector is retrieved via cosine similarity between the target modifier text and dictionary entries (considering both the full label and modifier for context), then added to the coarse weight to form the new subcategory weight. A lightweight MLP alignment module, trained on modifier and category text-to-vector pairs from the dictionary, extends this to unseen modifiers by directly synthesizing modifier vectors from text. For low-shot settings, only the new subcategory head weights are fine-tuned (isolated fine-tuning), initialized from the zero-shot edit, which substantially improves generality while maintaining near-perfect locality.

## Results

On SSv2-Split, the proposed zero-shot method achieves 46.3% generality and 98.9% locality (mean 72.6) on subset A, substantially outperforming the best VLM baseline FG-CLIP (30.9% generality, 65.4 mean). On FineGym-Split, it reaches 34.2% generality and 97.8% locality (mean 66.0) on subset A, compared to VideoPrism's 21.7% generality (60.9 mean). The method consistently improves generality by 10–15 points over all VLM baselines while maintaining near-perfect locality (~98–99%), demonstrating that exploiting the classifier's latent compositional structure is more effective than external vision-language embeddings for fine-grained video splitting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against six vision-language model baselines (CLIP, FLAIR, FG-CLIP, VideoCLIP-XL, VideoPrism, InternVideo2) that act as external fine-grained classifiers on top of the base video model. All VLMs achieve perfect locality (100.0) by construction since they do not modify model parameters, but their generality is consistently low. On SSv2-Split, the best VLM (FG-CLIP at 30.9% generality on subset A) is outperformed by the proposed method by 15.4 points (46.3%), with video-specific models (VideoCLIP-XL, VideoPrism, InternVideo2) performing no better than or worse than CLIP. On FineGym-Split, VideoPrism is the strongest VLM at 21.7% generality (subset A), still trailing the proposed method by 12.5 points (34.2%). The gap widens on subset B of both datasets, where the proposed method's advantage over the best VLM reaches 7.6 points (SSv2) and 5.0 points (FineGym). The key takeaway is that exploiting the video classifier's own latent compositional structure via modifier retrieval/alignment yields substantially better fine-grained discrimination than relying on external VLM text-video similarity, despite the VLMs' access to large-scale paired corpora.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of model editing, continual learning, and fine-grained video understanding. It borrows the generality/locality evaluation framework from NLP model editing (Mitchell et al., 2022a) and the compositional decomposition idea from compositional zero-shot learning, but departs from both: unlike model editing which corrects existing labels, and unlike continual learning which adds entirely new categories with training data, category splitting divides an existing coarse label into fine-grained subcategories with zero or minimal supervision. The key conceptual departure is exploiting latent compositional structure (base concept + modifier) already encoded in video-only classifier heads, rather than relying on vision-language model embeddings or retraining the backbone.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical motivation is strong: as video understanding applications mature, taxonomies inevitably need refinement, and retraining with new annotations is costly. The finding that video-only backbones (particularly those pretrained with semantic objectives like MVD or SIGMA) implicitly encode compositional structure that supports zero-shot head editing is a useful insight for the fine-grained video understanding community. However, the method is constrained to classification-head edits, meaning the backbone must already encode the visual cues for new subcategories—genuinely novel visual distinctions (e.g., 'deflected', 'continues') remain failure cases. Open questions include whether deeper model edits could unlock richer adaptations, whether the approach extends to images/audio/multimodal settings, and how to handle multiple simultaneous modifiers or hierarchical taxonomies.
