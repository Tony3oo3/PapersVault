## Problem

Distributional optimization over high-dimensional discrete design spaces (e.g., 20^L protein sequences) is combinatorially intractable, and standard EDAs or policy optimization methods treat the search distribution as a single joint model over all variables. Many scientific property predictors are decomposable—factorizable over subsets of design variables—but existing algorithms cannot exploit this structure to reduce the effective optimization dimensionality.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262537/cropped_page2_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

DADO takes as input an undirected junction tree topology (derived, e.g., by thresholding AlphaFold3 residue distances at 4.5 Å for proteins) and node/edge component functions parameterized by neural networks fit to assay-labeled data. It produces a factorized generative model p_θ(x) = p_θ(x̃_r) ∏ p_θ(x̃_i | x̃_{p(i)}) whose DAG structure mirrors the rooted junction tree, with each factor an MLP-based autoregressive model over its node's discrete variables. The output is a search distribution that concentrates mass on high-scoring designs, from which candidate sequences can be sampled.

## Method

DADO replaces the single joint search distribution of a standard EDA with a factorized DAG matching the junction tree topology, and replaces the global f(x) weight with distributional Q-value functions computed via message passing. At each iteration, it (1) samples K designs from the factorized distribution, (2) computes per-node Q_i^θ values by propagating expectations (not maxes) up the tree from leaves to root, and (3) updates each factor independently via weighted MLE using its own Q-function as the weight. The Q-functions serve as messages that coordinate updates across coupled factors without requiring joint optimization, and because each factor operates on a lower-dimensional sub-space, the same K samples yield a higher sample-to-dimension ratio than a decomposition-unaware EDA. This yields a statistically more efficient optimization that scales with the treewidth of the decomposition rather than the full sequence length.

## Results

On synthetic problems (D=20, K=100 samples/iteration, 100 iterations, 20 seeds), DADO outperforms all three baselines at every sequence length tested (L=25, 50, 200), with the gap widening substantially as L increases; at L=25 baselines nearly catch up by iteration 100, but at L=200 DADO converges to markedly higher mean f(x) with non-overlapping 95% CIs. On four real protein property predictors (AAV, Amyloid, Gcn4, TDP-43; K=1000 samples/iteration), DADO achieves higher final-iteration mean f(x) than all baselines, with non-overlapping 95% CIs versus the best baseline for three of four proteins (Amyloid, Gcn4, TDP-43). All reported differences are statistically significant by two-sided paired t-test over 20 seeds.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DADO is compared against three baselines: (1) a naive EDA that uses a single joint search distribution over all design variables without exploiting decomposability (Brookes et al., 2020), (2) the Factorized Distribution Algorithm (FDA; Muhlenbein & Mahnig, 1999), which factorizes the search distribution but lacks message-passing coordination, and (3) PPO (Schulman et al., 2017), a policy-optimization method restricted to chain-graph topologies. DADO wins on every setting tested—both synthetic (L = 25, 50, 200) and all four protein predictors—with statistically significant paired t-tests over 20 seeds. The performance gap is most pronounced at larger sequence lengths and on the protein tasks, where the naive EDA and FDA converge to substantially lower mean f(x) and their 95% CIs do not overlap with DADO's at the final iteration (for Amyloid, Gcn4, and TDP-43). On the smallest synthetic problem (L = 25), the baselines nearly close the gap by iteration 100, suggesting that DADO's advantage scales with the combinatorial size of the design space, consistent with its lower-dimensional per-factor weighted-MLE updates being more statistically efficient.

## Relation to Prior Work

DADO unifies two previously separate lines of work: estimation of distribution algorithms (EDAs), which iteratively refine a generative search distribution via weighted MLE (Brookes et al., 2020), and classical max-plus message passing on junction trees for exact discrete optimization (Vlassis et al., 2004). The closest prior method, the Factorized Distribution Algorithm (Muhlenbein & Mahnig, 1999), factorizes the search distribution but lacks any mechanism to coordinate updates across overlapping variables via message passing. Similarly, policy-optimization approaches that do use message passing (Peters & Schaal, 2007; Peng et al., 2019) are restricted to chain-graph topologies, whereas DADO generalizes to arbitrary junction trees. The paper thus occupies a gap: it is the first to integrate distributional optimization with general junction-tree message passing for discrete design.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that many scientific design problems (proteins, circuits, materials) exhibit natural decomposability that standard EDAs and policy optimizers ignore, leading to statistically inefficient use of samples. DADO's empirical gains grow with sequence length and design-space size, suggesting increasing relevance as problems scale. Key open questions include: (1) how to combine DADO with pre-trained unconditional generative models and inaccurate property predictors (the 'conservative' setting), (2) whether the AlphaFold3-based decomposition heuristic generalizes beyond proteins, and (3) integration within Bayesian optimization loops. The paper's limitation is that it requires a known or approximated junction tree topology; fully data-driven decomposition discovery remains an active but separate research direction.
