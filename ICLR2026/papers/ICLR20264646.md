## Problem

While large vision-language models (LVLMs) have shown promise in urban understanding, no systematic benchmark exists to evaluate their ability to predict region-level socioeconomic indicators from imagery. Prior efforts are limited in geographic coverage, indicator diversity, and multi-modal integration, leaving it unclear how well LVLMs can perceive, reason about, and numerically estimate urban socioeconomic conditions across diverse cities and domains.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264646/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each prediction region is represented by one 256×256 satellite image (~4.7 m resolution from Esri World Imagery) and 10 street view images (from Google/Baidu APIs, or Mapillary for the open-source variant), paired with a task-specific text prompt that positions the model as a domain analyst in a named city. Under the Feature-Based Regression paradigm, the LVLM outputs 13 visual-attribute scores (0.0–9.9) per street view image, which are averaged into a single 13-dim vector per region and fed to a LASSO regressor; under the other two paradigms, the model directly outputs a single scalar (raw indicator value or a 0.0–9.9 normalized estimate). Gemini-series models are constrained to 10 images total (1 satellite + 9 street view) due to their input limit.

## Method

CityLens is a benchmark rather than a single model, but its core technical contribution is the design of three complementary evaluation paradigms that probe different facets of LVLM capability: (1) Direct Metric Prediction, where the model is prompted to output a precise indicator value; (2) Normalized Metric Estimation, which coarsens the target to a 0.0–9.9 scale to test ordinal spatial reasoning; and (3) Feature-Based Regression, where a structured prompt guides the LVLM to score 13 predefined urban visual attributes (following the Fan et al. 2023 taxonomy) on each street view image, the per-region scores are averaged, and a LASSO regressor trained with 5-fold cross-validation maps the 13-dim feature vector to the ground-truth indicator. The benchmark spans 17 cities across 6 continents, 11 indicators in 6 socioeconomic domains (economy, education, crime, transport, health, environment), and evaluates 17 state-of-the-art LVLMs. Region-level labels are constructed by mapping heterogeneous tabular sources (census tracts, MSOAs, GeoTIFF grids) to each region's spatial extent, with up to 500 samples per country-specific task and 1000 per global task. Preliminary supervised fine-tuning of Qwen2.5-VL-7B, Qwen3-VL-8B, and Llama3.2-VL-11B on additional CityLens data demonstrates the upper bound achievable by domain-adapted LVLMs.

## Results

Under the Feature-Based Regression paradigm, the domain-specific baseline UrbanVLP achieves the highest mean R² of 0.417 across 11 socioeconomic tasks, outperforming all 17 evaluated LVLMs. Among LVLMs, Gemma3-27B attains the best mean R² of 0.338, followed by Llama4-Maverick (0.324) and Gemini-2.0-Flash (0.319). Task difficulty varies dramatically: Building Height is the most predictable (best R² ≈ 0.590), while Mental Health and Bachelor Ratio remain near-zero for most models (e.g., Qwen2.5VL-3B scores 0.001 on Bachelor Ratio). Fine-tuned LVLMs (e.g., Qwen3-VL-8B) substantially close the gap, reaching R² values of 0.63–0.87 on several tasks, suggesting the current zero-shot ceiling is far below the achievable upper bound.

## Baselines & Comparisons

UrbanVLP (Hao et al., AAAI 2025), a domain-specific contrastive learning model, is the strongest baseline with a mean R² of 0.417, outperforming the best LVLM (Gemma3-27B, 0.338) by 7.9 percentage points on average. UrbanVLP leads on 9 of 11 individual tasks, including GDP (0.717 vs. 0.517), Population (0.559 vs. 0.243), and Building Height (0.807 vs. 0.590), confirming that task-specific pretraining still substantially outperforms general-purpose LVLMs in zero-shot urban socioeconomic sensing. UrbanCLIP (Yan et al., WWW 2024) performs markedly worse, with a mean R² of only 0.184, and is outperformed by every LVLM tested; it scores near-zero on most tasks except GDP (0.450) and Building Height (0.612). Notably, several LVLMs surpass UrbanCLIP on specific tasks (e.g., Gemma3-27B on Population: 0.141 vs. 0.316 is actually lower, but on Drive Ratio: 0.525 vs. 0.123), indicating that general LVLMs have partially closed the gap on visually grounded indicators while still lagging on abstract ones. Fine-tuned LVLMs (Qwen3-VL-8B, Qwen2.5-VL-7B, Llama3.2-VL-11B) dramatically exceed both baselines on available tasks (e.g., Building Height R² 0.829–0.872, Drive Ratio 0.256–0.638), suggesting the zero-shot evaluation significantly underestimates LVLM potential for this domain.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the urban socioeconomic sensing literature, which has primarily relied on classical deep learning methods (contrastive learning à la UrbanCLIP and UrbanVLP, knowledge graphs, and hand-crafted CV features), to the LVLM era. It builds directly on GeoLLM's normalized estimation paradigm and Fan et al.'s 13-attribute visual taxonomy for street view scoring, but departs from prior work by being the first to systematically evaluate 17 LVLMs across both street view and satellite imagery, 17 globally distributed cities, and 11 indicators spanning 6 socioeconomic domains. The paper positions itself against the limited geographic coverage (typically US-only), narrow indicator sets, and single-modality focus of existing urban sensing benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CityLens establishes the first comprehensive, reproducible benchmark for LVLMs in urban socioeconomic sensing, revealing a significant gap: zero-shot LVLMs achieve near-zero R² on many tasks (Mental Health, Bachelor Ratio), yet supervised fine-tuning of modest models (Qwen2.5-VL-7B) lifts R² above 0.6 on most indicators, suggesting the knowledge is learnable but absent from pretraining. The geographic bias finding (Global North R²=0.399 vs. Global South R²=0.168 on GDP) raises important fairness questions for deploying such models in policy contexts. Open questions include whether domain-specific agent frameworks combining visual perception, geospatial knowledge, and reasoning can close the zero-shot gap, and whether the 13-attribute feature space is expressive enough for abstract indicators like mental health or education level.
