## Problem

Existing uncertainty estimation methods for neural networks face a fundamental trade-off: sampling-based approaches (ensembles, MC dropout) are computationally expensive, distribution-based methods (Gaussian regression, evidential learning) impose restrictive parametric priors, and similarity-based methods do not extend naturally to regression. No prior framework simultaneously achieves sampling-free, distribution-free, task-agnostic, real-time, and interpretable uncertainty quantification in a single unified setting.

## Input / Output

The method accepts heterogeneous inputs—RGB images (32×32 CIFAR-10, 64×64 NYU-v2), text sequences (max length 128 for AG News), 400-dimensional spectral signals, or 1D scalars—and produces a prediction ŷ = R·d alongside a deterministic uncertainty score u(x) = R|‖d‖₂ − 1|. For scalar regression (D=1), targets are embedded into a minimal 2D space via duplication y_exp = (y, y) to enable the same magnitude-direction factorization. The uncertainty score is converted to a confidence value via temperature-scaled negative exponential exp(−u_cal), then normalized to [0,1] for downstream use.

## Method

HCM decomposes any target y ∈ ℝ^D into a scalar magnitude R > 0 and a unit-norm direction vector d with ‖d‖₂ = 1, reformulating prediction as a constrained optimization problem. The network outputs both R̂ and d̂, and the uncertainty score u(x) = R̂|‖d̂‖₂ − 1| measures the degree of violation of the hyperspherical constraint, computed in a single forward pass without sampling or auxiliary networks. The training loss combines a direction term weighted by the ground-truth magnitude R‖e_d‖₂, a magnitude term φ_R(e_R), and a soft norm penalty λ_norm·φ_norm(|‖d̂‖₂ − 1|), using a unified loss family (power-p, Huber, or smooth-ℓ₁) across all three components. Proposition 1 proves that u(x) provides a prediction-dependent lower bound on the true error ‖e_y‖₂, while Proposition 2 shows a derived variance-like quantity σ̂²(x) tracks Gaussian noise level up to higher-order terms, giving the score both an error-bound interpretation and a noise-sensitivity property. The approach is task-agnostic: classification uses one-hot targets in ℝ^C, regression uses continuous targets in ℝ^D, and mixup augmentation naturally generates intermediate directions on the hypersphere that improve OOD detection specifically for HCM.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 OOD detection, HCM mix achieves 89.44% average AUROC (87.90% near-OOD, 90.12% far-OOD), competitive with KNN (90.97%) and NCI (90.36%) while requiring only a single forward pass (0.19 ms/image, 226 MB peak memory). On NYU-v2 depth estimation, HCM attains the highest Pearson (0.4919) and Spearman (0.5425) uncertainty-error correlations among all methods. On the industrial semiconductor regression task, HCM achieves Pearson 0.8435 and Spearman 0.7579, far exceeding EDL (−0.2508), MC Dropout (−0.0785), and Ensembles (0.3227), confirming Proposition 1's lower-bound guarantee in practice.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HCM mix outperforms all softmax-based baselines (MSP, ODIN, Energy) and sampling-based methods (Ensembles, MC Dropout) on near-OOD AUROC, while remaining competitive on far-OOD where KNN (92.59%) and NCI (92.49%) lead. The critical advantage emerges in regression: on NYU-v2, HCM's Pearson correlation (0.4919) more than doubles that of Ensembles (0.2381) and MC Dropout (0.1932), and on the industrial dataset HCM's Pearson (0.8435) is 2.6× that of Ensembles (0.3227) while EDL and MC Dropout show negative correlations. On text OOD detection (AG News), HCM outperforms MSP and RAU (Gong et al., 2022) on all four OOD datasets but trails the similarity-based NUQ and HUQ methods, which leverage density estimation. In 1D regression under four noise regimes, HCM is the only method simultaneously sensitive to both aleatoric noise and epistemic uncertainty, whereas MDN captures only aleatoric variation and BNN produces unstable estimates. HCM's main trade-off is a modest RMSE increase (e.g., 0.1485 vs 0.1189 on NYU-v2) due to magnitude-direction reconstruction, but this does not degrade its uncertainty-error alignment.

## Relation to Prior Work

HCM carves new ground by reframing prediction as a constrained optimization problem where the unit-norm constraint on a direction vector defines uncertainty as geometric constraint violation, rather than relying on sampling (MC Dropout, Deep Ensembles), parametric distributions (Gaussian, Dirichlet, evidential), or feature-space similarity (energy, KNN, Mahalanobis). The closest conceptual relative is Gong et al. (2022), which applies a hyperspherical constraint to classification logits for text calibration, but HCM extends this by decomposing the target itself into a magnitude-direction pair and deriving a provable lower bound on prediction error from the norm deviation. A distinctive structural property is that mixup augmentation aligns naturally with the hyperspherical geometry (interpolated labels map to intermediate directions on the unit sphere), yielding a unique performance boost for HCM that does not transfer to other baselines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

HCM offers a unified, single-forward-pass uncertainty score with a theoretical guarantee (lower bound on prediction error) that is simultaneously applicable to regression and classification, which is rare among existing methods. The industrial semiconductor validation demonstrates practical value in safety-critical settings where filtering unreliable predictions is essential. Key limitations include the inability to explicitly separate aleatoric from epistemic uncertainty, a consistent RMSE penalty from the magnitude-direction reconstruction, and the requirement for light fine-tuning (ruling out frozen or zero-shot LLMs). Open questions include whether the geometric decomposition extends to multi-valued or multimodal outputs, and whether the constraint-violation signal can be made more robust to training dynamics (sensitivity to λ and learning rate).
