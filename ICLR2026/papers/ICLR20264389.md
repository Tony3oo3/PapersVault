## Problem

Classical hypernetworks synthesize neural network weights as a flat vector, ignoring the target architecture's internal structure and neuron-permutation symmetry. Because many distinct weight assignments implement the same function, this symmetry-induced redundancy fragments supervision and severely limits out-of-distribution generalization to unseen intents. The paper asks whether a structure-aware hypernetwork can collapse these equivalent parameterizations and learn a stable intent-to-weights mapping that extrapolates zero-shot to new program parameterizations.

## Input / Output

At deployment the meta-learner receives only a compact user intent U (a scalar prime p, a prefix length n, or a (family, parameter) pair) with no access to test input-output pairs. It emits a complete weight vector θ in a single forward pass, which is written into the fixed tensor slots of a pre-specified target architecture (1-hidden-layer MLP, 1-layer Transformer, or 1-layer Seq2Seq Transformer). The instantiated network m_{S,θ} then maps inputs X to outputs Y, and gradients of the task loss backpropagate through the target network into the meta-learner during training.

## Method

Meta-GNN converts the fixed target architecture into a directed neural graph (nodes = bias scalars, edges = weight parameters) and performs k rounds of structure-aware message passing with permutation-invariant aggregation over graph neighborhoods. Encoders and decoders are parameter-tied within permutation-equivalent neuron groups (DeepSet-style), so the meta-learner treats all neurons in a group identically, collapsing the symmetry-induced search space without loss of expressivity. For Transformers, the graph construction introduces parameter-free pass_thru edges (enabling head permutations) and align_one2one edges (enabling within-head dimensional shuffling), extending equivariance beyond MLPs and CNNs to multihead attention, residual pathways, and layer normalization. Positional encodings are injected only at initialization to break symmetry for slot assignment, while all learned modules remain permutation-invariant. Training is end-to-end: the semantic loss on the instantiated target network backpropagates through decoding, message passing, and encoding to update the meta-learner's parameters, with no post-hoc alignment or test-time adaptation required.

## Results

On the OOD test (unseen intents), Meta-GNN achieves 86.6% accuracy on SUMFIRST-n (vs. 14.5% for Meta-MLP and 14.0% for direct MLP), 94.3% on ADDMOD-p (vs. 94.7% for Meta-MLP and 69.9% for direct MLP), and 71.3% on 1D-ARC mixture (vs. 62.5% for Meta-MLP and 60.3% for direct Transformer). All meta-learners reach near-100% in-distribution test accuracy, confirming that complete neural programs (up to ~17k-parameter Seq2Seq Transformers) can be synthesized in a single forward pass. The largest gap between Meta-GNN and other methods appears on SUMFIRST-n, where structure-aware message passing yields a 6× improvement over Meta-MLP on OOD generalization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against direct prediction baselines (which map (U, X) → Y without a meta component), all meta-learners achieve 20–70 percentage-point gains on OOD test: on SUMFIRST-n, Meta-GNN reaches 86.6% vs. 14.0% for direct MLP; on ADDMOD-p, Meta-MLP reaches 94.7% vs. 69.9% for direct MLP; on 1D-ARC, Meta-GNN reaches 71.3% vs. 60.3% for direct Transformer. Among the three meta-architectures, Meta-GNN dominates on SUMFIRST-n (86.6% vs. 14.5% Meta-MLP, 34.4% Meta-gMLP) and 1D-ARC (71.3% vs. 62.5% Meta-MLP, 61.0% Meta-gMLP), but is marginally behind Meta-MLP on ADDMOD-p OOD (94.3% vs. 94.7%), where the simpler flat hypernetwork suffices for the single-parameter prime intent. Meta-gMLP consistently underperforms both Meta-MLP and Meta-GNN on ADDMOD-p and 1D-ARC, which the paper attributes to a capacity bottleneck in its global mixer as the target network size grows. On 1D-ARC per-family analysis, Meta-GNN is the only meta-learner to achieve high OOD accuracy on Flip (89.8%) and MoveRight (92.1%), while all meta-learners fail on RecolorBar (categorical intent with no ordinal structure).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the hypernetwork paradigm (Ha et al., 2016) by incorporating the target architecture's computation graph as a neural graph and applying structure-aware, permutation-equivariant message passing to generate weights, building directly on the neural-graph formulations of Lim et al. (2024) and Kofinas et al. (2024) and the weight-space equivariance principles of Navon et al. (2023) and Zhou et al. (2024). The key departure is generative: prior neural-graph methods process *trained* networks for analysis or editing, whereas Meta-GNN must emit a complete, executable weight vector from intent alone in a single zero-shot forward pass with no test-time supervision. It also extends the graph formulation from MLPs and CNNs to full multi-head Transformers with residual pathways and layer normalization, and it recasts classical discrete program synthesis (Solar-Lezama, 2008; Alur et al., 2013) as continuous optimization in weight space, trading exact symbolic correctness for differentiable, graded semantic loss.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first demonstration that complete neural programs (up to ~17k-parameter Seq2Seq Transformers) can be synthesized from a compact user intent in a single forward pass, and that structure-aware inductive bias (permutation-equivariant message passing over the target's computation graph) is the critical factor enabling zero-shot generalization to unseen intents—outperforming flat hypernetworks by 20–70 points on OOD test. The mechanistic recovery of the closed-form 'clock' mapping p → (W_E, W_U) on ADDMOD-p provides rare direct evidence that a meta-learner internalizes an algorithmic rule rather than memorizing instances. However, all benchmarks are small, synthetic, and use shallow single-layer targets; scaling to deeper or mixed architectures, natural-language intents, or ARC-style I/O-pair specifications remains open, and the paper does not compare against symbolic or neuro-symbolic synthesis systems, leaving the practical competitive landscape unclear.
