## Problem

LLM fine-tuning computes loss and updates parameters at the token level, yet most fine-tuning datasets are constructed at the sentence level, so not all tokens in label sentences contribute equally to performance improvement. No existing method can identify which specific tokens within label sentences act as noise during fine-tuning, and prior token-level approaches (pretraining, DPO, knowledge distillation) either assume noise-free data or require task-specific frameworks that do not generalize to standard supervised fine-tuning.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a fine-tuning dataset of (input, label) text pairs and a frozen base LLM, from which it extracts three per-token scores for every token in the label portion: an attention-based reasoning importance score, a probability-of-correct-prediction (PCP) knowledge novelty score, and an embedding-distance task relevance score. The output is a modified training set in which tokens flagged as noisy by any of the three attribute-specific thresholds (IQR for RI, PCP > 0.95 for KN, second-smallest Multi-Otsu cluster for TR) are assigned the ignore index (-100), so their gradients are excluded from the cross-entropy loss during fine-tuning.

## Method

XTF decomposes the contribution of each label token to fine-tuning quality into three interpretable attributes—reasoning importance (base-model attention over the concatenated input+label), knowledge novelty (1 minus the base model's probability of predicting the token), and task relevance (negative normalized distance between the token's context-free embedding and the dataset's domain centroid)—and declares a token noisy if it lacks any one attribute. Each attribute is thresholded with a distribution-adaptive rule (interquartile range, fixed 0.05 PCP cutoff, and Multi-Otsu clustering, respectively), and the three filtered sets are combined via union under a conservative 'completely devoid' criterion. The theoretical analysis, grounded in damped Fisher information geometry, proves that under a mixture model of core and noise tokens with a selector of non-trivial skill, filtering strictly increases the alignment between the preconditioned training gradient and the ideal gradient direction, yielding a provably larger one-step decrease in the ideal KL risk. Empirically, XTF is validated across 7 LLMs (DeepSeek, Llama, Mistral; 1B–14B) on math, code, and medicine tasks, achieving up to 13.7% accuracy gains over standard fine-tuning.

## Results

On GSM8K (math), XTF achieves an average accuracy of 45.7%, surpassing normal fine-tuning (37.1%) by 8.6% and the best baseline DF (41.4%) by 4.3%, with a peak improvement of 13.3% on DeepSeek-distilled-Qwen-1.5B (56.2% vs. 42.9%). On PubMedQA (medicine), XTF reaches 37.1% average accuracy, exceeding normal fine-tuning (30.4%) by 6.7% and the best baseline TC (33.7%) by 3.4%, with a peak gain of 13.7% on Llama-3.1-8B with LoRA (37.9% vs. 24.2%). On the code task (HumanEval), XTF yields up to 5.6% and 6.3% improvements on pass@1 and pass@10 respectively over normal fine-tuning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

XTF outperforms all four data-enhancement baselines on average across both math and medicine tasks. On GSM8K, XTF (45.7%) exceeds the strongest baseline DF (41.4%) by 4.3% and TC (41.0%) by 4.7%, while DA (36.5%) actually underperforms normal fine-tuning (37.1%), suggesting that LLM-based augmentation can introduce its own noise. On PubMedQA, XTF (37.1%) leads the best baseline TC (33.7%) by 3.4% and SLM (33.5%) by 3.6%; notably, DA (32.7%) and DF (31.4%) both trail normal fine-tuning (30.4%) only marginally, indicating that sample-level strategies are less effective in the medical domain. On the code task (HumanEval), XTF consistently surpasses normal fine-tuning, with the gap widening from pass@1 to pass@10 (up to 6.3%), whereas in some configurations normal fine-tuning actually degrades accuracy, highlighting the harm of unfiltered token-level noise. The ablation (Table 2) confirms that all three attributes (RI, KN, TR) are necessary: removing any single attribute drops the average from 40.1% to at most 36.9%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the token-level training line (SLM, Token Cleaning, token-level self-evolution) by explicitly addressing the noise problem in fine-tuning datasets, which prior token-level methods implicitly assume away. It departs from sample-level data optimization (Superfiltering, AugGPT) by operating at the token granularity, and from explainability work (attention-based, causal) by repurposing attention scores, prediction confidence, and embedding distance as interpretable filtering criteria rather than post-hoc explanations. The Multi-Otsu thresholding method is borrowed for the task-relevance score partitioning, while the theoretical analysis draws on Fisher information geometry and natural gradient to formalize why gradient masking of identified noisy tokens improves alignment with the ideal training direction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The three-attribute decomposition (reasoning importance, knowledge novelty, task relevance) provides a principled and interpretable framework for token-level data curation that is directly applicable to any supervised fine-tuning pipeline, and the Fisher-geometry theory gives practitioners confidence that the filtering is not heuristic but provably improves one-step risk reduction. Key limitations include the inference-level computational overhead (which scales with model size and sequence length), the reliance on a single base model for all three scores, and the conservative threshold strategy that may under-filter in some regimes. Open questions include whether a small proxy model could score tokens for much larger base models, whether additional attributes beyond the three identified could capture further noise structure, and whether the approach transfers to multi-turn or agentic fine-tuning settings where the teacher-forcing context assumption weakens.
