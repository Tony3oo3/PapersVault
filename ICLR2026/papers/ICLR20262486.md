## Problem

Training LLMs with FP4 quantization (W4A4G4) suffers from severe performance degradation because anisotropy in the singular value spectra of weights, activations, and gradients produces wide numerical distributions that misalign with block-level low-bit quantization. This quantization bias disproportionately distorts small singular components, causing spectral distortion that degrades both training loss and downstream accuracy by 3–4% relative to BF16.

## Input / Output

Metis operates on the GeMM matrices encountered during LLM training: weight matrices (m×n), activation tensors (reshaped to l×m where l = batch × sequence length), and output-gradient matrices (l×n). Each matrix is decomposed into a rank-k low-rank component (k ≈ 1.5% of the hidden dimension) and a residual, with the low-rank singular values retained in high precision while singular vectors and the residual are quantized to the E2M1 NVFP4 format. For weights, the low-rank and residual branches are stored as separate trainable variables updated independently; for activations and gradients, the decomposition is recomputed dynamically each iteration (with temporal reuse every 8 steps).

## Method

Metis partitions anisotropic singular value spectra into narrower sub-distributions via randomized SVD, quantizing the dominant low-rank branch and the residual independently so that small singular components are no longer overwhelmed by large ones. Two structural properties make this scalable: (i) sparse random sampling of <1% of sequences reliably recovers the dominant subspace (guaranteed by matrix Chernoff + Davis-Kahan bounds), and (ii) random projection onto a reduced hidden dimension preserves the dominant subspace with high probability (via randomized SVD theory). The decomposition cost is further reduced by a custom two-stage pipelined eig-SVD operator and temporal reuse across 8 gradient-accumulation steps, keeping total runtime overhead to ~10% over the NVFP4 baseline. Stochastic rounding is applied by default to mitigate residual quantization bias, and the forward/backward passes are reformulated so that all GeMMs in both directions operate entirely in FP4.

## Results

On LLaMA-3 8B trained with 100B DCLM tokens, Metis achieves a training loss of 2.45 (vs. 2.44 BF16, a 0.4% gap) and an average downstream accuracy of 51.0% (vs. 51.1% BF16, a 0.1% drop) under full W4A4G4 FP4 quantization. On GPT-2 130M and 1.1B, Metis actually surpasses the BF16 baseline on both loss (3.20 vs. 3.23 and 3.01 vs. 3.09) and average downstream accuracy (41.5% vs. 41.4% and 48.6% vs. 48.4%). Direct NVFP4 quantization without Metis incurs a 3–4% loss gap and ~1% downstream degradation across all models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against direct NVFP4 quantization (Alvarez et al.), Metis reduces the training loss gap from 3–4% to 0.4% on LLaMA-3 8B and from ~3% to negative (i.e., surpassing BF16) on both GPT-2 models, while improving average downstream accuracy by 0.2–1.1 points. Compared to Nvidia's FP4 recipe (Devleker & Ghodsian), which combines random Hadamard transforms with stochastic rounding, Metis achieves 1–2% lower test loss and 0.5% higher average downstream accuracy on GPT-2 models (Table 3), attributed to Metis's spectral decomposition producing substantially narrower residual distributions than Hadamard smoothing. In the post-training quantization (PTQ) setting on LLaMA-3 8B, Metis outperforms LQ-LoRA (Guo et al.) by 0.6% and OmniQuant (Shao et al.) by 0.7% in average downstream accuracy (Table 4), while adding only 3% FLOPs at inference. Overall, Metis wins or ties on every comparison across all four baselines and all three model scales.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of FP4 quantization work that relies on Hadamard rotations (QuaRot, QuIP, HALO, NVIDIA's own recipe) or channel-wise re-parameterization (SmoothQuant, OmniQuant) to handle outliers, arguing instead that the root cause of quantization failure is spectral anisotropy rather than isolated extreme values. It repurposes randomized SVD—a tool previously applied to inference-time outlier absorption (SVDQuant)—as a training-time mechanism applied jointly to weights, activations, and gradients, and introduces two enabling properties (sparse random sampling and random projection) that make repeated spectral decomposition tractable during optimization. The paper thus carves new ground at the intersection of spectral analysis and low-bit training, rather than extending any single prior method.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FP4 training on Blackwell-class hardware promises 1.8× memory reduction and 7× GeMM speedup over FP8, making a viable W4A4G4 recipe a practical prerequisite for the next generation of LLM training infrastructure; Metis's 0.4% loss gap and 10.12% overhead make this a credible path. Open questions include whether the 1.5% rank and 8-step reuse interval remain sufficient at 70B+ scale, whether the BF16-simulated FP4 results transfer faithfully to native hardware, and how the method interacts with emerging training paradigms (e.g., mixture-of-experts, long-context). The paper's main FP4 training results are limited to 8B parameters, leaving the most consequential scale untested.
