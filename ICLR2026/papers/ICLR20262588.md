## Problem

CLIP's dense latent representations are opaque, and the prevailing assumption is that enforcing sparsity for interpretability necessarily degrades downstream accuracy. Post-hoc methods like Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) partially address this but suffer from 1–2% performance loss due to imperfect reconstruction and collapse most learned features into unimodal representations, losing CLIP's inherent cross-modal structure. This paper challenges the interpretability–performance trade-off by asking whether sparsity can be integrated natively into CLIP training without sacrificing accuracy or multimodality.

## Input / Output

The model takes 224×224 RGB images and free-form text (captions or individual words) as input and produces 55,296-dimensional sparse embeddings where only ~300–470 features (0.47–0.66%) are active per sample. Unlike SAE outputs, these sparse vectors are directly usable for zero-shot classification, bidirectional image-text retrieval, and as a vision encoder feeding a VLM (Llama 3.1 8B) that generates text descriptions. A key structural property is that the same sparse features activate for both image and text inputs, enabling direct concept naming by identifying which vocabulary words maximally trigger each feature.

## Method

Sparse CLIP modifies standard CLIP pretraining with only two changes: (1) a ReLU activation is applied after the final projection layer, imposing non-negativity on all embedding dimensions, and (2) the projection dimension is expanded by a factor of 72 (768 → 55,296 for ViT-L/14). Training uses the standard CLIP contrastive loss with no reconstruction objective or SAE decoder. The theoretical justification rests on the equivalence between non-negative contrastive learning and Non-negative Matrix Factorization (Wang et al., 2024) and between spectral contrastive loss and matrix factorization (HaoChen et al., 2022), positioning Sparse CLIP as an NMF-style dictionary learned end-to-end via SGD. Sparsity level is controlled by capping the learnable logit scale (temperature) parameter: lower caps yield sparser representations, with an optimal range identified empirically. The resulting features are genuinely multimodal—activated by both images and text for the same semantic concept—enabling straightforward concept alignment and revealing how cross-modal knowledge emerges and evolves during training.

## Results

On zero-shot image classification, ViT-L/14 Sparse (0.66% sparsity) achieves 75.6% average accuracy across 6 standard benchmarks, outperforming the dense ViT-L/14 baseline (75.1%) by +0.5%, and 74.0% on fine-grained classification (+0.7% over baseline). ViT-L/14 Sparse+ (0.47% sparsity) matches the baseline at 75.1% average. On bounding box classification, both sparse variants significantly outperform the baseline (55.5/56.0 vs 53.3 Acc@1), though they underperform on zero-shot retrieval (43.7/41.8 vs 45.5 IR@1). In the VLM setting, Sparse+ achieves the best MMMU (41.7) and AI2D (70.6) scores among all variants.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the dense ViT-L/14 baseline, ViT-L/14 Sparse outperforms on zero-shot classification (+0.5% average) and fine-grained classification (+0.7% average), while Sparse+ matches the baseline exactly. On bounding box classification, both sparse variants beat the baseline by 2.2–2.7 points (Acc@1), but both underperform on zero-shot retrieval (IR@1: 43.7/41.8 vs 45.5), which the authors attribute to sparsity encouraging focus on dominant subjects. Compared to Prisma SAE (Joseph et al., 2025b) at ViT-B/32 scale, Sparse CLIP at ViT-L/14 achieves substantially higher accuracy (75.6% vs 56.6% Avg Class.), though the model-size difference limits direct comparison; the paper notes Prisma SAE suffers 1–2% loss from imperfect reconstruction. On interpretability metrics, ViT-L/14 Sparse+ achieves Clarity of 0.559 (vs 0.519 for Prisma and 0.521 for Daujotas' SAE) with far fewer active features (L0 = 344 vs 916 and >10k), and unlike post-hoc SAEs, its features are predominantly multimodal rather than unimodal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant post-hoc interpretability paradigm (Sparse Autoencoders applied to pretrained CLIP, as in Prisma and Daujotas) by integrating sparsity directly into CLIP pretraining via two minimal modifications: ReLU activation and dimension expansion. The theoretical justification draws on the equivalence between non-negative contrastive learning and Non-negative Matrix Factorization (Wang et al., 2024; HaoChen et al., 2022), positioning Sparse CLIP as a dictionary-learning variant that shares the same framework as SAEs but is trained end-to-end with the contrastive objective. It also contrasts with Papadimitriou et al. (2025), who found that SAEs on CLIP's multimodal space produce predominantly unimodal features, whereas Sparse CLIP's native cross-modal training yields genuinely multimodal sparse features.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges the widely-held assumption that interpretability and accuracy are fundamentally in tension, showing that native sparsity during CLIP training can match or slightly exceed dense baseline performance while producing interpretable, multimodal features. This opens a path toward designing future vision-language models with built-in interpretability rather than retrofitting it. Key open questions include whether the modest performance gains stem from the 14% parameter overhead rather than sparsity itself, whether the approach generalizes beyond ViT-L/14, and how to reduce the 72× memory bottleneck that currently limits dimension expansion on standard hardware.
