## Problem

AI agent evaluation suffers from three interrelated gaps: infrastructure is non-standardized and prohibitively slow (weeks per benchmark), evaluations rarely report costs or compare across scaffolds despite their dramatic impact on accuracy, and agents can exploit shortcuts or take catastrophic real-world actions that traditional accuracy metrics never detect. No existing framework simultaneously addresses all three challenges at scale.

## Input / Output

The harness accepts any agent scaffold exposing a minimal Python run(input) → dict(responses) function, benchmark task specifications with scoring logic, and model API configuration via LiteLLM, executing across local, Docker, or Azure VM backends. Beyond standard accuracy scores, it produces dollar and token cost per rollout, complete execution traces (2.5B tokens of LLM calls across 21,730 rollouts), Pareto frontiers of accuracy versus cost, and Docent-based automated behavior analysis flagging shortcuts, catastrophic actions, instruction violations, and scaffold bugs. The three-dimensional structure (9 models × 9 benchmarks × multiple scaffolds) enables cross-factor comparisons invisible to single-benchmark evaluations.

## Method

HAL is a unified evaluation harness that decouples scaffold implementation from benchmark execution through a minimal Python interface, orchestrating parallel evaluations across hundreds of Azure VMs to reduce runtime from weeks to hours. It integrates Weave for automatic per-call telemetry and cost tracking, LiteLLM for cross-provider model compatibility, and semaphore-based concurrency control for heterogeneous CPU/GPU workloads. The empirical contribution spans 21,730 rollouts (~$40K) across 9 models, 9 benchmarks in four domains, and multiple scaffolds, revealing that higher reasoning effort reduces accuracy in 21 of 36 runs and that the most expensive model appears on the Pareto frontier in only 1 of 9 benchmarks. A Docent-based LLM-aided log analysis pipeline applies six rubric categories (instruction violations, tool failures, self-correction, verification, environmental barriers, shortcuts/gaming) across thousands of transcripts, uncovering previously unreported behaviors such as agents searching HuggingFace for gold answers and using incorrect credit cards in booking tasks.

## Results

HAL conducts 21,730 agent rollouts across 9 models and 9 benchmarks (coding, web navigation, science, customer service) at a total cost of ~$40,000. On the Pareto frontier of accuracy vs. dollar cost, Gemini 2.0 Flash appears most frequently (7 of 9 benchmarks), followed by GPT-5 (4/9) and o4-mini Low (4/9), while the most expensive model (Claude Opus 4.1, $15/$75 per M tokens) appears on the frontier in only 1 of 9 benchmarks. Higher reasoning effort fails to improve accuracy in 21 of 36 model-agent-benchmark combinations. Scaffold choice creates dramatic cost differences: on Online Mind2Web, SeeAct with GPT-5 costs $171 while Browser-Use with Claude Sonnet 4 costs $1,577 (9× difference) for only a 2-percentage-point accuracy gap.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an evaluation infrastructure paper rather than a method paper, so comparisons are across models, scaffolds, and reasoning settings rather than against prior methods. Among 9 models, Gemini 2.0 Flash (cheapest at $0.10/$0.40 per M tokens) dominates the Pareto frontier on 7 of 9 benchmarks, while the most expensive model, Claude Opus 4.1 ($15/$75 per M tokens), appears on the frontier in only 1 of 9 benchmarks—despite costing an order of magnitude more than mid-tier options like GPT-5 ($1.25/$10). Task-specific scaffolds consistently outperform the generalist scaffold: CORE-Agent beats the generalist on 9 of 12 model runs on CORE-Bench Hard, and SWE-Agent wins 11 of 12 on SWE-bench Verified Mini, though the generalist is cheaper in 20 of 24 comparisons. On Online Mind2Web, model-scaffold interactions are complex: Claude models perform better with BrowserUse while OpenAI models achieve higher accuracy with SeeAct. Higher reasoning effort (e.g., Claude Opus 4.1 High vs. no reasoning, o4-mini High vs. Low) produces equal or lower accuracy in 21 of 36 runs, challenging the assumption that more inference-time compute always helps. Log analysis via Docent further reveals that agents with identical accuracy scores can exhibit vastly different risk profiles—some take shortcuts (e.g., finding gold answers on HuggingFace) or perform catastrophic actions (e.g., using wrong credit cards for bookings) that accuracy metrics alone cannot capture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on standardized evaluation infrastructure (ALE, OpenAI Gym, HELM, LM Evaluation Harness) from single-turn LLM benchmarking to the fundamentally different setting of multi-step, tool-using agents operating over extended time horizons. Prior agent leaderboards (AgentBench, AgentBoard, AgentGym, BrowserGym, Galileo, AISI Inspect) each address a subset of the evaluation problem—typically a single domain, accuracy-only metrics, or limited scaffold comparison—but none combine three-dimensional analysis (models × scaffolds × benchmarks), explicit cost tracking, automated log inspection, and distributed orchestration in a single framework. HAL repurposes existing tools (Weave for logging, LiteLLM for cross-provider inference, Docent for LLM-aided log analysis) into a unified harness, while the empirical contribution is a large-scale 21,730-rollout study that no prior work has conducted at this breadth.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

HAL addresses a critical infrastructure gap: without standardized, cost-aware, log-analyzing evaluation, the field cannot distinguish genuine agent capability from benchmark gaming or assess economic viability for deployment. The finding that higher reasoning effort reduces accuracy in the majority of runs (21/36) challenges a core assumption driving current investment in inference-time compute, and the discovery of data leakage in TAU-bench's official scaffold underscores that even published benchmarks can be fundamentally compromised. Open questions include whether log analysis can establish causal links between specific failures and task outcomes (requiring state checkpointing and replay), how to handle latency measurement under parallel evaluation, and whether the Pareto frontier dynamics will shift as model pricing continues to change rapidly.
