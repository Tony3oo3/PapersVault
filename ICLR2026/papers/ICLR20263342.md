## Problem

Reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards (RLVR) methods like GRPO cause reasoning models to inflate response length, trading brevity for accuracy. The paper shows that in 72% of cases on AIME 25, longer responses are more likely to be wrong, indicating verbosity is a distinct failure mode rather than a byproduct of difficulty. Existing fixes like Dr. GRPO and DAPO apply token-level normalization but still balloon response length from 4k to 14k tokens within 100 training steps, because they penalize long incorrect outputs while simultaneously amplifying rewards for long correct ones.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt (a math problem, coding question, or STEM query) and produces a chain-of-thought reasoning trace followed by a final answer, extracted via regex with GPT-4o fallback. During training, the model samples G candidate responses per question (G ∈ {8, 16, 24}) at temperature 1.0 within a 32k context window (1k reserved for the prompt), then retains only k ≤ 8 for policy gradient computation. At inference, responses are sampled at T=0.8, top-p=0.95 with 32k max length, and the key metric is excess length reduction (ELR) relative to the SFT baseline while matching GRPO pass@1 accuracy.

## Method

GFPO modifies GRPO's advantage estimation by sampling a larger group of G responses per question, ranking them by a user-specified metric (response length or token efficiency = reward/length), and computing normalized advantages only within the top-k retained subset while setting advantages to zero for all rejected responses. This selective learning acts as implicit reward shaping: it steers the policy toward concise, high-quality outputs without requiring complex multi-objective reward engineering, and is orthogonal to loss normalization choices (compatible with DAPO, Dr. GRPO, or standard GRPO). The retention ratio k/G is the critical hyperparameter, with 25–33% yielding the strongest length reductions. An Adaptive Difficulty variant uses a streaming t-digest to estimate per-question difficulty from average reward and dynamically assigns k=4 (easy) to k=8 (hard) out of G=16, allocating more exploration to harder problems. Token Efficiency GFPO (k=8, G=16) achieves the largest reductions (71–85% excess length cut) with only a 7% increase in training time, reducing end-to-end inference latency by ~30%.

## Results

On Phi-4-reasoning (14B), GFPO's Token Efficiency variant cuts GRPO's excess length inflation by 79.5% on average (70.9–84.6% across AIME 25/24, GPQA, Omni-MATH, LiveCodeBench) while maintaining 71.7% average pass@1 accuracy (vs. 72.1% for GRPO, a non-significant difference). The Adaptive Difficulty variant achieves the highest accuracy at 72.9% with 46.0% length reduction. With only a 7% increase in training step time, Token Efficiency GFPO reduces end-to-end inference latency by 28.6% (315.1s → 225.0s), saving ~90 seconds per hard query.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the SFT baseline (Phi-4-reasoning, 69.2% acc, 9.5k tokens), both GRPO and GFPO improve accuracy to ~72%, but GRPO inflates response length to 13k while GFPO keeps it at 10.2–11.4k. Against the GRPO baseline (Phi-4-reasoning-plus, 72.1% acc, 13k tokens), all GFPO variants match accuracy within non-significant margins (Wilcoxon signed-rank test) while reducing excess length inflation by 29.7–79.5%, with Token Efficiency achieving the largest reduction (79.5%) at 10.2k average length. Against Dr. GRPO (70.1% acc, 11.5k tokens, 47.2% ELR), GFPO wins on both axes: accuracy is 1.6–2.8% higher and excess length reduction is 10–70% greater (e.g., 70.9% vs. 43.6% on AIME 25, 79.7% vs. 7.2% on LiveCodeBench). The paper attributes Dr. GRPO's inferiority to its lack of per-response length normalization, which causes long trajectories to dominate gradient updates in models already SFTed for verbose chain-of-thought.

## Relation to Prior Work

GFPO builds directly on GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), which simplified PPO by replacing the value model with group-relative advantage estimation. It departs from concurrent length-control methods like Dr. GRPO (Liu et al., 2025) and DAPO (Yu et al., 2025), which modify token-level loss normalization to penalize long incorrect outputs; the paper argues these approaches inadvertently reinforce verbosity in SFT'd models by amplifying rewards for long correct ones. Instead, GFPO intervenes at the advantage-estimation level via rejection sampling over a larger group, drawing conceptually on iterative self-improvement (Zelikman et al., 2022) but integrating filtering directly into the RL update. The Adaptive Difficulty variant introduces, to the authors' knowledge, the first RLVR method that dynamically adjusts group size based on unsupervised difficulty estimates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: a 7% increase in training time yields ~30% end-to-end inference latency reduction (≈90 seconds faster on hard STEM queries), making this a compelling drop-in modification for any GRPO-based RLVR pipeline. The finding that verbosity is a distinct failure mode (72% of longer responses are wrong, independent of difficulty) reframes how the community should think about length inflation in reasoning models. Open questions include whether the k/G ratio and token-efficiency metrics transfer to non-mathematical reasoning domains, whether the approach scales to much larger models (100B+), and how GFPO interacts with test-time scaling strategies like best-of-N sampling that also benefit from shorter chains.
