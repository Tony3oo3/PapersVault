## Problem

While verbatim sequence memorization in LLMs is well-studied, the mechanisms by which models acquire and generalize factual knowledge from training data remain poorly understood. A key obstacle is the difficulty of controlling and quantifying fact exposure in real pretraining corpora, and existing synthetic datasets are either too templatic, too small, or lack the combination of diverse document styles and associated Q&A pairs needed for controlled memorization studies.

## Input / Output

The generation pipeline takes random inspiration words and a target year as seeds, then progressively expands them through GPT-4o into structured fictsheets (entities, events, locations, times, reasons), 1500 fictional documents across five webtext styles (news, social media, encyclopedia, corporate, blog), and 7500 Q&A pairs. After blind/informed feasibility annotation and deduplication, 3036 unique infeasible questions remain, which are further reformatted into 4-way and 10-way multiple choice questions. The hierarchical keying system (event_id → fiction_id → question_id) enables flexible train/validation splits along event, document, style, or fictsheets boundaries.

## Method

The core contribution is a multi-stage synthetic data generation pipeline that produces webtext-realistic documents about entirely fictional events, creating a 'cleanroom' where factual content is disjoint from any real-world pretraining knowledge while surface form mimics natural webtext. The pipeline uses GPT-4o with varying temperatures (1.0 for seeds/fictions, 0.7 for fictsheets, 0.1 for Q&A) and deliberate prompting strategies to avoid sci-fi tropes and enforce diversity. For experiments, base LLMs (Llama 3.1/3.2, Gemma 1/2) are continued-pretrained with 5% fictional data mixed into 95% Dolma webtext, using four split criteria (Event, Document, Style, Fictsheets) to isolate knowledge transfer effects. The key finding is that verbatim memorization and factual generalization occur under different conditions: structured fictsheets cause rapid overfitting with minimal transfer, while diverse multi-style documents enable both stylistic and factual generalization, and the observed 'leaky' transfer across event boundaries suggests models learn distributional patterns rather than purely atomic facts.

## Results

On the Event Split MCQ evaluation (4-choice), the Llama-3.1-8B model improves from 31.91% initial accuracy to 45.31% final accuracy on training questions, and from 30.51% to 43.44% on validation questions, demonstrating measurable knowledge transfer. Training on the Fictsheets split (structured fact lists) produces near-zero training loss but the least Q&A transfer, while the Doc Split and Style Splits (diverse document styles) yield the strongest factual transfer. No improvement is observed on TriviaQA (real-world facts) when training on fictional data, confirming the fictional facts remain isolated from real knowledge. The improvement on validation questions (which correspond to seed events wholly omitted from training) is 'leaky,' suggesting the model learns distributional patterns rather than purely atomic facts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a dataset paper without external method comparisons; instead, it contrasts internal training conditions. The base webtext-only control (Llama-3.2-1B trained on real webtext with identical hyperparameters) shows no improvement on fictional Q&A or MCQ accuracy, confirming all observed gains are attributable to the fictional data injection. Among split criteria, the Doc Split (holding out one document per style) and Style Splits produce the strongest validation loss reduction and MCQ transfer, while the Event Split (holding out entire seed events) shows less transfer, and the Fictsheets split (structured fact lists) shows the least transfer despite achieving near-zero training loss. Larger models (Llama-3.1-8B vs. 1B/3B variants) consistently achieve higher MCQ accuracy, confirming a model-size scaling effect. The near-identical improvement rates on training vs. validation MCQs (45.31% vs. 43.44%) indicate that the learned knowledge is primarily distributional rather than atomically tied to specific training documents.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by constructing a synthetic dataset that simultaneously enables controlled study of both verbatim memorization (in the tradition of Carlini et al.) and factual knowledge acquisition (in the tradition of Kandpal et al. and Allen-Zhu & Li), which prior datasets addressed separately or with less realistic surface forms. It repurposes synthetic data generation techniques from instruction-tuning literature (Chen et al., Zhang et al.) but applies them to a memorization-study purpose, producing webtext-like documents about fictional events that are factually disjoint from the real world. Unlike TOFU (questions only), Fictional Knowledge (completion-style probes), or New News (small, manually curated), FictionalQA provides both diverse-style documents and Q&A pairs at scale, with a regenerable 'living asset' pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dataset provides a controlled laboratory for studying memorization dynamics that is difficult to replicate with real webtext, and the finding that structured declarative Fictsheets yield the least Q&A transfer despite near-perfect verbatim memorization challenges the intuitive assumption that the most 'extractable' surface form is the most learnable. The 'leaky' generalization observed in Figure 8 suggests LLMs acquire knowledge through distributional patterns rather than purely atomic fact storage, with implications for how we think about knowledge editing, unlearning, and the conditions under which factual memorization occurs under limited repetition. Open questions include whether these dynamics hold at trillion-token pretraining scales, how document style affects learnability, and whether the dataset can serve as a benchmark for machine unlearning methods that must address both verbatim and distributional memorization.
