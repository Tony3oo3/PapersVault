## Problem

While the Less-is-More hypothesis shows that small, carefully curated datasets can match or exceed full-corpus fine-tuning, identifying the right subset—simultaneously difficult and diverse for a *specific* target model—remains an open challenge. Existing automated selection methods either require full-corpus annotation, rely on human curation, or fail to jointly optimize model-specific difficulty and semantic diversity, making them inflexible across different LLMs.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a small seed dataset (~19K questions with binary correctness labels from 23 open-source LLMs) and a large unannotated question corpus (e.g., 225K math problems), producing a compact subset of exactly k questions (default k=1,000) tailored to the target model's weaknesses. The seed set requires only binary correct/incorrect labels rather than full reasoning annotations, and the k-greedy selection computes only O(k·|D|) pairwise similarities on the fly, avoiding the O(|D|²) memory cost of a full similarity matrix. For unannotated corpora, DDCF acts as a front-end filter that concentrates expensive annotation (e.g., distillation via a teacher model) on only the selected k examples, reducing annotation cost by 100–200×.

## Method

DDCF recasts model-question interactions as a collaborative filtering problem, treating models as users and questions as items. A correctness predictor—comprising a model encoder, a question encoder (sentence transformer + residual MLP), and a linear classifier over the Hadamard product of embeddings—is trained on the seed correctness matrix to predict whether any model answers any question correctly, generalizing to unseen questions via the learned latent space. Difficulty is personalized per model (lower predicted correctness = harder), while diversity is measured by cosine similarity of sentence-transformer embeddings. The selection is formulated as an NP-hard combinatorial optimization balancing these two criteria via a weight λ, and solved with a k-greedy heuristic that iteratively adds the question minimizing a composite score of its difficulty and maximum similarity to already-selected items. This yields model-specific subsets that target each LLM's unique weaknesses while maintaining broad semantic coverage, with λ adjustable on the fly to shift the difficulty-diversity trade-off without retraining.

## Results

On Qwen2.5-Math-7B, DDCF selects 1,000 examples achieving 70.2% average in-distribution accuracy (vs. 75.8% full-data, 69.3% best baseline Perplexity) and 65.4% out-of-distribution accuracy (vs. 66.1% full-data). On Qwen3-8B-Base, DDCF reaches 85.0% ID and 80.5% OOD, matching or exceeding full-data fine-tuning (87.0% ID, 80.4% OOD) with only 0.45% of the training data. The largest gains appear on the hardest benchmark AIME24, where DDCF scores 49.0 (7B) and 82.2 (8B), outperforming random sampling by +10.4 and +1.3 points respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DDCF outperforms all seven baselines on both in-distribution and out-of-distribution averages for both Qwen2.5-Math-7B and Qwen3-8B-Base. Against Perplexity (the strongest annotation-dependent baseline), DDCF gains +0.9 ID and +0.3 OOD on the 7B model, and +1.4 ID and +2.2 OOD on the 8B model. Against Cartography, DDCF leads by +1.6 ID and +2.2 OOD (7B) and +0.3 ID and +2.2 OOD (8B). Against the annotation-free DiSF, DDCF improves by +1.6 ID and +3.0 OOD (7B) and +2.1 ID and +0.6 OOD (8B). The manually curated LIMO and s1.1-1K datasets trail DDCF by 2.8 and 1.7 ID points respectively on the 7B model, and by 1.6 and 4.3 ID points on the 8B model. Notably, DDCF also exceeds full-data fine-tuning on OOD for the 8B model (80.5 vs. 80.4), suggesting that model-aware difficulty-diversity selection can preserve or enhance generalization beyond what larger datasets provide.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper recasts the model-question interaction as a collaborative filtering (recommendation) problem, borrowing the matrix-factorization paradigm from Lee & Seung (2000) and He et al. (2017), and applies it to data selection for LLM fine-tuning. It departs from prior difficulty-only selectors (Perplexity, Cartography, S2L) and diversity-only selectors (DiSF) by jointly optimizing both criteria in a single combinatorial objective, and from gradient-matching methods (LESS, Grad-Match) by requiring only a small seed dataset rather than full-corpus annotation or a target set. The LLM-routing literature (RouteLLM, MetaLLM) provides the closest conceptual ancestor, but DDCF inverts the problem: instead of routing a question to the best model, it selects the best questions for a given model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 100–200× reduction in annotation cost makes high-quality fine-tuning feasible in settings where full-corpus labeling is prohibitively expensive, and the model-specific selection (different subsets for different backbones) addresses a gap that model-agnostic methods like DiSF or S2L cannot fill. A notable open question is the Small Model Learnability Gap: weaker models (e.g., 1.5B) initially degrade when fine-tuned on very small curated sets, requiring ~8K examples before recovery, which limits DDCF's applicability to the smallest architectures. The framework is also currently validated primarily on math reasoning; whether the collaborative-filtering signal generalizes to open-ended generation, code, or multilingual tasks remains to be tested.
