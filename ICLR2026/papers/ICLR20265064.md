## Problem

LLM-based reward models and judges perform well in English but degrade significantly on non-English inputs, and it remains unclear what training strategies (data selection, curriculum, reasoning language) are effective for building multilingual reward models. Existing multilingual efforts cover at most 13 languages, support only pairwise evaluation, and do not systematically study how instruction, rubric, response, and reasoning languages interact during training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20265064/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a structured prompt containing a task instruction, an input instance (question, conversation, or problem), one or more candidate responses, and an evaluation rubric — all of which can be in English or the target language. It produces a JSON-structured output with a free-form reasoning trace, a concise explanation, and a score whose format depends on the task: an integer label (1–7) for point-wise, a choice between 'Assistant A' or 'Assistant B' for pair-wise, or a boolean for binary evaluation. The rubric is customizable per task and can be human-crafted or LLM-generated, making the model rubric-agnostic.

## Method

MR3 is trained via supervised fine-tuning on Qwen3 (4B/8B/14B) using a 100K-example dataset spanning 72 languages, constructed by distilling reasoning outputs from GPT-OSS-120B under three aligned strategies (English instruction + English reasoning, target-language instruction + English reasoning, target-language instruction + target-language reasoning) and retaining only samples where all three strategies agree. Difficulty filtering removes examples that GPT-OSS-20B solves consistently across five trials, and an easy-to-hard curriculum ordering (by correctness then token length) is used during training. The key finding is that while English remains the strongest prompting and reasoning language in absolute terms, targeted multilingual SFT yields the largest relative gains for target-language reasoning, enabling interpretable evaluations even in extremely low-resource languages entirely unseen during training. The trained reward model is then used for off-policy DPO alignment of a 30B policy model, demonstrating end-to-end utility in preference optimization.

## Results

MR3-QWEN3-14B achieves an average pairwise accuracy of 85.04% across four benchmarks (m-RewardBench 89.18%, RewardBench 90.79%, MM-Eval 86.05%, IndoPref 74.14%), surpassing its teacher model GPT-OSS-120B by +0.91 points on average while being 9× smaller. On non-pairwise benchmarks, MR3-QWEN3-14B reaches 70.61% on INCLUDE-base-44, 94.00% on MGSM, and 90.19% F1 on RTP-LX. In DPO-based policy alignment, using MR3-QWEN3-14B as the reward model improves QWEN3-30B-A3B's m-ArenaHard-v2.0 winrate from 39.1% to 45.2% (23 languages) and INCLUDE-base-44 accuracy from 64.96% to 68.75%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MR3-QWEN3-14B outperforms GPT-OSS-120B (its own teacher) on all four pairwise benchmarks by margins of +0.13 to +1.99 points, despite being 9× smaller. Against NEMOTRON-MULTILINGUAL-49B, the strongest prior multilingual reward model, MR3 gains +4.21 points on average, with the largest gap on MM-Eval (+9.78) and IndoPref (+5.74), indicating substantially better multilingual generalization. MR3 also surpasses R3-QWEN3-14B-LoRA-4k (English-only training) on all multilingual benchmarks (m-RewardBench +1.11, MM-Eval +2.01, IndoPref +1.49) while trailing by only −0.21 on English-only RewardBench, confirming that multilingual supervision narrows the English–non-English gap. Compared to M-PROMETHEUS-14B, MR3 leads by +9.67 points on m-RewardBench and +25.98 on IndoPref, demonstrating the benefit of broader language coverage (72 vs. 6 languages) and more diverse task types. On non-pairwise benchmarks (Table 3), MR3-QWEN3-14B slightly trails GPT-OSS-120B on INCLUDE (70.61 vs. 71.65) and MGSM (94.00 vs. 94.71) but matches or exceeds it on RTP-LX safety (90.19 vs. 91.32 F1), while consistently outperforming R3 counterparts by 1–11 points.

## Relation to Prior Work

MR3 directly extends R3 (Anugraha et al., 2025), a rubric-agnostic reward reasoning model limited to English, into a massively multilingual setting covering 72 languages. It also builds on the multilingual reward model line initiated by M-Prometheus (6 languages) and Nemotron-Multilingual (13 languages), but departs from them by supporting all three evaluation formats (point-wise, pair-wise, binary) and by systematically studying how instruction/rubric language and reasoning language interact during training. The dataset construction pipeline—distilling reasoning traces from GPT-OSS-120B under three language strategies (ENG-ENG, TGT-ENG, TGT-TGT) and filtering by GPT-OSS-20B difficulty—repurposes standard distillation and curriculum-learning ideas in a new multilingual reward-modeling context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 72-language coverage and open-source release of models, data, and code make this the most accessible multilingual reward modeling resource to date, and the systematic ablation over language strategies gives practitioners concrete guidance on when to prompt or reason in English versus the target language. A key open question is whether the persistent English advantage in absolute performance can be closed without large-scale target-language pre-training, and whether the approach generalizes beyond the Qwen3 family. The paper also leaves iterative rubric refinement unexplored, and the human evaluation (20 annotators, 12 languages) provides only a preliminary validation of reasoning quality in extremely low-resource settings.
