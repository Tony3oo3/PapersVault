## Problem

Traditional multimodal representation learning relies on expensive paired datasets (e.g., image-caption pairs) to align modalities into a shared space, yet vast unpaired corpora from each modality exist independently. The paper asks whether unpaired data from an auxiliary modality can directly enhance representation learning in a target modality without any explicit correspondences, and under what conditions this is theoretically guaranteed to help.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262511/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes pre-extracted embeddings from frozen pretrained encoders (e.g., ViT-S/14 DINOv2 for images, OpenLLaMA-3B for text, AudioCLIP for audio), which are projected into a shared embedding space via modality-specific linear layers. In self-supervised mode, the shared transformer produces per-token/patch representations that are decoded back to the original modality dimension for next-token or patch prediction; in supervised mode, a shared classifier head outputs class labels. At inference, only the target modality's embedding is retained and used for linear probing on downstream tasks.

## Method

UML (Unpaired Multimodal Learner) applies a single shared transformer backbone to inputs from multiple modalities, with modality-specific encoders projecting raw features into a common embedding space and modality-specific decoders (self-supervised) or a shared classifier head (supervised) providing the training signal. The key mechanism is pure weight sharing: gradients from all modalities accumulate on the same transformer parameters, which the paper shows is an operational analogue of Fisher-information additivity on shared latent factors. Under a linear data-generating model, the paper proves (Theorems 1–3) that adding unpaired samples from an auxiliary modality strictly increases Fisher information along shared directions, and in some regimes a single auxiliary sample provides more information than an additional sample from the target modality itself. No surrogate alignment objectives, inferred correspondences, or paired data are required; the approach also extends naturally to three or more modalities and to transfer learning (e.g., initializing a ViT with pretrained BERT weights).

## Results

In the supervised setting (Table 2), UML with ViT-S/14 DINOv2 + OpenLLaMA-3B achieves 83.99% average top-1 accuracy on full fine-tuning across 10 image classification benchmarks, versus 81.54% for the unimodal baseline. Gains are most pronounced in few-shot linear probing: at k=1, UML reaches 51.36% average accuracy vs. 45.52% unimodal (+5.84 points), and at k=4, 68.53% vs. 65.84%. In the self-supervised setting (Table 1), UML outperforms the unimodal baseline on all MultiBench and vision-text benchmarks, with the largest gain on MUSTARD (63.28% vs. 59.66%). The paper also quantifies a marginal rate of substitution: 1 image ≈ 228 words with aligned CLIP encoders, and 1 image ≈ 1034 words with non-aligned DINOv2/OpenLLaMA encoders.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is UML against a unimodal baseline (same encoder, trained only on the target modality without auxiliary data). UML wins on 9 out of 10 datasets in full fine-tuning (the sole exception being SUN397, where it drops 0.17 points), with the largest gains on fine-grained categories: Stanford Cars (+6.94) and FGVC Aircraft (+6.45). In few-shot linear probing, UML wins on all 10 datasets across every shot setting (k=1, 2, 4), with the advantage growing as the number of shots decreases (e.g., +5.84 average at k=1 vs. +2.69 at k=4). In the self-supervised MultiBench setting, UML outperforms the unimodal baseline on all 8 benchmarks, with the largest margin on MUSTARD (+3.62), where text captures sarcasm cues absent from images. The paper also shows that initializing a ViT with pretrained BERT weights outperforms training from scratch on ImageNet, confirming cross-modal transfer without co-training. Notably, the paper does not compare against other multimodal methods (e.g., CLIP, ImageBind) in the main tables; the comparison is strictly UML vs. its own unimodal ablation, isolating the effect of adding unpaired auxiliary data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the theoretical framework of unpaired multi-domain causal representation learning (Sturma et al., 2023; Timilsina et al., 2024), which established conditions for recovering shared latent structure from unpaired multimodal mixtures, by deriving explicit Fisher information guarantees showing when unpaired auxiliary data strictly improves unimodal estimator variance. It departs from prior multimodal representation learning (CLIP, FLAVA, ImageBind) that requires paired (x,y) correspondences, and from recent unpaired approaches (Lin et al., 2023; Roth et al., 2023) that operate within pre-aligned spaces like CLIP, by studying the most general setting: unpaired modalities with unaligned encoders. The UML framework's simplicity—pure weight sharing without surrogate objectives, inferred alignments, or distribution matching—contrasts with the more complex alignment mechanisms in prior unpaired multimodal work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is significant: abundant unpaired data (independent image collections, text corpora, audio recordings) can enhance unimodal representations without the expensive curation of aligned pairs, which is particularly relevant for domains like medical imaging and robotics where auxiliary modalities exist but are rarely paired per-instance. The quantified exchange rate (1 image ≈ 228 words with CLIP, ≈ 1034 words with DINOv2) provides a concrete resource-allocation tool for practitioners. Key limitations include the restriction to classification tasks, the linear-theory assumptions that may not capture nonlinear regime behaviors, and the acknowledged but unaddressed risk of gradient interference and modality collapse in unpaired settings. Open questions include whether benefits extend to textual tasks, systematic study of missing-modality patterns, and characterizing the boundary conditions where unpaired multimodal training fails.
