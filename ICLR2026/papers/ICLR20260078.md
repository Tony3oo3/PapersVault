## Problem

Deploying ML systems with only a handful of real samples makes operational metrics (alert rates, mean scores) highly unstable, yet existing uncertainty quantification methods target the wrong quantity: frequentist intervals ignore the deployed predictive rule, Bayesian posteriors assume continual refitting, and conformal prediction offers per-example rather than long-run guarantees. The paper asks how to produce calibrated, time-consistent uncertainty intervals for the long-run metric under a frozen, deployed forecasting rule when only a few dozen real observations are available.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a small stream of real observations Y_{1:n}, a frozen pretrained generator Q_φ (any architecture with a fixed sampler: flows, diffusion, autoregressive LMs, GANs), and a bounded score function h: Y → R (e.g., NLL per token, CLIP rarity, alert indicator). It produces a shrinkage point estimate θ(P_n) and a calibrated predictive interval for the operational long-run limit θ_∞ = lim_{i→∞} ∫h dP_i under the deployed blend rule, plus an optional retraining trigger. For linear-in-F metrics, a Dirichlet-mean shortcut yields exact MP draws without forward simulation, reducing compute to a single Dirichlet draw per replicate.

## Method

The core contribution is a prequential forecasting rule P_i = (1−λ_i)F̂_i + λ_i Q_φ with the Dirichlet schedule λ_i = α/(i+α), which the paper proves is the unique predictable affine blend ensuring scalar prequential coherence — i.e., θ(P_i) = ∫h dP_i is a martingale for any bounded score h, guaranteeing almost-sure and L² convergence to θ_∞. The single hyperparameter α is set by a small-n minimax criterion (α* = σ²/Δ², independent of n) that trades off empirical sampling variance against model-data mismatch on the target functional, with a data-driven plug-in using empirical-Bernstein padding. Uncertainty is quantified via the martingale posterior: simulate future one-step forecasts under the deployed rule, track only running score values, and use long-horizon values as calibrated draws from Law(θ_∞ | F_n). A Freedman-type finite-time deviation bound controls the simulation horizon and provides a drift diagnostic, while the same minimax machinery yields a worst-case-safe retraining trigger comparing certified per-use loss reduction to retraining cost.

## Results

On GPT-2 (WikiText-2), DWS (Dirichlet-weighted shrinkage) achieves 90% predictive coverage at n=20, while Nonparametric Bootstrap (NPB) reaches only 50%, Bayesian Bootstrap (BB) 40%, and Jackknife (JK) 40%. On SVHN (OOD), DWS attains 100% coverage at n≤20 whereas all baselines under-cover severely (NPB/BB at 30–50%, JK at 40–50%). On CIFAR-10 (ID), DWS maintains near-nominal coverage (96.7–100%) at small n. Across all datasets, DWS interval widths shrink monotonically with n (e.g., from 1.259 at n=5 to 0.245 at n=100 on WikiText-2), reflecting the increasing effective sample size n+α.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three classic resampling baselines: Nonparametric Bootstrap (NPB, Efron 1979), Bayesian Bootstrap (BB, Rubin 1981), and Jackknife (JK, Quenouille 1949). DWS decisively outperforms all three in the small-n regime (n≤50) across every dataset. On GPT-2 (WikiText-2) at n=20, DWS achieves 90% coverage versus 50% (NPB), 40% (BB), and 40% (JK); on SVHN (OOD) at n=5, DWS reaches 100% while NPB and BB both sit at 30% and JK at 50%. On CIFAR-10 (ID) at n=5, DWS attains 100% coverage compared to 53.3% (NPB), 56.7% (BB), and 76.7% (JK). At larger n (≥100), the gap narrows: on WikiText-2 all methods converge to ~90% coverage, and on CIFAR-10 BB and NPB also reach 100%, though DWS retains a principled minimax guarantee. The key differentiator is that DWS targets the operational long-run metric under the deployed prequential rule (θ_∞), whereas the baselines resample the empirical distribution and ignore the frozen generator, causing systematic under-coverage when n is too small for the empirical law to be stable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of prequential (forecast-first) statistics (Dawid, 1984; Fortini & Petrone, 2025), Dirichlet-Pólya predictive updates (Blackwell & MacQueen, 1973; Ferguson, 1973), and martingale posterior inference (Fong et al., 2023). It departs from standard UQ by targeting the long-run operational metric θ_∞ under a deployed prequential rule rather than population parameters, and from prior small-data stabilization work (back-translation, diffusion augmentation, GAN synthesis) by providing a mathematically unique blend schedule with finite-time guarantees. The key conceptual departure is proving that the Dirichlet pseudo-count schedule is the *only* predictable affine blend yielding scalar-coherent (martingale) forecasts, and then connecting this to a minimax choice of the pseudo-count α and a retraining decision rule.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a lightweight, likelihood-free UQ tool specifically calibrated for the small-n deployment regime where bootstrap and conformal methods under-cover, with a single interpretable hyperparameter (α) set by a data-driven minimax criterion. The retraining trigger (Section 7) translates the same machinery into a stakeholder-friendly cost-benefit decision, which is practically valuable for operational ML teams. Limitations include: the Dirichlet-mean shortcut applies only to linear-in-F metrics (non-linear objectives require full MP resampling), the framework assumes a frozen generator (no online parameter updates), and the uncertainty is for the surrogate θ_∞ under the deployed rule rather than for θ(F*) itself. Open questions include extension to multi-step retraining cascades, interaction with online learning, and whether the minimax α generalizes beyond the bounded-score setting.
