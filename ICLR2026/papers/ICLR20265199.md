## Problem

While high-dimensional scaling limits for vanilla online SGD are well understood, the behavior of popular variants—SGD with Polyak momentum and SGD with adaptive step-sizes—remains largely uncharacterized in the critical scaling regime where dimension grows and step-size vanishes. In particular, it was unclear whether momentum amplifies or mitigates the emergent 'population corrector' that distinguishes high-dimensional dynamics from the classical gradient flow, and whether simple preconditioners like gradient normalization can rigorously stabilize training where vanilla SGD fails.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes an i.i.d. data stream (one sample per iteration), a loss function L_n, an initial parameter vector x_0 drawn from μ_n, a step-size δ_n = c_δ/n at critical scaling, and a momentum parameter β ∈ [0,1). Rather than tracking the full p_n-dimensional parameter vector, the method outputs the weak limit of low-dimensional summary statistics (e.g., alignment m = ⟨x,v⟩ and radius r² = ‖x‖² − m²) as a continuous-time SDE or ODE on ℝ^k. The output is thus a tractable deterministic or stochastic differential equation whose fixed points and stability properties characterize the algorithm's long-run behavior in the n→∞ limit.

## Method

The paper extends the effective dynamics framework of Ben Arous et al. (2024) by introducing δ_n-localizability and asymptotic closability conditions tailored to momentum, where the momentum recurrence p_ℓ = βp_{ℓ−1} − δ_n∇L_n(x_{ℓ−1}, y_ℓ) generates a geometrically weighted history of past gradients. The key result (Theorem 2.3) shows that the limiting SDE for summary statistics has drift h(β, u_t) = −[1/(1−β)]f(u_t) + [1/(1−β)²]g(u_t) and volatility scaled by 1/(1−β), revealing that momentum amplifies the population corrector g relative to the signal f as β→1. An equivalence theorem then shows SGD-M with step-size δ_n replicates online SGD with step-size δ_n/(1−β) after a time rescaling t↦t/(1−β). For adaptive step-sizes, the paper proves that a scalar Markovian preconditioner η_n(x,y) can be absorbed into the gradient to form η_n∇L_n, reducing the problem to the same framework with modified drift and volatility. On spiked tensor PCA and single index models, this yields explicit ODEs showing that gradient-normalized SGD (SGD-U) admits fixed points closer to the population minimum and supports step-sizes orders of magnitude larger than SGD-M before diverging, providing a rigorous account of why early preconditioners stabilize high-dimensional training.

## Results

For Spiked Tensor PCA (k=2), SGD-U achieves a strictly lower critical signal-to-noise ratio λ_crit^U(2,c_δ) than SGD-M's λ_crit^M(2,β,c_δ) for all β≥0, meaning SGD-U recovers the spike direction at SNR values where both SGD-M and online SGD fail. For Single Index Models with monomial link f(x)=x^k, SGD-M requires c_δ < (1−β)/k² · (2k−3)!!/(4k−5)!! for convergence to the population optimum (1,0), a bound that decays super-exponentially (order k^{−k−2}) in the polynomial degree. In contrast, SGD-U admits a universal fixed point at (1, c_δ²π/8) for every strictly increasing link function regardless of degree, with a threshold of 4/√(10π) ≈ 0.71 for even-powered monomials. In simulations (Figure 2), online SGD requires c_δ to be 1–2 orders of magnitude smaller than SGD-U to avoid exploding gradients, and for a degree-7 polynomial SGD fails to progress within one million iterations while SGD-U recovers the true direction at the same step-size.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three algorithms—online SGD (β=0), SGD-M with Polyak momentum, and SGD-U with unit-norm gradient normalization—on two canonical high-dimensional inference problems. On Spiked Tensor PCA, SGD-U wins by having a lower critical λ, enabling convergence in a regime where both SGD-M and online SGD remain subcritical; however, for sufficiently large λ relative to (1−β)²/8, SGD-M can also reach desirable fixed points. On Single Index Models, SGD-U decisively outperforms SGD-M: the admissible step-size for SGD-M shrinks super-exponentially with the link function's degree (e.g., c_δ ≤ 10^{−10} for a degree-7 polynomial), while SGD-U maintains a stable fixed point at m=1 for any degree with a universal threshold of ≈0.71. The paper also establishes that SGD-M and online SGD are dynamically equivalent after a time rescaling by (1−β)^{−1}, but at the same step-size SGD-M amplifies the high-dimensional 'population corrector' term, potentially degrading performance relative to online SGD. No baseline is beaten in a traditional benchmark sense; rather, the comparisons are theoretical characterizations of convergence regions and fixed-point quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the effective dynamics framework of Ben Arous et al. (2024), which established high-dimensional scaling limits for online SGD, to two important variants: SGD with Polyak momentum (SGD-M) and SGD with scalar adaptive step-sizes (SGD-U). In the fixed-dimensional setting, continuous-time analyses of momentum (Su et al., 2016; Kovachki & Stuart, 2021) and adaptive methods (Reddi et al., 2019; Malladi et al., 2022) are well-developed, while in the high-dimensional regime, Paquette & Paquette (2021) and Ferbach et al. (2025) studied momentum for specific models. The key departure here is showing that in the critical step-size regime, momentum *amplifies* the emergent population corrector rather than merely rescaling time, and that gradient normalization (originally proposed by Mikolov, 2012; Pascanu et al., 2013 for exploding gradients) can be rigorously shown to widen the admissible step-size range and improve fixed-point quality.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a rigorous theoretical account of why early preconditioners like gradient normalization stabilize training in high-dimensional settings where plain SGD or SGD-M fail, connecting empirical observations (exploding/vanishing gradients) to the population corrector mechanism in the effective dynamics. The universality result for SGD-U (Proposition 3.9), showing convergence to a fixed point near the optimum regardless of link function degree, offers a principled justification for why normalized gradients are robust across problem classes. Open questions include extending the framework to matrix-based preconditioners (Adam, AdaGrad), more general non-scalar preconditioners, and broader classes of statistical problems beyond the two canonical examples studied here.
