## Problem

Catalysis science lacks a specialized multi-task benchmark that reflects the full catalyst design workflow, where high-precision numerical regression, categorical decision-making, and open-ended mechanistic reasoning coexist within a single process. Existing chemistry and materials benchmarks either emphasize theoretical molecular-level understanding or constrained problem-solving and rarely integrate complementary knowledge from experimental literature, leaving LLM development in catalysis fragmented and inconsistently evaluated.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261061/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark accepts heterogeneous inputs ranging from SMILES strings and catalyst compositions to crystal plane/adsorption site descriptors and free-form scientific literature text describing synthesis and regulation schemes. Outputs span four qualitatively different modalities: discrete category labels (regulation method classification), continuous numerical values (adsorption energy, d-band center, formation energy, Faradaic efficiency in eV or %), extracted material/synthesis text, and open-ended explanatory text. A key design choice is that structured chemical descriptors (atomic coordination tables, facet specifications) are preferred over bare SMILES strings, as ablation shows structured input enables the model to learn internal atom connectivity and structure-activity relationships that a single SMILES sequence cannot convey.

## Method

The core contribution is a Multi-head Full-task (MFT) fine-tuning strategy applied to ChemLLM-7B via LoRA (rank 8, scale 16). A task-type token τ appended to the input sequence routes the final hidden representation h[t_{n-1}] to one of three decoupled output heads: an MLP classification head (cross-entropy loss), a regression head (MSE loss), or the original lm_head for text generation. All three heads are trained jointly across all 8 CatalystBench tasks with a weighted composite loss that balances the disparate loss scales (MSE for regression is orders of magnitude larger than cross-entropy for classification). This architecture is contrasted against Single-Task (ST), Full-Task with a unified generative head (FT), and Multi-head Single-Task (MST) baselines; MFT achieves a 12.44% average improvement over ST, outperforming FT (9.24%) and MST (5.13%), demonstrating that shared-backbone multi-task training captures cross-task domain synergies while decoupled heads prevent loss-landscape interference between heterogeneous objectives.

## Results

CatalystLLM achieves the best performance across all 7 tasks and 12 metrics on CatalystBench-test. On numerical regression tasks, it reaches R²=0.73 (FEP), R²=0.81 (AP), R²=0.73 (d-CP), and R²=0.80 (FP), substantially outperforming the next-best closed-source model GPT-4.1 (R²=0.56, 0.61, 0.59, 0.65 respectively). On classification and extraction tasks, it attains ACC=0.98 (ME), ACC=0.89 (SE), and ACC=0.81 (RMC). On the open-ended semantic comprehension task (RSC), CatalystLLM scores 0.79 STS and 0.75 expert score, the highest among all evaluated models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CatalystLLM outperforms all 17 baselines on the majority of metrics. Against the strongest closed-source model GPT-4.1, CatalystLLM wins decisively on all four regression tasks (e.g., AP R²: 0.81 vs. 0.61; FEP R²: 0.73 vs. 0.56) and on RMC classification (ACC 0.81 vs. 0.75), but GPT-4.1 edges ahead on SE (ACC 0.91 vs. 0.89) and ties on ME (both 0.98). Against the domain-specific baselines ChemLLM and Darwin1.5, CatalystLLM shows large gains on regression (AP R²: 0.81 vs. 0.63/0.59) and classification (RMC ACC: 0.81 vs. 0.52/0.50), confirming the benefit of multi-task catalytic fine-tuning over single-domain instruction tuning. Compared to general open-source 7B/8B models (LLaMA2, Qwen3, Mistral, ChatGLM), CatalystLLM leads by 10–50+ points on R² for regression tasks. On the open-ended RSC task (Table 2), CatalystLLM achieves the highest STS score (0.79) and expert score (0.75), surpassing DeepSeek-V3 (0.73 STS, 0.57 expert) and GPT-4.1 (0.72 STS, 0.56 expert), though GPT-4.1 and DeepSeek-V3 score slightly higher on the LLM-judged criteria (gpt-4o: 0.85/0.84 vs. 0.82; deepseek-r1: 0.88/0.86 vs. 0.86). Against traditional ML baselines (Table 6), CatalystLLM achieves R²=0.73 on d-CP (best among all methods) and R²=0.81 on AP (close to GAP-CatBERTa's 0.86), while using only text input rather than 3D atomic coordinates.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on ChemLLM-7B as its base model, extending the domain-specific LLM line (ChemLLM, Darwin 1.5, Llasmol) by introducing a Multi-head Full-task (MFT) fine-tuning architecture that decouples classification, regression, and generation output heads on a shared backbone. Conceptually, it departs from prior chemistry/materials benchmarks (ChemBench, LLM4Mat-Bench, MaScQA) that evaluate isolated task types or homogeneous output formats, by constructing a unified multi-task benchmark that mirrors the sequential catalyst R&D workflow and integrates both DFT-derived theoretical data and curated experimental literature. The MFT strategy itself draws on multi-task learning with task-specific heads from broader ML literature, but this is the first systematic empirical validation of such an architecture in the catalysis domain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CatalystBench fills a concrete gap: no prior benchmark simultaneously covers the full catalyst design pipeline (extraction → classification → numerical prediction → mechanistic explanation) with both theoretical and experimental data, making it a practical evaluation tool for the catalysis-AI community. The MFT architecture offers a reusable recipe for handling heterogeneous output modalities in scientific LLMs, and the ablation showing that multi-task joint training contributes more (9.24%) than head decoupling alone (5.13%) is a useful design insight. Key limitations include the 7B parameter scale, the benchmark's heavy concentration on CO2 electroreduction (64.3% of reaction types), and reliance on GPT-4o for Q&A generation, which introduces potential source bias. Open questions include whether the MFT advantage persists at larger model scales, whether the benchmark generalizes beyond the Cu-centric experimental data, and how to integrate CatalystLLM into a true closed-loop discovery pipeline.
