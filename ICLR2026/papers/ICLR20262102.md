## Problem

Neural scaling laws are widely used for compute planning and ROI forecasting, yet their sensitivity to data quality interventions—deduplication, heuristic filtering, LLM-guided rewriting—remains poorly understood. Prior theoretical work links power-law exponents to data manifold dimensionality and Zipfian token distributions but predicts only exponents, leaving coefficients and the irreducible loss term unexplained. The unresolved Kaplan-vs-Chinchilla discrepancy in compute-optimal token-to-parameter ratios (21× vs 1×) further suggests that data quality may fundamentally reshape scaling behavior in ways existing theory does not capture.

## Input / Output

The study takes 23 systematically curated datasets from Common Crawl (QualityPajama), each applying a distinct intervention from a sequential filter pipeline (NSFW removal → aesthetic filtering → PageRank partitioning → fuzzy deduplication at four Jaccard thresholds → grammar/sentence-length filtering) or a synthetic curation strategy (HQ, QA, or TB rephrasing at 100/67/33% mixing ratios with natural data). LLaMA3-architecture models (100M–8B parameters) are trained on 100M–200B tokens per dataset, and performance is evaluated on 16 external non-code/math domains from The Pile plus a held-out CC validation set, enabling cross-domain ranking of interventions. The output is a full decomposition of the joint scaling law L(N,D) = A·N^(−α) + B·D^(−β) + E per dataset, from which compute-optimal design points, token-to-parameter ratios, and scale-dependent quality rankings are derived.

## Method

The core contribution is a large-scale empirical study training over 2,000 models across 23 datasets to measure how each data intervention perturbs all five scaling-law parameters simultaneously. The key finding is that text quality interventions shift both coefficients (A, B) and exponents (α, β) in conflicting directions—stronger filtering does not uniformly improve all components—creating a tension where exponent gains are offset by coefficient increases that raise loss at practical compute scales. Compute-optimal token-to-parameter ratios vary by up to 182× across interventions at the same compute budget, and data quality rankings cross over as scale changes, meaning no single intervention is universally optimal. In Appendix G, the authors develop a distance-dependent E-regularization scheme (pulling the irreducible loss toward an information-theoretic floor scaled by train-test JS divergence) that stabilizes scaling-law fits under distribution drift, reducing asymptotic-floor collapse from 59% to 3.3% and confirming that observed parameter shifts reflect genuine distributional effects rather than fitting artifacts.

## Results

Across 23 QualityPajama datasets and 2,000+ model training runs (100M–8B params, 100M–200B tokens), the paper shows that data quality interventions shift all five scaling-law parameters (α, β, A, B, E), not just exponents. At today's compute scale (~10²⁴ FLOPs), the compute-optimal token-to-parameter ratio varies by up to 182× across interventions, optimal model size by 14×, and optimal token count by 13×. Fuzzy deduplication at threshold 0.7 yields ~300× compute savings over no deduplication while reducing data volume by only ~17%. Spearman correlations for cross-validation-set ranking reliability are moderate (0.32–0.46) for heuristic filters but strong (0.76–0.91) for synthetically curated datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare a single proposed method against external baselines; instead it benchmarks 23 data quality interventions against each other using compute scaling laws as the evaluation lens. Among heuristic filters, fuzzy deduplication at Jaccard threshold 0.7 dominates: it requires ~3× less compute than dedup_0.9, ~10× less than exact deduplication, and ~300× less than the unfiltered baseline, while only reducing data volume to ~83%. PageRank filtering shows a counterintuitive result: while high_pr > med_pr > low_pr in quality ordering, the no_pr (pages absent from the PageRank table) variant actually outperforms the unfiltered baseline, likely due to recency effects. For synthetic data, mixing synthetic and natural data at intermediate ratios (e.g., 67/33) consistently outperforms 100% synthetic or 100% natural across all three rephrasing strategies (HQ, QA, TB), but the optimal mixing ratio shifts with compute scale. Critically, no single intervention remains optimal across all scales: the paper documents frequent crossovers in scaling curves, meaning a dataset that minimizes loss at 100M tokens may be outperformed by another at 10B tokens, and the 'best' strategy also depends on whether compute, model size, or data volume is the binding constraint.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the neural scaling law framework (Kaplan et al. 2020; Hoffmann et al. 2022) by treating data quality as a first-class variable that reshapes all components of the joint scaling law, rather than a fixed input. Prior theoretical work (data manifold theory, Zipfian distribution theory) predicts exponent behavior under distributional assumptions but remains silent on coefficients and irreducible loss, while prior empirical studies of data interventions were limited to NMT or image classification. The synthetic data techniques (WRAP rephrasing, Phi-style textbook generation) are adapted from prior pretraining data work, and the base corpus (RedPajama/Common Crawl) follows the standard open-data pipeline, but the systematic 23-dataset × 2000+ model experimental design is novel in scope.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that compute-optimal token-to-parameter ratios vary by up to 182× across data interventions at the same compute scale directly challenges the practice of treating data curation as a one-time preprocessing step independent of scaling strategy. The scale- and resource-constraint-dependence of data quality rankings means that small-scale proxy experiments can mislead practitioners about which filtering or mixing strategy to deploy at production scale. Open questions include: whether a theoretical account can predict which interventions shift exponents versus coefficients (current Zipfian and manifold theories fail to do so consistently), whether findings generalize beyond Common Crawl-derived English text and the LLaMA3 architecture family, and how to efficiently identify the optimal data strategy without training thousands of models. The paper focuses on upstream loss rather than downstream task performance, leaving the connection to capability metrics unresolved.
