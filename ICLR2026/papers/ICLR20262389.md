## Problem

Current MLLMs perform near-random on intuitive physics understanding, yet existing benchmarks (ContPhy, PhysBench) entangle physical reasoning with vision, language, and common-sense tasks, making it impossible to isolate whether failures stem from inadequate dynamic perception or other cognitive gaps. This paper disentangles the problem by introducing two low-level diagnostic tasks—Next Frame Selection (NFS) and Temporal Coherence Verification (TCV)—that specifically probe whether a model can perceive physical motion and change over time in continuum objects, and then proposes a targeted enhancement method to close the identified gap.

## Input / Output

For NFS, the model receives 5 RGB video frames (sampled at stride 2 or 4 from 5-second clips) plus 4 candidate images and must select the correct next frame; for TCV, it receives a sequence with a randomly inserted distractor frame and outputs a binary Yes/No coherence verdict. The SDF method additionally injects a velocity-to-color mapped image (blue-channel gradient encoding particle velocity magnitude) as a visual prompt at strategic positions in the frame sequence. Distractor candidates are generated by excluding temporally adjacent frames (buffer δ) and pruning those with SigLIP cosine similarity above a threshold τ to the ground-truth frame, ensuring semantically distinct but plausible alternatives.

## Method

SDF leverages the Flip Fluids Blender addon to simulate particle-based fluid dynamics, extracting per-particle velocity vectors at each timestep and projecting them into a camera-frame blue-channel gradient via a line-integral formulation (Eq. 6) that weights velocity magnitude with spatial attenuation. This clean simulator-derived velocity signal—unlike noisy optical flow reconstructed from real videos—serves as a perceptually calibrated visual prompt. The training strategy is a multi-task fine-tuning framework with three components: (1) Dynamic Perception, where the model identifies the correct SDF image among distractors given an RGB video; (2) SDF-guided CoT, a three-step reasoning chain (analyze dynamics → integrate SDF prompt → predict next frame) that uses the SDF as an explicit visual anchor; and (3) the original NFS/TCV tasks. Reasoning supervision combines expert-generated CoT (Gemini-2.5-Pro) with self-distilled data at a 1:10 ratio, and full-parameter SFT is performed via SWIFT at lr=1e-5 over 3 epochs. The key insight is that abstracting simulator physics into a simple velocity-to-color representation provides a scalable, architecture-agnostic bridge that teaches MLLMs to perceive underlying dynamics rather than memorize domain-specific patterns, yielding up to 20.7% NFS gains and strong transfer to cloth, sand, smoke, and plasticine.

## Results

On the proposed NFS benchmark (4-choice next-frame selection), the best open-source model Qwen2.5-VL achieves only 32.73% accuracy (stride 2), barely above the 25% random baseline, while the best closed-source model GPT-4o reaches 39.79%. On TCV (binary temporal coherence verification), open-source models cluster around 52–58%, only marginally above the 50% chance level. The proposed SDF method yields up to 20.7% NFS accuracy gains on GLM-4.1V and 14.40% on Qwen2-VL over zero-shot, and uniquely maintains improvements when transferred to unseen physical domains (cloth, smoke, sand) where standard fine-tuning collapses back to zero-shot levels.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks six open-source MLLMs (InternVL2.5, InternVL3, GLM-4.1V, mPLUG-Owl3, Qwen2-VL, Qwen2.5-VL) and two closed-source models (GPT-4o, Gemini-2.5-Flash) on the new NFS and TCV tasks. Qwen2.5-VL leads among open-source models on NFS (32.73%) and TCV (57.80%), while GPT-4o dominates closed-source on NFS (39.79% at stride 4) and TCV (70.10%). When SDF is applied via multi-task fine-tuning, it substantially outperforms both the zero-shot baseline and a matched-budget standard fine-tuning: on the fluid NFS task, SDF-Ours gains 14.40% over zero-shot on Qwen2-VL and 20.7% on GLM-4.1V, whereas standard fine-tuning provides negligible improvement. In transfer experiments to cloth, smoke, and sand, SDF-Ours retains meaningful gains (e.g., ~23.64% vs. 22.42% zero-shot on cloth for Qwen2-VL), while standard fine-tuning performs nearly identically to zero-shot, confirming that SDF captures transferable physical dynamics rather than domain-specific patterns. CoT prompting and thinking modes offer modest improvements (e.g., GLM-4.1V+Thinking gains +11.77 on NFS and +25.06 on TCV) but still fall well short of SDF's gains, and model scaling from 3B to 72B (Qwen2-VL) yields only ~4% NFS improvement, underscoring that architecture size alone cannot close the physics perception gap.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from existing physics-reasoning benchmarks (ContPhy, PhysBench) that evaluate high-level physical reasoning through tasks entangling vision, language, logic, and common sense, arguing they cannot isolate the foundational perceptual capability of intuitive physics understanding. Drawing on curriculum learning principles, the authors introduce NFS and TCV as low-level diagnostic tasks focused specifically on continuum-object dynamics, filling what they identify as a gap in the evaluation landscape. Methodologically, SDF repurposes physics simulators (Blender/Flip Fluids) as a data source for visual prompting, building on the 'think with images' paradigm and self-distillation literature, but applies them in a novel multi-task fine-tuning framework that combines dynamic perception, SDF-guided CoT, and the original benchmark tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, reproducible diagnostic for a capability gap that prior benchmarks could only measure indirectly, and the SDF approach offers a cost-effective path to injecting physics knowledge without architectural changes. However, the evaluation is limited to fluid/continuum physics (with only exploratory transfer to cloth, sand, smoke), and the transfer experiments use small datasets, leaving open whether SDF generalizes to rigid-body dynamics, thermodynamics, or optics. A key open question is whether the perceptual gains from SDF transfer to the higher-level physical reasoning tasks (counterfactual inference, causal prediction) that the paper explicitly positions as the next curriculum step.
