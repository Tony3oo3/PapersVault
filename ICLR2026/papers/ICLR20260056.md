## Problem

Cryo-EM atomic model refinement remains a major bottleneck: traditional tools like Phenix.real_space_refine and Rosetta are computationally expensive, require expert case-by-case parameter tuning, and manual tools like Coot are labor-intensive. Existing AI-based refinement methods (GNNRefine, AtomRefine) learn geometric priors from databases but lack direct, differentiable integration of experimental cryo-EM density maps during optimization, leaving a gap for neural network methods that refine structures under the explicit constraint of experimental data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260056/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a cryo-EM density map (3D volume with grid points and density values, resolutions ranging 1.8–5.9 Å) and an initial all-atom coordinate structure (typically an AlphaFold3 prediction) as input, and produces a refined all-atom coordinate structure as output. The density map's resolution and voxel size are used to parameterize the synthetic density generator, ensuring the simulated map matches the experimental map's physical scale. The framework handles both protein-only and DNA/RNA-protein complexes, though nucleic-acid-specific stereochemical restraints are not yet incorporated.

## Method

CryoNet.Refine reformulates AlphaFold3's multi-step stochastic diffusion into a one-step deterministic refinement using preconditioned parameterization (Karras et al., 2022), initialized from Boltz-2 weights with only the Diffusion Module trainable while the Atom Encoder, Sequence Embedder, and Pairformer remain frozen. The key architectural innovation is a fully differentiable, physics-based density generator that constructs Gaussian spheres at each atom position to produce a synthetic map, enabling a cosine-similarity density loss to be backpropagated through the network for the first time. This is combined with a suite of differentiable geometry losses (Ramachandran outlier, rotamer outlier, Cβ deviation, bond angle RMSD, and steric violation) derived from the MolProbity Top8000 reference distributions. Rather than a single inference pass, the method employs a test-time optimization (recycling) strategy: for each specific structure, the diffusion module's parameters are iteratively updated via gradient descent on the composite density + geometry loss over up to 300 cycles with early stopping, progressively fitting the output to the experimental map while maintaining stereochemical validity.

## Results

On a benchmark of 110 protein complexes (resolutions 1.8–5.9 Å), CryoNet.Refine achieves a mean CCmask of 0.59 and CCmc of 0.60, surpassing Phenix.real_space_refine (0.54 / 0.55) and AlphaFold3 (0.38 / 0.40). Geometrically, it reduces Angle RMSD to 0.36° (vs. 0.72° for Phenix and 1.58° for AlphaFold3), raises Ramachandran favored to 98.92%, and cuts rotamer outliers to 0.49% (vs. 1.15% for Phenix). On 10 DNA/RNA-protein complexes, CryoNet.Refine further extends its lead with CCmask 0.65 and CCsc 0.67, outperforming both baselines across all six correlation metrics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CryoNet.Refine is compared against two baselines: AlphaFold3 (the initial model predictor) and Phenix.real_space_refine (the standard cryo-EM refinement tool). Against AlphaFold3, CryoNet.Refine wins decisively on every metric—CCmask improves from 0.38 to 0.59 (+55%), Angle RMSD drops from 1.58° to 0.36°, and Ramachandran favored rises from 95.73% to 98.92%. Against Phenix.real_space_refine, CryoNet.Refine achieves consistent gains on all six model-map correlation coefficients (e.g., CCmask 0.59 vs. 0.54, CCmc 0.60 vs. 0.55) and on key geometric metrics: Angle RMSD is halved (0.36° vs. 0.72°), Ramachandran favored increases by ~2.5 percentage points (98.92% vs. 96.39%), and rotamer outliers are reduced by ~57% (0.49% vs. 1.15%). The one exception is Ramachandran outlier percentage, where Phenix achieves a slightly lower value (0.02% vs. 0.06%), though both are negligible. On the DNA/RNA-protein subset, CryoNet.Refine extends its advantage over Phenix with CCmask 0.65 vs. 0.57 and CCsc 0.67 vs. 0.58, confirming broad applicability beyond protein-only complexes.

## Relation to Prior Work

CryoNet.Refine repurposes the Boltz-2/AlphaFold3 diffusion architecture (initializing from Boltz-2 weights and reusing its Pairformer) but fundamentally redefines its role: instead of a multi-step stochastic generator from Gaussian noise, it becomes a one-step deterministic refiner initialized from an existing atomic model, using Karras et al.'s preconditioned parameterization. It departs from prior AI-based refinement methods (DeepAccNet, GNNRefine, AtomRefine) by being the first to integrate experimental cryo-EM density maps as a differentiable training signal during test-time optimization, and from traditional tools (Phenix, Rosetta, Coot) by replacing iterative simulated annealing with gradient-based neural network updates. The differentiable Gaussian-sphere density generator and the set of differentiable geometry losses (Ramachandran, rotamer, Cβ) are novel contributions not present in any prior refinement pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first fully automated, end-to-end deep learning alternative to labor-intensive cryo-EM refinement, with the differentiable density loss being broadly applicable to any AI-based model building or refinement task that needs to respect experimental maps. Key limitations include the per-structure test-time optimization cost (up to 300 recycles), the absence of nucleic-acid-specific stereochemical restraints, and the reliance on a physics-based (rather than learned) density simulator that may not capture experimental noise characteristics. Open questions include whether the approach scales to very low-resolution maps (<8 Å), whether the test-time optimization can be amortized across structures, and whether replacing the Gaussian density generator with a learned generative model would further improve the density loss signal.
