## Problem

Recent LLM agent designs for sequential decision-making (Reflexion, ICRL, ICPI) lack principled exploration strategies, relying instead on stochasticity in LLM outputs or generic self-reflection to implicitly emulate RL algorithms. Meanwhile, classic algorithms with provably-efficient exploration (e.g., PSRL) require technical machinery—parametric posteriors, conjugate updates, exact planning—that is difficult to operationalize in purely natural language environments. The paper asks whether LLMs can be used to explicitly implement a known, data-efficient RL algorithm rather than being coaxed into implicitly imitating one.

## Input / Output

The agent receives a natural language state description, a textual prior distribution over MDPs (e.g., 'Beta(1,1)' or 'Dirichlet(0.1,0.1,0.1)' specified in prose), and per-step reward/transition observations. It produces a natural language action, a textual posterior sample (a free-form hypothesis describing how transitions and rewards unfold, potentially using an environment proxy like a target word in Wordle as a sufficient statistic), and an updated textual posterior. The posterior is not a parametric distribution but a natural language summary that must explicitly communicate the degree of uncertainty, giving agent designers full expressivity over how prior knowledge is encoded.

## Method

The paper implements PSRL by decomposing it into three atomic LLM subroutines: (1) a posterior sampling LLM that draws one plausible MDP hypothesis from the current textual posterior, (2) an optimal sample policy LLM that selects actions maximizing value under that hypothesis, and (3) a posterior update LLM that reconciles the episode's full trajectory against the prior to produce an updated textual posterior. The epistemic state is held fixed within each episode and updated only at episode end (lazy posterior update), mirroring classic PSRL. No LLM is explicitly prompted to 'explore'; instead, the algorithmic structure of Thompson sampling—sampling a hypothesis then acting optimally—induces strategic exploration emergently. A key empirical finding is that LLM capability is critical: upgrading from GPT-4o to o1-mini transforms linear regret into sub-linear regret on stochastic environments, while the same upgrade has negligible or negative effects on baseline agents.

## Results

On a 5-armed Bernoulli bandit (T=100), LLM-based PSRL with κ_sampling=1.2 achieves lower cumulative regret than classic Thompson Sampling. On the RiverSwim environment (3 states, H=6), upgrading from GPT-4o to o1-mini transforms LLM-based PSRL from near-linear regret to sub-linear regret on par with vanilla tabular PSRL (40 trials vs. 1,000 for vanilla). In the combination lock task (K=8 episodes, 720 possible codes), uniform random selection has <0.14% success, while LLM-based PSRL most effectively identifies the correct code. In Wordle (K=6 episodes, H=5), LLM-based PSRL with GPT-4o outperforms all baselines even when those baselines are upgraded to DeepSeek-R1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares LLM-based PSRL against three LLM agent baselines (ICPI, ICRL, Reflexion) plus classic TS and vanilla tabular PSRL as algorithmic references. ICPI (Brooks et al., 2023) collapses to a random policy in both combination lock and Wordle because it never observes non-zero reward, making its ICL-based policy iteration inoperable without pre-collected data. ICRL (Monea et al., 2024) with p=1 performs poorly in Wordle (consistent with Klissarov et al., 2025) and degrades further when the keep probability p is decreased; in RiverSwim, ~60% of trials fall back to the suboptimal downstream reward. Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2024) fails in RiverSwim by settling for the small downstream reward after early upstream failures, and in Wordle/combination lock its self-reflections only become action-specific after uncertainty is largely resolved. Against vanilla PSRL on RiverSwim, LLM-based PSRL (o1-mini) matches sub-linear regret, while GPT-4o incurs near-linear regret. The key finding is that no baseline benefits as much from upgrading to a stronger reasoning model (DeepSeek-R1) as PSRL does, and even PSRL with the weaker GPT-4o beats all baselines running DeepSeek-R1 in Wordle.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in LLM agent design (Reflexion, ICRL, ICPI, ReAct), which composes LLMs to implicitly induce RL behavior through in-context learning or self-reflection, by instead explicitly implementing the well-studied PSRL algorithm (Strens, 2000; Osband et al., 2013) with LLMs serving as subroutines for posterior sampling, optimal policy execution, and posterior updating. The underlying exploration mechanism is Thompson Sampling (Thompson, 1933), whose regret guarantees in tabular MDPs and beyond are well-established in the Bayesian RL literature. The key conceptual reframe is that LLMs should implement existing RL algorithms rather than be coaxed (via finetuning or ICL) into implicitly imitating one, drawing on the Bayesian RL framework (Bellman & Kalaba, 1959; Duff, 2002) where the MDP is treated as a random variable with a prior.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that decades of RL theory on statistically-efficient exploration can be operationalized in natural language domains (Wordle, combination lock, customer service) where classic tabular or neural implementations are infeasible, and that the algorithmic structure of PSRL/TS provides exploration benefits that even strong reasoning models (DeepSeek-R1) cannot replicate through free-form LLM agent designs. Key limitations include failure to scale beyond small stochastic environments (RiverSwim length 4 causes linear regret), dependence on LLM capability for planning under stochastic dynamics, and the need for well-specified priors. Open questions include whether regularization methods for inaccurate transition models can fix stochastic planning failures, whether information-directed sampling (IDS) can be similarly implemented with LLMs, and whether the approach extends to larger, more complex natural language tasks as constituent LLMs improve.
