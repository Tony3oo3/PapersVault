## Problem

Long-horizon trajectory prediction for physical systems (molecules, proteins, human joints) is difficult because low-frequency global trends and high-frequency local oscillations become entangled across space and time, and their relative importance varies by system. Existing frequency-aware methods model temporal and spatial spectra in isolation rather than jointly, and do not adaptively prioritize which spectral modes matter most for stable long-term forecasting.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20265167/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, the method consumes only the initial state graph: 3D positions and velocities of N particles, the physical connectivity edges, and time-invariant node features (e.g., atomic types). It outputs a sequence of T future 3D position tensors. During training, the teacher encoder additionally receives the ground-truth future state sequence as privileged information, which is discarded at test time. The spatio-temporal graph is constructed by linking the spatial particle graph across T time steps via a 1D temporal chain, yielding NT joint frequency modes.

## Method

SGDD employs a teacher-student distillation scheme where a dynamics encoder (STSGNN) processes the full future trajectory to produce a spatio-temporal representation, while an initial encoder (GAT with T attention heads) produces a matching representation from the initial state alone. Both representations are refined by a spectral-guided enhancement module that projects them onto a truncated spatio-temporal joint basis (Kronecker product of spatial and temporal Laplacian eigenvectors), applies learnable per-mode frequency weights, and adds back the orthogonal residual. Dual-level alignment—representation-level MSE in the spatio-temporal domain plus coefficient-level MSE in the spectral domain—forces the student to mimic the teacher's frequency-aware encoding. Training follows a staged strategy: a pretraining phase with teacher forcing ratio 1.0 (decoder sees only the teacher representation), followed by joint training at ratio 0.5 where the decoder alternates between teacher and student representations, with gradients detached from the teacher to prevent distortion. The distilled student representation then serves as an inductive bias for a physical dynamics decoder (EGNO or GFNODE) at inference.

## Results

On the MD17 dataset (S2S, MSE ×10⁻²), SGDD-EGNO achieves state-of-the-art on 6 of 8 molecules, with the largest gain on Benzene (12.97 vs. EGNO's 48.85, a 73.4% improvement) and Aspirin (7.84 vs. 9.18, +14.5%). SGDD-GFNODE similarly leads on 6/8 molecules, with a 43.2% improvement on Benzene (2.74 vs. 4.82). On the CMU Motion Capture dataset (S2S), SGDD-EGNO reduces MSE from 8.1 to 6.7 (Walk) and 33.9 to 28.2 (Run) over EGNO, while SGDD-GFNODE achieves ~30% gains over GFNODE. On the protein (Adk) dataset with 855 nodes, SGDD-EGNO achieves MSE 1.75, a 21.5% improvement over EGNO (2.23).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SGDD-EGNO outperforms EGNO on 6 of 8 MD17 molecules in S2S (losing narrowly on Malonaldehyde by 1.4% and Uracil by 1.9%), with the most dramatic gain on Benzene (73.4% improvement, MSE 12.97 vs. 48.85). SGDD-GFNODE beats GFNODE on 6/8 molecules in S2S, with a 43.2% improvement on Benzene (2.74 vs. 4.82), but underperforms on Toluene (5.16 vs. 4.82, −7.1%). On the CMU Motion Capture dataset, both SGDD variants achieve state-of-the-art on all four S2S and S2T settings, with SGDD-GFNODE gaining ~30% over GFNODE (Walk: 6.5 vs. 9.3; Run: 31.5 vs. 44.9). On the protein dataset (855 nodes), SGDD-EGNO achieves 1.75 MSE, beating EGNO (2.23) by 21.5% and EGHNO (1.80) by 2.8%. In the S2T evaluation on MD17, SGDD-EGNO leads on 5/8 molecules while SGDD-GFNODE leads on only 3/8, with notable losses on Malonaldehyde (−16.1%) and Toluene (−28.8%) attributed to the authors' inability to fully reproduce the original GFNODE results. Overall, SGDD consistently surpasses all prior equivariant baselines (EGNN, SE(3)-Transformer, TFN, RF, MPNN) by large margins, confirming that the spectral-guided distillation provides a strong inductive bias for long-horizon prediction.

## Relation to Prior Work

SGDD sits at the intersection of three research threads: equivariant graph architectures for physical dynamics (EGNO, GFNODE, EGNN), knowledge distillation with privileged information (Vapnik & Vashist; Sun et al. 2022 for motion prediction), and graph knowledge distillation with frequency-aware decomposition (Wu et al. 2023b). The key conceptual departure from prior frequency-aware dynamics models (EGNO, GFNODE) is that they treat spatial and temporal spectral representations in isolation, whereas SGDD constructs a unified spatio-temporal joint basis via Kronecker products of spatial and temporal Laplacian eigenvectors, enabling joint spectral filtering. The privileged-information distillation mechanism—where a teacher encoder sees future trajectories and a student encoder sees only the initial state—adapts the LUPI paradigm to the physical dynamics setting, with the spectral-guided enhancement module providing a learnable frequency reweighting that prior distillation work in graphs has not applied to trajectory prediction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a practical recipe for long-horizon physical dynamics prediction: at inference, only the initial state is needed, yet the distilled representation carries frequency-aware inductive bias learned from privileged future data. Consistent gains across three scales (small molecules, 31-joint human skeletons, 855-node protein backbones) suggest the spectral-guided distillation principle is broadly applicable. However, the method is constrained by its reliance on existing equivariant decoders (EGNO, GFNODE) whose capacity bounds overall performance, and it assumes fixed spatio-temporal graph topology—extending to time-varying graphs or decoder-agnostic formulations remains open. The finding that K≈64 low-frequency modes suffice across all datasets provides a useful practical guideline but also hints that the spectral truncation may be overly conservative for systems with rich high-frequency dynamics.
