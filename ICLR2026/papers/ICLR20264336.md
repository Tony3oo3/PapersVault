## Problem

Solving multi-commodity flow (MCF) problems at scale remains a fundamental bottleneck: LP solvers scale poorly (O(d^2.37) worst-case) with millions of variables in modern networks, while existing ML-based methods (DRL, GNNs) inherit the curse of dimensionality, poor generalization to unseen topologies or demand shifts, and high engineering costs for retraining. No prior approach simultaneously achieves near-optimal allocation quality, orders-of-magnitude speedup over LP, and robustness to unforeseen events like link failures or demand bursts.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264336/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For each source node, PRAM renders a sub-graph image (240 dpi PNG via Matplotlib/NetworkX) showing all candidate paths from that source with link capacities labeled, which is fed through a CLIP vision encoder. A parallel text prompt encodes the commodity subset's recent demands, historical rolling averages, and task-specific instructions. The output is a vector of path-weight split ratios (Sigmoid-bounded for MLU) or allocated per-path demands (ReLU-bounded, then capacity-scaled for MTF/MCF) for every candidate path in that source's subset, reducing the per-agent decision space from O(|V|²) to O(|V|).

## Method

PRAM decomposes the MCF problem by source node into parallel subproblems, each solved by a shared multimodal language model agent (Qwen2.5-VL-7B, first 8 layers). The adaptation framework adds three lightweight components: LoRA adapters on the QV attention projections, learnable global context embeddings prepended to prompts and aligned to the frozen tokenizer via multi-head cross-attention (reprogramming), and a policy head that samples actions from a Gaussian. Fine-tuning uses counterfactual multi-agent policy gradients (COMA-style), where each agent's advantage is computed by simulating the global MCF objective with only that agent's action replaced by a Monte Carlo sample, exploiting the one-step nature of MCF (allocations do not affect future demands). Theoretically, the paper proves that MCF objectives are convex/concave in path weights (Theorem 1) and that a constant-depth/width transformer can internally simulate multi-step gradient descent via in-context learning (Theorem 2), establishing that the adapted MLM converges to the optimum.

## Results

On real-world topologies (Meta DB, Meta WEB, Abilene, CERNET, GÉANT), PRAM achieves the second-best average performance across all three MCF objectives (MLU, total flow, concurrent flow), ranking just behind LP with perfect future prediction, with an average gap of less than 8% from optimal. On the largest topology Kdl (754 nodes, 1,790 links, >2M path weights), PRAM completes each allocation in under 25 seconds — 5× faster than POP, 7× faster than LP-top, and 100× faster than LP — while maintaining >90% of LP's solution quality. Notably, for MLU minimization, PRAM even surpasses LP by ~21% on CERNET and ~45% on GÉANT. Under link failures and flow bursts (α=2), PRAM degrades by <10–15%, substantially outperforming all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PRAM consistently outperforms all six baselines on average across the three MCF objectives. Against the LP solver (Gurobi), PRAM achieves >90% of its near-optimal quality on large-scale topologies while being 100× faster; on real-world MLU minimization, PRAM actually beats LP by 21% (CERNET) and 45% (GÉANT), attributed to MLU's stronger convexity. Against partition-based POP, PRAM is 5× faster on Kdl and delivers higher solution quality, as POP's random demand splitting introduces a visible optimality gap. Against the LP-top heuristic, PRAM is 7× faster and more stable, since LP-top's focus on top-10% demands causes inconsistency on smaller flows. Against GNN-based HARP, PRAM improves MLU by 6.1%, total flow by 16.6%, and concurrent flow by 24.8% on average; HARP's recurrent architecture limits expressiveness on large topologies. Against Aether (multi-agent graph transformer), PRAM leads by 17.2% on MLU, 7.3% on total flow, and 13.5% on concurrent flow, as Aether's link-path graph fails to model large-scale topology correlations. Against DRL (PPO), PRAM substantially outperforms in solution quality, as DRL's small policy network lacks the expressiveness for complex MCF structures, though DRL has the fastest inference time.

## Relation to Prior Work

PRAM sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) classical MCF optimization via LP solvers and partition-based methods (POP, LP-top), (2) ML-based routing (DRL, GNN-based HARP/Aether), and (3) the emerging use of pretrained language models for structured reasoning tasks. It departs from prior ML-based MCF solvers by replacing task-specific architectures (GNNs, small policy networks) with an off-the-shelf multimodal language model (Qwen2.5-VL) adapted via LoRA and reprogramming-style context embeddings, and from prior partition-based methods by using the MLM's in-context reasoning rather than an LP solver in each subproblem. The theoretical analysis builds on results showing transformers can simulate gradient descent in-context (Ahn et al., 2023; Fu et al., 2023), while the multi-agent fine-tuning directly adopts COMA's counterfactual advantage computation (Foerster et al., 2018).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PRAM offers a practically compelling 10–100× speedup over LP solvers for large-scale MCF while maintaining near-optimal quality (<8% gap), which is significant for production network operators who must re-optimize flows every few minutes. The theoretical guarantee that an adapted MLM internally approximates gradient descent on convex MCF objectives provides a rare convergence justification for ML-based optimizers, though it relies on simplifying assumptions (linear regression in token space, constant depth/width). Key open questions include whether the visual encoding of topology introduces systematic biases (acknowledged by the authors), whether the approach extends to non-convex or stochastic MCF variants, and whether the fine-tuning cost (still resource-intensive despite LoRA) limits adoption. The robustness to link failures and demand fluctuations without retraining is a strong practical differentiator over both LP and prior ML baselines.
