## Problem

Language models are optimized to answer user queries, yet many high-stakes applications (scientific discovery, medical diagnosis) require agents to strategically seek information by forming hypotheses, asking targeted questions, and acting under uncertainty. It remains unclear whether current LMs can behave as rational information-seeking agents, and what inference-time strategies can close the gap between LM performance and idealized Bayesian reasoning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260783/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The Captain receives a partially-revealed 8×8 board as a numpy array (−1 for hidden, 0 for water, 1–4 for ship colors), the full game history of Q&A pairs, and remaining question/shot budgets; it must output a binary decision (question vs. move), a natural-language yes/no question, or a tile coordinate. The Spotter receives both the partial board and the true board, plus the Captain's question and dialogue context, and must produce a grounded Yes/No answer—optionally by generating and executing a Python function that computes the answer from the two numpy arrays. Questions are translated to deterministic Python predicates over board configurations, enabling exact EIG computation against a sampled hypothesis space.

## Method

The paper introduces three inference-time Bayesian strategies built on a Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) particle approximation to the posterior over valid board configurations (~2³⁰ states). Bayes-Q samples up to 10 candidate questions from the LM, translates each to a Python predicate, estimates the predictive probability p_t by executing the predicate across weighted particles, and selects the question maximizing the closed-form EIG under a binary symmetric channel noise model (ε=0.1): H_b(ε+(1−2ε)p_t) − H_b(ε). Bayes-M computes per-tile hit probability by marginalizing the particle set and selects the MAP tile. Bayes-D uses a discounted one-step lookahead (γ=0.95) comparing the expected post-question MAP hit probability against the current MAP hit probability to decide whether to ask or shoot. For the Spotter role, a code-generation strategy (CoT+Code) has the LM write a generic Python function over true_board and partial_board, which is executed to produce the answer, boosting accuracy by up to 14.7% over direct answering.

## Results

On CaptainQA, adding full Bayesian strategies (Bayes-QMD) to Llama-4-Scout raises targeting F1 from 0.367 to 0.764, and for GPT-4o from 0.450 to 0.782, surpassing GPT-5's LM-only F1 of 0.716. On SpotterQA, code generation (CoT+Code) boosts answering accuracy by up to 14.7 percentage points over direct LM baselines (e.g., Claude 4 Opus: 86.8%→94.4%; GPT-4.1: 75.2%→90.9%). The Bayesian question strategy (Bayes-Q) raises mean per-question EIG by up to 0.227 bits (94.2% of the information-theoretic ceiling) and eliminates redundant zero-EIG questions (Llama-4-Scout: 18.5%→0.2%). On Guess Who?, the same strategies yield +42.4 p.p. for Llama-4-Scout (0.300→0.724) and +28.3 p.p. for GPT-4o (0.617→0.900).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between pure LM strategies and LM+Bayesian inference strategies on CaptainQA. Llama-4-Scout with Bayes-QMD (F1=0.764) outperforms GPT-5's LM-only play (F1=0.716) at roughly 1% of the cost, while GPT-4o with Bayes-QMD (F1=0.782) also exceeds GPT-5 at ~35% cost. Both Bayesian-enhanced weaker models achieve 82–83% win rate against human players and 67% against GPT-5, demonstrating that Bayesian inference-time strategies can compensate for weaker LM capabilities. On SpotterQA, code generation consistently outperforms both direct answering and chain-of-thought baselines across all 15 LMs tested (14.7 p.p. absolute gain for CoT+Code), with the largest improvements for mid-tier models like GPT-4.1 (+15.7 p.p.) and Claude 4 Opus (+7.6 p.p.). Notably, GPT-5 itself does not significantly benefit from the Bayesian question or move selection strategies, suggesting it already employs effective internal versions of these heuristics. The framework generalizes to Guess Who? (TextArena), where Bayes-QM improves success rates by +42.4 p.p. (Llama-4-Scout) and +28.3 p.p. (GPT-4o) over LM-only baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the cognitive-science Battleship paradigm (Rothe et al., 2017–2019; Gureckis & Markant, 2009) from single-player static snapshots to a full multi-turn, two-player dialogue game with a binary information bottleneck, and replaces the hand-engineered DSL with Python programs for question representation. The core inference machinery draws directly on Bayesian Experimental Design (Lindley, 1956; MacKay, 1992) for expected information gain and Sequential Monte Carlo (Doucet et al., 2001) for belief tracking over a combinatorial hypothesis space. The key conceptual departure is framing these classical probabilistic tools as inference-time strategies layered on top of LMs, rather than as standalone planners, thereby bridging the resource-rationality literature in cognitive science with modern LM agent evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a reusable evaluation harness (BATTLESHIPQA) and a concrete demonstration that Bayesian inference-time strategies can compensate for weak LM capabilities, enabling small models to outperform both humans and frontier models at a fraction of the cost. This suggests that for information-seeking tasks, the bottleneck is often the inference strategy rather than raw model scale, which has implications for how we deploy agents in resource-constrained, high-stakes settings (scientific discovery, medical diagnosis). Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to open-ended (non-Yes/No) questions, whether generative world models can be learned rather than hand-coded, and how to adapt the noise parameter ε to individual informants rather than fixing it globally.
