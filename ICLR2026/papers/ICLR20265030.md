## Problem

Preference-based reinforcement learning suffers from two compounding failures: query-policy misalignment, where sampled queries drawn from outdated replay buffer episodes no longer reflect the agent's evolving behavior, and primacy bias, where the reward estimator overfits to early (often trivial or ambiguous) feedback, causing persistent reward overestimation that propagates into the Q-function. Existing mitigations—near on-policy sampling (NOS) for query selection and naive periodic resetting for bias—each address only one issue and introduce their own problems: recency is a poor proxy for policy relevance in goal-conditioned tasks, and fixed-interval resets are computationally wasteful while leaving residual Q-function bias from stale reward estimates.

## Input / Output

The method consumes fixed-length trajectory sequences of state-action pairs (H=50 for DMControl, H=25 for Meta-World) as vector observations or RGB images (96×192 lateral view for Walker Walk; 288×288 dual-view for Window Open in pixel-based experiments). At each feedback session, trajectory pairs are presented to a human annotator (or synthetic B-Pref annotator) who returns a binary preference label y ∈ {(1,0), (0,1), (0.5,0.5)}. The system outputs a learned reward function r̂_ψ: S×A→[-1,1] (bounded via tanh), a policy π_φ producing actions, and a Q-function Q_θ used for policy optimization, all built on SAC (vector) or DrQ-v2 (pixel) backbones.

## Method

PoLiCER combines two complementary mechanisms. First, Policy Likelihood-Based Query Sampling (PLS) computes the average log-likelihood of each candidate trajectory under the current policy π_φ, converts these to inverse-rank weights for robustness against density outliers, and samples 2×L×K trajectories with probability proportional to w_i^α (temperature α controls sharpness) before randomly pairing them into K preference queries—requiring only 2×L×K forward passes versus N× more for disagreement sampling. Second, Critic-Exploited Reset (CER) continuously tracks the maximum critic output Q_max since the last reset and, at each feedback session, triggers a joint reset of both the reward estimator r_ψ and Q-function Q_θ whenever Q_max exceeds a monotonically increasing threshold β_i = β_0 + δ·ρ^i (which saturates at β_0 + δ/(1−ρ)), followed by a temporary increase in replay ratio to accelerate adaptation. The threshold's monotonic growth accommodates genuine policy improvement while the bounded reward scale (|r/(1−γ)|=100) enables task-agnostic monitoring. A theoretical analysis proves that reducing reward overestimation error by δ tightens the Q-function approximation error bound by 2δ/(1−γ), providing a principled guarantee for the reset mechanism.

## Results

On Meta-World manipulation tasks, PoLiCER achieves over 80% success rate with consistently lower variance than all baselines, reaching nearly 100% success on Drawer Open where no other method exceeds 60% (with nearly half of competing runs at 0%). On DMControl locomotion tasks, PoLiCER matches QPA in average return but exhibits significantly lower variance (narrower 95% bootstrap CIs). In pixel-based Window Open, PoLiCER achieves ~80% success, doubling PEBBLE's ~40%. The method also reduces reward overestimation from PEBBLE's ~150% plateau to substantially lower levels via critic-exploited reset.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PoLiCER outperforms PEBBLE across all seven tasks (three DMControl locomotion, four Meta-World manipulation) in both average performance and variance, with the largest gap on Meta-World where PEBBLE shows high variance and poor success rates. Against QPA (the closest competitor using near on-policy sampling), PoLiCER achieves comparable average returns on DMControl but with significantly lower variance, and clearly superior success rates on Meta-World where QPA's recency-based sampling fails in goal-conditioned environments. On pixel-based tasks, QPA fails to improve over PEBBLE in Cheetah Run and Window Open, while PoLiCER doubles PEBBLE's success rate in Window Open (~80% vs. ~40%). SURF, RUNE, and MRN all underperform PoLiCER on both benchmarks, with particularly poor results on Meta-World manipulation. In the human-in-the-loop experiment, PoLiCER produces qualitatively superior behavior (coordinated bipedal walking vs. PEBBLE's crawling), confirming that the advantage translates beyond synthetic annotators.

## Relation to Prior Work

PoLiCER builds directly on PEBBLE (Lee et al., 2021b) as its backbone PbRL framework and adapts the periodic reset concept from the primacy bias literature (Nikishin et al., 2022) into a dynamic, critic-triggered mechanism. Its key departure is from QPA (Hu et al., 2024), which uses temporal recency as a proxy for policy relevance in query sampling; PoLiCER argues this proxy degrades in goal-conditioned environments and instead selects queries by their log-likelihood under the current policy. The CER component further extends naive reward-estimator resets by jointly resetting the Q-function and using an adaptive monotonic threshold, addressing residual TD-learning bias that prior reset strategies leave unaddressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies and empirically demonstrates a non-obvious interaction: policy-aligned query sampling (PLS) can itself exacerbate reward overestimation by localizing the policy distribution, meaning that simply fixing query relevance is insufficient without a complementary bias-mitigation mechanism. The human-in-the-loop experiment showing PEBBLE converging to crawling while PoLiCER learns coordinated bipedal walking provides compelling evidence that these technical fixes translate to qualitatively different learned behaviors. Limitations include reliance on synthetic annotators for most experiments, environment-specific reward-task misalignment (Drawer Open), and moderate computational overhead from CER's increased replay ratios. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to language-based or multi-agent preference settings and how it performs with genuinely noisy human annotators at scale.
