## Problem

Simple, interpretable models (decision trees, first-principles equations) capture low-order data structure but miss subtle patterns, while complex models are accurate yet opaque. Existing model-augmentation schemes correct the primary model's output directly, which sacrifices interpretability precisely where the primary model fails. This paper asks whether corrections applied in the input space rather than the output space can simultaneously improve accuracy and expose the primary model's failure modes in a human-readable form.

## Input / Output

The framework is demonstrated on three tasks: a 2D binary classification problem (inputs x₁, x₂ → class label), an ideal-gas pressure prediction (inputs T, V → pressure P), and MNIST digit recognition (28×28 grayscale images → digit label). In each case the Tutor outputs a small correction vector ε in the input space; for MNIST, ε is applied in a 32-dimensional VAE latent space and decoded back to a 28×28 image, so the final output is both the corrected prediction and a visually inspectable modified image. The correction magnitude is explicitly regularized to remain minimal.

## Method

The Tutor-Pupil scheme freezes a task-specialized Pupil model (decision tree, ideal gas law, or logistic regression) and trains a flexible Tutor neural network to produce minimal input perturbations ε such that the Pupil evaluated at x+ε yields the correct output. The Tutor is trained with a composite loss combining the Pupil's prediction loss on the corrected input with an L₂ penalty on ||ε||, ensuring corrections are as small as possible. For the high-dimensional MNIST setting, the Tutor operates in a pretrained VAE's latent space rather than pixel space, with an additional KL-divergence term enforcing latent consistency and a reconstruction-fidelity term bounding pixel-level change. Because the Pupil is frozen, gradients flow through it solely to update the Tutor, and the learned ε vectors directly reveal where and how the Pupil's decision boundary deviates from the true data structure—offering a global, multi-feature diagnostic that contrasts with local methods like SHAP or counterfactuals.

## Results

On the toy binary classification task, the decision-tree Pupil achieves 85.88% test accuracy, while the Tutor-Pupil augmented model reaches 97.05% test accuracy (a ~13% improvement, confirmed statistically significant with p = 2.648×10⁻¹¹ via one-sided Welch's t-test). On MNIST, the logistic-regression Pupil scores 91% test accuracy, and the Tutor-Pupil augmented model (using a VAE latent-space correction) improves this to 98.57%. In the ideal-gas simulation, the Tutor-Pupil model tracks the simulated pressure almost perfectly across all volume and temperature settings, whereas the ideal gas law systematically underestimates pressure at small volumes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary quantitative baseline is the Pupil model alone (decision tree for the toy task, logistic regression for MNIST, ideal gas law for the thermodynamics example). The Tutor-Pupil augmentation consistently outperforms the Pupil: 97.05% vs. 85.88% test accuracy on the toy classification (p < 10⁻¹⁰), and 98.57% vs. 91% on MNIST. For the MNIST setting, the paper also notes that VAE reconstruction alone (without the Tutor) yields only 91.7%, confirming that the performance gain comes from the learned latent-space correction rather than the generative model. The sole external method compared is SHAP (Lundberg & Lee, 2017), used as an explainability baseline on the same misclassified MNIST digits; the comparison is qualitative, with the authors arguing that SHAP heatmaps are diffuse and hard to interpret, whereas the Tutor's pixel-level corrections (e.g., closing an open loop in a '0', extending a stroke in a '4') provide a direct, human-readable account of what the Pupil needs to succeed.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the model augmentation paradigm (Ghosh et al., 2019; Sun et al., 2020; Hoekstra et al., 2024; Györök et al., 2025), where a primary model grounded in prior knowledge is paired with a flexible auxiliary model, by shifting the correction from output space to input space. Prior augmentation schemes (parallel, series, feedback) typically train the auxiliary model to adjust the primary model's output, which the authors argue sacrifices interpretability precisely where the primary model fails. The Tutor-Pupil scheme instead trains the auxiliary model to propose minimal perturbations to the input, preserving the primary model's decision logic while exposing its failure modes. The paper also positions itself against local explanation methods (counterfactuals, SHAP) by arguing that a globally trained input-correction network captures systematic, multi-feature failure patterns rather than instance-specific attributions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Tutor-Pupil framework offers a principled way to simultaneously improve accuracy and gain diagnostic insight into a model's limitations, which is valuable for safety-critical and scientific applications where interpretability is non-negotiable. The ideal-gas example is compelling in showing that data-driven input corrections can rediscover known physics (van der Waals volume exclusion), suggesting the Tutor could serve as a discovery tool for unmodeled effects. However, the empirical evidence is limited to a 2D toy classification, a single physics simulation, and MNIST with a deliberately weak logistic-regression Pupil; no comparison is made against other augmentation topologies (parallel, series, feedback) or against stronger Pupil models (e.g., CNNs). Open questions include whether joint training of Tutor and Pupil yields complementary specializations, whether the approach scales to high-dimensional or multi-modal inputs, and whether the interpretability gains persist when the Pupil is a deep, non-linear model.
