## Problem

Existing chemistry benchmarks for LLMs are either too simplistic (SciEval), limited to a handful of task types (ChemLLMbench's 8 tasks), restricted to multiple-choice formats (ChemBench), or lack coverage of critical tasks like information extraction and inductive generation from chemical literature. No prior benchmark provides a hierarchical, fine-grained assessment that spans the full progression from basic chemical concepts through molecular-level reasoning to advanced synthetic deduction, nor does one incorporate multimodal inputs such as spectral images and molecular structure diagrams alongside text.

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes 3,160 evaluation instances split into a 1,960-example text subset and a 1,200-example multimodal subset. Text inputs include chemical questions in multiple formats (multiple choice, fill-in-the-blank, true/false, short answer, calculation), literature excerpts for extraction and generation, and molecular representations (IUPAC names, SMILES, SELFIES). Multimodal inputs add 2D molecular structure diagrams, reaction equations, and ten types of spectral/chart images (IR, Raman, UV-Vis, NMR, mass spectrum, diffraction patterns, kinetic charts, potential energy surfaces, statistical charts/tables). Outputs are scored with task-appropriate metrics—F1, Accuracy, BLEU, Exact Match, NRMSE, Tanimoto similarity, LLM-as-judge scores, and Overlap—aggregated into a 4-level × 13-dimension capability profile per model.

## Method

ChemEval organizes 62 tasks into a four-tier progressive hierarchy: Advanced Knowledge QA (15 tasks), Literature Understanding (19 tasks), Molecular Understanding (15 tasks), and Scientific Knowledge Deduction (13 tasks), each tier building on the previous. Data is sourced from two channels: 25 tasks adapted from open-source repositories (ChemRxnExtractor, Mol-Instructions, ChemLLMBench, SMolInstruct) and 37 novel tasks authored by chemistry experts using ~500 university textbooks and ~9,000 real experimental records, with a three-tier quality-assurance pipeline (undergraduate annotation → graduate cross-examination → faculty audit) and systematic de-contamination against existing model training corpora. Evaluation employs both zero-shot and 3-shot prompting with task-specific instructions iteratively refined via GPT-4o feedback, and greedy decoding across 12+ general LLMs (GPT-4o, o1/o3-mini, Claude-3.7-Sonnet, Gemini-2.5-Pro, Qwen2.5-7B–72B, LLaMA3.3-8B, Grok3, DeepSeek-V3/R1) and five chemistry-specialized models (ChemDFM, LlaSMol, ChemLLM, ChemSpark, ChemCrow). The multimodal track evaluates four MLLMs (GPT-4o, Claude-3.7-Sonnet, Qwen-VL Max, Phi-Vision-3.5) on visual chemistry tasks, and the paper further ablates the effects of few-shot prompting, model scaling (Qwen2.5 7B→72B), and thinking-model architectures on chemical task performance.

## Results

Across 62 chemical tasks spanning four progressive levels, Gemini-2.5-Pro achieves the highest scores on basic knowledge (87.60 MCTask accuracy, 82.40 CalcTask LLM Score), while ChemSpark dominates molecular understanding (87.84 Tanimoto on IUPAC2SMILES, 85.57 MolPC accuracy). Scientific Knowledge Deduction tasks prove extremely challenging for all models, with most scoring below 50 on Synthetic Pathway Recommendation and below 25 on Reaction Product Prediction (F1). Few-shot prompting yields substantial gains for general LLMs (e.g., OpenAI-o1 ReactTR F1: 25→60, +35 points) but often degrades chemistry-specialized models, suggesting catastrophic forgetting of in-context learning during domain fine-tuning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The benchmark evaluates 12+ LLMs, revealing a clear trade-off between general and specialized capabilities. General LLMs (Gemini-2.5-Pro, GPT-4o, OpenAI-o1) dominate Advanced Knowledge QA and Literature Understanding—Gemini-2.5-Pro scores 87.60 on MCTask vs. 43.60 for ChemSpark—while chemistry-specialized models (ChemSpark, ChemDFM) excel in Molecular Understanding, with ChemSpark achieving 87.84 Tanimoto on IUPAC2SMILES versus 34.71 for GPT-4o. Among chemistry LLMs, ChemSpark substantially outperforms ChemDFM, LlaSMol, and ChemLLM across nearly all molecular and deduction tasks; ChemLLM and LlaSMol score near zero on multiple information extraction and molecular translation tasks, indicating severe instruction-following failures. Thinking models (OpenAI-o1, DeepSeek-R1) do not consistently outperform their non-thinking counterparts on chemistry tasks, suggesting the bottleneck is domain knowledge rather than reasoning depth. Few-shot prompting benefits general LLMs (e.g., Qwen2.5-72B ReactTR: 22→61, +39) but often harms chemistry models (ChemSpark shows 7 tasks with decreased performance), pointing to degraded in-context learning from domain fine-tuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

ChemEval extends the line of chemistry-specific LLM benchmarks (ChemLLMbench, ChemBench, MaCBench) by introducing a four-tier progressive hierarchy spanning 13 dimensions and 62 tasks, where prior work offered only 8–12 flat task categories. It departs from these predecessors by incorporating information extraction and inductive generation tasks (absent from all prior chemistry benchmarks), multimodal evaluation over spectral images and molecular structure diagrams, and expert-validated data drawn from ~500 textbooks and ~9,000 laboratory records. The evaluation protocol follows the standard zero-shot/few-shot prompting paradigm established by MMLU-style benchmarks, but applies it within a domain-structured taxonomy co-designed with chemistry researchers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that neither model scaling (7B→72B) nor reasoning-augmented architectures (o1, R1) meaningfully improve performance on the hardest chemical tasks (molecular translation, retrosynthesis, reaction prediction), suggesting that domain-specific knowledge integration—not raw compute or chain-of-thought depth—is the bottleneck. The systematic quantification of catastrophic forgetting in chemistry-specialized models (poor instruction-following, degraded literature comprehension) provides concrete evidence for the trade-off between domain fine-tuning and general capability. Open questions include whether dynamic, agent-based workflows with external chemistry tools can close the gap, and whether the limited per-task instance counts (~20–50) suffice for reliable ranking as models improve.
