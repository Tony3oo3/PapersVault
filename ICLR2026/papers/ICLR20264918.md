## Problem

Despite numerous claims of 1.4×–2× speedups over AdamW from new pretraining optimizers, these methods have not been widely adopted in production LLM training. The paper identifies two methodological shortcomings that obscure fair comparisons: (i) baselines suffer from improperly tuned hyperparameters (a single learning-rate tweak in the GPT-3 recipe yields up to 2× apparent speedup), and (ii) evaluations are confined to small model scales and single data-to-model ratios, with rankings that can flip during learning-rate decay if judged at intermediate checkpoints.

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes Llama 2 architecture models (130M, 300M, 520M, 1.2B parameters, 32 layers, 4096 sequence length) trained on a ~4.1T-token corpus (DCLM-baseline, StarCoder 2, ProofPile 2) tokenized with the Llama 3 tokenizer, and produces final validation loss on C4/EN plus downstream accuracy on 10 benchmarks (ARC, BoolQ, HellaSwag, etc.). Speedup is computed by fitting a power-law scaling curve L̂_N(D) = α_N D^{−B_N} + β_N for AdamW at each model size, then mapping each optimizer's final loss to the equivalent AdamW data budget. The unusual constraint is that comparisons are made at the end of training (target token budget), not at intermediate checkpoints, and across four Chinchilla ratios (1×, 2×, 4×, 8×) to capture regime-dependent behavior.

## Method

The paper introduces a three-phase hyperparameter-tuning and benchmarking framework for eleven optimizers. Phase I performs exhaustive coordinate-descent sweeps over all hyperparameters (learning rate, weight decay, warmup, β₁, β₂, ε, gradient-norm clipping, batch size) for each optimizer across six settings (130M/300M/500M at 1× Chinchilla; 130M at 2×/4×/8×), accepting updates only if validation loss improves by >3×10⁻³. Phase II identifies which hyperparameters are scaling-sensitive (their optimal value shifts with model size or data budget) versus scaling-insensitive, then refines only the sensitive ones on six additional mid-scale settings (300M/500M at 2×/4×/8×). Phase III fits a smooth scaling law h(N,D) = αN^{−A}D^{−B} + β for each scaling-sensitive hyperparameter via non-linear least-squares on the 12 observed (N,D,h) triples, enabling extrapolation to 1.2B parameters and 16× Chinchilla without re-running full sweeps. The key empirical finding is that matrix-based optimizers (Muon, Soap, Kron, Scion) consistently outperform scalar-based ones (AdamW, Lion, Mars, Cautious) by ~1.3× for models under 520M, but this advantage decays to ~1.1× at 1.2B parameters, and the optimal optimizer shifts from Muon to Soap/Kron as the data-to-model ratio increases beyond 8× Chinchilla.

## Results

Across 11 optimizers benchmarked on Llama-2 architectures (130M–1.2B parameters) at 1×–8× Chinchilla data-to-model ratios, no optimizer achieves the 2× speedup claimed in prior literature. The maximum observed speedup over a well-tuned AdamW baseline is 1.4× (matrix-based optimizers on 130M models at 1× Chinchilla), and this decays to ~1.1× at 1.2B parameters. Matrix-based optimizers (Muon, Soap, Kron, Scion) consistently outperform scalar-based ones (AdamW, NadamW, Mars, Cautious, Lion, Adam-mini), with the latter group achieving less than 1.2× average speedup. At 8× Chinchilla, Soap and Kron surpass Muon, and at 16× Chinchilla (300M models), Soap clearly outperforms Muon.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the well-tuned AdamW baseline, matrix-based optimizers (Muon, Soap, Kron, Scion) deliver a consistent ~1.3× speedup on models under 520M parameters, but this advantage shrinks to ~1.1× at 1.2B parameters. Among matrix-based methods, Muon is the strongest at 1× Chinchilla, but Soap and Kron overtake it at 8× Chinchilla, and Soap clearly wins at 16× Chinchilla (300M models). Within the scalar-based family, variance-reduced variants (NadamW, Mars, Cautious) provide a small but consistent lift (~1.1×) over vanilla AdamW, while memory-efficient variants (Lion, Adam-mini) track AdamW within 5%, with Lion's relative disadvantage widening and Adam-mini's narrowing as model size increases. The paper's central finding is that previously reported 2× speedups (e.g., for Sophia, Muon, MARS, Cautious AdamW, FOCUS, SWAN, DION) largely stem from under-tuned AdamW baselines; once hyperparameters are properly optimized via coordinate descent, the maximum speedup caps at 1.4×. A key methodological finding is that early-training loss curves can mislead: optimizer rankings can flip during learning-rate decay, so only end-of-training comparisons at the target budget are reliable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a rigorous re-evaluation of the rapidly growing family of pretraining optimizers (Muon, Soap, Kron, Sophia, Mars, etc.), building on the re-evaluation methodology of Schmidt et al. (2021) for general deep learning and the concurrent work of Semenov et al. (2025) for LLM pretraining. It departs from prior optimizer papers by identifying two specific methodological failures—unequal hyperparameter tuning and single-regime evaluation—and addressing them through a three-phase coordinate-descent tuning framework and multi-scale, multi-data-ratio benchmarking. The hyperparameter scaling-law extrapolation (Phase III) draws on the tensor program tradition (Yang et al.) and recent LLM-specific scaling law work (Li et al. 2025a, Everett et al. 2024), but applies it to optimizer hyperparameters rather than architecture parameters.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This benchmark corrects a widespread misconception in the field: the 1.4–2× speedups claimed by recent optimizer papers largely reflect under-tuned AdamW baselines, and the true advantage of the best alternatives (Muon, Soap, Kron) is capped at ~1.4× and decays to ~1.1× at 1.2B parameters. The finding that optimizer rankings can flip during learning-rate decay and that the optimal optimizer shifts with data-to-model ratio (Muon best at 1×, Soap/Kron best at 8×+) provides practical guidance for practitioners choosing optimizers for specific training regimes. Key limitations include the 1.2B parameter ceiling (frontier models at 70B+ may behave differently), the TPU-specific batch-size regime that may not transfer to GPU clusters, and the open question of why matrix-preconditioning advantages diminish with scale—whether this reflects a fundamental property of large-model optimization or an artifact of the tested range.
