## Problem

Scenario-based Sample Average Approximation (SAA) for stochastic programming becomes computationally intractable when each scenario's second-stage recourse involves an NP-hard combinatorial structure, forcing most SP literature to restrict the second stage to linear or simplified models. This paper addresses the gap by making structured integer recourse operators—specifically dynamic-programming-based evaluations—tractable at scales of 10^6 or more scenarios, without relying on surrogate learning models or structural relaxations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263930/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a fixed first-stage decision (a giant tour permutation for CVRPSD or a Day-1 delivery/routing plan for DSIRP) together with a large batch of scenario demand realizations (up to 10^6), vehicle capacity and cost matrices, and inventory capacity/horizon parameters. It produces per-scenario second-stage recourse costs, the optimal route split or replenishment schedule for each scenario, and the aggregated expected total cost. A key structural detail is that infeasible transitions (e.g., capacity violations) are encoded as +∞ entries in a masked transition matrix, and variable-sized state spaces are padded to regular tensor shapes so that all scenarios share a uniform GPU memory layout.

## Method

The core reformulation expresses each Bellman DP update as a min-plus matrix-vector product in the (min,+) semiring, J_{t+1} = (A_t)^⊤ ⊗ J_t, where infeasible transitions are masked with +∞. Because second-stage evaluations are independent across scenarios once the first-stage decision is fixed, the paper exploits multidimensional GPU parallelism: 2D (scenarios × predecessor states) for the CVRPSD split operator and 3D (scenarios × inventory transitions × route options) for the DSIRP reinsertion DP. Custom CUDA kernels assign one thread per (scenario, predecessor, route-option) tuple, compute the partial cost J(p) + A(p,i), and perform warp-/block-level min-reductions to yield the Bellman minimum on-chip. This design achieves near-linear scaling in the number of scenarios with 2–5 orders of magnitude speedup over multithreaded CPU baselines, and the entire 10^6-scenario tensor fits within a standard 11 GB GPU, confirming memory is not the limiting factor.

## Results

On CVRPSD, the GPU split operator achieves ~80× speedup over single-thread CPU and ~20× over 8-thread CPU at 10⁶ scenarios, with runtime growing nearly linearly (seconds range) while the Gurobi MILP hits time limits or OOM beyond 500 scenarios. On DSIRP, the GPU reinsertion DP attains ~9.3×10⁴ speedup over single-thread CPU and ~2.26×10⁴ over multi-thread CPU at 2×10⁵ scenarios. GPU memory usage scales linearly: 10⁶ CVRPSD scenarios consume 3.98 GB and 100,000 DSIRP scenarios consume 1.66 GB, both fitting within a standard 11 GB GPU. Under fixed time budgets, GPU consistently discovers higher-quality first-stage solutions by evaluating orders-of-magnitude more candidates.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Gurobi MILP solver on CVRPSD, GPU SPLIT dominates on scalability: at 6 customers, GPU runtime grows linearly (13.76 s → 420 s from 100 to 10,000 scenarios) while MILP hits the 3600 s time limit at 2000 scenarios and OOM at 10,000; at 10 customers, MILP is infeasible beyond 100 scenarios. On objective quality, GPU SPLIT matches or beats MILP at every scenario count where both complete (e.g., 3391.17 vs. infeasible at 10,000 scenarios for 6 customers; 3800.62 vs. 3913.01 at 1000 scenarios for 10 customers). Against the state-of-the-art single-scenario DSIRP algorithm of Coelho et al. (2012), the multi-scenario GPU method achieves 4.21%–6.37% lower objective values (5.09% average) across small, medium, and large instances, while keeping runtimes in the same order of magnitude (e.g., 7200 s vs. 9249 s on the large class). The paper also demonstrates that under identical wall-clock budgets, GPU evaluation of far more first-stage candidates and scenarios consistently yields lower out-of-sample cost than CPU single-thread or multi-thread baselines on CVRPSD instances x-n128 and x-n105.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper GPU-parallelizes two well-known DP subroutines: Vidal's (2016) O(n) split operator for CVRP and the inventory reinsertion DP from Zhao et al. (2025) for DSIRP, reformulating both as masked min-plus matrix-vector products in the (min,+) semiring. It departs from prior GPU-accelerated optimization work (Schubiger's GPU-ADMM, Lu & Yang's primal-dual methods, Gade-Nielsen's interior-point solver), which targets continuous problems, and from routing-specific GPU efforts (Boschetti et al. 2017 for q-route relaxations, Maleki et al. 2014 for rank-convergence DP), by introducing a general scenario-batched kernel design that simultaneously parallelizes across scenarios, DP layers, and action/route options. The paper positions itself as filling a gap: no unified GPU framework existed for discrete, combinatorial, scenario-based stochastic optimization, with prior approaches either requiring problem-specific kernels or forcing reformulation into library abstractions like JAX or GraphBLAS.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial for stochastic operations research: evaluating 10^6 scenarios in seconds (vs. minutes-to-hours on CPU) makes data-driven SAA with large scenario sets computationally routine, directly translating into measurably better first-stage decisions (4–6% objective improvement over the state-of-the-art DSIRP baseline, and consistent gains over MILP at scale). Key limitations include the restriction to forward DPs with additive costs, the absence of constrained or risk-aware objectives, and dependence on regular tensor shapes (padding/masking for variable state spaces). Open questions include extending the kernel recipe to irregular or backward DPs, integrating learned surrogate components while preserving numerical reliability, and whether the min-plus semiring abstraction generalizes cleanly to problems with non-additive or coupling costs across scenarios.
