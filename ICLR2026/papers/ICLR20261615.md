## Problem

Spatial visualization—the ability to mentally manipulate visual images to infer unseen spatial relationships—remains a largely unassessed blind spot in MLLM evaluation. Existing benchmarks either focus on spatial perception of visible information or bury spatial visualization tasks under broader categories like mathematical or logical reasoning, while their reliance on publicly sourced problems (IQ tests, math contests) risks data contamination and undermines evaluation reliability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261615/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each problem presents one or more reference images of a spatial configuration (e.g., an isometric cube stack, a 2D grid pattern, or a mechanical system diagram) rendered at standardized resolution via FreeCAD, paired with a text question and four image-based answer options (A–D) arranged in a unified visual input. The model outputs a single letter corresponding to the correct option. A small subset of tasks (e.g., Cube Counting) also accepts a numeric text answer, but the dominant format is image-to-image multiple choice, forcing precise visual analysis rather than textual pattern matching.

## Method

SpatialViz-Bench is organized around four cognitive sub-abilities—mental rotation, mental folding, visual penetration, and mental animation—each assessed by three tasks (12 total, 1,180 problems) with 2–3 parameter-controlled difficulty levels. For 11 of 12 tasks, a programmatic pipeline integrating Python with FreeCAD generates novel test cases by explicitly manipulating cognitive load parameters (e.g., rotation axis/angle, number of folds, assembly dimensions, number of constraint views), while systematically constructing distractors with recorded explanations for diagnostic error analysis. This dynamic generation prevents data contamination by allowing the test bank to be continuously refreshed. The sole exception, the Mechanical System task, is manually designed from open-source simulations by domain experts. Evaluation is conducted zero-shot across 27 MLLMs under both direct-answering and Chain-of-Thought prompting, with 95% Wilson confidence intervals to validate performance hierarchies and a six-category error taxonomy (perceptual, spatial transformation, methodological, instruction following, spatial memorization, calculation) to localize failure modes.

## Results

Across 27 MLLMs evaluated on SpatialViz-Bench (1,180 problems, 12 tasks, 4 sub-abilities), the top performer Gemini-2.5-pro achieves 44.66% overall accuracy (95% CI: [41.85%, 47.51%]), followed by o1 at 41.36%. The human baseline is 82.46% and the random-chance baseline is 25.08%, revealing a substantial capability gap. The best open-source model, LLaMA-4-Scout, reaches only 34.24%, a statistically significant ~10% deficit versus the top closed-source model. A text-only LLM (Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct) scores 25.86%, confirming the benchmark's vision-dependence.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The benchmark reveals a clear hierarchy: Gemini-2.5-pro (44.66%) leads all models, followed by o1 (41.36%), Gemini-2.5-flash (36.86%), and Doubao-1.5-vision-pro (37.54% w/o CoT). Among open-source models, LLaMA-4-Scout (34.24%) and Qwen2.5-VL-72B (35.00% w/o CoT) are the strongest, but both trail the top closed-source model by a statistically significant ~10% gap (non-overlapping 95% Wilson CIs). A striking 'CoT paradox' emerges: Chain-of-Thought prompting improves closed-source models (e.g., Claude-3.5-sonnet gains +5.68%) but degrades most open-source models (e.g., SAIL-VL-1.5-2B drops from 29.32% to 24.15%, Kimi-VL-A3B drops from 32.37% to 23.90%), with losses concentrated in pure-visual tasks like 3D Rotation and Three-View Projection. On the Arrow Moving task, Gemini-2.5-pro reaches 95.00% (surpassing the 90% human baseline), while most open-source models remain near random (~25%). Error analysis shows Perceptual and Spatial Transformation errors account for ~60% of all failures across models, and scaling from Qwen2.5-VL-7B to 72B yields only marginal improvement on these core error types, indicating that parameter scaling alone does not resolve fundamental spatial visualization deficits.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by isolating spatial visualization as a distinct evaluation target for MLLMs, departing from prior spatial reasoning benchmarks (What'sUp, Blink, SpatialRGPT-bench) that focus on perception of visible information, and from general-purpose benchmarks (MM-IQ, MathVision) that bury spatial visualization under broader mathematical or logical reasoning categories. Methodologically, it adapts the programmatic generation paradigm from CLEVR (Blender-based) to a Python+FreeCAD pipeline, extending it to 11 of 12 tasks with controlled difficulty parameters and systematic distractor generation. The cognitive framework is grounded in Thurstone's primary mental abilities taxonomy, decomposing spatial visualization into four sub-abilities (mental rotation, mental folding, visual penetration, mental animation) that prior MLLM evaluations have not jointly assessed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark addresses a critical methodological gap: web-sourced spatial problems risk data contamination from pretraining corpora, undermining evaluation validity. The finding that CoT prompting paradoxically degrades open-source models on pure-visual spatial tasks (validated across prompt templates and extraction rules) has immediate practical implications for how practitioners deploy MLLMs. The error analysis showing that perceptual and spatial transformation failures (~60%) dominate over reasoning errors, and that 10× scaling (7B→72B) does not resolve these core deficits, redirects the field toward improving visuospatial representations rather than simply increasing model size or reasoning depth. Open questions include whether RL-based training paradigms (à la DeepSeek-R1) can address the fundamental perceptual bottleneck, and whether the benchmark's difficulty gradient can be further refined to differentiate mid-tier models.
