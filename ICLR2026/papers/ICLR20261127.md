## Problem

Adjoint Matching (AM) has proven highly effective for fine-tuning continuous generative models with differentiable rewards, but extending it to discrete generative modeling—where state spaces are non-differentiable and models are formulated as Continuous-Time Markov Chains (CTMCs)—remains largely unexplored. The paper asks whether a discrete fixed-point equation analogous to AM's continuous adjoint exists, and if so, how to derive and implement it for practical discrete generative models such as diffusion-based LLMs.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretrained masked CTMC model (e.g., LLaDA-8B-Instruct), a non-differentiable terminal loss (negative reward, such as correctness + format scores for math problems), and text prompts of 128 or 256 tokens. It produces a fine-tuned CTMC model whose transition rates are matched to the optimal rate via the discrete adjoint, enabling generation of reward-optimized text sequences. The discrete state space is a product of vocabulary indices (|X| = M^N), and the model operates under the masked diffusion constraint where each jump unmasks exactly one token.

## Method

DAM derives the discrete adjoint—a backward ODE estimator of the exponential value difference e^{-V_t(y)+V_t(x)}—by applying Dynkin's formula to CTMC processes, providing a purely statistical derivation that avoids the control-theoretic framework of original AM. The matching objective uses generalized KL divergence between the parametrized model rate and the base rate scaled multiplicatively by the discrete adjoint (in contrast to the additive perturbation in continuous AM). For tractability in extremely large state spaces, the paper introduces an importance-weighted discrete adjoint estimator that uses self-normalizing importance sampling over model trajectories, and exploits the masked diffusion structure to reduce modeling complexity from O(M^N) to O(MN). The final algorithm samples model inputs via reciprocal projection from a replay buffer, debiases the objective with model probability weights, and trains with a stop-gradient on the sampling distribution.

## Results

On mathematical reasoning tasks with LLaDA-8B-Instruct, DAM achieves 75.7% accuracy on GSM8K and 32.6% on MATH500 at sequence length 128, and 79.9% and 36.4% respectively at sequence length 256. The most dramatic gains appear on Countdown (60.2% vs. 43.8% for D1 at seq 128) and Sudoku (89.2% vs. 23.8% for D1 at seq 128), where DAM substantially outperforms the prior state-of-the-art. On synthetic 2D examples, DAM converges stably to the analytically known optimal distribution, while D1 and SVDD plateau without reaching it.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is D1 (Zhao et al., 2025), the current state-of-the-art for fine-tuning masked CTMC models on reasoning tasks. DAM matches or slightly exceeds D1 on GSM8K (75.7% vs. 75.6% at seq 128; 79.9% vs. 79.8% at seq 256) and wins on MATH500 at seq 128 (32.6% vs. 31.2%), though it narrowly loses at seq 256 (36.4% vs. 37.2%). The largest margins are on Countdown, where DAM achieves 60.2% vs. D1's 43.8% (seq 128) and 55.5% vs. 31.3% (seq 256), and on Sudoku, where DAM reaches 89.2% vs. D1's 23.8% (seq 128) and 88.1% vs. 12.9% (seq 256). The second baseline, SVDD (Li et al., 2024), is compared only on synthetic 2D examples where DAM converges stably to the optimal distribution while SVDD plateaus, likely due to heavy approximations in its value-function estimation. The authors note that D1's task-specific approximations may be reasonably effective on GSM8K and MATH500, narrowing the gap, while DAM's more general formulation yields larger advantages on structured combinatorial tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Adjoint Matching (AM, Domingo-Enrich et al., 2025) from continuous SDE-based generative models to discrete CTMC-based models, a transition that was previously considered infeasible due to the non-differentiability of discrete state spaces. The key conceptual departure is replacing AM's control-theoretic (SDE drift) derivation with a purely statistical one via Dynkin's formula, which reframes the adjoint as an unbiased estimator of the optimal rate rather than a solution to a Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation. Among discrete fine-tuning methods, DAM differs from policy-gradient approaches (D1, DRAKES, SEPO) and value-based methods (SVDD) by directly matching the parametrized rate to an adjoint-based estimator of the optimal rate, avoiding the need for reward differentiability or value function regression.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DAM establishes that adjoint-based fine-tuning is viable in discrete domains, opening a new algorithmic family for CTMC generative models that is more general than policy-gradient or value-based alternatives. The statistical derivation via Dynkin's formula is model-agnostic and could potentially extend to other stochastic process classes beyond CTMCs. However, the method is currently validated only on masked CTMC models (a single architecture, LLaDA-8B-Instruct), and the performance advantage over D1 is highly task-dependent—massive on Sudoku and Countdown but marginal on GSM8K and MATH500—suggesting that D1's task-specific approximations remain competitive where the reward structure is simpler. Applying DAM to non-masked CTMCs and larger model scales remains open.
