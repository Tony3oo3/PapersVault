## Problem

Existing LLM decoding strategies for reasoning tasks pursue two conflicting objectives: increasing exploration at uncertain steps (via higher temperature or larger candidate sets) and filtering low-confidence samples post-generation. These approaches conflate aleatoric uncertainty (genuine ambiguity among valid continuations) with epistemic uncertainty (systematic model errors), and the paper shows that in reasoning tasks, low-confidence steps are predominantly error-amplifying states rather than branch points, making broader sampling at those steps actively harmful to sequence-level accuracy.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt and a pre-computed calibration grid — a 2D table mapping 10 uniform confidence bins to rank-wise expected correctness (up to rank 20) — built by teacher-forcing on a calibration dataset such as GSM8K-train or alpaca-gpt4-en. At each decoding step, the model's logit vector is processed through this grid to determine the active token set, and the output is a generated reasoning chain with a final answer. The calibration grid requires only a single forward pass over calibration data and adds negligible per-step overhead (a table lookup or single scalar transform) during inference.

## Method

The core insight is that rank-wise correctness, not raw probability, should drive truncation decisions. The paper constructs a calibration grid by teacher-forcing on calibration text and computing, for each (confidence bin, rank) pair, the average token probability and the fraction of steps where the gold token appears at that rank. Three learning-free samplers exploit this: Greedy-Threshold forces greedy decoding when the maximum token probability drops below 0.3; Calibrated-TopK adaptively caps the rank at the highest rank whose average correctness exceeds a threshold (0.05) within the current confidence bin; and Calibrated-ε fits a log-log linear relationship between probability and correctness across the grid, then truncates any token whose predicted correctness (10^A · p^B) falls below a threshold (0.05). All three methods are composable (their active sets can be intersected), add negligible inference cost, and consistently improve majority-voted accuracy on reasoning benchmarks (GSM8K, MMLU-Pro, Big-Bench-Hard, AIME) by up to 6.5%, with the largest gains for smaller models where epistemic uncertainty dominates.

## Results

On Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct, Calibrated-ε achieves the strongest overall gains: GSM8K maj@8 of 40.8 (vs. 30.2 no-restrictions baseline, +10.6 pts), MMLU-Pro maj@8 of 18.9, and Big-Bench-Hard maj@32 of 32.0. On the harder AIME benchmark with GPT-OSS-20B (thinking mode), Greedy-Threshold raises AIME24 overall correct from 48.7% to 55.2% (+6.5 pts) and AIME25 from 56.1% to 59.9%, while reducing unique answers only marginally (15.1→13.3 on AIME24). Calibrated-TopK achieves comparable GSM8K performance (47.1 maj@32) and the best MMLU-Pro score (17.9 maj@8), confirming that rank-wise correctness is an effective truncation signal across benchmarks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Calibrated-ε outperforms all five baselines on GSM8K maj@8 (40.8 vs. top-k 32.6, top-p 35.5, min-p 38.7, EDT 40.2, η-sampling 31.6) and MMLU-Pro maj@8 (18.9 vs. 16.8, 16.8, 17.7, 17.7, 16.5 respectively). On Big-Bench-Hard, Calibrated-ε matches min-p and EDT at maj@8 (30.6) but edges them out at maj@32 (32.0 vs. 31.7), while substantially outperforming top-k (22.0), top-p (25.5), and η-sampling (20.6). The fixed-threshold ε-sampling (ε=0.05) performs on par with min-p and EDT, confirming that simply removing tail tokens helps, but the correctness-calibrated variants (Calibrated-TopK, Calibrated-ε) extract additional gains by adapting the cutoff to estimated rank-wise correctness. Greedy-Threshold alone is weaker in isolation (31.2 GSM8K maj@8) but consistently adds +1 to +2.4 points when stacked on top of existing samplers, especially for smaller models (Qwen2.5-0.5B: +1.0 to +2.4; Qwen2.5-1.5B: +1.3 to +2.8). On AIME24, Greedy-Threshold improves maj@4 from 71.4 to 77.3 and overall correct from 48.7% to 55.2%, while ε-sampling raises pass@32 from 93.3% to 92.6% (slight drop) but boosts overall correct to 54.9%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on ε-sampling (Hewitt et al., 2022) and top-k truncation (Fan et al., 2018), but departs from the prevailing 'sample more when uncertain' paradigm embodied by min-p, EDT, and temperature-based adaptive decoding. The key conceptual departure is reordering the uncertainty hierarchy for reasoning tasks: rather than treating high entropy as a signal of aleatoric variability warranting broader exploration, the authors argue it predominantly reflects epistemic uncertainty (systematic model error) that should trigger stricter truncation. Their calibration grid, which maps confidence bins × token ranks to expected correctness, provides a learning-free mechanism to operationalize this correctness-first principle, distinguishing their approach from post-generation filtering methods like DeepConf or self-certainty that operate after the fact.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a widely-held heuristic in LLM decoding (increase exploration at uncertain steps) with consistent empirical evidence across model sizes and benchmarks, offering a simple, composable, near-zero-overhead correction that practitioners can adopt immediately. However, the practical gains are modest (1–6% on reasoning benchmarks) and diminish substantially for larger models that already produce high-confidence predictions, limiting the ceiling of impact. Open questions include whether the confidence-to-correctness mapping is truly model-internal or dataset-dependent, how calibration should be updated online for novel tasks, and whether the correctness-first principle extends meaningfully to open-ended generation where 'correctness' is ill-defined. The MBPP failure case (where diversity-driven pass@k rewards broader exploration) highlights that the approach is task-specific rather than universally applicable.
