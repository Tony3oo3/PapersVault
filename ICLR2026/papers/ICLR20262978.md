## Problem

Prior fMRI-to-image reconstruction methods assume that vision-based latent representations are essential for decoding visual stimuli, yet it remains unclear which type of latent space actually best aligns with neural activity. Additionally, existing approaches use a unified hidden representation that conflates objects with their attributes, causing object detection errors (e.g., generating a tiger instead of a gray tiger-striped cat) that reflect a fundamental mismatch with the brain's object-centric, compositional visual processing.

## Input / Output

The method takes a preprocessed 1D fMRI vector of v voxels (selected from brain regions) and produces a reconstructed RGB image (H×W×3) via a pre-trained diffusion model (Stable Diffusion 2.1 or SDXL). As an intermediate step, it generates a structured text description consisting of exactly two object-level tuples—each containing an object name, a 2-sentence description with attributes and spatial relationships, and a location label from {left, right, top, bottom}—plus a 3–5 word background style descriptor. The fixed two-object constraint and predefined spatial vocabulary are used consistently during both training and inference.

## Method

PRISM projects fMRI signals into the text space of a T5 language model (rather than a vision or vision-language space), motivated by the finding that fMRI aligns most strongly with LM text embeddings under CKA, CCA, and Generalization Gap metrics. An attribute/relationship search module uses an ε-greedy strategy with GPT-4o-mini as a VLM to automatically discover the optimal prompt keyword (converging to 'Spatial Layout') that yields descriptions best aligned with both fMRI signals and image reconstruction quality. During training, per-object MLPs encode fMRI signals independently, and a fine-tuned T5 generates the structured descriptions as supervision. At inference, an object-centric diffusion module generates each object's latent representation independently via cross-attention with its description, spatially concatenates them according to predicted locations, and blends with a global-context latent (β=0.5) to ensure seamless boundaries, reducing attribute-binding errors inherent in holistic generation.

## Results

On the NSD dataset, PRISM achieves the best performance across all five metrics: PixCorr 0.3404, SSIM 0.4640, LPIPS 0.5963, CLIP 0.9467, and InceptionV3 0.9516. The headline result is a ~6% reduction in LPIPS (perceptual loss) over the strongest baseline Mindeye2 (0.6338), with similar gains on BOLD5000 (0.6198 vs. 0.6416) and GOD (0.6213 vs. 0.6586). With the SDXL backbone, PRISM further improves LPIPS to 0.5563 on NSD. On a downstream QA task using reconstructed NSD images, PRISM reaches 60.54% accuracy, outperforming all baselines (best competitor: Mindeye2 at 57.65%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PRISM outperforms all six baselines on every metric across all three datasets (NSD, BOLD5000, GOD). The closest competitor is Mindeye2, which PRISM surpasses by ~5.9% in LPIPS on NSD (0.5963 vs. 0.6338), ~7.7% in PixCorr (0.3404 vs. 0.3160), and ~2.9% in CLIP score (0.9467 vs. 0.9201). MindBridge, despite achieving competitive CLIP scores (0.9427 on NSD), lags significantly in pixel-level metrics (PixCorr 0.1802, SSIM 0.2823), indicating its reconstructions are semantically aligned but visually poor. Takagi and Mindvis, which rely on VAE latent or masked-modeling representations, show the largest gaps in LPIPS (0.7665 and 0.6789 respectively on NSD). The advantage persists with the SDXL backbone, where PRISM+SDXL maintains a clear lead over Mindeye2+SDXL in LPIPS (0.5563 vs. 0.6002) and InceptionV3 (0.9765 vs. 0.9602) on NSD.

## Relation to Prior Work

PRISM departs from the dominant paradigm in fMRI-to-image reconstruction, where prior methods (Mindeye, Mindvis, MindBridge, NeuralDiffuser) map brain signals into vision-based latent spaces (CLIP image embeddings, LDM latents) before feeding a diffusion model. The paper's core conceptual departure is demonstrating that pure language-model text space (T5, LLaMA3) aligns more strongly with fMRI signals than any vision or vision-language space, motivated by the hypothesis that the brain encodes semantic meaning rather than pixel-level detail. On the generation side, PRISM repurposes object-centric compositional generation (inspired by Yang et al., 2024) and VLM-assisted prompt optimization (from the LLM prompt-tuning literature) into the fMRI decoding pipeline, combining these in a way not seen in prior brain-decoding work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that text space outperforms vision space for fMRI alignment challenges a widely held assumption in the field and could redirect future work toward language-grounded intermediate representations for brain decoding. The neuroscientific interpretation linking the optimal 'Spatial Layout' keyword to Presubiculum activation provides a biologically grounded explanation, though the authors acknowledge this needs further validation. Key limitations include the fixed two-object constraint (which limits scalability to complex scenes), reliance on GPT-4o-mini for description generation, and the modest 6% LPIPS improvement over strong baselines. Open questions include whether the text-space advantage persists for dynamic stimuli (video), whether it generalizes across brain regions beyond visual cortex, and whether the object-centric approach can scale beyond two objects without the diffusion model dropping instances.
