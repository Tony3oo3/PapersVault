## Problem

Existing factuality metrics fail to measure whether LLMs appropriately refuse questions beyond their knowledge. Refusal-rate-based metrics (e.g., SimpleQA's F-score) fluctuate by up to 70% under different prompting strategies, while calibration-based metrics require expensive auxiliary models or repeated sampling and do not directly reflect refusal behavior. This paper identifies a critical gap: LLMs achieve high factual accuracy yet exhibit unreliable, fragile refusal behavior that no standard metric captures.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a factual QA dataset with ground-truth answers and runs the model in two passes: a first pass where the model may answer or refuse (outputting <answer>...</answer> or <answer>UNANSWERED</answer>), and a second pass that forces answers on previously refused questions by removing abstention options from the prompt. From the two passes, it extracts four aggregate statistics—correct answer rate on attempted questions, refusal rate, and error rate under forced answering—and produces a single scalar Refusal Index score (Spearman rank correlation in [-1, 1]) that quantifies how well the model's refusal decisions align with question difficulty.

## Method

The Refusal Index is defined as the Spearman rank correlation between per-question refusal probability r_i and error probability w_i, capturing whether a model refuses more on questions it would get wrong. Since these probabilities are latent (only binary refusal/correctness is observed), the paper estimates the correlation via a Gaussian copula: the two-pass observations (R_i, Ŵ_i) are modeled as thresholded bivariate standard normal variables, and the tetrachoric correlation ρ is recovered by maximizing the multinomial log-likelihood over the 2×2 contingency table. The Spearman coefficient is then computed as ρ_S = (6/π)arcsin(ρ/2). This rank-based formulation is key: unlike absolute-discrepancy metrics (ECE), RI is invariant to shifts in overall refusal rate, making it stable across prompting strategies. The Gaussian copula is validated against Student-t, Gumbel, and Clayton alternatives via AIC/BIC, confirming it provides the best fit-to-complexity trade-off for 2×2 data with fixed margins.

## Results

Across 16 models on SimpleQA, RI scores range from 0.12 (Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite) to 0.52 (Claude-Sonnet-4 and Claude-3.5-Haiku), with an average of ~0.35. RI demonstrates ~70% lower variability across different refusal rates than heuristic metrics (average CV of 0.09 vs. 0.22 for F-score and 0.47 for Weighted Score). RI achieves 85% correlation with AUROC computed via 100-sample P(Answering) estimation, while requiring only 2N generations versus SN generations. After removing monotonic effects of accuracy and refusal rate via isotonic regression, RI retains Kendall's W of 0.47–0.50, whereas F-score drops to 0.10–0.39 and Weighted Score to 0.25–0.32.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the SimpleQA F-score (Wei et al., 2024), RI is substantially more stable: when models are prompted to vary refusal rates, the F-score's coefficient of variation averages 0.22 while RI's is only 0.09, and the F-score's normalized difference across prompts reaches −0.71 (Qwen3-32B) versus +0.14 for RI. In ranking stability (Table 3), after removing monotonic effects of correct answer rate, F-score's Kendall's W collapses from 0.90 to 0.10, while RI actually increases from 0.47 to 0.50, confirming RI captures an intrinsic property independent of accuracy. Against APRICOT (Ulmer et al., 2024), RI reveals over-confidence that APRICOT masks: on Qwen3-32B, APRICOT yields ECE of 0.039 (suggesting near-perfect calibration), while RI of 0.34 indicates substantial miscalibration in refusal behavior. RI also requires only 2N generations versus APRICOT's calibrator training plus N generations plus N inferences, making it orders of magnitude cheaper. RI correlates at 85% with the sampling-based AUROC (P(Answering)), the only external calibration method whose reliability diagram aligns with RI's diagnosis, while linear probes and APRICOT produce misleadingly optimistic calibration curves.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of LLM calibration and factuality evaluation by proposing the Refusal Index (RI), a rank-correlation metric that directly measures the alignment between a model's refusal decisions and its answer incorrectness. It departs from refusal-rate-based heuristics (SimpleQA's F-score, Weighted Score) by arguing these conflate overall accuracy with refusal quality, and from calibration-based methods (ECE, AUROC with verbalized confidence, APRICOT, P(True)) by showing their uncertainty estimates do not reflect actual refusal probability. Methodologically, it repurposes the classical tetrachoric correlation / Gaussian copula framework to estimate Spearman rank correlation from binary observations via a lightweight two-pass evaluation, avoiding the expensive multi-sampling or auxiliary-model requirements of prior calibration approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals a practical gap: LLMs achieving high factual accuracy can still exhibit unreliable refusal behavior, and this gap is not resolved by prompting or scaling. The finding that model family (training pipeline) is a stronger predictor of refusal quality than parameter count or accuracy suggests that current training objectives do not adequately optimize for knowledge-aware abstention. Open questions include whether RI can serve as a training signal to improve refusal calibration, whether it generalizes beyond short-form factual QA to open-ended or multi-hop settings, and whether the two-pass protocol's requirement for instruction-following limits applicability to smaller or less controllable models.
