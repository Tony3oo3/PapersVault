## Problem

Existing Theory of Mind (ToM) evaluations for LLMs rely on classical Sally-Anne-style tasks with limited scenario diversity, explicit mentalizing verbs that serve as trigger words, and a focus solely on explicit belief attribution. This leaves open whether models can actually *apply* inferred mental-state knowledge to predict behavior or judge the appropriateness of observed actions in diverse, everyday settings where information asymmetries arise implicitly.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261706/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each input is a concise two-sentence story: the first sentence states a key piece of hidden information (e.g., 'The can of Pringles has moldy chips in it'), and the second shows a person acting on the associated object while implicitly unaware of that information, with no explicit percept or mentalizing verbs. For each story, the model must answer three binary questions of increasing ToM depth: (a) a yes/no mental-state question about the character's information awareness, (b) a two-way behavior-prediction choice between an 'unaware' and an 'aware' next action, and (c) a yes/no judgment of whether the 'correct' action was reasonable given the character's mental state. The full benchmark contains 1,147 stories and 3,441 questions spanning 10 scenario types (grocery stores, healthcare, locked devices, second-hand markets, etc.).

## Method

SimpleToM is constructed by first manually seeding one example story per each of 10 information-asymmetry scenarios, then prompting LLMs (GPT-4, Claude-3-Opus, GPT-4o, Claude-3.5-Sonnet) to generate diverse entity sets and three severity-graded stories per set, yielding 3,600 candidate stories. A strict human annotation pipeline—where crowdworkers must pass a rigorous qualification test (only 19% of 100 candidates did) and all three annotators must independently validate every aspect of each story—filters this down to 1,147 final stories. The key design constraint is that characters' unawareness must be implicit, forcing models to perform commonsense inferences about physical objects, spatial access, and social context rather than pattern-matching on mentalizing keywords. The paper then evaluates 22 LLMs across the three question types and probes whether inference-time interventions (mental-state reminder, system-prompt guiding, generic and ToM-specific chain-of-thought prompting) can close the observed gap, finding that only the combination of a question-specific mental-state reminder with a ToM-specific CoT prompt brings frontier models to near-perfect scores, underscoring the fragility of their applied ToM capabilities.

## Results

On SimpleToM, frontier models achieve >95% accuracy on explicit ToM (mental state inference) but suffer dramatic drops on applied ToM: GPT-4o falls from 95.6% (mental state) to 49.5% (behavior) and 15.3% (judgment); Llama-3.1-405B drops from 97.8% to 58.2% and 10.0%; Claude-3.5-Sonnet from 97.9% to 67.0% and 24.9%. Among reasoning models, o1-preview achieves the best behavior prediction (84.1%) and DeepSeek-R1 the best judgment (65.8%), yet both remain well below their own mental state scores. The overall average accuracy for the best model (o1-preview) is 79.7%, with a persistent 15–40 point gap between explicit and applied ToM across all 22 evaluated models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 22 LLMs from OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta, Mistral, Qwen, and DeepSeek-AI on SimpleToM. All frontier non-reasoning models (GPT-4o, Llama-3.1-405B, Claude-3-Opus, GPT-4, Claude-3.5-Sonnet) achieve >95% on mental state inference but collapse on applied ToM: GPT-4o scores only 49.5% on behavior and 15.3% on judgment, while Llama-3.1-405B and Claude-3-Opus score below random (10.0% and 9.6%) on judgment. Reasoning models narrow but do not close the gap: o1-preview reaches 84.1% on behavior (best) and 59.5% on judgment, while DeepSeek-R1 achieves 73.8% behavior and 65.8% judgment (best judgment). Smaller models (GPT-3.5, Qwen-2.5-7B, Claude-3-Haiku) perform poorly across all three question types, with GPT-3.5 scoring just 7.6% on behavior prediction. The central finding is not a single model winning but a systematic, model-independent gap: no non-reasoning model exceeds 67.8% on behavior or 26.7% on judgment despite >95% mental state accuracy, exposing a fundamental decoupling between explicit ToM knowledge and its implicit application.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of LLM Theory-of-Mind benchmarks (ToMi, ToM-bAbI, Hi-ToM, OpenToM, BigToM, ToMBench) by introducing two key departures: (1) replacing explicit mentalizing verbs ('sees,' 'thinks,' 'does not notice') with implicit commonsense inference over everyday scenarios, and (2) adding 'applied ToM' question types (behavior prediction and judgment) beyond the standard explicit mental-state query. The explicit-vs-applied ToM distinction is borrowed from developmental psychology (Hutchins et al., 2016; Lee et al., 2024), and the dataset construction pipeline follows the LLM-generate-then-human-verify paradigm of MacGyver (Tian et al., 2024). The ten diverse information-asymmetry scenarios (grocery stores, healthcare, locked devices, etc.) deliberately move beyond the over-reliance on classical Sally-Anne object-transfer templates that dominates prior ToM datasets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that frontier models achieve >95% on explicit mental-state inference but collapse to 15–50% on behavior prediction and 10–25% on judgment, and that neither CoT prompting nor system-prompt guidance reliably closes this gap—reveals a fundamental decoupling between 'knowing' and 'applying' social knowledge in LLMs. This has direct implications for deploying LLMs in social contexts (personal assistants, AI judges, therapeutic chatbots) where implicit ToM application, not explicit belief attribution, is what matters. Open questions include whether the gap can be closed through training (not just prompting), whether it reflects a genuine architectural limitation or a distributional artifact, and how it scales with model size and reasoning depth. The benchmark itself (1,147 stories, 3,441 questions, 10 scenario types) provides a reusable diagnostic tool for tracking progress on applied ToM.
