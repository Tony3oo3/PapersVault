## Problem

Local loss geometry in machine learning is inherently a two-operator concept involving both training and test losses, yet the literature has predominantly characterized it through single-loss Hessian spectra alone, implicitly equating spectra with geometry. This paper identifies eigenvector overlaps between train and test Hessians as the fundamental missing ingredient that determines how training perturbations route into test error, and establishes general theoretical foundations for this two-loss geometric framework.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264924/cropped_page5_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

For large-scale networks the method never forms explicit Hessians; it consumes only Hessian-vector products (computed via standard PyTorch autograd) to estimate spectral densities and eigenvector overlaps. The primary output is a two-dimensional overlap function O(λ₁, λ₂) — a kernel mapping pairs of eigenvalues from the train and test operators to the mean squared cosine angle between their eigenvectors — from which the expected test-loss increment is obtained as a weighted double integral over both spectral measures. For ridge regression the overlap function is available in closed form via self-consistent equations; for neural networks it is estimated numerically at a chosen smoothing width σ.

## Method

The paper derives a universal local fluctuation law (Theorem 1) showing that the expected test-loss increment under small training perturbations equals ½∫∫ λ₁λ₂ O(λ₁,λ₂) μ_test(dλ₁) μ_train(dλ₂), and a free transfer law (Theorem 2) proving that overlaps compose under noise as O_{A, B̂}(a,b̂) = ∫ O_{A,B}(a,b) O_{B,B̂}(b,b̂) μ_B(db) when the noise is free from the operators. Applied to anisotropic ridge regression, operator-valued free probability yields asymptotically exact overlap decompositions that resolve multiple descent as an eigenspace misalignment phenomenon rather than a spectral ill-conditioning effect, and identify O_{Σ_test,Σ_train} as the natural quantitative measure of covariate shift. For practical large-scale computation, the authors develop scalable estimators combining subspace iteration for outlier eigenspaces with a generalized kernel polynomial method using truncated Chebyshev series and Hutchinson trace estimation for the bulk, enabling overlap analysis of a ResNet-20 on CIFAR-10 with runtimes linear in model size and number of examples.

## Results

The paper's primary contribution is theoretical: it derives a universal local fluctuation law (Theorem 1) showing that expected test-loss increment under small training perturbations equals a double integral of train/test eigenvalues weighted by an eigenvector overlap kernel O(λ₁,λ₂). In ridge regression with two-scale anisotropic data (s₁²=1, s₂²=10⁻⁴), the theory predicts multiple descent peaks at critical sampling densities α=1/2 and α=1, matching simulations with d=5000 to high precision. In MLP validation (widths 5-5-5-1, tanh, MSE loss), the predicted vs. measured perturbation-induced ΔL/L₀ agrees across several orders of magnitude of input and label noise. On a ResNet-20 trained on CIFAR-10 (92.6% top-1 accuracy), the overlap function between train and balanced-test Hessians shows strong diagonal alignment, while restricting the test set to classes 0,1,2 (class imbalance) causes a large fraction of outlier energy to be lost in low-outlier and bulk spaces of the other Hessian, indicating severe train-test misalignment.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without direct method-to-method benchmarking; the 'compares_to' set is empty. Instead, the paper validates its overlap-based fluctuation law against (i) exact high-dimensional ridge regression simulations (d=5000, theory lines vs. simulation crosses in Figs. 1–3), where agreement is essentially exact in the proportional asymptotic limit, and (ii) finite-width MLP experiments (Fig. 4), where the quadratic two-loss prediction tracks measured test-loss increments across noise amplitudes spanning multiple orders of magnitude. The ResNet-20/CIFAR-10 experiment (Fig. 5) serves as a scalability demonstration rather than a baseline comparison: it shows that the KPM-based overlap estimator (built on the kernel polynomial method of Lin et al., 2016) runs in a few hours on commodity hardware with runtimes linear in model size, and that class imbalance produces a qualitatively distinct overlap pattern (strong misalignment) compared to the balanced case (strong diagonal alignment). The paper positions its overlap framework as complementary to, rather than competing with, existing spectrum-only analyses (e.g., Mel & Ganguli 2021; Chen & Mei 2022), arguing that those prior works implicitly attribute multiple descent to Hessian ill-conditioning, whereas the overlap decomposition shows it is governed by eigenspace misalignment.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant single-loss Hessian spectrum paradigm (Sagun et al., Ghorbani et al., Pennington & Bahri) by arguing that local loss geometry is fundamentally a two-operator concept requiring eigenvector overlaps in addition to spectra. It draws on operator-valued free probability (Mingo & Speicher; Potters & Bouchaud) to derive exact asymptotic overlap formulas, generalizes the kernel polynomial method (Lin et al., 2016) to compute overlap functionals between matrix pairs, and adapts the eigenvector-consistency framework from spiked random matrix models (Johnstone, Paul, Benaych-Georges & Nadakuditi) to learning-theoretic questions. In the multiple descent literature (Chen et al., Mel & Ganguli, Li & Wei), the paper explicitly corrects spectrum-only explanations by showing that peaks are governed by eigenspace misalignment rather than Hessian ill-conditioning alone.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The overlap function provides a single geometric quantity that unifies explanations of covariate shift and multiple descent, offering a diagnostic for predicting when a domain change will help or hurt beyond what spectral analysis can capture. The scalable KPM-based estimators make overlap analysis feasible at modern network scale, as demonstrated on ResNet-20/CIFAR-10, though the deep-network application remains preliminary (single architecture, single dataset, exploratory class-imbalance experiment). Key open questions include tracking Hessian overlaps dynamically through training, extending the local quadratic theory to regimes where it breaks down, and developing alignment-aware optimization that explicitly encourages train-test eigenvector alignment to improve generalization.
