## Problem

Personalized medicine requires finding optimal treatment regimens conditioned on individual patient features, but ground-truth clinical evaluations are infeasible in humans, forcing reliance on surrogate models (digital twins, ML predictors) that systematically fail on out-of-distribution patient populations due to enrollment biases in clinical trials. Existing optimization methods either assume access to the surrogate model architecture or treat the objective as easily evaluable, leaving a gap for principled black-box optimization under distribution shift where the surrogate is an opaque, potentially miscalibrated proxy.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a patient conditioning vector z (e.g., pharmacogenetic variables, viral mutations, cancer genomics), a black-box surrogate model f̂(x;z) trained on a source population, a source design dataset D_src of historically observed treatments, and domain-specific prior knowledge synthesized via tool-calling over medical textbooks, knowledge graphs (HetiNet, PrimeKG), and clinical databases (COSMIC, GDSC, DepMap). The LLM (gpt-4o-mini, zero-shot, no fine-tuning) is prompted with the task description, patient features, prior knowledge statement, and a running table of previously proposed designs with their LEON scores. Output is a single personalized treatment design x* (e.g., warfarin dose in mg/week, antiretroviral drug combination, cancer therapy strategy) selected from 2048 surrogate evaluations, represented in natural language and embedded via text-embedding-3-small for equivalence-class assignment.

## Method

LEON formulates personalized medicine as a constrained conditional black-box optimization problem with two novel constraints: (1) a 1-Wasserstein distance bound enforced via an adversarial source critic c* (a 2-layer FC network with Lipschitz constraint via ℓ∞-norm parameter clipping ≤ 0.01) that limits extrapolation against the surrogate, and (2) an upper bound on the ∼-coarse-grained entropy of the design distribution, encouraging the LLM to converge on high-certainty proposals. A key theoretical result (Lemma 4.2) shows the optimal distribution collapses to placing all mass on the best design within each equivalence class, reducing the problem to a Boltzmann-like distribution over classes parameterized by two Lagrange multipliers: λ (source-critic certainty) and μ (LLM certainty). μ is estimated empirically each iteration via linear regression on the fractional occupancies of equivalence classes observed in a batch of 32 LLM samples, while λ is updated by gradient descent on the Lagrangian dual without requiring gradients from the black-box functions. In practice, LEON operates as 'optimization by prompting': the LLM proposes design batches, designs are clustered into equivalence classes via k-means on text embeddings, scored by μ[f̂(x;z) + λc*(x)], and fed back as prompt context, with a reflection step where the LLM analyzes recent scores to refine its sampling strategy.

## Results

LEON achieves an average rank of 1.2 across 5 personalized medicine optimization tasks, outperforming all 10 baselines. On the four tasks where it wins, LEON improves over the best baseline by 3.6 months TTNTD on Breast cancer (72.43 vs. 69.90 for GA), 3.4 months on Lung cancer (32.71 vs. 29.29 for Sim. Anneal), 0.02 copies/mL on HIV viral load (4.50 vs. 4.52 for Grad.), and 10.8 NLL on ADR (12.4 vs. 23.2 for BO-qEI). LEON also surpasses the Human baseline (the treatment patients actually received) on Warfarin, Breast, and Lung tasks, indicating it proposes treatments better than those retrospectively administered by clinicians.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LEON outperforms all six compared baselines on 4 of 5 tasks (Warfarin, HIV, Breast, Lung), losing only on ADR where the Majority baseline exploits severe class imbalance. Among LLM-based methods, Eureka (rank 6.8) is the strongest competitor but still trails LEON by 8.95 months on Breast TTNTD (63.48 vs. 72.43) and 7.61 months on Lung TTNTD (25.10 vs. 32.71); OPRO (rank 7.0) and LLAMBO (rank 7.0) perform even worse, with LLAMBO particularly weak on Warfarin (3.28 vs. 1.36 RMSE). Among traditional optimizers, BO-qEI (rank 3.4) is the best non-LLM method, matching LEON on Warfarin (1.36 vs. 1.36) but falling short on Breast (67.05 vs. 72.43) and Lung (27.97 vs. 32.71). Sim. Annealing (rank 5.0), CMA-ES (rank 6.2), and GA (rank 5.2) all underperform LEON by 3–10 months on cancer TTNTD tasks. Notably, LEON surpasses the Human baseline (actual clinician-prescribed treatments) on Warfarin, Breast, and Lung, suggesting the optimizer identifies superior regimens than those retrospectively administered.

## Relation to Prior Work

LEON sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) LLM-based black-box optimization via iterative prompting (OPRO, Eureka, LLAMBO), which the authors adopt as the stochastic proposal engine; (2) adversarial source-critic methods from WGAN and prior GAN-based optimization work (Yao et al., 2024/2025) that bound the 1-Wasserstein distance between proposed designs and historically observed designs to limit surrogate extrapolation; and (3) entropy-based uncertainty estimation for LLMs (Kuhn et al., 2023; Farquhar et al., 2024), repurposed here as a 'certainty' signal that upweights designs the LLM proposes consistently. The key departure from prior LLM-optimizer work is the conditional (patient-specific) formulation under explicit distribution shift, combined with a mathematically derived Lagrangian solution that dynamically adjusts both the distribution-shift penalty (λ) and the entropy-based certainty weight (μ) without fine-tuning the LLM.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a zero-shot consumer LLM (gpt-4o-mini), augmented with domain-specific prior knowledge via tool-calling, can outperform both traditional black-box optimizers and other LLM-based methods on five real-world treatment-design tasks, achieving an average rank of 1.2 and surpassing the actual treatments patients received in retrospective data. This is practically significant because it suggests that clinical optimization need not rely on accurate surrogate models for out-of-distribution populations—a persistent barrier in personalized medicine. Key limitations include reliance on simulation-based benchmarks (no prospective clinical validation), sensitivity to the quality and accuracy of injected prior knowledge, and evaluation on a single LLM architecture. Open questions include whether the entropy-guided framework generalizes to sequential treatment decisions, how to integrate physician oversight, and whether the approach holds under the heterogeneity and rare-disease subtypes of real clinical practice.
