## Problem

Pre-trained vision-language models like CLIP remain highly vulnerable to adversarial perturbations, yet existing defenses either modify prompts (ignoring the encoder's internal structure), fully fine-tune weights (overfitting in few-shot regimes), or require large held-out datasets for zero-shot robustness. This paper asks: what is the most effective way to achieve adversarial robustness on pre-trained VLMs in few-shot downstream settings without altering their pre-trained weights?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262484/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes RGB images (processed by a ViT encoder at standard patch resolutions) paired with hand-crafted text prompt templates (e.g., 'a photo of a {CLASS}') and produces class predictions via cosine similarity in the joint CLIP embedding space. During training, only 1–16 labeled samples per class are available, and adversarial perturbations are generated via 2-step PGD under the l∞ norm (ε=1/255). The learned output is a set of binary masks applied to the multi-head self-attention layers of the vision encoder (~20% of its parameters), which are stored as lightweight binary values rather than updated weights.

## Method

AdvMask learns binary masks over the MHSA layers of CLIP's ViT encoder via a min-max optimization: the inner loop generates adversarial examples with PGD, and the outer loop updates real-valued mask parameters using the Straight-Through Estimator to approximate the non-differentiable binarization (threshold α=0.005). The key training signal is the Layer-wise Adaptive Feature Alignment (LAFA) loss, which aligns intermediate-layer features from adversarial samples to their clean counterparts at every transformer block, weighted by each sample's predictive confidence to prevent noisy alignment targets from destabilizing optimization under scarce data. The final objective combines adversarial cross-entropy with λ·LAFA (λ=50.0), jointly enforcing prediction-level robustness and feature-level consistency. Pre-trained weights remain entirely frozen; only the binary masks are optimized, making the approach parameter-efficient (training time ~0.27s/batch, memory ~1581 MB vs. 7724 MB for full fine-tuning) while preserving the base model's generalization.

## Results

On the base-to-new generalization benchmark (16-shot, averaged over 11 datasets), AdvMask achieves 43.6% adversarial accuracy on base classes and 26.1% on unseen new classes, with a harmonic mean of 41.9, substantially outperforming the strongest prompt-tuning baseline FAP (38.0% / 21.9%, H=37.6). In the zero-shot robustness setting (tuned on TinyImageNet, tested on 10 unseen datasets), AdvMask using only 16-shot (3.2% of source data) reaches 42.0% clean and 19.4% adversarial accuracy, surpassing TGA-ZSR with the full source dataset (38.6% clean) on clean accuracy while remaining competitive on adversarial accuracy (19.4% vs. 22.9%). Across all few-shot settings (1–16 shots), AdvMask consistently improves adversarial accuracy by 5–10+ points over FAP while maintaining or exceeding clean accuracy, with the gap most pronounced in low-shot regimes (e.g., 1-shot: 18.4% vs. 9.3% adversarial accuracy).

## Baselines & Comparisons

AdvMask decisively outperforms zero-shot CLIP, which collapses from ~62% clean to ~3% adversarial accuracy, by raising adversarial accuracy to 43.6% (base) and 26.1% (new) while retaining competitive clean accuracy. Against the strongest prompt-tuning baseline FAP (Zhou et al., 2024), AdvMask gains +5.6 points on base-class adversarial accuracy (43.6% vs. 38.0%) and +4.2 points on new-class adversarial accuracy (26.1% vs. 21.9%), at the cost of a marginal 1-point drop in base-class clean accuracy (69.5% vs. 70.5%). AdvMask also surpasses AdvVP (Mao et al., 2023), AdvVLP, and AdvMaPLe (Khattak et al., 2023) by 9–29 points in adversarial accuracy, confirming that prompt-level adaptation alone is insufficient for robustness. In the zero-shot robustness setting, AdvMask with only 3.2% of the source data (16-shot) achieves 42.0% clean accuracy, exceeding TGA-ZSR (Yu et al., 2024) which uses 100% of the source data (38.6%), though TGA-ZSR retains a ~3.5-point edge in adversarial accuracy (22.9% vs. 19.4%). In the 16-shot few-shot comparison with full fine-tuning (Table 17), AdvMask matches full fine-tuning on clean accuracy (67.3% vs. 67.7%) while achieving higher adversarial accuracy (47.1% vs. 43.9%) at roughly 3× lower training time and 5× lower memory usage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mask-tuning paradigm (Zheng et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2020) from downstream task adaptation to adversarial robustness, introducing the concept of a 'robust neural pathway'—a subnetwork within a pre-trained VLM that inherently resists adversarial perturbations. It departs from the dominant adversarial prompt-tuning line (FAP, AdvVP, AdvMaPLe, TGA-ZSR), which modifies input-level prompts or fully fine-tunes weights, by instead learning binary masks over the vision encoder's multi-head attention layers while keeping all pre-trained weights frozen. The LAFA loss, which aligns intermediate-layer features between clean and adversarial samples with adaptive confidence weighting, is a novel training objective not present in prior adversarial tuning methods that supervise only the final output space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

AdvMask offers a practical, parameter-efficient route to adversarial robustness for VLMs in data-scarce regimes (1–16 shots per class), where full fine-tuning overfits and prompt tuning underperforms. The finding that a small binary mask over ~20% of encoder parameters (MHSA layers) can approach the robustness of full-parameter methods trained on 100% of source data (TGA-ZSR) suggests that adversarial vulnerability is concentrated in a sparse set of parameters, a structural insight with broader implications for efficient robustness. Open questions include whether the learned masks are truly task-agnostic or merely dataset-specific, how the approach scales to much larger encoders (ViT-H, ViT-G), and whether the LAFA alignment objective generalizes to non-classification tasks (e.g., detection, segmentation) without task-specific redesign.
