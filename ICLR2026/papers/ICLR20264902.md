## Problem

Existing morphology-control co-design methods adopt a single-level formulation that treats the control policy as fixed when updating morphology, ignoring that the controller must adapt to structural changes. This misalignment between morphology updates and the controller's best response leads to unstable optimization and degraded final performance. The paper addresses this gap by recasting the co-design problem as a Stackelberg game where the morphology designer (leader) explicitly anticipates the controller's (follower's) adaptive response.

## Input / Output

The method starts from a bare-bones tree-structured morphology (a root node with a few limb branches) and iteratively edits it over T leader steps using discrete topology-altering actions (adding/removing limbs, adjusting lengths, attaching joints) under constraints on tree depth, branching factor, and joint degrees of freedom. The terminal morphology then defines the follower's action space (available joints/actuators), state space (joint positions, velocities, forces), and underlying MuJoCo dynamics, on which the control policy is trained. The output is a task-specific evolved body (e.g., arm-like structures for pushing, leg-like limbs for locomotion) paired with a trained motor-control policy, all within a MuJoCo simulation environment.

## Method

Stackelberg PPO formulates morphology-control co-design as a Phase-Separated Stackelberg Markov Game, where the leader acts for T steps to edit morphology and the follower then optimizes control on the resulting body. Because the leader-follower interface is non-differentiable (discrete topology edits), the paper derives Stackelberg policy gradients via the log-derivative trick, yielding a surrogate (Theorem 1) that expresses the cross-derivative ∇_{θ^L,θ^F} J^F as an importance-weighted advantage estimator, bypassing the need to differentiate through the discrete transition. The Hessian in the implicit differentiation term is approximated by the Fisher information matrix (via KL divergence) to ensure positive semi-definiteness and numerical stability, and the leader's full Stackelberg gradient is computed efficiently using conjugate gradient for Hessian-vector products. PPO's likelihood-ratio clipping is adapted to the Stackelberg surrogate to constrain policy divergence and stabilize training under large policy shifts, with a data-dependent gradient normalization preventing the response-induced term from dominating the direct gradient.

## Results

Stackelberg PPO achieves the highest final episode reward across all nine morphology-control co-design environments, yielding an average +20.66% improvement over the strongest baseline (BodyGen) and +32.02% on complex 3D tasks (Crawler, Stepper-Regular, Stepper-Hard, Pusher). On the most challenging 3D manipulation task (Pusher), it scores 3462.77 ± 368.09 versus 2779.95 ± 509.18 for BodyGen. In terms of sample efficiency, Stackelberg PPO reaches performance thresholds with approximately 39% fewer environment samples than BodyGen, while incurring only a modest +13% wall-clock training overhead (33.07 h vs. 29.43 h on 10 CPU cores + 1 A100 GPU).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Stackelberg PPO outperforms all four baselines across every environment. Against the closest competitor BodyGen (the PPO-based transformer co-design framework it builds upon), the gap is most pronounced on 3D tasks: +21.4% on Crawler (11048 vs. 9099), +53.9% on Stepper-Regular (7215 vs. 4686), +28.1% on Stepper-Hard (6004 vs. 4685), and +24.6% on Pusher (3463 vs. 2780). Against Transform2Act (concurrent RL co-design with GNNs), the advantage is even larger, reaching +180% on Crawler and +241% on Pusher. The evolutionary baselines NGE and ESS are substantially outperformed, with ESS achieving only ~6% of Stackelberg PPO's score on Crawler (632 vs. 11048) and failing to reach any performance threshold within the training budget on most tasks. The paper also shows that the performance gap widens as the morphology design space grows larger, confirming that the Stackelberg gradient's ability to anticipate controller adaptation becomes increasingly valuable in high-dimensional structural search.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the morphology-control co-design literature (Sims 1994, BodyGen 2025, Transform2Act 2022) and the Stackelberg reinforcement learning line (Stackelberg DDPG/MADDPG, Yang et al. 2023), but departs from both in a key way: prior co-design methods adopt a shared-objective formulation that treats the control policy as fixed during morphology updates, while prior Stackelberg RL methods assume alternating leader-follower actions with differentiable interfaces. Here, the authors introduce a Phase-Separated Stackelberg Markov Game where the leader acts for T steps before the follower acts, and the interface is non-differentiable (discrete topology edits). They derive new Stackelberg policy gradients using the log-derivative trick to bypass this non-differentiability, then integrate PPO-style clipping into the resulting surrogate for stability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The work provides a principled, gradient-based mechanism for incorporating controller adaptation into morphology optimization, yielding +20.66% average and +32.02% on complex 3D tasks over the strongest baseline, with ~39% fewer samples needed to reach target performance. The ablation showing that SID without PPO clipping collapses (e.g., 35.77 vs. 11047.90 on Crawler) underscores that the Stackelberg surrogate is inherently unstable without trust-region constraints, a practical insight for anyone extending bilevel RL. Open questions include sim-to-real transfer under unmodeled hardware constraints, scalability to higher-dimensional or soft-body morphologies, and whether the phase-separated Stackelberg formulation generalizes to other leader-follower co-design problems beyond robotics.
