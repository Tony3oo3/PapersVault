## Problem

Existing MLA decoding kernels (e.g., FlashMLA) rely exclusively on the absorb formulation, which is compute-bound and therefore cannot exploit data reuse from shared prefixes such as system prompts or tree-of-thought branches. While the naive formulation is more compute-efficient when KV-cache is shared across multiple queries, it is memory-bound and unsuitable for non-shared portions. No prior MLA kernel combines both formulations to capture the strengths of each in their respective operating regimes.

## Input / Output

The decode kernel receives query vectors after the W_Qb up-projection and RoPE layers, a shared KV-cache stored in uncompressed multi-head form (C_K, C_V), a non-shared KV-cache in compressed latent form (C_N for noPE, C_R for RoPE), and the KV up-projection matrices W_KVb1 and W_KVb2. It produces a single attention output by computing partial results from both the naive (shared) and absorb (non-shared) components and merging them via a log-sum-exp (LSE) combine operation. The key structural constraint is that the shared prefix must be expanded to uncompressed form during prefill, incurring only ~3% additional HBM overhead relative to the absorb baseline.

## Method

TyphoonMLA partitions the decode attention computation into two stages based on arithmetic intensity: a naive stage that processes the shared KV-cache in uncompressed form (exploiting data reuse across the batch to reduce FLOPs per query) and an absorb stage that processes the non-shared KV-cache in compressed latent form (minimizing HBM bandwidth). The naive stage computes softmax(Q_K C_K^T) C_V directly, while the absorb stage up-projects queries via W_KVb1, computes attention against the latent caches, and down-projects via W_KVb2. Partial outputs are merged using a CombineLSE epilogue analogous to FlashAttention's split-K reduction. A batch-size threshold B_θ (derived from hardware throughput and bandwidth) determines when the naive stage becomes beneficial; below this threshold, TyphoonMLA falls back to an absorb-only kernel to avoid memory-bound penalties. The method is mathematically equivalent to standard MLA, requires no retraining, and is compatible with PagedAttention, RadixAttention, and tensor/sequence parallelism.

## Results

TyphoonMLA achieves up to 3× attention throughput speedup on Ascend NPUs and up to 3.24× on GPUs compared to absorb-only baselines, with the largest gains observed for Kimi K2 (fewer attention heads) and longer system prompts (e.g., 26k-token Claude-4 prompt). End-to-end, TyphoonMLA boosts token generation rate for DeepSeek-v3 from 1.01 to 1.48 kToken/s (1.48×) at batch size 128 with the 26k-token system prompt, while incurring only ~3% additional HBM footprint. The method falls back to absorb-only at batch sizes below a hardware-dependent threshold (B_θ ≈ 61 for DeepSeek-v3 on Ascend NPU), ensuring no regression at small batch sizes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TyphoonMLA is compared against two absorb-only baselines: FlashMLA (Li, 2025) and FlashInfer's absorb-only MLA kernel (Ye, 2025). On GPUs, TyphoonMLA outperforms both across all batch sizes (64–1024), datasets (MMLU, GSM8K, SimpleQA), and system prompt lengths, reaching up to 3.24× higher attention throughput; the gap widens with longer shared prefixes and smaller head counts (Kimi K2 > DeepSeek-v3). On Ascend NPUs, the analogous baselines are TorchNPU PagedAttentionMLA and a custom CATLASS absorb-only kernel, and TyphoonMLA again wins consistently with 1.2×–3× speedups. The latency breakdown confirms the theoretical prediction: at batch size 1024, the shared-prefix attention portion is 3.3× faster in TyphoonMLA's naive stage (1.63 ms) versus the absorb baseline's equivalent (5.37 ms), while the non-shared portion remains identical. At batch sizes below the threshold (~64), TyphoonMLA falls back to absorb-only and matches the baseline exactly, so it never underperforms.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the MLA architecture introduced by DeepSeek-v2/v3 and Kimi K2, which admits two functionally equivalent but computationally distinct kernel formulations (naive and absorb). Prior prefix-sharing work (Hydrogen, SGLang, FlashInfer-Cascade, FastTree, DEFT, FlashForge) targets MHA/GQA kernels where decode is memory-bound, and thus cannot be directly applied to MLA, whose decode stage is compute-bound. Existing MLA decode kernels (FlashMLA, ThunderMLA) are absorb-only and agnostic to shared prefixes. TyphoonMLA departs from both lines by identifying that the naive formulation becomes computationally superior in the shared (compute-bound) portion when data reuse is present, and fusing the two formulations into a single hybrid kernel.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

With MLA now underpinning frontier models like DeepSeek-v3 and Kimi K2, a 3× attention speedup at only 3% HBM overhead is a practically significant efficiency gain for large-scale inference deployments, and the method integrates seamlessly into existing serving stacks (vLLM, SGLang, PagedAttention, RadixAttention) without retraining. Open questions include how the batch-size threshold B_θ shifts with architectural changes (e.g., different LoRA ranks or head counts), whether the hybrid approach extends to other low-rank attention variants, and how it interacts with emerging quantization or sparsity techniques. The paper does not explore multi-level or non-uniform prefix trees in depth, leaving room for further kernel-level optimization.
