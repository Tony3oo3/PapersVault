## Problem

While LLM-based multi-agent systems are increasingly deployed for collaborative decision-making, their susceptibility to collective cognitive biases—specifically the Mandela effect, where socially reinforced false memories overwrite correct knowledge—remains unexplored. Prior work treats hallucination as a stateless, one-shot failure or studies simple conformity, overlooking the memory-consolidation and persistent-recall dynamics that define the Mandela effect. No benchmark existed to systematically measure how multi-agent interactions implant and solidify shared false memories.

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance pairs a multiple-choice question (from 20 BIG-Bench Hard tasks spanning History, Misconceptions, General Knowledge, and Domain-Specific Knowledge) with a generated social context: a multi-agent conversation in which other agents deliver specious evidence targeting a pre-selected primary distractor. In long-term protocols, the agent's initial response is distilled into a memory summary and re-queried in a fresh context to test belief persistence. Outputs are scored via three metrics: error rate, reality shift rate (fraction of baseline-correct answers overwritten per protocol), and maximal reality shift rate (union across all four implantation protocols).

## Method

MANBENCH introduces five interaction protocols: a Baseline Reality Protocol (isolated questioning) plus four implantation protocols that cross two axes—group composition (Generic Group with undifferentiated agents vs. Role-based Group with five specialized roles: Error Conclusion Initiator, Detail Support Provider, Group Consensus Reinforcer, Authority Endorser, Questioning Compromiser) and memory timescale (Short-term in-context vs. Long-term with a consolidation-then-retrieval step). For mitigation, the paper proposes two prompt-level defenses: cognitive anchoring (primacy of internal knowledge, skepticism toward external claims, burden of proof for belief change) and source scrutiny (presumption of influence, narrative deconstruction, credibility as an output), which together achieve a 74.40% average reduction in the Mandela effect. A model-level defense uses supervised fine-tuning on a balanced 2,000-example dataset combining a resilience set (reasoning chains that resist false narratives) and a cooperative set (corrective and enriching guidance scenarios), preventing the model from either blindly accepting or dogmatically rejecting all social input.

## Results

All 13 evaluated LLMs are susceptible to the Mandela effect, with error rates increasing substantially under social influence protocols. GPT-5, the strongest model, has a baseline error rate of 17.63% but more than doubles to 41.59% under the Role-based Short-term Protocol; Qwen3-235B's error rate surges from 25.48% to 74.75% under the same protocol. The reality shift rate (measuring correct-to-incorrect memory overwrites) ranges from 1.67% (GPT-5, long-term) to 99.47% (Llama3.1-8B, role-based short-term), revealing that some models solidify false memories into long-term beliefs while others self-correct. Prompt-level defenses (cognitive anchoring and source scrutiny) achieve an average 74.40% reduction in the Mandela effect compared to the undefended baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods but instead contrasts models and protocols against each other. Role-based group composition consistently induces a stronger Mandela effect than generic groups: for Claude 4 Sonnet, the reality shift rate rises from 15.45% (GS) to 35.21% (RS), and for GPT-4o from 46.04% to 55.95%. On memory timescale, GPT-5 and Llama3.3-70B show strong self-correction (GPT-5's σ drops from 31.03% to 1.67% from short-term to long-term), while Claude 3.5 Haiku and the Qwen3 series retain false memories persistently (Claude 3.5 Haiku: 63.67% → 55.63%; Qwen3-235B: 68.69% → 56.85%). Among mitigation strategies, cognitive anchoring outperforms source scrutiny on short-term protocols (e.g., GPT-4o σ^RS: 56.0% → 17.0% vs. 25.2%) but both converge on long-term protocols. Model-level SFT on a balanced resilience + cooperative dataset reduces Llama3's σ^RS from 99.5% to 21.5% while maintaining the ability to accept correct guidance (σ^C = 1.1%), whereas training on the resilience set alone causes the model to dogmatically reject all social input (σ^C surges to 38.5%). Scaling does not uniformly help: Qwen3 shows an inverse scaling law with σ_max rising from 89.3% (8B) to 92.2% (235B), while Claude 3.5 improves from 72.0% (Haiku) to 39.6% (Sonnet).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by transplanting the human cognitive psychology concept of the Mandela effect (collective false memory) into the LLM multi-agent setting, explicitly distinguishing it from prior work on LLM conformity, sycophancy, and hallucination. While prior studies on social influence in LLMs (Weng et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025) examine short-term in-context compliance, and hallucination research treats errors as stateless one-shot failures, this paper introduces the dimension of memory consolidation and persistent recall as the defining axis. It also departs from multi-agent robustness work (Huang et al., 2025) that focuses on malicious agents injecting errors, instead studying how persuasive social narratives—absent any adversarial intent—can overwrite correct memories. The benchmark is built on BIG-Bench Hard tasks repurposed with LLM-selected primary distractors, creating a controlled substrate for measuring reality shifts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The findings have direct implications for deploying LLM multi-agent systems in high-stakes domains (medical diagnosis, contract review, fact-checking) where collectively reinforced false memories could have severe consequences. The inverse scaling law observed in Qwen3 (larger models more vulnerable) challenges the default assumption that scaling improves robustness, and the 'suspicion-induced vigilance' effect (very large groups paradoxically reduce the Mandela effect) suggests a latent detection capability that could be harnessed in defense design. Key limitations include the multiple-choice format that simplifies real-world unstructured dialogue, and the controlled experimental setting that may overestimate or underestimate effects in open-ended collaborative tasks. Open questions include whether the findings generalize to long-form debate, dynamic role changes, and whether critic-agent architectures can provide more adaptive defenses than the prompt-level and SFT approaches tested here.
