## Problem

Current evaluation of uncertainty estimation (UE) methods for natural language generation relies almost exclusively on selective prediction over QA datasets using approximate correctness functions (ROUGE, BLEU, LLM-as-a-judge) that exhibit substantial mutual disagreement. This disagreement enables 'correctness hacking,' where apparent UE performance can be inflated by selecting a favorable metric and threshold, undermining the reliability of empirical comparisons. The paper identifies the lack of marginalization over correctness function parameters as a leading cause of these evaluation biases.

## Input / Output

Each UE method takes a prompt x and model parameters w and produces a scalar uncertainty estimate ũ(x,w;θ_u), which is correlated via AUROC against a risk indicator. The risk indicator varies by task: binary correctness labels averaged over K diverse LLM-as-a-judge invocations (SP-MoJI), exact pass/fail unit-test results for code and constrained generation, binary OOD labels, or continuous perturbation strength. The final output is an Elo rating per UE method, computed by treating each dataset/model risk-correlation experiment as a pairwise game with standard Elo update rules.

## Method

The paper proposes four complementary remedies to the identified pitfalls. First, SP-MoJI computes AUROC separately for each of K diverse LLM-as-a-judge invocations (varying model family, prompt template, and sampling temperature) and averages the results, marginalizing over judge-specific bias and stochastic sampling noise. Second, it introduces structured tasks—BigCodeBench code completion and COLLIE constrained text generation—where correctness is verified exactly via unit tests or constraint checks, eliminating approximate correctness entirely. Third, it adds OOD detection (using Known-Unknowns and SQuADv2 unanswerable questions) and perturbation detection (controlled word-shuffling) as risk indicators grounded in the univariate classification literature. Finally, it aggregates all results via an Elo rating system where each dataset/model pair constitutes a game, enabling probabilistic interpretation of relative performance and indirect comparisons across partially overlapping experimental suites.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative finding is that adversarially selecting a correctness function can inflate a UE method's Top-3 membership frequency by up to +37.5 percentage points (Min Token Log-Probability: 0.125→0.500) and +31.9 points (Perplexity: 0.125→0.444) on QA benchmarks, demonstrating severe evaluation vulnerability. Using SP-MoJI with 4 diverse judge models reduces the standard deviation of the AUROC performance estimator by approximately 2× compared to a single judge (from ~0.04 to ~0.02). The Elo rating aggregation (Fig. 3) reveals no single UE method dominates across all task partitions: Semantic Entropy (LN) leads on QA selective prediction, while EigenScore and G-NLL excel on code generation, and simple sequence-length heuristics remain competitive outside the QA domain.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 14 UE methods (Predictive Entropy ±LN, Semantic Entropy ±LN, G-NLL, Perplexity, EigenScore, TokenSAR, SentenceSAR, SAR, P(True), Min Token Log-Probability, and two sequence-length heuristics) across QA, code completion, constrained text generation, OOD, and perturbation tasks. Semantic Entropy (LN) is the most robustly top-ranked method under the SP-MoJI reference protocol (56.2% Top-3 frequency), while G-NLL (37.5%) and SentenceSAR (43.8%) follow. However, the correctness-hacking experiment (Table 2) reveals that Perplexity and Min Token Log-Probability are most vulnerable to favorable correctness-function selection, gaining +31.9 and +37.5 points respectively, whereas Semantic Entropy (LN) is the least affected (+10.4 points). On structured tasks (BigCodeBench, COLLIE), EigenScore and G-NLL outperform entropy-based methods for longer sequences, while simple sequence-length heuristics remain competitive outside QA. All methods perform poorly on pretrained (non-instruction-tuned) models, as evidenced by the high Elo of the random baseline on that partition. The authors conclude there is no one-size-fits-all UE method, with task type and model type both shifting the relative ranking substantially.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a meta-evaluation study that builds on the risk-uncertainty correlation framework from univariate classification (Kotelevskii & Panov, 2025; Lahlou et al., 2023) and adapts it to NLG, where the correctness function is inherently approximate and parametric. It extends LLM-as-a-judge (Zheng et al., 2023) by proposing SP-MoJI, which marginalizes over multiple judge models, prompts, and sampling temperatures to reduce evaluation bias. Concurrently with Santilli et al. (2024/2025), who diagnose spurious interactions between uncertainty scores and answer evaluation metrics, this paper goes further by proposing concrete remedies: exact-correctness structured tasks (BigCodeBench, COLLIE), OOD/perturbation detection as alternative risk indicators, and Elo-based aggregation inspired by LLM arena evaluations (Chiang et al., 2024).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The correctness-hacking result demonstrates that the apparent ranking of UE methods can be substantially manipulated by selecting an opportune correctness function, undermining confidence in prior comparative claims across the NLG-UE literature. The finding that simple heuristics (sequence length, perplexity) remain competitive with complex semantic-entropy-based methods—especially outside QA—challenges the implicit assumption that more sophisticated uncertainty measures are necessary. Open questions include how these evaluation principles extend to longer-generation settings (CoT, multi-agent), whether base (pretrained) models require fundamentally separate evaluation protocols, and whether perturbation-detection results generalize across perturbation types and strengths.
