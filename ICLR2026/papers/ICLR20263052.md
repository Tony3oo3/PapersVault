## Problem

Density ratio estimation methods are highly sensitive to hyperparameter choices, and standard cross-validation-based model selection is computationally expensive, inapplicable to certain methods (e.g., Kernel Mean Matching), and fundamentally limited to selecting a single model from a pre-trained sequence. No existing aggregation or averaging method for DRE can guarantee fast convergence rates when the individual models in the sequence are suboptimal, even as sample size grows to infinity.

## Input / Output

The method takes K pre-trained DRE models (each trained with a distinct hyperparameter setting, e.g., a grid of regularization parameters λ ∈ {10⁻⁶, …, 10⁴}) along with the combined labeled sample set z = {(x_m, 1)} ∪ {(x'_n, −1)} from distributions p and q. It outputs an aggregated density ratio estimator β̂(x) = g(Σ α_k f_k(x)) where the aggregation weights α = Ĝ⁻¹r̂ are computed analytically from an empirical Gram matrix and inner-product vector, with all Hessian terms ∇²ℓ(y, f(x)) obtained in closed form at zero additional computational cost. The aggregation operates in the binary-classifier space (before applying the link function g), which allows the combined estimator to approximate density ratios not achievable by any single model in the sequence.

## Method

The approach reformulates DRE as binary classification via a Bregman divergence framework (following Menon & Ong, 2016), then bounds the Bregman divergence between the aggregated estimator and the true density ratio by a Hessian-weighted norm (following Marteau-Ferey et al., 2019). The aggregation weights are obtained by solving a least-squares problem in this norm, yielding the closed-form solution α = G⁻¹r where G is the empirical Gram matrix of the K classifiers weighted by the Hessian of the loss at the first model, and r is the vector of inner products between each classifier and the target. The key theoretical contribution is proving minimax-optimal convergence rates of order (M+N)^{−(2rα+α)/(2rα+α+1)} by constructing an admissible geometric sequence of regularization parameters via Lepskii's balancing principle and applying self-concordance-based concentration bounds. This is the first provably minimax-optimal parameter choice procedure for DRE, and unlike cross-validation it requires training only K models (vs. K×n for n-fold CV) while remaining stable as the number of suboptimal models increases.

## Results

On 10 geometric Gaussian-mixture datasets (50-dim), the proposed aggregation method reduces twice the Bregman divergence error to 10.683 (KuLSIF), 11.529 (Exp), 11.002 (LR), and 11.348 (SQ), compared to 11.205, 13.392, 11.400, and 11.810 for cross-validation-based model selection. On large-scale domain adaptation, aggregation improves target classification accuracy on MiniDomainNet (e.g., 0.535 vs 0.528 for KuLSIF), Amazon Reviews (0.795 vs 0.787 for KuLSIF), and HHAR (0.780 vs 0.737 for KuLSIF), setting new state-of-the-art on MiniDomainNet and Amazon Reviews. The method also requires training n-fold fewer models than 10-fold CV (e.g., 1 vs 10 models per hyperparameter setting), offering a clear computational advantage.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed aggregation method consistently outperforms all four baselines across every evaluated setting. Against cross-validation (the standard model selection approach), aggregation reduces Bregman divergence error by 0.5–1.9 points on geometric datasets (e.g., 10.683 vs 11.205 for KuLSIF; 11.529 vs 13.392 for Exp) and improves domain adaptation accuracy on all three modalities (image, text, time-series). Against Bayesian Model Averaging (Fragoso et al., 2018; Yeung et al., 2005), aggregation achieves lower error on all four DRE methods (e.g., 10.683 vs 11.119 for KuLSIF, 11.529 vs 12.972 for Exp). Against Super Learner (Wu & Benkeser, 2024), the gap is similar (10.683 vs 11.009 for KuLSIF, 11.529 vs 12.386 for Exp). Against the model averaging baseline of Raza & Samothrakis (2019) in Ablation 3, aggregation also wins on all 10 geometric datasets (e.g., 8.532 vs 8.553 on c3,d1.70; 9.421 vs 10.357 on c3,d1.46). Notably, while BMA and Super Learner exhibit severe performance degradation as the number of suboptimal models increases (Fig. 2 Right), the proposed method remains stable, a property attributed to its minimax-optimal convergence guarantee.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: the Bregman divergence framework for DRE (Sugiyama et al., 2012b; Menon & Ong, 2016), self-concordant loss analysis yielding fast rates in RKHS (Bach, 2010; Marteau-Ferey et al., 2019), and Lepskii's balancing principle for adaptive regularization (Lepskii, 1991; De Vito et al., 2010). It departs from prior DRE aggregation methods (model averaging, BMA, Super Learner) by showing that a simple linear aggregation—solving a single linear system—provably achieves minimax-optimal convergence rates, whereas those baselines cannot guarantee fast rates for finite sequences of suboptimal models. The closest prior work is Zellinger et al. (2023), which obtains similar rates but relies on a heuristically fixed constant in the balancing principle; this paper replaces that heuristic with an analytic solution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first provably minimax-optimal convergence rate for a parameter choice procedure in DRE, removing the need for prior knowledge of density ratio smoothness—a practical barrier that has limited the adoption of kernel-based DRE methods. The empirical validation across 9,000+ neural networks and three modalities (image, text, time-series) demonstrates consistent improvements over cross-validation and sets new SOTA on MiniDomainNet and Amazon Reviews. A key limitation is that the theoretical guarantees require the RKHS setting, while the large-scale experiments use neural networks beyond the theory's scope; extending the minimax-optimal guarantees to non-RKHS model classes remains open. The method's computational advantage over n-fold CV (training n-times fewer models) makes it attractive for expensive DRE pipelines.
