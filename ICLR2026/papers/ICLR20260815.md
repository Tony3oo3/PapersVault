## Problem

Existing medical AI benchmarks rely on board-exam-style questions that test factual recall rather than the ambiguous, judgment-heavy decision-making that defines real psychiatric practice. In psychiatry, many tasks (triage, documentation) lack a single correct answer, and models exhibit demographic biases that are invisible in single-case qualitative analysis but surface statistically at scale. No prior dataset combines clinician-authored real-world psychiatric tasks with explicit ambiguity modeling and systematic demographic parameterization for fairness evaluation.

## Input / Output

Each evaluation prompt is a multiple-choice clinical vignette with five answer options, where all task-irrelevant patient demographics are replaced with swappable variables for age (three bands), ethnicity (six categories), and gender (male/female/non-binary), enabling controlled fairness comparisons across 183–1,098 prompts per demographic axis. For the unambiguous categories (diagnosis, treatment, monitoring), the expected output is a single correct option; for the ambiguous categories (triage, documentation), the ground truth is a preference probability distribution over all five options derived from expert annotations. The evaluation pipeline produces per-category accuracy, demographic-stratified accuracy deltas (bias metrics), and free-form decision-consistency scores comparing open-ended model responses against the expert-annotated option set.

## Method

MENTAT is a 203-question dataset authored and verified by nine practicing U.S. psychiatrists with zero LM involvement, spanning five clinical domains. For the two ambiguous categories, eight expert annotators independently rate each of the five answer options on a 0–100 scale via a randomized web interface (jsPsych), yielding 657 annotations. These raw scores are converted into preference probabilities using a hierarchical Bradley-Terry model that extends the standard pairwise-comparison formulation with per-annotator offset (γ_a) and slope (α_a) parameters, capturing individual rating behaviors (e.g., tendency to break ties or concentrate scores on a single option) while preserving inter-annotator disagreement as a signal of genuine clinical ambiguity. The model is fit via SLSQP on the negative log-likelihood with L2 regularization on annotator parameters, bounded to γ_a ∈ [−3, 3] and α_a ∈ [0.5, 2.0], and final probabilities are extracted via softmax over the question-level β parameters. A post-processing step zeroes out objectively incorrect options (identified by a clinician) and renormalizes the remaining probabilities. The dataset is then used to evaluate 22 LMs (16 off-the-shelf, 6 health-fine-tuned) on task accuracy, demographic sensitivity, and free-form consistency.

## Results

Across 21 evaluated models on the MENTAT dataset (183 evaluation questions), average task-specific accuracy ranges from 0.77±0.03 (diagnosis) and 0.74±0.02 (treatment) down to 0.51±0.03 (triage) and 0.44±0.03 (documentation). The strongest closed-source model, o1, achieves 0.81 overall accuracy with near-perfect monitoring (0.98) and diagnosis (0.96), while GPT-4o leads in treatment (0.98). Notably, fine-tuned medical models (e.g., MMedS-Llama-3-8B at 0.58 overall) do not outperform their base Llama3.1-8b (0.65), and all models cluster near chance-level (~0.5) on the ambiguous triage and documentation categories.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 16 off-the-shelf and 6 (mental) health fine-tuned LMs on MENTAT's five clinical tasks. o1 leads overall (0.81) with near-perfect monitoring (0.98) and diagnosis (0.96), while GPT-4o achieves the highest treatment accuracy (0.98) and Claude 3.5 Sonnet ranks third overall (0.77). Critically, fine-tuned medical models fail to outperform their base models: MMedS-Llama-3-8B (0.58) underperforms Llama3.1-8b (0.65), and MentaLLaMa-7b (0.39) matches Llama2-7b (0.39), suggesting that LM-generated clinical training data does not transfer to real-world psychiatric decision-making. In the ambiguous triage and documentation categories, newer open-source models (MedGemma-27b at 0.76 triage, Qwen3-4B at 0.63 documentation, Gemma-3-12b at 0.59 documentation) outperform their closed-source counterparts (GPT-4o at 0.42 triage, o1 at 0.46 triage), indicating that safety fine-tuning in closed models may conflict with clinical triage reasoning. All models cluster near chance (~0.5) on triage and (~0.44) on documentation, highlighting a fundamental gap in current LMs' ability to handle ambiguous real-world psychiatric tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

MENTAT departs from the dominant paradigm of medical LM evaluation that relies on board-exam-style fact recall (MedQA, MedMCQA, MMLU) and from synthetic, LM-generated clinical task datasets (MedS-bench), instead grounding all questions and annotations in practicing psychiatrists' real-world decision-making. Methodologically, it extends the Bradley-Terry preference model (Bradley & Terry, 1952; Hunter, 2004) with annotator-specific offset and slope parameters to handle the inherent ambiguity in psychiatric triage and documentation tasks, where multiple answers are clinically defensible. The fairness evaluation design—systematically varying patient demographics (gender, age, ethnicity) as controlled variables—builds on prior work documenting demographic biases in LMs (Zack et al., 2024; Gabriel et al., 2024) but applies it specifically to psychiatric clinical tasks rather than general medical QA.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MENTAT fills a genuine gap: no prior dataset captures the ambiguity, jurisdiction-specificity, and demographic sensitivity of real-world psychiatric decision-making with expert annotation. The finding that statistically significant demographic biases persist across all 21 tested models (e.g., +10% accuracy for male-coded patients in monitoring) and that medical fine-tuning (MMedS) does not improve performance over base models on real-world tasks are practically important negative results for the field. Key limitations and open questions include the US-only scope, the 203-question size, the multiple-choice format's inability to capture dynamic multi-turn psychiatric interactions, and whether the identified biases can be mitigated through targeted fine-tuning or prompting strategies. The dataset's open licensing and modular annotation pipeline (supporting alternative preference models like Plackett-Luce) position it as a foundation for future fairness-aware psychiatric AI research.
