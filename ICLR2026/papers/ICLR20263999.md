## Problem

Existing imitation learning approaches—explicit regression, implicit energy-based, and generative (diffusion/flow-matching) policies—all compress demonstrations into parametric models that must be retrained to incorporate new data, discard the geometric structure underlying expert behavior, and suffer from slow multi-step inference or brittleness under distribution shift. The paper asks whether imitation can be made more direct by treating demonstrations as geometric curves in state space rather than collections of state-action samples, yielding a policy that is non-parametric, interpretable, and composable without retraining.

## Input / Output

The method consumes robot states (joint angles, end-effector positions/orientations) and optionally RGB images (e.g., 320×240 crops from a top-down camera on ALOHA, or 128×128 PushT frames). For vision-based inputs, a lightweight encoder (ResNet-18, VAE, SAM, or CLIP) maps images to compact latent embeddings that are concatenated with normalized agent positions; all state dimensions are normalized to [0,1] before distance computation. The output is a velocity command in the robot's actuated subspace (first derivative of actuated state), not a position target or torque, and the policy is reactive by default with a one-step horizon (H=1), though it can be extended to receding-horizon planning up to H=16.

## Method

GPI represents each expert demonstration as a geometric curve in state space that induces a distance field d(x_o | Γ⁽ⁱ⁾) measuring proximity to the trajectory. From this field, two complementary control primitives emerge in the actuated subspace: a progression flow given by the demonstrated tangent velocity at the nearest point, and an attraction flow given by the negative gradient of the distance field with respect to actuated coordinates. The local policy is their weighted superposition π_i(x_o) = λ₁·u_κ − λ₂·∇d, where λ₁ and λ₂ can be distance-dependent so that attraction dominates far from demonstrations and progression dominates near them. A global policy composes the K nearest local policies via softmax-weighted averaging over demonstration distances, with a temperature β controlling selection sharpness. A Lyapunov analysis (V = ½d²) proves that the resulting first-order dynamical system is globally stable and asymptotically converges to the demonstrated trajectory, leveraging the orthogonality between the distance gradient and the trajectory tangent at the projection point. The key architectural decoupling is between metric learning (choosing d_rob and d_env, potentially via pretrained encoders) and policy synthesis (purely non-parametric flow composition), enabling new demonstrations to be added by simply appending to the distance field without any retraining.

## Results

On PushT (state-based), GPI achieves 85.8% average / 89.0% max reward, outperforming all baselines (DDPM 82.3%, DDIM 81.5%, FMP 77.6%, SFP 83.1%) while running at 0.6 ms per step—nearly 100× faster than DDPM (641 ms) and 85× faster than DDIM (65 ms)—with only 0.7 MB memory versus 240–252 MB for baselines. On PushT (vision-based), GPI reaches 83.3% average reward at 3.3 ms inference and 44 MB memory, again surpassing all diffusion/flow-matching baselines. On RoboMimic and Adroit Hand (state spaces of 9–46 dimensions), GPI matches or exceeds Diffusion Policy on 6 of 7 tasks without any parametric training (e.g., Pen: 0.95 vs 0.89; Hammer: 0.88 vs 0.83).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPI outperforms all four PushT baselines on both state-based and vision-based settings. Against DDPM (Ho et al., 2020), GPI gains +3.5% average reward (state) and +2.4% (vision) while being ~1000× faster at inference. Against DDIM (Song et al., 2021), GPI improves by +4.3% (state) and +4.2% (vision) with ~100× lower latency. Against FMP (Zhang & Gienger, 2024), the margin is largest: +8.2% (state) and +8.2% (vision). Against SFP (Jiang et al., 2025), the closest competitor, GPI still leads by +2.7% (state) and +5.8% (vision) while running 85× faster. On the broader RoboMimic and Adroit Hand benchmarks, GPI (non-parametric, no training) matches or beats Diffusion Policy (Chi et al., 2023) on 6 of 7 tasks—winning on Can (+0.02), Pen (+0.06), and Hammer (+0.05), tying on Lift, Door, and Relocate, and losing slightly on Square (−0.05)—demonstrating that the geometric flow formulation generalizes across state dimensions from 9 to 46 without any learned policy network.

## Relation to Prior Work

GPI directly extends the distance-field-to-dynamical-systems framework of Li & Calinon (2025), which showed that progression and attraction flows form a stable first-order system converging to demonstrated trajectories. Conceptually, it bridges classical movement primitives (DMP, ProMP) and modern generative policies (Diffusion Policy, Flow Matching) by arguing that the geometric structure of demonstrations—preserved as distance fields—subsumes what diffusion denoising implicitly achieves as an attraction flow. The closest prior work, VINN (Pari et al., 2022), also uses kNN retrieval with learned visual features, but GPI generalizes to diverse latent representations, synthesizes explicit flow fields rather than retrieving fixed actions, and provides a Lyapunov-based convergence argument. The key departure from the generative-policy paradigm is the rejection of parametric compression: demonstrations remain as geometric curves, and policy synthesis is a non-parametric composition of distance and flow fields.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GPI offers a compelling efficiency-interpretability tradeoff: 20–100× faster inference than diffusion policies with higher success rates, zero retraining for new demonstrations, and transparent action composition. The conceptual reframing—that diffusion denoising steps are an implicit attraction flow—suggests a unifying lens for understanding why generative policies work and where simpler geometric alternatives suffice. Open questions include making the distance metric learnable and co-optimized with policy synthesis, incorporating dynamics models for formal stability certificates under model mismatch, and bounding memory growth for very large demonstration sets. The reliance on manually specified metrics and the gap between the continuous-time Lyapunov proof and discrete-time implementation remain practical limitations for safety-critical deployment.
