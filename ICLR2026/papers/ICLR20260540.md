## Problem

Designing effective LLM-based multi-agent systems (MAS) is inherently complex: prompts are sensitive and their compounding effects across cascaded agents amplify degradation, while the combinatorial search space over both prompt designs and topology configurations is vast. Prior automated MAS design methods (e.g., ADAS, AFlow) focus on topology search without proper prompt optimization, and the interplay between these two design dimensions remains unexplored. This paper asks: what factors most influence MAS performance, and how can the joint prompt-topology optimization be made tractable?

## Input / Output

MASS takes as input a task with a validation set, a configurable set of agentic building blocks (Aggregate, Reflect, Debate, Summarize, Tool-use) each parameterized by a search dimension (e.g., number of parallel agents, rounds of reflection), and a plug-and-play prompt optimizer. It produces an optimized MAS specifying both the workflow topology (which blocks are active, their parameters, and their arrangement in a fixed order [summarize, reflect, debate, aggregate]) and optimized prompts (instructions + few-shot exemplars) for every agent in the system. The resulting MAS then consumes task questions and context passages to produce final answers, with inference cost comparable to strong baselines (~6.6K input + 3.3K output tokens per query).

## Method

MASS is a three-stage optimization framework that interleaves prompt and topology optimization from local to global. Stage 1 (block-level prompt optimization) uses a modular prompt optimizer (MIPRO) to jointly optimize instructions and exemplars for each building block individually, starting from the base predictor and conditioning subsequent blocks on the warmed predictor, thereby ensuring each agent is primed before composition. Stage 2 (workflow topology optimization) computes the incremental influence of each block relative to the base agent, applies a softmax-weighted rejection sampling to prune the search space to only influential topologies, then samples valid configurations within a token budget and evaluates them on the validation set. Stage 3 (workflow-level prompt optimization) performs a final round of joint prompt optimization over all agents simultaneously, conditioned on the best-found topology, to model inter-agent interdependence. The key design insight is that beneficial topologies represent only a small fraction of the full search space, and that optimizing prompts locally before scaling topology yields substantially better token-effectiveness than scaling agents with default prompts.

## Results

MASS achieves an average accuracy of 78.79% across 8 tasks (MATH, DROP, HotpotQA, MuSiQue, 2WikiMQA, MBPP, HumanEval, LiveCodeBench) with Gemini 1.5 Pro, and 74.30% with Gemini 1.5 Flash. On individual tasks, MASS reaches 84.67% on MATH, 90.52% on DROP, 91.67% on HumanEval, and 82.33% on LiveCodeBench (test-output prediction). The training cost of MASS (~$5.09) is comparable to ADAS ($5.61) and AFlow ($3.89), while inference cost per query (6,645 input + 3,263 output tokens) is in the same range as Multi-Agent Debate and ADAS.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MASS outperforms all seven baselines by a substantial margin on average. Against the standard multi-agent baselines, MASS leads CoT by +13.5%, Self-Consistency by +10.6%, Self-Refine by +11.9%, and Multi-Agent Debate by +8.5% (all on Gemini 1.5 Pro average). Against the automated design baselines, MASS surpasses ADAS by +9.1% on average; ADAS proposes complex topologies but without prompt optimization, resulting in only marginal gains over handcrafted agents. AFlow is the closest competitor, being competitive on 2WikiMQA (76.51 vs. 73.34) and HumanEval (88.00 vs. 91.67), but MASS clearly wins on MATH (84.67 vs. 76.00) and MuSiQue (51.40 vs. 32.05), which the authors attribute to AFlow's lack of an explicit prompt optimization phase. Against the graph-optimization baseline GPTSwarm (Table 8), MASS gains +8.7% on MATH (84.7 vs. 76.0) and +6.7% on HumanEval (91.7 vs. 85.0) with Gemini 1.5 Pro, and +20.0% and +11.7% respectively with Gemini 1.5 Flash, indicating that prompt optimization contributes more to task performance than graph topology optimization alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

MASS carves new ground by unifying two previously separate optimization threads in LLM-based multi-agent systems: automatic prompt optimization (MIPRO, DSPy, OPRO) and agentic topology search (ADAS, AFlow, GPTSwarm). Prior automated MAS design methods (ADAS, AFlow) focus exclusively on discovering new workflow topologies while leaving prompts handcrafted, and prior prompt optimizers target single-agent pipelines rather than multi-agent interdependence. The paper explicitly draws an analogy to neural architecture search, arguing that the search *space* design (which topologies to include, which to prune) is as critical as the search *algorithm*, and repurposes this NAS insight for the MAS domain. MASS's multi-stage interleaving—block-level prompt warm-up, influence-weighted topology search via rejection sampling, then workflow-level joint prompt optimization—is a novel composition of these existing ideas rather than a departure from any single lineage.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway—that properly optimizing prompts for individual agents yields larger and more reliable gains than searching for novel topologies with default prompts—redirects attention in the MAS design community toward a component that has been systematically under-studied. The influence-weighted pruning of the topology search space (where only a small fraction of topologies are beneficial per task) offers a concrete recipe for reducing combinatorial complexity without sacrificing performance. Open questions remain: the topology space is limited to serial/parallel/mixture patterns with fully-connected debate, sparse communication topologies are not yet explored, and the framework's sample efficiency could improve with Bayesian optimization or feedback-based prompt optimizers. The plug-and-play design (arbitrary prompt optimizer, customizable topology space) positions MASS as a generalizable meta-framework rather than a one-off system.
