## Problem

Existing backdoor attacks in RL rely on reward manipulation during training, which is ineffective against trajectory optimization (TO) models that minimize reconstruction loss over action-state-RTG sequences rather than maximizing reward. The high-dimensional continuous action space further compounds the difficulty, as all components of the output must simultaneously match a target action. This paper presents the first systematic study of action-level backdoors against TO models, revealing that trigger design and target action selection are the critical factors while reward manipulation is negligible.

## Input / Output

The backdoored TO model receives the standard input—a sequence of past actions, states, and returns-to-go over a context window of K=20 steps—but with a small trigger perturbation δ added to specific dimensions (e.g., 3 out of 11–59 state dimensions) of the most recent state. When triggered, the model must output a continuous action vector (3–24 dimensions) where every component simultaneously falls within a threshold ε of a predefined target action; on benign inputs it must reproduce the original policy's output. The all-components-must-match requirement in continuous spaces makes the attack substantially harder than discrete-action backdoors.

## Method

TrojanTO is a post-training attack that modifies a pretrained TO model using only ~0.3% of trajectories (10 out of ~3318), decoupling the attack from the original training process. It combines three modules: (1) trajectory filtering retains only long trajectories to prevent overfitting to suboptimal behaviors and preserve benign performance; (2) batch poisoning applies the trigger to a single random transition per batch (consensus poisoning) so that the trigger's training context matches its evaluation context, avoiding out-of-distribution issues inherent to Transformer teacher-forcing; (3) alternating training jointly optimizes the trigger via MI-FGSM and the model parameters in a bi-level framework, forging a strong trigger-to-target-action coupling through the reconstruction loss. The key insight is that reward manipulation is entirely unnecessary—the attack works purely by establishing a direct association between the trigger perturbation and the target action, achieving 0.719 average ASR with 0.914 BTP across DT, GDT, and DC on six D4RL environments.

## Results

TrojanTO achieves an average CP of 0.701, ASR of 0.719, and BTP of 0.914 across six D4RL environments and three TO model architectures (DT, GDT, DC), using only 0.3% of trajectories for poisoning. This represents a 105% improvement in CP over Baffle (0.342) and a 27.2% gain over IMC (0.551). TrojanTO also maintains the highest BTP (0.914 vs. 0.792 for Baffle and 0.853 for IMC), indicating superior stealth. Notably, TrojanTO achieves near-perfect ASR (≥0.99) on Half and Walk with DT and DC, while Baffle collapses to 0.000 CP on Walk with DT.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TrojanTO outperforms both baselines across nearly all settings. Against Baffle (Gong et al., 2024), TrojanTO achieves a 105% higher average CP (0.701 vs. 0.342) while using 33× less poisoning data (0.3% vs. 10% of trajectories). Baffle suffers catastrophic failures in specific configurations, e.g., 0.000 CP on Walk with DT and 0.075 CP on Half with DT, whereas TrojanTO maintains CP ≥ 0.30 in all 18 model-environment combinations. Against IMC (Pang et al., 2020), TrojanTO achieves a 27.2% higher average CP (0.701 vs. 0.551) and a substantially higher BTP (0.914 vs. 0.853), indicating better stealth. IMC shows extreme instability, dropping to 0.013 CP on Hopper and 0.133 on Ant with DT, while TrojanTO remains stable. The only setting where TrojanTO does not clearly win is Kit with DT (CP 0.614 vs. Baffle's 0.766) and Pen with DC (CP 0.477 vs. IMC's 0.655), suggesting manipulation tasks with high-dimensional action spaces (24-dim for Pen) remain challenging.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of RL backdoor attacks, which are training-time interventions relying on reward manipulation (TrojDRL, SleeperNets, Baffle). It repurposes the bi-level optimization framework from IMC (Pang et al., 2020) for alternating trigger-model co-optimization, and adapts MI-FGSM from the adversarial examples literature for trigger value generation. The key conceptual departure is shifting from training-time to post-training attacks and from reward-based to action-reconstruction-based poisoning, motivated by the observation that TO models minimize reconstruction loss rather than maximize reward. Baffle (Gong et al., 2024) is the closest prior work as an offline RL data-poisoning backdoor, but it is policy-level, requires 10% poisoning, and targets traditional offline RL rather than sequence-modeling TO architectures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As TO models (DT, GDT, DC) become foundational for embodied intelligence and robotic control, the demonstrated vulnerability to post-training backdoors with only 0.3% data poisoning represents a practical supply-chain threat that is difficult to detect. The finding that standard defenses (activation clustering, spectral analysis, weight pruning) are largely ineffective—due to inter-type activation variance masking trigger perturbations—highlights a fundamental security gap in sequence-modeling architectures. Open questions include whether model editing techniques can implant backdoors without fine-tuning, how to automate trigger dimension selection (current methods show no correlation with gradient-based saliency), and whether these attacks extend to image-based state spaces with physically plausible triggers.
