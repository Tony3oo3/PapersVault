## Problem

Fourier Neural Operators and other machine-learned operators have been claimed to support zero-shot super-resolution, meaning a model trained at resolution m can accurately infer at any higher resolution n > m without additional training. This paper rigorously evaluates that claim and finds it false: MLOs fail at both interpolating across resolutions and extrapolating to unseen frequency content, exhibiting aliasing artifacts that compound over time for time-dependent PDEs. Two previously proposed corrective strategies—physics-informed optimization constraints and band-limited learning—also fail to enable reliable multi-resolution generalization.

## Input / Output

Models consume discretized PDE input fields (e.g., a 2D viscosity coefficient field for Darcy flow, a 1D initial condition for Burgers, or a 2D velocity/vorticity field for Navier-Stokes) sampled on a regular grid of resolution N×N (or N for 1D), and produce the corresponding solution field at the same or a different resolution. The paper's key experimental manipulation is decoupling resolution from frequency content: low-pass filtering controls which frequencies are populated while downsampling controls the sampling rate, enabling independent study of interpolation vs. extrapolation. Resolutions range from 16 to 1024 (2D) or 128 to 1024 (1D), with frequency limits expressed in units of f = 2π/N.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a systematic empirical decomposition of multi-resolution inference into two sub-tasks—resolution interpolation (fixed frequency content, varying sampling rate) and information extrapolation (fixed sampling rate, varying frequency content)—and a demonstration that FNOs, CNOs, and DeepONets fail at both in zero-shot settings. To address the failure, the authors propose multi-resolution training: composing training datasets from randomly sampled proportions of data at multiple resolutions (e.g., 70% at the lowest resolution, 10% each at the next three). The key practical finding is that datasets dominated by cheap low-resolution data with only a small fraction of expensive high-resolution data (e.g., ratio 0.9/0.5/0.3/0.2) achieve near-optimal multi-resolution test loss while reducing dataset size by 96–98% and training time by up to 86% compared to single-resolution training at the highest resolution. The authors also show that physics-informed loss terms consistently degrade performance and that band-limited architectures (CNO, CROP) cannot predict frequencies beyond their predetermined limit, making them unsuitable for true multi-resolution inference.

## Results

The paper demonstrates that zero-shot FNO inference fails across resolutions: test losses vary by 1×, 2×, and 10× across test resolutions for Darcy, Burgers, and Navier Stokes respectively (e.g., training at resolution 16 and testing at 128 on Darcy yields MSE 2.0e-3 vs. 2.7e-4 when trained and tested at 128). Multi-resolution training with a (0.9, 0.5, 0.3, 0.2) resolution ratio achieves 96–98% dataset size reduction (e.g., Darcy: 0.54 GB → 0.02 GB) while maintaining competitive test loss across all resolutions. Training time savings are 86% (Darcy), 3.2% (Burgers), and 56% (Navier Stokes) compared to single-resolution training at the maximum resolution.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's proposed multi-resolution training outperforms all five baselines on multi-resolution inference. Zero-shot FNO (Li et al., 2020a) is the primary target: it exhibits aliasing artifacts and 1–10× loss degradation when test resolution differs from training resolution, failing both resolution interpolation and information extrapolation. Physics-informed FNO (Li et al., 2024b) consistently underperforms pure data-driven FNO at every tested weighting (w ∈ {0.1, 0.25, 0.5}), and its predicted spectra diverge more from ground truth across resolutions. CNO (Raonic et al., 2023) and CROP (Gao et al., 2025) are band-limited methods that accurately fit only the low-frequency range of their training data and cannot predict higher frequencies, making them unsuitable for multi-resolution inference where the resolved frequency range changes. DeepONet (Lu et al., 2021a) also fails at zero-shot multi-resolution, though multi-resolution training improves its performance; however, DeepONet is more sensitive to data ratios across resolutions than FNO due to its fixed-resolution input architecture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly challenges the zero-shot super-resolution claim made by the Fourier Neural Operator (FNO) literature (Li et al., 2020a; Tran et al., 2021), which posited that mesh-invariant architectures can perform accurate inference at arbitrary resolutions without retraining. It builds on the multi-resolution active learning framework of Li et al. (2024a) and extends it from dual-resolution to full multi-resolution training. The paper also evaluates and refutes two proposed corrective strategies—physics-informed optimization constraints (Li et al., 2024b) and band-limited learning via CNO (Raonic et al., 2023) and CROP (Gao et al., 2025)—by reframing the core failure as an out-of-distribution generalization problem rather than an architectural deficiency.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work has significant practical implications for the scientific ML community: practitioners relying on FNO or similar mesh-invariant operators for adaptive mesh refinement or multi-scale simulation cannot assume zero-shot multi-resolution accuracy, and must instead incorporate multi-resolution training data. The proposed fix—training on predominantly low-resolution data with small amounts of high-resolution data—is computationally cheap (96% dataset size reduction, 86% training time reduction for Darcy) and immediately actionable. Open questions include optimal resolution-ratio selection (the paper notes this remains a future direction), whether the findings extend to 3D PDEs and larger-scale problems, and whether the out-of-distribution framing generalizes to other operator architectures beyond FNO and DeepONet.
