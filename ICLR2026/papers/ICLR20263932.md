## Problem

Scaled post-training now drives the largest capability gains in LLMs, yet its effect on pretrained knowledge remains poorly understood. Traditional task-level aggregate metrics conflate forgetting with backward transfer and ignore the substantial contribution of random guessing in multiple-choice benchmarks, masking large sample-level redistributions of competence. The paper asks: what specific knowledge is forgotten, at which post-training stage, and how much?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263932/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a pre-trained checkpoint and its post-trained counterpart, evaluates both on the same set of multiple-choice questions from 18 public datasets (~100 sub-benchmarks), and requires only the number of answer options (k), per-sample correctness labels, and marginal accuracies—no logits or repeated sampling. Outputs are per-sample and per-category chance-adjusted forgetting (F_true) and backward transfer (BT_true) scores, alongside theoretical ceilings (F_max, BT_max) that contextualize how much headroom exists for further degradation or improvement.

## Method

The core contribution is a sample-wise, chance-adjusted metric that assigns each benchmark item to one of four quadrants (retention, backward transfer, forgetting, non-acquisition) based on correctness before and after post-training. Raw forgetting (F) counts 1→0 transitions and backward transfer (BT) counts 0→1 transitions; chance baselines are then subtracted under a simple response model where the model either knows the answer or guesses uniformly among k options, yielding F_true and BT_true that depend only on aggregate accuracies and k. The empirical study applies this framework across nearly 30 model pairs spanning four post-training regimes—domain-continual pretraining, instruction tuning, SFT/RL from base models, and SFT/RL from instruction-tuned models at low and high data scales—plus EMA model-merging experiments, using zero-shot chain-of-thought prompting for instruct models and few-shot format-teaching prompts for base models with up to 32,768 generated tokens per sample.

## Results

Across nearly 30 model pairs and ~100 sub-benchmarks, the paper finds that domain-continual pretraining induces low-to-moderate forgetting (total F_true ≈ 5–13% depending on model and domain) with limited backward transfer. Instruction tuning yields total forgetting of 6.7–13.0% (Qwen2.5 Instruct 3B–32B) with moderate backward transfer spikes in Math (BT_true ≈ 16–19%). SFT/RL reasoning training from base models produces minimal forgetting but moderate-to-high backward transfer in Math and Logic, while low-data reasoning training from instruct models shows uniformly low forgetting and backward transfer. Model merging (EMA/linear interpolation) does not reliably reduce forgetting; in fact, large weight drift (e.g., 87% L₂ norm ratio for Qwen2.5-Coder) causes merging to degrade performance rather than mitigate it.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against a single competing method but instead benchmarks its sample-wise, chance-adjusted forgetting metric (F_true) against the standard aggregate metric (F_standard = max(a̅_pre − a̅_post, 0)). The sample-wise metric reveals substantially more forgetting than the aggregate version in several cases, reclassifying what appeared as low forgetting into moderate forgetting (e.g., the Coder model in Figure 9). Across post-training stages, the paper finds that instruction tuning and SFT/RL from base models produce comparable forgetting levels, but SFT/RL yields larger backward transfer gains in Math and Logic. Comparing SFT versus RL objectives shows negligible differences in forgetting dynamics, suggesting data composition matters more than the loss function. For model merging, the paper contrasts two-checkpoint EMA interpolation against the post-trained model and finds it fails to mitigate forgetting when weight drift exceeds ~20% (OpenThinker3, Qwen2.5-Coder), while only marginally helping when drift is small (~1.8%, OpenThinker). The chance-adjusted metric is validated against a two-seed agreement metric, showing high correlation and confirming it captures robust knowledge loss rather than random guessing artifacts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the task-level backward transfer formulation of Lopez-Paz & Ranzato (2017) to a sample-wise, chance-adjusted metric that resolves individual knowledge items rather than treating benchmarks as fungible i.i.d. tasks. It positions itself against the extensive LLM catastrophic-forgetting literature (instruction tuning, SFT, RL, continual pretraining) by arguing that aggregate accuracy comparisons conflate genuine knowledge loss with redistribution and guessing artifacts. The model-merging experiments connect to the TIES-merging and weight-interpolation literature but deliberately test a simpler two-checkpoint setting, finding that the optimistic results from multi-model merging do not transfer to the post-training forgetting scenario.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical, low-overhead metric (requiring only per-sample correctness and the number of answer choices) that practitioners can adopt to audit post-training pipelines for hidden knowledge redistribution that aggregate scores mask. Its central empirical finding—that current post-training pipelines induce far less forgetting than the continual-learning literature predicts—reframes the problem from 'preventing catastrophic forgetting' to 'managing moderate, category-specific trade-offs,' with data composition emerging as the dominant lever over training objective. Open questions remain for the high-data reasoning regime, where no single factor robustly explains forgetting dynamics, and for whether model merging can be made to work with larger weight drift or more than two checkpoints.
