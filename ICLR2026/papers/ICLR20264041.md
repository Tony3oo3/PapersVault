## Problem

Prior convergence guarantees for Riemannian diffusion models (De Bortoli et al., 2022) require exponentially small stepsizes, L∞-accurate score estimates, and smooth strictly positive data densities, yielding exponential iteration complexity in dimension. This paper asks whether polynomial iteration complexity can be achieved on manifolds using only L2-accurate score estimates and mild geometric assumptions, without any smoothness or positivity requirements on the data distribution.

## Input / Output

The algorithm operates on a connected, compact d-dimensional Riemannian manifold (M, g) satisfying bounded geometry (injectivity radius ≥ 1/K, curvature and its derivatives bounded by K), and consumes L2-accurate score estimates ŝ_t along with a discretization step size h and horizon T. It produces a sample Y_0 on the manifold via an Euler-Maruyama scheme with rejection sampling (resetting to uniform μ when the tangent update exceeds h^{1/4}), and guarantees TV(p_δ, Law(Y_0)) ≤ ε + C'ε_score + √(hT)·poly(d, K, δ^{-1}) where δ is an early-stopping time that avoids the singularity at t=0.

## Method

The proof decomposes the total TV error into four components—initialization, score matching, drift discretization, and Brownian motion simulation—and controls each with distinct geometric tools. A localization scheme constructs auxiliary transition kernels K_k^aux that freeze the estimated score as a constant vector field in normal coordinates (via a cutoff η_ω) while preserving continuous Brownian motion, cleanly separating drift discretization from BM simulation errors. The drift discretization error is bounded using Itô/Stratonovich calculus, which reveals that third-order derivatives of log p_t cancel, reducing the problem to second-order terms controlled by Li-Yau gradient estimates on the heat kernel. The BM simulation error—the core manifold-specific challenge—is handled via a quantitative Minakshisundaram-Pleijel parametrix expansion showing that the manifold heat kernel and its Euclidean proxy agree to first order in time within a polynomially small neighborhood. Together these yield a polynomial stepsize h = ε²/(poly(d,K,δ^{-1})·T) and iteration complexity N = poly(d,K,δ^{-1})/(λ₁ε)², replacing the prior exponential dependence.

## Results

The main result (Theorem 1) establishes that the RSGM sampler achieves TV error bounded by ε + C′ε_score + √(hT)·poly(d, K, δ⁻¹), requiring only a polynomially small stepsize h = ε²/(poly(d,K,δ⁻¹)·T) with horizon T ≍ λ₁⁻¹(d log d + log(d/ε)). This yields an iteration complexity of N = T/h ≍ poly(d, K, δ⁻¹)/(λ₁ε)², which is polynomial in the manifold dimension d. In contrast, the prior state-of-the-art for manifold diffusion (De Bortoli et al., 2022) requires an exponentially small stepsize, giving iteration complexity that scales as exp(O(d)). The result holds under only mild bounded-geometry assumptions (injectivity radius and curvature bounds) with no smoothness or positivity requirements on the data distribution.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the only prior non-asymptotic manifold diffusion guarantee (De Bortoli et al., 2022), this work achieves a dramatic improvement: polynomial iteration complexity Õ(poly(d)/(λ₁²ε²)) versus exponential Õ(exp(O(d))/ε^{-1/λ₁}), while simultaneously relaxing the data assumption from smooth and strictly positive to none (via early stopping), the score accuracy from L∞ to L₂, and the metric from Wasserstein to the stronger total variation. Against the three Euclidean baselines (Benton et al. 2024, Li et al. 2024, Li & Yan 2025), this paper addresses a fundamentally different setting (manifold-constrained diffusion rather than Euclidean diffusion), so direct numerical comparison is not possible; however, the polynomial dependence on dimension d is of comparable order to the best Euclidean results (Õ(d/ε) for Li & Yan 2025), suggesting the manifold geometry does not introduce an additional exponential penalty. The key distinction is that this work's guarantee is the first to provide polynomial convergence for diffusion models whose forward and reverse processes are intrinsically constrained to a Riemannian manifold, rather than merely adapting Euclidean samplers to low-dimensional structures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Riemannian Score-Based Generative Model (RSGM) framework of De Bortoli et al. (2022), which established the first non-asymptotic convergence guarantee for diffusion models on manifolds but required exponentially small stepsizes, L∞-accurate scores, and smooth strictly positive data densities. The analysis adapts the stochastic localization and Itô/Stratonovich cancellation techniques from the Euclidean convergence literature (Benton et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2023) to the manifold setting, while introducing two new geometric ingredients: Li-Yau gradient bounds for the manifold heat kernel to control score norms without positivity assumptions, and a quantitative Minakshisundaram-Pleijel parametrix to bound the one-step deviation between manifold heat flow and its Euclidean proxy. The key conceptual departure is replacing the Wasserstein metric with total variation and the exponential stepsize with a polynomial one, at the cost of a localization scheme that freezes drifts in tangent spaces while preserving continuous Brownian motion.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first polynomial convergence guarantee for Riemannian diffusion models, removing the exponential-in-dimension barrier that made prior manifold diffusion theory impractical for high-dimensional settings. The result is significant for scientific domains where data naturally lives on manifolds (SO(3) orientations, SPD matrices, toroidal angles), as it provides a theoretical justification for using polynomially many sampling steps. However, the polynomial degree in the bound is not optimized, the empirical validation is limited to low-dimensional tori and spheres, and important extensions remain open: sharper bounds via adaptive discretization schedules, deterministic (DDIM-style) samplers, and conditional sampling for inverse problems. The gap between the theoretical polynomial rate and practical performance on high-dimensional manifolds remains to be closed.
