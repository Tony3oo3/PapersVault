## Problem

Existing ensemble-based causal discovery methods aggregate multiple graph predictions using heuristic distance or voting rules without theoretical guarantees on when the consensus structure recovers the true causal graph. There is no principled understanding of how ensemble size, expert competency, and diversity affect aggregation accuracy, leaving practitioners without guidance for configuring ensemble strategies.

## Input / Output

The method consumes repeated graph predictions (p samples per expert via bootstrapping or data partitioning) from n heterogeneous causal discovery algorithms, yielding up to N=p^n voting profiles over edge-level sub-structures. It produces a consensus DAG, per-edge posterior probabilities that quantify prediction reliability, and estimated competence transition matrices for each expert. The feature-level decomposition restricts the state space to m=3 mutually exclusive states per node pair (i→j, i←j, no edge), making the otherwise intractable 2^{d²}×2^{d²} competence estimation problem reducible to binom(d,2) individual 3×3 matrices.

## Method

The paper formulates structural ensembling as a multi-class classification problem over an exponentially large DAG space and derives a weighted Bayes voting rule whose weights are log-odds of each expert's correct versus incorrect prediction probability. To make this tractable, the graph is decomposed into edge-level features (l=2, m=3 states), reducing the problem to independent per-edge voting tasks. Expert competence matrices and priors are estimated via a minimum Kantorovich (optimal transport) distance objective between the empirical voting distribution and the model distribution, solved with a Gumbel-Softmax reparameterization and a differentiable Sinkhorn operator that enforces diagonal dominance. Theoretical guarantees show the collective decision converges to the true graph at an exponential rate in n·KL-informativeness (Theorem 1), and the OT estimator is consistent given at least 2m−1 informative experts (Theorem 2), with robustness to noisy parameter estimates (Proposition 1).

## Results

The paper evaluates structure learning performance using SHD (lower is better), SID, and F1 scores for adjacency and orientation on synthetic graphs (d=50) and real-world benchmarks (Sangiovese, d=14; Child, d=20), averaging over 2000 random aggregated graphs and 30 model initializations. In simulations, Bayes Est (the proposed noisy Bayes voting) consistently outperforms Plurality voting across all competency levels (strong, medium, weak) and approaches the oracle Bayes True as the number of experts grows. On real-world data, the two-phase combination Rank + Bayes Est achieves the strongest and most stable performance among all ensemble configurations tested, with Bayes Est performing no worse than other ensemble methods and outperforming them in most cases with lower variance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against Plurality voting (unweighted majority) and the rank-based ensemble method Rank (Malmi et al., 2015), which uses Kendall-Tau distance to align partial orderings of causal DAGs. Bayes Est consistently outperforms Plurality voting across all simulated competency levels and real-world benchmarks, with the gap widening when experts are weakly competent. Against Rank, Bayes Est matches or exceeds performance in most cases while exhibiting lower variance; the two-phase combination Rank + Bayes Est is identified as the most effective and stable strategy overall. Compared to individual causal discovery experts, results are mixed: when experts are homogeneously competent, ensembling helps, but with heterogeneous competence, Bayes Est's weighted mechanism allows it to approach the performance of the single best expert in the ensemble—something Plurality and Rank fail to achieve since they assign equal weight to all voters. The paper notes that across all real-world experiments (9 benchmarks), Bayes Est performs no worse than other ensemble-based methods and outperforms them in most cases.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the voting-theoretic framework of Nitzan & Paroush (1982) for optimal weighted decision rules and the minimum Kantorovich estimator of Bassetti et al. (2006), repurposing both for the structurally novel problem of aggregating noisy causal graph predictions. It departs from existing ensemble-based causal discovery methods (distance-based approaches like MEDIAN and DAGBag, and heuristic voting methods like Plurality or Rank) by providing the first formal recovery guarantees and a principled parameter estimation procedure via optimal transport, building directly on Vo et al. (2024) for tractable OT-based estimation in latent variable models. The key conceptual move is decomposing the intractable graph-level voting problem into edge-level sub-problems with finite state spaces, making the Bayes voting rule computationally feasible while preserving theoretical guarantees.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work fills a significant theoretical gap in ensemble-based causal discovery by establishing when and how aggregated structures recover the true causal graph, giving practitioners concrete guidance on ensemble size, expert competency, and diversity. The finding that ensemble performance can underperform the best individual expert when competency is highly heterogeneous challenges the default assumption that 'more experts is always better' and motivates the weighted voting mechanism. Key limitations include the assumption of no latent confounders, the i.i.d. requirement on voting profiles, and the computational cost of OT-based parameter estimation; extending the framework to confounded settings and handling highly heterogeneous expert pools remain open problems.
