## Problem

Pointwise overparameterization (m⊙w) in methods like PILoT and Sign-In induces a beneficial hyperbolic implicit bias that promotes sparsity, but suffers from a vanishing inverse Riemannian metric near zero that slows parameter movement and impedes sign flips. This forces explicit doubling of parameters for memory/compute overhead, and the remedy of iterative re-initialization (as in Sign-In) introduces unstable hard perturbations. The paper asks whether the essential hyperbolic geometry can be captured without explicit overparameterization and without the small-inverse-metric bottleneck.

## Input / Output

HAM is a plug-and-play optimizer step that consumes the current model weights θ ∈ ℝⁿ and the gradient ∇f(θ) already computed by any first-order optimizer, and produces updated weights via an additional element-wise exponential scaling. It requires no extra parameters, no additional forward/backward passes, and no extra memory beyond reusing the known gradient and current weight signs. In experiments, it is applied to standard RGB image classification (ImageNet 224×224, CIFAR-100 32×32) with ResNet-50 and DeiT architectures, producing class labels as output.

## Method

HAM alternates a standard optimizer step (e.g., gradient descent) with a lightweight hyperbolic mirror step: θ_{k+1} = θ_{k+1/2} ⊙ exp(−η(α·sign(θ_{k+1/2})·∇f(θ_k) + β)). In the continuous-time limit (η→0), this yields a Riemannian gradient flow with inverse metric g⁻¹(θ) = 1 + α|θ|, which is always ≥ 1 and thus avoids the vanishing inverse metric of m⊙w (where g⁻¹ = √(θ²+γ²) can be ≪ 1 near zero). The hyperbolic step accelerates learning around zero when the sign is correct (refining magnitude) and drives parameters exponentially fast toward zero when the sign is wrong, while the intervening gradient step enables the actual sign flip. The Bregman function R_α interpolates between L₂ (α→0) and L₁ (α→∞) norms, giving HAM a controllable implicit sparsity bias. The hyperparameter α tunes the strength of this bias, and β adds explicit weight-decay-like regularization; together they make HAM complementary to SAM and effective across dense, dense-to-sparse, and dynamic sparse training regimes.

## Results

On ImageNet with ResNet50, HAM improves dense training top-1 accuracy from 76.72% to 77.51% (100 epochs) and from 77.94% to 78.56% when combined with SAM (200 epochs). For sparse training, HAM delivers the largest gains on AC/DC (e.g., 77.20% vs. 75.83% at 80% sparsity, +1.37%) and STR (75.01% vs. 72.40% at 90% sparsity, +2.61%). HAM also outperforms the overparameterization-based methods PILoT and Sign-In across all sparsity levels without doubling parameter count.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HAM consistently outperforms Sign-In (the closest overparameterization-based competitor) across all sparsification settings: e.g., AC/DC+HAM reaches 77.20% vs. AC/DC+Sign-In's 75.90% at 80% sparsity, and Random+HAM reaches 70.05% vs. Random+Sign-In's 69.38% at 95% sparsity. HAM dramatically improves STR, which suffers severe degradation at high sparsity (64.94% at s=0.95), lifting it to 71.41%—a 6.47% gain that surpasses even PILoT (71.30%) at that sparsity level. For AC/DC, HAM yields the largest absolute improvement among all baselines (+1.37% at s=0.8, +1.91% at s=0.9, +2.86% at s=0.95), attributed to AC/DC's indiscriminate parameter reactivation benefiting from HAM's sign-flip acceleration. HAM also improves RiGL by 1.20% at s=0.8 and 1.04% at s=0.9, though the gain narrows at s=0.95 (72.93% vs. 73.07% for RiGL+Sign-In, a slight loss). In dense training, HAM is complementary to SAM: combining both (SAM-HAM) achieves 78.56% at 200 epochs, exceeding either method alone. HAM requires no extra parameters (unlike PILoT and Sign-In which double parameter count via m⊙w) and incurs only negligible compute overhead.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper distills the hyperbolic geometry of the pointwise overparameterization m⊙w (used in PILoT and Sign-In) into a lightweight alternating optimizer step, eliminating the need to double parameters while preserving the beneficial implicit sparsity bias. The derivation draws on the mirror flow framework of Li et al. (2022) and the exponential gradient descent connection from Wu & Rebeschini (2021), but the key departure is fixing the vanishing inverse metric problem that causes m⊙w to stall near zero and impede sign flips. HAM is positioned as complementary to SAM (which promotes flatness) rather than competing with it, and its Fisher-information interpretation links it to natural gradient descent and the Bayesian learning rule literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

HAM provides a plug-and-play optimizer step with negligible overhead that consistently improves both dense and sparse training, addressing a practical bottleneck (vanishing inverse metric) that limits the effectiveness of overparameterization-based sparsification methods. The clean theoretical characterization—showing the implicit bias interpolates between L2 and L1 via a tunable α—gives practitioners a principled knob for controlling sparsity without explicit regularization. Open questions include whether task-specific mirror maps (e.g., incorporating robustness or optimizer momentum) could yield further gains, and whether the approach extends beyond vision to other modalities. A limitation is that HAM alone requires very large α to induce strong sparsity, making it best deployed in combination with dedicated sparsification methods.
