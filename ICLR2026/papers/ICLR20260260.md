## Problem

Standard MoE pretraining relies solely on a load-balancing loss that enforces uniform expert usage but provides no signal for what each expert should learn, leading to expert homogenization where experts develop redundant, overlapping functionalities. This paper asks whether explicitly guiding expert specialization through domain-aware supervision can unlock the full capacity of MoE architectures.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260260/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes pretraining sequences (up to 8192 tokens) each annotated with a domain label from either a coarse 3-class scheme (English/Chinese/Math source) or a fine-grained 49-class topic scheme (24 English topics + 24 Chinese topics + 1 Math). Beyond the standard next-token prediction output, the training loop additionally computes per-domain average router probability vectors over the N experts at each MoE layer, which are used to evaluate the pairwise divergence loss. The domain labels are the only extra input beyond raw tokens, and the loss operates on the low-dimensional router outputs (N-dim vectors) rather than full hidden states.

## Method

Expert Divergence Learning introduces an auxiliary loss L_ED that maximizes the pairwise Jensen-Shannon Divergence between domain-averaged expert routing distributions within each training batch. Concretely, token-level router probabilities are first averaged per sequence, then per domain, and the negative log of the JS divergence between every pair of domain distributions is averaged to form the loss, which is added to the standard LM and load-balancing objectives with a small weight β. The theoretical contribution formalizes a divergence decomposition showing total routing diversity splits into inter-domain and intra-domain components, and proves via second-order Taylor expansion that maximizing pairwise domain divergence (the practical objective) is locally proportional to increasing inter-domain divergence (the theoretical target), thereby channeling the global diversity promoted by load-balancing into structured, domain-specific expert specialization. The approach requires no architectural changes, adds negligible computational overhead, and is validated by pretraining MoE models up to 15B parameters from scratch on 100B tokens.

## Results

Across three model scales (15B-A1.5B, 8B-A0.8B, 3B-A0.3B), Expert Divergence Learning with the 49-class domain scheme consistently improves the average benchmark score over the standard MoE baseline: 36.65 vs. 35.59 (+1.06) for 15B, 35.77 vs. 35.10 (+0.67) for 8B, and 34.91 vs. 34.13 (+0.78) for 3B. The 49-class scheme outperforms the coarser 3-class scheme at every scale, with the largest gap on the 15B model (36.65 vs. 36.34). All Expert Divergence variants also achieve a lower language modeling loss than the baseline throughout pre-training, and the method introduces negligible computational overhead (training throughput drops <2%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the standard MoE training paradigm (language modeling loss + load-balancing loss only), which the paper compares against at three model scales. Expert Divergence Learning with 49-class labels wins on the average score at every scale: +1.06 (15B), +0.67 (8B), and +0.78 (3B), with the 15B model showing the largest absolute gain and a statistically significant margin (1.10 > 3σ across four late-stage checkpoints). The 3-class scheme also beats the baseline on 15B (+0.75) and 8B (+0.44) but underperforms the baseline on 3B (−0.15), suggesting that finer-grained domain signals are more critical for smaller models. In a secondary comparison (Appendix K), the paper shows that combining Expert Divergence Loss with the ERNIE 4.5 routing-orthogonality loss on the 8B model yields additional validation-loss reductions (e.g., math_val_1000: 1.7533 vs. 1.7556 for ERNIE 4.5 alone), confirming the two objectives are complementary rather than redundant.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the standard MoE training paradigm (Shazeer et al., 2017; Fedus et al., 2022) by adding a supervised, label-driven auxiliary loss that explicitly guides expert specialization, departing from prior approaches that either modify architecture (shared experts in DeepSeek-V3, expert-choice routing) or apply unsupervised regularizers (router orthogonality in ERNIE 4.5). It builds on the domain classification methodology of Wettig et al. (2025) to construct fine-grained topic labels, and on Qiu et al. (2025)'s observation that load-balancing loss granularity affects specialization. The key conceptual departure is framing expert specialization as a routing-diversity allocation problem: rather than letting the load-balancing loss distribute diversity indiscriminately, the proposed JS-divergence loss channels it specifically into inter-domain distinctions, supported by a clean theoretical decomposition (D_total = D_inter + D_intra).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The technique is remarkably lightweight—requiring only domain labels already present in pretraining corpora and a small pairwise divergence computation—yet it consistently improves both training loss and downstream benchmarks across three model scales with negligible throughput cost, making it an attractive drop-in addition to existing MoE pipelines. The document-level ablation (treating each document as a unique domain degrades performance below baseline) is a useful negative result confirming that semantically meaningful grouping is essential, not just any granularity. Open questions include whether the approach scales to hundreds of billions of parameters, whether alternative supervision signals (e.g., learned embeddings rather than discrete labels) could replace the need for domain classifiers, and how the method interacts with emerging MoE variants like auxiliary-loss-free balancing or multi-token prediction.
