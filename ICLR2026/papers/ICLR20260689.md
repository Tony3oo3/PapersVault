## Problem

Current model-brain alignment benchmarking relies on fMRI (limited to ~1s temporal resolution) and static image stimuli, leaving the fine-grained temporal dynamics of video processing in the brain unexplored. No prior work has systematically compared 100+ deep video models against dynamic EEG recordings of natural video viewing, nor has it leveraged the temporally unfolded internal features of models to probe when specific model representations align with neural activity.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260689/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes 102 short (3s) natural videos from Moments in Time, 128-channel EEG recordings from 6 participants (downsampled to 50 Hz, epoched −0.2s to 3.5s, MVNN-normalized), and 110 DNN models (44 image object, 10 image action, 49 video action, 7 additional) whose features are extracted from all higher-level blocks and time-unfolded into T×S per-video timepoints. For each EEG timepoint, CT-RSA outputs the maximum Spearman correlation across all model layers and all model timepoints, along with the identity of the best-aligning layer and timepoint, yielding over 10⁷ alignment scores total. Results are partitioned into posterior (35 electrodes over visual cortex) and frontal (54 electrodes over prefrontal cortex) regions, scaled by the upper noise ceiling.

## Method

The core contribution is Cross-Temporal Representational Similarity Analysis (CT-RSA), which extends standard RSA by computing Representational Dissimilarity Matrices for both the super-subject EEG signal at each timepoint and each model layer at each time-unfolded frame, then computing Spearman correlations across all model-time × EEG-time combinations and selecting the maximum per EEG timepoint—imposing no a priori mapping between model and neural time. Model features are flattened to (C,H,W) and reduced via Sparse Random Projection + PCA to 100 components; EEG channels are reweighted by multivariate noise normalisation. Statistical significance is assessed via 10,000-permutation null distributions with FDR correction across timepoints, and pre-stimulus baseline subtraction corrects for inflation from the maximization step. The benchmark systematically varies four axes—temporal integration (static vs. video), classification task (object vs. action), architecture (CNN, Transformer, SSM), and pretraining (none, supervised image, supervised video, self-supervised video)—to identify which model properties best capture the evolving neural preference across four identified processing stages.

## Results

The paper benchmarks 110 DNN models (44 image object, 10 image action, 49 video action, 7 additional) against EEG responses to 102 short (3s) natural videos using the proposed CT-RSA, distilling over 10⁷ alignment scores. Four temporal processing stages emerge in posterior electrodes: Stage I (0.06–0.24s) is dominated by static image models (best: AlexNet) via early layers; Stage II (0.24–0.8s) peaks for object recognition models (best: DenseNet) via late layers; Stage III (0.8–2s) is captured by temporally-integrating video models (best: MViT-v2) via mid-level layers; Stage IV (2–3s) shows stable but lower scores with video models less significantly outperforming. Posterior electrodes exhibit strong temporal correspondence (best model timepoint tracks EEG time up to ~0.6s), while frontal electrodes show no temporal correspondence and are best aligned with static high-level action features (peaks at 0.24s and 0.5s). Among architectures, state-space models (SSMs) best capture mid-level intermediate posterior processing in Stage II, and self-supervised pretraining is superior in Stage II while no pretraining is best in Stage III.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a single new model but systematically compares 110 models along four axes. On temporal integration, video (temporally-integrating) models significantly outperform image (static) models in Stages III–IV of posterior electrodes, while image models win in Stages I–II. On classification task, object recognition models peak in Stage II while action recognition models dominate Stage III, with action models corresponding to earlier layers than object models in Stages I and III. On architecture, SSMs (e.g., VideoMamba) significantly outperform both CNNs and Transformers in posterior Stage II via mid-depth layers, while Transformers beat CNNs in Stage I; in frontal electrodes CNNs have only a marginal edge in Stages I–II. On pretraining, self-supervised video pretraining achieves the highest alignment in Stage II (superior to all other types), while no pretraining is significantly best in Stage III, with supervised video pretraining showing a slight advantage in frontal electrodes. Context window length and sampling rate (fps) show significant positive correlations with posterior alignment for video models specifically in Stages III–IV, reinforcing the role of temporal integration in late processing.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the model-brain alignment benchmarking framework from Kriegeskorte et al.'s RSA to a cross-temporal setting (CT-RSA), and builds directly on Sartzetaki et al. (ICLR 2025), which benchmarked 99 models against video fMRI. The key departures are: (1) shifting from fMRI to EEG to access millisecond-scale neural dynamics, (2) unfolding the temporal dimension of model features rather than aggregating them, and (3) adding state-space models (VideoMamba) to the architecture space. Conceptually, the paper moves beyond the standard 'temporal hierarchy' narrative (low-to-high level processing over time) toward a 'dynamic mixture of experts' framing where the brain switches between processing modes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first large-scale EEG-based video alignment benchmark (110 models, 102 videos, 24 repeats), and the CT-RSA method is broadly applicable to any sequential model-brain comparison. The finding that no single model type captures the full EEG timecourse, and that frontal electrodes show no temporal correspondence to the video, challenges the prevailing temporal hierarchy account and suggests brain-inspired architectures should support dynamic switching between semantic tasks and temporal integration strategies. Key limitations include only 6 subjects, coarse EEG spatial resolution, and 3-second video clips; the authors identify EEG-fMRI fusion and intermediate-duration (10–20s) videos as critical next steps to resolve whether frontal-to-posterior feedback shapes late-stage processing.
