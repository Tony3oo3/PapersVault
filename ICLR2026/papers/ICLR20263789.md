## Problem

Standard Gaussian-process Bayesian optimization degrades sharply beyond a few dozen dimensions due to cubic training costs, kernel hyperparameter sensitivity, and the curse of dimensionality. Although TabPFN v2 extends tabular foundation model surrogates to 500-dimensional inputs, prior analyses show its predictive accuracy deteriorates in high-dimensional regimes without additional structural guidance. The paper asks whether a frozen TFM can be made competitive for high-dimensional BO by coupling it with classical subspace-discovery mechanisms, rather than relying on iterative GP retraining or additive decompositions.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a black-box objective f over a compact domain [0,1]^D (D up to 500) and an iteratively growing context of observed evaluations D_obs, which is fed as in-context input to TabPFN v2 (capped at 10k total samples). At each iteration it produces a single next query point x_next selected by UCB acquisition over candidates projected from a fixed r=10 gradient-informed subspace back into the full D-dimensional space via x_ref + V_r z, where z is drawn uniformly from [-1,1]^r and x_ref is the centroid of observed data. The final output is the best-found x* over the 500-iteration budget, alongside per-iteration predictive means, variances, and the evolving Fisher-information eigenvector matrix.

## Method

GIT-BO replaces the GP surrogate with a frozen TabPFN v2 transformer that performs amortized Bayesian inference in a single forward pass over the growing observation context, eliminating all kernel re-fitting. The key architectural contribution is a gradient-informed active subspace: a single backpropagation step through TabPFN yields predictive-mean gradients ∇_x μ_m(x), from which a Fisher-information matrix H = E[∇μ ∇μ^T] is estimated and its top-r eigenvectors define the search subspace V_r. Candidate points are sampled uniformly in the r-dimensional hypercube, mapped back to the full space, and scored with a UCB acquisition (β=2.33, sampling-based with S=512 posterior draws). Theoretically, the paper extends the Srinivas et al. GP-UCB regret framework to TabPFN surrogates by introducing an approximation-error term and a subspace information-gain bound derived from φ-Sobolev certificates, yielding sublinear regret R_T = O(√(rT(log T)^{r+2})) when r ≪ D. No online retraining or hyperparameter tuning is required; the entire loop is a forward pass, a backward pass for gradients, an eigendecomposition, and an acquisition maximization.

## Results

Across 60 benchmark variants (9 synthetic families × 5 dimensions + 10 real-world tasks), GIT-BO achieves the best overall statistical performance rank of 1.92 (lower is better) after 500 iterations, finding the known optimum on 29 of 60 problems. On real-world engineering tasks (power systems, car crash, Mazda, Walker-2D), GIT-BO dominates all baselines, while BAxUS leads on synthetic benchmarks. In runtime, GIT-BO reaches competitive or superior regret within minutes, whereas BAxUS and Vanilla BO require roughly an additional hour of wall-clock time to match final solution quality, with GIT-BO's runtime advantage growing as dimensionality increases from 100D to 500D.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GIT-BO outperforms all four GP-based baselines (SAASBO, TuRBO, Vanilla BO, BAxUS) in overall optimization quality across the 60-problem suite, achieving the top statistical rank (1.92) and the best Pareto frontier position when both performance and runtime are considered. BAxUS is the strongest competitor on synthetic benchmarks (where it achieves the best rank), but drops to fourth place on real-world engineering problems, where GIT-BO clearly dominates. TuRBO offers the fastest runtime among all methods but sacrifices optimization quality, placing it on the Pareto frontier only for speed. SAASBO and Vanilla BO are consistently outperformed by GIT-BO in both regret and runtime across most dimensions. Notably, GIT-BO's convergence advantage is most pronounced at higher dimensions (400–500D), where GP-based methods degrade due to kernel sensitivity and retraining costs, while TabPFN's frozen in-context inference maintains stable performance. GIT-BO does lose on two specific tasks (Rover and Styblinski-Tang), consistent with the no-free-lunch theorem, but these are exceptions rather than the pattern.

## Relation to Prior Work

GIT-BO synthesizes two previously separate research threads: tabular foundation models (TabPFN v2, building on the PFN in-context learning paradigm of Müller et al.) as frozen surrogates, and classical gradient active subspace methods (Zahm et al., Li et al.) for certified dimension reduction. Prior TFM-based BO work (PFNs4BO, Rakotoarison et al.) demonstrated speedups on low-dimensional tasks but did not address the high-dimensional regime where TabPFN v2's predictive quality degrades. Conversely, all existing high-dimensional BO methods (SAASBO, TuRBO, BAxUS, Vanilla BO) rely on GP surrogates requiring iterative kernel re-fitting. The key departure is using the frozen TFM's own predictive-mean gradients to construct the Fisher information matrix and identify the active subspace, eliminating both surrogate retraining and the need for a separate dimensionality-reduction model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first demonstration that a frozen foundation model surrogate, paired with a lightweight gradient-informed subspace, can match or exceed GP-based high-dimensional BO methods across 60 problems up to 500 dimensions, with runtime advantages that grow with dimensionality. The ablation showing vanilla TabPFN v2 without the GI subspace fails by ~8.6× in regret confirms that structural guidance is the critical ingredient, not merely the foundation model's capacity. Key limitations include the 500D hard cap, substantial GPU memory requirements, a fixed r=10 subspace dimension (adaptive selection shows further gains), and performance plateaus tied to TabPFN's known bias behavior. Open questions include memory-efficient TFM architectures, automated subspace dimension selection, and extension to constrained, mixed-variable, and multi-objective settings.
