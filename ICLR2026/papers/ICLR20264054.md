## Problem

Current reward modeling relies on binary Bradley-Terry models, but when annotators provide graded Likert-scale preferences (e.g., 'significantly better' vs. 'slightly better'), existing methods patch the binary loss with ad-hoc margins, scaling factors, or soft probability labels. These heuristics lack a mathematical model of how ordinal preference data is generated and require manually tuned hyperparameters whose values must be retuned whenever the number or definition of preference levels changes.

## Input / Output

The method takes a prompt x, two candidate responses y and y', and an ordinal preference label z ∈ {-K,...,0,...,K} encoding both direction and magnitude of preference (z=0 indicates approximately equal responses). It produces scalar reward scores r_φ(x,y) and r_φ(x,y') from a language model with a single-layer reward head, plus 2K learned threshold parameters that map reward differences to ordinal levels. Sequences are capped at 2048 tokens with prompts truncated to 1600, and the ordinal label space is symmetric around zero, with |z| indicating preference strength.

## Method

The core contribution reframes reward modeling with ordinal feedback as discrete ordinal regression, introducing 2K learned thresholds ζ_{-K},...,ζ_{-1},ζ_{1},...,ζ_{K} that partition the continuous reward-difference space into 2K+1 segments corresponding to preference levels. Two loss functions are derived: a negative log-likelihood loss from the ordered logit model (probabilistic) and an all-threshold loss from margin-based ordinal regression, both learning thresholds directly from data rather than requiring manual specification of margins or scaling weights. A key theoretical result (Theorem 3.1) proves that without L2 regularization on thresholds, the optimization admits unbounded solutions, motivating the regularized objective that ensures finite optima. The framework supports symmetric (ζ_{-k} = -ζ_k) and asymmetric threshold variants, with the symmetric model validated by a theorem showing it arises naturally when human preferences exhibit symmetry. Thresholds are reparameterized via exponential increments to enforce ordering constraints, enabling standard gradient-based joint optimization of reward and threshold parameters.

## Results

On RewardBench (trained on HelpSteer2), NLL-Symmetric achieves the highest average score of 0.843 with Llama-3.1-8B, outperforming Margin BT (0.802), Scaled BT (0.757), and Soft Label (0.722) by 4.1%, 8.6%, and 12.1% respectively. On RM-Bench, NLL-Symmetric and All-Threshold achieve the best Total scores (0.651 and 0.658) for Llama trained on HelpSteer2. Beyond binary accuracy, the ordinal framework reduces mean error severity by 87% (from 3.827 to 0.501) compared to standard Bradley-Terry, and achieves ~85% accuracy within one ordinal level on held-out data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against three heuristic baselines: Margin BT (Touvron et al., 2023), Scaled BT (Wang et al., 2024a), and Soft Label (Gunter et al., 2024). NLL-Symmetric clearly wins on Llama-3.1-8B across both training datasets, achieving 0.843 vs. 0.802 (Margin BT) and 0.757 (Scaled BT) on RewardBench/HelpSteer2, and 0.807 vs. 0.771 and 0.781 on HelpSteer3. However, the advantage is less uniform: on Mistral-7B trained on HelpSteer2, Scaled BT (0.800) actually outperforms NLL-Symmetric (0.779), and on Zephyr-7B, Soft Label (0.807) edges out NLL-Symmetric (0.768). The All-Threshold variant consistently underperforms the NLL variants but still beats baselines in several configurations. The most compelling advantage is in error severity: NLL-Symmetric reduces mean error margin by 87% (3.827 → 0.501) compared to standard BT, meaning when the model errs, it does so only on genuinely ambiguous cases rather than high-confidence misrankings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously disconnected literatures: the statistical ordinal regression framework (McCullagh 1980; Rennie & Srebro 2005) and LLM reward modeling built on the Bradley-Terry model. Prior attempts to incorporate Likert-scale preference data into reward models (Margin BT in Llama-2, Scaled BT in HelpSteer2, Soft Label in Apple's models) modified the binary BT loss with manually specified margins or scaling weights, lacking any generative model for how ordinal labels arise. The paper's key departure is deriving both a probabilistic NLL loss and a margin-based all-threshold loss from an explicit ordered-logit model of the reward difference, with all thresholds learned from data rather than hand-tuned. The DPO extension in Appendix A follows directly from the structural equivalence between BT reward modeling and DPO pseudo-rewards.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides the first principled mathematical treatment of ordinal preference data in reward modeling, eliminating the need for dataset-specific hyperparameter tuning and offering interpretable learned thresholds. The 87% reduction in mean error severity (3.827 → 0.501) compared to standard BT is practically significant for downstream RL, where confidently wrong rewards can derail policy optimization. However, the DPO extension remains purely theoretical with no empirical validation, and the framework has only been tested on 7-level Likert scales with three 7-8B models, leaving open questions about scalability to more complex annotation schemes (multi-aspect ratings, confidence scores) and larger model families. The robustness to systematic label noise (essentially unchanged performance at 100% shift corruption) suggests the learned thresholds absorb annotator calibration biases, but this has not been tested in production-scale settings.
