## Problem

Iterative adversarial attacks (e.g., PGD) are the standard oracle for robustness evaluation and adversarial training, but each step requires a full forward and backward pass, making them 40–200× more expensive than natural inference. The only common lever for reducing cost is coarsely cutting the number of iterations, which substantially weakens attack strength. The paper asks: given a fixed computation budget, how can one maximize attack strength by exploiting the fine-grained redundancy that exists across both iterations and layers?

## Input / Output

The method takes a clean RGB image (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 64×64 for Tiny-ImageNet) or a citation graph (Cora, Citeseer) together with its ground-truth label, and produces an adversarial example constrained to an ℓ_p ball of radius ε around the input. The key operational parameter is the spiking threshold ρ ∈ [0,1], which controls the fraction of full-precision layer operations executed across the T attack iterations; the relative computational cost is measured as the proportion of full-precision operations actually performed versus a vanilla T-step attack.

## Method

Spiking-PGD reformulates the per-iteration forward pass using the linearity of each layer: the current output equals the previous output plus the layer applied to the activation residual. A spiking gate S_ρ then decides, per layer and per iteration, whether the relative activation change ‖a_t − a_{t−1}‖/‖a_t‖ exceeds threshold ρ; if not, the previous output is reused and the layer computation is skipped entirely. To prevent the resulting gradient vanishing (autograd yields zero when an output is cached), the paper introduces a virtual surrogate gradient that manually applies the transpose of the layer operator A^{(l)⊤} to the upstream gradient from the previous iteration, restoring a faithful backward signal via register_hook. The paper further formalizes the attack as a combinatorial optimization over a binary T×L mask Δ selecting which layer computations to execute, proving that coarse-grained early stopping is a strict subproblem of this fine-grained formulation, and shows that the single threshold ρ provides a practical, event-driven solution that expands the efficiency–effectiveness Pareto frontier.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 adversarial training, Spiking-PGD with exponential decay (λ=2.0) achieves 82.33% clean accuracy and 47.45% robust accuracy (sum 129.78%) while using under 30% of the computation of vanilla PGD-AT (80.18% clean, 48.75% robust, sum 128.93%). In the attack-strength evaluation (Figures 5–6), Spiking-PGD consistently achieves lower model accuracy (i.e., stronger attack) than PGD, I-FGSM, and MI-FGSM at every matched computation cost across CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, Tiny-ImageNet, Cora, and Citeseer, with the largest gains in the low-budget regime (≤50% cost). The ablation on the spiking threshold ρ shows that increasing ρ from 0 to 0.05 reduces computation cost substantially while degrading attack strength only moderately.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against PGD (Madry et al., 2017), Spiking-PGD achieves strictly better attack strength at every matched computation cost on all five benchmarks; in adversarial training, the λ=2.0 schedule matches or exceeds PGD-AT's clean/robust accuracy (82.33/47.45 vs. 80.18/48.75) at roughly 30% of the compute. Against I-FGSM (Kurakin et al., 2018), Spiking-PGD dominates across the entire cost range on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and Tiny-ImageNet, with the gap widening as budget shrinks. Against MI-FGSM (Dong et al., 2017), which is the strongest baseline due to momentum, Spiking-PGD still outperforms at low-to-moderate budgets where MI-FGSM's attack strength drops sharply with fewer iterations. Against the PGD topology attack (Xu et al., 2019) on graph data, Spiking-PGD achieves noticeably higher attack strength on both Cora and Citeseer, particularly at small-to-moderate budgets, confirming that activation reuse preserves enough signal for structure perturbations. Overall, Spiking-PGD expands the efficiency–effectiveness Pareto frontier: it never loses to any baseline at equal cost and requires substantially less cost to match their peak attack strength.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on PGD (Madry et al., 2017) as its base iterative attack, but departs from the standard paradigm where iteration count is the sole lever for controlling computational cost. Prior efficiency-oriented attacks (MI-FGSM, DI-FGSM, TI-FGSM) improve per-iteration quality but still perform full-precision computation at every step; this work instead introduces a fine-grained, layer-wise spiking mechanism that selectively skips redundant forward computations based on activation change magnitude. The combinatorial-optimization framing (Eq. 2) formalizes why coarse-grained early stopping is suboptimal, and the virtual surrogate gradient is a novel technical device to preserve backward signal when activations are reused—addressing a gradient-vanishing issue that prior activation-reuse or quantization methods in other contexts have encountered but not in the adversarial-attack setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for anyone running adversarial training or robustness evaluation at scale: reducing adversarial training cost to ~30% of standard PGD-AT while matching final robust accuracy could make robust training feasible for larger models and more training steps. However, the experiments are limited to ResNet-18 and GCN on small datasets (CIFAR-10/100, Tiny-ImageNet, Cora, Citeseer), and the spiking mechanism's analysis relies on linearity of layer mappings, leaving open how well it transfers to deeper, non-linear architectures (e.g., transformers, ResNet-50+) with BatchNorm and ReLU. An open question is whether the single global threshold ρ is sufficient or whether per-layer or per-iteration adaptive thresholds would further improve the efficiency-accuracy trade-off.
