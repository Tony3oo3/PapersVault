## Problem

Existing hardware-aware gradient-based NAS methods integrate hardware metrics as regularization terms in the loss function, requiring differentiable hardware metrics and careful tuning of regularization weights. This often leads to architectures that either over-penalize resource-intensive operations (yielding suboptimal accuracy) or fail to satisfy the target device's hardware constraints. The paper asks whether hardware constraints can be enforced directly during gradient-based search without differentiable hardware metrics or weight tuning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262600/cropped_page4_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a search space defined as an overparameterized DAG with candidate operations per edge, explicit hardware constraint upper bounds (e.g., latency ≤ 5.31 ms, peak memory ≤ 655 kB), and standard training data. Hardware metrics are evaluated via lookup tables, on-device measurements, or analytical computation from the architecture definition—no differentiability with respect to α is required. The output is a discrete architecture A derived by argmax over the learned architecture weights α, guaranteed to lie within the feasibility region defined by all imposed constraints.

## Method

CONNAS modifies the gradient of architecture weights α whenever the current architecture violates a hardware constraint, rather than adding a regularization term to the loss. For each violated constraint, a direction vector dir_C(α) is computed per edge by evaluating hardware metrics of all candidate operations under the current configuration and aggregating unit vectors that point from infeasible candidates toward feasible ones (or, when no candidate is feasible, from higher-metric to lower-metric candidates). The modified gradient is G_α + R · dir_C(α) · max{‖G_α‖, ε}, where R > 1 is a rescale factor whose exact value is not sensitive, eliminating the need for per-constraint hyperparameter tuning. Operation weights w are updated via standard SGD, while α is updated with the constraint-modified gradient using Adam, following the interleaved bi-level optimization of DARTS with Gumbel-Softmax relaxation.

## Results

On the NATS-Bench size search space, CONNAS achieves 93.28% top-1 accuracy on CIFAR-10 under a peak memory constraint of 655 kB, within just 0.14% of the optimal feasible architecture. Across all constraint configurations (parameters, FLOPs, peak memory, and combined), CONNAS satisfies hardware constraints in 5/5 runs while achieving relative errors of at most −0.14% to −5.13% compared to the best feasible architecture. On the topology search space with real on-device measurements (NVIDIA Jetson TX2), CONNAS reaches 93.14% on CIFAR-10 under combined latency and energy constraints, again with 5/5 constraint satisfaction. In the practical induction motor use case, CONNAS outperforms the best handcrafted architecture by up to 1.55% accuracy under a 64 kB model size and 18 kB memory budget.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CONNAS consistently outperforms all five baselines on constraint satisfaction: it achieves 5/5 feasible runs across every configuration, whereas FBNet and TAS frequently produce infeasible architectures (0/5 runs in most settings). On the size search space, CONNAS substantially outperforms ProxylessNAS (e.g., 91.74% vs. 85.87% on CIFAR-10 under the parameter constraint, a 5.9-point gap) and HDX (91.74% vs. 86.10%), both of which suffer from overly aggressive hardware regularization. Against TF-NAS, the closest competitor that also satisfies constraints, CONNAS achieves comparable or slightly better accuracy (e.g., 93.28% vs. 92.99% on CIFAR-10 under the memory constraint) while eliminating the need for per-constraint hyperparameter tuning. Under stricter constraints (25% and 10% feasible architectures), the gap widens dramatically: HDX collapses to ~10% accuracy while CONNAS maintains 89–92%, and TF-NAS also degrades severely. The key practical advantage is that CONNAS requires no weighting factor tuning, whereas baselines like ProxylessNAS and HDX show extreme sensitivity to their λ_hardware hyperparameter (e.g., ProxylessNAS drops from 92.29% to 42.92% when λ changes from 1.0 to 1.5).

## Relation to Prior Work

CONNAS adapts the constraint-guided gradient descent (CGGD) framework of Van Baelen & Karsmakers (2023) from the setting of enforcing output-level inequality constraints to the problem of steering architecture weights toward hardware-feasible regions during differentiable NAS. It operates within the standard DARTS-style overparameterized supernet with Gumbel-Softmax relaxation, but departs fundamentally from prior hardware-aware NAS methods (ProxylessNAS, FBNet, TAS, TF-NAS, HDX) that embed hardware metrics as weighted regularization terms in the loss. By modifying gradients directly rather than augmenting the objective, CONNAS eliminates the need for differentiable hardware metrics and the associated hyperparameter tuning of regularization weights.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that practitioners no longer need to iteratively tune hardware-metric weighting factors or rerun searches to find feasible architectures—a tedious and time-consuming process acknowledged by the authors. The per-edge heuristic for computing the gradient direction, while losing the strict convergence guarantees of CGGD, proves effective in practice across multiple search spaces and constraint tightness levels. Open questions include whether the per-edge approximation remains sufficient for very large or highly coupled search spaces, and whether the approach extends naturally to multi-objective or Pareto-front settings beyond simple inequality constraints. The validation is limited to NATS-Bench-scale search spaces and a single practical use case, leaving scalability to industrial-scale architectures untested.
