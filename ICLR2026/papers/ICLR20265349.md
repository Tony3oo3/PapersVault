## Problem

AI evaluations have historically focused on problem-solving capacity (e.g., how well models play chess or Go), but reasoning also involves evaluating whether a problem is worth solving at all. This paper introduces a formalism and empirical benchmark for assessing how well language and reasoning models evaluate games—specifically their expected payoff (fairness) and perceived funness—before any play, comparing model judgments against human evaluations and game-theoretic optima.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20265349/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Models receive a text description of a novel two-player grid-based strategy game (board dimensions, win conditions, and any asymmetric rules) and must produce two types of evaluations: (1) for payoff, separate 0–100 estimates of P(P1 wins | not draw) and P(draw), which are combined into a single expected payoff in [−1, 1]; and (2) for funness, a single 0–100 rating relative to the broader class of grid-based games. Each model is sampled with 20 rollouts per game to match the ~20 human participants who rated each game as novices before any play, across 121 novel Tic-Tac-Toe variants unlikely to appear in training corpora.

## Method

The paper evaluates a spectrum of models—non-reasoning LLMs (LLaMA 3.1 70B, GPT-4, DeepSeek v3) with direct and chain-of-thought prompting, and reasoning models (DeepSeek R1, Gemini 2.5 Flash/Pro, o1, o3, GPT-5) at medium reasoning effort—against human judgments (450+ participants) and symbolic baselines (Intuitive Gamer, Expert Gamer, MCTS, Random) from Collins et al. (2025). The primary similarity metric is R² between averaged model and human judgments over all 121 games, supplemented by split-half human correlation (R²=0.82) and, for 78 games with computable game-theoretic optima, accuracy and absolute deviation from the optimal payoff. Reasoning traces from open models are automatically coded (via o3) for explicit game simulation, analogical reasoning, and mathematical computation, while reasoning token counts are tracked as a proxy for resource expenditure. The key finding is a non-monotonic relationship: as reasoning models approach game-theoretic optimality, their alignment with human judgments can degrade, and funness evaluations show greater cross-model variability consistent with the difficulty of quantifying that query.

## Results

On the payoff (fairness) query, reasoning models substantially outperform non-reasoning LLMs relative to the game-theoretic optimal (GTO): GPT-5 achieves R²=0.82, o3 achieves R²=0.71, and Gemini 2.5 Pro achieves R²=0.66, compared to 0.19–0.35 for direct (non-CoT) non-reasoning models. A key non-monotonic finding emerges: GPT-5 is closest to GTO (R²=0.82, Deviation=0.15) yet is *less* aligned with human judgments than o3, which sits at R²=0.71 to GTO but closer to the human split-half ceiling (R²=0.82). For the funness query, reasoning models generally capture more human variance than non-reasoning models, but performance is notably 'jagged' across model sophistication—more advanced models are not consistently more human-aligned—consistent with the greater difficulty of quantifying fun.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Collins et al. (2025) computational baselines, GPT-5 (R²=0.82, Deviation=0.15) approaches but does not match the Expert Gamer (R²=0.87, Deviation=0.08) or MCTS (R²=0.89, Deviation=0.06) on proximity to GTO, while o3 (R²=0.71) sits between the Intuitive Gamer (R²=0.69) and Expert Gamer. All non-reasoning LLMs in direct mode (R²=0.19–0.35) perform at or below the Random baseline (R²=0.39), indicating that statistical associations from pretraining alone are insufficient for game evaluation. The MCTS baseline (Coulom, 2006) remains the strongest single comparator to GTO, with GPT-5 closing the gap to within 0.07 R² points. Critically, the paper reveals a non-monotonic trade-off: as models approach game-theoretic optimality (GPT-5 > o3 > o1), their alignment with *human* judgments degrades, meaning the best GTO-matching model is not the best human-aligned model. For funness, no single baseline dominates; the Intuitive Gamer and MCTS comparisons are not directly applicable since funness baselines are regression models fit to a subset of human data, making the LLM-to-human R² the primary comparison axis.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant AI evaluation paradigm of assessing how well models *play* games (chess, Go, poker, ARC-AGI) by introducing the distinct task of assessing how well models *evaluate* games before any play. It builds directly on Collins et al. (2025), which provided the 121 novel Tic-Tac-Toe-variant games and the computational cognitive baselines (Intuitive Gamer, Expert Gamer, MCTS, Random), but reframes the question from 'can the model solve this game?' to 'can the model judge this game's properties?' The conceptual grounding draws on meta-reasoning and resource-rationality literature from cognitive science (Griffiths 2020; Lieder & Griffiths 2020; Wong et al. 2025), positioning game evaluation as a form of higher-order problem selection rather than problem solving.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The non-monotonic relationship—where increasing game-theoretic rationality (GPT-4 → o3 → GPT-5) eventually *decreases* alignment with human judgments—challenges the implicit assumption that 'more optimal = more human-aligned' and raises fundamental questions about what target evaluations should aim for. The finding that resource usage (reasoning tokens) is highly variable and uncorrelated with game complexity or evaluation accuracy points to a clear gap in current reasoning models' meta-reasoning capabilities. Key limitations include the restriction to Tic-Tac-Toe variants (limiting generalizability to other game categories), the focus on 'novice' evaluators only, and sensitivity to prompting; open questions include whether results extend to cooperative or asymmetric games, how to balance rationality vs. human-alignment in evaluation targets, and how to make models dynamically allocate compute based on query difficulty.
