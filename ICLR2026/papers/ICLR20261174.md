## Problem

Current RL and expert iteration approaches for formal theorem proving rely on fixed theorem statement sets, causing wasted computation on trivial or unsolvable tasks and preventing the prover from adapting to increasingly complex problems. No prior method systematically aligns problem difficulty with the prover's evolving capability during training, limiting progress on advanced theorems.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261174/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The statement fuser consumes a pair of natural-language statements sampled from a 793,243-statement repository (Lean-Workbook + NuminaMath) and outputs a single fused NL statement that is more challenging than either input. The prover receives an NL-FL statement pair (the fused NL statement plus its autoformalized Lean4 counterpart) and generates 16 candidate Lean4 proofs per statement, which are verified by the Lean4 compiler. Proofs involving statement modification or the 'sorry'/'admit' tactic are counted as incorrect.

## Method

GAR is an iterative two-stage framework that jointly trains a statement fuser and a prover via adversarial GRPO. In the generation stage, the fuser (initialized as Qwen3-8B with a reinitialized thinking process via a dedicated <analysis> token) fuses two NL statements into a harder one, which is autoformalized by Kimina-Autoformalizer-7B and checked by Lean4; the prover then generates 16 proofs per statement. In the adversarial RL stage, the fuser is rewarded by (1−p)(1−m)·𝕀{p≠0} (encouraging harder-but-solvable problems) while the prover is rewarded by 1−0.5·m on statements with empirical pass rate in (0, 0.5) (encouraging correct proofs on medium/high-difficulty problems). A soft statement-modification penalty in both objectives prevents reward hacking from self-correction. This adversarial loop establishes an implicit curriculum: the fuser progressively raises difficulty while the prover progressively raises capability, with both models co-evolving across iterations.

## Results

GAR achieves an average relative improvement of 4.20% in pass@32 on MiniF2F-Test across two base models: Goedel-Prover-V2-8B improves from 77.87% to 80.33% (+3.16% relative), and DeepSeek-Prover-V2-7B improves from 70.49% to 74.18% (+5.23% relative). On the more challenging ProofNet-Test benchmark, DeepSeek-Prover-V2's pass@32 increases from 22.58% to 25.81%. On PutnamBench, the GAR-trained DeepSeek-Prover solves 24/660 problems versus 22/660 for the base model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GAR on Goedel-Prover-V2 (80.33% MiniF2F-Test) outperforms all eight baselines, including the strongest prior method Goedel-Prover-V2 itself (77.87%) by 2.46 absolute points. GAR on DeepSeek-Prover-V2 (74.18%) surpasses all baselines except Goedel-Prover-V2, and achieves the best ProofNet-Test score (25.81%) among all methods reporting on that benchmark, beating the next-best STP-Lean (19.50%) by 6.31 points. Notably, GAR improves upon its own base models (DeepSeek-Prover-V2: +3.69 pts; Goedel-Prover-V2: +2.46 pts), demonstrating that adversarial co-training with a statement fuser yields gains even on top of heavily RL-optimized models. In contrast, direct GRPO training on a static dataset degrades Goedel-Prover-V2 from 77.87% to 77.46%, confirming that the dynamic curriculum is essential. On PutnamBench, GAR-trained DeepSeek-Prover solves 2 additional problems (24 vs. 22 out of 660), further validating the generalizability of the gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the GRPO reinforcement learning framework (Shao et al., 2024) and the recent wave of Long-CoT formal provers (DeepSeek-Prover-V2, Goedel-Prover-V2, Kimina-Prover) that rely on fixed statement sets during RL training. It departs from this paradigm by introducing an adversarial co-training loop where a statement fuser and prover are jointly optimized, creating an implicit curriculum that dynamically adjusts problem difficulty. The closest prior work is STP (Dong & Ma, 2025b), which also generates new conjectures during training, but STP operates via offline expert iteration/SFT rather than online RL, and GAR's statement fusion (combining two NL statements) differs from single-problem restatement approaches. The paper positions itself as a general RL paradigm for co-evolution of problem generation and solving under verifiable environments, extending beyond theorem proving.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical bottleneck in formal theorem proving: fixed datasets cause wasted computation on trivial or unsolvable problems and prevent the prover from tackling increasingly complex theorems. The finding that standard GRPO on a heavily RL-trained model actually degrades performance (77.87% → 77.46%) while the adversarial curriculum improves it (→ 80.33%) suggests that for already-optimized models, the *type* of training signal matters more than additional RL on static data. Limitations include evaluation only on 7-8B scale models, a small number of training iterations (3-5), and reliance on a separate autoformalizer and fuser base model (Qwen3-8B) rather than an end-to-end system. Open questions include whether the adversarial dynamic converges or oscillates at scale, whether the approach transfers to other verifiable reasoning domains (e.g., code, physics), and how the method behaves with larger prover models.
