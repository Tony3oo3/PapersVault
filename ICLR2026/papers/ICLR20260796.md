## Problem

Formal reasoning with LLMs has been studied almost exclusively in mathematics, leaving physics and other natural sciences without a Lean4-based benchmark or supporting library. This raises the question of whether expert Lean provers trained on math benchmarks can transfer their formal reasoning capabilities to other domains, or whether they are overfit to mathematical structures.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260796/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each problem is presented as a pair: a natural-language physics statement and its corresponding Lean4 theorem (with hypotheses and goal), optionally augmented with the PhysLib source code in the prompt context. The model must generate a complete Lean4 proof that compiles and passes the Lean4 verifier. Evaluation uses pass@16 (16 sampling attempts per problem) across 200 problems spanning college-level, competition-easy, and competition-hard difficulty tiers, with results reported per difficulty and overall.

## Method

The framework consists of two components built bottom-up. PhysLib extends Mathlib with a full SI unit system (seven base units plus derived quantities like velocity via first-order derivatives) and organizes topic-specific theorems into six namespaces (mechanics, waves & acoustics, thermodynamics, electromagnetism, optics, modern physics), each with its own unit types, constants, and proved lemmas. LeanPhysBench is constructed via a formalization pipeline that transforms NL question-answering problems into closed-end proof statements by extracting physics laws, initial conditions, and proving targets, then writing and verifying Lean4 code with at least two expert reviewers per statement. The empirical study evaluates eight LLMs (general models like Gemini-2.5-pro and Claude-Sonnet-4, plus expert provers like DeepSeek-Prover-V2 and Goedel-Prover-V2) in two modes—with and without PhysLib in context—revealing that expert provers do not outperform large general models on physics, and that PhysLib yields a consistent 11.90% average improvement across all models.

## Results

On LeanPhysBench (200 Lean4 physics theorems), Gemini-2.5-pro with PhysLib achieves the best overall Pass@16 of 40.00%, followed by Claude-Sonnet-4 at 34.50%. Expert Lean4 provers (DeepSeek-Prover-V2, Goedel-Prover-V2, Kimina-Prover) achieve only 13.50–14.83% overall, failing to outperform general closed-source models as they do in the math domain. Adding PhysLib to the context yields a consistent average improvement of 11.90% across all models and difficulty levels. On the hardest subset (Comp-Hard), all models score near zero (≤6.86%), indicating severe limitations in formal physics reasoning involving complex calculus-based operations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 8 LLMs (5 open-source, 3 closed-source) on LeanPhysBench. Gemini-2.5-pro dominates overall (40.00% with PhysLib), outperforming Claude-Sonnet-4 (34.50%) by 5.5 points and all expert provers by over 25 points. Among expert Lean4 provers, DeepSeek-Prover-V2-7B (14.83%) marginally leads Goedel-Prover-V2-8B (14.67%) and Kimina-Prover-8B (13.50%), but all trail GPT-4o (14.17%) and are far behind the top closed-source general models. The critical finding is that expert provers, which significantly outperform general LLMs on Lean4 math benchmarks, fail to transfer this advantage to physics: they score only ~14% overall versus 34–40% for Gemini and Claude. On Comp-Hard specifically, expert provers (5.88–6.86%) slightly edge out closed-source models (0.00%), suggesting their complex deduction skills have limited but nonzero value for the hardest problems. General open-source models (DeepSeek-R1-8B at 5.83%, Qwen3-8B at 6.52%) perform worst overall, indicating that neither scale nor domain-specific Lean training alone suffices for formal physics reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Lean4 formal reasoning literature—dominated by mathematical benchmarks (MiniF2F, ProofNet, PutnamBench) and expert provers (DeepSeek-Prover, Goedel-Prover, Kimina-Prover)—into the physics domain by introducing a unit-aware formalization framework. It builds directly on Tao's Lean analysis companion for the SI unit system and on Mathlib for foundational algebraic infrastructure, but departs from prior work by treating physics theorems as first-class formal objects with dimensional consistency constraints rather than pure symbolic manipulation. In contrast to PhysLean (Tooby-Smith), which targets expert-driven formalization of advanced theoretical physics (fiber bundles, index notation), Lean4PHYS focuses on college-level problems and LLM-evaluable infrastructure, positioning itself as a bridge between natural-language physics and machine-verifiable proofs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that expert Lean4 math provers fail to outperform general-purpose LLMs on physics problems challenges the assumption that formal reasoning capability is domain-agnostic and suggests current provers overfit to mathematical structure rather than learning transferable formal reasoning. This has implications for how the community should approach multi-domain formalization: simply scaling math-trained provers may not suffice for new domains with novel type systems (e.g., units). Key limitations include the small benchmark size (200 problems), the early-stage PhysLib covering mechanics most thoroughly, and the focus on verifying given physical laws rather than deriving them from first principles. Open questions include retrieval-augmented integration as PhysLib grows beyond context windows, autoformalization of physics knowledge, and whether unit-aware training can close the cross-domain gap.
