## Problem

Existing SAE training algorithms for LLM interpretability (L1-regularized, TopK) lack rigorous theoretical guarantees for feature recovery and suffer from practical limitations including hyperparameter sensitivity, activation shrinkage, and cross-seed inconsistency. The paper asks two fundamental questions: what conditions on a neuron's activation frequency enable it to reliably learn a monosemantic feature, and can we design a training algorithm with provable recovery guarantees that remains practical for modern LLMs?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263579/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes row-normalized LLM internal activations (MLP outputs at specific layers of Qwen2.5-1.5B or Gemma2-2B, or synthetic s-sparse feature mixtures) and produces a high-dimensional sparse code z ∈ R^M with M ≫ d, where each active neuron ideally encodes a single interpretable feature, plus the reconstructed activation x̂ ≈ x. A key architectural constraint is that neuron biases are excluded from gradient-based optimization entirely and are instead controlled through a separate periodic feedback mechanism, while tied encoder/decoder weights and output scales are updated via Adam/AdamW on the reconstruction loss alone.

## Method

The core insight is 'neuron resonance': neurons reliably recover monosemantic features when their activation frequency p falls within a resonance band around the feature's occurrence frequency f (specifically f ≲ p ≲ min{√f, df}, up to logarithmic factors), with a phase transition at d = √n between light and heavy superposition. Group Bias Adaptation (GBA) operationalizes this by partitioning M neurons into K groups with geometrically-spaced target activation frequencies (e.g., 10% down to 0.01%), creating a spectrum of resonance bands that covers the long-tail feature frequency distribution in language. Within each group, biases are adaptively adjusted via feedback control—decreasing bias proportionally to max pre-activation for over-active neurons, and increasing bias using the group baseline for under-active ones—while biases are clamped to [-1,0] and excluded from gradient steps. The paper provides the first theoretical guarantee (Theorem 6.1) proving that under a decomposable data model X = HV with Gaussian features, the single-group Bias Adaptation variant recovers all monosemantic features within O(1) iterations of spherical gradient descent when the activation frequency satisfies the resonance condition, with network width scaling as M ≳ n · p^{-s/(1-ε)²}.

## Results

On SAEBench (Gemma2-2B, layer 12, L0 ≈ 300–400), GBA achieves the best performance in 4 of 9 interpretability metrics: Explained Variance (0.902 vs. 0.871 for GatedSAE), Absorption Score (0.0041 vs. 0.0269 for TopK), Alive Fraction (0.970 vs. 0.872 for TopK), and the lowest L0 (309.0). On the reconstruction-sparsity frontier (Qwen2.5-1.5B, 66k neurons, Pile Github/Wikipedia), GBA matches TopK's best curve while significantly outperforming L1 and single-group BA. For cross-seed consistency, GBA yields a higher fraction of neurons with MCS > 0.9 than TopK across all selection percentiles (e.g., 0.0366 vs. 0.0317 at top-10%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against TopK SAE (Gao et al., 2024), GBA matches the reconstruction-sparsity frontier but decisively wins on cross-seed consistency (higher MCS > 0.9 fraction at every α) and on SAEBench metrics: Explained Variance (0.902 vs. 0.859), Absorption Score (0.0041 vs. 0.0269), and Alive Fraction (0.970 vs. 0.872). Against L1/Standard SAE (Bricken et al., 2023), GBA achieves substantially lower reconstruction loss at the same sparsity level and dominates on Explained Variance (0.902 vs. 0.816) and Absorption Score (0.0041 vs. 0.1355). Against GatedSAE (Rajamanoharan et al., 2024a), GBA wins on Explained Variance (0.902 vs. 0.871), Absorption Score (0.0041 vs. 0.0696), and Alive Fraction (0.970 vs. 0.918). Against Matryoshka (Bussmann et al., 2025) and JumpReLU SAE (Rajamanoharan et al., 2024b), GBA leads on Explained Variance and Absorption Score but trails on TPP Metric (0.184 vs. 0.312 for Matryoshka) and SCR (0.296 vs. 0.332 for TopK). On the imbalanced synthetic toy example, GBA with full frequency coverage achieves 100% Feature Recovery Rate while TopK degrades from 98.4% (K=20) to 23.4% (K=50), demonstrating GBA's robustness to unknown sparsity levels.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the SAE-based LLM interpretability line (Bricken et al. 2023; Gao et al. 2024; Templeton et al. 2024) and the broader sparse dictionary learning literature (Olshausen & Field 1996; Spielman et al. 2012), but departs from prior SAE training methods (L1 penalty, TopK, Gated SAE) by reframing the problem through neuron activation frequency rather than indirect sparsity constraints. The key conceptual departure is the 'neuron resonance' principle: neurons must operate at an activation frequency matching the feature's occurrence rate to reliably learn it, which motivates the Group Bias Adaptation algorithm that directly controls per-neuron frequencies via adaptive bias updates. Theoretically, the paper provides the first provable feature recovery guarantee for an SAE training algorithm, extending spherical gradient descent analyses from single-index and polynomial learning to the sparse superposition setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The neuron resonance framework offers a principled, tuning-free alternative to TopK and L1 SAEs, with the practical benefit of eliminating dataset-specific hyperparameter search (e.g., choosing K or λ). The theoretical guarantee, while relying on Gaussian features and a simplified SAE model, establishes a foundation for understanding when and why SAEs succeed or fail at feature recovery. Open questions include extending the theory to non-Gaussian features and more complex SAE architectures, validating the resonance principle in deeper/more entangled LLM layers, and determining whether the geometric group spacing is truly optimal or merely sufficient. The moderate empirical gains over TopK (comparable reconstruction, better cross-seed consistency, 99% neuron aliveness) suggest the method is a solid but not transformative improvement for practitioners already using TopK SAEs.
