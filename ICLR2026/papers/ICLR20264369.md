## Problem

Classical influence functions, the standard tool for training data attribution, require inverting the Hessian of the training loss. For deep neural networks, the Hessian is typically non-invertible (singular) and intractable to compute at scale, forcing practitioners to rely on architecture-specific approximations (e.g., Kronecker-factored curvature) that introduce structural biases and restrict applicability to certain layer types. This paper asks whether a principled, Hessian-free alternative can match or exceed the accuracy of these approximations while scaling to models with billions of parameters.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a single trained model checkpoint w*, a training dataset D_train, and a query dataset D_query, and produces a pairwise influence matrix B of shape |D_train| × |D_query| where each entry is the negative posterior covariance between the corresponding training and query losses. For autoregressive language models, the loss decomposes per-token, yielding an S|D_train| × S|D_query| matrix of per-token influence scores computed in a single batched pass. The normalized variant (Pearson correlation over the posterior) is bounded in [-1, 1] and is used for qualitative analysis; raw covariance scores are used for quantitative retraining benchmarks.

## Method

The local Bayesian influence function (BIF) replaces the Hessian inverse in classical influence functions with a covariance over a localized Gibbs posterior p_γ(w | D_train, w*) ∝ exp(−Σℓ_i(w) − (γ/2)‖w − w*‖²), where γ controls the localization strength around the checkpoint. The key identity is BIF_γ(z_i, φ) = −Cov_γ(ℓ_i(w), φ(w)), a standard result from statistical physics that requires no Hessian computation. In practice, the covariance is estimated by running multiple independent SGLD chains (stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics) from w*, collecting loss traces over both training and query sets at each draw, and computing the sample covariance across all draws. The approach is architecture-agnostic (any differentiable model), requires no expensive fit phase, and for non-singular models the leading-order Taylor expansion of the local BIF recovers the classical dampened influence function, establishing it as a higher-order generalization. Batching is exploited in two independent ways: mini-batch gradients for the SGLD update and parallel forward passes over the full train and query sets for loss collection, enabling efficient computation of the entire influence matrix in one pass.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 (ResNet-9, ~2M params), the local BIF matches EK-FAC (the SOTA Hessian-based method) on the Linear Datamodelling Score (LDS), slightly outperforming EK-FAC in the small-data regime and slightly underperforming in the large-data regime (within margin of error). On Pythia-14M Wikitext finetuning, the BIF underperforms EK-FAC (LDS 0.110 vs. 0.221), which the authors attribute to SGLD sampling difficulties in the language model regime. In the scaling analysis across the Pythia suite (14M–2.8B params), the BIF is 2 orders of magnitude faster than EK-FAC for the largest models while using the same peak GPU RAM (4×A100), and avoids EK-FAC's expensive up-front Hessian fitting phase.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against EK-FAC (the primary SOTA baseline), the BIF achieves comparable LDS on CIFAR-10 ResNet-9 retraining experiments, with BIF slightly ahead in the small-data regime and slightly behind in the large-data regime (within error bars). However, on Pythia-14M Wikitext finetuning, EK-FAC clearly wins (LDS 0.221 vs. 0.110), a gap the authors attribute to immature SGLD hyperparameter tuning in the LLM setting. In the scaling benchmark across Pythia models up to 2.8B parameters, the BIF is 2 orders of magnitude faster than EK-FAC for the largest models and avoids EK-FAC's large up-front fitting cost (41 GiB disk for Pythia-2.8B). Against TRAK, the BIF consistently outperforms on CIFAR-10 LDS across all dataset sizes. Against GradSim, the BIF also outperforms on CIFAR-10 LDS, though on the Pythia-14M task GradSim (0.198) beats the BIF (0.110), suggesting the BIF's advantage is most pronounced in the vision/small-model regime where SGLD sampling is more tractable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Bayesian influence function (BIF) framework from Giordano et al. (2017) and the Bayesian Infinitesimal Jackknife (Giordano & Broderick, 2024; Iba, 2025) by introducing a *local* variant that operates on individual model checkpoints rather than the intractable global posterior. The localization mechanism is borrowed from the local learning coefficient estimator of Lau et al. (2025), which samples from a Gaussian-regularized posterior centered at a trained checkpoint. The key conceptual departure from classical influence functions (Cook, 1977; Koh & Liang, 2020) and their Hessian-approximation descendants (EK-FAC, TRAK, GradSim) is replacing the ill-posed inverse-Hessian-vector product with a covariance estimate over the local posterior, computed via SGLD (Welling & Teh, 2011). This makes the method Hessian-free, architecture-agnostic, and well-defined for the singular loss landscapes of deep networks, grounded in singular learning theory (Watanabe, 2009).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The local BIF offers a principled, Hessian-free path to training data attribution that scales to billion-parameter models and enables batched per-token influence computation that is impractical with classical IF approximations. Its practical impact is strongest for fine-grained, targeted attribution on large models where EK-FAC's expensive fit phase and architectural restrictions (Linear/Conv2D only) become bottlenecks. However, the method's accuracy is currently limited by SGLD sampling quality and hyperparameter sensitivity, particularly in the language-model regime where it underperforms EK-FAC on retraining benchmarks. Open questions include developing more efficient MCMC samplers, establishing principled hyperparameter selection (inverse temperature β, localization strength γ), and exploring how data influence evolves dynamically across training checkpoints—a capability unique to the local BIF's checkpoint-agnostic definition.
