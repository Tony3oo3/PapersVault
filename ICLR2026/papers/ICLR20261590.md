## Problem

Shared representations in multitask learning are designed to be the minimal unit of information exchanged across tasks, yet they can inadvertently leak whether a specific task was included in training. Prior MTL privacy work was limited to sample-level membership inference requiring access to task-specific heads and shadow models, leaving the task-level, purely black-box threat to the shared representation unexplored.

## Input / Output

The adversary receives a small batch of k samples (as few as 4) from a challenge task and makes black-box queries to the shared representation to obtain embedding vectors. For the strong adversary, these are actual training samples; for the weak adversary, they are fresh, unseen samples from the same task distribution. The output is a binary IN/OUT prediction with a scalar test statistic, thresholded at fixed percentiles (50th, 75th, 90th) without any shadow model calibration. Each attack run takes roughly 0.1 seconds per task on a single RTX 4090 GPU.

## Method

The key insight is that embeddings from the same task are codependent due to distribution-level memorization in the shared representation, allowing the adversary to amplify the membership signal across multiple samples without reference models. Two purely black-box attacks exploit this: (1) a coordinate-wise variance attack that computes the trace of the empirical covariance matrix of the challenge task's embeddings, and (2) a pairwise inner product attack that averages absolute inner products (or cosine similarities) across all embedding pairs after a whitening transformation. The theoretical analysis reduces MTL to Gaussian mean estimation over a task mixture, proving that the strong adversary's test statistic has expectation d/T(σ̄² + σ²/N) while the weak adversary's is d/T·σ̄², explaining the empirical separation between the two. The approach is validated across ResNet and BERT/Gemma models on vision (CelebA, FEMNIST) and language (Stack Overflow, Reddit) datasets for both personalization and multi-problem MTL settings.

## Results

In the personalization setting, the pairwise inner product attack achieves a TPR of 98.5% at 1% FPR for the strong adversary on Stack Overflow (BERT), while the coordinate-wise variance attack reaches 61.2% TPR at 1% FPR on CelebA (ResNet-34). The weak adversary (fresh samples only) still achieves non-trivial success: 8.2% TPR at 1% FPR on Stack Overflow and 19.0% TPR at 1% FPR on Reddit (Gemma 3). In the multiple learning problems setting, the strong adversary reaches an AUC of 0.745 on CelebA (ResNet-50), and the gap between strong and weak adversaries narrows substantially, with the weak adversary achieving 19.6% TPR at 0.2% FPR on Stack Overflow.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against prior MIA or property-inference methods (e.g., Shokri et al. 2016, Carlini et al. 2022, Yan et al. 2024) under a shared protocol; instead, the central comparison is between the strong adversary (access to actual training samples) and the weak adversary (only fresh, unseen samples from the target task's distribution). The strong adversary consistently dominates: on Stack Overflow personalization, the gap is dramatic (98.5% vs. 8.2% TPR at 1% FPR), while on Reddit the gap nearly vanishes (19.7% vs. 19.0%), suggesting that when task distributions are broad and the model uses low-rank adapters, distribution-level memorization alone suffices. Between the two proposed attacks, the coordinate-wise variance attack outperforms the inner product attack in the low-FPR regime on CelebA and Reddit, while the inner product attack achieves higher TPR at moderate FPR on Stack Overflow. In the multiple learning problems setting, the strong–weak gap shrinks substantially (e.g., 19.9% vs. 19.6% TPR at ~0.1% FPR on Stack Overflow), because the model must achieve high utility on each task head, making task-level memorization unavoidable regardless of whether the adversary holds training samples.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies and generalizes several existing privacy attack formulations—sample-level membership inference (Shokri et al., 2016), user-inference (Kandpal et al., 2023), and property/dataset inference (Ateniese et al., 2015; Chaudhari et al., 2022)—into a single 'task-inference' threat model targeting the shared representation in multitask learning. It departs from prior MTL privacy work (Yan et al., 2024) by restricting the adversary to black-box queries on the shared encoder rather than task-specific heads, and from encoder-attack literature (Song & Raghunathan, 2020; Liu et al., 2021a) by studying implicitly learned supervised representations rather than contrastive ones. The theoretical analysis is grounded in tracing attacks on Gaussian mean estimation (Dwork et al., 2015), adapted from sample-level to task-level detection, and the practical attacks (coordinate-wise variance, pairwise inner product) repurpose the augmentation-robustness insight from membership inference by treating same-task samples as natural augmentations of one another.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Because the shared representation is the minimal information exchanged in collaborative and federated multitask learning, demonstrating that it leaks task-inclusion information even to a weak adversary with only fresh samples raises concrete deployment concerns for MTL/FL systems. The mechanistic finding that strong-adversary success tracks the train-validation gap while weak-adversary success tracks the IN-task generalization advantage over OUT tasks provides a clearer picture of *why* leakage occurs, potentially guiding targeted defenses. Open questions include whether the observed leakage persists under stronger regularization or differential privacy, whether the attacks transfer to more complex MTL architectures (e.g., mixture-of-experts, hierarchical heads), and what practical mitigations exist that preserve the utility gains of shared representations.
