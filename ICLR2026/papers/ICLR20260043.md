## Problem

Existing MLLM benchmarks predominantly evaluate static, single-capability tasks in fixed layouts, providing limited insight into how multiple cognitive abilities interact in dynamic, multi-step settings. The authors argue that, analogous to how the Wechsler Intelligence Scales decompose children's intelligence into interpretable subtests, MLLMs need an ability-oriented battery that profiles coordinated competencies rather than isolated skills in a single question-and-answer format.

## Input / Output

Each task frame presents a 2D grid scene image containing letter/number-labeled items, a backpack with labeled slots, and a hint bar, accompanied by a text instruction specifying the goal. The model outputs a sequence of high-level actions (e.g., 'pick up the basketball', 'put item A into container 2') rather than atomic step-by-step movements. Evaluation is conducted over 100 zero-shot rounds per task, reporting success rate under the ground-truth optimal solution and five-dimensional capability scores (Execution, Perception Reasoning, Memory, Learning, Planning).

## Method

KIDGYM is a 2D grid-based benchmark comprising 12 tasks—6 targeting a single capability and 6 targeting composite dual capabilities—each available at three difficulty levels (L1–L3). Tasks are built on the Gym API with procedural generation that randomizes item positions, agent spawns, and scene contexts (supermarkets, canteens, farms) to prevent memorization and reduce evaluation variance. The benchmark introduces a backpack and hint bar as persistent state components to mitigate MLLMs' known weakness in maintaining contextual consistency across turns, and uses high-level actions to match the macroscopic reasoning strengths of MLLMs over low-level control. The five capabilities are adapted from Wechsler indicators but redefined for MLLM architecture: Execution (goal-to-action translation), Perception Reasoning (visual inference beyond recognition), Memory (long-range contextual retention), Learning (ingesting novel or contradictory rules), and Planning (multi-step strategy with foresight). The system is fully user-customizable, allowing researchers to add new tasks, adjust difficulty, and extend the benchmark as the MLLM community evolves.

## Results

On KIDGYM's 12 tasks at 3 difficulty levels, the top closed-source models (o3, GPT-5, Gemini-2.5-Pro) achieve near-perfect success rates (1.00) on several single-capability tasks such as Classification, Selection, and Memory Decode at L1, but performance drops sharply on abstract visual tasks: the best Puzzle (PU) score is only 0.30 (GPT-5, L1) and the best Counting (CO) score is 0.72 (Gemini-2.5-Pro, L1), far below the human baseline of 1.00. Composite-capability tasks (e.g., Memory Maze, Memory Filling) show substantially lower success rates than their single-capability counterparts across all models. Open-source models (QwenVL-2.5, InternVL-3, DeepSeekVL-2) trail closed-source models by a wide margin, with the best open model (QwenVL-2.5-72B) reaching only 0.48 on CL-L1 and 0.00 on CO across all levels.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the six closed-source models, o3, GPT-5, and Gemini-2.5-Pro form a clear first tier, achieving 1.00 on multiple tasks (CL, SE, MDE) at L1 and dominating the capability radar across all five dimensions; GPT-5 leads on Puzzle (0.30 vs. 0.26 for o3 at L1) and Sorting (1.00), while Gemini-2.5-Pro is the strongest on Counting (0.72 vs. 0.36 for GPT-5 at L1) and Maze (0.95 at L1). GPT-4o, Gemini-2.5-Flash, and Claude-3.7-Sonnet form a second tier with markedly lower scores on planning and composite tasks (e.g., GPT-4o scores 0.00 on CO and MMA at all levels). Among open-source models, QwenVL-2.5-72B and InternVL-3-78B are the strongest, but they still trail the best closed-source models by 30–60 percentage points on most tasks (e.g., QwenVL-2.5-72B achieves 0.48 on CL-L1 vs. 1.00 for o3/GPT-5). Smaller open models (7B/8B) and DeepSeekVL-2 perform near or below random on many tasks, with DeepSeekVL-2 scoring 0.18 on CL-L1 and 0.01 on CL-L3. The human baseline (0.93–1.00 across all tasks) reveals a substantial gap, particularly on PU, CO, and composite tasks where even the best MLLM falls 28–70 points short.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the multi-ability decomposition philosophy of the Wechsler Intelligence Scales to MLLM evaluation, following the psychometric-AI line of work (Voudouris et al., 2024; 2025) that argues ability-oriented batteries outperform task-specific benchmarks. It departs from existing MLLM benchmarks (MME, MM-Vet, LogicGame, EgoPlan, ARC-AGI-2) by introducing dynamic multi-turn interaction in a 2D grid world with procedurally generated layouts, graded difficulty, and composite-capability tasks, rather than the static single-turn Q&A format that dominates the field. The Gym API-based architecture draws from RL environment design (MiniGrid, Crafter) but targets MLLMs with high-level semantic actions rather than atomic RL primitives, and the backpack/hint-bar mechanism is a novel interface design to probe memory without requiring the model to track hidden state purely from visual context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

KIDGYM surfaces four specific failure modes of frontier MLLMs—non-semantic visual reasoning, quantity insensitivity, composite-rule interaction, and weak planning—that are not well isolated by existing static benchmarks, giving the community concrete targets for improvement. The extensible Gym-based framework and randomized procedural generation reduce data contamination and allow the benchmark to grow as models improve. Limitations include the 2D grid abstraction (limited ecological validity compared to real-world 3D scenes), only 12 tasks, and primarily zero-shot evaluation; open questions include whether the five-ability decomposition is the right granularity for MLLMs and whether findings transfer to more realistic, open-ended visual environments.
