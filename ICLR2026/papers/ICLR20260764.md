## Problem

Prior work on differentially private equilibrium computation in games either fails to achieve high accuracy and low privacy budget simultaneously, or only provides a weaker form of privacy where the adversary can still infer part of the utility functions. This paper establishes fundamental impossibility results showing that no algorithm can guarantee both vanishing exploitability and vanishing privacy budget as the number of players grows, when the adversary accesses all communication channels or when accuracy is measured by Euclidean distance to the equilibrium set. It then identifies the precise conditions under which simultaneous guarantees become achievable.

## Input / Output

The algorithm takes as input a polymatrix game specified by an interaction graph, per-edge utility matrices in [-1,1]^{A_i × A_j}, and per-player action sets, and produces the time-averaged strategy profile over T iterations as a coarse correlated equilibrium. The privacy guarantee is expressed as a Rényi divergence bound on the marginal observation distribution of a single player (the adversary's view), with the overall budget scaling linearly in the number of channels the adversary accesses. The key structural constraint is that the adversary may only observe a bounded number of communication channels, not all of them.

## Method

Algorithm 1 is a distributed iterative procedure in which each player broadcasts a Gaussian-noised copy of her strategy to neighbors, then performs a regularized proximal gradient step (equivalent to projected gradient descent with weight decay) using the noisy neighbor strategies to compute her gradient. The key novelty is an adaptive regularizer τ_i proportional to the harmonic mean of all players' degrees divided by player i's own degree, so that low-degree players receive stronger regularization because their gradients are more sensitive to a single utility matrix change. The gradient descent step starts from the projected noisy strategy rather than the player's own strategy, preventing observations from diverging across adjacent games over time. The analysis exploits the graph structure: for dense graphs, the harmonic-mean regularizer dominates and the privacy budget shrinks as N^{-4p/27}; for sparse graphs, most players are far from the changed edge so the privacy budget shrinks as (log N)^{-1/3}. This yields simultaneously vanishing exploitability and privacy budget as N → ∞, which prior methods cannot achieve.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 7.1) shows that for dense graphs (harmonic mean degree N̄ ≥ N^p), both the average exploitability and the Rényi DP privacy budget scale as O((log N)^(2/3) / N^(4p/27)), and for sparse graphs (max degree constant), both scale as O(1/(log N)^(1/3)). This means both accuracy and privacy budget vanish as the number of players N grows, which is the first such guarantee in equilibrium computation. Experimentally, on Erdős-Rényi and clustered graphs, the proposed algorithm consistently achieves lower exploitability than the Huang et al. (2015) baseline under the same DP budget, and its exploitability decreases with N while the baseline's does not.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against a single experimental baseline: an adaptation of Huang et al. (2015) from single-objective distributed optimization to the game setting. Under the same differential privacy budget, the proposed algorithm consistently attains lower exploitability on both Erdős-Rényi graphs (Figure 3) and clustered-topology graphs (Figure 4). Crucially, as the number of players increases, the proposed method's exploitability decreases while the baseline's remains flat, confirming the theoretical prediction that the privacy-accuracy trade-off improves with N. Theoretically, prior work (Gade et al., 2020; Ye et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022; Shakarami et al., 2022) achieves a privacy budget of O(ε) and accuracy of O(1/ε), meaning one cannot be driven to zero without the other blowing up regardless of N. In contrast, this paper achieves privacy budget O(ε/f(N)) and accuracy O(1/(ε·g(N))) where f(N), g(N) → ∞. Additionally, Wang & Nedić (2024) and Wang & Başar (2024) provide only a weaker form of DP where the adversary can still infer the utility function in a neighborhood of the equilibrium, which for multilinear utilities (as in polymatrix games) allows full recovery of the utility matrices.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the game-adjacency framework from Huang et al. (2015) on differentially private distributed optimization, adapting it to the multi-agent equilibrium-finding setting. It departs from prior DP equilibrium work (Ye et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022; Gade et al., 2020; Shakarami et al., 2022) that studied aggregative games but could not simultaneously drive both accuracy error and privacy budget to zero as the number of players grows. The key methodological departure is an adaptive regularizer scaled by the harmonic mean of degrees divided by each player's degree, which exploits the pairwise-interaction structure of polymatrix games to cancel noise effects in both sparse and dense graph regimes. The paper also distinguishes itself from DP minimax optimization work (Yang et al., 2022; Bassily et al., 2023) by considering multi-player general-sum games and protecting the full utility matrix rather than individual component utilities.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first result showing that simultaneously vanishing exploitability and privacy budget are achievable in distributed equilibrium computation, breaking a fundamental tradeoff that constrained all prior work. The impossibility theorems (Lemma 3.1, 3.2) are valuable guardrails: they show that Euclidean distance to the equilibrium set is fundamentally incompatible with strong privacy, and that full adversarial channel access precludes any meaningful guarantee. The main limitation is scope—the framework is specific to polymatrix games, and extending to normal-form or extensive-form games remains open. Additionally, the privacy guarantee assumes the adversary accesses only a bounded number of channels, and the overall budget scales linearly with the number of accessed channels, which may be restrictive in adversarial network settings.
