## Problem

Existing activation steering methods for LLMs are driven by empirical insights and lack theoretical performance guarantees. The paper demonstrates that popular approaches (ActAdd, DirAblate, Mean-AcT) are all instances of proportional (P) controllers, which inherently admit a nonzero steady-state error due to system disturbances, limiting their ability to fully close the gap between source and target activation distributions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20265236/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes pairs of contrastive prompts (e.g., harmful vs. harmless) to compute layer-wise difference-in-means error signals that feed the PID controller, producing per-layer steering vectors. At inference, a single text prompt (or image-generation prompt) is processed through the model with these precomputed vectors applied at each layer, yielding steered text or image output. The steering vectors are computed once offline and inserted as a drop-in replacement within existing steering frameworks (ActAdd, Mean-AcT, Angular Steering), requiring no weight updates or retraining.

## Method

The core insight is reframing activation steering as a state-feedback control problem: LLM layers form a discrete-time dynamical system, and the difference-in-means vector between contrastive datasets serves as the tracking error signal. Existing methods correspond to P controllers (u(k) = Kp·r(k)), which guarantee input-to-state stability but leave a steady-state error proportional to the disturbance. PID Steering extends the control law to u(k) = Kp·r(k) + Ki·Σⱼ r(j) + Kd·(r(k) − r(k−1)), where the integral term accumulates past errors to exactly cancel matched disturbance components and the derivative term damps the overshoot that PI control introduces. The framework is method-agnostic (compatible with ActAdd, DirAblate, Mean-AcT) and supports both non-sequential and sequential (causal) error computation. Theoretical analysis establishes ISS for the closed-loop system and proves that the derivative term strictly reduces the first-overshoot amplitude relative to PI control.

## Results

On toxicity mitigation (Table 1), PID-AcT reduces classifier toxicity to 0.51% on Gemma2-2B (from 4.17% original) and 0.72% on Llama3-8B (from 5.80%), achieving up to 8.2× and 8.1× reduction respectively, while preserving MMLU accuracy (51.30% and 64.50%). On jailbreaking (Table 2), PID achieves the highest attack success rate (ASR) across all tested models, reaching 94.85% on Llama3.1-8B and 92.65% on Qwen2.5-14B, while maintaining near-original TinyBenchmarks scores. In image style control (FLUX-Schnell), PID-AcT outperforms Mean-AcT at mid intervention strengths (0.4–0.8) on CLIP zero-shot style classification with only a small CLIPScore trade-off.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PID-AcT outperforms all six baselines on toxicity reduction. Against the closest sequential competitor Mean-AcT (Rodriguez et al., 2025), PID-AcT achieves 0.51% vs 0.68% CLS toxicity on Gemma2-2B and 0.72% vs 1.21% on Llama3-8B, with similar margins on 0-shot and QVQ metrics. Against non-sequential methods, PID-AcT beats ActADD (Turner et al., 2024) by 7.7× (0.51% vs 3.96%), CAA (Rimsky et al., 2024) by 2.4× (0.51% vs 1.20%), AURA (Suau et al., 2024) by 4.2× (0.51% vs 2.12%), and ITI-C (Li et al., 2023) by 1.5× (0.51% vs 0.74%) on Gemma2-2B. On jailbreaking, PID dominates RePE (Zou et al., 2023) by a wide margin—92.65% vs 25.42% ASR on Qwen2.5-14B and 94.85% vs 70.42% on Llama3.1-8B—while also exceeding DIM by 1.5–2 points. ITI (Li et al., 2023) collapses on larger models (33.65% ASR on Qwen2.5-14B) whereas PID scales robustly. Utility (MMLU, perplexity, TinyBenchmarks) remains comparable across all methods, with PID-AcT's MMLU slightly lower than non-AcT baselines, attributed to the AcT framework rather than the PID controller itself.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reframes activation steering—previously treated as a static algebraic operation (difference-in-means vectors applied via ActAdd, Directional Ablation, or Mean-AcT)—as a closed-loop feedback control problem, showing that all existing methods are instances of proportional (P) controllers. It builds directly on Rodriguez et al.'s Mean-AcT (sequential activation transport), Turner et al.'s ActAdd, and Arditi et al.'s refusal-direction work, extending them with integral and derivative terms to form PID Steering. The control-theoretic lineage connects to Soatto et al.'s controllability results for LLMs, Luo et al.'s open-loop prompting-as-control, and Cheng & Amo Alonso's closed-form activation control, but departs from these by operating at the layer-wise feature-direction level rather than the token-generation or subspace-projection level.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By establishing that existing steering methods are P controllers with inherent steady-state error, the paper provides the first theoretical performance guarantees (input-to-state stability, explicit error bounds) for activation steering, moving the field from empirical heuristics toward principled design. The PID framework is a lightweight, drop-in replacement validated across text and diffusion models, multiple architectures (3B–27B), and three distinct tasks. A key limitation is the 'stability-first, one-gain-at-a-time' tuning strategy, which the authors acknowledge may miss optimal gain combinations; they suggest LMI-based numerical optimization as future work. Open questions include whether the linearized error dynamics (Proposition 2) remain valid for very deep or highly nonlinear layers, and whether the framework extends to multi-objective steering (simultaneously controlling several features).
