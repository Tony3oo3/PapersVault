## Problem

The state-of-the-art membership inference attack, RMIA, uses a discrete test statistic whose precision is bounded by the size of a separate population dataset, making it computationally expensive and less accurate. Additionally, the standard sequence-level membership inference framework for LLMs compresses rich token-level signals into a single bit, obscuring where memorization actually occurs and misestimating true privacy risk, especially when sensitive information is localized to a small subset of tokens.

## Input / Output

The method takes a target model θ, a set of reference models Θ (trained on disjoint halves of the training data), and a target query x (text sequence, image, or tabular record). For sequence-level InfoRMIA, a population dataset Z is also required; for token-level InfoRMIA, Z is replaced by all other tokens in the vocabulary, eliminating the need for a separate population dataset. The output is a continuous membership score per query, and for token-level analysis, k−1 per-token scores for a length-k sequence, which can be rendered as a memorization heatmap over the input text.

## Method

InfoRMIA replaces RMIA's discrete dominance-counting test statistic with a continuous log Bayes Factor: log(p(x|θ)/p(x)) + D_KL(p(z)‖p(z|θ)), derived by casting the membership inference problem as a composite hypothesis test. This eliminates the threshold hyperparameter γ and decouples score granularity from population size, yielding a lower-cost, higher-power attack. For token-level analysis, InfoRMIA is applied at each autoregressive prediction step, treating each token as the label for its prefixal subsequence and using all other vocabulary tokens as the data-dependent population Z. The resulting per-token scores can be aggregated (via averaging or min-k) to recover sequence-level membership inference, or inspected individually to localize memorization to specific tokens, enabling targeted privacy auditing and potential exact unlearning.

## Results

On the primary cross-modality benchmark (Table 1), InfoRMIA achieves AUC 0.8784 on AG News, 0.8330 on CIFAR-10, and 0.5754 on Purchase100, consistently outperforming RMIA (0.8574, 0.8229, 0.5311) and LiRA (0.8641, 0.8242, 0.5398). The most dramatic gain is in TPR@0.1%FPR: InfoRMIA reaches 12.0% on AG News and 5.82% on CIFAR-10, while RMIA achieves 0.00% and LiRA only 1.80% and 0.12% respectively. Critically, InfoRMIA's scores are invariant to population size |Z| (identical at |Z|=100 and |Z|=1000), whereas RMIA's AUC improves from 0.8574 to 0.8766 on AG News as |Z| grows, confirming the elimination of the discrete-granularity bottleneck. On the MIMIR pretrained-LLM benchmark, token-level InfoRMIA (averaging) achieves the highest average TPR@1%FPR across Pythia model sizes (e.g., 3.7% at 2.8B), surpassing the prior best reference-based method Ref (3.5%) and Min-K%++ (3.4%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

InfoRMIA dominates RMIA (Zarifzadeh et al., ICML 2024) on every dataset and metric: AUC gains of +0.021 (AG News), +0.010 (CIFAR-10), and +0.044 (Purchase100), with TPR@0.1%FPR jumping from 0% to 12% on AG News. Against LiRA (Ye et al., CCS 2022), InfoRMIA wins on all three modalities in both AUC and TPR, with the largest gap on Purchase100 (0.5754 vs 0.5398 AUC). On the MIMIR pretrained-LLM benchmark, token-level InfoRMIA (averaging) outperforms all six reference-free baselines—Loss (Yeom et al., CSF 2018), Zlib and Ref (Carlini et al., USENIX Sec 2021), Min-K% (Shi et al., ICLR 2024), Min-K%++ (Zhang et al., ICLR 2025), and DC-PDD (Zhang et al., EMNLP 2024)—on average TPR@1%FPR across Pythia 160M–6.9B. The closest competitor is Ref (Carlini et al.), which trails InfoRMIA by 0.2–0.4 percentage points on average TPR@1%FPR; notably, InfoRMIA also surpasses Ref on AUC for the 160M and 1.4B models. A key practical advantage is that InfoRMIA requires no separate population dataset (in the token-level variant), matching the computational cost of the simplest Ref baseline while delivering stronger inference power.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the reference-model-based membership inference lineage (LiRA → RMIA) by replacing RMIA's discrete dominance-counting test statistic with a continuous information-theoretic formulation grounded in the Bayes Factor for composite hypothesis testing. The key conceptual departure is twofold: (1) the test statistic becomes a KL-divergence-based continuous score that eliminates sensitivity to population dataset size, and (2) the analysis granularity shifts from sequence-level to token-level for LLMs, treating each autoregressive prediction step as an independent membership query. The token-level framework also connects to the broader LLM memorization literature (Carlini et al. 2021/2023, Nasr et al. 2023) by providing a per-token signal that can be aggregated or inspected independently.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that non-private tokens often exhibit higher average membership scores than private tokens challenges the assumption that sequence-level MIA AUCs faithfully reflect true privacy risk, suggesting that current auditing protocols may overestimate leakage while missing where it actually occurs. The token-level framework opens a path toward targeted machine unlearning (removing only sensitive tokens rather than entire documents), but this application is left as future work. A key limitation is that the fine-grained analysis requires prior knowledge of which tokens are private, making it an inspection tool for knowledgeable auditors rather than an automatic risk quantifier. The MIMIR results on pretrained LLMs remain modest (AUCs near 50–54%), indicating the method's strongest gains are on finetuned models where reference models are well-matched.
