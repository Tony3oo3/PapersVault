## Problem

Scaling MLLMs from single images to hour-long videos inflates the visual token budget far beyond practical limits, and existing keyframe selection methods either uniformly subsample (missing critical moments) or require pre-filtering all frames with a vision-language encoder before scoring, which undermines the goal of identifying the most informative frames from the full video. The paper addresses how to adaptively allocate a limited scoring budget across a long video to select query-relevant keyframes without any pre-filtering step.

## Input / Output

The method takes a long video (up to 1 hour at 30 fps, yielding over 10^5 frames) and a text query as input. It outputs a small set of k keyframes (typically 32 or 64) that are fed to a downstream MLLM for answer generation. Crucially, FOCUS scores only ~1.6% of all frames via BLIP ITM forward passes (vs. 100% for exhaustive methods), and the final frame selection within each chosen clip uses nearest-neighbor interpolation of observed relevance scores to construct a per-arm sampling distribution from which frames are drawn without replacement.

## Method

FOCUS exploits the strong short-range temporal autocorrelation of frame-query relevance (ACF > 0.5 for ~5 s) to formulate keyframe selection as a Combinatorial Pure-Exploration (CPE) problem in a multi-armed bandit, where fixed-length temporal clips serve as arms and the goal is to identify the top-m arms maximizing cumulative relevance under a sampling budget. Each arm maintains an empirical mean and a Bernstein-style variance-adaptive confidence radius, and computation is allocated via an optimism-in-the-face-of-uncertainty principle. To enable parallel GPU inference, the sequential iterative policy is reduced to a two-stage batched procedure: Stage I pulls every arm q times in parallel and selects the top-αm arms by optimistic UCB scores; Stage II pulls those arms z times in a single batch and selects the final top-m arms by unbiased empirical means. Within each selected arm, observed rewards are interpolated to all frames via nearest-neighbor assignment, and k_a frames are sampled without replacement from the resulting distribution. The entire pipeline is training-free, model-agnostic, and requires no pre-filtering of the video.

## Results

On LongVideoBench with LLaVA-Video-7B (64 frames), FOCUS achieves 63.5% overall accuracy, outperforming uniform sampling (58.9%) by 4.6 points and the SOTA baseline AKS (62.1%) by 1.4 points. The gains are most pronounced on long videos (>20 min), where FOCUS reaches 63.7% versus 51.8% for uniform sampling (+11.9%) and 56.1% for AKS (+7.6%). On Video-MME, FOCUS scores 65.4% overall, surpassing uniform sampling (64.4%) and AKS (64.6%). FOCUS achieves these results while scoring only 1.6% of all frames (5.5 GPU hours on a single H100), compared to 9.3 GPU hours for AKS with pre-filtering and 255 GPU hours for AKS without pre-filtering.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against AKS (Tang et al., 2025), FOCUS wins on both benchmarks overall: 63.5% vs. 62.1% on LongVideoBench and 65.4% vs. 64.6% on Video-MME. The advantage is most dramatic on long videos (>20 min on LongVideoBench), where FOCUS scores 63.7% versus AKS's 56.1% (+7.6 points), reflecting FOCUS's ability to adaptively concentrate scoring on relevant segments without the uniform pre-filtering that AKS requires. Against Q-Frame (Zhang et al., 2025b), FOCUS consistently outperforms across all three tested MLLMs (Qwen2-VL-7B, LLaVA-OV-7B, LLaVA-Video-7B) on both benchmarks, with the largest gap on LongVideoBench (e.g., 62.3% vs. 57.4% on Qwen2-VL-7B). FOCUS also matches or exceeds Top-K on short videos while substantially outperforming it on medium and long videos, suggesting that the bandit-based exploration-exploitation strategy provides the most benefit as video length increases and uniform or greedy scoring becomes less effective.

## Relation to Prior Work

FOCUS departs from existing training-free keyframe selection methods (AKS, Q-Frame, Top-K) that require pre-filtering via uniform downsampling before scoring all frames, which the authors argue undermines the goal of identifying the most informative moments. Instead, it repurposes combinatorial pure-exploration (CPE) multi-armed bandit theory—specifically Bernstein confidence bounds and optimism-in-the-face-of-uncertainty—to adaptively allocate scoring budget across temporal clips, leveraging the empirically observed short-range temporal autocorrelation of frame-query relevance. The two-stage coarse-to-fine procedure is a practical, parallelizable reduction of the sequential CPE algorithm, drawing on the batched bandit literature (Perchet et al., 2016; Jun et al., 2016) to avoid per-step scheduling overhead.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FOCUS provides a simple, training-free, model-agnostic module that processes under 2% of video frames while delivering up to 11.9% accuracy gains on long videos (>20 min), making it a practical drop-in for any MLLM pipeline handling hour-long content. A key open question is the i.i.d. assumption on frame relevance scores: the authors acknowledge that temporal dependencies between clips violate this, and that Lipschitz/metric bandits or contextual bandits would be more faithful formulations, leaving a clear path for future work. The modest gains on Video-MME (1.0–2.3%) versus LongVideoBench (4.6–6.7%) also suggest the method's benefit is concentrated in event-localized queries, limiting its universality for global-understanding tasks.
