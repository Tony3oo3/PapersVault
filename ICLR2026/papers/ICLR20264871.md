## Problem

Diffusion models are known to memorize training data rather than generate novel samples, but existing theoretical analyses are largely asymptotic or restricted to specific distribution families, leaving the finite-sample mechanism of memorization unexplained. This paper asks whether memorization can be formally disentangled from generalization in practical regimes and whether this understanding can drive effective mitigation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264871/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The theoretical analysis operates on sub-Gaussian Hölder density distributions (including K-component Gaussian mixtures) and ReLU networks of specified width, depth, and parameter count. The practical pruning method takes a trained Diffusion Transformer (DiT) and its training dataset, computes per-head importance scores using a Beta(0.8,2) time-sampling distribution biased toward small t, prunes the lowest-importance heads at a chosen ratio (e.g., 20%), and fine-tunes the remaining network. The output is a pruned DiT that generates RGB images (evaluated on CIFAR-10 at 32×32) with reduced memorization ratio while maintaining competitive FID.

## Method

The paper establishes a dual-separation framework. First, a statistical separation: the ground-truth score function does not minimize the empirical denoising score matching loss, and the resulting loss gap equals the Fisher divergence between the empirical and true noisy distributions; for well-separated sub-Gaussian mixtures, this gap is lower-bounded by Ω(dσ_t^{-2} + tr(Σ)) even with polynomially many samples, meaning strong optimizers will drive expressive networks toward the empirical (memorizing) score. Second, an architectural separation: approximating the empirical score requires ReLU network width and parameter count scaling linearly with sample size n, whereas the ground-truth score admits a compact representation depending only on ε^{-d/(2β)}, explaining why larger networks and duplicated samples increase memorization. Guided by these insights, the paper proposes a one-shot pruning method for trained DiTs that removes attention heads contributing least in the small-t regime (where the loss gap is largest and the empirical score is hardest to represent), followed by fine-tuning, effectively forcing the reduced-capacity model to learn the ground-truth score rather than overfit to individual training points.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with a 5,000-sample subset and a trained DiT, the proposed attention-head pruning method (20% pruning ratio, Beta(0.8,2) time sampling) reduces the memorization ratio from 73.82% to 68.58% while improving recall from 0.08 to 0.12 and achieving a slightly better FID of 15.07 (vs. 15.47 for the original model). Random pruning at the same ratio achieves a lower memorization ratio (66.87%) but at the cost of worse FID (17.14) and lower recall (0.09). On synthetic Gaussian mixture data, the experiments confirm that larger networks and smaller sample sizes increase memorization, while appropriate weight decay and network width constrain memorization without harming generalization (measured by mean log-likelihood).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the proposed importance-score-based attention-head pruning against two baselines: the original unpruned DiT and random pruning at the same 20% ratio. The proposed method achieves a memorization reduction of ~5.2 percentage points (73.82% → 68.58%), slightly less than random pruning (~6.9 pp) but with substantially better FID (15.07 vs. 17.14) and recall (0.12 vs. 0.09), indicating that targeted pruning preserves generation diversity and fidelity more effectively. Precision drops from 0.39 to 0.33, which the authors attribute to the removal of memorized samples that artificially inflate precision. On the synthetic Gaussian mixture experiments, the paper further validates that weight decay and moderate network width are effective regularization strategies: with reduced sample size (n=3.2K), proper width and weight decay prevent memorization and improve mean log-likelihood, whereas wide networks with light decay lead to high memorization and poor generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant asymptotic/statistical-physics line of work on diffusion memorization (Biroli et al. 2024, George et al. 2025, Bonnaire et al. 2025) by providing a non-asymptotic, finite-sample analysis grounded in classical statistical estimation and neural network approximation theory. It is most closely related to Buchanan et al. (2025), which studies memorization in well-separated Gaussian mixtures with a specific denoiser parameterization; here the authors generalize to generic sub-Gaussian Hölder densities and add an architectural separation result (network size must scale with n for the empirical score but not the ground-truth score). The practical pruning method adapts importance-score computation from Liang et al. (2021) and builds on the neuron-localization ideas of Hintersdorf et al. (2024), but reframes pruning as a capacity-reduction strategy motivated by the theoretical separation rather than as a targeted removal of memorized-sample neurons.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dual-separation framework (statistical Fisher-divergence gap + architectural network-size gap) gives the first non-asymptotic theoretical account of why memorization emerges in practical finite-sample regimes, complementing the asymptotic phase-transition picture from statistical physics. The practical implication—that pruning attention heads in the small-t regime reduces memorization while preserving FID—offers a plug-and-play mitigation for trained DiTs, though validation is limited to CIFAR-10 at 5K samples. Key open questions include extending the theory to heavy-tailed or non-mixture distributions, validating the pruning strategy at ImageNet scale, and understanding whether the architectural separation persists for architectures beyond feedforward ReLU networks (e.g., convolutions, attention with positional encodings).
