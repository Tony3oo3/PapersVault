## Problem

Time-to-first-spike (TTFS) spiking neural networks promise maximal energy efficiency by emitting at most one spike per neuron, but this advantage is negated in practice because each spike's timing must be converted to a value via a digital decay function followed by a multiplication with the synaptic weight. This paper asks whether the costly w·ε(t) computation can be eliminated entirely by finding a physical analog that naturally performs it, and how to deploy such a hardware-dependent SNN in complex transformer architectures that are difficult to train directly in the spiking domain.

## Input / Output

The model accepts tokenized text sentences or sentence pairs (sequence lengths up to 128 tokens) and produces either a class label (for entailment, sentiment, paraphrase, and question-equivalence tasks) or a continuous similarity score (for STS-B). Internally, each neuron's output is a single spike whose arrival time within a 15-step window (T=15, corresponding to 4-bit quantization) encodes a discrete value; the 13.4M-parameter transformer uses 1-bit weights and 1-bit Key/Value projections throughout, with the physical In₂O₃ TFT providing the temporal decay at each synapse.

## Method

Otters repurposes the natural signal decay of a custom-fabricated In₂O₃ optoelectronic thin-film transistor as the TTFS temporal decay function, so the analog output at spike arrival time directly yields the fused product of synaptic weight and decay, eliminating both the digital decay evaluation and the multiplication. A dynamic, step-wise decreasing firing threshold—computed from the inverse of the measured non-linear decay curve—reconciles the device's physical nonlinearity with the uniform quantization grid required for lossless QNN-to-SNN conversion. To avoid the over-sparsity problem of direct SNN training, the authors first train a 1-bit-weight, 4-bit-activation quantized BERT via knowledge distillation from full-precision BERT, then apply a proven conversion (Proposition 1) that maps each quantization level to a specific spike time and sets per-synapse scaling factors and thresholds accordingly. In the attention module, 1-bit KV projections reduce the Q·Kᵀ dot product to selective additions and subtractions, and a Canon-inspired dataflow architecture pre-loads binary K/V vectors into PE local memory to minimize data movement. A Hardware-Aware Training variant injects calibrated Gaussian noise into QNN activations during training to improve resilience against device-to-device variability in the physical decay parameters.

## Results

On the GLUE benchmark (7 tasks), Otters achieves a state-of-the-art average accuracy of 83.22% among all SNNs, surpassing Sorbet (79.80%) by 3.42% and SpikeLM (80.51%) by 2.98%. In energy efficiency, Otters (1-bit KV) consumes only 4.06 mJ per inference for one attention block, yielding a 41.36× energy saving over full-precision BERT and a 1.77× improvement over SpikingLM (7.2 mJ) and 3.04× over Sorbet (12.34 mJ). The model uses only 13.4M parameters with 1-bit weights and 1-bit KV projections, matching the size of BiT while outperforming it by 5.65% in average accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among SNN baselines, Otters outperforms all competitors on average GLUE accuracy: it beats Sorbet (79.80%) by 3.42%, SpikingBERT (80.83%) by 2.39%, SpikeLM (80.51%) by 2.71%, and SpikingFormer (73.43%) by 9.79%. Against quantized ANN baselines of similar size, Otters (13.4M) surpasses BiT (13.4M, 77.57%) by 5.65% and Q2BERT (43.0M, 54.51%) by 28.71%, while remaining slightly below the much larger DistilBERT (207M, 83.64%) and TinyBERT6 (207M, 84.85%). In energy, Otters (4.06 mJ) is 1.77× more efficient than SpikingLM (7.2 mJ), 3.04× more efficient than Sorbet (12.34 mJ), and 5.72× more efficient than SpikingBERT (23.22 mJ), while achieving higher accuracy than all of them. Compared to full-precision BERT_base (167.92 mJ, 87.31% avg), Otters trades 4.09% accuracy for a 41.36× energy reduction. The 1-bit KV quantization in Otters further reduces energy by 10% (4.49→4.06 mJ) at only a 0.23% accuracy cost on SST-2, a trade-off no other baseline in the table achieves.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Dynamic Firing Threshold (DFT) scheduling mechanism from Wei et al. (ICCV 2023) for TTFS SNNs and the optoelectronic neuron hardware demonstrated by Li et al. (Nature Communications 2024), but departs fundamentally from both by repurposing the natural signal decay (volatility) of an In₂O₃ thin-film transistor—traditionally treated as a defect to suppress—as the physical implementation of the temporal decay function required by TTFS. For the software side, it follows the QNN-to-SNN conversion paradigm established by Sorbet, SpikingLM, and SpikingBERT, but introduces a lossless conversion proof (Proposition 1) that maps quantized integer values to discrete spike times via a dynamic, step-wise threshold calibrated to the device's non-linear decay curve. The 1-bit KV quantization for attention, which reduces the Q·Kᵀ dot product to selective additions/subtractions, is a specific architectural adaptation not present in prior TTFS transformer work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes a concrete hardware-software co-design paradigm where fundamental device physics (optical signal decay) directly serves as a computational primitive, eliminating the digital decay-function evaluation and weight multiplication that have historically negated TTFS's theoretical energy advantage. The energy-efficiency claims are grounded in 22nm process measurements covering compute, data movement, and memory access, making them more credible than the operation-count-only analyses common in prior SNN literature. Key open questions include device scaling (the current 0.012 mm² footprint is ~3 orders of magnitude above the 40 nm channel lengths demonstrated in the literature), CMOS-compatible 3D integration, and whether the approach generalizes to architectures beyond BERT-base. The Hardware-Aware Training method, while effective, still shows accuracy dropping to ~81% under 20% noise, suggesting a gap between the demonstrated robustness and the variability expected in large-scale fabrication.
