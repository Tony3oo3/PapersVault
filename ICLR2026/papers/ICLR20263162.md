## Problem

Existing DNA language models are locked into a fixed tokenization strategy chosen before training: single-nucleotide tokens preserve resolution but produce sequences too long for efficient transformer processing, while multi-nucleotide schemes (k-mers, BPE) sacrifice the single-base granularity critical for variant effect prediction. No prior approach offers a way to adapt the segmentation strategy after pretraining or to incorporate biological priors (e.g., evolutionary conservation) into how the sequence is partitioned.

## Input / Output

The model consumes raw DNA nucleotide sequences (up to 131,072 bp) together with a per-nucleotide scoring function (PhyloP conservation scores by default, or DNase-seq signal for cell-type-specific re-patching) that determines patch boundaries. Outputs are nucleotide-level embeddings extracted from the penultimate decoder layer, retaining single-base resolution for fine-grained tasks, alongside next-nucleotide prediction logits for autoregressive generation. Because the patching function is external to the architecture, the same pretrained weights can be re-patched with a different scoring function at inference or fine-tuning time without any weight updates.

## Method

PatchDNA extends the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) architecture to DNA by replacing fixed tokenization with dynamic, conservation-guided patching. The three-component architecture consists of a Perceiver-style local encoder (sliding-window self-attention alternating with cross-attention from patch queries to nucleotide keys), a deeper latent global transformer with rotary positional encodings operating on the compressed patch sequence, and a lightweight local decoder that restores nucleotide-level representations via cross-attention. Patch boundaries are set where the PhyloP conservation score exceeds the 95th-percentile threshold (yielding ~20-nucleotide average patches), concentrating model capacity on evolutionarily constrained regions. The key differentiator is re-patching: because the patching function depends only on a swappable scoring function and threshold, the model can be re-patched with task-specific signals (e.g., DNase-seq for cell-type-specific expression, single-nucleotide patches for splice-site tasks) after pretraining with zero architectural changes or retraining from scratch. Pretraining uses next-nucleotide prediction on the human reference genome, producing a 19.2M-parameter model (16 kbp context) and a 7.7M-parameter model (131 kbp context) that outperform or match models up to 25× larger across regulatory, splicing, and gene-expression benchmarks.

## Results

PatchDNA (19.2M parameters) achieves the best overall mean rank (2.0) on the DART-Eval benchmark across five regulatory genomics tasks, outperforming models up to 25× larger (NT-MS-500M, 500M params; MistralDNA, 1.6B params). On the BEND benchmark, PatchDNA outperforms all baselines in 3 of 4 tasks, including gene finding (MCC 0.58) where it trails only NT-MS-500M (0.64). On the CAGE prediction task (114 kbp context), PatchDNA-7M achieves the highest gene-level Pearson correlation (0.369) and cell-level Pearson (0.771), surpassing HyenaDNA (0.362/0.745) and Caduceus-ps (0.365/0.766). With DNase-aware re-patching, cell-type-specific CAGE prediction improves to 0.828 (K562), 0.727 (Hepatocyte), and 0.831 (Neuron) Pearson correlation, all without retraining.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PatchDNA (19.2M) achieves the best mean rank (2.0) on DART-Eval, beating HyenaDNA (3.8), GENA-LM-Large (4.2), NT-MS-500M (4.8), Caduceus-ps (5.8), DNABERT2 (6.0), and MistralDNA (6.4), despite being 5–80× smaller than the largest baselines. On BEND, PatchDNA wins 3 of 4 tasks (chromatin accessibility AUROC 0.84, histone modification 0.79, CpG methylation 0.92) but loses gene finding to NT-MS-500M (0.58 vs 0.64 MCC), a model with 25× more parameters trained on multi-species data. On CAGE prediction, PatchDNA-7M outperforms HyenaDNA and both Caduceus variants across gene, cell, and full-sequence Pearson correlations, and fine-tunes 3× faster than HyenaDNA (22.4 min vs 76.6 min per epoch). Conservation-guided patching consistently outperforms entropy-based and fixed-size patching ablations across all benchmarks, while PatchDNA-Entropy still beats most external baselines, confirming the architectural benefit of patching independent of the specific scoring function. On cell-type-specific CAGE, DNase-aware re-patching yields substantial gains (e.g., K562: 0.754→0.828) without any architectural change or retraining, a capability unavailable to all tokenization-based baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) framework from Pagnoni et al. (2025), which replaced fixed tokenization with dynamic, entropy-guided patching for natural language, to the genomic domain. It departs from the standard DNA tokenization landscape—k-mers, byte-pair encoding, single-nucleotide representations, and learned discrete tokenizers (VQDNA, MxDNA)—by eliminating the fixed vocabulary entirely and instead using a scoring function to determine variable-length patch boundaries. The key conceptual departure is substituting BLT's entropy-based boundary detection with evolutionary conservation scores (PhyloP), introducing a biologically informed inductive bias that directs computational resources toward functionally relevant regions. The re-patching mechanism, which allows the patching strategy to be swapped post-pretraining, has no analogue in any existing tokenization paradigm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The re-patching capability is the most practically significant contribution: it enables adaptation to cell-type-specific or task-specific contexts (e.g., DNase-seq-guided patching for expression prediction) without retraining, a flexibility that fixed tokenization fundamentally cannot offer. The demonstrated parameter efficiency—19.2M parameters matching or exceeding 500M-parameter models across multiple benchmarks—suggests that architectural choices around sequence compression may matter as much as scale for DNA modeling. Key limitations include the autoregressive, decoder-only architecture (limiting bidirectional context critical in genomics), pretraining on only the human reference genome, and the absence of reverse-complement equivariance. Open questions include whether the framework scales favorably with multi-species pretraining, how it behaves under bidirectional encoding, and whether re-patching generalizes beyond the demonstrated cell-type-specific and splice-site applications.
