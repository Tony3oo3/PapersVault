## Problem

Practitioners using human preference data for RLHF lack a clear understanding of what their feedback actually encodes: black-box reward models predict preferences without explaining why, and prior interpretability work requires pre-specifying candidate features, constraining what can be discovered. This paper asks whether one can automatically extract a small set of human-interpretable features from preference data that account for most of the predictive signal, without any pre-specified hypotheses.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262018/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes preference pairs (prompt, response A, response B, binary label) and operates on the difference of OpenAI text-embedding-3-small vectors for the two responses (e_rA − e_rB), deliberately excluding the prompt embedding since it does not improve prediction. It outputs a sparse M×N activation matrix (M=32 features, K=4 active per pair) with natural-language descriptions for each feature, plus logistic-regression coefficients (β_j) and Δ win-rate values that quantify how each feature shifts predicted preference, optionally with annotator-specific coefficients for personalization.

## Method

WIMHF is a three-step pipeline. First, a Matryoshka BatchTopK sparse autoencoder with identity (signed) activations is trained on embedding differences e_Δ to learn a sparse, interpretable basis of how response pairs differ; the small dimensionality (M=32, K=4) is chosen empirically to avoid redundancy, and a Matryoshka loss encourages both coarse and fine-grained features. Second, an LLM generates natural-language descriptions for each feature from top-activating examples, and descriptions are retained only if their fidelity (Pearson correlation between signed activations and independent LLM annotations over 300 held-out pairs) is statistically significant after Bonferroni correction. Third, a logistic regression with length as a control variable identifies which features predict the preference label, yielding expressed preferences; a random-slopes mixed-effects model further quantifies per-annotator subjectivity. The key architectural choice—replacing ReLU with identity activations—ensures a single feature's sign encodes which response exhibits the concept, avoiding the redundant positive/negative feature pairs that ReLU SAEs produce.

## Results

WIMHF's sparse SAE features (mean 4 active per input) achieve an average AUC of 0.672 in predicting preference labels, capturing 67% of the improvement over random achieved by a finetuned Llama-3.2-3B reward model (AUC 0.766) and 84% of the gain from dense text embeddings. 60.4% of annotator-written explanations on Community Alignment match at least one active SAE feature (vs. 33.3% for random features, p<0.001), and 47/47 discovered features are rated interpretable by expert annotators. In practical applications, flipping labels on Arena's top anti-refusal feature increases RewardBench2 safety accuracy from 8.9% to 46.2% (+37%) with no degradation to non-safety performance, and learning annotator-specific coefficients for the most subjective feature (paragraphs vs. lists) yields up to +1.1% held-out AUC at k=16 examples.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is Inverse Constitutional AI (ICAI; Findeis et al., 2025), a prompting-based method that also aims to explain preference data without pre-specifying attributes. WIMHF produces substantially more statistically significant features: 43/50 vs. 28/50 in separate regressions, and 34/50 vs. 21/50 in a joint regression that tests whether each method's features remain predictive after controlling for the other's. Qualitatively, WIMHF discovers more specific and granular patterns (e.g., Arena's anti-refusal preference, CA's dispreference for environmental sustainability), while ICAI tends to surface broader, more general principles (e.g., 'addresses the user's request with creativity and clarity'). ICAI notably misses the safety-critical anti-refusal feature on Arena and the Markdown-formatting preference, both of which have large effects on win-rate. The authors note the two methods are complementary: both produce significant features in the shared regression, suggesting they capture different aspects of preference structure. WIMHF also uniquely studies measurable preferences (what a dataset *can* measure), which ICAI does not address.

## Relation to Prior Work

WIMHF repurposes sparse autoencoders (BatchTopK/Matryoshka SAEs from the LLM interpretability literature) to operate on text-embedding differences between response pairs, rather than on neuron activations. The most directly comparable method is Inverse Constitutional AI (Findeis et al., 2025), which also aims to explain preference data without pre-specifying attributes but uses a prompting-and-clustering pipeline; WIMHF departs by learning a fixed sparse basis via an SAE and by introducing the distinction between 'measurable preferences' (axes of variation in the response distribution) and 'expressed preferences' (axes that actually predict labels). Prior work on preference data has typically studied one or a few pre-specified attributes (length, sycophancy, overconfidence), whereas WIMHF's SAE-based discovery is hypothesis-free and dataset-specific.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: WIMHF gives practitioners a principled, pre-labeling tool to audit what their preference data can and does measure, and the learned features enable concrete interventions (label-flipping for safety, selective personalization) that are interpretable and controllable in ways black-box reward models are not. The finding that widely-used datasets encode directly conflicting preferences (e.g., Reddit/Arena favor informality and jokes while HH-RLHF/PRISM disprefer them) has immediate implications for the common practice of mixing datasets for PFT. Key limitations include a remaining AUC gap versus finetuned reward models (especially on HH-RLHF), English-only analysis, response-only embeddings that ignore prompt context, and personalization evaluated on a single dataset; closing these gaps and extending to multilingual or prompt-conditioned settings are natural next steps.
