## Problem

While LLMs' memory retention and long-context retrieval have been extensively benchmarked, their ability to selectively forget outdated or conflicting information during inference—without any parameter updates—remains largely unexplored. Existing long-context benchmarks implicitly assume all historical context should be retained, and machine unlearning work targets parameter-level erasure rather than dynamic, in-context suppression, leaving a systematic evaluation gap for the 'what to forget' dimension of conversational memory.

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance is a multi-turn dialogue containing memory information (M), optionally a forgetting interference signal (F), and interleaved multi-session conversation turns (MC) drawn from LMSYS-Chat-1M, followed by a final evaluation query (Q). Every sample is instantiated in two paired forms—NoForget (no F present) and Forget (F inserted)—so the same query is answered under both conditions. The model's response is judged by GPT-O4mini against task-specific rubrics, producing three metrics: NoForget Accuracy, Forget Accuracy, and the Selective Forgetting Retention Rate (SFRR), which conditions on originally-correct responses to isolate forgetting-specific degradation. Context length is controlled from 0.5k to 30k tokens by varying the amount of irrelevant dialogue prepended.

## Method

ICF-Bench synthesizes 2k annotated multi-turn dialogues across three realistic scenarios: Instructional Forgetting (built on ChatAlpaca, where explicit 'forget' instructions are inserted and a test query probes whether the target information was suppressed), Subtask Revision (built on FollowBench, where multi-task instructions are partially modified and the model must discard outdated subtasks while retaining updated ones), and Dynamic Preference (built on PrefEval, where user preferences shift and the model must prioritize the latest preference). The key methodological contribution is the SFRR metric, defined as the fraction of originally-correct NoForget responses that remain correct under Forget interference, which normalizes out baseline competence and isolates forgetting-specific fragility. The paper evaluates 11 models (8 open-source from 7B to 235B parameters, 3 proprietary including GPT-5) using greedy decoding, and further ablates context length, prompt-engineering strategies (adding explicit forgetting reminders), and validates the automated evaluator against three human annotators with Cohen's κ and Fleiss' κ.

## Results

Across 11 LLMs evaluated on ICF-Bench, GPT-5 achieves the highest average NoForget Accuracy (88.75%) and Selective Forgetting Retention Rate (70.54%), yet still drops to 64.83% Forget Accuracy—a 23.92 pp decline when forgetting interference is introduced. Llama3-8B suffers the most dramatic collapse, falling from 69.85% NA to just 22.78% FA (47.07 pp drop). Among open-source models, Gemma3-27B achieves the best average SFRR (62.13%), surpassing larger models like Llama3-70B (46.60%) and Qwen3-235B-A22B (50.80%), confirming that stronger memory capacity does not transfer into stronger in-context forgetting ability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a benchmark paper, ICF-Bench compares 11 LLMs (8 open-source, 3 proprietary) rather than proposing a new method. GPT-5 leads on average SFRR (70.54%) and Dynamic Preference FA (72.45%), outperforming GPT-4o (59.01% avg SFRR, 44.90% DP FA) and GPT-O3mini (64.00% avg SFRR, 44.90% DP FA). Among open-source models, Gemma3-27B is the clear ICF leader with 62.13% average SFRR, beating the much larger Llama3-70B (46.60%) and Qwen3-235B-A22B (50.80%) despite having a lower NA—demonstrating the core asymmetry finding that memory strength does not predict forgetting ability. In Instructional Forgetting, all models collapse severely (SFRR drops below 63% even for GPT-5), while Subtask Revision is the most forgiving scenario (SFRR up to 79.63% for Llama3-70B). Prompt engineering with explicit forgetting reminders improves SFRR by 10–18 pp across all proprietary models, suggesting a practical but incomplete mitigation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a distinct niche between three existing research threads: machine unlearning (which modifies parameters to erase training data), long-context retrieval benchmarks (which evaluate what to remember but not what to forget), and preliminary in-context unlearning work (Pawelczyk et al., 2023; Forgetting Transformer, 2025) that explored forgetting in isolation without systematic multi-scenario evaluation. ICF-Bench repurposes the conceptual framing of in-context unlearning but grounds it in realistic multi-turn dialogue with paired NoForget/Forget task forms, explicitly separating inference-time selective forgetting from parameter-level erasure. The SFRR metric is novel in that it conditions forgetting performance on prior success, isolating interference-specific fragility from baseline competence—a diagnostic angle absent from prior long-context or unlearning benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a fundamental asymmetry—strong memory retention does not transfer to effective in-context forgetting—that has direct implications for privacy-sensitive dialogue systems, adaptive task assistants, and user-controlled context management. The finding that context length *improves* Subtask Revision SFRR while degrading other scenarios suggests that attention dilution and recency bias interact differently across forgetting types, opening questions about whether architectural interventions (forgetting gates, attention modulation) can selectively address each scenario. Key limitations include the dialogue-only scope (no multi-document or tool-augmented settings), the absence of systematic improvement methods beyond simple prompt engineering, and reliance on a single LLM judge (GPT-O4mini) despite human validation. Future work on representation disentanglement, attention-level modulation, and broader prompt strategies is needed to close the gap the benchmark reveals.
