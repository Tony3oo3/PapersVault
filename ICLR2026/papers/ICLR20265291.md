## Problem

Existing LLM safety evaluations rely on single-turn, static benchmarks that cannot capture interactive social behaviours like anthropomorphism, which typically emerge over multiple dialogue turns. There is no scalable, automated method to systematically measure the full spectrum of anthropomorphic behaviours in conversational AI systems, nor any empirical validation that such automated measurements actually predict how real users perceive those systems as human-like.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20265291/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The evaluation takes a Target LLM and 960 contextualized seed prompts (120 base prompts adapted across 4 use domains × 2 scenarios each) to generate 5-turn synthetic dialogues via a User LLM, yielding 4,800 Target LLM messages per system. Output is an 'anthropomorphism profile': per-message binary labels for 13 behaviours (via LLM judges) plus a regex count for first-person pronoun use, aggregated into category-level and individual-behaviour frequencies. The profiles are further broken down by use domain and by dialogue turn to reveal temporal dynamics.

## Method

AnthroBench operates in three stages. First, a User LLM (Gemini 1.5 Pro with a role-playing system prompt specifying scenario, tone, and conversational principles) generates 5-turn dialogues with the Target LLM, seeded by prompts contextualised to four use domains varying in empathy and professionalism (friendship, life coaching, career development, general planning). Second, three independent Judge LLMs (Gemini 1.5 Flash, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, GPT-4 Turbo) each produce three binary labels per message per behaviour using few-shot prompts with negative examples; the mode of three samples per judge is taken, then a two-out-of-three majority vote across judges yields the final label. Third, construct validity is established through a one-off interactive human subject study (N=1,101) in which participants converse for 10–20 minutes with high- or low-anthropomorphic variants of Gemini 1.5 Pro, and their Godspeed survey scores and AnthroScore descriptions are shown to correlate with the automated behaviour frequencies, confirming that the measured behaviours predict real users' anthropomorphic perceptions.

## Results

All four evaluated LLMs (Gemini 1.5 Pro, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, GPT-4o, Mistral Large) exhibit similar anthropomorphism profiles dominated by relationship-building behaviours and first-person pronoun use, with validation and first-person pronouns appearing in over 50% of messages across all systems. Over 50% of nine out of 14 anthropomorphic behaviours first appear only in turns 2–5, confirming the necessity of multi-turn evaluation. In the human validation study (N=1,101), the high-frequency anthropomorphic condition yielded a mean Godspeed score of 4.00 vs. 3.25 for the low-frequency condition (14.9% higher; U=213,636, p<0.001, r=0.411), and participants in the high-frequency condition were 33% more likely to implicitly frame the system as human (AnthroScore: 4× vs. 3×; U=158,699, p<0.05).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Rather than comparing against prior benchmarks, the paper evaluates four SOTA LLMs (Gemini 1.5 Pro, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, GPT-4o, Mistral Large) and finds them remarkably similar in their anthropomorphism profiles, all dominated by relationship-building and first-person pronoun use. The key validation comparison is between a high-frequency and a low-frequency anthropomorphic prompting of Gemini 1.5 Pro: the high-frequency variant scores significantly higher on both explicit (Godspeed survey, 4.00 vs. 3.25) and implicit (AnthroScore, 4× vs. 3×) human-likeness measures, confirming that the automated multi-turn evaluation tracks behaviours that genuinely shape user perceptions. Sensitivity analyses show that the rank order of behaviour categories (relationship-building > internal states > personhood > physical embodiment) remains stable across different User LLMs (Gemini 1.5 Pro, Gemini 2.5 Pro, GPT-4o) and user personas (support-seeking vs. adversarial), though absolute frequencies shift modestly. Judge LLM labels achieve weighted average precision above 85% for the majority of behaviours when aggregated by majority vote, with empathy being the hardest to discriminate (≈64% precision).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on prior taxonomies of linguistic anthropomorphic behaviours (Abercrombie et al., 2023; Akbulut et al., 2024) and the Godspeed Anthropomorphism survey (Bartneck et al., 2009), but departs from the dominant single-turn, static benchmarking paradigm by introducing a fully automated multi-turn evaluation using LLM-simulated users. It draws on role-playing principles from Louie et al. (2024) for user simulation and on LLM-as-judge approaches for scalable labeling, while distinguishing itself from adversarial red-teaming by targeting innocuous, non-adversarial interactions. The validation against a large-scale human subject study (N=1,101) using both explicit (Godspeed) and implicit (AnthroScore) measures is a novel construct-validity step not present in prior automated evaluations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that over 50% of anthropomorphic behaviours are first detected only in turns 2–5 provides a concrete, quantitative argument that single-turn benchmarks systematically undercount these behaviours, with direct implications for how developers and policymakers assess AI systems. The publicly released AnthroBench tool offers a reproducible pipeline for tracking behavioural drift during development and comparing systems across contexts. Key limitations include English/Western-only scope, a fixed five-turn horizon, and a single user-simulation type, leaving open how these behaviours evolve in longer, more diverse interactions and across cultures. The convergence of four architecturally distinct systems on similar anthropomorphism profiles raises the question of whether post-training alignment objectives are inadvertently standardising these behaviours.
