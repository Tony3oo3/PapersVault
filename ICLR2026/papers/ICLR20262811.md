## Problem

Existing molecule-language datasets (e.g., ChEBI-20, L+M-24) rely on ambiguous natural-language descriptions that can map to multiple distinct molecular structures, making precise model evaluation impossible. Prior work either reformulates domain-specific tasks (property/reaction prediction) into language queries—neglecting the prerequisite of accurate structural understanding—or focuses on description-to-structure translation without ensuring deterministic, unambiguous ground truth. No benchmark exists to systematically evaluate the fundamental, unambiguous tasks of molecular structure recognition, language-prompted editing, and generation from structural descriptions.

## Input / Output

The benchmark supports three tasks across multiple molecular representations (SMILES, SELFIES, molecular images, and molecular graphs). For recognition, the model receives a molecular structure plus a text query and must return both a categorical/numerical answer and explicit atom indices constituting the target substructure. For editing, the model receives a molecule (SMILES or image) and a natural-language modification instruction, returning a modified SMILES string evaluated for validity, Tanimoto similarity, and exact match. For generation, the model receives only a detailed structural description (mean ~131 words) and must produce a valid SMILES from scratch. Molecules are capped at 40 non-hydrogen atoms (~24 on average), and recognition tasks deliberately include cases where relevant atoms are non-adjacent in SMILES to prevent shortcut learning.

## Method

MolLangBench is constructed through two complementary pipelines. Recognition tasks (18 subtasks across local topology, functional groups, and stereochemistry) are built automatically: 10,000 candidate molecules are sampled per task from UniChem (~178M molecules), ground-truth labels are extracted via customized RDKit scripts, and 200 instances with balanced label distributions are selected per task. Editing and generation tasks (200 core + 200 extended instances each) are curated through a rigorous three-step human annotation pipeline: (1) a chemistry-trained annotator writes the instruction or description, (2) a second annotator performs independent peer review and iterative refinement, and (3) two independent validators reconstruct the molecule from scratch using chemical drawing software and confirm exact structural match via canonical SMILES/InChI comparison. Evaluation is conducted zero-shot across 15+ LLMs and vision-language models, reporting pass@1 accuracy, SMILES validity, and Tanimoto similarity, with variance analysis over five runs and pass@k metrics to assess robustness.

## Results

On MolLangBench, the strongest model (GPT-5) achieves 86.2% average recognition accuracy (with 8.6% localization accuracy), 85.5% editing accuracy, and only 43.0% generation accuracy on the core set. Most other language models score below 60% on recognition, and generation accuracy drops below 20% for all models except GPT-5 and Gemini-2.5-Pro. The vision-language model GPT Image 1 achieves a mere 8% editing accuracy and 0% generation accuracy, while the best vision-language recognition model (o4-mini with image input) scores only 70.0% average, trailing its text-based counterpart o4-mini (71.3%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-5 leads on all three task categories, achieving 86.2% recognition, 85.5% editing, and 43.0% generation accuracy, with a 7-point margin over o3 (78.5%) on editing and a 14-point margin on generation. Gemini-2.5-Pro is the strongest non-GPT model, matching o3 on recognition (85.2% vs. 79.2%) and achieving generation accuracy (43.0%) comparable to GPT-5, while also posting the highest SMILES validity (86.5%) among language models. GPT-4o and earlier non-reasoning models (GPT-4.1, GPT-4.5-preview) fall well behind, with recognition averages of 24.9–39.1% and generation accuracy below 6%. Vision-language models underperform their text-based counterparts: o4-mini with image input scores 70.0% on recognition (vs. 71.3% for o4-mini text), and GPT Image 1 achieves only 8% editing accuracy and 0% generation accuracy. Chemistry-specific LLMs (ChemLLM, Galactica, ChemDFM) and graph-language models (HIGHT, Llamole) perform substantially worse than even the weakest general-purpose LLM (GPT-4o), with recognition averages of 12.7–30.0% and near-zero editing/generation accuracy, confirming that domain-specific fine-tuning has not yet closed the gap for molecule-language interface tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by benchmarking *fundamental* molecule-language interface tasks—structure recognition, editing, and generation—rather than the downstream tasks (property prediction, reaction prediction) that prior LLM-for-chemistry work has focused on. It departs from earlier molecule-language datasets (ChEBI-20, L+M-24) by enforcing deterministic, unambiguous ground-truth answers and rigorous expert validation, and from vision-language alignment research by noting that molecular descriptions require scientific inference rather than direct visual observation. The benchmark also extends evaluation beyond text-only SMILES to molecular images and (preliminarily) molecular graphs, positioning itself as a multi-modal evaluation framework for the molecule-language interface.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that even the strongest current model (GPT-5) achieves only 43% on de novo generation and that stereochemistry reasoning is near-random for most models, exposing a fundamental gap between LLM language capabilities and precise structural manipulation. The finding that vision-language models gain no advantage from molecular images over SMILES text challenges the intuition that visual representations should be more intuitive for structure understanding. Open questions include scalable data generation for training (manual annotation is prohibitively expensive), whether atom-level tokenization can close the recognition-to-localization gap, and whether dedicated molecule-language pretraining can bridge the alignment gap that general-purpose LLMs currently exhibit.
