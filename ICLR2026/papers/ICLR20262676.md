## Problem

Most deep learning approaches for fluid-solid interaction (FSI) are limited to one-way scenarios with rigid, static solids, or use monolithic modeling that cannot distinguish inter- and intra-domain information. Even in two-way FSI, existing methods lack explicit handling of the dynamic fluid-solid coupling interface, making it difficult to capture the nonlinear, bidirectional dependencies between deformable solids and surrounding flows.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262676/cropped_page1_idx4.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes mesh-based observations of three explicitly separated components—fluid (geometry, pressure, velocity), solid (geometry, stress or material properties), and coupling interface (geometry, combined physical quantities)—at the current time step, with mesh sizes ranging from ~1,300 (2D) to ~37,000 (3D) points. It produces the corresponding predicted states at t+Δt in the same three-domain structure. The framework supports three task formulations: single-step prediction (Δt spans multiple frames), autoregressive rollout (feeding its own predictions forward), and steady-state inference (mapping problem parameters directly to equilibrium solutions).

## Method

Fisale introduces multiscale latent ALE grids: regular Cartesian grids deformed by geometry-aware RBF-weighted offsets from all three domains, creating a unified spatial representation that evolves independently of both material and fixed frames (mimicking the classical ALE principle). Physical quantities from fluid, solid, and interface are projected onto these grids via attention-based interpolation, forming a multi-physics embedding at each latent node. The core processing is a Partitioned Coupling Module (PCM) that mirrors classical partitioned solvers in four sequential substeps: (1) cross-attention updates solid and interface states attending to the full system, (2) Laplacian-smoothing updates grid coordinates based on solid motion, (3) cross-attention updates fluid and interface states on the updated grid, and (4) self-attention aligns information across all three domains. L stacked PCM levels operate in parallel across H pathways at different grid resolutions, with FFN-based aggregation enabling cross-scale communication at each level.

## Results

On the Structure Oscillation benchmark (2D, single-step prediction), Fisale achieves a mean Relative L2 error of 0.0066, outperforming the best baseline AMG (0.0089) by 26% and LNO (0.0104) by 36%. On the Venous Valve autoregressive simulation (2D, 101-frame rollout), Fisale attains the lowest RMSE across all physical quantities, e.g., solid geometry RMSE of 0.2794 vs. Transolver's 0.3262 and solid stress RMSE of 2658.59 vs. Transolver's 3055.56. On the 3D Flexible Wing steady-state inference task (37,441 mesh points), Fisale achieves a mean Relative L2 of 0.0136, beating Transolver (0.0164) and MGN (0.0202). Fisale also demonstrates the best out-of-distribution generalization on Structure Oscillation at Re=4000 (Relative L2 = 0.0637 vs. AMG's 0.0696).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Fisale achieves the best performance across all three tasks and all physical domains, winning against all 12 baselines. On Structure Oscillation, the closest competitors are AMG (mean Rel. L2 0.0089), LNO (0.0104), and Transolver (0.0115); Fisale's advantage is most pronounced in the fluid domain (0.0148 vs. AMG's 0.0211, a 30% reduction). On Venous Valve, Transolver is the strongest baseline across most quantities, but Fisale leads by 14% on solid geometry RMSE (0.2794 vs. 0.3262) and 13% on solid stress (2658.59 vs. 3055.56), with the gap widening in later rollout frames. On Flexible Wing, Transolver (0.0164) and MGN (0.0202) are the nearest baselines, while GINO (0.4745) and ONO (0.2179) degrade severely on the 37K-point 3D mesh. Notably, GNN-based methods (MGN, HOOD) and monolithic neural operators (GINO, ONO) struggle most with the large-scale 3D wing, whereas Fisale's partitioned design and explicit interface modeling maintain accuracy. In the OOD test at Re=4000, Fisale (0.0637) outperforms AMG (0.0696) and LNO (0.0715), confirming better extrapolation under stronger nonlinear coupling.

## Relation to Prior Work

Fisale draws its architectural principles from two classical numerical methods: the ALE framework (Hirt et al., 1974), which provides a unified mesh-motion representation bridging Lagrangian and Eulerian descriptions, and the partitioned coupling algorithm (Degroote et al., 2009), which decomposes the monolithic coupled system into sequential substeps. It departs from prior deep-learning FSI work in a key conceptual way: whereas GNN-based simulators (MGN, HOOD) use undifferentiated message passing and neural operators (CoDA-NO, Geo-FNO) treat all physical domains homogeneously in a monolithic view, Fisale explicitly elevates the fluid-solid coupling interface to a first-class component and processes solid, fluid, and interface through separate attention-based substeps within a Partitioned Coupling Module. The multiscale latent ALE grid further distinguishes it from prior geometry-aware operators by providing a learnable, geometry-deformed reference grid that evolves independently of both material and spatial frames.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Two-way FSI with deformable solids remains a significant gap in data-driven simulation, and this paper demonstrates that principled decomposition (partitioned coupling) combined with explicit interface modeling yields consistent gains across 2D and 3D tasks of varying complexity, including long autoregressive rollouts where error accumulation is a major challenge. The approach is modular and could extend to other multi-physics coupling problems (e.g., thermo-mechanical, electro-mechanical) where distinct domains interact at interfaces. Open questions include scalability to fully 3D time-dependent problems with millions of mesh points, real-time inference for control applications, and whether the partitioned structure can be further accelerated (e.g., via learned substep counts or adaptive coupling frequency).
