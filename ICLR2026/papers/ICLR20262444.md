## Problem

Time series foundation models (TSFMs) are tuned via trial-and-error on established benchmarks, but design choices that improve benchmark performance can be deleterious on qualitatively different data (e.g., chaotic systems). The community lacks a systematic understanding of how specific architectural and training 'knobs'—patch size, embedding strategy, and loss function—induce implicit biases that shape fundamental model properties such as temporal behavior, geometric structure, and regression aggressiveness.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262444/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The paper analyzes existing pretrained TSFMs (primarily Chronos and Chronos-Bolt, both using a T5 Transformer backbone) by probing them with controlled synthetic inputs: sinusoidal waves at varying frequencies, chaotic Lorentz oscillator trajectories, multi-scale and multi-offset signals, and outlier-injected sequences. Inputs are discrete time series sequences grouped into patches of size k (ranging from 1 to 16) and embedded either via quantization into 4096 discrete bins (Chronos) or via a two-layer ReLU MLP (Chronos-Bolt). Outputs depend on the training loss: cross-entropy yields a probability distribution over discrete value tokens, L1 quantile loss yields quantile predictions, and L2 loss yields point forecasts.

## Method

The paper identifies and characterizes three classes of implicit biases through a combination of theoretical proofs and controlled empirical evaluation. (1) Temporal bias: Theorem 1 proves via concentration inequalities and matrix norm bounds that patches of similar frequencies are embedded in a low-dimensional subspace (rank bounded by bandwidth ω) while patches of different frequencies occupy nearly orthogonal subspaces, explaining why larger patch sizes favor low-frequency learning and disrupt periodicity alignment. (2) Geometric bias: Quantization embeddings induce angular bias (bimodal attention favoring local neighbors), distance bias (magnifying fine-scale patterns), while continuous ReLU-MLP embeddings induce norm bias (dominating attention toward large-magnitude values). (3) Regression-to-the-mean bias: L2 loss regresses to the mean, L1 to the median, while cross-entropy preserves sharper multimodal outcomes. The primary comparison isolates design choices by contrasting Chronos (k=1, discrete embedding, cross-entropy) against Chronos-Bolt (k=16, continuous MLP embedding, L1 quantile loss), which share the same T5 backbone and similar training corpora. A case study on outlier handling demonstrates how all three biases interact in complex, sometimes counterintuitive ways.

## Results

This is an analysis paper rather than a model proposal, so results are characterized through controlled experiments demonstrating bias effects. Key quantitative findings include: Chronos-Bolt with patch size k=16 fails significantly at capturing high-spectral information compared to k=1 (Figure 3b); Chronos achieves significantly lower MASE than Chronos-Bolt on chaotic-system context parroting (Figure 5c); as the ratio between two scales increases, all continuous-embedding models (Chronos-Bolt, TimesFM, Moirai) degrade on fine-scale pattern learning (Figure 6c); and in outlier handling, the k=1 model outperforms k=16 without outliers, but k=16 becomes more effective as outlier magnitude or count increases (Figure 9). The paper also shows that as uncertainty parameter p increases in a periodic-walk context, TimesFM (L²) and Chronos-Bolt (L¹) regress more strongly toward mean/median than Chronos (cross-entropy) (Figure 8).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between Chronos (quantization embedding, no patching, cross-entropy loss) and Chronos-Bolt (continuous MLP embedding, k=16 patching, L¹ quantile loss), chosen because they share the same T5 backbone and similar training corpora. Chronos wins on chaotic-system forecasting (lower MASE on context parroting, Figure 5c), fine-scale multi-scale pattern learning (Figure 6b,c), and near-zero offset signal forecasting (Figure 7b,c), owing to its angular bias favoring local information, distance bias magnifying small scales, and mode-seeking cross-entropy loss. Chronos-Bolt wins on denoising noisy low-frequency signals (larger patch size suppresses high-frequency noise, Figure 9c), sparse signals where nonzero motifs should dominate (norm bias, Appendix A.2.3), and avoiding spurious outlier predictions (lower variance in outlier handling, Figure 9b). TimesFM (L² loss, decoder-only) is compared as a third model: it regresses most aggressively to the mean under uncertainty (Figure 8b), its decoder-only architecture dilutes periodic structure more than encoder-only designs (Figure 4a), and its continuous embedding shows similar fine-scale degradation as Chronos-Bolt (Figure 6c). Moirai is included in the multi-scale comparison (Figure 6c) and shows the same continuous-embedding limitation on fine-scale patterns.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant TSFM paradigm of proposing new architectures and benchmarking them, instead systematically analyzing how three specific design knobs (patch size, embedding strategy, training loss) in existing Transformer-based TSFMs induce three corresponding implicit biases (temporal, geometric, regression-to-the-mean). It builds on the spectral bias literature for generic neural networks (Rahaman et al., 2019; Xu, 2020) and the NTK-based frequency analysis (Jacot et al., 2018; Arora et al., 2019), extending these ideas to the patching and embedding mechanisms specific to time series Transformers. The regression-to-the-mean analysis connects to the 'regression as classification' line of work (Stewart et al., 2023) and the chaotic systems forecasting literature (Gilpin, 2021; Zhang & Gilpin, 2025a), but the paper's core contribution is the unified, controlled comparison of Chronos vs. Chronos-Bolt (same T5 backbone, comparable size, similar training corpora) to isolate each bias's effect.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a diagnostic framework for understanding why TSFMs that excel on standard benchmarks (M4, ETT, Monash) can fail on qualitatively different data such as chaotic systems, and vice versa. The key open question it raises is how to dynamically adapt these biases at inference time (via in-context learning, retrieval, or RL-based adaptation) rather than committing to a single set of design choices. A limitation is that the analysis is primarily grounded in the Chronos/Chronos-Bolt family with ablations to a few other models, so generalization to fundamentally different architectures (e.g., state-space models) remains to be established. The outlier-handling case study demonstrates that biases interact in non-trivial ways, suggesting that single-knob optimization is insufficient for robust TSFM design.
