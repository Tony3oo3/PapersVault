## Problem

Existing text-rich image benchmarks (DocVQA, ChartQA, OCRBench) primarily assess text extraction and perception, which can be solved by fast-thinking processes, leaving a critical gap in systematically evaluating MLLMs' deeper reasoning capabilities on text-rich visual scenarios. Moreover, in most existing datasets the answers are directly retrievable from OCR results, so models can succeed without genuine reasoning. This paper addresses the absence of a dedicated benchmark that requires models to perform multi-step reasoning over text-rich images rather than merely reading text off the page.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20265217/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each example pairs a single text-rich image (documents, charts, infographics, handwritten notes, web screenshots, receipts) with a textual question, and the model must produce both a final answer (multiple-choice letter or free-form: integer, float, or string) and a step-by-step reasoning chain. For pure-LLM baselines, the image is replaced by PP-OCRv3 extracted text while retaining the same question. A distinguishing property is that in the vast majority of examples the answer is NOT present verbatim in the OCR output, forcing the model to perform computation, spatial interpretation, or logical inference rather than retrieval. The average combined reasoning-plus-answer length is 421 characters (max 3,106), reflecting multi-step analytical chains.

## Method

The core contribution is a four-stage data curation framework: (1) collecting 1,022 images from internet sources, real-world photographs, and six established benchmarks; (2) having PhD-level annotators independently propose three questions per image, then generating reasoning paths and answers via two parallel pathways (manual annotation and closed-source MLLM generation); (3) three annotators scoring both pathways to select the highest-quality reasoning chain; and (4) a two-stage majority-voting taxonomy assigning each example to one of six reasoning categories (spatial, numerical analysis, mathematical, enumerative, logical, multidisciplinary knowledge). Evaluation uses a three-stage protocol (response generation → GPT-4o-based answer extraction → normalized accuracy scoring) for final answers, and an LLM-as-judge approach validated against human scores for reasoning-path quality. The benchmark deliberately restricts to single-image inputs to isolate reasoning ability from long-context processing and to remain compatible with document-oriented MLLMs that only accept one image.

## Results

On the OCR-Reasoning benchmark (1,069 examples), no evaluated model exceeds 50% overall accuracy. The best performer is DouBao-1.5-Vision-Pro at 46.8%, followed by OpenAI-o1 (44.4%) and GLM-4.1V-Thinking-9B (44.1%). OCR+LLM pipelines (replacing images with PP-OCRv3 text) drop to 26.5–33.3%, and document-oriented MLLMs (mPLUG-DocOwl2, Docopilot, DocMark, TokenVL) score only 3.3–14.3%, confirming that text-rich image reasoning demands genuine multimodal reasoning beyond perception or OCR extraction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DouBao-1.5-Vision-Pro leads all 25 evaluated models at 46.8% overall, outperforming OpenAI-o1 (44.4%) by 2.4 points and GLM-4.1V-Thinking-9B (44.1%) by 2.7 points, yet still falls well short of the 50% threshold. Replacing image input with PP-OCRv3 text causes a dramatic drop: DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B scores only 26.5% with OCR text versus Qwen2.5-VL-32B's 36.2% with image input, a 9.7-point gap showing that OCR alone cannot substitute for visual understanding. Among open-source models, GLM-4.1V-Thinking-9B (44.1%) and MiMo-VL-RL-7B (38.8%) are the strongest, while RL-trained models like VL-Rethinker-7B (14.6%) and MM-Eureka-Qwen-7B (13.2%) underperform their non-RL baselines, suggesting current RL reward functions are misaligned with text-rich reasoning. Document-oriented MLLMs (mPLUG-DocOwl2-8B at 3.3%, DocMark-2B at 7.4%) are far weaker than general MLLMs, indicating that document perception training does not transfer to reasoning. On the comparison with existing benchmarks (Table 6), the same models that score 80–96% on DocVQA/ChartQA/OCRBench/TextVQA drop to 11.5–15.7% on OCR-Reasoning, highlighting the benchmark's substantially higher difficulty.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of two established benchmark lineages: text-rich image understanding (DocVQA, ChartQA, OCRBench) and multimodal reasoning evaluation (MathVista, MMMU). Prior text-rich image benchmarks focus on perceptual extraction (reading text from documents, charts, infographics) and can be solved via fast-thinking, while mathematical reasoning benchmarks evaluate multi-step reasoning but in non-text-rich visual contexts. OCR-Reasoning deliberately bridges this gap by requiring slow-thinking, multi-step reasoning over text-rich images, and introduces dual annotation (final answer + step-by-step reasoning trajectory) that neither prior family provides. The paper does not build on a specific method but repurposes the LLM-as-judge paradigm from NLP evaluation to score reasoning processes in the multimodal setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a stark capability gap: no evaluated MLLM exceeds 50% accuracy, and document-oriented models (mPLUG-DocOwl2, DocMark, TokenVL) score below 15%, suggesting that strong OCR/perception alone is insufficient for text-rich reasoning. The error analysis showing calculation errors (37.5%) and spatial comprehension errors (27.2%) dominate over text perception errors (7.7%) reframes the bottleneck as higher-level cognition rather than reading. Open questions include designing reward functions tailored to text-rich reasoning (existing RL rewards target math), selecting diverse training data beyond printed math problems, and whether 'thinking with images' approaches can close the gap. The dataset's manual annotation limits scale (~1,069 examples), and the LLM-as-judge evaluation introduces potential biases the authors acknowledge.
