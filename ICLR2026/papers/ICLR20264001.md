## Problem

While Masked Diffusion Models (MDM) offer parallel and any-order token generation as an alternative to autoregressive models, their fundamental computational limitations remain poorly understood. The paper shows that any-order generation alone does not expand what autoregressive models can already solve, and that both ARM and standard MDM are inherently unable to handle problems requiring backtracking, rewriting, or structural editing (e.g., NP-hard tasks, code with global constraints, non-sequential scientific objects). The paper asks what provably more powerful generation paradigms exist beyond next-token and any-order generation.

## Input / Output

The model accepts a text prompt or partially masked sequence as input (unlike vanilla MDM, no initial full-mask state is required) and iteratively produces a variable-length output sequence whose length can grow or shrink during generation via insert and delete operations. For structured tasks, inputs include directed graphs with designated source/target nodes (min-cut), binary strings (parity), or empty grids (Sudoku), and outputs are the corresponding solved structures. The stopping criterion is flexible—generation halts on convergence, a special token, or a maximum iteration limit rather than requiring a fully unmasked sequence.

## Method

Any-Process MDM (AP-MDM) extends standard masked diffusion by adding three learned operations—remask (revert a decoded token to a mask for self-correction), insert (add a new mask token at any position), and delete (remove a mask token)—implemented as three additional binary classification heads on a shared encoder-only Transformer backbone with no architectural changes. A parameter-free transition function g applies these operations coordinate-wise each step, enabling variable-length sequences, adaptive parallelism, and test-time scaling without fixed mask-ratio schedules. Theoretically, AP-MDM achieves both optimal parallel time and space complexity (simulating PRAM with O(S(n)) context and O(T(n)) steps), expanding solvable problems from P to PSPACE with polynomial context. Training supports both self-supervised objectives (augmentation-based losses for each operation) and supervised learning on state-transition data, with the minimal head additions enabling direct fine-tuning from existing diffusion models.

## Results

On Sudoku (an NP-complete problem), AP-MDM achieves 99.28% accuracy using only 100 training instances and 1.2M parameters, substantially outperforming ARM (87.18%) and AO-MDM (89.49%) that use 1.8M training instances and 5× more parameters. On graph min-cut generation, AP-MDM maintains near-perfect accuracy (≥99.92%) up to 10-node graphs, while ARM degrades from 90.32% at 4 nodes to 0.30% at 6 nodes and fails entirely beyond 7 nodes. On parity checking, AP-MDM achieves 100% length-generalization after training on only length-2 sequences (~200 parameters), whereas ARM with orders of magnitude more parameters and samples fails to generalize beyond training lengths.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary empirical comparison is against ARM and AO-MDM baselines from Kim et al. (2025c) on Sudoku, where AP-MDM wins decisively: 99.28% vs. 87.18% (ARM) and 89.49% (AO-MDM), while using 18,000× fewer training samples (100 vs. 1.8M) and 35× fewer parameters (1.2M vs. 42M). On graph min-cut generation, AP-MDM achieves 100% accuracy up to 7 nodes and ≥99.92% up to 10 nodes, while the ARM baseline collapses from 90.32% (4 nodes) to 0.30% (6 nodes) and cannot be evaluated beyond 7 nodes. On parity, AP-MDM with ~200 parameters and 4 training samples achieves 100% generalization to arbitrary sequence lengths, while ARM trained on up to 10K instances fails to generalize beyond its training length. The paper also provides theoretical comparisons: Theorem 3 shows any-order MDM is computationally equivalent to ARM (no expressivity gain from ordering alone), while Theorem 4 shows AP-MDM achieves optimal parallel time AND space complexity (O(S(n)) context, O(T(n)) steps) versus standard MDM's O(P(n)·T(n)) space requirement.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the masked diffusion model (MDM) lineage (Austin et al. 2021; Lou et al. 2024; Sahoo et al. 2024) by adding remask, insert, and delete operations, while simultaneously building on the theoretical analysis of autoregressive models (Merrill & Sabharwal 2024; Feng et al. 2024; Li et al. 2024) and the PRAM parallel computation model to provide a unified complexity-theoretic comparison of generation paradigms. It departs from concurrent editing/remasking work (Wang et al. 2025; Kim et al. 2025a,b; Wu et al. 2025; Havasi et al. 2025) by providing the first systematic analysis of provable computational benefits from these operations, rather than treating them as ad-hoc heuristics. The paper also subsumes earlier non-autoregressive generation approaches (Insertion Transformer, Levenshtein Transformer, InsNet, XLNet) as special cases within its any-process generation framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes a formal theoretical framework for comparing generation paradigms, with the key insight that any-order generation (MDM's headline feature) does not expand ARM's computational power when controlling for parallelism and architecture (Theorem 3), while any-process generation provably breaks the PSPACE barrier that limits both ARM and vanilla MDM. This provides concrete design principles for next-generation LLMs targeting code, science, and other non-sequential domains. However, all empirical validation is on small-scale tasks (Sudoku, parity with ~200 parameters, graphs up to 10 nodes), leaving open whether these theoretical advantages translate to large-scale language modeling and whether the additional control heads can be effectively trained on web-scale corpora.
