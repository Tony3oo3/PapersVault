## Problem

Existing models of social learning control either assume two-way communication between planner and agents, directly alter agents' choice rules, or restrict information design to a one-shot setting. This paper addresses the gap of a dynamic, sequential setting where an information-mediating planner (e.g., an LLM) can only adjust the precision of binary private signals while agents simultaneously learn from each other's observed actions, creating information externalities that shape future public beliefs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261185/cropped_page7_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The planner's state is the public belief b_i ∈ [0,1] (a Markov sufficient statistic for the full action-precision history), and its control is a scalar signal precision q_i ∈ [0.5,1] with a concave cost β(·). Agents receive a binary symmetric signal (matching the true state ω ∈ {G,B} with probability q_i) plus the observed action history, and output a binary action. The system produces an updated public belief via Bayesian updating, and the planner's objective is the discounted sum of instantaneous rewards (negative cost plus negative expected misclassification), with the true state and private signals never observed by the planner.

## Method

The paper formulates the planner's problem as an infinite-horizon discounted MDP with state b_i, control q_i, and a piecewise transition function derived from the social learning dynamics. The key theoretical contribution is proving convexity of the altruistic value function (Theorem 2) via an inductive argument over a binary decision tree, which is non-trivial because agent actions depend on the belief state and standard bounding techniques fail. This convexity enables a full characterization of optimal policies: the altruistic planner operates in three belief-dependent modes (no investment at extremes, full precision near 0.5, minimum informative precision in between), while the biased planner exhibits five modes including intentional signal obfuscation (decreasing precision below baseline) to prevent favorable beliefs from being overturned. Empirically, LLMs are deployed in three roles—agent, planner, and oracle—to validate the theory against non-Bayesian agents exhibiting underreaction to confirming signals and overreaction to disconfirming ones, with the LLM planner's emergent policy showing structural alignment with the analytical optimum while adapting to these cognitive biases.

## Results

The LLM planner's emergent policy deviates from the analytically optimal policy by less than 10% for the majority of belief states in both altruistic and biased settings (Figure 2b). When misaligned (true state B, planner seeking action G), both the analytical and LLM biased planners decrease social welfare by 40–50% relative to the no-control baseline, achieved through intentional signal obfuscation (reducing precision below baseline). The hybrid setting (analytically optimal policy applied to non-Bayesian LLM agents) shows the optimal policy is 'brittle' and underperforms compared to the LLM planner's adapted policy, confirming that the LLM planner strategically accounts for agents' non-Bayesian cognitive biases (underreaction to confirming signals, overreaction to disconfirming signals, and resistance to cascades).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baselines in the traditional benchmark sense; instead, it compares three internal settings: (1) analytical (optimal policy + Bayesian agents), (2) LLM (LLM planner + LLM agents), and (3) hybrid (optimal policy + LLM agents). The LLM planner's policy closely mirrors the analytically optimal policy in structure—both cease investment at extreme beliefs, invest heavily near 0.5, and (in the biased case) reduce precision to obfuscate unfavorable information—though the LLM planner shows a more gradual tapering and avoids extreme precisions (0.5 or 1.0) due to central tendency bias. The hybrid setting reveals that the analytically optimal policy, designed for Bayes-rational agents, suffers performance degradation when applied to non-Bayesian LLM agents, while the LLM planner's policy, though structurally similar, is better calibrated to the agents' actual cognitive biases. Myopic planners (δ=0) perform substantially worse than non-myopic ones in both analytical and LLM settings, confirming that accounting for social learning externalities is critical for effective information mediation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes three research threads—sequential social learning (Banerjee 1992; Bikhchandani et al. 1992), Bayesian persuasion/information design (Kamenica & Gentzkow 2011), and LLM-based signal generation (Duetting et al. 2025)—into a novel dynamic control problem. It departs from prior social-learning control work (Wei & Anastasopoulos 2022; Smith et al. 2021) by eliminating two-way communication and preserving agent autonomy, and from prior information-design-in-social-learning work (Arieli et al. 2022; Wu et al. 2025) by making the signal structure dynamic rather than one-shot. The key theoretical contribution is proving convexity of the value function in a setting where agent actions depend on the belief state, which does not follow from standard results (e.g., Nyarko 1994) where actions are belief-independent.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a biased planner can reduce social welfare by 40–50% through mere precision reduction (vagueness) under stringent transparency constraints—no lying, no cherry-picking, full observability—challenges the assumption that transparency alone protects against algorithmic manipulation. The framework provides a tractable basis for studying and regulating LLM information mediators in socially embedded settings, and the LLM simulations confirm that the theoretical structure is robust to non-Bayesian agent behavior. Open questions include generalization beyond binary states and 0-1 losses, heterogeneous agents, and concrete regulatory mechanisms to mitigate biased-planner welfare losses. A key limitation is the reliance on LLMs as human surrogates, whose fidelity remains contested.
