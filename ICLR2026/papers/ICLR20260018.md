## Problem

RL-posttraining (e.g., GRPO) has become the dominant paradigm for enhancing LLM reasoning, but it is unclear whether the resulting capabilities are genuinely novel or merely a sharpened version of the base model distribution. This paper asks whether comparable single-shot reasoning can be elicited from base models at inference time through pure sampling alone, without any additional training, curated datasets, or external verifiers.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260018/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt (e.g., a math problem, coding task, or science question) and a frozen base LLM, and produces a single token sequence (reasoning trace) sampled from the power distribution p^α rather than the base distribution p. The output is a single-shot response, but internally the algorithm makes multiple inference calls per block of B=192 tokens, with N_MCMC=10 resampling steps per block and a maximum sequence length of T=3072. No external reward signal, verifier, or training data is required at any point.

## Method

The paper defines the power distribution p^α as the target sampling distribution, arguing it captures the 'distribution sharpening' effect of RL while remaining fully specified by the base model. A key theoretical result (Proposition 1) shows that standard low-temperature sampling does NOT sample from p^α: low-temperature sampling computes an exponent of sums over future paths, whereas p^α computes a sum of exponents, meaning p^α upweights tokens with few but high-likelihood futures (critical for reasoning) while low-temperature sampling upweights tokens with many low-likelihood completions. To sample from the intractable unnormalized p^α, the paper employs Metropolis-Hastings MCMC with a random-resampling proposal distribution. To avoid exponential mixing time in the high-dimensional token sequence space, the algorithm (Algorithm 1) introduces a sequential block-wise schedule: it progressively samples from intermediate distributions π_k ∝ p(x_{0:kB})^α, using each block's output to initialize the next block's MCMC chain, with the base model itself (at temperature 1/α) serving as the proposal LLM.

## Results

On MATH500 (in-domain for GRPO), power sampling achieves 0.748 accuracy with Qwen2.5-Math-7B, approaching GRPO's 0.785, and 0.706 with Qwen2.5-7B vs. GRPO's 0.740. On out-of-domain HumanEval, power sampling substantially outperforms GRPO, reaching 0.732 vs. 0.134 with Phi-3.5-mini-instruct (+59.8%) and 0.622 vs. 0.561 with Qwen2.5-7B. On the non-verifiable AlpacaEval 2.0 benchmark, power sampling consistently beats GRPO (e.g., 2.88 vs. 2.38 for Qwen2.5-Math-7B; 8.59 vs. 7.62 for Qwen2.5-7B). The method requires no training, no curated dataset, and no verifier, using only the base model's own likelihoods with α=4.0 and 10 MCMC steps per block.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GRPO (the standard RL posttraining algorithm), power sampling achieves near-parity on the in-domain MATH500 benchmark (0.748 vs. 0.785 for Qwen2.5-Math-7B; 0.706 vs. 0.740 for Qwen2.5-7B) but clearly outperforms on out-of-domain tasks: HumanEval (0.573 vs. 0.537 for Qwen2.5-Math-7B; 0.622 vs. 0.561 for Qwen2.5-7B; 0.732 vs. 0.134 for Phi-3.5-mini-instruct) and the non-verifiable AlpacaEval 2.0 (2.88 vs. 2.38; 8.59 vs. 7.62; 17.65 vs. 16.74). On GPQA, the two methods are essentially tied (0.389 vs. 0.399; 0.318 vs. 0.354; 0.364 vs. 0.359). Against low-temperature sampling, power sampling wins on every task and model, with the largest gaps on MATH500 (e.g., 0.748 vs. 0.690) and AlpacaEval (e.g., 2.88 vs. 2.09), confirming that the power distribution's 'sum of exponents' structure captures future-path information that greedy per-token sharpening misses. Additionally, power sampling preserves generation diversity (pass@k matches the base model at large k), whereas GRPO's pass@k tapers off, indicating RL-induced mode collapse.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Metropolis-Hastings MCMC framework (Metropolis et al., 1953) and the annealed/tempered distribution concept from statistical physics to target the power distribution p^α of a base LLM, using a sequential block-wise proposal that exploits autoregressive structure to avoid exponential mixing time. Methodologically it is closest to Faria et al. (2024), which applies MH to LLM generation, but departs critically by eliminating the external reward function entirely—the target is specified solely by the base model's own likelihoods. The paper also explicitly contrasts its approach with low-temperature sampling, proving (Proposition 1) that the two are not equivalent and that the power distribution's 'sum of exponents' structure captures future-path information that greedy per-token sharpening misses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The training-free, dataset-free, verifier-free nature of the method opens a path to reasoning improvements in domains where no automated reward exists, directly addressing a key limitation of RLVR. The finding that pass@k diversity is preserved (unlike RL's characteristic collapse) suggests a practical 'best of both worlds' for multi-shot settings. Open questions include whether the approach scales to frontier-scale models (experiments use 7B and mini models), how the 8.84× token-generation overhead compares to the cost of an RL training run at scale, and whether the power distribution can be combined with other inference-time techniques (e.g., self-consistency, tree search) for further gains.
