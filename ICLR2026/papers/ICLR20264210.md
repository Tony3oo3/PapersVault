## Problem

Supervised learning baselines like Rejection Fine-Tuning (RFT) for verification-driven math reasoning discard all negative (incorrect) self-generated answers, preventing the model from reflecting on its mistakes. This has led to the prevailing view that self-reflective improvement is exclusive to reinforcement learning. The paper challenges this by asking whether a purely supervised approach can also leverage negative feedback to achieve performance comparable to leading RL algorithms like GRPO and DAPO.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264210/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of math question prompts (from DAPO-Math-17k, with integer ground-truth answers) and a binary verifier. At each iteration, the LLM generates K=16 answers per question, which are split into positive and negative subsets based on correctness. The output is a fine-tuned LLM policy (Qwen2.5-Math-7B or 32B) that produces math answers. Only a single model copy is maintained in memory, with the old policy likelihood pre-computed during data generation, making the approach memory-efficient compared to RL methods that require separate reference models.

## Method

NFT constructs an implicit negative policy π⁻_θ by re-parameterizing the target positive policy π⁺_θ through the identity r_q·π⁺(a|q) + (1−r_q)·π⁻(a|q) = π_old(a|q), where r_q is the per-question correctness rate. This means training the implicit negative policy on negative answers via maximum likelihood directly optimizes the underlying positive LLM, with no separate model needed. The combined loss applies standard MLE on positive data and MLE on the implicit negative policy for negative data, with token-level loss decomposition, a straight-through clipping mechanism (ε=1.0) to prevent training collapse, and prompt weighting ω(q) that prioritizes harder questions. The paper further proves that NFT and GRPO are gradient-equivalent under strict on-policy conditions, revealing that GRPO's group-normalized advantage is implicitly encoded in NFT's loss function, and that their only difference lies in off-policy gradient clipping strategies.

## Results

On 7B models, NFT achieves an average accuracy of 51.7% across six math benchmarks (AIME24, AIME25, AMC23, MATH500, OlympiadBench, Minerva Math), surpassing DAPO (51.2%), GRPO (49.5%), Dr.GRPO (49.8%), DPO (48.9%), and RFT (48.3%). On 32B models, NFT reaches 59.2% average, closely matching DAPO (59.9%) while substantially outperforming RFT (52.8%). The largest individual gains appear on AMC23 (88.5% for NFT vs. 85.0% for DAPO on 7B) and Minerva Math (40.8% vs. 39.3% for DAPO).

## Baselines & Comparisons

NFT (51.7 avg) outperforms all SL baselines on 7B: RFT (48.3) by 3.4 points, DPO (48.9) by 2.8 points, and GRPO (49.5) by 2.2 points. Against the strongest RL baseline DAPO (51.2), NFT achieves a marginal 0.5-point advantage on 7B, with notable wins on AMC23 (+3.5) and Minerva (+1.5) but a slight loss on AIME25 (−0.4). On 32B, NFT (59.2) trails DAPO (59.9) by 0.7 points but decisively beats RFT (52.8) by 6.4 points, confirming that the gap between SL and RL in online training largely stems from SL's inability to leverage negative feedback. The paper further shows that NFT and GRPO are theoretically equivalent under strict on-policy conditions, with their only practical difference being the gradient clipping strategy in off-policy settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

NFT extends Rejection Fine-Tuning (RFT), the standard SL baseline for verification-driven training, by constructing an implicit negative policy parameterized by the same positive LLM, enabling direct maximum-likelihood optimization on negative generations. The implicit-policy design is conceptually analogous to DPO's implicit reward model, but applied to the SL/RL boundary in binary-feedback settings. The paper's central theoretical contribution is proving that NFT and GRPO yield identical gradients under strict on-policy training, despite originating from entirely different frameworks (KL minimization vs. policy gradient), thereby dissolving the perceived SL/RL divide in this regime.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result reframes the SL-vs-RL debate in verification-driven training: the performance gap between RFT and RL stems primarily from SL's inability to leverage negative feedback, not from any inherent superiority of RL. This opens a path for practitioners to use simpler, memory-efficient SL pipelines (single model copy, no separate reward model) while retaining RL-level performance. However, negative data still accounts for only ~20% of the total gain in 32B models, leaving the question of how to more effectively exploit self-reflective learning as an open challenge. The method is currently validated only on math reasoning with binary verifiers; extension to open-ended tasks or continuous rewards remains to be explored.
