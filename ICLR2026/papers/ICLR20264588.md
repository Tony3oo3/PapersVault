## Problem

Existing unsupervised anomaly detection methods require large amounts of normal training data from the target domain and struggle to generalize across different anatomical contexts and imaging modalities. Few-shot anomaly detection in medical imaging—where a model must adapt to a novel task using only a handful of normal reference images—remains largely unexplored, despite being the practical clinical scenario where physicians encounter rare conditions with limited reference cases.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264588/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, the method takes a single query image (resized to 128×128) and K normal support images (K ∈ {2, 4, 8}) from the same previously unseen task (i.e., a new organ-modality-disease combination not encountered during training). It produces a multi-scale anomaly map by aggregating per-layer similarity maps, from which a scalar image-level anomaly score is derived as the spatial mean. The model is trained exclusively on normal samples in an episodic format, with no access to any abnormal examples during training.

## Method

D²4FAD employs a frozen pre-trained WideResNet-50 as a teacher encoder and a learnable student decoder, connected through two complementary distillation losses. Teacher-student distillation (TSD) forces the student to match the teacher's multi-scale features on query images via cosine similarity, while student self-distillation (SSD) aligns the query's student features with those of the support images, establishing a normal reference baseline. A learn-to-weight mechanism replaces uniform support weighting with query-conditioned scaled dot-product attention over reshaped multi-scale features, dynamically down-weighting less relevant or anomalous support images. The combined objective L = λ·L_TSD + L_SSD_l2w is optimized on normal-only episodic data, and at inference anomalies are flagged by the residual discrepancy between the query representation and the weighted support consensus.

## Results

D²4FAD achieves image-level AUROC of 94.2–94.3% on HIS, 94.7–97.3% on LAG, 100% on APTOS, 88.9–99.2% on RSNA, and 95.3–95.5% on Brain Tumor across 2/4/8-shot settings. The method achieves 100% AUROC on APTOS with as few as 2 normal reference images, and outperforms the second-best method (MVFA-AD) by 13.6 points on HIS (8-shot) and 16.3 points on LAG (8-shot). On Brain Tumor, D²4FAD (95.5% at 8-shot) is slightly below MVFA-AD (96.3%), the only dataset where it does not lead. The model requires only 5 GB inference memory and runs 2–3× faster than competing few-shot methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

D²4FAD outperforms all 18 baselines on four of five datasets (HIS, LAG, APTOS, RSNA) across every shot setting. The largest margins are on HIS (94.3% vs. MVFA-AD's 80.7% at 8-shot, +13.6 pts) and LAG (97.3% vs. 81.0%, +16.3 pts). On APTOS, D²4FAD reaches 100% AUROC even at 2-shot, surpassing InCTRL (89.5% at 8-shot) by over 10 points. On Brain Tumor, MVFA-AD narrowly leads (96.3% vs. 95.5% at 8-shot), the only dataset where D²4FAD does not rank first. Among unsupervised methods, FastFlow is the strongest (e.g., 91.0% on Brain Tumor 8-shot) but still trails D²4FAD by 4.5 points there and by 13–23 points on the other datasets. Few-shot generalization methods (RegAD, WinCLIP, AnomalyGPT) perform substantially worse than D²4FAD, with RegAD and WinCLIP falling below 60% on several datasets, highlighting the advantage of the dual distillation + learn-to-weight design over registration-based or CLIP-based few-shot approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the knowledge distillation paradigm for anomaly detection (Salehi et al., CVPR 2021; Deng and Li, CVPR 2022) by introducing a dual distillation mechanism: teacher-student distillation on query images combined with student self-distillation on support images, all within an episodic few-shot framework. It departs from prior few-shot anomaly detection work (RegAD, TDG, DiffNet) that targets industrial defects by addressing the high anatomical variability and multi-modality challenges specific to medical imaging, and by introducing a learn-to-weight mechanism that dynamically assesses support image relevance. The paper also formalizes the few-shot medical anomaly detection task with a comprehensive benchmark spanning four organs, four modalities, and five disease categories, filling a gap the authors identify in the literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is substantial: in clinical settings, physicians often have only a handful of normal reference cases when evaluating new patients or rare conditions, and this work provides a computationally efficient solution (5 GB inference memory, 2–3× faster than competing few-shot methods) that requires no anomalous training data. The curated benchmark of 13,084 images across diverse medical contexts offers a standardized evaluation platform that was previously lacking. Key limitations include the absence of pixel-level annotations (only image-level AUROC is reported), the relatively modest improvement from the learn-to-weight component (~1.9% average), and the weaker results on Brain Tumor MRI where anatomical variability is highest. Open questions include whether the approach extends to 3D volumetric data, whether the distillation gap can be closed with stronger student architectures, and whether the 100% AUROC on APTOS generalizes to more challenging clinical scenarios.
