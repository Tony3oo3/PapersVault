## Problem

While attention mechanisms are known to dynamically reweight input tokens, a rigorous characterization of when and how they learn to detect weak, rare, and sparsely located signals in sequential data has been lacking. Prior theoretical work on sparse token tasks focused on square loss and did not simultaneously address signal weakness (signal norm far below noise) and rarity (signal absent from negative samples), leaving open whether attention's adaptive reweighting provides a fundamental advantage over linear baselines in these harder regimes.

## Input / Output

Each sample is an L×d matrix of token embeddings, where positive samples embed a unit-norm signal vector ξ scaled by strength θ into a random subset of R tokens (R ≪ L) plus i.i.d. Gaussian noise, while negative samples are pure noise. The signal is deliberately weak (‖θvξᵀ‖ = O(θ√R) ≪ ‖Z‖ = O(√(Ld))) and its location shifts randomly across samples, forcing any successful classifier to adaptively identify which tokens carry the signal. The model outputs a binary label via a sign function applied to a weighted sum of attention-aggregated token features.

## Method

The paper analyzes a single-layer attention classifier A_{q,w,b}(X) = sign(⟨Xᵀ softmax(βXq), w⟩ + b), where the query vector q determines softmax attention weights that dynamically reweight tokens. The training protocol splits data into D₀ and D₁: two gradient steps on D₀ (first on b, w, q; second on q alone) suffice to align q with the hidden signal ξ, after which w and b are fully optimized via ERM on D₁. In the high-dimensional limit (d, n → ∞ with n/d fixed), the authors derive exact asymptotic expressions for test error and training loss as solutions to self-consistent equations, and show that in the long-sequence limit attention achieves vanishing error at signal strength θ = O(log L), whereas linear classifiers require θ = Ω(√L). The analysis extends to arbitrary convex losses (logistic, quadratic) and includes a capacity characterization comparing the maximal separable dataset size across attention and linear baselines.

## Results

In the long-sequence limit (L→∞, R=Θ(1)), the attention model achieves vanishing test error when the signal strength θ grows only logarithmically (θ = Ω(log L)), whereas both the pooled and vectorized linear classifiers require θ = Ω(√L) for perfect generalization (Theorem 2 vs. Theorem 1/Proposition 1). In the high-dimensional finite-L regime (d,n→∞ with n/d=α fixed), Theorem 4 provides an exact asymptotic characterization of the attention model's test error after just two gradient steps on the query weights followed by full ERM on the readout. Numerical experiments (Fig. 1, d=1000, L=10, R=1, θ=5, π=0.5) confirm that the attention model achieves substantially lower test error than both linear baselines across all sample complexities α₁, with theoretical curves matching simulations to within one standard deviation over 8 trials.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the single-layer attention model against two linear baselines: a vectorized linear classifier (flattening the L×d input) and a pooled linear classifier (averaging tokens before a linear readout). In the oracle (infinite-parameter) setting, the attention model requires θ = Ω(log L) for zero test error while both linear classifiers require θ = Ω(√L), an exponential separation in the required signal strength. In the finite-sample high-dimensional regime, the attention model achieves lower test error than both linear baselines for moderate and large query-signal alignment s_q, but can underperform the linear classifiers when s_q is too small (e.g., insufficient pretraining data α₀ or poor hyperparameter choices). In terms of capacity (max dataset size perfectly separable with logistic loss), the probed setting yields α_vec* > α_A* > α_pool*, where the vectorized classifier's advantage is attributed to operating in an L-times higher-dimensional space, while the attention model's advantage over the pooled classifier reflects its adaptive token reweighting. The two linear classifiers converge to a common infinite-sample test error limit, consistent with their shared oracle behavior.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of theoretical work on sparse token tasks (Oymak et al., 2023; Marion et al., 2024; Sanford et al., 2023) by simultaneously incorporating signal weakness (additive noise corrupting the signal) and signal rarity (signal absent from negative samples), challenges that prior works addressed separately or not at all. It also generalizes the loss function from the squared loss used in most prior analyses to arbitrary convex losses (including logistic loss), and provides exact asymptotic characterizations of test error and capacity rather than the looser bounds or oracle-based protocols in earlier work. The high-dimensional asymptotic machinery builds on the generalized linear model framework of Loureiro et al. (2021) and the feature-learning analysis of Ba et al. (2022), adapted to the non-linear attention feature map.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a rigorous, constant-level explanation for why attention mechanisms outperform linear classifiers on sparse detection tasks: the required signal strength scales as log(L) rather than √(L), an exponential gap that quantifies the value of adaptive token selection. The finding that merely two gradient steps suffice for the query vector to acquire meaningful signal alignment offers a concrete mechanistic account of how attention 'learns to look' at the right tokens. Limitations include the single-layer, single-head model, the high-dimensional (d→∞) limit, and the simplified training protocol; open questions include whether these separations persist in multi-layer, multi-head architectures and under more realistic training dynamics, and how the capacity results extend to deeper networks.
