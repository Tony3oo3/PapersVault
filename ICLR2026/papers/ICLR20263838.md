## Problem

Existing conformal inference methods for filtering false claims from LLM responses are either overly conservative (BCI uses a single global threshold, discarding most true claims) or rely on adaptive error rates and linear threshold functions that cannot capture complex semantic group structures (CCI). Both approaches define conformity scores based on a single worst-case claim, making calibration highly sensitive to estimation error and further reducing retention. The paper asks whether conformal inference can be reformulated to simultaneously achieve group-conditional coverage guarantees and substantially higher true-claim retention in high-stakes domains.

## Input / Output

The method takes a prompt P, a set of atomic claims C decomposed from a fixed LLM response, and verbalized factuality-scores in [0,1] produced by an ensemble of M LLMs (Llama-3.3-70B, Qwen-2.5-72B, DeepSeek-V3) queried per (prompt, claim) pair. It outputs a filtered subset F_{n,α}(P,C) ⊆ C containing only factual claims, accompanied by a group-conditional coverage guarantee P(F ⊆ A | g(P,C)=k) ≥ 1−α for each of K user-defined groups. All multiplicative score computations are performed in log-space via a complement-based transform to avoid numerical underflow, and the grouping function g is an arbitrary measurable function (e.g., medical intent, false-claim risk index, Wikipedia view count) rather than a parametric model.

## Method

MACI reformulates conformal inference around a multiplicative conformity score: claims are ordered by decreasing estimated factuality-score, and the document-level score is the cumulative product of retained claim scores, so the filtering threshold τ controls the joint probability that all retained claims are factual. This replaces the single worst-case score used by BCI/CCI, making calibration far less sensitive to individual estimation errors. Group-conditional (Mondrian-style) calibration computes a separate conformal quantile per group, guaranteeing finite-sample, distribution-free coverage within each group. To improve score quality, MACI optimizes a weighted multi-LLM ensemble that minimizes empirical FPR subject to a TPR ≥ 1−δ tolerance, and Theorem 3 provides the first retention-gap analysis in conformal inference, bounding the retention loss by a polynomial in the MSE of the score estimator under a margin condition. The full pipeline (Algorithm 2) runs per-group ensemble optimization, per-group conformal calibration, and multiplicative filtering at test time.

## Results

On MedLFQA at 80% target coverage, MACI achieves 0.80 coverage with 0.71 retention, compared to BCI's 0.80/0.06 and CCI's 0.81/0.56. On WikiBio at the same setting, MACI reaches 0.81 coverage with 0.43 retention versus BCI's 0.81/0.02 and CCI's 0.79/0.19. On ExpertQA at 80%, MACI attains 0.80 coverage with 0.45 retention, outperforming BCI (0.91/0.13) and CCI (0.85/0.18). In wall-clock time for 500 WikiBio test samples, MACI completes in 598.98 s versus CCI's 1643.91 s, a 2.7× speedup.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against BCI (Mohri & Hashimoto, ICML 2024), MACI wins decisively on retention: on MedLFQA at 80% coverage, MACI retains 71% of claims versus BCI's 6%, while both achieve the target coverage. BCI's single global threshold and single-extremal-claim conformity score make it overly conservative and prone to group-specific under/over-coverage (e.g., 0.73 coverage on MedLFQA High-risk group at 80% target). Against CCI (Cherian et al., NeurIPS 2024), MACI achieves comparable or better coverage while retaining substantially more claims (e.g., 0.71 vs 0.56 on MedLFQA at 80%); CCI's adaptive α relaxes the fixed-guarantee requirement, and its linear threshold function struggles with complex semantic groupings, leading to undercoverage in several subgroups. Against sampling-based methods (SelfCheck, FSC-KG), MACI achieves the lowest wall-clock time (598.98 s vs. CCI's 1643.91 s for 500 WikiBio samples) by using a single-pass ensemble scoring rather than repeated response generation. The multi-LLM ensemble component further improves retention over a single-LLM MACI variant, confirming that ensemble quality directly translates to higher claim preservation (Figure 3c).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the conformal inference framework for LLM false-claim filtering initiated by Mohri and Hashimoto (2024, BCI) and Cherian et al. (2024, CCI), departing from both in two key ways: it replaces the single worst-case claim score with a multiplicative (cumulative product) conformity score, and it replaces CCI's adaptive per-sample error rate with fixed group-conditional (Mondrian) calibration. The adaptive threshold calibration mechanism draws on Romano et al. (2020), while the multi-LLM ensemble for improving factuality-score quality is motivated by a new retention-gap theorem (Theorem 3) that, to the authors' knowledge, is the first theoretical analysis linking estimator error to claim retention in conformal inference.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MACI offers a practical, distribution-free guarantee for LLM factuality in high-stakes domains (medicine, law) where a fixed error rate is non-negotiable, and it substantially outperforms BCI in retention (e.g., 0.71 vs. 0.06 on MedLFQA at 80% coverage) while matching CCI's coverage without relaxing α. Open questions include how the method behaves when group sizes are very small (calibration becomes conservative), whether the margin condition in Theorem 3 holds in practice for real LLM score distributions, and how the ensemble cost scales with the number of base LLMs. The covariate-shift variant (MACI-DRE) is a useful but lightweight fix; a more principled treatment of distributional drift in deployed systems remains open.
