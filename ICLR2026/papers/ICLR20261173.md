## Problem

In Euclidean space, flow matching objectives that predict endpoints, velocities, or noise are equivalent up to affine transformations, but on curved Riemannian manifolds this equivalence breaks down because tangent spaces vary across points and curvature introduces higher-order deviations. Existing Riemannian Flow Matching (RFM) uses a velocity-based loss that, as the paper shows via Jacobi field analysis, is only a first-order linear approximation that discards curvature-dependent information. The paper asks how endpoint-based (variational) and velocity-based (vanilla) objectives differ on curved manifolds and demonstrates that the variational formulation provides a strictly stronger learning signal.

## Input / Output

The model takes a Gaussian noise sample (either intrinsic on the manifold or extrinsic in the ambient Euclidean space) and, for application-specific tasks, conditioning information such as MOF building blocks or a target protein residue count. It outputs points on Riemannian manifolds: for MOF generation, roto-translations (SO(3) rotations + R³ translations) and lattice parameters; for protein backbone generation, sequences of SE(3) frames (quaternion rotations + zero-mean α-carbon coordinates). The method requires the target manifold to admit closed-form geodesics (e.g., Sⁿ, Hⁿ, Tⁿ, SO(n)), and the model predicts endpoint distributions μ_t(x) whose loss is the squared geodesic distance to the target, rather than predicting velocity fields directly.

## Method

RG-VFM extends Variational Flow Matching to Riemannian manifolds by parameterizing the variational posterior q_t(x₁|x) as a Riemannian Gaussian distribution, whose log-likelihood on a homogeneous manifold with closed-form geodesics reduces to the squared geodesic distance between the predicted endpoint μ_t(x) and the target x₁. Two variants are defined: an intrinsic version (prior on the manifold, geodesic interpolation via exp/log maps) and an extrinsic version (prior in ambient Rⁿ, linear interpolation), the latter avoiding per-step exp/log computations while retaining a geometry-aware loss. The key theoretical contribution is a Jacobi field reformulation showing that the RFM velocity loss equals ‖D_τJ(0)‖² (the linear term) while the RG-VFM endpoint loss equals ‖J(1)‖² (the full field), so their difference is a curvature-dependent functional involving the Riemannian curvature tensor R and its covariant derivatives. In practice, this means endpoint prediction directly minimizes geodesic distances and captures higher-order geometric structure that velocity regression misses. The method is applied by variationalizing the rotational (SO(3)/quaternion) component of MOFFlow and ReQFlow, replacing their velocity-matching loss with a geodesic-distance endpoint loss while keeping all other architecture and training details unchanged.

## Results

On protein backbone generation (PDB, lengths 50–300), V-ReQFlow achieves the best designability fraction (0.980) and V-QFlow achieves the best scRMSD (0.923±0.787), both outperforming their vanilla counterparts (ReQFlow: 0.972/1.071; QFlow: 0.936/1.163). On MOF structure prediction, V-MOFFlow with 5 samples reaches 50.14% match rate at stol=0.5 (vs. 46.97% for reproduced MOFFlow) and 0.4384 RMSE (vs. 0.4603). On synthetic spherical/hyperbolic checkerboard benchmarks, RG-VFM-𝓜 achieves the highest Coverage (84.21% sphere, 68.38% hyperboloid) while RG-VFM-ℝ³ achieves the lowest manifold distance (0.010 sphere, 0.021 hyperboloid).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between vanilla (velocity-based) and variational (endpoint-based) objectives applied to the same architecture. V-QFlow outperforms QFlow (paper) by +3.2 points in designability (0.968 vs. 0.936) and reduces scRMSD from 1.163 to 0.923; V-ReQFlow similarly beats ReQFlow (paper) in designability (0.980 vs. 0.972) and scRMSD (0.961 vs. 1.071). On MOF prediction, V-MOFFlow surpasses reproduced MOFFlow at stol=0.5 (50.14% vs. 46.97% MR) and stol=1.0 (97.18% vs. 95.82% MR), with lower RMSE in both settings. Against external baselines, V-ReQFlow matches or exceeds RFDiffusion, Genie2, and FoldFlow2 on designability while using far fewer integration steps (500 vs. 50–1000). On synthetic benchmarks, RG-VFM-𝓜 achieves the best Coverage on both sphere (84.21%) and hyperboloid (68.38%), while RG-VFM-ℝ³ achieves the lowest distance-to-manifold (0.010/0.021), confirming that the variational + geometric combination captures both distributional sharpness and manifold geometry better than CFM, VFM, or RFM alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two independent extensions of Flow Matching: Variational Flow Matching (VFM), which reframes training as posterior inference over trajectories with flexible variational posteriors, and Riemannian Flow Matching (RFM), which extends FM to manifolds with closed-form geodesics. The key departure is showing that the near-equivalence between endpoint prediction (VFM) and velocity prediction (FM) that holds in Euclidean space breaks down on curved manifolds, and formally characterizing this gap via Jacobi fields as a curvature-dependent penalty absent from RFM. Practically, the paper variationalizes the rotational losses of existing hybrid models (MOFFlow, ReQFlow) that already used variational objectives for Euclidean parameters but vanilla RFM for non-Euclidean ones, unifying the treatment under a single geometric variational framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Jacobi field analysis provides the first clean theoretical justification for why endpoint-based (variational) training should outperform velocity-based training on manifolds, resolving a practical heuristic that prior works observed empirically without explanation. The method is minimally invasive—replacing a velocity-matching loss with a geodesic-distance loss on the rotational component—making it easy to adopt in existing pipelines for molecular and protein generation. A key limitation is the requirement for homogeneous manifolds with closed-form geodesics (spheres, hyperbolic spaces, SO(3), tori), which covers most practical cases but excludes more complex geometries. Open questions include whether the curvature-dependent advantage persists for manifolds without closed-form geodesics, and whether the L1 (Riemannian Laplace) variant hinted at in the paper could yield further gains in hyperbolic settings.
