## Problem

Real-world LLM deployments generate abundant implicit user dissatisfaction (DSAT) signals—refinements, corrections, and expressed preferences—while explicit satisfaction feedback is scarce (e.g., 12% DSAT vs. 5% SAT in WildFeedback). Existing preference learning methods either depend on costly human annotations or assume plentiful positive responses, leaving the most naturally occurring and informative signal (dissatisfaction) largely unexploited for scalable post-training.

## Input / Output

DRIFT consumes DSAT-annotated prompt-response pairs extracted from real-world conversations (WildFeedback) or synthetic LLM-labeled data (UltraFeedback), along with a current policy model and a frozen reference model. The rejected response is a fixed, authentic DSAT reply from the dataset, while the chosen response is dynamically sampled from the evolving policy at each iteration using a prompt that includes the full conversation context plus an explicit improvement instruction. The output is updated model parameters after K iterations of one-epoch DPO training, with a warm-start stage on 491 curated DSAT→SAT seed pairs providing the initial aligned policy.

## Method

DRIFT (Dissatisfaction-Refined Iterative preFerence Training) anchors each preference pair with a genuine real-world DSAT negative and samples the positive from the current policy, then optimizes via the standard DPO loss. The key design insight is twofold: (1) authentic dissatisfaction reflects concrete deployment failure modes, providing more informative and reliable negative supervision than synthetically constructed or fixed positives, and (2) iteratively resampling fresh positives from the evolving policy maintains a non-vanishing margin between chosen and rejected responses, preventing the gradient collapse that plagues methods like SPIN where chosen and rejected responses become increasingly similar. Theoretically, the paper proves that under a positive-mass improvement event, DRIFT's expected gradient stays bounded away from zero (Lemma 1) and a small DPO step improves true utility (Theorem 1), while at SPIN fixed points on a finite SAT catalogue the signal is controlled by catalogue log-density-ratio variance and can quantitatively degenerate (Proposition 1). Empirically, DRIFT also preserves exploratory capacity, covering a broader set of high-reward semantic regions than SPIN or IterDPO as measured by UMAP-based reward topography analysis.

## Results

On the real-world WildFeedback dataset (Full Setting), DRIFT achieves a WildBench Task Score of 51.69 (7B) and 58.30 (14B), representing gains of +6.23% and +5.97% over base models respectively, and AlpacaEval2 win rates of 46.64% (7B) and 45.33% (14B), up +8.95% and +12.11%. On the synthetic UltraFeedback dataset, DRIFT reaches 59.27 Task Score (14B, +7.61%) and 48.94% AlpacaEval2 win rate (14B, +12.29%). Notably, the 14B DRIFT model (58.30 Task Score) surpasses GPT-4o-mini (57.14) on WildBench. DRIFT also demonstrates long-horizon stability, sustaining improvements through 4 iterations before plateauing, while SPIN and IterDPO degrade after iteration 1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DRIFT consistently outperforms both SPIN and IterDPO across all metrics in both the Controlled (~4k) and Full (11k) WildFeedback settings. SPIN degrades severely with iterations—its 7B WildBench Task Score drops from 42.86 (iter1) to 37.86 (iter2), falling below even the base model—due to its reliance on a fixed SAT catalogue that becomes stale. IterDPO performs better than SPIN but still lags DRIFT: in the Full Setting, IterDPO's 14B Task Score declines from 52.34 to 51.38 across iterations, while DRIFT improves from 57.27 to 58.30. DRIFT's controlled setting (4k samples) already matches or exceeds IterDPO's full-setting performance, demonstrating the efficiency of dissatisfaction-anchored learning. On UltraFeedback, DRIFT achieves additional gains of +2.14 (7B) and +4.49 (14B) Task Score over the best SPIN/IterDPO results, and +6.51% (7B) / +6.10% (14B) on AlpacaEval2 win rate. The 14B DRIFT model surpasses GPT-4o-mini on WildBench Task Score (58.30 vs. 57.14), a result neither baseline achieves.

## Relation to Prior Work

DRIFT builds directly on DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) as its optimization backbone but repositions the data construction strategy: rather than requiring curated positive examples (as in SPIN, which fixes SAT responses as chosen) or reward-model-ranked self-generated pairs (as in IterDPO), it anchors each training pair on a real-world DSAT negative and samples the positive dynamically from the evolving policy. This departs from the self-improvement line (SPIN, Self-Rewarding LMs, CREAM) that the authors identify as suffering from vanishing preference margins as chosen and rejected responses converge. The approach also extends the WildFeedback/WildChat-1M data ecosystem (Shi et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2024) by exploiting the natural DSAT-over-SAT imbalance (~12% vs ~5%) as a feature rather than a limitation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DRIFT offers a practical, low-overhead recipe for post-training that exploits the most abundant signal in deployed LLM systems—user dissatisfaction—without requiring gold-standard positives, reward models, or additional human annotation. The consistent gains across real-world (WildFeedback) and synthetic (UltraFeedback) settings, both model scales (7B/14B), and a different architecture (Gemma-3-12B) suggest broad applicability, and the 14B model surpassing GPT-4o-mini on WildBench is a concrete milestone for open-weight post-training. Open questions include whether the DSAT signal remains informative as the policy improves (i.e., whether the fixed negatives eventually become too easy), how the method behaves at 70B+ scale, and whether the exploratory-capacity advantage translates to downstream task diversity in production settings.
