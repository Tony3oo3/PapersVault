## Problem

Protein language models exhibit counterintuitive scaling behavior: larger models in the same family (e.g., ESM-2 15B) often underperform smaller ones (e.g., 650M) on downstream tasks, and embeddings across scales are disconnected, lacking the Matryoshka property where prefixes of a larger embedding are themselves functional representations. This makes model selection unreliable and prevents the 'embed once, reuse prefixes' paradigm that works in NLP.

## Input / Output

The method takes a protein sequence and invokes multiple ESM-2 models of increasing size (8M through 15B) to produce a single reverse-distilled embedding whose first k_r dimensions are exactly the smaller model's output and whose remaining dimensions encode orthogonal residual information from the larger model. Training the decomposition requires only 1,000 sequences from UniRef50 (with ≤30% identity to evaluation sets), treating all residue positions as samples for the linear regression. At inference, the Matryoshka structure means any prefix of the embedding is a valid representation at the corresponding model scale, enabling adaptive compute without re-embedding.

## Method

Reverse distillation decomposes a larger model's representation space into orthogonal subspaces anchored by a smaller model from the same family. Concretely, it learns a linear mapping W* from the smaller model's embeddings to the larger model's via principal component regression (applying the Johnstone threshold to discard noise PCs), computes residuals R = H_p − H_r·W*, and extracts the top (k_p − k_r) right singular vectors of R via SVD as the residual subspace basis V_res. The final embedding is H_rd = [H_r, R·V_res], which is proven MSE-optimal among all k_p-dimensional representations preserving the smaller model's embeddings in their first k_r coordinates (by Eckart-Young). Chaining this decomposition across the full ESM-2 hierarchy (8M→35M→150M→650M→3B→15B) yields Matryoshka-style embeddings where each prefix is a valid reverse-distilled representation at that scale, restoring monotonic scaling with no model retraining.

## Results

On ProteinGym DMS benchmarks (28 single-mutation datasets), reverse-distilled ESM-2 15B (rd.15B) achieves a mean Spearman correlation of 0.904 (±0.037), outperforming the baseline 15B (0.899 ± 0.036) and all smaller models. For 2-mutation datasets, rd.15B reaches 0.720 (±0.120) vs. 0.714 (±0.115) for baseline 15B. On protein property prediction (Table 4), rd.15B achieves the best AUPR on 4 of 5 tasks (SSP Q3: 0.861, SSP Q8: 0.431, MIB: 0.901, R2/R1: 0.468). Critically, reverse distillation restores monotonic scaling: rd.15B outperforms rd.3B on 85.71% of 1-mutation and 75% of 2-mutation datasets, whereas baseline 15B only outperforms 3B on 92.86% and 85.71% respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares reverse-distilled ESM-2 models (rd.650M, rd.3B, rd.15B) against their non-decomposed ESM-2 counterparts (650M, 3B, 15B) from Lin et al. (2023). On ProteinGym 1-mutation DMS datasets, rd.650M beats 650M on 71.43% of datasets, rd.3B beats 3B on 85.71%, and rd.15B beats 15B on 67.86%. The most important comparison is scaling consistency: baseline 15B outperforms 3B on only 92.86% of 1-mutation and 85.71% of 2-mutation datasets, while rd.15B outperforms rd.3B on 85.71% and 75% respectively—showing that reverse distillation makes larger models more reliably better. On protein property prediction (Table 4), rd.15B achieves the best AUPR on SSP Q3 (0.861 vs. 0.845 for 15B), SSP Q8 (0.431 vs. 0.418), MIB (0.901 vs. 0.900), and R2/R1 (0.468 vs. 0.368), but slightly trails on LOC (0.743 vs. 0.745). Inference overhead is modest: rd.15B requires six model invocations but only 1.70× the time of baseline 15B.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper inverts the traditional knowledge distillation paradigm (Hinton et al., 2015) by using smaller models as structural anchors to decompose larger model representations, rather than compressing large models into small ones. The Matryoshka-style nested embedding structure draws on Kusupati et al. (2022), while the orthogonal subspace extraction via SVD echoes continual learning methods like o-LoRA (Wang et al., 2023) and Adaptive SVD (Nayak et al., 2025), though here applied task-agnostically across a model family rather than across tasks. The work is motivated by a well-documented PLM scaling failure (Li et al., 2024; Notin et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024) and positions itself as a post-hoc, training-free fix that requires no architectural modification or retraining of the underlying models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that a purely linear, training-free decomposition restores monotonic scaling in PLMs suggests the information needed for consistent performance is already present in large models but is obscured by feature entanglement—a reframing that could guide future PLM training objectives. Practical limitations include the requirement for same-family models with strictly increasing embedding dimensions, the multi-model inference overhead (1.5–1.7×), and the restriction to linear mappings that may leave nonlinear cross-scale relationships unexploited. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to model families without natural dimension progression, whether LoRA-based single-pass inference can eliminate the multi-model cost, and whether the bias-variance interpretation (small models encode shared features, large models add task-irrelevant variance) holds beyond the ESM-2 family.
