## Problem

Existing Hopfield networks and associative memory models rely on fixed, pre-defined similarity measures (e.g., dot product) and evaluate retrieval success via proximity (ε-retrieval), which cannot guarantee that the retrieved pattern is the one most strongly associated with the query. This paper identifies that correctness is inherently generative: a query is a variant of a stored pattern, and the ideal similarity should approximate the likelihood of that pattern generating the query under a context-dependent variant distribution—something no fixed metric can achieve.

## Input / Output

The method takes a query vector x ∈ R^d (a noisy, masked, or biased variant of some stored pattern) and a memory matrix Ξ ∈ R^{d×N} of N stored patterns. For each stored pattern, it computes a d-dimensional similarity footprint vector (one entry per subspace dimensionality k=1..d) by sorting dimension-wise similarities and taking cumulative sums, then applies a learned weight vector to collapse this into a scalar score. The final output is a retrieved pattern y = Ξ·softmax(s(Ξ,x)), where s(Ξ,x) is the vector of adaptive similarity scores across all N memories, effectively a probability-weighted readout.

## Method

The paper introduces a variant distribution V(Ξ) that models how queries are generated from stored patterns, and formalizes correct retrieval as maximizing the posterior p_V(ξ|x), which reduces to maximizing the likelihood p_V(x|ξ). To approximate this intractable likelihood, it proposes a similarity footprint: for each k from 1 to d, the k-optimal similarity (maximum similarity over all k-dimensional subspaces) is computed, and for decomposable base similarities this reduces to summing the top-k sorted dimension-wise similarities in O(d log d). The adaptive similarity is a learnable linear combination w^T·ftpt(ξ,x) of the footprint, aggregated across multiple base similarities (negative squared Euclidean distance and dot product) with learnable scalar weights β. Training minimizes cross-entropy between the softmax of adaptive similarity scores and the true variant likelihood using samples drawn from V(Ξ). Theoretically, the paper proves optimal correct retrieval for noisy, masked, and biased variants, and shows the energy E(x) = −lse(s(Ξ,x)) is monotonically decreasing, convergent, and bounded from below even as ‖ξ‖₂→∞.

## Results

On the primary memory retrieval task with 64-dim synthetic patterns under mixed corruption (masking + noise + bias), A-Hop achieves 72.4% accuracy at difficulty 0.4 and 84.9% at difficulty 0.7 on MNIST, outperforming the best baseline (M-Hop/U2-Hop) by 20.4 and 18.8 percentage points respectively. On tabular classification, A-Hop leads all associative memory baselines across 5 datasets (e.g., 73.15% on Heart vs. 64.73% for U2-Hop) and surpasses Extra Trees, Random Forest, and AdaBoost on all five, trailing XGBoost by only 0.55% on average. On image classification (CIFAR-10/100, Tiny ImageNet) and multiple instance learning, A-Hop consistently achieves the highest accuracy/AUC-ROC among all Hopfield variants, e.g., 56.37% on CIFAR-10 vs. 54.89% for U-Hop.

## Baselines & Comparisons

A-Hop decisively outperforms all Hopfield baselines on the core retrieval task: at the hardest MNIST setting (difficulty 0.7), A-Hop reaches 84.9% accuracy while M-Hop and U2-Hop drop to ~66%, U-Hop to 52.6%, and N-Hop to just 17.6%. On tabular classification, A-Hop beats M-Hop and U2-Hop by 4.6–8.4% across all five datasets, and also surpasses Extra Trees, Random Forest, and AdaBoost on every dataset, though XGBoost retains a narrow 0.55% average edge (e.g., 86.40% vs. 86.34% on Adult). For image classification, A-Hop improves over U-Hop by 1.5–2.4% (CIFAR-10: 56.37% vs. 54.89%; CIFAR-100: 29.04% vs. 28.77%) and over M-Hop by 5.1–4.4%. In multiple instance learning, A-Hop leads S-Hop and M-Hop on all four benchmarks (e.g., Fox: 67.53% AUC vs. 64.33% for S-Hop; UCSB: 89.35% vs. 88.15% for M-Hop). The largest relative gains appear under masking and bias corruption, where fixed-similarity baselines collapse below 20% accuracy while A-Hop maintains near-perfect retrieval, confirming that adaptive similarity is critical when the variant distribution deviates from pure Gaussian noise.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Modern Hopfield Network lineage (Ramsauer et al., 2021) and the unified three-step retrieval framework (Millidge et al., 2022) by replacing the fixed, pre-defined similarity measure (typically dot product) with a learnable adaptive similarity built on a multi-scale 'similarity footprint' descriptor. The key conceptual departure is reframing retrieval correctness through a generative lens: rather than evaluating retrieval by proximity (ε-retrieval), the paper defines correctness via a variant distribution that models how queries are generated from stored patterns, making the ideal similarity a likelihood function. This positions A-Hop as a principled response to the observation that all prior Hopfield variants (D-Hop, E-Hop, M-Hop, N-Hop, S-Hop, U-Hop) anchor their energy and dynamics to a task-agnostic similarity that cannot adapt to context-dependent corruption patterns.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The reframe from ε-retrieval to correct retrieval under a variant distribution provides a more meaningful and rigorous evaluation criterion for associative memories, exposing a fundamental limitation of all prior models: fixed similarities cannot distinguish irrelevance from missing data (masking) or learn systematic offsets (bias). The adaptive similarity mechanism is general and could potentially transfer to other content-addressable or attention-based systems. However, the theoretical optimality results require specific (sometimes discrete) weight configurations, and the continuous parameter-efficient version only achieves high-probability correctness rather than guaranteed optimality. Open questions include how the approach scales to more complex or unknown variant distributions, whether the footprint computation (O(d log d)) remains practical at very high dimensions, and whether the learnable similarity can be integrated into end-to-end training without the separate variant-distribution sampling step.
