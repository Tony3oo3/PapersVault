## Problem

When differentiable simulators contain discontinuities (contacts, friction), 1st-order policy gradients can exhibit 'empirical bias'—appearing low-variance in finite samples while remaining highly inaccurate. The existing fix, AoBG, detects this by building confidence intervals around the notoriously noisy REINFORCE estimator, requiring task-specific hyperparameter tuning (γ spanning 5×10⁻³ to 10⁸) and showing poor sample efficiency. This paper asks whether empirical bias is truly the primary obstacle, or whether variance control alone suffices in practical robotics control.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a batch of N parallel rollouts from a differentiable simulator, with per-step state observations, sampled actions, and policy parameters (Gaussian mean θ with fixed σ). It produces a composite gradient computed elementwise at each (time-step, actor, action-dimension) tuple by mixing 0th-order and 1st-order components, which is then backpropagated through the policy network via a vector-Jacobian product to yield the parameter update. The DDCG variant additionally evaluates a scalar inequality from function-value variance and gradient variance within the same batch to gate the 1st-order term, requiring no extra simulator calls.

## Method

The paper contributes two methods. DDCG replaces AoBG's REINFORCE-based confidence interval with a lightweight statistical test (Eq. 14) that compares the empirical 1st-order gradient variance against a quadratic-model proxy built from function-value variance; if the inequality holds (indicating local smoothness and reliable variance estimation), IVW weights are applied, otherwise the method falls back to the 0th-order estimator—needing only a single hyperparameter c (fixed at 0.3) rather than AoBG's task-specific γ. IVW-H is a per-step, per-action-dimension inverse-variance weighting that computes empirical variances across parallel actors at each (t, a) pair, forms elementwise composite gradients, and backpropagates through the policy network without any explicit discontinuity detection. The key empirical finding is that on MuJoCo-style benchmarks (CartPole, Hopper, Ant with elevated contact stiffness), IVW-H matches or exceeds GIPPO and AoBG, indicating that stepwise variance control—not bias detection—is the dominant practical concern. Theoretically, DDCG's test statistic is O(d) times more precise than AoBG's REINFORCE-based statistic in d-dimensional settings, as confirmed by Monte-Carlo experiments.

## Results

On the MuJoCo-style continuous control benchmarks (CartPole, Hopper, Ant with elevated contact stiffness), IVW-H attains the best episodic returns on Ant and further improves upon standard IVW on Hopper, while GIPPO fails to optimize on Hopper. In the explicit empirical-bias regimes (Ball with Wall, Pushing, Friction, Tennis), DDCG with a unified hyperparameter c=0.3 matches or exceeds AoBG's performance, whereas AoBG requires task-specific γ values spanning [5×10⁻³, 10⁸]. DDCG's discontinuity-detection statistic is shown to be O(d) times more sample-efficient than AoBG's REINFORCE-based confidence interval (Appendix D, Table 1: ratio ≈ d/√2). On CartPole, all 1st-order-based methods (1st-order, AoBG, IVW, IVW-H, GIPPO) converge to similar final rewards, while 0th-order underperforms.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against AoBG (Suh et al., 2022), DDCG achieves comparable or better performance on all discontinuity tasks (Ball with Wall, Pushing, Friction, Tennis) while using a single fixed hyperparameter c=0.3 instead of AoBG's task-specific γ that ranges from 5×10⁻³ to 10⁸; in small-sample regimes (N=10, N=3), AoBG becomes overly conservative (α→0) while DDCG continues to exploit 1st-order gradients. Against standard IVW (Parmas et al., 2018), IVW-H consistently improves upon it on Ant and Hopper with elevated contact, and the sensitivity analysis (Figure 16) shows AoBG cannot outperform IVW in these high-contact settings, indicating variance rather than bias is the dominant bottleneck. Against GIPPO (Son et al., 2023), IVW-H matches or exceeds GIPPO on Ant and Hopper, while GIPPO fails entirely on Hopper; on CartPole both reach similar final rewards. Against pure 0th-order (REINFORCE) and 1st-order (reparameterization) baselines, both composite methods (DDCG, IVW-H) clearly outperform either estimator alone in discontinuous settings, and on MuJoCo tasks the 0th-order baseline underperforms on CartPole and Ant while 1st-order alone struggles on Hopper.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two threads: the composite gradient mixing framework from Parmas et al. (2018, 2023), which introduced inverse-variance weighting (IVW) to blend 0th- and 1st-order estimators, and the discontinuity-detection mechanism of AoBG (Suh et al., 2022), which gates IVW using confidence intervals around the REINFORCE estimator. DDCG departs from AoBG by replacing its noisy REINFORCE-based confidence interval with a lightweight variance-based statistical test that requires only function values and gradient variances, while IVW-H departs from parameter-space IVW implementations (e.g., GIPPO) by operating per-step and per-action-dimension, mirroring the Total Propagation X formulation. The paper's central repositioning is empirical: it argues that the 'empirical bias' phenomenon motivating AoBG is not the dominant practical obstacle in standard robotics control, shifting emphasis from bias detection to variance stabilization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a simple per-step IVW implementation (IVW-H) matches or exceeds GIPPO and AoBG on MuJoCo-style tasks with elevated contact stiffness provides practitioners with a clear, low-complexity recipe for differentiable-simulation policy optimization, reducing the need for task-specific hyperparameter tuning. DDCG's robustness across a wide range of its single hyperparameter c ∈ [0.1, 0.9] makes it a practical drop-in replacement for AoBG in explicitly discontinuous settings. However, the boundary between regimes where bias detection is necessary and where variance control suffices remains poorly characterized; the paper tests only three MuJoCo tasks with artificially elevated contact stiffness, and the 'high-contact' modification may not capture the full range of real-world nonsmoothness. Future work should delineate this boundary more sharply and test on broader task suites including deformable bodies and multi-contact scenarios.
