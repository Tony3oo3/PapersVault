## Problem

Prior works adapting video models for robot policies either require multiple training stages with separate action modules (e.g., inverse dynamics models or action diffusers) or train unified video-action architectures from scratch, failing to leverage pretrained spatiotemporal priors. This paper asks whether a large pretrained video diffusion model can be converted into an effective robot policy through a single stage of post-training with no architectural modifications, while also enabling model-based planning via future state and value predictions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262427/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes multi-view camera images (e.g., two third-person and one wrist camera), robot proprioceptive state (14 joint angles for ALOHA), and a textual task description, all tokenized into a unified latent diffusion sequence of 11 latent frames. Outputs include a robot action chunk (16–50 timesteps depending on platform), future state images from all cameras, future proprioception, and a scalar state value (expected cumulative reward in [0,1]). Non-image modalities are encoded by normalizing to [-1,+1] and duplicating the low-dimensional vector to fill a full H'×W'×C' latent volume, making them indistinguishable from image latents to the diffusion transformer.

## Method

The core mechanism is latent frame injection: new modalities (actions, proprioception, values) are inserted as additional latent frames directly into the video model's existing latent diffusion sequence, requiring zero architectural changes to the Cosmos-Predict2-2B backbone. Training uses a single-stage fine-tuning with a 50/25/25 batch split for policy, world model, and value function objectives, where the conditioning mask (which latent frames remain clean vs. noised) determines which conditional distribution is being learned. The policy and world model are trained with auxiliary targets (jointly predicting future state and value alongside their primary outputs), which ablations show improves performance by 1.5–3.9 points. For planning, a dual-deployment scheme uses the original checkpoint as the policy and a rollout-fine-tuned checkpoint as the planning model, performing best-of-N sampling with ensembled world model and value predictions. The noise schedule is modified from the base model's log-normal to a hybrid log-normal-uniform distribution with more weight on high-σ levels, improving action prediction precision.

## Results

Cosmos Policy achieves state-of-the-art performance across all three evaluation domains: 98.5% average success rate on LIBERO (outperforming the previous best, CogVLA, by 1.1 points), 67.1% on RoboCasa (beating the prior best, FLARE at 66.4%, while using only 50 training demos per task versus 300+ for all competitors), and 93.6% average score on real-world ALOHA bimanual manipulation tasks. With model-based planning using refined world model and value function predictions from 648 policy rollouts, Cosmos Policy achieves a further 12.5-point average score increase on the two most challenging ALOHA tasks (multimodal grasping and high-precision ziploc bag manipulation).

## Baselines & Comparisons

On LIBERO, Cosmos Policy (98.5%) outperforms all 9 compared methods, with the largest margins over Diffusion Policy (+26.1 pts) and Dita (+6.2 pts), and the smallest over CogVLA (+1.1 pts) and OpenVLA-OFT (+1.4 pts). On RoboCasa, Cosmos Policy achieves 67.1% with only 50 demos per task, surpassing GR00T-N1.5 (64.1%, 300 demos), FLARE (66.4%, 300 demos), and Video Policy (66.0%, 300 demos), and dramatically outperforming GR00T-N1 (49.6%) and UVA (50.0%). On the real-world ALOHA tasks, Cosmos Policy scores 93.6% overall, beating π0.5 (88.6%), π0 (77.9%), OpenVLA-OFT+ (62.0%), and Diffusion Policy (33.6%); the gap is most pronounced on the high-precision 'put candy in ziploc bag' task (85.4 vs. 61.5 for π0.5) and the multimodal 'put candies in bowl' task (89.6 vs. 95.2 for π0.5, where π0.5 actually leads slightly). Notably, π0.5 edges out Cosmos Policy on OOD ALOHA trials (92.5 vs. 89.3), suggesting the video-model prior may generalize slightly less to unseen object configurations than the VLA's language-grounded representations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior video-based robot policies (Video Policy, UVA, UWM) that either require multi-stage training with separate action modules or train unified video-action models from scratch without leveraging pretrained video priors. It also contrasts with VLA models (π0.5, OpenVLA-OFT, CogVLA) that fine-tune vision-language backbones trained on static image-text pairs rather than video. The key conceptual departure is the 'latent frame injection' mechanism: encoding robot actions, proprioception, and values as additional latent frames within the existing video diffusion sequence, requiring zero architectural modifications to the pretrained Cosmos-Predict2 model. This unifies policy, world model, and value function in a single architecture, unlike prior works (FLARE, SAILOR, Latent Policy Steering) that maintain separate modules for each.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a pretrained video model can be adapted for precise robot control with no architectural changes—just latent frame injection and single-stage fine-tuning—offers a significantly simpler recipe than prior multi-stage approaches, and the 50-demos-per-task data efficiency on RoboCasa (vs. 300+ for competitors) suggests video spatiotemporal priors are a strong inductive bias for manipulation. The unified policy/world-model/value-function architecture enables model-based planning that yields a 12.5-point improvement on challenging real-world tasks, though this requires collecting rollout data and incurs ~5 seconds inference latency per action chunk. Open questions include accelerating the best-of-N search for dynamic tasks, reducing the rollout data needed for effective planning, and extending the prediction horizon beyond one action chunk for deeper search.
