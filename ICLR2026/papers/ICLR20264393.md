## Problem

Achieving streaming, fine-grained drag-style control over autoregressive video diffusion model outputs remains unsolved: existing drag-based methods are not designed for streaming, and finetuning-based approaches require hundreds to thousands of H100 GPU hours. Two specific obstacles make this difficult in a training-free setting—drag-induced perturbations accumulate in latent space causing severe distribution drift that halts the drag process, and context frames interfere with subsequent generation, producing visually unnatural content.

## Input / Output

The method consumes the latent code of the current frame at denoising timestep T'=3, user-specified drag operations (handle region masks plus instructions specifying translation, deformation, or 2D/3D rotation with target points or rotation centers), cached KV pairs from preceding context frames, and a binary mask delineating non-editable regions. For editing, it outputs a single modified frame; for animation, it outputs a sequence of m new frames generated autoregressively from the dragged frame. The drag operation format distinguishes between editing (one target point per handle) and animation (m discrete target points along a trajectory assigned to subsequent frames), and the method operates at 0.30s additional runtime per frame on an NVIDIA H20 GPU with 4 optimization iterations.

## Method

DragStream is a training-free, plug-and-play approach that performs iterative latent region optimization on autoregressive VDMs (e.g., Self-Forcing, CausVid). It extracts multi-layer DiT features, maps handle regions to target positions via rotation or translation, and updates the latent code using a reconstruction loss (aligning dragged-region features to target) plus a constraint loss (preserving non-editable regions). Two key mechanisms address the identified challenges: (1) Adaptive Distribution Self-Rectification (ADSR) normalizes the latent code after each optimization iteration using the mean and standard deviation of neighboring frames' latents, suppressing cumulative distribution drift; (2) Spatial-Frequency Selective Optimization (SFSO) combines a Switchable Frequency-domain Selection (applying Butterworth filters with randomly sampled cutoff frequencies to cached KV pairs before self-attention, balancing high- and low-frequency context information) with a Criticality-driven Spatial-domain Selection (multiplying back-propagated gradients by a Gaussian map centered on the edited region to prevent background over-optimization). The method requires no training and integrates seamlessly into existing autoregressive VDMs.

## Results

On the REVEL benchmark (204 annotated video clips), DragStream (I=4 iterations) achieves ObjMC of 23.05 and DAI of 0.051, substantially outperforming the no-drag baseline (I=0) which scores ObjMC 90.39 and DAI 0.133. The method also attains the lowest FVD and FID among all compared approaches, indicating superior video quality. At I=4, the additional runtime cost is only 0.13s per frame (0.30s total vs. 0.17s baseline) on an NVIDIA H20 GPU, and even I=2 (0.24s/frame) already yields ObjMC 27.67 and DAI 0.054, clearly surpassing the baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares DragStream against two training-free baselines adapted to the REVEL task: SG-I2V (Namekata et al., 2024) and DragVideo (Deng et al., 2024). DragStream consistently outperforms both baselines on all four evaluation metrics—ObjMC (motion fidelity), DAI (drag editing quality), FVD (video quality), and FID (frame quality)—as reported in the main results (Figure 5). The paper notes that SG-I2V and DragVideo, while following the same latent optimization paradigm, lack the ADSR and SFSO strategies that address latent distribution drift and context interference respectively, leading to drag failures, visual artifacts, and degraded video quality. Finetuning-based methods such as Tora and DragAnything are excluded from direct comparison because they require hundreds to thousands of H100 GPU hours and are not designed for streaming autoregressive generation, making comparison with a training-free, plug-and-play method unfair. Ablation results further confirm that removing either ADSR or SFSO causes significant performance drops, validating that both components are necessary for the observed gains over baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the latent optimization paradigm of DragVideo (Deng et al., ECCV 2024) for drag-based video editing and adopts the drag operation notation from DragNeXt (Zhou et al., 2025), but departs fundamentally by transplanting these techniques into the autoregressive/streaming video generation setting. Prior streaming video generation work (StreamDiffusion, SVDiff, StreamDiT) supports text, camera, or pose conditioning but not fine-grained drag operations, while prior drag-based methods (DragVideo, SG-I2V, Tora, DragAnything) operate on bidirectional VDMs and cannot modify frames on the fly during autoregressive generation. The key conceptual departure is identifying two challenges specific to streaming drag—latent distribution drift from cumulative perturbations and context-frame interference—that have no analogue in non-streaming settings, and addressing them with a training-free ADSR + SFSO pipeline that is plug-and-play with existing autoregressive VDMs like Self-Forcing and CausVid.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By framing streaming drag manipulation as a unified task (REVEL) that encompasses both editing and animation with translation, deformation, and rotation, the paper establishes a standard paradigm that prior fragmented approaches (editing-only or animation-only) could not address. The training-free, plug-and-play nature makes it immediately applicable to any autoregressive VDM without the hundreds of H100 GPU-hours that finetuning-based alternatives require, which is significant for resource-constrained practitioners. Limitations include a small evaluation benchmark (204 clips), failure under physically implausible drag instructions, and reliance on the quality of the underlying autoregressive VDM for long-video consistency. Open questions include whether finetuning-based approaches could surpass the training-free ceiling, how the method scales to multi-object simultaneous dragging, and whether the identified challenges generalize to other streaming control modalities beyond drag.
