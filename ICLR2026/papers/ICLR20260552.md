## Problem

Conventional video diffusion models enforce a single scalar timestep across all frames, rigidly synchronizing their noise evolution and preventing fine-grained temporal control needed for tasks like image-to-video, start-end frame conditioning, and video extension. Existing remedies—task-specific fine-tuning (e.g., Wan-I2V) or autoregressive architectures—require massive compute (≥10M samples, ≥100K cost), destroy pretrained priors through catastrophic forgetting, or restrict generation to unidirectional sequences. The paper asks whether a pretrained T2V model can be adapted to support per-frame timesteps with minimal data and compute while fully preserving its original capabilities.

## Input / Output

For I2V, the method takes a single condition image (encoded to one VAE latent frame) and a text prompt, producing an 81-frame 720p video; the condition frame's timestep is clamped to zero throughout the 10-step Euler sampling so its latent never changes. The same interface generalizes to start-end frames (first and last frames or first and last 4 frames as conditions), video completion (first k and last m frames given), and video extension (first k frames given), all zero-shot by simply setting different subsets of the timestep vector to zero or partial noise levels. T2V generation is preserved by setting all frame timesteps to the same scalar value, recovering the base model's behavior exactly.

## Method

Pusa introduces Vectorized Timestep Adaptation (VTA), which replaces the scalar timestep embedding in a pretrained DiT-based video diffusion model with a per-frame timestep vector τ ∈ [0,1]^N, producing frame-specific modulation parameters (scale, shift, gate) for each DiT block. Training follows the Frame-Aware Flow Matching (FAFM) objective with fully random per-frame timesteps (p_async = 1), requiring only 4K T2V-generated samples and ~$500 of compute via LoRA (rank 512, 900 iterations on 8×80GB GPUs). The adaptation is non-destructive: setting all τ^i equal recovers the base model's outputs exactly, and mechanistic analysis shows parameter drift is confined to self-attention blocks (an order of magnitude smaller than Wan-I2V), surgically injecting temporal correlation without overwriting generative priors. This avoids the combinatorial explosion of the N-dimensional timestep space by leveraging the base model's inherent robustness to timestep asynchronization, requiring only brief fine-tuning to master independent frame evolution.

## Results

On VBench-I2V, Pusa achieves a total score of 87.32 with an I2V sub-score of 94.84, outperforming its direct architectural baseline Wan-I2V (86.86 / 92.90) while using only 4K training samples and ~$500 compute (vs. ≥10M samples and ≥$100K for Wan-I2V). Pusa leads on key I2V fidelity metrics: I2V Background Consistency (99.24 vs. 96.44) and I2V Subject Consistency (97.64 vs. 96.95), as well as Dynamic Degree (52.60 vs. 51.38) and Motion Smoothness (98.49 vs. 97.90). Among all open-source models, Pusa ranks second overall behind Magi-1 (89.28) and Step-Video-TI2V (88.36), but is the top performer when restricted to models adapted from the same Wan-T2V base.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against its direct architectural baseline Wan-I2V (same Wan-T2V base model), Pusa wins on Total (87.32 vs. 86.86), I2V sub-score (94.84 vs. 92.90), I2V Background Consistency (99.24 vs. 96.44, a +2.8 gap), I2V Subject Consistency (97.64 vs. 96.95), Dynamic Degree (52.60 vs. 51.38), and Motion Smoothness (98.49 vs. 97.90), while trailing on Camera Motion (29.46 vs. 34.76) and overall Quality (79.80 vs. 80.82). Among other open-source I2V models, Pusa outperforms DynamiCrafter-512 (86.99), CogVideoX-5b-I2V (86.70), I2VGen-XL (85.28), and VideoCrafter (82.57) on the Total score, but falls short of Magi-1 (89.28) and Step-Video-TI2V (88.36), which were trained from scratch with substantially larger datasets. The paper's central efficiency claim is that Pusa matches or exceeds Wan-I2V using roughly 1/2500th the training data (4K vs. ≥10M samples) and 1/200th the compute (~$500 vs. ≥$100K), with the gap attributable to VTA's non-destructive adaptation preserving the base model's generative priors rather than overwriting them via mask-based fine-tuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Frame-Aware Video Diffusion Model (FVDM) paradigm, which introduced vectorized timesteps for per-frame noise evolution, to industrial scale by proposing a non-destructive Vectorized Timestep Adaptation (VTA) strategy applied to the Wan-T2V base model. It departs sharply from the mainstream I2V approach exemplified by Wan-I2V, which modifies the base model with mask mechanisms and image-clip embeddings that overwrite pretrained generative priors; instead, VTA only inflates the scalar timestep to a vector and performs minimal fine-tuning (4K samples, ~$500 compute). The flow-matching formulation is extended to the frame-aware setting (FAFM objective), and the DiT architecture's temporal conditioning is re-engineered to accept per-frame timestep embeddings, building directly on the DiT and Flow Matching foundations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ~2500× reduction in training data and ~200× reduction in compute relative to Wan-I2V, while matching or exceeding its VBench-I2V scores, makes high-quality I2V generation accessible to small research groups and suggests a general recipe for adapting large pretrained video models to new temporal tasks without catastrophic forgetting. The zero-shot generalization to start-end frames, video completion, and video extension from a single fine-tuned model is a practical advantage over task-specific pipelines. Open questions include whether VTA scales to even larger or architecturally different base models (e.g., non-DiT backbones), how it interacts with long-video generation beyond the 81-frame clips tested, and whether the fully random timestep sampling strategy remains optimal at larger model scales or with more diverse training data.
