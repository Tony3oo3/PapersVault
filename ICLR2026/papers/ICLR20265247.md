## Problem

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) substantially improves language models on mathematical reasoning, but it remains unclear whether these gains reflect genuine improvements in reasoning capability or merely upweighting of solution strategies and formats already present in the pre-RL reference distribution. This paper isolates the 'strategy upweighting' component by restricting optimization to only the first k tokens of a solution—the prefix that typically encodes the solution plan—while generating the remainder from the frozen reference model.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text math problem as input. A small adapter model (~1B parameters, same family as the target) generates only the first k tokens (k ∈ {32, 64} in main experiments) as a prefix, which is then fed to a much larger frozen target model (7B–72B) that autoregressively completes the full solution. The final output is the concatenated prefix-and-completion text, scored with a binary verifiable reward (correct/incorrect) via math_verify. A key constraint is that the adapter and target must belong to the same model family (e.g., Qwen2.5-1.5B adapter with Qwen2.5-7B target), as cross-family combinations degrade performance.

## Method

The core method, Prefix-RL, trains a lightweight adapter with PPO to generate only the first k tokens of a solution, while a frozen target model completes the rest; the adapter is optimized against a binary verifiable reward on the full generated answer. Because the target model is never updated, any improvement must come from steering the solution strategy encoded in the prefix rather than from teaching new token-level skills. A second, training-free baseline (Prefix Clustering) clusters 16-token prefixes from reference-model rollouts via k-means and selects the single fixed prefix that maximizes training-set accuracy. The training procedure uses the OpenRLHF pipeline with 8 rollouts per prompt, batch size 512, KL penalty β=0.001, and 10 episodes (~140–170 steps), requiring only 4 H100 GPUs for the adapter plus separate inference nodes for the target. This decouples training cost from target-model size: for a 1.5B adapter steering a 72B target, training FLOPs are reduced by roughly 3,000× compared to standard full-model RL, and the approach works even with quantized FP8 targets.

## Results

On Qwen2.5-7B, Prefix-RL(k=32) improves MATH-500 accuracy from 67.4% to 74.4% (+7.0), AIME from 23.0% to 25.8% (+2.8), AMC23 from 40.3% to 50.0% (+9.7), and Minerva from 19.1% to 22.4% (+3.3). On Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct-FP8, the largest gains appear: AIME jumps from 32.8% to 49.1% (+16.3) and MATH-500 from 62.0% to 67.8% (+5.8). Full RL fine-tuning of Qwen-7B reaches 78% on MATH-500, while Prefix-RL recovers most of that gain (74.4%) at a fraction of the compute cost (~3,000× fewer training FLOPs when using a 1.5B adapter for a 72B target). Gains are robust across 4 random seeds (effect sizes 2–25× larger than standard deviations) and transfer to OOD physics benchmarks (OCW Courses +6.3, UGPhysics +0.9 at k=16).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LoRA RL (Hu et al., 2021), Prefix-RL(k=32) clearly wins on all four Qwen-7B benchmarks: MATH-500 74.4 vs. 70.2, AIME 25.8 vs. 24.4, AMC23 50.0 vs. 36.2, and Minerva 22.4 vs. 20.9, while LoRA actually degrades AMC23 by 4.0 points below the base model. Against supervised Prefix-Tuning (Li & Liang, 2021), Prefix-RL is dramatically superior: the SFT prefix-tuning baseline collapses MATH-500 to 45.4 (−22.0) and AIME to 4.9 (−18.1), whereas Prefix-RL improves both substantially. Against full RL fine-tuning (SimpleRL, Zeng et al., 2025), Prefix-RL recovers most of the gain on Qwen-7B MATH-500 (74.4% vs. 78% for full RL) while requiring only a 1.5B adapter trained with PPO and inference-only access to the 7B target, reducing training FLOPs by roughly 3,000× compared to standard RL on a 72B target. Prefix Clustering, the training-free baseline, shows mixed results: it helps Llama models (e.g., +13.1 on AMC23 for Llama-8B) but hurts Qwen models (−8.0 on MATH-500), confirming that input-conditional prefixes learned by Prefix-RL are necessary for cross-family generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper is directly motivated by the 'echo chamber' finding of Zhao et al. (2025), who showed that RL post-training concentrates output distributions on generation formats already present in the pretraining data rather than teaching new reasoning skills. The authors operationalize this insight by restricting optimization to only the first k tokens (the prefix that encodes solution strategy), contrasting their approach with Prefix-Tuning (supervised, continuous embeddings), LoRA (backprop through the full backbone), and step-wise controllable generation methods (PPLM, GeDi, IPA). Prefix-RL is positioned as a new methodological middle ground: it uses RL with verifiable rewards (like standard RLVR) but decouples training from the target model by training a small adapter in isolation, requiring only inference access to the large model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: Prefix-RL reduces GPU requirements by ~4× (8 vs. 32 GPUs for a 70B target) and training FLOPs by ~3000×, while working with quantized FP8 models and closed-weights APIs—settings where standard RL is infeasible. Scientifically, the finding that a substantial fraction of RL's math-reasoning gains comes from upweighting solution strategies (capturable in 32–64 tokens) reframes how the community should interpret RLVR results and motivates cheaper alternatives. Key limitations include the requirement that adapter and target share a model family, the inability to match full-RL performance, and the restriction to single-pass generation (no multi-step reflection). Open questions include whether prefix policies transfer to multi-pass solvers, whether cross-family adapters can be made to work, and whether the approach extends to domains beyond verifiable math.
