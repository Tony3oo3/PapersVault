## Problem

RF-based material identification can reveal intrinsic electromagnetic properties (permittivity, conductivity) that optical sensors cannot, yet the field lacks a large-scale public dataset, standardized benchmarks, and systematic evaluation of out-of-distribution robustness under realistic geometric perturbations such as varying incidence angle and stand-off distance. Existing RF material datasets are small, non-public, cover narrow frequency bands, and provide no multi-protocol or multi-split evaluation framework.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264408/cropped_page4_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The input is a complex-valued RF signal sampled at 2,048 frequency bins across 4–43.5 GHz, represented as a dual-channel (I/Q) real-valued vector of shape (2048×2) in the frequency domain or a 10,240-sample time-domain signal obtained via IFFT with zero-padding and band-pass filtering. The output is a material class label from 16 fine-grained categories (grouped into 5 superclasses: bricks, glass, synthetic, woods, stones), with evaluation also performed under a 4-class stone-subclass division. Each sample is acquired at a specific distance (200–2000 mm, 50 mm steps) and incidence angle (0°–10°, 1° steps), and the benchmark evaluates 5 frequency-allocation protocols including US and China legal bands.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is the RF-MatID dataset (142k samples, 71k per domain) and a comprehensive benchmark evaluating 11 deep learning models (spanning CNNs, RNNs, Transformers, MLPs, and RF-specific architectures) under 5 frequency protocols × 7 split settings × 3 category divisions. A custom baseline model is designed with frequency-aware positional encoding to handle non-contiguous frequency slices, parallel ConvNeXt (spatial) and Transformer (temporal) feature extractors, and MLP-based fusion. Key empirical findings include: (1) raw frequency-domain dual-channel representation outperforms time-domain signals, eliminating the need for IFFT preprocessing; (2) distance-domain shifts cause a 16.59% average accuracy drop versus 4.02% for angle shifts; (3) millimeter-wave bands (P2) are more robust to distance variation while centimeter-wave bands (P3) tolerate angle changes better; and (4) country-specific legal frequency bands (P4/P5) achieve near-full-spectrum accuracy, confirming practical deployability under regulatory constraints.

## Results

On the full-spectrum Protocol 1 (4–43.5 GHz) with random split (S1), LSTM-ResNet achieves the highest in-distribution accuracy at 99.84%, followed by MLP (99.19%), TimesNet (99.66%), and the proposed baseline (99.57%). Under the most severe cross-distance shift (S2-mod2, closest 11 distances as test), performance drops dramatically: LSTM-ResNet falls to 49.95%, ResNet-50 to 48.90%, and Transformer to 40.49%, while AirTac retains 86.95% and the baseline holds at 69.47%. Under cross-angle shift (S3-mod3, largest 3 angles), ConvNeXt (94.84%) and DINOv3 (95.21%) outperform most other models, and the baseline reaches 94.21%. Across all protocols and divisions, the baseline averages 96.78% accuracy under S1 and S2/S3-mod1, with distance shifts causing a 16.59% average drop versus only 4.02% for angle shifts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LSTM-ResNet (Choi et al., 2018) leads in-distribution (99.84% on P1-S1) but collapses under severe distance shift to 49.95% (S2-mod2), a ~50-point drop. ResNet-50 (He et al., 2016) similarly degrades from 98.85% to 48.90% under S2-mod2, confirming its vulnerability to geometric perturbations. MLP (Gardner & Dorling, 1998) maintains relatively stable performance across settings (73–99%) but at the cost of the largest parameter count (67.2M). Vision-based DINOv3 (Siméoni et al., 2025) and ConvNeXt (Liu et al., 2022) are the most robust under cross-angle shifts, reaching 95.21% and 94.84% on S3-mod3 respectively, outperforming the baseline (94.21%) by ~1%. Sequence models (BiLSTM, Transformer, TimesNet) consistently underperform, with Transformer dropping to 40.49% on S2-mod2. RF-specific models Material-ID (Chen et al., 2025) and AirTac (Zhang et al., 2024) show moderate OOD robustness (AirTac: 86.95% on S2-mod2) but trail the best performers in-distribution. The proposed baseline (frequency-aware positional encoding + parallel ConvNeXt/Transformer extractors + MLP fusion) achieves 99.57% on S1 and remains competitive across all 21 protocol-setting combinations, with an average of 85% across all configurations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in RF-based material identification by introducing the first open-source, wide-band (4–43.5 GHz), geometry-diverse dataset, whereas all prior RF material datasets (RFID, Wi-Fi, UWB, mmWave) are non-public and cover only narrow frequency bands. Conceptually, it departs from the dominant vision-based material identification paradigm by exploiting electromagnetic interactions that reveal intrinsic properties (permittivity, conductivity) rather than surface appearance. For benchmarking, it repurposes architectures from computer vision (ResNet-50, ConvNeXt, DINOv3), NLP (Transformer, BiLSTM), time-series (TimesNet), and RF sensing (Material-ID, AirTac) into a unified evaluation framework, alongside a purpose-built baseline with frequency-aware positional encoding and parallel spatial/temporal extractors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By providing the first public large-scale RF material identification dataset with systematic geometric perturbations and multi-protocol evaluation, RF-MatID enables reproducible research and fair algorithmic comparison that was previously impossible. The finding that country-specific legal frequency bands (US, China) achieve near-identical accuracy to the full 39.5 GHz spectrum is a practically significant result for real-world deployment under regulatory constraints. Key open questions remain: performance degrades substantially under distance shifts (avg. 16.59% drop) versus angle shifts (4.02%), the dataset lacks multi-layer composites and environmental variability, and the authors identify physical-constraint incorporation (PINNs) and domain generalization as critical directions for closing the in-distribution-to-OOD gap.
