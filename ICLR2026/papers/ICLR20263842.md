## Problem

While reward modeling is well-established for text-only LLMs, the field lacks a systematic, empirically grounded guide for building state-of-the-art Multimodal Reward Models (MRMs). Existing MLLMs each employ ad-hoc reward strategies (generative, critic-based, dual-model) without a unified comparison of how architectural choices, data curation, backbone selection, and training regularizations interact to determine final performance.

## Input / Output

BaseReward takes a multimodal prompt (an RGB image plus a text query) concatenated with a candidate response as input, and produces a single unbounded scalar reward score via a 2-layer MLP head appended to the Qwen2.5-VL-7B backbone. During RL deployment, the model scores each of 8 generated rollouts per prompt, and a hybrid scheme combines exact-match rule rewards (for objective tasks) with BaseReward's sigmoid-normalized scores (for subjective tasks). The ensemble variant averages scores from two independently trained 7B models (Qwen2.5-VL and Qwen2-VL backbones) to improve robustness.

## Method

BaseReward adopts a Naive-RM paradigm: a 2-layer MLP reward head with SiLU activation (chosen over 1-layer linear, Tanh, or deeper MLPs via ablation) is trained with the standard Bradley-Terry pairwise ranking loss on 2.8M curated preference pairs drawn from seven multimodal and text-only datasets, with no auxiliary regularization (zero-coefficient and length normalization both degrade performance). A key empirical finding is that text-only preference data (e.g., Ultra-Hard, Olmo-2) substantially boosts multimodal RM accuracy—particularly on safety and math dimensions—by bridging the supervision gap that multimodal datasets leave for language-intensive reasoning. The model is trained with lr=3e-6, batch size 128 on 64 H100 GPUs, and is validated end-to-end in a GRPO reinforcement learning pipeline on Qwen-2.5-VL-3B, where the hybrid rule-based + BaseReward reward consistently outperforms both pure rule-based and R1-Reward (LongCoT-GRM) baselines across perception, reasoning, and conversational benchmarks.

## Results

BaseReward (Ensemble) achieves 92.94% Acc and 80.43% Acc+ on MM-RLHF-Reward Bench, surpassing the previous SOTA (Claude 3.7 Sonnet at 82.35% Acc) by ~10.6% and improving Acc+ by ~15.2%. On VL-Reward Bench, the ensemble reaches 84.41% Overall Accuracy and 82.91% Macro Accuracy, outperforming R1-Reward (71.92%) by 12.5%. On Multimodal Reward Bench, BaseReward (Ensemble) scores 73.6 Overall, placing second only to R1-Reward (82.2), with the gap attributed to the absence of coding-specific preference data in training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BaseReward decisively outperforms all prior reward models on MM-RLHF-Reward Bench: it beats R1-Reward (80.59 Acc / 54.35 Acc+) by 12.4/26.1 points, MM-RLHF-Reward (82.00/63.00) by 10.9/17.4 points, and IXC-2.5-Reward (71.18/50.00) by 21.8/30.4 points. It also surpasses the strongest proprietary model, Claude 3.7 Sonnet (82.35/65.22), by 10.6/15.2 points. On VL-Reward Bench, BaseReward (Ensemble) leads R1-Reward (71.92 Overall) by 12.5 points and IXC-2.5-Reward (65.80) by 18.6 points. However, on Multimodal Reward Bench, BaseReward (73.6) trails R1-Reward (82.2) by 8.6 points, primarily due to the absence of coding and related preference data in its training set. In the RL fine-tuning experiments (Table 11), BaseReward+Rule-Base consistently outperforms R1-Reward across all nine downstream benchmarks (e.g., 54.0 vs 51.4 WildVision Win Rate, 64.3 vs 61.2 MathVista), while also being computationally more efficient since it avoids the multi-step generative inference required by R1-Reward.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a systematic empirical study that unifies and compares the three dominant multimodal reward modeling paradigms—Naive-RM (as in IXC-2.5-Reward), Critic-based RM (as in MM-RLHF), and Generative RM (as in R1-Reward and Seed-1.5-VL)—under a standardized training protocol, something prior work has not done. It builds on the Bradley-Terry preference loss and the Qwen2.5-VL backbone but departs from the prevailing assumption that more complex generative or critic-based architectures are necessary, instead showing that a simple two-layer SiLU MLP reward head trained on a curated mix of multimodal and text-only preference data achieves state-of-the-art performance. The paper also extends the RLHF literature by explicitly studying the interaction between reward model architecture and downstream RL effectiveness (GRPO), rather than treating benchmark accuracy as the sole evaluation criterion.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the community with a concrete, empirically-backed 'recipe' for constructing high-performance MRMs, filling a gap where prior SOTA models each used ad-hoc, domain-specific strategies without systematic comparison. Key open questions remain: the coding-task gap (no single dataset closes it), whether the 1:0.5 multimodal-to-text mixing ratio generalizes to other backbones or scales, and whether the modality-specialization finding (LLM-based RMs outperform MLLM-based ones on pure-text tasks) will persist as MLLM architectures improve. The practical impact is significant—practitioners can now build competitive MRMs without resorting to expensive generative reward models, and the RL integration results demonstrate that static benchmark superiority (R1-Reward) does not translate to better downstream policy performance.
