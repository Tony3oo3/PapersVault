## Problem

Learning a probability density from only pairwise comparisons (which candidate is preferred) rather than direct samples is a fundamentally harder problem than standard density estimation, yet it is the natural format for expert elicitation and LLM-based preference queries. Prior work (Mikkola et al., 2024) addressed this with normalizing flows and an empirically motivated constant-tempering heuristic, but lacked a rigorous theoretical link between the target density and the observable winner distribution, and required more informative multi-item rankings. This paper closes the theoretical gap by proving an exact score-level relationship and delivers a score-based method that works with only pairwise comparisons.

## Input / Output

The method consumes triplets (x, x', x ≻ x') where both candidates are drawn independently from a known sampling density λ(x) (uniform for d≤4, diagonal Gaussian for higher dimensions), and the expert indicates the preferred point. For non-uniform λ, a Rosenblatt transformation maps the space so that λ becomes uniform on a hypercube before training. The output is approximate samples from the belief density p(x) generated via score-scaled annealed Langevin dynamics, along with the estimated position-dependent tempering field τ(x) and an optional trained diffusion model. The method operates in the low-data regime (hundreds to ~1000d comparisons), well below typical diffusion model training budgets.

## Method

The core theoretical contribution is proving that under the Bradley-Terry or exponential RUM, the score of the belief density is collinear with the score of the marginal winner density (MWD): ∇log p(x) = τ(x)∇log p_w(x), where τ(x) is a position-dependent tempering field with a closed-form expression involving the 1/s-tempered density ratio. Practically, a single EDM-style MLP score network is trained via denoising score matching on concatenated (winner, loser) pairs, with a binary 'joint' flag: 50% of the time the loser is masked with Gaussian noise so the network learns the MWD marginal score, and 50% of the time it learns the full joint score. The tempering field τ(x) is estimated by training a separate density-ratio network r_θ(x,x') ≈ p(x')/p(x) via Bradley-Terry MLE (Softplus loss), then plugging it into the analytical formula with importance sampling from the MWD diffusion model. Sampling from p(x) is performed by running annealed Langevin dynamics with the scaled score τ(x)·s_θ(x,σ), where the step size is inversely proportional to τ(x) for stability. This replaces the prior flow-based approach with a score-based one, enabling better multimodal modeling and eliminating the need for multi-item rankings.

## Results

On 8 synthetic target distributions (2D–16D, including multimodal and non-Gaussian geometries), the score-based method with full tempering field score-τ(x) achieves at least 50% lower Wasserstein distance and 25% lower MMTV compared to the flow-based baseline (Mikkola et al., 2024). For example, on Twomoons2D, score-τ(x) reaches 0.44 Wasserstein vs. 1.29 for flow, and on Mixturegaussians4D it reaches 1.09 vs. 3.75. The constant-tempering variant score-τ* also outperforms flow on all targets, confirming that the score-based framework itself (not just the full field) drives the improvement. In the LLM expert-elicitation experiment (220 pairwise comparisons on California housing features), the score-based estimate shows clear distributional similarity to the empirical data distribution.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The only prior method for density estimation from pairwise comparisons is the flow-based model of Mikkola et al. (2024), which learns normalizing flows from rankings and pairwise comparisons. The score-based method wins decisively on Wasserstein distance across all 8 targets: the gap ranges from ~0.5 (Gaussian16D: 5.00 vs. 5.50) to ~2.7 (Mixturegaussians4D: 1.09 vs. 3.75), with the paper reporting at least 50% relative reduction. On MMTV, the score-based method wins on 7 of 8 targets (the exception being Gaussian4D where score-τ* at 0.41 is marginally better than score-τ(x) at 0.44, both well below flow's 0.72). The full tempering field τ(x) generally improves over the constant τ* (e.g., Twomoons2D: 0.44 vs. 0.54 Wasserstein), but the exception is Mixturegaussians10D where score-τ* (1.10) outperforms score-τ(x) (1.33), suggesting the field estimate can be noisy in higher dimensions with limited data. Visually, the flow method overestimates low-density regions (e.g., the center of Ring2D), while the score-based method with the tempering field correctly downweights such regions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Mikkola et al. (2024), which introduced the first method for density estimation from pairwise comparisons using normalizing flows and an empirically motivated constant tempering parameter. The key departure is both theoretical and methodological: the authors prove that the correct relationship between the belief density and the marginal winner density is governed by a position-dependent tempering field τ(x), not a constant, and they switch from flows to score-based diffusion models (EDM-style) with a masking scheme to jointly learn the winner-loser joint and the winner marginal. The score-scaled annealed Langevin dynamics from Song & Ermon (2019) serve as the sampling mechanism, and the EDM framework (Karras et al., 2022) provides the training architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the missing theoretical foundation for preference-based density estimation, showing that the target density can be exactly recovered from the estimable marginal winner density via a provably collinear score relationship. This opens the door to using powerful modern generative models (diffusion, score-based) for expert elicitation and LLM knowledge quantification from only comparative queries, which are cognitively easier and more reliable than direct probability estimates. Key open questions include: characterizing the approximation error of score-scaled ALD at σ>0, extending the tempering field result to RUMs without closed-form choice probabilities (e.g., Thurstone-Mosteller), and developing active learning strategies for the sampling distribution to handle high-dimensional or low-overlap settings.
