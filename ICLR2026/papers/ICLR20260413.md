## Problem

Existing generative adversarial sampling methods (GAN-based and diffusion-based) produce constrained adversarial samples that do not faithfully resemble natural test-time errors, and they fail to account for the link between learned erroneous contextual cues and the high transferability of natural adversarial samples. The paper addresses this by asking how to guide a diffusion model to generate adversarial images that incorporate structural features from the adversarial class—mimicking the shortcut-learning behavior that causes real-world misclassifications—while preserving image quality and maximizing cross-architecture transferability.

## Input / Output

The method takes a true class label, an adversarial target class (randomly assigned for targeted attacks, or selected via CLIP text-encoder cosine similarity for untargeted attacks), and an off-the-shelf surrogate classifier (ResNet-50, Inception-v3, or ViT-H) whose gradients are used during sampling. It operates entirely in the latent space of Stable Diffusion 1.5 (200 DDIM steps), with a VAE decoder mapping latents to 512×512 RGB images; adversarial classifier guidance is delayed until timestep t ≤ 700 to avoid early-manifold disruption. The output is a fully synthetic adversarial image (not a perturbation of a source image) that is misclassified by the surrogate and evaluated for transferability across eight victim models including adversarially trained variants.

## Method

NatADiff introduces adversarial boundary guidance, which augments the standard classifier-free guidance vector v_y with an intersection vector v_{y∩y̌} = ε(x_t, t, y∩y̌) − ε(x_t, t) that steers the diffusion trajectory toward the manifold region where true-class and adversarial-class features coexist, controlled by a scalar μ. To prevent the surrogate classifier gradient from producing constrained perturbations, the method applies differentiable image transforms (crops, rotations) to average out the local adversarial signal before backpropagating through the VAE decoder, using Tweedie's formula to estimate x_0 from the noisy latent x_t. Time-travel sampling (re-running forward and reverse diffusion steps R times at selected timesteps) is incorporated to recover from suboptimal trajectories and maintain image quality. For untargeted attacks, a CLIP-based similarity targeting scheme selects the adversarial target as the semantically closest class to the true label in the text-embedding space, reducing the difficulty of incorporating adversarial features while preserving visual coherence.

## Results

NatADiff achieves comparable white-box attack success rates (96.9–99.6%) to state-of-the-art methods while substantially outperforming all baselines in transferability, with average ASR of 56.8–73.2% across nine victim models versus 39–53% for the next-best method (ACA). Notably, NatADiff transfers to adversarially trained ResNet and Inception at 55–76% ASR, where perturbation-based attacks (PGD, AutoAttack) drop to 4–13%. In terms of alignment with natural adversarial samples (FID-A), targeted NatADiff achieves 77.3 (RN-50 surrogate) and 93.5 (ViT-H surrogate), outperforming AdvClass (92.4 and 95.2) and approaching the clean ImageNet-Val baseline of 58.0.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NatADiff outperforms all six baselines in transferability across every surrogate–victim combination. Against the closest competitor ACA (Chen et al., 2023b), NatADiff^U with an RN-50 surrogate achieves 68.2% average ASR versus ACA's 52.9%, a 15.3-point gap; with ViT-H, the gap widens to 69.7% vs. 53.2%. AdvClass (Dai et al., 2024) is outperformed by NatADiff in all experiments by 11–27 points in average ASR, confirming that adversarial boundary guidance and classifier augmentations substantially improve over vanilla adversarial classifier guidance. Perturbation-based attacks (PGD, AutoAttack) show the lowest transferability (16–18% average ASR), as their small ε-neighborhood perturbations do not generalize across architectures. NCF and DiffAttack are constrained to modifying a fixed source image, limiting their attack surface and capping average ASR at 35–47%. Adversarial training provides no meaningful defense: NatADiff transfers to AdvRes and AdvInc at 55–76% ASR, while PGD and AutoAttack drop to 4–13%.

## Relation to Prior Work

NatADiff extends the adversarial diffusion sampling line initiated by AdvDiff (Dai et al., 2024), which injected non-robust classifier gradients into the diffusion trajectory but produced samples that were effectively constrained perturbations rather than true natural adversarial examples. The key conceptual departure is 'adversarial boundary guidance,' which repurposes the classifier-free guidance vector (Ho & Salimans, 2022) to steer the trajectory toward the intersection of the true and adversarial classes, motivated by the observation that natural adversarial samples contain structural elements from the adversarial class. The method also incorporates time-travel sampling (Lugmayr et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2023) and classifier augmentations to preserve image quality and suppress the constrained-perturbation regime, positioning NatADiff as a fully generative, perturbation-free attack rather than a modification of a clean source image.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Natural adversarial samples represent the strongest class of unconstrained attack because they are valid, perturbation-free inputs that bypass most existing defences, and their high cross-architecture transferability exposes shared shortcut-learning vulnerabilities across independently trained classifiers. NatADiff's ability to generate samples that both transfer at 60–70% ASR across CNN and transformer architectures and align closely with the ImageNet-A distribution (FID-A) provides a practical tool for stress-testing robustness and probing which contextual cues models rely on. Key limitations include the ~103-second per-sample generation cost, the conservative μ=0.2 setting that avoids dual-class or flipped-class artifacts, and evaluation restricted to ImageNet classifiers; extending the approach to specialized domains and understanding whether any training-time intervention can mitigate natural adversarial transferability remain open.
