## Problem

Existing online conformal prediction methods (ACI, OGD, Conformal PID, ECI) are designed for fully adversarial environments and adjust prediction thresholds purely through reactive gradient corrections, ignoring any predictable structure in the non-conformity scores. This results in overly conservative (wide) prediction sets even when the underlying data exhibits learnable patterns. The paper asks: can one incorporate distributional information about the scores into the update rule while preserving distribution-free, finite-sample coverage guarantees?

## Input / Output

At each time step t, the method receives a sequential input X_t, a base model point prediction f̂_t(X_t), and a sliding window of w=100 historical non-conformity scores {s_{t-w+1},...,s_t}. It outputs an asymmetric prediction interval [f̂_t(X_t) - q_t^l, f̂_t(X_t) + q_t^u] constructed from two one-sided quantile thresholds at level α/2 each, targeting a long-term miscoverage rate of α (default 10%). The estimated CDF is computed from the recent score window (ECDF by default, or Gaussian KDE with Silverman bandwidth), and the scale factor λ/η ≤ 1 controls confidence in the CDF estimate.

## Method

COP performs a two-step threshold update: first, a standard OGD step on the (1−α)-quantile loss yields a primary radius q̂_{t+1} = q̂_t + η(err_t − α); second, a refinement step adjusts the threshold using the estimated CDF: q_{t+1} = q̂_{t+1} − λ_{t+1}(F̂_{t+1}(q̂_{t+1}) − (1−α)). This refinement is derived by minimizing a local linear approximation of the expected quantile loss plus a quadratic regularization, and is equivalent to an optimistic online gradient descent (OOGD) step where the optimistic term M_{t+1} is a distribution-informed hint rather than the previous gradient. Theoretically, the paper establishes a joint regret-coverage bound showing that a well-chosen M_t simultaneously tightens both bounds, proves distribution-free finite-sample coverage under arbitrary learning rates, and shows asymptotic convergence to the true (1−α)-quantile under i.i.d. scores with Robbins-Monro learning rates. The approach avoids computing the inverse of the estimated CDF (unlike SPCI or Lee et al.), reducing computational cost and numerical error.

## Results

On four real-world time-series datasets (Amazon Stock, Google Stock, Electricity Demand, Delhi Temperature) with a 90% target coverage, COP maintains coverage within 0.4% of nominal while achieving the tightest or near-tightest prediction intervals. For example, with the AR base predictor, COP achieves average widths of 17.09 (Amazon), 19.87 (Google), 0.117 (Electricity), and 5.85 (Temperature), outperforming OGD (19.10, 33.76, 0.133, 6.82) and ECI (17.12, 19.95, 0.117, 6.39). ACI produces infinitely wide intervals in most settings, rendering it impractical. On simulation datasets with changepoints and distribution drift, COP similarly achieves the best or near-best median widths (e.g., 6.89 vs. 6.98 for ECI on Distribution Drift with AR).

## Baselines & Comparisons

COP outperforms all seven baselines on average and median prediction interval width across the four real-world datasets while maintaining coverage at the 90% target. Against ACI (Gibbs & Candès, 2021), COP is decisively superior: ACI produces infinitely wide intervals in nearly all settings, making it practically useless. Against OGD and SF-OGD (Bhatnagar et al., 2023), COP achieves 10–40% narrower intervals (e.g., 19.87 vs. 33.76 on Google Stock with AR), and is less sensitive to learning rate choice. Against decay-OGD (Angelopoulos et al., 2024), COP wins on most datasets (e.g., 5.85 vs. 6.36 on Temperature) while decay-OGD occasionally edges ahead on median width in stable regimes. Against Conformal PID (Angelopoulos et al., 2023b), COP is substantially tighter (e.g., 19.87 vs. 64.88 on Google Stock) and avoids the need to train a separate scorecaster. Against ECI (Wu et al., 2025), COP achieves comparable or slightly tighter widths (e.g., 5.85 vs. 6.39 on Temperature, 17.09 vs. 17.12 on Amazon) while also capturing underlying distributional patterns that ECI's error-quantification approach cannot. Against LQT (Areces et al., 2025), COP is more stable and avoids the grid-search hyperparameter sensitivity; LQT's coverage can deviate more (e.g., 91.9% on Distribution Drift with AR) while COP stays within 0.5% of target.

## Relation to Prior Work

COP extends the online conformal prediction line initiated by ACI (Gibbs & Candès, 2021) and its OGD-based successors (SF-OGD, decay-OGD, Conformal PID, ECI, LQT), which treat the data stream as fully adversarial and update the threshold reactively based on past miscoverage. The key conceptual departure is borrowing the optimistic online gradient descent framework (Rakhlin & Sridharan, 2013) to inject a distribution-informed 'hint' — an estimated CDF of non-conformity scores — into the update rule, bridging the gap between purely adversarial online CP (which ignores predictable structure) and distribution-estimation methods like SPCI (Xu & Xie, 2023) that capture patterns but lack distribution-free coverage guarantees. The method is positioned as a refinement step on top of standard OGD, reducing to OGD when the scale factor is zero.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The joint regret-coverage bound (Theorem 1) provides a unified theoretical lens showing that a well-chosen optimistic term simultaneously tightens both regret and coverage, which is a clean contribution to the online CP theory. Practically, the robustness experiment (coverage maintained even when the CDF is replaced by pure uniform noise, γ=0) confirms that the method degrades gracefully to conservative OGD-like behavior rather than failing catastrophically. Open questions include: how to adaptively select the scale factor λ without grid search, whether the approach extends to multi-dimensional or categorical prediction sets, and whether more sophisticated CDF estimators (e.g., neural network-based) can further tighten intervals without sacrificing the distribution-free guarantee. The empirical gains over strong baselines like ECI are modest, suggesting the current ECDF/KDE estimators may not fully exploit available distributional structure.
