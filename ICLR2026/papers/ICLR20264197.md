## Problem

AI Control (AIC) protocols rely on weaker models monitoring stronger ones, but practical adoption hinges on the operational and financial overhead of such monitoring. No prior work systematically connects a monitor's empirical detection performance (ROC curves) to long-term deployment safety under realistic auditing budgets, nor quantifies the cost of achieving a given safety level. This paper introduces the 'Control Tax'—the safety-cost trade-off—and provides both the empirical data and the theoretical framework to make that connection.

## Input / Output

The monitor receives a programming problem statement, a candidate code solution (either correct or backdoored), and is prompted to assign a fine-grained suspiciousness score from 1 to 100 (rather than a binary label), which is essential for operating under low auditing budgets where only the top-scoring submissions are audited. The theoretical framework then consumes the resulting ROC curves and a fixed auditing budget fraction c to produce a safety probability (probability of detecting at least one backdoor over N rounds), an optimal time-varying audit allocation function p(τ), and a control tax curve mapping monitor cost to achievable safety. Backdoored solutions are generated by an iterative agentic pipeline that requires the modified code to pass all public tests while failing on at least one hidden input.

## Method

The paper combines an empirical backdooring/monitoring pipeline with a game-theoretic safety model. Empirically, attacker LMs are prompted via a multi-step agentic loop (brainstorm → critique → generate → verify) to insert subtle flaws into correct APPS solutions, while monitor LMs assign 1–100 suspiciousness scores using chain-of-thought reasoning; this yields ROC curves for ~20 attacker–monitor pairs. Theoretically, the paper models the deployment as a two-player game between a stateless red team (inserting backdoors at rate p_b) and a blue team with a fixed audit budget c, analyzing both batch (all solutions available, audit top-scoring) and sequential not-anytime (SNAC, solutions arrive one-by-one) settings. In the large-N limit, attacker insertions follow a Poisson point process, and the blue team's optimal strategy is characterized as a variational saddle point problem over non-increasing allocation functions p(τ). A key structural result is that batch monitoring is equivalent to upfront auditing with an effective budget t₀ = t(s(c,0)) read off the ROC curve, while sequential monitoring requires front-loaded auditing (proven via an exchange argument showing any non-increasing rearrangement weakly improves safety). The optimal attacker rate scales as Θ(1/N), making concealment of the deployment horizon a critical safety practice.

## Results

The strongest monitor evaluated, o3-mini, achieves AUC scores of 0.92–0.96 in detecting code backdoors across diverse attacker models. Under optimal sequential monitoring with a 5% fixed auditing budget, the Control Tax analysis reveals that moving from Gemini 2.0 Flash to o3-mini on the Pareto frontier requires a 45× increase in cost to gain only 0.15 absolute safety improvement (from ~0.70 to ~0.85) against a GPT-4o attacker. The optimal attacker backdooring probability scales as Θ(1/N), and the game value stabilizes by N=100. Batch trusted monitoring generally provides better safety than sequential monitoring at low auditing budgets, though this is not universally true and relates to ROC curve convexity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is upfront auditing from Greenblatt et al. (2023), where the blue team simply audits the first cN samples without using a monitor. The paper shows that batch trusted monitoring is analytically equivalent to upfront auditing with a higher effective auditing budget t₀ = t(s(c,0)), meaning the monitor's discrimination ability amplifies the effective audit capacity. Sequential monitoring (SNAC) with an optimally non-increasing allocation strategy can match or slightly exceed batch safety in some attacker–monitor pairs, but batch generally dominates at low budgets. A key practical finding is that the most expensive, highest-performing monitor is not always optimal: a cheaper monitor (e.g., Gemini 2.0 Flash) can yield higher overall safety because cost savings enable a higher-volume auditing strategy. Even the strongest monitors (o3-mini, AUC ~0.96) do not guarantee long-term security against a dedicated, patient attacker in infinite-horizon deployments, where the optimal attacker strategy drives p_b → 0.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the AI Control (AIC) framework introduced by Greenblatt et al. (2023) and the agentic monitoring protocols of Bhatt et al. (2025) by introducing the 'Control Tax'—a formal quantification of the operational and financial cost of integrating control measures into AI pipelines. Where Griffin et al. (2024) model AIC as a safety-usefulness trade-off and Engels et al. (2025) study scaling laws for oversight with binary classifiers, this paper focuses specifically on the safety-versus-cost dimension using fine-grained suspiciousness scores and an agentic backdooring pipeline. The game-theoretic treatment of both batch and sequential (not-anytime) monitoring generalizes the upfront auditing analysis from Greenblatt et al. into a variational saddle-point problem over time-dependent audit allocations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides practitioners with a principled, quantitative framework for making cost-safety trade-off decisions in AI deployment, showing that the most expensive monitor is not always optimal—a cheaper monitor enabling higher-volume auditing can yield better overall safety. Key practical insights (conceal the deployment horizon, front-load auditing in sequential settings, beware of patient attackers in infinite-horizon deployments) are actionable for real-world AIC implementation. Open questions include extending beyond code backdooring to agentic tasks (SWE-bench), properly treating the anytime monitoring case, incorporating usefulness metrics alongside safety, and understanding whether the observed insensitivity of backdoor subtlety to attacker strength persists under more adversarial prompting or future model generations.
