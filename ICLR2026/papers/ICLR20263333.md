## Problem

Existing LLM-based autoformalization approaches are bottlenecked by pre-defined formal libraries, struggle with complex statements involving nested quantifiers and higher-order objects, and fail to transfer across formal languages or domain-specific DSLs. All prior methods treat each statement independently and expect the LLM to directly generate a complete formalization in one shot, without leveraging shared structure across a corpus or the semi-structured nature of mathematical statements.

## Input / Output

Phase I consumes an entire informal mathematical corpus plus the target language's existing documentation, and produces a library of reusable formal abstractions (definitions, relations, functions) that extend the DSL. Phase II takes a single informal statement and the extended documentation, producing a complete formal statement verified by a symbolic equivalence checker (BEq+ for ProofNet-Hard, an enhanced E3 checker for LeanEuclidPlus). The framework is entirely zero-training: no fine-tuning on the target language is required, and the only external input is the language documentation provided in-context to the LLM.

## Method

DNA is a two-phase, zero-training framework. Phase I (Language Extension) extracts common mathematical concepts from the corpus, filters those already present in the target language, constructs a Concept Dependency Graph (CDG) to determine formalization prerequisites, then formalizes concepts in topological order, refactors for conciseness, and generates user-facing documentation. Phase II (Statement Formalization) hierarchically decomposes each statement into semi-formalized clauses (quantifiers, premises, conclusions, with recursive decomposition of nested quantified sub-statements), translates each clause individually using the learned abstractions, composes them into a complete formal statement, and iteratively refines using a symbolic validator that checks syntax, variable-name faithfulness, and logical consistency. The key insight is that abstraction learning and hierarchical decomposition are complementary: abstractions make individual clause translations tractable, while decomposition exposes the precise semantic structure needed to apply those abstractions correctly, yielding synergistic gains that exceed either component alone.

## Results

On LeanEuclidPlus, DNA achieves an average pass@1 of 40.6% (non-reasoning) and 51.1% (reasoning) across six model families, compared to 18.7% and 31.5% for the direct-generation baseline, yielding a 1.17× to 1.62× average improvement. The most dramatic gain is Qwen3-14B, which advances from 1.0% to 9.6% (9.6× gain), while GPT-4.1-mini jumps from 4.8% to 42.4%. On ProofNet-Hard, all baselines—including GPT-5 and Claude-4-Sonnet—achieve 0% success, whereas DNA enables 7.9% (non-reasoning) and 10.7% (reasoning) pass@1, demonstrating that the framework unlocks formalization in domains where even state-of-the-art models fail entirely.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The two fine-tuned autoformalizer baselines—Kimina Autoformalizer 7B (Wang et al., 2025) and Mathesis Autoformalizer 7B (Xuejun et al., 2025)—achieve 0% pass@1 on LeanEuclidPlus under all conditions (Baseline, Divide, Abstract, DNA), failing even basic compilation due to their inability to follow instructions for generating correct LeanEuclid DSL syntax. On ProofNet-Hard, Kimina reaches only 8.4% under the Abstract condition and Mathesis 5.3%, while DNA with general-purpose LLMs substantially outperforms both (e.g., GPT-4.1 achieves 10.5% and Claude-4-Sonnet 12.6% with DNA). More critically, on ProofNet-Hard all general-purpose LLM baselines (GPT-5, Claude-4-Sonnet, Qwen3-235B) also score 0% without corpus-specific abstractions, meaning DNA is the only configuration that enables any successful formalization in this low-resource domain. The paper further shows that DNA with smaller models (Qwen3-14B at 9.6%) approaches or exceeds what much larger baseline models achieve (e.g., GPT-4.1-mini at 4.8%), and that the learned abstractions combined with decomposition (DNA) frequently surpass even oracle human-written abstractions (OracleA), indicating the automated pipeline can match or exceed expert-curated libraries.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in LLM-based autoformalization, where models are expected to directly generate complete formal statements using pre-existing library abstractions (Mathlib, HOL sessions). It repurposes two ideas from adjacent fields—hierarchical decomposition from chain-of-thought reasoning and subgoal-based theorem proving, and library/abstraction learning from program synthesis and lemma discovery—and combines them into a zero-training, two-phase framework. Unlike fine-tuned autoformalizers (Kimina, Mathesis) that are tightly coupled to Lean Mathlib, DNA operates purely through in-context prompting with the target language's documentation, making it a plug-and-play system that requires no training on the target formal language. The authors explicitly position this as the first work to apply decomposition directly to the formalization process and the first to apply abstraction learning to statement autoformalization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The zero-training design is practically significant for low-resource domain-specific languages where no large-scale parallel data exists for fine-tuning, and the finding that learned abstractions can match or even exceed human-written oracle abstractions (e.g., the limit-point compactness case) suggests automated abstraction curation could complement expert library development. However, absolute performance remains modest (best ~71% pass@1 on LeanEuclidPlus, ~25% on ProofNet-Hard), benchmarks are small (100 and 19 problems), and the framework's six-step Phase I pipeline introduces substantial LLM call overhead. Open questions include scalability to larger corpora, applicability to proof (not just statement) autoformalization, and whether the approach degrades gracefully as target libraries evolve or grow beyond context limits.
