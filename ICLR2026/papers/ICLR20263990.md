## Problem

Standard SFT cold-start for RL preparation selects checkpoints based on evaluation performance, but this is misaligned with actual RL potential: distributional forgetting from the base model occurs before traditional overfitting, causing the best-performing SFT checkpoint to yield suboptimal post-RL results. The paper asks how long cold-start should last and whether the CE imitation objective is truly suited for RL preparation.

## Input / Output

The method takes a base LLM (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-Math-7B, or Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct) and a small set of 1k–6k long-CoT demonstrations (subsampled from Openr1-Math-46k-8192, less than 1/10 the RL data volume) as input. During training, AESL additionally consumes the model's own logits and prefix log-probabilities to compute adaptive per-token weights. The output is a fine-tuned checkpoint that serves as the initialization for GRPO-based RLVR, evaluated on mathematical reasoning benchmarks (AIME, AMC, MATH-500, Minerva, OlympiadBench) and general reasoning benchmarks (ARC-C, GPQA-Diamond, MMLU-Pro).

## Method

The paper first shows empirically that diversity metrics (entropy, self-BLEU) peak before evaluation performance peaks, and that checkpoints at the diversity turning point yield superior post-RL results—revealing that distributional forgetting precedes overfitting. Building on this, AESL replaces the uniform CE loss with a weighted variant where each token's loss is scaled by p = 1 − softmax[y(s*_t) / (−t_scaling · mean prefix log-prob)], which down-weights tokens the model already predicts confidently (preserving base knowledge) while still learning new patterns. At the subsequence level, the denominator uses the average log-probability of the prefix context, so tokens following high-probability prefixes receive stronger early-stopping pressure, preserving sequence-level diversity where it matters most for RL exploration. The single hyperparameter t_scaling (optimal range 3–5) controls the preservation–adaptation trade-off, and the full pipeline adds only ~20% overhead to the cold-start phase (~2% of total post-training time).

## Results

On Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, AESL cold-start followed by GRPO achieves a macro-average of 42.26 across six mathematical reasoning benchmarks (AIME24, AIME25, AMC23, MATH-500, Minerva Math, OlympiadBench), outperforming direct RL (38.09) and standard CE cold-start + RL (40.19). On Qwen2.5-Math-7B, AESL + RL reaches 50.04 average, surpassing the direct RL baseline (44.67) by over 5 points. The method also generalizes to Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, where AESL + RL achieves 25.5 average versus 20.78 for direct RL. AESL consistently produces the highest post-cold-start entropy (0.553) and lowest self-BLEU (0.694), confirming its role in preserving distributional diversity for downstream RL.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AESL outperforms both GEM (Li et al., ICLR 2025) and PSFT (Zhu et al., 2025) on both base models. On Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, AESL + RL achieves 42.26 average, beating GEM + RL (40.53) by 1.73 points and PSFT + RL (40.64) by 1.62 points. On Qwen2.5-Math-7B, AESL + RL reaches 50.04, surpassing GEM + RL (49.42) by 0.62 and PSFT + RL (48.02) by 2.02. AESL also consistently exceeds the simpler CE-ES baseline (diversity-based early stopping with standard CE loss) by 0.72 points on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and 1.44 points on Qwen2.5-Math-7B, demonstrating that the adaptive token-level weighting provides meaningful gains over uniform early stopping. Notably, AESL achieves the best or second-best score on nearly every individual benchmark, with particularly large margins on AMC23 (56.48 vs. 53.83 for GEM) and OlympiadBench (43.11 vs. 40.3 for GEM) on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits within the growing body of work refining the SFT cold-start phase that precedes RLVR training in LLM post-training pipelines (e.g., Tulu 3, DeepSeek-R1). It positions itself alongside GEM (Li et al., 2025), which promotes diversity preservation during SFT, and PSFT (Zhu et al., 2025), which targets OOD generalization with large SFT datasets, but distinguishes itself by targeting the lightweight cold-start regime (less than 1/10 the data of RL) and introducing a token- and subsequence-level adaptive weighting mechanism. The GRPO RL framework (Shao et al., 2024) and the standard CE cold-start objective serve as the baseline from which AESL departs, replacing uniform demonstration imitation with a dynamic balance between pattern acquisition and base-distribution preservation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that distributional forgetting degrades RL potential before traditional SFT overfitting occurs challenges the common practice of selecting cold-start checkpoints by evaluation performance, offering diversity metrics (entropy, self-BLEU) as more reliable early-stopping signals. AESL provides a practical, lightweight loss modification (~20% overhead in the already-cheap cold-start phase) that consistently improves post-RL performance across model families and dataset configurations. However, the gains over the simpler CE-ES baseline are modest (roughly 0.5–1.5 points in average score), and the evaluation is confined to mathematical reasoning; whether the distributional-forgetting phenomenon and AESL's benefits generalize to broader domains or larger models remains an open question. The paper also flags the lack of a predictive metric for the upper bound of subsequent RL performance as a key future direction.
