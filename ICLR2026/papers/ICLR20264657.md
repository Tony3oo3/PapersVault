## Problem

Existing video editing benchmarks (VE-Bench, EditBoard, FiVE, TDVE-Assessor) are designed for source-target prompt-based methods and offer little to no support for instruction-guided video editing (IVE). They suffer from limited source diversity, narrow task coverage (mostly subject/attribute/style editing), and evaluation metrics that lack multidimensional assessment and MLLM-based semantic understanding, making them inadequate for the rapidly growing IVE paradigm.

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes a source video (32–1024 frames, organized into a short subset of 400 videos at 32–128 frames and a long subset of 200 videos at 129–1024 frames), a natural-language edit prompt drawn from 8 categories and 35 subcategories, and the target video produced by an IVE method at its native resolution (ranging from 384×240 for ICVE to 1280×720 for VACE). It outputs 12 per-metric scores grouped into three weighted dimensions—Video Quality, Instruction Compliance, and Video Fidelity—combined into a single total score via human-annotated importance weights.

## Method

IVEBench constructs a 600-video corpus spanning 7 semantic dimensions and 30 fine-grained topics, collected from Pexels, Mixkit, UltraVideo, and OpenHumanVid, with structured captions generated by Qwen2.5-VL-72B. Edit prompts are auto-generated by Doubao-1.5-pro conditioned on these captions and then expert-reviewed, covering 8 categories (style, subject, attribute, subject motion, camera motion, camera angle, quantity, visual effect) with 35 subcategories. The evaluation protocol comprises 12 metrics across three dimensions: Video Quality (DINO-based subject consistency, CLIP-based background consistency, frame-difference flickering, motion-interpolation smoothness, VTSS), Instruction Compliance (VideoCLIP-XL2 semantic similarity for overall and phrase-level alignment, Qwen2.5-VL five-point instruction satisfaction, Grounding DINO quantity accuracy), and Video Fidelity (VideoCLIP-XL2 source-target similarity, CoTracker3 trajectory matching via Hungarian algorithm, Qwen2.5-VL content fidelity). Metric and dimension weights are derived from a 30-annotator user study, and human alignment is validated via Spearman's Rho (all metrics ≥ 0.72) and Fleiss' Kappa of 0.78.

## Results

Across 8 state-of-the-art IVE methods evaluated on IVEBench (600 source videos, 32–1024 frames), no method exceeds a Total Score of 0.67 or an Instruction Compliance score of 0.49, indicating substantial room for improvement. InsV2V and Ditto achieve the highest Total Scores (0.67 on the short subset), while Lucy-Edit-Dev leads in Video Quality (0.82) and VACE in Video Fidelity (0.83). All methods show strong frame-to-frame consistency (SC > 0.83, BC > 0.91) but weak per-frame quality (VTSS ≤ 0.053) and limited instruction adherence, particularly on advanced tasks like subject motion, camera editing, and quantity editing.

## Baselines & Comparisons

InsV2V achieves the highest Total Score (0.67) and best Video Fidelity (0.82) on the short subset, benefiting from its chunked inference strategy that preserves temporal continuity, though its conservative editing approach yields the lowest Instruction Satisfaction (3.10). Ditto matches InsV2V on Total Score (0.67) and leads in Instruction Compliance (0.49) and Instruction Satisfaction (3.87) by successfully handling more diverse editing cases, but trails in Video Fidelity (0.73). Lucy-Edit-Dev delivers the best Video Quality (0.82) and highest VTSS (0.051) with the fastest inference (1.52 s/frame), yet its Instruction Compliance (0.34) and Video Fidelity (0.75) are below InsV2V and Ditto. VACE, though not a native IVE model, achieves strong fidelity (0.83) and high-resolution output (720P) but has the worst Instruction Compliance (0.25) and lowest IS (2.16), confirming its limited ability to follow editing instructions. StableV2V adopts an aggressive editing strategy that boosts IS (3.56) but severely degrades Video Fidelity (0.41) and CF (1.79), the lowest among all methods. AnyV2V and Omni-Video occupy the middle range, with AnyV2V failing on 65 long videos due to OOM errors and Omni-Video restricted to 17 frames. ICVE shows moderate compliance (0.45) but the lowest VTSS (0.017) and is capped at 81 frames, limiting its applicability to longer sequences.

## Relation to Prior Work

IVEBench builds directly on VBench (Huang et al., 2024) for its four temporal/spatial quality metrics (SC, BC, TF, MS) and extends the video editing benchmark lineage (VE-Bench, EditBoard, FiVE, TDVE-Assessor) by shifting the evaluation paradigm from source-target prompt pairs to natural-language instruction-guided editing. It departs from prior benchmarks in three key ways: (1) the dataset is organized around 7 semantic dimensions and 35 task subcategories rather than a narrow set of subject/style/attribute edits, (2) MLLMs (Qwen2.5-VL) are integrated as first-class evaluators for instruction compliance and content fidelity, and (3) the dataset explicitly supports long-sequence evaluation up to 1,024 frames. The prompt generation pipeline leverages Doubao-1.5-pro with structured video captions from Qwen2.5-VL-72B, positioning the benchmark at the intersection of diffusion-based video editing and multimodal LLM-based assessment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

IVEBench fills a critical gap: no prior benchmark was designed to systematically evaluate instruction-guided video editing, which has become the dominant paradigm in the field. The finding that all eight SOTA methods achieve instruction compliance scores below 0.5 and total scores below 0.7 quantifies a substantial capability gap, particularly for temporally complex tasks like subject motion, camera angle, and quantity editing. Open questions include whether current first-frame-propagation architectures can fundamentally handle mid-sequence edits (transitions, event insertion), how methods will scale to 1080P+ resolutions expected by real users, and whether the MLLM-based metrics remain reliable as editing models improve. The benchmark's high human alignment (Spearman's ρ > 0.72 across all 12 metrics, Fleiss' Kappa = 0.78) gives confidence that it will serve as a stable reference point for tracking progress.
