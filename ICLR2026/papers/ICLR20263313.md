## Problem

RL fine-tuning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has proven effective for LLM reasoning but is bottlenecked by the scarcity of high-quality, verifiable training data: human annotations are expensive, LLM-generated data is hallucination-prone and costly, and LLM-based verifiers are slow and inaccurate. This paper asks whether rule-generated synthetic data—questions built from templates and context-free grammars over entirely fictional entities with zero factual overlap to the real world—can nonetheless teach generalizable multi-hop reasoning skills that transfer to knowledge-intensive real-world benchmarks.

## Input / Output

The model receives a multi-hop question together with all relevant evidence in-context: for PhantomWiki this is the full set of 25 Wikipedia-like articles from a randomly generated fictional universe; for GSM-∞ and ReasoningGym it is a self-contained problem statement. The model produces a chain-of-thought reasoning trace followed by a final answer enclosed in <answer>...</answer> tags. Rewards are computed by extracting the tagged answer and comparing it to ground truth via exact match (binary) for GSM-∞, RG-Family, and RG-Knights, or via F1 score for PhantomWiki where questions may have multiple valid answers.

## Method

The paper RL fine-tunes four LLMs (Qwen3-0.6B, Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, Phi-4-mini-reasoning) using GRPO with outcome-only rewards on four rule-generated synthetic datasets: PhantomWiki (multi-hop relational QA), GSM-∞ (arithmetic word problems), and ReasoningGym's Family-Relationships and Knights-Knaves (logical deduction). The key finding is that despite zero factual overlap between the fictional training worlds and real-world evaluation benchmarks (HotpotQA, 2WikiMultihopQA, MuSiQue, CofCA, SynthWorlds-RM), RL fine-tuning on synthetic data yields 56–131% relative F1 improvements, demonstrating that models acquire knowledge composition as a domain-independent skill rather than surfacing memorized pretraining knowledge. Ablations confirm this: format-only RL training does not help models that already produce correct formatting, and SFT on synthetic solution traces improves in-domain accuracy but fails to transfer to real-world benchmarks, whereas RL fine-tuning achieves both in-domain and cross-domain gains. Performance scales monotonically with the number of synthetic training samples without overfitting, and intermediate reasoning traces become more grounded (higher fraction of correct intermediate answers) even though only the final answer is rewarded.

## Results

RL fine-tuning Qwen3-0.6B on PhantomWiki (a rule-generated synthetic multi-hop dataset with zero factual overlap to real-world benchmarks) yields relative F1 score improvements of 56%–131% across five real-world multi-hop QA benchmarks (HotpotQA, 2WikiMultihopQA, MuSiQue, CofCA, SynthWorlds-RM). On HotpotQA specifically, Qwen3-0.6B improves from a base F1 of 0.357 to 0.479 under RL, while Qwen3-1.7B improves from 0.594 to 0.666. SFT on the same synthetic data (GSM-∞) boosts in-domain accuracy from 2.4% to 77.4% but fails to transfer to HotpotQA (0.357→0.400), whereas RL achieves both in-domain gains (2.4%→64.5%) and real-world transfer (0.357→0.479). Performance scales monotonically with the number of synthetic training samples with no sign of overfitting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external competing methods but instead contrasts three training strategies: (1) no fine-tuning (base), (2) SFT on ground-truth solution traces, and (3) RL fine-tuning (GRPO) with verifiable rewards. RL fine-tuning on synthetic data consistently outperforms both the base model and SFT on real-world transfer: on HotpotQA, Qwen3-0.6B goes from 0.357 (base) to 0.479 (RL) versus only 0.400 (SFT), and Qwen3-1.7B from 0.594 to 0.666 (RL) versus 0.576 (SFT, which actually degrades). SFT achieves higher in-domain GSM-∞ accuracy (77.4% vs. 64.5% for Qwen3-0.6B) but overfits to the synthetic task and fails to teach transferable knowledge composition. A format-only RL ablation (Table 1) confirms that the gains for Qwen3 and Phi-4-mini-reasoning come from learning to compose knowledge rather than merely learning output formatting, since these models already produce correctly formatted answers at initialization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the RLVR (Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards) paradigm and the GRPO algorithm (Shao et al., 2024) to study a previously unexplored training signal: rule-generated synthetic data. Prior synthetic data work for LLM training has relied on LLM-generated or distilled data (Liu et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025), which inherits verification challenges and contamination risks, while rule-generated datasets like PhantomWiki and GSM-Infinite have been used almost exclusively for evaluation rather than training. The paper's key departure is demonstrating that these semantically simple, fictional, template-based datasets—previously dismissed as too narrow for real-world transfer—can serve as effective RL training signals that teach a domain-independent knowledge composition skill.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is significant: rule-generated synthetic data is free, requires no GPU or API access, and can be produced at arbitrary scale, offering a scalable alternative to expensive human-annotated or LLM-generated reasoning data. Conceptually, the finding that knowledge composition transfers from fictional worlds with zero factual overlap to real-world multi-hop QA challenges the assumption that domain-specific knowledge or linguistic richness in training data is necessary for reasoning improvement. Open questions include the extent of transferability to other reasoning skills (causal, counterfactual, analogical), how synthetic and knowledge-intensive data interact during training, and why larger models show less consistent transfer—partly confounded by suboptimal hyperparameters due to memory constraints.
