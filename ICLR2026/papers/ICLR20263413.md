## Problem

Existing physics-based human-object interaction (HOI) methods either depend on expensive motion capture data or require labor-intensive manual reward engineering by domain experts, limiting scalability and generalizability across interaction types. No unified framework previously supported long-horizon interactions with diverse object types (static, dynamic, and articulated) while automatically constructing both goal states and reward functions for reinforcement learning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263413/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The VLM planner receives a high-level textual instruction, a top-view rendered image of the scene, and modular prompts, then outputs a sequence of N structured interaction steps as JSON, where each step is a triplet of human-root target, object-root target, and a bipartite RMD graph with edge weights in {0,1,2,3}. The downstream policy consumes a 223-dimensional proprioceptive state plus an automatically constructed goal representation (RMD state vector, destination targets, 9×9 heightmap, and object bounding-box kinematics) to produce 28-DoF joint-position targets for PD controllers. The RMD graph's edge weights encode four discrete relative-motion patterns (stationary contact, approaching, separating, no consistent trend), giving the policy fine-grained spatio-temporal guidance beyond simple point-reaching.

## Method

The core contribution is Relative Movement Dynamics (RMD), a bipartite graph between human body parts and object parts whose edges carry weights encoding relative-motion patterns, enabling GPT-4V to generate semantically grounded, interaction-aware plans without manual reward tuning. Each RMD step is automatically transformed into a goal state (concatenating per-edge relative position-velocity pairs, one-hot edge weights, spatial targets, heightmap, and object kinematics) and a composite reward function with three terms: human-root tracking, object-root tracking, and an RMD alignment reward whose form changes per edge weight (e.g., exp(−(p̃·ṽ)²) for stationary contact, velocity-direction alignment for approaching/separating). The policy is trained with PPO plus an AMP-style adversarial discriminator for natural motion, using a two-stage curriculum (single-task pre-training followed by long-horizon multi-task post-training on the new InterPlay dataset of 1,210 plans). Stage transitions between planned sub-steps trigger automatically when the task reward exceeds 0.9, with adaptive weighting to balance reward terms across the interaction sequence.

## Results

In the long-horizon multi-task scenario (Table 2), the proposed method achieves 75.1% Completion Rate on static interactions, 71.2% on dynamic interactions, and 53.8% on hybrid tasks, substantially outperforming the best baselines (InterPhys: 21.3%/47.8%/27.5%; TokenHSI: 25.2%/52.5%/36.0%). Sub-step Completion Ratio reaches 86.2% (static) and 84.3% (dynamic), while Sub-step Precision drops to 7.7 cm (static) and 13.0 cm (dynamic). In the single-task scenario (Table 3), the method achieves the highest Completion Rate across all six tasks (e.g., 92.6% for Sit, 91.2% for Open, 88.3% for Carry), with the largest gaps over baselines on recovery-heavy tasks like Sit (+69.4% over InterPhys) and Lie (+59.3% over InterPhys).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against InterPhys (Hassan et al., 2023), the proposed method wins decisively on every metric: Completion Rate improves from 21.3% to 75.1% (static) and 47.8% to 71.2% (dynamic), while Sub-step Precision improves from 13.8 cm to 7.7 cm (static). Against TokenHSI (Pan et al., 2025), gains are similarly large: 75.1% vs. 25.2% Completion Rate (static) and 71.2% vs. 52.5% (dynamic), with the gap narrowing somewhat on dynamic tasks where TokenHSI's task tokenizer provides some benefit. Against UniHSI (Xiao et al., 2024), the method achieves 75.1% vs. 37.2% Completion Rate on static interactions and additionally handles dynamic and hybrid tasks that UniHSI cannot support at all. In the single-task setting (Table 3), the method outperforms AMP (Peng et al., 2021) by 35.1 points on Carry Completion Rate (88.3% vs. 53.2%) and 84.6 points on Lie (62.0% vs. 0.9%), confirming that the RMD-based automatic reward design eliminates the need for task-specific handcrafted rewards while producing more natural motions. The ablation replacing GPT-4V with a text-only LLM planner drops Completion Rate from 75.1% to 62.8% (static), underscoring the value of visual scene understanding in the planning stage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the physics-based HOI line (AMP, InterPhys, TokenHSI) by replacing hand-crafted, task-specific reward functions with VLM-generated ones, and departs from the LLM-based reward-code generation paradigm (Eureka, Grove) by using a structured bipartite representation (RMD) that VLMs can reason about spatially rather than iteratively searching over code. It also generalizes UniHSI's chain-of-contacts formulation by adding temporal dynamics (relative velocity, motion-pattern weights) to the part-level interaction graph, enabling dynamic and articulated object interactions that CoC cannot express. The VLM planner (GPT-4V) serves as a plug-in that translates high-level instructions and scene images into executable RMD plans, bridging semantic reasoning and low-level goal-conditioned RL.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Automating both goal-state construction and reward design removes the primary scalability bottleneck in physics-based HOI, making it feasible to cover diverse interaction types without per-task expert engineering. The InterPlay dataset (1,210 long-horizon plans) fills a gap between static-interaction and object-rearrangement benchmarks, enabling systematic evaluation of multi-task, multi-object-type scenarios. Key open questions remain: the framework is single-agent and simulation-only, the VLM planner still produces contradictory labels at 9–15% rates (higher for hybrid tasks), and real-world deployment would require distillation or residual adaptation. Extending to multi-agent coordination, hierarchical long-horizon planning, and tighter planner–controller co-training are natural next steps.
