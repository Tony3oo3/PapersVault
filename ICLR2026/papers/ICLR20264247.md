## Problem

Previous hallucination-mitigation work for LVLMs has focused almost exclusively on the LLM decoder, leaving the visual encoder's role unexplored. This paper is the first to trace object hallucinations to three specific deficiencies in the visual encoder: statistical bias (overemphasis on frequent visual patterns distorting fine-grained perception), inherent bias (generation of erroneous dominant-object representations regardless of input), and vulnerability (sensitivity to minor perturbations producing unreliable features).

## Input / Output

SHIELD takes a standard RGB image and a text prompt (e.g., 'Please describe this image in detail' or a VQA question) and produces a text description or VQA answer through modified autoregressive decoding. At inference time, the method first generates a naive caption via the vanilla LVLM, then applies three correction modules to the visual tokens before feeding them to the LLM. The final output is drawn from a contrastive probability distribution that combines bias-corrected logits with adversarial logits, subject to an adaptive plausibility constraint that truncates low-probability tokens.

## Method

SHIELD is a training-free, three-module framework that corrects visual encoder deficiencies at inference time. (1) Token re-weighting alleviates statistical bias by encoding a naive caption with the paired CLIP text encoder, computing a cosine-similarity matrix between visual and caption tokens, and applying the per-token maximum similarity as a residual weight to redistribute attention toward ground-truth-relevant tokens. (2) Token subtraction mitigates inherent bias by passing K random noise images through the visual encoder, averaging the resulting tokens to estimate the encoder's dominant-object bias, and subtracting this estimate from the visual tokens (pre-computable per model). (3) Contrastive decoding addresses vulnerability by constructing a learnable adversarial perturbation that minimizes cosine similarity between the perturbed image's visual encoding and the naive caption's text encoding, then contrasting the LLM's logits from the natural image against those from the perturbed image during decoding (scaled by α), with an adaptive plausibility constraint (threshold β) to prevent implausible outputs.

## Results

On the CHAIR benchmark (COCO 2014 val, 500 images), SHIELD reduces sentence-level hallucination (C_S) from 48.8 to 36.6 and instance-level (C_I) from 14.2 to 10.3 on LLaVA-1.5, an 18% improvement over the best prior training-free method (OPERA, C_S=44.6). On POPE COCO, SHIELD achieves 87.0% average accuracy and 87.4 F1 on LLaVA-1.5, outperforming OPERA (84.7/85.4) and VCD (84.6/84.4). On the MME hallucination subset, SHIELD raises the total score from 565.3 (vanilla) to 668.3 on LLaVA-1.5, and on the full MME benchmark it improves the overall score from 1632.1 to 1810.8, demonstrating gains beyond hallucination mitigation into general perception.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SHIELD outperforms all training-free baselines (VCD, OPERA, DoLa, ICD, TAME, SID) on every main benchmark. On CHAIR with LLaVA-1.5, SHIELD (C_S=36.6) beats OPERA (44.6) by 8.0 points and VCD (46.8) by 10.2 points; on the extended comparison (Table 16), it also surpasses the strongest training-based methods MCA-LLaVA (38.0) and Less is More (40.2) without any fine-tuning. On POPE COCO adversarial split, SHIELD leads LLaVA-1.5 with 82.5% accuracy vs. OPERA (79.1%) and VCD (80.8%), and on MME hallucination it achieves 668.3 total vs. OPERA (592.3) and VCD (604.6). The one notable exception is InstructBLIP on POPE, where OPERA (84.6/85.1 avg) slightly edges SHIELD (85.0/84.8) on accuracy, attributed to InstructBLIP's Q-Former module constraining the use of modified visual features. On AMBER, SHIELD scores 88.0 vs. OPERA's 86.5, and on the full MME benchmark it reaches 1810.8 vs. OPERA's 1717.2, confirming broad applicability across perception and cognition tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of hallucination-mitigation work (VCD, OPERA, HALC, MARINE, VTI) that targets the LLM decoding stage, instead tracing hallucinations to the visual encoder and identifying three distinct failure modes: statistical bias, inherent bias, and vulnerability. It repurposes the contrastive decoding paradigm from VCD but applies it to adversarially perturbed encoder outputs rather than blurred images, and introduces two novel encoder-level operations (token re-weighting via caption-token similarity and token subtraction via noise-input averaging) that have no direct precedent in prior hallucination literature. The adversarial attack component draws from the robustness literature (FGSM, PGD) but is conditioned on both the image and a naive caption to specifically expose encoder weaknesses relevant to hallucination.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By localizing hallucination to the visual encoder, this work opens a new axis of intervention that is orthogonal to LLM-side decoding strategies, and the training-free design makes it immediately applicable to any CLIP-based LVLM without retraining. Key limitations include the Q-Former bottleneck in InstructBLIP (which constrains the benefit of modified visual features), the computational overhead of caption generation and adversarial optimization (~7.3 s/sample vs. 2.6 s vanilla), and degraded performance on cognition tasks that rely on LLM prior knowledge rather than visual grounding. Open questions include whether visual encoders can be made inherently more robust during pretraining, whether the adversarial attack cost can be reduced for real-time deployment, and whether the encoder-level diagnosis generalizes to non-CLIP architectures.
