## Problem

Modern diffusion-based image editors produce photorealistic, semantically coherent local manipulations that cause existing forgery localization systems to collapse, yet current IFL methods fail to exploit perturbation-driven forensic signals from foundation models, cannot efficiently adapt to newly released editors without catastrophic forgetting, and lack automated pipelines to keep training data current. This paper addresses all three gaps simultaneously with an adaptive architecture, a lightweight fine-tuning protocol, and an automated edit-generation pipeline.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260769/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image (processed at 512×512) and produces a binary pixel-level forgery mask at the original image resolution, where each pixel is labeled as edited or authentic. Internally, the input is also passed through simple image-space perturbations (Gaussian blur, Gaussian noise, JPEG compression) to generate forensic feature streams; the mask adapter outputs a heatmap prompt at the finest HIERA scale that is bilinearly upsampled to 256×256 before entering SAM2's frozen mask decoder, and the final logits are upsampled to full resolution and thresholded at 0.5 via sigmoid.

## Method

Detective SAM freezes SAM2's HIERA encoder, prompt encoder, and mask decoder, and adds two lightweight trainable modules: (i) three single-layer 1×1 convolutional feature adapters (81k params total) that take concatenated original and perturbed HIERA embeddings at each scale and output a residual ΔF correction, retargeting the decoder from object segmentation to forgery localization; and (ii) a mask adapter (887k params) that fuses all feature streams via cross-scale convolutional mixing, applies a lightweight transformer at 16× downsampled resolution for global context, and blends coarse and refined logits through a learned spatial gate to produce an automatic heatmap prompt. Training uses focal + Dice + IoU loss with only the adapters updated, enabling fine-tuning in ~2 hours on a single H100. To keep data current, AutoEditForge is a two-pass pipeline (Florence-2 scene analysis + Gemma 3 LLM strategy selection, then SAM2 segmentation + instruction-based diffusion editing) that generates pixel-accurate edits across four operation types (Replace, Remove, Add, Change Partially), supplying fresh samples for periodic adapter fine-tuning with 20% replay of original training data to prevent catastrophic forgetting.

## Results

Detective SAM achieves an average OOD IoU of 34.68 and F1 of 42.03 across four unseen datasets (CoCoGLIDE, UltraEdit, AutoSplice, NanoBanana), representing a 38.94% relative IoU gain over the strongest baseline (TruFor, 24.96 IoU). On in-distribution data, it reaches 46.48 IoU on MagicBrush and 54.55 IoU on SIDA. On the hardest SOTA benchmarks (FLUX-Bench, QWEN-Bench), all models collapse to ~10–22 IoU, but fine-tuning Detective SAM on just 500 AutoEditForge samples per editor restores performance to 43.08 and 41.44 IoU respectively, while improving OOD NanoBanana IoU from 19.34 to 27.00.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Detective SAM outperforms all seven baselines on every OOD dataset and in average OOD IoU (34.68 vs. 24.96 for the best baseline, TruFor). TruFor is the strongest single baseline, scoring 43.34 IoU on AutoSplice but collapsing to 2.59 on NanoBanana; SAFIRE leads on CoCoGLIDE (42.22) yet degrades sharply elsewhere (11.39 on NanoBanana). The MLLM-based localizers SIDA-7B and FakeShield are particularly weak on recent editors, with SIDA-7B scoring only 0.09 IoU on NanoBanana and 0.89 on FLUX-Bench. PSCC-Net and AdaIFL, designed for legacy splicing/copy-move forgeries, show the largest gaps (12–23 IoU below Detective SAM on average). On the SOTA AutoEditForge benchmarks (Table 2), all baselines drop to 6–22 IoU, confirming that no existing method generalizes to the latest diffusion editors; Detective SAM^SOTA, after fine-tuning on 500 samples per editor, recovers to 43.08 IoU on FLUX-Bench and 41.44 on QWEN-Bench while also improving OOD NanoBanana IoU from 19.34 to 27.00.

## Relation to Prior Work

Detective SAM extends the SAM2-Adapter line of work (Chen et al., 2024) by repurposing lightweight feature adapters for forgery localization rather than camouflage/shadow segmentation, and introduces a mask adapter that converts perturbation-driven forensic embeddings into an automatic heatmap prompt for SAM2's frozen decoder. It departs from prior SAM-based IFL methods (SAFIRE, IMDPrompter) that rely on fixed point grids, reconstruction-error signals, or decoder retraining, instead leveraging explicit perturbation artifacts in the embedding space (building on RIGID, BLUR, MINDER) as a localization prior. The AutoEditForge pipeline differentiates itself from prior synthetic dataset generators (RADAR, GRE, SAFIRE-AUTO, SID-Set) by using LLM-guided scene analysis (Florence-2 + Gemma 3) to select among four instruction-based edit types with SOTA editors, rather than text-conditioned inpainting or random region selection.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's most consequential finding is that all evaluated IFL systems—including MLLM-based detectors—collapse to near-random performance on SOTA diffusion edits (FLUX, QWEN, NanoBanana), demonstrating that current localization methods are not future-proof against rapidly evolving editors. The practical implication is that IFL deployment requires a continual fine-tuning loop, which AutoEditForge operationalizes with only 500 samples per new editor and a 20% replay rate to avoid catastrophic forgetting. Key limitations include sensitivity to perturbation type and strength, inability to handle classical copy-move/splicing forgeries, and still-modest absolute IoU (~35 OOD), leaving open questions about adaptive perturbation strategies, broader forgery coverage, and calibration for real-world deployment with authentic images.
