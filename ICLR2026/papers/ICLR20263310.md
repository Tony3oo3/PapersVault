## Problem

Despite the practical success of model merging, it remains unclear why some independently trained models merge well while others fail, even when achieving comparable individual performance. While mode connectivity establishes that low-loss paths can exist between solutions, the role of well-studied optimizer hyperparameters—learning rate, weight decay, batch size, and data augmentation—on the geometry of the loss landscape *between* solutions has been largely unexplored.

## Input / Output

The method takes two independently trained model weights (bifurcated from a shared checkpoint or trained from separate random initializations) that differ only in their optimizer hyperparameters, and produces merged weights via linear interpolation (α=0.5) or task arithmetic (base model plus scaled task vectors). Merging effectiveness is quantified as the accuracy or loss gain of the merged model relative to the individual single-model baselines, evaluated on held-out test sets. The key experimental design holds architecture, dataset, and training budget fixed while sweeping one optimizer component at a time, enabling causal attribution of merging behavior to specific noise sources.

## Method

The paper identifies the effective noise scale S_eff ∝ η/(B(1−μ)) · tr(Σ_A) as a single unifying quantity that captures the joint influence of learning rate, batch size, momentum, and data augmentation on merging compatibility. The central empirical finding is that merging success is a non-monotonic function of S_eff: too little noise yields negligible gains, too much destabilizes training, and an intermediate 'critical point' maximizes the merging benefit. By decomposing S_eff into its individual components, the authors show that larger learning rates, stronger weight decay (via the effective learning rate mechanism in scale-invariant networks), smaller batch sizes, higher momentum, and data augmentation each independently shift the noise level and exhibit the same qualitative trend. The analysis extends to task arithmetic merging, where the loss landscape geometry depends on the pretrained initialization, and to permutation symmetry and feature alignment (measured via linear CKA), revealing that the optimal noise regime promotes feature diversity that merging can exploit. Results are validated across MLP, ResNet18, DenseNet121, GPT, and CLIP ViT architectures on vision and language tasks, including transfer learning and multi-task settings.

## Results

Merging effectiveness is a non-monotonic function of the effective noise scale S̃ = η/(B(1−μ)), with a distinct optimum: too little or too much noise yields near-zero gains, while a moderate regime maximizes benefits. On CIFAR-100/ResNet18, solutions trained with η=0.2 achieve a +1.2% median accuracy gain over single models, versus only +0.2% for η=0.01, despite similar single-model accuracy (~75%). In transfer learning (CLIP ViT-B/16 on WILDS-FMoW), accuracy gain correlates almost perfectly with learning rate (Pearson r=0.981), with the largest η=1e-4 yielding 4× the gain of η=1e-6. For multi-task merging, TIES achieves 88.0% normalized accuracy versus 85.9% for Task Arithmetic at the optimal η=3e-5, a +2% improvement.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares Task Arithmetic (Ilharco et al., 2023) and TIES Merging (Yadav et al., 2023) as the two merging methods under varying optimizer noise. At the optimal learning rate η=3e-5, TIES outperforms Task Arithmetic by +2.1% normalized accuracy (88.0% vs. 85.9%), and TIES also shapes a wider, more stable loss landscape across α-interpolation. However, at lower learning rates (η ≤ 1e-5), Task Arithmetic actually outperforms TIES by 3–7%, suggesting TIES's sign-magnitude conflict resolution is most beneficial when the task vectors are large (high noise). Both methods exhibit the same non-monotonic dependence on effective noise: merging degrades sharply at η=1e-4 (the stability limit), where TA drops to 69.3% and TIES to 61.4%. The paper also shows that merging models trained with mismatched learning rates (off-antidiagonal) consistently underperforms, with the largest η=1e-4 model being the most incompatible partner for all other settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mode connectivity literature (Garipov et al., 2018; Frankle et al., 2020) and the task arithmetic / TIES merging framework (Ilharco et al., 2023; Yadav et al., 2023) by identifying a single unifying quantity—the effective noise scale—that governs merging compatibility. Prior work connected optimizer noise to the flatness or generalization of *individual* minima (Jastrzebski et al., 2017; Keskar et al., 2016; Mandt et al., 2017), and the effective learning rate concept explained weight decay's role in scale-invariant networks (Van Laarhoven, 2017; Hoffer et al., 2018). The key departure is showing that this same noise magnitude also shapes the *global* loss landscape geometry between independently trained solutions, predicting when they can be successfully merged via linear interpolation or task arithmetic.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The non-monotonic relationship between effective noise and merging success provides a practical, unified criterion for practitioners: rather than trial-and-error over multiple hyperparameters, one can target an intermediate noise regime to maximize mergeability. This suggests hyperparameter search for merging can be simplified to fewer dimensions. However, the paper offers no theoretical guarantees, no large-scale validation, and only studies standard merging methods (linear interpolation, task arithmetic, TIES). Open questions include how to systematically tune noise, architecture, and pretraining to produce inherently mergeable models, and whether the critical noise point shifts with model scale or task complexity.
