## Problem

Existing ML-based DFT acceleration methods predict Hamiltonian matrices or density matrices as SCF initial guesses, but these targets are intrinsically non-transferable: they encode global pairwise interactions and fail catastrophically on molecules larger than those seen during training (RIC >179% or non-convergence). Although the electron density is the fundamental observable in Kohn-Sham DFT and is inherently local and transferable, no practical procedure existed to convert a predicted density into a usable SCF initial guess, leaving this principled paradigm unexplored.

## Input / Output

The model takes a 3D molecular structure (atomic coordinates and species) and outputs per-atom electron density expansion coefficients in a compact atom-centered auxiliary basis (def2-universal-jfit or even-tempered Gaussian basis), with irreducible representations from l=0 to l=4. Because the auxiliary basis is atom-centered, the number of coefficients scales linearly with system size rather than quadratically as with Hamiltonian or density matrices. These coefficients are then converted into an initial Fock matrix by constructing the Coulomb matrix via three-center integrals and the XC matrix via grid evaluation, which serves as the starting point for the SCF iteration.

## Method

The core contribution is a new paradigm that targets electron density coefficients rather than Hamiltonian or density matrix elements, leveraging the fact that density is local and transferable across chemical environments. Two E(3)-equivariant backbones (NequIP and QHNet) are adapted by replacing their original prediction heads with a single species-dependent equivariant linear layer that maps final node features to density coefficients spanning irreps l=0–4. Training uses a composite MAE + RMSE loss on the coefficients, with the ground truth derived from L2 projection of the converged DFT density onto the auxiliary basis. The predicted coefficients are converted to an initial Fock matrix via density fitting: the Coulomb matrix is assembled from three-center two-electron integrals, and the XC matrix is evaluated on Becke grids using the density and its gradient (with von Weizsäcker approximation for meta-GGA kinetic energy density and SAD-based approximation for hybrid HF exchange). A model trained exclusively on molecules up to 20 atoms achieves ~33% SCF iteration reduction on 60-atom systems and maintains 100% convergence up to 900 atoms without retraining, while Hamiltonian-based baselines fail entirely beyond ~120 atoms.

## Results

On the SCFbench OOD test set (molecules 26–60 atoms, 3× larger than the 20-atom training set), the best density-coefficient model (NequIP-L, def2-universal-jfit) achieves a Relative Iteration Count (RIC) of 66.68% with 100% SCF convergence, compared to 179.47% RIC and 97.43% convergence for Hamiltonian prediction (QHNet) and 91.69% RIC for density-matrix prediction. On the QMugs scalability test (100–200 atoms), the density-based method maintains 73–82% RIC with 100% convergence, while Hamiltonian and density-matrix methods drop to 0% convergence beyond 120 atoms. The method also generalizes across functionals and basis sets: a model trained on PBE/def2-SVP achieves 85.47% OOD RIC on B3LYP5/def2-TZVP without retraining.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the QHNet Hamiltonian baseline (Yu et al., 2023), the density-coefficient approach wins decisively on OOD transferability: 66.68% vs. 179.47% RIC (NequIP-L vs. QHNet-H), meaning the Hamiltonian model actually *increases* SCF iterations by ~80% on larger molecules while the density model reduces them by ~33%. Against the density-matrix baseline (Shao et al., 2023, implemented with QHNet), the density approach also wins on OOD (66.68% vs. 91.69% RIC) and maintains 100% convergence versus 99.71%. The Wavefunction Alignment Loss (WALoss, Li et al., 2025c), designed to improve Hamiltonian scalability, fails to resolve the transferability problem: all tested WALoss variants still yield OOD RICs of 170–183%, confirming the issue is inherent to the Hamiltonian target rather than the training objective. QHFlow (Kim et al., 2025), a high-order equivariant flow-matching Hamiltonian model, achieves 56.80% ID RIC but degrades to 146.97% OOD RIC, again far worse than the density-based method's 66.68%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of ML-accelerated DFT, which has focused on predicting the Kohn-Sham Hamiltonian (QHNet, PhiSNet, SPHNet, QHFlow) or the density matrix (Shao et al., Hazra et al.), by instead targeting electron density expansion coefficients in a compact auxiliary basis. It builds on the equivariant GNN backbones of NequIP and QHNet, replacing their prediction heads with a species-dependent equivariant linear layer that outputs density coefficients. The key conceptual departure is implementing the previously missing procedure to convert predicted density coefficients into a usable SCF initial guess (via density-fitting Coulomb and XC matrices), which the authors identify as the direct reason this principled approach was never realized despite prior electron density prediction work (Brockherde et al., Grisafi et al., Fu et al.).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first method to demonstrate robust size transferability for ML-accelerated DFT: a model trained on ≤20-atom molecules accelerates 900-atom systems without retraining, while Hamiltonian-based baselines fail catastrophically (RIC >170%, non-convergence) beyond ~120 atoms. The released SCFbench dataset (43,862 molecules, 7 elements, three auxiliary basis sets, dedicated OOD test set) provides a much-needed public benchmark for evaluating transferability in this space. Open questions include closing the performance gap for more expressive auxiliary bases (ETB β=1.5), extending to the full periodic table and periodic systems, and reducing the pre-SCF overhead (currently ~1.3× wall-time speedup, with the eigensolver and Python-based integration as bottlenecks).
