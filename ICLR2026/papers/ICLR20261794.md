## Problem

Peptide ML lacks standardized benchmarks: data sources are scattered across heterogeneous databases with inconsistent representations (especially for non-canonical peptides), preprocessing steps like redundancy removal and dataset splitting vary arbitrarily across studies, and evaluation metrics differ, making fair comparison of methods impossible. This fragmentation obscures genuine methodological progress and inflates apparent model performance through data leakage and overly simplified tasks.

## Input / Output

Inputs are peptide sequences in FASTA format for canonical peptides (20 standard amino acids, length ≤150, with a recommended ≤50 version) and SMILES/BILN/HELM representations for non-canonical peptides (613 unique monomers); PepPI tasks additionally take a protein sequence. Outputs are binary classification labels (active/inactive) evaluated by ROC-AUC, or continuous regression values (MIC in lg(μM), IC50, binding affinity in -lg(M), permeability in log(cm/s)) evaluated by MAE. All 35 datasets use a fixed 8:1:1 train/validation/test split with task-specific splitting strategies (hybrid-split, ECFP-split, or cold-start protein-based split).

## Method

PepBenchmark unifies three components: (1) PepBenchData, a curated collection of 35 datasets (29 canonical, 6 non-canonical) across 7 pharmacological groups with a consolidated 613-monomer library and mutual BILN/HELM/SMILES conversion; (2) PepBenchPipeline, which introduces BDNegSamp—a four-step negative sampling strategy combining task-correlation filtering, similarity deduplication, and distribution-matched sampling on length/charge/hydrophobicity—and a hybrid-split protocol that first clusters sequences by representative kmers (identified via Fisher's exact test) to prevent kmer leakage, then applies MMseqs2 similarity clustering to the remainder; (3) PepBenchLeaderboard, which benchmarks four model families (ECFP6+RF/XGBoost, GCN/GAT/GIN/Pepland, ChemBERTa/PeptideCLM/PepDoRA, ESM2/DPLM/ProtBERT) under unified metrics. A key empirical contribution is ESM-150M-F, obtained by continued pretraining ESM2-150M on 1.9M short peptides (<50 AAs) from UniRef50, which achieves state-of-the-art classification performance by addressing the severe underrepresentation of short sequences in standard protein pretraining corpora.

## Results

Across 22 canonical peptide classification datasets (SCPP), the peptide-finetuned PLM ESM2-150M-F achieves the best average ROC-AUC, with standout scores of 93.2% on antibacterial and 92.0% on antimicrobial (52,941 samples). FP-based Random Forest with ECFP6 fingerprints remains a strong data-efficient baseline, ranking first or second on small datasets (e.g., 82.2% on ace_inhibitory, 81.1% on antioxidant). For non-canonical peptide classification (SNCPP), FP-based models dominate with ~96% average ROC-AUC (RF: 95.9%, XGBoost: 96.0%), far outperforming GNN (GIN: 89.3%) and SMILES-based (ChemBERTa: 89.1%) models. On PepPI tasks, GNN (GIN: 61.3% AUC) and SMILES-based (PepDoRA: 59.4%) models become competitive, reversing the hierarchy seen in single-peptide prediction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PLM-based models (ESM2 family, DPLM) consistently outperform all other families on canonical peptide classification, with ESM2-150M-F achieving the highest average ROC-AUC; the pretrained ESM2-8M (80.3 avg) beats its randomly initialized counterpart ESM2-8M-S (77.2 avg), confirming the value of large-scale protein pretraining. FP-based Random Forest with ECFP6 is the strongest non-PLM baseline, frequently matching or exceeding PLMs on small datasets (<4,000 samples) such as ace_inhibitory (82.2 vs. 80.2) and antioxidant (81.1 vs. 61.5). GNN-based models (GCN, GAT, GIN) and SMILES-based models (ChemBERTa, PeptideCLM, PepDoRA) systematically underperform on single-peptide tasks, with GIN averaging ~75% AUC versus ~92% for top PLMs on large AMP datasets. Notably, domain-specific pretrained GNNs (Pepland) and SMILES models (PepDoRA) perform *worse* than their non-pretrained counterparts (GIN, ChemBERTa), suggesting negative transfer from naive peptide-specific pretraining. On non-canonical peptide tasks where PLMs are inapplicable, FP-based models (RF: 95.9%, XGBoost: 96.0% avg AUC) decisively outperform GNN (GIN: 89.3%) and SMILES-based (ChemBERTa: 89.1%, PeptideCLM: 70.3%) approaches. For PepPI, the hierarchy shifts: GIN (61.3% AUC) and PepDoRA (59.4%) become competitive with or surpass PLMs (ESM2-8M: 60.2%), indicating atom-level representations are better suited for cross-molecular interaction modeling.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the peptide ML benchmarking lineage initiated by UniDL4BioPep, Peptipedia, and AutoPeptideML, but departs from them by unifying data curation, preprocessing, and evaluation into a single reproducible framework. Methodologically, it builds directly on ESM2/ProtBERT for PLM-based representations and on AutoPeptideML's negative-sampling-pool and similarity-based splitting ideas, but introduces two key departures: BDNegSamp (adding task-correlation filtering to avoid pseudo-negatives) and kmer-split/hybrid-split (addressing the previously overlooked kmer leakage problem that inflates model performance). For non-canonical peptides, it unifies the fragmented BILN, HELM, and SMILES representations into a single 613-monomer library, a practical contribution absent from prior peptide benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PepBenchmark is likely to become the de facto standard for peptide ML evaluation, resolving the fragmentation that has made cross-study comparisons unreliable. The kmer leakage finding has broad implications: any peptide classification benchmark that uses random or similarity-only splitting may be overestimating model generalization, and the hybrid-split protocol should be adopted as a minimum standard. Key open questions remain: the absence of structural data limits the benchmark to sequence-level tasks; non-canonical peptide modeling is still data-starved (only ~9.5K sequences); and the negative transfer observed from domain-specific pretraining (Pepland, PepDoRA underperforming their non-pretrained baselines) suggests that current atom-level pretraining strategies for peptides are fundamentally misaligned with what downstream tasks require.
