## Problem

Multi-domain graph pre-training has achieved empirical success in building graph foundation models, but a fundamental question remains unaddressed: how is knowledge actually integrated or transferred across domains? Existing solutions lack a principled theoretical framework that consistently relates pre-training to domain adaptation, limiting the ability to assess transfer difficulty, especially for unseen graphs.

## Input / Output

The method takes text-free graphs (node feature matrices X ∈ R^{|V|×F} and adjacency structures) from multiple source domains for pre-training, and a single target graph with k labeled samples per class (k ∈ {1,5}) for few-shot adaptation. Outputs include standard task metrics (node/edge classification ACC, graph classification AUC) plus a Geometric Transfer Metric (GTM) that decomposes into holonomy disagreement (measuring 'twisting') and curvature disagreement (measuring 'bending'), providing an interpretable, per-sample measure of how geometrically alien the target is to the pre-trained manifold.

## Method

GRAPHGLUE establishes a theory of Neural Manifold Gluing that merges arbitrary graph datasets into a unified, smooth Riemannian manifold. Local geometry at each graph is characterized by a (k,M)-sparse perturbation that generates tangent vectors, followed by QR-decomposition with length recovery to form an Adaptive Orthogonal Frame (AOF) defining the local metric tensor. Local pieces are glued via edge tangent translations (proven isometries ensuring metric compatibility), a holonomy loss enforcing triangle triviality (eliminating path-dependent offsets), and a curvature loss based on log-determinant smoothness (ensuring C² continuity). Pre-training uses EMA-based Riemannian prototyping for efficient batched processing with a contrastive loss separating domain semantics on the manifold; adaptation employs a learnable prompt matrix Q, a Riemannian Mixture-of-Experts where prototypes act as experts, and gluing the target to the pre-trained manifold via holonomy and curvature penalties. The framework also yields a geometric scaling law: more pre-training datasets produce a smoother manifold and improve transferability, validated empirically with logarithmic scaling curves.

## Results

GRAPHGLUE achieves the best or near-best performance across all six datasets in the leave-one-out cross-domain few-shot setting. In the 1-shot regime, it leads on Computers (59.5% ACC vs. 54.5% for GCOPE) and Reddit (67.1% ACC vs. 62.8% for SAMGPT). In the 5-shot regime, the advantage widens on Reddit (85.0% vs. 75.1% for SAMGPT) and FB15k-237 link classification (81.5% vs. 79.3% for GCOPE). On PROTEINS graph classification, GRAPHGLUE reaches 65.3% AUC (5-shot), marginally ahead of GCOPE (64.8%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GRAPHGLUE outperforms all 12 baselines on the majority of dataset–shot combinations. Against the strongest graph foundation model baselines, it leads by 5.0 points on Computers 1-shot (59.5 vs. GCOPE 54.5), 4.3 points on Reddit 1-shot (67.1 vs. SAMGPT 62.8), and 9.9 points on Reddit 5-shot (85.0 vs. SAMGPT 75.1). On FB15k-237 link classification, it edges out GCOPE by 1.5 (1-shot) and 2.2 (5-shot) points. On Arxiv 5-shot, GCOPE (39.1) slightly surpasses GRAPHGLUE (37.0), the only clear loss in the table. Supervised GNNs (GCN, GraphSAGE, GIN) and self-supervised GNNs (DGI, GCC, GraphMAE) are consistently 10–50 points behind, confirming that multi-domain pre-training is essential for few-shot cross-domain transfer. The case study (Fig. 4) further shows that while GCOPE suffers negative transfer when adding semantically distinct domains (PROTEINS, HIV), GRAPHGLUE maintains steady gains, validating the geometric gluing mechanism.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant multi-domain graph pre-training line (GFT, SAMGPT, GCOPE, MDGFM) that learns shared or invariant representations via codebooks, motifs, or computation trees without a principled account of how knowledge is actually integrated across domains. It also extends Riemannian graph representation learning (hyperbolic GCN, product-manifold GNNs, RiemannGFM) from task-specific fixed manifolds to a general, data-driven unified manifold constructed via 'neural manifold gluing.' The local geometry learning draws on Cartan's method of moving frame (Tron et al., 2024), repurposing it for deep learning by replacing the classical frame with an adaptive orthogonal frame derived from sparse graph perturbations, while the global consistency conditions (holonomy triviality, Ricci curvature smoothing) are novel applications of differential geometry to graph pre-training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first differential-geometry-based theoretical framework for multi-domain graph pre-training, offering interpretable quantities (holonomy disagreement, curvature disagreement) that quantify transfer difficulty and a geometric scaling law linking dataset quantity to manifold smoothness and transferability. However, the practical implementation relies on approximations (2-order smoothness in place of ∞-order, sampled triangles in place of full cycle space), and the empirical validation covers only six benchmark datasets with moderate gains over strong baselines (4–5% in the best cases). Open questions include whether the geometric perspective generalizes beyond node/edge/graph classification, how the framework scales to very large graphs where triangle sampling becomes infeasible, and whether the Riemannian MoE and EMA prototyping components are truly necessary or whether simpler geometric regularizations suffice.
