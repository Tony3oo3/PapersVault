## Problem

Despite the widespread empirical use of low-bit quantization in training large-scale models, a rigorous theoretical characterization of how quantization affects generalization performance (population risk) remains absent, even for the simplest linear regression setting. Prior theoretical work focused on convergence guarantees for training loss of quantized optimizers, leaving open the fundamental question of how quantization error interacts with model dimension, dataset size, and the resulting excess risk.

## Input / Output

The method operates on i.i.d. batches of feature vectors (in a finite or countably infinite-dimensional Hilbert space) and scalar labels, with quantization applied independently to five components: data features, labels, model parameters, activations, and output gradients. The output is the iterate-averaged weight vector after N steps of constant-stepsize SGD, along with closed-form excess risk upper bounds expressed as functions of the full eigen-spectrum of the quantized data covariance, sample size, stepsize, batch size, and per-component quantization error parameters. The analysis distinguishes two quantization error models—additive (constant-step, integer-like) and multiplicative (input-dependent-step, FP-like)—that map to practical INT and FP formats.

## Method

The paper establishes the first systematic excess risk bound for quantized SGD in high-dimensional linear regression, decomposing the risk into variance error, bias error, and an additional approximation error term arising from data quantization. The key technical insight is a two-fold effect: data quantization distorts the effective data covariance spectrum (additive quantization adds a constant noise floor ε_d across all eigenvalues, flattening the tail; multiplicative quantization scales the entire spectrum by (1+ε_d), preserving relative decay), while parameter, activation, and gradient quantization amplify the effective noise variance σ_G^(q) during training. Crucially, the noise amplification from activation and gradient quantization under additive quantization is suppressed by a factor of 1/B (batch size), whereas under multiplicative quantization it is coupled with the signal magnitude and independent of B. Under polynomial-decay spectra (λ_i ≈ i^{-a}), the excess risk under multiplicative quantization is dimension-free, while additive quantization incurs a d^{a}ε_d term that diverges as d→∞, yielding a concrete criterion for when FP quantization outperforms integer quantization (m_d ≥ b_d − (a/2)log₂ d).

## Results

The paper's headline result is a dimension-free excess risk bound under multiplicative (FP-like) quantization: E[E(w̄_N)] ≲ ε_d/(1+ε_d) + d^{1−a} + N^{1/a−1}(1+ε_o)[1+ε_p+ε_a(1+ε_p)](1+ε_d)^{1/a}, which remains bounded as d→∞. In contrast, the additive (INT-like) quantization bound contains the term d^a·ε_d/(1+d^a·ε_d), which diverges in high dimensions. Numerically, with d=200, B=1, and ε∈{0.001, 0.005, 0.01}, multiplicative quantization largely retains full-precision generalization across all levels, while additive quantization shows progressive degradation. When varying d∈{50,100,200,400} at fixed ε=0.01, multiplicative quantization preserves performance but additive quantization deteriorates sharply with dimension.

**Table: Key theoretical differences between multiplicative and additive quantization (Corollary 4.3, polynomial-decay spectrum λ_i ≈ i^{−a}).**

| Property | Multiplicative (FP-like) | Additive (INT-like) |
|---|---|---|
| Data quantization risk term | ε_d / (1+ε_d) (dimension-free) | d^a·ε_d / (1+d^a·ε_d) (diverges as d→∞) |
| Spectral distortion | Scales spectrum by (1+ε_d), preserves decay | Adds constant floor ε_d, flattens tail |
| Activation/gradient noise scaling | Coupled to ‖w*‖²_H, independent of B | Suppressed by 1/B |
| Parameter noise scaling | Coupled to ‖w*‖²_H, independent of B | Scales with tr(H)+d·ε_d, independent of B |
| High-dim applicability (d→∞) | Yes (dimension-free bound) | No (bound diverges) |
| FP vs INT crossover | FP preferred when m_d ≥ b_d − (a/2)log₂ d | INT preferred when b_d ≥ m_d + (a/2)log₂ d |

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is full-precision constant-stepsize SGD with iterate averaging (Zou et al., 2023), whose excess risk is R_0 = (k_0*/N + Nγ²Σ_{i>k*}λ_i²)·[α_B(‖w*‖²_{I_{0:k*}}/(Nγ) + ‖w*‖²_{H_{k*:∞}}) + σ²/B] / (1−γα_B tr(H)) + bias terms. The paper's central comparison shows that multiplicative quantization can match R_0 in order under the relatively mild condition ε_d ≲ 1 ∧ R_0/‖w*‖²_H, while additive quantization requires the much stricter spectrum-dependent condition ε_d ≲ √((k_0*/N + Nγ²Σλ_i²)/(Nγ²(d−k_0*))) ∧ R_0·λ_d/‖w*‖²_H. For activation and output gradient quantization, the additive scheme enjoys a favorable 1/B suppression (ε_a, ε_o ≲ σ² + Bα_B·(variance terms)), meaning larger batches relax precision requirements, whereas the multiplicative scheme's noise is signal-dependent and does not benefit from batch averaging. Empirically (Figure 1), multiplicative quantization at ε=0.01 with d=400 and B=1 maintains population risk close to the full-precision baseline, while additive quantization at the same settings shows substantially elevated risk, confirming the theoretical prediction that additive quantization is ill-suited for high-dimensional settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the sharp finite-sample excess risk analysis of constant-stepsize SGD for high-dimensional linear regression (Zou et al., 2023) to the quantized setting, incorporating the stochastic quantization framework from Markov et al. (2023) and the polynomial-decay spectrum framework from Lin et al. (2024). The key departure from prior quantization theory is a shift from convergence guarantees on training loss to generalization bounds on population risk: while Markov et al. and others characterized how quantization affects optimization convergence, this paper is the first to rigorously characterize how quantization of data, parameters, activations, and gradients jointly affects the excess risk of the learned model. The analysis also builds on the NTK-based generalization study of quantized networks by Zhang et al. (2022), but overcomes its limitations by providing explicit bounds in terms of dimension, sample size, and quantization error under a broader set of quantization targets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first theoretical lens for understanding how low-precision training shapes generalization, offering practitioners concrete conditions under which FP (multiplicative) versus integer (additive) quantization is preferable — notably that FP quantization can outperform integer quantization even with fewer bits in high-dimensional settings. The finding that additive quantization's constant noise floor destroys spectral decay (making high-dimensional learning vacuous) while multiplicative quantization preserves it is a clear conceptual insight with direct implications for hardware design. Key limitations include the restriction to linear regression, single-pass SGD with constant step size, and the absence of matching lower bounds; extending the framework to over-parameterized neural networks, multi-pass training, and learning rate schedules are identified as important open directions.
