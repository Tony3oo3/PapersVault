## Problem

Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) frequently generate far more reasoning steps than necessary, a phenomenon called 'overthinking' that wastes substantial computational resources. Existing RL-based efficiency methods struggle because LRMs naturally produce only verbose outputs during rollout, so artificially shortened answers introduce a distribution mismatch that destabilizes training. The paper asks how to expose the model to genuinely short, distributionally consistent reasoning paths so it can learn to stop thinking proactively.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text problem (e.g., a math word problem) as input and produces a reasoning chain followed by a final answer enclosed in \boxed{} tags. During training, each query yields a group of 12 rollouts: 3 full-length trajectories and 9 truncated-and-completed variants at progressive cutoff points (25%, 50%, 75% of the original length), each terminated by an explicit stop-thinking cue. At inference, the model autonomously decides when sufficient information has been accumulated and emits the stop signal, yielding outputs that are typically 30–50% shorter than the base model while maintaining or improving accuracy.

## Method

JET (Just-Enough Thinking) is a reinforcement learning method built on DAPO that trains LRMs to terminate reasoning once sufficient evidence has been gathered. Its two-stage rollout construction first generates full autoregressive trajectories, then applies Progressive Early-Stopping (PES) to truncate each trajectory at multiple intermediate points and append a stop-thinking cue, producing short reasoning paths that remain aligned with the model's own generation distribution. A quality-controlled length reward is applied only to correct answers within each group, normalized per-question between the shortest and longest correct responses, so that shorter correct trajectories receive higher reward while incorrect ones receive zero length reward. The paper provides a gradient analysis showing that the short forced suffix introduced by truncation contributes negligibly to the sequence-level importance ratio, preserving stable policy updates within the trust region. Pilot experiments on MATH500 confirm the core hypothesis: retaining only the first 75% of a reasoning chain preserves over 90% of originally correct solutions, and truncation can even correct some initially wrong answers by removing misleading redundant steps.

## Results

On the DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B backbone, JET achieves an average accuracy of 63.3% (+4.4 over Base) while reducing average output length by 29.8% (from 4,765 to 2,710 tokens) across five math benchmarks. The headline result is on Olympiad, where JET improves accuracy by 4.6% (47.0→51.6) while cutting output length by 46.3% (7,679→4,121 tokens). On the 7B model, JET gains +0.8% average accuracy with a 26.3% length reduction. JET also generalizes to out-of-domain tasks, achieving +4.4% average accuracy on CSQA/GPQA-Diamond/MMLU with 39.7% token compression (1.5B).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DAPO, JET improves average accuracy by +2.2% (63.3 vs. 61.1) while achieving 29.8% token compression versus DAPO's mere 2.0%, making JET substantially more efficient. Compared to AdaThink, JET gains +4.5% accuracy (63.3 vs. 58.8) and compresses 29.8% vs. 20.3%, as AdaThink's binary think/no-think mode selection fails to adapt granularity within the 'think' mode. Against Laser-D, JET trades a small 0.5% accuracy drop (63.3 vs. 63.8) for a dramatically better 29.8% compression versus Laser-D's 3.6%, and on the 7B model JET's 26.3% compression far exceeds Laser-D's 9.8%. Versus LCR1, JET achieves +8.9% higher accuracy (63.3 vs. 54.4) at the cost of slightly less compression (29.8% vs. 36.8%), confirming that LCR1's aggressive pruning sacrifices correctness. Overall, JET is the only method that simultaneously improves accuracy over Base and achieves >25% token compression across all five math benchmarks on the 1.5B model.

## Relation to Prior Work

JET builds directly on DAPO as its RL backbone, extending the objective to incorporate truncated-and-completed trajectories alongside full ones. Conceptually, it departs from two dominant prior lines—adaptive thinking-mode selection (AdaThink, DAST) and explicit length-penalty rewards (Laser, LCR1, O1-Pruner)—by addressing the distributional mismatch problem: rather than externally constructing short answers or switching between think/no-think modes, JET truncates the model's own generated rollouts, preserving alignment with the model's natural generation distribution. The motivation draws on Evidence Accumulation Models from cognitive science, framing LRM reasoning as a threshold-based process where early steps suffice.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for anyone deploying LRMs at scale: a 46% token reduction with simultaneous accuracy gains on Olympiad-level math, generalizing to commonsense and professional QA benchmarks, directly lowers inference cost. The pilot finding that truncation can *correct* initially wrong answers (ECR > 0) challenges the assumption that more reasoning is always better, suggesting a non-monotonic accuracy-length relationship. Open questions include whether the approach transfers to full-scale (non-distilled) reasoning models, whether the fixed progressive truncation schedule (25% increments) is near-optimal, and whether the method extends to multi-turn or tool-augmented reasoning settings where 'stopping' has different semantics.
