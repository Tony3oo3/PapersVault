## Problem

Existing studies on LLM-as-a-Judge bias are limited to verifying a small set of predefined, known biases (e.g., length, position, self-preference), leaving a critical gap: there is no systematic, automated method to discover previously unknown biases at scale. This paper asks how potential biases in judge models can be uncovered in an active, large-scale, and fully LLM-driven manner rather than relying on manual effort and fixed bias lists.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes a target judge LLM, a preference dataset of (instruction, chosen, rejected) triplets (RewardBench, ~3k samples), a teacher LLM (Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct) that performs perturbation and bias identification, an initial library of 7 known biases, and a held-out test set (JudgeBench, 620 pairs) for validation. It outputs an expanded bias library (14–48 validated biases per model depending on scale), per-domain error rates on bias-perturbed data, and the 1,178-sample JudgeBench-Pro benchmark. A notable design choice is that the teacher model performs reasoning-based analysis of misjudgment explanations rather than subjective preference judgments, explicitly to avoid preference leakage.

## Method

BIASSCOPE is a two-phase iterative loop (up to 4 rounds) that self-expands and self-converges a bias library. In the Discovery phase, the teacher model perturbs each rejected response using a randomly sampled bias from the current library; the target model evaluates the perturbed pairs, and misjudged instances are collected. A key mechanism—error cascading (DeeperExplain)—prompts the target model to re-explain its own erroneous reasoning, eliciting deeper and more diverse bias signals. The teacher model then identifies candidate biases from these enriched explanations, merges near-duplicates via pairwise comparison, and removes already-known biases. In the Validation phase, each candidate bias is used to perturb the entire test set, and the bias is retained only if the target model's error rate on the perturbed set exceeds its baseline error rate. Discovered biases can further be used to construct adversarial preference data for DPO training, which empirically reduces the model's evaluation error rate compared to DPO on unperturbed data.

## Results

BIASSCOPE discovers 14–48 validated biases per target model (depending on model size), and injecting these biases into JudgeBench increases average error rates by +6.9% overall, with the math domain most vulnerable (+11.1%). LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct shows the largest degradation (+10.8% overall), while stronger models like Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct still degrade by +10.1%. On the newly constructed JudgeBench-Pro, four of five powerful closed-source models perform at or below random guessing (50%), with GPT-4o reaching 74.7% error rate and an average error rate 25.9% higher than on the original JudgeBench.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark BIASSCOPE against competing bias-detection frameworks; instead, its primary comparison is internal: original error rates versus error rates after injecting discovered biases. Across seven target models, the average error rate increases by +6.9% (range: +4.5% to +10.8%), confirming that the discovered biases are genuine and model-specific. In ablation comparisons, the Early-Validate strategy discovers more biases (29 vs. 27 for LLaMA-3.1-8B) than Late-Validate, and the DeeperExplain error-cascading step adds 2–5 additional biases per model. For teacher model choice, GPT-OSS-120B identifies 19 validated biases on LLaMA-3.1-8B (vs. 9 for GPT-OSS-20B), yet even the weaker teacher still raises error rates above the original baseline (+6.0% vs. +6.3%), indicating the effects are not artifacts of teacher-model bias. On JudgeBench-Pro, the ten biases originally discovered in Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct transfer to closed-source models, with GPT-4o degrading from 36.7% to 74.7% error rate and Kimi-k2-Instruct from 27.4% to 56.0%, while Doubao-seed-1-6-250615 remains most robust at 20.4%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM-as-a-Judge bias literature, which has primarily focused on verifying a small set of known biases (length, position, self-preference, gender) through targeted benchmarks like CALM (Ye et al., 2024) and JudgeBench (Tan et al., 2025). BIASSCOPE departs from this manual, predefined-bias paradigm by introducing an iterative, LLM-driven loop that automatically discovers previously unidentified biases through perturbation, error cascading, and teacher-model analysis. The framework also connects to DPO (Rafailov et al., 2024) by demonstrating that bias-augmented preference data can mitigate discovered biases during alignment training, bridging bias detection and bias mitigation in a single pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Given the widespread adoption of LLM-as-a-Judge in benchmarking, data curation, and model evaluation, the finding that even GPT-4o achieves a 74.7% error rate on JudgeBench-Pro underscores a critical gap in current evaluation robustness. The framework's ability to scale bias discovery across model families and sizes provides a practical tool for the community, while the JudgeBench-Pro benchmark offers a more challenging stress test. Key limitations include the computational cost of iterative mining on large datasets, reliance on a single objective benchmark (JudgeBench) for validation, and the open question of whether discovered biases generalize to non-objective, preference-based evaluation settings.
