## Problem

Hyperbolic embedding research has progressed independently in three communities—machine learning, network theory, and algorithms—yet cross-referencing and comparative studies between them are virtually absent. The ML community largely treats Nickel & Kiela's Poincaré and Lorentz embeddings as foundational, unaware of earlier and faster algorithmic approaches like BFKL (ESA 2016), while the algorithmic community lacks awareness of ML-driven embedders. This paper addresses the gap by providing the first systematic experimental comparison of 14 hyperbolic embedders across all three communities.

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes 38 real-world graphs (7 hierarchies, 21 connectomes, 10 other networks ranging from 29 to 82,115 nodes) and 600 simulated HRG networks (sizes 500–2000, temperatures 0.1–0.9), and produces per-embedder quality scores across seven measures: MAP, MeanRank, greedy success rate, greedy stretch factor, greedy routing efficiency, log-likelihood, and the newly proposed Information Control Value (ICV). A key output distinction is that ICV, unlike all prior measures, penalizes embeddings for higher dimensionality and larger radius, thereby removing the trivial advantage that higher-dimensional methods gain from having more free parameters. Embedding times and radii are also reported, enabling speed-quality trade-off analysis.

## Method

The paper is primarily an empirical benchmark: it applies 14 embedders (BFKL, Poincaré/Lorentz 2D/3D/5D, CLOVE, DHRG local search, Mercator fast/full, d-Mercator, TreeRep, LTiling, LPCS, HMCS, Coalescent, KVK, Anneal, HypViewer) to all benchmark graphs using official implementations and hyperparameters, then evaluates results on standard and novel quality measures. The core technical novelty is the Information Control Value (ICV), derived from the Minimum Description Length principle, which computes a total description length combining the log-likelihood of edge predictions under the HRG connection model with the bits needed to encode each node's radial and angular coordinates at a given precision ε; the final score is N/(N+L) where N is the description length of a naive uniform-probability model. A Penalty variant of DHRG further optimizes a radius-reduction cost term (K·log(r_δ) per node) alongside log-likelihood to produce compact embeddings. On simulated networks, logit regressions quantify how graph size and temperature modulate the probability that BFKL outperforms 2D Lorentz, revealing that larger graphs favor BFKL while higher temperature erodes its advantage. The headline empirical finding is that BFKL, a quasi-linear algorithm, runs approximately 100× faster than Poincaré or Lorentz embeddings while achieving comparable or sometimes superior quality, and that the apparent superiority of higher-dimensional embeddings largely vanishes under ICV.

## Results

On the WordNet Noun hierarchy (82K nodes), BFKL achieves MAP 0.284 and MR 62.6 in 428 s, while Lorentz 2D achieves MAP 0.194 and MR 42.3 in 38,578 s (~90× slower) and Poincaré 2D achieves MAP 0.105 and MR 89.3 in 51,794 s. The newer CLOVE embedder attains the best hierarchy quality (MAP 0.776 on NOUN, 0.816 on MESH) in 117 s and 76 s respectively. On simulated scale-free networks (600 graphs, n ∈ {500, 1000, 2000}), logit regression shows BFKL outperforms Lorentz 2D with increasing probability as graph size grows (coefficient +3.80 for n=2000, p<2e-16 on MAP), but this advantage diminishes at higher temperature T (coefficient −4.42 for T=0.7, p<2e-16). The proposed ICV metric, which penalizes embedding radius and dimension via MDL, reveals that 2D embeddings are preferred for most real-world networks once complexity is controlled.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BFKL (Bläsius et al., ESA 2016) is the paper's central fast baseline and is ~100× faster than both Poincaré 2D and Lorentz 2D (e.g., 428 s vs. 38,578 s on NOUN) while achieving higher MAP on NOUN (0.284 vs. 0.194), VERBF (0.348 vs. 0.246), and MESH (0.321 vs. 0.183); however, Lorentz 2D retains an edge on greedy routing (GSR, GSF) for scale-free networks. CLOVE (Balogh et al., 2025) dominates on hierarchies with MAP 0.776–0.816 in under 120 s, outperforming all other methods by a wide margin, but its advantage narrows on simulated HRG networks where it rarely enters the top 10%. DHRG local search improves BFKL and Lorentz 2D MAP (e.g., NOUN: 0.284→0.322 for Lorentz+DHRG) at modest time cost, and the Penalty variant reduces embedding radius for better ICV. Mercator (García-Perez et al., 2019) achieves intermediate quality (MAP 0.456–0.753 on hierarchies) but is slower than Lorentz 2D on large graphs; its fast mode is generally worse than BFKL. TreeRep (Sonthalia & Gilbert, 2020) performs well on tree-like hierarchies but significantly worse on networks such as Facebook and connectomes. LPCS and HMCS (Wang et al.) are extremely fast (<5 s) and comparable to BFKL on hierarchies, but LPCS performs poorly on connectomes. Euclidean 200D embeddings (from Nickel & Kiela 2017) achieve the highest raw MAP/MR on hierarchies (e.g., 0.946/1.3 on NOUN), but the paper notes this is an artifact of dimensionality that ICV corrects, and the original paper's claim that Euclidean underperforms Poincaré is not reproduced.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as the first systematic experimental comparison of hyperbolic embedders across three communities (ML, network theory, and algorithms) that have developed in relative isolation. It builds on the MDL principle (Rissanen, 1978) to introduce the Information Control Value (ICV) metric, and on the authors' own DHRG discrete hyperbolic framework (SEA 2022) as a post-processing improvement tool applied to other embedders' outputs. Conceptually, it departs from the ML community's narrative that Poincaré (2017) and Lorentz (2018) embeddings were pioneering, by foregrounding the earlier HRG model (2010) and the BFKL quasilinear algorithm (2016) from the algorithmic/NT literature, and by explicitly documenting the lack of cross-citation between these communities.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical finding that BFKL is ~100× faster than Poincaré/Lorentz embeddings while achieving comparable (sometimes better) quality is directly actionable for anyone building ML pipelines that use hyperbolic representations, especially on large graphs where RSGD-based methods become infeasible. The ICV metric addresses a real evaluation gap—standard measures (MAP, MR, GSR) systematically favor higher-dimensional embeddings as an optimization artifact rather than genuine quality improvement. Open questions include whether conclusions hold in other network regimes (PSO, nPSO), how the framework extends to the rapidly growing set of new embedders, and whether the observed non-reproducibility of Nickel & Kiela's original results (e.g., Euclidean baselines performing differently) reflects implementation issues or deeper methodological problems.
