## Problem

Differential Attention (DA) was introduced to suppress redundant or noisy context via a subtractive structure, but its adversarial robustness properties remained entirely unexplored. This paper asks whether the same subtraction that sharpens focus on clean inputs also introduces a structural vulnerability to adversarial perturbations, and formalizes the answer as the 'Fragile Principle': DA's design inherently encourages negative gradient alignment between its two branches, amplifying local sensitivity.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262442/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis operates on standard RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 224×224 for COCO/ImageNet) and text prompts for CLIP-based models, with perturbations bounded by ℓ∞ or ℓ2 norms. Rather than proposing a new classifier, the paper extracts diagnostic quantities from existing DA-based models: per-layer local Lipschitz constants, cosine similarity between branch gradients (measuring negative alignment frequency), and attack success rates under PGD, Carlini-Wagner, and AutoAttack. Depth-dependent experiments vary the number of DA layers (D ∈ {1,2,4,8,12}) to characterize how fragility and noise cancellation interact with architecture depth.

## Method

The core theoretical contribution identifies negative gradient alignment (cos θ < 0) between the two attention branches as the structural driver of sensitivity amplification in DA's subtractive formulation A_DA = A_1 − λA_2. The paper derives closed-form expressions showing that under negative alignment the DA gradient norm scales as (1 + λρ)‖∇A_1‖ versus (1 − λρ)‖∇A_1‖ under positive alignment, and proves this amplification translates into higher local Lipschitz constants and smaller certified robustness radii relative to standard attention. A second result shows that stacking DA layers introduces a per-layer noise-cancellation factor α < 1, yielding a robustness crossover: DA is more fragile than standard attention at shallow depths but becomes asymptotically more robust at sufficient depth for small perturbations. Empirically, the paper validates these predictions by training ViT/DiffViT from scratch on CIFAR-10/100 and evaluating pretrained CLIP/DiffCLIP on COCO and ImageNet, measuring ASR, Lipschitz estimates, and gradient alignment frequency across five datasets and multiple attack methods.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, DiffViT (λ_init=0.8) achieves 87.0% clean accuracy but suffers 84.98% ASR under PGD at ε=1/255, compared to 40.74% at λ_init=0.5, confirming that stronger subtraction increases adversarial vulnerability. On Tiny ImageNet, DiffViT consistently shows higher ASR than ViT at small perturbation budgets (e.g., 28.2% vs 23.1% at ε=1/255; 23.6% vs 18.7% at ε=0.5/255), while the gap narrows or reverses at ε=4/255 (63.2% vs 64.9%). Mean local Lipschitz estimates are highest in DA-equipped models, with maxima at layers using larger λ, and the frequency of negative gradient alignment (cos < 0) is dominant even in single-layer DiffViT variants.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against ViT (Dosovitskiy et al., 2021), DiffViT is uniformly more vulnerable: on CIFAR-10 at ε=1/255, DiffViT's ASR reaches 84.98% versus substantially lower values for ViT, and on Tiny ImageNet the ASR gap is 5.1 points at ε=1/255 and 4.9 points at ε=0.5/255, though at ε=4/255 the two converge (63.2% vs 64.9%). Against CLIP (Radford et al., 2021), DiffCLIP shows consistently higher ASR on COCO across all perturbation budgets and patch sizes, supporting the Fragile Principle; on ImageNet the picture is more nuanced—DiffCLIP remains slightly more vulnerable under full-image adversarial examples, but CLIP marginally outperforms DiffCLIP under patch attacks, suggesting dataset scale and diversity modulate DA's fragility. In both comparisons, DA-equipped models exhibit higher mean local Lipschitz constants and a dominant frequency of negative gradient alignment, confirming that the subtractive structure amplifies sensitivity rather than providing robustness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Differential Transformer (Ye et al., 2025), which introduced the subtractive Differential Attention (DA) mechanism to suppress redundant context and reduce hallucination. It departs from the broader attention-robustness literature (which typically proposes new architectures or robustness-enhancing training) by instead analyzing DA as a fixed, unmodified mechanism and identifying its intrinsic structural sensitivity. The theoretical tools draw on Lipschitz-based robustness analysis for self-attention (Kim et al., 2021; Dasoulas et al., 2021), but the key conceptual contribution—the 'Fragile Principle' linking DA's subtractive design to negative gradient alignment and amplified local sensitivity—is novel. The depth-dependent noise-cancellation analysis extends this single-layer fragility into a multi-layer perspective not previously explored for DA.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals a fundamental trade-off in DA: the same subtractive structure that sharpens clean-input focus and reduces hallucination structurally increases adversarial vulnerability, a concern for safety-critical deployments (autonomous driving, medical AI). The depth-dependent robustness crossover—where stacking DA layers attenuates small perturbations via cumulative noise cancellation but loses this protection under larger attack budgets—offers a practical mitigation lever but also highlights that the fragility is not simply resolved by scaling depth. Key limitations include the reliance on local linearization, layer isolation from downstream modules, and evaluation restricted to small gradient-based perturbations rather than natural or semantic adversarial examples. Open questions include how to jointly optimize for selectivity and robustness in attention design, the full training dynamics of λ, and whether the fragility persists under distribution-shift or physical-world attacks.
