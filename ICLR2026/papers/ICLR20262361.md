## Problem

Prior work on structure discovery in neural networks (Mousavi-Hosseini et al., 2023) required strong regularization, frozen biases, two-layer architectures, and SGD-specific dynamics to show that first-layer weights converge to a low-rank subspace. This paper asks whether the same low-rank structure emerges under far more natural conditions: arbitrary depth, all parameters trainable, any smooth loss, arbitrarily small regularization, and any optimizer that reaches a second-order stationary point.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262361/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For the neural network application, the input is a standard Gaussian vector x ~ N(0, I_d) with labels generated by a multi-index teacher model, and the output is a first-layer weight matrix W whose perpendicular component W_⊥ (relative to the teacher subspace) is driven to near-zero. For MAXCUT, the input is the matrix of unit vectors from the Goemans-Williamson SDP relaxation, and the output is a deterministic vertex partition matching the 0.878 approximation factor. For JL embeddings, the input is n unit vectors in R^d with target dimension k, and the output is a deterministic projection matrix satisfying the JL guarantee with O(√(log n / k)) distortion.

## Method

The core contribution is a Key Derandomization Lemma (Lemma 3.1) proving that any ρ-SOSP of the objective f(W,b;θ) = E_x[g_θ(Wx+b)] + λ‖W‖²_F satisfies ‖W‖_F ≤ ρ/(2λ−√(Kρ)), where K is the Hessian Lipschitz constant. The proof hinges on Stein's Lemma, which converts the gradient w.r.t. W into an expectation of the Hessian of g_θ multiplied by W, and then uses the second-order stationarity condition on the Hessian w.r.t. b to show this matrix is positive definite, forcing W to vanish. This derandomization mechanism is then applied in three settings: (i) reformulating the NN regularized risk to isolate W_⊥ and applying the lemma to suppress it, (ii) replacing the randomized Gaussian rounding in Goemans-Williamson with optimization over a mean vector μ that drives the variance to zero, and (iii) optimizing over the mean and covariance of a Gaussian distribution over projection matrices to recover a deterministic JL embedding. The framework requires only that λ > √(Kρ)/2, making regularization arbitrarily small at the cost of more optimization iterations.

## Results

The paper's primary contribution is theoretical: Lemma 3.1 establishes that any ρ-SOSP of the objective E_x[g_θ(Wx+b)] + λ‖W‖_F² satisfies ‖W‖_F ≤ ρ/(2λ − √(Kρ)), implying W→0 as ρ→0. In the MAXCUT experiment on a 15-vertex graph (edge probability 0.6), the optimization-based derandomization reaches the exact optimum of 41 cut edges, substantially outperforming the classical Goemans-Williamson randomized rounding baseline of approximately 36. For the neural network experiment (two-layer ReLU_2, width 1000, d=2, λ=10⁻⁵), PGD training with 10,000 steps drives the first-layer weights to converge to the teacher's principal subspace, confirming the low-rank structure discovery guarantee.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Goemans-Williamson randomized rounding (1995), the paper's optimization-based derandomization matches the theoretical approximation factor α=0.878 (Theorem 5.2) and empirically surpasses the randomized baseline: on a 15-vertex graph with edge probability 0.6, the method reaches the exact MAXCUT optimum of 41, while the classical randomized algorithm achieves only ~36. The key distinction is that this is the first optimization-based derandomization of the Goemans-Williamson algorithm, avoiding explicit combinatorial constructions or pseudorandom generators. Against the standard random Gaussian JL projection (Johnson & Lindenstrauss, 1984), the paper's deterministic construction (Theorem 5.5) matches the JL guarantee with distortion O(√(log n/k)) and the experiment in Appendix H.5 shows that directly minimizing worst-case distortion via optimization can reduce JL distortion beyond what standard Gaussian random projections achieve. The paper does not directly compare against other derandomization methods (e.g., method of conditional expectations, small-bias spaces) quantitatively, positioning its contribution as a conceptual/algorithmic novelty rather than a strict performance improvement over those approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the structure discovery framework of Mousavi-Hosseini et al. (2023), which showed that a two-layer student NN trained with SGD and strong regularization converges to low-rank first-layer weights in a multi-index teacher-student setting. The key departure is the use of ρ-second-order stationary points (from Jin et al., 2017) combined with Stein's Lemma to relate the Hessian with respect to biases to the gradient with respect to weights, yielding a general derandomization lemma that holds for arbitrary-depth NNs, all trainable parameters, any smooth loss, and arbitrarily small regularization. The same lemma is then repurposed to derandomize the Goemans-Williamson MAXCUT rounding procedure and to recover the deterministic JL embedding construction of Tsikouras et al. (2024), demonstrating its cross-domain generality.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean, minimal theoretical explanation for why gradient-based optimization inherently discovers low-rank structure in neural networks, resolving an open question about whether strong regularization is truly necessary. The finding that training biases is essential for derandomization under tiny regularization corrects a common analytical simplification in prior work. However, the results are limited to Gaussian inputs and smooth activations, and the paper explicitly does not establish generalization guarantees, leaving open the question of how structure discovery translates to improved test performance in finite-sample, non-Gaussian regimes. Extending the framework to non-Gaussian distributions and connecting the low-rank structure to concrete generalization bounds are natural next steps.
