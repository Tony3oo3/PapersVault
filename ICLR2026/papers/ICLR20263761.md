## Problem

Q-learning theory has been developed almost exclusively for constant or polynomially decaying learning rates, which respectively suffer from persistent asymptotic bias and prohibitively slow convergence. The empirically successful linear decay to zero (LD2Z) schedule and its power-law generalization (PD2Z-ν) lack any sharp non-asymptotic error bounds, central limit theory, or strong invariance principle in the Q-learning setting, leaving their statistical properties and practical advantages theoretically unexplained.

## Input / Output

The method consumes MDP transition tuples (state, action, reward, next state) generated under a behavior policy, along with schedule parameters (base rate η, decay exponent ν, and pre-specified total horizon n) and an initial Q-vector Q₀. The primary outputs are: (i) the final Q-iterate Q_{n,n} with explicit L_p error bounds, (ii) a tail Polyak-Ruppert averaged estimator that averages only the last ⌊c·n^{ν/(ν+1)}⌋ iterates (rather than all n), and (iii) a covariance-matching Gaussian process Y_t that approximates the partial-sum trajectory of Q-iterates for bootstrap-based time-uniform inference. The pre-specification of n is a key structural constraint inherited from the LD2Z schedule, though the paper shows robustness to mild misspecification (n − n₀ ≤ α√n).

## Method

The paper establishes three interlocking theoretical results for Q-learning under PD2Z-ν schedules η_{t,n} = η(1−t/n)^ν. First, a sharp non-asymptotic L_p bound (Theorem 3.1) decomposes the error into an exponentially decaying initialization term exp(−c₃ηt(1−n⁻¹)^ν)|Q₀−Q⋆| and a polynomial convergence term n^{−ν/(2(ν+1))}, revealing a two-regime structure where early iterates behave like a constant step-size (fast forgetting) before transitioning to a slow polynomial tail. Second, a novel tail Polyak-Ruppert averaging estimator (Theorem 3.5) that averages only the last c·n^{ν/(ν+1)} iterates is shown to be asymptotically normal at rate n^{ν/(2(ν+1))}; the tail restriction is necessary because the full PR average is contaminated by early non-converged iterates whose step-sizes remain O(1). Third, a strong invariance principle (Theorem 4.1) constructs an explicit non-stationary Gaussian process Y_t = (I−η_{t,n}G)Y_{t−1}+η_{t,n}ℵ_t with covariance-matching increments, yielding a time-uniform approximation of the partial-sum process at rate o_P(n^{1/p+1/(ν+1)}), which enables bootstrap-based confidence sequences. The proofs proceed via a four-step linearization: replacing the true iterate with an oracle version, bounding the nonlinear Bellman residual via a local attraction condition (Assumption 3.2), projecting onto a linear stochastic approximation, and applying Lindeberg-Feller CLT or Komlós–Major–Tusnády-type strong approximation.

## Results

The paper establishes that Q-learning with PD2Z-ν learning rates achieves an L₂ error bound of exp(−cn)|Q₀−Q*| + n^{−ν/(2(ν+1))}, where the initialization effect decays exponentially (vs. only exp(−ct^{1−α}) for polynomial schedules). For the LD2Z special case (ν=1), the convergence rate is n^{−1/4}. A novel tail Polyak-Ruppert averaging estimator satisfies n^{ν/(2(ν+1))}(Q̄ₙ−Q*) → N(0,Σ), and a time-uniform strong invariance principle is proven with rate o_P(n^{1/p+1/(ν+1)}). Empirically on a 4×4 Frozen Lake gridworld, LD2Z outperforms polynomially decaying schedules by a wide margin in the transient phase, while PD2Z-ν with ν∈{1,2,3} shows stable final-iterate performance and clearly beats the constant step-size baseline which retains persistent bias.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the polynomially decaying step-size baseline (Chen et al. 2020b; Li et al. 2023b), PD2Z-ν achieves the same or better convergence rate in the asymptotic regime (n^{−ν/(2(ν+1))} vs. t^{−α/2}) but with exponentially faster forgetting of initialization (exp(−cn) vs. exp(−ct^{1−α})), which is the key practical advantage. Against the constant step-size baseline (Zhang & Xie 2024), PD2Z-ν eliminates the persistent O(η) bias entirely while maintaining comparable performance in the initial phase, as confirmed in Figures 2 and 7. Against the standard full Polyak-Ruppert average used in Li et al. (2023b), the proposed tail PR-averaged estimator (averaging only the last ⌊cn^{ν/(ν+1)}⌋ iterates) achieves substantially lower L∞ error (Figure 4), because the early iterates under LD2Z do not converge to Q* and would contaminate a full average. The strong invariance principle (Theorem 4.1) also provides a sharper time-uniform Gaussian approximation than the functional CLT of Li et al. (2023b), as demonstrated by the Q-Q plots in Figure 3 where the Brownian-motion-based approximation visibly under-covers the true distribution.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Q-learning-as-stochastic-approximation framework (Chen et al. 2020b; Li et al. 2023b) to a new class of learning rate schedules—power-law decay to zero (PD2Z-ν), of which linear decay to zero (LD2Z) is the ν=1 special case. It builds directly on Goldreich et al. (2025), who provided the first LD2Z analysis for strongly convex SGD, by transferring and adapting those ideas to the nonlinear, non-convex Q-learning setting. The strong invariance principle leverages the covariance-matching Gaussian process construction of Bonnerjee et al. (2024) for nonstationary time series, while the novel tail Polyak-Ruppert averaging estimator departs from the classical PR framework (Polyak & Juditsky, 1992) by restricting averaging to the final convergence window where the step-size has become sufficiently small.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LD2Z schedules are empirically dominant in modern deep learning (LLMs, vision transformers) but had no theoretical justification in the Q-learning context; this paper closes that gap and provides the first non-asymptotic bounds, central limit theory, and strong invariance principle for these schedules. The practical implication is significant: standard Polyak-Ruppert averaging is shown to be suboptimal (or even invalid) under LD2Z, and practitioners must instead use the proposed tail averaging to obtain valid asymptotic normality. Key limitations include the requirement of a pre-specified horizon n (restricting applicability to offline RL) and the restriction to finite MDPs; extending to online RL, function approximation, and establishing Berry-Esseen rates remain open.
