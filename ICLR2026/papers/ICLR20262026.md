## Problem

Existing RL methods for LLM reasoning, such as PPO and GRPO, rely on surrogate losses, KL divergence constraints, and reference models that complicate training and introduce reward bias through group-level standard deviation normalization. Additionally, when all sampled responses in a group are uniformly correct or incorrect, the resulting zero-advantage samples bias the gradient estimate without any correction mechanism. The paper asks whether a minimalist policy gradient that directly optimizes the original RL objective—free of surrogate losses, distributional constraints, and both bias sources—can match or exceed these more complex baselines.

## Input / Output

For unimodal tasks, the model receives a text question or instruction and autoregressively generates a reasoning chain followed by a final answer, scored by an accuracy reward (1.0 for correct, 0.0 for incorrect). For multimodal tasks, the input is an RGB image paired with a text prompt, and the output varies by task: a reasoning chain with answer (geometry, visual reasoning), a class label (fine-grained classification), or a bounding box with IoU-based reward (reasoning grounding). Each prompt is sampled G times (typically 8) to form a group, and the group mean reward serves as the baseline for advantage computation.

## Method

GPG replaces the clipped surrogate objective and KL penalty of PPO/GRPO with a plain policy gradient loss: −log π_θ(o) · Â, where the advantage Â is computed as (r_i − mean{R}) without standard-deviation normalization, thereby eliminating the reward bias that GRPO's std normalization introduces. The key technical contribution is Accurate Gradient Estimation (AGE): when M out of B samples in a batch produce zero gradient (all-right or all-wrong groups), the loss is scaled by α = B/(B−M) to correct the underestimation from standard backpropagation averaging over the full batch. A thresholding mechanism with β_th ensures the valid-sample proportion stays above a minimum; if it falls below, valid samples are accumulated into subsequent batches until the threshold is met, reducing gradient variance without the training inefficiency of full resampling. The method requires no critic model, no reference model, no surrogate loss, and no policy constraint, making it the simplest RL formulation in the comparison while achieving the strongest results.

## Results

On the 7B math reasoning benchmark suite (AIME24, MATH-500, AMC23, Minerva, OlympiadBench), GPG-Zero-7B achieves a 57.7% average pass@1, outperforming the strongest baseline Oat-Zero-7B (51.4%) by 6.3 points and SimpleRL-Zero-7B (46.6%) by 11.1 points. In multimodal settings, GPG surpasses GRPO by 3.85 points on GEOQA (51.33% vs 47.48%), 7.1 points on fine-grained classification (89.0% vs 81.9%), 16.68 points on CV-Bench visual reasoning (76.15% vs 59.47%), and 14.2 points on LISA grounding mIoU (51.8% vs 37.6%). Compared to DAPO-7B, GPG achieves a higher average (57.7% vs 56.0%) while using only 0.45× training cost, 0.39× data cost, and 24G vs 28G memory.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPG consistently outperforms all compared baselines. Against GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), GPG wins on every evaluated task: +3.85 pts on GEOQA, +7.1 pts on classification, +16.68 pts on CV-Bench, and +14.2 pts on LISA grounding. Against Oat-Zero-7B / Dr.GRPO (Liu et al., 2025a), GPG leads by 6.3 pts average on the 7B math suite (57.7% vs 51.4%), with the largest gap on AMC23 (82.5% vs 62.7%). Against SimpleRL-Zero-7B (Zeng et al., 2025), GPG gains 11.1 pts average, and against OpenReasoner-Zero-7B (Hu et al., 2025) it gains 11.8 pts. Against Eurus-2-7B-PRIME (Cui et al., 2025), GPG leads by 8.8 pts average. Against DAPO-7B (Yu et al., 2025), GPG achieves a 1.7 pt average improvement while reducing training cost to 0.45×, data cost to 0.39×, and memory from 28G to 24G. On the 1.5B distilled model, GPG-RS1 (55.7% avg) surpasses Open-RS1 (53.1%) and Open-RS3 (52.0%) from Dang & Ngo (2025), with the largest margin on MATH-500 (87.6% vs 67.5%).

## Relation to Prior Work

GPG traces a direct lineage from the classical policy gradient theorem (Sutton et al., 1998) through TRPO, PPO, and GRPO, but deliberately strips the method back to its minimal form: a direct policy-gradient objective with group-level mean-reward advantage, no surrogate loss, no KL constraint, and no reference or critic model. The key conceptual departure from GRPO is the identification of two previously unaddressed biases — reward normalization bias (std-based F_norm acting as a state-dependent function) and gradient estimation bias from invalid (all-correct/all-wrong) group samples — corrected via a simple α = B/(B−M) rescaling (AGE). The paper positions itself not as a new algorithmic family but as a 'revisit' that questions whether the stability mechanisms inherited from Atari-era RL (clipping, KL penalties, value functions) are actually necessary when the policy is a pretrained LLM with strong representations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: GPG reduces training cost to 0.45× and data cost to 0.39× of DAPO while achieving higher scores, and the AGE correction is a few lines of code that any existing GRPO/DAPO pipeline can adopt. The broader implication is that the field may have been over-engineering RL for LLM reasoning by carrying forward constraints designed for a different regime (small policies learning from scratch in Atari). Open questions include whether the simplicity advantage persists at 70B+ scale (the paper only validates up to 7B), whether the thresholding mechanism generalizes to continuous-reward settings beyond binary accuracy, and whether the absence of any distributional constraint could cause instability in longer training runs or more diverse task distributions.
