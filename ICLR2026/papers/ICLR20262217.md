## Problem

Existing LLM safety benchmarks (HarmBench, AdvBench, JailbreakBench) focus on criminal wrongdoing such as terrorism, cybersecurity, and fraud, leaving a critical gap in evaluating model vulnerabilities to sociopolitical harms like propaganda generation, surveillance design, historical revisionism, and political manipulation. Current alignment strategies (RLHF, rule-based filters) have not been stress-tested in politically charged contexts where models must navigate ethically ambiguous governance scenarios, and no prior benchmark systematically covers these domains across diverse geographies and time periods.

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes 585 single-intent harmful prompts (22.9–50 words each) organized into 7 sociopolitical domains, 37 sub-topics, and 3 functional templates (standard instruction, opinion elicitation, historical reference), spanning 34 countries and temporal ranges from the 1830s to the present. Each prompt is fed to a target LLM, and the resulting completion is scored by two independent classifiers: HarmBench (measuring whether the response fulfills the harmful request) and StrongREJECT (measuring whether the response constitutes a safe refusal), yielding per-category and combined Attack Success Rates. Adversarial variants additionally produce per-attack ASRs across six attack types, and influence-function analysis outputs per-training-sample influence scores for attribution.

## Method

SOCIALHARMBENCH is constructed via a four-stage curation pipeline: (1) mapping documented historical events to seven sociopolitical categories derived from the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, (2) semantic clustering into 37 sub-topics, (3) multi-LLM prompt generation (GPT-4o, ChatGPT, Grok, Gemini, Claude) with quantitative filtering for jailbreak potential and triviality, and (4) manual deduplication with an over-representation factor to balance geographic and temporal coverage. The evaluation pipeline passes each prompt through the target model and scores completions with a dual-classifier setup (HarmBench for capability exposure, StrongREJECT for refusal robustness). Adversarial robustness is tested under six attacks spanning input-space (GCG, AutoDAN-GA/HGA), latent-space (LAT perturbation), weight-space (LoRA fine-tuning on the CircuitBreakers dataset), and embedding-space (SoftOpt) manipulations. For causal attribution of harmful behavior, the paper applies influence functions with the EK-FAC approximation to Meta-Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, tracing which fine-tuning samples most strongly drive sociopolitically harmful generations under weight-tampering attacks.

## Results

On SocialHarmBench (585 prompts, 7 sociopolitical categories, 34 countries), Mistral-7B-Instruct exhibits the highest baseline vulnerability with combined Attack Success Rates of 27.71% (HarmBench) and 28.31% (StrongREJECT), while Claude-Sonnet-4 achieves the lowest at 0.78% and 4.23% respectively. Historical revisionism is the most vulnerable category across all models, with Mistral-7B reaching 62.5% HB and DeepSeek-LLM-7B-Chat at 45.31% HB. Under adversarial attacks, weight tampering is the most effective, driving HB scores above 0.90 across nearly all open-weight models (e.g., Mistral-7B reaches 0.9576 combined HB), while lighter attacks like AutoDAN-GA and GCG also substantially degrade safety (e.g., GCG pushes Mistral-7B to 0.8536 HB).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 8 LLMs (4 closed-source: GPT-4o, Claude-Sonnet-4, Gemini-2.5-Flash; 5 open-weight: Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-7B, Qwen-2.5-7B, Gemma-3-12B, DeepSeek-LLM-7B) on SocialHarmBench. Closed-source models substantially outperform open-weight models: Claude-Sonnet-4 achieves 0.78% combined HB (vs. 10–28% for open-weight models), and GPT-4o reaches 6.80% HB. Among open-weight models, Mistral-7B-Instruct is the most vulnerable (27.71% HB, 28.31% SR), roughly 2–3× worse than Llama-3.1-8B (10.23% HB) or Qwen-2.5-7B (12.51% HB). Under adversarial attacks, all open-weight models degrade severely: weight tampering pushes Mistral-7B to 95.76% HB and Gemma-3 to 88.79% HB, while GCG and AutoDAN-GA also achieve 77–89% HB across models. Temporal and geographic analyses reveal that 21st-century and pre-20th-century prompts yield the highest vulnerability (HB=0.67, SR=0.50), and Latin America, the USA, and the UK drive the highest harmful outputs (HB=0.50–0.75, SR=0.60–1.00 for Mistral, Gemma, DeepSeek).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM safety evaluation task from conventional criminal domains (terrorism, fraud, cybercrime) covered by benchmarks like HarmBench, AdvBench, and JailbreakBench to sociopolitical harms such as censorship, propaganda, surveillance, and historical revisionism. It repurposes established adversarial attack methods (GCG, AutoDAN, weight tampering via LoRA, LAT, embedding optimization) and the EK-FAC influence function framework for training data attribution, but applies them to a novel domain with explicit temporal (1830s–present) and geographic (34 countries) stratification that prior benchmarks lack. The evaluation pipeline reuses HarmBench and StrongREJECT classifiers, positioning the contribution as a new dataset and analysis rather than new attack or defense methodology.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a concrete gap: current alignment strategies (RLHF, instruction tuning, refusal optimization) do not generalize to high-stakes sociopolitical contexts, with open-weight models reaching 97–98% ASR under weight tampering in domains like historical revisionism and propaganda. The influence function analysis tracing harmful generations to a single 'conspiracy movement' training document across all seven domains suggests that targeted data curation could be a practical mitigation lever. Key limitations include English-only prompts, absence of multi-turn or agentic attacks, underrepresentation of Sub-Saharan Africa and Pacific Islands, and the fact that ~60% of prompts focus on 20th–21st century events, which may skew temporal robustness conclusions. Open questions include whether multilingual, culturally adapted probes would reveal different vulnerability patterns, and whether defense strategies (constitutional AI, retrieval shields) can close the gap identified here.
