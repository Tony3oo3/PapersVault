## Problem

In exploratory randomized controlled trials, scientists often lack a priori knowledge of which latent outcomes a treatment affects, yet classical causal inference requires pre-specifying the outcome of interest, introducing the 'Matthew effect' bias toward previously studied phenomena. When high-dimensional observations are decomposed into interpretable sparse autoencoder codes, a fundamental statistical challenge emerges: entangled neurons cause all weakly associated codes to appear significantly treatment-responsive as sample size or effect magnitude grows, a phenomenon the paper formalizes as the Paradox of Exploratory Causal Inference.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264645/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes raw high-dimensional observations (face images from CelebA or video frames from the ISTAnt ant-colony trial) along with a binary treatment assignment vector from a randomized controlled trial. Observations are first encoded by a pretrained foundation model (SigLIP at 768-dim or DINOv2 at 384-dim) and then projected through a trained sparse autoencoder into a high-dimensional sparse code vector (9216 or 4608 codes). The output is an ordered set of SAE neuron indices, each interpreted as a distinct treatment-affected outcome factor, with the procedure halting when no further codes pass the Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold.

## Method

The pipeline combines a pretrained foundation model with a sparse autoencoder trained on the trial data to produce a dictionary of interpretable measurement channels, then applies Neural Effect Search (NES), a recursive hypothesis-testing procedure. At each iteration, NES tests all remaining unselected neurons for a treatment-control mean shift using a stratified t-test that conditions on the codes of previously discovered effects (via pooled quantile binning and optional arm-wise residualization), applies Bonferroni correction across the remaining m hypotheses, and selects the single most significant neuron. This recursive stratification progressively removes the contribution of already-identified effects from the test statistics, so that entangled leakage no longer inflates significance of non-principal neurons. The paper proves consistency: under assumptions of representation sufficiency, principal alignment, and bounded arm-wise transport error, NES recovers exactly the r principal neurons corresponding to the r true affected outcome factors with probability tending to one as n grows, in sharp contrast to vanilla multiple-testing procedures that collapse under the paradox.

## Results

On the semi-synthetic CelebA benchmark (r=2 effects), NES is the only method that maintains high Precision and IoU as sample size N or effect magnitude τ increases, while all baselines (Bonferroni, FDR, vanilla t-test, top-k) suffer the Paradox of Exploratory Causal Inference — their Precision and IoU collapse toward 0 as power grows, even though Recall → 1 for all methods. When the number of affected outcomes r is known (yellow dot in Figure 5), NES still recovers both effects most of the time, whereas all baselines fail to reach Precision or Recall above 0.5. On the real-world ISTAnt ecology trial (n=44 videos), NES without Bonferroni returns exactly two significant neurons: code 394 (grooming behavior, F1=0.398, the most predictive of all 4,608 SAE codes) and code 550 (background position marking, F1=0.568), both in full agreement with prior rationalist analyses.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares NES against four baselines: vanilla Bonferroni correction, FDR (Schweder & Spjøtvoll 1982), uncorrected t-test, and top-k selection (assuming r is known). All baselines lose decisively to NES on Precision and IoU as test power increases (larger N or stronger τ): they eventually flag all entangled neurons as significant, causing Precision to drop well below 0.5 even when r is known, because they cannot distinguish a neuron's own causal effect from leakage of another concept. NES wins on every metric in the high-power regime by recursively stratifying over already-discovered effects, peeling off one true direction per round. In the low-power regime (n=30, τ=0.1), the paradox does not yet emerge and simpler methods like vanilla t-test or top-k can be competitive at the cost of more false positives. On ISTAnt, NES (without Bonferroni due to small n) identifies exactly the two expected signals, matching prior manual-annotation results, whereas no baseline procedure is explicitly run on this dataset.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes sparse autoencoders (originally developed for mechanistic interpretability of language models, Bricken et al. 2023) as interpretable measurement dictionaries for causal inference, combined with pretrained foundation models (SigLIP, DINOv2) as feature extractors. It departs from the 'rationalist' causal inference paradigm (where the outcome Y is pre-specified and partially labeled, as in Prediction-Powered Causal Inference by Cadei et al. 2024/2025) by introducing an 'empiricist' setting where Y is entirely unknown and must be discovered from data. The closest prior work, Causal Feature Learning (Chalupka et al. 2017), also seeks to discover affected outcomes but commits to a single discrete clustering; here, the authors instead identify all statistically significant effects via a novel recursive stratification procedure (Neural Effect Search) that explicitly handles SAE entanglement.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a fundamental limitation of standard multiple-testing frameworks in exploratory settings (the 'Paradox of Exploratory Causal Inference') and provides both a theoretical characterization and a practical algorithmic fix, which is a clean conceptual contribution. However, the approach relies on strong identifiability assumptions (linear representation hypothesis, approximate monosemanticity of SAEs, full column rank of effect directions) that are currently untestable in practice, and the empirical validation is limited to a toy semi-synthetic benchmark and a single small-scale ecological trial (n=44). Open questions include extending to continuous outcomes, multi-modal measurements, and establishing identifiability guarantees for SAEs that would make the interpretability claims rigorous rather than empirical.
