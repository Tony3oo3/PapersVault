## Problem

Hallucination in sign language translation (SLT) — where models generate fluent text unsupported by the signed video — remains entirely unaddressed, despite being especially critical because the visual input constitutes the source language rather than an auxiliary cue. Existing hallucination detection methods rely on text-intrinsic signals (confidence, entropy, perplexity) that capture linguistic uncertainty but cannot distinguish whether a confident prediction is grounded in visual evidence or merely plausible under language priors. This is the first work to investigate and quantify hallucination specifically in SLT, with particular focus on why gloss-free models, which lack intermediate gloss supervision, are systematically more susceptible.

## Input / Output

The method consumes sign language video clips through an encoder-decoder SLT model (SpaMo or TwoStream-SLT), requiring three parallel teacher-forced decoder passes per sentence — with the true video, with no video (cross-attention disabled), and with a batch-shuffled mismatched video — to extract hidden states, cross-attention maps, and token-level probabilities. Outputs are token-level reliability scores in [0,1] fused from feature-based and counterfactual signals, pooled into sentence-level scores (primarily tail-10% averaging over the least reliable tokens), which feed downstream binary classifiers for hallucination detection or isotonic regressors for continuous CHAIR estimation. The three-pass requirement triples inference cost relative to standard decoding, and the fusion weights are trained with CHAIR labels derived from reference translations, though the reliability signal itself is computable without references at inference time.

## Method

The core contribution is a token-level reliability score that quantifies visual grounding through two complementary signal families. Feature-based signals capture internal reliance: the normalized angular change in decoder hidden states when video is removed, and the difference in cross-attention mass directed to video encoder positions versus masked positions, both rescaled to [0,1]. Counterfactual signals capture external evidence: log-probability, logit, and normalized probability margins between the clean video pass and the strongest counterfactual (the maximum of no-video or mismatched-video probabilities for the chosen token), plus absolute probability deltas isolating the effect of removing versus replacing the video. These seven signals are fused via a linear layer with sigmoid activation into per-token reliabilities, which are then pooled (tail-10% averaging) into sentence-level scores. The META variant concatenates grounding signals with text-only baselines (confidence, entropy, perplexity) and fits a linear classifier or regressor, demonstrating that visual grounding provides complementary information beyond linguistic uncertainty. A symbolic proof formalizes why gloss-free training increases hallucination prevalence through weak visual use, with the reliability signal acting as the mediating variable between training paradigm and hallucination rate.

## Results

On hallucination detection, the proposed Grounding signal achieves up to 97.0% ACC (CSL-GF) and 0.998 AP, while the META variant (grounding + text baselines) reaches the best AUC of 0.963 (CSL-GF) and 0.940 (PHOENIX-GF). For CHAIR regression, META attains the strongest Spearman correlation (ρ = −0.736 on CSL-GF) and isotonic fit (0.755), while Grounding alone remains competitive with ρ = −0.680 (CSL-GF) and ISO = 0.722. Across all four dataset–model settings, Grounding outperforms the best text-only baseline by up to +0.20 AUC and +0.30 ACC, with the largest gains observed on gloss-based models where text-only signals are weakest.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the proposed Grounding and META signals against three text-only baselines: token confidence, self-perplexity, and token entropy. On detection, Grounding consistently outperforms all text-only baselines, with the largest margins on gloss-based models (e.g., +0.194 AUC over Perplexity and +0.353 ACC on CSL-GB; +0.232 AUC over Perplexity on PHOENIX-GB). On gloss-free models, the gap narrows because text-only signals are already strong (Confidence reaches 0.953 AUC on CSL-GF), but Grounding still edges ahead on ACC (0.970 vs. 0.868 for Confidence). For CHAIR regression, text-only baselines—especially token entropy (ρ = −0.698 on CSL-GF)—are competitive with Grounding alone (ρ = −0.680), but the META fusion of both signal types achieves the best Spearman (−0.736) and isotonic fit (0.755) on CSL-GF, confirming that visual grounding and linguistic uncertainty are complementary rather than redundant. Cross-dataset and cross-model transfer experiments further show that reliability weights generalize with only modest degradation, whereas text-only baselines do not transfer as robustly.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends hallucination detection from LVLMs and image/video captioning (where visual input is auxiliary) to sign language translation, where the visual modality constitutes the source language itself. It builds on representation-erasure techniques (Li et al., 2017; 2024) for measuring internal state sensitivity and on counterfactual perturbation ideas (Chlon et al., 2025) for quantifying probability margins, but repurposes both into a unified token-level reliability score specific to encoder-decoder SLT architectures. The CHAIR metric from Rohrbach et al. (2018) is adapted from object-level to content-word-level hallucination counting, and the gloss-based vs. gloss-free architectural comparison draws on the recent shift toward LLM-backed gloss-free SLT (Hwang et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2022).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic study of hallucination in SLT, providing a reusable reliability framework that distinguishes visually grounded tokens from language-prior guesses without requiring reference translations at inference time. The finding that gloss-free models hallucinate more because they systematically under-utilize visual information (not merely because of weaker encoders) reframes the design question for future SLT systems: the bottleneck is decoder reliance on linguistic priors, not encoder quality. Key limitations include the 3× inference cost from counterfactual passes, dependence on CHAIR-derived labels for calibration (not fully unsupervised), and evaluation restricted to two weather-forecast datasets and two architectures. Open questions include real-time applicability, fully unsupervised calibration, and whether the reliability signal transfers to other sign languages and domains.
