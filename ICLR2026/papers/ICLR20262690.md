## Problem

Existing video benchmarks for Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) predominantly rely on text-only queries, which are inherently too abstract to specify fine-grained visual attributes or identify specific instances. This leaves image-grounded video perception and reasoning—a capability critical for real-world applications like visual search and media analysis—systematically untested in current evaluation frameworks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262690/cropped_page0_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

Each evaluation sample pairs a video (minimum 5 minutes, uniformly sampled to 32 frames at 480p by default) with an externally sourced reference image and a multiple-choice text query (up to 10 options, randomly shuffled at inference). The externally sourced images are explicitly not extracted from the video itself, preventing frame-matching shortcuts and forcing reliance on generalized visual knowledge. The model outputs a single letter answer, scored by exact-match accuracy across 13 tasks spanning 5 video categories.

## Method

IV-Bench is constructed through a rigorous annotation pipeline: annotators watch each video in full, assign one of 13 task types (7 perception, 6 reasoning), retrieve an external image from online sources, and craft a question with up to 9 plausible distractors. A two-round quality control process (64.4% video retention, 56.9% question retention from an initial pool of 1,500 videos and 4,500 questions) ensures that no sample is solvable without both the image and the video, and that at least two 'effective' distractors would become correct under a different image. The evaluation protocol tests 28 MLLMs under three inference patterns (image-first, video-first, text-only) and ablates frame count (8–512) and resolution (72p–240p) under fixed token budgets. Key findings include a large model–human gap (28.9% vs. 88.8%), the superiority of placing the image after video frames for larger models, and the greater sensitivity of performance to frame count than to resolution.

## Results

On IV-Bench (966 videos, 2,560 image-text queries across 13 tasks), the best-performing model Qwen2.5-VL-72B achieves only 28.9% overall accuracy, far below the 88.8% human baseline. Among closed-source models, Gemini-2-Pro leads at 27.7%, while GPT-4o reaches 26.1%. Perception tasks (P-Avg) are consistently easier than reasoning tasks (R-Avg); for example, Qwen2.5-VL-72B scores 33.7% on perception vs. 21.9% on reasoning, and Temporal Reasoning remains the hardest task with a best score of only 16.7%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All 28 evaluated MLLMs fall dramatically short of the 88.8% human accuracy, with the best model (Qwen2.5-VL-72B) reaching only 28.9%—a 60-point gap. Within the Qwen2.5-VL family, scaling from 7B (18.5%) to 72B (28.9%) yields a 10.4-point gain, with the largest improvements on perception tasks (Existence: +30.8 pp; NLI: +43.1 pp) but minimal gains on reasoning (Counting: +1.4 pp). Similarly, InternVL2.5 scales from 17.4% (8B) to 28.6% (78B), again with stronger perception gains than reasoning. Among closed-source models, Gemini-2-Pro (27.7%) and Gemini-2-Flash (27.4%) outperform GPT-4o (26.1%) and Claude-3.7-Sonnet (21.7%), with Gemini-2-Pro achieving the best reasoning average (24.9%). Smaller models (<10B) perform near random-guessing level (11.11% for 9 options), with the best (Qwen2.5-VL-7B) at only 18.5%, and ablation studies show that adding the reference image actually *degrades* performance for these smaller models, indicating insufficient image-grounded video training data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by defining image-grounded video perception and reasoning as a distinct evaluation task, departing from two existing lines of work: (1) text-only video benchmarks (Video-MME, LongVideoBench, MVBench, etc.) that cannot specify fine-grained visual references, and (2) image-text video benchmarks (V2P-Bench, VideoRefer-Bench) that source images from within the video itself, reducing the task to frame matching. The key conceptual departure is making an externally sourced image a *mandatory* input (not auxiliary, as in VideoMMMU) that must be jointly reasoned with the video, drawing on the visual search literature (Pinterest Lens, Alibaba image search) to motivate why language alone is insufficient for specifying visual targets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The stark 28.9% (best model) vs. 88.8% (human) gap, particularly on temporal reasoning (16.7% best), reveals a fundamental limitation in current MLLMs' ability to leverage external visual context for video understanding. Two findings have broader implications: smaller models are actively *hurt* by image context (performing worse than text-only), suggesting a capacity threshold for multimodal grounding, and the 'static bias' result—where 128 frames *degrade* temporal reasoning versus 32 frames—indicates that current architectures cannot exploit additional temporal information for sequential logic. As a 2,560-sample, 13-task benchmark with rigorous two-round quality control (Fleiss' κ = 0.86), IV-Bench is likely to become a standard evaluation for video MLLMs, and its findings point toward the need for specialized temporal modeling mechanisms rather than mere scaling.
