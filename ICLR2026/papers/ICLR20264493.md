## Problem

The rapid proliferation of LLM benchmarks (from 252 NeurIPS D&B submissions in 2021 to 1,820 in 2024) raises the question of how much overlap actually exists across them. Existing overlap measures—semantic similarity of question text and raw performance correlations—are confounded by benchmark-orthogonal factors such as question format and benchmark family, making them poor indicators of true capability overlap. The paper asks whether a more principled, capacity-grounded measure of benchmark overlap can be derived from model behavior on in-the-wild data.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a token-level perplexity matrix of shape 32×1.69×10^7 (32 LLMs × downsampled RedPajama tokens, each token conditioned on up to 30 preceding whitespace-separated pieces as context) and a 32×89 performance matrix from lm-evaluation-harness. It produces per-benchmark signatures of ~30 salient tokens (each with its 30-piece pre-context), signature-level overlap scores computed as Spearman correlations of z-scored mean perplexities across models, and a three-level comparison against semantic (MPNet bootstrapped cosine) and performance (Spearman rank) overlaps.

## Method

The core pipeline is a two-stage high-dimensional feature selection operating in the ultra-high-dimensional regime (d≈8.45×10^9 tokens vs. m=32 models). Stage 1 screens tokens via the Thrush correlation—a robust rank-based concordance coefficient between each token's cross-model perplexity vector and the benchmark performance vector—retaining the top and bottom 1% of tokens to reduce the candidate pool to ~3.4×10^5. Stage 2 applies AIC-guided stepwise forward selection on the candidate set to extract a parsimonious signature (~30 tokens) whose perplexity patterns maximally predict benchmark performance while penalizing redundancy. The key insight is that in-the-wild corpora implicitly encode benchmark characteristics because the abilities benchmarks measure emerge from the distribution of real-world text, so perplexity on natural tokens serves as a proxy for training exposure to the relevant capacity. Signature-level overlap is then computed by having all 32 models read each signature's context and measuring the Spearman correlation of z-scored mean perplexities, which is shown to be robust to question-format and benchmark-family confounds that inflate raw performance correlations.

## Results

Across 32 LLMs and 89 benchmarks, signature-level overlap demonstrates substantially stronger discriminative ability than both semantic-level (narrow 0.1–0.4 range regardless of category) and performance-level (nearly universally high, ~0.8 within benchmark families or question formats) analyses. The signature resolves the evaluation bias: within-family/format overlap drops from ~0.8 (performance) to ~0 (signature, statistically insignificant by Mann-Whitney U test), while cross-function overlaps reveal structured entanglement (e.g., math–logic correlation of 0.21 vs. average cross-function overlap of 0.105 and within-function overlap of 0.285). Coding emerges as the most isolated function, interacting only moderately with AbsenceBench (detecting missing information). Cross-task prediction using one benchmark's signature to predict another's performance yields MAE of 0.223, far worse than the task-specific signature (MAE 0.021) and even the naive mean baseline (MAE 0.181), confirming signatures encode benchmark-specific rather than generic signals.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against external competing methods; instead it compares three internal levels of overlap analysis—semantic (MPNet cosine similarity on bootstrapped question sets), performance (Spearman rank correlation of model scores), and signature (Spearman correlation of z-scored perplexity on selected salient tokens). The signature level decisively outperforms both alternatives in discriminative power: semantic overlap remains in a narrow 0.1–0.4 band regardless of whether benchmarks share a category, while performance overlap is inflated to ~0.8 within the same benchmark family or question format (e.g., MMLU history aligns more closely with MMLU chemistry than with a different history benchmark). The signature level eliminates this confound, yielding statistically insignificant within-family/format differences (~0) while preserving genuine within-category structure (e.g., higher overlap among reasoning, science, and social-science knowledge benchmarks). In the cross-task robustness experiment, using one BBH subtask's signature to predict another subtask's performance (MAE 0.223) performs worse than the naive mean baseline (MAE 0.181), confirming that signatures capture benchmark-specific capacity demands rather than generic model-quality signals.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the perplexity-correlation framework from data selection (Thrush et al., 2025; Shum et al., 2025) for a fundamentally different purpose: instead of selecting which training documents to keep, it mines which in-the-wild tokens' perplexity patterns predict benchmark performance, yielding 'benchmark signatures.' It positions itself as a third level of analysis beyond the two existing approaches to benchmark overlap—semantic similarity via embeddings (which captures surface question wording) and performance correlation (which the paper shows is heavily confounded by question format and benchmark family). The theoretical grounding draws on Sure Independence Screening (Fan & Lv, 2008) to justify marginal correlation screening in the ultra-high-dimensional regime (billions of tokens vs. 32 models), while the forward-selection regression with AIC is a standard high-dimensional variable selection technique applied here in a novel context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that performance-level benchmark correlations are dominated by benchmark-orthogonal factors (question format, family membership) rather than shared underlying capabilities is a significant diagnostic for the benchmarking community, suggesting that current benchmark agreement tests may substantially overestimate true capability overlap. The signature framework offers a reusable, computationally tractable tool for identifying redundant benchmarks and underrepresented capability gaps as the benchmark ecosystem continues to grow (7× increase in NeurIPS D&B submissions from 2021–2024). Key limitations include restriction to QA/true-false task formats, a fixed set of 32 models, and the qualitative finding that only knowledge-domain signatures align with their stated semantic content—implying LLMs' internal capacity organization may diverge from human conceptual structure. Open questions include extending signatures to open-ended generation tasks, finer-grained probes (layer-level activations), and establishing causal rather than correlational links between perplexity exposure and benchmark competence.
