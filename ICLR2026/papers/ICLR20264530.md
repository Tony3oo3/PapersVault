## Problem

Evaluating machine unlearning effectiveness remains impractical in realistic deployment settings because existing approaches either require a retrained reference model (defeating the purpose of standalone unlearning) or rely on membership inference attacks that need access to training statistics, shadow models, or supervision labels. Since unlearning typically removes only a small random subset (5–20%) of training data, per-sample MIA cues become statistically weak, and there is no lightweight, standalone procedure that directly tests whether a subset's training influence has been erased.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a trained (or unlearned) neural network, a target subset S of n samples (typically 400–2000) drawn from the forgetting set, and two reference subsets of equal size: one known in-training (S_IT ⊂ D_r) and one known out-of-training (S_OOT ⊂ D_te). It operates on penultimate-layer or intermediate-layer activations (not just final logits), computing 200 HSIC values per subset via random shuffling to estimate a dependence distribution. The output is a binary in/out-of-training decision per target subset, aggregated into an Out-of-Training Rate (OTR) over m sampled subsets, with the underlying HSIC distributions available for continuous comparison across models.

## Method

SDE exploits the insight that supervised training induces a shared influence component Δθ_S in the model parameters, which couples the representations of all training samples and creates measurable statistical dependence among their activations. Given a target subset S, the method randomly splits it into two equal halves S₁ and S₂, then computes the Hilbert-Schmidt Independence Criterion (HSIC) between h(S₁) and h(S₂) using a Gaussian RBF kernel with bandwidth σ = √dim, repeating 200 times with shuffled S₂ to estimate the full dependence distribution H(S,h). The theoretical analysis (Appendix A) proves via influence-function decomposition that in-training subsets yield a strictly positive cross-covariance (due to the shared Δθ_S appearing in both halves), while out-of-training subsets yield zero cross-covariance, making the split-half HSIC a provable discriminator. For unlearning evaluation, the target's HSIC distribution is compared against both reference distributions using Jensen-Shannon Divergence; the subset is classified as successfully unlearned if its distribution is closer to S_OOT than to S_IT. This requires no retrained model, no auxiliary classifier training, and no access to original training hyperparameters.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with ResNet-18 at R=10%, SDE achieves F1 scores of 0.88–0.95 for distinguishing in- vs. out-of-training subsets across unlearned models, with the Retrain baseline reaching 87% OTR (final layer) and 94% OTR (penultimate layer). The method outperforms distribution-based alternatives (MMD, Wasserstein) by 10–25 F1 points at small subset sizes (|S|=400). Most strikingly, SDE reveals that Unroll—whose ASR (0.30) closely matches the Retrain baseline—actually retains training influence on 97% of forgetting subsets (OTR=3%), exposing a critical gap between traditional metrics and true unlearning effectiveness.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates four unlearning baselines using SDE's OTR metric alongside traditional metrics (Acc_r, Acc_f, ASR). RandLabel (Fan et al., 2024) demonstrates strong unlearning with 84% OTR, closely approaching the Retrain gold standard (87%), and its ASR (0.29) matches Retrain. Unroll (Thudi et al., 2022) is the most revealing comparison: its ASR (0.30) and accuracies are nearly identical to Retrain, suggesting effective unlearning by traditional criteria, yet SDE shows only 3% OTR—meaning 97% of forgetting subsets still exhibit in-training dependence, exposing Unroll as fundamentally ineffective. SalUn (Fan et al., 2024) achieves moderate OTR (~52%), indicating partial but incomplete unlearning despite matching Retrain's ASR. Sparsity (Jia et al., 2023) shows the weakest overall performance with lower accuracies (92.72% vs. 98.57% for Retrain), higher ASR (0.42), and only ~51% OTR. The key takeaway is that SDE's OTR metric discriminates unlearning quality far more sharply than ASR, which clusters all methods (except Sparsity) around 0.29–0.30.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Hilbert-Schmidt Independence Criterion (HSIC), a well-established kernel-based dependence measure from Gretton et al. (2005, 2007), into a novel split-half test for unlearning evaluation. It departs from two dominant prior paradigms: (1) the retrained-model-as-gold-standard comparison, which requires impractical access to a model trained without the forgetting data, and (2) per-sample membership inference attacks (MIA) that rely on shadow models, confidence scores, or auxiliary classifiers. The theoretical grounding draws on influence functions (Koh & Liang, 2017) to show that training-induced shared parameter shifts create detectable inter-sample dependence in model representations, a mechanism absent for out-of-training data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SDE provides a practical, standalone unlearning evaluation that requires no retrained reference model, no auxiliary classifier training, and no access to original training hyperparameters—addressing a key practical gap in deployment. The finding that Unroll achieves near-retrained-model ASR and accuracy yet retains in-training dependence on forgetting data (OTR ~3%) challenges the field's reliance on standard metrics and motivates rethinking how unlearning success is certified. Open questions include adaptive kernel bandwidth selection (the √dim heuristic fails for diffusion models), robust reference set construction, and distinguishing intentional unlearning from natural representation drift or catastrophic forgetting.
