## Problem

Chain-of-thought monitoring is a key tool for AI safety oversight, but adversaries or misaligned models could in principle evade it by reasoning in ciphered text (encrypted, translated, or compressed). While ciphered text has been used in adversarial attacks, no prior work systematically measured whether LLMs can actually *reason* in ciphered language as opposed to merely decoding it. This paper is the first detailed study of that capability, finding a striking asymmetry: models translate ciphered text fluently yet suffer large accuracy drops when reasoning in it.

## Input / Output

For the reasoning task, the model receives a math problem from MATH 500 plus a system-prompt cipher instruction (one of 28 ciphers, from rot13 to gzip-to-base64) and must produce a ciphered chain-of-thought with the final answer left unciphered in \boxed{}. For the translation task, ciphered reasoning traces from PRM800K are input and the model outputs the English equivalent, scored by BLEU against ground truth. The primary metric is the ratio of ciphered-adherent-and-correct answers to identity-adherent-and-correct answers, which isolates the capability loss attributable to ciphering from baseline difficulty.

## Method

The paper evaluates 10 models (GPT 4.1/mini/nano, Qwen2.5 3–14B, GPT 5, Claude Sonnet 3.5/4, Opus 3) across 28 ciphers using three paradigms: supervised fine-tuning on 16k NuminaMath-CoT problems (with three co-located example types per problem to preserve IID properties), 8-shot prompting with paired ciphered/plain-text demonstrations, and GRPO reinforcement learning with cipher-adherence-shaped rewards. The central finding is an asymmetry between high translation BLEU and large reasoning accuracy drops, which correlates strongly (R²=0.906 for structure-preserving ciphers) with cipher prevalence in pretraining data as measured by 4-gram frequencies in RedPajama via Infini-gram. Scaling laws show that even a simple letter-to-word cipher requires over 3.7B tokens of fine-tuning for Qwen2.5 14B to approach plain-text accuracy, while translation saturates under 125M tokens. Ablations over qualitative cipher characteristics reveal that syntactic and lexical transformations (leet speak, replacing math expressions with black boxes) cause far greater degradation than stylistic changes or distractor insertion.

## Results

Across 10 models and 28 ciphers, the paper finds a striking asymmetry: models lose 19–72% math accuracy when reasoning in ciphered text (e.g., Qwen2.5-14B drops from 66% to 34% on rot13 and to 3% on base64), yet simultaneously achieve BLEU scores of 85–99 on translating the same ciphers back to English. Even frontier models (GPT 4.1, Sonnet 4) retain <10% accuracy loss only on well-known ciphers like rot13 and base64, while lesser-known ciphers (reverse fibonacci indices, base64 2x/3x) cause >45% drops. Pretraining prevalence of a cipher correlates strongly with reasoning accuracy (R²=0.906 for structure-preserving ciphers), and scaling experiments show Qwen2.5-14B still cannot match plain-text accuracy after 3.7B tokens of ciphered fine-tuning, whereas translation fluency saturates below 125M tokens.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary baselines are identity (plain English reasoning) and direct answering (no reasoning). Ciphered reasoning consistently underperforms identity across all models and ciphers tested, with the gap widening as cipher obscurity increases. Against direct answering, many ciphers (base64 2x, base64 3x, gzip-to-base64, reverse fibonacci for smaller models) perform worse than simply guessing without reasoning, indicating the cipher actively impairs rather than merely obscures computation. In the RL comparison (Table 3), GRPO improves ciphered accuracy by up to 8.4 percentage points over SFT (e.g., base64 goes from 2.6% to 11% of identity for Qwen2.5-7B) but does not close the gap to identity. For few-shot prompted frontier models, Sonnet 4 and Sonnet 3.5 best preserve accuracy in well-known ciphers (<10% loss on rot13, base64), while GPT 5 and GPT 4.1 show larger drops on the same ciphers. Generally prompted translation models (simulating CoT monitors) can decode all ciphers where fine-tuned or few-shot models beat direct answering, suggesting current monitors remain effective against ciphered reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of CoT monitoring, LLM steganography, and cipher-based adversarial attacks, but carves out a distinct problem: whether models can actually *perform* reasoning (not just decode or extract) in ciphered text. Prior steganography work (Roger & Greenblatt, 2023; Zolkowski et al., 2025) studied encodings often unsuitable for general reasoning or externally-implemented mechanisms, while cipher-based attack literature (Halawi et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2023) focused on adversarially extracting harmful information rather than measuring reasoning capability. The paper explicitly frames ciphered reasoning as a more general prerequisite capability that includes encoded reasoning as a special case, and uses GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) for its RL experiments to test whether reinforcement learning can close the ciphered-reasoning gap.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The findings directly inform whether CoT monitoring—a cornerstone of frontier AI oversight—remains viable against ciphered reasoning evasion. The 3.7B-token scaling requirement and the strong correlation with pretraining prevalence (R²=0.906) provide concrete, actionable guidance: filtering ciphered text from pretraining data and monitoring for this capability in future models. Key open questions include the mechanism behind the comprehension-reasoning asymmetry, whether naturally emergent ciphers from RL could bypass the data-scaling barrier, and whether adversarially designed ciphers could be more effective than the 28 human-designed ones tested. The paper's limitations (no adversarial cipher optimization, no access to fine-tune GPT-5 or Claude, no RL optimization of the cipher itself) mean the reassuring findings may not transfer to future, more capable models.
