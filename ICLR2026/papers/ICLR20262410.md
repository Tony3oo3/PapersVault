## Problem

Existing GNNs face an unavoidable trade-off between cost-effectiveness and global receptive field: deep local models require stacking many layers for long-range propagation, while shallow global models (Graph Transformers) incur quadratic pairwise interaction costs. This dilemma is rooted in the inherent structure of existing paradigms, where total cost equals per-structure cost times number of structures, and no single structure type simultaneously controls both factors.

## Input / Output

The method takes an undirected graph with node feature matrix X ∈ R^{n×d}, adjacency matrix A, and a small set of labeled nodes (semi-supervised setting). Before tree sampling, the graph is augmented with k-NN edges derived from pseudo-labels to ensure connectivity and increase homophily. The output is a unified node embedding H'' ∈ R^{n×d} that feeds a linear classifier to predict class labels for all nodes, combining global tree-aggregated messages with local neighborhood information via a residual connection.

## Method

FGL reinterprets message passing as transportation over a forest of spanning trees, where each tree is the minimal subgraph achieving global coverage. A local attention-based homophily estimator assigns edge scores that bias the tree distribution toward homophilous trees (proven asymptotically tight in Theorem 2), and N_T trees are sampled via the weighted Wilson algorithm in near-linear time per tree. A general tree aggregator then propagates messages along each tree in O(n) time using two recursions: a bottom-up pass computing subtree aggregates S_u, and a top-down pass recovering each node's global embedding H'_v via add/delete operators M⁺/M⁻. Finally, a tree fuser averages row-normalized embeddings across all trees and blends them with local information through a residual connection, achieving linear overall complexity while capturing quadratic node-pair interactions.

## Results

FGL achieves the best average rank of 1.22 across all 9 semi-supervised node classification benchmarks, winning on every dataset. Headline accuracies include 85.46% on Cora, 81.00% on Pubmed, 91.89% on Texas, 86.27% on Wisconsin, 56.47% on OGBN-ArXiv, and 47.22% on Flickr. The paper reports 11.90% and 16.14% average relative accuracy gains over GCNII (best deep GNN) and DIFFormer (representative Graph Transformer), respectively, while running in under 0.25 s/epoch on ArXiv versus 0.545 s for DIFFormer and 2.843 s for GCNII.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FGL outperforms all 26 baselines on every one of the 9 datasets, achieving an average rank of 1.22 (effectively first on all benchmarks). Against the strongest deep GNN, GCNII (85.34/73.24/79.88 on Cora/Citeseer/Pubmed), FGL gains 11.90% average relative accuracy, with particularly large margins on heterophilous graphs (e.g., 91.89 vs. 69.19 on Texas, +32.9% relative). Against the best-performing Graph Transformer, SGFormer (avg. rank 7.22), FGL still leads on 8 of 9 datasets, with the largest gap on Texas (91.89 vs. 78.92) and Wisconsin (86.27 vs. 80.00). Against DIFFormer, the paper reports a 16.14% average relative gain, and against GOAT (52.46 on ArXiv) FGL achieves 56.47. On the large-scale OGBN-ArXiv benchmark, several baselines (GT, SAN, Graphormer, TDGNN) run out of memory, while FGL completes in 0.246 s/epoch with 56.47% accuracy, surpassing the best memory-feasible baseline (DIFFormer at 53.60%). On heterophilous benchmarks (Texas, Wisconsin, Cornell, Actor), FGL's advantages are most pronounced, e.g., 91.89% on Texas vs. 78.92% (SGFormer) and 77.84% (NodeFormer), confirming the paradigm's strength in capturing long-range homophilous structure under label scarcity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against the two dominant paradigms for long-range graph learning: deep local GNNs (GCNII, DropEdge) that stack many cheap local layers, and shallow global Graph Transformers (GT, Graphormer, GOAT) that perform expensive quadratic pairwise interactions in few layers. It departs from both by introducing spanning trees as an intermediate structure that simultaneously controls per-structure cost and number of structures, building on Wilson's (1996) algorithm for near-linear-time random spanning tree sampling. The homophily-guided tree distribution and the two-recursion tree aggregator are novel contributions, while the local attention mechanism for edge scoring and the residual tree fuser draw on standard GNN/Transformer components.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The forest-based paradigm offers a principled escape from the cost-vs-receptive-field trade-off that has constrained graph learning, achieving linear complexity with competitive or superior accuracy across nine benchmarks. The theoretical guarantee (Theorem 2) that refining the homophily estimator provably improves the induced tree distribution provides a clear optimization target for future work. Open questions include scalability to very large or highly disconnected graphs (acknowledged in Appendix A.9), extension beyond node classification to link prediction or graph-level tasks, and whether the forest paradigm can be combined with other recent advances like state-space models or diffusion-based attention.
