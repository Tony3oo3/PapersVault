## Problem

In federated learning with heterogeneous data distributions, each agent's model quality depends on *who* supplies each training sample, not merely the total count, yet prior incentive mechanisms either assumed exchangeable data or ignored strategic behavior entirely. This paper asks how to align self-interested agents with distinct local distributions so that each meets a PAC accuracy threshold on its own distribution at minimal total cost, and shows that uncoordinated play can be arbitrarily inefficient (unbounded Price of Stability) or even lack pure Nash equilibria.

## Input / Output

The mechanism takes as input each agent's local marginal data distribution (reported as finite pairwise-disagreement probability vectors over the hypothesis class), per-sample costs c_i, PAC parameters (ε, δ), and the hypothesis class H (with VC dimension d). It outputs an integer contribution vector m specifying how many labeled samples each agent must supply, and a payment vector p where each agent receives c_i·m_i + C_i (pay-what-you-contribute plus a constant). The LP requires only summary distributional statistics, never raw examples, keeping communication finite and privacy-preserving.

## Method

The paper formulates a game where each agent's binary PAC-threshold utility depends on the identity of every sample's source, then proves three core results: (1) pure Nash equilibria may not exist and the best equilibrium's cost exceeds the cooperative optimum by Ω(log(1/ε)); (2) computing the cost-minimizing contribution vector is NP-hard (via reduction from SET COVER), but a polynomial-time LP over pairwise disagreement constraints yields a logarithmic-factor approximation; (3) pairing this LP allocation with a pay-what-you-contribute payment rule (p_i = c_i·m_i + C_i) produces a strategyproof mechanism that is unique within the class of contribution-based payment rules, as shown via a local-obliviousness property of the approximation algorithm. For infinite hypothesis classes, the LP is solved over a finite γ-cover (sized polynomially in 1/ε and 1/δ via Haussler's bound) and the solution is scaled by d + log(1/δ'') to recover full-class PAC guarantees.

## Results

The paper's headline results are theoretical: the Price of Stability is unbounded, scaling as Ω(log(1/ε)), and computing the cost-minimizing contribution vector is NP-hard even for a single agent. The LP relaxation achieves a (log(1/δ) + log|H|) / log(1/δ) approximation for finite hypothesis classes, and the pay-what-you-contribute (PWYC) mechanism is proven strategyproof and unique within the contribution-based class. Empirically, on FEMNIST with a finite hypothesis class, the LP-to-optimum cost ratio ranges from 1.67 (|H|=5) to 4.24 (|H|=25), consistent with the logarithmic guarantee.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a primarily theoretical paper, so there are no direct method-vs-method benchmark comparisons. The empirical validation in Appendix A.2 compares the LP-based allocation against a brute-force discrete search for the true optimum on FEMNIST, confirming the logarithmic approximation factor predicted by Theorem 3 (ratios between 1.67× and 4.24× as |H| grows from 5 to 50). The theoretical analysis compares uncoordinated Nash equilibria to the cooperative optimum, showing the gap can be Ω(log(1/ε)) (Theorem 1), and contrasts the PWYC mechanism against VCG, noting that VCG is infeasible due to the NP-hardness of computing the exact optimum (Theorem 2). The paper positions itself as extending Blum et al. (2021) by introducing PAC-threshold utilities and a concrete mechanism, rather than competing with a specific prior method on a shared benchmark.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the collaborative PAC learning framework of Blum et al. (2017) and the game-theoretic federated learning model of Blum et al. (2021) by introducing a heterogeneous-data utility structure where each agent's payoff depends on which agent supplies each sample, not merely the total count. It departs from prior incentive mechanisms in FL (Karimireddy et al. 2022; Murhekar et al. 2024) that assumed exchangeable data, and from reputation-based or VCG-style schemes, by proving that under PAC threshold objectives the allocation problem is NP-hard and that the only strategyproof contribution-based mechanism is the trivial pay-what-you-contribute rule. The LP approximation with logarithmic guarantees bridges the gap between the intractable optimum and a computable, incentive-compatible allocation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unbounded Price of Stability result quantifies how badly uncoordinated FL can fail, while the uniqueness of PWYC (Theorem 4) is a substantive negative result: no clever payment scheme can reduce the planner's reimbursement burden within the contribution-based class, meaning external budget or cost-sharing is structurally unavoidable in heterogeneous FL. Open questions include whether the uniqueness extends beyond two agents, whether richer (non-threshold) utility models admit more flexible mechanisms, and whether the LP approximation's logarithmic factor is tight in practice. The centralized-ERM benchmark and linear-cost assumption, while noted as simplifications, limit direct transfer to real FL protocols like FedAvg where optimization dynamics matter.
