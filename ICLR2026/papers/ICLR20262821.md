## Problem

Scaling imitation learning to high-DoF whole-body robots is fundamentally constrained by expert demonstration scarcity, while the abundant suboptimal data from teleoperation and policy rollouts introduces two unsolved challenges: principled extraction of informative signals from imperfect trajectories, and effective credit assignment in high-dimensional, long-horizon control. Existing offline RL methods have been restricted to low-dimensional arm-based tasks and single-modality settings, failing to scale to structured 21-DoF whole-body systems with multi-modal observations.

## Input / Output

The method consumes four input modalities processed through specialized encoders: three egocentric RGB views (front, left, right) encoded by SigLIP, depth-derived point clouds encoded by PointNet, natural language task instructions tokenized and embedded, and proprioceptive state (joint angles, velocities, end-effector poses) normalized into a compact vector. All modality tokens are concatenated and processed by a PaliGemma transformer backbone. The output is a 21-DoF joint-level action chunk of size k=20, structured hierarchically into three sub-actions (base, torso, arm) where each level's Q-value conditions on progressively more complete subsets of the action space, enabling component-specific credit assignment.

## Method

HVD introduces hierarchy directly into the Q-value function through kinematic spatial decomposition (base → torso → arm), rather than decomposing the policy. Three hierarchical Q-heads compute value estimates over temporal action chunks, where each level conditions on an increasingly complete action subset, and a multi-level TD loss aligns each partial Q-value with its chunk-aggregated return. An implicit exponential value loss (adapted from IDQL) is applied across all hierarchical levels to establish a soft lower bound on value estimates and mitigate OOD overestimation. Policy extraction uses a hierarchical variant of advantage-weighted regression with diffusion-based action generation, where importance weights are computed independently at each kinematic level, allowing critical sub-actions to be emphasized even when other components generate lower returns. The total policy loss combines this RL term (trained on the full mixed-quality dataset) with a BC term (trained on a smaller expert subset) for stabilization. The entire framework is implemented on a PaliGemma transformer backbone that natively supports multi-modal and multi-task learning without architectural modification.

## Results

On the WB-50 dataset across five whole-body manipulation tasks, HVD consistently outperforms imitation learning baselines trained on expert-only data. Average success rates improve from 0.44 to 0.58 for π₀, from 0.30 to 0.46 for WB-VIMA, and from 0.26 to 0.32 for Diffusion Policy. Gains are most pronounced on high-complexity tasks: Wipe Board (π₀: 0.18→0.32) and Basket Carry (π₀: 0.26→0.44). On the BEHAVIOR-1K simulator, π₀-HVD achieves 77.0% and 22.0% success on the approaching and grasping stages respectively, versus 45.0% and 5.0% for π₀-IL.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HVD is compared against three imitation learning baselines—π₀ (a VLA flow model), WB-VIMA (a 3D point-cloud policy), and Diffusion Policy (DP)—each trained on expert-only demonstrations, while HVD is trained on the full mixed-quality WB-50 dataset (43.7% expert, 14.6% imperfect teleoperation, 41.7% policy rollouts). HVD wins on every task for every baseline, with the largest absolute gains on the hardest tasks: Wipe Board (+0.14 for π₀, +0.14 for WB-VIMA) and Basket Carry (+0.18 for π₀, +0.00 for WB-VIMA which already fails at 0.10). The relative improvement is most dramatic for π₀ on Wipe Board (77% relative gain) and for DP on Basket Carry (from 0.00 to 0.08). On the BEHAVIOR-1K simulator, π₀-HVD nearly quadruples the grasping-stage success rate over π₀-IL (22.0% vs. 5.0%) and substantially improves the approaching stage (77.0% vs. 45.0%). In multi-task settings, HVD also avoids the negative transfer that plagues standard IL (multi-task IL drops from 0.44 to 0.36 average SR, while multi-task HVD improves from 0.58 to 0.60).

## Relation to Prior Work

HVD extends the IDQL/IQL offline RL framework (implicit value learning + diffusion-based policy extraction via advantage-weighted regression) to the high-DoF whole-body control setting, where prior offline RL for robotics was largely confined to arm-based manipulators and single-modality inputs. The key conceptual departure from prior hierarchical control work (which decomposes the policy, e.g., Sentis & Khatib) is decomposing the Q-value function along the robot's kinematic chain (base → torso → arm) with temporal chunking, enabling component-specific credit assignment while maintaining a unified Transformer-based policy. The multi-modal architecture (SigLIP for RGB, PointNet for depth, PaliGemma as backbone) positions HVD as a drop-in offline RL layer on top of modern VLA-style models like π0, rather than a standalone policy architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that structured offline RL over abundant imperfect data (43.7% expert, 14.6% suboptimal teleop, 41.7% rollouts) can outperform imitation learning on expert-only data for 21-DoF whole-body tasks, directly addressing the expert-data bottleneck that limits scaling of VLA and diffusion policies to high-DoF robots. The multi-task result—where HVD shows positive transfer (0.60 avg SR) while IL shows negative transfer (0.36 avg SR)—suggests hierarchical value decomposition acts as a regularizer that enables shared learning across tasks. Key limitations include reliance on human-annotated rewards (~30 hours of labeling for 50 hours of data), evaluation on a single robot platform (Galaxea R1) with five tasks, and the open question of whether automated reward labeling (e.g., via VLMs) can remove the annotation bottleneck. The released WB-50 dataset and code provide a practical starting point for the community.
