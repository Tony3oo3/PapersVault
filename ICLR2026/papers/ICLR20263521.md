## Problem

Existing block-pruning methods for LLMs (LLMDrop, ShortGPT, LaCo) uniformly rely on cosine similarity between adjacent block inputs and outputs to identify redundant computation blocks. Cosine similarity captures geometric alignment of hidden representations but does not directly quantify the information content each block contributes, potentially leading to suboptimal pruning decisions. This paper asks whether an information-theoretic measure—specifically Shannon entropy of hidden states—can serve as a more principled criterion for identifying which blocks to remove.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pre-trained decoder-only Transformer (Llama3.1-8B, Mistral-7B-v0.3, Qwen3-14B) and a small calibration dataset (C4, Wikitext2, Law, or Medicine). A single unmasked forward pass over calibration samples yields hidden states at every computation block; no masked or auxiliary forward passes are required. The output is a structurally pruned model with K attention (or full Transformer) blocks removed, yielding near-linear inference speedup proportional to the fraction of pruned blocks (e.g., ~37.5% FLOPs reduction for 12 of 32 attention blocks).

## Method

EntroDrop is grounded in an empirical observation that hidden-state entropy follows a two-stage pattern across depth: it decreases in the first few layers (information compression) and then progressively increases in subsequent layers (contextual expansion). Only blocks in the entropy-increasing stage (Stage 2, starting at a data-determined transition point S_start) are candidates for pruning. Each candidate block is scored by its entropy increase ΔH^l = H(Z^l) − H(Z^{l−1}), and the K blocks with the smallest ΔH are removed. Entropy is estimated by discretizing hidden activations via bucket-based frequency counting or KNN density estimation; both outperform Rényi entropy in practice. The approach is applied at the attention-block granularity (since attention is more redundant than MLP) and requires only one calibration forward pass, making it more efficient than transfer-entropy-based alternatives that need masked passes.

## Results

On Llama3.1-8B, EntroDrop (attention pruning) retains 93% of the base model's average accuracy (0.5467 vs. 0.5872) after pruning 12 of 32 attention blocks (37.5%), while layer-pruning baselines LaCo and ShortGPT collapse to 0.3141 and 0.3346 respectively. On Mistral-7B-v0.3, EntroDrop achieves 0.5071 average at L=12 versus 0.3110 (LaCo) and 0.3014 (ShortGPT). At the most aggressive budget (L=16), EntroDrop still leads LLMDrop (cosine-similarity attention pruning) by 4 points on Llama3.1-8B (0.4603 vs. 0.4207) and by 3.5 points on Mistral-7B (0.4407 vs. 0.4060).

## Baselines & Comparisons

EntroDrop (attention pruning) dominates both layer-pruning baselines by a wide margin: at L=12 on Llama3.1-8B, it scores 0.5467 average versus 0.3141 for LaCo and 0.3346 for ShortGPT, a gap of over 20 points. Against the closest competitor LLMDrop (cosine-similarity-based attention pruning), EntroDrop pulls ahead as the pruning budget grows—matching it at L=4–8 (≈0.59) but leading by 4.0 points at L=16 on Llama3.1-8B (0.4603 vs. 0.4207) and by 3.5 points on Mistral-7B (0.4407 vs. 0.4060). The advantage is most pronounced on knowledge-intensive tasks: at L=16 on Llama3.1-8B, EntroDrop retains 0.3586 MMLU accuracy while LLMDrop drops to 0.2735. On Mistral-7B at L=12, EntroDrop achieves 0.5071 average compared to LLMDrop's 0.3339, confirming that entropy-based block selection identifies more redundant attention layers than cosine similarity. Layer-pruning variants of EntroDrop (Ours Layer) also outperform LaCo and ShortGPT at moderate budgets (L=4–8) but degrade sharply at L≥12, reinforcing that attention-only pruning is the more effective granularity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits within the rapidly growing line of LLM structural pruning work (LaCo, ShortGPT, LLMDrop) that identifies redundant Transformer blocks for removal. Its key departure is replacing the near-universal cosine-similarity criterion with an entropy-increase metric, motivated by the empirical observation that hidden-state entropy follows a two-stage compression-then-expansion pattern across depth. Conceptually, it repurposes Shannon entropy—an information-theoretic quantity previously applied to parameter-level sparsity (E-Sparse) or depth reduction in non-LLM networks (NEPENTHE)—as a block-level importance signal, requiring only a single forward pass over a calibration set rather than the masked-forward-pass sensitivity analysis used by transfer-entropy approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that ~37.5% of attention blocks can be removed from 7–14B decoder-only LLMs with under 5% average accuracy drop, offering a straightforward path to near-linear latency reduction without retraining. However, the two-stage entropy pattern is not universal across all architectures, and the method still requires a calibration dataset and a chosen entropy estimator (Bucket/KNN outperform Rényi), leaving open questions about architecture-agnostic applicability and whether the gains compound with other compression techniques (quantization, MoE expert pruning). The post-training recovery experiments suggest entropy pruning preserves more intrinsic plasticity than cosine pruning, but recovery remains partial even with task-aligned fine-tuning, indicating a hard ceiling on how much structure can be discarded.
