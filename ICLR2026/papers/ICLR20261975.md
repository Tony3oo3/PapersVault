## Problem

Systematically evaluating and improving LLMs' persuasive capabilities is inherently challenging because persuasion effectiveness varies across domains, prior evaluations rely on heterogeneous setups and subjective human judgments, and no unified theoretical framework exists to measure persuasion in a principled, scalable way. This paper addresses the gap by grounding LLM persuasion in Bayesian persuasion theory to provide both a rigorous evaluation metric and a training objective.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261975/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The Sender LLM receives a claim over a controversial topic, a prior belief distribution, utility functions for both agents, a discrete 7-point Likert action space, and the realized state of nature; it produces structured argument text (opinion, thinking, argument tags). The Receiver LLM (a fixed proxy for human decision-makers) outputs a stance score on the 1–7 scale along with its reasoning and follow-up questions. The primary evaluation metric is the persuasion gain—the change in the Sender's expected utility relative to the prior—measured over 475 instances across four repurposed human persuasion datasets in both single-round (static) and three-round (dynamic) settings.

## Method

The paper constructs a scalable benchmark by repurposing four human-human persuasion datasets (Anthropic, CMV, DDO, Perspectrum) into controlled Sender–Receiver strategic interactions where both roles are implemented by LLMs, grounded in the Bayesian persuasion framework of Kamenica & Gentzkow (2011) and its dynamic extension by Ely (2017). Persuasion is formalized as strategic information revelation: the Sender designs signals to shift the Receiver's posterior beliefs in a favorable direction subject to Bayes plausibility, with the Sender's utility defined as a simple score-mapping over the Receiver's final stance. For training, the paper applies reinforcement learning (PPO and GRPO via the verl framework) to update a small Sender model (Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct) against a fixed Receiver (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct), using the persuasion gain relative to the prior as the reward signal. The key finding is that even 3B-parameter models trained this way achieve persuasion gains comparable to much larger frontier models, and the learned strategies generalize across different Receiver architectures, suggesting the models acquire transferable principles of information design rather than exploiting a specific Receiver.

## Results

Across four opinion-change datasets (Anthropic, CMV, DDO, Perspectrum), frontier models achieve substantially higher persuasion gains than smaller models, with the gap widening dramatically in dynamic (3-round) settings. DeepSeek-R1 attains the highest average persuasion gain of 0.23 (static) and 1.27 (dynamic) on a 1–7 Likert scale, corresponding to roughly 3.3% and 18.1% of the full utility range. Claude 3.7 Sonnet follows closely (0.14 / 1.04), while 7B-class open-source models (Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-7B, Qwen2.5-7B) cluster near 0.01–0.04 static and 0.23–0.42 dynamic. Reinforcement learning (PPO/GRPO) on Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct lifts dynamic persuasion gains from 0.21 to 0.38 against Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, approaching the performance of much larger untrained models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks seven Sender models against a fixed Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct Receiver. DeepSeek-R1 dominates all baselines in both settings, achieving 1.27 average dynamic gain versus 1.04 for Claude 3.7 Sonnet, 0.62 for GPT-4o, 0.44 for Llama-3.3-70B, and 0.23–0.42 for the 7B open-source models (Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-7B, Qwen2.5-7B). In static (single-round) settings, gains are modest across the board (0.01–0.23), but the dynamic setting amplifies the gap by roughly 5×, indicating that multi-round adaptive disclosure is where model capability differentiates most. The 70B Llama model (0.44 dynamic) outperforms the 7B Llama (0.42) only marginally, while GPT-4o (0.62) and Claude 3.7 Sonnet (1.04) show that frontier closed-source models substantially exceed open-weight counterparts of similar or larger parameter count. In the RL training experiments (Table 2), the trained Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct (PPO/GRPO) reaches 0.38 dynamic gain against Llama-3.1-8B, surpassing the untrained 7B baselines (Mistral 0.31, Qwen 0.23) but still trailing GPT-4o and DeepSeek-R1, confirming that RL can close part of the capability gap but not eliminate it.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper grounds LLM persuasion evaluation in Bayesian persuasion theory (Kamenica & Gentzkow, 2011) and its dynamic extension (Ely, 2017), providing a principled Sender-Receiver game formulation where persuasion is strategic information revelation. It departs from prior LLM persuasion work (Durmus et al. 2024; Salvi et al. 2024) that relied on heterogeneous human-evaluation protocols without a unified theoretical foundation, instead repurposing human-human persuasion datasets (CMV, DDO, Perspectrum, Anthropic) into controlled multi-agent LLM interactions. The RL training component (PPO, GRPO via verl) draws on the broader post-training literature for LLMs, while the strategic-reasoning positioning connects to recent work on LLMs in matrix games and mechanism design, though information design had been largely unexplored in that context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a scalable, theory-grounded alternative to expensive and subjective human evaluation of LLM persuasiveness, and the finding that RL can meaningfully close the gap between small and frontier models in strategic persuasion is practically encouraging. However, key limitations remain: LLMs are imperfect Bayesian receivers (incoherent judgments, limited goal-directedness), the learned strategies may not transfer to real human receivers, and the 7-point Likert scale is a coarse proxy for belief states. Open questions include whether the framework generalizes beyond opinion-change to other persuasion domains (health, finance, security), how to account for receiver heterogeneity in training, and whether the strategic behaviors observed in LLM-LLM interactions predict real-world persuasive impact.
