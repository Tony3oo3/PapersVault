## Problem

Post-training methods like SFT and RFT are widely used to adapt multimodal LLMs to downstream tasks, but their differential impact on retaining prior knowledge remains unexplained. While SFT rapidly acquires new tasks, it causes severe catastrophic forgetting; RFT learns more slowly but preserves prior knowledge. The paper asks why this asymmetry exists and whether it stems from the training algorithm itself or from the distribution of training data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260517/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For the jigsaw puzzle task, the model receives 9 shuffled image patches (from a 3×3 grid of a COCO image, resized so dimensions are divisible by 3) and must output the canonical row-major indices 0–8 enclosed in brackets, optionally preceded by a reasoning trajectory in <think> tags. For math and science QA, inputs are text problems or four-choice questions, and outputs are verifiable answers. The jigsaw task is verified as genuinely novel: GPT-4o and Qwen2.5-VL-72B achieve 0% accuracy and near-chance hit rates, confirming it is absent from pretraining corpora.

## Method

The paper introduces jigsaw puzzles as a controlled novel task and systematically compares SFT (on non-reasoning data, GPT-4o-generated reasoning, and RFT-collected rollouts) against GRPO-based RFT on Qwen2.5-VL-3B/7B. The key analytical tool is a learning-dynamics decomposition (Theorem 5.1) that expresses the one-step change in prior-knowledge likelihood as a product of model sensitivity vectors and the empirical neural tangent kernel (eNTK) between training and prior examples, separating interference into magnitude (measured via a lower-bound kernel norm, LBK) and direction (measured via base-model perplexity of training samples). The central finding is that RFT naturally samples from low-perplexity regions of the base model's output distribution, and by the symmetry property of learning dynamics (Theorem 5.2), reinforcing these already-aligned samples causes minimal degradation of prior knowledge. Consequently, SFT trained on RFT-generated rollouts (Rea-GRPO-Rollout) matches RFT's new-task accuracy while forgetting far less than SFT on GPT-4o reasoning or non-reasoning data, demonstrating that data distribution—not algorithmic choice—is the primary driver of forgetting.

## Results

On Qwen2.5-VL-3B, RFT (GRPO, 27,360 steps) achieves 66% jigsaw accuracy while preserving prior knowledge (RefCOCOval drops only 0.4 pts, MME drops 3 pts). SFT-Non-Rea reaches 53% jigsaw in just 200 steps but causes catastrophic forgetting (RefCOCOval drops 82.8 pts, MME drops 509 pts). SFT-Rea-GRPO-Rollout matches RFT's jigsaw accuracy (70%) in only 2,670 steps while retaining prior knowledge far better than SFT-Non-Rea (RefCOCOval drops 4.2 vs. 82.8) and SFT-Rea-4o-Rollout (RefCOCOval drops 4.2 vs. 14.6). On Qwen2.5-VL-7B, the same hierarchy holds: RFT reaches 75% jigsaw with <1 pt drops on prior benchmarks, while SFT-Non-Rea drops RefCOCOval by 57.2 pts and MME by 1,854 pts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four post-training strategies: RFT (GRPO), SFT on non-reasoning data (SFT-Non-Rea), SFT on GPT-4o-generated reasoning (SFT-Rea-4o-Rollout), and SFT on model-generated GRPO rollouts (SFT-Rea-GRPO-Rollout). SFT-Non-Rea is the worst on prior knowledge retention: on 3B it drops RefCOCOval by 82.8 pts and MME by 509 pts, while on 7B it drops RefCOCOval by 57.2 pts and MME by 1,854 pts. SFT-Rea-4o-Rollout reduces but does not eliminate forgetting (RefCOCOval drops 14.6/37.5 pts on 3B/7B). SFT-Rea-GRPO-Rollout and RFT both preserve prior knowledge well (RefCOCOval drops ≤8.6 pts), with SFT-Rea-GRPO-Rollout matching or exceeding RFT's jigsaw accuracy (70% vs. 66% on 3B; 81% vs. 75% on 7B) at a fraction of the training cost (2,670–3,000 steps vs. 27,360 steps). The consistent hierarchy of forgetting severity across all benchmarks and both model scales is Non-Rea > Rea-4o > Rea-GRPO ≈ RFT, and this pattern replicates on LLM math reasoning (Qwen2.5-Instruct) and scientific QA tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the GRPO algorithm (Shao et al., 2024) for reinforcement fine-tuning and the learning-dynamics framework of Ren & Sutherland (2024), which decomposes how one training example's gradient affects another example's likelihood via the empirical neural tangent kernel. It departs from prior catastrophic-forgetting work in MLLMs (e.g., Jigsaw-R1, RL's Razor) by shifting the explanatory focus from algorithmic differences (SFT vs. RFT) to the distribution of training data, showing that model-generated low-perplexity rollouts are the operative factor. The jigsaw puzzle task extends Jigsaw-R1's limited-accuracy RFT results by training for tens of thousands of steps, and the analysis generalizes beyond vision to LLM math and science QA.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway—that a short RFT phase (even one epoch with <5% accuracy) suffices to generate SFT data that preserves prior knowledge while rapidly acquiring new tasks—offers a concrete recipe for stable continual post-training without requiring full RFT convergence. The learning-dynamics decomposition (magnitude via eNTK norm, direction via perplexity alignment) provides a principled lens for designing fine-tuning data, though the analysis relies on the eNTK stability assumption and is validated only on Qwen2.5-VL-3B/7B and Qwen2.5-3B/7B. Open questions include whether the low-perplexity alignment mechanism generalizes to larger models, different architectures, and a broader range of genuinely novel tasks beyond jigsaw puzzles and math.
