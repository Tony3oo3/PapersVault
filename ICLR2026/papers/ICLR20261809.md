## Problem

Existing science agents target open-ended scientific problems (hypothesis generation, experiment design, report writing) whose outputs are inherently subjective and hard to evaluate rigorously, while scientific coding benchmarks with objectively verifiable, executable outputs are typically approached with general-purpose agents or engineering-centric pipelines that lack structured framework design. This paper addresses the gap by introducing a principled agent framework for scientific coding tasks that operates under constrained search budgets without relying on pre-specified success metrics or prolonged search cycles.

## Input / Output

The agent receives a natural-language task description and input data files stored in a './input' directory, and produces a single-file, self-contained Python program that must execute within a 15-minute time limit and save outputs (e.g., a 'submission.csv' for ML tasks) in a './working' directory. The search is constrained by a fixed budget: 5 initial solution candidates, at most 3 self-debug steps per candidate, and a total of 10 exploration steps (including self-improvement), with K=2 branches retained at each tree-search iteration.

## Method

SciNav employs Top-K Comparative Tree Search (TKCTS), a structured tree search where candidate solutions are ranked via pairwise relative judgments rather than absolute LLM scoring. The framework has four interacting components: (1) initial planning generates multiple diverse high-level plans and corresponding programs to seed the search; (2) self-debug uses a code interpreter to detect and repair execution errors on-the-fly; (3) self-improve prompts the model to identify and apply a single atomic refinement to a frontier solution; and (4) a Frontier Comparator performs iterative pairwise comparisons between candidate solutions, updating an Elo-based ranking (K=32) after each comparison. At each iteration, only the top-K (K=2) highest-ranked branches are retained for expansion while lower-ranked ones are pruned, balancing exploration breadth with cost control. The key insight is that comparative judgments reveal finer-grained quality differences than pointwise scoring, providing sharper and more reliable signals for branch selection under constrained budgets without requiring ground-truth evaluation criteria at runtime.

## Results

On ScienceAgentBench, SciNav achieves a Success Rate (SR) of 16.1% with GPT-4o (2024-05-13), 18.6% with GPT-4o (2024-11-20), 25.5% with Claude-3.7, and 19.6% with DeepSeek-R1, consistently outperforming the strongest baseline Self-Debug by 1.4–3.0 absolute SR points across all four base models. On DA-Code, SciNav delivers 29 absolute points improvement over Self-Debug on data manipulation and statistical analysis tasks, and 13 absolute points on average, with gains of 10–23 points across easy, medium, and hard difficulty levels. The frontier comparator analysis confirms that relative judgments (SR 18.6%, VER 69.9%) outperform both random selection (SR 15.2%) and LLM absolute scoring (SR 16.2%) on GPT-4o (2024-11-20).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Self-Debug (the strongest baseline), SciNav wins on SR across all four base models: 16.1 vs. 14.7 (GPT-4o 0513), 18.6 vs. 15.0 (GPT-4o 1120, a 24% relative gain), 25.5 vs. 22.5 (Claude-3.7), and 19.6 vs. 18.6 (DeepSeek-R1). On VER, SciNav wins on GPT-4o (1120) and DeepSeek-R1 but trails Self-Debug on GPT-4o (0513) and Claude-3.7, which the authors attribute to Self-Debug being allowed 10 debug steps versus SciNav's 3. Against OpenHands, SciNav achieves a 22.9% relative SR gain (16.1 vs. 13.1) and a 3.2-point VER advantage (66.0 vs. 62.8) while costing roughly half as much ($0.512 vs. $1.093 per task). On DA-Code, SciNav outperforms Self-Debug by 29 absolute points on data manipulation and statistical analysis, 13 points on average, and 23 points on hard tasks, with a statistically significant difference (Mann-Whitney U p = 0.0177). The frontier comparator ablation further shows that relative judgments beat both random selection (SR 15.2%) and LLM absolute scoring (SR 16.2%), validating the core design choice of pairwise comparison over pointwise scoring.

## Relation to Prior Work

SciNav builds on the tree-search paradigm for code generation introduced by AIDE (Jiang et al., 2025) and the self-debugging loop from Chen et al. (2024), but departs from both by replacing task-defined success metrics (e.g., leaderboard accuracy) with LLM-based pairwise relative judgments as the selection signal. It also positions itself against general-purpose agents like OpenHands and Auto-GPT, which the authors argue are engineering-driven pipelines lacking structured exploration, and against open-ended science agents (ResearchAgent, AgentLab, AlphaEvolve) whose outputs resist objective evaluation. The key conceptual departure is the Top-K Comparative Tree Search (TKCTS) mechanism, which uses Elo-ranked pairwise comparisons to prune and expand solution branches under a fixed comparison budget, rather than relying on absolute scores or ground-truth rubrics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a real gap: scientific coding benchmarks have objective, executable outputs, yet prior agents either target open-ended science (subjective evaluation) or use generic engineering pipelines without principled search. The relative-judgment frontier comparator is a practical, generalizable selection strategy that does not require pre-specified metrics, making it applicable to diverse scientific domains where evaluation criteria are heterogeneous. However, absolute success rates remain low (16–25% SR on ScienceAgentBench), and the error analysis reveals that 84% of failures stem from exploration rather than selection, suggesting the framework would benefit from substantially more test-time compute. Open questions include whether the approach scales to longer-horizon multi-step scientific workflows and whether the cost premium (~10× over Self-Debug) is justified in deployment settings.
