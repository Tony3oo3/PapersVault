## Problem

Small Language Models (SLMs) are attractive for cost-sensitive and latency-constrained deployments but struggle with knowledge-intensive multi-hop reasoning tasks that require structured problem decomposition, iterative evidence retrieval, and multi-step aggregation. Existing retrieval-augmented approaches (standard RAG, Search-o1) either use fixed single-step retrieval or rely on the model's own limited reasoning capacity, leaving SLMs without an effective mechanism to acquire strategic retrieval planning. The paper asks whether structured thinking-pattern priors can be transferred from large teacher models to small student models in a training-free manner to close this reasoning gap.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a natural-language multi-hop question as input and, through a ReAct-style orchestration loop (max 10 reasoning steps), issues parallel web search queries via the Google Search API (top-10 results per query). The output is twofold: a structured four-stage reasoning plan (decomposed objectives, mechanistic conditions, selected retrieval strategy, and retrieval guidelines) produced by the teacher model's FutureMind tool, and a final concise answer generated by the student model after integrating retrieved evidence. The student model consumes the teacher's strategic guidance as a tool-call response within its reasoning trace, rather than as a fixed prompt template.

## Method

FutureMind is a training-free, four-stage modular pipeline—Problem Analysis, Logical Reasoning, Strategy Planning, and Retrieval Guidance—coordinated by a Thinking Module that a larger teacher LLM executes as a callable tool. The key mechanism is adaptive thinking-pattern distillation: the teacher generates a complete logical chain from problem decomposition through retrieval strategy selection, and the student SLM follows this plan within a ReAct-style ToolCall loop augmented with parallel search. Strategy Planning dynamically selects among three retrieval paradigms (Forward Stepwise Reasoning, Backward Constraint Focusing, Parallel Intersection Reasoning) based on the topology of extracted conditions, formalized as sequential, reverse, or parallel set-intersection operations. A central empirical finding is the 'cognitive bias bottleneck': mid-scale teachers (e.g., 14B) outperform both smaller and larger teachers because overly complex plans exceed the student's capacity to execute, making teacher-student architectural and cognitive alignment more critical than raw model scale.

## Results

On four multi-hop QA benchmarks (2WikiMultihopQA, Bamboogle, FRAMES, MuSiQue), FutureMind (TC+FM*) achieves average ACC_E of 54.80 and ACC_L of 63.75 with Qwen-72B, and 32.16 / 30.41 with Qwen-3B, consistently outperforming all baselines across every model scale. The largest relative gains appear on smaller models: Qwen-3B improves from 24.39 (Search-o1) to 32.16 on average ACC_E (+7.77 points), and Llama3.1-8B improves from 35.17 to 40.71 (+5.54 points). The framework is training-free, requiring no gradient updates, and uses two complementary metrics: Coverage-based Exact Match (ACC_E) and LLM-as-Judge (ACC_L, GPT-4o-mini).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FutureMind (TC+FM*) decisively outperforms both baselines across all six model configurations. Against Standard RAG, the gains are substantial: on average ACC_E, Qwen-7B improves from 25.39 to 41.38 (+15.99) and Qwen-3B from 18.15 to 32.16 (+14.01), reflecting that single-shot retrieval cannot handle multi-hop decomposition. Against Search-o1, the strongest baseline, TC+FM* still wins on average ACC_E for every model: Qwen-3B (32.16 vs 24.39, +7.77), Qwen-7B (41.38 vs 36.61, +4.77), Qwen-14B (50.21 vs 40.27, +9.94), Qwen-32B (51.59 vs 46.63, +4.96), Qwen-72B (54.80 vs 50.44, +4.36), and Llama3.1-8B (40.71 vs 35.17, +5.54). The advantage is most pronounced on smaller models (3B–7B), where Search-o1's fixed-prompt retrieval logic is least effective, while on the 72B model the gap narrows to ~4–5 points, suggesting that larger models partially compensate for the lack of explicit strategy planning. On the ACC_L (LLM-as-Judge) metric, the same pattern holds with TC+FM* leading in every setting, e.g., Qwen-72B reaches 63.75 vs Search-o1's 58.92.

## Relation to Prior Work

FutureMind builds on the ReAct-style orchestration paradigm (Yao et al., 2023) and the search-as-a-tool line of work (Search-o1, Li et al., 2025a), but departs from both by replacing fixed-prompt or self-initiated retrieval with a structured four-stage reasoning pipeline (Problem Analysis → Logical Reasoning → Strategy Planning → Retrieval Guidance) whose strategic plans are generated by a teacher LLM and consumed by a student SLM in a training-free manner. It also extends the knowledge-distillation literature (CoT distillation, prompt distillation) by targeting the transfer of *thinking-pattern priors*—i.e., retrieval strategy selection and query decomposition logic—rather than compressing output distributions or static prompt templates. The three retrieval paradigms (forward stepwise, backward constraint, parallel intersection) formalize a decomposition of multi-hop retrieval logic that prior RAG and deep-search systems treat implicitly.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The training-free, modular design makes FutureMind immediately deployable for resource-constrained SLMs without fine-tuning, and the consistent gains over Search-o1 across six model scales (3B–72B, two architectures) demonstrate broad applicability. The 'cognitive bias bottleneck' finding—that a 14B teacher can outperform a 72B teacher for small students, and that architectural alignment (Qwen→Qwen > Llama→Qwen) matters—offers a practical guideline for distillation system design that challenges the default assumption that larger teachers are always better. Open questions include whether the bottleneck generalizes beyond multi-hop QA, how to quantitatively measure 'planning quality' to predict distillation success a priori, and whether the framework's reliance on a live search API limits its applicability to offline or privacy-sensitive settings.
