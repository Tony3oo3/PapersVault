## Problem

The prevailing model of visual cortical neurons assumes a 'one-tailed' response distribution where a neuron selectively responds to a single preferred feature while silencing all other stimuli. This paper challenges that assumption by discovering 'two-tailed' response distributions in macaque V4 neurons, revealing that these neurons possess both preferred (response-maximizing) and anti-preferred (response-minimizing) natural image stimuli—a property that had been largely overlooked and whose computational role in encoding, tuning, and population selectivity was unexplored.

## Input / Output

The method consumes pools of 224×224 RGB natural images (up to 30M from the ImageBeagle dataset), macaque V4 spike-count responses binned in 100 ms windows after stimulus onset (preceded by gray blank screens to avoid adaptation), and pre/post-ReLU activations from task-driven DNNs (ResNet50, VGG-19, AlexNet, Xception, NASNetMobile). Outputs include the top/bottom 10 preferred and anti-preferred images per neuron, response-distribution skewness (κ) values, predicted V4 responses via linear mappings, tuning R² from data-pruning analyses, Caltech-101 object-recognition accuracy, and smooth tuning curves traversing the image manifold. A key unusual aspect is the use of data-driven compact DNN 'V4 model neurons' (distilled from an ensemble model trained on ~10 recording sessions) as surrogates for real neurons, enabling analyses over 500K images that would be infeasible with live recordings.

## Method

The paper introduces three interlocking technical contributions. First, a Linear-ReLU-Linear (LRL) mapping that applies a 1×1 convolution to linearly combine DNN filter channels, passes the result through LayerNorm and ReLUs, then linearly maps to V4 responses—this outperforms standard linear mappings by accommodating mismatches in anti-preferred features between DNN units and biological neurons. Second, a data-pruning framework that trains 5-layer CNN student models on curated subsets (preferred-only, anti-preferred-only, both, random, non-preferred, or gradient-synthesized images) to quantify how much each image type contributes to tuning estimation, revealing that both tails are jointly necessary. Third, ImageBeagle, a search tool that navigates a precomputed nearest-neighbor graph over 30M images (neighbors defined by Euclidean distance in ResNet50 layer-33 features) by alternating between global coreset sampling and local greedy walks along the manifold, achieving near-optimal preferred/anti-preferred image identification after only ~10K evaluations versus millions for random search. Experimental validation used closed-loop recordings from awake macaques where model-predicted preferred and anti-preferred images were presented in a subsequent session, confirming that anti-preferred images suppress V4 responses below baseline firing rate.

## Results

V4 neurons exhibit two-tailed response distributions (median skewness κ=0.87, n=219) in stark contrast to post-ReLU ResNet50 units (median κ=2.06), confirming the existence of anti-preferred stimuli. The proposed LRL (linear-ReLU-linear) mapping significantly outperforms the standard linear mapping of post-ReLU DNN features to V4 responses for both pre- and post-ReLU inputs. For object recognition on Caltech-101, encoding both preferred and anti-preferred features yields a compression ratio of 2.5× (i.e., 2.5× more preferred-only units are needed to match the accuracy of the full two-tailed population), versus only 1.6× for pre-ReLU DNN units. ImageBeagle identifies preferred and anti-preferred images approaching the 30M-image optimum after evaluating only ~10k images, substantially outperforming random search.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the standard linear mapping of post-ReLU DNN features to V4 responses (Yamins et al., 2014; the dominant approach in the field), the paper's LRL mapping (1×1 convolution → LayerNorm → ReLU → linear) significantly improves prediction R² for both pre-ReLU and post-ReLU inputs (Fig. 2a, models iv/v vs. ii), demonstrating that the standard approach discards useful anti-preferred information. Compared to gradient-synthesized stimuli (Bashivan et al., 2019), model-chosen natural anti-preferred images suppress real V4 responses significantly more than synthesized anti-preferred images (Supp. Fig. 5b), while both approaches elicit comparable responses for preferred images; however, synthesized images perform poorly in data-pruning tuning estimation (Fig. 3b, 'synthesized' trace) due to out-of-distribution artifacts. Relative to the V4 model neurons from Cowley et al. (2026), this paper extends their framework by identifying and experimentally validating anti-preferred images (not just preferred ones), showing that both tails are necessary for accurate tuning estimation (Fig. 3b) and that the two-tailed structure doubles population selectivity capacity (compression ratio 2.5× vs. 1.6× for DNN units, Fig. 5h). Against ResNet50 as a model of V4 (He et al., 2016), the paper reveals a fundamental representational mismatch: V4 neurons are two-tailed while deep ResNet50 layers are strongly one-tailed (κ=4.43 in late layers), and the anti-preferred features of DNN units do not align with V4's anti-preferences (flipping signs before ReLU is the worst predictor, Fig. 2a iii).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Cowley et al. (2026, Nature), which introduced compact data-driven DNN models of individual V4 neurons trained on natural image responses, and extends the stimulus-optimization literature (Bashivan et al. 2019; Ponce et al. 2019; Walker et al. 2019) from identifying only preferred (response-maximizing) stimuli to also characterizing anti-preferred (response-minimizing) stimuli in the natural image space. It departs from the standard linear-nonlinear (LN) modeling paradigm and the common practice of using post-ReLU DNN features as the basis for V4 response prediction, proposing instead a linear-ReLU-linear (LRL) mapping that accounts for mismatches in anti-preferred features between DNNs and biological neurons. The data-pruning analysis draws on the data-diet literature (Paul et al. 2021; Sorscher et al. 2022), and the psychophysics task is inspired by explainable-AI work on human interpretability of DNN activations (Borowski et al. 2020). The ImageBeagle tool offers an alternative to gradient-based image synthesis by navigating a precomputed nearest-neighbor graph over 30M natural images, positioning itself as a more accessible and ecologically valid search strategy for experimental neuroscientists.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes anti-preferred stimuli as a fundamental and previously underappreciated property of V4 (and broader visual cortical) neurons, challenging the long-standing 'one-tailed' LN model assumption and revealing a concrete representational gap between task-driven DNNs and biological visual cortex. The finding that encoding anti-preferred features effectively doubles a V4 population's feature-selectivity capacity (compression ratio ~2.5×) has direct implications for how downstream readout and object recognition should be modeled. Key open questions include whether anti-preferences generalize across the full visual hierarchy, how they interact with efficient/sparse coding principles, and whether architectural changes to DNNs (e.g., reducing ReLU thresholding) could close the biological-artificial gap. Limitations include the small sample of ~200 V4 neurons, potential multi-unit contamination, and the approximate nearest-neighbor graph in ImageBeagle.
