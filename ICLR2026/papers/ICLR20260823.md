## Problem

Existing multimodal masking strategies rely on static, uniform distributions over a limited subset of input combinations, failing to address modality imbalance—where dominant modalities capture most predictive signal and gradient flow, impeding optimization of complementary ones. This is especially critical in ecological applications where data are heterogeneous, frequently incomplete (e.g., cloud-obstructed satellite images, temporal gaps), and require fine-grained interpretability of which inputs drive predictions.

## Input / Output

The method consumes heterogeneous multimodal inputs tokenized at fine granularity: satellite images as 5×5 patches × spectral channels, time series as individual years × variables, and tabular predictors as one token per variable (e.g., 48 tokens for GeoPlant's environmental variables). All tokens within a modality share a single masking probability p_m, so the model must produce species presence/absence (multi-label, GeoPlant) or species class (single-label, TaxaBench) predictions from arbitrary subsets of tokens, including single-token or single-patch configurations used for contribution analysis.

## Method

MIAM defines masking as a probability distribution over the M-dimensional unit hypercube [0,1]^M and constructs it as a mixture of product beta distributions, each anchored at one of the 2^M corners with a sharpness parameter κ controlling concentration. Corner weights are asymmetric, allocating half the mass to the all-present (1,...,1) and all-absent (0,...,0) corners to reflect ecological data patterns. To address modality imbalance, κ is dynamically modulated per modality by the ratio ρ_{s_m}/ρ_{d_m}, where ρ_{s_m} is the modality's unimodal validation performance normalized by the geometric mean across modalities and ρ_{d_m} is the absolute temporal derivative of that performance; modalities with high, stable performance (dominant) are masked more aggressively. The resulting distribution is sampled each training step, and masked tokens are replaced by a learned mask embedding before fusion by a 3-block, 8-head transformer. A hyperparameter λ controls the strength of the imbalance adjustment, trading off performance between dominant and dominated modalities.

## Results

On GeoPlant (species distribution modeling, 3 modalities, 76+126+100+48 tokens), MIAM achieves an average AUC of 86.1 across 12 input subsets, outperforming the second-best method (OPM, 83.8) by 2.3 points and approaching the oracle upper bound (87.2). The largest gains appear on the dominated satellite imagery modality, where MIAM reaches 70.8 AUC versus 61.2 (Uniform), 55.1 (Constant), and 68.1 (OPM). On TaxaBench (multimodal species classification, 5 modalities, 1 token each), MIAM achieves 38.7% average top-1 accuracy, beating Uniform (37.7%), Dirichlet (37.4%), Modality dropout (35.9%), and OPM (31.2%), with the oracle at 40.0%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MIAM outperforms all five baselines on average across both datasets. On GeoPlant, the 2.3-point average gain over OPM (86.1 vs 83.8 AUC) is driven primarily by the satellite imagery modality, where MIAM scores 70.8 versus OPM's 68.1 and Uniform's 61.2, directly addressing modality imbalance. On TaxaBench, MIAM's advantage is even more pronounced: 38.7% vs 31.2% top-1 accuracy over OPM (a 7.5-point gap), as OPM's near-static masking distribution fails to explore subsets it never encounters during training. Uniform masking (MaskSDM) is the strongest non-adaptive baseline, competitive on tabular and time-series subsets but consistently weaker on satellite imagery (61.2 vs 70.8 AUC on GeoPlant). Modality dropout and Dirichlet (4M) lag further behind, with Dirichlet's simplex constraint limiting input diversity. MIAM slightly underperforms Modality dropout and Uniform on the 'All modalities' column (91.5 vs 91.7/91.8 AUC), but this gap closes by reducing the imbalance-awareness hyperparameter λ, confirming a controllable trade-off between dominant and dominated modalities.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on two intersecting lines of work: (1) masking strategies for robustness to missing inputs in multimodal models (4M's Dirichlet sampling, MaskSDM's uniform masking, modality dropout), and (2) modality imbalance mitigation techniques (OPM's performance-based dropout, gradient modulation, adaptive training schedules). The key departure is a formalization of masking as probability distributions over the unit hypercube, from which three design principles emerge—full support, corner prioritization, and imbalance-awareness—that existing methods individually or jointly fail to satisfy. MIAM unifies these principles through a corner-anchored mixture of product beta distributions whose sharpness parameters are dynamically modulated by per-modality performance scores and their temporal derivatives, going beyond OPM's binary, near-static modality-level masking.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

For ecological applications where data are inherently heterogeneous and incomplete (cloud-obstructed satellite images, temporal gaps in climate records), MIAM provides a principled way to train a single model that remains robust across arbitrary input subsets while enabling fine-grained contribution analysis (e.g., identifying NDVI-relevant spectral bands or heatwave years). The demonstration that modality imbalance can cause a multimodal model to underperform its unimodal counterpart on a specific modality, and that dynamic masking closes this gap, is practically important for any multimodal system where one modality dominates. Open questions include extending to token-level Shapley values for systematic attribution, scaling to more modalities and larger models, and developing principled per-modality scores for self-supervised settings where labels are absent.
