## Problem

Existing multimodal jailbreak methods rely on single-image masking or isolated visual cues that only modestly extend reasoning paths, achieving limited effectiveness against strongly aligned commercial closed-source MLLMs. The paper addresses how to substantially extend and structure the reasoning chain while delaying the exposure of malicious semantics, so that safety attention is diverted long enough for harmful content to be generated.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262919/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single harmful text query and produces a multi-image, multi-turn-structured prompt: the query's top-k risk-bearing tokens (k=3) are decomposed into fragments and embedded into H=6 separately generated game-style puzzle images (Letter Equation, Jigsaw Letter, Rank-and-Read, Odd-One-Out, Navigate-and-Read, CAPTCHA), while the text channel carries a sanitized skeleton with <img_k> placeholders wrapped in a hierarchical role-compliance template and a persona prompt. The model's output is a structured two-part response—a per-image decoded string showing the reasoning trace, followed by a persona-consistent strategic plan exceeding 600 words—generated in a single black-box shot with no iterative optimization.

## Method

MIDAS operates as a single-shot black-box pipeline with three coupled stages. First, a Dispersion Engine extracts the top-k most risk-bearing tokens from the harmful query, decomposes each into sub-fragments, and assigns them to multiple images under three constraints: every token spans at least two images (cross-image dispersion), each image carries fragments from only one token (single-unit isolation), and fragment counts are balanced across images. Second, each image is rendered as a benign-looking Game-style Visual Reasoning (GVR) puzzle from a library of six templates, so that the embedded fragment can only be recovered by solving the puzzle logic, forcing the model into step-by-step visual reasoning. Third, a Reconstruction Module in the text channel masks the risky tokens with placeholders, binds them sequentially to the image set via a hierarchical role template (authority-compliance → investigator/strategist → persona), and appends a persona prompt that shapes the perspective of the final plan. The key insight is that distributing harmful semantics across multiple puzzle images and a persona-driven text prompt enforces a long, structured cross-image reasoning chain (avg. ~3200 tokens vs. ~420 for VisCRA) that delays harmful keyword exposure to ~65% of the response length, thereby slipping past both input-level safety filters and the model's internal alignment checks.

## Results

On the HADES benchmark, MIDAS achieves an average ASR of 81.46% across four closed-source MLLMs (Gemini-2.5-FT: 93.34%, Gemini-2.5-Pro: 84.55%, GPT-4o: 61.49%, GPT-5-Chat: 72.18%) and up to 97.36% ASR on Qwen2.5-VL. It also attains the highest Harmfulness Rating (HR) across all models, reaching 4.32/5 on Gemini-2.5-FT. On the stricter AdvBench subset (50 most harmful requests), MIDAS still achieves 90.00% ASR on Gemini-2.5-FT and 64.00% on GPT-5-Chat, where most baselines drop to 0%. MIDAS also runs faster than baselines, requiring only 190s on Gemini-2.5-Pro vs. 3357s for HIMRD.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MIDAS outperforms all five baselines on every model and every benchmark. The strongest competitor is VisCRA, which reaches 65.87% ASR on QVQ-Max and 46.98% on Gemini-2.5-FT, but MIDAS still leads by 28.4 and 46.4 percentage points respectively. HIMRD is the second-strongest baseline (65.80% on Qwen2.5-VL, 40.20% on Gemini-2.5-Pro), yet MIDAS exceeds it by 31.6 and 44.4 points on those models. The image-centric methods FigStep and HADES are largely ineffective against closed-source models, with ASR below 13% on GPT-4o and GPT-5-Chat, while MIDAS maintains 61–72% ASR in the same settings. On the hardest AdvBench subset, FigStep and HIMRD both yield 0% ASR on GPT-5-Chat, whereas MIDAS achieves 64.00%, and VisCRA (not reported on AdvBench) is the only other method that shows non-trivial success on that model in other benchmarks. The gap is most pronounced on strongly aligned commercial models, where MIDAS's multi-image dispersion and game-based reasoning templates provide a decisive advantage over single-image or text-only attack strategies.

## Relation to Prior Work

MIDAS extends the line of multi-image reasoning jailbreaks initiated by VisCRA (Sima et al., 2025) and SI-Attack (Zhao et al., 2025), which showed that extending a model's reasoning chain weakens safety alignment. However, those methods rely on single-image masking or isolated visual cues, yielding shallow reasoning extensions. MIDAS departs by dispersing harmful semantics across multiple game-based puzzle images (Letter Equation, Jigsaw, Navigate-and-Read, etc.) combined with a persona-driven textual reconstruction template, enforcing substantially longer and more structured cross-image reasoning. The textual channel draws on hierarchical role-play and persona induction strategies from prior LLM jailbreak literature, while the visual channel repurposes benign puzzle formats as steganographic carriers for risk-bearing fragments.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that current input-level safety filters (LlamaGuard, ShieldLM) and system-prompt defenses (Self-Reminder) are largely ineffective against multi-image dispersion attacks, since each individual image appears innocuous and harmful semantics only emerge through late cross-modal fusion. This reveals a structural vulnerability in autoregressive MLLMs: once engaged in a prolonged reasoning trajectory, alignment mechanisms become progressively less effective, a phenomenon the authors term 'attention slipping.' Open questions include whether process-aware monitoring (e.g., retrospective 'Think-Back' checks on reconstructed fragments) can close this gap, and whether the approach generalizes to future architectures with stronger mid-generation safety anchoring. A limitation is that the attack is single-shot and template-dependent, and its effectiveness may degrade as models improve at multi-image compositional reasoning.
