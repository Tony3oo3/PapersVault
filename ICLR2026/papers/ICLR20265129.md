## Problem

Existing data attribution methods for LLMs rely on parameter gradients, which are prohibitively high-dimensional, diffuse across billions of weights (yielding low signal-to-noise), and semantically disconnected from model behavior. This makes it infeasible to trace undesirable outputs—harmful content, backdoor triggers, or factual errors—back to the specific training samples that caused them, a critical gap for AI safety auditing and data curation.

## Input / Output

Given a test sample (prompt + undesirable response) and a fine-tuning dataset, RepT computes hidden states and their gradients at a single selected layer for all samples, then constructs a 2d-dimensional signature vector per sample by concatenating the last prompt token's hidden state with the first response token's representation gradient. Sample-level attribution outputs a ranked list of training documents by cosine similarity of signatures; token-level attribution outputs a per-token influence score vector (one score per training token) obtained via inner products of row-wise normalized gradient vectors, requiring only a single backward pass per sample rather than repeated per-token gradient computations.

## Method

RepT shifts attribution from parameter space to representation space by computing the gradient of the training loss with respect to intermediate hidden states (representation gradients) rather than model weights, yielding a lower-dimensional, semantically meaningful signal that directly encodes how the model's internal representation should change to produce the target output. An adaptive layer selection strategy identifies the 'phase transition' layer—the layer where inter-layer representation similarity is minimized—using a small probing set, and caches both hidden states and their gradients at that layer for all train/test data. For sample-level attribution, each sample is summarized by a signature vector concatenating the last prompt token's hidden state (contextual summary) with the first response token's gradient (direction of adjustment), and influence is measured by cosine similarity between signatures. For token-level attribution, row-wise normalized representation gradients are used to compute per-token influence scores via a single inner product, enabling fine-grained identification of causal words without repeated backward passes. This one-shot design makes RepT both more accurate and substantially more memory-efficient than gradient-based baselines, achieving near-perfect precision with 14 KB of cached storage versus 8+ MB for TracIn.

## Results

RepT achieves near-perfect attribution performance across all three tasks and all three models (Llama2-7B, Qwen2.5-7B, Llama3-8B). On harmful data identification and backdoor poisoning detection, RepT reaches 0.996–1.000 P@10 and 0.997–1.000 auPRC, substantially outperforming all baselines. On knowledge contamination attribution, RepT achieves 0.904–0.997 P@10 and 0.932–0.998 auPRC. In terms of efficiency, RepT uses only 14 KB of memory and 0.37 h of compute for Llama2-7B with LoRA, compared to 20.11 h for Influence Functions and 193 h for RapidIn on full-parameter fine-tuning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RepT dominates all six baselines across every task and model configuration. Influence Functions (Koh & Liang, 2017) and DataInf (Kwon et al., 2023) perform worst on harmful data and backdoor detection (P@10 ≤ 0.053), though IF recovers moderately on knowledge contamination (P@10 up to 0.975 on Qwen2.5-7B) before degrading sharply at larger k. TracIn (Pruthi et al., 2020) and its layer-normalized variant show moderate performance on harmful data (P@10 up to 0.922) but remain weak on backdoor detection (P@10 ≤ 0.156). RapidIn (Lin et al., 2024) is consistently the weakest gradient-based method (P@10 ≤ 0.219 across all tasks), likely due to its random shuffling disrupting positional information. LESS (Xia et al., 2024) is the strongest baseline on harmful data identification (P@10 0.904–0.978) but drops to P@10 ≤ 0.242 on backdoor detection and degrades rapidly as k increases. LoGra (Choe et al., 2024) performs poorly to moderately across all tasks (P@10 ≤ 0.877). RepT's advantage is most dramatic on backdoor detection, where clean and poisoned examples are highly similar in input space, making parameter-gradient signals nearly uninformative while representation-space signatures remain discriminative.

## Relation to Prior Work

RepT departs from the dominant gradient-based data attribution lineage (influence functions, TracIn, LESS, RapidIn, DataInf, LoGra) by shifting the attribution signal from parameter gradients to representation-space gradients, drawing conceptually on representation engineering (Zou et al., 2023a; Li et al., 2024a) which showed that LLM hidden states encode semantically meaningful, manipulable information. Rather than asking how weights should change, RepT asks how the model's internal representation should be corrected, using a signature of the last prompt token's hidden state concatenated with the first response token's representation gradient. This repurposes the representation-engineering insight for a diagnostic purpose, and the token-level attribution emerges as a free byproduct of the same single backward pass that gradient-based methods would need to repeat per token.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a practical, low-memory (14 KB vs. 25 GB for TracIn) diagnostic tool for auditing LLM fine-tuning data, with near-perfect attribution accuracy across harmful content, backdoor triggers, and knowledge contamination on 7–8 B models. However, all experiments use small, curated poisoning sets (≤250 examples among ~5,000 clean) with known ground truth, leaving open whether the signal remains discriminative in the multi-trillion-token pre-training corpora where individual examples are far less separable. The authors also note that effectiveness on subtle, emergent biases arising from aggregation of many data points is untested, and the natural next step—using the representation gradient to construct inference-time steering vectors for direct mitigation—remains future work.
