## Problem

Existing log-based failure attribution for LLM multi-agent systems produces untested hypotheses and relies on single-step, single-agent labels that are often ill-posed: multiple distinct interventions can independently repair the same failure, and ground-truth annotations are uncertain due to multi-trial sessions, inter-agent misalignment, and cross-annotator disagreement. This paper asks whether debugging can be reframed as an intervention-driven, outcome-oriented process that validates or refutes failure hypotheses through actual re-execution rather than log inspection alone.

## Input / Output

DoVer consumes a failed multi-agent session log (a sequence of agent messages with step indices and stateful context), the original task description, the ground-truth answer, and a checkpoint state that enables in-situ replay from any step. It outputs a targeted intervention (an edited orchestrator message or revised plan), a counterfactual execution trace produced by resuming from the intervention point, and a four-way hypothesis verdict (Validated, Partially Validated, Refuted, Inconclusive) determined by three repeated runs. A milestone-based progress score (ratio of additional human-annotated milestones achieved, bounded in [-1,1]) quantifies partial improvement when full success is not reached, and a boolean 'intervention fulfilled' flag distinguishes faithful execution failures from incorrect hypotheses.

## Method

DoVer is a four-stage do-then-verify pipeline: (1) trial segmentation splits the full session log into planning-execution trials using re-plan steps as cut points, enabling independent and parallel interventions per trial; (2) failure attribution generates a per-trial hypothesis (suspected agent, step index, natural-language rationale) via an LLM prompt over the segmented log; (3) intervention generation converts each hypothesis into a minimal, actionable edit at the orchestrator's message-passing layer—either modified instructions to sub-agents or a revised high-level plan; (4) intervention execution replays the trajectory in-situ, preserving all steps before the intervened point, and differentially evaluates the new trace against the original using trial success rate and milestone progress. The key design insight is treating attribution as a testable hypothesis rather than a definitive label: if the failure resolves after faithful intervention the hypothesis is validated, if it persists the hypothesis is refuted, and if the agent fails to carry out the intervention the outcome is inconclusive. The framework is architecture-agnostic, demonstrated on Magentic-One (GAIA, AssistantBench) and AutoGen2 (GSMPlus), requiring only a checkpoint/replay interface and operating exclusively at the textual message layer without modifying sub-agent code.

## Results

DoVer achieves a Trial Success Rate of 17.6% on WW-AB and WW-GAIA (M1 framework), 27.5% on GAIA-Level-1, and 49.0% on GSMPlus (AG2 framework), flipping a substantial fraction of previously failed trials into successes. The Progress Made metric shows up to +15.7% milestone improvement on GAIA-Level-1, indicating that even non-successful interventions move the system closer to the correct answer. DoVer also validates or refutes 30–60% of failure attribution hypotheses, with the remaining cases classified as inconclusive due to tool limitations or agent non-compliance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DoVer is compared against two self-improvement baselines adapted from Self-Refine (Madaan et al., 2023) and CRITIC (Gou et al., 2023) in the WW-GAIA setting. Both baselines achieve 0% failure recovery across all 26 failed cases, whereas DoVer recovers 17.6% of intervened trials. The paper attributes the baselines' failure to their end-of-trace refinement strategy: trial trajectories between the initial failure point and the final answer are long, noisy, and highly divergent, making post-hoc correction insufficient. DoVer's in-situ intervention at the identified failure step enables targeted correction that the end-of-trace methods cannot replicate. Additionally, the paper's reproduction of the Who&When (WW) log-based attribution baseline shows that even with prompt refinements (adding step indices and annotator guidance), step-level attribution accuracy remains below 25% for GPT-4o and 53% for GPT-5 on certain cases, motivating DoVer's shift from untested log-based hypotheses to intervention-verified debugging.

## Relation to Prior Work

DoVer builds directly on the Who&When (WW) log-based failure attribution framework of Zhang et al. (ICML 2025) and the checkpoint/replay infrastructure of AGDebugger (CHI 2025), but departs from both in a key conceptual way: rather than treating attribution as the end goal, it reframes debugging as a do-then-verify loop where hypothesized failure points are tested through targeted interventions and counterfactual re-execution. This positions DoVer against a growing family of attribution-only methods (AgentRacer, GraphTracer, spectrum-based approaches) that produce untested hypotheses, and against self-improvement baselines (Self-Refine, CRITIC) that operate only at the end of a trace. The trial-segmentation design is motivated by the paper's own empirical finding that multi-trial ReAct loops make single-step attribution inherently ambiguous, a limitation it documents through re-annotation of the WW benchmark.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's most consequential finding is that ground-truth failure-step labels are uncertain in roughly half of benchmark cases (14/29 GAIA), which undermines the evaluation paradigm of the entire attribution literature and motivates an outcome-oriented alternative. Practically, the 49% recovery rate on GSMPlus across a different framework (AG2) suggests the intervention-driven approach generalizes, while the 60% inconclusive rate on harder GAIA/AssistantBench tasks exposes a clear boundary: orchestrator-level message edits cannot repair sub-agent capability gaps (missing tools, broken PDF handling). Open questions include whether richer intervention spaces (tool augmentation, code edits) can close this gap, whether the approach scales to production workloads with safety constraints, and whether the LLM-as-a-judge components for milestone scoring and intervention-fulfillment checks introduce systematic biases that affect the validation taxonomy.
