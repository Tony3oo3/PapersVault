## Problem

Existing sharpness measures such as trace(H) and λ_max(H) correlate weakly or even contradictorily with generalization because they capture only part of the Hessian spectrum (middle or top eigenvalues, respectively). The paper identifies that the average spread (unevenness) of the full Hessian spectrum is what truly governs generalization, and that no existing functional captures this property in a reparameterization-invariant way.

## Input / Output

For correlation analysis, the method consumes the per-layer loss Hessian at a trained minimum and outputs a scalar Rényi sharpness value (negative Rényi entropy of the normalized eigenvalue spectrum), estimated via Stochastic Lanczos Quadrature to avoid O(n³) eigendecomposition. For the RSAM training algorithm, the input is standard RGB images with class labels (32×32 CIFAR-10/100, 64×64 TinyImageNet), and the output is a trained classifier; the Hessian is approximated by the squared gradient magnitude (GM) to keep the regularizer computationally tractable during training.

## Method

Rényi sharpness is defined as −H_α(H), the negative Rényi entropy of the normalized Hessian eigenvalue spectrum, where the order α is selected based on spectral shape (α=0.5 for multi-cluster spectra, α=1.5 for uniform spectra). The key theoretical contribution is proving that this quantity is invariant under layer-wise scaling reparameterizations (for positively homogeneous activations), which enables deriving PAC-Bayes generalization bounds where the Rényi entropy appears directly as the complexity term replacing log-det(H). For practical training, RSAM approximates the Hessian by the diagonal gradient-magnitude matrix GM = Diag(g)², yielding a closed-form regularizer −sign(1−α)·Σ|g_j|^{2α}/(Σg_j²)^α that is added to the loss via a single extra forward-backward pass, analogous to SAM's perturbation step but with a spectrum-aware penalty rather than a worst-case eigenvalue penalty.

## Results

RSAM achieves the best test accuracy across all evaluated settings. On CIFAR-10, it reaches 93.69% (ResNet-20), 95.26% (ResNet-56), and 97.13% (WideResNet-28-10); on CIFAR-100, 70.91%, 75.71%, and 83.67% respectively; and 63.33% on TinyImageNet (ResNet-50). For ViT-B-16 fine-tuning, RSAM attains 98.59% on CIFAR-10 and 89.58% on CIFAR-100, consistently outperforming all SAM variants by 0.1–1.1 percentage points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RSAM consistently outperforms all six SAM variants across every dataset–architecture combination where both are reported. Against the strongest baseline ASAM, RSAM gains +0.07 to +0.77 pp (e.g., 95.26% vs 95.12% on CIFAR-10 ResNet-56; 63.33% vs 62.56% on TinyImageNet ResNet-50). Against Eigen-SAM, which regularizes only the top Hessian eigenvalue, RSAM leads by +0.42 to +0.52 pp on CIFAR-10/100 and by a large +2.12 pp on TinyImageNet. Against FSAM (Riemannian-metric sharpness), RSAM wins by +0.15 to +0.80 pp, with the largest gap on TinyImageNet (+2.12 pp). Against SSAM (sparsified SAM), RSAM leads by +0.19 to +0.77 pp. On ViT-B-16 fine-tuning (Table 2), RSAM achieves 98.59% on CIFAR-10 and 89.58% on CIFAR-100, beating SAM (+0.09/+0.20 pp), ASAM (+0.20/+0.80 pp), and FSAM (+0.17/+0.17 pp). The authors note that margins over ASAM are modest on some tasks, attributing this to the use of a gradient-magnitude approximation of the Hessian rather than the exact Rényi sharpness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes Rényi entropy from information theory as a sharpness measure for the loss Hessian, departing from the conventional trace or max-eigenvalue sharpness that underpin SAM (Foret et al., 2020) and its variants. The theoretical development builds on the PAC-Bayesian framework of Jia & Su (2020), replacing the log-determinant term with Rényi entropy and leveraging a reparameterization invariance property that trace-based measures lack. For practical training, RSAM follows the gradient-magnitude-as-Hessian-approximation trick from Khan et al. (2018) and Kim et al. (2022), while the estimation algorithm draws on Stochastic Lanczos Quadrature (Ubaru et al., 2017) to avoid full eigendecomposition. The key conceptual departure is the argument that the *unevenness* (average spread) of the full Hessian spectrum, rather than its extremes, is what governs generalization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a well-recognized gap: existing sharpness measures correlate only weakly (or contradictorily) with generalization, undermining the theoretical foundation of the entire SAM family. By showing that Rényi sharpness captures spectral unevenness and is reparameterization-invariant, the authors provide both a more principled diagnostic tool and a competitive training algorithm. However, the practical RSAM relies on a gradient-magnitude approximation of the Hessian, introduces a warm-up phase that complicates fair compute comparison, and the accuracy gains over ASAM/FSAM are modest (typically 0.1–0.5%). Open questions include whether exact Rényi sharpness regularization would yield larger gains, whether the method scales to larger models and datasets beyond CIFAR/TinyImageNet, and whether the order-selection heuristic (α=0.5 vs 1.5 based on spectral shape) generalizes robustly.
