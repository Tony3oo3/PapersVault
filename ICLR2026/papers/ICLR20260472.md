## Problem

Causal discovery from i.i.d. observational data is generally ill-posed, as multiple DAGs are distributionally equivalent. While multi-environment approaches with soft interventions have been explored, no prior result guarantees recovery of the unique causal graph with a constant number of environments under arbitrary nonlinear mechanisms. This paper closes that gap by proving that just two sufficiently different auxiliary environments suffice for full causal graph identifiability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260472/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a multi-environment data tensor X ∈ R^{k×n×d} (k environments, n observations of d causal variables each) along with index sets E₁, E₂ that partition the auxiliary environments into two groups, with the first environment serving as the base. The output is the support of the inverse Jacobian J_{f⁻¹}, which directly encodes the parent-child structure of the causal DAG. A key practical constraint is that environments must induce rescaling of source variances (not merely mean shifts), and the algorithm requires estimating the data point corresponding to the source mean in each environment via score-difference minimization.

## Method

The core insight exploits the duality between structural causal models and ICA: causal discovery only requires identifying the support of the inverse Jacobian at a single faithful point, rather than the full Jacobian at every point as ICA demands. The proof analyzes the Hessian of the log-likelihood difference between the base and auxiliary environments at the source mean, where Gaussianity ensures the Hessian of the source density is diagonal (independence) and the gradient vanishes. This yields two diagonal matrices Ω₁, Ω₂ such that the indeterminacy function's Jacobian M must satisfy MᵀΩₗM = Ω̂ₗ for l=1,2, forcing M to be a scaled permutation when the diagonal entries of Ω₁⁻¹Ω₂ are pairwise distinct; the permutation is then removed via acyclicity. Algorithmically, the method estimates the score and Hessian of the data density via the Stein gradient estimator, locates the observation corresponding to the source mean in each environment, computes Hessian differences between environments, and diagonalizes the resulting matrix to recover the Jacobian support up to permutation.

## Results

The main theoretical contribution (Theorem 1) proves that the causal graph of an arbitrary nonlinear SCM with Gaussian noise is identifiable from just two sufficiently different auxiliary environments. Empirically, on bivariate graphs, Algorithm 1 achieves average SHD ≈ 0 (out of 1) across all tested model types—including observationally non-identifiable linear Gaussian and arbitrary nonlinear SCMs—when the theorem's assumptions hold, with no notable degradation as the number of environments increases from 3 to 9. For multivariate linear Gaussian SCMs, the method reduces topological order divergence by ~75% at 10 nodes, ~45% at 20 nodes, and ~30% at 50 nodes compared to a random baseline, using only 3 environments. In the nonlinear multivariate setting, performance degrades beyond 5 nodes, with the method no longer outperforming random at 10 nodes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against named competing causal discovery methods; its contribution is a theoretical identifiability guarantee rather than an algorithmic benchmark. The empirical validation is structured as a self-consistency check: the method is tested on model classes that are already identifiable from pure observations (ANM, PNL, LSNM) as a sanity check, and on classes known to be non-identifiable from i.i.d. data (linear Gaussian, arbitrary nonlinear mechanisms). In all cases where Theorem 1's assumptions are satisfied, the method recovers the correct causal direction (SHD ≈ 0), confirming the theory. A key contrast with the ICA literature is that while nonlinear ICA identifiability requires O(d) environments (Hyvärinen & Morioka, 2016), this paper shows only 2 auxiliary environments suffice for causal graph recovery regardless of graph size. The paper also notes that adding more than 2–3 environments does not improve accuracy, consistent with the theoretical constant-environment guarantee.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the well-established duality between structural causal models and independent component analysis, extending the line from Shimizu et al. (2006) for linear models and Reizinger et al. (2023) for nonlinear models. It departs from prior multi-environment causal discovery work (Peters et al. 2015; Heinze-Deml et al. 2018; Ghassami et al. 2017/2018; Rothenhäusler et al. 2015), which either provides only partial identifiability (parents of a target node), is restricted to linear mechanisms, or does not guarantee full graph recovery with a bounded number of environments. Crucially, it also departs from multi-environment ICA identifiability (Hyvärinen & Morioka 2016), which requires O(d) environments, by showing that the causal discovery problem is fundamentally easier: only the support of the Jacobian at a single point is needed, not the full mixing function everywhere.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first result guaranteeing full causal graph identifiability from a constant number (two) of auxiliary environments for arbitrary nonlinear mechanisms, a regime where prior theory required the number of experiments to scale with graph size. The Gaussianity constraint on noise terms is the main theoretical limitation, though the paper identifies a broader class of distributions (those with a vanishing gradient at some point) where the result extends, and empirical results with Gamma sources suggest further generalization is possible. Algorithmic scalability remains a significant open problem: the method struggles beyond 5 nodes in the nonlinear setting, and developing efficient multi-environment causal discovery algorithms in high dimensions is explicitly left as future work. The conceptual insight that causal discovery is strictly easier than ICA in the multi-environment setting (O(1) vs. O(d) environments) may inspire new identifiability theory that exploits this asymmetry.
