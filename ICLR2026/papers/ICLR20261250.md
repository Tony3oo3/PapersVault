## Problem

Existing LLM privacy audits face two critical practical barriers: formal differential privacy (DP) auditing requires inserting canaries during training, rendering it inapplicable to already-trained models without prohibitively expensive retraining, and dataset inference (DI) demands a private non-member held-out set from the same distribution as the suspect data, which is typically unavailable in real-world settings. These limitations prevent scalable, post-hoc privacy assessment of deployed LLMs.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretrained LLM (no retraining or fine-tuning required for the audit itself), a suspect dataset from which natural identifiers (NIDs—e.g., MD5, SHA-1, SHA-256, Ethereum addresses, Java serialization IDs) are extracted via regex and blind-baseline filtering, and a generated set of identifiers (GIDs) produced by sampling from the same known distribution while preserving exact format and casing. Each NID/GID is embedded in up to 256 tokens of surrounding context with a fixed 64-token suffix. The outputs are: (1) a lower bound on the DP parameters (ε, δ) with 95% confidence intervals for the auditing task, and (2) a p-value from a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test for the DI task, where p < 0.01 indicates the suspect set was likely used in training.

## Method

The core insight is that NIDs—structured random strings with known generation functions (e.g., cryptographic hashes, shortened URLs, crypto wallet addresses)—naturally occur in LLM training corpora and can be used as free, post-hoc canaries. For DP auditing, the paper adapts Steinke et al.'s one-run framework from a binary include/exclude canary selection to a ranking-based setting: each NID is paired with c−1 GIDs to form a candidate set V_i of size c, and the auditor ranks all candidates by likelihood of being the training sample, checking whether the true NID falls within a top-r threshold. Theorems 1 and 2 provide stochastic-dominance bounds under ε-DP and (ε,δ)-DP, showing that higher cardinality c tightens the lower bound and reduces sample complexity. For DI, the method extracts NIDs from the suspect set, generates 127 GIDs per NID, computes membership scores using multiple MIAs (Loss, Min-K%, ReCaLL, Hinge), trains a gradient-boosting classifier via K-Fold to learn the feature-to-membership correlation, and applies a KS test to determine whether NID ranks deviate significantly from uniform. The entire pipeline is zero-run: no retraining, no canary injection, and no synthetic data generation—only regex extraction and uniform sampling from known identifier distributions.

## Results

For post-hoc DP auditing, increasing the NID cardinality from c=2 (Steinke et al. baseline) to c=8 or c=32 substantially tightens the lower bound on ε: for Pythia-1b at true ε=10, the estimated lower bound improves from 0.106 (c=2) to 0.789 (c=8), and at ε=100 from 0.245 (c=2) to well above 1.0 (c=32). For dataset inference, the method achieves zero false positives across all evaluated models and subsets: all training subsets yield p-values ≤ 0.01 (e.g., 0.0001 for most Pile subsets on Pythia-12B), while all test sets yield p-values >> 0.01 (e.g., 0.8182 for GitHub test, 0.2847 for Full Pile test), correctly rejecting or failing to reject the null hypothesis in every case.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the one-run DP auditing framework of Steinke et al. (2023), which corresponds to the special case c=2 (binary inclusion/exclusion of a single canary). The NID-based method with c=8 and c=32 consistently outperforms this baseline in the tightness of the estimated lower bound on ε, particularly at larger privacy budgets typical of LLM training (ε ≥ 8). For example, on Pythia-1b at true ε=10, the c=2 baseline yields an estimated lower bound of only 0.106, while c=8 achieves 0.789 — a 7.5× improvement. At ε=100, the gap widens further (0.245 vs. >1.0 for c=32). For dataset inference, the paper does not compare against a numerical DI baseline since prior DI methods require a same-distribution held-out set that is unavailable post-hoc; instead, the contribution is enabling DI at all without retraining or injected canaries, achieving perfect classification (zero false positives, zero false negatives) across 8 Pile subsets and 7 Dolma subsets on three model families.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the one-run DP auditing framework of Steinke et al. (2023) from a binary (c=2) inclusion/exclusion test to a multi-candidate ranking-based test, and crucially eliminates the need for retraining by exploiting natural identifiers (NIDs) already present in training data as implicit canaries. For dataset inference, it builds on the DI protocol of Maini et al. (2024) but removes the critical dependency on a private same-distribution held-out set by generating held-out samples (GIDs) from the known generation functions of NIDs. The approach departs from Zhang et al. (2024a), which requires injecting artificial canaries into training data, and from Zhao et al. (2025), which uses computationally expensive synthetic data generation with residual distribution shift, by leveraging identifiers that are structurally guaranteed to be from the exact same distribution as the suspect data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work makes post-hoc privacy auditing of already-trained LLMs practically feasible for the first time without retraining, which is critical for real-world regulatory and litigation scenarios where models are already deployed. The finding that even heavily filtered corpora like Dolma retain tens of thousands of NIDs suggests the approach is robust to standard data curation pipelines. Key limitations include the requirement that the suspect dataset actually contains NIDs (less common in small task-specific datasets, though the GSM8K case study partially addresses this), and the fact that the method is inherently limited to structured random strings rather than arbitrary training data. Open questions include how the approach performs when NIDs are deliberately stripped by adversarial filtering, whether it generalizes to non-text modalities, and whether the statistical power is sufficient for very small suspect sets in high-stakes legal contexts.
