## Problem

Existing ad scheduling policies rely on simple heuristics like uniform spacing and frequency caps that ignore how user interest evolves over time due to psychological effects such as mere exposure, hedonic adaptation, and operant conditioning. This paper asks: given a continuous time horizon T, how many ads should be shown and at what times to maximize long-term user interest, and can the optimal schedule be computed efficiently under a psychologically-grounded reward model?

## Input / Output

The algorithm takes the number of ads n+1, a decay parameter δ ∈ [0,1] encoding the strength of operant conditioning (user sensitivity to repeated exposure), a time horizon T, and a concave interest function B(i) capturing mere exposure and hedonic adaptation. It outputs a continuous-time schedule (t_0, …, t_n) with t_0 = 0 and t_n = T, where multiple ads may be shown simultaneously at the endpoints, along with the optimal number of ads found via linear search over n. The schedule is near-optimal with per-ad timing error bounded by 1/2^n, and the algorithm runs in quasi-linear time O((3n + T·log(1/δ))·ln(nT)).

## Method

The reward for the i-th ad is modeled as B(i) − γ·Σ_{j<i} δ^{t_i−t_j}, where the concave B(i) captures diminishing returns from mere exposure and hedonic adaptation, and the exponential decay term captures operant conditioning (inspired by Ebbinghaus's forgetting curve). The key structural insight is that for a fixed number of ads, maximizing reward reduces to minimizing the loss L(t̄) = Σ_{j<i} δ^{t_i−t_j}, which depends only on the operant conditioning function. The paper proves L is strictly convex over the feasible polytope, guaranteeing a unique global minimum, then derives closed-form expressions showing every T_i = δ^{t_i} can be written as T_i = T_1^i / (1+T_1)^{i−1}. A binary search on the monotonically increasing function g(T_1) = T_1^n / (1+T_1)^{n−2} = δ^T recovers T_1 to 1/8^n accuracy, from which all t_i follow with additive error 1/2^n. The analysis further reveals that as δ increases, the optimal schedule transitions from uniform spacing to clustering ads at the endpoints 0 and T, explaining why front-loading and back-loading heuristics outperform uniform spacing in high-sensitivity regimes.

## Results

The paper's main theoretical result is a quasi-linear time algorithm that produces an ad schedule within 1/2^n of the optimal schedule (i.e., each ad time t_i is approximated with additive error at most 1/2^n). Empirically, with n+1=15 ads over T=100 and δ > 0.9 (the regime suggested by Curmei et al., 2022), the near-optimal strategy outperforms all three baselines (Uniform, Corner, Random) by at least 10% in loss at δ ≈ 0.98. The loss function grows approximately linearly with the number of ads, and the optimal number of ads is found via a simple linear search over the sigmoid reward function B(i).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its near-optimal strategy against three baselines: Uniform (equal spacing), Corner (half ads at t=0, half at t=T), and Random (first/last fixed, rest random). When δ is small (≤ 0.4), the Uniform strategy performs well and the near-optimal schedule is nearly uniform. As δ increases toward 1, the Corner strategy becomes more competitive, but the near-optimal strategy consistently outperforms all baselines across the full range of δ ∈ [0,1]. At δ ≈ 0.98 (the value reported in real-world settings by Curmei et al., 2022), the near-optimal strategy achieves at least a 10% reduction in loss compared to every baseline. The key insight is that no single heuristic (uniform or corner) is universally optimal; the optimal schedule adapts its structure as δ changes, concentrating more ads at the endpoints while keeping interior ads evenly spaced.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classical goodwill-stock model of Nerlove & Arrow (1962) and the Ebbinghaus forgetting curve by explicitly incorporating three psychological effects—mere exposure, hedonic adaptation, and operant conditioning—into a unified reward function for ad scheduling. It departs from prior ad-scheduling work (Sahni 2015, Naik et al. 1998, Rafieian 2023, Theocharous et al. 2015) that relied on heuristics, optimal control, or reinforcement learning, by providing a closed-form structural characterization of the optimal schedule and a quasi-linear-time algorithm with provable near-optimality. The psychological grounding follows Curmei et al. (2022), but the paper is the first to derive an efficient algorithmic solution under this combined model and to show how the optimal schedule's structure transitions qualitatively with the decay parameter δ.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a theoretically grounded explanation for why empirical studies consistently find that ads at the beginning and end of a session outperform mid-roll placements, and quantifies exactly when uniform spacing (the industry default) becomes suboptimal. Key limitations include the assumption of a single advertiser with homogeneous ads, stationary rewards, and known δ; open questions include extending to non-stationary settings (time-of-day effects), multi-advertiser competition, and learning δ from data in a bandit framework. For practitioners in ad-tech and recommendation systems, the framework offers a tunable, interpretable alternative to black-box RL schedulers, though its practical impact depends on accurately estimating the psychological parameters.
