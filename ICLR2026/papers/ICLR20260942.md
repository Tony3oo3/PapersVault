## Problem

Prior work adapts MLLMs to closed-set action understanding by autoregressively generating action labels as free-form text (generative classifiers), but this approach is inefficient and introduces semantic overlap through shared subwords across labels, causing confusion between similar actions. The paper asks why generative classifiers systematically underperform discriminative ones and whether generative modeling can still complement discriminative learning without sacrificing inference speed.

## Input / Output

The model consumes video frames encoded by task-specific visual encoders (ResNet50@4FPS, DINOV2@1FPS, TSN@4FPS, or SigLIP-ViT-L-384@2FPS) and a short task-specific text query (e.g., 'What is the action in the last frame?'). For online action detection, frames are sampled backwards from the current timestamp within a fixed 128-second window to capture both short- and long-term context. At inference, the model outputs a single action class label via a classification head on a learnable [CLS] token representation; the auxiliary generative branch (which produces previous-action or task-context text during training) is disabled, yielding single-step prediction.

## Method

The GAD classifier uses a LLaVA-style backbone (frozen visual encoder, fine-tuned vision-language adaptor, LoRA-adapted LLM) with a learnable [CLS] token appended to the input sequence to produce a global representation for a cross-entropy classification head. During training, an auxiliary language-modeling loss generates contextual text (e.g., the previous action or the overall task label) conditioned on the [CLS] token, regularizing the discriminative representation with semantic and temporal cues. The key insight is that generative classifiers fail because shared subwords across action labels (e.g., 'add' in 'add onion' vs. 'add rice') create semantic overlap in the output space, whereas the discriminative head ignores label semantics entirely; the paper proves equivalence between the two when action labels are added as single vocabulary tokens. At inference, only the discriminative branch is active, preserving the efficiency of one-step classification while benefiting from the richer representations learned through the generative auxiliary objective.

## Results

GAD (Llama3.2-1B-Instruct) achieves state-of-the-art on all four OAD datasets: 58.1/40.2 (S-F1/P-F1) on THUMOS'14, 50.3/34.5 on CrossTask, 24.1/20.1 on EPIC-Kitchens-100, and 11.0/4.3 on Ego4D GoalStep. On the COIN benchmark, GAD (Llama3-8B) reaches 67.3% Top-1 on step recognition, 51.6% on next-step forecasting, and 94.5% on task recognition. The 1B GAD model outperforms all prior 8B generative MLLM baselines, and inference is ~3× faster than generative classifiers due to single-step discriminative prediction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On OAD tasks, GAD (1B) decisively outperforms all prior non-LLM baselines: it surpasses CMeRT (the strongest prior method) by 6.4% S-F1 on EPIC-Kitchens-100 (24.1 vs. 17.7) and by 1.3% S-F1 on CrossTask (50.3 vs. 50.1), while also leading on THUMOS'14 (58.1 vs. 49.4 S-F1 over MAT) and Ego4D GoalStep (11.0 vs. 9.7 S-F1). On the COIN benchmark, GAD (Llama3-8B) beats the strongest generative MLLM baselines—StreamMind-8B (63.7/49.9/93.2), VideoLLM-MoD-8B (63.4/49.7/92.8), and VideoLLM-online-8B (63.1/49.1/92.7)—by 3.6%, 1.7%, and 1.3% Top-1 on Step, Next, and Task respectively, despite using a 1B model that already matches or exceeds these 8B methods. Notably, the 1B GAD model (65.3/51.4/93.5) surpasses all 8B generative baselines on every COIN task, demonstrating that the discriminative formulation with generative assistance is more parameter-efficient than scaling the LLM. The zero-shot Qwen2.5-VL-7B baseline (16.1% Step, 8.9% Next) confirms that fine-tuning is essential for closed-set action understanding with semantically overlapping labels.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the LLaVA-style MLLM architecture (Liu et al., 2023) and the growing line of work that repurposes MLLMs as generative classifiers for video action understanding (VideoLLM-online, VideoLLM-MoD, StreamMind). It departs from this generative paradigm by demonstrating that a discriminative formulation—appending a learnable [CLS] token and a classification head—consistently outperforms autoregressive label generation for closed-set tasks, and by identifying the specific mechanism (shared subword semantics across action labels) that explains the gap. The proposed GAD framework then re-introduces generation as an auxiliary regularizer during fine-tuning only, drawing on the broader discriminative-vs-generative classifier literature (Ng & Jordan, 2001) and on tokenization strategies from retrieval and QA settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clear, well-ablated diagnosis of why MLLMs underperform as generative classifiers for closed-set video action tasks, and offers a practical fix (GAD) that requires no changes to pretraining and preserves 3× inference speedup—important for real-time streaming applications. The finding that a 1B discriminative model surpasses an 8B generative one challenges the assumption that scale compensates for architectural mismatch. Open questions remain around open-world generalization (the paper shows fine-tuning causes task-induced forgetting), whether GAD's auxiliary generation strategy transfers to other closed-set MLLM tasks beyond action understanding, and how to reconcile discriminative efficiency with the open-ended flexibility that makes MLLMs attractive in the first place.
