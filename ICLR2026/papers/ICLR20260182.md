## Problem

Existing video LLMs are evaluated in offline settings where the full video is preloaded, but real-world visual agents must respond at the precise moment sufficient evidence first appears in a streaming video. This online regime exposes three unaddressed challenges: opaque binary answer/defer gates that provide no decision transparency, misalignment between response timing and the earliest sufficient-evidence timestamp, and the difficulty of maintaining a globally consistent, causally coherent understanding under tight token and latency budgets.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260182/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes a video stream segmented into 32-frame clips at 1 fps (448×448 resolution) alongside a text query, processing clips sequentially in a single-pass streaming regime. Beyond the final text answer, it emits a response timestamp t_r aligned to the first-sufficient-evidence time t*, and streams real-time telemetry including per-sub-question progress ρ∈[0,1], confidence c∈[0,1]^K, question-guided captions, sub-question decompositions, and rationale snippets at every step, making the decision process auditable to the user.

## Method

Thinking-QwenVL (built on Qwen2.5-VL-7B) decouples reasoning control from memory integration via two modules. HPSI inserts learnable multi-level aggregation tokens at three transformer depths (layers 0, L/3, 2L/3) with 3:2:1 token ratios, using structured sparse attention masks that enforce hierarchical visibility—each level attends only to the preceding level's tokens—so visual evidence is progressively condensed into a compact cognition state h_t that is propagated causally across clips without inflating the token budget. ATDM implements a five-part chain-of-thought controller that generates question-guided captioning instructions, decomposes the query into sub-questions, produces per-clip captions, extracts sub-answers with explicit progress and confidence metrics, and self-triggers cross-clip reflection when confidence drops, emitting the final answer only when all sub-goals reach ρ=1. Training proceeds in three stages (integration pre-training, time-perception learning on binary answerability labels, and interaction-focused QA fine-tuning) with only the LLM and merge layers updated while the visual encoder remains frozen.

## Results

On StreamingBench (the primary online video understanding benchmark), Thinking-QwenVL achieves 71.60% overall accuracy, improving the previous state-of-the-art online model Dispider from 67.63% by +3.97%. On OVOBench, it reaches 46.9% (vs. Dispider 41.8%, Flash-VStream 33.2%), and on OVBench it attains 35.6% (vs. Flash-VStream 31.2%). Under the 100% token setting (no frame dropping), HPSI alone lifts the Qwen2.5-VL-7B backbone to 67.7% on VideoMME, 68.3% on MLVU, 62.0% on LongVideoBench, and 43.6% on LVBench, surpassing the base model by +4.5%, +1.4%, +0.5%, and +0.5% respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On StreamingBench, Thinking-QwenVL (71.60%) outperforms the strongest online baseline Dispider (67.63%) by +3.97% and the best offline model Qwen2.5-VL (73.68%) is only marginally ahead, while all other online models (VideoLLM-Online 35.99%, Flash-VStream 22.53%) trail by large margins. On OVOBench, the method achieves 46.9%, beating Dispider (41.8%) by +5.1% and Flash-VStream (33.2%) by +13.7%, and approaching TimeChat-Online (46.7%) at 100% tokens. On OVBench, Thinking-QwenVL (35.6%) surpasses Flash-VStream (31.2%) and VideoLLM-Online (9.6%) but remains below proprietary Gemini-1.5-Flash (50.7%). On offline long-video benchmarks under the 100% token setting, HPSI lifts the Qwen2.5-VL backbone above TimeChat-Online by +5.7% on MLVU (68.3 vs. 62.6), +6.6% on LongVideoBench (62.0 vs. 55.4), and +5.3% on VideoMME (67.7 vs. 62.4), while the 93.75% aggregation variant (56.3% VideoMME) still exceeds Dispider (57.2%) and VideoChat-Online (52.8%) on VideoMME. The ATDM controller is shown to be framework-agnostic: applied to Flash-VStream it yields a +4.05% gain (22.53→26.58%), and a 3B variant reaches 62.62%, only ~5% below the 7B Dispider model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Qwen2.5-VL-7B backbone and positions itself at the intersection of two lines of work: offline long-video understanding (LongVA, Kangaroo, VideoLLaMA3) that assumes full-video access, and emerging online/streaming video models (Flash-VStream, VideoLLM-Online, Dispider, TimeChat-Online) that handle sequential clips but fix response timing to the query moment or use opaque binary answerability gates. The key conceptual departure is the formalization of evidence-aligned response timing (t_r ≈ t*, the first-sufficient-evidence timestamp) coupled with a transparent, multi-stage decision process (ATDM) that exposes progress and confidence metrics, rather than collapsing timing into a single black-box gate. On the memory side, HPSI's multi-depth, multi-level aggregation tokens with structured sparse attention differ from single-shot adaptive pooling (LongVA) and from Dispider's binary decision head, treating transformer depth as a staged aggregation pipeline rather than a single compression step.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap in deploying video LLMs as interactive agents: existing systems either wait for the full video (offline) or answer immediately at query time without verifying whether sufficient evidence has appeared, leading to either unnecessary latency or premature/incorrect responses. The evidence-aligned timing formulation and transparent decision telemetry (progress ρ, confidence c, streamed rationales) are directly relevant to safety-critical applications (robotics, driver assistance) where knowing *when* and *why* a system answers matters as much as *what* it answers. Limitations include the still-weak Forward Active Responding subtask on OVOBench (28.6%), the modest ~3.7s added latency from the decision pipeline, and evaluation limited to 7B-scale models; open questions include whether the approach extends to multi-modal (audio+vision) evidence alignment and whether standardized benchmarks for decision-timing quality can be established.
