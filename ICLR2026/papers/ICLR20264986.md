## Problem

Prior work demonstrated that expert merging outperforms expert pruning on discriminative benchmarks (perplexity, multiple-choice QA), leading to the assumption that merging is the superior one-shot compression strategy for SMoE models. However, no systematic evaluation on generative tasks (code generation, creative writing, mathematical reasoning) had been conducted, and the fundamental theoretical reason why merging might fail on such tasks remained unexplored.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264986/cropped_page5_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pre-trained SMoE model and calibration data consisting of router gate values and expert activation norms recorded over a set of calibration tokens (1,024 samples packed to 2,048 tokens for models ≤110B; 12,228 samples up to 16,384 tokens for larger models). The output is a one-shot compressed SMoE model with 25% or 50% of experts removed per layer, requiring no fine-tuning. Unlike merging methods that must record activations from every expert for every token, REAP only needs data for active experts, making calibration more memory-efficient and compatible with quantized models without re-quantization.

## Method

REAP (Router-weighted Expert Activation Pruning) prunes experts by computing a saliency score S_j = (1/|X_j|) Σ_{x∈X_j} g_j(x)·||f_j(x)||₂, the conditional mean of the gate-weighted activation norm over tokens where expert j is active. This criterion directly minimizes the upper bound on the substitution error (g_j·(||f_j||+||f_i||)) derived in the paper's theoretical analysis, targeting experts that contribute minimally to the layer output even when the router selects them. The conditional averaging over X_j (rather than a global average) decouples an expert's functional impact from its activation frequency, preventing the erroneous pruning of rare-but-important specialist experts. The theoretical analysis establishes that merging with summed gates incurs an irreducible error proportional to Var[r(x)]·G_ij (router policy variability times expert functional gap), which is unavoidable whenever the router uses input-dependent mixing ratios, whereas pruning preserves the router's independent control over surviving experts and only incurs error on the support where the pruned expert was active.

## Results

At 50% compression on Qwen3-30B, REAP achieves 0.530 Code Avg, substantially outperforming M-SMoE (0.397) and HC-SMoE (0.364), while also beating the EAN pruning baseline (0.452). On large-scale models, REAP achieves near-lossless code generation: 0.619 Code Avg on Qwen3-Coder-480B (baseline 0.636) and 0.624 on Kimi-K2 (baseline 0.631) after pruning 50% of experts. Across all generative benchmarks, REAP yields a mean accuracy decrease of only 1.9% at 25% and 6.9% at 50% compression, compared to >5% and >20% for both merging methods. On MC question answering, REAP remains competitive (mean ~13% decrease at 50%) but merging methods (HC-SMoE ~4%) retain a slight edge on this discriminative task.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against M-SMoE (Li et al., ICLR 2023), REAP decisively wins on all generative tasks at 50% compression: on Qwen3-30B coding, REAP scores 0.530 vs. M-SMoE's 0.397, and on GLM-4.5-Air the gap widens to 0.487 vs. 0.284. M-SMoE retains a slight edge on MC benchmarks (0.451 vs. 0.493 on Qwen3-30B) and on low-granularity architectures like Mixtral for coding. Against HC-SMoE (Chen et al., ICML 2025), REAP wins on generative tasks across all models, with the most dramatic gap on creative writing (0.702 vs. 0.008 on Qwen3-30B at 50%), while HC-SMoE is marginally better on MC (0.542 vs. 0.493). Against EAN (Jaiswal et al., 2025), the closest pruning baseline, REAP pulls ahead at 50% compression on large models: on Qwen3-Coder-480B, REAP achieves 0.619 Code Avg vs. EAN's 0.580, and on Kimi-K2, 0.624 vs. 0.487. At 25% compression, REAP and EAN are nearly tied on most tasks, confirming that REAP's advantage grows with compression ratio. Frequency-based pruning collapses catastrophically at 50% on large models (e.g., 0.010 Code Avg on Qwen3-Coder-480B), underscoring the importance of activation-aware saliency criteria.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the merging-favoring narrative established by Li et al. (2023, M-SMoE) and Chen et al. (2025, HC-SMoE), which demonstrated that expert merging outperforms pruning on discriminative benchmarks (perplexity, MC QA). It builds on the pruning literature—particularly Jaiswal et al. (2025) for EAN and Lu et al. (2024) for reconstruction-loss-based criteria—but introduces a new theoretical framework showing that merging incurs irreducible error proportional to the router's policy variability, a loss that pruning avoids by preserving independent gate control. REAP's saliency criterion (conditional mean of router gate × activation norm) is a novel combination that explicitly targets the upper bound of the substitution error derived in the analysis, distinguishing it from both frequency-based and EAN pruning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work challenges the field's prevailing direction toward expert merging for SMoE compression by showing that the merging advantage is an artifact of discriminative evaluation, and that on generative tasks (code, math, creative writing) pruning is consistently superior—especially at 50% compression. The near-lossless 50% compression on Qwen3-Coder-480B and Kimi-K2 (1T) for code generation has direct practical impact for deploying large SMoEs in resource-constrained settings. Open questions include whether post-compression fine-tuning could close the gap for merging, whether the theoretical analysis extends rigorously to non-linear expert functions, and whether REAP's advantage persists at compression ratios beyond 50%.
