## Problem

Existing multimodal mathematical reasoning research suffers from three interrelated gaps: (1) no comprehensive, hierarchical knowledge system to systematically supervise and evaluate mathematical reasoning across subfields; (2) difficulty annotation based on human learning stages that misaligns with how MLLMs actually learn; and (3) training methods that promote problem memorization over reasoning generalization, causing models to fail on subproblems and same-type variants of problems they can solve.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20265095/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a math question in text form paired with a precise geometric diagram rendered via GeoGebra (not arbitrary photographs or hand-drawn figures), and produces a structured response containing a chain-of-thought reasoning process in <think> tags followed by a final answer (number, option, phrase, or LaTeX expression) in <answer> tags. All problems are in multiple-choice or fill-in-the-blank format, and the diagrams are generated from XML scripts to ensure reproducible, parameter-controllable geometric constructions that support systematic variant generation.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-stage reinforcement learning framework (MathBook-RL) grounded in a five-level hierarchical knowledge system of 491 knowledge points and 1,819 fundamental principles. Stage 1 (Cold-Start SFT) uses 1K knowledge-oriented CoT samples to instill a structured reasoning paradigm. Stage 2 (Progressive Alignment RL) operates in two sub-phases: first, a pre-aligned RL phase on one-question-multi-image variants using an average-reward mechanism that aggregates rollout rewards across all visual instantiations of the same knowledge principle to encourage analogical reasoning; second, a dynamic scheduling RL phase on MathBook-Pro, where each seed problem is expanded into 7 variants along three orthogonal difficulty axes (step complexity via added knowledge points, visual complexity via auxiliary geometric elements, contextual complexity via real-world recontextualization). When the model fails at a curriculum transition, incremental learning mechanisms (Knowledge Increment Scheduling for new reasoning steps, Modality Increment Scheduling for new visual/contextual demands) adaptively redirect training to targeted bridging samples before re-attempting the harder problem. The RL objective uses GRPO with a combined reward of answer accuracy (0.9) and format compliance (0.1).

## Results

MathBook-7B achieves an average score of 48.7 across four benchmarks (MathVista 73.0, MathVision 28.0, We-Math 48.4, MathVerse 45.2), improving over the Qwen2.5-VL-7B backbone by +6.1 points on average and +12.4 on We-Math. Notably, this is achieved with only 1K SFT samples and 9.8K RL samples, substantially less data than competing reasoning models (e.g., URSA-8B uses 2.96M, Math-PUMA-7B uses 1.88M). On MathBookEval, MathBook-7B scores 50.4% overall accuracy, outperforming all 7B-scale open-source reasoning baselines (MM-Eureka-7B: 50.0, R1-VL-7B: 38.0) and approaching GPT-4o (50.8).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MathBook-7B outperforms all open-source reasoning baselines on average: it beats WeThink-7B (47.5) by 1.2 points, VLAA-Thinker-7B (46.0) by 2.7, and MM-Eureka-7B (45.2) by 3.5, while using far less training data (9.8K vs. 15K–290K). On We-Math specifically, MathBook-7B (48.4) surpasses WeThink-7B (48.0) and dramatically outperforms R1-VL-7B (22.7) and Math-PUMA-7B (19.2), validating the progressive alignment RL's effectiveness on knowledge generalization. MathBook-7B ties MM-Eureka-7B on MathVista (both 73.0) but leads on MathVision (28.0 vs. 26.9) and We-Math (48.4 vs. 34.5). It trails closed-source GPT-4o (54.0 avg) and Gemini-1.5-Pro (53.6 avg) by roughly 5–6 points, and also falls short of the larger Qwen2.5-VL-72B (57.1 on MathBookEval). On MathBookEval, MathBook-7B (50.4) edges out MM-Eureka-7B (50.0) and substantially outperforms R1-VL-7B (38.0) and OpenVLThinker-7B (32.6), while remaining close to GPT-4o (50.8).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on visual mathematical reasoning in MLLMs by unifying three previously separate concerns—knowledge-structured dataset design, model-centric difficulty modeling, and RL-based training—into a single system. It builds directly on GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) for the RL objective and on curriculum-based RL approaches (Huang et al., 2025; Meng et al., 2025), but departs from prior work by replacing human-defined difficulty levels with a three-dimensional (step, visual, contextual) difficulty space and by introducing dynamic data scheduling that adaptively redirects the model to incremental-step or single-modality samples upon failure. The knowledge system itself extends the We-Math 1.0 benchmark (Qiao et al., 2024a) from a knowledge-annotated evaluation set into a full five-level hierarchical taxonomy with 1,819 fundamental principles, and the GeoGebra-based diagram pipeline differentiates it from prior datasets that rely on collected or manually drawn figures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The structured knowledge system and the 3D difficulty modeling provide a principled, interpretable framework for diagnosing *where* and *why* MLLMs fail at mathematical reasoning, which is more actionable than aggregate accuracy scores. The finding that only ~10K carefully curated samples suffice to unlock strong RL performance (outperforming models trained on 15K–3M samples) suggests that data quality and structure may matter more than scale for math reasoning, a claim that could reshape how future datasets are designed. However, the approach is tightly coupled to the Qwen2.5-VL backbone and the GeoGebra pipeline, and the absolute performance (48.7 avg) still trails GPT-4o (54.0), leaving open whether the knowledge-driven paradigm generalizes to other architectures or to non-geometric math domains. The reliance on GPT-4o for both knowledge-system construction and CoT rewriting also raises questions about circularity in the annotation pipeline.
