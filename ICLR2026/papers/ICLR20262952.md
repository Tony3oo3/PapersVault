## Problem

Learning-based humanoid locomotion policies trained in simulation often fail under real-world disturbances due to the sim-to-real gap, while existing robust RL approaches that optimize worst-case performance produce overly conservative policies that sacrifice task completion. The paper addresses how to learn a control policy that dynamically balances goal-tracking performance with safety recovery under mismatched deployment dynamics, without requiring the entire policy to be conservative at all times.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262952/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The low-level policies consume a temporal stack of proprioceptive observations (joint positions/velocities, base angular velocity, roll/pitch, projected gravity) plus a velocity command (linear x/y and angular z), augmented at deployment by VAE-estimated privileged features (body velocity and ZMP features with frequency encoding). The goal-tracking and safety-recovery policies each output continuous joint target angles (19 DoF for Unitree H1, 23 DoF for G1) fed to a PD controller at 100 Hz. The high-level planner outputs a discrete one-hot selection signal choosing which low-level policy to activate, with a switch penalty discouraging frequent transitions.

## Method

HWC-Loco decomposes the robust locomotion objective into a three-level hierarchy: a goal-tracking policy (π1) trained with PPO under a Wasserstein-1 imitation constraint (implemented via a Lipschitz-constrained adversarial discriminator with gradient penalty, retargeted from CMU MoCap data), a safety-recovery policy (π2) trained under an extreme-case uncertainty set (multiscale external forces, sensor/actuator noise, malicious velocity resampling, domain randomization) with a ZMP-based feasibility constraint, and a high-level Double-DQN planner (π0) that selects between π1 and π2 based on temporal observations and ZMP features. The key insight is separating worst-case feasibility (handled by π2) from reward maximization (handled by π1), so the policy is only conservative when safety is at risk. At deployment, a VAE-based estimator infers privileged information (terrain, disturbances) from observations, and a Butterworth low-pass filter smooths policy-switching transients. The constrained RL objectives are solved via their Lagrange dual forms, exploiting the zero duality gap property.

## Results

On simulated locomotion across slopes and stairs, HWC-Loco achieves 100% success rate on slopes (both low and high speed) and 99.98% on low-speed stairs, substantially outperforming DreamWaQ (74.32%) and AHL (93.73%) on low-speed stairs. The most dramatic gap appears on high-speed stairs, where HWC-Loco reaches 84.34% success versus 60.58% (DreamWaQ) and 67.48% (AHL), indicating its safety-recovery mechanism prioritizes stability over aggressive velocity tracking. Under robustness tests with constant external forces (up to 200 N), HWC-Loco attains 75.95% success with 6.61% ZMP deviation, compared to 51.31% and 7.71% for DreamWaQ. On the Unitree G1 embodiment, HWC-Loco achieves 98.14% success rate and 3.11 Wasserstein human-likeness distance, confirming cross-embodiment generalization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HWC-Loco is compared against two external baselines: DreamWaQ (Nahrendra et al., ICRA 2023), a VAE-based quadrupedal locomotion method adapted for humanoid control, and AHL (Cui et al., CoRL 2024), a two-phase history-aware training approach for humanoid terrain traversal. HWC-Loco decisively outperforms both on the most challenging setting—high-speed stairs—achieving 84.34% success versus 60.58% (DreamWaQ, +23.8 pp) and 67.48% (AHL, +16.9 pp), while also leading on low-speed stairs (99.98% vs. 74.32% and 93.73%). Under robustness tests, the gap widens further: on constant external forces, HWC-Loco reaches 75.95% success versus 51.31% (DreamWaQ) and 60.72% (AHL); on high-impulse CoM perturbations, it attains 81.27% versus 45.34% and 62.94%. On goal-tracking and human-likeness metrics, HWC-Loco is competitive but slightly trails the Goal-tracking ablation (which removes safety recovery), confirming a deliberate trade-off: the safety-recovery mechanism sacrifices ~0.1–0.2 in goal-tracking score to gain 10–25 pp in success rate under stress. Against strong domain-randomization baselines (Large-DR-Hist at scale 4.0), HWC-Loco still leads on constant forces (75.95% vs. 70.53%) and high-impulse (81.27% vs. 71.36%), demonstrating that the hierarchical safety architecture provides benefits beyond what aggressive randomization alone can achieve.

## Relation to Prior Work

HWC-Loco builds on the DreamWaQ framework (Nahrendra et al., 2023) for VAE-based privileged information estimation and the constrained RL formulation with zero duality gap (Paternain et al., 2019), but departs from the monolithic single-policy paradigm by introducing a hierarchical architecture that explicitly separates goal-tracking from safety-recovery. The imitation objective replaces KL/JS divergence with Wasserstein-1 distance via Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality, drawing on adversarial imitation learning (Ho & Ermon, 2016), while the robustness formulation follows the mismatched-POMDP framework (Moos et al., 2022; Viano et al., 2022) but shifts from worst-case reward maximization to worst-case feasibility constraints. The ZMP-based stability constraint, rooted in classical bipedal robotics (Wieber, 2006; Morisawa et al., 2006), is repurposed as a learned feasibility indicator within the RL objective rather than a hard penalty.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The hierarchical safety-recovery mechanism addresses a practical gap in current humanoid locomotion: most learning-based controllers lack an explicit, principled mechanism for handling safety-critical events during real-world deployment, relying instead on domain randomization that may not capture worst-case scenarios. The paper's real-world validation on a Unitree H1 demonstrates that the approach transfers, though the 19-DOF limitation and frozen low-level controllers constrain the expressiveness of recovery behaviors. Key open questions include whether jointly optimizing the hierarchy (rather than freezing low-level policies) yields smoother transitions, whether the ZMP constraint generalizes to more dynamic or underactuated platforms, and how the framework extends to loco-manipulation tasks where safety and task objectives are more tightly coupled.
