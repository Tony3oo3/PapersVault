## Problem

Identity-consistent image generation models suffer from a 'copy-paste' failure mode where they rigidly replicate the reference face rather than preserving identity across natural variations in pose, expression, and lighting. This is driven by the scarcity of large-scale paired datasets (multiple images per identity), forcing most methods into reconstruction-based training where reference and target coincide, which inherently encourages direct copying. The paper formalizes this artifact, quantifies it with a new metric, and breaks the long-observed trade-off between identity fidelity and copy-paste.

## Input / Output

The method takes 1–4 reference face images (individual portraits of target identities) and a text prompt describing the desired group scene as input, and produces a single multi-person group photo (1–4 people) that preserves each identity while following the prompt. Each reference is encoded by ArcFace into a 512-dim embedding projected to 8×3072 tokens, with optional SigLIP tokens for mid-level attribute retention; binary attention masks derived from ground-truth bounding boxes restrict each reference token to attend only to its corresponding face region in the output. The model is built on the FLUX DiT backbone and operates in latent space via flow matching.

## Method

WithAnyone integrates a lightweight identity adapter into the FLUX DiT backbone, injecting ArcFace-derived identity tokens (and optional SigLIP tokens) via cross-attention after each transformer block, with attention masks enforcing spatial identity disentanglement. Training follows a four-phase pipeline: reconstruction pre-training with fixed prompts, reconstruction with full captions, paired tuning (where 50% of samples use a different reference image than the target for the same identity to break the copy-paste shortcut), and quality/style tuning. Three losses are combined: a flow-matching diffusion loss, a GT-aligned ID loss that uses ground-truth landmarks to align the generated face (enabling single-step denoising and ID supervision at all noise levels), and an InfoNCE contrastive loss with an extended negative pool of up to 4096 embeddings drawn from the MultiID-2M reference bank. The paired dataset MultiID-2M (500k identified group photos with hundreds of references per identity, plus 1.5M unpaired images) and the MultiID-Bench evaluation protocol (435 test cases with a Copy-Paste metric measuring angular bias toward reference vs. ground truth) are also introduced as core contributions.

## Results

On the single-person subset of MultiID-Bench, WithAnyone achieves Sim(GT)=0.460 (second only to InstantID's 0.464) while maintaining a copy-paste score of 0.144, substantially lower than comparable high-similarity methods (InstantID: 0.337, UMO: 0.359, PuLID: 0.315). On the 2-person subset, WithAnyone attains the best Sim(GT) of 0.405 among all methods, and on the 3–4-person subset it achieves 0.414 (best excluding GPT-4o, which benefits from prior knowledge of TV-series identities). WithAnyone also records the highest CLIP-I score (0.798) on the single-person benchmark and the best overall score (6.52) among face-customization models on OmniContext.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The central finding is that WithAnyone breaks the trade-off curve observed among all 13 baselines: most methods lie on a fitted curve where higher Sim(GT) correlates with higher copy-paste, whereas WithAnyone achieves near-top Sim(GT) (0.460) with a CP score (0.144) comparable to low-similarity general models like OmniGen2 (0.142) and FLUX.1 Kontext (0.099). Against face-customization baselines, WithAnyone outperforms InstantID (Sim(GT) 0.460 vs 0.464, CP 0.144 vs 0.337), UMO (0.460 vs 0.458, CP 0.144 vs 0.359), PuLID (0.460 vs 0.452, CP 0.144 vs 0.315), and UniPortrait (0.460 vs 0.447, CP 0.144 vs 0.265) on the single-person benchmark. On the multi-person subsets, WithAnyone achieves the best Sim(GT) in the 2-person setting (0.405) and the 3–4-person setting (0.414, excluding GPT-4o which exploits prior knowledge), while also recording the lowest identity-blending score (0.045). On OmniContext, WithAnyone (overall 6.52) trails general-purpose models like OmniGen2 (8.34) and GPT-4o (8.12) but leads all face-customization methods (next best: PuLID at 5.78). The ablation in Table 3 confirms that the paired-data phase (Phase 3) is the primary driver of CP reduction (0.239→0.161) without sacrificing Sim(GT), and that extended negatives in the InfoNCE loss are critical (removing them drops Sim(GT) from 0.405 to 0.368).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the IP-Adapter paradigm of injecting identity embeddings via cross-attention into a diffusion backbone, and on PuLID's contrastive alignment objective, but departs from both by replacing reconstruction-based training (where reference equals target) with paired training on distinct images of the same identity. The architectural foundation is FLUX (a DiT/flow-matching model), and the identity adapter follows the lightweight cross-attention design of prior UNet-era methods, extended with an optional SigLIP encoder for mid-level attribute retention and attention masks for spatial localization. The key conceptual departure is the identification of 'copy-paste' as a systematic failure mode induced by reconstruction training, and the introduction of an ID contrastive loss with a large extended negative pool (4096 negatives from a reference bank) that prior single-reference methods cannot support.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the community with a large-scale paired multi-ID dataset (MultiID-2M, 2M images) and a benchmark (MultiID-Bench) with a Copy-Paste metric that more honestly evaluates ID-consistent generation than the previously dominant Sim(Ref) metric, which inadvertently rewards trivial copying. The finding that most state-of-the-art models lie on a tight similarity-vs-copy-paste trade-off curve, and that paired training breaks this curve, reframes a practical limitation as a training-data problem rather than an architectural one. Limitations include the celebrity-centric dataset, non-commercial licensing, and reliance on ArcFace for identity measurement; open questions include generalization to non-celebrity and non-frontal identities, scalability beyond 4–5 people per image, and whether the copy-paste metric captures all perceptually relevant artifacts.
