## Problem

SVD-based PEFT methods like SVFT empirically exploit the spectral structure of pre-trained weights but lack a theoretical explanation for why this constitutes an effective inductive bias for fine-tuning. Additionally, existing parameter-efficient methods (LoRA, DoRA, VeRA) rely on randomly initialized low-rank matrices that do not leverage the geometry encoded in pre-trained weights, and prior weight-sharing strategies are highly sensitive to random projections and often underperform standard LoRA. The paper addresses both gaps by providing a rigorous theoretical foundation for column-space projection and pairing it with a structure-aware weight-sharing mechanism.

## Input / Output

PiCa takes frozen pre-trained weight matrices W_0^{f,i} ∈ R^{m×n} from each layer i of each functional group f (query, key, value, etc.) and produces a single shared trainable matrix B^f ∈ R^{r×n} per group, with the fine-tuned weight recovered as W^{f,i} = W_0^{f,i} + U_r^{f,i} B^f. At inference time, only the compact B^f matrices are stored per task; the layer-specific projectors U_r^{f,i} are recomputed via SVD at load time (or optionally cached). Downstream, the adapted model consumes standard text prompts, images, or text-plus-reference-image inputs and produces text responses, class labels, or generated images depending on the task.

## Method

PiCa projects gradients onto the principal column space spanned by the top-r left singular vectors of each pre-trained weight matrix, justified by Theorem 1 which bounds the projection error of ΔW onto U_r by the Eckart-Young rank-r approximation error plus a negligible O(ε) term. Theorem 2 further shows that sequentially projecting gradients at each step closely approximates a single accumulated projection, with error O(η²LGT(T−1)). Concretely, for each functional group f, a single compact matrix B^f ∈ R^{r×n} is shared across all layers i of that group; layer-specific projectors P^{f,i} = U_{[:,1:r]}^{f,i} (from the SVD of W_0^{f,i}) map gradients into the compact space where Adam updates are performed, then map the shared update back to each layer. This weight-sharing across layers with the same function role reduces trainable parameters by up to 7× compared to per-layer variants, while the use of structure-derived (rather than random) projectors per layer preserves the distinct spectral characteristics of each pre-trained weight.

## Results

On Mathematical Reasoning (GSM-8K/MATH), high-rank PiCa (r=256) achieves 52.77/16.36 on Gemma-2B with only 5.37M trainable parameters, outperforming SVFT^R (50.03/15.56 at 6.35M) and DoRA (44.27/16.18 at 13.5M). In the low-rank setting, PiCa (r=32, 0.67M params) reaches 41.32/15.22, surpassing rank-1 LoRA (32.97/13.04 at 0.82M) and SVFT^P (40.34/14.38 at 0.19M). On Commonsense Reasoning (Gemma-7B), PiCa (r=128, 5.11M) achieves 84.47 average across 8 benchmarks, beating SVFT^R (83.35 at 9.80M) and LoRA (83.69 at 68.8M). On GLUE (DeBERTaV3-base), PiCa (r=16, 0.11M) scores 89.69 average, exceeding SVFT^R (89.10 at 0.28M) and BOFT (89.89 at 0.75M) with 2.5–7× fewer parameters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PiCa consistently outperforms all five baselines (LoRA, DoRA, BOFT, VeRA, SVFT) across every model size and task domain. Against LoRA, PiCa achieves comparable or superior accuracy with 5–13× fewer trainable parameters (e.g., 5.37M vs. 26.2M on Gemma-2B high-rank, 0.67M vs. 0.82M on low-rank). Against DoRA, PiCa wins on GSM-8K by 8.5 points (52.77 vs. 44.27) on Gemma-2B while using 2.5× fewer parameters. Against SVFT, the closest competitor, PiCa surpasses SVFT^R on all three models in the high-rank setting (e.g., 52.77 vs. 50.03 GSM-8K on Gemma-2B) with ~15% fewer parameters, and beats SVFT^P on 7/8 commonsense datasets in the low-rank setting. On vision tasks, PiCa achieves the best VTAB-1K overall score (0.697) with the fewest parameters (0.44M vs. 0.45M for VeRA and 0.93M for SVFT^B), and the highest DreamBooth DINO score (0.634) with fewer parameters than all baselines. The ablation in Table 5 confirms that the column-space projection (vs. random projection) contributes a 4.42-point accuracy gain, validating the theoretical motivation.

## Relation to Prior Work

PiCa sits at the intersection of two PEFT research threads: SVD-based methods (SVFT, SVDiff, DiTASK) that exploit the spectral geometry of pre-trained weights, and weight-sharing strategies (VeRA, Tied-LoRA, ShareLoRA) that reduce parameters by sharing trainable matrices across layers. The key departure is twofold: (1) it provides the first theoretical justification (via Wedin's theorem and a sequential projection analysis) for why projecting updates onto the principal column space of pre-trained weights constitutes an effective inductive bias, whereas prior SVD-based methods were justified only empirically; and (2) it combines this projection with a novel weight-sharing scheme that uses layer-specific SVD-derived projectors rather than random projections, enabling cross-layer sharing of a single compact matrix per functional group.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By grounding SVD-based PEFT in a formal approximation guarantee, PiCa gives practitioners a principled reason to prefer column-space projection over random or low-rank alternatives, and the 7× parameter reduction from weight-sharing without performance loss is practically significant for multi-adapter deployment scenarios. A notable limitation is the SVD computation required at load time to recover projection matrices, creating a storage-vs-loading trade-off that the paper acknowledges but does not fully resolve. Open questions include whether the theoretical assumptions (small-update regime, subspace alignment) hold under more aggressive fine-tuning, multi-task settings, or continual learning, which the authors identify as future directions.
