## Problem

Existing hierarchical RL methods for long-horizon sparse-reward tasks select sub-goals using value functions that rely on cross-trajectory Bellman backups, which suffer from generalization noise and signal attenuation. This noise causes the high-level policy to underestimate optimal sub-goals and select sub-optimal ones, ultimately producing sub-optimal trajectories. The paper identifies that the fundamental issue is the high-level policy's decision space requiring generalization across trajectories, and proposes to eliminate this dependency entirely.

## Input / Output

The method takes the current state s_t and task goal g as input, and produces a three-level output hierarchy: a decision-level target (a discrete transition region selected by the TI module), an action-level target (a concrete sub-goal state generated by the high-level policy conditioned on the decision-level target), and a low-level action. The transition regions are pre-extracted from the offline dataset via K-Means clustering with a Future Diversity Index filter, so the TI module operates over a finite set of candidate regions rather than the continuous state space. For visual scenarios (procgen-500), raw RGB observations are flattened and PCA-reduced before clustering, or DINOv2/HIQL embeddings are used instead.

## Method

RD-HRL introduces a reliability-driven decision mechanism with three modules that decouple sub-goal selection from generalization noise. The Transition Region Extraction (TRE) module applies K-Means clustering to the offline dataset and identifies transition regions—clusters where multiple trajectories converge—using a Future Diversity Index (FDI) that counts distinct successor clusters. The Target Identification (TI) module is an MLP trained with an AWR objective that selects one transition region as the decision-level target, restricting the high-level policy's decision space to regions where direct cross-trajectory value propagation is possible. The Target Evaluation (TE) module learns a value function exclusively on transition-region skeletons, performing cross-trajectory Bellman updates with temporal abstraction (collapsing intermediate steps into single macro-steps), which the paper proves (Proposition 1) yields lower accumulated noise than stepwise value updates. The high-level policy then generates action-level targets conditioned on the selected transition region rather than the global goal, and the low-level policy executes actions toward those targets, all trained with standard AWR objectives.

## Results

RD-HRL achieves top-3% normalized scores on 8 out of 9 benchmark tasks, with the sole exception being kitchen-partial (69.6±7.4) where the dataset lacks cross-subtask completion trajectories needed for transition region identification. The most dramatic gains appear on the hardest antmaze-ultra tasks: RD-HRL scores 81.1±6.3 on antmaze-ultra-diverse and 72.9±5.1 on antmaze-ultra-play, outperforming its backbone HIQL (52.9±17.4 and 39.2±14.8) by 53.3% and 85.9% respectively. On the high-dimensional CALVIN manipulation benchmark, RD-HRL reaches 68.8±9.7, a 57% improvement over HIQL (43.8±39.5).

## Baselines & Comparisons

RD-HRL outperforms all 12 baselines on 8 of 9 main tasks, with the sole exception being kitchen-partial where PlanDQ (75.0), HD-DA (73.3), and DiffuserLite (71.4) all exceed RD-HRL's 69.6. Against its direct backbone HIQL, RD-HRL's advantage is most pronounced on the hardest environments: +28.2 points on antmaze-ultra-diverse (81.1 vs 52.9) and +33.7 points on antmaze-ultra-play (72.9 vs 39.2), while on easier antmaze-medium/large tasks the margin narrows to 5–9 points. Among the HRL baselines, PlanDQ and DTAMP are the strongest competitors on antmaze-medium tasks (within 1–2 points), but both fall 10+ points behind RD-HRL on antmaze-ultra. The median-based HILP/HILP-Plan methods are consistently the weakest HRL baselines (21–65 range), confirming that value-function-free sub-goal selection without reliability guarantees is insufficient. Against flat GCRL methods (GCBC, GC-POR, GC-IQL), RD-HRL leads by 20–60 points across all tasks, underscoring the benefit of the hierarchical decomposition. In the online RL transfer experiment (RD-HAC vs HAC on ant-four-rooms), RD-HAC reaches 91% success at 1200k steps versus HAC's 89% at 1900k steps, demonstrating the reliability-driven mechanism also accelerates sample efficiency in online settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

RD-HRL builds directly on HIQL's two-level hierarchical RL architecture (high-level sub-goal proposer + low-level action policy trained via AWR), but departs from the standard HRL paradigm by inserting a reliability-driven decision mechanism between the task goal and the high-level policy. The key conceptual departure is identifying transition regions—clusters of states where multiple trajectories converge—as noise-immune decision spaces, thereby decoupling sub-goal selection from the cross-trajectory generalization that plagues conventional value functions. This connects to trajectory stitching literature (BATS, MBRCSL) and option-based HRL (DDO, HAC), but differs by using a simple K-Means + Future Diversity Index pipeline rather than learned skill discovery or graph-based decomposition.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete and previously underappreciated failure mode in HRL: generalization noise in value functions systematically biases sub-goal selection toward suboptimal targets, and provides both a theoretical noise-reduction proof and a practical mechanism (temporal abstraction over transition region skeletons) to mitigate it. However, the method's reliance on K-Means clustering for transition region extraction is acknowledged as naive and limits applicability to high-dimensional or vision-based settings, where preliminary experiments on Procgen-500 show significant degradation. The kitchen-partial failure—where the dataset lacks cross-subtask trajectories—highlights a fundamental data-coverage requirement that may constrain real-world deployment. An open question is whether end-to-end joint optimization of region extraction and policy learning (rather than the current decoupled pipeline) could close the gap to vision-based tasks.
