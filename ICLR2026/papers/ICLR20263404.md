## Problem

Existing benchmark contamination detection methods rely on separability in log-probability or generation diversity between seen and unseen samples, but it remains unclear whether these signals survive the SFT+RL training pipeline that produces Large Reasoning Models. This paper shows that contamination detection is alarmingly fragile: RL training actively conceals SFT contamination evidence, and CoT contamination applied to already-advanced LRMs leaves near-random detection performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263404/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The contamination setup takes benchmark question-answer pairs (split into member/non-member halves) and distills CoT responses from an advanced LRM (QwQ-32B) for SFT contamination, or uses verifiable-reward RL for RL contamination. Detection methods consume 8 model-generated rollouts per question and compute per-token log-probabilities, edit distances, or compression ratios to produce a scalar detection score per question; AUROC is then computed between member and non-member sets within each benchmark. The paper also reports pass@1 (10 rollouts for AIME/AMC, 3 for others) to quantify performance inflation alongside detectability.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a theoretical decomposition showing that PPO-style importance sampling and clipping in GRPO contracts the NLL gap between members and non-members: under a small natural gradient step, the NLL drift Δ_x splits into a mean term μ(x) and a covariance term β(x), and the clipping-induced covariance Cov(ℓ_k, Σρ_t·m_t) becomes strongly negative for non-members (whose correct trajectories have higher loss variance), driving G_{k+1}−G_k<0. Ablations confirm that removing clipping from RAFT++ or GRPO eliminates the concealment, while plain RAFT (no importance sampling) preserves detectability. For Stage II, the paper shows that extensive CoT SFT on advanced LRMs causes the model to generalize to distributionally similar non-members rather than memorize specific trajectories, so both members and non-members see similar log-prob increases, collapsing the signal that all 10 tested detectors exploit.

## Results

In Stage I (pre-LRM), SFT contamination to Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct is initially detectable: LiRA achieves 89.13% AUROC, Min-K% 74.96%, and Loss 75.48% across six benchmarks. However, after just 156 steps of GRPO training on clean data, AUROC drops sharply (e.g., Max-K% falls from 69.83% to 52.35%, Min-K% from 74.96% to 61.27%), with reference-free methods approaching random guessing. In Stage II (post-LRM), extensive SFT contamination with CoT on advanced LRMs (e.g., DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B) inflates pass@1 by ~11.8 points (48.38%→60.14%), yet nearly all 10 detection methods perform at or near 50% AUROC, indistinguishable from random guessing.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 10 existing contamination detection methods across two LRM contamination scenarios. In Stage I, reference-based LiRA (Mireshghallah et al., 2022) is the strongest pre-RL detector at 89.13% AUROC but drops to 80.14% after GRPO (−8.99 points); reference-free Min-K% (Shi et al., 2023) and Loss (Carlini et al., 2021) start near 75% but fall to ~61% post-GRPO. Max-K% (Maini et al., 2024) suffers the largest relative drop (−17.48 points to 52.35%), essentially reaching random. Generation-based (Verbatim, CDD) and perturbation-based (Neighbor) methods are already near-random before RL and show minimal further degradation. In Stage II, all 10 methods collapse to 46–65% AUROC on contaminated advanced LRMs, with LiRA (the prior SOTA) achieving only 58.74% average—far below its 89% in Stage I. The ablation (Table 3) confirms that PPO-style clipping is the key concealment driver: removing clipping from GRPO restores Loss AUROC from 61.26% to 73.28%, while plain RAFT (no clipping) actually *improves* detectability (+2.03 points over baseline).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the benchmark contamination detection literature (LiRA, Min-K%, Max-K%, Loss, CDD, Verbatim, Neighbor, etc.) from the LLM setting to Large Reasoning Models, where the training pipeline (SFT followed by RL) and chain-of-thought reasoning fundamentally alter the detectability landscape. It departs from prior concealment work (Dekoninck et al., Samuel et al., which explored rephrasing-based evasion) by identifying the RL optimization objective itself—specifically PPO-style importance sampling and clipping—as an inherent concealment mechanism, a level of analysis the authors claim is novel. The theoretical framework builds on natural gradient analysis of PPO-style losses to decompose NLL drift into mean and covariance terms, connecting RL training dynamics to the statistical separability that detection methods exploit.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The findings expose a critical vulnerability: model developers can inflate LRM leaderboard scores through contamination while leaving minimal detectable traces, undermining the fairness and integrity of public evaluations. The paper does not propose a new detection method, leaving the central open question—how to reliably detect contamination in models that generalize across distributionally similar samples via long CoT—unresolved. Future work must move beyond memorization-based assumptions and account for the generalization capacity of LRMs, and evaluation protocols may need to incorporate intermediate checkpoint auditing or fundamentally new detection paradigms.
