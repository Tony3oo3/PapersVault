## Problem

Denoising generative models (diffusion and flow-based) suffer from a pervasive yet overlooked training-inference misalignment: accumulated errors from network approximation, discretization, and stochastic factors cause intermediate states during sampling to encode a higher effective noise level than the pre-defined schedule intends. This 'noise shift' leads to out-of-distribution generalization errors and inaccurate denoising updates, degrading generation quality in a way that is systematic and not addressed by prior work focused on individual error sources.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261171/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates in the latent space of a pre-trained SD-VAE (32×32×4 tensors) at 256×256 image resolution, taking Gaussian noise and an optional class condition as input and producing a generated RGB image. A key practical requirement is that the denoising network must be trained (or fine-tuned) with noise-condition dropout (10–20%), so the same weights serve both the noise-conditional and noise-unconditional branches needed for the guidance computation. For pre-trained checkpoints, only ~10 additional epochs (~0.7% of original training cost) are needed to enable NAG.

## Method

Noise Awareness Guidance (NAG) steers sampling trajectories back toward the intended noise level by reinforcing the conditioning on t. The classifier-free variant computes a score mixture s^{w_nag}(x|t) = (w_nag+1)·s(x|t) − w_nag·s(x), where s(x|t) is the existing noise-conditional score and s(x) is a noise-unconditional score obtained via noise-condition dropout during training (analogous to CFG's class-condition dropout). This eliminates the need for an external noise-level classifier. The NAG guidance axis is orthogonal to CFG's class-conditional axis: NAG directly reduces the noise shift δ by pulling intermediate states toward their pre-defined noise level, while CFG biases toward high-confidence class regions. Empirically, NAG alone approaches CFG-level quality, and combining both yields further gains, with the noise shift δ reduced most substantially in the mid-to-high SNR regime (t ≈ 0.5).

## Results

On ImageNet 256×256 with converged DiT-XL/2, NAG reduces FID from 9.62 (no CFG) to 2.55, and from 2.27 (with CFG) to 2.14, while improving precision from 0.67 to 0.79 (no CFG) and recall from 0.57 to 0.61 (with CFG). For SiT-XL/2, NAG drops FID from 8.61 to 2.26 (no CFG) and from 2.06 to 1.72 (with CFG). On supervised fine-tuning across seven downstream datasets, NAG alone reduces average FID from 18.65 to 13.72, and when stacked on top of DoG, further improves average FID from 11.55 to 10.05.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NAG dramatically outperforms all three prior exposure-bias baselines on DiT-XL/2 without CFG: it achieves FID 2.55 versus 12.25 for Epsilon Scaling (ES), 13.14 for Time Shift (TS), and 10.20 for Input Perturbation (IP), representing a 3.8×–5.1× FID reduction. The gap is even more pronounced on SiT-XL/2, where NAG reaches FID 2.26 while ES, TS, and IP only achieve 8.70, 8.65, and 8.06 respectively. Under CFG, NAG still leads on FID (2.14 vs. 2.19–2.20 for DiT; 1.72 vs. 1.94–1.96 for SiT) and substantially improves recall (0.61 vs. 0.60–0.61 for DiT; 0.66 vs. 0.59–0.61 for SiT). On supervised fine-tuning, NAG stacked on Domain Guidance (DoG) reduces average FID from 11.55 to 10.05 across seven datasets, and NAG alone (13.72) already matches or exceeds CFG-only sampling (13.69) without any class-conditional guidance. The key distinction is that ES, TS, and IP rely on hand-crafted assumptions about error structure (fixed scaling, time-step search, or Lipschitz regularization), whereas NAG adaptively corrects the noise shift at every sampling step via a learned guidance signal, explaining its consistent superiority across architectures and settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classifier-free guidance (CFG) paradigm by introducing an orthogonal noise-condition dropout axis, repurposing the CFG training recipe to produce a noise-unconditional branch that enables guidance along the noise-level dimension. It departs from prior exposure-bias work in diffusion models (Epsilon Scaling, Time Shift, Input Perturbation) by rejecting their assumption of a single dominant error source; instead, NAG treats noise shift as the collective effect of approximation, discretization, and stochastic errors, and corrects it via a learned guidance signal rather than hand-designed rescaling or schedule manipulation. The stochastic interpolants framework of Albergo et al. and the SiT/DiT architectures provide the unified mathematical and architectural substrate, while the noise-level posterior estimator g_φ draws on the concentration-of-measure property of high-dimensional data noted by Sun et al. (2025).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of noise shift as a systematic, persistent misalignment that survives even in fully converged 1400-epoch models challenges the implicit assumption that sufficient training eliminates training-inference gap in denoising samplers. The fix is remarkably lightweight—noise-condition dropout plus a single guidance weight—yet yields FID drops from ~9 to ~2.5 without CFG, a magnitude that suggests the issue was a major hidden bottleneck. Open questions include whether a purely theoretical characterization of δ is possible, whether models can be made inherently robust to inference-time shift (eliminating the need for guidance), and how the effect interacts with very few-step or one-step samplers where discretization error dominates. The paper also acknowledges that the external estimator g_φ has limited accuracy at very low noise levels, so the quantified δ is a lower bound on the true misalignment.
