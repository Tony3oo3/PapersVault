## Problem

Training score-based diffusion models requires clean ground-truth images, which are unavailable in many imaging applications due to hardware limits or patient discomfort. Existing self-supervised approaches (Ambient diffusion, GSURE, SURE-score) attempt to approximate the full image score from degraded measurements, an unnecessarily difficult objective since measurements lie in structured subspaces. The paper asks whether it is more natural and effective to learn partial measurement scores directly within these subspaces rather than recovering full-image scores.

## Input / Output

The method takes noisy, subsampled measurements as input—32×32 masked patches (40% masked) from 128×128 RGB FFHQ faces, or 1-D Cartesian k-space masks at acceleration rates R=4 or R=6 from 256×256 complex-valued multi-coil fastMRI slices—along with the known forward (subsampling) operator. It produces fully-sampled measurements that map to images (via inverse Fourier transform and coil-sensitivity for MRI), and for inverse problems, reconstructed images from partial measurements. The model operates in the measurement domain (k-space for MRI, pixel space for natural images), with domain-specific transformations applied only at the input/output of the U-Net denoiser.

## Method

MSM trains a U-Net denoiser on subsampled measurements by adding diffusion noise and minimizing MSE to predict the clean subsampled measurement; the partial measurement score is then obtained via Tweedie's formula, explicitly conditioned on the subsampling mask. For noisy measurements, a two-case strategy is used: when diffusion noise exceeds measurement noise, a residual-noise denoising loss combined with SURE loss is applied; when diffusion noise is smaller, the model first denoises measurement noise using its own prediction as a pseudo-clean reference. The key sampling innovation defines the MSM score as an expectation over all mask-conditioned partial scores (a product-of-experts formulation), approximated stochastically by drawing w random masks per diffusion step, denoising each partial measurement, and aggregating with a coverage-weighting vector that compensates for overlapping regions. A theoretical guarantee (via Girsanov's theorem) shows the KL divergence between the stochastic and ideal sampling distributions scales as O(1/w). For inverse problems, a likelihood gradient term is inserted between the aggregation and reverse-diffusion steps, enabling posterior sampling without retraining.

## Results

On FFHQ face generation (128×128), MSM trained on 40% subsampled data achieves 29.14 FID, nearly halving the 55.90 FID of Ambient diffusion; with added noise (ρ=0.1), MSM reaches 37.14 FID versus 89.71 for GSURE diffusion. For compressed-sensing MRI reconstruction at R=4, MSM achieves 30.71 dB PSNR and 0.145 LPIPS, outperforming both A-DPS (27.28 dB, 0.173 LPIPS) and Robust SSDU (29.65 dB, 0.160 LPIPS). On box inpainting, MSM reaches 24.71 dB PSNR and 0.076 LPIPS, substantially surpassing A-DPS (20.14 dB, 0.305 LPIPS) and even the input image (18.26 dB, 0.304 LPIPS).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MSM substantially outperforms Ambient diffusion (Daras et al., 2024) on unconditional generation, achieving 29.14 vs. 55.90 FID on FFHQ and 64.37 vs. 70.07 on multi-coil MRI, and also beats it on inverse problems (e.g., 24.71 vs. 20.14 dB PSNR on inpainting). Against GSURE diffusion (Kawar et al., 2024), MSM achieves 37.14 vs. 89.71 FID under noisy subsampled training, a more than 2× improvement. For inverse problems, MSM dominates A-DPS (Aali et al., 2025) across all metrics: on 4× super-resolution it reaches 28.11 dB PSNR and 0.117 LPIPS versus A-DPS's 22.61 dB and 0.277 LPIPS. In CS-MRI reconstruction, MSM outperforms Robust SSDU (Millard & Chiew, 2024) in PSNR (30.71 vs. 29.65 dB at R=4) and LPIPS (0.145 vs. 0.160), though SSDU edges ahead slightly on SSIM (0.847 vs. 0.839). Compared to clean-data-trained solvers DPS (Chung et al., 2023) and DDNM (Wang et al., 2023), MSM trained solely on subsampled measurements approaches their performance (e.g., 30.71 vs. 31.31 dB PSNR vs. 32.84 dB for CS-MRI R=4), confirming that the measurement-domain learning strategy recovers most of the quality gap without any clean training data.

## Relation to Prior Work

MSM departs from the dominant strategy in clean-data-free diffusion training (Ambient diffusion, GSURE diffusion, SURE-score) of attempting to recover full-image scores from degraded measurements, instead learning partial measurement scores restricted to observable regions in the measurement domain. This reframing is conceptually analogous to how patch-based methods improve scalability in the image domain, but transplanted to the measurement domain where each subsampled observation is uniquely defined by its acquisition operator, removing the ambiguity of infinitely many images mapping to the same measurement. The stochastic sampling algorithm with a KL-divergence guarantee via Girsanov's theorem is a new technical contribution, and the posterior sampling extension builds on DPS/DDNM-style likelihood gradients but applies them within the measurement-space stochastic loop rather than in the image domain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MSM provides a principled and practical framework for training diffusion models entirely from degraded measurements, which is critical in settings like high-resolution imaging or long MRI scans where clean ground truth is prohibitively expensive. The large performance gaps over prior clean-data-free methods (e.g., FID 29 vs 56 for Ambient diffusion; PSNR 24.7 vs 20.1 for A-DPS in inpainting) suggest the measurement-domain perspective is not merely a reformulation but a genuinely more effective inductive bias. Key limitations include the requirement that training measurements collectively cover the full measurement domain (ruling out globally uniform degradations like motion blur), the restriction to linear inverse problems, and the computational overhead of the stochastic loop parameter w. Open questions include extending to non-linear forward operators, handling globally applied degradations, and whether the measurement-domain advantage persists at larger scales or with different architectures.
