## Problem

Existing LLM routing methods assign entire queries to a single model, implicitly assuming that a stronger model's intervention is equally necessary at every generation step. In multi-step reasoning tasks, however, errors are localized: a single incorrect step can derail the solution, while most subsequent steps are routine continuations that a cheaper model can handle. This paper asks whether routing at the granularity of individual reasoning steps—intervening only where the trajectory risks diverging—can dramatically reduce expensive-model token usage while preserving accuracy.

## Input / Output

TRIM consumes a math problem query and incrementally builds a solution trace one step at a time (steps delimited by double newlines). At each step boundary, the router receives the partial reasoning trace, the PRM's step-level correctness scores, and per-step token counts, then emits a binary action: continue (accept the cheap model's step) or regenerate (replace that single step with the expensive model's output before resuming with the cheap model). The final output is the complete solution trace, with cost measured solely as the number of tokens decoded by the expensive model, since prefill overhead can be amortized via parallel chunked prefilling.

## Method

TRIM operates a two-model setup (cheap M_w, expensive M_s) guided by a process reward model (Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-7B) that scores each intermediate step. Three routing policies are developed: (1) TRIM-Thr, a myopic threshold on the current step's PRM score; (2) TRIM-Agg, a PPO-trained MLP policy over aggregated features (current PRM score, minimum of prior scores, current token count, step index) that optimizes a return balancing terminal correctness against cumulative expensive-model token cost; and (3) TRIM-POMDP, which models PRM scores as noisy observations of a three-class latent correctness state (correct-so-far, irrecoverably-wrong, current-step-wrong-but-recoverable) and solves the resulting POMDP with SARSOP, enabling policy recomputation for arbitrary cost budgets without retraining. A key design choice is that the regenerate action replaces only the single erroneous step with M_s's output and immediately returns control to M_w, rather than handing over all remaining steps—empirically, 1–3 targeted interventions account for the vast majority of accuracy gains.

## Results

On MATH-500, TRIM-POMDP achieves CPT(95%) at only 17.98% of expensive-model tokens (83.12 tokens) with ΔIBC=5.86, while the best query-level baseline (AutoMix-PRM) requires 53.96% (249.49 tokens) with ΔIBC=0.95. On the harder AIME benchmark, TRIM-POMDP reaches CPT(95%) at 28.17% of expensive tokens (244.86) with ΔIBC=5.00, compared to 80.96% (703.77) and ΔIBC=0.07 for AutoMix-PRM. Even the simplest TRIM-Thr policy achieves 5× higher cost efficiency than the best prior method on MATH-500 (ΔIBC=4.75 vs 0.95) and 2.3× on AIME (ΔIBC=1.81 vs 0.79). Cross-dataset, TRIM-Agg trained solely on AIME generalizes to OlympiadBench (ΔIBC=2.57) and Minerva Math (ΔIBC=3.12), while query-level routers degrade sharply (e.g., BERT drops from ΔIBC=0.44 on AIME to −0.04 on OlympiadBench).

## Baselines & Comparisons

TRIM dominates all three query-level routing baselines across every benchmark and metric. Against RouteLLM (BERT, MF, SW Ranking), TRIM-Thr alone achieves 5–60× higher ΔIBC on MATH-500 (4.75 vs 0.08–0.49) and 2.3–2.7× on AIME (1.81 vs 0.44–0.79); the gap widens further for TRIM-Agg and TRIM-POMDP. Against Smoothie, TRIM-POMDP requires only 28.17% of expensive tokens for CPT(95%) on AIME versus Smoothie's 94.57%, a 3.4× reduction, while also achieving higher ΔIBC (5.00 vs 0.03). Against AutoMix (and its PRM-enhanced variant), TRIM-Thr cuts CPT(50%) on MATH-500 from 175.67 (AutoMix) / 110.42 (AutoMix-PRM) to 43.68 tokens, and on AIME TRIM-POMDP reduces CPT(95%) from 857.89 / 703.77 to 244.86 tokens. Notably, query-level routers exhibit poor cross-dataset generalization (BERT's ΔIBC drops from 0.44 on AIME to −0.04 on OlympiadBench), whereas TRIM-Agg trained on AIME maintains strong efficiency on OlympiadBench (ΔIBC=2.57) and Minerva Math (ΔIBC=3.12), confirming that step-level routing captures transferable reasoning structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

TRIM departs from the dominant query-level routing paradigm (RouteLLM, AutoMix, Smoothie, Hybrid-LLM) by operating at the granularity of individual reasoning steps rather than assigning entire queries to a single model. It repurposes process reward models—previously used for candidate selection in beam search or exploration shaping (Lightman et al., Wang et al.)—as real-time routing signals during generation, and extends AutoMix's POMDP formulation from a single-step greedy decision to a full multi-step POMDP solved with SARSOP. The closest prior work, Reward-Guided Speculative Decoding and SpecReason, also uses step-level accept/regenerate signals but optimizes for latency in a fixed high-budget regime, whereas TRIM explicitly optimizes accuracy under a constrained expensive-token budget.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that 1–3 targeted step-level interventions recover most of the accuracy gap between a small and large model (Table 6) provides a concrete, actionable principle for cost-efficient LLM deployment in multi-step reasoning. Cross-dataset generalization (AIME-trained policies transferring to OlympiadBench and Minerva Math) suggests step-level difficulty patterns capture transferable structure rather than dataset-specific artifacts, which could reduce the need for per-dataset router training. Open questions include whether the approach extends to token-level granularity (discussed but not implemented), how it performs in domains without reliable PRMs (GPQA results are weaker, ΔIBC=0.26), and whether the POMDP formulation scales to longer horizons or more than two models.
