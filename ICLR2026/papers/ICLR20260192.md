## Problem

Estimating relative free-energy differences between multiple thermodynamic states is central to computational biochemistry, but traditional non-equilibrium estimators (Jarzynski-based) suffer from high variance and finite-sample bias when the instantaneous distribution lags behind the target. Escorted protocols that combine deterministic advection with stochastic dynamics can in principle eliminate this variance, but finding the jointly optimal vector field and time-dependent potential that solve the continuity equation has been non-trivial and previously unaddressed in a learnable framework.

## Input / Output

The method takes equilibrium molecular configurations (10,000 samples per state generated via umbrella sampling with harmonic flat-bottom constraints on dihedral angles) and the corresponding potential energy functions as input. For conformational free-energy problems, all states share the same underlying potential but are restricted to different phase-space regions defined by dihedral-angle boundaries. The output is a learned escorted switching protocol consisting of a time-dependent SE(3)-equivariant GNN vector field and a time-dependent discrete normalizing-flow potential, from which free-energy differences between all pairs of states are computed via the MBAR estimator applied to forward and reverse escorted work values.

## Method

The paper jointly learns the escorting vector field b via standard Conditional Flow Matching (CFM) and the time-dependent potential U via a newly proposed Conditional Density Matching (CDM) objective, which extends maximum-likelihood estimation with a time index and uses the conditional probability path to avoid sampling from the unknown marginal. A key theoretical result shows that the CDM and its marginal counterpart have identical gradients, so training on the accessible conditional distribution recovers the correct marginal density, and the CFM+CDM pair collectively solves the continuity equation required for zero-variance estimates. Two practical extensions are introduced: a Lie-Trotter splitting that decouples the expensive divergence evaluation from the numerically unstable stationarity-preserving dynamics, and an Escorted Protocol Flow Graph (EPFG) that concatenates K−1 learned protocols through a central reference node to obtain all K(K−1)/2 pairwise free-energy differences without training a separate model for each pair.

## Results

On the Alanine Dipeptide (ADP) six-state benchmark, the best E-NEQ configuration (overdamped Langevin with EPFG) achieves a Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of 0.59 kJ/mol across all state pairs, compared to 1.17 kJ/mol for TFEP and 3.92 kJ/mol for the basic Jarzynski Equality at 50,000 steps. The E-NEQ estimator with underdamped Langevin and EPFG achieves MAE 0.88 kJ/mol, and all E-NEQ variants outperform TFEP. Correlation with the Umbrella Sampling baseline reaches R²=0.91 and Pearson r=0.96 for E-NEQ versus R²=0.90 and Pearson r=0.95 for TFEP, with most estimates within 1 kJ/mol of the US reference.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Umbrella Sampling (US) reference, E-NEQ estimates closely track the true free-energy differences, with most states within ~1 kJ/mol (e.g., α_L: 7.35 vs 7.42; C5: 1.31 vs 1.37 for UL+EPFG), though the β state remains a notable outlier across all methods. Compared to TFEP (MAE 1.17), E-NEQ consistently achieves lower MAE across all three integrators, with the best configuration (OL + EPFG) halving the error to 0.59 kJ/mol; the EPFG concatenation also improves estimates for directly connected states beyond what pairwise summation provides. Against the basic Jarzynski Equality estimator, E-NEQ is dramatically superior: even at 50,000 interpolation steps JE achieves only MAE 3.92, while E-NEQ at just 100 steps reaches MAE 0.59–0.99, highlighting the benefit of the learned escorting vector field. The β state is the one consistent exception where E-NEQ does not clearly beat TFEP, with E-NEQ overestimating ΔF_β by ~1.4 kJ/mol versus TFEP's ~1.9 kJ/mol error.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Conditional Flow Matching (CFM) framework (Lipman et al., 2023) to the domain of non-equilibrium free-energy estimation by introducing a companion Conditional Density Matching (CDM) objective that jointly learns the time-dependent potential alongside the escorting vector field. It builds directly on the Escorted Non-Equilibrium (E-NEQ) estimator framework of Vaikuntanathan and Jarzynski (2008), which requires the vector field and stationary distribution to collectively solve the continuity equation for zero-variance estimation, but previously had no practical method for learning this pair. The closest prior work is FEAT (He et al., 2025), which also parameterizes escorted switching protocols but uses separate forward/backward protocols and does not address the multi-state setting. The paper further departs from Targeted Free-Energy Perturbation (TFEP, Jarzynski 2002) by adding stochastic stationarity-preserving dynamics to the purely deterministic transport, and introduces the Escorted Protocol Flow Graph (EPFG) to reduce multi-state protocol training from O(K²) to O(K).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Free-energy difference estimation is a bottleneck in drug discovery pipelines (binding affinity prediction, lead optimization), and this work provides a principled ML-based approach that reduces variance relative to TFEP while maintaining the theoretical guarantees of the Jarzynski/Crooks framework. The CDM objective is a clean, general extension of CFM that could apply beyond free-energy estimation to any setting where both a transport field and a density must be learned jointly. However, the evaluation is limited to the Alanine Dipeptide toy system with six metastable states, and the β state consistently deviates from the Umbrella Sampling baseline across all methods, suggesting the approach may struggle with certain conformational transitions. Key open questions include scalability to larger biomolecular systems, whether the MH correction step can be removed with adaptive integrators, and whether hard constraints on the continuity equation (rather than soft learning objectives) would improve accuracy.
