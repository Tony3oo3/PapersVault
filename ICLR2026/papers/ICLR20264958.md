## Problem

Prior work evaluating LLMs' knowledge of human opinions relies solely on next-token outputs, leaving it unknown whether LLMs internally encode more opinion knowledge than they express. This paper asks what LLMs truly know about group-specific opinions, where that knowledge resides in the network, and whether it can be causally manipulated—questions that matter for pluralistic alignment, synthetic survey respondents, and understanding what LLMs learn during training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264958/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes 7,062 prompts (22 demographic groups × 321 questions from OpinionQA and SubPOP) fed into Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-7B, and Vicuna-7B, extracting residual stream activations at all 32 layers at the demographic token position. Probes map these D=4096-dimensional activations to K-way answer distributions (K=2 or 3), evaluated via KL divergence against ground-truth human survey distributions. For the SAE and steering analyses, the input is concatenated attention head activations (32 heads × 128 dims = 4096) per layer, and the output is a set of group-selective sparse features whose manipulation produces steered answer distributions measured by the fraction of source-to-base KL distance closed.

## Method

The paper trains a multinomial logistic regression probe per layer on residual stream activations to predict each group's answer distribution, showing that internal knowledge exceeds next-token outputs by 52–82% in KL divergence reduction, with linear probes matching MLPs (supporting the linear representation hypothesis for answer distributions). Layer-by-layer analysis reveals opinion knowledge emerges rapidly in middle layers (10–15) and is not lost in deeper layers; instead, the entire probing-to-prompting gap is attributed to the unembedding matrix, confirmed by showing that fine-tuning only unembeddings recovers 46–62% of the KL improvement. To trace the source of this knowledge, the authors train k-sparse autoencoders (M=2048, K=100) on concatenated attention head activations per layer, identifying features with F1 > 0.7 that fire selectively for specific demographic groups. Steering experiments then turn off base-group features and amplify source-group features in the SAE representation across layers 9–18, demonstrating causal control: the intervention closes up to 94% of the KL distance between two groups' predicted distributions (e.g., <HS → College+), with socioeconomic attributes showing the strongest steerability.

## Results

Probing LLM residual stream activations reduces KL divergence from the human answer distribution by 66%, 67%, and 82% (2-option) and 52%, 50%, and 71% (3-option) for Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-7B, and Vicuna-7B respectively, compared to next-token probability prompting. Probing achieves over 85% of the KL improvement from LoRA fine-tuning while using 278× fewer parameters. Fine-tuning only the unembedding layer (a linear projection to vocabulary) recovers 61.8% (2-option) and 46.4% (3-option) of the prompting-to-probe gap, confirming the unembedding as the source of the internal-external knowledge discrepancy. SAE-based steering of demographic features in middle layers (9–18) yields a mean improvement ratio of 0.440, with education and income transitions exceeding 0.85.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the prompting baseline (Santurkar et al., 2023), which extracts opinions via next-token probabilities over answer-option letters, probing the residual stream achieves a 52–82% reduction in KL divergence across all three models and both question types, with the largest gains for Vicuna-7B (82% on 2-option). Against LoRA fine-tuning (Suh et al., 2025), probing recovers over 85% of the KL improvement (e.g., 0.073 vs. 0.046 KL for Llama-3.1-8B on 2-option questions) while training only D×K ≈ 8,192 parameters versus the full LoRA adapter, a 278× parameter reduction. Fine-tuning only the unembedding matrix achieves 75–78% of LoRA's improvement (0.082 vs. 0.046 KL on 2-option), demonstrating that the bulk of the fine-tuning gain comes from re-weighting the final linear projection rather than updating transformer weights. Linear (multinomial logistic regression) probes match non-linear MLP probes within 0.005 average KL across all layers, models, and groups, supporting the linear representation hypothesis for opinion distributions. On the non-US GlobalOpinionQA dataset, probing still yields 26–39% KL reduction over prompting, confirming generalization beyond US demographics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the probing methodology (Alain & Bengio, 2016; Kissane et al., 2024) from scalar or binary concepts (truthfulness, political spectrum) to the multi-group, multi-dimensional setting of public opinion distributions, using multinomial probes and KL divergence. It departs from prior opinion-evaluation work (Santurkar et al., 2023; Suh et al., 2025), which exclusively measured LLM knowledge through next-token outputs, by opening the model and probing residual stream activations across all layers. The SAE-based steering approach generalizes binary steering vector methods (Subramani et al., 2022; Rimsky et al., 2024) to 22 demographic groups simultaneously, and the unembedding fine-tuning analysis builds on the broader observation (Burns et al., 2022; Azaria & Mitchell, 2023) that internal representations can exceed output quality, while providing a precise mechanistic account of where the gap arises.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that probes achieve 85% of LoRA fine-tuning improvement with 278× fewer parameters, and that fine-tuning only unembeddings recovers 75–78% of that gain, offers a concrete, low-cost pathway for pluralistic alignment and synthetic-respondent applications in survey research. The identification of middle-layer attention head features as causal levers for group-specific steering opens a route to interpretable, targeted alignment without full model retraining. Key limitations include English-only prompts, US-centric demographic groups (though partially addressed via GlobalOpinionQA), and restriction to multiple-choice survey items, leaving open how well the framework generalizes to open-ended, personal, or cross-linguistic opinion settings.
