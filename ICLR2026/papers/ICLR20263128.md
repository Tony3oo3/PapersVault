## Problem

In multimodal learning, heterogeneous modalities converge at different rates, causing dominant modalities to drag weaker ones into sharp regions of the loss landscape and degrading generalization. While Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM) finds flat minima in unimodal settings, directly applying it to multimodal training fails because SAM's uniform perturbation overemphasizes the dominant modality and its gradient direction is distorted by cross-modal interference, preventing weaker modalities from reaching their own flat minima.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263128/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

MASAM operates on paired (or tri-modal) multimodal instances—e.g., EHR time-series + chest X-ray, audio + video, food image + web text, MRI + tabular biomarkers, or text + audio + video—encoded by modality-specific encoders and combined via late fusion (concatenation + linear projection). The output is a single class prediction per sample under a unified cross-entropy objective, with auxiliary unimodal classification heads providing per-modality supervision. The method is task-agnostic: it produces disease phenotype labels, mortality predictions, emotion/action/food/humor class labels depending on the downstream task.

## Method

MASAM introduces two mechanisms to make SAM modality-aware. First, the Adaptive Perturbation Score (APS) quantifies each modality's dominance at every training step by combining a loss-decay signal (short-term loss reduction relative to a moving average) with a gradient-alignment score (cosine similarity between the unimodal and fusion gradients); only the modality with the highest APS receives a SAM perturbation. Second, Modality-Decoupled Perturbation Scaling (MDPS) scales the SAM perturbation magnitude by the same gradient-alignment coefficient γ_m, effectively projecting the unimodal gradient onto the fusion gradient direction so that the perturbation explores flatness along the shared-information axis rather than along a direction contaminated by other modalities. Non-dominant modalities are updated with standard gradient descent on both the fusion and unimodal losses, avoiding misaligned perturbations. A convergence theorem (Theorem 1) proves that under standard smoothness and step-size conditions, the modality-specific MASAM update converges to a stationary point of the joint multimodal objective.

## Results

MASAM achieves the best performance across all six datasets and seven downstream tasks. Headline results include 0.498 AUPRC on MIMIC-Phenotype (+3.53% over the strongest baseline InfoREG), 0.603 AUPRC on MIMIC-Mortality (+3.08% over MMPareto), 0.814 accuracy on CREMA-D (+5.71% over AUG), 0.740 accuracy on Kinetics-Sounds (+7.40% over AUG), 0.935 accuracy on UPMC-Food101 (+0.75% over MLA), 0.857 mAP on ADNI (+1.18% over AUG), and 0.644 accuracy on UR-FUNNY (+1.90% over OGM). All results are averaged over four random seeds.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MASAM outperforms all ten baselines on every dataset where they are directly comparable. The largest gains are on Kinetics-Sounds (+7.40% over AUG, the strongest baseline at 0.689 accuracy) and CREMA-D (+5.71% over AUG at 0.770), where gradient-modulation methods like OGM (0.677/0.769) and AGM (0.688/0.746) also trail significantly. On the noisy clinical MIMIC dataset, MASAM surpasses the best baselines by 3.53% (Phenotype, over InfoREG at 0.481) and 3.08% (Mortality, over MMPareto at 0.585), while clinical-specific methods DrFuse (0.479/0.576) and MedFuse (0.470/0.549) lag further behind. On UPMC-Food101, the margin is modest (+0.75% over MLA at 0.928) but statistically significant (p=0.0046). On ADNI, MASAM leads AUG (0.847) and DrFuse (0.836) by 1.18% and 2.15% mAP respectively. On the tri-modal UR-FUNNY dataset, MASAM (0.644) beats OGM (0.632) and AGM (0.630), the only baselines supporting three modalities.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM) from its original unimodal formulation to the multimodal setting, identifying two specific failure modes when SAM is naively applied: amplified modality imbalance and modality-agnostic perturbation. It departs from the dominant line of multimodal balancing work (G-Blend, OGM, AGM, MLA, MMPareto, PMR), which modulates gradient magnitudes or training schedules but ignores the geometry of the loss landscape. The key conceptual contribution is reframing modality imbalance as a sharpness problem—faster-converging modalities get dragged into sharp regions by cross-modal interference—and addressing it with per-modality adaptive perturbations (APS) and decoupled scaling (MDPS) rather than uniform gradient rescaling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper fills a clear gap: SAM has been widely adopted for unimodal generalization but its interaction with multimodal imbalance was unexplored, and the diagnosis that SAM's uniform perturbation can *worsen* imbalance is a useful caution for practitioners. The convergence proof (via inexact gradient descent) and the Hessian-trace / loss-landscape visualizations provide mechanistic evidence beyond black-box accuracy gains. The main limitation is the additional training overhead from extra backward passes, and the paper acknowledges this as a direction for future lightweight variants. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond late fusion to early or cross-attention fusion, and whether the two-modality APS/MDPS design scales cleanly to many more modalities.
