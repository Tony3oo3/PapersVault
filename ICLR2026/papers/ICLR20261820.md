## Problem

The microscopic organization enabling complex behaviors in neural networks remains poorly understood, and existing interpretability tools such as ablations and influence functions lack a unified theoretical framework that directly links structure in the data distribution to structure in model internals. This paper asks whether a principled linear-response measure, derived from Bayesian statistical mechanics, can quantify how individual network components respond to controlled shifts in the data distribution and thereby reveal and attribute internal functional structure.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261820/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a trained transformer checkpoint and a set of probe data distributions (Pile subsets), from which it draws SGLD samples from a localized Gibbs posterior centered at the checkpoint weights, with a small mixture fraction δh=0.1 controlling perturbation strength. The output is a response matrix of per-token susceptibility scores (one entry per probe distribution × attention head), which after row-standardization and PCA yields principal components whose data loadings identify token patterns (word boundaries, induction patterns, delimiters) and whose component loadings identify functional modules (induction circuit, multigram heads, Dyck heads).

## Method

The paper treats a neural network as a Bayesian statistical mechanical system in which an infinitesimal mixture perturbation of the data distribution induces a first-order linear response in the posterior expectation of a component-localized observable (the loss difference restricted to that component's weights). The resulting susceptibility equals the negative covariance between this observable and the loss difference under the localized posterior, and is estimated efficiently using local SGLD samples rather than intractable full-posterior sampling. The susceptibility factorizes into signed per-token contributions that serve as attribution scores, with negative values indicating expression (the component predicts the token) and positive values indicating suppression (the component opposes the token). By assembling these susceptibilities into a response matrix across probe distributions and components and applying PCA, the method performs structural inference: the low-rank structure of the matrix separates functional modules such as the induction circuit from multigram heads, with principal-component loadings directly identifying the data patterns each module is sensitive to.

## Results

Applying PCA to the per-token susceptibility response matrix of a 3M-parameter two-layer transformer, the top three principal components explain 95.34%, 1.83%, and 0.73% of variance in layer 0 and 99.14%, 0.39%, and 0.11% in layer 1. PC2 cleanly separates the induction circuit (heads 1:6, 1:7, 0:1, 0:4, 0:5) from the layer-1 multigram heads (1:0–1:5), and PC3 isolates Dyck (bracket-matching) heads (0:7, 1:3, 1:5). The per-token susceptibility estimates correlate with overall susceptibilities at Pearson r = 0.958 (slope 9.794 vs. the theoretically expected 10), while their correlation with zero-ablation loss differences is negligible, confirming the signal is non-redundant. The induction-circuit separation holds across all four independently trained seeds.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary baseline comparison is against zero ablation (Zhou et al., 2018). Density-map visualizations (Figure 8) show that per-token susceptibilities and zero-ablation loss differences have negligible Pearson correlation for both attention heads 0:0 and 1:2 across GITHUB and DM_MATHEMATICS, demonstrating that susceptibilities capture structural information not accessible through activation ablation. A concrete example (Figure 9) shows that induction patterns are clearly visible in positive susceptibility values but absent from both ablation deltas and raw per-token loss. The method is also validated against the circuit identifications of Wang et al. (2024): structural inference via PCA independently recovers the induction circuit, the multigram heads, and the Dyck heads without any prior labeling, matching the ablation-based characterizations in that work. The authors note that their susceptibility-based notion of expression/suppression does not reduce to the logit-level prediction/suppression neurons studied by Gurnee et al. (2024b) or Lad et al. (2025), though a simple relation was not found in preliminary investigation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper derives a linear-response (susceptibility) framework for interpretability by treating a neural network as a Bayesian statistical mechanical system, building directly on the singular learning theory of Watanabe (2009) and the refined local learning coefficient of Wang et al. (2024, ICLR). It departs from the dominant ablation-based paradigm in mechanistic interpretability by measuring correlations in weight-space loss perturbations rather than activation perturbations, and it introduces 'structural inference'—PCA on a response matrix of susceptibilities—as a new circuit-discovery procedure. The mode-decomposition factorization X=CP draws on Chen and Murfet (2025), and the expression/suppression interpretation is positioned alongside but distinct from logit-space suppression work (Gurnee et al. 2024, McDougall et al. 2024).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a theoretically grounded, generalization-aware alternative to ablations for probing internal structure, with a clean link between susceptibility sign and expression/suppression that is absent from activation-based methods. However, all empirical validation is on a 3M-parameter, 16-head transformer, and the authors explicitly note that PCA alone is unlikely to suffice for larger models where patterns are more entangled. Key open questions include whether SGLD sampling quality limits signal at scale, whether the method captures non-linear or higher-order structure, and whether the expression/suppression decomposition aligns with or contradicts the logit-space suppression literature. The practical cost (separate posterior samples per component) also remains a barrier to routine use on billion-parameter models.
