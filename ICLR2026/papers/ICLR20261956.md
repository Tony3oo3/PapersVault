## Problem

Neural networks trained with gradient descent consistently learn solutions of increasing complexity over time (simplicity bias), yet existing theoretical treatments are architecture-specific and lack a unifying framework. It was unclear whether a single mechanism drives stage-like dynamics across linear, ReLU, convolutional, and attention-based architectures, what the operative notion of simplicity is, and how data distribution and initialization control the progression.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261956/cropped_page1_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The theory takes as input a network layer defined by a general activation function φ(x;u) with H units, a supervised training set (characterized by data covariance matrices Σ_yz or Σ_yZ), and a weight initialization (scale ε and structure: isotropic, low-rank, or near an invariant manifold). It produces: (1) a nested hierarchy of embedded saddles parameterized by effective width, (2) invariant manifolds connecting consecutive saddles, (3) closed-form predictions for plateau duration as a function of singular value gaps (linear case) or initialization gaps (quadratic case), and (4) a disentanglement of data-induced (low-rank) versus initialization-induced (sparse) weight structures at each stage.

## Method

The framework rests on three pillars. First, Theorem 1 proves that fixed points of a width-(H−1) network embed as fixed points (typically saddles) in a width-H network, with four construction cases depending on whether φ is general, has a zero, is homogeneous, or is linear in u. Second, Theorem 3 shows that weight constraints (equality, zero, proportionality, linear dependence) define invariant manifolds under gradient flow, on which the network behaves as an effectively narrower network. Third, Section 5 identifies two distinct timescale separation mechanisms that steer dynamics along these manifolds: for linear activations, distinct singular values of the data correlation matrix Σ_yz induce exponential growth along top singular directions (data-induced, yielding low-rank weights); for quadratic activations, the rich-get-richer dynamics of the form ṽ_i = v_i² cause the unit with the largest initialization to grow orders of magnitude faster than the rest (initialization-induced, yielding sparse weights). The interplay of the saddle hierarchy, invariant manifolds, and timescale separation produces iterative saddle-to-saddle transitions where the network recruits one or a few additional effective units per stage, explaining the progressive simplicity bias across all studied architectures.

## Results

This is a theoretical paper with no traditional benchmark table. The key quantitative findings are: (1) In linear networks, the timescale separation between singular-value directions (Theorem 4) predicts that the projection on non-dominant directions is O(ε^{1−s_{r+1}}/s_1) when the dominant projection reaches O(1), explaining rank-r weight growth during plateaus. (2) In quadratic networks (including linear self-attention), Proposition 5 shows that when one unit reaches O(1), all other units remain O(ε) almost surely, explaining sparse weight growth. (3) Empirical validation on MNIST binary classification (784-dim input, width 1000, init N(0,10⁻¹²)) confirms that the first and second singular values of the first-layer weight matrix grow in sync with the first and second loss drops, with the third singular value remaining near zero (rank ≤ 2). (4) Figure 2 validates that increasing width H shortens plateaus in linear self-attention but not in linear networks, and that setting the power-law exponent κ=0 eliminates plateaus in linear networks but not in linear self-attention.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing methods in the standard benchmarking sense; it is a theoretical framework validated through simulations. The key 'comparisons' are between architectural regimes: linear networks (timescale separation between directions, data-induced, low-rank weights) versus quadratic/attention networks (timescale separation between units, initialization-induced, sparse weights). The paper's predictions are validated against its own simulations in Figure 2, confirming that (a) width scaling benefits linear self-attention but not fully-connected linear networks, (b) data distribution (power-law exponent κ) controls plateau duration in linear networks but only partially in self-attention, and (c) large low-rank initialization still produces saddle-to-saddle dynamics even without starting near a saddle — a regime not previously observed. The framework unifies prior results from Fukumizu & Amari (2000) on fixed points and Saxe et al. (2014) on deep linear network dynamics, extending them to ReLU, convolutional, and attention architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the fixed-point hierarchy first discovered by Fukumizu & Amari (2000) for two-layer fully-connected networks to convolutional and self-attention architectures, and goes beyond fixed points to characterize invariant manifolds and the gradient flow dynamics connecting them. It unifies previously architecture-specific analyses of saddle-to-saddle dynamics (Saxe et al. 2014 for deep linear networks, Jacot et al. 2022 for linear networks, Boix-Adsera et al. 2023 for transformers) under a single framework based on permutation symmetry of units. A key departure from prior work is the explicit disentanglement of data-induced timescale separation (leading to low-rank weights) from initialization-induced timescale separation (leading to sparse weights), which had not been distinguished in the literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides concrete, testable predictions about how network width, data distribution (singular value spectrum), and initialization structure/scale affect the number and duration of plateaus, offering a principled explanation for when stage-like dynamics will or will not emerge. Open questions include whether the identified fixed points and invariant manifolds are exhaustive, how the dynamics generalize to deep networks (which layers recruit units at each stage), and whether the framework extends to recurrent architectures, reinforcement learning, and self-supervised settings where stage-like curves have also been observed. The theory also clarifies a subtlety in the lazy-vs-rich learning distinction: exponential loss curves can arise from feature learning (low-rank solutions) when initialization is near the correct invariant manifold, complicating the common heuristic that exponential decay implies lazy training.
