## Problem

Existing unlearnable example methods rely on deep neural networks as surrogate models for perturbation generation, incurring substantial computational costs (e.g., REM requires over 15 GPU hours for CIFAR-10). Moreover, the fundamental mechanism by which unlearnable perturbations degrade model generalization remained unexplained, limiting the design of more efficient and robust protection strategies.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263329/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes clean RGB training images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100 and SVHN, 224×224 for ImageNet-100) paired with their class labels, and produces perturbed images where the perturbation δ is subtracted from the original (x_i − δ_i*) under an L∞ budget of 8/255 to ensure visual imperceptibility. The perturbation is a per-sample vector of the same dimensionality as the flattened image, optimized jointly across the dataset rather than independently per sample.

## Method

PIL uses a bias-free linear classifier f_lin(x;w)=xw as the sole surrogate, first pretrained on the dataset for 30 SGD iterations to capture basic semantic structure. Perturbations are then optimized via a PGD-like procedure (30 steps, step size 8/2550) to jointly minimize a unified loss: a cross-entropy term (weight λ=0.9) that forces the linear model to predict the label directly from δ (shortcut learning), and a KL-divergence term (weight 1−λ) that pushes the prediction on x−δ toward a uniform distribution (semantic obfuscation). The key insight is that this design explicitly induces linearization in downstream deep models, which the paper demonstrates is the shared underlying mechanism of all effective unlearnable example methods. A theoretical analysis further shows that under gradient orthogonality between clean and perturbed samples, unlearnable perturbations cannot interfere with learning from remaining clean data, explaining the limited effectiveness under partial perturbation.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with ResNet-18, PIL reduces clean test accuracy from 92.11% to 12.77%, and on CIFAR-100 from 72.70% to 2.11%, approaching random-guessing levels. Across five architectures (ResNet-18/50, VGG-19, DenseNet-121, MobileNet-V2) and four datasets (SVHN, CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, ImageNet-100), PIL consistently degrades accuracy to 1–28% from 64–96%. In terms of efficiency, PIL generates perturbations for CIFAR-10 in 40.53 seconds, compared to 1,650–54,460 seconds for other surrogate-based methods (EM, SEP, TAP, REM).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PIL outperforms all surrogate-based baselines (EM, REM, TAP, NTGA) across nearly all augmentation settings on CIFAR-10; for example, under Basic augmentation PIL achieves 12.87% vs. EM (24.82%), REM (23.24%), TAP (19.11%), and NTGA (30.22%). Against surrogate-free methods, PIL dominates CUDA* (12.87% vs. 87.45% under Basic) and beats SP (12.87% vs. 23.92%) and AR (12.87% vs. 17.57%) on most augmentations. The closest competitor is SEP, which achieves slightly lower accuracy under Basic (8.94% vs. 12.87%) and MixUp (10.48% vs. 11.05%), but PIL is more consistent across the full augmentation suite and is ~660× faster (40.53s vs. 26,780s). Under JPEG compression, PIL and SP are the most robust methods, maintaining accuracy below 55% even at JPEG-40 quality, while EM, REM, TAP, and NTGA degrade to near-clean levels. In time comparison, PIL (40.53s) is 40× faster than EM, 660× faster than SEP, and 1000× faster than REM, while remaining only ~16× slower than the fastest surrogate-free method SP (2.50s).

## Relation to Prior Work

PIL departs from the dominant paradigm in unlearnable example generation, where deep neural networks (EM, REM, TAP, NTGA, SEP) serve as computationally expensive surrogates for perturbation optimization. Instead, it repurposes the shortcut learning observation (Yu et al., 2022) that perturbations are often linearly separable, and pushes this to its logical extreme by using a bias-free linear classifier as the sole surrogate. The paper also connects to the adversarial examples literature (Goodfellow et al., 2014) by proposing that the locally linear behavior of DNNs—normally exploited by FGSM at inference—is the very mechanism that unlearnable perturbations exploit at training time, unifying two previously separate threads.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The linearization hypothesis, if correct, reframes unlearnable examples from a collection of ad-hoc optimization tricks into a single mechanistic story: perturbations constrain DNNs to a lower-capacity linear regime, and PIL simply targets this mechanism directly. This opens the door to designing stronger protections by explicitly controlling the degree of induced linearity, and suggests that the field may not need ever-more-complex surrogate architectures. Key limitations remain: partial perturbation (the realistic scenario where only some data is protected) still allows models to learn from clean samples, and the gradient-orthogonality analysis, while empirically validated, relies on an assumption that may not hold for all architectures or training regimes. The FGSM-based linearity proxy, while clever, is indirect and could conflate linearity with other forms of local sensitivity.
