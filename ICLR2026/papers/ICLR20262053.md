## Problem

Autoregressive speech-text models pre-trained on interleaved tokens are substantially less compute-efficient than text LLMs because the same semantic content requires orders of magnitude more speech tokens than text tokens. This severe information-density mismatch disproportionately allocates pre-training and inference compute to speech, hindering effective cross-modal alignment and slowing performance scaling by up to three orders of magnitude compared to text.

## Input / Output

The model consumes Hubert speech tokens (501-entry codebook at 25 Hz) and BPE text tokens (Llama 2 SentencePiece tokenizer, 32K vocabulary) as interleaved sequences. Before the global transformer, speech tokens are compressed into variable-length latent patches by a patch encoder, so the autoregressive backbone operates on a mixed sequence of text tokens and speech patches rather than raw speech tokens. The patch decoder then re-expands each patch back into individual speech tokens for next-token prediction, enabling S→S, S→T, T→S, and T→T tasks from a single model.

## Method

LST introduces a three-stage architecture: a shallow patch encoder (1 layer, sliding-window self-attention + cross-attention) aggregates local speech-token spans into latent patch embeddings; a deep global transformer (25 layers, 2048 dim, RoPE) autoregressively models interleaved text tokens and speech patches; and a lightweight patch decoder (9 layers, cross-attention to patches and text) re-expands patches into individual speech tokens. The key design choice is the patching strategy: static (fixed 4-token patches), aligned (word-boundary patches from Wav2Vec2+CTC forced alignment, with silence grouped separately), mixed, or curriculum (linearly decaying probability of aligned patching over training). Curriculum patching is the strongest variant because it captures alignment benefits early while converging to simple static-only inference, reducing effective autoregressive sequence length by ~4× and yielding up to +6.5% absolute gain on speech HellaSwag under compute-controlled training.

## Results

Under compute-controlled training (same number of iterations), LST with Curriculum Patching achieves 45.5% on HellaSwag S→S and 52.2% on T→T, improving over the Base SpeechLLM baseline by +6.5 and +5.2 points respectively. On StoryCloze, LST(Curriculum) reaches 61.2% (S→S) and 71.6% (T→T), and on TopicStoryCloze 87.9% (S→S) and 96.1% (T→T). Under data-controlled settings (same speech/text token budget), LST(Curriculum) delivers the same HellaSwag gains (45.5/52.2 vs. 40.2/49.6) while reducing autoregressive sequence length by ~20%, yielding 19.7% compute savings. Gains grow with scale: at 1.8B parameters under compute-optimal training, LST reaches 39.0% (S→S) vs. 35.3% for the baseline, and at 7B under a fixed 70B-token budget, LST maintains a consistent 2.2-point speech advantage.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Base SpeechLLM baseline (a SpiritLM-style interleaved model without patching), LST(Curriculum) wins on every benchmark and modality: +6.5 on HellaSwag S→S (45.5 vs. 39.0), +5.2 on HellaSwag T→T (52.2 vs. 47.0), +2.1 on StoryCloze S→S, and +3.8 on StoryCloze T→T. The BPE SpeechLLM baseline (which applies SentencePiece subword tokenization to speech tokens, following Cuervo & Marxer 2024) is consistently worse than even the unpatched baseline on speech tasks (38.0 vs. 39.0 on HellaSwag S→S) and also underperforms LST by 7.5 points on HellaSwag S→S. Notably, LST's gains are not limited to speech: text-to-text performance also improves by 4–5 points, indicating that latent patching facilitates cross-modal knowledge transfer rather than merely compressing the speech stream. Under the data-controlled setting (Table 4), LST(Curriculum) achieves identical HellaSwag scores while processing ~20% fewer autoregressive units, confirming that the improvement stems from better information density rather than simply seeing more data. On downstream ASR (LibriSpeech), LST reaches 6.8% WER at 1k fine-tuning steps versus 140% for the baseline, and on TTS reconstruction it matches the baseline CER (14.1% clean) while reducing generation steps by ~4×.

## Relation to Prior Work

LST directly extends the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) paradigm of Pagnoni et al. (2024), which compresses byte sequences into dynamic patches for text, to the speech-text domain by applying latent patching exclusively to HubERT speech tokens. It builds on the interleaved speech-text pre-training framework established by SpiritLM (Nguyen et al., 2025) and TWIST (Hassid et al., 2023), but departs from prior alignment strategies (e.g., BPE on speech tokens explored by Cuervo & Marxer, 2024) by introducing a learned patch encoder/decoder that compresses speech into information-dense latent units rather than expanding the vocabulary. The curriculum patching strategy is a novel contribution that bridges alignment-based and static patching, eliminating the need for forced alignment at inference while retaining its training-time benefits.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By reducing the effective autoregressive sequence length for speech by ~4×, LST addresses the core compute bottleneck that Cuervo & Marxer (2024) identified as requiring up to three orders of magnitude more data for speech LLMs to match text LLMs. The finding that patching improves both speech and text performance simultaneously, with gains growing from 420M to 7B parameters, suggests this is a fundamental architectural fix rather than a narrow trick. Key limitations include the half-duplex-only setting (no full-duplex dialogue), reliance on forced alignment during training, and restriction to speech-text without extension to other modalities. Open questions include whether curriculum patching can be fully replaced by alignment-free methods, and whether the approach transfers to instruction-tuning and downstream task adaptation.
