## Problem

Existing mechanistic interpretability methods for vision models rely on the activation-magnitude assumption (larger activation implies stronger concept presence) and are predominantly input-centric, failing to capture the causal mechanisms that actually drive model outputs. These limitations produce inaccurate and unreliable interpretations, particularly when the model makes incorrect predictions, because they do not ground internal units in the model's actual decision-making process.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264539/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a frozen vision model (ResNet-50, ViT-B-16, or CLIP/ViT-B-16), extracts activation vectors from a specified residual-stream layer, and pairs them with both the input image and the model's predicted label. A trainable linear neuron encoder projects these activations into CLIP's 512-dimensional embedding space, where cosine similarity against frozen CLIP text and image embeddings yields concept descriptions, top-τ mechanism indices for a given concept, or steered predictions via scaling (×2) or zeroing (×0) of identified units. The framework also supports k-SAE features as an alternative representation unit, and produces feature-conditioned attention saliency maps for spatial verification.

## Method

MICLIP extends CLIP's symmetric InfoNCE contrastive loss to jointly align internal representation units with both input image embeddings and output label embeddings in a shared semantic space, capturing the full causal trajectory from input through internal units to model output. Only a linear neuron encoder E_n is trained (single epoch, 100k ImageNet images, Adam at lr=1e-4, batch 1024); CLIP's text and image encoders remain frozen. The dual grounding—neuron-concept loss plus neuron-image loss—replaces heuristic activation-magnitude correlations with a learned mapping, and a theoretical proof shows the linear projection guarantees consistency between training on dense activation vectors and inference on single basis vectors. For polysemantic neurons, k-SAE disentangles the activation space into sparse features that are then aligned through the same contrastive framework, enabling both concept-to-mechanism localization and mechanism-to-concept description via cosine similarity in the unified embedding space.

## Results

On the closed-set ImageNet-1k neuron description task, MICLIP achieves 100% accuracy on both ResNet-50 and ViT-B/16, surpassing CLIP-Dissect (95.60% / 95.00%) and Act-Values (99.40%). On open-vocabulary concept sets, MICLIP consistently outperforms CLIP-Dissect: e.g., on Common-20k it reaches a CLIP score of 0.8145 (ResNet-50) and 0.8138 (ViT-B/16) versus 0.7900 and 0.7563 respectively. In intervention experiments, MICLIP produces the largest and most consistent accuracy shifts: +5.32% enhancement and −17.24% removal on ResNet-50 neurons, and +5.88% / −17.70% on CLIP SAE features, confirming precise causal localization of concept-relevant mechanisms.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MICLIP outperforms CLIP-Dissect on every reported metric across all concept set sizes (Common-3k/10k/20k and ImageNet-1k) for both ResNet-50 and ViT-B/16, with the largest gaps on open-vocabulary sets (e.g., +0.057 CLIP score on Common-20k for ViT-B/16). In intervention experiments (Table 2), MICLIP achieves the strongest and most consistent accuracy shifts for both enhancement and removal across all three target models, whereas baselines like Act-Values and V-Interp produce contradictory results (e.g., Act-Values shows −8.05% on CLIP enhancement, meaning the intervention degrades rather than improves performance). Network Dissection and V-Interp are substantially weaker on intervention tasks, with near-zero or negative effects on ViT-B/16 and CLIP. On the unseen DTD texture dataset, MICLIP remains the only method that reliably enhances accuracy (+0.38% neurons, +2.00% SAE features) while CLIP-Dissect and V-Interp show negligible or contradictory effects, confirming MICLIP's superior generalization to out-of-distribution concepts.

## Relation to Prior Work

MICLIP extends CLIP's contrastive learning paradigm (Radford et al., 2021) from image-text alignment to mechanism-concept alignment, replacing the heuristic activation-magnitude correlations used by Network Dissection, CLIP-Dissect, and V-Interp with a learned linear projection into CLIP's semantic space. It departs from the predominantly input-centric interpretability paradigm by jointly grounding internal units in both input image semantics and the model's predicted output, drawing on recent output-centric insights from language model interpretability (Gur-Arieh et al., 2025) and CLIP-specific second-order effect analysis (Gandelsman et al., 2025). The framework further generalizes beyond individual neurons to sparse autoencoder features (Huben et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2025), unifying interpretation across different representation unit types within a single contrastive objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MICLIP provides a practical, unified tool that simultaneously enables interpretation (describing what a unit computes), localization (finding units relevant to a concept), and control (steering model behavior via targeted interventions), which prior methods addressed in isolation. The ablation revealing that input-only and output-only grounding are complementary—each failing where the other succeeds—highlights that faithful mechanistic interpretability requires capturing the full causal trajectory from input through internals to output. Key limitations include dependence on CLIP's semantic coverage and biases, and validation restricted to discriminative models; extending the framework to generative models and mitigating inherited CLIP biases remain important open questions.
