## Problem

SOTA quantization-aware pre-training (QAPT) methods like QuEST and LSQ use compute-efficient data types (integers) that are not information-theoretically optimal, causing them to under-utilize the model's learning capacity. Conversely, ITO methods like NF4 maximize entropy but require dequantization to full precision before computation, making them unsuitable for energy-constrained edge devices. The paper asks whether ITO quantization can outperform non-ITO methods while still producing outputs that hardware can accelerate with low-precision arithmetic.

## Input / Output

BBQ takes continuous-valued weights and activations from linear layers and produces quantized values stored as INT4 (for 3–4 bit) or MX FP4 (for 1–3 bit) data types, along with a per-channel (weights) or per-tensor (activations) learnable scaling factor γ. The dequantization is a single linear operation (γ/2^(b-1) × q), so the quantized matrices can be multiplied directly by hardware tensor cores without any intermediate full-precision decode. A zero-point hyperparameter z ∈ {0, −0.5} shifts the quantized range to be symmetric around zero, with z=0 for b≥3 (preserving exact zero) and z=−0.5 for b≤2 (preserving zero-mean activations).

## Method

BBQ's key insight is that learning is domain-agnostic: the quantizer's output need not reside in the same domain as its input. It performs ITO quantization in the input domain via three steps—(a) Hadamard Transform + RMS Normalization to Gaussianize the data, (b) Probability Integral Transform using the standard Gaussian CDF Φ to map to a uniform distribution (replacing QuEST's clip function with a smoother, entropy-maximizing operation), and (c) uniform quantization to b-bit integers—then returns the result in a compute-efficient domain (INT4/MX FP4) with a simple linear dequantization. Unlike QuEST and LSQ, which are 'same-domain' quantizers that reverse all transforms to reconstruct values in the original space, BBQ is a 'cross-domain' quantizer that does not invert the Hadamard transform or the CDF, instead relying on a learnable scaling factor γ (initialized to ζ*σ₀ ≈ 1.694σ₀ to preserve activation magnitude) to bridge the two domains. This design achieves the theoretical maximum entropy at every precision while remaining compatible with hardware-accelerated low-precision matrix multiplication.

## Results

On LLaMA-95M pre-trained on 3B C4 tokens, BBQ achieves perplexity of 25.51 (4-bit), 26.55 (3-bit), 31.34 (2-bit), and 49.22 (1-bit), with corresponding weight entropies of 3.93, 2.96, 1.97, and 1.00 bits — near the theoretical maximum for each precision. BBQ consistently outperforms QuEST by 0.86–18.56 perplexity points and LSQ by 1.09–18.36 points across precisions, with the largest gains at 1-bit (49.22 vs. 67.78 for QuEST). For larger models (200M, 300M), LSQ diverges entirely while BBQ maintains stable convergence, e.g., achieving 16.10 perplexity at 4-bit on LLaMA-300M vs. 16.26 for QuEST.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BBQ outperforms QuEST (Panferov et al., 2025) on perplexity at every precision and model size tested, with the gap widening as precision decreases: 0.86 points at 4-bit (95M) growing to 18.56 points at 1-bit (95M). BBQ also beats LSQ (Esser et al., 2020) wherever LSQ converges (e.g., 31.34 vs. 36.58 at 2-bit, 95M), and LSQ diverges entirely for LLaMA-200M and above, while BBQ remains stable. Against NormalFloat (NF4/NF3/NF2, Dettmers et al., 2023) on GPT-95M, BBQ achieves 26.7 vs. 28.5 perplexity at 4-bit and 28.61 vs. 36.16 at 3-bit, while remaining compute-efficient (NF4 requires dequantization to FP16 before matrix multiplication). BBQ also outperforms N2UQ (Liu et al., 2022) at 1-bit (53.8 vs. 29.87 perplexity on GPT-95M) and ParetoQ-SEQ (Liu et al., 2025) at 2-bit (34.36 vs. 40.07), confirming that domain-agnostic ITO quantization with compute-efficient output encoding is superior to both same-domain and non-ITO cross-domain approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

BBQ inherits the Hadamard Transform + RMS Normalization pipeline from QuEST and the learnable scaling factor from LSQ, but departs from both by replacing their clip-based rounding with a Probability Integral Transform (Gaussian CDF) that achieves Information-Theoretically Optimal quantization. Its key conceptual departure is treating the quantizer as a cross-domain feature extractor rather than a same-domain compressor/reconstructor: ITO quantization is performed in the input domain, but the output is mapped to a compute-efficient data type (INT4/MX FP4) without reversing the transform. This positions BBQ as the first method to combine ITO quantization with hardware-accelerated low-precision arithmetic, bridging the gap between information-theoretic methods like NF4 (which lack compute efficiency) and compute-efficient methods like QuEST/LSQ (which under-utilize learning capacity).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

BBQ resolves a practical dilemma for edge deployment: ITO quantization maximizes the information stored in limited bits, but existing ITO methods (NF4) require dequantization to full precision before computation, negating hardware acceleration. By returning outputs directly in INT4/MX FP4, BBQ enables 40%+ inference speedup over FP16 while achieving up to 18 perplexity points lower than QuEST at 1-bit. However, BBQ's cross-domain design means it does not bound the Euclidean distance between x and x̂, making it unsuitable for QAFT or PTQ where a full-precision reference exists. Open questions include whether replacing the Gaussian CDF with an empirical CDF approximation of HT(x) would further improve accuracy as training progresses and the distribution drifts from Gaussian.
