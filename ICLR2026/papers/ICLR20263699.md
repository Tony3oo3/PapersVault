## Problem

Diffusion-posterior-sampling (DPS) algorithms for linear inverse problems lack a proper statistical benchmark: existing evaluations rely on perceptual metrics that are ill-suited for posterior-sampling quality, or on Gaussian-mixture priors whose light tails cannot reproduce the power-law extremes common in real signals (asset returns, image statistics). Without gold-standard posterior samples from realistic priors, it is impossible to isolate algorithmic errors in the likelihood-score approximations from errors due to learned denoisers.

## Input / Output

The benchmark operates on 1D signals of dimension d=64 synthesized from discretized Lévy processes with one of six increment distributions (Gauss(0,0.25), Laplace(1), BL(0.1,1), St(1), St(2), St(3)), and applies one of four forward operators (identity, convolution, random masking, partial Fourier). For each signal–measurement pair, DPS algorithms produce N_samples=50 posterior samples; the benchmark computes the MMSE optimality gap in dB (ratio of algorithm error to gold-standard Gibbs MMSE error) and the highest-posterior-density coverage at various confidence levels. The Gibbs methods additionally supply arbitrary-precision Monte Carlo estimates of the MMSE denoiser and its Jacobian (via the conditional covariance), which can substitute for learned components to isolate algorithmic from approximation errors.

## Method

The key insight is that discretized Lévy processes with independent, stationary increments yield posteriors that factorize over increments, making them amenable to efficient Gibbs sampling via latent-variable augmentations: the Gaussian latent machine (GLM) handles Gaussian, Laplace, and Student-t increments by representing each as an infinite-component Gaussian mixture, while a custom (d+2)-block Gibbs sampler with Woodbury-Sherman-Morrison rank-one updates handles the Bernoulli-Laplace (spike-and-slab) case. These Gibbs methods serve a dual role: they generate gold-standard posterior samples for distribution-level comparison, and they sample the denoising posteriors p(X₀|Xₜ=xₜ) at each reverse-diffusion step, enabling arbitrary-precision Monte Carlo estimation of the MMSE denoiser, its Jacobian (equal to the conditional covariance up to known scaling), and other statistics needed by DPS update rules. The authors introduce a generalized DPS template (Algorithm 2) in which each reverse step first draws S samples from the denoising posterior and then applies an algorithm-specific update S that may use any statistic of those samples, unifying C-DPS (which needs the Jacobian), DiffPIR (which needs the MMSE mean), and DPnP (which needs a single posterior sample). The benchmark evaluates all three algorithms with both learned and Gibbs-based denoisers across four inverse problems, reporting MMSE optimality gap and HPD coverage, and finds that all tested DPS algorithms are substantially miscalibrated (coverage far below nominal levels).

## Results

The benchmark evaluates three DPS algorithms (C-DPS, DiffPIR, DPnP) against ℓ₁ and ℓ₂ model-based baselines via the MMSE optimality gap (in dB; lower is better, 0 = perfect) across four inverse problems and six increment distributions. DiffPIR is consistently the top DPS performer, achieving gaps as low as 0.07±0.14 dB (deconvolution, St(2)) and 0.08±0.42 dB (imputation, St(3)). For spike-and-slab (BL(0.1,1)) increments, DPS algorithms substantially outperform model-based baselines (e.g., DiffPIR 0.72 dB vs. ℓ₂ 8.61 dB for denoising), while for St(1) increments all methods degrade severely (C-DPS reaches 23.33±8.69 dB for imputation). Coverage analysis reveals that all DPS algorithms are uncalibrated, with empirical coverage far below the nominal level α.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks three DPS algorithms—C-DPS (Chung et al., ICLR 2023), DiffPIR (Zhu et al., NTIRE 2023), and DPnP (Xu & Chi, NeurIPS 2024)—against ℓ₁ and ℓ₂ model-based MAP estimators. DiffPIR is the strongest DPS method overall, winning or tying in the majority of problem–distribution combinations (e.g., 0.08±0.42 dB for imputation with St(3) vs. C-DPS 0.14±0.57 and DPnP 0.27±0.58). For spike-and-slab (BL(0.1,1)) increments, all DPS algorithms substantially outperform both model-based baselines across every operator (e.g., DiffPIR 0.24 dB vs. ℓ₂ 1.10 dB for imputation; DiffPIR 0.83 dB vs. ℓ₂ 12.22 dB for Fourier). For St(1) increments, all methods degrade sharply, but DPnP still edges out C-DPS and DiffPIR on deconvolution (7.84 vs. 10.45 and 18.30 dB) and Fourier (2.45 vs. 3.19 and 4.29 dB). Replacing the learned denoiser with arbitrary-precision Gibbs samples yields the largest gains for DPnP (up to 10.46 dB improvement for imputation with St(1)), while C-DPS and DiffPIR require hyperparameter retuning to benefit. On calibration (HPD coverage), all three DPS algorithms are uncalibrated with coverage well below the nominal level, confirming findings by Thong et al. (2024).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of DPS benchmarking initiated by Crafts & Villa (2025), Cardoso et al. (2024), and Boys et al. (2024), all of which evaluate conditioning strategies under Gaussian-mixture priors that are inherently light-tailed. The key departure is the use of discretized Lévy processes with heavy-tailed increments (Student-t, Laplace, Bernoulli-Laplace) as priors, for which the Gaussian Latent Machine (Kuric et al., 2025) and an adapted Bernoulli-Laplace Gibbs method (Bohra et al., 2023) provide parameter-free, bias-free gold-standard posterior samples. The paper also introduces a generalized DPS template (Algorithm 2) that unifies C-DPS, DiffPIR, and DPnP under a common two-stage structure, enabling the substitution of arbitrary-precision Monte Carlo estimates for learned components to isolate algorithmic errors from approximation errors—a capability absent from prior benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark fills a critical gap: prior evaluations of DPS algorithms relied on perceptual metrics (FID, LPIPS) ill-suited for statistical assessment or on toy Gaussian-mixture settings that overstate posterior quality. The systematic finding that all three tested DPS algorithms are severely miscalibrated (coverage far below nominal levels) across nearly all settings provides a concrete, reproducible target for future algorithm design. Limitations include restriction to 1D signals of dimension 64, linear forward operators, and Gaussian measurement noise; the authors note that extension to nonlinear measurements, non-Gaussian noise, and higher-dimensional signals (images) requires additional sampler development. The open question of whether flow-matching-based DPS algorithms fare better under this statistically rigorous protocol is left for future work.
