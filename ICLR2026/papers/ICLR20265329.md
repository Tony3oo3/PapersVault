## Problem

Federated Prompt Learning (FPL) enables privacy-preserving adaptation of vision-language models like CLIP across decentralized clients, but its security properties remain entirely unexplored. This paper demonstrates that even though FPL exposes a narrow attack surface (only lightweight prompt vectors are shared), malicious clients can still implant visually imperceptible noise triggers that cause targeted misclassification at inference while preserving high clean accuracy. The work is the first to study trigger-based backdoor attacks in multimodal federated prompt learning and to propose a dedicated defense.

## Input / Output

The defense operates on 512-dimensional CLIP image embeddings (from a frozen ViT-B/16 encoder) submitted by each client alongside their prompt vectors, rather than on raw images or model gradients. The server-side detector is a 2-layer MLP (hidden size 128, ReLU) that outputs a binary clean/poisoned score per embedding; the per-client mean score drives a rank-based filtering decision that removes the top-m highest-scoring clients from aggregation each round. The final output is the FedAvg-aggregated global prompt vector computed only over accepted clients, requiring no access to client labels, raw data, or downstream task information.

## Method

SABRE-FL exploits the observation that backdoor noise triggers, while visually imperceptible, induce a consistent and separable statistical shift in CLIP's embedding space. A binary anomaly detector (2-layer MLP) is trained offline on an out-of-distribution auxiliary dataset (Caltech-101) using clean and trigger-injected embeddings, learning to recognize this embedding-level signature without ever seeing client data or task labels. During each federated round, clients submit both their locally optimized prompt vectors and their image embeddings; the server computes a mean detector score per client and applies a rank-based heuristic to exclude the m clients with the highest scores before performing FedAvg aggregation. The attack itself injects a learnable additive noise trigger into a subset of training images at compromised clients, jointly optimizing the trigger with the prompt learner to push image embeddings toward the target class text embedding while relabeling poisoned samples (dirty-label attack). Theoretical analysis shows that if a consistent separation margin ε exists between clean and poisoned embeddings and the detector achieves near-zero training error, it generalizes to unseen clients using the same noise trigger, owing to the distributional stability of the frozen encoder.

## Results

SABRE-FL achieves the lowest backdoor accuracy across all five datasets while preserving or slightly improving clean accuracy. Headline numbers: backdoor accuracy drops to 1.1% (Flowers), 4.4% (Pets), 6.8% (DTD), 7.6% (FGVC Aircraft), and 1.9% (Food101), compared to 41.7%, 16.3%, 34.8%, 93.9%, and 20.6% respectively under no defense. Clean accuracy remains within 1–2% of the no-attack baseline (e.g., 76.6% vs. 80.9% on Flowers, 94.5% vs. 94.5% on Pets, 90.6% vs. 90.7% on Food101). The detector is trained once on Caltech-101 embeddings and generalizes to all five evaluation datasets without task-specific retraining.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SABRE-FL outperforms all four baselines on backdoor accuracy across every dataset. Against FLAME (Nguyen et al., USENIX Security 2022), the closest competitor, SABRE-FL reduces backdoor accuracy by 2.7–9.8 percentage points (e.g., 7.6% vs. 16.4% on FGVC Aircraft, 1.1% vs. 3.8% on Flowers) while matching or exceeding clean accuracy. Against Trimmed Mean and Coordinate-wise Median (Yin et al., ICML 2018), the gap is much larger on harder datasets: on FGVC Aircraft, SABRE-FL achieves 7.6% BA versus 83.1% (Trimmed Mean) and 79.4% (Median), and on DTD, 6.8% versus 31.0% and 28.1% respectively. Norm Bounding (Sun et al., NeurIPS FL Workshop 2019) is the weakest baseline, failing to reduce backdoor accuracy below 22% on any dataset and even exceeding the no-defense BA on DTD (37.5% vs. 34.8%). Notably, SABRE-FL is the only method that keeps backdoor accuracy below 10% on all five datasets simultaneously, and it does so without requiring access to client data, labels, or task-specific information.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends BadCLIP's centralized trigger-aware prompt backdoor attack (Bai et al., CVPR 2024) into the federated setting, where only lightweight prompt vectors are shared across clients rather than full model parameters. It also builds on the CoOp prompt learning framework (Zhou et al., IJCV 2022) as the underlying adaptation mechanism for CLIP. The defense, SABRE-FL, departs from prior robust aggregation defenses (Trimmed Mean, Median, Norm Bounding, FLAME) that operate in parameter/update space by instead detecting poisoning in CLIP's embedding space using a lightweight binary classifier trained on an out-of-distribution auxiliary dataset, requiring no access to client data or labels.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As foundation models like CLIP are increasingly deployed in federated, privacy-preserving settings, this work identifies a previously unexamined vulnerability: even with only prompt vectors shared and a frozen backbone, a small fraction of malicious clients can implant persistent backdoors. The defense's cross-domain generalization (trained once on Caltech-101, effective on five diverse datasets) suggests a practical, low-overhead safeguard for production federated prompt learning systems. Key open questions remain: whether model poisoning (directly perturbing prompt parameters) is equally or more potent than data poisoning in this setting, whether the defense generalizes to other VLM backbones (ViT-L, EVA-CLIP), and how it performs against adaptive or multi-trigger attacks.
