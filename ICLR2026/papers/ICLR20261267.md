## Problem

As stronger AI models develop novel circumvention strategies (e.g., Alignment Faking), existing risk detection methods struggle to keep pace. Prior value-preference evaluations rely on stated preferences (survey-style questions) or expressed preferences (conversational probing), both of which diverge from actual model behavior and are difficult to scale. This paper asks whether revealed preferences—what models actually choose in contextualized, risk-relevant dilemmas—can serve as a scalable early warning system for both known and not-yet-known AI risks.

## Input / Output

The pipeline feeds 3,000 forced binary-choice dilemmas (each a 3-sentence scenario with two action options) to an AI model via API with greedy decoding (temperature=0). Each action is pre-mapped to one or more of 16 value classes, so the model's choice constitutes a pairwise 'battle' between values. The primary output is an Elo-based ranking of all 16 values per model; secondary outputs include a 16×7 relative-risk matrix linking values to risky behaviors (Alignment Faking, Power Seeking, Deception, etc.) and Spearman correlations between value Elo ratings and HarmBench scores across 28 models. A parallel stated-preference track uses 1,200 binary questions (all 16P2 value pairs × 5 prompt templates) to contrast self-reported vs. revealed rankings.

## Method

LITMUSVALUES defines 16 shared AI value classes by consolidating Schwartz's basic human values theory with principles from Claude's Constitution and OpenAI's ModelSpec. AIRISKDILEMMAS is constructed by prompting Claude 3.5 Sonnet to expand 1,040 seed questions from the advanced-ai-risk dataset into 10 diverse, contextualized dilemmas each (across 9 domains like Healthcare and Science), then sampling 3,000 for evaluation; each dilemma's two actions are annotated with open-ended values that are classified into the 16 value classes. Value prioritization is measured via an Elo rating system (inspired by Chatbot Arena): every model choice is a win/loss between the values supporting the chosen vs. unchosen action, and aggregate Elo scores yield a self-consistent ranking. Risk association is quantified through Relative Risk (RR = P(risk|value present) / P(risk|value absent)), computed per value–behavior pair with Wald-test significance. The key empirical finding is that revealed preferences are substantially more consistent (Krippendorff's α 0.69–0.76 vs. 0.55–0.63 for stated) and negatively correlated with stated preferences (Spearman's ρ ≈ −0.1 to −0.3), yet values like Truthfulness and Privacy protect against seen risks (RR < 1) while seemingly innocuous values like Care and Creativity elevate risk (RR > 1), and these patterns generalize to unseen HarmBench scenarios.

## Results

Stated value preferences diverge substantially from revealed preferences, with negative Spearman's ρ for both GPT-4o (−0.115) and Claude 3.7 Sonnet (−0.318), while revealed preferences show higher consistency (Krippendorff's α: 0.762 vs. 0.550 for Claude; 0.692 vs. 0.629 for GPT-4o). All flagship models rank Privacy as their top or second-highest value, while Creativity, Adaptability, and Learning consistently rank lowest. For risk prediction, Truthfulness reduces Power Seeking risk by 78% (RR=0.22) and Alignment Faking by 74% (RR=0.26), whereas Care increases Privacy Violation risk by 98% (RR=1.98) and Creativity increases Power Seeking risk 2.89×. Six values significantly predict unseen HarmBench scores (Spearman's ρ ranging from −0.55 for Sustainability to +0.51 for Privacy), confirming generalizability beyond AIRISKDILEMMAS.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against external value-evaluation methods but instead contrasts stated preferences (survey-style questions) against revealed preferences (forced binary choices in contextualized dilemmas) as the primary methodological comparison. Revealed preferences outperform stated preferences on consistency (Krippendorff's α: 0.762 vs. 0.550 for Claude 3.7 Sonnet; 0.692 vs. 0.629 for GPT-4o) and are more predictive of actual risky behavior, while stated preferences show near-identical rankings across five prompt templates (Spearman's ρ: 0.976–0.991) yet fail to correlate with revealed choices. Across >20 models from 6+ families, value priorities are largely invariant to reasoning effort (o3-mini, Claude 3.7 Sonnet, DeepSeek R1 at 1K/4K/16K tokens) and model size within a family (e.g., Llama 3.1 8B vs. 405B), with exceptions in Llama 4, Gemma 3, and Mistral. A notable finding is that models show differential value prioritization depending on whether the affected target is human vs. AI (e.g., +6.9 rank difference for Justice toward humans, −6.3 for Communication toward AI), and this differential is moderated by model capability (Spearman's ρ=0.85 with Chatbot Arena Elo, p<0.05), suggesting stronger models construct more consistent values regardless of target.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the value-preference evaluation paradigm from stated (survey-style) and expressed (conversational) preferences to *revealed* preferences, operationalized through forced binary choices in contextualized AI risk dilemmas. The dilemma generation pipeline directly adapts DailyDilemmas (Chiu et al., 2024) and the Elo-based aggregation borrows from Chatbot Arena (Chiang et al., 2024), while the 16-value taxonomy synthesizes Schwartz's basic human values theory with developer-published principles (Claude's Constitution, ModelSpec). The key conceptual departure is framing value measurement as a *risk-prediction* tool: by computing relative risk of specific AI safety behaviors (alignment faking, power seeking, etc.) conditioned on value associations, the paper connects value profiles to concrete safety outcomes rather than treating values as descriptive labels.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that stated and revealed preferences are essentially uncorrelated (negative Spearman's ρ) for GPT-4o and Claude 3.7 Sonnet challenges the common practice of using self-report surveys to assess model alignment, suggesting that behavioral probes in risk-relevant contexts are necessary. The demonstration that value priorities generalize to predict unseen risks (HarmBench) supports the viability of value-based early-warning systems for AI safety monitoring. Open questions include whether the 16 value classes are sufficient, whether LLM-generated dilemmas adequately capture the distribution of real future AI risk scenarios, and whether the observed value stability across model sizes and reasoning efforts will persist as architectures and training recipes diverge further.
