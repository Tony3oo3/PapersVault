## Problem

Existing video understanding benchmarks rely almost exclusively on text prompts, which require users to craft complex referential language and force models to decode ambiguous textual descriptions to identify targets—especially problematic in multi-object or visually similar scenes. While visual prompts (e.g., bounding boxes, scribbles, arrows drawn on a frame) offer a more intuitive and precise interaction mechanism, no comprehensive benchmark exists to evaluate LVLMs' ability to understand video visual prompts in realistic human-model interaction settings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260951/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance consists of a sequence of sampled video frames (4–128 frames depending on model context length), a single visual prompt frame annotated with exactly one of eight prompt types (rectangle, mask contour, ellipse, triangle, scribble, point, arrow, or Set-of-Mark), and a four-option multiple-choice question. The model outputs a single option letter. The benchmark spans 980 videos (3 s to 2 h duration) and 1,172 QA pairs organized into 3 tasks and 12 dimensions, with the visual prompt frame always appended after the video frames in the input sequence.

## Method

V2P-Bench is constructed by curating 980 videos from 12 existing video datasets, reclassifying them into 20 categories, and manually annotating 1,172 QA pairs each paired with a single visual prompt frame chosen to anchor the key moment or location. Quality control involves a two-stage pipeline: (1) blind LLM filtering, where GPT-4o and Gemini-2.5-Pro are given only the text QA pairs and any sample answerable without visual context is removed, and (2) manual and rule-based review to eliminate option-length bias and shuffle answer positions. The benchmark is then used to evaluate 15 LVLMs (3 closed-source, 12 open-source) in zero-shot settings, supplemented by a 20-participant user study comparing text versus visual prompts on accuracy, completion time, and satisfaction. A key empirical finding is the prevalence of 'hack phenomena'—models guess answers rather than ground them in visual evidence, with hack ratios rising from ~8% to ~34% as videos lengthen and frame sampling becomes sparser.

## Results

On V2P-Bench (980 videos, 1,172 QA pairs), the best model o1 achieves 71.8% average accuracy, substantially below the human expert score of 88.3%. Among open-source models, InternVL3-8B leads at 61.7%, while most others fall below 60%. Spatiotemporal dimensions are the weakest: Object Direction averages only 34.4% and Action Sequence 50.0% across all models. Converting visual prompts to text prompts causes a significant drop (e.g., Gemini-2.5-Pro drops from 69.8% to 54.7%, a 15.1% decline), confirming visual prompts are more model-friendly.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the 15 evaluated LVLMs, o1 leads overall (71.8%) but is notably weak on Object Direction (23.1%) and Action Sequence (50.0%), while Gemini-2.5-Pro (69.8%) excels on OD (68.2%) but collapses on Counterfactual Inference (22.6%). GPT-4o (65.4%) is the weakest closed-source model, trailing o1 by 6.4 points. Among open-source models, InternVL3-8B (61.7%) and Qwen2.5-VL-72B (59.8%) lead, with the 72B variant consistently outperforming its 7B counterpart (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL: 59.8% vs 48.1%). Notably, LLaVA-NeXT-INST-IT-7B, fine-tuned specifically on visual prompt data (INST-IT), shows only a marginal 0.3% improvement over its base LLaVA-NeXT-7B (46.3% vs 46.0%), revealing that training on a single prompt type (SoM) causes forgetting on other types. The user study further shows visual prompts reduce task completion time from 25.2s to 18.1s and increase user satisfaction from 5.3 to 7.5 (out of 10), with 64.5% of participants preferring visual over text prompts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the visual prompting paradigm from the image domain (ViP-LLaVA, Set-of-Mark, Draw-and-Understand) to video understanding, and differentiates itself from the closest prior video-visual-prompt benchmarks (INST-IT, VideoRefer) by restricting annotations to a single representative frame per QA pair—mirroring realistic human-model interaction—while sourcing from twelve diverse video datasets spanning 3 seconds to 2 hours rather than exclusively from video instance segmentation corpora. It also builds on the broader video QA benchmark lineage (Video-MME, MVBench, EgoSchema, LVBench) by adding an instance-level, visually-grounded evaluation axis that those benchmarks lack. The paper carves new ground by framing visual prompts as a first-class interaction modality for video rather than merely an annotation convenience.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a substantial gap between SOTA models (o1 at 71.8%) and human experts (88.3%), with spatiotemporal dimensions (Object Direction, Action Sequence) being the weakest links, and reveals a pervasive 'hack phenomenon' where models default to guessing under sparse frame sampling—a finding that likely inflates scores across existing video benchmarks. The user study demonstrating that visual prompts reduce task time by ~28% and boost satisfaction by 2.4 points has direct implications for interaction design in consumer video-AI products. Key limitations include the absence of audio, single-turn MCQ-only format, and offline-only evaluation; open questions include how to train models to proactively request clarification when visual context is insufficient and whether open-ended evaluation can fully eliminate hack behaviors at scale.
