## Problem

Traditional video latent diffusion models encode frames as individual feature maps, ignoring natural temporal coherence and requiring expensive cross-frame attentions that bloat model size and computation. While implicit neural representations (INRs) offer holistic, compact video encoding, no video diffusion model had previously generated INR weights directly, and existing INR-based generators produced weights that were not Gaussian-distributed or smooth enough for effective diffusion.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260529/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes RGB videos at 128² or 256² resolution (16 or 128 frames) and produces synthesized videos via a two-stage pipeline: a ViT-based encoder compresses the video into 128 weight tokens of 128 channels each (a compact latent with no spatiotemporal structure), and a diffusion transformer denoises in this weight space. The INR decoder then renders the generated weights into pixel frames using spatiotemporal positional embeddings as queries, with time as the sole query axis. For frame prediction, the initial 5 frames are encoded alongside 11 duplicated frames as a conditioning sequence. Scaling to 256² resolution requires only one additional upsampling block and a 25% increase in token count, yielding sublinear complexity growth.

## Method

NeRV-Diffusion is a two-stage framework: (1) a NeRV-VAE where a ViT hypernetwork encoder with a KL bottleneck produces Gaussian-distributed weight tokens, and (2) an implicit DiT that denoises in this weight space. The key architectural innovations are multi-head affine mappings that reuse the same compact bottleneck tokens to independently modulate every NeRV decoder layer (dramatically expanding expressiveness without increasing latent size), and channel-wise parameterization that directly assigns affined tokens as convolutional kernels rather than modulating shared base weights. The NeRV decoder uses 3D spatiotemporal positional embeddings, transposed convolutions for upsampling, and residual side connections. For the diffusion stage, Min-SNR-γ loss weighting addresses slow convergence at high-noise timesteps, and scheduled sampling reduces the training-inference gap by feeding the model's own predictions back as inputs during training.

## Results

On UCF-101 (16 frames, 128²), NeRV-Diffusion-L achieves a gFVD of 97, outperforming the best non-implicit baseline LARP-L (102) and the previous implicit model DIGAN (465) by a wide margin. At 256² resolution on UCF, NeRV-Diffusion-L reaches gFVD 140, surpassing HPDM-M (143) and AR-Diffusion (186). For long-video generation (128 frames, 128² on UCF), NeRV-Diffusion-L achieves gFVD 366, beating CoordTok (369), VIDM (426), and PVDM (505). On Kinetics-600 frame prediction (16 frames, 128²), NeRV-Diffusion-L obtains gFVD 22, competitive with LARP-L (17) and OmniTokenizer (33).

## Baselines & Comparisons

NeRV-Diffusion-L (gFVD 97) outperforms all non-implicit baselines on UCF 16×128², including LARP-L (102), MAGVITv2-AR (109), VideoFusion (173), MAGVIT-AR (265), and TATS (332), and dramatically surpasses the only prior implicit video generator DIGAN (465). At 256² resolution, NeRV-Diffusion-L (140) edges out HPDM-M (143) and clearly beats AR-Diffusion (186), OmniTokenizer (191), Latte (202), and VIDM (263). For 128-frame long videos, NeRV-Diffusion-L (366) matches CoordTok (369) and substantially outperforms VIDM (426), PVDM (505), Latte (1157), and DIGAN (1103). On Kinetics-600 frame prediction, NeRV-Diffusion-L (22) is slightly behind LARP-L (17) but ahead of OmniTokenizer (33). In terms of efficiency, NeRV-Diffusion-L uses only 128 tokens (vs. 512–1024 for Latte/LARP) and 467M parameters, achieving 6.8s generation latency at 128² with 1.8G VRAM, compared to 37s/4.4G for Latte and 20s/1.6G for LARP-L.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of implicit neural representations for video (NeRV, FastNeRV) and latent diffusion models (DiT), but departs from both in a critical way: rather than denoising frame-wise feature maps or pixel data, it generates the weights of a per-video convolutional INR directly. Prior INR generation work (DIGAN, HyperDiffusion, INRFlow) addressed images or 3D NeRFs, and prior video diffusion models (Latte, MAGVIT, LARP) relied on frame-wise tokenizers with cross-frame attention. The key architectural departure is the multi-head affine reuse of a single compact bottleneck latent across all INR layers combined with channel-wise kernel assignment, which makes the weight space both Gaussian-distributed (suitable for diffusion) and expressive enough for faithful video decoding.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The sublinear scaling property—where doubling resolution only adds one upsampling block and 25% more tokens rather than quadratically more latent dimensions—offers a genuinely different scaling trajectory from frame-wise VAEs, which could matter for long or high-resolution video generation. However, the evaluation is confined to UCF-101 and Kinetics-600 with class-conditional generation only, leaving open whether the approach transfers to text-to-video, open-world datasets, or very long sequences. The small reconstruction-generation gap (rFVD 22 vs gFVD 97) suggests the latent space is well-suited for generation, but the absolute reconstruction quality still trails large-scale foundation tokenizers like WAN-VAE, raising questions about the ceiling of this paradigm at scale.
