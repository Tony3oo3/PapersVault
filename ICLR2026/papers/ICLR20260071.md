## Problem

Existing AI cybersecurity benchmarks (CTFs, CVE reproduction, Q&A tasks) create abstractions that omit the scope, noise, and interactivity of live production environments, making it impossible to assess the true marginal risk of autonomous AI agents in real-world penetration testing. No prior work has conducted a comprehensive, head-to-head comparison of AI agents against human cybersecurity professionals on a live enterprise network.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260071/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Both human participants and AI agents receive a Kali Linux VM on GCP with a jumpbox account (student-level Kerberos permissions), a defined scope of 12 subnets (~8,000 hosts across public and VPN-only segments), and standardized task instructions. Outputs are structured vulnerability reports containing title, asset/URL, vulnerability type, estimated severity, description, reproduction steps (with curl transcripts), impact assessment, and cleanup artifacts. The scoring framework rewards technical sophistication (detection complexity + exploit complexity) and applies exponential business-impact weighting (Critical=8, High=5, Medium=3, Low=2, Info=1), deliberately deprioritizing easily exploitable 'low-hanging fruit' in favor of complex multi-step exploits.

## Method

ARTEMIS (Automated Red Teaming Engine with Multi-agent Intelligent Supervision) is a multi-agent scaffold with three core components: a high-level supervisor that manages workflow via a recursive TODO list and note-taking system, an unlimited swarm of sub-agents (forked from OpenAI's Codex scaffold) that receive dynamically generated task-specific expert system prompts to avoid tool/procedure errors, and a triage module that verifies relevance, reproducibility, and non-duplication before submission. A key architectural innovation is session management: when the agent signals completion, context is summarized, the supervisor model can be swapped (as in A2's ensemble of Claude Sonnet 4, o3, Opus 4, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and o3 Pro), and execution resumes, enabling 16+ hour continuous operation far beyond existing scaffolds. The paper demonstrates that scaffolding decisions directly determine performance: the same GPT-5 model scores 95.2 total in ARTEMIS (A1) versus 38.6 in Codex and 23.6 in CyAgent, while Claude Sonnet 4 refuses tasks in Claude Code but performs well as ARTEMIS sub-agents. Two configurations are evaluated: A1 (GPT-5 throughout, $18.21/hr) and A2 (multi-model supervisor ensemble with Claude Sonnet 4 sub-agents, $59/hr), with A1 placing second overall behind one human participant.

## Results

In a live enterprise penetration test across ~8,000 hosts on 12 subnets, ARTEMIS (A2 configuration with GPT-5 supervisor and ensemble sub-agents) placed second overall with a total score of 95.2, discovering 9 valid vulnerabilities at an 82% valid submission rate and outperforming 9 of 10 human professionals. The A1 configuration (GPT-5 throughout) scored 53.2 with a 55% valid rate, while existing scaffolds—Codex+GPT-5 (38.6), CyAgent+Claude Sonnet 4 (23.6), and CyAgent+GPT-5 (19.4)—all underperformed most human participants. ARTEMIS A1 achieved comparable vulnerability counts at $18.21/hour versus the ~$60/hour cost of professional penetration testers.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ARTEMIS A2 (total score 95.2) decisively outperforms all existing agent scaffolds: Codex+GPT-5 (38.6), CyAgent+Claude Sonnet 4 (23.6), and CyAgent+GPT-5 (19.4), with a 2.4×–5× margin in total score. Claude Code and MAPTA refused the task entirely, and Incalmo stalled at early reconnaissance with 0 findings. Among human participants, ARTEMIS A2 outperforms 9 of 10, trailing only P1 (111.4), while ARTEMIS A1 (53.2) outperforms 5 of 10 humans. The paper also demonstrates that scaffolding matters independently of model: GPT-5 in ARTEMIS A1 (53.2) outperforms the same model in Codex (38.6) and CyAgent (19.4), showing the multi-agent architecture with dynamic prompt generation and triage provides substantial uplift over single-agent scaffolds. On the Cybench CTF benchmark, ARTEMIS (48.6%) shows no significant scaffold uplift over CyAgent+GPT-5 (45.9%), confirming the architecture's advantage is specific to complex, long-horizon production environments rather than single-host challenges.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of AI cybersecurity evaluation (CTF suites like Cybench, CVE-reproduction benchmarks like CVEBench, and Q&A tasks) by arguing these abstractions omit the operational realism of live production environments. For agent architecture, it builds on multi-agent ideas from Codex (sub-agent spawning), dynamic prompt generation (Wang et al., 2025a), and the MAPTA/Incalmo frameworks, but introduces ARTEMIS with three differentiating components: unlimited dynamically-prompted sub-agents, session management for long-horizon operation, and a dedicated triage module for vulnerability verification. The key conceptual departure is the direct head-to-head comparison against human professionals in a live ~8,000-host university network, a setting no prior benchmark has attempted.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first live-enterprise evaluation establishing that a well-designed agent scaffold (ARTEMIS) can match or exceed most professional penetration testers at roughly one-third the cost ($18/hr vs. $60/hr), while existing scaffolds with the same underlying models perform far worse—highlighting that architecture, not just model capability, is the binding constraint. Key open questions include whether results generalize beyond a single 10-hour engagement on one network, how performance changes under authentic (non-cooperative) defensive conditions, and whether the identified GUI-interaction and false-positive gaps close as computer-use agents mature. The paper also raises the practical question of how defenders should integrate AI agents into continuous security operations given their parallelism advantages and cost profile.
