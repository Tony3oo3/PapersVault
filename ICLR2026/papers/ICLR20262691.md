## Problem

Existing SNNs model membrane potential dynamics with first-order ODEs (IF/LIF), imposing a Markovian property where the current state depends only on the immediate past. Neurophysiological evidence shows real neurons exhibit long-range temporal correlations, fractal dendritic structures, and spike-frequency adaptation that integer-order models cannot capture. The integration of fractional-order dynamics—which naturally encode non-Markovian memory—into a trainable, generalizable SNN framework remained largely unexplored.

## Input / Output

Inputs are discretized into fixed-length temporal sequences: neuromorphic event streams (N-MNIST, DVS128Gesture, HarDVS, etc.) are converted to 8–16 frame representations at 128×128 resolution, graph node features with adjacency matrices use 100 timesteps with Poisson spike encoding, and static images (CIFAR-10/100, ImageNet) are similarly frame-encoded. The key structural difference from standard SNNs is that each timestep's membrane potential is computed as a weighted sum over all prior timesteps via a power-law kernel (or a truncated sliding window of width M for efficiency), rather than a single-step recurrence. Outputs are standard class labels or node labels produced by a readout head over the final spike train.

## Method

The f-SNN framework replaces the first-order derivative d/dt in IF/LIF neuron dynamics with the Caputo fractional derivative D^α (0 < α ≤ 1), discretized via the fractional Adams-Bashforth-Moulton predictor to yield a history-convolution U_k = U_0 + Σ c_m^(α)·X_{k−m} with a causal power-law kernel c_m^(α) ∝ m^(α−1). Setting α = 1 exactly recovers the standard Euler-based IF/LIF recurrence, making the framework a strict generalization. Training uses surrogate gradients (sigmoid, arctan, piecewise-linear, or Gaussian) to backpropagate through the non-differentiable Heaviside spike function. Theoretically, the paper proves three distinguishing properties: (i) membrane potential relaxation follows a Mittag-Leffler function with a power-law tail (t^(−α)) rather than exponential decay, (ii) a single f-IF neuron is irreducible to any finite ensemble of integer-order LIF neurons (requiring W→∞ for exact equivalence), and (iii) perturbation growth is sub-linear (t^α) versus linear (t) in the integer-order case, yielding tighter spike-timing robustness bounds. The framework is architecture-agnostic, plugging into CNN, Transformer, ResNet, MLP, and GNN backbones via the open-source spikeDE toolbox.

## Results

On neuromorphic event-based vision benchmarks, f-SNN (f-LIF) consistently outperforms both SpikingJelly and snnTorch LIF baselines across all five datasets and both CNN and Transformer architectures. Headline numbers include 99.48% on N-MNIST (vs. 99.27% SpikingJelly), 95.83% on DVS128Gesture with Transformer (vs. 95.14%), and 76.27% on N-Caltech101 with Transformer (vs. 72.63%). On graph node classification, f-SNN extensions of SGCN and DRSGNN achieve the best accuracy on all six datasets, with the largest gains on Computers (89.12% vs. 81.81% for SGCN-SpikingJelly) and Photo (92.49% vs. 87.72%). The paper also reports comparable energy efficiency and superior robustness under noise injection, occlusion, temporal truncation, jitter, and frame discard.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares f-SNN against two integer-order SNN toolboxes (SpikingJelly and snnTorch) and two spiking GNN architectures (SGCN and DRSGNN). f-SNN wins on every single entry in both the neuromorphic and graph tables, with no losses or ties. Against SpikingJelly LIF, the largest neuromorphic gains are +3.64% on N-Caltech101 (Transformer) and +1.40% on DVS128Gesture (CNN); against snnTorch LIF, gains are even larger (e.g., +8.68% on N-Caltech101 Transformer). On graph tasks, f-SNN-extended SGCN beats SGCN-SpikingJelly by up to +7.31% (Computers: 89.12% vs. 81.81%) and f-SNN-extended DRSGNN beats DRSGNN-SpikingJelly by up to +12.22% (Computers: 88.77% vs. 76.55%). The snnTorch baselines are notably weaker on graph tasks (e.g., SGCN-snnTorch drops to 21.55% on ogbn-arxiv vs. 51.10% for f-SNN), suggesting implementation sensitivity. The paper emphasizes that the comparison is fair: only the charging phase (IF/LIF → f-IF/f-LIF) is swapped, keeping all other architecture and hyperparameters identical.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes the standard IF/LIF neuron dynamics used in SNNs by replacing the first-order ODE with a Caputo fractional-order ODE, building directly on the f-LIF biological neuron models of Teka et al. (2014) and Deng et al. (2022) that explained spike-frequency adaptation. It departs from prior fractional-neural-network work (fractional GNNs by Kang et al., fractional diffusion by Nobis et al.) by embedding fractional dynamics inside the neuron as a computational engine rather than using fractional operators as solvers or optimizers. The paper also distinguishes itself from fractional gradient descent methods (Khan et al., Shin et al.) which modify the weight-update rule rather than the neuron's membrane-potential dynamics. The key positioning is that this is the first generalizable, end-to-end trainable f-SNN framework compatible with modern deep architectures (CNN, Transformer, ResNet), subsuming all integer-order SNNs as the α=1 special case.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The f-SNN framework provides a mathematically principled way to inject long-range temporal memory into SNNs, with theoretical guarantees (irreducibility to finite LIF ensembles, sub-linear perturbation growth) that justify the design choice beyond empirical tuning. The open-source spikeDE toolbox lowers the barrier for the SNN community to experiment with fractional dynamics across architectures. However, the paper explicitly does not target SOTA, and the accuracy gains (typically 1–3%) are modest; the computational overhead of the full-memory convolution (mitigated by short-memory truncation or FFT) and the question of how α interacts with neuromorphic hardware constraints remain open. A key future direction is whether the power-law memory advantage translates to genuinely harder long-horizon tasks where integer-order SNNs demonstrably fail.
