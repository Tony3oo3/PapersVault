## Problem

Standard reinforcement learning for reasoning models (RLVR) relies on binary correctness rewards that treat confident guesses and well-justified answers identically, inadvertently degrading calibration and increasing hallucination rates. This paper asks whether reasoning models can be trained to simultaneously optimize for task accuracy and calibrated confidence estimation, and whether the reasoning chains themselves can improve calibration.

## Input / Output

The model receives a text question, optionally accompanied by context paragraphs (e.g., 8 passages in HotPotQA-Modified where 0–2 are relevant). It produces a structured four-part output: a chain-of-thought reasoning trace in <think> tags, a final answer in <answer> tags, an explicit uncertainty analysis in <analysis> tags that identifies specific ambiguities or potential errors in the solution, and a scalar verbalized confidence score in [0,1] in <confidence> tags. Correctness is evaluated via exact string match (QA) or math-verify (math), while calibration is measured via Brier score, ECE, and AUROC.

## Method

RLCR augments the standard binary correctness reward with a Brier score term, yielding R_RLCR(y, q, y*) = 1_{y≡y*} − (q − 1_{y≡y*})², and trains the model via GRPO to maximize this combined reward. The key theoretical result (Theorem 1) proves that because the Brier score is a bounded proper scoring rule, the expected reward is jointly maximized when the model outputs the most likely correct answer and reports its true success probability as confidence—so calibration comes at no cost to accuracy. The paper further shows that replacing the Brier term with the unbounded log-loss breaks this guarantee, as a toy experiment demonstrates the model collapsing to degenerate zero-confidence abstentions. At inference, the verbalized confidence enables confidence-weighted majority vote and best-of-N selection without external reward models, and ensembling multiple uncertainty analyses for a fixed answer further reduces residual calibration noise.

## Results

On HotpotQA (in-domain), RLCR achieves 62.1% accuracy with ECE of 0.03, compared to RLVR's 63.0% accuracy but ECE of 0.37, showing that the calibration reward costs <1% accuracy while reducing calibration error by 12×. On out-of-distribution evaluation (averaged over 6 datasets), RLCR improves ECE from 0.46 (RLVR) to 0.21 while also improving accuracy from 53.9% to 56.2%. On Math benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH-500, Big-Math), RLCR reaches 72.7% accuracy with ECE 0.10, matching RLVR's 72.9% accuracy while reducing ECE from 0.26 to 0.10. The SFT+RLCR variant further improves in-domain ECE to 0.08 but suffers OOD accuracy drop to 43.8% (partially recoverable via prompt adjustment to 49.8%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

RLCR outperforms all RL-calibration baselines on both accuracy and calibration. Calibration RL (Full-Sequence) from Stangel et al. (2025) and Xu et al. (2024) collapses to 0% accuracy when applied to the full sequence (degenerate reward hacking), and even the restricted Analysis+Confidence variant (62.0% acc, ECE 0.08 in-domain) is outperformed by RLCR on AUROC (0.53 vs 0.69) and OOD ECE (0.25 vs 0.21). Abstention-RL (Wei et al. 2025; Mohamadi et al. 2025; Song et al. 2025) achieves comparable accuracy (62.1%) but substantially worse calibration (ECE 0.31 in-domain, 0.35 OOD) because it only teaches a binary abstain/answer decision rather than fine-grained confidence. Against the post-hoc classifier baselines (RLVR + BCE/Brier Classifier), RLCR matches or exceeds their in-domain calibration (ECE 0.03 vs 0.07/0.09) while being a single-model solution that avoids the cost of training and running a second 7B classifier. On OOD, RLCR's advantage over classifiers is most pronounced: ECE 0.21 vs 0.24 (BCE) and 0.33 (Brier), with the gap widening because classifiers do not generalize their calibration signal to new domains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the standard RLVR (Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards) paradigm for training reasoning models by augmenting the binary correctness reward with a Brier-score calibration term, drawing on the statistical decision theory of proper scoring rules (Gneiting & Raftery, 2007; Brier, 1950). It departs from prior RL-calibration work (Xu et al., 2024; Stangel et al., 2025) that applied proper scoring rules as standalone rewards on non-reasoning tasks, which can reward-hack by incentivizing deliberately incorrect low-confidence answers. The key conceptual departure is the proof that only *bounded* proper scoring rules (like Brier) preserve the correctness incentive when combined with a binary accuracy reward, while unbounded rules (like log-loss) can invert the incentive structure—a distinction absent from prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that a single-line modification to the RLVR reward (adding a Brier term) provably and empirically improves calibration without sacrificing accuracy is a low-cost, high-impact fix for a well-documented failure mode of reasoning model training. The bounded-vs-unbounded scoring rule distinction provides a clear theoretical criterion for choosing calibration rewards, and the toy experiment demonstrating log-loss collapse to degenerate abstention is a useful cautionary result. Open questions remain: OOD calibration error is still high in absolute terms, models can assign high confidence to multiple contradictory answers, and the SFT warmup variant suffers catastrophic forgetting that a single prompt line partially resolves—suggesting the interaction between calibration training and domain-specific RL is not yet fully understood.
