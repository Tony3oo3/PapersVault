## Problem

Most neural VRP solvers assume symmetric Euclidean distances and rely on coordinate inputs, making them inapplicable to real-world routing where only pairwise asymmetric distance matrices (e.g., one-way streets, directional traffic) are available. The core challenge is encoding both static asymmetry (directional discrepancies in the input matrix) and dynamic asymmetry (layer-dependent interaction differences in attention) into compact, generalizable node-level representations without the geometric scaffold that coordinates provide in Euclidean settings.

## Input / Output

The primary input is an n×n asymmetric distance matrix (z-score normalized), optionally augmented with node features such as demand and time windows, and optionally node coordinates for data augmentation. The model outputs a complete routing solution: a set of vehicle routes that visit each customer exactly once, start and end at a depot, and satisfy task-specific constraints (capacity, time windows, backhauls, duration limits). The SVD decomposition of the distance matrix is computed per-instance at inference time, and the constructive decoder sequentially selects the next node via greedy or sampled decoding until the tour is complete.

## Method

RADAR augments a MatNet-style constructive solver with two asymmetry-aware components. For static asymmetry, it computes a truncated SVD of the distance matrix D ≈ U_k Σ_k V_k^T and forms node embeddings by concatenating the scaled left singular vectors (U_k√Σ_k, encoding departure/source roles) with the scaled right singular vectors (V_k√Σ_k, encoding arrival/destination roles), yielding embeddings that provably reconstruct D through a bilinear form XW₁(XW₂)ᵀ ≈ D. For dynamic asymmetry, it replaces row-wise softmax in encoder attention with Sinkhorn normalization, which iteratively normalizes both rows and columns of the attention score matrix to produce doubly stochastic weights, ensuring each attention score A_{i,j} reflects the full neighborhood structure of both nodes i and j rather than only i's local context. The architecture uses 5 encoder layers with multi-head attention (8 heads, 256-dim embeddings), a feed-forward block, and a constructive decoder; training uses POMO-style reinforcement learning with 2100 epochs on 100-node instances, and the model generalizes zero-shot to 1000-node instances.

## Results

On synthetic ATSP, RADAR achieves gaps of 0.72%, 1.01%, 2.13%, and 4.13% at sizes 100, 200, 500, and 1000 respectively, substantially outperforming the strongest neural baseline ReLD (1.64%, 3.75%, 13.39%, 31.67%). On ACVRP, RADAR reaches 1.64%, −0.75%, 3.39%, and 0.96% gaps, surpassing even LKH-1000 at size 200. On real-world RRNCO benchmarks (ATSP, ACVRP, ACVRPTW), RADAR achieves 0.74%, 2.61%, and 2.71% in-distribution gaps, beating RRNCO by 1.06, 0.84, and 1.22 percentage points respectively. In the 16-variant multi-task setting, RADAR attains the lowest average gap of 1.33% versus 1.99% for RF-NN.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RADAR consistently outperforms all 11 learning-based baselines across every problem size and variant. Against the strongest constructive solver ReLD, RADAR reduces the ATSP gap from 31.67% to 4.13% at size 1000 (a 27.5 pp improvement) and from 13.39% to 2.13% at size 500. MatNet and its variants (One-hot, Random, Demand) degrade sharply beyond size 200 (gaps exceeding 20–100%), while RADAR maintains gaps under 5% even at size 1000. UNICO (MatPOENet-8x) and ICAM suffer the worst generalization collapse (gaps of 249.91% and 84.69% at ATSP1000 respectively), whereas RADAR stays within 4.13%. On real-world RRNCO benchmarks, RADAR beats RRNCO by 1.06 pp (ATSP), 0.84 pp (ACVRP), and 1.22 pp (ACVRPTW) in in-distribution gap, and by 1.31, 0.94, and 1.58 pp out-of-distribution. In the 16-variant multi-task setting, RADAR achieves 1.33% average gap versus 1.99% for RF-NN and 2.47% for RF, confirming its advantage across diverse constraint combinations. Traditional solvers (LKH-1000) remain slightly better on ATSP at all sizes, but RADAR surpasses LKH-1000 on ACVRP200 and approaches LKH-10000 on ACVRP1000 with orders-of-magnitude lower inference time (11.6 min vs. 36.25 h).

## Relation to Prior Work

RADAR extends the MatNet lineage (Kwon et al., 2021), which first introduced matrix-encoded dual embeddings for asymmetric VRPs, by replacing its one-hot row/column initialization with a truncated SVD of the distance matrix that provably reconstructs the asymmetric cost structure. It also repurposes Sinkhorn normalization—a classical tool from optimal transport—as a drop-in replacement for row-wise softmax in the encoder attention, addressing what the authors term 'dynamic asymmetry' in layer-wise interactions. The multi-task and real-world evaluations build on RouteFinder (Berto et al., 2025) and RRNCO (Son et al., 2026), respectively, positioning RADAR as a modular upgrade to existing constructive solver architectures rather than a new end-to-end framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Real-world routing is inherently asymmetric (one-way streets, directional traffic, time-dependent costs), yet most neural VRP solvers assume symmetric Euclidean geometry, creating a significant deployment gap. RADAR's SVD-based initialization provides a theoretically grounded, size-agnostic way to encode directional structure at the node level, and the Sinkhorn attention mechanism captures bidirectional neighborhood context that row-wise softmax structurally ignores. Open questions include whether the approach extends to problems where edge features are not a single distance matrix (e.g., multi-criteria costs), how it interacts with improvement-based or search-based solvers, and whether the SVD step remains tractable beyond ~1000 nodes in dynamic or streaming settings.
