## Problem

Prior work on model collapse has studied what happens when a single model trains recursively on its own generated outputs, but has overlooked the more realistic scenario where multiple heterogeneous models ingest each other's synthetic outputs through shared internet data. This paper asks: what are the long-term evolutionary dynamics—both beneficial and harmful—when K models interact through data-mediated training on one another's generated content?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264822/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes K entities, each with a private real dataset (e.g., SciQ, GSM8K, ARC) and a shared public real dataset (BookCorpus), plus synthetic data generated by all K models at each generation, with mixing parameters α (fraction of new synthetic data per update) and β (relative weight of public vs. private data) controlling composition. The theoretical output is exact recursive formulas for the mean and covariance of each model's parameter estimate, yielding closed-form MSE, variance bounds, and convergence conditions. Experimentally, the system produces fine-tuned OPT-350m, Llama 3.2 1B, and Llama 3.2 3B models evaluated via token-level cross-entropy loss on own and cross-model tasks, plus cosine similarity of SentenceTransformer embeddings to measure homogenization.

## Method

The paper formalizes an iterative interactive training pipeline where at each generation, all K models produce synthetic data that is pooled and used (mixed with retained real data) to update every model, following the 'accumulate-and-subsample' paradigm. The theoretical analysis reduces to linear regression and derives a discrete-time linear dynamical system (analogous to a Kalman filter) for the stacked parameter vector, yielding exact recursive expressions for conditional mean and covariance (Theorem 1). A key structural insight is that the interaction matrix Q introduces nonzero off-diagonal blocks in the mean, meaning private data from one entity implicitly benefits all others through synthetic data sharing—something absent in single-model collapse analyses. Convergence is characterized via the spectral radius of Q, with Lemma 1 providing sufficient conditions (S proportional to a mixture of private and public Gram matrices) ensuring bounded variance for all α < 1. Experiments validate the theory by fine-tuning K=2 and K=3 model systems over 8–15 generations, confirming that α=0.5, β=0.5 achieves optimal cross-task performance while α=1.0 (pure synthetic) degrades original-task accuracy.

## Results

Across K=2 interacting Llama 3.2 (1B) models at β=0.5 over 15 generations, the α=0.5 setting (mixed real and synthetic data) yields the best cross-entropy loss on both own-task and cross-task evaluation: Model 1 on Task 2 drops from 2.0→1.4 and Model 2 on Task 1 drops from 3.7→3.0, while own-task losses remain stable (2.8→2.9 and 1.2→1.3). In contrast, α=1.0 (synthetic-only) degrades own-task performance (e.g., Model 2 on Task 2: 1.2→1.9) and α=0 (real-data-only) shows no cross-task improvement (Model 1 on Task 2: 2.0→2.5). The theoretical analysis (Theorem 3) confirms that α=β=0.5 maximizes relative efficiency toward the oracle MSE for all models in the system.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external methods but instead contrasts three data-mixing regimes that serve as natural baselines for recursive training. The α=0 setting (retraining on the same real data each generation) acts as a no-interaction baseline: models remain stable on their own tasks but show no cross-task transfer (e.g., Model 2 on Task 1 stays at 3.7→4.2 for Llama 1B). The α=1.0 setting corresponds to the 'replace' scenario studied by Shumailov et al. (2024), where models train exclusively on generated outputs; here own-task performance degrades (Model 2 on Task 2: 1.2→1.9) while cross-task loss improves modestly. The proposed α=0.5 'accumulate-and-subsample' setting dominates both baselines: it preserves own-task accuracy while achieving the largest cross-task gains (Model 1 on Task 2: 2.0→1.4 vs. 2.0→2.5 for α=0 and 2.0→1.8 for α=1.0). Cosine similarity of output embeddings (Table 3) further confirms that α=0.5 produces the most task-differentiated representations (PCA clusters remain separated), whereas α=0 and α=1.0 lead to homogenization or generic outputs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the model collapse literature from single-model recursive training (Shumailov et al., 2024; Hataya et al., 2023) to a multi-model interactive setting where K entities train on each other's generated outputs. It adopts the accumulate-and-subsample data mixing paradigm from Kazdan et al. (2025) and the linear regression analytical framework from Gerstgrasser et al. (2024) and Dey & Donoho (2024), but introduces a new interaction structure via the projection matrix Π that couples entities through shared synthetic data. The authors explicitly distinguish their setting from transfer learning, emphasizing that the data-mediated interaction is unintentional and occurs through public internet content rather than deliberate weight or data sharing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a practically inevitable scenario—future LLMs will inevitably ingest other models' outputs from the internet—that prior model collapse work overlooked, and provides the first formal characterization of its dynamics. The dual finding (cross-model knowledge transfer benefits coexisting with homogenization risk) has direct implications for AI safety and content diversity, particularly as AI-generated content reaches 40% of platforms like Quora. Key limitations include the restriction to linear regression theory and small-scale LLM experiments (OPT-350m, Llama 1B/3B), leaving open whether these dynamics persist at frontier model scale, in other modalities, or under more realistic data mixes containing both real and synthetic content.
