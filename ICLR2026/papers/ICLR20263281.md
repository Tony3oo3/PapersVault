## Problem

Credal sets (convex sets of probability distributions) provide a principled representation of epistemic uncertainty, but existing construction methods rely on training ensembles or Bayesian pipelines that are computationally prohibitive for large pretrained models such as foundation models, CLIP, and TabPFN. This paper asks whether credal predictions can be obtained efficiently from a single trained model without any retraining or ensembling.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263281/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method requires only the logits of a single trained classifier (no gradients, no parameter access, no retraining) plus a training set to compute the relative-likelihood budget. It produces a box credal set: for each of K classes, a probability interval [p_k^lower, p_k^upper] whose Cartesian product (intersected with the simplex) defines the credal set. Computationally, this reduces to solving 2K small convex optimization problems per value of the plausibility threshold α, making it applicable to API-gated or frozen models where only forward-pass logits are available.

## Method

The core mechanism is 'decalibration': starting from the maximum-likelihood estimator's logits, the method adds a class-specific scalar bias t·e_k to each class logit and applies softmax, systematically pushing predicted probabilities away from the MLE while enforcing a global relative-likelihood constraint Δℓ(c) ≥ log α. The key theoretical result is that this logit-shift family induces a concave change in training log-likelihood, making the feasible set F(α) convex (and compact on the identifiability hyperplane 1ᵀc = 0). In the one-dimensional class-specific slice, the feasible set reduces to a simple interval [t_k^-, t_k^+], and since p_k(x; t·e_k) is strictly monotone in t, the lower and upper probability bounds are obtained by evaluating p_k at the two interval endpoints, which solve two small convex programs (or equivalently, bisection on Δℓ_k(t) = log α). This yields nested credal sets as α increases, with clear semantics: 'probabilities reachable without sacrificing more than a (1−α) fraction of training likelihood.' The approach is model-agnostic, requires no retraining, and is orders of magnitude faster than ensemble-based baselines.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, EffCre Pareto-dominates CreRL in the high-coverage region and Pareto-dominates CreBNN, CreWra, and CreNet across the full coverage-efficiency trade-off curve. On CHAOSNLI, EffCre matches CreRL in the high-coverage region and CreEns in the low-coverage region, while uniquely spanning both regions (unlike baselines restricted to one). For OOD detection, EffCre achieves competitive AUROC with zero additional training cost, whereas ensemble baselines (CreWra, CreEns, CreNet, CreBNN) require ~10 fully trained models and substantially more GPU hours. The method further enables credal prediction on TabPFN (tabular foundation model) and CLIP/SigLIP/SigLIP-2 (vision-language models), where all ensemble-based baselines are infeasible.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CreRL (Löhr et al., 2025), EffCre Pareto-dominates in the high-coverage region on CIFAR-10 and performs comparably on CHAOSNLI, while being orders of magnitude faster since it requires no ensemble training. Against CreWra (Wang et al., 2025a), CreBNN (Caprio et al., 2024a), and CreNet (Wang et al., 2025b), EffCre Pareto-dominates on CIFAR-10 and dominates CreBNN (α=0.95) on CHAOSNLI; however, CreWra and CreNet are restricted to the low-coverage region, limiting their practical utility. Against CreEns (Nguyen et al., 2025), EffCre performs similarly in the low-coverage region on CHAOSNLI but extends to high coverage as well. On OOD detection, all ensemble baselines achieve slightly higher AUROC than EffCre, but at the cost of training 10+ models (hours of GPU time), whereas EffCre adds zero training overhead. The decisive advantage of EffCre is that it is the only method applicable to frozen foundation models (TabPFN, CLIP, SigLIP, SigLIP-2, BiomedCLIP), where retraining or ensembling is computationally infeasible or disallowed.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends the relative-likelihood framework for credal prediction established by Löhr et al. (2025) (CreRL), which defines plausibility via likelihood-ratio α-cuts but requires training ensembles to populate the feasible region. The key departure is replacing ensemble-based exploration with a post-hoc logit perturbation ('decalibration') that, starting from a single MLE, systematically pushes class probabilities away from the maximum-likelihood fit while respecting a global relative-likelihood budget. This draws an explicit analogy to calibration (adjusting probabilities toward correctness) but inverts it: decalibration explores how far probabilities can be pushed while remaining data-supported. The method is positioned against a family of credal baselines (CreWra, CreEns, CreBNN, CreNet) that all rely on multi-model training, and against lightweight single-pass uncertainty methods (evidential deep learning, DDU) that do not produce set-valued predictions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: by requiring only logits from a single forward pass plus 2K scalar convex optimizations per class, the method enables credal prediction for foundation models (TabPFN, CLIP, SigLIP, SigLIP-2, BiomedCLIP) where retraining, fine-tuning, or ensembling is infeasible or disallowed. The main limitation is the one-logit (class-specific) restriction used for tractability; the fully coupled K-dimensional case leaves lower bounds as a non-convex optimization, and the paper acknowledges this as open. For open-vocabulary and multimodal models, additional challenges arise because the label set is chosen at inference time, meaning uncertainty should also reflect label selection and prompt choice—designing credal formalisms for this setting is identified as important future work.
