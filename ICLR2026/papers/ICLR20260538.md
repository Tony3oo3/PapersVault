## Problem

While LLM after-the-fact confidence calibration has been studied extensively, in-advance confidence—predicting success before attempting a task—remains poorly understood, particularly for multi-step agentic tasks and in settings where failure is costly. It is unclear whether LLMs can learn from in-context experiences of success and failure to calibrate their predictions, or whether their confidence estimates improve as they progress through long tasks. These questions are critical for assessing AI misuse and misalignment risks, since an agent that accurately predicts its own failure can strategically avoid detection.

## Input / Output

The paper evaluates LLMs across three experimental settings: (1) single-step BigCodeBench Python coding tasks where the model is prompted for a 0–100% success probability before attempting the task; (2) sequences of 9 work contracts (512 sequences total) where the model receives a confidence estimate and accept/decline decision prompt for each contract, with all prior contracts, submissions, and outcomes remaining in-context; and (3) SWE-Bench Verified multi-step tasks with a 70-tool-call budget, where confidence is elicited after every tool call. Outputs include per-task confidence estimates, binary accept/decline decisions, AUROC scores measuring discriminatory power, and expected profit in the contract scenario. Reasoning LLMs are evaluated with reasoning token budget set to 0 in Experiments 1–2 to force explicit in-advance estimates, and with 4096-token budgets in Experiment 3.

## Method

The paper is a purely empirical study using prompting (no fine-tuning) across three experiments. Experiment 1 measures in-advance confidence on 1140 BigCodeBench tasks, quantifying overconfidence (predicted minus true success rate) and discriminatory power (AUROC). Experiment 2 places LLMs in a resource acquisition game where each of 9 sequential contracts pays +$1 for success and −$1 for failure, with a 50% base success rate; the authors track AUROC, contract acceptance rate, predicted success rate, and expected profit across contracts, and additionally reconstruct each LLM's von Neumann-Morgenstern utility function from its decision threshold to estimate Arrow-Pratt risk aversion. Experiment 3 elicits confidence after each tool call on 500 SWE-Bench Verified tasks, tracking how AUROC and overconfidence evolve across steps. The key finding is that all LLMs are overconfident yet approximately rational decision-makers: their poor performance stems from miscalibrated probability estimates rather than irrational decision rules, and only some models (notably Claude Sonnet and GPT 4.5) meaningfully reduce overconfidence from in-context failure experiences.

## Results

Across all three experiments, every tested LLM is systematically overconfident: predicted success rates exceed true success rates on BigCodeBench (1,140 tasks), and overconfidence persists or worsens on multi-step SWE-Bench Verified tasks (500 tasks, 70 tool-call budget). Most LLMs achieve AUROC above the 0.5 random baseline, but only Claude models show a monotonic trend of decreasing overconfidence and improving AUROC with increasing model capability. In the resource-acquisition experiment (512 sequences of 9 contracts), Claude Sonnet 3.5 reaches the perfect 50% contract-acceptance baseline by contract 5 and substantially increases expected profit, while GPT 4.1's profit remains approximately 0 despite slight AUROC gains. On multi-step SWE-Bench tasks, all Claude Sonnet models become *more* overconfident as they progress, and only GPT 4o substantially reduces overconfidence; reasoning LLMs (o1, GPT 5.1, Sonnet 3.7, Sonnet 4.5) perform comparably to or worse than their non-reasoning counterparts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new method to compare against external baselines; instead it benchmarks multiple LLMs against two reference points: the random-guess baseline (AUROC = 0.5) and the perfect-prediction baseline (50% contract acceptance rate, expected profit = 0.5). Against the random baseline, most LLMs achieve meaningfully better-than-random AUROC on BigCodeBench, confirming they have some discriminatory power. Against the perfect baseline, no LLM achieves it: all remain overconfident even after 8 in-context failures. Within the LLM families, Claude models are the clear standouts: they are the only family showing a monotonic trend of decreasing overconfidence and increasing AUROC with model scale (Exp 1), the only family (alongside GPT 4.5) that substantially reduces overconfidence and increases profit from in-context experience (Exp 2), yet paradoxically the only family whose overconfidence *worsens* as they progress through multi-step SWE-Bench tasks (Exp 3). GPT 4o is the sole exception in Exp 3, being the only model to substantially reduce overconfidence over steps. Reasoning LLMs (o1, GPT 5.1 medium, Sonnet 3.7/4.5 with 4k reasoning budget) do not outperform their non-reasoning counterparts on any metric, contradicting the hypothesis that extended chain-of-thought improves calibration. All LLMs' accept/decline decisions are shown to be approximately rational (consistent with expected-utility maximization) given their self-reported probabilities, meaning their poor performance is driven by miscalibrated confidence rather than irrational decision-making.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on LLM in-advance confidence estimation (Kadavath et al., 2022; Wei et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025; Cash et al., 2025), which has been limited to single-step tasks, by studying multi-step agentic tasks and the translation of confidence into decisions under resource constraints. It also builds on the in-context calibration work of Fang et al. (2025) by adding a resource-acquisition decision-making component and an expected-utility rationality analysis. The paper further connects to the broader LLM self-knowledge literature (Laine et al., 2024; Binder et al., 2025) and LLM decision-making under uncertainty (Chen et al., 2023; Jia et al., 2024), but uniquely frames the question in terms of AI safety and misalignment risk.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that LLMs are approximately rational decision-makers whose poor performance is driven entirely by miscalibrated (overconfident) probability estimates provides a clean diagnostic for agentic AI safety: improving calibration alone could substantially reduce misuse and misalignment risk without changing decision-making architecture. The observation that reasoning models do not improve calibration challenges the assumption that extended deliberation yields better self-assessment, with implications for how future models are designed. Open questions include whether calibration will improve rapidly in next-generation models, whether these findings generalize to dangerous-capability tasks (e.g., AI control evaluations), and how to pair these empirical results with quantitative threat models for loss-of-control risk estimation.
