## Problem

Visual instruction tuning (VIT) datasets have scaled in volume but individual sample informativeness has been largely overlooked. Existing VIT questions are predominantly low-complexity (k≤2 atomic capabilities), under-utilizing the rich visual information in each image. The paper asks whether explicitly scaling the complexity of each training sample—by combining multiple atomic visual capabilities into a single question—can yield a more data-efficient tuning recipe than blind scaling or subset selection.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264290/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images sampled from LLAVA-665K and produces synthetic multi-capability question-answer pairs where each question naturally integrates k_gen ∈ {1,2,3} atomic visual capabilities (e.g., object recognition + spatial relationship + color attribution). Answers are constrained to be extremely concise (single word or short phrase), and the final training set mixes 32K compositional tuning samples with a 5% random subset of LLAVA-665K VIT data (≈33K) to preserve instruction-following and response-formatting capability. The trained model (LLaVA-v1.5-7B-LoRA) takes an RGB image and a text question as input and produces a text answer as output.

## Method

COMPACT introduces a four-step synthetic data recipe that controls per-sample complexity via a k-value metric (the number of atomic visual capabilities required to answer a question). Step 1 samples k_gen capabilities from a predefined taxonomy of 10 atomic capabilities (grouped into attribution, recognition, and relation); Step 2 prompts Gemini-2.0-Flash to generate a single integrated question that naturally combines all sampled capabilities without conjunctions; Step 3 verifies quality via confidence scoring, word-overlap deduplication, and a second VLM call confirming the question truly requires all specified capabilities; Step 4 assembles the final dataset by mixing compositional tuning data with 5% of LLAVA-665K for response formatting. The key insight is that higher-k samples force the model to extract more visual information per image, making each training example more information-dense. Ablations show that a range of k-values (1,2,3) outperforms any single k, and that matching LLAVA-665K's original low-k distribution recovers only ~half of COMPACT's gains, confirming that complexity scaling—not just synthetic regeneration—drives the improvement.

## Results

COMPACT achieves 100.18% relative performance across eight multimodal benchmarks using only 65K training samples (10% of the full LLAVA-665K VIT dataset), matching or exceeding the full 665K baseline (100.00%). The largest gains appear on complex compositional benchmarks: MM-Vet (31.74 vs. 29.22, +8.6%), InfoVQA (23.68 vs. 20.80, +13.8%), and MMStar (36.13 vs. 35.11, +2.9%). COMPACT also uses 46.88% fewer tokens per sample than the equivalent LLaVA subset, translating to faster training. With an open-source generator (Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct), COMPACT still achieves 98.31% relative performance, confirming the recipe's robustness.

## Baselines & Comparisons

COMPACT (65K samples) achieves 100.18% relative performance, surpassing the full LLAVA-665K baseline (100.00%) with only 10% of the data. Against the strongest data-reduction baseline, ICONS (97.47%), COMPACT gains 2.71 percentage points in relative performance, with particularly large margins on InfoVQA (+2.68) and MM-Vet (+0.51). COMPACT also outperforms all other 65K-budget baselines: Random (95.38%), EL2N (97.09%), Perplexity (96.09%), SemDeDup (93.31%), D2-Pruning (97.13%), Self-Sup (96.04%), and Self-Filter (90.51%). Notably, COMPACT exceeds the full 665K baseline on MM-Vet (31.74 vs. 29.22), MMStar (36.13 vs. 35.11), and InfoVQA (23.68 vs. 20.80), suggesting that compositional complexity in training data benefits complex reasoning tasks more than sheer data volume. The only benchmark where COMPACT trails the full baseline is MME (1379.94 vs. 1478.48), which includes non-visual cognition subtasks outside the scope of visual compositional tuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the two dominant strategies in VIT data curation—upscaling (Cambrian-10M, Eagle 2) and subset selection (ICONS, EL2N, D2-Pruning, etc.)—by introducing a third axis: controlling the *complexity* of each training sample through compositional question generation. It repurposes the concept of atomic visual capabilities, previously studied mainly in evaluation contexts (MMComposition, geometric reasoning benchmarks), as a generative tool for training data synthesis. The closest prior work is ICONS, which selects informative samples from existing VIT data, but COMPACT instead synthesizes new, higher-complexity questions for the same images, shifting the problem from selection to controlled generation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a 10% data budget with compositional complexity control not only matches but *outperforms* full-scale VIT on complex benchmarks (MM-Vet +8.6%, MMStar +2.9%) challenges the prevailing 'quantity over quality' scaling assumption for visual reasoning. The k-value framework provides a principled, interpretable metric for data complexity that could guide future data curation beyond ad-hoc heuristics. Key limitations include reliance on a closed-source generator (Gemini-2.0-Flash), evaluation on a single architecture (LLaVA-v1.5-7B-LoRA), and scope restricted to vision-centric capabilities (modest gains on knowledge-intensive tasks like MMMU). Open questions include whether the approach generalizes across architectures, how image content complexity interacts with question complexity, and whether compositional tuning benefits reinforcement learning or other training paradigms.
