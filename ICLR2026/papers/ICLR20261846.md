## Problem

Existing neural scaling laws characterize only end-of-training performance as a function of data or model size, leaving the full training dynamics unexplained. This paper asks whether the implicit bias of gradient descent—known to drive weights toward maximum-margin solutions in the infinite-norm limit—also induces predictable scaling behavior at every intermediate stage of training, not just at convergence.

## Input / Output

The method takes standard image classification datasets (MNIST at 28×28, CIFAR-10/100 at 32×32) and trains CNN, ResNet, or ViT architectures on random subsets of varying size P, tracking the spectral complexity norm at each epoch. The key unusual aspect is that learning curves are plotted against the evolving norm λ(t) rather than training time, revealing power-law structure that is invisible in conventional ε(t) plots. The output consists of two dynamical exponents (γ₁, γ₂), a universal master curve obtained by rescaling all learning curves by their optimal norm and minimum error, and a predicted end-of-training scaling exponent γ = γ₁γ₂.

## Method

The core insight is that for logistic loss, the loss depends only on the product λΔ where Δ is linear in the weight norm, making a fixed-norm analysis with varying λ equivalent to a free-norm analysis with varying norm. For perceptrons, the authors use the replica method to solve the fixed-norm problem analytically, showing that the free-norm gradient descent trajectory is qualitatively captured by the sequence of fixed-norm optima, and derive two scaling laws: an early-training power law ε̂_gen = k₁λ^(−γ₁) + q₁ independent of dataset size, and a scaling of the optimal norm λ_opt = k₂α^γ₂ + q₂. For deep networks, the spectral complexity norm (Bartlett et al., 2017) serves as the analogous norm measure, and the same two scaling laws are observed empirically across CNN, ResNet, and ViT on three datasets. Combining the two laws yields ε(P) = k₁(k₂P^γ₂ + q₂)^(−γ₁) + q₁, which in the intermediate regime reduces to the known power law ε ~ P^(−γ₁γ₂), recovering the Hestness et al. scaling. The spectral complexity norm is shown to be the correct choice—alternative norms (L1, L2, group 2,1, spectral product) reproduce the qualitative picture but yield incompatible predicted versus measured exponents.

## Results

The paper identifies two dynamical scaling laws — an early-training power law ε̂_gen = k₁λ^{-γ₁} + q₁ and an optimal-norm scaling λ_opt = k₂α^{γ₂} + q₂ — whose combination γ_pred = γ₁γ₂ recovers the end-of-training scaling exponent. Across all nine model–dataset combinations (CNN/ResNet/ViT × MNIST/CIFAR-10/CIFAR-100), γ_pred is compatible with the directly measured exponent γ_meas within fitting uncertainty (σ = 0.03–0.09). The predicted exponents range from 0.14 (ViT/CIFAR-100) to 0.60 (CNN/MNIST), with the closest agreement at CNN/CIFAR-100 (γ_pred = 0.16, γ_meas = 0.16, σ = 0.03). For the analytically solvable fixed-norm perceptron, the theory gives γ₁ = −1/2 and γ₂ = 1, recovering the known ε ∼ α^{-1/2} scaling exactly.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against competing methods in the traditional sense; instead, its central comparison is internal: whether the product of two independently fitted dynamical exponents (γ_pred = γ₁γ₂) matches the directly measured end-of-training exponent (γ_meas). In all nine settings the two are compatible within errors, validating the dynamical scaling-law framework. A second key comparison is the choice of norm: when the spectral complexity norm (Bartlett et al., 2017) is replaced by L1, Frobenius (L2), group (2,1), or spectral-product norms, the qualitative two-regime structure persists but γ_pred becomes incompatible with γ_meas (e.g., for CNN/CIFAR-10, γ_pred drops to 0.08–0.12 under alternative norms versus 0.28 with spectral complexity), identifying spectral complexity as the correct generalization of the L2 norm used in the perceptron analysis. Robustness checks show that moderate weight decay (1e-5 to 1e-3) preserves the qualitative picture and keeps γ_pred compatible with γ_meas, and switching from Adam to SGD on CNNs changes the individual dynamical exponents (γ₁^SGD > γ₁^Adam, γ₂^SGD < γ₂^Adam) while leaving their product — and hence the end-of-training scaling — unchanged.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate research threads: neural scaling laws (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hestness et al., 2017), which characterize end-of-training power-law behavior, and the implicit bias of gradient descent (Soudry et al., 2018; Lyu and Li, 2020), which describes how optimization favors maximum-margin solutions. The key departure is showing that the norm growth driven by implicit bias acts as a unifying variable throughout training, producing two dynamical scaling laws whose combination recovers the known end-of-training scaling. The analytical backbone draws on the fixed-norm perceptron solution of Aubin et al. (2020) and the teacher-student framework of Gardner and Derrida (1988), while the deep-network analysis relies on the spectral complexity norm of Bartlett et al. (2017) as the natural generalization of the L2 norm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a mechanistic explanation for why scaling laws exist: norm growth is the hidden variable that controls the learning trajectory, and the early-training regime's independence from dataset size offers a practical route to predicting large-dataset behavior from small-dataset experiments. However, the analysis is limited to image classification on small datasets (MNIST, CIFAR-10/100) with fixed architecture sizes, and the connection between perceptron theory and deep networks remains qualitative rather than quantitative. Extending the framework to joint scaling with model width/depth, to language models, and to provide rigorous guarantees for deep architectures are the most important open questions for the field.
