## Problem

Morphological traits are critical for understanding how organisms interact with their environment, yet extracting them from biological images remains a slow, expert-driven process that limits large-scale ecological studies. A major bottleneck is the absence of high-quality datasets linking organismal images to trait-level annotations, as manual measurement of even simple characters takes minutes per specimen and protocols vary across taxa. This paper addresses the gap by demonstrating that sparse autoencoders over foundation-model features can serve as interpretable part-detectors for automated, spatially grounded trait annotation.

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes insect specimen images (from BIOSCAN-5M) paired with species-level taxonomic labels as input; no pixel-level or part-level annotations are required. It produces natural-language morphological trait descriptions structured as body-part: attribute pairs (e.g., 'Leg: Thin, elongated, light brown, segmented'), each spatially grounded to a specific region of the image. The resulting BIOSCAN-TRAITS dataset contains 80.8K trait samples across 19.1K unique images (736 species, 417 genera), averaging 4.2 traits per image, with each trait tied to a bounding box derived from SAE activation masks.

## Method

The core mechanism trains a ReLU sparse autoencoder on DINOv2-base features extracted from the penultimate ViT layer, producing monosemantic latent units whose activations map to tight, spatially coherent anatomical regions (e.g., wings, antennae, legs). A species-contrastive ranking then identifies salient traits by retaining only latents whose normalized activation frequency is significantly higher within a focal species than across its genus (f_s(z) > f_g(z)), filtering out shared, non-discriminative features. High-ranked latents are converted into bounding boxes overlaid on the original image, and three such annotated patches per species are jointly prompted to Qwen2.5-VL-72B with a lightweight template to generate concise trait descriptions. The multi-image consensus constraint forces the MLLM to report only traits visually consistent across specimens, suppressing idiosyncratic details and background leakage. This modular design—SAE for spatial grounding and taxonomic focus, MLLM for verbalization—reduces the language model's task from 'describe the whole scene' to 'describe this part,' sharply improving precision over both MLLM-only and Grad-CAM baselines.

## Results

The pipeline produces BIOSCAN-TRAITS, a dataset of 80.8K morphological trait annotations across 19.1K insect images (736 species, 417 genera), averaging 4.2 traits per image. In human evaluation, the SAE-guided MLLM+SAE pipeline (3 images) achieves a mean-normalized rating of 3.91/5, substantially outperforming the MLLM-only baseline (3.15/5). Downstream, fine-tuning BioCLIP on BIOSCAN-TRAITS improves zero-shot species classification on the in-the-wild Insects benchmark from 34.8% to 39.9%, and BioCLIP2 from 55.3% to 56.23%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary quantitative baseline is the MLLM-only approach (prompting Qwen2.5-VL-72B with specimen images without spatial grounding). MLLM+SAE with 3 images achieves a mean-normalized rating of 3.91 versus 3.15 for MLLM-only with 3 images, a 0.76-point improvement, demonstrating that SAE-derived spatial patches substantially reduce hallucination and background leakage. Qualitatively, the paper compares against Grad-CAM (Selvaraju et al., 2017) for saliency localization: Grad-CAM produces diffuse heatmaps that blend multiple anatomical cues and lack species-level disentanglement, whereas the SAE approach isolates monosemantic, species-contrastive neurons tied to fine-grained traits (e.g., specific leg segments or wing veins). For downstream utility, trait-level fine-tuning of BioCLIP on BIOSCAN-TRAITS yields a +5.1-point gain over the pretrained baseline (34.8% → 39.9%) on the Insects benchmark, and BioCLIP2 improves from 55.3% to 56.23%, confirming that trait-level supervision provides complementary signal beyond species-level labels alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the monosemantic sparse autoencoder (SAE) framework—originally developed for interpreting language model internals (Bricken et al., 2023; Templeton et al., 2024) and recently adapted to vision-language embeddings (Pach et al., 2025; Stevens et al., 2025)—into the domain of biological vision, repurposing SAE latents as spatially grounded part detectors rather than interpretability tools. The key conceptual departure is the species-contrastive ranking mechanism, which leverages taxonomic labels to filter SAE units that fire strongly for a focal species but remain silent for congeners, a filtering strategy absent from prior SAE-for-vision work. On the downstream side, the paper positions itself within fine-grained visual recognition and morphological trait extraction literature, departing from prior automated approaches (e.g., triplet networks for phenotypic embeddings, segmentation-based herbarium tools) by producing natural-language trait descriptions rather than numerical measurements or pixel masks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine bottleneck in trait-based ecology: millions of digitized specimens lack standardized trait annotations, and manual extraction by experts is prohibitively slow. BIOSCAN-TRAITS (80K annotations over 19K images) is one of the first large-scale, interpretable image-to-trait datasets, and the demonstrated downstream gains in zero-shot species classification (e.g., 34.8→39.9 for BioCLIP) provide initial evidence that trait-level supervision improves generalization. Key limitations include the assumption that DINOv2 features already encode morphology-relevant signals, the absence of ground-truth trait annotations for systematic evaluation, and the risk that some SAE latents conflate co-occurring traits. Open questions include whether the pipeline transfers cleanly to non-insect taxa (plants, birds, fungi), whether SAE latents truly correspond to biologically meaningful traits or merely spatially coherent regions, and whether the modest downstream gains scale with larger trait corpora.
