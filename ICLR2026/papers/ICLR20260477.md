## Problem

Second-order optimization methods with approximate Hessians (e.g., Fisher or Gauss-Newton matrices) lack strong global convergence guarantees, with existing theory suggesting they may converge as slowly as gradient descent. This paper addresses the gap by developing a unified convergence framework that matches state-of-the-art exact-Newton rates while accommodating Hessian inexactness, bridging the geometry of the objective with conditions on the approximation error.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260477/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method requires only first-order oracle access (gradient evaluations) plus a Hessian approximation matrix H(x) that is positive semidefinite and can have simple structure (e.g., low-rank or diagonal), making linear solves cheap via Woodbury-Sherman-Morrison or conjugate gradient. The step-size γ_k is determined by the Gradient-Normalized Smoothness, which automatically adapts to the problem's smoothness class and the Hessian approximation quality without requiring the user to specify either. The output is an ε-optimal point with a provable iteration bound that decomposes into the exact-Newton complexity plus additive terms depending on the Hessian error constants C₁, C₂ and approximation degree β.

## Method

The core contribution is a new local quantity, Gradient-Normalized Smoothness γ(x), defined as the maximal radius of a Euclidean ball around x within which the linear approximation ∇f(x+h) ≈ ∇f(x) + H(x)h is relatively small across all feasible directions. The algorithm performs a gradient-regularized Newton step x_{k+1} = x_k − (H_k + ‖∇f(x_k)‖_*/γ_k · B)^{-1} ∇f(x_k), where γ_k = γ(x_k) serves as a universal step-size that simultaneously encodes the problem's smoothness degree α and the Hessian approximation error. Under a structural assumption that γ(x) is bounded below by a harmonic mean of monomials in the gradient norm, the theory recovers state-of-the-art rates for Hölder-continuous Hessians, Hölder third derivatives, Quasi-Self-Concordant and Generalized Self-Concordant functions, and (L₀,L₁)-smooth classes. A key insight is that when the smoothness degree α exceeds the Hessian approximation degree β and C₁ ≈ 0, the objective's smoothness dominates the approximation error, yielding the same global rate as exact Newton—demonstrated for logistic regression with Fisher approximation and nonlinear equations with Gauss-Newton/Fisher combinations.

## Results

The paper establishes global iteration complexity bounds for a gradient-regularized Newton method with approximate Hessians across multiple problem classes (Table 1). For the exact Hessian case, it recovers state-of-the-art rates: O(M₀D²/ε) for bounded Hessian variation, O(M₁/²D³/²/ε¹/²) for Lipschitz Hessian, O(M₁/³D⁴/³/ε¹/³) for Lipschitz third derivative, and Õ(M₁D) for Quasi-Self-Concordant functions. The key new result is that for inexact Hessians satisfying ‖∇²f(x)−H(x)‖ ≤ C₁ + C₂‖∇f(x)‖¹⁻ᵝ, the total complexity becomes C_NEWTON(ε) + C₁D²/ε + C₂D¹⁺ᵝ/εᵝ, and when α≥β and C₁≈0, the inexact method achieves the same rate as full Newton. Numerical experiments on LogSumExp, Nonlinear Equations, Rosenbrock, and Chebyshev problems confirm that the Fisher/Gauss-Newton approximations match exact Newton convergence while reducing wall-clock time substantially.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its gradient-regularized Newton method with approximate Hessians against four baselines: (1) Exact Newton with full Hessian, (2) plain Gradient Descent (H≡0), (3) Gauss-Newton preconditioned gradient method (B=AᵀA), and (4) the Super-Universal Newton Method from Doikov et al. (2024a). On the LogSumExp problem (a9a, ionosphere datasets), the inexact Hessian (Weighted Gauss-Newton, Eq. 19) achieves convergence nearly identical to Exact Newton, while significantly outperforming both Gradient Descent and Gauss-Newton preconditioned gradient. On Nonlinear Equations with linear operators (MNIST, covtype, mushrooms), the Fisher-type approximation (Eq. 21) matches Exact Newton convergence but runs substantially faster in wall-clock time due to the Woodbury-Sherman-Morrison formula. On non-convex Rosenbrock and Chebyshev polynomial problems, the inexact Hessian method again matches exact Newton while gradient methods require thousands of additional iterations. The method's adaptive step-size γ_k consistently produces larger (less conservative) values than the alternative adaptive search from Doikov et al. (2024a), confirming tighter theoretical bounds.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the gradient-regularized Newton framework of Polyak (2009) and the super-universal Newton method of Doikov et al. (2024a) by relaxing the exact Hessian requirement to allow approximate second-order information. The key conceptual departure is the introduction of Gradient-Normalized Smoothness, a problem-class-free local modulus that unifies the treatment of objective smoothness and Hessian approximation error into a single quantity γ(x). This framework subsumes and extends prior results for Hölder-continuous Hessians, Hölder-continuous third derivatives, Quasi-Self-Concordant functions (Doikov, 2023), and newly covers Generalized Self-Concordant functions (Sun & Tran-Dinh, 2018) and (L₀,L₁)-smooth functions under a single theoretical umbrella.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first unified global convergence theory for second-order methods with approximate Hessians that recovers state-of-the-art exact Newton rates, challenging the prevailing view that Hessian inexactness necessarily degrades convergence to gradient-descent levels. The practical implication is significant: widely-used Fisher and Gauss-Newton approximations in machine learning (logistic regression, softmax, nonlinear least squares) now come with provable global rates matching full Newton, at near first-order computational cost. Open questions include developing accelerated variants that simultaneously adapt to smoothness and Hessian inexactness, and comparing with recently proposed anisotropic and ℓ-smoothness frameworks. The theory is currently limited to unconstrained problems and requires the Hessian approximation to satisfy a specific polynomial bound on the gradient norm.
