## Problem

Existing zero-shot detectors for LLM-generated text rely on proxy models to approximate token-level probability distributions, making them sensitive to proxy model choice, computationally expensive, and vulnerable to black-box generators. Supervised classifiers, while accurate, operate as uninterpretable black boxes. Both paradigms model detection at a single feature level (typically token probability), missing the systematic distributional differences that emerge at syntactic and semantic levels of language.

## Input / Output

The method takes a raw text sequence as input and transforms it into four parallel feature sequences: word tokens, part-of-speech tags, dependency relations, and context-target embedding pairs from a pretrained encoder. It produces a binary AI/human label via an XGBoost classifier that consumes a 4-dimensional vector of length-normalized log-likelihood ratios (one per linguistic level), along with per-token LLR scores at each level that enable interpretable attribution heatmaps. The n-gram databases and semantic embedding stores are built offline from a small corpus (~100k words suffices), so inference requires no proxy model calls.

## Method

HLD-Detector approximates the conditional probability distributions of machine-generated and human-written text at three hierarchical levels—word, syntactic (POS and dependency), and semantic—using n-gram models under a Markov assumption with context length k (optimal k=3–5). At the word and syntactic levels, it applies additively smoothed n-gram counts with back-off; at the semantic level, it replaces discrete n-grams with a non-parametric KDE-inspired estimator that retrieves M nearest context neighbors from an offline embedding database and computes conditional probabilities via softmax-weighted cosine similarities. The log-likelihood ratio between the two source distributions is computed token-by-token at each level, length-normalized, and the resulting 4-dimensional feature vector is fed to a lightweight XGBoost classifier trained on a small balanced corpus. This design avoids online proxy-model inference entirely, requires only ~100k words of offline data, and achieves SOTA AUROC (99.12% multi-LLM, 99.60% multi-domain) while remaining robust to paraphrase attacks and generalizable across domains and model generations.

## Results

On the DetectRL benchmark, HLD achieves a mean AUROC of 99.12% across four LLMs (GPT-3.5, Claude, PaLM-2, Llama-2) and 99.60% across four domains (Arxiv, XSum, Writing, Review), setting new state-of-the-art in both multi-LLM and multi-domain detection. Under adversarial attacks (paraphrase, perturbation, data mixing), HLD maintains a 97.85% average AUROC, outperforming the next-best method (DPIC) by 4.82 points. In cross-domain and cross-model generalization, HLD achieves 86.61% and 92.46% average AUROC respectively, again leading all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HLD decisively outperforms all six zero-shot baselines (LRR, DetectGPT, Binoculars, Fast-DetectGPT, DNA-GPT, Lastde++), which average only 34–86% AUROC and exhibit severe instability across source models (e.g., DetectGPT drops to 26.72% on PaLM-2). Among supervised methods, HLD surpasses RoBERTa-base by 0.88 points on multi-LLM average (99.12% vs. 98.24%) and by 0.66 points on multi-domain average (99.60% vs. 98.94%), while beating DPIC by 2.37 and 2.06 points respectively. HLD is the only detector that exceeds 97% AUROC on every individual LLM and every individual domain. Under paraphrase attacks—the most challenging adversarial scenario—HLD retains 97.04% AUROC versus 76.84% for DPIC and 73.00% for RoBERTa-base, a gap of over 20 points. In cross-model generalization (trained on GPT-3.5, tested on Claude/PaLM-2/Llama-2), HLD achieves 92.46% average AUROC, outperforming RoBERTa-base (91.93%) and DPIC (88.61%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from both dominant paradigms in AI-text detection: zero-shot methods (DetectGPT, Fast-DetectGPT, Binoculars) that rely on expensive proxy LLMs to approximate token-level probability distributions, and supervised classifiers (RoBERTa, DPIC, RAIDAR) that operate as uninterpretable black boxes. HLD carves new ground by replacing proxy-model inference with n-gram statistics under a Markov assumption, and by elevating detection from a single token-level feature to a hierarchical framework spanning word, syntactic (POS/dependency), and semantic (KDE-based embedding) distributions unified under a Bayesian log-likelihood ratio formulation. The semantic level draws conceptually on Dipper's retrieval-based defense, while the overall Bayesian decomposition echoes the likelihood-ratio testing tradition in statistical signal detection.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: HLD achieves SOTA detection (99.12% avg AUROC on multi-LLM, 99.60% on multi-domain) while running in 5.24 seconds per document versus 3,264 seconds for RAIDAR, making it far more deployable in real-world settings. Its robustness to paraphrase attacks (97.04% AUROC) and cross-model generalization (92.46% when trained on GPT-3.5 and tested on Claude/PaLM-2/Llama-2) address two of the most critical failure modes of prior detectors. Open questions remain: the n-gram Markov approximation may struggle with very long-range dependencies, the method still requires an offline corpus per domain, and the paper acknowledges that more advanced adversarial strategies could potentially circumvent the hierarchical signals. Whether the approach extends naturally to multimodal or code-generation detection is unexplored.
