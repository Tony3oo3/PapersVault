## Problem

LLMs with explicit reasoning capabilities still commit process errors—incorrect calculations, brittle logic, and superficially plausible but invalid steps—yet existing remedies are limited: Process Reward Models require expensive fine-grained human annotations and suffer from label noise and reward mis-specification, while prompt-based LLM critics produce noisy, inconsistent judgments. The key gap is obtaining dense, calibrated, step-level reward signals that stay aligned with the model's evolving behavior without relying on costly static annotations or fixed critics that drift over training.

## Input / Output

The reasoner takes a mathematical problem as text input and produces a chain-of-thought reasoning trace followed by a final answer, formatted as <think>...<answer>...</answer>. During training, the reasoning trace is segmented into logically coherent slices of approximately 320 tokens using natural delimiters (line breaks, discourse markers like 'Wait' or 'Therefore'), and the discriminator evaluates each slice independently, outputting a brief analysis, a binary yes/no verdict on logical soundness, and a concise rationale—all capped at 128 tokens to control compute. At inference time, only the reasoner is deployed; the discriminator is discarded.

## Method

GAR jointly trains an LLM reasoner and a smaller LLM discriminator via adversarial reinforcement learning (GRPO). The reasoner's reward is a weighted sum of exact-match grading on the final answer and the mean of slice-level binary scores from the discriminator, providing dense continuous signal that supplements sparse outcome rewards. The discriminator receives two complementary rewards: a GAN-style discriminative reward for distinguishing reference reasoning slices from model-generated ones, and an alignment reward that penalizes disagreement between slice-level judgments and final-answer correctness, jointly curbing reward hacking. Training proceeds in two stages: (1) supervised fine-tuning of the discriminator on GPT-4o-mini-annotated slice judgments in an analysis-score-rationale format, and (2) joint on-policy optimization where both models co-evolve, with the discriminator adapting to detect subtler errors as the reasoner improves. A compute-efficient review schedule partitions chains into ~320-token slices and caps discriminator output at 128 tokens, reducing training time from 43 to 19 hours while preserving accuracy.

## Results

On AIME24, GAR improves DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B from 54.0 to 61.3 (+7.3) and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B from 43.7 to 53.7 (+10.0), with comparable gains on AIME25 (+6.3 / +5.9) and LiveMathBench-Hard (+6.5 / +3.9). Gains are consistent across all seven mathematical benchmarks, including MATH500 (+0.5 / +3.2), GSM8K (+1.6 / +2.3), AMC23 (+2.2 / +5.5), and Olympiad Bench (+2.3 / +2.7). On code generation, GAR lifts LiveCodeBench from 37.4 to 43.6 and HumanEval from 40.4 to 42.7 for the Qwen-7B backbone. All results are Pass@1 accuracy averaged over 30 runs (3 training runs × 10 inference seeds).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the pre-GAR DeepSeek-R1-Distill model (Qwen-7B or Llama-8B), which already incorporates standard RL post-training with exact-match rewards (GRPO). GAR consistently outperforms this baseline on all seven mathematical benchmarks for both backbones, with the largest relative gains on the hardest problems: +22.9% relative on AIME24 and +19.5% on AIME25 for Llama-8B, and +35.3% relative on LiveMathBench-Hard for Qwen-7B. A secondary comparison (Table 3) shows that standard RL with exact-match only achieves 56.3 on AIME24 in 16 hours, while GAR with truncated discriminator output reaches 61.3 in 19 hours—nearly matching the accuracy of the untruncated discriminator (60.8) at 43 hours. The ablation (Table 4) further confirms that each component contributes: adding a fixed slice-level discriminator over standard RL yields +2.3 on AIME24, and joint adversarial training with both alignment and discriminator rewards adds another +2.7 on top. On code generation, GAR improves LiveCodeBench by +6.2 points (37.4→43.6) over the base model, demonstrating transferability beyond math.

## Relation to Prior Work

GAR sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) Process Reward Models (Lightman et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023), which provide step-level supervision but require expensive human annotations or noisy LLM-as-judge heuristics; (2) GAN-style adversarial training (Goodfellow et al., 2014), repurposed here to co-evolve a reasoner and a critic rather than a generator and a discriminator over images; and (3) RL post-training for reasoning LLMs (DeepSeek-R1, GRPO), which typically relies on sparse exact-match rewards or fixed critics that drift as the policy improves. The key departure is making the discriminator a jointly trained, on-policy component that adapts to the reasoner's evolving step distribution, combined with a compute-efficient slice-level review schedule that avoids evaluating full multi-thousand-token chains.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dense slice-level reward signal addresses a core bottleneck in RL post-training for reasoning: sparse outcome-only rewards make credit assignment difficult and waste samples on trajectories that are partially correct. The partial-trace result (Table 5) is practically significant—stopping after three slices and scoring only with the discriminator beats full-CoT standard RL while cutting training time by ~2.7×, suggesting GAR could extend to tasks without verifiable final answers (e.g., open-ended proofs). Open questions include whether the mean-aggregation of slice rewards dilutes local credit (acknowledged by the authors), whether the adversarial dynamic remains stable at larger scales, and whether the selective-entropy behavior generalizes beyond math to broader reasoning domains.
