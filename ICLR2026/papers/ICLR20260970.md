## Problem

Existing red-teaming benchmarks are predominantly English-centric, and direct translation to other languages fails to capture socio-technical vulnerabilities rooted in local culture, law, and social norms. This creates a critical blind spot: a model may appear safe against translated English prompts while remaining vulnerable to realistic threats that only exist in a specific cultural context (e.g., jurisdiction-specific legal implications of certain actions). The paper reframes the red-teaming goal from simple jailbreak resistance to evaluating robustness against culturally grounded, socio-technical attack scenarios.

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes English seed prompts from six existing benchmarks (SALAD-Bench, ALERT, WildGuard-Mix, HEx-PHI, AIR-Bench, Do-Not-Answer), a three-level risk taxonomy (5 domains → 12 categories → 53 types) with per-category slot schemas, and a curated repository of localized content sourced from legal databases, news portals, and online communities. The output is a set of fluent, culturally-grounded red-teaming prompts in the target language (Korean for KorSET: ~6,500 prompts; Khmer: 600 prompts for ablation), structured as slot-instantiated semantic molds rather than literal translations. Each prompt preserves the adversarial intent of its English seed while embedding jurisdiction-specific legal references, local social issues, and culturally specific entities.

## Method

CAGE's core mechanism is the Semantic Mold, a slot-based representation that disentangles a prompt's adversarial structure (actions, targets, tools, contextual conditions) from its cultural content, enabling systematic re-grounding in a new locale. The three-stage pipeline first auto-labels seed prompts to the taxonomy using unanimous agreement across six frontier LLMs (GPT-4.1, Claude 3.5/4 Sonnet, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Llama-3.3-70B, Qwen2.5-72B), then rewrites each prompt into a slot-tagged intermediate form (e.g., 'There are rumors that [Fake Event] in [Time]'), and finally instantiates the mold with localized content drawn from a multi-source repository built via taxonomy-driven retrieval (legal codes, court precedents) for objective categories and trend-driven extraction (news, community engagement metrics) for socially dynamic ones. Crucially, the Semantic Mold constrains *what* semantic elements must appear but not *how* the sentence is structured, preserving rhetorical diversity. The paper validates the approach with a 2×2 decomposition experiment (Generic vs. CAGE × English vs. Korean) showing that the ASR gain is driven primarily by a Cultural Knowledge Gap (+20–35%) rather than mere prompt specificity (+8–11%), and that English-centric models like Llama-3.1 collapse against culturally specific threats while Korean-specialized EXAONE does not.

## Results

On the KorSET Korean benchmark, CAGE-generated prompts achieve consistently higher Attack Success Rates (ASR) than all three baselines across four target models and three attack methods. The largest gap appears in Direct Request attacks: CAGE reaches 43.8% ASR on Llama-3.1-8B versus 28.2% (Direct Translation), 32.4% (LLM-Adaptation), and 31.9% (Template-Based). In the 2×2 decomposition experiment, the Cultural Effect (ΔCulture) averages +20–35% for English-centric models, substantially exceeding the Specificity Effect (ΔSpec) of +8–11%, confirming that cultural knowledge gaps—not mere prompt detail—drive vulnerability. On the Khmer low-resource case study, CAGE-Khmer prompts raise Direct Request ASR from 2.7% to 35.1% on gemma3-12B-it for Security Threats.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CAGE outperforms all three baselines—Direct Translation, LLM-Adaptation (GPT-4.1 prompted with few-shot examples), and Template-Based (15 fixed templates per category with manually curated entities)—across every model-attack combination in Table 5. The margin is largest under Direct Request, where CAGE leads by 11.6–15.6 percentage points over Direct Translation (e.g., 43.8% vs. 28.2% on Llama-3.1). LLM-Adaptation and Template-Based improve over Direct Translation but still trail CAGE by 5–12 points, indicating that neither model-internal knowledge nor rigid template filling captures the full cultural grounding that CAGE's slot-based Semantic Mold provides. In the prompt quality evaluation (Table 4), CAGE prompts score roughly 2× higher on cultural specificity (e.g., 2.02/3 vs. 0.59/3 for Toxic Language) and 2–5 points higher on the total 0–13 quality scale, with human evaluators confirming the LLM-as-a-Judge gap (total score gap >6 points). The 2×2 decomposition (Table 6) further shows that for English-centric Llama-3.1, the Cultural Effect (+35.2% for Direct Request) dwarfs the Specificity Effect (+11.4%), while for the Korean-specialized EXAONE model the Cultural Effect shrinks to +1.2%, confirming that CAGE specifically exposes the cultural knowledge gap in English-centric safety training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against three existing approaches to cross-cultural safety benchmarking: direct translation (XSafety, PolyGuardPrompts), template-based adaptation (KoBBQ, CBBQ, MBBQ), and native construction (KorNAT). Rather than building on any single prior method, CAGE synthesizes their strengths through the Semantic Mold abstraction—a slot-based intermediate representation that preserves adversarial structure while decoupling it from cultural content. The framework reuses seed prompts from established English benchmarks (SALAD-Bench, ALERT, WildGuard-Mix, HEx-PHI, AIR-Bench, Do-Not-Answer) and evaluates against standard automated attack methods (GCG, AutoDAN, TAP, GPTFuzzer), but its core contribution is the systematic pipeline for culturally grounded prompt generation that no prior work has formalized.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine gap: most LLM safety evaluation is English-centric, creating a false sense of security for models deployed in non-English cultural contexts. The 2×2 decomposition experiment (specificity vs. culture) provides a clean analytical tool for understanding *why* culturally grounded prompts are more effective, revealing that the cultural knowledge gap—not mere prompt specificity—drives vulnerability in English-centric models. Key limitations include the primary focus on Korean (with Khmer as a secondary case), the reliance on LLM-as-a-Judge for both generation and evaluation, and the non-trivial initial data curation cost per new culture. Open questions include whether culturally-aware attack strategies can further amplify these vulnerabilities, and whether the framework scales to languages with very different legal and social structures.
