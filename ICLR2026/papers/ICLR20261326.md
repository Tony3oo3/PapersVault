## Problem

Cascaded inference policies for efficient ML serving—deciding when to exit a cascade and which sub-model to consult—remain largely heuristic and case-specific, with no general theoretical guarantees. The paper identifies a fundamental gap: no-recall strategies (the standard confidence-threshold approach) provably cannot achieve any constant-factor approximation to the optimal accuracy-latency trade-off, yet no recall-based strategy with provable optimality existed for the DAG topologies that arise in practice.

## Input / Output

At training time, T-Tamer consumes input-output pairs from all sub-models in the cascade to fit the routing and stopping policy, independent of how the sub-models themselves were trained. At inference time, given a single classification query (image or text) and a trade-off parameter λ weighting accuracy loss against latency cost, the policy incrementally inspects sub-models along the DAG, updating its belief after each observation, and outputs both a class label (from the best-inspected sub-model under recall) and the sequence of routing/stopping decisions. The DAG structure (directed line, transitive closure, or tree) determines which routing choices are available at each step.

## Method

T-Tamer formulates cascaded inference as a Markovian costly exploration problem over a DAG, where nodes are sub-models with correlated losses and edges encode both precedence constraints and inspection costs. The central theoretical result is that recall—the ability to return the prediction of any previously inspected model rather than only the last one—is both necessary and sufficient for provable guarantees: no-recall policies suffer an information-theoretic impossibility (no constant approximation), while recall-based policies admit an optimal dynamic indexing strategy. This strategy generalizes the Gittins index: at each step it computes a dynamic index σ for the next available model(s) via backward dynamic programming, stopping when the current minimum loss falls below σ and otherwise continuing (and, for trees and transitive closures, also selecting which model to probe next). Preprocessing takes O(n·|V|²T) time for lines and trees (O(n²·|V|²T) for transitive closures) and O(n|V|²) space, while inference runs in O(1) per node and O(n) per query. The framework is model-agnostic and plug-in: it learns the optimal policy from sub-model I/O pairs without modifying the sub-models themselves, and a soft state aggregation (SSA) approximation further accelerates the matrix-multiplication step for large transition matrices.

## Results

On vision classification (VGG-11/13 backbones on Auburn and Oxford datasets), T-Tamer attains approximately 95% accuracy with about 50% latency savings; specifically, Fig. 4a shows latency reduced to 45% of the original while sacrificing less than 7% accuracy. On NLP workloads (GPT-2 and BERT-Base on IMDB and Amazon), T-Tamer achieves around 75% accuracy with roughly 90% latency savings. The soft-state-aggregation (SSA) approximation remains within ±0.02 accuracy of the exact transition-matrix formulation once the number of meta-states exceeds 100, and closely matches vanilla T-Tamer (within 0.02%) at approximately 300 meta-states.

## Baselines & Comparisons

T-Tamer is compared against two early-exit baselines: (1) a confidence-threshold method (BerXit, Xin et al. 2021) and (2) a patience-based rule method (Zhou et al. 2020). Against the patience-based baseline, T-Tamer consistently outperforms it across all NLP workloads (GPT-2 and BERT-Base on IMDB and Amazon). Against the 1-threshold (confidence-based) baseline, T-Tamer identifies the best operating points in the high-accuracy region for both GPT-2 and BERT-based models, while remaining competitive (within 10%) in lower-accuracy regimes for BERT. Notably, for BERT-based workloads the 1-threshold Pareto front violates monotonicity—deeper exits are not uniformly beneficial—whereas T-Tamer's recall mechanism provides a more effective stopping signal, directly validating the paper's theoretical motivation for recall-based strategies.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies the Gittins index framework (Gittins, 1979) with the Pandora's Box and Prophet Inequality literatures to formalize cascaded inference routing and stopping as a costly exploration problem over DAGs. It departs from the dominant heuristic paradigm in early-exit and cascaded inference—confidence thresholds (Xin et al., 2021; Teerapittayanon et al., 2016) and patience-based rules (Zhou et al., 2020)—by providing the first provably optimal dynamic indexing strategy with polynomial-time computation. The key conceptual departure is the recall/no-recall distinction: the paper shows that the no-recall setting (which encompasses all standard confidence-based early exits) is information-theoretically intractable, while the with-recall setting admits an optimal solution via dynamic programming, extending the Gittins index from single-line to tree and transitive-closure DAG topologies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The impossibility result for no-recall strategies is a significant negative finding: it proves that the most widely deployed early-exit heuristics (confidence thresholds) cannot achieve any constant-factor approximation to the optimal trade-off, regardless of computational resources. This provides a principled justification for recall-based strategies and a general plug-in framework (T-Tamer) that is model-agnostic and agnostic to sub-model training. Open questions include whether the Markovian correlation assumption holds in practice for very deep or transformer-based cascades, how the framework scales to DAGs with thousands of nodes, and whether the polynomial-time dynamic programming approach remains tractable when loss distributions are high-dimensional or continuous. The empirical validation, while consistent, is limited to four datasets and two model families, leaving open whether the theoretical advantages translate to large-scale production deployments.
