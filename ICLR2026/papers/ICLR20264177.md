## Problem

Existing AI text detectors frame detection as binary (human vs. AI) or ternary (human vs. AI vs. mixed) classification, but cannot quantify the degree of AI editing present in a text. As LLM usage shifts toward editing and co-writing rather than full generation, binary detectors produce false positives on lightly AI-polished text, and categorical approaches treat all mixed text as equivalent regardless of whether it received minor proofreading or a full rewrite. This paper addresses the gap by formulating AI-edit detection as a continuous regression problem that estimates the magnitude of AI involvement.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264177/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, the model takes only the edited text as input (no source text is required) and outputs a continuous score in [0,1] where 0 indicates fully human-written text and 1 indicates fully AI-generated text, with intermediate values reflecting varying degrees of AI editing. The score can be thresholded to recover binary (human vs. any AI) or ternary (human vs. AI-edited vs. AI-generated) predictions. Training, however, uses source–edited text pairs to compute supervision targets via similarity metrics, so the model learns discriminatively from the edited text alone while the source is never available at test time.

## Method

The paper constructs a 60k-example dataset by applying 303 diverse editing prompts to human texts from six domains using GPT-4.1, Claude 4 Sonnet, and Gemini 2.5 Flash, alongside synthetic mirrors for fully AI-generated examples. Supervision targets are computed using two lightweight similarity metrics—cosine distance of Linq-Embed-Mistral embeddings and a precision-based soft n-grams score—between source and edited texts, validated against human annotator judgments (Krippendorff's α ≈ 0.72). A Mistral-Small-24B (or smaller Llama/Mistral) backbone is fine-tuned with QLoRA, trained either with a direct regression head (MSE loss) or an N-way classification head (N ∈ {4,5,6}) decoded via weighted-average of bucket midpoints rather than argmax. The key architectural insight is that the model absorbs inductive biases for edit magnitude (lexical volatility, style drift, fluency cues) from the edited text alone, approximating the Bayes-optimal conditional expectation E[Δ(X,y) | Y=y] without reconstructing the source. The 4-bucket classification variant with cosine supervision achieves the best ternary macro-F1 of 90.4%, outperforming the strongest ternary baseline (GPTZero) by 16% and the best binary baseline (Pangram) by 8%.

## Results

On the ternary classification task (human vs. AI-generated vs. AI-edited), EDITLENS (Cosine) achieves 90.2% accuracy and 90.4% macro-F1, substantially outperforming the best ternary baseline GPTZero (74.7% acc, 72.7% macro-F1) by 16+ points. In binary classification (human vs. any AI), EDITLENS (SNG) reaches 94.0% accuracy and 95.6% F1, surpassing the best binary baseline Pangram (80.7% acc, 83.7% F1). On the APT-Eval AI Polish dataset, EDITLENS achieves a Pearson correlation of 0.799 with Levenshtein distance and 0.606 with semantic similarity, compared to Pangram's 0.615 and 0.491 respectively, demonstrating its ability to track edit magnitude continuously rather than collapsing to binary predictions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EDITLENS decisively outperforms all five baselines on the ternary classification task. Against the strongest ternary baseline GPTZero (72.7% macro-F1), EDITLENS (Cosine) improves by 17.7 points to 90.4%, with the largest gap on the AI-edited class (86.8% vs. 50.9% F1). Among binary baselines, Pangram is the strongest (69.5% macro-F1 when converted to ternary), but EDITLENS still leads by 20.9 points; Binoculars and FastDetectGPT perform poorly on the AI-edited class (37.4% and 61.0% F1 respectively). DetectAIve, the only other purpose-built ternary detector, achieves only 52.5% macro-F1 and collapses on the AI-generated class (16.1% F1). On the APT-Eval correlation analysis, EDITLENS tracks edit magnitude far more faithfully than any binary detector: its Levenshtein correlation (0.799) exceeds Pangram's (0.615) by 0.184, and its semantic similarity correlation (−0.606) is 0.115 stronger than Pangram's (−0.491), confirming that the regression formulation captures nuance that binary classifiers cannot.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant binary (human vs. AI) and ternary (human/AI/mixed) detection formulations by introducing a continuous regression task that quantifies the magnitude of AI editing. It explicitly distinguishes 'homogeneous' mixed authorship (where editing entangles authorship across all tokens, e.g., paraphrasing) from the 'heterogeneous' case studied by boundary-detection work (RoFT, SeqXGPT, PaLD), arguing that the latter is ill-posed for layered collaborative editing. Methodologically, it repurposes lightweight embedding-based similarity metrics (cosine distance, soft n-grams) as intermediate supervision for a QLoRA-finetuned LLM with a weighted-average decoding head, rather than relying on statistical curvature or watermarking signals used by training-free detectors like DetectGPT or Binoculars.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The continuous score enables flexible, calibratable policy (e.g., permitting light grammar fixes while flagging full rewrites), directly addressing the false-positive problem that plagues binary classifiers in high-stakes settings like academic integrity. Key limitations include the one-dimensional nature of the score (tone adjustment and content addition are conflated), reliance on off-the-shelf retrieval embeddings that may not optimally capture AI-writing style, and the single-pass editing assumption in training. Open questions include multi-axis editing quantification, adversarial robustness, and whether purpose-built embeddings could further close the gap between metric-based supervision and human perception of AI involvement.
