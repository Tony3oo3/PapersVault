## Problem

Current interpretability tools for LLMs struggle to capture the dynamic, multi-faceted value trade-offs (e.g., truthfulness vs. social niceness) that underpin human-like communication. While alignment methods like RLHF shape these trade-offs, there is no principled way to quantify how specific training decisions—reasoning budget, base model, feedback dataset, or alignment algorithm—shift the internal weighting of competing utilities. This paper bridges cognitive science and LLM interpretability by using a formal cognitive model of polite speech as a structured lens to make these trade-offs measurable and comparable.

## Input / Output

The method consumes two types of LLM behavioral data: (1) utterance preference counts from a polite speech task where models choose among 8 descriptor utterances given a 5-star true state across 13 vignettes in three framings (first/second/third person), and (2) literal semantics estimates obtained from a yes/no sub-task measuring the probability that a given utterance is true of a given state. The output is a posterior distribution (via MCMC) over the cognitive model parameters: φ (the first-order speaker's projected information-vs-social mixture), ω = (ω_inf, ω_soc, ω_pre) (the second-order speaker's weighting of informational, social, and presentational utilities), and α (softmax optimality), reported as posterior means with 95% high-density intervals.

## Method

The paper fits the second-order pragmatic speaker model S₂ from Yoon et al. (2020)—a recursive Rational Speech Acts model where a speaker balances informational utility (how well a listener infers the true state), social utility (expected social value induced in the listener), and presentational utility (how well the speaker projects a particular φ to the listener)—to LLM behavioral data using Stan's Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (NUTS) sampler with 4 chains of 2000 warm-up + 2000 samples. Two evaluation suites are designed: a closed-source suite spanning three frontier model families (Anthropic, Google, OpenAI) at three reasoning budgets (none, low, medium) with system-prompt manipulations simulating informative, social, or both goals; and an open-source suite that disentangles base model (Qwen2.5-7B vs. Llama-3.1-8B), feedback dataset (UltraFeedback vs. HH-RLHF), and alignment method (DPO vs. PPO) by tracking inferred parameters across evenly spaced training checkpoints. The key insight is that this IRL-style reverse-engineering of implicit reward functions, grounded in a cognitively validated model, reveals interpretable signatures—such as sycophancy corresponding to low φ with high ω_pre and low ω_inf/ω_soc—that are not accessible through standard benchmark scores.

## Results

For closed-source models, reasoning variants (low and medium effort) show significantly higher projected informational utility φ than non-reasoning counterparts (β_low = 0.228, t = 6.338, p < .001; β_medium = 0.211, t = 5.846, p < .001), with no significant difference between low and medium effort (p = 0.627). Speaker optimality α exceeds 1 for all model families (Anthropic: 3.52, Gemini: 6.19, OpenAI: 4.78), confirming that utility weightings factor into utterance choice. For open-source models, the largest shifts in all four utility parameters (ω_inf, ω_soc, ω_pre, φ) occur within the first quarter of RL post-training, and the choice of base model (Qwen vs. Llama) has a more persistent effect on final utility weightings than the choice of feedback dataset or alignment method (DPO vs. PPO). The inferred parameters generalize to held-out data with mean MSE of 0.03, significantly lower than random parameter sampling (0.06; z = -12.49, p < 0.001).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is human behavior from Yoon et al. (2020), where speakers under informative, social, and both-goal conditions produced φ = 0.49, 0.37, and 0.36 respectively, with ω_pre = 0.62, 0.44, and 0.54. LLMs under the same goal-condition prompts shift in the same directional pattern as humans (informative → higher ω_inf and φ; social → higher ω_pre and lower φ), but the magnitude of these shifts is substantially larger for models than for humans, indicating that prompt-based goal manipulation has a more extreme effect on LLMs. The relative ordering of ω_inf across goal conditions closely matches the human signature, but the patterns of ω_pre and ω_soc diverge from humans, suggesting informational utility is more stably represented in models while social and presentational utilities are less faithfully captured. For open-source models, the comparison across 8 configurations (2 base models × 2 datasets × 2 alignment methods) reveals that base model identity (Qwen vs. Llama) produces more persistent differences in final utility weightings than either the feedback dataset (UltraFeedback vs. HH-RLHF) or the alignment algorithm (DPO vs. PPO), with Qwen consistently showing higher ω_inf and lower ω_pre than Llama regardless of downstream training choices.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Rational Speech Acts (RSA) framework from cognitive science—specifically the polite speech model of Yoon et al. (2020)—to the domain of LLM interpretability, building most directly on Liu et al. (2024), who used a related cognitive model of honesty/helpfulness trade-offs to evaluate LLMs in a signaling bandits paradigm. The key departures are: (1) a systematic disentanglement of base model, feedback dataset, and alignment method effects on value trade-offs during RL post-training, and (2) evaluation of closed-source frontier models across reasoning budgets with goal-condition prompt manipulations. The paper also draws on the Inverse Reinforcement Learning view of RLHF (Wulfmeier et al., 2024; Joselowitz et al., 2025) and Theory-of-Mind inference (Jara-Ettinger, 2019) as conceptual bridges between cognitive models and learned reward functions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a structured, interpretable lens for probing LLM value trade-offs that goes beyond scalar alignment metrics, enabling fine-grained hypotheses about behaviors like sycophancy (identified via a specific parameter signature: low φ, high ω_pre, low ω_inf/ω_soc). The finding that base model and pretraining data exert a larger influence on final utility weightings than the choice of feedback dataset or alignment method has practical implications for alignment strategy, suggesting that post-training may be less powerful at reshaping deep-seated value structures than previously assumed. Key limitations include the domain-specificity of the cognitive model (polite speech vignettes), the technical challenges of sampling-based inference for complex hierarchical models, and the open question of whether human-derived utility decompositions are the right ones for characterizing LLM behavior. Future work on mapping open-ended language to low-dimensional cognitive model spaces and on co-training for stable multi-value alignment would extend the framework's reach.
