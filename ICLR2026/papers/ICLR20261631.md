## Problem

While LLMs have shown promise in generating formulaic alpha factors for quantitative investment, their capabilities across the full factor-mining workflow remain poorly characterized, with no standardized benchmark to measure performance in generation, evaluation, or iterative searching. The behavior of different LLM configurations—model type, prompting paradigm, reasoning strategy—on these tasks is unexplored, leaving practitioners without guidance on which models and settings to deploy.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261631/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes natural-language factor descriptions (stratified into easy/medium/hard difficulty), raw factor expressions for zero-shot evaluation, and seed factors paired with search instructions for iterative refinement. All generated outputs are constrained to Qlib-compatible DSL expressions drawn from a fixed operator library (arithmetic, rolling statistics, regression, ranking, and conditional operators), enabling direct execution in the Qlib backtesting engine on CSI300 daily OHLCV data spanning 2020–2025. Evaluation outputs include factor rankings (Precision@K, NDCG), absolute quality scores (IC estimates, 1–5 integer ratings), and search trajectories annotated with per-round token costs and validity rates.

## Method

AlphaBench decomposes formulaic alpha factor mining into three tasks—generation (Text2Alpha and Directional Mining), evaluation (Ranking and Scoring), and searching (Chain-of-Experience, Tree-of-Thought, and Evolutionary Algorithm)—totaling 687 generation instructions, 1,170 evaluation instructions, and 27 searching configurations. Each task is scored with tailored metrics: generation uses reliability, stability, and accuracy; evaluation uses precision and signal-classification accuracy; searching uses cost (token rounds, valid-generation rate) and quality (improvement rate and ratio over the seed factor). The benchmark evaluates ten commercial and open-source LLMs under vanilla and Chain-of-Thought prompting, with ablations on temperature, EA population capacity, and supervised fine-tuning for evaluation subtasks. A multi-stage factor filter (parenthesis/operator validation, 30-second compute-time cap, 1% NaN threshold) ensures only executable candidates enter backtesting, and diversity is quantified via AST tree-edit distance and output-signal correlation.

## Results

On the generation task, GPT-5 achieves the best overall score of 0.72 (Reliability 1.00, Stability 0.62, Accuracy 0.56) under vanilla prompting, with Gemini-2.5-Flash close behind at 0.71. On the evaluation task, all models perform poorly, with the best overall scores around 0.47–0.48 (GPT-5, Gemini-2.5-Pro), and no model exceeds 0.28 in ranking precision. On the searching task, GPT-5 leads in both search quality (0.656) and cost-efficiency (0.940), while Gemini-2.5-Flash offers a competitive quality/cost balance (0.646/0.850). Open-source models (DeepSeek-V3, LLaMA-3.1-8B, Qwen-2.5-14B) trail commercial models by 0.10–0.50 in overall generation scores.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a benchmark paper, AlphaBench compares 10 LLMs (commercial and open-source) across three tasks rather than proposing a single method. GPT-5 dominates generation (0.72 overall) and searching (0.656 quality, 0.940 cost), outperforming Gemini-2.5-Flash (0.71/0.646/0.850) and GPT-4.1-Mini (0.65/0.608/0.904). Gemini-2.5-Flash is the strongest mid-sized model, matching or exceeding GPT-4.1-Mini on generation and searching while being more cost-efficient. Open-source models (DeepSeek-V3 at 0.52, LLaMA-3.1-8B at 0.48, Qwen-2.5-14B at 0.54) consistently trail commercial models by 0.10–0.24 in generation overall scores. On the evaluation task, no model clearly wins: GPT-5 and Gemini-2.5-Pro tie at ~0.47–0.48 overall, while even the best ranking precision is only 0.28 (LLaMA-3.1-70B). CoT prompting yields marginal or negative gains for most models, with Gemini-2.5-Flash dropping from 0.71 to 0.69 and GPT-4.1-Mini from 0.65 to 0.59 in generation overall.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three previously studied-in-isolation LLM tasks in quantitative finance—factor generation (building on FAMA, QuantAgent, Alpha-GPT), factor evaluation (building on AlphaEval), and factor searching (building on CoE, ToT/MCTS, and EvoAlpha)—into a single systematic benchmark. It draws on the broader LLM benchmarking tradition (EvoCodeBench, VerilogEval, Text-to-SQL) but is the first to target an executable financial DSL (Qlib) with domain-specific metrics (IC, ICIR, Sharpe). The search paradigms are direct adaptations of Chain-of-Thought, Tree-of-Thought, and evolutionary algorithms from the general LLM reasoning literature, repurposed for the combinatorial space of alpha factor expressions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a critical asymmetry: LLMs are reliable generators of syntactically valid factors but consistently fail as zero-shot evaluators, with even frontier models scoring near random on ranking and scoring. This has direct practical implications for anyone building LLM-driven factor mining pipelines, as the evaluation bottleneck limits search efficiency and forces continued reliance on expensive backtesting. Open questions include whether structured representations (ASTs, operator graphs) or weak-label alignment via automated backtests can close the evaluation gap, and whether the findings generalize beyond daily CSI300 equity factors to intraday, cross-asset, or multi-regime settings.
