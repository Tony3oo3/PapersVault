## Problem

Existing symmetry-enforcing techniques for normalizing flows (canonicalization, equivariant architectures) can only enforce exact symmetries where all modes carry equal probability mass, and they require the prior to be invariant under the symmetry group. This limits their applicability to physically relevant systems with broken symmetries, where modes have unequal weights. Additionally, reverse-KL variational training is prone to mode collapse, causing flows to miss entire sectors of the target distribution.

## Input / Output

The method consumes only prior Gaussian samples z ~ q_0 and the closed-form unnormalized action f[x] (no target samples required), operating in a fully data-free variational inference setting. It produces samples from the learned variational density q_θ(x) that approximates the target Boltzmann distribution p(x) = exp(−f[x])/Z, along with a learned symmetry-breaking parameter b that encodes the relative probability mass across symmetry-related modes. The stochastic modulation introduces a discrete (or continuous) random variable u that selects which symmetry sector each sample is assigned to, making the output a mixture over symmetry-transformed flow outputs.

## Method

SESaMo replaces the fixed canonicalization map with a learnable stochastic modulation S_u: the flow g_θ first shifts the prior into a canonical cell Ω (enforced by a penalty term Λ that prevents samples from leaving Ω, preserving bijectivity), capturing a single mode. A random variable u ~ p_{S,b} then selects a symmetry transformation T_u, redistributing probability mass across all symmetry-related modes. For exact symmetries, b is fixed (e.g., b = ln 0.5 for Z₂); for broken symmetries, b is a learnable parameter optimized via a REINFORCE estimator through the discrete sampling of u. The training objective is the reverse KL divergence augmented with the bijectivity penalty, and the approach generalizes to continuous symmetries (e.g., U(1)) by replacing the discrete u with a continuous variable and a trainable spline map h_b(u) that parametrizes the rotation angle.

## Results

SESaMo achieves the highest Effective Sample Size (ESS) across all six benchmark configurations, ranging from 0.941 (broken U(1) φ⁴, 8×8) to 0.9986 (exact Z₈ GMM). On the large Hubbard model (18×100, broken (Z₂)¹⁸ symmetry), SESaMo reaches ESS 0.74(1), dramatically outperforming FAB (0.06(5)) and canonicalization (0.024(1)). Where the normalization constant is known, SESaMo also achieves the lowest KL divergence, e.g. 0.0008(1) on the exact Z₈ GMM versus 0.013(2) for canonicalization and 1.19(37) for FAB.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SESaMo outperforms all three baselines (FAB, VMoNF, and canonicalization) on every benchmark where all methods are applicable. Against FAB, the gap is most dramatic on the large Hubbard model (18×100), where SESaMo achieves ESS 0.74 versus FAB's 0.06, and on the φ⁴ theory where SESaMo reaches ~0.94 ESS while FAB plateaus around 0.26–0.28. Against VMoNF, SESaMo consistently wins; VMoNF suffers from mode collapse (e.g., collapsing to only three of eight modes in the GMM) and achieves the lowest ESS on most tasks. Against canonicalization, SESaMo wins on all comparable benchmarks (e.g., ESS 0.9947 vs. 0.747 on broken Z₈ GMM; 0.996 vs. 0.839 on Hubbard 2×1), and additionally handles the broken-symmetry cases and the complex φ⁴ theory where canonicalization is inapplicable. The KL divergence results further confirm the advantage: on the exact Z₈ GMM, SESaMo achieves 0.0008(1) versus 0.013(2) for canonicalization and 1.19(37) for FAB.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the equivariant normalizing flow framework established by Kohler et al. (2020) and the canonicalization technique developed by Kanwar et al. (2020) and Boyda et al. (2021) for lattice field theory sampling. The key departure is replacing the fixed canonical-cell mapping (which requires an invariant prior and cannot handle unequal mode probabilities) with a stochastic modulation that randomly applies symmetry transformations with learnable probabilities, thereby enabling the model to capture broken symmetries. The penalty term for numerically enforcing bijectivity of the flow within the canonical cell is also a new contribution, addressing a practical constraint that prior canonicalization approaches handled less rigorously.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SESaMo addresses a genuine capability gap: canonicalization cannot learn broken symmetries (unequal probability mass across symmetric modes), which is common in physically relevant systems like the Hubbard model with finite-temperature effects. The method scales to large lattice volumes (18×100 for the Hubbard model) where FAB becomes infeasible, and the learned breaking parameter b provides an interpretable diagnostic for symmetry-breaking strength. Key limitations include the requirement that symmetry sectors be known a priori and the approximate bijectivity when target density is non-zero at canonical-cell boundaries. An open question the authors flag is whether SESaMo can compete with established MCMC methods like Hamiltonian Monte Carlo on these benchmarks.
