## Problem

Despite substantial investment in scaling pre-training, it remains poorly understood how next-token prediction loss relates to downstream post-training success (e.g., Best-of-N sampling or RL fine-tuning). Cross-entropy, the standard pre-training metric, can be anti-correlated with downstream performance and exhibits spurious dependence on sequence length, making it an unreliable predictor. The paper asks: what quantity actually governs downstream success, and through what mechanism does maximum likelihood produce models with that quantity?

## Input / Output

The framework takes a prompt x (a text string, e.g., a graph edge list with source/target nodes) and a token sequence prefix y_{1:h-1}, producing a next-token distribution over the vocabulary. The key analytical output is the coverage profile Cov_N(π_D ∥ π̂), which measures the probability that the data distribution's responses receive at least 1/N of their probability mass under the pre-trained model. Downstream, this translates to Pass@N scores: the paper shows Cov_N is necessary and sufficient for Best-of-N to achieve near-optimal reward, and empirically predicts Pass@N better than KL divergence, especially at large N.

## Method

The paper introduces the coverage principle: the logarithmic loss structure of next-token prediction causes the coverage profile to generalize faster than cross-entropy, with a rate that avoids dependence on sequence length H and density ratios. The main theorem (Theorem 4.1) decomposes coverage generalization into a fine-grained term (scaling as C_fine(Π,n)/log N, capturing the implicit bias of log-loss) and a coarse-grained term (capturing missing mass at scale log N), both expressed via covering numbers. For autoregressive linear models, an inherent variance σ_*² replaces the sequence length as the complexity parameter, reflecting that most tokens are near-deterministic. The paper further shows that gradient normalization provably removes the H-dependence in SGD, that token-level SGD combined with test-time training achieves horizon-independent coverage, and that a tournament-based checkpoint selection procedure outperforms cross-entropy-based selection for downstream Pass@N.

## Results

The paper's main results are theoretical. Theorem 4.1 establishes that the maximum likelihood estimator achieves Cov_N(π̂) ≲ (1/log N)·C_fine(Π,n) + C_coarse(Π,N,n), where the fine-grained term avoids explicit dependence on sequence length H and density ratio log W_max (unlike KL-based bounds). For overparameterized autoregressive linear models (Theorem 4.2), coverage scales as √(σ*²/(n·log N)) + B²/n, where σ*² is an instance-dependent 'inherent variance' capturing effective sequence length. Empirically, on a graph reasoning task, the coverage profile Cov_{N/2} is a stronger predictor of Pass@N than KL divergence (especially for large N), and KL at convergence scales linearly with horizon H while coverage does not (Figure 2).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between the coverage profile and KL divergence/cross-entropy as predictors of downstream Best-of-N performance. Empirically (Figure 1), KL divergence improves monotonically during training while coverage can degrade, and Cov_{N/2} correlates more strongly with Pass@N than KL for large N. Theoretically, Proposition 3.1 shows KL-to-coverage bounds are tight and scale with log W_max (which is BH for autoregressive linear models), making them vacuous for long sequences; Theorem 4.1 removes this dependence. For optimization, vanilla SGD (Proposition 5.1) incurs a B²H/T term in its coverage bound, while gradient-normalized SGD (Theorem 5.1) achieves a horizon-independent B²/T rate. Token-level SGD with test-time training (Theorem 6.1) further improves the leading term by a factor of 1/√(log N) over the MLE bound in Theorem 4.2. For model selection, the tournament procedure (Theorem 6.2) provably selects checkpoints with better coverage than naive cross-entropy minimization, as confirmed empirically in Figure 1 where tournament-selected checkpoints (◆) outperform KL-minimizing checkpoints (red) on Pass@N.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new theoretical ground by introducing the coverage profile as the missing link between next-token prediction and downstream post-training success, building on classical MLE generalization theory (Wong & Shen, 1995; van de Geer, 2000) and the small-ball method of Mendelson (2014, 2017) but applying them to a fundamentally different objective. It provides theoretical grounding for empirical observations that cross-entropy is a poor predictor of Pass@N (Liu et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2025; Lourie et al., 2025) and formalizes the coverage assumptions that post-training theory (Huang et al., 2025a,b,c; Song et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2024) has previously taken as given. The practical interventions—gradient normalization (related to Adam/SignSGD via Bernstein & Newhouse, 2024), test-time training (Krause et al., 2019; Sun et al., 2024), and tournament-based model selection—repurpose existing algorithmic ideas through the new coverage lens.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The coverage principle—that next-token prediction implicitly optimizes a metric (coverage) that generalizes faster than cross-entropy and avoids spurious sequence-length dependence—offers a unifying explanation for why pre-training enables post-training and why standard scaling-law metrics fail. The introduction of inherent variance σ²* as an instance-dependent complexity measure for autoregressive models is a potentially reusable conceptual tool. Key open questions include whether these results extend to misspecified models, heterogeneous prompt distributions, and whether 'semantic' notions of coverage (accounting for learned representations rather than just probability mass) can sharpen the theory; the empirical validation is limited to small graph-reasoning tasks rather than real language models.
