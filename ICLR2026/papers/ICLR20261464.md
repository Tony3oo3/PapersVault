## Problem

While full-graph and mini-batch GNN training are distinct paradigms with different system design demands, the joint impact of batch size and fan-out size on convergence, generalization, and computational efficiency remains insufficiently understood. Existing studies either examine these hyperparameters in isolation, rely on hardware-dependent time-based metrics, or depend on strong simplifications (infinite-width assumptions, linear models) that obscure the effects of graph structure on training dynamics. This paper asks: how do batch size and fan-out size non-isotropically influence GNN optimization and generalization, and does full-graph training always outperform well-tuned mini-batch settings?

## Input / Output

The method operates on homogeneous undirected graphs with fixed node features and independently sampled labels in a transductive node-classification setting, where the model is trained on a subset of labeled nodes and evaluated on unlabeled test nodes. The theoretical outputs are iteration-to-convergence bounds (as functions of batch size b and fan-out size β) and a PAC-Bayesian generalization gap bounded by a Wasserstein distance Δ(β,b) that quantifies structural differences between training and testing subgraphs. Empirically, the paper introduces iteration-to-accuracy as a hardware-agnostic convergence metric alongside time-to-accuracy and throughput (nodes/s), enabling fair comparisons across GPU/CPU and single/multi-device setups.

## Method

The paper provides a unified theoretical framework analyzing a one-layer GNN with ReLU activation on irregular graphs, decoupling the nonlinear activation from aggregated node features via dual activation functions and position-wise indicator matrices. For convergence, it derives iteration bounds under both MSE and CE losses, revealing that batch size has opposite effects under the two losses while fan-out size consistently reduces iterations, with the magnitude of fan-out's impact diminishing as either b or β grows. For generalization, it introduces a novel Wasserstein-distance-based PAC-Bayesian bound where Δ(β,b) captures per-node structural discrepancies between full-graph and mini-batch adjacency, showing fan-out size has a stronger (and slightly non-monotonic) effect than batch size. Empirically, the study validates these findings across four real-world datasets (Reddit, OGBN-ARXIV, OGBN-Products, OGBN-Papers100M) and three GNN architectures (GCN, GraphSAGE, GAT), demonstrating that full-graph training does not consistently outperform mini-batch settings with batch size below half the training nodes and fan-out size under 15.

## Results

After graph-based hyperparameter tuning (grid search over batch size and fan-out size), well-tuned mini-batch GraphSAGE matches or exceeds full-graph training on 3 of 4 datasets: Reddit (96.32 vs. 96.13), OGBN-ARXIV (71.16 vs. 70.96), and OGBN-Products (78.80 vs. 77.92), while trailing by 1.02% on OGBN-Papers100M (58.52 vs. 59.54). The paper's central empirical finding is that full-graph training does not consistently yield superior generalization or computational efficiency. Theoretical convergence bounds show that under MSE, iterations scale as O(n_train · h² · b^{5/2} · β^{-1/2}), while under CE they scale as O(n_train² · b^{-1} · β^{-5/2}), revealing non-isotropic effects of batch size b and fan-out size β.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new GNN architecture but instead compares two training paradigms (full-graph vs. mini-batch) across four real-world datasets using GCN, GraphSAGE, and GAT. Mini-batch training with tuned hyperparameters (batch size < n_train/2, fan-out size < 15) outperforms full-graph training on Reddit (+0.19%), OGBN-ARXIV (+0.20%), and OGBN-Products (+0.88%), but underperforms on OGBN-Papers100M (−1.02%), where resource constraints limited the model to 2 hidden layers of dimension 128. The paper further shows that performance degradation occurs with very large fan-out sizes (>15) or batch sizes (>n_train/2) under cross-entropy loss, attributed to convergence to sharp minima, while this degradation is less pronounced under MSE. In terms of computational efficiency, mini-batch training achieves higher throughput (nodes/s) than full-graph training, as fixed computations (parameter updates) are amortized over fewer nodes per iteration, while larger fan-out sizes increase message-passing cost. The key practical takeaway is that adjusting fan-out size is more reliable for accelerating convergence (consistent trend under both MSE and CE), while adjusting batch size is more stable for improving generalization (less non-monotonic fluctuation).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the GNN convergence analysis of Awasthi et al. (2021) from regular graphs with linear models to irregular graphs with ReLU activations, and introduces a novel generalization analysis using Wasserstein distance within a PAC-Bayesian framework (McAllester, 2003) to quantify how graph structural differences between training and testing sets affect generalization. It departs from prior work that studied batch size or fan-out size in isolation (Hu et al., 2021; Yuan et al., 2023) by jointly analyzing their interplay and revealing non-isotropic effects. The paper also contrasts with the only prior full-graph vs. mini-batch comparison (Bajaj et al., 2024), which was purely empirical and hardware-dependent, by providing hardware-agnostic iteration-based metrics and theoretical grounding.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides actionable hyperparameter guidance (batch size below half training nodes, fan-out under 15 for sparse graphs) and challenges the default assumption that full-graph training is always superior, showing that well-tuned mini-batch settings can match or exceed full-graph performance in both generalization and computational efficiency. Key limitations include the theoretical analysis being restricted to one-layer GNNs and transductive node classification, with multi-layer generalization only discussed qualitatively. Open questions include whether the non-isotropic effects persist with different activation functions, inductive settings, and link prediction tasks, and whether the Wasserstein-based generalization framework extends to deeper architectures.
