## Problem

Translating dense scientific research into actionable policy briefs remains a critical bottleneck, yet no benchmark or training dataset existed to evaluate or improve LLMs on this specific scientific-to-policy generation task. Prior work showed LLMs hallucinate scientific content, produce inappropriate tone, and generate weakly supported policy recommendations, but no systematic, fine-grained evaluation framework captured these failure modes across the full brief-writing workflow.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262583/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

For generation tasks (the core contribution), the model receives the full text of a scientific paper (typically 10–30 pages from Nature Energy, Nature Climate Change, Nature Cities, Nature Sustainability, or J. Health and Social Behavior) and must produce structured policy brief sections—Policy Problem, Research Findings, Study Methods, Policy Implications, or a complete brief. Autocompletion and reordering tasks consume three-sentence triplets extracted from papers or briefs, while verification tasks pair a claim or policy implication with its source paper for a support/contradict label. Evaluation of free-form outputs uses task-specific LLM-as-a-judge rubrics (Gemini-2.5-Pro) rather than BERTScore or ROUGE, which the paper demonstrates fail to capture brief-writing quality.

## Method

The paper introduces a five-stage Sci2Pol-Taxonomy (Autocompletion → Understanding → Summarization → Generation → Verification) that decomposes the human brief-writing process into 18 evaluation tasks, grounded in the complete set of 85 expert-written paper-brief pairs from high-impact journals. For training data, Sci2Pol-Corpus is built in three steps: (i) citation-based retrieval linking 5.6 million policy documents (from Overton.io) to their cited scientific papers, yielding 140,000 candidate pairs filtered to documents citing ≤3 papers; (ii) two-stage GPT-o3 filtering (coarse-grained on abstracts, fine-grained on full texts) to retain 639 high-quality pairs where the policy document genuinely centers on the cited paper; and (iii) in-context polishing using three expert-written briefs as style references to align tone and structure. Supervised fine-tuning on this corpus is applied to LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct, Gemma-3-12B, and Gemma-3-27B, with the fine-tuned Gemma-27B surpassing GPT-4o and DeepSeek-V3 (671B) on the benchmark, demonstrating that targeted domain-specific supervision can outweigh model scale for this task.

## Results

Across 13 LLMs evaluated on Sci2Pol-Bench (18 tasks spanning five taxonomy stages), Grok-3-beta achieves the highest average score of 77.01, followed by DeepSeek-R1 (75.05) and Qwen3-235B (74.81). Even top performers show notable weaknesses: Grok-3-beta scores only 50.77 on Autocompletion tasks, and GPT-4o lags at 76.39 on Generation despite leading on Verification (85.45). After supervised fine-tuning on Sci2Pol-Corpus, Gemma-3-27B improves from 71.40 to 73.43 (+2.03), surpassing both GPT-4o (72.12) and DeepSeek-V3 (73.35), while LLaMA-3.1-8B gains the largest absolute improvement of +7.64 (56.63 → 64.27).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 13 LLMs, with the five key commercial/open-source baselines being GPT-4o, DeepSeek-V3 (671B), Qwen3-235B, Gemma-3-27B, and LLaMA-3.1-8B. On the raw benchmark, GPT-4o (72.12) and DeepSeek-V3 (73.35) rank 5th and 4th respectively, trailing Grok-3-beta (77.01) and DeepSeek-R1 (75.05) by 5–11 points. The critical comparison emerges after SFT on Sci2Pol-Corpus: fine-tuned Gemma-3-27B reaches 73.43, narrowly beating DeepSeek-V3 (73.35) by 0.08 points and GPT-4o (72.12) by 1.31 points, despite being roughly 25× smaller than DeepSeek-V3. LLaMA-3.1-8B, the weakest baseline at 56.63, gains the most from SFT (+7.64 to 64.27) but still trails all commercial models. Gemma-3-12B improves by +3.12 to 71.59, approaching but not matching GPT-4o. The results demonstrate that domain-specific fine-tuning on 639 curated pairs can close or reverse the gap between small open-source models and much larger commercial systems on this specific task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by defining scientific-to-policy brief generation as a distinct LLM task, rather than treating it as a variant of general summarization or text generation. Conceptually, it draws on the progressive ability-oriented evaluation framework of LexEval (Li et al., 2024a) to structure its five-stage taxonomy, and repurposes existing scientific-understanding benchmarks (MMLU-Pro, SciRIFF) as sub-tasks within a broader policy-oriented pipeline. The corpus construction leverages the Overton policy database and citation metadata in a way not previously applied to LLM training data curation, and the in-context polishing step using expert-written references as style anchors is a novel curation technique. No prior work provides a dedicated benchmark or training set for this specific science-to-policy translation task.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work addresses a real-world gap: policymakers struggle to digest dense scientific literature, and scientists lack policy-writing expertise. The finding that a 27B fine-tuned model outperforms 671B general-purpose models on this task suggests that domain-specific supervision can be more effective than raw scale for narrow, high-stakes translation tasks. However, the evaluation set is small (85 pairs), the training corpus is modest (639 pairs), and the LLM-as-a-judge evaluation introduces its own reliability concerns. Open questions include whether these gains generalize to other science-to-policy domains (e.g., medicine, economics), whether the taxonomy transfers to non-English contexts, and whether the small corpus size limits the ceiling of fine-tuning benefits.
