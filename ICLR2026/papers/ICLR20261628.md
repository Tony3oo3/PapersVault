## Problem

Boosting methods (XGBoost, CatBoost) empirically outperform specialized OOD generalization methods on real-world tabular data, yet the mechanism behind this success under hidden confounding shifts remained unexplained. Prior attributions focused on variance reduction, feature selection, and multicalibration, but did not account for how boosting implicitly identifies the environments induced by unobserved confounders that drive distribution shift. This paper asks: what information-theoretic property does boosting learn that enables robust generalization when the confounder is latent?

## Input / Output

The method consumes pooled tabular covariates from multiple environments without requiring explicit environment labels or access to the hidden confounder U; labels are used only during training to compute pseudo-residuals. Beyond the standard scalar prediction (regression value or class probability), the boosted tree ensemble produces leaf embeddings of dimension equal to the number of trees, which serve as the model's internal representation and are the object analyzed for alignment with hidden confounder structure. The framework also outputs the alpha-predictive sufficiency residual I(Y-Yhat; E|Yhat) as a diagnostic for whether the predictor has achieved environment-independent errors.

## Method

The paper introduces alpha-predictive sufficiency, defined as I(Y-Yhat; E|Yhat) <= alpha, and proves via an information-theoretic decomposition that minimizing this quantity is equivalent to maximizing predictive information I(Y;Yhat) under hidden confounding shift (Proposition 4.1). The central result (Theorem 5.1) shows that standard boosting, under mild weak-learning and reweighting assumptions, returns an alpha-predictive sufficient predictor after a finite number of iterations T bounded by (H(Y) - H(Y|X,E) - alpha - I(Y;Yhat_0))/(p*gamma). Mechanistically, each boosting round fits a weak learner to pseudo-residuals, which implicitly partitions the data into leaf-induced reference classes that align with hidden confounder values; the additive aggregation of these learners progressively reduces the conditional mutual information between prediction errors and environments. A corollary (Corollary 5.1) further establishes that the leaf embeddings recover the hidden confounder U up to bounded uncertainty H(U|Yhat_T) <= beta, explaining why boosting's internal representations cluster by confounder value without ever observing U or E.

## Results

On California Housing (regression), CatBoost achieves the lowest MSE (0.29±0.00) with the highest predictive information (0.56±0.10), while XGBoost achieves MSE 0.31±0.00 with predictive information 0.47±0.03; both achieve zero predictive sufficiency residual. On 20 Newsgroups (classification), CatBoost reaches 62.61% accuracy with predictive information 0.68±0.08 and zero predictive sufficiency, while XGBoost reaches 62.35% accuracy with predictive information 0.27±0.10 and predictive sufficiency 0.03±0.00. The key empirical finding is that higher predictive information I(Y;Ŷ) correlates with better downstream performance, and the predictive sufficiency residual I(Y−Ŷ; E|Ŷ) decreases toward zero as the number of boosting iterations increases, confirming the theoretical guarantee.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares boosting (XGBoost, CatBoost) against two prominent OOD generalization methods: Invariant Risk Minimization (IRM, Arjovsky et al. 2019) and Group DRO (Sagawa et al. 2019) on a synthetic hidden confounding shift setup (Appendix C, Figure C5). When only the primary covariate X is available as input, both XGBoost and CatBoost outperform IRM and Group DRO in OOD MSE, indicating that boosting's implicit environment identification is more effective than explicit invariance or worst-case regularization under hidden confounding. When an additional proxy covariate S (a proxy for the second hidden confounder U₂) is included, Group DRO performs on par with boosting, while IRM still underperforms. This demonstrates that invariance-based methods (IRM) are insufficient for generalization under hidden confounding shift, whereas distributionally robust methods (Group DRO) can match boosting only when proxy information is available. The paper's broader empirical evidence across TableShift benchmark datasets (Readmission, Foodstamps, Diabetes, Income, Unemployment) further confirms that boosting methods achieve near-zero predictive sufficiency residuals and increasing predictive information with more trees, validating the theoretical convergence guarantee.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Reddy et al. (2026), which introduced the predictive-information decomposition for OOD generalization under hidden confounding shift, and on Federici et al. (2021)'s information-theoretic framework for measuring distribution shifts. It departs from prior explanations of boosting's empirical success (variance reduction, feature selection, multicalibration connections à la Wu et al. 2024a and Globus-Harris et al. 2023) by introducing α-predictive sufficiency as a new information-theoretic lens: boosting implicitly partitions data into reference classes aligned with hidden confounder values, and its leaf embeddings recover the latent environment structure. The causal-graph modeling of hidden confounding follows the lineage of Alabdulmohsin et al. (2023), Tsai et al. (2024), and Prashant et al. (2025), while the reference-class framing draws on Hajek (2007) and Hu (2025).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled theoretical account for why boosting consistently outperforms specialized OOD methods (IRM, Group DRO) on real-world tabular data, shifting the explanation from ad-hoc empirical observations to an information-theoretic guarantee (finite-round convergence to α-predictive sufficiency). Key limitations include the common-confounder-support assumption (which rules out genuinely novel test-time confounder values), loose theoretical bounds, and real-world experiments that rely on artificially induced confounding shifts rather than naturally occurring ones. Open questions include whether α-predictive sufficiency can be explicitly estimated or regularized during training, whether the framework extends beyond tabular/tree-ensemble settings to deep networks, and whether the common-support assumption can be relaxed to handle truly unseen confounding patterns.
