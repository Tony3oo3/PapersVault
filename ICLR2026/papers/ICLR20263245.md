## Problem

Prior work on subspace-based safety defenses (SafeLoRA, SaLoRA) assumes that safety alignment corresponds to identifiable linear directions in weight or activation space that can be isolated, preserved, or filtered during fine-tuning. This paper empirs that assumption by asking whether any linear subspace uniquely encodes safety without also encoding general-purpose learning, and whether harmful components of a fine-tuning update can be selectively removed via projection.

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes base, aligned, and safety-tuned checkpoints of five LLMs (Llama 2/3.2, Qwen-2.5 at 1B–7B), fine-tunes them on useful (20K MetaMathQA), harmful (4K BeaverTails), or contaminated (80/20 mix) datasets, and records last-token hidden states for 5,000 prompts per category. Beyond projected model weights, it produces per-layer energy-kept ratios, GSM8K accuracy, AdvBench harmfulness scores (1–5 scale via GPT-4o-mini), and Mode Subspace Overlap (MSO) values computed at swept energy thresholds η, all reported at multiple SVD rank fractions (0.01–0.99).

## Method

The method constructs candidate safety subspaces by reshaping alignment (Δ_A = W_A − W_0) and safety-tuning (Δ_S = W_S − W_0) weight deltas into matrices, performing thin SVD, and selecting top-k right singular vectors to define projection operators P_k and P_k^⊥. Task-specific fine-tuning updates are then projected onto these subspaces (parallel) or their orthogonal complements, and the resulting models are evaluated for utility and harmfulness to test whether safety directions amplify or suppress harmfulness disproportionately. A pairwise MSO metric compares the dominant subspaces of useful, harmful, and negated-alignment updates at matched energy fractions, revealing that useful–harmful overlap exceeds useful–alignment overlap. The analysis extends to activation space by stacking last-token hidden states from useful (MATH) and harmful (BeaverTails, ToxiGen) prompts into matrices and computing MSO across prompt categories at layers in the 65–90% depth range, with random-subspace baselines and control projections (Random-K, Random) throughout.

## Results

Across five LLMs (Llama 3.2 1B, Llama 2 7B, Qwen-2.5 1B/3B/7B), the energy-kept ratio of task-specific updates in the top-k alignment/safety subspace increases linearly with subspace rank and is nearly identical for useful, harmful, and contaminated updates, showing no preferential concentration of safety-relevant energy. For parallel projections (Table 1), Top-K and Random projections yield almost indistinguishable behavioral outcomes: e.g., on Qwen-2.5 1.5B at SVD fraction 0.99, Top-K achieves utility 0.58 and harmfulness 2.09, while Random achieves 0.56 and 2.09; on Llama-3.2 1B, Top-K gives utility 0.34 and harmfulness 4.07 versus Random's 0.34 and 4.07. For orthogonal projections on contaminated data (Table 2), removing top-k alignment components degrades utility and harmfulness at comparable rates (e.g., Qwen-2.5 7B: utility drops from 0.81 to 0.78 while harmfulness drops from 1.96 to 1.67 at fraction 0.99), confirming no selective suppression of harmfulness. MSO analysis reveals that the strongest subspace overlap is between useful and harmful fine-tuning updates, not between alignment and harmful updates, directly contradicting the hypothesis of a shared safety subspace.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new method to compare against external baselines; instead, it uses three internal projection strategies—Top-K (top singular vectors of the alignment/safety update), Random-K (randomly sampled singular vectors from the same SVD), and Random (SVD of a random matrix)—as controls to test whether the alignment/safety subspace is meaningfully distinct. The central finding is that Top-K projections produce behavioral outcomes (both utility gains and harmfulness increases) that are statistically indistinguishable from Random and Random-K projections across all five models and all SVD fractions, indicating the alignment subspace carries no unique safety signal. In the contaminated fine-tuning setting (orthogonal projection), removing Top-K alignment components reduces utility more sharply than Random projections while harmfulness decreases at a similar rate, ruling out selective harmfulness suppression. The MSO pairwise comparisons further show that useful and harmful fine-tuning updates share the greatest subspace overlap (exceeding the random baseline), while the alignment–harmful overlap is never the highest and is sometimes the lowest, directly refuting the hypothesis that a shared safety subspace exists. These patterns hold consistently across both RLHF-aligned and DPO-safety-tuned models, and across all individual layers, confirming the entanglement is architectural rather than method-specific.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the task arithmetic paradigm (Ilharco et al., 2022) for representing model modifications as weight-space deltas, and specifically on SafeLoRA (Hsu et al., 2024) and SaLoRA (Li et al., 2025), which construct candidate 'safety subspaces' via SVD of alignment or safety-tuning deltas and project fine-tuning updates onto or away from them. The paper's conceptual departure is to treat these subspaces as hypotheses to be tested rather than established mechanisms, asking whether the top singular directions of Δ_A or Δ_S actually encode safety-specific information or merely general learning sensitivity. It also positions itself relative to activation steering work (Arditi et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2024), explicitly distinguishing local linear controllability of refusal from the stronger claim of a stable, model-level safety subspace that is invariant under fine-tuning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By demonstrating across five models, two alignment methods (RLHF and DPO), and both weight and activation spaces that safety is deeply entangled with general learning rather than linearly separable, this paper identifies a fundamental limitation for an entire class of projection-based defenses (SafeLoRA, SaLoRA, and similar). Practitioners relying on subspace filtering to preserve safety during fine-tuning should expect proportional utility loss alongside any safety gain, with no selective suppression of harmfulness. The open question it raises is what alternative strategies—non-linear constraints, distributional methods, or architectural modifications—can preserve safety under continued training without the strict utility trade-off that linear projections impose.
