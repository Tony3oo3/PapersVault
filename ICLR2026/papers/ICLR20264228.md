## Problem

Bridging the sim-to-real gap in dexterous grasping requires accurate physical parameters (especially object mass) in the simulation, but existing real-to-sim methods either rely on domain randomization that obscures true dynamics or on system identification that is non-differentiable and scales poorly. No prior framework jointly performs geometric reconstruction, end-to-end mass identification from video and robot control signals, and force-aware policy learning in a single differentiable pipeline.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264228/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes ~300–340 RGB images per object captured from a mobile device (for scene and object reconstruction), human demonstration videos processed frame-by-frame through HaMeR and MCC-HO to extract 6-DoF hand and object poses, and consistent robot control signals executed in both the real world and simulation. Outputs include a collision mesh (13K–68K vertices), a 3D Gaussian Splat rendering representation, an optimized scalar mass value, and a 19-dimensional policy output (16 joint positions, 2 contact-constraint terms, and 1 scalar grasping force computed as m·g/n_active).

## Method

D-REX is a four-stage differentiable real-to-sim-to-real engine. First, 3D and 2D Gaussian Splats are trained separately on scene/object videos to produce a photorealistic rendering set and a surface-normal-consistent collision mesh. Second, object mass is identified by executing identical robot pushing actions in MuJoCo/Brax and the real world, then minimizing the L2 trajectory discrepancy via backpropagation through a semi-implicit Euler integrator coupled with a penalty-based contact model (GradSim), requiring no ground-truth mass or contact points. Third, human hand-object poses from video are retargeted to the robotic hand via Dex-Retargeting to produce executable robot demonstrations. Fourth, a multi-head policy network conditioned on positionally-encoded mesh vertices and the identified mass predicts joint positions, contact constraints, and a mass-scaled grasping force, trained in two stages (supervised position learning, then force-aware fine-tuning) to achieve stable grasps across varying object masses.

## Results

For mass identification across six diverse objects (varying shapes, sizes, and mass scales from 59 g to 726 g), D-REX achieves percentile errors ranging from 4.8% (Cookie) to 12.0% (Letter U), with a mean error of approximately 8.6%. For objects with identical geometry but different internal densities, mass is recovered with deviations under 13 g. In the force-aware grasping cross-evaluation, mass-matched policies achieve 75–95% success rates, while mass-mismatched policies drop to 15–40%, confirming that accurate mass conditioning is critical. Against two baselines on eight tabletop objects, D-REX consistently outperforms both in grasp success rate with substantially lower variance, and its advantage grows as object mass increases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares D-REX against two baselines on eight tabletop objects with diverse geometries and masses: DexGraspNet 2.0 (a large-scale simulation-trained dexterous grasping policy) and Human2Sim2Robot (a recent real-to-sim-to-real method that learns from RGBD human demonstration videos). D-REX consistently outperforms both baselines across all eight objects in grasp success rate, with substantially lower variance. A key differentiator is robustness to object mass: while both baselines show degrading performance as object mass increases, D-REX's force-aware policy (conditioned on the identified mass) maintains stable, reliable grasps across the full mass range. The cross-evaluation experiment (Table 3) further demonstrates that the advantage is specifically due to mass-aware force control: policies trained on a mismatched mass drop to 15–40% success, whereas mass-matched policies reach 75–95%, a gap that position-only baselines cannot close.

## Relation to Prior Work

D-REX sits at the intersection of three research threads: differentiable physics for system identification (GradSim, Brax), Gaussian Splatting-based real-to-sim reconstruction (3DGS, 2DGS, Robo-GS), and human-to-robot demonstration transfer (HaMeR, MCC-HO, Dex-Retargeting). Its key departure from GradSim is replacing manually specified external forces with consistent robotic control signals executed in both simulation and the real world, enabling end-to-end mass optimization without ground-truth contact information. Unlike prior real-to-sim frameworks that produce geometrically accurate but physically uncalibrated digital twins, D-REX closes the loop by using the reconstructed geometry as input to a differentiable dynamics engine that identifies mass from trajectory matching. The force-aware policy conditioned on identified mass extends position-only imitation learning approaches (DexGraspNet 2.0, Human2Sim2Robot) by adding a physics-grounded control dimension.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that accurate mass identification via differentiable simulation is a practical and necessary prerequisite for force-aware dexterous grasping, offering an alternative to domain randomization for reducing the sim-to-real gap. However, the scope is limited: only mass is identified (not friction, center-of-mass offset, or other parameters), the identification relies on a specific planar pushing task with a virtual fulcrum assumption, and FoundationPose z-axis errors sometimes require manual correction. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to multi-object scenes, deformable objects, or online mass adaptation during manipulation, and whether the 30–35 minute offline reconstruction per object is acceptable for deployment at scale.
