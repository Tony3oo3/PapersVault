## Problem

Amortized Bayesian inference (ABI) with neural networks produces highly biased posterior approximations when evaluated on observations outside the simulated training distribution, and this failure cannot be corrected by additional simulations due to the poor pre-asymptotic behavior of neural posterior estimators. Existing robustness methods either require ground-truth parameters for calibration, apply post-hoc corrections, introduce adversarial defenses, or alter the target distribution away from the analytic posterior. This paper addresses the gap by enabling robust ABI without any of these compromises.

## Input / Output

The method consumes two data streams during training: labeled simulated pairs (θ, x) from the generative model and unlabeled observations x* from any source (including real data with no known parameters). At inference time, a single forward pass through the trained normalizing flow (neural spline flow or cINN) yields the full posterior density q(θ|x), from which posterior samples, means, and standard deviations are extracted. The unlabeled data can be as few as 4 observations, and the approach handles inputs ranging from 2D to 784-dimensional (flattened MNIST images) and 200-dimensional time series (Hodgkin-Huxley membrane potentials).

## Method

The core contribution is a semi-supervised training objective that augments the standard simulation-based NPE loss with a Bayesian self-consistency (SC) loss evaluated on unlabeled data. The SC loss minimizes the variance of the log marginal-likelihood ratio log p(x*|θ) + log p(θ) − log q(θ|x*) over θ samples drawn from the current approximate posterior, enforcing that Bayes' rule holds on observed data. The authors prove (Propositions 1–4) that this loss is strictly proper: it is globally minimized if and only if q(θ|x) equals the analytic posterior, meaning it targets the same distribution as the NPE loss with no trade-off. In practice, the SC loss is weighted by λ (default 1, with a linear warmup schedule for high-dimensional settings) and uses the current posterior q_t(θ|h(x*)) as the proposal distribution for θ sampling, concentrating the consistency check in high-density regions. The approach is architecture-agnostic and applies to any density-evaluable neural posterior estimator, demonstrated on neural spline flows and conditional invertible neural networks across four case studies.

## Results

On the Eurostat air-traffic autoregressive model (15 countries, 5 parameters), adding the self-consistency loss with M=8 unlabeled countries reduces mean posterior bias for the autoregressive coefficient β from 0.153±0.024 (NPE) to 0.031±0.023 (NPE+SC), and Wasserstein distance from 0.161±0.024 to 0.054±0.023, closely matching the Stan MCMC reference. In the multivariate normal toy problem, standard NPE completely collapses (zero variance) for observations at μ_obs ≥ 2, while NPE+SC maintains near-perfect posterior estimation even at μ_obs > 3, with MMD to the analytic posterior reduced by orders of magnitude. On the Hodgkin-Huxley neuron model (7 parameters, 200-dim time series), NPE+SC consistently reduces mean absolute predictive bias across 1000 out-of-simulation datasets. For MNIST image denoising (784-dim), NPLE+SC produces visibly smoother reconstructions with coherent edge-localized uncertainty, whereas NPLE alone yields pixelated, patchy outputs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Stan MCMC reference, NPE+SC (M=8) achieves near-reference accuracy on all five air-traffic parameters (e.g., β mean bias 0.031 vs. NPE's 0.153), while standard NPE shows large deviations for many countries. Under prior misspecification in the multivariate normal model (Figure 8), NPE+SC dramatically outperforms NPE-MMD (Huang et al., 2023), NPE-DANN (Elsemüller et al., 2025), and NNPE (Ward et al., 2022), all of which fail or degrade further as misspecification increases; NPE+SC also requires far fewer unlabeled samples (as few as 4 vs. large unlabeled sets for UDA methods). In the air-traffic setting (Table 2, Figure 12), NPE+SC substantially outperforms a VAE baseline (Kingma & Welling, 2013) on all metrics (e.g., log(σ) Wasserstein: 0.016 vs. 0.144) and also outperforms the supervised SC method of Schmitt et al. (2024), which evaluates the SC loss only on simulated data and thus cannot overcome model misspecification (log(σ) Wasserstein: 0.016 vs. 0.459). The key distinction is that NPE+SC targets the analytic posterior via a strictly proper loss, whereas UDA and regularization-based methods implicitly shift the target distribution.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Schmitt et al. (2024), which introduced Bayesian self-consistency (SC) losses to improve simulation efficiency in a fully supervised, simulation-only setting, by applying them to unsupervised training on unlabeled real observations. It departs from prior robustness methods for amortized Bayesian inference (post-hoc corrections by Ward et al. 2022, adversarial regularization by Gloeckler et al. 2023, labeled calibration by Wehenkel et al. 2024, domain adaptation by Elsemüller et al. 2025) by proving that SC losses are strictly proper and target the same analytic posterior as standard simulation-based losses, thereby avoiding the accuracy-robustness trade-off that plagues all other approaches. The method is positioned as one of the first instantiations of semi-supervised ABI, requiring no ground-truth parameters for the unlabeled data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The strict properness result is practically significant: it means practitioners can add SC losses to any existing NPE pipeline without altering the statistical target, removing a key barrier to robust deployment of ABI in real-world settings where model misspecification is the norm. The empirical finding that as few as four unlabeled observations yield substantial robustness gains (versus 1024 labeled simulations) makes the approach accessible in data-scarce scientific domains. Key limitations remain: the variance-based SC loss requires fast density evaluations, ruling out flow matching and score-based diffusion; the approximate-likelihood (NPLE) setting still shows meaningful degradation compared to known-likelihood cases; and joint learning of very high-dimensional likelihoods remains open.
