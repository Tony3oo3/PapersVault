## Problem

Prior LLM reasoning evaluations focus on overall accuracy, leaving open whether the *composition* of errors mirrors known human fallacy patterns. No existing benchmark systematically links model errors to predictions from a formal cognitive theory of human reasoning, making it impossible to test whether stronger models converge toward human-like error profiles rather than simply reducing error rates.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263519/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each reasoning problem is a list of ETR views — disjunctive sets of monadic atomic propositions in prenex form — rendered into natural language via one of 12 thematic templates (e.g., alchemy, quantum particles, biotech organisms) to control for content effects and resist data contamination. The model's free-text response is parsed back into ETR view format (via GPT-4.1-mini as a translation layer without access to the original premises) and evaluated on two axes: logical correctness (checked with PySMT) and ETR fallacy prediction (checked with PyETR's default_procedure_does_it_follow), yielding a per-model fallacy rate defined as the proportion of logically incorrect answers that are ETR-predicted fallacies.

## Method

The paper uses PyETR, an open-source implementation of the Erotetic Theory of Reasoning, to programmatically generate 383 reasoning problems with guaranteed ETR-predicted fallacies. Starting from a bank of template problems (modus ponens, disjunction fallacy, quantified inferences), a generative pipeline applies random mutation rules (predicate/constant/variable addition, atom negation, disjunctive state insertion) and filters for problems where ETR's default procedure yields a single categorical fallacious conclusion of 4–11 atoms. The key analytical contribution is shifting the evaluation axis from error rate to error composition: across 38 models, the proportion of incorrect answers matching ETR-predicted fallacies rises significantly with Chatbot Arena Elo (ρ=0.360, p=0.0265), while overall correctness shows no correlation with capability. A premise-order reversal intervention further demonstrates that LLMs exhibit the same non-commutativity effects documented in human reasoners, with significant fallacy-blocking on a majority of models.

## Results

Across 38 models evaluated on 383 PyETR-generated reasoning problems, a significant positive Spearman correlation is found between Chatbot Arena Elo and the proportion of logically incorrect answers that match ETR-predicted human fallacies (ρ=0.360, p=0.0265; Pearson r=0.364, p=0.0247). In contrast, overall logical correctness shows no correlation with model capability (r=0.004, p=0.981), with mean accuracy of only 40.6% (σ=16.7%) across all models. The premise-order intervention study (Table 4) shows that reversing premise order significantly blocks fallacy production for a majority of models, with blocking rates ranging from 20% to 88% and statistically significant z-tests (p<0.05) for 24 of 38 models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new method to compare against baselines; instead, it evaluates 38 existing LLMs against ETR's formal predictions of human fallacy patterns. The key cross-model comparison reveals a dissociation: stronger models (higher Chatbot Arena Elo) produce a larger *proportion* of their errors as ETR-predicted fallacies (ρ=0.360, p=0.0265), yet show no improvement in overall logical correctness (r=0.004, p=0.981). This pattern is robust across alternative capability proxies: training compute (ρ=0.489, p=0.0334) and HELM composite scores (r=0.796, p=0.0103). The premise-order intervention further differentiates models: 24 of 38 show statistically significant fallacy-blocking when premise order is reversed, with top-Elo models like gpt-4.5-preview (68% blocked) and claude-3.5-sonnet (65% blocked) showing strong effects, while some mid-range models (e.g., o1-mini, p=0.563) show no significant order sensitivity. The authors explicitly note this is a correlational finding and do not attribute causality to any specific architectural or training mechanism.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper operationalises the Erotetic Theory of Reasoning (ETR) and its open-source implementation PyETR as a generative engine for LLM evaluation, extending the first application of ETR to LLMs by Koralus & Wang-Maścianica (2023), which was limited to the GPT model family and did not use PyETR. It sits at the intersection of the human fallacy literature (Tversky & Kahneman, Evans, Walsh & Johnson-Laird) and the growing body of work documenting human-like reasoning failures in LLMs (distractability, content effects, order effects), but departs from prior LLM reasoning evaluations by shifting the analytic focus from error rate to error composition—specifically, whether errors match theory-predicted fallacies. The synthetic, mutation-based problem generation pipeline is novel in providing unbounded, contamination-resistant tasks grounded in a formal cognitive theory rather than fixed benchmark items.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper introduces a complementary evaluation axis (error composition) that reveals a dissociation invisible to standard accuracy metrics: stronger models produce a higher proportion of human-like fallacies among their errors while overall correctness remains flat. This challenges the implicit assumption that scaling drives convergence toward normative reasoning and raises open questions about whether the effect stems from training on human reasoning traces, RLHF convergence, or architectural properties. Limitations include the correlational (not causal) nature of the main finding, moderate effect size (ρ=0.360), restriction to monadic predicates, and the use of Chatbot Arena Elo as a single primary capability proxy. The open-source PyETR pipeline and 383-problem corpus provide a reusable foundation for future work on theory-grounded LLM reasoning evaluation.
