## Problem

Existing logic puzzle benchmarks (e.g., Enigmata, ZebraLogic) focus on canonical puzzle formats, making it impossible to distinguish whether Large Reasoning Models genuinely apply logical rules or merely recall memorized solution patterns. Whether LRMs can flexibly adapt to non-canonical variants—irregular subgrids, novel constraints, altered rules, or deliberately unsolvable instances—remains an open question, and no systematic benchmark exists to probe this long-tail of logical reasoning.

## Input / Output

Each puzzle is presented as a text-based grid or constraint description instantiated from Jinja2 templates, with a standardized JSON output schema requiring the model to return either a structured solution array or {"solvable": false, "solution": null} for unsolvable instances. The evaluation protocol samples 4 runs per puzzle at temperature 0.6 with a 32,768-token reasoning budget, and correctness is determined by exact match against the ground-truth solution. The benchmark comprises 5,250 puzzles across 10 games (Sudoku, Skyscraper, Kakurasu, Crypto, Navigation, Binario, Minesweeper, Hanoi, Hitori, Zebralogic) spanning 6 puzzle categories.

## Method

HardcoreLogic systematically transforms canonical puzzles along three dimensions: Increased Complexity (IC1 search-space expansion via larger grids/fewer givens; IC2 constraint strengthening via looser relational operators or hidden clues), Uncommon Elements (UE1 form variation such as letter encoding or irregular subgrids; UE2 rule variation such as diagonal constraints or landmine-cluster semantics), and Unsolvable Puzzles (UP) constructed by introducing contradictions into previously solvable instances. Complexity is quantified algorithmically—closed-form search-space formulas, Z3 solver decisions/conflicts for CSP puzzles, Dijkstra node counts for graph puzzles, and LRM perplexity for form variation—and a weighted multiple linear regression attributes accuracy drops to each transformation type. The paper evaluates 18+ open- and closed-source LRMs, then conducts a systematic error analysis classifying failures into six categories (misunderstanding, misapplied framework, brute-force, factual errors, over-verification, infinite repetition) for solvable puzzles and four categories for unsolvable ones, using GPT-5, Gemini-2.5-Pro, and Claude-Sonnet-4.5 as majority-vote annotators with manual adjudication.

## Results

Across all evaluated LRMs, HardcoreLogic induces substantial accuracy drops relative to the Original benchmark (sourced from Enigmata and ZebraLogic). Among open-source models, gpt-oss-120b achieves the highest accuracy on both Original and HardcoreLogic, while GPT-5 leads among closed-source models; Minimax-M1 performs worst overall. A weighted multiple linear regression (Figure 6) quantifies the per-transformation accuracy penalty: IC1 (search space expansion) dominates, with coefficients as low as −74.09 for Binario and −43.45 for Hitori; UE1 (form variation) peaks at −23.11 for Sudoku (irregular subgrids); and UE2 (rule variation) reaches −29.60 for Minesweeper (cluster hints). On unsolvable puzzles, closed-source models outperform open-source ones, and stronger models (gpt-oss-120b, Qwen3-235B) provide justified unsolvability explanations while weaker models (Minimax-M1) frequently output unjustified 'unsolvable' claims.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between the Original benchmark (1,389 puzzles from Enigmata and ZebraLogic) and the proposed HardcoreLogic (5,250 long-tail-transformed puzzles). Every evaluated model—ranging from 8B distilled models to 1T-parameter Kimi-K2-Instruct and closed-source GPT-5—suffers a significant accuracy drop on HardcoreLogic, confirming that even SOTA models relying on memorized canonical patterns fail on non-canonical variants. Kimi-K2-Instruct exhibits the largest relative degradation, with ~50% of its errors attributable to misunderstanding puzzle rules or misapplying solution frameworks. Among open-source models, gpt-oss-120b maintains the highest absolute accuracy on both datasets, while Minimax-M1 is consistently the weakest, often forcing incorrect 'solutions' on unsolvable puzzles or outputting 'unsolvable' without genuine logical justification. The regression analysis shows that IC1 (search space expansion) is the dominant difficulty driver across most puzzles, but UE1 (form variation, e.g., irregular Sudoku subgrids) and UE2 (rule variation, e.g., Minesweeper cluster hints) also cause substantial drops even without increasing the combinatorial search space, indicating that models struggle with structural novelty independent of raw complexity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Enigmata (Chen et al., 2025a) and ZebraLogic (Lin et al., 2025a) as its 'Original' baseline, extending their canonical puzzle instances through a systematic long-tail transformation framework spanning three dimensions: Increased Complexity, Uncommon Elements, and Unsolvable Puzzles. It positions itself within the broader 'long-tail benchmarking' literature (JustLogic, LINT, SATbench) that targets out-of-distribution generalization, but is distinctive in applying structured, multi-dimensional transformations to a diverse set of 10 logic puzzle games rather than a single task. The work also connects to the observation (Inger et al., 2025) that LLMs overfit to canonical puzzle formats, providing a controlled empirical test of this hypothesis through matched Original-vs-Transformed comparisons.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

HardcoreLogic provides a scalable, reproducible protocol for stress-testing LRM reasoning beyond memorized patterns, and its 5,000+ puzzles across 10 games make it likely to become a standard evaluation for reasoning models. The finding that even SOTA models like GPT-5 suffer substantial accuracy drops, combined with the systematic error taxonomy (factual errors, brute-force, misunderstanding), gives practitioners concrete diagnostic signals for where reasoning breaks down. A key open question the paper surfaces is whether the observed failures reflect a fundamental limitation in LLMs' ability to flexibly apply rules to novel conditions, or whether targeted training on long-tail variants could close the gap. The unsolvable-puzzle dimension also opens a new evaluation axis—testing whether models can genuinely detect logical inconsistency versus merely failing to find an answer.
