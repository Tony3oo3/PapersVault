## Problem

Existing brain activity models—whether statistical (ICA, DCM) or continuous-valued deep learning (GNNs, Transformers, JEPA-style foundation models)—ignore the discrete, event-driven firing characteristics inherent to biological neural systems. This limits their ability to capture the temporal driving relationships and causal interactions between brain regions, constraining both prediction accuracy and clinical interpretability for diseases like epilepsy and Alzheimer's.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263694/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes resting-state fMRI time series (90 AAL-template ROIs × 240 or 197 time points) and first converts them into spike trains via a 3-layer IIR-filter-based LIF network before any Transformer processing. With K=89 anchor regions fixed, the model predicts the single remaining target region's fMRI time series; downstream, the same trained model yields disease classification labels (binary), statistically significant abnormal region maps (via paired t-tests on group-averaged predictions vs. ground truth), and effective connectivity matrices (via Gaussian pulse perturbation of individual regions).

## Method

Spike-DB is a self-supervised masked-prediction framework that operates entirely in spike representation space. fMRI signals are encoded into spike trains by a stack of IIR-filter-based LIF neurons (capturing high/low-frequency and long-range temporal dynamics), then processed by a Spike Transformer backbone serving as anchor encoder, target encoder, and predictor. The target encoder is updated via Exponential Moving Average (EMA) of the anchor encoder weights (JEPA-style), and the predictor is trained with MSE loss on average spike firing rates rather than raw values. A fully connected spike decoder maps predicted embeddings back to fMRI time series. For effective connectivity inference, a Gaussian pulse perturbation (amplitude k=1, width α=20, centered at T/2) is injected into individual regions, and the prediction difference between perturbed and unperturbed runs quantifies directed connectivity.

## Results

On the primary fMRI time series prediction task, Spike-DB achieves state-of-the-art R² scores across all six datasets, ranging from 0.954 (EMCI) to 0.983 (NC-E), with corresponding RSE values from 0.131 to 0.213. Compared to the strongest baseline BrainSymphony, Spike-DB improves R² by approximately 1% on epilepsy data (FLE, TLE, NC-E) and 0.8% on ADNI data (EMCI, SMC, NC-A), while reducing RSE by 14.9% and 11.4% respectively. On the downstream brain disease classification task, Spike-DB achieves the best ACC and F1 on all four binary tasks, reaching 86.95% ACC and 87.32% F1 on the most challenging SMC vs. NC-A task. The model also uses only 2.7M parameters and 96.73 s per epoch, the fewest parameters and shortest training time among all compared methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Spike-DB outperforms all six baselines on every dataset for both R² and RSE in the primary prediction task. The closest competitor, BrainSymphony (a lightweight Transformer with parallel spatial-temporal streams), is surpassed by ~0.9–1.0% in R² and ~11–15% in RSE; for example, on NC-E Spike-DB reaches R²=0.983 vs. BrainSymphony's 0.974 and RSE=0.131 vs. 0.162. Brain-JEPA (JEPA-based latent prediction) and BrainMass (masked ROI self-supervised learning) trail by 2–3% in R², while BrainLM (masked autoencoding) lags furthest at 0.922–0.936 R². On the classification task, Spike-DB beats Starformer (the strongest classification-specific baseline) by 1.9% ACC on SMC vs. NC-A (86.95% vs. 85.92%) and by 1.86% ACC on FLE vs. NC-E (91.67% vs. 89.81%). Notably, Spike-DB achieves these gains with only 2.7M parameters and 96.73 s/epoch training time, compared to Brain-JEPA's 22.0M parameters and BrainMass's 284.65 s/epoch, indicating a favorable accuracy-efficiency trade-off.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of brain activity fundamental models (BrainLM, BrainMass, Brain-JEPA, BrainSymphony) by replacing their continuous-valued Transformer encoders with a spike-based architecture. It draws on the IIR-filter-based LIF neuron formulation (Fang et al., 2020) and the Spike Transformer (Yao et al., 2023) to encode fMRI BOLD signals as discrete spike trains, then applies a JEPA-style masked prediction objective in spike space. The key conceptual departure from prior digital brain models (virtual brain, digital twin brain) is the explicit use of a spiking computing paradigm to capture the discrete firing characteristics of biological neurons, rather than relying on purely statistical or continuous-valued frameworks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Spike-DB offers a biologically grounded inductive bias for brain time series modeling, and its downstream analyses (abnormal region identification, effective connectivity via Gaussian pulse perturbation) align well with established clinical findings in epilepsy and Alzheimer's disease, lending interpretive credibility. The efficiency gain is notable—2.7M parameters and ~97 s/epoch versus 22M and ~128 s for Brain-JEPA—suggesting spike-based architectures could scale to higher-resolution or longer-duration fMRI data. Open questions include whether the spike mechanism's advantage persists at higher spatial resolution (beyond 90 AAL ROIs), whether the IIR filter parameters are truly capturing biologically meaningful dynamics or simply acting as a learned temporal filter, and how the approach generalizes to other neuroimaging modalities (EEG, MEG) where spike-like signals are more directly observable.
