## Problem

Differential privacy (DP) is widely applied to adapt pretrained LLMs for sensitive tasks, offering formal guarantees, yet its practical effectiveness remains unclear because pretraining data overlaps and interdependencies with adaptation data can undermine privacy despite DP efforts. Prior work has studied pretraining leakage, non-private adaptation leakage, or synthetic canary auditing in isolation, but no structured benchmark exists that quantifies how the distributional relationship between pretraining and adaptation data, the choice of adaptation method, and the privacy budget jointly determine empirical privacy risk.

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes pretrained LLMs (Pythia 70M–1.4B, GPT-Neo 125M–1.3B, OLMo 1B, OLMo2 1B), adaptation datasets spanning a controlled spectrum from exact overlap with the Pile pretraining set through IID validation splits to OOD data (SAMSum, GermanWiki), four DP adaptation methods (Full Fine-Tune, Head Fine-Tune, LoRA, Prefix Tuning), and privacy budgets ε ∈ {0.1, 0.5, 1, 3, 8, ∞}. Distributional distance is quantified via Wasserstein distance on Sentence-BERT embeddings to validate dataset classifications. Outputs include RMIA AUC scores under multiple shadow/reference model configurations, canary exposure scores (with k=10 extractability), privacy-utility trade-off curves (perplexity vs. AUC), and counts of verbatim memorized pretraining samples, all reported per model size, dataset, method, and ε.

## Method

The paper constructs a systematic benchmark that varies three axes simultaneously: (1) adaptation data distribution relative to pretraining (overlap, IID, OOD), (2) DP adaptation method (Full Fine-Tune, Head Fine-Tune, DP-LoRA, DP-Prefix Tuning), and (3) privacy budget, across seven pretrained LLMs trained on the Pile and six adaptation datasets. For privacy auditing, it deploys RMIA (the strongest SOTA MIA) in offline mode with a single shadow model, complemented by Reference and Min-K% attacks, plus canary data extraction with adversarial prefixes to measure exposure and k-extractability. A key empirical finding is that distributional closeness—not direct overlap—drives privacy risk: IID validation data leaks as much as overlapping training data, and LoRA consistently achieves the lowest AUC for OOD data. The paper further formalizes a four-stage holistic privacy auditing framework (pretraining audit, adaptation audit, joint audit, post-adaptation pretraining audit) by redefining the membership inference adversarial game to account for two datasets S and D and two sequential training stages T and T′, with distinct null/alternative hypotheses per stage.

## Results

Using RMIA (shadow) on Pythia 1B, the average MIA AUC at ε=8 is 0.73 for OOD data (SAMSum, GermanWiki) versus 0.78 for IID data (Bookcorpus2, GitHub, Enron), confirming that distributional closeness to pretraining drives leakage even without overlap. At the strongest privacy regime (ε=0.1), LoRA on OOD data achieves an AUC of 0.58 (near random), while Full Fine-Tune and Head Fine-Tune remain at 0.59 and 0.66 respectively. For IID data at ε=0.1, all methods still show elevated AUCs (0.52–0.75), with Full Fine-Tune reaching 0.75, indicating that moderate privacy budgets (ε=8) are insufficient for practical protection of in-distribution adaptation data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The benchmark compares four DP adaptation methods (Full Fine-Tune, Head Fine-Tune, LoRA, Prefix Tuning) under two attack setups (RMIA with shadow model, Reference with pretrained model) across OOD and IID datasets. For OOD data at ε=8, LoRA achieves the lowest average AUC (0.64) versus Full Fine-Tune (0.77) and Head Fine-Tune (0.87), making it the most protective; Prefix Tuning is competitive at 0.63. For IID data at ε=8, the gap narrows: Head Fine-Tune (0.70) and LoRA (0.71) are marginally better than Full Fine-Tune (0.80) and Prefix Tuning (0.90). The RMIA shadow attack is dramatically more effective than the Reference attack—e.g., at ε=8 on OOD, RMIA achieves AUC 0.63–0.87 while Reference stays near 0.50–0.51—highlighting that attacker knowledge of the pretrained model is the dominant risk factor. At ε=0.1, LoRA on OOD reaches AUC 0.58 (essentially random), while Full Fine-Tune on IID still leaks at AUC 0.75, showing that only the tightest privacy budgets combined with PEFT methods provide practical protection for in-distribution data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the privacy auditing of LLM adaptations (PrivAuditor, PrivLM-bench, LLM-PBE) by systematically studying how the distributional relationship between pretraining and adaptation data affects empirical privacy leakage under DP. While prior benchmarks either focused on non-private adaptations or treated pretraining as a fixed, opaque component, this work explicitly varies the adaptation data from exact overlap through IID to OOD relative to the known Pile pretraining corpus. It also departs from standard single-stage MIA formulations by introducing a four-stage adversarial game framework that jointly models pretraining and adaptation leakage, building on the membership inference game of Yeom et al. (2018) and Jayaraman et al. (2020).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that distributional closeness alone (without direct overlap) drives privacy leakage as strongly as overlap is a practical warning for deploying DP-adapted LLMs in sensitive domains: choosing a pretrained model whose training distribution resembles the adaptation data undermines the formal DP guarantee. The holistic auditing framework formalizes a gap that practitioners face but that prior work treated in isolation, though it remains a formalization without fully instantiated attacks for all four stages. Open questions include how to audit when pretraining data is undisclosed (the common case), whether the distributional-closeness effect persists at larger model scales, and how to compose privacy budgets across pretraining and adaptation stages.
