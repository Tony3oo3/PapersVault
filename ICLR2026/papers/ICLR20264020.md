## Problem

Understanding why RL policies succeed or fail is difficult because parameter-rich neural networks map complex, high-dimensional observation spaces to actions with indirect, nonlinear consequences on cumulative reward. Existing policy-level explanation methods either require predefined causal graphs, explain only individual actions, or rely on linear reductions that cannot capture the nonlinear, trajectory-dependent behavioral patterns typical of continuous-control policies. This paper addresses the gap by formulating policy explanation as a causal model reduction problem and extending the linear TCR framework to the nonlinear regime with theoretical identifiability guarantees.

## Input / Output

The method consumes pre-collected RL episodes from continuous-state/action environments (Pendulum, robot table tennis), where each episode's actions are perturbed by independent Gaussian shifts δA_t ~ N(0, σ_t) to create shift interventions; episodes are padded to fixed length T and the target is the cumulative reward over a designated time window. The output is a pair of reduction maps: τ₁ decomposed per feature per timestep as a weighted sum of fixed Gaussian kernels (yielding interpretable heatmaps over state/action trajectories), and ω₁ giving the corresponding high-level intervention mapping, together with a scalar causal coefficient α linking the high-level cause Z to the target Y.

## Method

nTCR learns nonlinear reduction maps τ₁ and ω₁ by minimizing an interventional consistency loss that matches the push-forward interventional distributions of the low-level SCM to a linear additive Gaussian high-level model, augmented by a normality regularization term (1-Wasserstein distance between the standardized high-level cause distribution and N(0,1)) to prevent overfitting to non-Gaussian multimodal solutions. The reduction maps are parameterized by an interpretable function class: each feature's trajectory is encoded through equally spaced Gaussian kernels with learned weights, enabling direct attribution of which state/action values at which timesteps drive the high-level cause. The paper proves uniqueness of exact solutions under non-vanishing Fourier transform conditions on the exogenous noise (Prop. 4.1) and constructs a family of nonlinear additive-noise SCMs admitting exact solutions (Prop. 4.2), validating the approach on synthetic data before applying it to Pendulum and table tennis policies where it uncovers directional biases and failure modes invisible to linear TCR.

## Results

On synthetic causal models (11-dimensional, 9 cause + 2 target variables), nTCR achieves near-zero consistency loss and converges to the unique ground-truth solution predicted by Proposition 4.2, validating both theory and implementation. On the Pendulum task, nTCR uncovers a previously unnoticed clockwise-vs-counterclockwise performance bias in Policy A (higher rewards for right-quadrant starts) and correctly identifies that Policy B would benefit from more negative torque near the top position. On the robot table tennis task, nTCR reveals that the arm swinging forward too early (before ball arrival) causes low rewards, and that balls traveling toward the outside table edge or bouncing closer to the net are systematically harder to return.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is linear TCR [Kekić et al., UAI 2024], compared on the Pendulum task (Appendix F.2, Fig. F). For Policy A, linear TCR fails to capture the critical clockwise/counterclockwise asymmetry: its τ-maps show only predominantly negative weights in the second half of the episode for θ and torque, with inconsistent, uninterpretable patterns in the first half, completely missing the two distinct trajectory classes that nTCR clearly separates. For Policy B, both linear TCR and nTCR agree that shifting torque toward more negative values would improve performance, confirming that this simpler failure mode is capturable by linear relationships. The paper does not report a quantitative metric (e.g., consistency loss) for the linear TCR comparison on the RL tasks; the comparison is qualitative, based on whether the learned reduction maps reveal the correct behavioral patterns. On the table tennis task, only nTCR is applied, with no linear TCR baseline shown.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends linear Targeted Causal Reduction (TCR) [Kekić et al., UAI 2024] to nonlinear reduction maps, introducing theoretical guarantees (uniqueness and existence of exact solutions) for a class of nonlinear additive noise SCMs. It draws on the broader causal abstraction literature (Rubenstein et al., Beckers & Halpern) for the interventional consistency criterion, and positions itself within the policy-level explanation (PLE) subcategory of explainable RL (Milani et al. taxonomy). The key departure from prior work is the combination of nonlinear expressivity with an interpretable Gaussian-kernel function class and a Wasserstein-based normality regularization, applied specifically to RL policy explanation via shift interventions on actions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, model-agnostic framework for generating causal explanations of trained RL policies, which is critical for safety assessment before real-world deployment (e.g., robotics, autonomous systems). Key limitations include the requirement for many perturbed episodes (making it primarily suited to simulation), the linear-Gaussian high-level model assumption, and restriction to shift interventions on continuous action spaces. Open questions include extending to discrete action spaces, image-based observations, and whether the identifiability guarantees hold under finite intervention sampling (the paper conjectures a negative result). The finding that linear TCR completely misses trajectory-class distinctions (e.g., clockwise vs. counterclockwise pendulum bias) empirically validates the need for nonlinear reductions.
