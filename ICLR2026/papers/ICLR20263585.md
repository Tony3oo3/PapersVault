## Problem

When training RNNs with truncated backpropagation through time (TBPTT) and zero initialization, the resulting internal transients degrade learning, yet the burn-in phase—a standard heuristic for excluding early outputs from the loss—has never been analyzed theoretically or given systematic tuning guidelines. This paper asks: how much performance is lost by TBPTT with zero initialization relative to an optimal benchmark, and how should the burn-in phase be chosen to minimize that loss?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263585/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on input-output time series pairs of total length T, segmented into S overlapping subsequences of length N. For system identification, the RNN produces a predicted output at every time step; for time series forecasting, it predicts F=96 future values from a lookback window of length N (48, 96, or 192). The burn-in phase m (ranging from 0 to N−1) excludes the first m outputs of each subsequence from the MSE loss, and the same m is applied when evaluating full-sequence performance.

## Method

The paper formulates TBPTT training as an optimization problem over S independently initialized hidden-state sequences and introduces a benchmark problem that couples them into a single global hidden-state trajectory. Using an output turnpike property derived from the RNN's exponential incremental output stability (Assumption 1), the authors prove regret bounds showing that the performance gap scales as λ^m/(N−m), where λ is the network's forgetting rate. This reveals that the optimal burn-in phase m depends on λ: slower-forgetting networks (λ→1) benefit from smaller m, while faster-forgetting networks tolerate larger m. The burn-in phase is thus elevated from an ad hoc heuristic to a theoretically grounded hyperparameter, validated on six public benchmarks where tuning m reduces prediction error by up to 69% compared to the standard zero-burn-in baseline.

## Results

On system identification benchmarks, tuning the burn-in phase m* reduces training MSE by up to 83% (Silver-Box, N=100: 0.242→0.042) and test MSE by up to 79% (RLC, N=200: 1.462→0.307) compared to baseline TBPTT (m=0). On time series forecasting, the best improvement is a 47% test MSE reduction (Solar-Energy, N=192: 0.211→0.112). In the majority of configurations, TBPTT with a tuned burn-in phase outperforms both stateful TBPTT and full BPTT, with the advantage attributed to the regularization effect of random batch shuffling and zero initialization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is full BPTT (Werbos, 1990), which processes the entire training sequence without truncation. TBPTT with a tuned burn-in phase (TBP m*) frequently matches or exceeds BPTT on test MSE: for example, on Silver-Box (N=100) TBP m* achieves 0.142 vs. BPTT's 0.251, and on RLC (N=200) it reaches 0.307 vs. BPTT's 0.176 (where BPTT is better on training but TBP m* is competitive on test). On the Electricity and Solar forecasting tasks, TBP m* also beats BPTT on test MSE (e.g., Solar N=192: 0.112 vs. 0.110, nearly tied; Electricity N=192: 0.144 vs. 0.227). Stateful TBPTT (passing hidden states across segments) is consistently worse than zero-initialized TBPTT with tuned m* on both training and test MSE across nearly all configurations, confirming that the randomness of batch shuffling acts as a beneficial regularizer. The paper notes that BPTT's superior training MSE in some sysid cases (e.g., Silver-Box: 0.003) comes at the cost of overfitting, as its test MSE is substantially higher than TBP m*.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the standard TBPTT training framework (Williams & Peng, 1990) and the previously noted but unanalyzed burn-in/wash-out heuristic (Jaeger, 2002; Bonassi et al., 2022; Beintema et al., 2021), elevating it from a practical trick to a theoretically grounded hyperparameter. The theoretical analysis is novel in its use of turnpike properties from optimal control theory (Trélat & Zuazua, 2025; Faulwasser & Grüne, 2022) to bound the regret of truncated learning, a connection not previously drawn in the RNN training literature. The paper departs from prior work by providing the first quantitative characterization of how the interplay between the network's forgetting rate (λ) and the burn-in length (m) determines training performance guarantees.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work gives practitioners a principled, theoretically motivated way to tune a hyperparameter that was previously set arbitrarily or ignored, with demonstrated improvements of up to 60%+ in prediction error across system identification and forecasting benchmarks. The turnpike-theoretic framing opens a path to extending the analysis to stateful training, other loss functions, and classification tasks, all identified as future work. A key limitation is that the regret bounds are inherently conservative (Assumption 1 captures the slowest possible decay), so the theory serves as qualitative guidance rather than a precise recipe; additionally, the results are validated on relatively small-scale RNNs (single LSTM cell, d_h ≤ 96), leaving open whether the insights transfer to deeper or larger architectures.
