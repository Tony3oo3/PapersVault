## Problem

Gaussian Probability Path based Generative Models (GPPGMs) for 3D molecular generation require hundreds to thousands of trajectory steps for both training and sampling, creating a major computational bottleneck. Existing accelerators either coarsen trajectories with reduced-step solvers (sacrificing granularity) or only address inference speed without reducing training cost. No principled criterion existed to identify when the noised data distribution has already attained sufficient Gaussianity, making the remaining high-noise trajectory segments redundant.

## Input / Output

The method takes a standard Gaussian noise sample as input and generates 3D molecular structures consisting of atom types (one-hot categorical vectors) and 3D Euclidean coordinates. A key preprocessing step is zero-mean centering of molecular coordinates (subtracting the empirical mean), which is lossless due to the translational invariance of molecular geometry. The forward noising trajectory is discretized into T steps, but GAGA identifies a truncation point T* < T at which the remaining trajectory is replaced by a closed-form Gaussian N(0, ṽ_t I), reducing the effective trajectory length by 35–40%.

## Method

GAGA exploits zero-mean invariance of molecular data to derive an analytic Gaussian approximation for the noised distribution at any timestep: N(0, ṽ_t I) where ṽ_t = 1 − ᾱ_t(1 − v̂) and v̂ is the empirical per-sample variance. The critical timestep T* is determined by two statistical criteria evaluated along the forward trajectory: (1) data dependency decay, measured via mutual information across feature-wise and component-wise slices, and (2) distributional similarity, measured via the Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance between the empirical marginals and the reference Gaussian CDF. T* is set to max(T_ID, T_DS), the earliest step where both independence and marginal Gaussianity are simultaneously satisfied. A Proposition establishes that data distributions inherently closer to Gaussian (as molecular data is, due to sparsity and low dimensionality) reach sufficient Gaussianity earlier, justifying the earlier truncation. The method is architecture-agnostic and compatible with existing ODE/SDE solvers like DDIM, improving both training efficiency (by eliminating optimization on over-noised inputs) and sampling fidelity (by preserving full-resolution granularity on the retained trajectory segments).

## Results

On QM9, GAGA consistently improves generation quality while reducing trajectory length by 20–45%. EDM+GAGA achieves 92.0% Valid×Uniq (vs. 90.7% for EDM) and 85.6% molecule stability (vs. 82.0%), while cutting sampling time from 0.65 to 0.36 GPU-sec and training time from 5.6 to 3.1 GPU-days. GeoLDM+GAGA reaches 94.4% Valid×Uniq (vs. 92.7%) with 34% faster sampling. On GEOM-Drugs, EDM+GAGA improves atom stability from 81.3% to 84.3% and reduces per-sample generation time from 10.9 to 6.4 GPU-sec.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GAGA is applied as a plug-in trajectory truncation on top of existing GPPGM backbones, so the primary comparisons are each backbone with and without GAGA. EDM+GAGA outperforms EDM by +1.3% Valid×Uniq and +3.6% molecule stability while halving both sampling time (0.36 vs. 0.65 GPU-sec) and training time (3.1 vs. 5.6 GPU-days). GeoLDM+GAGA improves over GeoLDM by +1.7% Valid×Uniq and +2.9% molecule stability, with 34% faster sampling and 38% less training. EquiFM+GAGA yields a smaller but consistent gain (+0.2% Valid×Uniq, +2.9% molecule stability) with 12% faster sampling and 21% less training, reflecting that EquiFM already uses a short 200-step trajectory. Against non-GPPGM baselines, GAGA-augmented models substantially outperform ENF (39.4% vs. 92–94% Valid×Uniq) and G-SchNet (80.3% vs. 92–94%), confirming that the efficiency gains do not come at the cost of generation quality. GAGA is also shown to be compatible with DDIM solvers: combining DDIM (2× acceleration) with GAGA on EDM yields 83.9% Valid×Uniq at 275 steps, versus 83.7% for DDIM alone at 500 steps, demonstrating orthogonality with existing sampling accelerators.

## Relation to Prior Work

GAGA builds on the Gaussian Probability Path framework (Lipman et al., 2022) and the zero-mean invariance property established for 3D molecular data (Hoogeboom et al., 2022), but departs from prior acceleration strategies that either coarsen trajectories (DDIM, DPM-Solver) or modify the noise schedule. Rather than reformulating the generative dynamics, GAGA identifies a data-dependent characteristic timestep T* via joint mutual-information and Kolmogorov-Smirnov criteria, then replaces the remaining trajectory with a closed-form Gaussian whose mean and variance are analytically derived from the VP schedule and empirical per-sample variance. This positions GAGA as orthogonal to and composable with existing SDE/ODE solver accelerators, unlike prior methods that are mutually exclusive with trajectory coarsening.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GAGA is notable because it improves both training and sampling efficiency simultaneously—most prior accelerators only reduce inference cost—while also improving generation quality by eliminating optimization on over-noised segments that provide no structural guidance. The practical impact is significant for 3D molecular generation, where hundreds-to-thousands of steps are standard and compute budgets are tight. A key limitation is the zero-mean invariance assumption, which does not hold for modalities like natural images or video where the absolute mean carries semantic content; extending GAGA to such settings remains an open problem. The Gaussianity evaluator itself could also serve as a diagnostic tool for timestep reallocation in quality-focused (rather than speed-focused) settings.
