## Problem

While spatial superposition in feedforward networks is well-characterized, the effect of time (memory) as an additional capacity-constraining pressure on representational geometry in recurrent models remains poorly understood. This paper asks how memory demands shape the geometric strategies RNNs adopt to represent features across time, and whether distinct regimes of behavior emerge as a function of sparsity and network dimensionality.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264013/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The RNN receives scalar or vector time-series inputs generated from an IID stochastic process with controllable temporal sparsity (parameter p), and must reproduce each input after a fixed delay of k timesteps (the k-delay task). The primary analytical outputs are the learned feature direction vectors w_s = W_h^s W_x, their angular distribution relative to the readout w_y, the spectral radius of W_h, and the projection of each feature direction onto the readout. For visualization, the hidden state is restricted to 2 dimensions with scalar I/O, then extended to 5- and 10-dimensional inputs with hidden sizes up to 100.

## Method

The paper derives an exact decomposition of the expected squared-error loss for a linear RNN on the k-delay task into four interpretable terms (task benefit, mean correction, projection interference cost, and composition interference), revealing the competing geometric incentives. It then shows that adding a ReLU readout creates an interference-free half-space opposite the readout direction, into which the model can pack feature directions without incurring projection interference. By varying temporal sparsity, the authors identify a phase transition between a dense regime (features arranged in a ~90° spiral sink, similar to the fully linear case) and a sparse regime (features spread across ~270° exploiting the free space, with reduced spectral radius). Nonlinear RNNs with ReLU recurrence are shown to fully exploit the interference-free space, implementing sharp forgetting by routing irrelevant features into the negative quadrant. Finally, the paper studies the interaction of spatial and temporal superposition, showing an all-or-none tradeoff where the RNN prioritizes representing the most important input features for all k+1 timesteps over representing many features briefly.

## Results

The paper derives a four-term loss decomposition (task benefit, mean correction, projection interference, composition interference) whose expected value closely matches empirical loss for linear RNNs on the k-delay task. A phase transition is identified: as temporal sparsity increases, the angular span of task-relevant feature directions (kθ) jumps from ~90° (dense regime, spiral-sink geometry) to ~270° (sparse regime, interference-free-space geometry), accompanied by a decrease in spectral radius ρ. Nonlinear RNNs with ReLU recurrence fully exploit the interference-free space, packing all k intermediate feature directions into the half-space opposite the readout and implementing sharp forgetting (vs. smooth forgetting in linear models). In the spatial-temporal interaction study (N_x=5, varying k), RNNs adopt an all-or-none strategy: a feature is represented for all k+1 timesteps or dropped entirely, with higher-k models retaining only the most important feature (A).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against external methods but instead compares three internal model families on the same k-delay task: (1) fully linear RNNs, which converge to a spiral-sink geometry with smooth forgetting (spectral radius < 1) regardless of sparsity; (2) state-space models (linear recurrence + ReLU readout), which in the sparse regime group the largest feature directions into the ReLU-induced interference-free space but remain constrained to an elliptical spiral due to linear recurrence; and (3) fully nonlinear RNNs (ReLU recurrence + ReLU readout), which achieve the ideal predicted geometry by packing all k intermediate features into a single quadrant of the interference-free space and implementing sharp forgetting. The SSM's dense-regime solution (spiral sink, ~90° angular span) is shown to be suboptimal in the sparse regime, where the nonlinear RNN's ~270° spread and smaller spectral radius yield lower projection interference. Across hidden sizes (N_h = 2, 5, 10) and a 100-dim / 75-feature setting, all architectures converge to the same qualitative strategy (positive diagonal projections for output features, negative/zero projections for intermediate features), with larger hidden states simply capturing more features along the diagonal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the spatial superposition framework of Elhage et al. (2022) from feedforward networks to the temporal domain in RNNs, introducing 'temporal superposition' as a distinct capacity-constraining pressure arising from memory demands. It builds on the learning dynamics literature for linear RNNs (Saxe et al., 2014; Proca et al., 2025) and the echo state network memory capacity framework (Jaeger, 2002), but departs from both by explicitly studying how nonlinearity (ReLU) creates an interference-free half-space that RNNs exploit, and by characterizing a phase transition between dense and sparse geometric regimes. The work also connects to state space models (S4, Mamba) and low-rank RNN theory, showing that complex eigenvalues support temporal superposition through rotation within the free space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a mechanistic, geometric explanation for why RNNs adopt specific representational strategies under memory pressure, bridging interpretability and neuroscience (working memory, serial recall). The identification of an interference-free space and the phase transition between dense/sparse regimes offer testable predictions for how recurrent architectures handle capacity constraints. Key limitations include the assumption of temporal independence and sparsity, the restriction to small (2D) hidden states for most analysis, and the focus on a single k-delay task; open questions include how these geometric principles extend to non-sparse data, manipulation tasks, and whether the all-or-none spatial-temporal tradeoff persists in larger, overparameterized models.
