## Problem

Clinical ML models deployed at one hospital frequently degrade under distribution shift at another due to differences in measurement policies, case mix, and coding conventions. Existing solutions either standardize data formats (OMOP, FHIR) without guaranteeing predictive portability, or adapt model parameters to the target site at the cost of additional labels and calibration. The paper asks whether LLMs can produce portable, site-agnostic patient representations that let a downstream predictor trained at one hospital transfer to another with minimal or no retraining.

## Input / Output

The method consumes irregular multivariate ICU records over 48-hour windows (60–75 clinical variables spanning labs, vitals, and interventions with heterogeneous sampling rates and missingness), serialized into a canonical text format that preserves raw magnitudes and native clinical units without normalization. The output is a fixed-length embedding vector (from Qwen3 text-embedding) that plugs directly into standard downstream predictors (PatchTSMixer, MLP, LSTM, TimeMixer) for five tasks: 24-hour multivariate forecasting, length-of-stay regression, in-hospital mortality, vasopressor/antibiotic use prediction, and 10-label blood-test ordering. A no-summarization control embeds the raw serialization directly, passing ~25× more tokens to the embedder than the summarized version.

## Method

Record2Vec is a summarize-then-embed pipeline in which a frozen LLM (Gemini-2.0 Flash, MedGemma, or Llama-3.1) converts each irregular 48-hour window into a concise clinical summary using structured slot-based prompts that elicit vitals, labs, therapies, trajectories, salient events, and gaps; a frozen text encoder (Qwen3) then maps that summary to a fixed-length vector. The key insight is that natural language acts as a semantic transformation layer: it normalizes heterogeneous variable names, units, and site-specific coding artifacts into a shared clinical vocabulary before embedding, reducing the burden on downstream decoders to re-learn local conventions. Both the summarizer and embedder remain frozen throughout, decoupling representation learning from downstream optimization and constraining overfitting to site-specific patterns. The fixed-length output standardizes the downstream interface, enabling zero- or few-shot transfer without architectural modification, and the pipeline is evaluated against grid imputation, self-supervised TSDE, the TimesFM foundation model, and a GenHPF variant across three ICU cohorts (MIMIC-IV, HiRID, PPICU) in in-distribution, cross-site, and few-shot settings.

## Results

Record2Vec achieves the strongest in-distribution performance, winning 13 of 15 tasks across MIMIC-IV, HiRID, and PPICU (e.g., Forecast MSE 0.021 on HiRID, 0.027 on MIMIC, 0.017 on PPICU; Mortality AUROC 0.930/0.888/0.900; Drug Recall 0.911/0.903/0.937). For cross-site transfer (training on HiRID or MIMIC, testing on PPICU), Record2Vec wins all 10 columns, with the largest gaps over grid imputation baselines (e.g., Forecast MSE 0.183 vs. 0.306 for Mean imputation in HiRID→PPICU). In the 16-sample few-shot setting, Record2Vec fine-tuned from a source-trained model approaches the in-distribution upper bound trained on 36,019 samples, substantially outperforming all baselines under the same supervision budget.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against TSDE (Senane et al., KDD 2024), Record2Vec wins on 13/15 in-distribution tasks; TSDE is best only on PPICU mortality (AUROC 0.890 vs. 0.890, a tie) and is competitive on HiRID forecast MSE (0.029 vs. 0.021). Against TimesFM (Das et al., ICML 2024), Record2Vec dominates on classification tasks (e.g., MIMIC mortality AUROC 0.888 vs. 0.791; PPICU mortality 0.890 vs. 0.658) while TimesFM is competitive on forecasting (MIMIC MSE 0.030 vs. 0.027) and lab recall. Against GenHPF (Hur et al., IEEE JBHI 2024), Record2Vec substantially outperforms on all shared tasks (e.g., HiRID mortality AUROC 0.930 vs. 0.836; MIMIC drug recall 0.903 vs. 0.720). Grid imputation baselines (Mean, Right-shift, Interpolation) are consistently the weakest, with interpolation performing particularly poorly on forecasting (MIMIC MSE 0.103 vs. 0.027 for Record2Vec). Under cross-site transfer, the gap widens dramatically: grid imputations degrade to near-chance on classification (e.g., HiRID→PPICU mortality AUROC 0.50 for Mean), while Record2Vec maintains strong performance (0.72), and even TSDE and TimesFM trail Record2Vec on most transfer columns.

## Relation to Prior Work

Record2Vec departs from the dominant paradigm in clinical ML where portability is achieved by adapting the *model* to a new site (domain adaptation, invariant risk minimization) or by standardizing data *formats* (OMOP, FHIR). Instead, it repurposes frozen LLMs as an information transformation layer that converts dense, irregular ICU time series into natural-language summaries, which are then embedded by a frozen text encoder—positioning the LLM as a data-level normalizer rather than a predictor. This contrasts with prior LLM-for-EHR work (TabLLM, DeLLiriuM, Hegselmann et al.) that fine-tunes LLMs on serialized records for direct prediction, and with numeric representation learners (TSDE, TimesFM) that learn site-specific embeddings. The conceptual lineage draws on clinical handoff narratives, where physicians already abstract heterogeneous measurements into shared semantic summaries, and on the observation that language provides a site-agnostic representational substrate for aligning heterogeneous coding, units, and sampling policies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper tackles a concrete deployment bottleneck—clinical models degrade across hospitals due to distribution shift—and offers a simple, frozen-pipeline solution that requires no per-site model retraining, potentially reducing the engineering overhead of multi-site deployment. The cross-site transfer advantage (winning 10/10 transfer columns) and the few-shot result (16 labeled target samples approaching in-distribution performance) are practically significant for smaller hospitals lacking data volume. However, the approach is constrained by LLM inference cost and latency, the lack of theoretical grounding for when and why the semantic abstraction preserves task-relevant signal, and the acknowledged gap between research-grade and production-grade deployment (on-prem, IRB-compliant LLM access). Open questions include whether the portability gains persist with smaller or fully local LLMs, how the method handles genuinely novel clinical concepts absent from the LLM's training distribution, and whether the privacy analysis extends beyond demographic attributes to membership inference or embedding inversion attacks.
