## Problem

Smaller, deployable Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) exhibit a critical deficiency in understanding temporal and procedural visual sequences, a bottleneck for real-world embodied AI applications like robotic manipulation and GUI navigation. This gap stems from a systemic failure in training paradigms: existing multi-image datasets treat frames as unordered collections or emphasize general sequential content rather than fine-grained procedural logic, leaving small models evaluated on a skill they were never systematically taught.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262656/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a short image sequence of 3–4 frames drawn from embodied scenarios (robotic manipulation, LEGO assembly, mobile GUI, embodied navigation) alongside a natural-language description, and in two of three tasks a set of candidate frames as distractors. Outputs are structured as: a permutation string restoring correct temporal order (Temporal Reordering), a single selected frame index (Next-Frame Prediction / Previous-Frame Review), or the explicit rejection label 'None of the choices provided' when the text description is deliberately mismatched with the visual content (negative samples). All sequences are pre-filtered by Qwen2.5-VL-72B to ensure visual clarity, scene consistency, and discernible temporal progression.

## Method

TPRU is a 24,750-sample, 126,000-image dataset organized around three complementary temporal tasks—Temporal Reordering, Next-Frame Prediction, and Previous-Frame Review—sourced from four diverse embodied domains to foster generalizable sequential understanding. A key design choice is the inclusion of challenging negative samples where the text description is deliberately inconsistent with the visual content, forcing the model to perform active cross-modal validation rather than relying on textual priors. Training employs Group-wise Preference Optimization (GRPO) via the Easy-R1 framework on Qwen2.5-VL backbones (3B/7B/32B), using KL regularization (coefficient 0.01), 5 rollouts per sample, AdamW at lr 1e-6, and 2 epochs on 8 A800 GPUs. The RL objective, combined with a format reward for output structure, moves the model beyond pattern imitation toward genuine procedural reasoning, yielding TPRU-7B accuracy of 75.70% on TPRU-Test versus 50.33% for the base model and surpassing GPT-4o.

## Results

On the proposed TPRU-Test, TPRU-7B achieves 75.70% accuracy (up from 50.33% for the base Qwen2.5-VL-7B), surpassing GPT-4o by a significant margin. On MuirBench, TPRU-32B reaches 68.42% overall accuracy (vs. 68.00% for GPT-4o and 63.73% for the base Qwen2.5-VL-32B), with the Ordering sub-task jumping from 26.56% to 45.31%. On LEGO-Puzzles, TPRU-7B achieves 42.8% overall (vs. 36.5% base), with the Ordering sub-task quadrupling from 8.0% to 32.0%. General benchmarks (MMMU, MMCR, BLINK, etc.) show stable or slightly improved performance, confirming no catastrophic forgetting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Qwen2.5-VL base models, TPRU-finetuned variants show dramatic gains: TPRU-7B improves MuirBench Ordering from 14.06% to 34.38% (+20.32 pp) and TPRU-Test from 50.33% to 75.70% (+25.37 pp). TPRU-32B (68.42%) narrowly outperforms GPT-4o (68.00%) on MuirBench overall, while TPRU-7B (75.70%) decisively beats GPR-4o on TPRU-Test. On MuirBench, TPRU-7B (65.04%) surpasses InternVL3-38B (64.12%) and approaches InternVL3-78B (64.65%) despite being 10× smaller. Against video-temporal baselines in Table 6, TPRU-7B dominates: it outperforms Qwen2.5-Omni-7B (59.11%), Long-VITA-16K (53.07%), LLaVA-Video-Qwen2-7B (37.88%), and SmolVLM2-256M-Video (27.92%) on MuirBench overall, with the Ordering gap being especially stark (34.38% vs. 18.75% for Qwen2.5-Omni). On LEGO-Puzzles, TPRU-7B (42.8%) beats all video baselines (best: LLaVA-Video-Qwen2-72B at 41.64%) and approaches GPT-4o (57.7%) and Qwen2.5-VL-72B (52.7%). The only model that outperforms TPRU-32B on MuirBench overall is Gemini-2.5-Flash (73.73%), but TPRU-32B matches or exceeds it on the critical Ordering sub-task (45.31% vs. 46.88%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant multi-image instruction-tuning paradigm (Mantis-Instruct, LLaVA-NeXT-Interleave, MMDU) that treats image collections as unordered or emphasizes general sequential content, by introducing a structured training framework with three complementary temporal tasks (reordering, next-frame prediction, previous-frame review) and deliberate negative samples. For the training methodology, it adopts GRPO (from DeepSeekMath) via the Easy-R1 framework to fine-tune Qwen2.5-VL models, positioning RL as an alternative to standard SFT for instilling procedural reasoning. Conceptually, it bridges the gap identified in the literature between evaluation-only benchmarks (MuirBench, TempVS, LEGO-Puzzles) that repeatedly confirm MLLMs' temporal failures and the absence of large-scale, procedurally coherent training data to address those failures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for embodied AI deployment: demonstrating that a 7B model can achieve SOTA-level temporal reasoning (outperforming GPT-4o on TPRU-Test) makes procedural understanding feasible on resource-constrained edge devices. However, the RL-over-SFT advantage is modest (75.70% vs 72.88% on TPRU-Test), and general benchmark gains are mixed with occasional small drops, suggesting the improvements are highly specialized to temporal/procedural tasks. Open questions include whether the three-task formulation generalizes to longer sequences or continuous video, whether the negative-sample strategy scales to more complex multi-step procedures, and whether the gains persist when the model is further fine-tuned on other downstream tasks.
