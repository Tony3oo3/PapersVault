## Problem

Modern visual generation models produce aesthetically pleasing natural images but fail at structured visuals—charts, diagrams, mathematical figures—that demand composition planning, precise text rendering, and multimodal reasoning for factual correctness. No prior work has systematically addressed this gap with a dedicated dataset, training methodology, or fine-grained evaluation protocol, leaving structured image generation and editing as an underexplored setting for unified multimodal models.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260965/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

For text-to-image generation, the model takes a dense text prompt (or a GPT-5-generated CoT reasoning trajectory at inference time) and produces a structured image at dynamic resolution near 512×512 while preserving native aspect ratios. For editing, it takes an input structured image plus a natural-language editing instruction, with the VLM encoder processing both jointly; at inference, an external GPT-5 reasoner first produces a three-step analysis (summarize salient elements, localize edits, forecast the target) that is fed as expanded context to the generator. All training data are strictly code-aligned: source and target images are rendered from executable Python/LaTeX programs, ensuring verifiable state transitions.

## Method

The model builds on FLUX.1 Kontext (a diffusion transformer) and replaces its CLIP text encoder with Qwen2.5-VL-7B connected via a lightweight MLP, enabling the VLM to supply high-level semantic features (e.g., quantitative relationships in a bar chart) that the VAE alone cannot capture. Training follows a three-stage curriculum: Stage 1 freezes the backbone and trains only the MLP connector on simple data (with T5 features removed to prevent shortcut learning); Stage 2 jointly fine-tunes the backbone and connector on the 1.3M structured dataset plus natural-image data, using a mask-based loss that downweights background and unchanged regions; Stage 3 injects GPT-5-generated chain-of-thought annotations as long-context inputs to Qwen-VL, teaching the model to follow explicit reasoning instructions and enabling inference-time compute scaling via an external reasoner. The accompanying StructBench (1,714 items across six domains) is evaluated with StructScore, a multi-round Q&A protocol that decomposes each image into atomic verification questions, elicits open-ended answers from a VLM on the generated image, and computes a weighted accuracy (0.1× visual-consistency + 0.9× instruction-following) to prioritize factual correctness over pixel similarity.

## Results

On StructEditBench, the proposed model achieves 55.98% overall StructScore accuracy, outperforming all open-source baselines and even closed-source GPT-Image (52.20%) and Seedream 4.0 (52.85%), though trailing Nano Banana 2.0 (67.05%). On StructT2IBench, the model scores 28.80% overall accuracy, below the top closed-source systems (GPT-Image: 49.58%, Nano Banana 2.0: 92.00%) but above most open-source alternatives. Even the strongest model reaches only ~50–67% accuracy on editing and ~50–92% on generation, confirming that structured visual generation remains far from solved. Adding explicit GPT-5 reasoning at inference time yields consistent gains across architectures, e.g., boosting Bagel from 28.87% to 38.44% on editing.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On StructEditBench, the proposed model (55.98%) surpasses all open-source editing baselines—Qwen-Edit (38.12%), FLUX.1 Kontext (37.56%), Step1X-Edit (34.11%), and Bagel-Think (33.34%)—by 12–28 points, and also beats closed-source GPT-Image (52.20%) and Seedream 4.0 (52.85%), though it trails Nano Banana 2.0 (67.05%) by ~11 points. On StructT2IBench, the model (28.80%) lags behind closed-source leaders (GPT-Image 49.58%, Nano Banana 2.0 92.00%) and the strongest open-source model Qwen-Image (41.03%), but outperforms FLUX.1 Kontext (20.36%), OmniGen2 (16.24%), and Bagel (4.69%). The paper further shows that applying the same GPT-5 reasoning trajectory to Bagel (28.87% → 38.44%) outperforms Bagel's native thinking variant (33.34%), indicating that trajectory quality matters more than architecture. On the general-domain ImgEdit-Full benchmark, the model (3.75) remains competitive with FLUX.1 Kontext (3.52) and GPT-Image (4.20), confirming that structured-visual training does not degrade general editing capability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on FLUX.1 Kontext, a diffusion transformer for unified image generation and editing, but departs from the natural-image-centric focus of prior T2I and editing work by targeting structured visuals (charts, diagrams, math figures) that demand factual accuracy, precise text rendering, and composition planning. Unlike prior editing datasets that rely on expert models as renderers (InstructPix2Pix, OmniEdit), the authors construct a code-aligned pipeline where source and target images are rendered from executable programs, ensuring strictly verifiable state transitions. The model architecture replaces heavy transformer-based projectors (e.g., MetaQuery) with a lightweight MLP connector to bridge Qwen-VL features into the diffusion backbone, and introduces a three-stage curriculum (alignment → hybrid visual learning → thinking enhancement) that progressively injects structured-visual knowledge and chain-of-thought reasoning. The evaluation metric StructScore departs from CLIP/DINO similarity scores and naive VLM-as-a-judge by decomposing assessment into atomic Q&A pairs with open-ended answers, reducing hallucination and enabling fine-grained factual scoring.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals a striking gap: even the strongest closed-source systems (GPT-Image, Nano Banana 2.0) achieve only ~50–67% accuracy on structured image editing, and the understanding-generation asymmetry is pronounced—VLMs handle structured image comprehension well, yet generation models fail at the same content. The finding that code-aligned data quality, rather than architectural novelty, is the dominant driver of performance, and that inference-time reasoning yields consistent gains across diverse model families, offers actionable guidance for the field. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to more structured domains (molecular formulas, musical notation, structured video), whether the 1:9 visual-consistency-to-instruction-following weighting generalizes beyond charts, and whether the benchmark's reliance on GPT-5 as both data annotator and evaluator introduces circularity that could mask systematic biases.
