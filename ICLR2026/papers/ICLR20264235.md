## Problem

Despite rapid adoption of LLMs for graph node classification, the field lacks a principled, controlled understanding of how different LLM-graph interaction strategies (prompting, tool-use, code generation) perform across key axes of variability including dataset domain, homophily, text length, and model scale. Prior work evaluates specific domains or tasks in isolation, leaving practitioners without actionable guidance on which interaction mode suits their graph's characteristics.

## Input / Output

The method takes an unweighted graph with textual node features, an adjacency structure, and partially known labels, and produces a single integer class label for a query node. The graph is consumed in mode-specific formats: as a serialized k-hop neighborhood text string (prompting), as a set of discrete API calls returning neighbor IDs, feature text, or labels (GraphTool), or as a typed pandas DataFrame indexed by node_id with columns for features, neighbors, and label (Graph-as-Code). A critical practical constraint is the LLM's context window: prompting variants hit token limits on long-text or high-degree graphs, while code and tool modes retrieve information iteratively to stay within budget.

## Method

The paper conducts a large-scale zero-shot evaluation of three LLM-graph interaction modes across 14 datasets spanning citation, web-link, e-commerce, and social domains. Prompting serializes the target node's k-hop neighborhood (0, 1, or 2 hops) into a single-turn text prompt; GraphTool/GraphTool+ implements a ReAct-style think-act-observe loop with a fixed tool set for retrieving neighbors, features, labels, or exact-k-hop information; Graph-as-Code lets the LLM iteratively generate and execute pandas expressions over a typed DataFrame representation of the graph, enabling compositional and adaptive queries. The key finding is that Graph-as-Code achieves the strongest overall performance, with especially large gains on long-text or high-degree graphs where prompting exhausts the token budget, and that all modes remain effective on heterophilic graphs. Dependency analysis via controlled feature truncation, edge deletion, and label removal reveals that Graph-as-Code flexibly shifts reliance between structure, features, and labels to exploit the most informative signal, while prompting is brittle and requires all three simultaneously.

## Results

On long-text homophilic datasets (Table 3), Graph-as-Code achieves the best accuracy across all six benchmarks: 71.80% on CiteSeer, 61.60% on Reddit, 86.20% on Computer, 86.40% on Photo, 56.40% on Instagram, and 82.20% on Wiki-CS. On short-text homophilic datasets (Table 1), Graph-as-Code leads on Cora (85.16%) and Products (82.70%), while 2-hop prompting is competitive on PubMed (91.80%) and ArXiv (74.40%). On heterophilic web-link datasets (Table 2), Graph-as-Code achieves 92.70% on Cornell and 89.17% on Wisconsin, outperforming all prompting and tool-use variants. A key finding is that 2-hop prompting hits token limits on 5 of 6 long-text datasets, whereas Graph-as-Code remains feasible and superior.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against classic GNNs, zero-shot Graph-as-Code clearly outperforms GCN on Computer (86.20% vs. 71.44%) and Photo (86.40% vs. 69.25%), and GAT on Computer (86.20% vs. 83.39%) and Photo (86.40% vs. 80.40%), but loses on Instagram (56.40% vs. 63.50% for GCN, 63.56% for GAT) where the binary label makes majority-class baselines strong. Against hybrid LLM+GNN models that require per-dataset training, Graph-as-Code is consistently outperformed: LLaGA leads on all five long-text datasets (e.g., 74.15% on CiteSeer vs. 71.80%, 88.80% on Computer vs. 86.20%), and TAPE and Encoder GCN also edge ahead on Reddit and Computer. The paper acknowledges this gap, attributing it to hybrid models combining GNN structural message-passing with LLM text understanding, whereas Graph-as-Code operates purely at inference time with a frozen LLM. The paper's primary contribution is not to beat trained GNNs but to establish that among LLM-only interaction modes, Graph-as-Code dominates—particularly where prompting becomes infeasible due to token limits—and to show that all LLM modes remain effective on heterophilic graphs, contradicting prior claims of collapse under low homophily.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ReAct (Yao et al., 2023) paradigm to graph reasoning by introducing Graph-as-Code, a mode where the LLM generates and executes arbitrary pandas programs over a typed graph table, going beyond the fixed action sets of prior tool-calling approaches (Zhang, 2023; Edge et al., 2024). It positions itself against the dominant prompting-based textualization paradigm (Fatemi et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2024a; Ye et al., 2024) and contrasts with hybrid LLM+GNN architectures (LLaGA, TAPE, UniGraph) that require training auxiliary encoders. The paper's core contribution is not a single new method but the first large-scale, controlled evaluation that factorizes interaction mode, dataset domain, homophily, text length, model size, and reasoning capability into independent axes of variability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that all LLM-graph interaction modes remain effective on heterophilic graphs directly challenges the widely-cited conclusion of Huang et al. (2024a) that LLMs collapse under low homophily, potentially reshaping how practitioners select methods for diverse graph regimes. The practical guidance that Graph-as-Code is preferred for long-text or high-degree graphs—where prompting hits token limits—is immediately actionable for real-world deployments in e-commerce, social networks, and recommendation systems. Open questions include whether the code-generation advantage persists as LLM context windows grow substantially, how these findings transfer to dynamic or temporal graphs, and whether the adaptive reliance behavior of Graph-as-Code can be formalized or controlled more explicitly.
