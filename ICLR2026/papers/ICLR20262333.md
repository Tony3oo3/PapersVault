## Problem

Optimizing indicator matrices for clustering and graph partitioning is NP-hard, and existing relaxations each have critical limitations: the Stiefel manifold requires O(n²c) time and post-processing via K-means, the single stochastic manifold ignores column-sum constraints (causing empty or imbalanced clusters), and the double stochastic manifold demands O(n³) time plus exact knowledge of class distributions as prior. The paper asks whether a single relaxation can flexibly accommodate varying levels of prior information while remaining computationally tractable at scale.

## Input / Output

The method takes a data matrix or similarity/Laplacian matrix (n×c with n ≫ c) and user-specified column-sum bounds l and u that control how much prior class-distribution information is incorporated; for denoising, it takes a noisy image with a TV regularization coefficient ξ. The output is a relaxed indicator matrix F on the RIM manifold, which after row-wise argmax discretization yields hard cluster assignments, or a denoised image. The (l,u) interval is the key design knob: setting l=u recovers the double stochastic manifold, while l<0 and u>n recovers the single stochastic manifold, making the framework a strict generalization of both.

## Method

The paper defines the Relaxed Indicator Matrix Manifold M = {X | X1_c = 1_n, l < X^T 1_n < u, X > 0}, proves it is an embedded submanifold of dimension (n−1)c, and equips it with the restricted Euclidean inner product to derive closed-form Riemannian gradient, Hessian, and Levi-Civita connection expressions that each require only O(n) operations (versus O(n³) for the double stochastic manifold). Three Retraction methods are provided: a Dykstra's-algorithm-based projection that provably yields geodesics, a dual gradient-ascent solver, and a Sinkhorn-iteration variant equivalent to entropy-regularized optimal transport. Built on this toolbox, the authors develop Riemannian Gradient Descent, Conjugate Gradient, and Trust-Region algorithms, apply them to Ratio Cut and Max Cut with Lipschitz-continuity and convergence-rate proofs, and demonstrate 70–200× speedups over double stochastic manifold methods on problems with millions of variables while achieving lower loss and superior clustering metrics (ACC, NMI, ARI) across eight real-world datasets.

## Results

On the Ratio Cut optimization problem (l=u), RIM manifold methods (RIMRGD, RIMRCG, RIMRTR) achieve 70–200× speedup over doubly stochastic manifold methods (DSRGD, DSRCG) while attaining lower loss values across all 8 datasets. For example, on PalmData25, RIMRCG achieves a loss of 642.3 in 2.58s versus DSRCG's 1054 in 54.48s. In end-to-end clustering, RIMRcut achieves the best or near-best ACC on 6/8 datasets (e.g., 96.71 on JAFFE, 90.85 on PalmData25, 82.53 on Digit), outperforming all 10 comparison clustering algorithms on average across ACC, NMI, and ARI.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the doubly stochastic manifold (DSM) methods of Douik & Hassibi, the RIM manifold achieves a 70–200× speedup (e.g., 0.265s vs 9.53s on a 5000×5 problem) while reaching orders-of-magnitude lower loss (3.09E-23 vs 4.38E-11). In the Ratio Cut optimization comparison (Table 5), RIMRCG consistently outperforms DSRGD (Tripuraneni) and DSRCG (Sato) in both time and loss on all 8 datasets; for instance, on COIL20, RIMRCG reaches loss 27.46 in 0.685s versus DSRGD's 28.17 in 8.978s. RIM methods also dominate FWA (Jaggi) and PGD (Chen) on most datasets in both speed and final loss. In end-to-end clustering (Table 6), RIMRcut surpasses the classic Ratio Cut (Hagen & Kahng) on 7/8 datasets in ACC (e.g., 96.71 vs 84.51 on JAFFE, 90.85 vs 87.03 on PalmData25) and leads in NMI and ARI on most datasets, though FSC slightly edges it on COIL20 ACC (82.76 vs 79.72) and BKNC on JAFFE ACC (93.76 vs 96.71 is actually RIMRcut winning; BKNC wins on JAFFE NMI at 92.40 vs 96.24 is RIMRcut winning).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the lineage of indicator matrix relaxations for clustering and graph partitioning, which has progressed from the Stiefel manifold (Ng et al., 2001) through the single stochastic manifold (Sun et al., 2015) to the double stochastic manifold (Douik & Hassibi, 2019; Fettal et al., 2024). The proposed RIM manifold generalizes both the single and double stochastic manifolds by replacing the strict column-sum equality with flexible bounds (l, u), and it inherits the Riemannian optimization framework from the doubly stochastic manifold literature while replacing the O(n³) gradient/Hessian computations with O(n) ones. The Sinkhorn-based retraction also draws on entropic optimal transport (Cuturi, 2013), connecting the manifold geometry to the OT literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The O(n) vs. O(n³) complexity reduction makes Riemannian optimization on stochastic-matrix constraints practically feasible for large-scale problems (millions of variables), which was previously infeasible. The flexible (l, u) parameterization offers a principled way to incorporate varying levels of class-size prior knowledge, bridging the gap between fully unconstrained (single stochastic) and fully specified (double stochastic) settings. However, the paper acknowledges that no precise bound exists between the relaxed RIM solution and the original NP-hard indicator matrix solution, and the clustering improvements over state-of-the-art methods, while consistent, are moderate rather than transformative. Open questions include whether the geodesic retraction property translates to faster convergence in practice for non-convex objectives and whether the framework extends naturally to other combinatorial relaxations beyond indicator matrices.
