## Problem

Reinforcement learning for formal theorem proving has relied on a single binary outcome signal (proof passes or fails), leaving the rich, fine-grained tactic-level feedback available from proof assistants like Lean untapped during training. The central challenge is a representational mismatch: Lean produces symbolic, tree-structured verification feedback, while LLMs learn from scalar token-level rewards, creating a nontrivial credit-assignment problem that prior work has not addressed.

## Input / Output

The model receives a formal Lean 4 theorem statement in a non-CoT prompt format (no natural-language chain-of-thought) and generates a complete proof as an autoregressive sequence of Lean tactics. Each generated proof is parsed into an AST and submitted to Lean's REPL for verification with a 15-second timeout; Lean returns both a global pass/fail signal and per-tactic elaboration status (locally sound vs. type-error), which together form the training reward. Proofs are capped at 1024 tokens, and evaluation uses pass@32 and pass@64 sampling on MiniF2F and ProofNet test sets.

## Method

The paper uses Lean itself as a symbolic process oracle during online RL, extracting both an outcome reward g(Y) ∈ {0,1} and a per-tactic score φ(Y,T) ∈ {1, d₁, d₂} from Lean's elaboration and error logs. A first-error propagation rule marks all tactics after the first failing tactic as erroneous, enforcing causal validity under dependent type theory. Tactic-level advantages are then assigned exclusively to the first token of each tactic (the tactic keyword, e.g., `intro`, `apply`), which ablations show is the most effective credit-assignment point. These process advantages are combined additively with a group-normalized outcome advantage inside a GRPO objective, using the mean outcome reward across the group as a difficulty-adaptive baseline. The result is a dense, verifier-grounded training signal that requires no auxiliary process reward model, no natural-language annotations, and no Monte Carlo rollouts—only the Lean REPL already used for outcome verification.

## Results

On MiniF2F-Test, STP-Lean+Ours achieves 59.2%±0.5 pass@64, improving over the STP-Lean baseline (56.7%±0.2) by +2.5 percentage points, and over Goedel-Prover-SFT (57.9%±0.5) by +1.3 pp. On ProofNet-Test, the same model reaches 18.6%±0.3 pass@32, a +1.4 pp gain over the STP-Lean baseline (17.2%±0). For the DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5+STP variant, the method yields 57.8%±0.4 pass@64 on MiniF2F (+0.6 pp over baseline) and 18.5%±0.3 pass@32 on ProofNet (+0.8 pp). Notably, the single-shot whole-proof approach (59.2% pass@64) matches or exceeds the tree-search baseline InternLM2.5-StepProver (58.5%±0.9) without any search-time compute.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the STP-Lean baseline, the proposed method consistently wins: +2.5 pp on MiniF2F pass@64 (59.2% vs 56.7%) and +1.4 pp on ProofNet pass@32 (18.6% vs 17.2%), with a negligible −0.1 pp drop on ProofNet pass@64. Against Goedel-Prover-SFT, the method surpasses it on MiniF2F pass@64 (59.2% vs 57.9%) and ProofNet pass@32 (18.6% vs 15.6%), though Goedel-Prover-SFT retains a slight edge on ProofNet pass@64 (16.7% vs 19.0% — actually the method wins here too). For the DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5+STP variant, gains are smaller but consistent: +0.6 pp on MiniF2F pass@64 and +0.8 pp on ProofNet pass@32. Compared to tree-search methods, the single-shot whole-proof approach (59.2% pass@64) matches InternLM2.5-StepProver (58.5%±0.9) while avoiding large search-time compute, and outperforms Lean-STaR (46.3%) and InternLM2-Math-Plus-7B (48.8%) by a wide margin. The method does not beat DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-RL+RMaxTS on ProofNet (18.5% vs 21.5% pass@32), which uses 3,200 search expansions, but achieves competitive MiniF2F accuracy (57.8% vs 55.0%) with far less inference-time budget.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the GRPO-based RL framework for theorem proving (Shao et al., 2024) by replacing the sparse binary outcome reward with a composite signal that incorporates Lean's tactic-level elaboration feedback. Prior work on LLM-based theorem proving (Lean-STaR, RMaxTS, DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5) used Lean either as an inference-time step-checker or as a whole-proof verifier at training time, but did not exploit the fine-grained per-tactic error information that Lean's elaboration already produces. The paper also departs from learned process reward models (PRMs) that require large annotated step-level datasets or Monte Carlo rollouts, instead treating the symbolic verifier itself as a free, deterministic process oracle. The credit-assignment mechanism—first-error propagation combined with first-token reward placement—is a new design choice not present in prior GRPO or PPO formulations for formal reasoning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that proof assistants can serve a dual role: not only as evaluation-time verifiers but also as structured, dense reward sources during RL training, eliminating the need for learned PRMs or human step annotations in the formal reasoning setting. However, the practical gains are modest (+2.5% pass@64 on MiniF2F, +1.4% pass@32 on ProofNet), the reward values (d₁, d₂) are hand-tuned and model-sensitive, and no comparison with learned PRMs is possible due to the absence of Lean-specific step-annotated datasets. Open questions include how to generalize the fixed-score tactic rewards to learned or adaptive estimators, how to extend the approach to long CoT or multi-step reasoning formats, and whether the first-token credit assignment principle transfers to other structured generation tasks beyond Lean tactics.
