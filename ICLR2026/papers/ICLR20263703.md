## Problem

Long-context video modeling in MLLMs faces a fundamental efficiency-performance tradeoff: context-extension approaches (e.g., Gemini-1.5-Pro converting a 1-hour video to 921,600 tokens) incur prohibitive compute costs, while aggressive token compression methods (e.g., LLaMA-VID at 2 tokens/frame) lose so much detail that they underperform even image-only MLLMs on video benchmarks. No existing method achieves both extreme compression and competitive long-video understanding simultaneously.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263703/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model ingests a raw video of arbitrary duration D, sampled via a duration-based strategy (T = min(T_max, max(D, T_min)) frames, with T_min=64 and T_max=512) at 224×224 or 448×448 resolution, plus a free-form text instruction. Frames are grouped into 4-frame clips, each compressed to 64 tokens (16 tokens/frame), and a lightweight text timestamp prompt ('The video lasts for N seconds, and T frames are uniformly sampled from it') is appended. Outputs include multiple-choice or open-ended text answers, dense video captions, and temporal grounding intervals (start-end timestamps). The system handles up to 10,000+ input frames within a single A100-80G GPU.

## Method

The core contribution is HiCo, a two-stage hierarchical compression paradigm. At the Clip-level, a UMT-L video encoder with spatio-temporal attention captures inter-frame redundancy, and ToMe (similar token merging) compresses each 4-frame clip to 64 tokens; an information-theoretic analysis shows this reduces conditional entropy loss versus image encoders because the video encoder explicitly models mutual information between consecutive frames. At the Video-level, a progressive visual dropout is applied during LLM inference only: uniform token dropping at shallow layers preserves spatio-temporal structure, while text-guided attention-based selection at deep layers retains task-relevant tokens, exploiting the observation that attention becomes focused on local regions in deeper layers. The full pipeline (UMT-L + ToMe + MLP connector + Qwen2-7B) is trained via a four-stage short-to-long curriculum—alignment, short-video pre-training, joint short/long instruction tuning on the new LongVid dataset (114K videos, 3.4M QA pairs), and high-resolution post-finetuning—achieving ~1/50 compression with 99.1% accuracy on 10,000-frame NIAH.

## Results

VideoChat-Flash (7B, 16 tokens/frame) achieves 74.0 on MVBench, 74.7 on MLVU, 65.3/69.7 on VideoMME (w/o & w/ subtitles), and 48.2 on LVBench, setting new state-of-the-art among open-source models at the 7B scale. On the Single-Hop NIAH benchmark, it reaches 99.1% accuracy over 10,000 frames, far surpassing LongVA (91.8% at 3k frames) and LLaMA-VID (55.0% at 10k frames). On the proposed Multi-Hop NIAH, VideoChat-Flash scores 31.3% (CAP) and 25.4% (QA), outperforming LongVA by ~8 points and LLaMA-VID by ~11 points. The 2B variant also leads its size category, achieving 70.0 MVBench and 65.7 MLVU with only 16 tokens per frame.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VideoChat-Flash (7B, 16 tok/frame) outperforms GPT-4o on MVBench (74.0 vs 64.6), MLVU (74.7 vs 64.6), LVBench (48.2 vs 30.8), and Charades-STA (48.0 vs 35.7), but trails on VideoMME Overall (65.3 vs 71.9) and LongVideoBench (64.7 vs 66.7). Against Gemini-1.5-Pro, it wins on MVBench (74.0 vs 60.5), LVBench (48.2 vs 33.1), and AuroraCap (42.9 vs 41.7), yet loses on VideoMME (65.3 vs 75.0). Among open-source 7B models, it leads all baselines by substantial margins: +4.0 over InternVL2.5 on MVBench, +4.5 over Qwen2.5-VL on MLVU, and +17.3 over LLaVA-OneVision on MVBench. Against long-video-specific baselines, the gains are even larger: LLaMA-VID (2 tok/frame) scores only 41.9 on MVBench and 33.2 on MLVU, while LongVA (144 tok/frame) reaches 56.3 MLVU and 52.6 VideoMME—both far below VideoChat-Flash. On the NIAH evaluations, VideoChat-Flash achieves 99.1% at 10k frames (Single-Hop) and 31.3%/25.4% (Multi-Hop CAP/QA), compared to LongVA's 91.8% at 3k and ~23%/17%, and LLaMA-VID's 55.0% and 20%/7%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the video token compression line (Llama-VID, LongVU, LongVA) and the context-extension line (Gemini-1.5-Pro, LongVILA), but departs by proposing a two-stage hierarchical compression (HiCo) that decouples clip-level spatio-temporal redundancy (via UMT-L + ToMe) from video-level cross-modal redundancy (via progressive visual dropout in the LLM). It also repurposes ToMe's parameter-free token merging as a native, trainable compression mechanism within a video encoder rather than as a post-hoc accelerator, and introduces a short-to-long multi-stage training curriculum with a purpose-built long-video corpus (LongVid). The paper explicitly positions itself against training-free frame-selection methods, arguing that learning compressed representations natively is essential for a general-purpose long-video foundation model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 1/50 compression ratio with minimal performance loss (99.1% NIAH at 10,000 frames, competitive or superior scores on MVBench, MLVU, LVBench, and Charades-STA at 7B scale) offers a practical path to deploying long-video MLLMs on consumer hardware, where raw context extension is infeasible. The MH-NIAH benchmark addresses a real gap in evaluation by testing multi-hop reasoning rather than single-image retrieval, and the finding that compression can *improve* performance by enabling denser temporal sampling within a fixed token budget reframes the efficiency-accuracy tradeoff. Open questions include whether the progressive dropout can be made training-compatible without the observed train-inference mismatch penalty, whether the approach generalizes to audio-visual or 3D video inputs, and whether the 16-tokens-per-frame regime can be pushed further without degrading fine-grained temporal grounding.
