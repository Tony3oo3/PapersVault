## Problem

Whether language models can systematically generalize remains debated, but empirical failures on reasoning benchmarks are confounded by training data properties, training paradigms, and inference-time strategies, making it impossible to attribute failures to a single cause. This paper introduces a controlled synthetic environment based on shortest-path planning to cleanly separate these factors and test two orthogonal axes of generalization: spatial transfer to unseen maps and length scaling to longer-horizon problems.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261904/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a prompt of the form '<s> i j :' where i and j are start and end node identifiers, and must generate the complete shortest path as a sequence of cardinal directions (E, W, N, S) without intermediate reasoning steps. The grid map topology (adjacency, blocked edges, sparsity) is not provided as explicit input but is implicitly encoded in the model weights through pretraining on random-walk trajectories over the map. Using direction tokens rather than node indices prevents the model from trivially memorizing n-gram sequences of identifiers.

## Method

The paper trains 8-layer, 8-head LLaMA-style transformers (with RoPE) from scratch in two stages: pretraining on 10M random-walk trajectories to learn map primitives and adjacency semantics without leaking shortest-path structure, followed by fine-tuning on shortest paths via either SFT or RL (Dr.GRPO with a binary verifiable reward). The key experimental contribution is a systematic disentanglement of three pipeline stages: (i) data properties (budget allocation between questions vs. solutions, primitive coverage, and combination diversity), (ii) training paradigm (SFT vs. RL with varying warm-start quality and rollout counts), and (iii) inference-time strategies (greedy, majority-of-10 self-consistency, shortest-of-10 objective-guided sampling). The central finding is an asymmetry: models achieve >90% spatial transfer to entirely disjoint maps but consistently fail under length scaling, with a decomposition analysis showing the failure is driven by recursive instability (Pr(Long|Sub1∧Sub2) drops from 0.811 to 0.589) rather than hardness accumulation. Coverage of distinct primitives sets the ceiling of transfer, minimal diversity is needed to unlock it, RL stabilizes training but never exceeds the best SFT bound, and inference-time search shifts performance upward without rescuing length-scaling failures.

## Results

On spatial transfer to unseen maps, the model achieves consistently high success rates above 90% (e.g., 94% average SR across three disjoint test maps even with only 16% of the training region). Under length scaling, performance degrades sharply: full-path success drops from 77.4% at length (20,30) to 53.0% at (30,40), driven primarily by recursive instability (Pr(Long|Sub1∧Sub2) falls from 0.811 to 0.589). In the MathQA case study, allocating budget to more distinct questions with high coverage improves accuracy from 0.70 to 0.82 on the 'gain' category and from 0.68 to 0.77 on 'physics' over the Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct baseline. RL (GRPO) stabilizes training across 20 epochs but never exceeds the best SFT checkpoint, and inference-time strategies (Majority-of-10, Shortest-of-10) shift the performance curve upward without rescuing length-scaling failures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external method baselines; instead it ablates internal pipeline choices. On data allocation, 'More Questions – High Coverage' consistently outperforms 'More Solutions' by 8–12 points on MathQA (e.g., 0.82 vs. 0.70 on gain), and also beats 'More Questions – High Diversity' (0.82 vs. 0.74 on gain), confirming that breadth of primitive coverage dominates over solution diversity or structural variety. On training paradigm, RL (Dr.GRPO) with up to 20 epochs and 16 rollouts per prompt never exceeds the best SFT checkpoint on either spatial transfer or length scaling, and exhibits nearly identical error-type distributions to SFT, indicating RL stabilizes optimization rather than expanding capability. On inference-time strategies, Shortest-of-10 improves SR over greedy decoding for both SFT and RL models, but the degradation trend with increasing path length remains unchanged, and RL models underperform their SFT counterparts under every inference setting, suggesting RL contracts the effective solution space available for test-time search.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the compositional generalization literature, adopting the coverage-diversity framework from Chang et al. (2025) and formalizing spatial transfer and length scaling following established definitions from Wiedemer et al. (2023b) and Sinha et al. (2024). It departs from prior work by constructing a fully controlled synthetic shortest-path environment that cleanly disentangles three factors—training data properties, training paradigm (SFT vs. RL), and inference-time strategy—that are typically confounded in natural-language reasoning benchmarks. The use of composable sequential optimization problems as the task family, with shortest-path as a canonical instantiation, positions the work at the intersection of the systematic generalization debate and the growing RL-for-reasoning literature (Dr.GRPO, GRPO variants).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides actionable, data-centric guidance for practitioners: coverage of distinct questions dominates over solution diversity, and a minimal diversity threshold is needed to unlock coverage's full effect. The finding that length scaling failure is driven by recursive instability rather than hardness accumulation, and that neither RL nor inference-time search can rescue it, reframes how the field should think about the limits of current training pipelines. Key limitations include the small model scale (8-layer, 8-head) and synthetic setting, though MathQA validation on 7B models partially addresses this. Open questions include whether the recursive instability finding persists at scale, whether the data allocation principles transfer to more complex compositional domains, and whether architectural changes (e.g., looped transformers) could address the length-scaling gap.
