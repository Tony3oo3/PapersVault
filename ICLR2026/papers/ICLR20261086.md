## Problem

Existing LLM-based robot task planners assume human instructions are clear and unambiguous, yet real-world users—especially the elderly and children—frequently employ implicit referring expressions (e.g., 'it', 'this heavy thing') whose meaning depends on dialogue context and scene. No prior benchmark systematically models this coreferential vagueness in embodied task planning, leaving it unclear how much such vagueness degrades planner performance or what causes the failures.

## Input / Output

The planner receives a 'human pending instruction' (a single natural-language sentence) preceded by multi-turn dialogue context memory (typically 6 rounds of human-robot conversation) and a text-based AI2-THOR scene description listing all objects present. The context is varied along two axes: three RE levels (Explicit, Mixed, Implicit) controlling how many noun phrases in the instruction are replaced by pronouns or descriptive phrases, and three context types (Standard, Noised with ambiguous brand/person names, Short with omitted task-relevant phrases), yielding nine difficulty levels. The output is a sequence of executable robot actions; under TOCC, an intermediate rephrased instruction is produced before planning.

## Method

The paper contributes two components. First, REI-Bench is an automatic pipeline that starts from ALFRED seed instructions, uses GPT-4o-mini to expand each into a six-round dialogue context, then applies controlled transformations: inserting ambiguous names (e.g., 'Mug Star' for a mug) for the Noised variant, randomly deleting noun phrases for the Short variant, and replacing explicit REs with pronouns or attributive phrases (sourced from OntoNotes) at three graduated levels. This yields 2,700 examples across nine vagueness levels. Second, TOCC (Task-Oriented Context Cognition) is a two-stage prompting strategy that decouples reference resolution from planning: in stage 1, the LLM is prompted to rewrite the vague instruction into a concise, fully explicit form using the dialogue context; in stage 2, the planner generates the next action solely from the rewritten instruction. This avoids the LLM devoting excessive attention to plan generation while simultaneously resolving coreference, and it outperforms aware prompts, chain-of-thought, and in-context learning with only ~4% additional token overhead.

## Results

On REI-Bench (1,000-task subset, LLaMA3.1-8B + SayCan, Standard Context), the baseline planner achieves 53.1% overall error rate with explicit REs, rising to 77.9% with fully implicit REs. The proposed TOCC method reduces overall error to 41.0% (explicit), 66.4% (mixed), and 70.7% (implicit), yielding an average success rate improvement of 6.5% over the baseline. Object omission error—the dominant failure mode—drops from 53.9% to 40.1% at the implicit REs level (−13.8 pp). Across all six LLMs and four planning frameworks, success rates degrade by 7.4–36.9% as implicit REs increase, confirming that coreferential vagueness is a systematic challenge.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the four planning frameworks (SayCan, DAG-Plan, HPE, LLM+P), all exhibit consistent success-rate degradation as implicit REs increase, with no framework immune to the vagueness challenge; LLM+P performs worst in highly vague settings due to its rigid symbolic constraints. Among prompting methods, TOCC clearly outperforms all three baselines: it reduces overall error by 12.1 pp over the baseline at the explicit level, 3.5 pp at mixed, and 7.2 pp at implicit REs. Aware Prompt (AP) provides only marginal gains (≤1.1 pp) and can even hurt performance on explicit instructions by inducing hallucinated vagueness detection. Chain-of-Thought (CoT) yields modest improvements (≤0.8 pp) but at 2× the token cost and latency of TOCC. In-Context Learning (ICL) actually degrades performance in most categories (e.g., +7.7 pp error at explicit level) because small onboard LLMs struggle to learn from few-shot examples. TOCC achieves the best results while adding only 3.95% token overhead and 26.18% latency over the vanilla model, making it practical for edge deployment.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM-based robot task planning line (SayCan, DAG-Plan, HPE, LLM+P) by introducing a systematic evaluation axis—coreferential vagueness from implicit referring expressions—that prior benchmarks (ALFRED, LoTA-Bench, ProgPrompt) entirely omit. Conceptually, it draws on Levinson's pragmatic theory of referring expressions and bridge inference to formalize three levels of RE difficulty and three context memory types, bridging linguistics and embodied AI. The proposed TOCC method is a lightweight prompting strategy that builds on the observation that LLMs can resolve implicit REs when explicitly prompted, decoupling language comprehension from the planning step rather than relying on heavier techniques like CoT or ICL.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

REI-Bench is the first benchmark to systematically quantify how coreferential vagueness degrades robot task planning (up to 36.9% success rate drops), providing a much-needed evaluation tool for HRI scenarios involving non-expert users like the elderly and children. The finding that object omission—not execution error—is the dominant failure mode reframes the problem as a language-understanding bottleneck rather than a planning-algorithm one, suggesting that even strong planners will fail if the LLM misidentifies the target object. Key limitations include text-only evaluation in AI2-THOR (no visual/spatial grounding), restriction to simple short-horizon single-objective tasks, and reliance on small open-source LLMs; open questions include whether findings transfer to VLM-based planners, long-horizon tasks, and physical robots, and how deictic and syntactic vagueness interact with coreferential vagueness.
