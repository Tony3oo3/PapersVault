## Problem

Autoregressive music generators produce convincing music through statistical learning, yet their internal representations remain opaque. Prior interpretability work on music models has focused on probing for known concepts (chords, tempo, genre), leaving open the question of what additional or unexpected musical structures these models encode. This paper addresses that gap by asking 'what concepts do models encode?' without any supervision, rather than testing whether a specific known concept is present.

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes ~10-second music clips from MusicSet (~160K samples) by feeding them through pre-trained MusicGen-Large (2048-dim) and MusicGen-Small (1024-dim) to extract residual stream activations at five depth positions per model. Outputs include sparse latent feature codes, automatically generated concept labels (from Gemini multimodal LLM and Essentia classifiers), CLAP alignment scores quantifying label-audio consistency, and steered audio generations produced by injecting scaled decoder weight vectors into the residual stream during inference with a fixed text prompt.

## Method

The core contribution is training k-sparse autoencoders (k∈{32,100}, expansion factors 4× or 32×) on MusicGen residual stream activations to extract interpretable musical features, the first application of SAEs to the audio/music domain. After training, features are filtered by activation prevalence (discarding those active in <1% or >25% of validation tracks), then characterized by their top-10 activating audio examples. A multi-stage automated labeling pipeline combines Gemini Flash 1.5 generative captioning, Essentia pre-trained audio classifiers, and CLAP cross-modal alignment scoring to name and validate thousands of features at scale. For steering, the corresponding SAE decoder weight vector is added to the residual stream at the hook layer during generation, scaled by the feature's maximum activation strength, enabling direct manipulation of discovered concepts in generated output.

## Results

Across 10 SAE configurations (2 expansion factors × 2 sparsity levels) and 10 model-layer combinations, the pipeline retains a total of 4,697 interpretable features after filtering, with the best single configuration (MGL, Layer 2, EF=32, k=100) yielding 2,344 features. For generation steering, 15–35% of tested features show positive CLAP alignment improvement under steering (best: 35.1% at MGL L36, EF=32, k=100). In a human listening study, participants correctly identified the SAE-steered audio in 66/100 trials versus 17/100 for both baseline and random-direction controls (χ²=48.02, p<.0001). For automated labeling, Essentia classifier tags outperform Gemini LLM labels on human-rated confidence (3.96/5 vs. 3.19/5; 71% vs. 47% rated >4/5).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an interpretability study without external method baselines; comparisons are internal to the pipeline. On labeling quality, Essentia pre-trained audio classifiers consistently outperform Gemini multimodal LLM labels: human raters gave Essentia tags higher confidence (3.96/5, 71% above 4) versus Gemini (3.19/5, 47% above 4), and Essentia tags achieve stronger CLAP alignment scores overall. On model scale, MusicGen-Large produces substantially more interpretable features than MusicGen-Small (e.g., 2,344 vs. 59 at the best layer) and shows sharper layer-specific feature organization (MLP layer-probe accuracy 50.29% vs. 40.51%). On SAE hyperparameters, the combination of high expansion factor (EF=32) and high sparsity (k=100) yields the most features, while early layers (L2) dominate in feature count but later layers (L24–L36) produce more interpretable and steerable features (35.1% steering improvement at L36 vs. 15.3% at L46). For steering, SAE-driven feature manipulation clearly outperforms both unsteered baselines and random-direction matched-norm steering in perceptual identification (66% vs. 17% vs. 17%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the SAE-based interpretability framework developed for LLMs (Templeton et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2024) and the k-sparse autoencoder formulation (Makhzani & Frey, 2013) to the music domain, claiming to be the first application of SAEs to audio. It departs from prior music-model interpretability work (Wei et al., 2024a; Ma & Xia, 2024; Vásquez et al., 2024), which relies on probing for pre-specified concepts, by instead performing unsupervised feature discovery that can surface patterns with no existing theoretical label. The automated labeling pipeline combines multimodal LLM captioning (Gemini), pre-trained Essentia classifiers, and CLAP cross-modal alignment, drawing on techniques from protein-language-model interpretability (Simon & Zou, 2024; Gujral et al., 2025) adapted to the musical setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first empirical evidence that autoregressive music generators encode coherent musical regularities that lack counterparts in existing theory (e.g., 'Romantic Poppy MIDI Piano,' 'Oscillating Bell-like Timbres'), offering a concrete method for music theorists to identify and formalize such patterns. However, steering success is limited to 15–35% of features, CLAP-based evaluation is acknowledged as imperfect, and the text-conditioned analysis the authors identify as a natural next step remains open due to dataset and methodological gaps. The finding that model scale substantially alters representational organization (MGL yields thousands of interpretable features vs. ~100 for MGS) raises open questions about how interpretability scales with architecture size in generative models more broadly.
