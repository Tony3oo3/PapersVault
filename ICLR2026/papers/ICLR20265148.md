## Problem

Existing MLLM benchmarks fail to comprehensively assess real-world reasoning: spatial datasets focus on simple object-level relationships rather than multi-scale environments (rooms, buildings, cities), commonsense benchmarks lack spatial grounding, and most evaluate static image understanding rather than interaction and decision-making. SpaCE-Eval addresses these gaps by jointly testing spatial reasoning, commonsense knowledge, and environment interaction across a wide range of spatial scales.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20265148/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each question pairs a newly created infographic diagram (not a statistical chart) with a single-answer, four-option multiple-choice question. Unusually, 40% of questions present purely visual answer options rendered as part of the image rather than as text, requiring the model to interpret visual descriptions of alternatives. The diagrams span nine spatial scales from individual objects (furniture, tiles) through rooms, buildings, spatial structures, and urban contexts, and the answer positions are randomly shuffled to prevent positional bias.

## Method

The benchmark is constructed by 51 design-trained university students who draw brand-new infographics for every subcategory, eliminating data-contamination risk and ensuring visual diversity. Two questions per diagram are written with explicit requirements that reasoning (not pattern matching) is necessary and linguistic shortcuts are avoided. A five-stage quality pipeline follows: weekly meta-annotator calibration meetings, volunteer peer review for clarity and logic, independent external reviewer sessions, adversarial rewriting of 50 questions to remove surface-level cues (e.g., option length or sentiment), and final author filtering that removes 41 diagrams and 345 questions, yielding 701 diagrams and 1,139 questions across three categories and twelve subcategories. The paper then evaluates 22 proprietary and open-source MLLMs plus a human baseline, using GPT-4o-mini as a judge for semantically equivalent but differently phrased predictions.

## Results

SpaCE-Eval is a highly challenging benchmark: the best overall accuracy across all 21 evaluated MLLMs is only 56.37% (GPT-5), while the highest Spatial Reasoning score is merely 42.25% (also GPT-5), barely above the 25% random-guess baseline for 4-choice questions. Commonsense Knowledge and Environment Interaction are comparatively easier, with GPT-5 reaching 66.08% and 61.63% respectively. Human volunteers achieve 79.00% overall, with 84.18% in Spatial Reasoning and 81.34% in Environment Interaction, revealing a large gap between current MLLMs and human performance on reasoning-intensive tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-5 dominates all three categories, achieving 56.37% overall, 42.25% in Spatial Reasoning, 66.08% in Commonsense Knowledge, and 61.63% in Environment Interaction, outperforming the second-best proprietary model (GPT-5-mini at 52.15%) by over 4 points overall. Among proprietary models, claude-sonnet-4 (48.64%) and gemini-2.5-flash (47.50%) form a mid-tier group, while GPT-4o-mini (39.68%) and grok-2-vision (42.32%) trail significantly. In the open-source tier, Llama-4-Maverick (45.92%) is the strongest, followed by glm-4.1v-9b (43.37%) and llava-onevision-7b (42.41%), all of which exceed the majority of open-source models that cluster below 40%. A consistent pattern across all models is the dramatic drop in Spatial Reasoning (best 42.25%) versus Commonsense Knowledge (best 66.08%), a gap of over 24 points, indicating that spatial reasoning remains the principal bottleneck. Larger models within the same family consistently outperform smaller ones (e.g., gemma-3-27b-it at 39.77% vs. gemma-3-4b-it at 36.00%; Qwen2.5-VL-72B at 37.84% vs. Qwen2.5-VL-7B at 37.14%), though the margin is modest. No model approaches the human average of 79.00%, with the largest gap appearing in Spatial Reasoning (84.18% human vs. 42.25% best model).

## Relation to Prior Work

SpaCE-Eval carves new ground by unifying three evaluation dimensions—multi-scale spatial reasoning, commonsense knowledge, and environment interaction—into a single VQA benchmark, whereas prior work addresses these in isolation. Spatial reasoning benchmarks (Spatial VQA, SpatialEval) focus on simple relative positions or abstract mazes rather than real-world spaces spanning rooms to cities; commonsense benchmarks (PIQA, VisualCOMET, CulturalVQA) lack spatial grounding; and interaction datasets (ALFRED, TEACH) target embodied agents following instructions rather than open-ended decision-making. The benchmark's use of freshly created human-drawn infographics (rather than photographs or synthetic scenes) is a deliberate departure from both photographic VQA datasets and synthetic 3D environments, aiming to eliminate data contamination while preserving visual diversity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a stark and consistent capability gap: the best model (GPT-5) achieves only 42.25% on spatial reasoning—barely above the 25% random baseline—while humans score 84.18%, and performance degrades monotonically as spatial scale increases from objects to urban contexts. This has direct implications for deploying MLLMs in robotics, autonomous navigation, and embodied agents, where multi-scale spatial reasoning is a prerequisite. Open questions include whether the scale-dependent degradation reflects a fundamental architectural limitation or a data-coverage gap, and whether the visual-options gap (models perform significantly worse on purely visual answer choices) can be closed through better visual tokenization or training. The benchmark's 1,139 hand-crafted items, while rigorous, are modest in scale, and whether findings generalize beyond architecture-style diagrams remains to be tested.
