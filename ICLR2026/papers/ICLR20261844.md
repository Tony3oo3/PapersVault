## Problem

Despite widespread empirical use of test-time scaling strategies like self-consistency, best-of-n, and self-correction in LLMs, their theoretical sample efficiency and representational capabilities remain poorly understood. Specifically, no prior work provides matching upper and lower bounds on the number of samples needed by self-consistency versus best-of-n, nor a formal characterization of whether a single Transformer can express multiple task-solving capabilities and adapt at inference time via self-correction.

## Input / Output

The method takes a user query (e.g., a math problem or 3-SAT instance) and, in the self-correction setting, iteratively consumes verifier reward signals appended to the prompt after each generated response. For self-consistency and best-of-n, the output is a single selected answer chosen from n i.i.d. samples; for self-correction, the output is a sequence of (action, response, reward) tuples where the action designates which expert to invoke and the response is the expert's generated solution, with the final response returned after T iterations. The self-correction protocol (Algorithm 1) autoregressively updates the prompt by concatenating each new action, response, and reward, making the dependence structure fundamentally non-i.i.d. compared to repeated sampling.

## Method

The paper establishes two main theoretical results. First, it proves a sample complexity separation: self-consistency requires Θ(1/Δ²) samples (via Hoeffding's inequality with a union bound over all incorrect answers for the upper bound, and a two-answer hard instance for the lower bound), while best-of-n requires only Θ(1/Δ) samples (via a geometric tail argument on the probability of at least one correct sample). Second, it introduces the General-Purpose Transformer framework, constructing a unified Transformer of type (O(K), O(log N_max)) from K expert Transformers by embedding block-diagonal attention matrices, set-membership-based positional encodings, and dedicated attention heads that route computation to the correct expert based on indicator tokens. The unified Transformer simulates a regret-minimization bandit algorithm over the expert pool at test time: it autoregressively selects an expert (action), generates a response, queries a verifier for reward, and updates its selection, achieving regret o(1) and thus solving any task in the expert pool without prior knowledge of which task the query belongs to.

## Results

On AIME 2024 & 2025, self-correction with only 4 samples achieves 79.29% (Qwen3-1.7B), 81.19% (Qwen3-4B), and 24.52% (Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct) accuracy, substantially outperforming both best-of-n (4 samples) and self-consistency (64 samples). Best-of-n with just 4 samples consistently exceeds self-consistency with 64 samples (e.g., 59.68% vs. 58.33% for Qwen3-1.7B), empirically confirming the theoretical Θ(1/Δ) vs. Θ(1/Δ²) sample complexity separation. In the synthetic 3-SAT experiment, models without self-correction plateau at 63.19% accuracy regardless of size, while larger models (GPT-mini, Gopher-44M) reach near-perfect accuracy when equipped with verifier feedback.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Self-correction (the paper's focus) decisively outperforms both baselines across all three models. Against self-consistency (Wang et al., 2023), self-correction with 4 samples beats self-consistency with 16× more samples by 20.96 points on Qwen3-1.7B (79.29% vs. 58.33%) and by 22.85 points on Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct (24.52% vs. 1.67%). Against best-of-n (Qiu et al., 2024), self-correction with the same 4-sample budget wins by 19.61 points on Qwen3-1.7B (79.29% vs. 59.68%) and by 19.68 points on Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct (24.52% vs. 4.84%). Notably, best-of-n with only 4 samples already surpasses self-consistency with 64 samples on every model, validating the paper's theoretical prediction that best-of-n requires Θ(1/Δ) samples versus Θ(1/Δ²) for self-consistency. The advantage of self-correction is most pronounced for smaller models, suggesting that verifier-guided iterative refinement compensates for limited model capacity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Transformer expressiveness literature (Yun et al. 2020; Feng et al. 2023; Li et al. 2024b), which has largely focused on single-task representational capacity, to a multi-task setting via the concept of a General-Purpose Transformer that composes expert models into a unified architecture. For sample complexity, it builds on the sharpening framework of Huang et al. (2024a) and the bandit-algorithm-in-Transformers constructions of Lin et al. (2023), but provides the first explicit separation between self-consistency and best-of-n in terms of the probability gap Δ. The self-correction expressiveness result departs from prior work by showing that verifier feedback enables non-i.i.d. response generation that provably increases correctness over time, in contrast to the i.i.d. structure of repeated sampling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Θ(1/Δ²) vs. Θ(1/Δ) separation gives practitioners a concrete theoretical justification for preferring best-of-n over self-consistency, and the multi-task expressiveness result provides the first formal account of why a single LLM can adapt to diverse tasks at test time via self-correction. Key limitations include the assumption that the correct answer is the most probable (Δ > 0), the dependence on a well-behaved verifier, and the theoretical construction's O(K) width scaling that may not directly translate to practical architectures. Open questions include whether the regret guarantees hold under noisy or adversarial verifiers, how the results extend to open-ended generation tasks beyond discrete answer selection, and whether the sample complexity gap persists when the probability gap Δ is very small in practice.
