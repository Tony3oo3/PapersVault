## Problem

Vision-language reinforcement learning has been confined to narrow domains like geometry and chart reasoning, leaving broader training scenarios underexplored and limiting VLMs' exploration through RL. Video games inherently provide rich visual elements, easily verifiable mechanics, and fully controllable difficulty, yet prior work has only used games as evaluation benchmarks rather than training resources. This paper asks whether RL training on synthesized game tasks can transfer to general multimodal reasoning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261189/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each training and evaluation sample consists of a procedurally generated static game-state image (e.g., a Sokoban grid, a Tangram puzzle, a 3D cube reconstruction view) at roughly 500×470 pixels, paired with a text question that specifies the game rules and asks for a specific answer. The model produces a step-by-step reasoning chain followed by a final answer in a fixed format: either a multiple-choice option number (from 7–8 options) or a short fill-in-the-blank value such as coordinates or a number. Difficulty is controlled at two axes — QA Level (reasoning complexity) and Plot Level (image/grid complexity) — both adjustable via code parameters during data generation.

## Method

The core pipeline, Code2Logic, establishes a mapping from game code to reasoning logic in three steps: (1) LLMs (GPT-4o, Claude 3.5) construct the target game's code, defining its state space and core transition functions (e.g., Sokoban's move function); (2) LLM-assisted design of QA templates that extract one reasoning pattern per template (Target Perception, State Prediction, Strategy Optimization); (3) a data engine reuses the game code's core functions to batch-generate unlimited task instances by randomly initializing game states, proposing action sequences, simulating outcomes, and filling templates. Training uses GRPO with full-parameter fine-tuning of the language model (visual encoder frozen), 12 rollouts per question, and an LLM-as-a-judge (Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct-AWQ) that assigns a binary reward based solely on whether the final answer is semantically equivalent to ground truth. The key insight is that game code inherently encodes verifiable transition rules, enabling scalable synthesis of diverse, difficulty-graded reasoning data without human annotation.

## Results

Training solely on GameQA with GRPO yields consistent improvements across all 7 general vision benchmarks: Game-RL-Qwen2.5-VL-7B improves average score from 50.00 to 52.65 (+2.65), Game-RL-InternVL2.5-8B from 45.80 to 48.40 (+2.60), and Game-RL-InternVL3-8B from 54.15 to 56.05 (+1.90). On the GameQA benchmark itself, in-domain accuracy improves by +6.34 to +7.22 points and out-of-domain by +1.16 to +3.82 points. Notably, GameQA-5K training outperforms MAVIS-8K and Multimodal-Open-R1-8K on out-of-domain game generalization (29.87 vs. 27.61 and 28.33) while using fewer training samples.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Game-RL consistently outperforms its own base models across all 7 general vision benchmarks, with the largest gains on CharXiv (+4.16 for Qwen2.5-VL-7B) and MathVista (+1.86 to +4.52). Compared to larger open-source models, Game-RL-Qwen2.5-VL-7B (52.65 avg) surpasses InternVL2.5-38B (55.62) on CharXiv (42.08 vs. 41.50) and approaches Qwen2.5-VL-32B (60.70) on several benchmarks, though it remains well below proprietary models like Gemini-2.5-Pro (73.1) and GPT-4o (56.9). In the comparative training experiment (Table 4), GameQA-5K with GRPO achieves 29.87 on out-of-domain games, outperforming MAVIS-8K (27.61), Multimodal-Open-R1-8K (28.33), and MultiMath-8K (28.38) despite using fewer samples (5K vs. 8K). On general benchmarks, GameQA-5K (52.31 avg) is competitive with MultiMath-8K (52.81) and clearly beats MAVIS-8K (51.53) and Multimodal-Open-R1-8K (51.86). The paper also shows that pure RL (GRPO) outperforms SFT-then-RL on general benchmarks (+2.33% vs. −2.5%), demonstrating that RL better preserves out-of-domain generalization compared to SFT which causes catastrophic forgetting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of vision-language RL work that has been confined to narrow domains (geometry via MAVIS/Geo170K, charts via MultiMath) by introducing video games as a new training domain. It departs from prior game-based VLM research (ING-VP, BALROG, VideoGameBench, VCbench), which used games exclusively as evaluation benchmarks, by repurposing game mechanics as a source of verifiable RL training signals. The Code2Logic pipeline draws conceptual parallels to procedural geometry synthesis (Trinh et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024c) but generalizes the idea to arbitrary game code, and the training recipe builds directly on GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) with an LLM-as-a-judge reward for handling diverse answer formats.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that 5K game samples outperform 8K geometry/math samples on general vision benchmarks challenges the assumption that RL training data must be in-domain to transfer, suggesting game environments are an underexplored scaling axis for VLM reasoning. The demonstrated scaling effects (both data volume and game diversity) point toward a practical recipe for generating unlimited verifiable training data at low cost (~7.5 hours per game). Key limitations include the single-turn QA format, the large gap to human performance (30% vs. 84%), and the observation that SFT on the same data causes catastrophic forgetting while pure RL avoids it—open questions include whether multi-turn game interactions and process-level supervision can close the remaining gap.
