## Problem

Existing contextual integrity benchmarks for LLMs evaluate simple scenarios with minimal information (e.g., a single secret to protect), failing to capture the compositional nature of persistent memories where the same personal attribute may be essential for one task but inappropriate for another. This paper asks whether frontier LLMs can appropriately control information flow from accumulated user memories based on the specific task context and recipient.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263238/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes a synthetic user profile (averaging 147 attributes across nine information domains, formatted as natural-language memory statements in OpenAI's template) concatenated with a task context specifying a goal and recipient (e.g., 'Apply for personal loan' → 'Bank Loan Officer'). The model produces a free-text response, which is then scored by an LLM judge (DeepSeek-R1) implementing a REVEAL function that maps each attribute to its inferred value or ⊥. Two complementary metrics are computed: attribute-level Violation@n (worst-case over n=5 generations, measuring whether any inappropriate attribute is ever revealed) and task-level Completeness (average-case, measuring the fraction of necessary attributes shared per task).

## Method

CIMemories introduces two forms of compositionality absent from prior CI benchmarks: (1) flexible memory composition, where the number and designation of attributes as necessary versus inappropriate are dynamically varied to study how memory density affects privacy adherence, and (2) multi-task composition, where each user is evaluated across ~45 contexts with per-task, per-attribute annotations so the same attribute can be labeled necessary in one context and inappropriate in another. Contextual integrity labels are generated by prompting GPT-5 with three Westin privacy personas (fundamentalist, pragmatic, unconcerned), sampling 10 labels per persona, and retaining only pairs where all personas agree (zero-entropy label distributions), focusing on unambiguous violations. The evaluation framework concatenates memory statements as a prefix to the task prompt, samples multiple responses, and uses DeepSeek-R1 as an automated judge to determine which specific attributes were revealed, enabling scalable measurement of both violation and completeness without human annotation at test time.

## Results

Across 10 synthetic user profiles (avg. 147 attributes, 45 contexts each), all frontier models exhibit substantial contextual integrity violations: GPT-5 achieves 25.08% Violation@5 with 56.61% completeness, GPT-4o achieves the lowest violations (14.82%) but at the cost of the lowest completeness (43.95%), and Qwen-3 32B reaches the highest violations (69.14%) with near-highest completeness (57.63%). Violations accumulate with usage: GPT-5's violations rise from 0.1% (1 task) to 9.6% (40 tasks), and further to 25.1% when the same prompt is sampled 5 times. On the simpler ConfAide benchmark, GPT-5 achieves 0% violation, confirming that CIMemories surfaces new compositional failure modes invisible to prior CI evaluations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a benchmark paper, so the 'baselines' are the frontier models being evaluated. No single model dominates both metrics: GPT-4o minimizes violations (14.82%) but sacrifices completeness (43.95%), while Claude-4 Sonnet achieves the highest completeness (59.07%) yet still leaks 44.44% of inappropriate attributes. Qwen-3 32B represents the opposite extreme—near-best completeness (57.63%) at the cost of the worst violations (69.14%). Reasoning models (o3, GPT-5) do not clearly outperform non-reasoning counterparts on violations; o3 actually shows higher violations (38.51%) than GPT-5 (25.08%). Scaling within the Qwen-3 family (1.7B→32B) initially improves both metrics but saturates, and privacy-conscious prompting on GPT-5 merely shifts the violation-completeness tradeoff without resolving it. The paper also shows that GPT-5's 0% violation on the simpler ConfAide benchmark contrasts sharply with its 25.08% on CIMemories, indicating that compositional, multi-attribute memory settings expose failure modes that single-secret benchmarks miss.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of contextual integrity (CI) evaluation for LLMs established by ConfAide, PrivacyLens, CI-Bench, GoldCoin, and LLM-CI, which typically assess simple scenarios with a single secret to protect. The key departure is a compositional design targeting memory-augmented assistants: synthetic profiles with 100+ attributes across nine domains are paired with 45+ task contexts, and per-attribute labels are generated using Westin's privacy personas (fundamentalist, pragmatic, unconcerned) with a powerful LLM judge. Unlike prior CI benchmarks that treat information as a monolithic secret, CIMemories explicitly models the setting where the same attribute is necessary in one context but inappropriate in another, and where violations accumulate across repeated interactions with a persistent memory.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that frontier models exhibit up to 69% attribute-level violations and that these violations accumulate over both tasks and repeated runs, exposing a fundamental granularity failure where models identify the correct information domain but cannot distinguish necessary from unnecessary details within it. Critically, neither scaling (improvements saturate) nor privacy-conscious prompting (which merely trades completeness for violations) resolves the issue, pointing toward the need for contextually aware reasoning or dedicated post-training objectives. Limitations include synthetic profiles that may not capture real-world nuance, single-turn interactions, and non-tool-use settings, leaving open whether the findings generalize to agentic, multi-turn deployments.
