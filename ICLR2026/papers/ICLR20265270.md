## Problem

Gradient methods for convex optimization require careful tuning of stepsizes and other hyperparameters that depend on unknown problem-specific quantities such as smoothness constants and the distance to the minimizer. Existing parameter-free distance-adaptation methods (DoG, DoWG, D-Adaptation, Prodigy) either fail to extend to constrained problems, require bounded feasible sets, or do not achieve convergence rates that adapt to the local structure of the objective across diverse function classes. This paper asks whether a single parameter-free first-order method can simultaneously handle constrained and unconstrained problems on unbounded domains while delivering problem-dependent convergence rates for nonsmooth, Lipschitz-smooth, Hölder-smooth, quasi-self-concordant, high-order smooth, and (L₀,L₁)-smooth functions.

## Input / Output

The method requires only a subdifferentiable convex function f, a closed convex constraint set Q with efficient projection, a starting point x₀, and a small positive lower-bound estimate r̄ on the distance ‖x₀−x*‖ (no upper bound or exact value needed). It outputs the best iterate x*_T = argmin_{k} f(x_k) along with an explicit suboptimality bound f(x*_T)−f* ≤ ω(v*_T), where ω is the local growth function of f around x* and v*_T is the minimum subgradient-distance-to-hyperplane over all iterates. The convergence rate T_v(δ) = O(D²/δ² · log²(eD̄/r̄)) depends on the function class only through ω, making the guarantee automatically problem-adaptive without any class-specific parameter selection.

## Method

DADA instantiates the Weighted Dual Averaging (WDA) scheme with dynamically adapted coefficients a_k = r̄_k/‖g_k‖_* and β_k = c√(k+1), where r̄_k = max{max_{1≤t≤k} ‖x₀−x_t‖, r̄} tracks the running maximum distance from the starting point. The key insight is that normalizing each gradient by its own norm (rather than accumulating squared norms à la AdaGrad) makes the method universal: the subgradient-distance-to-hyperplane v(x_k) provably converges to zero, and the convergence rate adapts to the local growth ω(t) of f around x*. The distance adaptation reduces the penalty for an unknown initial distance D₀ from a multiplicative factor ρ² (as in classical WDA) to a logarithmic factor log²ρ, where ρ = D₀/r̄. The analysis proceeds by bounding r̄_k uniformly via an induction argument (Lemma 7), then applying a combinatorial inequality on nondecreasing sequences (Lemma 2) to extract the min-over-iterates rate. The optimal constant c = 2√2 minimizes the leading constant in the complexity bound, and the method requires no line search, no restarts, and no prior knowledge of the number of iterations or target accuracy.

## Results

DADA achieves a universal convergence rate of v*_T ≤ (eD/√T)·log(eD̄/r̄), where the only penalty for not knowing the initial distance D₀ is a logarithmic factor log²(D̄₀/r̄) rather than the multiplicative ρ² penalty of standard Weighted Dual Averaging. Across function classes, DADA matches standard (non-accelerated) gradient method rates up to this log² factor: O(L₀²D̄₀²/ε²) for nonsmooth Lipschitz, O(L₁D̄₀²/ε) for Lipschitz-smooth, O([H_ν/ε]^{2/(1+ν)}D̄₀²) for Hölder-smooth, and O([M²D̄₀² + ‖∇²f(x*)‖D̄₀²/ε]) for quasi-self-concordant functions. The method requires no line search, no knowledge of smoothness constants, and works on both constrained and unconstrained problems with unbounded domains.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DADA is the only method in Table 1 that simultaneously achieves universality (problem-dependent rates across all six function classes), handles constraints, works on unbounded domains, and requires no internal search procedure. Against DoG, DADA's key advantage is that its convergence rate adapts to the local smoothness of the objective (e.g., O(ε^{-2/(p+1)}) for p-th order smoothness), whereas DoG's rate remains O(ε^{-2}) regardless of smoothness; empirically, DADA converges significantly faster than DoG on the worst-case function as p increases. Against Prodigy, DADA handles constrained problems (which Prodigy does not) and shows more stable convergence in experiments—Prodigy's convergence stalls after a few iterations on the Softmax and worst-case problems. Against UGM, DADA avoids line search (making it applicable to constrained and composite problems) and extends to function classes beyond Hölder-smooth (QSC, (L₀,L₁)-smooth, high-order smooth), though UGM's log factor is additive rather than multiplicative. Against standard WDA, DADA replaces the ρ² penalty for misestimating D₀ with a much milder log²ρ penalty, a substantial improvement when D₀ is poorly known.

## Relation to Prior Work

DADA builds directly on Nesterov's Weighted Dual Averaging (WDA) framework, replacing the fixed user-supplied estimate of the initial distance D₀ with a dynamically updated quantity based on observed iterate distances. It departs from the AdaGrad-style accumulation of squared gradient norms used by DoG, DoWG, D-Adaptation, and Prodigy, instead normalizing each gradient by its own norm—a simple modification that, combined with the DA structure, yields universality across function classes (QSC, high-order smooth, (L₀,L₁)-smooth) that prior distance-adaptation methods do not cover. The paper also extends the scope of universal methods beyond Hölder-smooth functions (the domain of Nesterov's UGM) and handles constrained problems with unbounded domains, which Prodigy and D-Adaptation do not support.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DADA provides a single parameter-free first-order method whose convergence rate automatically adapts to the local growth of the objective, eliminating the need to know smoothness constants, Lipschitz parameters, or the distance to the optimum. The QSC class in particular had not been studied with first-order methods before, making this a genuine gap-filling contribution. Key open questions include extending DADA to stochastic and nonconvex settings, and evaluating whether the theoretical universality translates to practical advantages in large-scale deep learning where the function class is rarely known a priori. The logarithmic (rather than multiplicative) penalty for an inaccurate initial distance estimate is a meaningful practical improvement over WDA, though the method remains deterministic and non-stochastic.
