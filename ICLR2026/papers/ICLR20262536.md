## Problem

Existing LLM benchmarks produce contradictory model rankings even for tasks targeting similar skills, because models are trained on heterogeneous data mixes that confound inherent capability with task-specific pretraining exposure. This ranking disagreement makes it impossible to reliably select or compare models across benchmarks, undermining both model development decisions and the scientific value of benchmarking.

## Input / Output

The method takes 61 language models (base and instruction-tuned, ≤14B parameters, from six families) and 24 benchmarks each providing at least 1,000 training examples (capped at 50K train / 1K validation / 10K test). It also uses three 2025-only text corpora (Wikipedia, StackExchange, arXiv) for perplexity measurement, ensuring no pretraining contamination. Outputs include per-benchmark model rankings, pairwise Kendall's τ correlations across all 276 benchmark pairs, PCA decompositions of the 24×61 score matrix, and perplexity rankings in bits-per-byte.

## Method

The core procedure, called train-before-test, fine-tunes every model on the same benchmark-specific training data before evaluation, using LoRA (rank 8, α=32, dropout 0.1) with AdamW (weight decay 0.01) for 5 epochs over learning rates {1e-5, 2e-5, 5e-5}, selecting the best checkpoint via a held-out validation set. This equalizes task preparation across models, removing the confound of differential pretraining exposure. The paper then measures cross-benchmark ranking agreement via Kendall's τ, correlates perplexity rankings with downstream rankings, and applies PCA to the benchmark-model score matrix. The key finding is that train-before-test raises average cross-benchmark Kendall's τ from 0.52 to 0.76, restores the perplexity–downstream connection (τ from 0.48 to 0.74), and reduces the score matrix to essentially rank one (PC1 variance from 70% to 86%), revealing that model potential is dominated by a single latent factor.

## Results

Train-before-test improves average cross-benchmark ranking agreement (Kendall's τ) from 0.52 (direct evaluation, 0-shot) to 0.76, with 274 out of 276 benchmark pairs showing improvement. The method also restores the perplexity–downstream connection: ranking correlation between perplexity and downstream benchmarks rises from 0.48 to 0.74, and for base models, pre-fine-tuning perplexity alone predicts post-fine-tuning downstream performance at τ = 0.78. PCA of the benchmark–model score matrix shows PC1 explained variance increasing from 70% (direct) to 86% (train-before-test) across all models, and reaching 93% for the QWEN family, indicating model potential is dominated by a single latent factor.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between direct evaluation (0-shot and 5-shot) and the proposed train-before-test protocol across 24 benchmarks and 61 models. Train-before-test outperforms 0-shot direct evaluation on 274 of 276 benchmark pairs (average τ: 0.76 vs. 0.52) and outperforms 5-shot direct evaluation on 89% of benchmark pairs (0.76 vs. 0.61). The largest gains appear for benchmarks that were outliers under direct evaluation: NQ-Open improves from τ = 0.23 to 0.74, and MRPC from 0.34 to 0.71. For perplexity alignment, train-before-test raises the correlation between perplexity rankings and downstream rankings from 0.48 to 0.74, and for base models, even pre-fine-tuning perplexity predicts post-fine-tuning downstream performance at τ = 0.78 (vs. 0.51 for instruction-tuned models). In the PCA analysis, train-before-test increases PC1 explained variance from 70% to 86% overall and to 93% for the QWEN family, whereas direct evaluation leaves substantial variance in higher-order components (PC2 at 13% overall, 27% for GEMMA), which the authors attribute to differential pre-training data exposure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes parameter-efficient fine-tuning (LoRA/PEFT) as an evaluation tool rather than a training technique, building on the 'training on the test task' confound identified by Dominguez-Olmedo et al. (2024) and the observation that LLM benchmark rankings are inconsistent (Liang et al., 2023; Beeching et al., 2023). It extends the low-rank analysis of benchmark score matrices (Ruan et al., 2024; Burnell et al., 2023) by showing that standardized fine-tuning collapses the matrix to near rank-one, and revives the perplexity-downstream connection that was considered broken (Wei et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023). The methodology draws an analogy to ImageNet-era benchmarking where model rankings transferred well across tasks (Kornblith et al., 2019), arguing that the LLM era's inconsistent rankings stem from unequal pretraining exposure rather than genuinely multifaceted capabilities.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Train-before-test offers a practical, externally valid protocol for model selection when practitioners plan to adapt models to downstream tasks, potentially reducing the number of benchmarks needed for reliable comparison. The finding that pre-fine-tuning perplexity predicts post-fine-tuning performance for base models (τ=0.78) suggests a simple, cheap proxy for model potential that could guide early-stage model selection without running full benchmark suites. Key limitations include the requirement for benchmark training data (increasingly unavailable), dependence on PEFT rather than full fine-tuning, and the inability to apply the method to closed models that do not permit fine-tuning. Open questions include whether the rank-1 structure persists with full fine-tuning, whether it generalizes to open-ended generation tasks, and how it behaves at the 70B+ parameter scale where most frontier models reside.
