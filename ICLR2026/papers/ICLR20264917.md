## Problem

LLMs trained on academic papers and reports learn to mimic surface writing style but never observe the planning, organization, and citation-selection intents that guide human authors. These high-level intents remain invisible in final texts, leaving models without explicit signals for how to structure paragraphs or justify citation choices, which limits attribution quality in long-form scientific report generation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264917/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system takes a user query and a fixed set of retrieved document snippets (from Semantic Scholar keyword and snippet search APIs, with salient parts extracted by an LLM) as input. It produces a multi-section report where each paragraph is preceded by an inline tag of the form <bpit>[discourse mode] rationale <epit> (e.g., Exposition, Compare-contrast) and each inline citation is preceded by <bcit>[ACL-ARC category] rationale <ecit> (e.g., Background, Uses, Comparison or Contrast). The rationales are brief natural-language explanations linking the paragraph's function or the sentence-citation relationship to the chosen intent type.

## Method

The paper proposes a two-level intent-aware writing framework: paragraph intents drawn from discourse modes (Song et al., 2017) and citation intents from the six-category ACL-ARC taxonomy (Jurgens et al., 2018), both represented as inline tags with natural-language rationales. At inference time, models are prompted to generate reports with these tags embedded (verbalized intents), acting as a form of test-time scaling that elicits structured planning alongside content. For training smaller models, a teacher model (gemini-2.5-pro) generates intent-annotated reports on 1,000 OpenScholar queries, which are then used for SFT in three variants: intent-implicit (tags stripped before training), intent-explicit (tags and rationales retained as training signal), and intent-multiview (each report decomposed into four instruction-report pairs covering all, paragraph-only, citation-only, and no-intent views, following Liang et al., 2023). The multiview decomposition reduces per-instance instruction complexity while exposing the student model to all intent perspectives, and the approach is plug-and-play, leaving existing RAG architectures intact.

## Results

On SQA-CS-V2, intent-aware SFT yields +7.9, +22.8, and +6.1 absolute points over base models for qwen3-8b, llama3.1-8b, and qwen3-4b respectively, with the best 8B variants (intent-multiview SFT) reaching 88.6 and 89.2 overall, surpassing gemini-2.5-pro (88.1). At inference time, verbalized intents improve large commercial models by +2.9 absolute points on average, with citation precision and recall gaining +3.7 points (large) and +18.7 points (small). On DeepScholar Bench, the best intent-aware configuration (Claude opus-4 + intent) achieves 59.9 overall, and on ResearchQA, gemini-2.5-pro + intent scores 79.3 on rubric evaluation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares verbalized intents against two inference-time baselines on SQA-CS-V2-dev using gemini-2.5-pro: zero-shot Chain-of-Thought (CoT, Wei et al., 2022) and ReAct (Yao et al., 2023). Verbalized intents (all categories) achieve 89.7 overall, outperforming CoT (81.3) by 8.4 points and ReAct (77.6) by 12.1 points, with the gap driven primarily by citation precision (95.7 vs. 83.3 and 76.5) and citation recall (86.1 vs. 76.1 and 72.0). For training, the key baseline is standard SFT on teacher-generated reports without intent awareness (baseline SFT); intent-aware SFT variants consistently outperform it by 4.8–5.8 points on overall score for 8B models. The intent-multiview variant further decomposes training into four sub-task views, yielding the best results for 8B models (88.6 for qwen3-8b, 89.2 for llama3.1-8b), while for the smaller qwen3-4b, intent-explicit SFT edges out multiview (87.5 vs. 87.0), suggesting that explicit intent tags serve as useful scaffolding for smaller models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes two established taxonomies—ACL-ARC's six-category citation intent classification (Jurgens et al., 2018) and discourse-mode paragraph types (Song et al., 2017)—as generation-time scaffolding rather than post-hoc analysis labels. It connects to the broader RAG and deep-research literature by inserting an explicit writing-planning step (verbalized intents at inference, intent-tagged distillation at training) without altering the underlying retrieval architecture, making it a plug-and-play extension. The multiview SFT variant draws on Liang et al. (2023) for decomposing instructions into sub-tasks, and the inference-time strategy is framed as a variant of confidence verbalization (Tian et al., 2023), positioning intent elicitation alongside chain-of-thought and ReAct as a test-time scaling technique.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The most practically significant result is that intent-aware SFT enables 8B-parameter models to match or exceed frontier commercial models (e.g., gemini-2.5-pro) on attributed report generation, suggesting that structured writing-process signals can substitute for raw scale in knowledge-intensive generation. The diagnostic finding that models systematically underuse Comparison/Contrast citation intents (~5% vs. 17% in human writing) reveals a concrete gap in synthesis ability that current benchmarks fail to capture. Open questions include whether hierarchical or graph-structured intent representations can model multi-paragraph argumentation, how intent schemas generalize beyond scientific domains to policy, law, or humanities writing, and whether grounding the schema in human writing-process data (outlining logs, document plans) would yield more faithful intent modeling.
