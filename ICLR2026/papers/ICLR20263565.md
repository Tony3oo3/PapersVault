## Problem

Standard equilibrium concepts (Nash, correlated, coarse correlated) guarantee only immunity to unilateral deviations and offer no protection against coordinated coalition deviations. Existing notions addressing coalitions—strong Nash equilibrium and coalition-proof equilibrium—generally fail to exist in general games. This paper introduces the Minimum Average-Strong Equilibrium (MASE), which minimizes the maximum average gain achievable by any coalition, and establishes both hardness results and a matching algorithm for computing it.

## Input / Output

The algorithm requires a succinctly represented multi-player game (utility functions encoded in polynomial bits in N), a set of coalitions S, and a precomputed tree decomposition of the Utility Dependency Graph (where an edge connects two players if both jointly affect some third player's utility). The output is a correlated strategy π over the joint action space, represented sparsely as a convex combination of at most Σ|S|·A^tw(G)+1 pure strategies, along with the coalition exploitability value. The exponential dependence on treewidth in the running time is the key structural constraint.

## Method

The paper reformulates MASE as a zero-sum meta-game between a correlator (choosing π) and a deviator (choosing coalition deviations), then applies Follow the Perturbed Leader (FTPL) with exponential noise to both sides. The critical technical contribution is an efficient update procedure: rather than enumerating the exponentially large joint action space, the algorithm performs dynamic programming over a tree decomposition of the Utility Dependency Graph, where each bag's local optimization is coupled to its children through consistency constraints on overlapping players. A Carathéodory argument (Theorem 5.1) guarantees the existence of a sparse MASE representation, making the per-timestep pure-strategy selection tractable. The regret analysis yields O(√T) convergence with running time O(T·|S|·|T|·A^(tw(G)+1)), which matches the SETH-based lower bound showing no algorithm can run in O*((A−ζ)^tw(G)) time for any ζ>0.

## Results

In the Prisoner's Dilemma, MASE achieves a social welfare of 1.0 (by mixing (C,D) and (D,C) with probability 0.5 each), while all no-regret baselines converge to the unique NE/CCE (D,D) with social welfare 0.4. In the Stag Hunt, MASE converges to the Pareto-superior Nash equilibrium, whereas all baselines converge to the inferior one. On unilateral exploitability, MASE remains comparable to the baselines, but its coalition exploitability is substantially lower, and the gap widens as game size grows in random polymatrix games.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares MASE against four no-regret learning baselines: FTRL (Euclidean regularizer), Hedge, FTPL (independent per player), and OMD (Euclidean regularizer). On social welfare, MASE clearly wins: in the Prisoner's Dilemma it achieves 1.0 versus 0.4 for all baselines, and in the Stag Hunt it selects the higher-payoff equilibrium while all baselines settle on the lower one. On unilateral exploitability, MASE is roughly on par with the baselines, indicating it does not sacrifice standard equilibrium robustness. On coalition exploitability (the paper's primary metric), MASE is substantially more robust than all four baselines, and Figure 4 shows that the coalition exploitability of the baselines grows with game size in random polymatrix games, while MASE's explicit minimization of this quantity keeps it bounded. The baselines from Cesa-Bianchi & Lugosi (2006) and Hazan et al. (2016) thus serve as the standard no-regret reference, and MASE's advantage is specifically in multilateral robustness rather than unilateral performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the correlator-deviator meta-game framework of Hart & Schmeidler (1989) for correlated equilibria and the Follow the Perturbed Leader (FTPL) algorithm of Hazan et al. (2016) for no-regret learning, combining them with dynamic programming over tree decompositions. It departs from prior coalition-robust equilibrium concepts (strong Nash, coalition-proof equilibrium) that fail to exist in general games by replacing the infeasible requirement of zero coalition gain with a minimization of the maximum average gain, yielding a guaranteed-to-exist solution. The Utility Dependency Graph introduced here is distinct from the interaction graph of graphical games: two players are connected if they jointly influence some third player's utility, rather than directly influencing each other's.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first tight (matching upper and lower bound) complexity characterization for computing a coalition-robust equilibrium, establishing treewidth of the Utility Dependency Graph as the definitive parameter. This matters because as games scale, standard equilibria become increasingly fragile to multilateral deviations (demonstrated empirically), and practitioners need a principled, computable notion of stability beyond unilateral deviations. Open questions include extending beyond uniform averaging within coalitions (e.g., minimizing the minimal incentive) and characterizing the exploitability–social welfare Pareto frontier more generally.
