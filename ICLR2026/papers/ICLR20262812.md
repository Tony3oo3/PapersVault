## Problem

Existing video understanding benchmarks emphasize general actions or medical imaging but neglect the fine-grained, long-horizon, and visually subtle nature of authentic wet-lab experimentation. No systematic evaluation exists that probes the full capability spectrum needed to assist research—from perceiving individual manipulations, through verifying procedural fidelity, to connecting experimental operations to published scientific conclusions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262812/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Models receive uniformly sampled video frames (8 for Level-1 clips, 32 for Level-2 segments, up to 512 for Level-3 full videos) resized to 224×224, paired with a text question. Outputs vary by task: a single letter for MCQ tasks (perception and most procedural tasks), a comma-separated list of step numbers for Sequence Generation, or comma-separated words/phrases for Level-3 fill-in-the-blank questions. Generation is constrained to 8192 tokens at temperature 0.1, and the benchmark comprises 7,800 QA pairs across 390 videos spanning 13 scientific disciplines.

## Method

ExpVid is constructed via a vision-centric, semi-automatic annotation pipeline over peer-reviewed JoVE experiment videos. ASR transcripts are first scored on five quality dimensions by DeepSeek-R1, then segmented into three temporal scales: ~8-second action clips, ~48-second stage segments, and ~8-minute full procedures. At each level, DeepSeek-R1 extracts experimental entities and generates MCQ candidates with task-specific distractor rules (visual/functional similarity for tools and materials, comparable numeric ranges for quantities, plausible permutations for step ordering), while Qwen2.5-VL verifies that target entities are actually visible in the corresponding frames. Level-3 questions are designed by PhD-level experts from the paired peer-reviewed paper, requiring answers solvable only through visual observation across the full experiment. All annotations undergo structured expert verification by ~50 multidisciplinary annotators using a dedicated online platform, enforcing video-grounded solvability, no answer leakage, and step-level fidelity.

## Results

On ExpVid, GPT-5 achieves the highest Level-2 (Procedural Understanding) average of 57.5 and Level-3 (Scientific Reasoning) average of 56.4, substantially outperforming all other models. Gemini-2.5-Flash with thinking attains the best Level-1 (Fine-grained Perception) average of 60.2, well above the human baseline of 37.6. Among open-source models, InternVL3-78B leads with L1/L2/L3 averages of 50.9/41.9/37.7, while Intern-S1 achieves the best open-source L3 score of 39.6—still nearly 17 points below GPT-5. The gap between closed-source and open-source models widens with task complexity, from ~10 points at L1 to ~16 points at L2 and ~17 points at L3.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-5 dominates Level-2 and Level-3, scoring 57.5 and 56.4 respectively, outperforming the next-best closed-source model Gemini-2.5-Pro (53.8/47.9) by 3.7 and 8.5 points. Gemini-2.5-Flash (Think) leads Level-1 at 60.2, surpassing GPT-5 (53.3) by 6.9 points, suggesting stronger fine-grained visual perception. Among open-source models, InternVL3-78B achieves the best L1 (50.9) and L2 (41.9), while Intern-S1 leads L3 (39.6), but both trail GPT-5 by 12–17 points depending on level. Notably, InternVL3-78B outperforms GPT-5 on Step Ordering (87.1 vs. 85.1) yet falls far behind on Step Prediction (15.5 vs. 51.8) and Sequence Generation (45.5 vs. 66.9), revealing uneven procedural capabilities. The human baseline (L1: 37.6, L2: 42.1) is exceeded by all top closed-source models but approached or matched by several mid-tier open-source models on L2, indicating that procedural understanding remains a shared challenge.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing the first benchmark dedicated to scientific experiment video understanding, departing from both general video benchmarks (MVBench, Video-MME, MLVU) that lack domain-specific experimental context and scientific/knowledge-driven benchmarks (ChemBench, Video-MMMU, SCI-VID) that focus on outcome recognition or single-discipline knowledge rather than whole-experiment procedural reasoning. Conceptually, it repurposes the MLLM evaluation paradigm (MCQ, sequence generation, fill-in-the-blank) but reorganizes it into a three-level hierarchy—fine-grained perception, procedural understanding, and scientific reasoning—that mirrors how scientists actually work, from observing individual manipulations to connecting procedures to published conclusions. The vision-centric annotation pipeline, which deliberately strips textual shortcuts and requires visual grounding, distinguishes it from prior video QA benchmarks where narration or background knowledge can substitute for visual evidence.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ExpVid provides the first systematic diagnostic of where MLLMs fail on authentic laboratory footage, revealing that even frontier models like GPT-5 achieve only ~56% on scientific reasoning and that open-source models lag by 15–17 points in high-order tasks. The finding that enabling extended thinking can *degrade* performance by causing models to drift from visual evidence toward prior-based reasoning challenges a core assumption in the MLLM community and has practical implications for deploying these models as laboratory assistants. Key limitations include the focus on wet-lab experiments (excluding physics, computational, and large-scale engineering), the absence of chain-of-thought evaluation in Level-3, and the relatively small video count (390), leaving open whether findings generalize to other experimental paradigms. The benchmark is likely to become a standard reference for evaluating MLLMs in scientific video understanding.
