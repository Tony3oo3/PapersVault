## Problem

Despite conceptual promise, GraphRAG frequently underperforms vanilla RAG on real-world tasks, yet existing benchmarks (HotpotQA, MultiHopRAG, UltraDomain) cannot diagnose why: they lack granular task-complexity differentiation, rely on low-information-density corpora without explicit domain hierarchies, and treat the GraphRAG pipeline as a black box. This paper asks: under what conditions do graph structures provide measurable benefits, and what pipeline stage is responsible for success or failure?

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes two complementary corpora—NCCN medical guidelines (explicit hierarchical protocols) and pre-20th-century Gutenberg novels (implicit, non-linear narratives)—alongside queries calibrated along two axes: knowledge breadth (number of ontology triples required) and reasoning depth (inference hops between triples). Outputs are structured across three pipeline stages: graph-quality statistics (node/edge counts, average degree, clustering coefficient), retrieval metrics (evidence recall and context relevance), and generation metrics (accuracy, ROUGE-L, faithfulness, evidence coverage), enabling stage-level diagnosis rather than end-to-end scoring alone.

## Method

GraphRAG-Bench constructs its evaluation through a six-stage pipeline: (1) corpus collection balancing structured and unstructured knowledge, (2) logic mining via GPT-4.1 to extract formalized ontologies encoding vertical hierarchies and horizontal dependencies, (3) evidence collection that isolates self-contained subgraphs for retrieval tasks and multi-hop relational chains for reasoning tasks, (4) question generation calibrated by the structural properties of the underlying evidence rather than simple hop count, (5) verification that every evidence triple is logically derivable from the corpus, and (6) question refinement to embed sufficient background context. The benchmark then systematically evaluates seven GraphRAG frameworks (MS-GraphRAG, HippoRAG/HippoRAG2, LightRAG, Fast-GraphRAG, RAPTOR, Lazy-GraphRAG, plus KGP, StructRAG, KET-RAG) against basic RAG with and without reranking, using GPT-4o-mini and Qwen2.5-14B as generation backends, revealing that GraphRAG excels in complex reasoning and creative generation but matches or lags basic RAG on simple fact retrieval, with HippoRAG2's denser graph structure (avg. degree 8.75–13.31) correlating with superior retrieval recall.

## Results

On GraphRAG-Bench (evaluated with GPT-4o-mini), HippoRAG2 ranks first among all GraphRAG systems with an average generation score of 56.48, achieving 60.14% ACC on Fact Retrieval and 53.38% ACC on Complex Reasoning (Novel dataset). Basic RAG with reranking matches or exceeds all GraphRAG models on simple Fact Retrieval (60.92% ACC on Novel, 64.73% on Medical), while GraphRAG models pull ahead on Complex Reasoning (HippoRAG2: 53.38% vs. RAG: 42.93%) and Contextual Summarize (MS-GraphRAG: 64.40% vs. RAG: 51.30%). On retrieval, RAG achieves 83.21% Evidence Recall for Fact Retrieval, whereas HippoRAG reaches 87.91–90.95% Recall for Complex Reasoning and Contextual Summarize. Token cost varies dramatically: MS-GraphRAG(global) requires ~331K tokens per query versus ~879 for vanilla RAG, while HippoRAG2 stays compact at ~1K tokens.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 10 GraphRAG systems (MS-GraphRAG local/global, HippoRAG, HippoRAG2, LightRAG, Fast-GraphRAG, RAPTOR, Lazy-GraphRAG, KGP, StructRAG, KET-RAG) against two RAG baselines (with and without reranking). On simple Fact Retrieval, RAG with reranking (60.92% Novel, 64.73% Medical) matches or beats every GraphRAG variant except HippoRAG2 on Medical (66.28%), confirming that graph traversal adds noise for single-hop queries. On Complex Reasoning, HippoRAG2 (53.38% Novel, 61.98% Medical) and LightRAG (49.07%, 61.32%) clearly surpass RAG (42.93%, 58.64%), with gains of ~10 and ~3 percentage points respectively. For Contextual Summarize, MS-GraphRAG local (64.40% Novel) and Fast-GraphRAG (67.88% Medical) lead, outperforming RAG by 13 and 2 points. On Creative Generation, the picture is mixed: RAPTOR achieves the highest faithfulness (70.85% Novel) but RAG covers more evidence (40.04% vs. 30.95%), while on Medical, HippoRAG (69.21% faithfulness) and LightRAG (78.76%) dominate. In terms of efficiency, HippoRAG2 (~1K tokens) and RAPTOR (~3.4K) are far more token-efficient than MS-GraphRAG global (~331K) or LightRAG (~100K), making them more practical for deployment.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a critical response to prior analysis papers (Han et al. 2025; Zhou et al. 2025) that reported GraphRAG underperforming vanilla RAG, arguing that existing benchmarks (HotpotQA, MultiHopRAG, UltraDomain) are structurally inadequate for evaluating graph-based retrieval. Rather than proposing a new GraphRAG architecture, it carves new ground in evaluation methodology by introducing a multi-stage pipeline assessment (graph construction → retrieval → generation) and a complexity-graded task hierarchy (fact retrieval → complex reasoning → contextual summarize → creative generation) that prior benchmarks lacked. The two-corpus design (structured NCCN medical guidelines + unstructured pre-20th-century novels) directly addresses the information-density gap identified in existing datasets, where average node degrees and component sizes were too low to meaningfully test graph traversal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This benchmark provides the first systematic, stage-specific evaluation framework for GraphRAG, offering practitioners concrete guidelines on when graph structures provide measurable benefits (complex multi-hop reasoning, contextual synthesis) versus when they introduce harmful redundancy (simple fact retrieval). The efficiency analysis revealing up to 377× token overhead (MS-GraphRAG global: 331K tokens vs. vanilla RAG: 879 tokens) is practically critical for deployment decisions. Open questions remain around how to actively manage context growth in GraphRAG pipelines, whether graph quality (clustering coefficient, community density) can be optimized independently of graph size, and whether the findings generalize beyond the two tested domains (medicine, literature) to other structured fields like law and finance.
