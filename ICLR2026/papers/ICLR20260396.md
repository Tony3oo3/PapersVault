## Problem

Concept Embedding Models (CEMs) provide interpretable concept-based explanations but treat all concepts as independent, failing to capture the hierarchical relationships that structure human reasoning (e.g., 'contains onions' only exists when 'contains vegetables' is present). Additionally, CEMs require expensive concept annotations at multiple granularities during training, limiting their practical applicability. This paper addresses both gaps by automatically discovering sub-concepts from a pretrained CEM's embedding space and modelling their hierarchical relationships in a new architecture.

## Input / Output

The method takes RGB images (processed through a frozen CLIP ViT-L/14 backbone, 512×512 for PseudoKitchens) and only coarse top-level concept labels during training (e.g., 'contains vegetables' rather than individual ingredients). It produces a task class prediction, top-level concept activation probabilities, and sub-concept activation probabilities organised in a two-level hierarchy. The sub-concept labels are generated automatically by Concept Splitting and require no manual annotation, reducing the annotation burden to a single level of coarse concepts that can be obtained cheaply via LLMs or class grouping.

## Method

The approach has two components. First, Concept Splitting extracts concept embeddings from a trained CEM, partitions them into positive and negative sets based on the CEM's own predictions, and trains BatchTopK sparse autoencoders (SAEs) on each partition to discover sub-concepts; SAE feature activations above a threshold define binary sub-concept labels. Second, the HiCEM architecture explicitly models the hierarchy: each top-level concept's positive (or negative) embedding is passed through a sub-concepts module that generates sub-concept embeddings via fully connected layers, scores them with a shared scoring function, and reconstructs the parent embedding as a probability-weighted mixture of sub-concept embeddings. The parent concept probability is estimated via a differentiable soft-max (α=200, β=100) over sub-concept probabilities, and the final concept embedding is a mixture of positive and negative embeddings weighted by the parent probability. Training jointly minimises task and concept cross-entropy losses with RandInt random intervention regularisation (p_int=0.25), and the architecture supports interventions at both the top-level and sub-concept granularity.

## Results

HiCEM + Concept Splitting achieves task accuracy within 2% of CEMs across all six datasets (e.g., 0.92 vs 0.92 on MNIST-ADD, 0.74 vs 0.76 on CUB, 0.78 vs 0.79 on ImageNet), while discovering sub-concepts with mean ROC-AUC of 0.85–0.93 (Table 1). On provided (top-level) concept accuracy, HiCEM matches CEMs exactly (0.99–1.00 ROC-AUC, Table 4). A user study on ImageNet confirms discovered sub-concepts are semantically coherent: 67.9% of participants judged sub-concept names as examples of their parent concept, versus 4.0% for random-word controls (p<0.01).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CEMs (the closest baseline), HiCEM + Concept Splitting matches task accuracy within 2% on every dataset and achieves identical provided-concept ROC-AUC (0.99–1.00), while additionally discovering sub-concepts with 0.85–0.93 ROC-AUC that CEMs cannot represent hierarchically. Against CBMs, HiCEM substantially outperforms on most datasets (e.g., 0.92 vs 0.23 on MNIST-ADD, 0.74 vs 0.65 on CUB), confirming that the embedding-based architecture avoids the accuracy-explainability trade-off. Against LF-CBMs, HiCEM achieves higher discovered-concept accuracy (0.93 vs 0.75–0.78 ROC-AUC) and higher task accuracy on SHAPES (0.87 vs 0.59). Against PCBMs and PCBM-h, HiCEM dominates on all datasets where both are evaluated (e.g., 0.92 vs 0.16/0.53 on MNIST-ADD, 0.74 vs 0.65/0.73 on CUB). The key differentiator is that HiCEM supports multi-level concept interventions: sub-concept interventions in HiCEMs yield greater task-accuracy gains than equivalent interventions in CEMs trained with the same discovered concepts (Figure 4), particularly on CUB and PseudoKitchens.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Concept Embedding Models (CEMs), which represent each concept as a mixture of positive and negative embeddings, by introducing a hierarchical architecture (HiCEM) where parent concept embeddings are constructed as weighted mixtures of sub-concept embeddings. The Concept Splitting method repurposes BatchTopK sparse autoencoders—previously used for monosemanticity in language models—to decompose a pretrained CEM's embedding space into interpretable sub-concepts, a use case not explored in prior SAE or concept-discovery work. Unlike prior hierarchical concept models (e.g., Panousis et al., 2024) that rely on spatial grounding or vision-language models, this approach discovers hierarchies purely by decomposing learned concept embeddings, and unlike post-hoc methods (PCBM, LF-CBM), it produces inherently interpretable models that support interventions at multiple granularity levels.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical contribution is reducing annotation burden: only coarse top-level concept labels are needed during training, while finer-grained sub-concepts are discovered automatically, making concept-based interpretability more accessible in real-world settings where exhaustive annotation is infeasible. The PseudoKitchens dataset provides a controlled benchmark with pixel-perfect spatial annotations that is useful for the broader concept-based interpretability community. Open questions include whether the approach scales to deeper hierarchies (beyond two levels), whether SAEs reliably discover meaningful sub-concepts in all embedding spaces, and whether offering multi-granularity interventions actually reduces cognitive load for human users interacting with the model.
