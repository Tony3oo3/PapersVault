## Problem

Cross-domain reinforcement learning faces two intertwined challenges: source and target domains often have distinct state and action spaces (e.g., different robot morphologies), making direct transfer infeasible, and the transferability of a pre-trained source model is unknown a priori, so naive transfer can cause negative effects that make performance worse than learning from scratch. Existing CDRL methods either assume identical state-action spaces or rely on unsupervised mapping objectives (e.g., dynamics cycle consistency) that provide no performance guarantees when the source model is poorly aligned with the target.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a frozen source-domain Q function (pre-trained with SAC) and on-policy target-domain state-action-reward-next-state tuples collected under the current target policy. It outputs a target-domain policy, learned inter-domain state mapping φ: S_tar → S_src and action mapping ψ: A_tar → A_src (constrained to feasible regions via normalizing flows in the practical version), and an adaptive scalar weight α(t) ∈ [0,1] that modulates how much the source Q function contributes to each policy update. In image-based experiments (DMC), observations are stacks of three 84×84 RGB frames with action repeat 4.

## Method

QAvatar introduces cross-domain Bellman consistency as a principled measure of source-model transferability and builds a hybrid critic that combines the target-domain Q function Q_tar with the mapped source Q function Q_src(φ(s), ψ(a)) via an adaptive, hyperparameter-free weight α(t) = ‖ε_td‖ / (‖ε_cd‖ + ‖ε_td‖), where ε_td and ε_cd are the target-domain TD error and cross-domain Bellman error, respectively. The inter-domain mappings φ and ψ are learned by minimizing a cross-domain Bellman loss that aligns Q_src with target-domain transitions, rather than relying on unsupervised dynamics or cycle-consistency objectives. The policy is updated via an NPG-style (or SAC in practice) exponential tilt using the weighted hybrid Q function. Proposition 3 establishes that the average sub-optimality bound is governed by the per-iteration minimum of the two error terms, guaranteeing that QAvatar never performs worse than vanilla target-domain learning: when the source is well-transferable, α(t)→1 and the source Q dominates; when it is not, α(t)→0 and the method gracefully falls back to standard TD-based policy evaluation. The practical deep RL version integrates SAC for policy and critic updates and employs normalizing flows to project mapping outputs into bounded action/state regions, avoiding the instability of adversarial training.

## Results

QAvatar achieves improved data efficiency across all five benchmark tasks, requiring only 44%–73% of the environment steps needed by SAC (from scratch) to reach a performance threshold. The largest speedup is on the Navigation task (218K vs. 490K steps, a 0.44 ratio) and Door Opening (48K vs. 98K, 0.49 ratio). In aggregated IQM evaluation (Figure 2), QAvatar significantly outperforms all CDRL baselines (CAT, CMD, PAR, FT) across locomotion, robot arm manipulation, and navigation tasks. The method also demonstrates robustness: it avoids negative transfer when the source model is low-quality or the domains are structurally unrelated, by adaptively reducing the source-critic weight α(t) toward zero.

## Baselines & Comparisons

QAvatar outperforms all five baselines in data efficiency and final performance. Compared to SAC (from scratch), QAvatar reduces time-to-threshold by 27%–56% across all tasks, with the largest gains on Navigation (56% fewer steps) and Door Opening (51% fewer steps). Against CAT (You et al., 2022), QAvatar achieves faster convergence on Robosuite manipulation tasks where CAT's parameter-based transfer (weighted combinations of source/target policy layers) fails due to dissimilar state-action representations; CAT performs moderately on MuJoCo but transfers slowly elsewhere. CMD (Gui et al., 2023) generally performs poorly and is unstable (e.g., on Ant), which the authors attribute to its unsupervised adversarial mapping that ignores target-domain rewards. PAR (Lyu et al., 2024) is outperformed by QAvatar in aggregated IQM across all task categories. FT (Ha et al., 2024) improves over SAC on MuJoCo but learns slowly in Robosuite due to representation mismatch between different robot arms, whereas QAvatar's learned inter-domain mapping via cross-domain Bellman loss handles this discrepancy effectively.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Natural Policy Gradient framework (Kakade, 2001) for its policy update mechanism and integrates with Soft Actor-Critic (Haarnoja et al., 2018) for practical deep RL implementation, while borrowing normalizing-flow-based action constraint handling from FlowPG (Brahmanage et al., 2023). It departs from prior cross-domain RL methods (CMD, CAT, PAR) that learn inter-domain mappings via adversarial training or cycle consistency without performance guarantees, by introducing cross-domain Bellman consistency as a principled transferability measure and a hybrid critic with adaptive, hyperparameter-free weighting. The key conceptual departure is that prior CDRL methods are 'oblivious to the issues of model transferability between domains,' whereas QAvatar explicitly quantifies and adapts to source-model quality at each iteration.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

QAvatar is the first CDRL method to provide sub-optimality guarantees for transfer across distinct state-action spaces, addressing a gap where prior methods could silently degrade below from-scratch baselines. The hyperparameter-free adaptive weighting (α(t)) offers a practical mechanism that automatically detects and mitigates negative transfer, which is valuable for real-world deployment where source-model quality is unknown a priori. Limitations include the assumption that target-domain data collection is costlier than training compute (QAvatar takes ~2× SAC training time due to mapping and flow-model overhead), and the tabular analysis does not directly transfer to the deep RL setting. The Bellman consistency and hybrid critic ideas are noted as potentially extensible to preference-based RL and imitation learning, but the paper does not explore these directions.
