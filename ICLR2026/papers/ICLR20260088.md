## Problem

Current AI safety frameworks treat harmfulness as binary, over-sampling unambiguous extremes and obscuring the ambiguous middle ground where systems are most likely to fail. Annotator disagreement on borderline cases is typically treated as statistical noise to be averaged out, rather than as a signal of diverse, legitimate value perspectives. This monolithic approach creates a barrier to developing pluralistic AI that can navigate context-dependent, value-laden safety considerations.

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes 150 text prompts (curated to span a continuous 0.0–1.0 harm spectrum) as input, each rated by 100 human annotators on a 0–100 continuous scale, yielding 15,000 ratings that capture the full distribution of human judgments per prompt rather than a single consensus label. Each prompt is enriched with 64 interpretable features: 16 harmful action types, 7 harmful effect types (from SafetyAnalyst harm trees), 39 value/right/duty categories (from KALEIDO), and a synthetic harm level. Annotator-level data includes 10 demographic variables and 21 psychological measures (reduced to 3 latent factors via factor analysis). For model evaluation, prompt-rating pairs serve as alignment examples, and the target output is a per-annotator harmfulness score (0–100), enabling both personalized and aggregated prediction.

## Method

The framework operates in four stages: (1) DeepSeek-V3 generates 11 prompt variants per seed (from 5,692 AIR-Bench 2024 seeds) spanning a fine-grained 0.0–1.0 harm spectrum, producing 62,612 candidates; (2) SafetyAnalyst and KALEIDO extract interpretable harm and value features, which are thematically clustered via BERTopic into 39 value categories; (3) a genetic algorithm (10,000 iterations, inverse Jensen-Shannon distance fitness) selects 150 prompts that over-sample intermediate harm levels (0.4–0.8) while maintaining uniform diversity across actions, effects, and values. For analysis, the paper fits lasso-regularized mixed-effects regression models with random intercepts to disentangle the contributions of prompt features, annotator traits, and their interactions to harm judgments. For model evaluation, safety models (WildGuard, SafetyAnalyst) and general LLMs (GPT-4.1/5, Claude, Qwen3) are benchmarked under personalized vs. aggregated alignment using k-shot in-context steering and value-profile steering, with MAE as the primary metric on a 50-prompt held-out test set.

## Results

On PLURIHARMS, the best personalized prediction is achieved by GPT-5 k-shot steering with a MAE of 0.195 (95% CI ±0.010), closely followed by GPT-4.1 k-shot at 0.196 and Qwen-8B k-shot at 0.197. In contrast, specialized safety models underperform: WildGuard-7B achieves 0.364 MAE (probability) and 0.403 (binary classification) in zero-shot, while SafetyAnalyst-8B reaches 0.311 (individual) and 0.361 (aggregated). Personalized alignment consistently reduces MAE by 0.04–0.07 over aggregated alignment across all model families, and treating safety as a continuous probability rather than a binary label improves WildGuard performance by ~0.04 MAE.

## Baselines & Comparisons

General-purpose LLMs with k-shot in-context steering substantially outperform both specialized safety models on PLURIHARMS: GPT-4.1/5 k-shot achieves 0.195–0.196 MAE (individual), compared to SafetyAnalyst-8B at 0.311 and WildGuard-7B at 0.364–0.403, a gap of roughly 0.11–0.21 MAE. Among general models, GPT-4.1 and GPT-5 k-shot are the strongest, with Claude Sonnet-3.7/Opus-4 and Qwen-8B following within 0.005 MAE. Personalized (individual) alignment consistently beats aggregated alignment by 0.04–0.07 MAE across every model and method tested, confirming that collapsing annotator disagreement into a consensus degrades predictive accuracy. Within GPT-4.1, k-shot steering (0.196) outperforms value-profile steering (0.233) by 0.037 MAE, suggesting that natural-language value summaries do not fully capture the latent factors driving individual harm judgments. WildGuard's probabilistic output (0.364) improves over its binary classification (0.403) by 0.039 MAE, supporting the paper's argument that harm should be modeled as a continuous spectrum rather than a binary label.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on SafetyAnalyst (Li et al., 2025) for interpretable harm-tree feature extraction and KALEIDO (Sorensen et al., 2024a) for value/rights/duties annotation, combining them into a unified prompt-feature space. It departs from prior safety benchmarks (HarmBench, WildGuard, AIR-Bench) that treat harmfulness as binary, and from pluralistic datasets (DICES, PRISM) that collect diverse annotator ratings without systematically targeting disagreement-prone cases. The key conceptual departure is treating annotator disagreement as a structured signal of value diversity rather than statistical noise to be averaged out, and using a genetic algorithm to curate prompts specifically along the disagreement axis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PLURIHARMS provides the first benchmark that explicitly operationalizes the full spectrum of human harm judgments (benign-to-harmful × agreement-to-disagreement) with interpretable prompt features and rich annotator traits, giving the pluralistic alignment community a principled evaluation target. The finding that general LLMs outperform specialized safety models on personalized harm prediction, and that k-shot steering beats value-profile summaries, suggests current safety architectures are not yet capturing the structured individual differences that drive disagreement. Key limitations include the US-only, English-language annotator pool, prompt-level (not response-level) evaluation, and modest scale (150 prompts, 100 annotators), leaving open whether the observed trait-prompt interaction patterns generalize across cultures and to realistic multi-turn interactions.
