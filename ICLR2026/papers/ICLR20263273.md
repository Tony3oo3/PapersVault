## Problem

Existing LLM evaluation suffers from a double bind: automated benchmarks (MMLU, HELM, BIG-Bench) measure technical capability but miss subjective interaction quality, while human preference platforms like Chatbot Arena rely on unrepresentative self-selected users, shallow single-turn interactions, and binary votes that collapse multidimensional quality into one number. This leaves the field without reliable, demographically representative data to guide model development and selection for diverse populations.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes multi-turn conversations (minimum 3 turns, median 6) in which each participant sends identical messages simultaneously to two anonymized models, ensuring controlled comparison on the same conversational context. Each comparison yields five three-way judgments (Model A / Tie / Model B) across four derived dimensions plus an Overall Winner, tagged with the rater's demographic profile across age, ethnicity, and political affiliation. The hierarchical BTD model outputs full posterior distributions over per-model skill parameters, per-demographic-group adjustments, and per-metric tie propensities, which are then post-stratified to US/UK census weights to produce population-adjusted leaderboards, demographic-specific rankings, and P(best) probabilities.

## Method

The core statistical engine is a hierarchical Bayesian Bradley-Terry-Davidson model that extends the classic paired-comparison framework to handle ties (via a per-metric tie-propensity parameter ν) and to capture demographic heterogeneity through additive group adjustments (u) that are partially pooled with learned heterogeneity scales (τ). A key design choice is the disentangling of mixed demographic effects: since each participant simultaneously belongs to an age, ethnicity, and political group, the model learns a single global skill per model-metric plus centred, axis-specific adjustments, attributing consistent preference patterns to the correct demographic driver while leveraging the full dataset for robust global estimates. Model pairings during data collection are selected by a TrueSkill-based adaptive algorithm that maximises information gain by targeting the most uncertain matchups, and each of the 22 demographic strata runs as a dedicated tournament. The four evaluation dimensions (Core Task Performance, Communication Style, Interaction Fluidity, Trust/Ethics/Safety) were derived via exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis on a 514-participant pilot with 30 initial metrics, yielding four factors with Cronbach's α > 0.89. A post-hoc LLM judge (gpt-4.1) annotates all transcripts for task type, domain, complexity, and engagement, serving purely as an explanatory layer with no influence on the primary preference scores.

## Results

On the HUMAINE benchmark (23,404 participants, 28 models, 5 dimensions), google/gemini-2.5-pro ranks first overall with a 95.6% posterior probability of being the top model (Score/Winshare out of 27 max). Age is the dominant axis of demographic preference heterogeneity, shifting model ranks by ±2.8 positions across age cohorts (vs. ±1.3 for ethnicity and ±1.5 for politics). Discriminative power varies dramatically across dimensions: Trust, Ethics & Safety yields a 65% tie rate, while Overall Winner is decisive at only 10% ties. Notably, gemini-2.5-pro ranks 13th on the technical HELM benchmark but 1st on HUMAINE, illustrating the gap between technical accuracy and human preference.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a benchmark paper rather than a method comparison, so it does not pit its evaluation framework head-to-head against Chatbot Arena or other preference platforms under a shared protocol. Instead, it positions HUMAINE against three existing paradigms: (1) automated benchmarks (MMLU, HELM, BIG-Bench), which it argues miss subjective interaction quality; (2) crowdsourced preference platforms like Chatbot Arena, which it criticises for unrepresentative sampling, shallow interaction, and single-metric reductionism; and (3) LLM-as-a-judge approaches, which it uses only for post-hoc explanatory analysis rather than as a ranking proxy. The most striking cross-paradigm contrast is that google/gemini-2.5-pro ranks 13th on HELM (a technical benchmark) yet 1st on HUMAINE with 95.6% P(best), quantifying the 'evaluation gap' the paper targets. Within HUMAINE itself, the key finding is that no single model dominates all dimensions: deepseek-chat-v3-0324 leads Communication Style but drops to 5th on Core Task Performance, while grok-3 is 2nd on reasoning but 8th on both communication and fluidity, demonstrating that the 'best model' is context-dependent.

## Relation to Prior Work

HUMAINE extends the Bradley-Terry-Davidson paired-comparison framework (itself rooted in Thurstone's Law of Comparative Judgment) with a hierarchical Bayesian structure that models demographic heterogeneity through partial pooling, and applies TrueSkill-based adaptive sampling for efficient data collection. It positions itself as a direct methodological correction to Chatbot Arena and similar crowdsourced preference platforms, addressing three specific validity threats—unrepresentative sampling, superficial interaction depth, and single-metric reductionism—while complementing (not replacing) automated benchmarks like MMLU and HELM. The multi-dimensional evaluation structure is derived from psychometric factor analysis of a pilot study, grounding the dimensions in HCI literature (Technology Acceptance Model, Amershi et al. guidelines) rather than ad-hoc selection.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that Trust, Ethics & Safety yields a 65% tie rate in open-ended pairwise comparison—versus 10% for Overall Winner—challenges the assumption that a single conversational format can reliably assess all model qualities, pointing toward the need for targeted, scenario-specific evaluation suites for nuanced attributes. The demographic heterogeneity results (±2.8 rank shifts across age cohorts) provide concrete evidence that aggregate leaderboards systematically mask population-specific performance gaps, with implications for equitable model deployment. Key limitations include geographic restriction to US/UK, text-only interaction, short multi-turn conversations, and only three demographic axes; open questions include whether the framework extends to multimodal evaluation, non-Western populations, and whether explicit interaction terms (which explain up to 50% of variance in some demographic pairs) should be incorporated into the model.
