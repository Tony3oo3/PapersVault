## Problem

Traditional recommender systems and LLM-based DPO methods rely on Revealed Preference (RP), assuming observed user choices faithfully reflect underlying interests. In practice, roughly 30% of ground-truth items in Amazon Review data cannot be logically explained by prior behavior, causing LLM recommenders to overfit to noisy signals and generate rationales of limited persuasiveness. The paper asks how to complement RP with a reasoning-based preference signal that captures what a user *should* prefer given coherent history.

## Input / Output

The model takes a user's sequential interaction history (item titles and descriptions) plus a candidate item list as a text prompt, and generates a recommended item title enclosed in <item> tags along with a natural-language rationale in <think> tags. Training data is structured as ordered triplets (chosen ≻ hard-rejected ≻ easy-rejected), where each element carries an LLM-generated rationale and a 1–7 coherence score; the two rejected items are distinguished by their coherence scores relative to user history. The backbone is Gemma-3-4B-it fine-tuned with LoRA (r=8), and inference uses a 30-item candidate set with temperature 0.7 sampling.

## Method

C-APO extends DPO from pairwise to triplet preference via a Plackett-Luce factorization that jointly enforces the RP ordering (chosen above both rejected items) and the CP ordering (hard-rejected above easy-rejected). The key innovation is a conflict-aware adaptive weight w_{i,j} for each item pair, computed by calibrating LLM coherence scores through a frozen SBERT text encoder and mapping the calibrated mean difference through a Gaussian CDF (Thurstone-Mosteller model), yielding a soft pairwise win probability in [0,1]. When RP and CP agree (chosen item also has highest coherence), w is large and the update is amplified; when they conflict (a rejected item is more coherent than the chosen one), w shrinks toward 0.5 or below, suppressing the noisy RP signal. Training proceeds in two stages: supervised fine-tuning on chosen items with rationales, followed by C-APO preference optimization over the full triplet, with the SBERT calibrator (109M params) providing an additional validation layer over the SOTA LLM's coherence judgments.

## Results

C-APO achieves the best HR@1 across all five Amazon Review domains, with relative gains over the second-best model of +12.34% (Fashion, 9.47 vs. 8.43 Rec-R1), +5.02% (Grocery, 6.90 vs. 6.57 S-DPO), +61.43% (Scientific, 12.22 vs. 7.57 S-DPO), +25.62% (Clothing, 7.11 vs. 4.16 S-DPO), and +24.48% (Health, 4.83 vs. 4.55 GRAM). On rationale quality, C-APO achieves 84.33% top-rated (score 3) rationales, outperforming the second-best Rec-SAVER (78.34%) by ~6 percentage points. In an online A/B test, C-APO delivers a 1.65× relative CTR improvement (15.03% vs. 9.34% ML baseline) with a statistically significant +1.47%p lift over the SFT control (p<0.001).

## Baselines & Comparisons

C-APO outperforms all 14 baselines across every domain and metric. Among CF-Rec models, the strongest is SASRec (e.g., Fashion HR@1=3.47), which C-APO surpasses by 5+ points in every domain. Among LLM-Rec baselines, S-DPO is the closest competitor in Grocery (6.57 vs. 6.90 HR@1) and Scientific (7.57 vs. 12.22), while Rec-R1 is closest in Fashion (8.43 vs. 9.47) and Clothing (5.62 vs. 7.11). GRAM is the strongest in Health (4.55 vs. 4.83 HR@1) but trails significantly in other domains. Rec-SAVER, SumRecDPO, Intuitor, SLMRec, LLMEmb, RecLM, NextItNet, GRU4Rec, TransRec, and STAMP are all consistently outperformed by C-APO by 2–9 HR@1 points depending on the domain. The paper further confirms that the gains are method-driven rather than backbone-driven: swapping Gemma-3-4B-it for Qwen-3-4B-Instruct still yields 15.38% higher HR@5 for C-APO over S-DPO.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) from pairwise chosen-vs-rejected comparisons to a triplet structure (chosen ≻ hard-rejected ≻ easy-rejected) via the Plackett-Luce model, and introduces a conflict-aware adaptive weight calibrated through a lightweight SBERT encoder grounded in the Thurstone-Mosteller random-utility framework. Conceptually, it departs from the standard LLM-Rec paradigm (Rec-SAVER, S-DPO, Rec-R1) that treats observed user interactions as ground-truth preference signals by introducing Coherent Preference (CP) as a behavioral-economics-inspired complement to Revealed Preference (RP), drawing on the critique that real-world choices are noisy and context-dependent. The method thus sits at the intersection of preference optimization for LLMs, sequential recommendation, and explainable AI, but its core novelty is the probabilistic reconciliation of two potentially conflicting preference orderings rather than a new architecture or training paradigm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical and underexplored limitation of LLM-based recommenders: that ~30% of observed user choices are logically incoherent with prior behavior, meaning RP-only training can overfit to noise and produce unpersuasive rationales. The 1.65× CTR lift in a production A/B test and the release of a costly LLM-annotated triplet rationale dataset make this work practically relevant for industry deployment. However, open questions remain: the triplet construction relies on expensive SOTA-LLM coherence scoring that limits retraining frequency (the authors acknowledge this and mention a distilled evaluator as future work), experiments are confined to text-only Amazon domains, and it is unclear whether CP truly captures 'genuine' user intent or merely smooths the preference signal toward the majority pattern, potentially reducing serendipity in long-tail scenarios despite the brief Appendix G analysis.
