## Problem

Prior work established that LMs retrieve bound entities via a positional mechanism, but this was validated only in narrow settings (2–3 entity groups) or achieved low causal faithfulness in longer contexts. This paper asks what happens as the number of bound entities grows: the positional mechanism becomes diffuse and unreliable in middle positions, and the paper identifies the two additional mechanisms—lexical and reflexive—that LMs deploy to compensate.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260947/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on templatic binding tasks where n entity groups (each containing m entities from disjoint sets) are presented in context followed by a query question. The causal model takes three indices—positional (i_P), lexical (i_L), and reflexive (i_R)—and produces a logit distribution over all n entity groups, where the positional term is a Gaussian with position-dependent width and the lexical/reflexive terms are one-hot peaks with learned weights. Training data consists of 8,000 mean probability distributions collected from 150 interchange interventions per index combination on paired original/counterfactual inputs designed so each mechanism predicts a distinct entity.

## Method

The paper identifies three retrieval mechanisms through counterfactual pairs where each mechanism predicts a different entity under interchange intervention on the last-token residual stream: the positional mechanism retrieves the entity at the counterfactual query's position, the lexical mechanism retrieves the entity bound to the counterfactual query in the original, and the reflexive mechanism follows a direct self-referential pointer to the counterfactual answer. They formalize these as a causal model M computing logits as a weighted sum of a Gaussian centered at i_P (with standard deviation as a quadratic function of i_P/n, making the signal wider in middle positions) plus one-hot terms at i_L and i_R with position-conditioned learned weights. The model achieves 95% Jensen-Shannon similarity with actual LM next-token distributions across 9 models (2–72B parameters) and 10 binding tasks, versus 44% for the positional-only prevailing view. They further validate the reflexive mechanism by showing that when the counterfactual answer is absent from the original context, the pointer cannot be dereferenced, confirming it is a true pointer rather than a copied entity representation.

## Results

The full causal model M (combining positional Gaussian, lexical one-hot, and reflexive one-hot mechanisms) achieves 95% Jensen-Shannon Similarity (JSS) with the LM's next token distribution on average across t_entity ∈ {1,2,3}, reaching 96% for t_entity=2. This is only slightly below the oracle variant (96–98% JSS) that uses the model's actual positional logit distributions. In stark contrast, the prevailing view (positional one-hot only) achieves just 42–46% JSS, and a uniform distribution baseline scores 44–57%, confirming that the three-mechanism mixture is essential for faithful prediction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is against the prevailing view that LMs retrieve bound entities solely via a positional mechanism (one-hot at the positional index), which achieves only 42–46% JSS—barely above the uniform baseline (44–57%). The full three-mechanism model M outperforms this by roughly 50 percentage points, demonstrating that positional retrieval alone is insufficient for middle entity groups. Ablations reveal that each mechanism contributes differently depending on target position: removing the lexical mechanism has minimal impact when t_entity=1 (0.94 vs 0.95) but causes a large drop when t_entity=3 (0.75 vs 0.94), while removing the reflexive mechanism has the opposite pattern (0.69 vs 0.95 at t_entity=1, but only 0.92 vs 0.94 at t_entity=3). Removing the positional Gaussian entirely drops JSS to 67–68% across all positions, and removing both positional and reflexive mechanisms collapses performance to 12–48%, confirming that all three mechanisms are necessary and that their interplay is position-dependent. These results are replicated across 9 models (2–72B parameters) from three families (Gemma, Qwen, Llama) and 10 binding tasks, with consistent trends in Tables 4–6.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mechanistic interpretability line of work on entity binding in LMs (Prakash et al., 2024; 2025; Feng & Steinhardt, 2024; Dai et al., 2024), which had established a positional mechanism as the primary retrieval strategy. It departs from that single-mechanism picture by showing the positional signal becomes diffuse and unreliable for middle entity groups, and by proposing two additional mechanisms—lexical and reflexive—that the model invokes to compensate. Methodologically, the paper builds on the causal abstraction framework of Geiger et al. (2021) and the model-combining approach of Pislar et al. (2025) to formalize the mixture as a single causal model, and uses interchange interventions (Vig et al., 2020) as the primary experimental tool.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a mechanistic account of the 'lost-in-the-middle' effect: the weakening of the positional signal in middle positions, compounded by a declining lexical mechanism under padding, offers a concrete explanation for why LMs struggle with mid-context retrieval. The causal model achieving 95% agreement across nine models (2–72B) and ten tasks establishes a general, quantitative description of entity retrieval that can guide future interpretability work and potentially inform architectural or training interventions. Open questions include whether the three-mechanism picture holds for more complex, multi-hop reasoning tasks, how the mechanisms interact in non-templatic natural language at scale, and whether the reflexive pointer mechanism has analogues in other retrieval settings (e.g., factual recall).
