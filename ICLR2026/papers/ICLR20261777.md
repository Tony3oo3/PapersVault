## Problem

While scaling laws for pretraining losses and discriminative benchmarks are well established, generative evaluations (e.g., mathematical problem-solving, software engineering) remain undercharacterized. Generative evaluations introduce new considerations—open-ended sampling, multiple attempts per problem, and the pass-at-k metric—that fundamentally alter how performance scales with resources, yet no systematic framework exists for fitting and predicting these scaling behaviors or for understanding how the number of attempts k modulates them.

## Input / Output

Each of the three scaling laws takes a different covariate (pretraining compute C ≈ 6ND, the pair (N, D), or the average gold reference log-likelihood) plus the number of attempts k, and produces a predicted −log(pass_B@k) along with fitted parameters (irreducible error, prefactor, exponent). Unusually, k is not merely a reporting choice but a hyperparameter that reshapes the scaling law parameters themselves: increasing k drives the irreducible error toward zero and steepens the power-law exponent. The gold reference likelihood is computed as the average per-token probability of benchmark-provided reference solutions, making it a cheap, model-agnostic covariate that requires no additional sampling.

## Method

The paper proposes three power-law scaling laws for −log(pass_B@k): one in pretraining compute, one in parameters and tokens, and one in gold reference log-likelihoods, all with parameters that are functions of k. Using the Pythia family (14M–12B parameters, up to 300B tokens) evaluated on GSM8K and MATH, the authors fit each law and perform backtesting—iteratively fitting on cheaper checkpoints to predict the most expensive model's performance. They show that the compute and parameters+tokens laws stabilize only within ~1.5–2.5 orders of magnitude of the target, while the gold reference likelihood law is uniquely stable across ~5 orders. The key theoretical result proves that the compute scaling law is the compute-optimal envelope of the parameters+tokens law (obtained by minimizing over N, D subject to ND = C/c), yielding a dimensionless misallocation penalty Φ(r) that quantifies the effective compute loss from suboptimal parameter-token allocation and connects to the overtraining literature.

## Results

All three scaling laws (compute, parameters+tokens, gold reference likelihood) achieve comparable predictive accuracy for the most expensive model (Pythia-12B, ~2.16×10²² FLOP), with relative errors plateauing at ~1×10⁻¹ for k∈{1, 10²} and below 1 for k=10⁴. The gold reference likelihood law is uniquely stable, with parameters converging across ~5 orders of magnitude of compute, versus only ~1.5–2.5 orders for the compute and parameters+tokens laws. For GSM8K, the compute exponent α(k) rises from ~0.121 (k=1) to ~0.375 (k=10⁴), while for MATH it rises to ~0.58, reflecting greater headroom on the harder benchmark. The theoretical analysis proves the compute law is the compute-optimal envelope of the parameters+tokens law, yielding a dimensionless misallocation penalty Φ(r)≥1 that quantifies efficiency loss from suboptimal N/D allocation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three scaling law formulations rather than external baselines. The compute-only law (Eqn. 3) and the parameters+tokens law (Eqn. 4) perform comparably in backtesting, with the parameters+tokens law showing slightly lower relative error for small k but no advantage at larger k; both require checkpoints within ~2 orders of magnitude of the target for reliable prediction. The gold reference likelihood law (Eqn. 6) matches the other two in overall predictive accuracy but is slightly better for small k and slightly worse for large k. Its decisive advantage is parameter stability: backtested estimates of ξ₀(k), K₀(k), and κ(k) converge using models up to ~5 orders of magnitude cheaper than the target, making it the only law suitable for long-range forecasting from very small models. The parameters+tokens law provides tighter in-range fits (less residual scatter) than the compute law but exhibits comparatively large relative error at the largest-compute checkpoint, suggesting weaker extrapolation to the frontier.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the well-established line of neural scaling laws (Kaplan et al. 2020; Hoffmann et al. 2022) from pretraining loss and discriminative benchmarks to generative evaluations scored by pass-at-k. It adapts the Hoffmann et al. (2022) N^{-β}+D^{-γ} form and the Schaeffer et al. (2024b) gold-reference-likelihood regression to the pass-at-k setting, and extends Gadre et al. (2024)'s overtraining/misallocation framework to generative metrics. The key conceptual departure is treating the number of attempts k as a first-class hyperparameter that modulates all scaling-law parameters, a degree of freedom absent from prior loss- or accuracy-based scaling laws.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework gives practitioners a concrete, backtested methodology for forecasting generative performance (e.g., math or code) from cheaper checkpoints, which is critical as frontier labs increasingly rely on pass-at-k for capability assessment. The finding that gold-reference log-likelihoods yield parameters stable across ~5 orders of magnitude—far exceeding the ~1.5–2.5 orders for compute or N+D laws—is practically valuable for long-range forecasting but remains unexplained theoretically and is validated only on Pythia across two benchmarks. Open questions include whether the gold-reference signal generalizes across diverse task distributions, how it interacts with sampling temperature and decoding strategy, and whether the compute-optimal envelope result holds for architectures or training recipes that deviate from the standard transformer-on-Pile setup.
