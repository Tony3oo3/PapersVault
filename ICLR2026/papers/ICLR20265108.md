## Problem

Existing offline preference-based RL algorithms either require full data coverage, rely on computationally intractable confidence-set construction for conservatism, or assume known transition dynamics and value-function realizability. No prior method is simultaneously provably sample-efficient, computationally tractable, and robust under partial data coverage without access to true dynamics.

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes a fixed offline dataset of N i.i.d. trajectory pairs (each of horizon H) annotated with binary preference labels under a Bradley-Terry-Luce link function, along with a reference policy distribution μ_ref and two regularization weights λ₁, λ₂. It outputs a single policy π^ALG (a uniform mixture over T mirror-descent iterates) and, as intermediate artifacts, conservative reward and transition models that remain close to their maximum-likelihood estimates. The key structural choice is that policy evaluation uses relative performance—J(π) minus the reference policy's expected return—rather than absolute value estimates, which eliminates the need for a separate value function.

## Method

MCP formulates a minimax objective: the policy maximizes relative performance under candidate reward/transition models, while those models minimize a regularized objective that penalizes deviation from their MLE estimates (terms E₁ for reward and E₂ for transition). This implicitly encodes conservatism without constructing explicit confidence sets, making the inner minimization a standard regularized optimization over the function class. The outer policy update uses a mirror-descent step (π_{t+1} ∝ π_t · exp(η · Q^{P^t}_{r_t})), bridging the policy space to the model classes without requiring a value function. The algorithm alternates T iterations between conservative model selection and policy improvement, then outputs the uniform mixture of all iterates. Under partial coverage (finite concentrability coefficients C_R and C_P), the regret bound decomposes into an optimization error O(√(log|A|/T)) plus statistical errors scaled by the concentrability coefficients, yielding O(ε⁻²) sample complexity—improving over the O(ε⁻⁴) of prior concurrent work.

## Results

On the Meta-World medium-replay benchmark with 8 tasks, MCP achieves the best average rank of 2.250 (out of 7 methods) across both 500 and 1000 preference samples, compared to APPO (2.813), MR (2.875), PT (3.812), IPL (3.938), and DPPO (5.312). With 500 preferences, MCP leads on 5 of 8 tasks (e.g., BPT: 56.00% vs. APPO 53.52%; drawer-open: 98.67% vs. APPO 87.68%; lever-pull: 80.27% vs. APPO 75.76%), while with 1000 preferences it leads on 6 of 8 tasks (e.g., BPT: 62.93% vs. APPO 59.04%; BPT-wall: 69.73% vs. APPO 62.96%). The Oracle (IQL with ground-truth rewards) remains the upper bound, e.g., 88.33% on BPT and 100% on drawer-open.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MCP outperforms the closest competitor APPO (Kang & Oh, 2025) on 10 of 16 task-sample combinations, with the largest margins on BPT-wall-500 (+0.21 pp), BPT-1000 (+3.89 pp), and drawer-open-500 (+10.99 pp); however, APPO wins on dial-turn (80.96% vs. 38.67% at 500 samples) and box-close (18.24% vs. 7.20% at 500 samples). Against MR (Kim et al., 2023), MCP wins on 11 of 16 combinations but trails on sweep (11.47% vs. 86.96% at 500 samples), where MR's reward-modeling approach excels. MCP dominates DPPO (An et al., 2023) and IPL (Hejna & Sadigh, 2023) across nearly all tasks, with DPPO consistently below 11% success rate. PT (Kim et al., 2023) is competitive on dial-turn (68.67% at 500 samples) but underperforms MCP on the majority of other tasks. Overall, MCP achieves the best average rank (2.250) across both sample sizes, confirming its robustness under partial data coverage without requiring known dynamics or extra value-function modeling.

## Relation to Prior Work

MCP sits at the intersection of offline PbRL and conservative model-based RL, directly addressing a gap left by prior work: Zhu et al. (2023) provided sample-complexity guarantees but only for linear reward models; Zhan et al. (2023a) and Pace et al. (2024) extended to general function approximation but required explicit confidence-set construction that is computationally intractable; Kang & Oh (2025, APPO) achieved tractability but at the cost of assuming known transition dynamics or value-function realizability. MCP's key departure is encoding conservatism implicitly through a relative-performance minimax objective over the reward and transition model classes, combined with mirror-descent policy updates, thereby avoiding both confidence-set optimization and auxiliary value-function modeling. The paper further refines its guarantees by exploiting structure in kernelized nonlinear regulators (Kakade et al., 2020) and factored MDPs (Kearns & Koller, 1999), tightening concentrability coefficients in each setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MCP is the first offline PbRL algorithm that is simultaneously provably sample-efficient (O(ε^{-2}) vs. O(ε^{-4}) for APPO) and computationally tractable under partial data coverage without known dynamics, resolving a long-standing tension between statistical guarantees and practical implementability in this area. The implicit-conservatism-via-relative-performance design is a clean conceptual contribution that could transfer to other offline RL settings where explicit pessimism is intractable. However, the empirical validation is limited to eight relatively simple Meta-World tasks with modest preference budgets (500–1000 samples), and the algorithm still requires realizability of both reward and transition models. Open questions include whether the approach extends to non-stationary or listwise preference models (e.g., Thurstone), scales to high-dimensional continuous control, and whether the concentrability coefficients remain well-behaved in practice for complex environments.
