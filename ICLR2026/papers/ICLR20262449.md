## Problem

In AI-assisted decision-making, human decision-makers with heterogeneous cognitive capacities (e.g., experts vs. general practitioners) exhibit different deviations between their confidence and the true label likelihood. The paper proves that existing AI confidence adjustment objectives—calibration and human-alignment—are theoretically insufficient to guarantee equal decision utility across such groups, creating utility disparities that risk reinforcing the Matthew Effect and eroding trust in AI systems.

## Input / Output

The AI confidence function f_A takes as input a sample feature vector x, the human decision-maker's confidence h, and a group membership label s (a sensitive attribute encoding cognitive capacity, e.g., education level), and outputs an adjusted confidence in [0,1]. The method operates on the joint space Z = {X, H, S}, partitioning samples into subsets Z_{h,s} for each (confidence, group) pair. A separate MLP-based decision policy π(h, a) is learned to map the pair of human and AI confidences to a binary final decision, and the calibration is evaluated against this learned policy rather than a fixed threshold.

## Method

The paper introduces inter-group-alignment (α_g) as a new confidence adjustment objective requiring that, for identical human confidence h and AI confidence a, the positive-label probability is statistically equal across decision-maker groups. It derives a tight upper bound on utility disparity as a function of both human-alignment (α_h) and inter-group-alignment (α_g), showing that fair utility requires both objectives simultaneously. To achieve them jointly, the paper proposes cognition-aware multicalibration (Method 4.3), which extends standard multicalibration by performing α-calibration within each (h, s) subset rather than each h-subset alone. Theorem 4.5 proves that α/2-cognition-aware multicalibration is a sufficient condition for both α-human-alignment and α-inter-group-alignment. Computationally, λ-discretization partitions [0,1] into bins of width λ, and Algorithm 1 iteratively adjusts confidence within each (h, s, bin) cell until no further updates are needed, followed by a group-level averaging step.

## Results

Across four real-world tasks (Art, Cities, Sarcasm, Census), Cognition-aware Multicalibration (Method 4.3) consistently achieves the best inter-group-alignment, reducing EIAE by 39–95% and MIAE by 68–92% compared to the No Adjust baseline (e.g., MIAE drops from 0.5702 to 0.0458 on Sarcasm, and from 0.4286 to 0.1360 on Art). Human-alignment (EAE/MAE) remains competitive, with only marginal degradation (≤0.01–0.02) relative to Cognition-unaware Multicalibration in some tasks. T-tests over 100 repeated experiments confirm that Cognition-aware Multicalibration significantly reduces utility disparity versus Cognition-unaware Multicalibration (p < 0.001 on Art, Cities, Census; p = 0.002 on Sarcasm), while overall decision utility is maintained at comparable levels.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against Cognition-unaware Multicalibration (Method 4.4, from Corvelo Benz & Rodriguez, NeurIPS 2023), which performs multicalibration over human-confidence bins without accounting for group-level cognitive disparities. On inter-group-alignment (EIAE/MIAE), the proposed Cognition-aware Multicalibration (Method 4.3) dominates across all four tasks: it reduces MIAE from 0.2291→0.1360 (Art), 0.2609→0.1132 (Cities), 0.5702→0.0458 (Sarcasm), and 0.5275→0.1062 (Census) relative to Method 4.4. On human-alignment (EAE/MAE), Method 4.4 is slightly better in most tasks (e.g., MAE 0.0179 vs. 0.0493 on Cities), but the gap is small and Method 4.3 remains well below the No Adjust baseline. Critically, on utility disparity (the fairness metric), Method 4.3 consistently achieves the lowest disparity across all tasks, with T-tests showing statistically significant improvement over Method 4.4 (p < 0.001 on three tasks, p = 0.002 on Sarcasm). Method 4.4 can even worsen fairness relative to No Adjust in some settings (e.g., Art task), whereas Method 4.3 never does. Overall decision utility is maintained at comparable levels between the two calibration methods, confirming that the fairness gains come without a utility trade-off.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the AI confidence calibration framework for AI-assisted decision-making established by Corvelo Benz & Rodriguez (2023), who introduced human-alignment as a superior objective over standard calibration. The key departure is identifying that neither calibration nor human-alignment guarantees fair utility across groups of decision-makers with heterogeneous cognitive capacities, and introducing inter-group-alignment as a complementary objective. The proposed cognition-aware multicalibration method adapts the multicalibration algorithm of Hebert-Johnson et al. (2018) by partitioning the sample space into group-specific subsets (Z_{h,s_i}) rather than confidence-level subsets alone, providing a unified mechanism to achieve both alignment objectives simultaneously.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work reframes fairness in AI-assisted decision-making from the perspective of decision-makers (e.g., doctors) rather than subjects (e.g., patients), addressing a previously unexplored dimension that could exacerbate the Matthew Effect in professional settings. The theoretical upper bound on utility disparity as a function of both α_h and α_g provides interpretable guidance for system designers. Limitations include the restriction to binary classification and two groups in main experiments, reliance on a single pre-collected dataset (Vodrahalli et al.), and the monotonicity assumption on decision policies. Open questions include generalization to multi-class tasks, dynamic human behavioral adaptation over long-term AI interaction, and validation through live user studies rather than retrospective analysis.
