## Problem

Prior real-to-sim pipelines for human-scene interaction either rely on data-driven priors without physics in the loop or produce noisy, non-watertight geometry (duplicate structures, missing regions, oversmoothed surfaces) that causes RL-driven humanoid controllers to fail during simulation. Even small reconstruction artifacts—bumpy ground planes, ghost surfaces, protruding geometry—can literally trip up a physical humanoid, making it impractical to generate simulation-ready assets from casual monocular video at scale.

## Input / Output

The method takes a casually-captured monocular RGB video (standardized to ~600 frames / 20 s) of a human interacting with a static scene and produces three outputs: (1) metric-scale 3D human motion as SMPL parameters in a shared world coordinate frame, (2) a compact set of ~50 convex planar cuboids (each with a default 0.05 m thickness) representing the scene geometry, and (3) a PPO-trained transformer policy that reproduces the observed motion in Isaac Gym while colliding with those primitives. The planar-primitive representation is the key structural choice: it replaces dense meshes or point clouds with lightweight, simulation-ready convex shapes that enable 43% faster RL throughput.

## Method

CRISP's core insight is to fit clean, convex planar primitives to a visual-SLAM point cloud rather than reconstructing dense meshes. The pipeline first recovers camera poses, intrinsics, and a scale-invariant point cloud via MegaSAM (with MoGe as the depth estimator) and lifts GVHMR SMPL meshes into world coordinates. Planar fitting proceeds in three stages: per-frame K-means clustering on finite-difference normals, spatial splitting via DBSCAN within each normal cluster, and cross-frame temporal merging using optical-flow warping and normal-cosine scoring, followed by RANSAC plane fitting and min-area-rectangle cuboid construction. Contact-guided scene completion uses InteractVLM to predict per-vertex contact masks, with temporal-kinematic filtering (retaining only consistently high-confidence, low-velocity windows) to suppress false positives and hallucinate occluded support surfaces such as chair seats or stair treads. Finally, a MaskedMimic-style PPO policy with a transformer encoder is trained in Isaac Gym (120 Hz sim, 30 Hz policy) to track the recovered motion against the planar scene, serving as both a physics-based validator and a motion refiner that reduces drift and jitter.

## Results

CRISP achieves a 93.1% RL motion-tracking success rate on PROX and 93.8% on EMDB, compared to 44.8% and 50.0% for VideoMimic respectively — an ~8× reduction in failure rate. In terms of reconstruction quality, CRISP's planar primitives yield a one-way Chamfer distance (Recon→GT) of 0.174 on PROX, nearly half that of VideoMimic (0.311), while also achieving the highest non-penetration score (0.947). For world-grounded HMR on EMDB, CRISP with RL refinement reaches 70.60 mm WA-MPJPE₁₀₀ and 175.93 mm W-MPJPE₁₀₀, outperforming all baselines. Simulation throughput reaches 23K FPS, a 43% speedup over VideoMimic's 16K FPS.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against VideoMimic (Allshire et al., 2025), the primary concurrent baseline, CRISP wins decisively on every metric: RL success rate improves from 44.8% to 93.1% (PROX) and 50.0% to 93.8% (EMDB), one-way Chamfer distance drops from 0.311 to 0.174, and W-MPJPE₁₀₀ falls from 505 mm to 176 mm. The paper attributes VideoMimic's failures to noisy dense-mesh reconstructions that cause penetrations, ghost surfaces, and unstable contacts during simulation. Against the HMR baselines on EMDB (Table 2), CRISP with RL refinement outperforms GVHMR (74.80→70.60 WA-MPJPE₁₀₀), TRAM (83.61→70.60), and WHAM (98.45→70.60), while also achieving the lowest jitter (8.14 vs. 15.50 for GVHMR, 24.00 for TRAM, 22.57 for WHAM) and lowest root translational error (1.90). Notably, without RL refinement, CRISP's raw HMR (78.16 WA-MPJPE₁₀₀) is already competitive with GVHMR (74.80) and superior to TRAM and WHAM, indicating that the physics-based tracking loop further refines rather than merely compensates for reconstruction errors.

## Relation to Prior Work

CRISP builds on a pipeline of existing components—MegaSAM for visual SLAM, MoGe for monocular depth, GVHMR for world-grounded human motion recovery, and InteractVLM for contact prediction—but departs from prior real-to-sim work (notably the concurrent VideoMimic) in its geometric representation: rather than producing dense meshes via TSDF fusion or neural kernel surface reconstruction, it fits a small set (~50) of convex planar primitives to point clouds via a clustering pipeline (K-means on normals, DBSCAN spatial splitting, temporal merging with optical flow). This is motivated by the observation that physics simulators require clean, convex collision geometry, and that dense meshes introduce artifacts that destabilize RL-driven humanoids. The RL-based motion tracking stage follows MaskedMimic/DeepMimic conventions, serving as both a controller and a physical-plausibility validator for the reconstruction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CRISP addresses a practical bottleneck in embodied AI and robotics: converting casual monocular video into assets that actually work in physics simulation, where even small geometric noise causes catastrophic policy failures. The 8× reduction in tracking failure rates and 43% throughput gain make real-to-sim RL training from in-the-wild video substantially more practical. Key limitations include the static-scene assumption (no dynamic objects, fluids, or deformables), the planar-primitive approximation for curved surfaces, and dependence on HMR accuracy for contact-guided completion. Open questions include extending to dynamic scenes, more expressive primitives (superquadrics), and seamless integration into sim-to-real robot deployment pipelines.
