## Problem

Aligning diffusion models with human preferences typically requires either massive human-annotated preference datasets (millions of pairs) or external reward models that introduce secondary biases and are vulnerable to reward hacking. This paper asks whether a diffusion model can serve as its own teacher—achieving strong preference alignment from only a minimal seed of human feedback (6% of typical data) without any auxiliary reward model, by iteratively leveraging its own generative and discriminative capabilities.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264595/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a small seed set of human-annotated preference pairs (50K pairs from Pick-a-Pic v2, ~6% of the full 851K dataset), a pre-trained diffusion model (SD1.5 or SDXL), and disjoint sets of new prompts introduced at each iteration. It produces an aligned diffusion model that generates images better matching composite human preferences (aesthetics, text-image alignment, realism). Notably, the model simultaneously acts as generator and evaluator: it samples N=8 candidate images per prompt and ranks them using its own implicit DPO reward, requiring no external scoring network. The mixup ratio is fixed at α=0.25, meaning 25% of each training batch comes from the original human seed data and 75% from self-generated pairs.

## Method

SAIL derives a closed-form self-rewarding signal from the DPO implicit reward: the relative preference between two images is computed as the difference in squared noise-prediction residuals between the current model and a frozen reference model, averaged over 10 random (t, q_t) draws (Eq. 8–9). The training loop proceeds in three phases per iteration: (1) generate N candidates per new prompt, (2) rank them via the self-reward and select best-worst pairs, and (3) perform DPO fine-tuning on a mixup of self-generated and seed preference data. A ranked preference mixup strategy (inspired by experience replay) prevents catastrophic forgetting by anchoring the model to original human priors, avoiding the distribution collapse and reward inflation observed when training purely on self-generated data. The best-worst selection strategy outperforms random pair selection, and the iterative process shows steady improvement across 3–5 iterations before saturating (probability in Eq. 4 exceeding 0.8).

## Results

Using only 50K preference pairs (6% of the 851K used by baselines), SAIL achieves consistent iterative gains across all benchmarks. On SDXL, SAIL(Iter2) reaches 28.74/27.80/27.78/28.09 on HPSv2 (Anime/Concept-Art/Painting/Photo) and 22.51/0.9844/6.16/27.32 on Pick-a-Pic V2 (PickScore/ImageReward/Aesthetics/HPSv2), surpassing full-data DiffusionDPO (28.71/27.75/27.82/27.89 and 22.59/0.9336/6.02/27.27) on most metrics. On SD1.5, SAIL(Iter3) achieves 27.88/27.16/27.15/27.79 on HPSv2 and 21.00/0.2329/5.49/26.75 on Pick-a-Pic V2, matching or exceeding DiffusionSPO which uses a dedicated step-wise reward model. On PartiPrompts (SD1.5), SAIL(Iter4) gains +0.24 PickScore, +0.1895 ImageReward, +0.10 Aesthetics, and +0.44 HPSv2 over the base model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DiffusionDPO (0.8M pairs), SAIL with only 50K pairs (6% of data) surpasses it on SDXL in Anime (+0.03), Concept-Art (+0.05), Photo (+0.20), ImageReward (+0.0508), Aesthetics (+0.14), and HPSv2 (+0.05), while slightly trailing on PickScore (22.51 vs 22.59) and Painting (27.78 vs 27.82). Against DiffusionSPO (0.8M pairs + step-wise reward model), SAIL matches its HPSv2 Photo score (27.79 vs 27.55 on SD1.5) and Pick-a-Pic HPSv2 (26.75 vs 26.75) while using 94% less data, though SPO leads on PickScore (21.20 vs 21.00) and Aesthetics (5.68 vs 5.49) on SD1.5. Against MaPO (0.8M pairs), SAIL clearly wins on all four HPSv2 subcategories and ImageReward (0.9844 vs 0.8227) on SDXL, while MaPO edges ahead on Aesthetics (6.16 vs 6.16, tied) and PickScore (22.24 vs 22.51, SAIL wins). Against Online DPO (DDPO with Aesthetic reward), SAIL wins on ImageReward (0.1137 vs 0.0936) and HPSv2 (26.49 vs 26.35) but loses on Aesthetics (5.46 vs 5.53), confirming that a single aesthetic reward model over-optimizes one dimension at the expense of holistic preference alignment.

## Relation to Prior Work

SAIL builds directly on the DPO framework (Rafailov et al., 2023) and its diffusion-model adaptation, DiffusionDPO (Wallace et al., 2024), by reinterpreting DPO's implicit reward reparameterization as a self-annotation mechanism: the current model and a frozen reference model jointly score generated candidates, eliminating the need for external reward models or large-scale human preference pairs. It departs from both offline DPO (static preference datasets) and online DPO variants that rely on LLM annotators or trained aesthetic scorers (DDPO, ImageReward+ReFL) by closing the loop entirely within the diffusion model itself. The ranked preference mixup strategy draws an analogy to experience replay in reinforcement learning, and the overall iterative self-training paradigm parallels recent LLM self-rewarding work (e.g., Kim et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024) but is, to the authors' claim, the first such framework applied to diffusion models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in reducing preference-alignment data requirements from ~800K pairs to ~50K (6%), which could make iterative preference tuning feasible for practitioners without access to large annotation budgets or well-calibrated reward models. However, the absolute metric gains are modest (e.g., +0.38 PickScore, +0.30 ImageReward on SDXL over the base model), and the self-rewarding signal's accuracy, while surpassing CLIP and Aesthetics on a 1K-pair validation slice, has not been stress-tested across diverse prompt distributions or model scales beyond SD1.5/SDXL. Open questions include whether the approach transfers to video generation (where preference data is even scarcer), whether intermediate step-wise reward signals could accelerate convergence, and how the method behaves when the seed data is extremely small or noisy. The saturation observed around iteration 4–5 also raises the question of whether further gains require architectural changes rather than more iterations.
