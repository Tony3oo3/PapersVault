## Problem

In multi-agent debate (MAD), LLM agents access all memories from the previous debate round to refine their reasoning, but these memories may contain erroneous responses that mislead agents away from correct answers. The paper identifies this vulnerability as underexplored and provides a theoretical analysis showing that MAD's success probability is tightly coupled to the number of erroneous memories, with performance degrading as erroneous memories increase—unlike simply scaling the number of agents or samples.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260183/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a text query prompt (task instructions plus question) and the set of reasoning responses (memories) generated by N_a agents in the previous debate round. Between rounds, it produces a binary mask vector M ∈ {0,1}^{N_a} that flags which memories to retain or discard, computed either via subjective LLM evaluation (YES/NO/NOT SURE labels) or objective perplexity scoring. The final output is a single answer selected by majority voting over the N_a responses from the last debate round, with the entire process consuming roughly 1.13×–1.44× the tokens of vanilla MAD for subjective masking, or ~0.65×–0.75× for objective masking.

## Method

MAD-M² inserts a critical evaluation and masking step between consecutive debate rounds: after each round, an LLM agent evaluates all N_a memories and generates a binary mask vector that is element-wise multiplied with the memory set to produce a filtered context for the next round. Two masking strategies are offered—subjective (the LLM labels each memory as correct, incorrect, or unsure, with a configurable strictness rule) and objective (only the response with the lowest perplexity is preserved, leveraging the observation that high perplexity correlates with hallucinated or fallacious content). Theoretically, the paper proves via Hoeffding-style concentration bounds that reducing the number of erroneous memories N_e consistently tightens the success probability bound of MAD in both hard-problem (p < 1/2) and easy-problem (p ≥ 1/2) regimes, whereas increasing the number of agents or samples can actually degrade performance in the hard regime. Empirically, MAD-M² outperforms vanilla MAD on GSM8K, MATH, AIME24/25, and MMLU-Pro across four open-source LLMs, with the objective strategy proving more effective on stronger models and harder tasks.

## Results

On Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct, MAD-M²(O) achieves the largest gains over MAD: 80.2% on MATH (vs. 71.2%, +9.0%), 37.0% on MMLU_Pro (vs. 34.2%, +2.8%), and 13.3% on both AIME24 and AIME25 (vs. 6.7%, +6.6%). On Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, MAD-M²(S) improves MATH from 55.6% to 56.8% and MMLU_Pro from 43.0% to 43.6%. With the strongest model QwQ-32B, MAD-M²(S) achieves 79.6% on MATH and 97.8% on GSM8K, marginally exceeding MAD (79.2% and 97.2%). The objective masking strategy (MAD-M²(O)) also reduces token consumption by ~30% compared to MAD by filtering out erroneous memories from prompts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the primary baseline MAD (3 agents, 2 rounds), MAD-M² wins in most settings: MAD-M²(O) with Qwen2.5-Math-7B improves MATH by +9.0% (80.2 vs. 71.2) and AIME24/25 by +6.6% each, while MAD-M²(S) with Qwen2.5-7B gains +1.2% on MATH and +0.6% on MMLU_Pro. However, MAD-M² does not uniformly dominate: on Qwen2.5-7B, MAD-M²(S) drops on AIME25 (3.3 vs. 6.7) and GSM8K (89.0 vs. 91.8), and MAD-M²(O) underperforms MAD on AIME24 (6.7 vs. 13.3). Against CoT-SC (6 paths), MAD-M²(O) with Qwen2.5-Math-7B matches or exceeds CoT-SC on MATH (80.2 vs. 82.0) and AIME24 (13.3 vs. 23.3, where CoT-SC wins), while MAD-M²(S) with QwQ-32B slightly trails CoT-SC on MATH (79.6 vs. 81.6). Against Sparse MAD (6 agents, 2 rounds, each agent sees only 2 neighbors), MAD-M²(S) and MAD-M²(O) outperform on AIME24/25 and MMLU_Pro with Qwen2.5-7B, but Sparse MAD wins on MATH with Qwen2.5-Math-7B (76.4 vs. 62.8/63.8) while suffering a catastrophic drop on MMLU_Pro (38.6 vs. 66.2/67.4), indicating weaker generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Multi-Agent Debate (MAD, Du et al., ICML 2023) by identifying a specific vulnerability—sensitivity to erroneous memories from prior debate rounds—and proposing memory masking (MAD-M²) as a targeted fix. It departs from structural approaches like Sparse MAD (Li et al., 2024) and S²-MAD (Zeng et al., 2025), which reduce communication via static topologies or random selection, by instead using content-aware filtering (subjective LLM evaluation or objective perplexity) to selectively discard incorrect memories. The theoretical analysis positions MAD relative to CoT-SC (Wang et al., ICLR 2023), showing that MAD's performance is bounded by CoT-SC under mild assumptions, which reframes MAD as a constrained variant of parallel sampling rather than a fundamentally superior paradigm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical result that CoT-SC serves as an upper bound for MAD is a useful conceptual clarification that explains why multi-agent debate often fails to outperform simpler self-consistency baselines, redirecting future work toward improving memory quality rather than adding more agents or rounds. Practically, the finding that the optimal masking strategy depends on model capability (subjective for weaker models, perplexity-based for stronger ones) offers actionable guidance. However, empirical gains are modest (0.6–9% depending on task and model), the theoretical analysis covers only 2-round MAD, and the subjective masking variant incurs 13–41% additional token overhead, limiting immediate adoption. Open questions include whether masking generalizes to many-round debates, heterogeneous agent ensembles, and whether the CoT-SC upper bound can be broken with more sophisticated memory management.
