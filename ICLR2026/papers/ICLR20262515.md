## Problem

No RAG benchmark exists for the gaming domain, where two coupled dynamics—frequent game content updates (knowledge evolution) and the systematic shifting of player community focus (user interest drift)—make static evaluations obsolete. Existing dynamic benchmarks track only knowledge updates and ignore interest drift, while their automated synthesis pipelines produce questions that lack the authentic phrasing and intent of real players, rendering them invalid for user-centered evaluation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262515/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes three raw input streams per game: structured wiki documents (e.g., Fandom pages), official patch notes with publication timestamps, and large corpora of player community posts (ranging from ~10K to ~60K posts). These are processed into an Authority Knowledge Base of timestamped, entity-annotated snippets, a game-agnostic Question Template Base with hierarchical topic labels (6 categories, 21 sub-categories), and a User Persona Base of synthetic player descriptions. The output is a sequence of benchmark slices B_t, each pairing a retrieval corpus K_t with an evaluation dataset D_t of tuples (Q, A, C_ref, θ, τ, σ), where the dataset is continuously resampled and regenerated as patches land and community topic distributions shift.

## Method

ChronoPlay integrates two tightly coupled mechanisms. First, a dual-source synthesis pipeline grounds questions in factual accuracy via the Authority Knowledge Base while capturing authentic player phrasing through community-mined templates and personas; it uses a HyDE-inspired step where an LLM generates a hypothetical Q&A from a sampled template to produce a semantically rich retrieval query, then a data synthesis agent conditions on the retrieved snippets, a matched persona, and a question type to produce candidate tuples, with an iterative LLM-as-Judge quality loop that re-samples templates on failure. Second, a dual-dynamic update mechanism keeps the benchmark current: a Knowledge Evolution pathway uses Self-ICL-based NER to identify entities affected by new patches, flags stale tuples by entity-set intersection, and regenerates them; a User Interest Drift pathway monitors community topic distributions over sliding windows and triggers resampling when a topic-weighted Jensen-Shannon Divergence (with prominence-weighted topics via a γ-exponent) exceeds a threshold, down-sampling waning topics and synthesizing new questions for emerging ones.

## Results

On retrieval (K=3), text-embedding-3 is the top performer in most phases, achieving R@3 of 0.521–0.551 on Dying Light 2 (Phases 1–3) and 0.343–0.381 on Dune: Awakening, though it drops to 0.419–0.422 in DL2 Phases 4–5. BGE-M3 catastrophically underperforms on Dune with R@3 of only 0.028–0.067, likely due to the game's heavy use of proper nouns. On generation (correctness), GPT-4o generally leads across phases, while Gemini 2.5-flash achieves the highest faithfulness scores. Performance volatility is significant: in PUBG, generator correctness scores swing by over 0.2 between adjacent phases, confirming that static benchmarks would mask critical performance shifts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the four retrievers, text-embedding-3 dominates in 16 of 18 phases, with the sole exception being PUBG Phase 3 where Qwen3-Embedding edges it out (R@3 0.578 vs. 0.577). BM25 is consistently competitive but trails the dense retrievers by 5–15 points in R@3 on most phases. BGE-M3 is the clear outlier on Dune: Awakening, scoring R@3 of 0.028–0.067 versus 0.343–0.381 for text-embedding-3, a gap the authors attribute to the game's dense proper-noun vocabulary (e.g., 'terrarium of muad'dib'). On the generation side, GPT-4o achieves the highest correctness in most phases across all three games, while Gemini 2.5-flash consistently leads on faithfulness, revealing a correctness–faithfulness trade-off. Claude-3.5-Sonnet and DeepSeek-V3 occupy the middle tier, while llama-4 and Qwen3-14B generally trail the closed-source models. The key finding is that no single retriever or generator is universally best: rankings shift across lifecycle phases, and the magnitude of these shifts (e.g., >0.2 correctness swings in PUBG) is precisely what a static benchmark would average away.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of dynamic RAG benchmarks (HOH, GrowOVER, EvolvingQA, DynaQuest, REALTIMEQA, LIVEXIV) that track knowledge evolution over time, by introducing a second orthogonal axis of change: user interest drift. Prior dynamic benchmarks treat the evaluation distribution as fixed and only update factual content, whereas ChronoPlay argues that the question distribution itself must evolve to remain representative of what users actually ask. The synthesis pipeline repurposes HyDE-style hypothetical Q&A generation for retrieval and Self-ICL for domain-adaptive NER, but the core novelty is the dual-source (official + community) data architecture and the topic-weighted Jensen-Shannon Divergence mechanism for detecting interest shifts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first RAG benchmark for the gaming domain and the first to demonstrate empirically that ignoring user interest drift produces a misleadingly stable evaluation that hides real performance changes (e.g., PUBG Phases 4 and 7). The framework's generalizability to other evolving, user-centric domains (e-commerce, social media) makes the dual-dynamic formulation a potentially reusable paradigm. Key limitations include the reliance on LLM-synthesized questions (validated but not identical to organic queries), the LLM-as-Judge's conservative recall (65% for correctness), and evaluation on only three games. Open questions include whether the framework scales to domains with continuous (rather than discrete) knowledge updates and how to incorporate personalized user histories into the evaluation loop.
