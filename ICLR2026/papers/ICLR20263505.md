## Problem

The CLIP vision encoder, widely used in MLLMs like LLaVA, suffers from two inductive biases: mesoscopic bias (uniform grid patchification treats fixed-scale cells as semantic units) and interpolation bias (fixed positional embeddings prevent native handling of arbitrary resolutions). These biases cause significant failures on fine-grained VQA tasks, yet prior fixes require expensive retraining of the entire vision encoder or LLM pipeline.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263505/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

QLIP accepts RGB images at arbitrary resolutions (no fixed 336×336 constraint) paired with a text question, and produces a free-form text answer. The number of visual tokens fed to the LLM is not fixed but adapts to image content via the quadtree: semantically uniform regions are merged into fewer tokens while detail-rich regions retain more, so a simple sky background might consume 1 token while a complex foreground object consumes many. The hyperparameter α controls the trade-off between token count and detail preservation.

## Method

QLIP replaces CLIP's uniform grid patchification with a content-aware Quadtree Patchification (QtP) that recursively subdivides the image into four quadrants and prunes branches where the maximum average gradient falls below a threshold α, then downsamples oversized leaf nodes to the CLIP patch size. To handle arbitrary resolutions, a small 4-layer MLP with 48 Fourier features is trained to interpolate CLIP's fixed 24×24 positional embeddings to continuous coordinates in [-1,1]², using a loss that combines [CLS] token invariance across resolutions with an L1 residual matching the original embeddings on the standard grid. The MLP is trained for 100 epochs on the small Imagenette dataset (~10k images, 11 hours on 4×L40S GPUs), while the CLIP encoder and LLM weights remain completely frozen. The result is a drop-in, few-lines-of-code modification that adaptively scales token count to image semantics without any retraining of the downstream MLLM.

## Results

QLIP achieves a headline +13.6% improvement on the V* benchmark (45.0 → 58.6) with LLaVA-1.5-13B and +11.0% (42.4 → 53.4) with LLaVA-1.5-7B, all without retraining the vision encoder or LLM. On the POPE hallucination benchmark, QLIP improves F1 by +5.2 (74.4 → 79.6) for 7B and +1.2 (82.4 → 83.6) for 13B. On general VQA benchmarks (MM-Bench, CV-Bench, ScienceQA, MME, RealWorld-QA), QLIP largely matches or slightly exceeds baseline performance, with the 7B model showing small regressions on MM-Bench (−2.8) and RealWorld-QA (−1.7) when tuned for fine-grained tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the LLaVA-1.5 baseline, QLIP delivers large gains on fine-grained VQA (V*: +11.0% for 7B, +13.6% for 13B) and hallucination mitigation (POPE F1: +5.2 for 7B, +1.2 for 13B), while remaining competitive on general benchmarks (MME +34 for 7B; MM-Bench, CV-Bench, Sci-QA within ±1 point for 13B). Compared to S2 (Shi et al., ECCV 2024), which also uses a frozen CLIP encoder with multi-scale tokens, QLIP-13B (58.6) outperforms both S2-13B (55.5) and S2-7B (55.5) on V*-Overall, while QLIP-7B (53.4) trails S2-7B (55.5) by 2.1 points. Against SEAL (Wu & Xie, 2024), which replaces the vision encoder entirely and requires full retraining, SEAL achieves a substantially higher V*-Overall score (75.4 vs. 58.6 for QLIP-13B), but QLIP-13B surpasses SEAL on POPE F1 (83.6 vs. 82.4), indicating better hallucination control despite using a frozen CLIP encoder. Overall, QLIP's key advantage is achieving competitive fine-grained performance with zero retraining of the MLLM, whereas S2 and SEAL both require pre-training and instruction tuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against two dominant strategies for improving CLIP-based MLLMs: (1) replacing the vision encoder entirely (S2, SEAL, LLaVA-UHD), which requires expensive pre-training and instruction tuning, and (2) post-encoding token pruning/merging (PUMER, FastV, Matryoshka), which trades accuracy for efficiency. QLIP departs from both by intervening at the pre-encoding patchification stage, using a quadtree structure (Hunter & Steiglitz, 1979) for content-aware token selection and a small coordinate-based MLP to interpolate CLIP's fixed positional embeddings to arbitrary resolutions. The key conceptual departure is the argument that the encoder weights are not the bottleneck—rather, the uniform grid patchification and absolute positional encodings impose inductive biases (mesoscopic and interpolation bias) that a lightweight, training-free modification can correct.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is high: QLIP is a drop-in, few-lines-of-code modification that requires no retraining of the vision encoder or LLM, making it immediately applicable to the large ecosystem of existing CLIP-based MLLMs. The finding that content-aware token reduction can simultaneously improve accuracy and reduce hallucination (POPE F1 +5.2) challenges the prevailing assumption that fewer tokens necessarily means degraded performance. Limitations include sensitivity of the 13B model to aspect ratio changes, modest or slightly negative results on general VQA benchmarks when tuned for fine-grained tasks, and the approach's dependence on CLIP's specific architecture—adaptation to newer encoders with relative/rotary position encodings (InternVL, Qwen-V) is left as future work. An open question is whether the quadtree selection criterion (gradient-based) generalizes to domains where semantic salience does not correlate with pixel-level gradients.
