## Problem

Existing temporal causal discovery methods model only finite lag orders, so when variables exhibit long-term carry-over effects (where distant past values directly influence the present), unmodeled historical nodes act as confounders and induce spurious causal edges. Naively unfolding variables across multiple time steps to capture longer lags causes a super-exponential blowup in the number of candidate causal structures, making the search intractable. The paper addresses both the identifiability gap (can causal structure be recovered when history is incomplete?) and the computational gap (how to search efficiently without temporal unfolding).

## Input / Output

The method consumes observational time-series data of d variables over T time steps, processed via a sliding window of length w (default w=5, step size 1) to yield m samples, each consisting of a flattened historical matrix H' ∈ R^{1×(w−1)·d} and a target vector x ∈ R^d. The theoretical guarantee (Theorem 6) requires only that the impulse response g(τ) satisfies a linear recurrence of order h, so the window need not span the full carry-over duration. The output is a sparse directed acyclic graph (the causal skeleton) with estimated edge weights, obtained by thresholding the weight matrix derived from the optimal R matrix.

## Method

LEVER operates in three stages. First, historical refinement: for each sliding-window sample, the target observation is regressed on the preceding w−1 steps via OLS, and the residual ẋ = x − H'B is used as the refined observation; Theorem 6 proves that under a linear-recurrence assumption on g(τ), this limited-history residual preserves causal identifiability. Second, variable reordering via Double DQN: the state is the upper-triangular R matrix from QR decomposition of the current data list (a compact O(d²) representation replacing neural-network encoders), the action appends one unselected variable's refined column, and the reward is the reduction in a global score combining R-diagonal terms and residual sums of squares for unselected variables. Third, structure recovery: edge weights are extracted from the optimal R* via W_pa(j),j = R_oo(j−1)⁻¹ · R_oo(j−1),j, and edges with |W| below a threshold are pruned. The key theoretical insight is that QR decomposition simultaneously yields the OLS score (diagonal of R) and the causal weight matrix, making it a natural bridge between linear algebra and causal identifiability.

## Results

On static synthetic graphs, LEVER reduces SHD by 17.29%–40.00% and improves F1-score by 5.30%–8.79% over the best baseline (SCORE or NOTEARS), e.g., on G(10,20) it achieves F1=0.8899 and SHD=4.00 versus SCORE's F1=0.8179 and SHD=6.67. On temporal synthetic data with long-term carry-over effects, LEVER achieves a 64%–88% reduction in SHD and a 45%–101% improvement in F1-score over the best temporal baseline. On the real-world wt_walks_v1 wind-tunnel dataset (16 nodes, 26 edges), LEVER attains SHD=22 and F1=0.5769, substantially outperforming PCMCI (SHD=35, F1=0.3636), NTS-NOTEARS (SHD=37, F1=0.3019), and Granger causality (SHD=48, F1=0.2941).

## Baselines & Comparisons

LEVER outperforms all five static baselines (PC, NOTEARS, RL, CORL, SCORE) on F1 and SHD across both graph sizes, with the largest gains over SCORE (the strongest static baseline): +8.79% F1 and −40% SHD on G(10,20), and +5.30% F1 and −14.29% SHD on G(20,40). On FPR, LEVER beats all baselines on G(10,20) but is slightly behind NOTEARS on G(20,40) (0.0090 vs. 0.0062). Among temporal baselines, LEVER dominates PCMCI, NTS-NOTEARS, and Granger causality by 64%–88% in SHD reduction and 45%–101% in F1 improvement on synthetic temporal data, and by a wide margin on the real-world wt_walks_v1 dataset (F1 0.5769 vs. 0.3636/0.3019/0.2941). In terms of efficiency, LEVER requires only 0.25 s per episode and 10.36 MB peak memory on a 20-node graph, compared to 19.45 s / 24.40 MB for RL and 1.71 s / 64.68 MB for CORL, owing to its O(d) action space and R-matrix state representation instead of a neural encoder.

## Relation to Prior Work

LEVER builds on CORL's formulation of causal discovery as optimal topological ordering via RL, but replaces the neural-network-based state encoder with the QR decomposition R matrix as a compact, theoretically grounded state representation. It departs from temporal causal discovery methods (PCMCI, NTS-NOTEARS, Granger) that assume finite lag orders by explicitly modeling long-term carry-over effects where distant history directly influences present variables, rather than only through observed intermediate time points. The theoretical contribution establishes a novel connection between QR decomposition and OLS-based causal scoring (Theorem 1), and proves that limited-history regression suffices to eliminate long-range confounding under a linear recurrence assumption on the impulse response kernel.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap where existing finite-lag temporal causal discovery methods introduce spurious edges due to unmodeled long-range historical influences, a problem common in industrial and economic time series. The Limited-History Causal Identifiability Theorem provides a clean theoretical guarantee that h-step regression residuals preserve causal identifiability, which is both practically useful and conceptually clarifying. However, the framework is restricted to linear time-invariant systems and requires the impulse response to satisfy a linear recurrence relation, limiting applicability to nonlinear or irregularly decaying systems. Open questions include extending the theory to nonlinear settings, relaxing the linear recurrence assumption, and validating on larger-scale real-world temporal systems beyond the single wind-tunnel dataset.
