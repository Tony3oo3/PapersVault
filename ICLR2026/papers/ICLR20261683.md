## Problem

It remains debated whether Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) genuinely extends the reasoning capability boundary of base LLMs or merely improves sampling efficiency over reasoning paths already present in the base model. Prior Pass@K experiments suggested base models could match post-RLVR models at moderate K, but these results were confounded by base models guessing correct answers with flawed reasoning, especially on hard math problems with simple answer formats. This paper resolves the debate by showing RLVR does extend the reasoning boundary, provided one accounts for the correctness of intermediate reasoning steps rather than answer correctness alone.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261683/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The LLM policy receives a text question prompt and generates a full response consisting of a chain-of-thought (CoT) followed by a final answer token. The verifiable reward is binary and determined solely by programmatic answer correctness (e.g., exact match for math, code execution for coding). For the proposed CoT-Pass@K metric, each generated CoT is additionally verified by an LLM-as-a-CoT-Judge (DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B) using n=3 independent verification passes aggregated via any-correct, all-correct, and majority-correct strategies, so that a sample counts as 'passing' only when both the answer and the reasoning steps are correct.

## Method

The paper introduces CoT-Pass@K, a metric that requires both a correct final answer and a verified-correct reasoning chain, and uses it to demonstrate extended reasoning boundaries for DAPO-Qwen-32B over Qwen2.5-32B on AIME 2024/2025 and for post-RLVR coding models over their distilled predecessors on LiveCodeBench. Theoretically, under a Logic Prior assumption (correct CoTs induce correct answers with probability α > β for incorrect CoTs), Theorem 1 proves that the GRPO policy gradient yields strictly positive expected advantage for correct CoTs and strictly negative for incorrect ones, so p_c (probability of generating a correct CoT) increases monotonically even though the reward signal is answer-only. Empirically, the authors reproduce DAPO training on 32 AMD MI300X GPUs and track per-prompt indicators P(CA) and P(CC|CA), showing that correct-reasoning incentivization begins within the first ~20 training steps and generalizes to unseen AIME problems. Finally, SFT experiments on CoTs generated at different RLVR stages confirm that post-RLVR CoTs are fundamentally higher quality: SFT on DAPO CoTs replicates the post-RLVR model's Pass@1 and CoT-Pass@K performance, while SFT on base-model CoTs yields only marginal gains.

## Results

The paper's central finding is that the proposed CoT-Pass@K metric (requiring both correct final answer and verified correct reasoning steps) reveals a persistent and significant performance gap between DAPO-Qwen-32B (post-RLVR) and Qwen2.5-32B (base) on AIME 2024 and AIME 2025 across all K values up to 1024, whereas standard Pass@K shows the base model catching up and surpassing the RLVR model for moderately large K. On LiveCodeBench (six versions), AceReason-Nemotron-7B demonstrates clear Pass@K improvements over its pre-RLVR counterpart DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, confirming extended reasoning boundaries for competitive coding. Training dynamics analysis shows P(CC|CA) (fraction of correct CoTs among correct answers) improves from the very first training steps, with the median reaching ~0.7 even after P(CA) saturates near 1.0 at ~400 steps. SFT on DAPO-generated CoT data replicates the Pass@1 performance of DAPO-Qwen-32B, confirming fundamental quality improvements in reasoning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the base model Qwen2.5-32B, DAPO-Qwen-32B wins decisively on CoT-Pass@K for AIME 2024 and 2025 (the gap persists across all K up to 1024), but loses/ties on standard Pass@K for large K — confirming that the base model can guess correct answers without valid reasoning. On MATH-500 and AMC23, the RLVR advantage is minimal, likely because the base model already solves these problems within its existing knowledge or training data. On Minerva, DAPO shows no improvement due to a train-test domain mismatch (physics problems, free-form answers vs. integer-answer math training). Against the distilled model DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, both AceReason-Nemotron-7B and Skywork-OR1-7B win on Pass@K for LiveCodeBench, demonstrating that RLVR extends reasoning boundaries even for already-capable distilled models in code. However, Skywork-OR1-Math-7B does not show a CoT-Pass@K gap over the distilled model on AIME, suggesting that for math, distillation may already capture most of what RLVR can incentivize. The paper's findings directly challenge the hypothesis of Yue et al. (2025) that RLVR merely improves sampling efficiency, by showing that the extended boundary is real when reasoning correctness (not just answer correctness) is measured.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly engages with the RLVR paradigm established by DeepSeek-R1 and its GRPO algorithm, and specifically challenges the influential hypothesis from Yue et al. (2025) that RLVR merely improves sampling efficiency without extending the reasoning boundary. It builds on the DAPO training recipe (Yu et al., 2025) and the AceReason-Nemotron/Skywork-OR1 coding results (Chen et al., 2025b; He et al., 2025), but departs from prior work by introducing the CoT-Pass@K metric to account for reasoning-step correctness and by providing a theoretical framework (the Logic Prior assumption) explaining how answer-only rewards implicitly incentivize correct CoTs. The LLM-as-a-CoT-Judge verification approach extends earlier process-reward modeling work (Lightman et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024) to unstructured math and code reasoning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This paper resolves a central debate in the RLVR community by showing that the widely-used Pass@K metric is unreliable for math (due to answer-guessing with flawed reasoning) and that a CoT-aware metric reveals genuine reasoning boundary extension. The practical finding that SFT on RLVR-generated CoTs nearly replicates full RLVR performance offers a cost-effective alternative for practitioners. Key limitations include reliance on LLM-as-judge verification (acknowledged false positives/negatives), the Logic Prior assumption not being formally guaranteed, and the theorem providing no generalization guarantee. Open questions include how to accelerate the slow improvement of P(CC|CA), the need for standardized lightweight CoT verifiers, and whether scaling RLVR can match the transformative impact of scaling pre-training.
