## Problem

LLMs trained via SFT and RLHF inherit a strong inductive bias toward standardized response formats, creating what the authors term 'cognitive inertia'—a persistent inability to follow instructions that explicitly contradict training norms (e.g., 'answer incorrectly,' 'use no bullet points,' 'write code without comments'). No prior benchmark systematically evaluates this counterintuitive instruction-following dimension, leaving a critical gap in assessing genuine instruction-following robustness under out-of-distribution conditions.

## Input / Output

Each evaluation item is a counterintuitive text instruction (one of 8 types) paired with a domain-specific question, available in matched Chinese and English versions (506 each, 1012 total across 23 domains). The model's free-form text response is scored by a type-specific LLM judge that outputs a per-type compliance score; these aggregate into an overall instruction-following score. Notably, the judge template structure varies by type—some provide the original question to the judge while others supply only scoring criteria and the model response—reflecting differing context dependencies across instruction categories.

## Method

The benchmark is constructed through a five-stage human-in-the-loop pipeline: (1) systematic inversion of canonical SFT annotation paradigms to derive 8 counterintuitive instruction types (Question Correction, Intentional Textual Flaws, Code without Comments, Counter-Conventional Formatting, Deliberately Incorrect Answers, Instructional Induction, Mid-turn Instruction Modification, Counterfactual Answering); (2) expert-crafted seed questions with multi-dimensional cross-validation; (3) LLM-based large-scale generation guided by a predefined disciplinary taxonomy; (4) automatic filtering (length constraints, semantic similarity); and (5) rigorous expert review with scoring rubric calibration. Evaluation employs an optimized LLM-as-a-Judge framework featuring a dedicated judge model matrix (a different SOTA model selected per instruction type for maximum scoring accuracy), type-specific template structures, and enhanced system prompts with detailed scoring logic and worked examples, achieving 98% judging accuracy validated against human-verified ground truth scores.

## Results

On the English version of Inverse IFEval, o3-high achieves the highest overall score of 75.66, followed by o3-mini (74.67) and GPT-5-high (73.72). Performance varies dramatically across the eight instruction types: Counterfactual Answering is the easiest (most models score >65), while Question Correction is the hardest (half of models score below 30). Fine-tuned Instruct models perform worst overall (e.g., Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct scores only 30.43), and thinking-mode models consistently outperform their non-thinking counterparts by 10–20 points. On the Chinese version, the ranking is broadly similar, with o3-high leading at 76.52.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is against IFEval (Zhou et al., 2023), the standard instruction-following benchmark. The paper shows that model rankings shift substantially between the two benchmarks: o3-high, Gemini-2.5-Pro, and o4-mini maintain top-3 positions on both, confirming their robustness. However, most other models experience significant rank drops on Inverse IFEval. Most strikingly, Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct falls from 5th on IFEval to 15th on Inverse IFEval, and non-thinking models generally rank much lower on Inverse IFEval than on IFEval. This demonstrates that Inverse IFEval captures a distinct dimension of instruction-following—resistance to cognitive inertia—that IFEval does not measure, revealing that high IFEval scores do not guarantee the ability to follow instructions that conflict with SFT-induced conventions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the IFEval instruction-following evaluation framework (Zhou et al., 2023) by inverting its logic: rather than testing compliance with conventional, verifiable constraints, it tests whether models can override the very formatting and correctness norms reinforced during SFT. The concept of 'cognitive inertia'—a persistent inductive bias toward standardized response paradigms inherited from annotation practices—is the paper's key conceptual departure from prior instruction-following benchmarks, which assume that following instructions is a uniform capability. The evaluation methodology (LLM-as-a-Judge with per-type optimized judge models) follows the paradigm established by Arena-Hard and IFEval-Audio, while the multi-stage human-in-the-loop data construction pipeline is standard for benchmark creation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a concrete blind spot: models that rank highly on IFEval can drop dramatically (e.g., Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct from 5th to 15th) when instructions conflict with training norms, suggesting current alignment pipelines actively entrench rigidity. The finding that fine-tuned Instruct variants underperform their base counterparts on counterintuitive tasks points to a trade-off in post-training that the field has not systematically addressed. Limitations include coverage of only two languages and text modality, eight instruction categories, and reliance on LLM-as-a-Judge despite the 98% reported accuracy. Open questions include whether targeted post-training can reduce cognitive inertia without sacrificing conventional performance, and whether the effects generalize to multimodal or agentic settings.
