## Problem

Prior theory on implicit low-rank bias in matrix completion via gradient descent is largely restricted to depth-2 factorizations, where data connectivity of the observation pattern determines whether low-rank solutions emerge. For deeper networks (L≥3), the mechanism by which depth strengthens the low-rank bias—especially under disconnected observations—remains theoretically unexplained, and the loss of plasticity phenomenon (where pre-training on sparse data followed by warm-starting on augmented data degrades performance) lacks a theoretical account in this setting.

## Input / Output

The method takes a ground-truth matrix W* ∈ R^{d×d} and an observation set Ω ⊆ [d]×[d] (e.g., block-diagonal entries with block size s and count n, so d = sn), and produces the converged product matrix W_{L:1}(∞) under gradient flow. The key analytical output is the full set of singular values of the converged matrix, which characterizes the implicit rank: coupled dynamics yield α-dependent singular values whose stable rank approaches 1, while decoupled dynamics yield α-independent singular values at rank n. The analysis operates in the overparameterized regime (d_l ≥ d for all intermediate layers) with gradient flow (infinitesimal step size) and a structured deterministic initialization family parameterized by scale α and rank-control parameter m.

## Method

The paper identifies coupled training dynamics as the key mechanism behind depth-induced low-rank bias. In depth-2 networks, coupling arises only when observations form a connected bipartite graph; in deeper networks (L≥3), intermediate layer parameters are shared across all observed entries, making coupling inherent regardless of observation pattern (Proposition B.1). To make the analysis tractable, they study block-diagonal observations with a deterministic initialization family W_l(0) = α(J_d + (m−1)I_d)/m that interpolates between all-ones (m→1) and identity (m→∞), allowing direct control over initial numerical rank. Theorem 3.3 derives explicit or implicit equations for all singular values of the converged matrix, showing that coupled dynamics (L≥3, finite m) produce α-dependent singular values with stable rank → 1 as α→0, while decoupled dynamics (L=2, or L≥3 with m=∞) produce α-independent rank-n solutions. For loss of plasticity, they prove that depth-2 networks pre-trained under decoupled dynamics (diagonal observations) and resumed under coupled dynamics (connected observations) enter a lazy training regime where the loss decays exponentially but the stable rank remains bounded away from 1 (Theorem 4.3), explaining why warm-starting fails to recover low-rank structure.

## Results

The paper's main theoretical result (Theorem 3.3 + Corollary 3.4) shows that for block-diagonal observations with L ≥ 3 and finite m (coupled dynamics), the stable rank of the converged product matrix satisfies srank(W_{L:1}(∞)) → 1 as the initialization scale α → 0. In contrast, for L = 2 (always decoupled), the stable rank is ((m+d−1)⁴ + (m−1)⁴(n−1)) / (m+d−1)⁴, which is independent of α and approximately n for large m. For the loss-of-plasticity setting (Theorem 4.2), a depth-2 model warm-started from diagonal pre-training has srank(W_{A,B}(∞)) ≥ 1 + exp(−8w*₁₂/w*) and predicts a negative value for the unobserved entry w₂₁, contradicting the true rank-1 ground truth. Theorem 4.3 extends this to d×d matrices, showing the stable rank remains lower-bounded by Ω(d) under lazy training after warm-start.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods in a standard benchmark sense; instead, it contrasts depth-2 (L=2) factorizations against deeper (L≥3) factorizations under the same observation patterns and initialization schemes. Under block-diagonal (disconnected) observations, depth-2 models always exhibit decoupled dynamics and converge to rank-n solutions regardless of initialization scale α, while depth-3+ models with finite m exhibit coupled dynamics and converge to rank-1 as α→0. The only exception for deep models is the m=∞ (i.e., αI_d) initialization, which restores decoupled dynamics and yields the same rank-n outcome as L=2. For the loss-of-plasticity comparison, depth-2 warm-started models provably fail to reduce rank (Theorems 4.2–4.3), whereas the paper's experiments (Figure 3) show that deeper models (L≥3) largely avoid this degradation, maintaining low effective rank even after warm-starting from sparse pre-training. The paper also notes that its results resolve an open problem posed by Menon (2024) for the 2×2 diagonal completion case, which prior theory (Bai et al., 2024) could not address for L≥3.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the connectivity-based implicit bias framework of Bai et al. (2024), which was restricted to depth-2 matrix factorizations, to arbitrary depth L ≥ 3 by identifying coupled training dynamics as the underlying mechanism. For depth-2 models, coupling is determined by the observation pattern (connected vs. disconnected bipartite graph), but the paper shows that for L ≥ 3, intermediate layers inherently couple all observed entries regardless of the observation pattern, making the low-rank bias an architectural property rather than a data-structure property. The paper also provides the first theoretical explanation for the loss-of-plasticity phenomenon empirically reported by Kleinman et al. (2024) in matrix completion, attributing it to lazy training from high-norm, high-rank pre-trained states.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper resolves an open problem posed by Menon (2024) by proving that deep (L ≥ 3) factorizations converge to rank-1 solutions under coupled dynamics, and establishes that coupling is necessary and sufficient for this convergence. The conceptual shift—that depth itself, not data connectivity, is the driver of low-rank bias in deep networks—reframes how the field should think about implicit regularization in overparameterized models. Limitations include the restriction to gradient flow, a specific deterministic initialization family, and block-diagonal observation patterns; whether the results extend to general random initializations, nonlinear activations, and practical optimizers remains open (though empirical evidence in the appendix is encouraging). The loss-of-plasticity analysis is confined to depth-2 models and lazy-training regimes, leaving the interplay with depth ≥ 3's inherent low-rank bias as a future direction.
