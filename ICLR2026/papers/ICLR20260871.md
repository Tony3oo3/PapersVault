## Problem

Standard influence functions were designed for single-level M-estimators and cannot handle the bilevel structure of meta learning, where data affects meta parameters both directly and indirectly through task-specific parameters. This makes existing data attribution tools inapplicable or inaccurate for meta learning, leaving no principled way to identify low-contribution or harmful tasks and noisy instances in large meta-training datasets.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a fully trained MAML model (meta parameters λ* and per-task parameters θ_i), the complete meta-training dataset partitioned into tasks (each with training and validation splits), and a target task or individual instance to evaluate. It outputs a closed-form influence vector (task-IF or instance-IF) that directly estimates the meta-parameter change upon removal, plus a scalar influence score (IS) computed against a held-out new task's validation loss gradient. No retraining is required; the estimated retrained parameters λ* − IF can be used immediately for model editing or data selection.

## Method

The key insight is that at the meta-optimum λ*, the partial gradient ∂_λ L_Total is non-zero (unlike single-level optimization), but the total gradient D_λ L_Total = 0. The paper therefore replaces the standard Hessian and gradient in the influence function with a total Hessian H_{λ,Total} (which includes cross-terms involving dθ_i/dλ and d²θ_i/dλ²) and the total gradient D_λ L_O. For task-level evaluation, task-IF = −(δI + H_{λ,Total})⁻¹ · D_λ L_O(λ*, θ_k; D_k^val) captures both direct and indirect effects. For training instances, a two-stage procedure first computes an inner-IF (classical IF on the inner loss to get the task-parameter shift), then propagates this shift to the outer level through a loss-difference term P(λ, θ_k; z̃) = −∇_θ L_O^T · inner-IF, yielding instance-IF = −H_{λ,Total}⁻¹ · D_λ P. Computational scalability is achieved via EK-FAC for inner Hessian-vector products, a Neumann series truncation for the total Hessian inverse, and an approximation that replaces third-order derivative terms with a gradient-norm diagonal, avoiding explicit Hessian storage.

## Results

At the task level, Task-IF achieves accuracy close to full retraining (e.g., 0.7804 vs. 0.7908 on Omniglot, 0.3403 vs. 0.3599 on FC100) while reducing runtime by 75–80% (e.g., 65.69s vs. 316.74s on Omniglot, 74.63s vs. 682.56s on Mini-ImageNet). At the instance level, Instance-IF (Training) reaches 0.7686 accuracy on Omniglot and 0.3645 on FC100 (vs. 0.7852 and 0.3549 for retraining) in only ~4.5s versus ~252s for retraining. In the harmful-task removal experiment, the influence-score-based method identifies ~100% of mislabeled tasks after checking only 40% of training data, compared to ~45% for random removal at 95% checking ratio.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Task-IF substantially outperforms all three external baselines on accuracy across all four datasets. Against EKFAC IF (Grosse et al., 2023), Task-IF achieves 0.7804 vs. 0.4872 accuracy on Omniglot and 0.3403 vs. 0.3133 on FC100, while also being ~10× faster (65.69s vs. 631.90s on Omniglot). Against TRAK (Park et al., 2023), Task-IF leads by a wide margin (0.7804 vs. 0.3076 on Omniglot) and is ~16× faster (65.69s vs. 1022.52s). Against TracIN (Pruthi et al., 2020), Task-IF edges ahead on accuracy (0.7804 vs. 0.7690 on Omniglot, 0.6118 vs. 0.5844 on MNIST) and is ~5.7× faster (65.69s vs. 375.42s). The internal Direct-IF baseline, which ignores the inner-level dependency, degrades severely (0.5422 on Omniglot), confirming the necessity of the total-gradient and total-Hessian formulation. At the instance level, Instance-IF achieves near-retraining accuracy (within 1–4%) while reducing runtime by 50–50× (e.g., 4.49s vs. 251.71s on Omniglot for training instances).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the influence function framework (Koh & Liang, 2017; Huber, 1981) from single-level optimization to the bilevel structure of meta learning (MAML, Finn et al., 2017). The key conceptual departure is showing that naively applying the standard influence function while ignoring the inner-level dependence (the 'direct-IF') is fundamentally flawed, and introducing total gradient and total Hessian formulations that capture both direct and indirect data effects through task-specific parameters. For computational tractability, the paper leverages EK-FAC (Kwon et al., 2023) for inner-level Hessian-vector products and proposes a Neumann series approximation for the outer-level total Hessian, avoiding explicit matrix inversion.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Meta learning is widely deployed for few-shot adaptation, yet its training data quality (noisy labels, unhelpful tasks) has lacked principled evaluation tools; this work provides the first closed-form attribution framework for the bilevel setting, enabling practical applications like harmful task removal, model editing, and interpretability of meta parameters. Key limitations include reliance on the total Hessian approximation (Theorem 5.1) that discards third-order derivative information, validation only on relatively small-scale benchmarks (Omniglot, MNIST, Mini-ImageNet, FC100), and non-trivial accuracy gaps versus retraining on harder datasets (e.g., ~4% on Mini-ImageNet). Open questions include whether the approximations remain accurate for deeper architectures or very large meta-training sets, and whether the framework extends naturally to non-gradient-based meta learning methods.
