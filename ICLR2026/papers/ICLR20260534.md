## Problem

Human cognition emerges from interacting, specialized brain networks (language, multiple demand, theory of mind, default mode), yet the internal organization of LLMs is largely unstructured, with any specialization being implicit and difficult to interpret or control. This paper asks whether explicitly encoding brain-inspired functional specialization into a modular architecture can yield models that are simultaneously interpretable, steerable at inference time, and competitive on reasoning benchmarks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260534/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes standard text prompts (reasoning questions, social cognition scenarios, multiple-choice items) and produces autoregressive text responses with intermediate reasoning chains. Uniquely, at every layer and for every token, the model also emits a discrete routing assignment to one of four expert modules, making the internal computation traceable. At inference time, users can override routing by ablating specific experts (e.g., retaining only the social expert to elicit empathetic responses), enabling fine-grained behavioral steering without retraining.

## Method

MICRO partitions each transformer layer into four full expert blocks (each with its own attention and FFN), forming a Mixture-of-Blocks (MoB) architecture with top-1 token routing, maintaining comparable active parameters to the original dense model. Specialization is induced via a three-stage curriculum: (1) experts are trained on a small curated dataset of 3,055 examples with deterministic per-token routing labels aligned to cognitive domains, (2) the frozen model's router is trained on the same data with soft top-2 routing to learn domain-appropriate expert selection, and (3) the full model is finetuned end-to-end on 939k TULU-3 instruction-tuning examples. The key insight is that the minimal Stage-1 inductive bias persists through large-scale Stage-3 training, producing stable, interpretable routing patterns (e.g., language expert dominates early layers, domain experts activate in deeper layers) that are causally validated by expert ablations and confirmed by neuroscience localizer experiments.

## Results

On Llama-3.2-1B, MICRO achieves 34.7% on GSM8K (0-shot CoT), 4.6% on Minerva Math, 31.2% on MMLU, and 29.8% on BBH, matching or exceeding the MoB baseline (30.5/3.7/27.1/27.4) while remaining close to the Dense baseline (36.8/4.8/29.7/30.4). On Llama-3.2-3B, MICRO reaches 57.2% GSM8K, 13.0% Minerva Math, 45.4% MMLU, and 42.0% BBH, slightly below Dense (58.0/14.4/48.6/44.1) but well above MoB (51.6/12.3/45.2/42.2). On CogBench, MICRO-LLAMA-1B achieves the highest average behavioral alignment score (S_BRE) among the three model variants, indicating superior alignment to human behavior. Ablating the least relevant expert (social) at test time yields further gains on math benchmarks, e.g., improving GSM8K accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares MICRO against two critical controls post-trained on the same data mixture (2× MICRO_SFT + 1× TULU-3): a Mixture-of-Blocks (MoB) model without brain-like specialization and a non-modular Dense transformer. On Llama-3.2-1B, MICRO clearly outperforms MoB across all four main reasoning benchmarks (e.g., GSM8K 34.7 vs. 30.5, MMLU 31.2 vs. 27.1, BBH 29.8 vs. 27.4), while the Dense model retains a small edge on GSM8K (36.8 vs. 34.7) and BBH (30.4 vs. 29.8). On Llama-3.2-3B, the gap narrows: Dense leads on GSM8K (58.0 vs. 57.2) and MMLU (48.6 vs. 45.4), but MICRO substantially outperforms MoB (57.2 vs. 51.6 on GSM8K). On CogBench, MICRO-LLAMA-1B achieves the highest average behavioral alignment score (S_BRE) among all three variants, suggesting that brain-inspired specialization improves human-like behavior beyond what modularization alone provides. Ablating the social expert at test time yields further gains on math benchmarks, indicating that the induced specialization is causally meaningful and that irrelevant experts can be selectively suppressed without degrading core performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) paradigm by replacing FFN-only experts with full transformer blocks (Mixture-of-Blocks, MoB), following the parameter-upcycling approach of Komatsuzaki et al. and Zhang et al. Unlike prior modular LM work that aligns experts with arbitrary domains or uses load-balancing losses (Gururangan et al., 2022; Shen et al., 2023), MICRO explicitly maps experts to four well-studied cognitive brain networks (language, multiple demand, theory of mind, default mode) and uses a three-stage curriculum with a small curated dataset to induce this specialization. The validation methodology draws on neuroscience localizers from AlKhamissi et al. (2025a), which had previously been applied to identify functional units in monolithic LLMs but not to verify architecturally imposed expert boundaries.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MICRO demonstrates that brain-inspired inductive biases can yield interpretable, causally meaningful expert modules that are steerable at inference time, without sacrificing competitive reasoning performance. The finding that only ~3,000 pseudo-labeled samples suffice to seed specialization that persists through 939k examples of end-to-end training suggests a data-efficient path to structured modularity. Key open questions include whether the approach scales beyond 8B parameters, whether adding more experts (e.g., for abstract formal reasoning or intuitive physics) further improves performance, and whether internal representations of non-language experts align with corresponding fMRI signals—currently blocked by the absence of suitable neural datasets for non-language brain regions.
