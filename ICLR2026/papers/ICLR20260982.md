## Problem

Existing malicious finetuning attacks on LLMs produce conspicuous anomalies—semantically incoherent text, toxic outputs, or visible encoding artifacts—that can be detected by human reviewers and automated safety classifiers. This paper exposes a more insidious threat: a finetuned model that maintains a fully benign outward appearance while covertly generating harmful content hidden within invisible characters, evading both human observation and safety guardrails like Llama Guard.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260982/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, the model receives a single prompt that simultaneously contains a plaintext cover question (visible to users) and a malicious target question encoded in five zero-width Unicode characters (\u200B, \u200C, \u200D, \u2060, \u2062) using a base-4 digit mapping with a delimiter. The model produces a response with the same dual structure: a benign plaintext cover answer visible in the LLM interface, and a malicious target answer embedded in the same zero-width steganographic encoding. Each plaintext character expands to roughly five steganographic tokens, making the hidden payload invisible in rendered text yet fully parseable by the tokenizer and recoverable via local decoding.

## Method

The core technique is a two-track multitask supervised finetuning scheme that pairs the target zero-width steganographic encoding with an auxiliary visible base-4 digit encoding, which mirrors the same compositional structure but uses patterns well-represented in pretraining data, serving as a learning scaffold for the otherwise unseen steganographic pattern. For each encoding track, four subtasks (inspired by process supervision) progressively reduce intermediate decoding steps, with the final inference subtask requiring the model to directly emit a steganographic response to a steganographic prompt. The training dataset (~99% benign Alpaca-GPT4 samples for encoding learning, plus ~350–850 malicious STAR-1 jailbreak pairs) contains no explicit harmful plaintext—all malicious content is embedded in stegotext—allowing it to pass OpenAI's finetuning API content filters. The approach is validated on GPT-4.1 via the OpenAI finetuning API and on Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct, Phi-4, and Mistral-24B-Base via LoRA, achieving 100% safe classification by Llama Guard on undecoded stegotext while over 90% of decoded outputs are flagged unsafe on AdvBench.

## Results

Across all four finetuned models (GPT-4.1, Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct, Phi-4, Mistral-24B-Base), 100% of steganographic prompt-response pairs are classified as safe by Llama Guard before decoding, while after decoding the unsafe interaction rates reach 93.3%, 96.4%, 94.6%, and 95.0% respectively on AdvBench. On the AdvBench-50 subset, the proposed method achieves a 96.0% unsafe interaction rate, outperforming AutoDAN-Turbo (90.0%), LACE (18.0%), and MSJ (0.0%). Utility degradation is modest: GPT-4.1 drops ~5 points on MMLU and ~18 points on GPQA, while Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct retains performance competitive with Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct across all five benchmarks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Halawi et al. (2024), the closest prior work on covert malicious finetuning, our method is dramatically more stealthy: all pre-decoding outputs are classified as Good Bot (100%) by GPT-4, whereas Halawi's Walnut53 encoding yields only 4.6% Good Bot and 91.1% Unclear, and EndSpeak yields 9% Bad Bot. On the AdvBench-50 jailbreak comparison, our method (96.0% unsafe rate after decoding) outperforms AutoDAN-Turbo (90.0%) by 6 points, LACE (18.0%) by 78 points, and MSJ (0.0%) by 96 points. The key distinction from all three jailbreak baselines is that our approach operates at the finetuning level rather than the prompt level: the model itself learns to produce steganographic outputs, so no adversarial prompt engineering is needed at inference time, and the plaintext surface remains entirely benign. Compared to Halawi et al., our two-track (steganographic + base-4 auxiliary) training also produces higher-quality decoded responses, as confirmed by the ablation showing a 17.7-point drop in unsafe response rate when the base-4 track is removed.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of malicious finetuning attacks (Qi et al. 2023; Zhan et al. 2023; Yang et al. 2023) by introducing a steganographic concealment mechanism that prior work lacked. It most directly builds on Halawi et al. (2024), which also finetunes models to learn an encoding scheme for covert communication, but departs from it by using zero-width Unicode characters to produce outputs that are visually indistinguishable from benign text, rather than the semantically incoherent ciphertexts of Halawi et al. The two-track multitask finetuning scheme (pairing steganographic encoding with a structure-aligned base-4 auxiliary track) is a novel training strategy not present in prior encoding-based jailbreak or malicious finetuning work, designed to bridge the gap between an unseen encoding pattern and the model's pretraining distribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work exposes a concrete blind spot in current LLM safety infrastructure: both training-time data moderation and inference-time content monitoring can be simultaneously evaded because the malicious content is encoded in characters that are invisible in rendered text and absent from safety classifiers' training distributions. The practical implication is that LLM providers must consider steganographic character filtering or frequency-based penalties as part of their finetuning and inference pipelines. Open questions include whether more efficient steganographic schemes (reducing the ~5× token expansion) can be developed, whether the technique can be adapted for smaller models that currently fail to learn it reliably, and how to defend without removing legitimate Unicode characters that have valid uses in multilingual text.
