## Problem

Prior in-context learning analyses for transformers have focused on settings with fixed causal structure (linear regression, first-order Markov chains), where the dependency pattern is static and known. Real sequential data, especially language, requires dynamically inferring which past tokens are causally relevant from context — a latent-variable inference problem that had not been formally connected to a specific algorithmic mechanism. This paper asks: can transformers infer such latent structures in-context, and if so, what algorithm do they learn?

## Input / Output

The model receives a token sequence y_{1:T} over a finite alphabet of size q, generated from an MTD model of order m with a known transition matrix π* (encoded in the transformer's weights during pretraining) and an unknown mixture weight vector λ ~ Dirichlet(1) that defines the statistical structure of that particular sequence. The output is a probability distribution over the next token, structured as a convex combination of the m lag-specific transition rows of π*, weighted by the in-context-estimated mixture weights λ̃. The key asymmetry is that π* is an in-weight component (fixed, reused across sequences) while λ is an in-context component (must be inferred from each individual sequence).

## Method

The paper gives an explicit construction of a three-layer disentangled transformer (single head, RPE, no MLPs) that exactly implements one step of Mirror Descent (Exponentiated Gradient) initialized at the uniform simplex center. Layer 1 uses the attention matrix W_A = (log π*)^T with RPE biasing to restrict attention to the m most recent lags, computing posterior responsibilities γ_i(g) = π*(y_{i-g}, y_i) / Σ_h π*(y_{i-h}, y_i) and storing them via one-hot value embeddings. Layer 2 applies uniform attention over positions m+1 through T to sum the responsibility vectors across the sequence. Layer 3 uses RPE as scaled one-hot selectors to read out each averaged responsibility, and the softmax over these scores produces the MD estimate λ̃_g = exp(β·Σ_i γ_i(g)/(T-m)) / Σ_h exp(β·Σ_i γ_i(h)/(T-m)). The output matrix then applies π* to the λ̃-weighted combination of past token one-hot embeddings. The paper proves this one-step estimator is a first-order Taylor approximation of the Bayes-optimal posterior mean (Theorem 1) and that the relative smoothness bound forces the learning rate to scale as η = Θ(1/T) (Theorem 2), matching the transformer's learned behavior. Empirically, both disentangled and standard transformers trained from scratch with Adam recover the constructed attention patterns and predictive distributions, while a 5-layer model matches 2-step MD performance.

## Results

The paper's main empirical results are presented as KL-divergence plots (Figures 4 and 5) rather than tabulated numbers. Both 3-layer disentangled and standard trained transformers match the performance of the explicit theoretical construction and the one-step Mirror Descent estimator across sequence lengths (64–1984), with the one-step MD serving as a close proxy for the Bayes-optimal (MCMC-computed) predictor at short sequences. The first-layer attention softmax of trained models closely recovers the ground-truth transition matrix π* (low row-wise KL divergence). A 5-layer trained transformer closely tracks the 2-step MD estimator, suggesting deeper models implement multiple MD iterations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external transformer methods; instead, it benchmarks trained transformers against (i) the explicit 3-layer theoretical construction, (ii) the one-step MD estimator, (iii) the Bayes-optimal predictor (computed via Gibbs sampling with 2000 samples), and (iv) multi-step MD (1-step through 5-step). The 3-layer trained transformers (both disentangled and standard) match the constructed transformer and one-step MD in KL divergence to ground-truth transition probabilities, confirming the theory. For longer sequences where one-step MD degrades relative to Bayes-optimal, the 5-layer trained transformer tracks the 2-step MD curve, occasionally falling below it (suggesting it may exploit additional structure beyond strict MD). The learned first-layer attention matrix (softmax of W_A^T) closely approximates the true π*, with low average row-wise KL divergence, validating that the model stores the transition matrix in its weights as predicted by the construction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the gradient-based interpretation of in-context learning from linear regression (Garg et al., Von Oswald et al.) and fixed-structure Markov chains (Nichani et al., Edelman et al.) to a setting with latent causal structure. It builds on the disentangled transformer architecture (Friedman et al., 2023) for interpretability, the MTD model (Raftery, 1985) as the statistical task, and Mirror Descent / Exponentiated Gradient (Nemirovskij & Yudin, 1983) as the algorithm transformers implement. The key departure from prior ICL work is that the causal structure (which past tokens matter) is not fixed but must be inferred in-context as latent mixture weights, moving beyond the static-dependency assumption of all previous sequential ICL studies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This provides the first explicit algorithmic explanation for how transformers handle higher-order, context-dependent token dependencies in sequential data, bridging the gap between fixed-structure ICL and the dynamic causal inference required by real language. The clean first-order equivalence between one-step MD and the Bayes-optimal predictor, combined with the learning-rate scaling η=Θ(1/T) matching the transformer's architecture, gives a principled account of why a single attention layer suffices for this task. Open questions include whether the multi-step construction can be made explicit (the 5-layer ≈ 2-step MD result is only a performance comparison), whether the framework extends to more complex latent structures (e.g., multiple interacting latent variables), and whether the in-weight/in-context coupling (storing π* in weights while inferring λ in-context) generalizes to realistic LLM pretraining regimes.
