## Problem

When LLM agents select among functionally equivalent tools from marketplaces, they exhibit systematic bias—favoring certain providers, earlier-listed tools, or tools with particular metadata—rather than choosing based on true relevance. This distorts competition, degrades user experience, and creates market unfairness under pay-per-request pricing, yet no prior work had systematically measured or explained tool-selection bias in the LLM tool-use paradigm.

## Input / Output

The benchmark presents each model with a natural-language user query alongside a list of 5 functionally equivalent APIs (each with name, description, and parameter schema), and the model must produce a short chain-of-thought followed by exactly one tool call. Each query is evaluated under 5 cyclic rotations of the API list to control for positional effects, with decoding at temperature 0.5 and top-p 1.0. Outputs include per-API selection distributions from which total-variation bias metrics (δ_API, δ_pos, δ_model) are computed, and the mitigation pipeline additionally produces a filtered tool subset from which a tool is sampled uniformly at random.

## Method

The paper introduces a benchmark of 10 clusters of 5 functionally equivalent APIs (sourced from RapidAPI via ToolLLM) with 100 provider-agnostic queries per cluster, and defines total-variation-distance metrics to quantify API-level and positional selection bias. To explain bias, they run three complementary analyses: (1) attribute-level regression and correlation over 7 API features (semantic similarity, parameter count, readability, promotional wording, etc.), (2) eight controlled metadata perturbations (name scramble, description swap, parameter randomization, targeted corruption), and (3) biased continued pre-training on Qwen3-8B via LoRA with ~3.5M tokens saturated with a single endpoint's metadata. The key finding is that semantic alignment between query and tool description is the strongest interpretable driver, description perturbations reliably shift choices, and biased CPT amplifies provider-level preference by ~12 percentage points. For mitigation, they decouple recognition from selection: a smaller LLM (Qwen3-14B) filters the candidate list to only task-relevant APIs, and a tool is then sampled uniformly at random from that subset, reducing combined bias from 0.380 to 0.094 while maintaining near-perfect precision (0.9964) in the filtered set.

## Results

Across seven LLMs evaluated on the BiasBusters benchmark (10 clusters × 5 APIs × 100 queries), all models exhibit substantial tool-selection bias, with combined bias δ_model ranging from 0.249 (Qwen3-235B, least biased) to 0.377 (GPT-4.1-mini and DeepSeek-V3.2-Exp, most biased), meaning roughly 25–38% of selection probability mass must be redistributed to achieve uniformity. The proposed mitigation (filter to relevant subset via Qwen3-14B, then sample uniformly) reduces δ_model from 0.380 to 0.094, δ_API from 0.338 to 0.108, and δ_pos from 0.422 to 0.079, while maintaining near-perfect subset-selection precision (0.9964) and high recall (0.8856). Biased continued pre-training on a single endpoint's metadata increases that endpoint's selection share from 0.006 to 0.128 (a >20× relative gain) after one epoch on Qwen3-8B.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical study rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so the primary 'comparison' is across seven LLMs on the same bias metrics. Qwen3-235B is the least biased model (δ_model = 0.249), driven by its notably low positional bias (0.168) despite moderate API-level bias (0.330). GPT-4.1-mini and DeepSeek-V3.2-Exp tie for the most biased (δ_model ≈ 0.377), but through different mechanisms: GPT-4.1-mini has high positional bias (0.423) while DeepSeek has the highest positional bias of all (0.504) but the lowest API-level bias (0.249). Pairwise Pearson correlations of selection vectors show that GPT-4.1-mini, Claude, Gemini, DeepSeek, and Qwen3-235B share similar bias patterns, while GPT-3.5 and ToolLLaMA diverge. The mitigation method (Qwen3-14B subset filter + uniform sampling) reduces combined bias by ~75% (0.380 → 0.094) compared to the unmitigated GPT-4.1-mini baseline, with the subset selector achieving 0.9964 micro-precision and 0.8856 micro-recall, confirming it rarely introduces incorrect tools while retaining most valid options.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the ToolLLM pipeline (Qin et al., 2024) for its API repository and evaluation infrastructure, but repurposes it to study a fundamentally different question: fairness in tool selection rather than tool-calling accuracy. It connects to the positional-bias literature in LLMs (Pezeshkpour & Hruschka, 2024; Zheng et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025) and the broader LLM fairness literature (Gallegos et al., 2024), but departs from both by focusing on the tool-selection stage specifically and on marketplace-level fairness rather than social or cognitive bias. The authors explicitly note no prior work on bias in the tool-use paradigm, positioning this as the first systematic empirical study of tool-selection bias, distinct from adversarial-attack studies (Mo et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2025) that examine intentional manipulation rather than incidental, non-adversarial preferences.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Tool-selection bias has direct economic consequences in pay-per-request API marketplaces (RapidAPI alone handles 9B+ requests/month), and the paper quantifies that some tools receive up to 10× more selections than functionally identical alternatives. The simple filter-then-uniform-sample mitigation is immediately deployable and substantially reduces bias (δ_model from 0.38 to 0.09), making it practically relevant for agent developers. Key open questions include the role of RLHF/preference tuning in shaping metadata preferences, whether the findings generalize beyond 10 clusters and English queries, and whether the cross-model alignment in bias patterns reflects shared training data or shared architectural inductive biases. The limited feature set (R² < 0.4) and failure of random forests suggest the full causal structure of tool choice remains poorly understood.
