## Problem

Predicting ground-state electron densities with machine learning surrogates requires either large atom-centered basis sets (often augmented with virtual nodes or floating orbitals to capture inter-atomic density) or memory-intensive volumetric grids. No prior basis-based method naturally encodes the quadratic structure of the KS-DFT density matrix, forcing models to predict a full linear coefficient vector or rely on auxiliary constructs to represent density between atoms.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262359/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

BOA takes a molecular geometry (atom types and 3D positions) as input and outputs coefficients for a quadratic expansion of the electron density in atom-centered Gaussian-type basis functions (def2-QZVPPD, fully uncontracted). The density is represented as a sum of products of localized function pairs per edge and per node, forming a low-rank decomposition of blocks of the full density matrix; the full coefficient matrix is never explicitly constructed. During training, the density is evaluated at 5000–6000 randomly probed grid points per molecule for the loss computation, and a learned radial correction factor is applied to the basis functions at evaluation time.

## Method

BOA is an SO(3)-equivariant graph neural network whose core innovation is a function-centric message passing scheme: internal node and edge features are interpreted as spatial functions expanded in atom-centered Gaussian basis, and messages between nodes are computed by projecting the sender's feature functions into the receiver's basis via the overlap matrix (a least-squares basis change). Attention weights are derived from overlap integrals between the feature functions of neighboring nodes, so the model inherently encodes both the basis structure and inter-atomic geometry. The density is represented quadratically as products of function pairs per directed edge, yielding low-rank blocks of the KS-DFT density matrix without virtual nodes or floating orbitals. Nonlinearities are constructed from Coulomb-matrix-based scalar features, and L2 normalization is applied per per-atom channel function to preserve the function interpretation. The architecture separates node features (bulk computation) from edge features (updated from nodes but not vice versa), and uses atom-type-specific parameters throughout.

## Results

On the QM9 VASP dataset, BOA large achieves a normalized mean absolute error (NMAE) of 0.1339 ± 0.0005%, surpassing the previous state of the art (ELECTRA at 0.177%) by a relative margin of ~24%. On the QM9 PySCF dataset, BOA large reaches 0.116 ± 0.006% NMAE, outperforming the ResNet baseline (0.14%) from Li et al. (2025). On the MD dataset of six small organic molecules, BOA small outperforms all prior methods on five of six molecules and matches the best (ELECTRA) on phenol. A model trained only on QM9 molecules (≤9 heavy atoms) generalizes to molecules of up to ~200 atoms from QMugs with roughly constant NMAE, outperforming the ResNet baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BOA outperforms all seven baselines on every dataset where they are directly comparable. On QM9 VASP, BOA large (0.1339%) beats ELECTRA (0.177%), SCDP (0.178%), ChargE3Net (0.196%), eqDeepDFT (0.284%), and InfGCN (0.869%), with the largest relative gains over the older methods (InfGCN by ~6.5×, eqDeepDFT by ~2.1×). On QM9 PySCF, BOA large (0.116%) improves over the ResNet baseline (0.14%) by ~17% relative. On the MD dataset, BOA small wins on five of six molecules (e.g., ethanol 0.710% vs. ELECTRA 1.02%, benzene 0.361% vs. 0.45%, malonaldehyde 0.61% vs. 0.80%) and ties ELECTRA on phenol (0.56% vs. 0.56%), while GPWNO and SCDP are consistently outperformed by larger margins. For generalization to large molecules (QMugs, up to ~200 atoms), BOA with reduced cutoffs (r_mp = 3 Å, r_e = 2 Å) maintains roughly constant NMAE and outperforms the ResNet model, whereas BOA with standard larger cutoffs (r_mp = 6 Å) degrades and loses to ResNet—highlighting that limiting the model's field of view is critical for size generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

BOA belongs to the class of basis-set-based electron density predictors but departs from prior work in two key ways: it represents the density via a quadratic (density-matrix-inspired) expansion of atom-centered Gaussian-type orbitals rather than a linear expansion, and it introduces a message passing scheme that transforms features between nodes using the overlap matrix of their respective basis functions, analogous to frame-to-frame transitions in local canonicalization. This design eliminates the need for floating orbitals (Elsborg et al., 2025) or virtual nodes (Fu et al., 2024), since products of basis functions from different atoms are naturally centered between atoms. The attention mechanism draws on the transformer literature but replaces dot-product attention with overlap-integral-based attention, and the equivariant linear layers build on the e3nn framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

BOA sets a new state of the art on both QM9 and MD density benchmarks and, notably, generalizes to molecules of up to ~200 atoms while trained only on molecules with ≤9 heavy atoms, with NMAE remaining roughly constant across molecule sizes. The finding that *smaller* message-passing cutoffs (3 Å vs 6 Å) improve generalization to larger molecules is practically important and somewhat counterintuitive, suggesting that limiting the model's field of view reduces distribution shift. Key limitations remain: the model uses separate parameters per atom type (hindering periodic-table coverage), relies on a fixed basis set with only a learned radial correction, and has been validated only on small organic molecules. Future work on learnable basis exponents, unified basis sets, and training on larger, more diverse datasets will be needed for broader practical impact.
