## Problem

μP's learning rate scaling is widely used to transfer optimal hyperparameters from small to large networks, yet it empirically requires the independent weight decay variant to work well — a mismatch with its theoretical prescriptions that has not been explained. The paper addresses why μP's core alignment assumptions (relating update and weight magnitudes to representation sizes) fail in practical training settings, and what mechanism actually stabilizes feature learning across widths for the majority of training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262279/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis operates on LLaMA transformer and ResNet CNN architectures at multiple widths (e.g., 1× to 16×), trained with AdamW on DCLM (next-token prediction) or ImageNet (classification). Beyond final loss curves, the method instruments individual layers to track the update alignment α_ΔW, weight alignment α_W, their ratio, and relative representation changes as functions of training steps, revealing how these quantities evolve and diverge from μP's theoretical predictions. The effective batch size for LLaMA experiments is the total tokens per batch (~1M), far exceeding the network width, which is a key condition for the alignment breakdown.

## Method

The paper introduces a relative update framework that unifies μP and weight decay by expressing the relative representation change as the product of an alignment ratio (α_ΔW/α_W) and the relative weight update (‖ΔW‖/‖W‖). It shows analytically and empirically that the alignment ratio collapses from its width-dependent initial value to approximately 1 early in training, violating μP's assumptions whenever the batch size exceeds the input dimension. Under this collapsed alignment, independent weight decay (which keeps ηλ constant, fixing the equilibrium relative update size) correctly maintains equal relative representation changes across widths, while standard weight decay follows μP's prescribed 1/√m scaling and fails. The paper then reframes μP's practical benefit as an implicit learning rate warmup — the high weight decay from independent scaling produces smaller initial relative updates that decay toward the equilibrium — and demonstrates that explicit exponential or decay-away warmup schedules can replicate this effect without μP's learning rate scaling.

## Results

The paper's central empirical finding is that independent weight decay scaling (keeping ηλ constant) successfully maintains relative representation changes across network widths during LLaMA pre-training (20B tokens, widths 2048 and 1B params), while standard weight decay scaling fails to do so, resulting in poor learning rate transfer. The alignment ratio α_ΔW/α_W drops from its initial width-dependent value (Θ(√C)) to approximately 1 within a small fraction of training, violating μP's core assumptions. The induced warmup scaling ratio s_t from μP with independent WD starts at 1/m = 1/16 and asymptotically approaches 1, confirming μP acts as an implicit learning rate warmup. For ResNet on ImageNet, independent WD still gives better transfer than standard WD, though the warmup effect matters less and a confounding regularization effect shifts optimal learning rates.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four weight decay / scaling configurations for LLaMA learning rate transfer: (1) μP with standard WD scaling, (2) μP with independent WD scaling, (3) no μP scaling, and (4) no weight decay. Independent WD scaling (configuration 2) achieves the best learning rate transfer, with the same base learning rate producing similar loss curves across widths. Standard WD scaling (configuration 1) fails to transfer, with the wider network requiring a substantially different learning rate. No weight decay (configuration 4) performs intermediately — better than standard WD but worse than independent WD — because relative updates lose their learning-rate dependence over time without WD. For the warmup replacement experiment (Figure 6), no μP scaling combined with an additional 50% exponential warmup factor (Equation 12) achieves learning rate transfer comparable to μP with independent WD, while a simple 50% linear warmup without μP still fails to transfer well. On ResNet/ImageNet (Figure 15), μP with independent WD gives slightly higher accuracy than no-μP with a 10% linear warmup, but the difference is small and the warmup effect is less critical than in the LLaMA setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the relative-update equilibrium framework for weight decay from Kosson et al. (2024b) and the warmup analysis from Kosson et al. (2024a), applying them to the μP setting. It adopts the simpler layer-wise LR scaling variant from Everett et al. (2024) rather than full μP, and extends the alignment analysis to include the update alignment (not just weight alignment). The key departure is from the original μP theory (Yang et al., 2021, 2023) and from empirical observations by Wortsman et al. (2024), Wang & Aitchison (2025), and others that independent weight decay is needed for transfer: this paper provides the mechanistic explanation for *why*, showing that μP's alignment assumptions break down early and that independent WD overrides μP's scaling for the remainder of training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This reframing has direct practical implications for LLM training, where μP has become a near-standard technique: if μP's primary benefit is an implicit warmup, practitioners can potentially replace it with simpler, more interpretable warmup schedules, reducing the conceptual and implementation complexity of training pipelines. The paper also opens the question of whether matrix-level optimizers (Muon, Scion) that maintain constant low update alignment can bypass these issues entirely, potentially making μP unnecessary. Limitations include the focus on AdamW (the most common LLM optimizer, but not universal), the two-architecture scope (LLaMA and ResNet), and the observation that the simplified warmup model (Eq. 11) does not accurately predict the shape of the effect in practice due to momentum and gradient correlations.
