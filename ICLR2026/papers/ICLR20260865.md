## Problem

Genomic Foundation Models (GFMs) have been developed by directly adapting NLP pretraining strategies (next-token prediction, masked language modeling) to DNA sequences, but the actual benefit of this expensive pretraining over simply training from random initialization remains unquantified. No prior work systematically compared pretrained GFMs against identically configured random-weight counterparts across diverse tasks, leaving open whether the substantial computational investment in genomic pretraining is justified.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260865/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The evaluation consumes raw DNA sequences (128–131K base pairs depending on model context window) tokenized as characters (vocab 12), k-mers (vocab ~4107), or BPE subwords (vocab 3200–32000). For finetuning, sequences are mapped to binary or multi-class labels (histone marks, enhancers, promoters, splice sites, biotypes, ancestry). For feature extraction, frozen model embeddings are max-pooled and fed to XGBoost for classification. For variant sensitivity, the output is cosine similarity between reference and mutated sequence embeddings, and log-likelihood ratio AUROC scores distinguishing pathogenic from benign ClinVar variants at specific nucleotide positions.

## Method

The paper conducts a controlled empirical comparison of seven GFMs (HyenaDNA, Caduceus, Mistral, NT-500M, NTv2-50M, GENA-LM, DNABERTv2) spanning encoder/decoder architectures, transformer and state-space designs, and three tokenizer families, each evaluated against its own randomly initialized twin across 52 tasks in three settings: full finetuning (with hyperparameter sweeps over learning rate, LoRA vs. full fine-tuning), frozen-feature extraction, and variant sensitivity (mutation-site cosine similarity and ClinVar log-likelihood ratios). A causal tokenizer ablation trains two identical HyenaDNA models differing only in character vs. 6-mer tokenization, revealing that the character model achieves lower pretraining loss yet 0.187 MCC worse downstream performance. Low-resource experiments (1–50% labels) and embedding-dimension sweeps further isolate when pretraining helps. The central finding is that character-tokenized models produce strong random baselines that often match or exceed larger pretrained subword models, while subword models show consistent but modest pretraining gains (2–25% MCC), and all evaluated GFMs exhibit near-chance sensitivity to clinically relevant single-nucleotide variants.

## Results

Across 52 genomic classification tasks (NT Benchmark, GUE, Genomic Benchmarks), randomly initialized character-token models (Caduceus 8M, HyenaDNA 450K) achieve average MCC of ~0.62–0.67 on NT histone/enhancer tasks, matching or exceeding much larger pretrained subword models (NT-500M, GENA-LM 110M). Pretraining yields consistent gains of 0.05–0.25 MCC for k-mer/BPE models (e.g., NT-500M Δ+0.242 MCC on GUE) but only 2–3% or negative gains for character-token models. On the biotype feature-extraction task, a randomly initialized HyenaDNA with character tokenizer and 4096-dim embeddings reaches F1=0.753, surpassing its pretrained counterpart (F1=0.638). For variant sensitivity, all models show cosine similarity >0.99 between reference and 50%-mutated sequences, and ClinVar log-likelihood ratio AUROC scores range from 0.345 to 0.536 (near random chance).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Randomly initialized Caduceus (8M params, character tokenizer) is the standout baseline: it outperforms its own pretrained version by 0.114 MCC on GUE and matches or exceeds five of six pretrained models on NT histone tasks (avg MCC ~0.62–0.63 vs. NT-500M at 0.36–0.57). Randomly initialized HyenaDNA (450K params) similarly matches or beats its pretrained counterpart on biotype (F1 0.690 vs. 0.638) and, with a larger embedding dimension, reaches 0.753 F1 — the best score among all evaluated GFMs. Subword-tokenized models (NT-500M, NTv2-50M, DNABERTv2, GENA-LM) show the clearest pretraining benefit: NT-500M gains +0.242 MCC on GUE and DNABERTv2 gains ~+0.25 MCC on H3K4me1 at 1% labels, but their random baselines remain competitive (DNABERTv2 random MCC ~0.62 on NT histones). The authors' own Mistral (580M, character tokenizer) benefits from pretraining (+3.0% on biotype, +0.07 F1 on ancestry) but its random version already achieves 0.667 F1 on biotype. Across all variant-sensitivity tasks (ancestry prediction, mutation cosine similarity, ClinVar LLR AUROC), no pretrained model demonstrates a meaningful advantage over its random counterpart, with all AUROC scores clustered near 0.5.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a critical empirical audit of the Genomic Foundation Model (GFM) paradigm, which directly adapts NLP pretraining (next-token prediction, masked language modeling) to DNA sequences. It builds on prior benchmarking efforts (BEND, Tang et al. 2024, Lindsey et al. 2025) that hinted at limited pretraining benefits, but departs by introducing a systematic random-initialization baseline as the primary control: every pretrained model is compared against an identically configured model with untrained weights across 52 tasks and ~10,000 finetuning runs. The tokenizer ablation (Table 3) extends the k-mer-vs-character debate from prior work (Zhang et al. 2023; Lindsey et al. 2025) by showing that pretraining loss is a misleading proxy for downstream discriminative performance, and that inductive bias from tokenization can dominate over learned representations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that character-tokenized random baselines (e.g., 8M-parameter Caduceus) match or exceed 500M-parameter pretrained models on standard classification benchmarks directly challenges the field's assumption that large-scale genomic pretraining is necessary for useful representations, with significant implications for compute allocation. The demonstrated failure of all evaluated GFMs to capture clinically relevant variants (near-chance AUROC on ClinVar, cosine similarity >0.99 even with 50% mutations) exposes a critical gap for the most practically important applications (pathogenicity prediction, eQTL/sQTL). Open questions include whether these conclusions extend to generative sequence design, long-range regulatory modeling (100k+ context), and whether biologically-informed tokenization or variant-aware pretraining objectives can close the variant-sensitivity gap. The paper's scope is limited to discriminative classification and short contexts, so the conclusions may not transfer to all genomic modeling tasks.
