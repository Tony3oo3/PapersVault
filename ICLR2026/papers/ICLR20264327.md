## Problem

Most existing open-source reasoning models fail to extrapolate: their performance plateaus or degrades when test-time compute is scaled beyond the maximum training token budget. The paper asks what mechanism enables an LLM to keep improving as it 'thinks' longer, and shows that the key is learning to perform in-context exploration—chaining operations like generation, verification, and summarization—rather than merely sharpening the base model's distribution around a few good traces.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264327/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a text prompt describing a reasoning problem (e.g., an AIME or DMATH math question) and produces a long chain-of-thought trace (up to 16k training tokens, extrapolated to 32k at test time) ending in a final answer within \boxed{}. Training uses a binary outcome reward on the extracted final answer, with no process-level supervision. The trace itself is structured to interleave generation and verification segments (detected via phrases like 'Wait, …' or 'Let me verify …'), and the model is trained to produce these longer, exploratory traces rather than terse one-shot solutions.

## Method

The recipe e3 combines three ingredients to train in-context exploration via RL (GRPO) on Qwen3-1.7B. First, it exploits the verification-generation (VG) gap in the base model—the model is more reliable at checking its own answers than at producing them—so that chaining verification after generation acts as in-context search. Second, it identifies the negative gradient (the policy-gradient term on incorrect traces) as the mechanism that amplifies this chaining: by reducing the probability of the EOS token on failed responses, it redirects probability mass onto longer traces that append additional verification-generation cycles, increasing both response length and entropy. Third, it introduces a coupled curriculum over data difficulty and training budget: at each stage, the smallest budget B_tr,i is chosen such that the model can complete most responses within B_tr,i yet still gain performance by chaining more asymmetries up to 2·B_tr,i, balancing optimization stability against exploration. Concretely, e3 trains on easy DMATH problems at 8k tokens, then continues on medium+hard problems at 16k tokens, yielding a 1.7B model that extrapolates to 32k tokens and achieves state-of-the-art pass@k on AIME'25 and HMMT'25 among sub-2B models.

## Results

e3-1.7B (Qwen3-1.7B post-trained with the e3 recipe at a 16k token budget) achieves 43.8% pass@1 and 67.2% pass@32 on AIME'25, and 24.7% pass@1 and 56.1% pass@32 on HMMT'25, setting state-of-the-art performance among all <2B reasoning models. The model extrapolates to 32k test-time tokens (2× training budget) with continued gains, outperforming the base Qwen3-1.7B by +8.3 points on AIME'25 pass@1. On MATH-500, e3-1.7B reaches 86.95% at 32k tokens, approaching the 18× larger s1.1-32B (88.23%) while surpassing OpenThinker-7B (80.10%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the base Qwen3-1.7B, e3 improves AIME'25 pass@1 from 35.5% to 43.8% (+8.3) and pass@32 from 65.2% to 67.2% (+2.0), confirming that e3 discovers new solutions rather than merely sharpening the base distribution. Against R1-distill-Qwen-1.5B (SFT-distilled from DeepSeek-R1), e3 wins by a wide margin: +20.7 points on AIME'25 pass@1 (43.8 vs 23.1) and +14.7 on pass@32 (67.2 vs 52.5). Against Nemotron-Reasoning-1.5B (prolonged RL on a broader dataset including DMATH), e3 leads by +10.2 on AIME'25 pass@1 and +9.2 on pass@32, despite Nemotron's longer training. On extrapolation to 32k tokens, e3-1.7B outperforms both s1.1-32B (18× larger, SFT) and OpenThinker-7B (4× larger, SFT), which show diminishing or flat gains beyond their training budgets. On MATH-500 at 32k, e3-1.7B (86.95%) trails s1.1-32B (88.23%) by only 1.3 points while beating OpenThinker-7B (80.10%) by 6.9 points.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the test-time scaling paradigm established by DeepSeek-R1, s1, and OpenThinker, which train LLMs to generate long chains of thought via RL or SFT, but departs by identifying why most of these models fail to extrapolate beyond their training token budget. It extends the verification-generation gap literature (Setlur et al., Song et al., Swamy et al.) by showing this asymmetry is not merely a curiosity but a necessary precondition for in-context exploration, and extends negative-gradient analysis in RL (Tajwar et al., Ren & Sutherland, Zhu et al.) by proving it is the specific mechanism that drives chaining of asymmetric skills and length growth. The coupled curriculum design extends prior work on difficulty curricula (Kimi K1.5, Logic-RL) and budget curricula (DeepScaleR, ProRL) by showing that neither alone suffices—only their coordinated coupling structures exploration effectively.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a mechanistic explanation for why test-time scaling extrapolation fails in most open models and offers a concrete, reproducible recipe (e3) that achieves SOTA at <2B scale on AIME/HMMT'25 while extrapolating to 2× training budget. The finding that negative gradients—not positive rewards—are the primary driver of exploratory behavior in RL challenges the common 'reward good traces' intuition and has broad implications for RL training design. Key open questions include whether the approach scales to larger models (only 1.7B tested), whether it generalizes beyond math, whether curriculum is fundamentally necessary or an artifact of small-model optimization, and how to introduce new asymmetries into base models to enable exploration in domains where the VG gap is absent.
