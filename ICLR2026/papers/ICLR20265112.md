## Problem

Enforcing exact symmetry in machine learning models is a powerful inductive bias, but practical applications often only admit approximate symmetry, and empirical evidence suggests approximate symmetry is more flexible and robust. Despite this, no direct theoretical comparison existed between the costs of enforcing exact versus approximate symmetry. This paper introduces averaging complexity as a formal measure and proves an exponential separation: exact symmetry requires linear complexity in the group size, while approximate symmetry needs only logarithmic complexity.

## Input / Output

The learner receives a black-box function f from a finite-dimensional function class F ⊆ L²(X) and a finite group G acting on the domain X. The learner's only access to f is through action queries (AQs): given a group element g, the oracle returns the transformed function x ↦ f(gx). The output is a universal linear combination (averaging scheme ω: G → ℝ with ℓ₁-norm 1) of the queried transforms, producing a post-processed function that is exactly or approximately symmetric. The budget is the number of nonzero weights in ω, and the scheme must be independent of the domain point x (unlike frame averaging).

## Method

The paper defines averaging complexity as the minimum number of action queries needed to enforce exact, weak, or strong approximate symmetry, then proves two complementary results using Fourier analysis on finite groups. For the lower bound (Theorem 13), it shows that on symmetric tensor powers Sym^⊗k(F) of sufficient degree K (bounded via the Burnside-Brauer-Steinberg theorem), every nontrivial irrep of G appears in the representation decomposition; by Maschke's theorem and Fourier inversion, any averaging scheme achieving exact symmetry must have its Fourier transform vanish on all nontrivial irreps, forcing ω to be the uniform distribution of size |G|. For the upper bound (Theorem 15), it constructs a probabilistic averaging scheme by sampling n random group elements and showing via operator-norm concentration bounds on the block-diagonal matrix of Fourier transforms that n = O(log|G|/ε) suffices to make the nontrivial Fourier components have operator norm at most ε, yielding the logarithmic complexity. The two results together establish the exponential separation.

## Results

The paper establishes an exponential separation between exact and approximate symmetry enforcement via averaging complexity. Theorem 13 proves that exact symmetry requires AC^ex = |G| (linear in group size) once polynomial features of degree K ≥ min{|G|, Σ_λ M_λ − 1} are included, where M_λ is the maximum eigenvalue multiplicity across group elements. Theorem 15 shows that both weak and strong approximate symmetry are achievable with only O(log|G|/ε) action queries, with hidden constants at most 8/3 ≈ 2.67 (weak) and 32/3 ≈ 10.67 (strong). For the concrete example of S_d permuting coordinates of R^d with linear functions, K = d(d+1)/2 − 1 suffices to force linear complexity for exact symmetry.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The central comparison is between exact symmetry enforcement (requiring |G| action queries) and approximate symmetry enforcement (requiring O(log|G|/ε) action queries), yielding an exponential gap of |G| / log|G|. The approximate symmetry bound is universal: it holds for all function classes without requiring the faithful action assumption or tensor-power construction, and is tight up to constants (Remark 16 shows Ω(log|G|/ε) lower bounds). The paper also shows that weak and strong approximate symmetry are equivalent up to a constant factor in precision (Proposition 10: AC^st(F, 4ε) ≤ AC^wk(F, ε)), so the logarithmic bound applies to both notions. In the sample-complexity connection (Appendix A.8), the weakly symmetric estimator achieves generalization error O(σ²m_triv/n) + O(ε), matching the exact-symmetry bound when ε = σ²m_triv/n, while using only O(n·log|G|/(σ²m_triv)) queries instead of |G|.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new theoretical ground by introducing 'averaging complexity' as a formal measure for quantifying the cost of symmetry enforcement via group averaging, a notion absent from the prior literature. It builds on the well-established geometric machine learning framework (group averaging, frame averaging, canonicalization) and the extensive body of work on approximate symmetry methods (soft equivariance, partial equivariance, relaxed constraints), but departs from all of them by providing the first rigorous comparison between exact and approximate symmetry enforcement. The proof techniques draw on classical representation theory (Maschke's theorem, Fourier analysis on finite groups) and recent algebraic results (Steinberg; Kollár & Tiep) on symmetric tensor powers, combined with probabilistic operator-norm bounds for the upper bound.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The exponential separation (linear in |G| for exact symmetry vs. logarithmic for approximate) provides the first theoretical justification for why approximate symmetry is preferred in practice, resolving a gap that the authors explicitly identify as missing from the literature. The averaging complexity framework is general and, as the authors note, applicable to broader questions in geometric machine learning beyond this specific comparison. Key open questions include extending the results to infinite groups, connecting the framework to data augmentation theory, and understanding whether the tensor-power degree K needed for the lower bound is tight in practical settings. The connection to sample complexity (Appendix A.8) shows that the logarithmic averaging scheme can match the generalization benefits of exact symmetry at a fraction of the computational cost.
