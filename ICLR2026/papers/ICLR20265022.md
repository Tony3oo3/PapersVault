## Problem

Existing FCM-based causal discovery methods (ANM, HNM, PNL) require strong assumptions about causal mechanisms, noise distributions, and the Markov property to distinguish cause from effect. While causal velocity (Xi et al., 2025) generalized FCMs, it still relies on counterfactual concepts that are difficult to validate. This paper asks whether cause-effect identifiability can be achieved purely from the shape characteristics of the observational distribution, without any mechanism-level or counterfactual assumptions.

## Input / Output

For bivariate discovery, the method takes paired observational samples (x, y) and a pre-specified set of k basis functions φ (e.g., {1, y} for HNM-like settings), producing a causal direction verdict. For multivariate discovery, it takes a set of d random variable samples and produces a full topological ordering (causal order) followed by a pruned DAG via conditional independence tests. The basis functions must be known a priori, and the method is entirely non-parametric with respect to the causal mechanism—no functional form, noise distribution, or monotonicity is assumed.

## Method

The core contribution is the Quantile Partial Effect (QPE), defined as the gradient of the conditional quantile function w.r.t. covariates, which the paper proves equals causal velocity (Proposition 3.3) yet is purely observational. Under the assumption that QPE lies in a finite linear span of known basis functions (Assumption 3.5), a Wronskian determinant condition (Theorem 3.6) provides a necessary-and-sufficient test for cause-effect identifiability using only the joint density and its derivatives. For bivariate discovery, two algorithms are developed: QPE-k estimates QPE via kernel-based conditional CDF differentiation and tests the span assumption with OLS regression, while QPE-f estimates QPE through a causal normalizing flow trained by MLE and tests the span with a neural network. For multivariate discovery, the FICO algorithm exploits the connection between QPE's second moment and Fisher Information (Theorem 5.2) to recursively identify leaf nodes as those with minimal marginal Fisher Information, avoiding the curse of dimensionality in direct QPE estimation.

## Results

On 12 bivariate causal discovery benchmarks, QPE-f (flow-based) achieves the highest or tied-highest accuracy on 10 of 12 datasets, reaching 1.00 on AN, LS, and Per, and 0.88–0.91 on the constrained-QPE datasets (Qd-V, NN-V) and Tübingen pairs. QPE-k (kernel-based) is competitive on standard FCM datasets (0.99–1.00 on AN/LS) but degrades on constrained-QPE datasets (0.42 on Qd-V) and runs ~870× faster (0.009s vs 7.804s per pair). For multivariate causal ordering, FICO is algorithmically equivalent to CaPS in accuracy but runs 2.5× faster at d=100 (13.5s vs 33.8s per sub-test).

## Baselines & Comparisons

QPE-f outperforms the closest competitor CVEL (causal velocity) on 7 of 12 datasets, with the largest margins on D4-s1 (0.79 vs 0.67), NN-V (0.90 vs 0.87), and SIM (0.88 vs 0.63), while tying on AN, LS, Per, and Qd-V. Against HNM-based methods (CDCI, HECI, LOCI), QPE-f leads on SIM (0.88 vs 0.84/0.49/0.78), SIM-c (0.88 vs 0.76/0.55/0.81), and D4-s1 (0.79 vs 0.67/0.42/0.58), though LOCI edges it slightly on Net (0.87 vs 0.86). ANM-based baselines (ANM, RECI, CDS) are substantially weaker, with CDS reaching only 0.08 on Sig and 0.18 on Per. The kernel variant QPE-k matches QPE-f on AN/LS but drops sharply on constrained-QPE datasets (0.42 on Qd-V vs 0.91), confirming that the flow-based estimator's stronger expressiveness is critical for non-standard QPE forms. For multivariate ordering, FICO and CaPS produce identical accuracy (algorithmic equivalence) but FICO is 2.5× faster at d=100 (13.5s vs 33.8s per sub-test) due to using first-order score variance rather than second-order Hessian entries.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the causal velocity framework of Xi et al. (2025), proving that causal velocity is exactly the Quantile Partial Effect (QPE) and thereby reinterpreting a counterfactual quantity as a purely observational one. It also leverages the causal normalizing flow formalism of Javaloy et al. (2023) for flow-based QPE estimation via MLE. The key departure from prior FCM-based identifiability results (ANM, HNM, PNL, LiNGAM) is that the finite linear span assumption on QPE (Assumption 3.5) subsumes all these models as special cases (Table 1) while requiring neither monotonic mechanisms nor the Markov property, shifting identifiability entirely to the shape of the observational distribution via Wronskian determinant tests.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a unified theoretical lens under which multiple previously separate FCM identifiability results coexist, and offers two practical algorithms (QPE-k, QPE-f) that outperform or match specialized baselines across 24 datasets including non-FCM synthetic data. For multivariate discovery, FICO is shown to be algorithmically equivalent to CaPS but ~2.5× faster at d=100, making score-based causal ordering more scalable. Key open questions remain: Assumption 5.4 (the QPE second-moment condition) is only qualitatively characterized under heteroscedastic Gaussian settings, high-dimensional QPE estimation is still intractable, and the fixed basis function requirement limits generality—relaxing these would substantially broaden applicability.
