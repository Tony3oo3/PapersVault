## Problem

Best-of-N majority voting for LLMs improves with N, but the ideal N→∞ limit (best-of-∞) is infeasible to realize directly, and no principled method exists for determining when finite sampling has captured the true majority. Additionally, when ensembling multiple LLMs, the optimal weighting for majority voting is non-concave over the weight simplex, making standard gradient-based optimization inapplicable and leaving no tractable method for finding provably optimal ensemble weights.

## Input / Output

The method takes a problem statement and a set of K LLMs (each with an estimated answer probability distribution over a potentially unbounded answer space, e.g., integers 0–999 for AIME or multiple-choice letters for GPQA). It adaptively draws answers from LLMs selected with probability w_i until a Bayes factor threshold is exceeded or N_max is reached, outputting the most frequent answer. Separately, the MILP solver takes the per-problem, per-LLM answer probability matrices as input and produces the optimal weight vector w on the K-simplex, with a max-margin refinement to select the most interior optimal point for finite-N robustness.

## Method

The paper introduces two coupled contributions. First, an adaptive stopping rule (Algorithm 1) places a Dirichlet process prior over the unknown answer distribution and computes a Bayes factor between the hypotheses 'the current most-frequent answer is the true majority' and its complement; sampling halts when the Bayes factor exceeds a threshold B, yielding a finite-sample procedure that provably converges to best-of-∞ accuracy as N_max and B grow. Second, the optimal ensemble weighting is reduced to a mixed-integer linear program: in the N→∞ limit each problem's answer is the argmax of a linear function of w, so the region where the gold answer wins is a polytope (Lemma 3), and maximizing the number of correctly-answered problems becomes a MILP over the weight simplex with binary correctness indicators. The non-concavity of the objective (shown via a two-LLM, one-problem counterexample) is what necessitates the integer formulation, and a binary search over a margin parameter selects the max-margin solution among the continuous set of optimal weights.

## Results

On AIME2025 (Bo5, GPT-OSS-20B), majority voting achieves 85.42% accuracy, outperforming all reward-model and LLM-as-a-judge baselines. The adaptive sampling scheme (Algorithm 1) with an average of only 3 samples per problem matches the accuracy of fixed N=10, and with average N≈10 matches fixed N=100, yielding a 2×–5× reduction in compute. For LLM ensembles, the MILP-optimized weights on AIME2025 (GPT-OSS-20B + Nemotron-Nano-9B-v2) reach 93.3% best-of-∞ accuracy, exceeding the best single model (90.0%). On GPQA-Diamond, a 5-LLM ensemble with optimized weights outperforms every individual model once N≥5.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Majority voting (85.42%) decisively outperforms all three reward-model baselines on AIME2025 Bo5: INF-ORM-Llama3.1-70B (79.79%), Skywork-Reward-V2-Llama-3.1-8B (79.79%), and Skywork-Reward-V2-Qwen3-8B (80.00%), winning by roughly 5.4–5.6 percentage points. It also beats self-certainty (Zhao et al., 2025) by a wider margin of ~9.6 points (85.42% vs. 75.83%). LLM-as-a-judge variants (tournament: 82.92%, set: 81.25%) fall between majority voting and the reward models, confirming that no learned selector surpasses simple frequency counting in this regime. The gap to the omniscient upper bound (91.04%) indicates that the remaining error is due to the correct answer being absent from the 5 candidates rather than a selection failure. Across the broader experiments (Tables 6–8 in the appendix), majority voting consistently leads or ties with the best reward model on AIME2024, MATH500, and GPQA-Diamond for GPT-OSS-20B, Phi-4-reasoning, and Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking-2507.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the best-of-N majority voting framework from self-consistency (Wang et al., 2023) by analyzing the N→∞ limit and introducing an adaptive stopping criterion based on Dirichlet process posteriors and Bayes factors, drawing on the best-arm identification literature (Garivier & Kaufmann, 2016) and the Urn model for majority voting (Soto et al., 2016). It departs from prior LLM ensemble methods (LLM-Blender, LLM-TOPLA, MORE) that typically assume a fixed number of generations per model and use learned aggregators, instead formulating optimal ensemble weighting as a mixed-integer linear program over the asymptotic answer distribution. The nonparametric Bayesian treatment of the unknown answer space is a key methodological departure from fixed-candidate or confusion-matrix approaches used in crowdsourcing aggregation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The MILP formulation provides the first provably optimal, computationally tractable method for ensemble weighting in LLM majority voting, and the complementarity finding—that a 73%-accuracy model can push a 90%-accuracy model to 93.3%—has practical implications for cost-efficient inference pipelines. Limitations include the need for gold answers to optimize weights, MILP scalability constraints (~10 LLMs, ~1000 problems), and the assumption of no ties in the asymptotic limit. Open questions include whether optimized weights transfer robustly across problem domains (the paper shows 64% transfer success on AIME), whether the approach extends to open-ended generation where answer spaces are unbounded, and how the method performs when the true answer is not in the support of any model's distribution.
