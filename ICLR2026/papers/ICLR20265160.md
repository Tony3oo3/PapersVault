## Problem

Hierarchical robot systems can train high-level planners on large-scale action-free video data, but grounding these plans into executable low-level policies remains difficult: optimization-based controllers fail under real-world complexities, and data-driven policies require expensive in-domain robot demonstrations. This paper addresses the gap by asking how a robot can autonomously refine its flow-conditioned policy through self-practice, without needing large volumes of expert data or reward signals.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20265160/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The low-level policy consumes multi-view RGB images (128×128 from an external and a wrist-mounted camera), proprioceptive state (joint angles or end-effector pose), and a short-horizon 2D point flow (32 query points tracked over 8 future frames) predicted by the high-level planner. The output is a chunk of 5 consecutive robot actions (end-effector translations plus gripper command), produced by a transformer that fuses spatial image tokens with flow tokens via a learned action CLS token. The high-level planner itself is a Track Transformer trained on action-free videos to predict future keypoint trajectories from task-centric point samples (segmented end-effector and object regions).

## Method

HinFlow operates in a self-improvement loop: the robot rolls out its hierarchical policy (flow planner + low-level policy) in the environment, then a video tracker (CoTracker3) retrospectively annotates the achieved 2D point flows from the recorded rollout. These achieved flows replace the planner's intended flows as goal labels, converting even failed trajectories into valid supervised training tuples (observation, action, achieved flow). The policy is updated via standard imitation learning on this hindsight-relabeled replay buffer, requiring no reward signal or RL. The key architectural choices include a transformer-based flow-conditioned policy with action chunking (size 5) and temporal ensemble for stability, task-centric point sampling via segmentation to focus flow on manipulation-relevant regions, and Gaussian exploration noise (σ=0.1) to encourage diverse rollouts. The high-level planner is a Track Transformer trained on ~300 action-free videos per task, predicting 16-frame flows for 32 points, while the low-level policy is pretrained on as few as 1–5 action-labeled demonstrations before entering the online loop.

## Results

HinFlow achieves an average success rate of 84.0% across seven manipulation tasks (4 LIBERO + 3 ManiSkill) within only 80K online interaction steps (~300–400 episodes), outperforming the strongest baseline by a factor of 1.45×. On the most challenging tasks (Hide Chocolate, Pull Cube Tool), HinFlow boosts success from near-zero to ~75%. In the real-world Franka Panda experiment, HinFlow improves from 40% to 95% success after just 86 online episodes (~1 hour), compared to 20% for BC and 40% for ATM (seg).

## Baselines & Comparisons

HinFlow outperforms all five baselines across simulation and real-world settings. ATM (Wen et al., 2023), in both grid and segmentation variants, is limited by the scarcity of action-labeled demonstrations and achieves substantially lower success rates than HinFlow on all seven tasks; in the real-world experiment it reaches only 40% vs. HinFlow's 95%. Online VPT (Baker et al., 2022) shows marginal gains on Place Book and Place Butter but struggles on the remaining tasks because its inverse dynamics model introduces substantial errors when pseudo-labeling action-free video data. UniPi (Du et al., 2023) fails to exceed 25% success on any LIBERO task due to the same data-scarcity bottleneck in its inverse dynamics model. PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) remains below 10% success on all three ManiSkill tasks within the same 80K-step budget, highlighting the sample inefficiency of RL with dense rewards. HuDOR (Guzey et al., 2025) yields only modest improvements on Place Book and Close Microwave but attains near-zero success on the other five tasks, likely because it cannot effectively leverage wrist-camera guidance for reward shaping.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of hierarchical robotic systems (high-level planner + low-level controller) and hindsight experience replay, but its specific contribution is the combination of point-flow subgoals with online self-imitation. It builds directly on ATM's Track Transformer for flow prediction (Wen et al., 2023) and the self-imitation principle from Ghosh et al. (2019), but departs from prior flow-to-policy translation by replacing analytical/optimization-based controllers or purely offline imitation with a closed-loop online refinement where achieved flows become hindsight goals. Unlike RL-based alternatives (PPO, HuDOR) or video-prediction-based methods (UniPi, Online VPT), HinFlow frames policy improvement as supervised learning on relabeled rollouts, avoiding reward design and long-horizon credit assignment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that a flow-based planner trained on action-free video data can be grounded into a robust manipulation policy with only 80K online steps and a handful of expert demos, substantially reducing the data collection burden that has bottlenecked scalable robot learning. The cross-embodiment transfer results (40+ point gains from Franka→Kinova/xArm) and the zero-shot robustness to distractors and unseen objects (90%+ vs. BC collapsing below 10%) suggest the flow abstraction genuinely filters task-irrelevant visual variation. Key limitations remain: the method still requires a small number of in-domain action-labeled demos for initialization, and 2D point flow is inherently ambiguous for complex 3D motions, motivating extension to 3D motion fields.
