## Problem

Implicit (deep equilibrium) models empirically match or exceed larger explicit networks by allocating more test-time iterations, but the mechanism behind this test-time scaling of expressivity remains unexplained. The paper asks whether a simple, well-behaved implicit operator can, through iteration, progressively express mappings of increasing complexity, and whether locally Lipschitz functions form the exact expressive boundary of such models.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260584/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a domain-specific input x (a blurred noisy image, a discretized force field on a periodic grid, a linear-program instance encoded as a bipartite graph, or a text prompt) and produces the corresponding target output y* (reconstructed image, vorticity field, LP optimal solution, or text response) by iterating a single shared operator G from an initial state y_0 = 0. The key structural detail is that the number of iterations T at inference is a free knob: the output y_t = G(y_{t-1}, x) is evaluated at each step, and both the effective complexity (measured by an empirical Lipschitz constant) and the solution quality improve monotonically with t before saturating near the fixed point.

## Method

The paper provides a non-parametric expressivity characterization via two theorems: (i) every locally Lipschitz target map F admits a 'regular' implicit operator G (globally Lipschitz in x with linear growth in ‖y‖, and contractive in y with a continuous contraction modulus) whose Picard iterates converge to F; and (ii) conversely, every regular G induces a locally Lipschitz fixed-point map, making the boundary tight. The sufficiency construction uses an adaptive step size ε(x) that vanishes near singularities of F, so the operator G itself remains globally smooth while the iterates progressively unlock the target's local steepness. For empirical validation, the authors instantiate G with problem-specific architectures—PGD/HQS-style operators with a learned DRUnet denoiser for image deblurring, a Fourier Neural Operator for steady Navier-Stokes, an implicit GNN on a bipartite LP graph, and a looped transformer for LLM reasoning—and show in all four domains that the empirical Lipschitz constant of y_t(·) grows with iteration count while reconstruction error decreases, confirming that expressivity scales with test-time compute rather than parameter count.

## Results

Across four domains, implicit models match or exceed explicit baselines at matched parameter counts. On image deblurring (BSDS500), implicit HQS achieves 31.53 dB PSNR versus 28.49 dB for an explicit DRUnet with identical 32.64M parameters (>3 dB gain). On steady Navier-Stokes, implicit FNO reaches 0.112 relative error versus 0.260 for explicit FNO (both 2.376M params). On linear programming, implicit GNNs consistently outperform explicit GNNs at matched embedding sizes, with the largest gap at small widths (e.g., implicit-4 test error 0.218 vs. explicit-8 test error 0.273). In all cases, the empirical Lipschitz constant of the t-th iterate grows with test-time iterations while reconstruction error decreases and stabilizes, confirming the theory that expressivity scales with test-time compute.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the explicit DRUnet/PnP baseline (Zhang et al., 2021), the implicit HQS model achieves 31.53 dB PSNR versus 28.49 dB on image deblurring with identical architecture and 32.64M parameters, a gain of over 3 dB; the implicit PGD variant also improves to 29.18 dB average. Against the vanilla FNO baseline (Li et al., 2021), the implicit FNO for Navier-Stokes reduces relative error from 0.260 to 0.112 (both 2.376M params), more than halving the error. Against the explicit GNN-for-LP approach (Chen et al., 2023), implicit GNNs win at every embedding size: at size 4, implicit test error (0.218) beats even the explicit size-8 model (0.273), and at size 16, implicit (0.152) beats explicit (0.283) by nearly 2×. Notably, explicit GNNs overfit as width grows (test error rises from 0.273 at size 8 to 0.318 at size 32), while implicit GNNs maintain or improve test performance (0.177 → 0.156), suggesting the fixed-point formulation provides implicit regularization. A smaller implicit model (size 4, 722 params) outperforms a larger explicit model (size 8, 2,088 params) on both train and test error, directly supporting the paper's claim that test-time iterations substitute for parameter count.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the expressivity analysis of Deep Equilibrium Models (Bai et al., 2019) and implicit deep learning (El Ghaoui et al., 2021) by providing the first complete non-parametric characterization of the representable function class for regular implicit operators. Prior work touched on universality in specific settings and separation results (Wu et al., 2024), but left the full characterization open; here the authors identify locally Lipschitz mappings as the exact boundary via matching sufficiency and necessity theorems. The empirical validation draws on DEQ-based neural operators for PDEs (Marwah et al., 2023), GNNs for LPs (Chen et al., 2023), and looped transformers for LLM reasoning (Geiping et al., 2025), unifying them under a single expressivity framework where test-time iterations progressively unlock complexity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean theoretical explanation for the empirically observed test-time scaling of implicit models: a simple, globally smooth operator can express arbitrarily complex (locally Lipschitz, potentially singular) mappings through iteration, scaling expressivity with compute rather than parameters. This reframes the design principle for implicit models—practitioners should avoid enforcing uniform global Lipschitz bounds (which constrain expressivity) and instead incorporate domain-specific structure. Limitations include the bounded-domain requirement in the main theorem, the somewhat restrictive 'regular' operator definition, and the qualitative (rather than rigorous) treatment of the LLM case study. Open questions include whether the theory extends to unbounded or infinite-dimensional settings and how to enforce regularity during training without sacrificing the expressivity gains.
