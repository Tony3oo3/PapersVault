## Problem

Behavior-targeted attacks aim to steer a DRL victim's behavior toward an adversary-specified target policy, a threat that cannot be realized by conventional reward-minimization attacks. Existing behavior-targeted attacks all require white-box access to the victim's policy, severely limiting practical applicability, and no defense has been specifically designed for this threat since existing robustness methods (adversarial training, regret minimization, certified defenses) are built around reward-based metrics that do not capture arbitrary behavior manipulation.

## Input / Output

For the attack (BIA), the adversary provides demonstrations (20 episodes of state-action trajectories) generated by the target policy and operates under black-box access (observing the victim's state inputs and action outputs) or no-box access (observing only state inputs). The output is an adversarial policy ν that, at each timestep, replaces the victim's true state s with a falsified state ŝ constrained to the L∞ ball B(s) = {ŝ : ‖ŝ − s‖∞ ≤ ε} (ε = 0.3 in experiments). For the defense (TDRT), the input is standard RL training data (state-action-reward tuples) and the output is a robust policy trained with PPO augmented by a time-discounted KL regularization term; the timestep t enters only through the discount factor γ^t and is not part of the policy's input representation.

## Method

The attack leverages Theorem 5.1, which reformulates the behavior-targeted objective (minimizing a divergence D(π∘ν, π_tgt)) as a cumulative reward maximization problem in a constructed MDP M̂ whose transition dynamics embed the victim's policy, eliminating the need for white-box access. This allows standard imitation learning algorithms (GAIL, DAC for ILfD; OPOLO for ILfO) to train the adversarial policy directly from target-policy demonstrations without reward modeling. The defense is grounded in Theorem 6.1, which upper-bounds the adversary's cumulative reward gain by a time-weighted sum of KL divergences D_KL(π(·|s) ‖ π∘ν(·|s)) at each timestep, revealing that early-stage action sensitivity dominates overall vulnerability. TDRT exploits this by adding a time-discounted regularization λ·Σ γ^t D_KL(π(·|s_t) ‖ π∘ν(·|s_t)) to the PPO objective, concentrating smoothing at critical early timesteps while preserving the policy's expressiveness in later stages, yielding 28.2% higher clean-task performance than uniform smoothing (SA-PPO) at comparable robustness.

## Results

On Meta-World (ε=0.3), TDRT-PPO achieves the best or near-best robustness across all 10 tasks, e.g., reducing the best attack reward to 7±1 on handle-pull-side (vs. 532±534 for ATLA-PPO) and 482±3 on window-close (vs. 4270±188 for ATLA-PPO). On clean-task performance, TDRT-PPO outperforms uniform smoothing (SA-PPO) by an average of 28.2%, with the largest gains on drawer-close (+96.5%) and door-unlock (+82.5%). For the attack side, BIA-ILfD (black-box, demonstrations only) achieves attack rewards competitive with white-box baselines, e.g., 3962±666 on window-close vs. 4255±300 for PA-AD (white-box, reward function), while Targeted PGD (white-box) only reaches 1666±936.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against adversarial-training defenses (ATLA-PPO [Zhang 2021] and PA-ATLA-PPO [Sun 2022]), TDRT-PPO is substantially more robust: on window-close, ATLA-PPO still yields 4270±188 attack reward (nearly unattacked level of 4505) while TDRT-PPO reduces it to 482±3; on handle-pull-side, PA-ATLA-PPO gives 512±982 vs. TDRT-PPO's 7±1. The regret-based defense RAD-PPO [Belaire 2024] shows moderate improvement over adversarial training (e.g., 736±22 vs. 1158±1026 on drawer-open) but remains far from TDRT-PPO (378±10) because it optimizes a reward-based metric that is misaligned with behavior-targeted objectives. WocaR-PPO [Liang 2022], which applies partial smoothing only to critical states, achieves moderate robustness (e.g., 33±6 on handle-pull-side) but consistently underperforms both SA-PPO and TDRT-PPO, which smooth uniformly. The closest competitor is SA-PPO [Zhang 2020b] (uniform policy smoothing without time-discounting), which matches or slightly beats TDRT-PPO on robustness in a few tasks (e.g., drawer-close: 4±2 vs. 4860±4; door-lock: 478±7 vs. 487±11) but suffers severe clean-task degradation—TDRT-PPO improves clean reward by 28.2% on average (up to 96.5% on drawer-close), confirming that time-discounting is the key differentiator.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the SA-MDP framework (Zhang et al., 2020b) for modeling state-observation adversarial attacks and on GAIL-style imitation learning (Ho & Ermon, 2016; Kostrikov et al., 2018) for policy matching. It departs from prior behavior-targeted attacks (Hussenot et al., 2020; Bai et al., 2024, 2025) by proving a theoretical reformulation (Theorem 5.1) that converts the attack objective into a standard MDP reward-maximization problem, thereby eliminating the white-box access requirement that plagues all existing behavior-targeted attacks. On the defense side, it extends policy smoothing (Shen et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2020b) with a time-discounted KL regularization term, grounded in a new upper bound (Theorem 6.1) showing that early-stage action sensitivity dominates the adversary's cumulative gain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TDRT is the first defense explicitly designed for behavior-targeted attacks, filling a gap where existing adversarial training, regret-based, and certified defenses are structurally misaligned with this threat. The 28.2% average clean-reward improvement over uniform smoothing (up to 96.5% on drawer-close) makes the defense practically deployable without sacrificing task performance. Key limitations remain: no certified robustness guarantees, reduced effectiveness in high-dimensional (e.g., image) state spaces, and evaluation limited to low-dimensional continuous/discrete control benchmarks. Open questions include whether the time-discounting principle generalizes to multi-agent or non-stationary settings, and whether certified bounds can be derived for the behavior-targeted threat model.
