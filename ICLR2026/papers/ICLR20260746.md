## Problem

Large Reasoning Models frequently produce rationales that sound plausible but do not reflect their true decision process, yet no formal, testable framework exists to verify whether stated reasoning actually governs the final answer in a way decoupled from task accuracy. Prior faithfulness work relied on input-level perturbations or explanation-coverage heuristics, leaving open whether the reasoning trace is structurally coherent and causally determinative of the output.

## Input / Output

Each RFEval instance pairs a problem (question, options, auxiliary context) with a hand-validated counterfactual reasoning trace r′ that encodes a specific incorrect stance. The target LRM is queried twice: once on the raw problem (baseline) and once with r′ appended after the model's own reasoning-start tag (intervened), yielding outputs decomposed into (reasoning, explanation, answer) components. The system produces a binary per-instance faithfulness label (RF), stance-consistency indicators χ(o) and χ(o′), a causal-influence indicator κ(o,o′), and a contrast-coverage rate c(M); only contrast-satisfying pairs (where S(r′)≠S(r)) are scored, and the final benchmark score is an instance-weighted micro-average across seven tasks.

## Method

RFEval formalizes reasoning faithfulness as the conjunction of two testable conditions: stance consistency (the flattened (r, e, a) sequence forms an unbroken chain of stance continuity, allowing only explicitly justified departures) and causal influence (the injected counterfactual reasoning r′ must shift either the reasoning stance or the answer stance). To ensure causal identifiability, a contrast precondition δ=1 is imposed so that r′ always opposes the model's baseline stance, creating a proper counterfactual contrast. The benchmark is constructed via a two-stage pipeline: o3 generates subtly flawed counterfactual reasonings with task-specific few-shot prompts, and gpt-5 plus eight human annotators validate them against four criteria (misleading sufficiency, logical soundness, plausible subtlety, uniqueness). Evaluation itself is LLM-as-judge: o3 extracts canonical stances per component and detects whether a transition explicitly identifies the injected flaw, achieving 0.952 micro-F1 on stance extraction and 0.938 accuracy on flaw identification against human annotations.

## Results

Across 12 open-source LRMs evaluated on RFEval (7,186 instances, 7 tasks), 49.73% of outputs are unfaithful. Overall contrast-conditional reasoning faithfulness (RF) ranges from 26.06% (Magistral-Small) to 73.29% (Qwen3-32B), with LN-Super_v1 at 68.52% and R1-Qwen-32B at 64.24%. Faithfulness is lowest in convergent tasks (Code Generation: 24.18%, Mathematical Reasoning: 28.06%) and highest in argumentative tasks (Legal Decision: 70.17%, Logical Reasoning: 58.28%). Within-family ablations show that adding RLVR-style objectives on top of SFT reduces RF (e.g., MiMo-7B: SFT-only 60.05% → SFT+RL 46.32%), while increasing parameter size does not reliably improve faithfulness (gpt-oss: 32.11% at 20B → 27.50% at 120B).

## Baselines & Comparisons

RFEval evaluates 12 open-source LRMs as the primary comparison set. Qwen3-32B achieves the highest overall RF (73.29%), substantially outperforming gpt-oss-120b (27.50%) and Magistral-Small (26.06%) by ~47 percentage points. Within the Qwen family, scaling from 8B to 32B nearly doubles RF (41.95% → 73.29%), whereas within gpt-oss, scaling from 20B to 120B *decreases* RF (32.11% → 27.50%), demonstrating that parameter count alone is not a reliable predictor of faithfulness. The DeepSeek-R1-Distill family shows moderate performance (56–64% overall), with R1-Qwen-32B (64.24%) slightly ahead of R1-Llama-70B (56.47%). LN-Super_v1 (68.52%) is the second-best model, while MiMo-RL-Zero (58.74%) outperforms its RL-trained sibling MiMo-RL (46.32%), consistent with the finding that RLVR-style post-training degrades faithfulness. Across all models, Code Generation (24.18%) and Mathematical Reasoning (28.06%) are the hardest tasks, while Legal Decision (70.17%) is the easiest, indicating that convergent, step-tight reasoning is most vulnerable to unfaithfulness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior faithfulness evaluation work that relies on input-level perturbations (Turpin et al., 2023; Arcuschin et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025b) or white-box activation patching (Zhang & Nanda, 2023; Dumas et al., 2025) by introducing output-level counterfactual interventions: appending a deliberately flawed reasoning trace to the model's own generated output and checking whether the model coherently absorbs and propagates the contradiction. It formalizes reasoning faithfulness through two testable, model-agnostic conditions—stance consistency and causal influence—that can be evaluated purely from textual behavior, bridging the gap between the formal faithfulness literature (Jacovi & Goldberg, 2020; Wiegreffe & Marasović, 2021) and the practical LRM evaluation setting. The RFEval benchmark operationalizes this framework across seven heterogeneous tasks, extending the scope of prior single-domain or single-task faithfulness probes (Lanham et al., 2023; Xiong et al., 2025) to a multi-task, multi-model setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that ~50% of LRM outputs are unfaithful, and that this unfaithfulness is largely driven by stance inconsistency under flawed premises rather than causal breakdown, provides a concrete diagnostic for reliability auditing. More critically, the within-family ablations showing that RLVR-style post-training degrades faithfulness while preserving accuracy, combined with the statistical insignificance of the accuracy-faithfulness link after controlling for model and task, challenge the field's implicit assumption that optimizing for correct answers suffices for trustworthy reasoning. Open questions include whether redesigned RL rewards that explicitly penalize stance inconsistency can restore faithfulness, whether per-step (rather than block-level) granularity reveals finer failure modes, and how these findings generalize to closed-source models where output-level intervention is impractical. The LLM-as-judge evaluation introduces a residual evaluator-bias limitation that future work with multi-evaluator aggregation or purpose-built faithfulness evaluators could address.
