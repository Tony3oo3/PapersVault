## Problem

LLM-based systems are highly sensitive to their prompts, yet no principled method exists to quantify uncertainty over those prompts or to incorporate human prior beliefs about them. Existing UQ approaches for LLMs perturb inputs, measure output variability, or elicit self-reported confidence, but none treat prompts as statistical parameters subject to Bayesian inference—leaving a gap in how we can formally express and propagate uncertainty about the system's configuration itself.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a small labeled dataset D, free-form textual prior constraints s_j (human-written strings describing desired properties of each prompt), and an LLM-based system that may be entirely closed-source. It produces a set of posterior prompt samples θ^(1),...,θ^(m) that are then used at inference to generate diverse answer sets; the variability across these answers yields semantic calibration scores (SECE), abstention scores, and—under conformal factuality—factuality-filtered outputs. A key unusual aspect is that the 'parameters' being inferred are discrete free-form text strings rather than continuous vectors, and the prior is specified as natural-language constraints fed to an LLM rather than a parametric distribution.

## Method

The core contribution is MHLP (Metropolis-Hastings through LLM Proposals), an MCMC algorithm that adapts the Metropolis-Hastings framework to discrete textual parameters by using a prompt optimization step (TextGrad) as the proposal distribution q(θ'|θ), so that each proposal is a semantically meaningful LLM-generated perturbation of the current prompt. The acceptance ratio evaluates the unnormalized posterior g(θ) = p(θ)p(D|θ), where the prior p(θ) is modeled by an LLM call conditioned on the textual prior constraints, and the likelihood p(D|θ) is estimated via mini-batch Monte Carlo using a surrogate open-source model (Llama-3.1-Nemotron-70B) to obtain token log-probabilities when the target LLM is closed-source. A tempered posterior with tunable temperature β further improves empirical performance. At inference, m posterior prompt samples each generate one answer, and the resulting answer set's semantic clustering yields a confidence score that quantifies predictive uncertainty. The paper also introduces Semantic ECE (SECE), a calibration metric that extends standard ECE to free-form outputs by using LLM-based semantic clustering to estimate per-answer confidence.

## Results

MHLP achieves the highest accuracy across all three benchmarks: 15.0% on AIME 2024, 48.6% on SimpleQA, and 60.9% on QASPER, outperforming the best frequentist baselines by 2.4–17.2 percentage points. On calibration (ECE/SECE), MHLP achieves the lowest error on SimpleQA (15.4%) and QASPER (17.7%), though it trails System-Message (19.7%) on AIME (22.0%). For abstention on QASPER unanswerable questions, MHLP attains the best ROC AUC at 77.9% (no context) and 71.7% (random context). In the conformal factuality setting, MHLP frequency scoring achieves the same empirical factuality coverage as the GPT-4 baseline while removing significantly fewer claims, indicating better-calibrated confidence.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the SPUQ perturbation baselines (Paraphrasing and System-Message), MHLP wins on accuracy across all three datasets (e.g., 15.0% vs. 12.6% on AIME; 60.9% vs. 59.7% on QASPER) and on ECE for SimpleQA (15.4% vs. 18.4%) and QASPER (17.7% vs. 23.9%), though System-Message edges out MHLP on AIME ECE (19.7% vs. 22.0%). Against the CoT baseline, MHLP improves accuracy by 6.0, 0.8, and 4.4 points on AIME, SimpleQA, and QASPER respectively, and reduces ECE from 31.5% to 22.0% on AIME and from 26.2% to 17.7% on QASPER. Against TextGrad (the prompt-optimization method used as MHLP's proposal), MHLP consistently outperforms on both accuracy (e.g., 15.0% vs. 11.9% on AIME) and calibration (15.4% vs. 17.7% ECE on SimpleQA; 17.7% vs. 21.6% on QASPER), which the authors attribute to MHLP's accept/reject mechanism preventing degradation of the prompt. In the conformal factuality experiment, MHLP frequency scoring matches the GPT-4 frequency scoring baseline on empirical factuality coverage (both within conformal bounds) but achieves a lower average claim removal rate, demonstrating better-calibrated factuality scores without sacrificing statistical guarantees.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Metropolis-Hastings MCMC framework (Robert, 2015) for a fundamentally new parameter space: free-form text. It builds directly on TextGrad (Yuksekgonul et al., 2025) as the proposal mechanism, repurposing a prompt-optimization tool as the q(θ'|θ) distribution within MH. Conceptually, it departs from both (a) Bayesian deep learning, which requires differentiable continuous parameters, and (b) frequentist prompt optimization, which seeks a single point estimate θ*. The paper positions itself as the first to perform full Bayesian inference over prompts, bridging the classical Bayesian statistics literature with the LLM-based systems paradigm where prompts serve as the only accessible 'parameters.'

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The work provides a principled, black-box-compatible path to uncertainty quantification for LLM-based systems, which is critical for deployment in high-stakes domains like medicine and finance. The MHLP algorithm is a general-purpose MCMC sampler for text with applications beyond Bayesian inference (demonstrated via conformal factuality), potentially enabling principled sampling from any unnormalized textual distribution. Key limitations include the high one-time MCMC cost, reliance on approximations (tempered posterior, mini-batch likelihoods, surrogate open-source models for log-probabilities), and the need for LLM-based semantic clustering in evaluation. Open questions include whether MHLP can be made computationally cheaper (e.g., via a trained proposal model) and whether the approximations introduce systematic biases in the posterior.
