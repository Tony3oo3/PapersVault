## Problem

Current Text-to-SQL evaluation platforms (BIRD, Spider) check correctness by executing generated and gold SQL on a single static test database, which can mark two genuinely different queries as equivalent by coincidence. The paper asks how often this test-based approach overestimates accuracy and whether a more rigorous, search-based evaluation can expose the true performance of Text-to-SQL methods.

## Input / Output

SPOTIT takes a natural language question, a database schema, a gold SQL query, and a generated SQL query, and searches for a minimal counterexample database (bounded to K tuples per relation, K=5 in experiments) on which the two queries produce different outputs. The counterexample is validated by re-executing both queries on a real DBMS (SQLite) to eliminate spurious results from over-approximated SMT encodings. The final output is either a differentiating database instance or a bounded-equivalence verdict, which feeds into a revised accuracy score (EX-SPOTIT) replacing the standard EX-TEST metric.

## Method

SPOTIT builds on VERIEQL, a bounded SQL equivalence checker that encodes symbolic execution of two queries as an SMT formula solvable by Z3. The paper's key technical contribution is extending VERIEQL with precise SMT encodings for date types (modeled as (year, month, day) triplets with leap-year constraints), string operations (PrefixOf, SuffixOf, Like, Contain, SubStr, Concat), date formatting (Strftime, JulianDay, DateShift), and implicit type conversions (ToInt, ToDate, ToStr) across Null, integer, date, and string types. The pipeline iterates over bounds k=1..K, checking bounded equivalence at each; upon finding a non-equivalence, it validates the counterexample on SQLite to filter spurious results. A cross-checking step (SPOTIT+) reuses counterexamples discovered for one Text-to-SQL framework to test all other frameworks, improving detection coverage. Formal correctness theorems prove that the symbolic encodings coincide with concrete execution semantics.

## Results

On the BIRD-dev benchmark (1,533 questions), SPOTIT reveals that all 10 evaluated Text-to-SQL methods lose 9.8%–13.5% absolute accuracy when switching from the standard test-based metric (EX-TEST) to verification-based evaluation (EX-SPOTIT). For example, CSC-32B drops from 71.32% to 58.80%, and ALPHA drops from 69.36% to 55.87%. On Spider 2.0 (135 SQLite questions), OMNISQL drops from 34.1% to 22.2% and GPT-5 from 42.2% to 36.3%. The average time to find a counterexample is under 4 seconds, and 93–96% of verifier-produced counterexamples are validated as non-spurious.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new Text-to-SQL method but rather a new evaluation pipeline (SPOTIT) and re-evaluates 10 state-of-the-art methods (CSC-32B, GENA-2, ALPHA, GENA-1, CSC-7B, RSL, OMNI-MAJ, GSR, CHESS, SLM) under both the standard EX-TEST and the proposed EX-SPOTIT metrics. Under EX-TEST, CSC-32B leads at 71.32%, but under EX-SPOTIT⁺ it drops to 4th place (57.82%), while GENA-2 rises from 2nd to 1st (59.13%) and CSC-7B rises from 5th to 3rd (57.95%). The bottom half of the ranking (OMNI-MAJ, GSR, CHESS, SLM) remains largely stable. Manual inspection of 50 counterexamples for CSC-32B reveals that 26% of discrepancies stem from incorrect generated SQL, but a comparable or larger share is due to incorrect gold SQLs or ambiguous natural-language questions, suggesting that even a perfect Text-to-SQL method could not exceed ~80% EX-TEST on BIRD-dev. On Spider 2.0, SPOTIT's coverage is lower (50.9–60.9% of query pairs supported) due to unsupported window functions, but it still finds differentiating databases for 16 (OMNISQL) and 8 (GPT-5) query pairs that passed EX-TEST.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends VERIEQL (He et al., 2024), a bounded SQL equivalence checker, by adding SMT encodings for string and date operations (strftime, substr, like, julian day, date shifts) and set-semantics equivalence, then repurposes the resulting verifier as a Text-to-SQL evaluation pipeline (SPOTIT). It departs from the dominant test-based evaluation paradigm used by BIRD and Spider 2.0, which executes queries on a single static database, by instead actively searching for minimal counterexample databases that differentiate generated and gold SQL. The work sits at the intersection of formal verification (bounded model checking, SMT solving) and NLP evaluation, and is the first to apply formal equivalence checking at scale to benchmark Text-to-SQL methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals that test-based evaluation overestimates Text-to-SQL accuracy by 11–14% and substantially distorts method rankings, while also uncovering that a large fraction of apparent 'errors' stem from incorrect gold SQLs or ambiguous questions rather than from the generated queries. This challenges the foundational assumption that gold SQLs are reliable ground truth and suggests that even a perfect Text-to-SQL system may not reach 100% on current benchmarks. Open questions include extending the verifier to window functions and recursive CTEs (which limit Spider 2.0 coverage to ~50–60%), systematically handling NL ambiguity in evaluation, and incorporating user preferences into counterexample search to make the tool more actionable for benchmark maintainers.
