## Problem

Frontier multimodal models are reported to perform at or below random chance on compositional reasoning benchmarks, yet this apparent failure is partly an artifact of the GroupScore metric, which imposes overly restrictive pairwise constraints and systematically underestimates model capability. The paper asks whether a more faithful evaluation metric can reveal hidden compositional reasoning ability, and whether that ability can be further bootstrapped at test time without any external supervision.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260383/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pretrained multimodal model (contrastive VLMs such as SigLIP/CLIP or generative MLLMs such as GPT-4.1/SmolVLM) together with a test set organized into small groups of images and captions (2×2, 1×k, or ungrouped) that differ in subtle systematic ways (e.g., identical words in different orders). It produces a test-time finetuned model whose image-caption similarity scores have been sharpened by iterative pseudo-labeling, along with the induced matching assignments. For contrastive models the similarity is the inner product of embeddings; for generative MLLMs it is estimated via VQAScore on a yes/no prompt.

## Method

The paper introduces two complementary contributions. First, a new evaluation metric GroupMatch that scores a group by whether the ground-truth permutation maximizes total similarity over all k! matchings, rather than requiring every diagonal entry to dominate both its row and column as GroupScore demands; this raises the random-guessing baseline from (k−1)!/(2k−1)! to 1/k! and reveals substantial hidden capability. A simple overfitting step (SimpleMatch) then translates GroupMatch correctness back into GroupScore correctness. Second, Test-Time Matching (TTM) is an iterative self-training loop: at each round the current model induces a matching per group, computes the margin between the best and second-best matchings, retains only pseudo-labels above a confidence threshold, and finetunes on them. A decaying threshold schedule (high→low) balances early precision against later coverage, and the same principle extends to a global assignment problem solved via the Hungarian algorithm for datasets without predefined group structure.

## Results

On Winoground, SimpleMatch lifts GPT-4.1 from 69.75 to 91.38 (surpassing the estimated human performance of 85.5), while TTM pushes SigLIP-B16 from 10.25 (raw) to 72.50, far exceeding the previous SOTA of 58.75. On MMVP-VLM, TTM enables SigLIP-B16 to reach 89.44, surpassing GPT-4.1's 88.52 and the prior SOTA of 70.7. On ColorSwap, TTM elevates SigLIP-L16 to 96.08 (near GPT-4.1's 97.42) and SigLIP-B16 to 94.25, both exceeding the previous no-training-set SOTA of 87.33. Relative error reductions from TTM over SimpleMatch reach up to 54.8% (SigLIP-L16 on ColorSwap).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is against the previous state-of-the-art on each benchmark: 58.75 on Winoground (GPT-4V with prompt tuning), 70.7 on MMVP (GPT-4o multi-agent with tool use), and 87.33 on ColorSwap (without training-set access). SimpleMatch alone already surpasses all three prior SOTAs for SigLIP-B16 (67, 81.48, 88) without any additional data. TTM further extends these leads, with SigLIP-B16 reaching 89.44 on MMVP-VLM—surpassing GPT-4.1's 88.52 and establishing a new SOTA—and SigLIP-L16 reaching 96.08 on ColorSwap, nearly matching GPT-4.1's 97.42. GPT-4.1 itself, under SimpleMatch, achieves 91.38 on Winoground, the first result to exceed the estimated human performance of 85.5. On 1×k benchmarks (SugarCrepe, WhatsUp) where GroupScore and GroupMatch coincide, TTM still delivers up to 85.7% relative gains (WhatsUp A: 30.58→56.8), confirming that the self-improvement mechanism is not solely a metric artifact.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the compositional reasoning evaluation literature (Winoground, MMVP-VLM, ColorSwap) by arguing that the standard GroupScore metric itself is the bottleneck, not model capability. It introduces GroupMatch, a matching-based metric that evaluates the best global assignment rather than isolated pairwise comparisons, and connects this to the classical assignment problem (Hungarian algorithm). The Test-Time Matching (TTM) algorithm draws on test-time training (Sun et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020), self-training with pseudo-labels (Kumar et al., 2020; Sohn et al., 2020), and confidence-based selection from active learning (Castro & Nowak, 2007), but applies them in a matching-specific way with a decaying threshold schedule that inverts the typical active-learning logic (most confident first, then relax).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that GroupScore systematically underestimates model capability by a large margin (e.g., 1/6 vs 1/2 random-guessing probability for 2×2 groups) reframes years of 'models perform at random chance' reports as a measurement artifact, with GPT-4.1 now surpassing estimated human performance on Winoground. TTM provides a practical, supervision-free test-time self-improvement loop that generalizes across contrastive VLMs, generative MLLMs, and even non-grouped settings, suggesting the matching-based pseudo-labeling principle may extend well beyond compositional reasoning. Open questions include whether the metric correction is the 'right' fix or masks genuine compositional failures, how TTM scales to larger group sizes or open-ended generation, and whether the decaying threshold schedule can be made adaptive rather than hand-tuned per dataset-model pair.
