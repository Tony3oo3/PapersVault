## Problem

Current Video-LLMs treat videos as a 'bag of frames' sampled uniformly, lacking any evolving belief about the video's narrative. This causes them to over-sample mundane, expected moments while missing rare surprising events that define a video's story. The paper asks whether Video-LLMs can proactively track and update beliefs as new visual evidence arrives, and whether detecting semantically surprising events ahead of downstream queries can improve video understanding.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264139/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At each timestep t, the method consumes three inputs: a prior window of W=4 frames immediately preceding t, a rolling historical text summary H_t (maintained via BART-Large-CNN compression when exceeding ~200 words), and the newly observed frame O_t. It produces per-timestep surprise scores in [0,1] (via Jensen-Shannon divergence for bounded reporting), a set of N=3 human-readable textual belief hypotheses with their prior and posterior probabilities, and a surprise-weighted frame allocation that replaces uniform sampling. The frame budget F scales with video duration (8 frames for ≤1 min, doubling per additional minute), and the final outputs include video captions and QA answers from the downstream Video-LLM.

## Method

SPIKE is an inference-time framework that quantifies Bayesian surprise by generating N textual belief hypotheses (via nucleus sampling from a Video-LLM) conditioned on the historical summary and prior frame window, then computing prior and posterior distributions over these hypotheses using the Video-LLM's negative log-likelihood scores before and after observing the new frame. The surprise score at each timestep is the KL divergence between posterior and prior belief distributions, following Itti & Baldi's formalization. SPIKE-RL further refines hypothesis generation using GRPO reinforcement learning: multiple belief trajectories are rolled out, each guiding surprise-weighted frame sampling to produce a final video caption, and an LLM-Match reward on caption quality is propagated back via Z-scored advantages to increase the likelihood of hypotheses along high-reward trajectories. The resulting surprise scores drive a softmax-weighted frame sampling strategy (temperature τ_s=0.7) that reallocates the fixed frame budget toward high-surprise segments, remaining query-agnostic and plug-and-play for any Video-LLM.

## Results

On the Oops! surprise localization benchmark, SPIKE-RL achieves 62.9% Acc@0.25s, slightly exceeding the human agreement baseline of 62.1%, while SPIKE (inference-only) reaches 60.0%. On FunQA, SPIKE-RL attains 68.2 IoU, surpassing the strongest prior method (LLaVA-NeXT-CR at 62.3) by 5.9 points. For downstream video understanding with surprise-weighted frame sampling, SPIKE-RL yields consistent gains over uniform sampling: +2.3% on BlackSwan, +4.6% on FunQA, +7.0% on ExFunTube, +2.7% on VideoMME-S, and +1.7% on NextQA (7B model).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SPIKE-RL dramatically outperforms the zero-shot Qwen2.5-VL baseline (~10× improvement on Oops! Acc@0.25s: 62.9% vs. 6.6%), confirming that explicit belief tracking is essential rather than relying on the VLM's implicit frame scoring. Against dedicated surprise-detection methods, SPIKE-RL surpasses VideoSpeed (36.6%) and F2C2V (39.5%) by 23–26 points on Oops! Acc@0.25s, while matching or exceeding their Acc@1s scores. On FunQA, SPIKE-RL (68.2 IoU) beats LLaVA-NeXT-CR (62.3), UniVTG (45.3), and TimeChat (9.6) by substantial margins, despite FunQA's positive/humor surprises being out-of-distribution for the RL-trained model. On the self-curated Mr. Bean benchmark, SPIKE-RL achieves 57.4% Acc@0.25s and 61.1 IoU, far exceeding the zero-shot baseline (11.2% / 13.8). For downstream frame-sampling comparisons, all shot-boundary-detection strategies (RGB Histogram, ECR, Katna, Optical Flow) consistently underperform even uniform sampling, whereas SPIKE and SPIKE-RL improve over uniform on all five downstream tasks, with the largest gain (+7.0%) on ExFunTube.

## Relation to Prior Work

SPIKE operationalizes the Bayesian surprise formalization of Itti & Baldi (2005) — KL divergence between prior and posterior belief distributions — for the first time in the video-understanding setting, using a Video-LLM to generate and score textual belief hypotheses over frame windows. The RL extension (SPIKE-RL) repurposes GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) to refine hypothesis generation via a caption-quality reward, bridging the gap between inference-time scoring and learned policy optimization. Conceptually, the work draws on Bayesian Theory of Mind and predictive coding literature (Baker et al., 2017; Kim et al., 2025) but departs from prior frame-selection methods (Frame-Voyager, AKS, VideoTree) by being query-agnostic, and from shot-boundary detection baselines by grounding surprise in semantic belief shifts rather than raw visual change.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By providing a query-agnostic, interpretable mechanism for reallocating a fixed frame budget toward semantically surprising moments, SPIKE/SPIKE-RL offers a drop-in improvement for any Video-LLM pipeline without architectural changes, yielding consistent (if modest, 1–7%) gains across five downstream tasks. The near-human surprise localization on Oops! (62.9% vs. 62.1% human) and the finding that standard SBD strategies underperform even uniform sampling highlight that semantic belief tracking is a fundamentally different and more effective signal than pixel-level change. Open questions include extending to real-time streaming, handling longer videos where the historical summary may degrade, and combining surprise with task-specific relevance signals for query-conditioned settings.
