## Problem

Conformal prediction guarantees valid coverage only under exchangeability, which breaks when training labels are corrupted (noisy or missing). Privileged Conformal Prediction (PCP) corrects the resulting distribution shift by re-weighting with likelihood ratios derived from privileged information (PI), but assumes access to true weights that are unavailable in practice. This paper asks: under what conditions does PCP remain valid with inaccurate weights, and can we construct valid prediction sets without relying on weight estimation at all?

## Input / Output

The method consumes n training samples (X_i, Ỹ_i, Z_i, M_i) where X are observed covariates, Ỹ are potentially corrupted labels, Z is privileged information available only during training, and M indicates corruption. At test time, only X_test is available (Z is absent), and the method outputs a prediction set C(X_test) ⊆ Y guaranteed to cover Y_test with probability ≥ 1−α. The key constraint is that all calibration and imputation must be completed at training time so that the final prediction set depends solely on X_test.

## Method

The paper contributes two complementary techniques. First, it derives explicit conditions (Theorems 2–3) under which PCP and WCP maintain valid coverage despite constant or bounded per-sample weight errors, revealing that validity depends on whether Naive CP over- or under-covers. Second, it introduces Uncertain Imputation (UI), which trains a second model ĝ(x,z) on the PI to predict Y, then imputes each corrupted calibration label as ĝ(X_i, Z_i) + E(Z_i) where E(Z_i) is a residual drawn from a reference set of holdout errors conditional on Z. This preserves the uncertainty of the imputed label, and combined with results on dispersive label noise, yields valid coverage without any weight estimation. Finally, TriplyRobust takes the union of Naive CP, PCP, and UI prediction sets, guaranteeing validity whenever at least one of the three underlying assumptions (Y|X, M|Z, or Y|Z is well-estimated) holds.

## Results

The primary metric is marginal coverage rate, with a target of 90% (1−α = 0.90). On the MEPS19 dataset with 20% missing labels, Naive CP and PCP with estimated weights both fail to reach the nominal 90% coverage, while the proposed UI method and WCP with true weights achieve the target. On the synthetic 'complex weights' experiment (Figure 4), PCP with estimated weights undercovers, whereas UI consistently attains 90% coverage. Across five real benchmarks (Facebook, Bio, House, MEPS19) with 20% artificial label removal, both PCP (with true weights) and UI consistently achieve the target 90% coverage, while Naive CP and Naive Imputation produce intervals that are too narrow and undercover.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four calibration schemes: Naive CP (standard conformal prediction on observed labels only), WCP (weighted conformal prediction requiring test-time PI), PCP (privileged conformal prediction with estimated or true weights), and the proposed UI (uncertain imputation). Naive CP consistently undercovers across all experiments because the missing-label mechanism induces a covariate shift between calibration and test data. WCP with true weights achieves valid 90% coverage but is infeasible in practice since it requires the test-time privileged information Z. PCP with estimated weights fails to reach nominal coverage on MEPS19 and the synthetic complex-weights experiment, confirming that weight estimation errors can invalidate the method. UI, which imputes corrupted labels using a model g(X,Z) plus sampled residual errors, achieves the target 90% coverage in all settings where PCP fails, because it does not depend on accurate weight estimation. The TriplyRobust union of Naive CP, PCP, and UI guarantees valid coverage whenever at least one component's assumptions hold, and experiments show it is not overly conservative despite being a union of three intervals.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the privileged conformal prediction (PCP) framework of Feldman & Romano (2024), which itself builds on weighted conformal prediction (Tibshirani et al., 2019) to handle missing privileged information at test time. The key departure is a robustness analysis showing that PCP's validity guarantee can survive significant weight estimation errors under specific structural conditions (e.g., when Naive CP over-covers), complementing prior worst-case analyses by Lei & Candès (2021) and Bhattacharyya & Barber (2024). The paper also introduces uncertain imputation (UI), which draws on the label-noise robustness results of Einbinder et al. (2023) and Sesia et al. (2024) to impute corrupted labels while preserving their uncertainty, offering a weight-free alternative to PCP.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical relevance is clear: in real deployments, true corruption probabilities are rarely available, so knowing when PCP still works (and when it doesn't) directly informs method selection. The triply robust combination (Naive CP ∪ PCP ∪ UI) provides a safety net that guarantees validity if any one component's assumptions hold, which is valuable in high-stakes settings. Key limitations include the strong ignorability assumption (X,Y) ⊥ M | Z and the requirement that Y be well-predictable from (X,Z); open questions include estimating the theoretical validity conditions from data without access to true weights, and extending to multi-annotator or ambiguous-label settings.
