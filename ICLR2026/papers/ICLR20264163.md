## Problem

Existing LLM benchmarks collapse performance to flat accuracy within topical categories, implicitly treating all questions as equally informative. This masks critical differences in model capability that hinge on question difficulty and can flip rankings when the easy-vs-hard item mixture shifts, making it impossible to distinguish routine pattern matching from advanced reasoning at scale.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a binary (or continuous [0,1]) response matrix A ∈ {0,1}^{Q×M} encoding which models answered which questions correctly, after filtering out questions universally solved or failed by all models (only ~2% in their 35,550-question, 30-model setup). It produces two coupled score vectors: π_Q (per-question difficulty) and π_M (per-model competency), from which difficulty-weighted model rankings are derived. The continuous extension allows partial-credit grading without any structural change to the propagation equations.

## Method

EIP constructs a directed bipartite graph where competency edges (question→model) fire on correct answers and difficulty edges (model→question) fire on failures, then performs damped bidirectional score propagation analogous to PageRank: a model's competency is the sum of difficulty weights from questions it solved (divided by each question's total solvers), while a question's difficulty is the sum of competency weights from models that failed it (divided by each model's total failures). Two row-stochastic transition matrices P_{Q→M} and P_{M→Q} drive iterative updates with a single damping factor α that breaks the inherent 2-periodicity of the bipartite walk, guaranteeing convergence to a unique stationary distribution via the Perron-Frobenius theorem. The entire procedure is non-parametric (no per-item logistic fits or curve fitting), requires only one hyperparameter (α), and runs in O(t·QM) time with t ≈ 9 iterations independent of problem scale, converging in ~0.006 s on consumer hardware for 30 models × 35,550 questions.

## Results

EIP achieves 90% consensus alignment with human difficulty judgments, substantially outperforming Simple Rank (62.9%), 1PL-IRT (50.0%), 2PL-IRT (51.4%), and Multi-IRT (52.9%). On the controlled case study with known ground-truth model ordering, EIP is the only method that recovers the correct ranking (M1>M2>M4>M5>M3), while all IRT variants and accuracy-based baselines fail on at least one pair. EIP converges in 0.006 s on consumer hardware (Intel i7) for 30 models × 35,550 questions, versus 1,783 s for 1PL-IRT and 3,787 s for 2PL-IRT. The Kendall's Tau correlation between EIP scores and raw accuracy is 0.8492, confirming EIP refines rather than replaces accuracy-based ranking.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EIP is compared against four baselines: Simple Rank (difficulty by raw error count), 1PL-IRT, 2PL-IRT, and Multi-IRT (all from the IRT literature, Van der Linden 2018). On the primary human-alignment metric (consensus with 20 evaluators), EIP wins decisively at 90% versus 62.9% (Simple Rank), 50.0% (1PL-IRT), 51.4% (2PL-IRT), and 52.9% (Multi-IRT) — a margin of 27–40 percentage points over every IRT variant. On convergence speed, EIP (0.006 s) is roughly 300,000× faster than 1PL-IRT (1,783 s) and 630,000× faster than 2PL-IRT (3,787 s), while Multi-IRT (18.76 s) is the closest IRT competitor but still ~3,000× slower. In the controlled case study (Table 6), EIP is the only method that simultaneously distinguishes M1 from M2 (hard-question performance) and orders M4 > M5 > M3 (medium-difficulty distinctions); 1PL-IRT and 2PL-IRT each get one pair correct but fail the other, while Multi-IRT diverges most severely from ground truth (ranking M4 first, M3 second). EIP also outperforms dataset-weighted accuracy, which uses coarse-grained per-dataset weights and cannot capture intra-dataset difficulty variation.

## Relation to Prior Work

EIP repurposes the damped random-walk mechanism from PageRank (Page et al., 1999) to the LLM evaluation domain, constructing a directed bipartite graph between models and questions and performing bidirectional score propagation. Conceptually, it addresses the same problem as Item Response Theory (separating item difficulty from person ability) and Glicko-2 (pairwise rating), but departs from both by being non-parametric: no per-item logistic fits or pairwise matchups are required, and difficulty emerges dynamically from the full interaction matrix rather than being a fixed latent parameter. The paper positions itself against Easy2Hard-Bench (Ding et al., 2024), which applied IRT and Glicko-2 to LLM benchmarks, and against recent IRT-based re-analyses of benchmark conclusions (Zhou et al., 2026), arguing that EIP's graph-based propagation is more scalable and better aligned with human difficulty judgments.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

EIP offers a practical, near-zero-cost alternative to IRT for difficulty-aware LLM ranking, converging in milliseconds versus hours on consumer hardware, which could make difficulty-weighted evaluation a standard complement to flat accuracy in leaderboard reporting. The finding that model families preserve consistent difficulty-response distributions across parameter scales (e.g., Llama 8B/3B/1B) suggests that scaling shifts absolute performance without altering the relative difficulty structure—a useful insight for benchmark design. Open questions include whether the framework generalizes to multi-turn, agentic, or open-ended generation tasks where binary correctness is ill-defined, whether the 90% human alignment (measured on 70 question pairs) holds at larger scale, and how the single damping hyperparameter should be tuned across domains with very different difficulty distributions.
