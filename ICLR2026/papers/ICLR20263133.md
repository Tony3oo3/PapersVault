## Problem

Despite the prevalence of LLMs, we lack a principled account of how their representational spaces are structured during training and how that structure relates to downstream performance. Existing interpretability methods (probing, mechanistic circuits) operate at the level of individual components and lack a clear connection to established theoretical frameworks for learning. This paper asks whether LLM pre-training can be understood as lossy compression governed by the Information Bottleneck, and whether the resulting representational structure predicts behaviour.

## Input / Output

The method consumes pre-trained LLM weights (across six families: OLMo2, Llama, Gemma, Qwen, Pythia, SmolLM2) and a fixed text corpus (10,000 samples from C4 for pre-training analysis, 10,000 from Tulu for preference analysis) with a 512-token context window. A single forward pass with teacher forcing yields layer-wise embeddings, which are then processed by the soft-entropy estimator to produce mutual information estimates at four n-gram backoff levels (token through quadgram). Outputs include complexity I(X;Z), expressivity I(Y;Z), an optimality ratio, information-plane trajectories over training checkpoints, and preference information I(Z; preferred), all computed per model and per backoff level.

## Method

The paper operationalises Rate Distortion Theory at LLM scale by computing mutual information between model representations and input/output labels using a soft-entropy estimator: each layer's embeddings are normalised to the unit hypersphere, then softly assigned to n=100 uniformly sampled reference points via cosine-similarity softmax, yielding a categorical distribution whose Shannon entropy approximates the representation's entropy. A key technical contribution is a per-model temperature calibration (ε* = 1/√(2d log m)) derived from von Mises-Fisher distribution properties, which prevents softmax saturation in high-dimensional spaces and makes estimates directly comparable across models of different hidden dimensions. Conditional distributions P(Z|X) and P(Z|Y) are approximated via n-gram backoff (token, bigram, trigram, quadgram), where each level conditions the single-token embedding on progressively wider preceding/trailing context rather than computing entropy over wider embedding windows. The IB bound is computed numerically via Blahut-Arimoto iteration where tractable, and approximated as the line I(X;Z)=I(Y;Z) below the saturation point for larger tokenizers. The central finding is that pre-training follows the predicted two-phase IB trajectory (expansion of expressivity, then compression of complexity toward the bound), and that the optimality ratio I(Y;Z)/I(X;Z) significantly predicts downstream benchmark performance across 47 models (r=0.52, p<0.001), while preference information independently predicts performance (r=0.76, p<0.001).

## Results

Across 47 open-weight models from 6 families, the optimality of a model's compression (I(Y;Z)/I(X;Z)) significantly predicts aggregate performance on six benchmarks (MMLU Pro, BBH, Math LVL5, IFEval, GPQA, MuSR) with r=0.52, p<0.001. Preference information in a model's representations is an even stronger predictor (r=0.76, p<0.001), while complexity alone (r=-0.38, p=0.006) and expressivity alone (r=0.08, p=0.575) are weaker or non-significant predictors. All 75 open-weight models analyzed converge near the Information Bottleneck bound on optimal compression, with the 7B and 32B OLMo2 models closely following the predicted two-phase expansion-then-compression trajectory, while the 1B model fails to achieve meaningful compression and oscillates off the bound.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines; instead it provides an information-theoretic analysis framework applied across model families. The key comparison is across 6 model families (OLMo2, Llama, Gemma, Qwen, Pythia, SmolLM2) and 3 scales (1B, 7B, 32B), showing that all converge to similar points on the IB bound regardless of architecture, training data, or hyperparameters. The 7B and 32B OLMo2 models follow the canonical two-phase IB trajectory most cleanly, while the 1B model completes the expansion phase but fails to compress, oscillating off the bound. Pythia 6.9B appears undertrained (still expanding late in training) due to seeing only 7.5% of the data compared to OLMo2 1B, while Pythia 1.4B achieves better compression given its smaller data budget. Post-training (e.g., Llama pre-trained vs. post-trained) increases preference information while minimally changing complexity, supporting the framing that pre-training establishes the compression structure and post-training edits the information content.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper operationalises the Information Bottleneck theory of deep learning (Tishby & Zaslavsky, 2015; Shwartz-Ziv & Tishby, 2017) at LLM scale, where prior empirical confirmations were limited to small feed-forward networks on MNIST. It departs from the dominant mechanistic-interpretability paradigm (sparse autoencoders, circuit analysis) by analysing the model as a whole through Rate Distortion Theory, and from prior information-theoretic transformer analyses (Voita et al., 2019) by introducing a scalable soft-entropy estimator (Conklin, 2025) that avoids the intractable memory requirements of dimension-wise binning or clustering-based quantisation. The work also connects LLM training to the broader cognitive-science literature on learning as compression (Feldman, 2016; Chater & Vitányi, 2003), positioning pre-training within a unified information-theoretic account of representation learning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that diverse model families (OLMo2, Llama, Gemma, Qwen, Pythia, SmolLM2) all converge near the same point on the IB bound suggests a kind of universality in how transformer architectures compress natural language, independent of training recipe or data mixture. The correlation between compression optimality and downstream performance (r=0.52) and between preference information and performance (r=0.76) offers a potential lightweight alternative to full benchmark suites for model selection or early stopping, though these applications remain unvalidated. Key limitations include the approximate nature of the entropy estimates (n-gram backoff caps context at quadgrams, single forward pass with teacher forcing), the restriction to autoregressive decoder-only models, and the fact that the IB bound is assumed linear rather than numerically computed for large tokenizers. Open questions include how the framework extends to non-autoregressive or multimodal models, whether the compression perspective explains emergent capabilities, and how post-training phases reshape the information structure beyond the preference signal measured here.
