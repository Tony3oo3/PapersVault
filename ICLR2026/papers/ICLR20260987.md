## Problem

Existing pursuit-evasion game (PEG) solvers either assume perfect information or incur exponential time complexity, making them impractical for real-time deployment on dynamically changing graphs. No prior method provides worst-case robust pursuit strategies under partial observability where the evader has global information and can predict pursuers' actions (asynchronous moves). This paper is the first to address the combined challenge of partial observability, asynchronous evader advantage, and real-time applicability across unseen graph structures.

## Input / Output

The GNN policy receives as input the graph adjacency structure, current pursuer positions, the set of possible evader positions (Pos) maintained by a belief preservation mechanism, and a belief distribution over evader positions. Node features are initialized with shortest-path distances to all pursuers, then concatenated with Pos and belief before encoding. The output is a sequential joint action for m homogeneous pursuers (each choosing to move to an adjacent vertex or stay), produced by a pointer network over each pursuer's neighbor set. The evader is only observable within graph distance 2 of any pursuer, and the evader acts asynchronously after observing the pursuers' chosen actions.

## Method

The paper proves that a dynamic programming (DP) distance table for Markov PEGs yields strictly optimal strategies even when the evader moves asynchronously (Theorem 2), then extends this to partial observability via a belief preservation mechanism that tracks a set of possible evader positions (Pos) and a probability distribution (belief) updated each timestep in O(|V|) time. The belief-averaged pursuit policy minimizes the expected worst-case capture distance over the belief distribution rather than the maximum over Pos, avoiding overly pessimistic behavior. For real-time generalization, the belief preservation is embedded into the Equilibrium Policy Generalization (EPG) framework: a parameter-sharing GNN (6-layer masked multi-head self-attention encoder + pointer network decoder) is trained via discrete SAC across 300 diverse graphs against the provably optimal asynchronous-move DP evader, with a KL-divergence guidance term toward the belief-averaged DP policy for efficient exploration. This yields a zero-shot generalizable pursuer with O(n²m) inference time versus O(n^{m+1}) for recomputing DP policies.

## Results

The primary metric is pursuit success rate (fraction of 500 episodes where the evader is captured within 128 timesteps) under partial observability (observation range = 2). Against the strongest evader (DPasync, the provably optimal asynchronous-move DP policy), the R2PS pursuer achieves success rates of 0.20–1.00 across 10 unseen real-world graphs, while PSRO (trained directly on those same test graphs) achieves only 0.00–0.52. Against the stationary evader (Stay), R2PS achieves 1.00 on all 10 graphs. Inference time for the GNN-based RL policy is under 0.01 s per timestep on GPU, compared to 6–139 s for recomputing the DP policy on the same graphs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is PSRO (Lanctot et al., 2017), a standard game-RL method trained directly on the 10 test graphs for 10 iterations (10,000 episodes each). R2PS is trained zero-shot on 300 unseen graphs (150 synthetic + 150 random urban) and never encounters the test graphs during training. Against the stationary evader (Stay), both methods perform well, with R2PS achieving 1.00 on all graphs versus PSRO's 0.93–1.00. The gap widens dramatically against the synchronous DP evader (DPsync), where R2PS scores 0.90–1.00 while PSRO drops to 0.00–0.97. The most striking comparison is against the asynchronous-move DP evader (DPasync), the provably optimal worst-case opponent: R2PS achieves 0.20–1.00 while PSRO collapses to 0.00–0.52, with PSRO failing completely (0.00) on 4 out of 10 graphs. Even against the best-responding asynchronous evader (BRasync), which is trained specifically against R2PS on the test graphs, R2PS maintains success rates above 0.50 on 5 of 10 graphs, confirming worst-case robustness under zero-shot generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Equilibrium Policy Generalization (EPG) framework of Lu et al. (2025a), which first demonstrated cross-graph zero-shot generalization for pursuit-evasion via GNN-based RL, by incorporating two critical real-world adversarial factors: partial observability for pursuers and asynchronous (predictive) moves for the evader. The theoretical foundation builds on the DP algorithm from the same EPG paper, with a new proof that the induced minimax policies remain strictly optimal under asynchronous evader moves. Conceptually, the paper departs from prior PEG-RL work (MT-PSRO, Grasper) that assumed perfect information and synchronous play, and from classical DP/marking algorithms that are computationally infeasible for real-time use on dynamically changing graphs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first approach to worst-case robust real-time pursuit strategies under partial observability, addressing a gap that limits the practical security applicability of existing PEG solvers. The ~10,000× inference speedup over DP (0.01s vs 100+s on large graphs) makes real-time deployment on dynamically changing urban graphs feasible. Key limitations include reliance on a uniform evader policy in belief updates when no prior is available, and success rates dropping to 20–40% on harder real-world graphs (Sagrada Familia, The Bund) with only 2 pursuers. Open questions include whether learned opponent models can replace the uniform belief assumption, how the approach scales to 3+ pursuers with partial observability, and whether the cross-graph generalization gap over PSRO persists under adversarially adapted evaders.
