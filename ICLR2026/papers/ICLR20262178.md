## Problem

Reinforcement learning agents are highly sensitive to reward function specification: sparse rewards provide insufficient learning signal, while hand-crafted dense rewards are vulnerable to reward hacking. Reward Machines (RMs) offer a principled automata-based formalism for structured, compositional reward design, but their practical use has been limited by the complexity of manual, expert-driven construction. A critical gap remains in translating the high-level task understanding that foundation models (FMs) can extract from natural language into the concrete, structured reward signals that RL agents require.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a high-level natural language task description (e.g., 'pick up the key, open the door, reach the goal') together with a visual observation of the environment as input to the FM generation pipeline. It produces three coupled artifacts: a formal RM specification (finite states, transition function, reward function), executable Python labeling functions that map MDP observations to RM event symbols, and a natural language instruction string for each RM state. During RL training, the agent's policy receives the environment state concatenated with the embedding of the current RM state's instruction, and the total reward is the sum of the base MDP reward and the RM's incremental reward for sub-goal completion.

## Method

ARM-FM introduces Language-Aligned Reward Machines (LARMs), which extend standard RMs by attaching a natural language instruction to each automaton state and mapping it to a fixed-dimensional embedding vector via a pretrained language model. LARMs are constructed automatically through an iterative self-improvement loop in which a generator FM proposes the RM specification, labeling code, and state instructions, and a critic FM evaluates and refines them over N rounds, with optional human intervention. During RL training, the agent operates in the cross-product MDP of environment and RM states, with the policy conditioned on the language embedding of the active RM state rather than a discrete state index; this creates a semantically grounded skill space where instructions like 'pick up a blue key' and 'pick up a red key' occupy nearby regions, enabling experience reuse and zero-shot generalization to novel task compositions. The paper proves that under a no-positive-reward-cycle condition (satisfied by all generated LARMs), the LARM-augmented reward preserves the optimal policy of the original sparse task, and empirically validates the approach across MiniGrid, Craftium (3D Minecraft), Meta-World (continuous robotics), and XLand-MiniGrid (multi-task generalization).

## Results

On MiniGrid DoorKey tasks of increasing size (Figure 6), DQN+RM consistently outperforms all baselines on both fixed and procedurally generated maps. On three complex long-horizon tasks (UnlockToUnlock, BlockedUnlockPickup, KeyCorridor; Figure 7), DQN+RM is the only method to achieve near-perfect reward, while all baselines fail to make any progress. In the Craftium 3D Minecraft environment, PPO+LARM consistently completes the full diamond-mining sequence (wood → stone → iron → diamond), whereas baseline PPO makes minimal progress. On Meta-World robotic manipulation, the LARM-augmented SAC agent achieves higher success rates than sparse-reward SAC across five tasks, and on XLand-MiniGrid the full method maintains high success even when trained on 10 simultaneous tasks, while baselines degrade.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four baselines across its evaluation suite. (1) Unmodified DQN: loses on all MiniGrid tasks (Figures 6–7), showing no meaningful learning on sparse-reward environments. (2) DQN+ICM (Intrinsic Curiosity Module, Pathak et al. 2017): the strongest exploration baseline tested, yet still fails to solve any of the complex long-horizon tasks (UnlockToUnlock, BlockedUnlockPickup, KeyCorridor) and underperforms DQN+RM on all DoorKey variants. (3) ReAct (Yao et al. 2023), an LLM-as-agent baseline that generates reasoning traces in-context: fails to make progress on all MiniGrid tasks, confirming that non-learning, in-context methods cannot handle the low-level perception and control required. (4) CLIP-based VLM zero-shot reward model (Rocamonde et al. 2023, Appendix A.7.4): fails completely across all evaluated MiniGrid tasks, attributed to VLMs' limitations with spatial reasoning and visually abstract environments. In every head-to-head comparison, ARM-FM (DQN+RM or PPO+LARM) wins decisively, with the gap being most dramatic on long-horizon tasks where baselines show zero learning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Reward Machine (RM) formalism (Icarte et al., 2022) by automating RM construction via foundation models and augmenting each automaton state with natural-language instructions and their embeddings, creating Language-Aligned Reward Machines (LARMs). It departs from prior FM+RL work (Eureka, Motif, ONI) that produces opaque reward models or programmatic rewards, instead generating structured, compositional, and interpretable automata directly from language. The self-improvement loop with paired generator/critic FMs (Tian et al., 2024) is repurposed for iterative RM refinement, and the language-embedding conditioning of the policy contrasts with prior approaches that treat RM states as isolated symbols (Alsadat et al., 2025) or use topology-based embeddings (RAD, Yalcinkaya et al., 2024).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ARM-FM provides a principled bridge between the high-level reasoning of FMs and the structured, dense reward signals that RL agents require, with a theoretical guarantee that the generated reward structure preserves the optimal policy of the original sparse task. The zero-shot generalization result—where a single policy solves a novel composite task purely because its sub-goal embeddings fall in a familiar region of the skill space—suggests a path toward truly compositional, reusable RL skills. Key limitations include the dependence on correct labeling functions (which are FM-generated Python code), the optional but quality-improving human verification step, and the fact that the zero-shot generalization is demonstrated only on simple compositional grid-world tasks. Open questions include whether formal verification can replace human checks, whether the approach scales to truly open-ended domains, and how robust the embedding-based skill transfer is to paraphrasing or semantically distant but functionally equivalent sub-goals.
