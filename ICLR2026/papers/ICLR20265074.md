## Problem

LLM agents excel in digital, symbolic environments but fail to transfer to physical-world tasks that require maintaining a robust 3D spatial mental model over long action sequences under partial observation. Existing benchmarks either couple these cognitive demands with complex visual perception (making failure attribution impossible) or test only one facet in isolation, leaving no controlled diagnostic tool to pinpoint whether an agent's failure stems from spatial reasoning, long-horizon planning, or belief-state construction.

## Input / Output

The agent receives one of three observation modalities depending on the tier: a 54-character symbolic string (Tier 1), a single 2D unfolded map image showing all 54 facelets (Tier 2), or a partial image of one face or a corner perspective showing three adjacent faces (Tier 3). The agent responds with sequences of Singmaster-notation face rotations (F, B, L, R, U, D and their primes) and, in Tier 3, view-transformation commands to explore. Evaluation is conducted under a ReAct protocol (Thought-Code-Observation blocks, max 20 steps, 30-minute timeout), and the primary outputs are pass rate, average number of make_move calls (#MM), and a normalized move ratio (#MR = #MM / optimal depth).

## Method

CubeBench formalizes the Rubik's Cube as a POMDP with a three-tiered observation space that progressively strips away perceptual scaffolding: full symbolic state → full visual state → partial visual state, thereby isolating planning, spatial reasoning, and active exploration respectively. The diagnostic framework runs three experiments: (1) a Basic Agent with only fundamental tools to establish baseline capability, (2) the same agent augmented with dense progressive rewards (sticker, face, or heuristic metrics) to test whether local feedback compensates for planning deficits, and (3) agents equipped with Kociemba two-phase solver tools in two variants—Standard-Solver (agent must convert its perceived state into the solver's strict facelet-string format) and Ideal-Solver (conversion is automated)—to separately isolate long-horizon planning, spatial-to-symbolic translation, and exploration under partial observation. Tasks are generated at verified optimal depths (1–4 for short-horizon, 8–20 for long-horizon) using a provably optimal solver that confirms no shorter solution exists, yielding 160 unique evaluation configurations per model. A Policy Gradient (REINFORCE) MLP baseline is also trained with curriculum learning to contextualize LLM performance against a classical RL agent.

## Results

Across all 18 evaluated LLMs, the pass rate on long-horizon tasks (depths 8–20) is a uniform 0.00% in every observation modality, exposing a fundamental failure in long-term planning. On short-horizon Full Symbolic tasks (depths 1–4), GPT-5 achieves the highest pass rate of 0.75, matching the MLP Policy Gradient baseline (0.75), while the next-best models (gpt-oss-120b, Grok-4) reach only 0.20. Performance drops sharply with visual inputs: GPT-5 falls to 0.20 on Full Visual and 0.40 on Face View, and most other models drop to 0.00–0.05. When equipped with an optimal solver tool, GPT-5's long-horizon Full Symbolic pass rate jumps from 0.00 to 0.95, confirming that planning—not perception—is the primary bottleneck.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 18 LLMs plus a trained MLP Policy Gradient baseline. GPT-5 leads on short-horizon Full Symbolic tasks (0.75), but this is matched exactly by the simple REINFORCE-trained MLP (0.75), indicating that even the best LLM does not surpass a classical RL agent in this setting. All LLMs uniformly fail (0.00) on long-horizon tasks regardless of modality, while the MLP also fails (0.00) but with far fewer moves (400 max vs. hundreds of thousands for search-based LLMs). Dense reward signals (face, sticker, heuristic) provide modest gains on short-horizon tasks (e.g., GPT-5 Full Visual improves from 0.20 to 0.55 with face reward) but yield zero improvement on long-horizon tasks. The solver-tool ablation (Table 4) shows that outsourcing planning to a Kociemba solver lifts GPT-5's long-horizon Full Symbolic pass rate from 0.00 to 0.95 (Standard-Solver) and 1.00 (Ideal-Solver), while Gemini 2.5 Pro and Claude Sonnet 4 remain near 0.00 on visual/partial-observation settings even with the ideal solver, isolating spatial reasoning and exploration as the remaining bottlenecks.

## Relation to Prior Work

CubeBench carves new ground by repurposing the Rubik's Cube—a well-studied combinatorial object—as a controlled diagnostic laboratory for LLM spatial cognition, rather than as a target for optimal solvers. It departs from embodied simulators (Habitat, ALFRED, BEHAVIOR-100) that entangle perception, control, and reasoning, and from static 3D scene benchmarks like MindCube that lack dynamic, state-altering interaction. The three-tier observation design (symbolic → full visual → partial visual) and the progressive solver-tool diagnostic (Basic → Standard-Solver → Ideal-Solver) are novel methodological contributions that allow factorized attribution of failure to specific cognitive faculties, a capability absent from prior agentic benchmarks (Search, Code, GUI, ARC-AGI-3).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The universal 0% pass rate on long-horizon tasks across all tested LLMs, combined with a simple Policy Gradient MLP matching GPT-5 on short-horizon symbolic tasks, provides a stark, reproducible diagnostic that current agents lack robust spatial mental models and long-horizon planning. The factorized diagnostic framework offers a practical 'first stop' before expensive embodied evaluations: if an agent cannot maintain a consistent 3D model in this simplified, deterministic domain, it is unlikely to succeed in complex physical worlds. Open questions include whether the findings generalize beyond a single rigid object, whether the symbolic-to-visual performance cliff can be bridged by better visual grounding, and whether the observed emergent tool-learning behaviors can be harnessed for more capable agents.
