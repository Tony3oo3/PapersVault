## Problem

Bayesian deep learning lacks a principled way to choose well-specified priors over neural network weights, which lack interpretability and for which standard convenience priors inadvertently reduce large BNNs to Gaussian processes. This paper asks whether meta-learning can design well-specified BNN priors by learning them from a collection of related datasets, bridging Bayesian deep learning and probabilistic meta-learning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264342/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The BNNP takes a context set of labeled input-output pairs and a set of target input locations, producing a posterior predictive distribution over target outputs. Unusually, the latent variable is the BNN's weight matrix itself and the decoder is the BNN parameterized by a sample from that latent variable, so the model simultaneously performs amortised inference and meta-learns a weights prior. Per-layer inference networks map each context point independently to pseudo-likelihood parameters (activation targets and noise levels), enabling within-task minibatching for scalability to large context sets without storing all observations in memory.

## Method

The core mechanism is the amortised linear layer, which computes each layer's weight posterior in closed form via Bayesian linear regression, conditioned on samples from previous layers' posteriors and pseudo-observations produced by per-layer inference networks. Stacking these layers yields a variational posterior factorised into layerwise conditionals, with the BNNP's latent variable being the full weight set and its decoder being the BNN itself. Training uses the PP-AVI objective, combining the log posterior-predictive density of the target set with the standard ELBO on the context set, which is proven to simultaneously target accurate approximate posteriors, a faithful prior, and high-quality predictions. The paper further introduces within-task minibatching via sequential Bayesian inference for memory-efficient prediction on large context sets, and a prior-flexibility knob that fixes a subset of prior parameters to prevent overfitting in data-scarce meta-learning regimes.

## Results

The BNNP achieves the lowest KL divergence between approximate and true posteriors among all tested VI methods (MFVI, GIVI, UCVI, LCVI, FCVI), demonstrating high-quality approximate inference under well-specified priors. On predictive performance (measured by per-datapoint LPPD and MAE across 16 test datasets), the BNNP consistently outperforms SWAG, MFVI, LMC, GIVI, and HMC under both standard and learned priors, with the learned prior boosting all methods but leaving the BNNP as the top performer. In data-scarce meta-learning settings (Abalone with 2 training tasks, Paul15 with 10), a BNNP with a partially learnable prior (80% and 50% of prior parameters trainable, respectively) outperforms fully-flexible neural processes (original NP, BNP, AR-TNP) that overfit.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MFVI (Blundell et al., 2015) and GIVI (Ober & Aitchison, 2021), the BNNP achieves substantially lower KL divergence to the true posterior (Figure 2), with the gap widening at smaller likelihood noise levels where unstructured approximations degrade most. Against SWAG (Maddox et al., 2019), LMC (Rossky et al., 1978), and HMC (Neal, 1992), the BNNP delivers the best LPPD and MAE under both standard and well-specified priors (Figure 6), confirming that even with a good prior, inference quality still matters. On the real-world ERA5 precipitation task, the BNNP outperforms SGLD (Welling & Teh, 2011) and SGHMC (Chen et al., 2014), particularly in regions with no context data where prior quality dominates. In the data-scarce meta-learning experiments (Figure 7), the BNNP with a partially learnable prior beats the original latent-variable NP (Garnelo et al., 2018b), BNP (Volpp et al., 2021), and AR-TNP (Nguyen & Grover, 2022), which all overfit when given full prior flexibility. The key finding is that no single method dominates universally: the BNNP's advantage is most pronounced under well-specified priors and in data-scarce regimes, while the gap narrows under standard priors where all methods suffer from prior misspecification.

## Relation to Prior Work

The BNNP sits at the intersection of two well-developed lines of work: layerwise variational inference for BNNs (Ober and Aitchison, 2021) and latent-variable neural processes (Garnelo et al., 2018; Volpp et al., 2021). It extends the former by amortising the pseudo-likelihood parameters via per-dataset inference networks and meta-learning the weight priors, and extends the latter by making the latent variable the BNN weights themselves rather than an abstract context representation. The PP-AVI training objective is novel in combining the posterior-predictive likelihood (standard for NPs) with the ELBO (standard for BNNs), and the paper provides a formal proof that this jointly targets accurate posteriors, faithful priors, and high-quality predictions. The approach departs from prior meta-learning of BNN priors (Ravi and Beatson, 2019; Rothfuss et al., 2021) by avoiding restrictive hierarchical hyper-priors and instead learning unitwise-factorised Gaussian priors directly.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The BNNP provides the first practical tool for studying BNNs under well-specified priors, enabling previously unanswerable questions about the relative importance of inference quality versus prior quality. The key empirical finding—that even under a learned, well-specified prior, approximate inference method still substantially affects predictive performance—challenges the hope that 'getting the prior right' might make the inference method less critical. Practical contributions include within-task minibatching (rare in NPs) and a prior-flexibility knob for data-scarce meta-learning. Limitations include unfavourable scaling with architecture width (cubic per layer), the single-dataset case remaining unsolved, and the BNAM variant lacking stochastic-process consistency, which restricts the model's generality as a drop-in NP replacement.
