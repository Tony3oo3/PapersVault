## Problem

Parallel sampling for test-time scaling is severely limited by diversity collapse: fine-tuning and RL cause models to concentrate on a few reasoning modes, so additional samples reproduce the same mistakes rather than exploring alternative strategies. Standard training on diverse data (e.g., multiple teachers or algorithms) fails to preserve this diversity at inference, and token-level temperature sampling does not produce meaningful variation in high-level reasoning modes. The paper asks whether explicitly structuring test-time compute allocation across reasoning modes can unlock superior Pass@k scaling.

## Input / Output

At inference, the method takes a text problem prompt and, for the prefix variant, prepends a discrete mode-conditioning token (e.g., [Mode 1], [Mode 2], or a teacher-identity token) to each sample. The k-sample budget is split evenly across modes rather than drawn i.i.d. from a single distribution, so the output is a structured set of trajectories guaranteed to cover all trained modes. For the separate-models variant, the same prompt is fed to multiple specialist models in parallel. The final answer is selected from the pooled candidates via verification or majority vote.

## Method

ModC introduces two training instantiations for explicit mode control: (i) partitioning training data by mode and training separate specialist models, and (ii) prepending mode-specific prefix tokens to a single model so that each prefix reliably elicits a distinct reasoning strategy. At test time, the sampling budget is allocated evenly across modes, and a Jensen's-inequality argument shows this strictly dominates sampling from the model's own uncertain mixture whenever mode success probabilities differ. For unlabeled data, gradient clustering (per-example gradients projected via Rademacher random projection, then clustered) automatically discovers modes, achieving up to 10% Pass@k gains on NuminaMath without any side information. The framework composes with RL: ModC-initialized policies retain their mode diversity after standard RL and further amplify the Pass@k gains of diversity-inducing RL methods like Pass@k RL.

## Results

On the Countdown graph-search task, ModC with separate models improves Pass@1024 by up to 8% on the natural test set and up to 20% on the adversarial test set (where each problem is solvable by only one of BFS or DFS). On MATH500 (short CoT), ModC achieves up to 10% Pass@k gains on Qwen2.5-0.5B and 15% on OLMo2-7B over the best single-teacher baseline. On AIME 2025 (long CoT), ModC delivers a ~4× inference efficiency gain, matching the Pass@1024 of standard training with only k=256 samples while also pushing the maximum attainable Pass@k higher. Gradient clustering for automated mode discovery yields up to 10% additional gains on NuminaMath without any predefined mode labels.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four training strategies: (1) mixed-teacher/mixed-data standard training, (2) best single-teacher training, (3) ModC with separate specialist models, and (4) ModC with mode-specific prefixes. A key finding is that naively mixing data from both teachers underperforms or is at best comparable to the stronger single-teacher baseline, confirming that standard training fails to exploit teacher diversity. ModC (both variants) consistently outperforms both the mixed and best-single-teacher baselines across all model families (Qwen2.5, OLMo2) and scales (0.5B–7B). On the Countdown task, ModC with separate models outperforms ModC with prefixes, suggesting knowledge transfer across modes is less critical for algorithmic search; conversely, on math reasoning (MATH500, AIME 2025), ModC with prefixes generally outperforms separate models, indicating shared linguistic/mathematical foundations matter. As a control, random partitioning of training data into two groups sometimes shows gains but never matches ModC with proper mode partitioning, and enforcing a 50-50 DFS/BFS data balance does not improve over standard training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the prefix-conditioning mechanism from CTRL (Keskar et al., 2019) and the gradient-clustering pipeline from LESS (Xia et al., 2024) and Prismatic Synthesis (Jung et al., 2025), repurposing them for a new purpose: explicitly allocating test-time compute across reasoning modes rather than for controllable generation or data selection. It departs from the dominant line of work on diversity collapse (Dang et al., 2025; Sessa et al., 2024; Chow et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025b), which modifies training objectives or ensembles weights to maintain diversity, by instead taking a data-centric conditioning approach that structures the sampling budget across modes at inference time. The paper also contrasts with Mixture-of-Experts, noting that ModC routes all data through the full model rather than partitioning parameters, and positions itself as complementary to (not competing with) RL-based diversity interventions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a 4× inference efficiency gain on long-CoT reasoning and consistent Pass@k improvements across model families (Qwen2.5, OLMo2) and scales (0.5B–7B) make ModC a low-cost, broadly applicable technique for any pipeline relying on parallel sampling. The counterintuitive finding that naively mixing diverse teacher data underperforms the best single teacher challenges the common assumption that more diverse training data automatically helps, and the gradient-clustering extension (98.7% F1 in recovering teacher identity) makes the approach practical for unlabeled data. Open questions include scaling beyond two modes, learning adaptive allocation policies at test time, and integrating ModC with RL in a way that gradually relaxes mode constraints as training progresses.
