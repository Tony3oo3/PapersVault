## Problem

Existing LLM fairness evaluations rely on at most nine demographic labels in general domains, lack theoretical grounding in legal philosophy, and ignore procedural fairness factors (e.g., defender type, court hierarchy, trial openness) that empirically influence judicial decisions. No prior work provides a systematic, statistically rigorous framework to assess whether LLMs acting as judges exhibit human-analogous unfairness across both substantive and procedural dimensions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260813/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes 177,100 counterfactually generated case-fact prompts (derived from 1,100 real Chinese criminal judicial documents) where a single trigger sentence is swapped with each of its alternative label values, and feeds them to an LLM in a zero-shot role-play prompt instructing it to render a sentencing decision in months. The LLM must output a JSON structure containing the predicted sentence; the evaluation pipeline then computes three fairness metrics per model: a weighted inconsistency rate, per-label bias coefficients from fixed-effect regressions, and per-group imbalanced inaccuracy (MAE/MAPE) relative to real sentences.

## Method

The core contribution is a three-metric evaluation protocol built on counterfactual prompting and high-dimensional econometric inference. For each of 65 labels (161 values) organized into a substance/procedure × demographic/non-demographic taxonomy, the paper generates separate queries per counterfactual value (inspired by APriCot) to isolate each factor's effect. Bias is measured via REGHDFE fixed-effect regressions of log(sentencing) on label dummies with document-level fixed effects and cluster-robust standard errors; a Bernoulli test over 96 label values then determines whether a model's significant results are systematic rather than random. Inconsistency is the effective-sample-size-weighted proportion of documents where the LLM's output changes across label values, and imbalanced inaccuracy applies the same regression framework to absolute prediction errors. The framework is validated across 16 LLMs at temperatures 0 and 1, with robustness checks and correlation analyses linking accuracy gains to increased bias.

## Results

Across 15 LLMs evaluated at temperature 0, overall bias rates range from 18% (LFM40BMoE) to 60% (Phi4), with procedural bias consistently exceeding substantive bias for most models. Average inconsistency exceeds 15%, meaning roughly 18% of judicial documents yield different outputs under varied label values. The mean Weighted Average MAE across all models is 64.871 months (over 5 years of sentencing deviation), and the mean Weighted Average MAPE is 219%, indicating LLM predictions are on average more than double the real sentence length. Bernoulli tests confirm systematic bias for 14 of 15 models and significant imbalanced inaccuracy for 14 of 15 models at temperature 0.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical evaluation paper rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison; the 16 LLMs serve as the subjects under test. Phi4 exhibits the highest overall bias (39/65 labels significant, 60%) while LFM40BMoE shows the lowest (12/65, 18%), though even LFM40BMoE's bias is not statistically ruled out (p=0.205 at 5%). Procedural bias is consistently more pronounced than substantive bias across models (e.g., Phi4: 55% procedural vs. 68% substantive; GLM4: 45% vs. 36%), and demographic labels trigger significantly more bias than non-demographic ones. Notably, increased model accuracy correlates with increased bias: Phi4 has the lowest MAE (47.995) yet the highest bias count (39), while LFM40BMoE has the highest MAE (111.115) and the lowest bias count (12). Model size, release date, and country of origin show no significant effect on any fairness metric, and raising temperature from 0 to 1 increases inconsistency but slightly reduces the number of significantly biased labels.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM fairness evaluation literature (BBQ, PANDA, Winogender, etc., which use at most nine labels and lack theoretical grounding) into the judicial domain by constructing a jurisprudentially grounded framework that explicitly separates substance from procedure and demographic from non-demographic factors. It repurposes the LEEC legal dataset's extra-legal labels and counterfactual prompting techniques from APriCot (Moore et al., 2024) and Kumar et al. (2024), but departs from prior work by incorporating 65 labels (including procedural factors like defender type, court hierarchy, and trial openness that are absent from judicial records) and by applying fixed-effect regression with Bernoulli tests borrowed from quantitative social science to distinguish systematic bias from random noise. The paper also builds on empirical legal scholarship (Quintanilla et al., Lopes, Pozzulo et al.) to identify which extra-legal factors are theoretically expected to influence judicial decisions, giving the evaluation a causal-inference flavor absent from prior LLM fairness benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first comprehensive, statistically rigorous benchmark for LLM judicial fairness, and its finding that all 16 tested models exhibit significant bias, inconsistency, and imbalanced inaccuracy underscores that current LLMs are not ready for high-stakes judicial deployment. The accuracy–bias trade-off (more accurate models are more biased) is a critical open question for model developers: improving predictive performance on real-world data may inherently entrench existing judicial disparities. Key limitations include the exclusive focus on Chinese criminal law, the small number of models tested (16), and the reliance on a single sentencing outcome (limited imprisonment in months), leaving open whether findings generalize to other legal systems, civil matters, or non-custodial penalties. The publicly released JudiFair dataset and JustEva toolkit lower the barrier for future replication and extension to other jurisdictions.
