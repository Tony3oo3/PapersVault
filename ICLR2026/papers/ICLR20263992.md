## Problem

Animal behavior video analysis in neuroscience relies on task-specific models (pose estimators, hand-crafted features, PCA) that require extensive manual labeling and fail to exploit the large volumes of unlabeled video generated by controlled experiments. No accessible framework existed for creating experiment-specific, multi-task behavior analysis models that leverage this unlabeled data, leaving a significant gap between the capabilities of modern self-supervised vision transformers and the practical needs of behavioral neuroscience labs.

## Input / Output

BEAST consumes raw RGB video frames from controlled behavioral experiments (static backgrounds, fixed camera angles) at resolutions ranging from 256×256 to 384×512 pixels, split into 16×16 patches by a ViT-B/16 backbone. The pretrained backbone outputs two types of features: a single CLS token per frame (global representation) and per-patch embeddings (spatial features), which are then fed into task-specific heads—reduced rank regression or TCN encoders for neural activity prediction, keypoint heatmap regression for pose estimation, and TCN classifiers for per-frame behavior labels. A key design choice is that pretraining is performed per experimental setup (e.g., one model for IBL head-fixed mice, another for CRIM13 social interactions), producing domain-tailored rather than universal representations.

## Method

BEAST pretrains a ViT-B/16 by jointly optimizing a masked autoencoding loss (75% patch masking, reconstruction via a transformer decoder with MSE) and a temporal contrastive InfoNCE loss computed on CLS token projections through a nonlinear head (linear + BatchNorm + ReLU + linear). The critical architectural innovation over VIC-MAE is the frame sampling strategy for the contrastive loss: positive pairs are restricted to frames within ±1 frame of the anchor, while negatives include both temporally distant frames from the same video and frames from other videos—addressing the fact that behavioral experiments produce long recordings with repeating behaviors that would make VIC-MAE's 'any same-video frame is positive' strategy degenerate. To ensure training efficiency, frames are selected via a two-stage pipeline: motion-energy filtering (removing the bottom 50th percentile of inter-frame pixel differences) followed by k-means clustering to pick visually diverse anchor frames. The model is initialized with ImageNet MAE weights, pretrained for 800 epochs on experiment-specific unlabeled frames (loss = L_MSE + λ·L_InfoNCE, λ=0.01–0.02), and then either used zero-shot or fine-tuned end-to-end for downstream tasks.

## Results

On action segmentation, BEAST (IN+PT) with patch embeddings achieves F1 of 0.87 on IBL and 0.81 on CalMS21, outperforming the TREBA baseline (0.72 on CalMS21) and the SimBA keypoint-based pipeline. On neural encoding, BEAST (IN+PT+FT) reaches 0.347 BPS on IBL and 0.326 BPS on IBL-whisker, surpassing all keypoint-based and PCA-based baselines in both linear (RRR) and nonlinear (TCN) regimes. On pose estimation with only 100 labeled frames, BEAST reduces pixel error relative to ensemble standard deviation across all four datasets (IBL, Mirror-mouse, Mirror-fish, CRIM13) compared to the AP-10K-pretrained ResNet-50 baseline, with the largest gains on the IBL dataset.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BEAST outperforms all 11 baselines across the three evaluated tasks. On neural encoding, BEAST (IN+PT+FT) achieves 0.347 BPS on IBL, beating frozen CEBRA, DINOv2, CLIP, and VideoMAE features, as well as keypoint-based and PCA-based representations; even the zero-shot ViT-M (IN) baseline (0.321 BPS) already exceeds prior keypoint methods. On action segmentation, BEAST patch embeddings (F1 0.81 on CalMS21) substantially outperform TREBA (0.72) and SimBA, while matching or exceeding keypoint-based models that required 15,000 labeled frames for pose estimation. On pose estimation, BEAST consistently outperforms the AP-10K-pretrained ResNet-50, DeepLabCut, fine-tuned DINO, DINOv2, and Segment Anything encoders, with the MAE component of the combined loss driving most of the pixel-level gains. The contrastive-only variant (ViT-C) underperforms MAE-only on pose estimation, confirming that the two losses capture complementary information: MAE for spatial detail and contrastive learning for temporal structure. Notably, BEAST eliminates the need for a separate pose estimation preprocessing step entirely, reducing the months-long labeling pipeline to unlabeled video pretraining alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

BEAST builds directly on VIC-MAE (Hernandez et al., 2024), which combines masked autoencoding with contrastive losses, and on MAE (He et al., 2022) for the reconstruction objective. The key conceptual departure is a redesigned frame sampling strategy for the contrastive loss: instead of treating any two frames from the same video as positives (as in VIC-MAE), BEAST restricts positives to a ±1-frame temporal window, motivated by the repetitive, long-duration nature of behavioral recordings. More broadly, the paper positions itself against the fragmented landscape of task-specific tools (DeepLabCut, Lightning Pose, SimBA, TREBA) and general-purpose foundation models (DINOv2, CLIP, VideoMAE), arguing that no prior solution unifies multiple neuro-behavioral tasks while leveraging unlabeled domain-specific video.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

BEAST's practical impact lies in eliminating the labor-intensive pose estimation preprocessing step that currently gates action segmentation and neural encoding pipelines, while matching or exceeding keypoint-based methods using only unlabeled video for pretraining. The finding that domain-specific pretraining on a single experimental setup (77 IBL sessions) consistently outperforms frozen general-purpose foundation models like DINOv2 validates a 'pretrain-on-your-own-data' paradigm accessible to individual labs. Open questions include the interpretability of ViT embeddings in scientific contexts where transparent representations (e.g., keypoints) are preferred, validation across more species and naturalistic environments, and whether cross-dataset foundation models of animal behavior can be built on this framework.
