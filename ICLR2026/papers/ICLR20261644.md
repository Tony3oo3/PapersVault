## Problem

Most GNN-based MLIPs are implemented for single-device inference only, and the conventional spatial partitioning approach used by LAMMPS introduces massive computational redundancy (ghost atoms) that scales cubically with interaction range, making it impractical for long-range MLIPs. There is no native, general-purpose multi-GPU inference infrastructure for the diverse architectures of modern MLIPs (equivariant, invariant, with or without three-body bond graphs), limiting simulations to tens of thousands of atoms rather than the millions needed for realistic materials and biomolecular systems.

## Input / Output

DistMLIP takes an existing pretrained MLIP checkpoint (e.g., MACE, CHGNet, TensorNet, eSEN) and an atomic system defined by positions, chemical identities, and periodic boundary conditions, and produces per-atom energies and forces for MD stepping. The interface is plug-in: the user loads the model as usual, wraps it with a DistMLIP distributed object specifying GPU IDs, and runs inference identically to the single-device case. Graph construction (neighbor lists, atom graphs, three-body bond graphs) is performed on CPU in high-performance C, independent of PyTorch, JAX, or LAMMPS, and the resulting subgraphs with border-node metadata are distributed across GPUs for the forward/backward passes.

## Method

DistMLIP partitions the atom graph into disjoint subgraphs per GPU using a simple vertical-wall scheme along the longest cell dimension, then augments each partition with 1-hop and 2-hop border nodes so that every message-passing layer has access to all incoming neighbor information. After each graph convolution, only the border node and border edge features are exchanged between GPUs, achieving zero computational redundancy compared to spatial partitioning's ghost-atom overhead. For three-body bond graphs, an edge-table backbone maps node IDs to outgoing edges, and recursive traversal constructs the line graph per partition in parallel, with border edges in the bond graph derived from 2-hop atom-graph neighbors. The partitioning strategy deliberately avoids expensive graph-partitioning algorithms (METIS, RCM) in favor of position-based vertical walls, which benchmarks show to be up to 8× faster per timestep. The entire framework is model-agnostic, supporting both conservative and direct force-prediction MLIPs with atom graphs and/or three-body graphs, and requires no architectural modification or retraining of the underlying potential.

## Results

On 8× A100-80GB GPUs, DistMLIP enables near-million-atom simulations: MACE-3.8M handles 250k atoms (GaN) at 9.6 μs/(atom×step), TensorNet-0.8M handles 140k atoms (Li₃PO₄) at 16.3 μs/(atom×step), and CHGNet-2.7M handles 77.8k atoms (GaN) at 41.9 μs/(atom×step). Compared to LAMMPS spatial partitioning (L-MACE-3.8M), DistMLIP MACE-3.8M simulates 3.4× more atoms (e.g., 216k vs. 65.9k for Li₃PO₄) with comparable per-atom-step speed (11.0 vs. 12.3 μs). The simple vertical-wall partitioning strategy is up to 8× faster than standard graph partitioning algorithms (METIS, RCMK) for million-atom graphs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LAMMPS spatial partitioning (L-MACE-3.8M, the only baseline with a direct same-model comparison), DistMLIP MACE-3.8M achieves 3.4× larger simulation capacity (216k vs. 65.9k atoms for Li₃PO₄, 250k vs. 77.8k for GaN) while maintaining comparable or faster per-atom-step times (11.0 vs. 12.3 μs for Li₃PO₄), despite L-MACE using compiled equivariant CUDA kernels that accelerate MACE by up to 7.2×. L-MACE cannot simulate the 2w49 protein at all (OOM on all GPU counts), whereas DistMLIP MACE handles 69.3k atoms. Against SevenNet (Park et al., 2024), the prior graph-parallel MLIP, DistMLIP models at matched 0.8M parameters achieve up to 10× higher maximum capacity and 4× faster inference on α-quartz SiO₂ supercells. Against standard graph partitioning algorithms (METIS and RCMK), DistMLIP's simple vertical-wall spatial partitioning is up to 8× faster in MD step time (e.g., 11.0 vs. 62.18 μs for Li₃PO₄ on 8 GPUs with METIS), because topology-based partitioners introduce multi-second overhead per timestep that compounds over MD trajectories.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant spatial partitioning paradigm (LAMMPS, DeepMD, Allegro) that relies on short-range interactions and ghost-atom padding, instead adopting graph-level partitioning with zero computational redundancy. It extends the graph-parallel inference first demonstrated for SevenNet (Park et al., 2024) by making the approach model-agnostic (no LAMMPS/TorchScript dependency), supporting higher-order bond graphs, and covering four diverse MLIP architectures. The partitioning strategy itself is a deliberate simplification: a vertical-wall spatial heuristic replaces the graph-topology-based algorithms (METIS, RCM) used in general graph partitioning, trading theoretical optimality for per-timestep speed in a setting where graphs are rebuilt every MD step.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DistMLIP makes near-million-atom MD simulations accessible on a single 8-GPU node, a capability previously requiring supercomputers or strictly local (short-range) models, thereby opening meso-scale phenomena (protein folding, interfacial reactions, nano-domain formation) to foundation-potential accuracy. Key limitations include single-node-only support, the absence of multi-node communication, and the fact that the pure-PyTorch MACE implementation still lags behind compiled CUDA kernels. Open questions include whether the zero-redundancy graph partitioning approach extends to transformer-based MLIPs with full self-attention, how it scales beyond 8 GPUs across nodes, and whether the simple vertical-wall heuristic remains optimal for highly anisotropic or disordered systems.
