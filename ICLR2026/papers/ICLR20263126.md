## Problem

While adversarial attacks on individual AI systems and dyadic human-AI trust have been studied, the decision-making dynamics of small human-AI teams (multiple humans + one AI) under adversarial manipulation remain largely unexplored. This paper asks whether an AI assistant can learn to strategically mislead human teammates by modeling their trust evolution, and whether LLMs exhibit similar vulnerabilities when deployed in such team settings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263126/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The MBRL attacker consumes the current round number, each agent's correctness over a 5-round sliding window (full context for the cognitive model), and current performance, producing a binary action (correct vs. incorrect AI answer) that determines whether the AI lies. The influence models output a 3×4 row-stochastic matrix mapping four agents' correctness to three humans' trust weights, which feeds the scoring rule Score = 1ᵀAp. For the LLM evaluation, the input is the round-wise correctness history, the team's chat transcript, and each agent's chosen answer, and the output is a 100-point influence allocation across the four agents.

## Method

The paper develops two complementary models of influence evolution: a cognitive model that adapts Guo & Yang's Beta-distribution trust into a deterministic update (Eq. 2) parameterized by failure sensitivity w_f, and a data-driven MLP (3 hidden layers, ReLU, width 16/32) trained with MSE loss on a 5-round window inspired by working-memory limits. These models are embedded in a Model-Based RL framework formulated as a 25-round MDP with binary actions, where the cognitive-model attacker uses full-horizon dynamic programming with a reward equal to the negative influence of the attack on team score, while the MLP attacker uses a 5-round lookahead with a reward that directly minimizes predicted team score. The adversarial agent operates in three modes—10 baseline rounds at fixed 75% accuracy followed by 15 attack rounds—where lying means aligning with the most-accurate-but-incorrect human to maximize influence misallocation. Finally, four LLMs (GPT-4o-mini, DeepSeek-V3, DeepSeek-R1, o3-mini) are evaluated on the same influence-allocation task with and without chat logs to test whether they replicate human trust patterns and share the same attack susceptibility.

## Results

The MLP-based adversarial attack reduces human-AI team performance by 24% (p<0.01 vs. no-attack), while the cognitive-model-based attack reduces it by 15% (p=0.12, not statistically significant). The MLP model achieves the lowest MSE for both influence matrix prediction and cumulative score prediction, outperforming the cognitive model and an equal-weights baseline. Among LLMs evaluated on the influence allocation task, all four models (GPT-4o-mini, DeepSeek-V3, o3-mini, DeepSeek-R1) are susceptible to the trust-based attack, with Chain-of-Thought models (o3-mini, DeepSeek-R1) being the most vulnerable despite outperforming humans in the non-adversarial setting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares two influence-evolution models (a cognitive model based on Guo & Yang's Beta-distribution trust framework and a data-driven MLP) against an equal-weights heuristic baseline. The MLP model achieves the lowest MSE on both influence matrix prediction and score prediction, while the cognitive model without current performance performs comparably to the naive equal-weights baseline; however, when current performance is added, the cognitive model becomes competitive with the MLP. For attack efficacy, the MLP-based MBRL attacker significantly outperforms the cognitive-model attacker (24% vs. 15% performance reduction; p<0.05 between attacks), with only the MLP attack reaching statistical significance (p<0.01). On the LLM evaluation, all four tested models allocate influence comparably to humans and are all susceptible to the trust-based attack, with CoT models (o3-mini, DeepSeek-R1) showing the greatest vulnerability despite being the strongest performers in the non-adversarial condition.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the dyadic human-AI trust modeling literature (Guo & Yang, 2021; Lu et al., 2023) to multi-agent team settings by combining Transactive Memory System theory with DeGroot-style influence matrices. It departs from prior adversarial-attack-on-AI-assisted-decision-making work by formulating the attacker as an MBRL agent that learns a model of influence evolution (either a cognitive Beta-distribution-based model or a data-driven MLP) rather than relying on fixed trust-decay heuristics. The experimental paradigm draws on intellective-task literature (Askarisichani et al., 2020; Mei et al., 2016) but introduces a novel scoring mechanism that explicitly elicits influence allocations, bridging cognitive psychology and reinforcement learning in a way prior cMARL adversarial-attack work (Li et al., 2025; Huang & Zhu, 2019) did not.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a single compromised AI agent in a small human-AI team can measurably degrade collective performance using only a learned model of trust dynamics, with the data-driven MLP attack achieving statistical significance (p<0.01) while the cognitively-grounded model does not (p=0.12). The finding that Chain-of-Thought LLMs are more vulnerable to the same trust-manipulation attack despite outperforming humans in the non-adversarial setting raises urgent questions about whether improved reasoning capability translates to improved robustness in adversarial team settings. Key open questions include whether these findings generalize beyond short-horizon trivia tasks with immediate feedback, how defenses might be designed, and whether the explicit influence-allocation interface is necessary or whether naturalistic proxies could capture the same dynamics.
