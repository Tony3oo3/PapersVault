## Problem

Existing trustworthiness evaluation frameworks for large language models are designed primarily for text and overlook the unique vulnerabilities introduced by audio's acoustic properties—timbre, accent, background noise, and paralinguistic cues that can be exploited to manipulate model behavior. No comprehensive benchmark exists to systematically assess how Audio Large Language Models (ALLMs) perform across multiple trustworthiness dimensions specific to the audio modality.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260850/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes over 4,420 audio samples (synthesized via F5-TTS from GPT-4o-generated text, drawn from Common Voice, and mixed with Freesound background audio) paired with text prompts, spanning 26 sub-tasks across 18 experimental configurations. Outputs are structured as per-dimension trustworthiness scores: a Group Fairness Score Γ (0–1), hallucination detection scores (0–10 via GPT-4o/Qwen3 evaluators), Defense Success Rate (DSR) on a 5-point Likert scale, refusal rates for privacy, 10-point robustness ratings, and Imposter Rejection Rate (IRR) for authentication. All automated scores are validated by human annotators with over 97% agreement.

## Method

AudioTrust is a decoupled two-stage evaluation framework (inference followed by automated scoring) that probes 14 open- and closed-source ALLMs across six trustworthiness dimensions—fairness, hallucination, safety, privacy, robustness, and authentication—each decomposed into specific attack scenarios. For each dimension, targeted attack strategies are constructed: e.g., emotional-deception jailbreaks with F5-TTS-synthesized affective speech for safety, physically impossible or temporally inverted audio scenes for hallucination, voice-cloning and hybrid spoofing with reverberation for authentication, and layered environmental noise/speaker overlap for robustness. Evaluation employs GPT-4o and Qwen3 as LLM-as-judge scorers with task-specific rubrics, supplemented by quantitative metrics (WER, CM-WER, CCR) where applicable. The dataset is purpose-built to isolate audio-native risks (paralinguistic bias, acoustic-physical violations, voiceprint inference) that text-based benchmarks cannot capture, and the entire pipeline is publicly released for reproducibility.

## Results

Across 14 ALLMs evaluated on 4,420+ audio samples spanning 26 sub-tasks, GPT-4o Audio achieves the strongest overall trustworthiness profile (0.995 Safety DSR, 0.938 Authentication IRR, 0.618 Privacy refusal rate), while Gemini-2.5 Flash leads on Hallucination detection (0.125 normalized score) and Safety (0.998 DSR). Open-source models exhibit extreme variance: Step-Fun reaches 0.581 on Fairness (comparable to top closed-source models), whereas Kimi-Audio scores only 0.061. A universal weakness is privacy inference leakage, where all models refuse to protect paralinguistically inferred attributes (age, ethnicity) at only 9.02% refusal rate, and authentication IRR drops from 97.2% (closed-source avg.) to 55.3% (open-source avg.) under identity-verification-bypass attacks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among closed-source models, GPT-4o Audio and GPT-4o mini Audio dominate Safety (0.995/0.996 DSR) and Authentication (0.938/0.953 IRR), outperforming Gemini-2.5 Flash (0.998 Safety but 0.670 Authentication) and Gemini-1.5 Pro (0.993 Safety, 0.748 Authentication). Gemini-2.5 Flash is the strongest on Hallucination detection (0.125 vs. 0.188 for GPT-4o Audio), while Gemini-2.5 Pro leads on Robustness (0.759). Among open-source models, Step-Fun is the clear leader on Fairness (0.581, approaching GPT-4o Audio's 0.600) and Robustness (0.704), whereas SALMONN (0.113 Fairness, 0.165 Authentication) and OpenS2S (0.087 Fairness, 0.180 Robustness) are the weakest across nearly all dimensions. Kimi-Audio presents a striking profile: near-perfect Safety (0.988) but the lowest Fairness score of all 14 models (0.061), indicating that strong safety alignment does not transfer to bias mitigation. The gap between closed- and open-source models is most pronounced in Authentication (97.2% vs. 55.3% average IRR) and Privacy inference protection, where no model exceeds 40% refusal rate on paralinguistic attribute leakage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the trustworthiness evaluation paradigm established for text LLMs (TrustLLM, SafetyBench, SafeDialBench) to the audio modality, arguing that existing frameworks are fundamentally unequipped to capture audio-native risks arising from paralinguistic cues such as timbre, accent, and background noise. It also builds on multimodal safety benchmarks (MMDT, MMSafetyBench) but departs from them by isolating the acoustic signal as the primary attack vector rather than treating audio as a secondary modality alongside text or vision. The evaluation methodology draws on LLM-as-judge protocols (GPT-4o, Qwen3) validated against human annotation, a pattern established in recent LLM evaluation literature, but applies it to a novel set of audio-specific sub-tasks spanning 26 categories.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As ALLMs are deployed in voice assistants, emergency services, and financial/healthcare interactions, this benchmark provides the first systematic map of where current models fail on trustworthiness, identifying concrete attack surfaces (emotional deception, voice cloning, paralinguistic privacy inference) that developers must address before safe deployment. A key open question is how to integrate acoustic and environmental cues into privacy-aware and safety-aligned decision boundaries, since current semantic-oriented defenses are shown to be largely ineffective against paralinguistic attack vectors. The finding that open-source models exhibit a substantial capability gap relative to closed-source counterparts across most dimensions raises practical concerns about the safety of deploying open ALLMs in high-stakes settings.
