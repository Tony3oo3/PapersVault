## Problem

Existing motion-conditioned video generation methods require expensive model-specific fine-tuning to ingest auxiliary control signals (optical flow, point trajectories), making them computationally costly, restrictive to particular architectures, and often degrading the quality of the original generator. There is no training-free, plug-and-play approach that gives users precise, fine-grained control over both what moves and where it moves in an image-to-video diffusion model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260615/cropped_page0_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes three inputs: a single source image I ∈ R^{3×H×W}, a coarse warped reference video V^w ∈ R^{F×3×H×W} produced by forward-warping I along a user-specified trajectory (or via depth-based reprojection for camera motion), and a binary mask video M ∈ {0,1}^{F×H×W} marking regions where motion guidance is enforced. The output is a realistic F-frame video that preserves the appearance of the input image while following the prescribed motion. The warped reference is constructed with identity mapping outside the mask and nearest-neighbor inpainting for disocclusion holes, and the mask can be as coarse as a human brush stroke.

## Method

TTM adapts SDEdit's noise-injection principle to video: it initializes the denoising process from the warped reference video noised to a chosen timestep t*, embedding the intended motion at the stage where coarse dynamics are established by the diffusion model, while image conditioning on the clean first frame preserves appearance. The key technical contribution is region-dependent dual-clock denoising, which assigns two distinct noise schedules across spatial regions: masked (motion-specified) regions are overridden with the warped reference noised to t−1 at each step between t_weak and t_strong, enforcing strong trajectory adherence, while unmasked regions denoise freely from the higher t_weak initialization, allowing natural background dynamics. The update rule x_{t−1} ← (1−M) ⊙ x̂_{t−1}(x_t, t, I) + M ⊙ x_{t−1}^w is a simple mask-blend that requires no retraining and adds no extra computation over standard sampling. Because conditioning operates on full reference frames rather than displacement fields alone, the framework simultaneously supports pixel-level appearance control (e.g., color changes, object insertion) alongside motion, a capability unavailable to trajectory-only or text-prompt interfaces.

## Results

On MC-Bench (SVD backbone), TTM achieves a CoTracker Distance (CTD) of 7.97, outperforming DragAnything (10.65) and MotionPro (8.69), while matching MotionPro on Subject Consistency (0.979) and Motion Smoothness (0.993). On the CogVideoX backbone, TTM reduces CTD to 13.67 versus GWTF's 32.55 (γ=0.7) and 27.84 (γ=0.5), while improving Subject Consistency to 0.980 and Imaging Quality to 0.579. On DL3DV camera motion, TTM achieves the best pixel MSE (0.022), FID (21.97), SSIM (0.586), and optical-flow MSE (60.56), reducing pixel MSE by 33% and FID by 15.5% over the best GWTF variant.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DragAnything (ECCV 2024), TTM achieves lower CTD (7.97 vs. 10.65) and higher Subject Consistency (0.979 vs. 0.956) and Imaging Quality (0.617 vs. 0.554), but DragAnything reports a much higher Dynamic Degree (0.981 vs. 0.427) and BG-Obj CTD (50.89 vs. 35.34), which the authors attribute to DragAnything inducing unintended scene-wide motion and local deformations. Against SG-I2V (ICLR 2025), TTM substantially outperforms on BG-Obj CTD (35.34 vs. 12.04), indicating far less unintended background co-motion, and matches on Subject Consistency (0.979 vs. 0.976), though SG-I2V achieves a lower CTD (5.80 vs. 7.97) at the cost of camera drift. Against MotionPro (CVPR 2025), TTM wins on CTD (7.97 vs. 8.69) and BG-Obj CTD (35.34 vs. 24.49) while matching on Subject Consistency and Motion Smoothness, with only minor losses in BG Consistency (0.967 vs. 0.975) and Aesthetic Quality (0.548 vs. 0.559). Against GWTF (CVPR 2025) on CogVideoX, TTM dramatically reduces CTD (13.67 vs. 27.84–32.55) and improves all VBench quality metrics (Subject Cons. 0.980 vs. 0.958–0.963; Imaging 0.579 vs. 0.539), while GWTF reports higher Dynamic Degree (0.74–0.76 vs. 0.36) at the cost of scene deformations. On DL3DV camera motion, TTM outperforms both GWTF variants across all six metrics, with pixel MSE dropping from 0.033 to 0.022 (−33%) and FID from 25.99 to 21.97 (−15.5%), confirming superior motion fidelity and temporal alignment.

## Relation to Prior Work

TTM adapts SDEdit's principle of imposing coarse structure via timestep-specific noise injection from the image domain to video, using a crudely warped reference animation as the motion signal. The region-dependent dual-clock denoising mechanism draws directly on the mask-blending strategies of RePaint and Blended Diffusion, but extends them to video by assigning two distinct noise schedules to masked and unmasked regions simultaneously. Unlike prior motion-control methods (DragAnything, MotionPro, GWTF) that require fine-tuning or architectural modifications to ingest trajectory or flow conditioning, TTM operates entirely at inference time on off-the-shelf I2V backbones. It also departs from training-free attention-manipulation approaches (TrailBlazer, PEEKABOO, FreeTraj) by conditioning on full reference frames rather than bounding boxes or sparse trajectories, which enables joint motion-and-appearance control.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The plug-and-play, training-free nature of TTM is practically significant as video diffusion models rapidly evolve in architecture and scale; a method that works across SVD, CogVideoX, and WAN 2.2 without retraining avoids the ~7,680 A100 GPU-hours that GWTF requires. The ability to control both motion and per-pixel appearance (e.g., changing an object's color along a trajectory) goes beyond what text prompts or trajectory-only interfaces can express. Key limitations include per-model tuning of (t_weak, t_strong), identity preservation restricted to the first frame, and reliance on full object masks. Open directions include generalizing dual-clock to multiple simultaneous regions, soft masks, articulated motion, and long-horizon generation.
