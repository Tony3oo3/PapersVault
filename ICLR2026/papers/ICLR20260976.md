## Problem

While researchers invest heavily in improving pre-training datasets, there is little understanding of how efficiently the pre-training process actually extracts knowledge from that data. This paper asks: how much dataset value is left behind by pre-training, and can it be recovered at test time? The key question is whether reusing the same pre-training corpus via retrieval at inference time yields meaningful gains, and how those gains scale with model size.

## Input / Output

The system takes a text question and retrieves relevant passages from the exact same pre-training corpus used to train the model, using Qwen3 Embedding 0.6B for dense retrieval (top-100 per dataset via FAISS FlatIP, merged and reranked by Qwen3 Reranker 0.6B). Retrieved documents are concatenated and prepended to the question prompt. For test-time compute, the reader model (Llama 3.1 8B Instruct) runs multiple chain-of-thought trials with varying retrieved document subsets, and answers are aggregated via majority voting (self-consistency) with optional MMR diversity and bagging variance reduction. Output is a single text answer scored against benchmark ground truth on MMLU, Math-500, SimpleQA, and GPQA.

## Method

The paper pre-trains dense decoder models (6.4B–77.8B parameters, ~20 tokens/param) on a fixed multi-source corpus (DCLM-baseline, FineWeb-edu, arXiv, peS2o, PubMed, Stack Exchange, Wikipedia, and math datasets), then evaluates each model both standalone and augmented with retrieval over that same corpus. A bounded sigmoid is fitted to base-model MMLU accuracy as a function of FLOPs, and the compute multiplier is defined as the additional pre-training compute needed to match the retrieval-augmented accuracy; this yields an average ~5× multiplier that diminishes to ~2.9× at the largest scale. On top of retrieval, the paper stacks self-consistency (parallel trials with majority voting), a cross-encoder reranker, and variance-reduction techniques (MMR for document diversity, bagging over document subsets), achieving an 11× compute multiplier on MMLU with Llama 3.1 8B. Decontamination via n-gram overlap confirms gains are not attributable to test-set leakage, and category-level analysis shows retrieval is a stronger multiplier for STEM (6.2×) and 'Other' (9.3×) than for Humanities (2.5×), suggesting pre-training under-absorbs certain knowledge types.

## Results

Using Llama 3.1 8B Instruct as the reader model, retrieval with reranking achieves 74.0% on SimpleQA (vs. 1.5% baseline), a +10.5 pp gain on MMLU (77.7% vs. 71.6%), a +15.7 pp gain on MATH-500 (56.8% vs. 48.7%), and a +6.2 pp gain on GPQA (34.8% vs. 30.6%). Combining retrieval, reranking, self-consistency, and variance reduction (MMR + bagging) pushes MMLU to 82.1% and MATH-500 to 64.4%, corresponding to an 11.1× compute multiplier over the pre-trained baseline on MMLU. Across five pre-trained model scales (6.4B–77.8B), retrieval from the same pre-training data yields an average 4.86× compute multiplier on MMLU, though this diminishes to 2.88× at the largest scale.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is against the base model without retrieval, showing that retrieval consistently outperforms the pre-trained baseline across all benchmarks and model scales. Against the Qwen3 Reranker 0.6B (Zhang et al., 2025a), the paper's inter-document consistency reranking approach achieves 77.6% MMLU All versus 73.7% for Qwen3 Reranker at k=1, a 3.9 pp improvement, though at a significantly higher inference cost since it requires running the reader model per document. Internally, retrieval and self-consistency are shown to be additive: retrieval alone gives +5.0 pp on MMLU All (76.6 vs. 71.6), self-consistency alone gives +3.7 pp (75.3 vs. 71.6), and their combination with reranking reaches 81.0%, exceeding either technique individually. The compute multiplier analysis (Table 2) shows retrieval provides 5.28×–7.17× savings at smaller scales but only 2.88× at the 77.8B scale, indicating diminishing returns as the base model absorbs more data during pre-training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of classical pre-training scaling laws (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022) and retrieval-augmented generation (Lewis et al., 2020; Guu et al., 2020), but introduces a new analytical lens: using the *same* dataset for both pre-training and retrieval to quantify how much knowledge pre-training leaves unextracted. It builds directly on self-consistency (Wang et al., 2022) and test-time compute scaling (Brown et al., 2024; Snell et al., 2024) as the mechanism for extracting additional value from retrieved documents, and is informed by recent work showing that compact subsets of pre-training data can aid reasoning (Lyu et al., 2025) and that datastore scaling improves knowledge-intensive tasks (Shao et al., 2024). Unlike prior RAG papers that treat the retrieval store as independent of the model's training data, this work deliberately conflates the two to isolate the 'residual value' in pre-training corpora.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that retrieval from standard pre-training datasets acts as a ~5× compute multiplier on MMLU (diminishing to ~2.9× at the largest scale) provides a concrete, quantified argument that current pre-training methods do not fully extract available knowledge, reframing the pre-training vs. inference-compute tradeoff. The observation that dataset quality for pre-training does not transfer to retrieval quality (FineWeb-edu underperforms DCLM in pre-training but matches or exceeds it in retrieval) challenges the assumption that optimizing for one objective optimizes for the other, with direct implications for dataset curation pipelines. Open questions include whether more advanced test-time techniques (query rewriting, test-time training, RL for retrieval) will close the remaining gap, how to encourage models to better utilize retrieved context rather than ignore it, and whether the compute-multiplier framing generalizes beyond knowledge-heavy benchmarks to broader reasoning tasks.
