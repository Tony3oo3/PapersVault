## Problem

Disease progression modeling from longitudinal EHRs must learn continuous-time dynamics from irregularly sampled encounters while respecting clinically validated progression trajectories. Existing Neural ODE approaches capture temporal dynamics but ignore known progression pathways, while graph- and hypergraph-based methods either model only pairwise interactions or treat hyperedges as static units without marker-level temporal detail, failing to encode high-order, time-aware dependencies within and across trajectories.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261155/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes a sequence of irregularly timestamped encounters, each providing a 34-dimensional risk factor vector (medications, labs, vital signs) and a 21-dimensional binary complication marker vector, alongside a patient-specific TD-Hypergraph that records the first-observation timestamp of each marker along predefined clinical trajectories (unobserved markers are marked with ∞). Output is the predicted binary marker status at the next encounter, trained with binary cross-entropy under the constraint that markers are irreversible (0→1 only). The TD-Hypergraph is reconstructed at each encounter, so the structural input evolves as new complications are observed.

## Method

TD-HNODE governs continuous-time latent state evolution via a Neural ODE whose right-hand side is a learnable TD-Hypergraph Laplacian applied to the concatenation of hidden marker states and embedded risk factors. The Laplacian replaces the static binary incidence matrix with an attention-based incidence matrix that uses cross-attention from the current progression point to each marker, employing continuous-time positional encodings for already-observed markers and discrete index encodings for future markers, thereby assigning patient-specific, time-aware importance within each trajectory. A second component replaces fixed diagonal hyperedge weights with a learnable m×m weight matrix derived from trajectory-level embeddings (aggregated via self-attention within past and potential marker subsets, then projected and outer-producted), capturing inter-trajectory correlations. The Laplacian is held fixed within each ODE integration interval [t_k, t_{k+1}] and solved with a 4th-order Runge-Kutta method (10 steps), after which a dense layer with sigmoid decodes the latent state into per-marker onset probabilities. Training is autoregressive: the model integrates forward encounter-by-encounter, accumulating binary cross-entropy loss at each step.

## Results

TD-HNODE achieves the best performance across all four metrics (Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-score) on both the University Hospital and MIMIC-IV datasets. On University Hospital, it reaches 79.4% accuracy and 20.4% F1-score, outperforming the strongest baseline ContiFormer (77.2% accuracy, 16.7% F1) by 2.2% and 3.7% respectively. On MIMIC-IV, TD-HNODE attains 87.9% accuracy and 42.9% F1-score, surpassing ContiFormer (86.2%, 36.5%) by 1.7% and 6.4%. The largest gains appear in Recall: TD-HNODE achieves 79.3% and 85.7% recall on the two datasets, exceeding the next-best baseline (TGNE at 75.4% and 72.8%) by 3.9% and 12.9% respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TD-HNODE outperforms all eight baselines on every metric across both datasets. Against the strongest sequential baseline ContiFormer, TD-HNODE gains 2.2% accuracy and 3.7% F1 on University Hospital, and 1.7% accuracy and 6.4% F1 on MIMIC-IV. Among temporal graph models, TGNE is the closest competitor (75.4% recall on University Hospital, 72.8% on MIMIC-IV), but TD-HNODE still leads by 3.9% and 12.9% recall respectively, demonstrating the advantage of hypergraph-based high-order interactions over pairwise edges. The temporal hypergraph baselines DHSL and HyperTime, which construct static hypergraph snapshots over discrete intervals, trail TD-HNODE by 4.4–6.4% F1 on University Hospital and 5.4–11.1% F1 on MIMIC-IV, confirming that marker-level temporal detail within hyperedges matters. Among Neural ODE baselines, NODE achieves only 62.3% recall on MIMIC-IV versus TD-HNODE's 85.7% (+23.4%), highlighting the benefit of incorporating clinically structured progression trajectories into the ODE dynamics. CODE-RNN, which combines ODEs with RNNs, reaches 74.5% recall on MIMIC-IV but still falls 11.2% short of TD-HNODE.

## Relation to Prior Work

TD-HNODE sits at the intersection of two established lines: Neural ODEs for continuous-time dynamics (Chen et al., 2018) and hypergraph neural networks for high-order relational modeling (Feng et al., 2019). It departs from prior Neural ODE disease-progression models (Qian et al., 2021; Dang et al., 2023) by embedding clinically validated progression trajectories as a structured hypergraph rather than treating dynamics as unstructured latent evolution, and from temporal hypergraph networks (Lee & Shin, 2023; Liu et al., 2022) by assigning timestamps at the individual marker level within each hyperedge rather than at the hyperedge level. The core architectural novelty is the learnable TD-Hypergraph Laplacian, which replaces the static binary incidence matrix with a cross-attention mechanism and the fixed diagonal weight matrix with a data-driven trajectory-correlation matrix, both conditioned on the patient's current progression state.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The TD-Hypergraph representation offers a principled way to inject clinical pathway knowledge into a data-driven continuous-time model, addressing a gap where mechanistic models lack adaptability and purely data-driven models ignore validated progression structure. The case study demonstrating clear patient subphenotyping clusters with distinct progression speeds has direct clinical utility for stratified treatment planning. Key limitations include reliance on pre-specified trajectories (no mechanism to discover unknown pathways), the irreversibility assumption that may not hold for all conditions, and evaluation limited to diabetes and cardiovascular complications. Open questions include whether the framework can be extended to infer partially characterized trajectories and whether causal inference can be integrated to evaluate treatment effects on progression.
