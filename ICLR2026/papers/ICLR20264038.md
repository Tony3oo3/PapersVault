## Problem

ML-based combinatorial optimization solvers have been evaluated almost exclusively on small-scale, synthetic instances (typically ≤10K nodes), leaving open whether they can match state-of-the-art human-designed algorithms on real-world, structurally irregular, and extremely large instances. FRONTIERCO addresses this by providing a unified benchmark spanning eight CO problems with instances drawn from competitions and public repositories, scaling to TSP with 10M nodes and MIS with 8M nodes—orders of magnitude beyond prior ML evaluations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264038/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Inputs are problem-specific: graph adjacency structures for MIS/MDS, 2D Euclidean city coordinates for TSP/CVRP, facility-customer capacity matrices for CFLP/CPMP, job-machine-operation tables for FJSP, and natural-language problem descriptions paired with instance data for LLM agents. Each problem provides two test sets (easy: historically challenging but now solvable; hard: open or computationally intensive with structurally complex cases like hypercube graphs or SAT-induced instances), plus standardized training/validation data generated from problem-specific synthetic generators. The primary output metric is the primal gap (bounded [0,1]) computed against precomputed best-known solutions, with a 1-hour per-instance time budget; infeasible solutions are assigned gap 1.

## Method

FRONTIERCO is a benchmark and empirical evaluation framework rather than a single solver. It curates instances from DIMACS challenges, TSPLib, BHOSLib, PACE 2025, SteinLib, and other competition sources, pairs them with standardized BKS (validated via SOTA solvers like KaMIS, LKH-3, HGS, SCIP-Jack, Gurobi, CPLEX), and provides a unified toolkit with data loaders, evaluation functions, and an abstract solving template for LLM agents. The evaluation covers 16 ML solvers across three paradigms—end-to-end neural solvers (diffusion models like DIFUSCO/DiffUCO, transformers like LEHD/SIL, RL-based branching like tMDP/SORREL/GCNN), hybrid neural-enhanced heuristics (IL-LNS, CL-LNS, DeepACO, MPGN, L-RHO), and LLM-based agentic methods (FunSearch, Self-Refine, ReEvo)—all compared against SOTA classical baselines under identical hardware (single CPU core, single RTX A6000 GPU) and time constraints. Key findings include a persistent and widening performance gap for ML methods on hard/large instances, scalability failures (out-of-memory, inference timeouts) for neural solvers, and the surprising but high-variance potential of LLM agents to generate competitive metaheuristic algorithms.

## Results

Across eight CO problems, SOTA classical solvers achieve near-optimal primal gaps on easy sets (e.g., LKH-3: 0.03% on TSP-easy; Gurobi: 0.00% on CFLP-easy; SCIP-Jack: 0.00% on STP-easy) and remain strong on hard sets (e.g., LKH-3: 2.89% on TSP-hard; KaMIS: 2.65% on MIS-hard). In contrast, the best neural solvers show substantially larger gaps—LEHD reaches 10.23% on TSP-easy and 76.84% on TSP-hard, while SIL achieves 2.51% on TSP-easy but 21.34% on TSP-hard—and suffer out-of-memory or timeout failures on 4 of 8 problems. LLM-based agents (FunSearch, Self-Refine, ReEvo) achieve average primal gaps of 20.35%, 15.11%, and 13.25% across all eight problems respectively, with ReEvo reaching 7.25% on easy sets and 19.25% on hard sets. Notably, the performance gap between ML and classical solvers is far larger in FRONTIERCO than in prior synthetic benchmarks: LEHD reports only 0.72% gap on a standard TSP benchmark but degrades to 10% (easy) and 77% (hard) under FRONTIERCO's real-world structure and scale.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SOTA classical solvers (LKH-3, KaMIS, HGS, Gurobi, CPLEX, SCIP-Jack, GB21-MH) dominate across nearly all problem–difficulty combinations, achieving gaps below 3% on easy sets and typically under 7% on hard sets. Neural solvers (LEHD, DIFUSCO, SIL, DiffUCO, SDDS, RLNN, DeepACO, tMDP, SORREL, GCNN, IL-LNS, CL-LNS, MPGN, L-RHO) consistently underperform their classical counterparts by 5–50+ percentage points, with the gap widening dramatically on hard and large-scale instances; several (LEHD on TSP-hard, DiffUCO/SDDS on MDS-hard, IL-LNS/CL-LNS/GCNN on CPMP-hard) fail entirely with OOM or timeout. LLM-based agents (FunSearch, Self-Refine, ReEvo) are the closest ML competitors: Self-Refine marginally beats KaMIS on MIS-easy (1.30% vs. 1.51%), and FunSearch edges out HGS on CVRP-hard (6.52% vs. 6.74%), but they exhibit high variance and still trail classical solvers on most other problems. The ablation (Table 3) confirms that neural modules do improve over their heuristic backbones (DIFUSCO 4.19% vs. 2-OPT 20.09% on TSP-easy; GCNN 3.22% vs. SCIP 6.50% on CFLP-easy), yet the enhanced methods remain far below SOTA classical solvers (LKH-3 0.03%; Gurobi 0.00%). Overall, FRONTIERCO reveals that the performance gap between ML and classical solvers is substantially larger than previously reported on synthetic benchmarks, primarily due to distribution shift, structural irregularity, and orders-of-magnitude larger instance sizes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by repurposing established CO instance libraries (TSPLib, DIMACS challenges, BHOSLib, SteinLib, PACE Challenge) and competition-grade benchmarks into a unified ML evaluation suite, departing from the synthetic-generator paradigm that dominates prior ML4CO evaluations (Kool et al. 2019; Luo et al. 2023; DIMES). It extends the evaluation scope from the 100–10K node range typical of neural TSP/MIS studies to 10M (TSP) and 8M (MIS) nodes, and introduces structurally irregular instances (SAT-induced MIS, hypercube STP) that prior benchmarks omitted. The benchmark also unifies two previously separate solver paradigms—neural (GNN, diffusion, RL) and symbolic (LLM agents like FunSearch, Self-Refine, ReEvo)—under a single protocol with standardized training data and best-known solutions, addressing the cross-paper comparability problem identified in the literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FRONTIERCO provides the field's first rigorous, large-scale, real-world evaluation framework for ML-based CO solvers, and its findings—particularly the dramatic widening of the performance gap under distribution shift and the complete failure of GNNs on non-Euclidean structure—challenge optimistic narratives about neural solvers' generalizability. The observation that LLM agents can occasionally surpass SOTA classical solvers (e.g., Self-Refine over KaMIS on MIS) while exhibiting high variance raises open questions about whether LLMs can develop reliable internal reasoning for algorithm selection. Key open directions include designing neural architectures that capture global (non-local) structure, integrating reduction/presolving techniques to address scalability, and building agentic frameworks that can self-assess algorithm effectiveness without external feedback.
