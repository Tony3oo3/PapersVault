## Problem

Existing expert-level benchmarks (MMLU, GPQA) prioritize multiple-choice or short-form evaluation, while ExpertQA remains QA-centric rather than capturing end-to-end expert workflows. No prior benchmark combines long-form generation tasks exceeding 5,000 tokens, domain-specific rubrics co-designed with experts, and grounded evaluation with human-written references. Additionally, prior checklist-based methods (WildBench, BiGGenBench) rely on model-generated checklists that fail to capture domain-specific requirements and lack reference grounding for recall estimation.

## Input / Output

Each task sample provides a long-form input context ranging from ~100 tokens (molecule/protein descriptions) to ~187K tokens (legal Statement of Fact generation from 14 transcripts) and requires a long-form natural language output of up to 5,000+ tokens. The CLEAR evaluation framework takes a model's generated output and a human-written reference, maps both into structured checklists aligned with the task-specific rubric (5–41 items per task), and produces item-level binary correctness scores aggregated into precision, recall, and F1 metrics per task.

## Method

ExpertLongBench comprises 1,050 samples across 11 tasks in 9 domains, each accompanied by an expert-designed checklist-based rubric and a human-written reference. CLEAR operates in two stages: first, a checklist mapper (Qwen2.5-72B for model outputs, GPT-4o for references) extracts information corresponding to each rubric item from the text, producing a checklist-mapped response; second, an LLM judge (GPT-4o) compares each model checklist item against the corresponding reference item, assigning a binary score of 1 only when all reference information is semantically contained in the model's extraction. This grounded, item-level comparison enables fine-grained evaluation of both precision and recall, avoiding the subjectivity of holistic LLM-as-a-judge scoring. The paper further validates that open-weight models can substitute for proprietary ones in both the mapper and judge roles, with Qwen2.5-72B achieving 90.1 F1 as a mapper and 0.88 Pearson correlation with GPT-4o as a judge, enabling low-cost and reproducible benchmarking.

## Results

Across 11 expert-level tasks spanning 9 domains (1,050 samples total), the best-performing model Gemini-2.5-Pro achieves only a 33.4 average F1 score, with GPT-5 (31.0) and o3 (29.3) following closely. Task T2 (Statement of Fact Generation) is the most challenging, with all 15 models scoring below 11 F1. Despite low accuracy, models cover over 67% of required checklist aspects, indicating they produce content that appears domain-aligned but is frequently incorrect. The CLEAR evaluation framework achieves 90.1 F1 for checklist mapping with Qwen2.5-72B and 91–92% human-LLM agreement on rubric-based judgments.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among proprietary models, Gemini-2.5-Pro leads with 33.4 avg F1, outperforming GPT-5 (31.0) by 2.4 points and o3 (29.3) by 4.1 points, though GPT-5 wins on T5 (56.5 vs 47.9) and T6 (54.7 vs 44.0). Proprietary models are not uniformly superior: Claude-3.7-Sonnet (23.2) and Claude-3.5-Haiku (19.3) rank near the bottom, with Claude-3.5-Haiku scoring the lowest overall. Open-weight Qwen3-32B (28.1) outperforms several proprietary models including GPT-4o (26.5) and GPT-4o-mini (26.1), while Qwen2.5-72B (23.8) is hampered by its 32K context window on T10 (64K avg input), trailing the best model by 17.5 points. Scaling within a family does not guarantee consistent gains: Mistral-Nemo-Instruct beats Mistral-Large-Instruct on T10 (50.8 vs 39.4) despite being smaller. A RAG-based agent underperforms direct full-context prompting on both T1 and T2, confirming that global document context is essential for these expert-level tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends two complementary lines of work: fact-decomposition evaluation (FActScore, WiCE) and checklist-based LLM-as-a-judge methods (WildBench, BiGGenBench). CLEAR unifies these by deriving task-specific checklists from expert-designed rubrics rather than generic or model-generated criteria, then performing grounded item-level comparison against human references. In terms of benchmarking, EXPERTLONGBENCH departs from prior expert-level evaluations (MMLU, GPQA, ExpertQA) that focus on short-form QA or multiple-choice answers, instead targeting end-to-end expert workflows with inputs exceeding 200K tokens and outputs exceeding 5K tokens, and it adapts the reference-based scoring methodology of Verga et al. for binary item-level judgment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that even the strongest model (Gemini-2.5-Pro) achieves only 33.4 F1, and more concerningly, models cover over 67% of required checklist aspects while producing incorrect content—a negative correlation between coverage and F1 that signals a reliability risk in high-stakes domains. The finding that RAG and test-time scaling do not substantially close the gap challenges the assumption that retrieval or additional reasoning compute can compensate for missing domain expertise. Open questions include whether automatically generating expert-quality rubrics is feasible, how the benchmark's difficulty will evolve as models improve, and whether the checklist-based evaluation paradigm generalizes beyond the 11 tasks covered.
