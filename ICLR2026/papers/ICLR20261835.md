## Problem

LLMs struggle to comprehend graph structure due to two dominant paradigms that each fail in complementary ways: Graph-to-Text verbalization consumes excessive tokens and scatters attention while remaining sensitive to node/edge ordering (structural hallucination), and Graph-to-Embedding projection into continuous soft prompts introduces severe modality mismatch with the LLM's native discrete token space. The paper asks how to feed graph topology into an LLM concisely and accurately while keeping structural information in the same representational space as text tokens.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text-attributed graph (e.g., a molecule with its SMILES string) and a task prompt, and compresses the entire graph topology into exactly one discrete structural token <SOG_k> drawn from a learned vocabulary of K=256 entries. This single token is concatenated with the textual attributes and prompt in the LLM's input sequence, so the model sees only one extra token regardless of graph size. The LLM then generates a class label (binary True/False for graph-level tasks, or a category name for node-level tasks) via standard autoregressive text generation, with the answer extracted by keyword matching from the generated output.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-stage pipeline. First, a topology-aware structural tokenizer assigns each node a hierarchical traversal attribute (hop distance and rank from a degree-based anchor node), embeds these with SentenceTransformers, encodes them through a two-layer GCN, and then quantizes the resulting continuous representations into one of K=256 discrete codebook entries via a VQ-VAE mechanism trained with adjacency-matrix reconstruction loss plus update and commitment losses; the codebook index assigned to a virtual global pooling node becomes the graph-level structural token. Second, three types of task-agnostic hybrid QA pairs (k-nearest token matching, true/false structural similarity judgment, and text-description-to-token mapping) are used to fine-tune the LLM with LoRA (rank 16), freezing all original text token embeddings and updating only the new structural token embeddings, thereby aligning structural tokens into the LLM's native token space. Downstream, a task-specific fine-tuning stage appends the structural token to the prompt alongside SMILES and task instructions, and the LLM is trained with standard cross-entropy on the generated label.

## Results

On five MoleculeNet graph-level benchmarks (BBBP, Tox21, ClinTox, HIV, BACE), the proposed <SOG_k> token with LLaMA3-3B achieves AUC-ROC of 76.9, 83.4, 85.5, 75.7, and 63.3 respectively, while LLaMA2-7B reaches 66.4, 72.4, 94.3, 83.2, and 98.4. The method reports a 9.9–41.4% AUC-ROC improvement over the strongest baselines across datasets. On node-level classification (Cora, Pubmed), LLaMA3-3B with <SOG_k> attains 91.58% accuracy and 78.62% F1 on Cora, and 97.46% accuracy and 85.50% F1 on Pubmed, surpassing all LLM and GNN baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed <SOG_k> method outperforms all 16 baselines across both graph-level and node-level tasks. Among large LLMs, Galactica-120B (zero-shot) is the strongest competitor on BBBP (66.1) and Tox21 (68.9), but <SOG_k> with only 3B parameters surpasses it by 10.8 and 14.5 AUC-ROC points respectively; GPT-4, GPT-4o, DeepSeek-R1, and LLaMA3-70B all trail by 10–40 points on most datasets. Non-LLM molecular methods (GIMLET, MolCA-GS, GPF variants) are competitive on individual datasets (e.g., GIMLET reaches 69.6 on BACE) but lack the cross-dataset consistency of <SOG_k>. Among small-LLM baselines, LoRA SFT with Graph-to-Text input is the closest competitor (e.g., 60.2 on BBBP-3B), yet <SOG_k> improves by 16.7 points; Soft Prompt (graph-to-embedding) performs worst, dropping 8.6–24.2 points below zero-shot on the 3B model due to severe modality misalignment. On node-level classification, <SOG_k> (LLaMA3-3B) achieves 91.58% accuracy on Cora, beating GPT-4o (68.62%) by 22.96 points, DeepSeek-chat (65.62%) by 25.96 points, and the best graph-LLM baseline ZEROG (62.52%) by 29.06 points; on Pubmed it reaches 97.46% accuracy vs. ZEROG's 79.08%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a third paradigm—'Graph-to-Token'—between the two dominant approaches for feeding graph structure into LLMs: Graph-to-Text (verbalizing topology into natural language, e.g., Fatemi et al. 2024, Ye et al. 2024) and Graph-to-Embedding (projecting continuous GNN embeddings into LLM input space, e.g., Tang et al. 2024, Chen et al. 2024). It builds directly on the VQ-VAE codebook mechanism from VQGraph (Yang et al., ICLR 2024) to quantize GCN-encoded topology into a discrete structural token, but departs from prior work by placing that token in the LLM's own vocabulary space and aligning it via a novel hybrid QA instruction-tuning strategy rather than an MLP projector. The use of a hierarchical traversal-based structural attribute (anchor node + hop-relative numbering) to break node-permutation symmetry is a practical design choice not seen in prior graph-to-LLM tokenization work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The core practical contribution is that a single discrete token, trained with a lightweight GCN + VQ codebook and aligned via LoRA, can replace either verbose text descriptions or misaligned soft-prompt embeddings, yielding 9.9–41.4% AUC gains over baselines on MoleculeNet and strong node-classification results on Cora/Pubmed. The scaffold-level interpretability finding (same Bemis-Murcko scaffold → same token) and the t-SNE evidence that structural tokens embed within the text-token neighborhood provide conceptual validation that the token truly lives in the LLM's representational space. Key limitations and open questions include: evaluation is confined to small molecular graphs and citation networks, making it unclear how the single-token bottleneck scales to large or heterogeneous graphs; the structural tokenizer is trained per-domain; and the comparison with large LLMs (GPT-4o, DeepSeek) is fine-tuned-small vs. zero-shot-large, which inflates the apparent gap. The authors themselves flag dynamic and heterogeneous graphs as future directions.
