## Problem

Existing uncertainty estimation methods for deep learning either address only a single type of uncertainty (aleatoric or epistemic) or require architectural modifications and full retraining of the base model, making them impractical for deployment in high-stakes systems. This paper tackles the challenge of jointly estimating both uncertainty types with a single, lightweight module that can be attached to any pretrained network without altering its parameters or retraining it.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20265015/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

CUPID consumes an intermediate feature map (e.g., a 512-dim vector from ResNet18's final residual block, or a spatial feature tensor from ESRGAN's pre-upsampling stage) and produces two outputs: a perturbed feature of the same dimensionality and a per-output-dimension log-variance vector. Epistemic uncertainty is then computed externally as the L1 norm between the original prediction and the prediction obtained by forwarding the perturbed feature through the frozen downstream layers. For classification tasks the aleatoric output is a per-class log-variance; for super-resolution it is a spatial uncertainty map aligned with the output image.

## Method

CUPID is a three-component plug-in module (Feature Extractor, Reconstruction Branch, Uncertainty Branch) inserted at any intermediate layer of a frozen predictive model. The Uncertainty Branch learns a Bayesian identity mapping that predicts input-dependent log-variance under a heteroscedastic Gaussian likelihood, yielding aleatoric uncertainty. The Reconstruction Branch is trained to produce a feature perturbation that is maximally different from the original (via a differential feature loss) while keeping the downstream prediction nearly unchanged (via a prediction-consistency loss); the resulting output discrepancy serves as the epistemic uncertainty estimate. A first-order Taylor analysis shows this epistemic measure decomposes into local Jacobian sensitivity times feature-space deviation, linking it to both model parameter uncertainty and distributional shift. The two branches are jointly optimized under a weighted sum loss, and ablations confirm that each branch regularizes the shared feature extractor, yielding better estimates than either branch trained in isolation.

## Results

On medical image misclassification detection, CUPID Aleatoric achieves the best AUC (0.870) and AURC (0.018) on GLV2, while CUPID Epistemic achieves the best AUC (0.855), AURC (0.047), and Spearman (0.907) on HAM10000. For OOD detection, CUPID Epistemic leads on PAPILA (AUC 0.877, AUPR 0.854) and ACRIMA (AUC 0.978, AUPR 0.984), while CUPID Aleatoric dominates on the extreme-domain CIFAR-10 (AUC 0.983, AUPR 0.998). On super-resolution, CUPID Aleatoric attains Pearson > 0.52 and UCE < 0.05 across Set5, Set14, and BSDS100, and CUPID Epistemic achieves the best Pearson (0.734) on the domain-shifted IXI MRI dataset.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CUPID outperforms all nine baselines on at least one dataset per task. On GLV2 misclassification, CUPID Aleatoric beats MC Dropout (AUC 0.870 vs. 0.768), Rate-in (0.870 vs. 0.815), and BNN (0.870 vs. 0.829), with only BNN slightly ahead on Spearman (0.954 vs. 0.941). On HAM10000, CUPID Epistemic surpasses Rate-in (AUC 0.855 vs. 0.846), MC Dropout (0.855 vs. 0.829), and DEC (0.855 vs. 0.837), while IGRUE and PostNet variants fall well behind (AUC ≤ 0.793). For OOD detection, CUPID Epistemic leads on both in-task shifts (PAPILA AUC 0.877 vs. MC Dropout 0.733; ACRIMA AUC 0.978 vs. IGRUE 0.941), and CUPID Aleatoric dominates the extreme cross-domain CIFAR-10 (AUC 0.983 vs. IGRUE 0.978 and MC Dropout 0.887). In super-resolution, CUPID Aleatoric achieves the best Pearson correlation (>0.52) and lowest UCE (<0.05) on all three natural-image benchmarks, outperforming BayesCap (Pearson 0.42–0.49) and in-rotate (Pearson 0.46–0.49), while CUPID Epistemic leads on the domain-shifted IXI MRI set (Pearson 0.734 vs. in-rotate 0.598). Notably, IGRUE and PostNet degrade severely on several datasets (e.g., IGRUE Spearman drops to 0.027 on HAM10000), and DEC shows poor calibration (rAULC = −0.627 on GLV2), underscoring CUPID's more balanced uncertainty modeling.

## Relation to Prior Work

CUPID sits at the intersection of two model-preserving uncertainty estimation lines: the Bayesian identity mapping approach of BayesCap (Upadhyay et al., 2022) for aleatoric uncertainty on frozen features, and the gradient/sensitivity-based epistemic quantification of Wang & Ji (2024), whose Propositions 3.1 and 3.4 are directly extended in Appendix B to formalize CUPID's structured feature perturbation as a proxy for parameter uncertainty. The heteroscedastic Gaussian loss with log-variance prediction follows Kendall & Gal (2017). The key departure from prior work is the unification of both uncertainty types into a single jointly-trained plug-in module with a shared feature extractor, rather than treating them as separate pipelines or requiring architectural redefinition (BNNs, deep ensembles, evidential networks).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CUPID offers a practical, drop-in solution for uncertainty estimation in deployed systems where retraining the base model is infeasible, and the layer-wise analysis (aleatoric improves near the output, epistemic benefits from earlier layers) provides a new diagnostic lens into how uncertainty propagates through networks. The observation that the dominant uncertainty type shifts with dataset characteristics (aleatoric on noisy/balanced GLV2, epistemic on imbalanced/diverse HAM10000, epistemic under domain shift to IXI) has direct implications for clinical deployment decisions. Limitations include evaluation only on ResNet-18 and ESRGAN backbones, relatively small training budgets (10–15 epochs for CUPID), and the fact that CUPID itself still requires supervised training on the target task, so it is not truly training-free. Open questions include scalability to transformer architectures, whether the joint-training synergy holds at larger scale, and whether the sensitivity×deviation decomposition generalizes beyond locally differentiable settings.
