## Problem

Prior benchmarking of time-series foundation models (TSFMs) for anomaly detection relied on aggregate metrics, single model sizes, and short context windows, leaving open whether these models genuinely outperform simple statistical baselines. This paper asks whether the core assumptions underlying reconstruction-based and forecasting-based TSAD — that anomalies are harder to reconstruct or forecast — actually hold for TSFMs, and finds that they do not.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264337/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each TSFM consumes a sliding context window of length w (64, 256, 512, or 1024 points) from a univariate time series. Forecasting-based models (Chronos, TimesFM, Time-MoE) predict the next single observation and score anomalies by squared prediction error; reconstruction-based models (MOMENT) reconstruct the entire window and score by mean squared reconstruction error. Evaluation is zero-shot on the TSB-AD-U benchmark (350 univariate series from 23 datasets), scored with VUS-PR, and compared against one-liner baselines: moving-window variance and squared-difference.

## Method

The paper systematically evaluates five TSFM families (MOMENT, Chronos, TimesFM, Time-MoE, TSPulse) across two model sizes and three-to-four context window lengths, comparing each against a matched one-liner baseline. A key mechanistic insight is that z-normalization decomposes MOMENT's anomaly score into window variance times normalized reconstruction MSE, and since the reconstruction MSE is nearly constant (~1.1) regardless of anomaly presence, the score reduces to the variance baseline. For forecasting-based models, the single-step prediction horizon is identified as the primary limitation: when the sliding window overlaps a sequence anomaly, the model sees anomalous context and can predict the next anomalous point accurately, violating the 'harder to forecast' assumption. The paper then proposes three future directions to unlock TSFM potential: ensembling short and long forecasting horizons via a MAX operation, detecting anomalies in TSFM hidden representations (e.g., KNN on MOMENT embeddings, which improves VUS-PR from 0.003 to 0.250 on a representative case), and end-to-end fine-tuning with labeled anomalies to align the training objective with detection.

## Results

The central finding is that all five TSFM families (MOMENT, Chronos, TimesFM, Time-MoE, TSPulse) achieve anomaly detection performance statistically indistinguishable from trivial one-liner baselines on the TSB-AD-U benchmark (350 univariate time series). Specifically, MOMENT-base-512 yields a mean VUS-PR of 0.311 versus 0.313 for moving-window variance (correlation 0.991, Cohen's d = −0.047), while forecasting-based models (Chronos-Bolt-base-512: 0.297; Time-MoE-large-512: 0.303; TimesFM-2.0-512: 0.302) all cluster around 0.30 versus 0.284 for the squared-difference baseline, with correlations of 0.92–0.93 and Cohen's d of only 0.10–0.15. Non-inferiority tests confirm that TSFMs do not outperform their baselines by more than 0.008–0.016 VUS-PR at 95% confidence, and the result is invariant to model size (base vs. large), context window length (64/256/512/1024), and the model's underlying reconstruction or forecasting quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MOMENT (reconstruction-based) is essentially equivalent to the moving-window variance baseline: across all sizes and window lengths, the correlation in per-series VUS-PR reaches 0.99 (base) and 0.86 (large), with Cohen's d ≤ 0.18, and the root cause is that z-normalization decomposes the anomaly score into window variance × a near-constant reconstruction MSE (~1.1), making the model a variance detector. All three forecasting-based TSFMs (Chronos-Bolt, TimesFM, Time-MoE) tie with the squared-difference baseline (Last-3 / Centered-5), with mean VUS-PR gaps of only 0.013–0.019 and Cohen's d of 0.07–0.19; the single-step forecasting horizon is identified as the limiting factor, as sequence anomalies become predictable once the context window overlaps the anomaly. TSPulse's reconstruction and forecasting heads individually match Var-96 (0.42) and Last-3 (0.28–0.30), and its triangulation/ensemble (0.44–0.48) is comparable to ensembling the two baselines (0.43–0.47). In contrast, non-TSFM detectors such as Sub-IForest, Sub-LOF, MatrixProfile, and KShapeAD show low correlation with the baselines (|r| < 0.35), confirming that the one-liner equivalence is specific to the TSFM error-based paradigm rather than a property of the benchmark itself. The only non-TSFM exception is TimesNet (r = 0.805 with Var), which also employs z-normalization, further supporting the normalization-driven explanation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a critical re-examination of the Liu & Paparrizos (2024) benchmarking study, which concluded that TSFMs 'show great promise' for TSAD. It departs from that prior work by evaluating multiple model sizes and context window lengths (rather than a single configuration per family) and by introducing deliberately simple one-liner baselines (moving-window variance, squared-difference) inspired by Wu & Keogh (2023). Conceptually, it challenges the foundational assumption inherited from classical TSAD literature—that anomalies are inherently harder to reconstruct or forecast—by showing this assumption is violated specifically for TSFMs due to architectural choices like z-normalization and single-step forecasting horizons.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that five diverse TSFM families, across multiple sizes and context lengths, perform no better than trivial statistical baselines for TSAD fundamentally undermines the current narrative that foundation models will automatically excel at anomaly detection. This has immediate practical implications: practitioners deploying TSFMs for TSAD should expect no advantage over a few lines of code. The paper also identifies concrete root causes (z-normalization coupling scores to window variance; single-step horizons making sequence anomalies predictable) and proposes three alternative directions—multi-horizon ensembling, hidden-representation-based detection, and anomaly-labeled fine-tuning—that could unlock TSFM potential. Open questions include whether these directions scale, whether the findings generalize beyond univariate settings, and whether the TSB-AD benchmark's labeling issues (50 problematic series identified) further distort the field's understanding of TSAD performance.
