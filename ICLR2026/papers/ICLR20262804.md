## Problem

While LLMs demonstrate impressive linguistic abilities, it remains unclear whether they navigate the fundamental compression-meaning trade-off in conceptual representation the way humans do. Prior work has either analyzed LLM conceptual structure without rigorous information-theoretic grounding or applied information theory to human cognition without connecting to modern LLMs, leaving a gap in quantitative comparison of how the two systems balance statistical efficiency against semantic richness.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262804/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes LLM embeddings at two levels—static input-layer vectors (context-free) and contextual hidden-layer representations extracted via a neutral prompt template with average pooling—alongside digitized human categorization data from three classic cognitive psychology studies (Rosch 1973, Rosch 1975, McCloskey & Glucksberg 1978) comprising 1,049 items across 34 categories with both membership labels and graded typicality ratings. Outputs are structured as a three-level analysis: categorical alignment scores (AMI, NMI, ARI) comparing k-means clusters to human categories, Spearman rank correlations between item-centroid distances and human typicality judgments, and the full L objective curve traced across varying numbers of clusters K, revealing each system's compression-meaning frontier.

## Method

The paper adapts Rate-Distortion Theory and the Information Bottleneck principle into a unified L objective that combines an information-theoretic complexity term I(X;C) (bits needed to specify items given their cluster) with a geometric distortion term (average squared distance from items to cluster centroids in embedding space), weighted by β. LLM embeddings are clustered via k-means at K values matching human category counts and evaluated against the digitized human benchmarks, while the same L objective is applied to human-defined categories to expose divergent optimization strategies. A key finding is that LLMs achieve lower (more 'optimal') L scores than human categories, indicating aggressive statistical compression at the expense of the graded typicality structure humans preserve. Training dynamics analysis of OLMo-7B across 57 checkpoints (1K–557K steps) reveals a two-phase trajectory: rapid initial concept formation (AMI rising to ~0.45 within 10% of training) followed by architectural reorganization where semantic processing migrates from deep layers (layer 29) to mid-network layers (layer 23), suggesting the model discovers increasingly efficient, sparser encodings without sacrificing categorical alignment.

## Results

Across 40+ LLMs, static embeddings achieve mean AMI ≈ 0.45 with human categories, rising to peak AMI ≈ 0.55 with contextual processing; BERT-large-uncased (340M) reaches AMI = 0.60, matching or exceeding models 100× larger. However, fine-grained typicality alignment is weak: Spearman ρ < 0.2 for most decoder models, with BERT at ρ = 0.38 and Word2Vec at ρ = 0.44 as the strongest. On the compression–meaning trade-off (L objective), all LLM-derived clusters achieve lower (more 'optimal') L values than human categories, yet L scores show no correlation with MMLU performance (r = −0.20, p = 0.508), indicating statistical efficiency does not predict functional capability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Encoder and representation-focused models consistently outperform decoder-only LLMs on both categorical alignment (AMI) and fine-grained typicality (Spearman ρ). BERT-large-uncased (340M) achieves AMI = 0.60, matching or exceeding Llama-3.1-70B and Qwen-72B, while the 63M-parameter CLIP ViT-B/32 text encoder attains the highest typicality correlation (ρ = 0.527) of any model tested. Classic static embeddings Word2Vec (ρ = 0.44) and GloVe (ρ = 0.35) rival or surpass all modern decoder LLMs on typicality alignment, despite predating Transformers by years. Among decoder models, no family (Llama, Gemma, Qwen, Phi, Mistral) exceeds ρ = 0.16 on any dataset, and several (GPT-2, Qwen2.5-7B) show near-zero or negative correlations. On the L objective, encoder models achieve lower distortion at every complexity level compared to decoders, and all LLM clusters score lower (more 'efficient') than human categories—yet this statistical advantage shows no correlation with MMLU (r = −0.20, p = 0.508), underscoring that compression optimality is orthogonal to downstream task performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate research streams: LLM conceptual representation analysis (Shani et al., 2023; Misra et al., 2021; Park et al., 2025) and information-theoretic approaches to human concept learning (Zaslavsky et al., 2018, 2020; Imel & Zaslavsky, 2024). It adapts Rate-Distortion Theory (Shannon, 1948) and the Information Bottleneck principle (Tishby et al., 2000) into a unified L objective that simultaneously measures compression (mutual information) and semantic distortion (within-cluster variance), then applies this to compare LLM embeddings against digitized classic cognitive psychology benchmarks (Rosch, 1973, 1975; McCloskey & Glucksberg, 1978). Unlike prior LLM representation studies that examine geometry or sparsity in isolation, this work explicitly quantifies the compression-meaning trade-off and contrasts it with human categorical structure, while also digitizing and releasing foundational datasets that were previously unavailable in machine-readable form.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that LLMs achieve information-theoretically 'optimal' compression while systematically underperforming humans on fine-grained semantic structure (typicality correlations ρ < 0.2) challenges the assumption that statistical efficiency in representation equates to understanding, suggesting that the apparent 'inefficiency' of human concepts reflects optimization for flexible reasoning rather than compression. The digitized 1,049-item, 34-category benchmark and the L objective provide the community with concrete, reproducible tools for monitoring the compression-meaning balance in future models. Key open questions include whether architecturally distinct or differently-trained models can preserve semantic richness without sacrificing compression, whether the encoder-decoder divergence reflects a fundamental representational split between understanding and generation, and whether these findings generalize beyond English and text-only settings.
