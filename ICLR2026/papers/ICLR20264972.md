## Problem

Multilevel Monte Carlo (MLMC) uses low-fidelity models as implicit control variates for high-fidelity models, but does not minimize the variance of the level-to-level differences. Conversely, non-parametric Stein-based control functionals (CFs) reduce variance for a single integrand but do not exploit the multi-fidelity hierarchy. The paper addresses this gap by asking whether one can simultaneously leverage the telescoping structure of multi-fidelity decompositions and the variance-reduction power of Stein-based control functionals at every level.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a hierarchy of multi-fidelity function evaluations f_0, …, f_L (where f_L is the target integrand), a target density π whose log-gradient ∇log π is evaluable pointwise (π need not be normalized, accommodating Bayesian posteriors), and a Stein kernel k_0 derived from a base kernel via the Langevin Stein operator. At each level l, m_l samples are used to build the kernel Gram matrix (incurring O(m_l³) cost) and the remaining n_l − m_l samples produce the estimate. The output is an unbiased estimate of Π[f] = ∫f(x)π(x)dx, or, in the variational inference extension, a per-iteration ELBO gradient estimate with a simplified SGD update rule.

## Method

MLCFs replace the plain MC estimator at each MLMC level with a control functional estimator applied to the level difference f_l − f_{l−1}, yielding a telescoping sum of CF estimators. The control functional at level l is a non-parametric Stein-based CV constructed in an RKHS: it solves a constrained least-squares problem using the Stein kernel k_0^l (obtained by applying the Langevin Stein operator to a base kernel k_l) and the m_l training samples, then evaluates the residual on the remaining n_l − m_l samples. The key theoretical contribution is a variance bound of O(m_l^{−2τ_l/d}/(n_l−m_l)) per level, where τ_l = min{a, b_l} depends on the smoothness of π and the kernel, giving a per-level convergence rate of O(n^{−τ/d−1/2}) that strictly improves over MLMC's O(n^{−1/2}). A Lagrange-multiplier derivation yields closed-form optimal sample sizes n_l^{MLCF} that allocate fewer samples to higher-cost, lower-variance levels. For variational inference, the method produces MLCFRG estimators whose SGD update reduces to a single CF evaluation at the current iteration, cutting per-iteration compute from O(d∑ln_l³) to O(dn_L³).

## Results

Across all four integration experiments (synthetic, boundary-value ODE, Lotka-Volterra Bayesian inference, and illustration), MLCF consistently achieves lower absolute integration error than MLMC, CF, and MLBQ under the same evaluation budget, with the gap widening at higher budgets. For variational inference on Bayesian neural networks (UCI Wine Quality Red), MLCFRG achieves a training ELBO of −2101.95 (±36.3) and test log-likelihood of −388.34 (±0.9) with 15 hidden units, outperforming MLMCRG (−2187.77, −388.42), MLMC (−5214.09, −1353.94), and MC (−6979.50, −1683.71). The theoretical convergence rate of MLCF is O(n^{−τ/d − 1/2}) per level versus O(n^{−1/2}) for MLMC, where τ = min{a, b_l} captures the smoothness of the density and integrand.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MLCF outperforms MLMC in all integration experiments (synthetic, ODE, Lotka-Volterra), with the advantage growing at higher budgets; in the ODE example, MLCF with QMC or LHS points further narrows the gap. Against CF (single-level control functionals), MLCF wins because it exploits the multi-fidelity telescoping structure, reducing variance at fine levels where f_L − f_{L−1} already has smaller variance than f_L. In the boundary-value ODE experiment, MLCF also beats MLBQ (multilevel Bayesian quadrature), which is restricted to specific kernel-distribution pairs. For the BNN variational inference extension, MLCFRG improves training ELBO over MLMCRG by ~86 points (−2101.95 vs −2187.77) with 15 hidden units while using only 1 Monte Carlo sample per iteration versus 5 for MLMCRG; with 20 hidden units the two methods are nearly tied (−2014.54 vs −2010.09). Both MLCFRG and MLMCRG dramatically outperform MLMC and MC, confirming that the multilevel re-parameterized gradient structure is the dominant source of variance reduction, with the control functional providing a further modest gain.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two established variance-reduction paradigms: non-parametric Stein-based control functionals (CFs) from Oates et al. (2017, 2019), which use RKHS and the Langevin Stein operator to construct zero-mean control variates, and multilevel Monte Carlo (MLMC) from Giles (2008, 2015), which exploits a multi-fidelity hierarchy via telescoping sums. The key departure from MLMC is that while MLMC implicitly treats f_{l-1} as a control variate for f_l, it does not minimize the variance of the level difference; MLCFs instead apply a learned Stein-based control functional at each level to further reduce variance. The extension to variational inference builds directly on the multilevel re-parameterized gradient (MLRG/MLMCRG) framework of Fujisawa & Sato (2021), replacing their per-level MC estimators with CF estimators. Unlike prior multilevel control variate approaches (Nobile & Tesei 2015, Fairbanks et al. 2017, Geraci et al. 2017) that required domain-specific constructions, MLCFs are kernel-based and broadly applicable without expert knowledge.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MLCFs provide a principled, broadly applicable recipe for combining multi-fidelity structure with Stein-based variance reduction, with theoretical variance bounds and closed-form optimal sample allocation across levels. The method is particularly valuable for Bayesian inference with unnormalized posteriors and for variational inference of Bayesian neural networks, where it outperforms MLMC, CF, and MCMC baselines across multiple experimental settings. Key limitations include the cubic cost of kernel Gram matrix inversion, the requirement for smooth integrands and densities (which limits high-dimensional applicability), and the need for a pre-constructed multi-fidelity hierarchy. Open questions include characterizing precisely when MLCFs yield sufficient improvement over MLMC, making the MLCFRG optimizer more efficient via hardware-accelerated operators, and jointly optimizing sample locations with cost allocation.
