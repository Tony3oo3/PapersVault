## Problem

Standard neural networks rely on fixed activation functions (ReLU, Sigmoid, etc.) that impose a rigid inductive bias, limiting their ability to adapt representations to task-specific structure and efficiently capture high-order multiplicative interactions. While implicit neural representations (SIREN, SPDER, WIRE) improved signal fidelity with specialized periodic activations, they remain fixed and task-specific; KANs introduced learnable activations but suffer from optimization instability and long training times. The paper addresses the gap of an architecture that learns adaptive, factorized activation functions while preserving computational efficiency and stable optimization.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a d-dimensional input vector (2D spatial coordinates for 256×256 images, 1D temporal coordinates for audio clips, or PDE domain coordinates) and produces K-dimensional outputs (pixel intensities, audio amplitudes, PDE solution values, or regression/class targets). Internally, the input is projected via r independent linear maps into r latent vectors in R^{d̄}, and each LRNN neuron computes a product of d̄ univariate functions over its projection, yielding a scalar that is weighted and summed across the r rank terms. The (1 + γg_j(z_j)) factorization ensures the network starts at a constant baseline and gradually learns interactions, with γ = d̄^{-1/2} controlling variance regardless of projection width.

## Method

LRNNs replace the standard scalar activation σ(w^T x + b) in each MLP neuron with a product-structured activation φ(z) = ∏_{j=1}^{d̄}(1 + γ g_j(z_j)), where z = Wx + b is a d̄-dimensional projection and each g_j is a small learnable univariate MLP (typically 2-layer with SIREN or SPDER activations). This multiplicative composition inherently captures all pairwise and higher-order interactions among the d̄ projected coordinates, and in the Fourier domain generates 2^{d̄}−1 combinatorial frequency combinations from just d̄ base frequencies, enabling richer spectral synthesis than additive MLPs. The scaling factor γ = d̄^{-1/2} provides variance-controlled initialization analogous to Xavier/He initialization, ensuring stable forward and backward propagation independent of projection width. Deep LRNNs stack L such layers with LayerNorm after each product-structured computation, and the architecture generalizes both standard MLPs (d̄=1) and the classical separated rank decomposition (identity projection, K=1). Theoretical analysis establishes universal approximation, polynomial parameter complexity for functions with decaying functional ANOVA structure, and adaptive spectral bias control through the product structure.

## Results

On 1,000 ImageNet images, LRNN-SPDER achieves a 100% success rate at the 40 dB PSNR target, whereas SIREN and SPDER fail in 98.2% and 73.6% of cases respectively. On audio representation, LRNN-SPDER achieves 3–11× lower MSE than SIREN and SPDER across all four datasets (e.g., 0.10×10⁻⁴ vs. 1.21×10⁻⁴ on Bach). On the Poisson PDE benchmark, a 16k-parameter LRNN matches or beats a 132k-parameter SIREN (8× parameter reduction), and LRNNs achieve 100–1000× lower error than KANs. On sparse-view CT reconstruction, LRNN attains the best PSNR (29.13 dB) and SSIM (0.7455) among all tested architectures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LRNN-SPDER consistently outperforms its SPDER baseline across all evaluated tasks: on ImageNet it reaches 100% success at 40 dB PSNR versus 26.4% for SPDER, and on audio it achieves 3–11× lower MSE (e.g., 0.10 vs. 1.12 ×10⁻⁴ on Bach). Against SIREN, LRNNs win on every benchmark—image (100% vs. 1.8% at 40 dB), audio (12× lower MSE on Bach), PDE (8× fewer parameters at comparable error), and CT (PSNR 29.13 vs. 27.46 dB). On the PDE benchmark, LRNNs surpass KANs by 100–1000× in error, a dramatic margin that underscores the advantage of product-structured activations over edge-based learnable activations. In CT reconstruction, LRNN also beats WIRE (PSNR 29.13 vs. 28.83 dB) and produces artifact-free reconstructions where WIRE exhibits high-frequency artifacts. The Gaussian-activated network (Ramasinghe & Lucey) and ReLU+PE (NeRF-style) baselines are both clearly outperformed on CT (PSNR 27.84 and 26.89 dB respectively), confirming that the multiplicative product structure provides a distinct inductive bias beyond what any single fixed activation can offer.

## Relation to Prior Work

LRNNs directly generalize the Separated Rank Decomposition (SRD) model of Beylkin et al. (2009), which approximates multivariate functions as sums of products of univariate basis functions, by replacing fixed basis functions with learnable univariate MLPs and adding linear projection layers. The architecture also draws on tensor decomposition theory (CP, Tucker, TT) but repurposes the multiplicative low-rank structure for expressivity rather than compression. In the INR landscape, LRNNs depart from the paradigm of designing fixed periodic activations (SIREN, SPDER, WIRE) or learning edge-based activations (KANs) by instead learning product-structured activations at nodes, where each neuron's non-linearity is a multiplicative composition of independently learned univariate functions. The (1+γg) parameterization connects to functional ANOVA decompositions, giving the architecture an explicit interaction structure absent in standard MLPs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The combinatorial frequency synthesis property (a single neuron with d̄ components generates 2^d̄−1 frequency combinations) provides a principled explanation for why LRNNs outperform additive architectures on high-frequency signals, and the theoretical results on curse-of-dimensionality mitigation for ANOVA-decaying functions offer a clear inductive bias story for scientific computing. Key limitations include a higher backward-pass memory footprint from intermediate product storage and the fact that component functions still rely on standard scalar activations internally. Open questions include whether the architecture scales to 3D scene reconstruction (NeRFs), video, and unsteady PDEs, and whether the product structure can be made more memory-efficient through kernel fusion or mixed-precision strategies.
