## Problem

Existing benchmarks for evaluating multimodal LLMs fail to jointly assess crosslingual and multimodal instruction following: they are typically English-only, single-modality, short-form, or lack human annotations. This makes it impossible to systematically measure how models handle the interaction of language, modality, and input length in realistic instruction-following scenarios.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263250/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Each sample pairs a scientific talk's content (available as full-length or ~16-second segmented speech, video, or text) with a text instruction prompt written in one of four languages (EN, DE, IT, ZH). The model must infer the task type, input modality, and target language solely from the prompt wording, simulating real user interaction. Outputs are always text in the target language: verbatim transcriptions, translations, open-ended QA answers, or ~200-word summaries. The benchmark is fully parallel, so every sample exists in all three modalities and all four languages, enabling controlled ablation studies.

## Method

MCIF is constructed from 100 ACL 2023 scientific talk videos (~10 hours, ~17k words of summaries) with human-annotated gold data: professional linguists produce English transcripts, 16 NLP experts create QA pairs labeled by required modality (A, V, AV, NA) and source type (General, Abstract, Transcript), and professional translators render all content into German, Italian, and Mandarin Chinese. Thirteen tasks are organized into four macro-tasks (recognition, translation, QA, summarization), each with both short-form and long-form variants. Two prompt variants are provided: MCIF_fix uses one fixed prompt per macro-task, while MCIF_mix samples randomly from a pool of ten paraphrased prompts to measure robustness to instruction reformulation. Evaluation of 23 models (7 LLMs, 5 SpeechLLMs, 5 VideoLLMs, 6 MLLMs) uses WER for recognition, COMET for translation, and rescaled BERTScore for QA and summarization, with the full parallel design enabling systematic ablation across modalities, languages, and context lengths.

## Results

On MCIF (averaged across 4 languages), Gemini 2.5 Flash achieves the best overall MLLM performance: 11.9 WER (long-form recognition), 76.4 COMET (long-form translation), 46.1 BERTScore (long-form QA), and 24.1 BERTScore (long-form summarization) under MCIF_fix. LLMs dominate translation, with Qwen3 reaching 84.8 COMET (short-form) and Llama 3.1 at 81.4. Summarization is the most challenging macro-task, with several models (MiniCPM-o-2, GraniteSpeech, LLaVA-NeXT) producing negative BERTScores (worse than random). Long-form inputs cause significant degradation across nearly all models, with SpeechLLMs and VideoLLMs suffering the largest drops (e.g., DeSTA2 recognition WER jumps from 54.0 to 112.9).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the 23 evaluated models, Gemini 2.5 Flash (commercial MLLM) is the strongest overall, leading long-form QA (46.1 BERTScore, vs. 37.6 for the best SpeechLLM Phi4-Multimodal and 35.6 for the best LLM Qwen3) and long-form recognition (11.9 WER, vs. 39.2 for Phi4-Multimodal). LLMs clearly dominate translation: Qwen3 (84.8 COMET short-form) and Llama 3.1 (81.4) outperform the best MLLM (Gemini 2.5 Flash at 76.4 long-form) by 8–18 COMET points. For short-form QA, results are more competitive, with Gemini 2.5 Flash (40.6), Phi4-Multimodal (37.1), and Qwen2.5-VL (37.8) all within ~4 BERTScore of each other. Summarization is the weakest task across all model families: even Gemini 2.5 Flash reaches only 24.1 BERTScore, while MiniCPM-o-2 and GraniteSpeech produce negative scores (−39.1 and 2.8 respectively), indicating outputs worse than random. Comparing MCIF_fix vs. MCIF_mix reveals limited prompt robustness: recognition WER fluctuates by up to 60+ points for some SpeechLLMs (DeSTA2: 54.0→83.0), while translation and QA remain relatively stable (≤5 COMET / ≤6 BERTScore variation for most models).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of three underexplored axes: crosslingual evaluation, multimodal (speech+video+text) instruction following, and long-form context processing. Prior IF benchmarks address at most two of these dimensions—speech-text benchmarks (Speech-ifeval, SAKURA, MMSU) are monolingual and short-form; vision-text benchmarks (MMMU, M3Exam, EXAMS-V) are English-centric and image-only; and the few tri-modal efforts (VideoMME, MF²) lack crosslingual coverage or do not evaluate speech. MCIF repurposes the standard IF paradigm (Hendrycks et al., 2020) and the scientific-talk domain (ACL 2023 presentations) to construct the first fully parallel, human-annotated benchmark spanning all three modalities, four languages, and both short- and long-form inputs simultaneously.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MCIF provides the first systematic evidence that current MLLMs fail to effectively integrate speech and video jointly—video often degrades rather than improves performance, and summarization shows no gains from adding modalities to text. The finding that a text-only LLM outperforms MLLMs on audio-visual QA questions challenges the core assumption that multimodal architectures should dominate on multimodal inputs. Open questions include how to design better multimodal fusion mechanisms, how to handle long-form degradation (under-generation, context window limits), and whether prompt robustness can be improved through contrastive instruction tuning or preference optimization. The benchmark's CC-BY 4.0 release and full parallelism across languages and modalities make it a practical foundation for future ablation studies.
