## Problem

Constrained generative modeling requires that every generated sample strictly satisfies problem-specific constraints (e.g., physical laws, joint limits, watermark regions), yet existing diffusion/flow-matching approaches either support only simple constraint sets (box, simplex) or incur prohibitive computational overhead from boundary localization, expensive projection, or intractable prior sampling over general convex sets. No prior method simultaneously guarantees strict feasibility, bounded distribution approximation error, and generation complexity comparable to unconstrained flow matching over arbitrary compact convex sets.

## Input / Output

Training consumes data samples x₁ from the target distribution over a compact convex set C, which are first transformed to the unit ball via the inverse gauge mapping Φ⁻¹ before standard flow-matching loss is applied. At inference, the model takes random prior samples z₀ drawn uniformly from the unit ball (not a Gaussian), integrates the learned vector field with a closed-form O(n) reflection term to keep trajectories inside the ball, and then applies the forward gauge mapping Φ to produce final samples in C. The gauge function evaluation cost depends on constraint type: O(n) for linear, O(n²) for quadratic, O(nk) for second-order cone, and O(nk²+k³) for matrix cone constraints, with a bisection fallback for general convex functions.

## Method

GFM introduces a bi-Lipschitz bijective gauge mapping Φ built from the Minkowski (gauge) function, which establishes a homeomorphism between any compact convex set C and a unit p-norm ball B by radially scaling from a precomputed interior point x° so that the ball boundary aligns with the set boundary. Training proceeds by inverse-mapping data to the ball and running standard flow matching on the transformed distribution q_data = Φ⁻¹_# p_data; inference integrates the learned vector field over the ball with a closed-form reflection term (O(n) per step) and maps results back via Φ, guaranteeing strict feasibility by construction. The bi-Lipschitz property of Φ (bounded by inner/outer radii r_i, r_o) ensures the transformed distribution retains regularity and yields a Wasserstein-2 error bound of L_Φ · e^{1/2+L_θ} · ε_θ. The framework extends naturally to star-convex sets (preserving radial visibility) and geodesic-convex sets on Riemannian manifolds (replacing Euclidean rays with geodesics via the exponential map), while maintaining the same computational structure.

## Results

On the robotic arm benchmark (joint linear + quadratic convex constraints), GFM achieves 100% feasibility with an MMD of 3.50×10⁻³, matching or beating unconstrained DDPM (4.79×10⁻³) and FM (8.57×10⁻³) in distribution quality while training in 0.18s and inferring in 0.63s—comparable to vanilla FM (0.18s/0.29s) and 22× faster than Reflection (14.0s). On CIFAR-10 watermarked image generation, GFM attains FID 5.85 with 100% watermark feasibility, outperforming Projection (FID 6.88) and Reflection (FID 6.06) at similar inference speed (167.6s vs 556.6s and 183.9s for 5K images). On PEMS-BAY traffic time series, GFM achieves 100% constraint satisfaction with KS statistic 0.35 (p-value 0.42) in 0.43s inference, versus Projection's 49.7s (116× slower) and worse KS of 0.37 (p-value 0.20).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GFM dominates all constrained baselines on both feasibility and quality: it matches their 100% feasibility while achieving 7.4× lower MMD than Reflection (3.50 vs 25.9×10⁻³), 37× lower than Metropolis (3.50 vs 130×10⁻³), and 27× lower than Projection (3.50 vs 93.5×10⁻³) on the robotic arm task. Inference speed is the clearest differentiator—GFM runs in 0.63s versus 14.0s (Reflection), 6.12s (Metropolis), and 7.12s (Projection), a 10–22× speedup, because reflection over a unit ball is O(n) closed-form rather than requiring expensive boundary localization on the original constraint set. Against unconstrained baselines (DDPM, FM), GFM adds a strict feasibility guarantee at negligible cost: MMD is actually lower (3.50 vs 4.79/8.57×10⁻³) and training/inference times are nearly identical (0.18s/0.63s vs 0.17s/0.59s and 0.18s/0.29s). On CIFAR-10 watermarked generation, GFM's FID of 5.85 beats both Projection (6.88) and Reflection (6.06) while maintaining 100% watermark feasibility, with inference time (167.6s for 5K images) close to vanilla FM (155.2s) and 3.3× faster than Projection (556.6s). On PEMS-BAY time series, GFM's 0.43s inference is 116× faster than Projection (49.7s) while yielding a better KS statistic (0.35 vs 0.37) and higher p-value (0.42 vs 0.20), indicating superior distributional fidelity under constraints.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes the gauge mapping originally proposed by Tabas & Zhang (2022) for polytope-to-cube transformations to arbitrary pairs of compact convex sets, and embeds it into the flow matching framework (Lipman et al., 2022) with a reflection mechanism (Xie et al., 2024) for feasibility enforcement. Conceptually, it is related to mirror map approaches (Liu et al., 2024b; Guan et al., 2025) that also use bijective transformations to simplify constrained generation, but departs from them by maintaining bounded bi-Lipschitz constants for general convex sets (whereas mirror maps send boundary points to infinity) and by requiring only a unit-ball prior rather than a prior supported on the constraint set itself. The key architectural departure from reflection/projection baselines is that all expensive constraint-specific operations are replaced by a single O(n) reflection on the unit ball plus a closed-form gauge mapping back to the original domain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GFM fills a notable gap in constrained generative modeling by providing the first framework that simultaneously guarantees strict feasibility, bounded Wasserstein approximation error, and near-vanilla flow-matching computational complexity over *general* compact convex sets, rather than only simple shapes like balls or simplices. The extension to star-convex and geodesic-convex sets broadens applicability to robotic planning and Riemannian geometry tasks. Key limitations include restriction to these specific non-convex classes (general non-convex sets remain open), reliance on multi-step ODE integration, and continuous-domain focus; the authors suggest decomposition strategies, discrete embeddings, and one-step generation as future directions. For practitioners in watermarking, robotics, and physics-informed generation, the framework offers a drop-in replacement for expensive projection or reflection pipelines with minimal quality loss.
