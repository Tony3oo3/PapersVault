## Problem

Unsupervised RLVR (RL with verifiable rewards without ground-truth labels) promises to scale LLM post-training beyond the human supervision bottleneck, but current intrinsic reward methods (majority voting, entropy-based metrics) show early gains followed by collapse, and the field lacks a unified understanding of why this happens and where the fundamental limits lie. This paper asks: can intrinsic rewards truly scale LLM training, and what determines when they succeed versus fail?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260504/cropped_page3_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes unlabeled text prompts (math problems from DAPO-17k, MATH500, or Countdown) without ground-truth answer labels, generating N rollouts per prompt at a specified temperature to compute intrinsic rewards (e.g., majority vote across rollouts, per-token entropy, or sequence probability). It produces an updated LLM policy via GRPO policy-gradient updates, and additionally yields the Model Collapse Step metric — the training step at which reward accuracy drops below 1% — as a lightweight diagnostic of a base model's RL trainability without requiring any ground-truth supervision.

## Method

The paper establishes a unified theoretical framework showing that all intrinsic reward methods (certainty-based and ensemble-based) can be expressed as cross-entropy manipulation between anchor and model distributions, and that under KL-regularized policy-gradient updates, they all converge geometrically toward a deterministic policy concentrated on the model's initial majority answer (Theorem 1). This sharpening mechanism succeeds when the model's initial confidence aligns with correctness but amplifies errors when misaligned, producing a universal rise-then-fall training pattern whose collapse timing is determined by model prior rather than hyperparameters. The paper proposes Model Collapse Step as a practical, label-free indicator of RL trainability that matches or surpasses pass@k in predictive accuracy while requiring 5.6× fewer inference tokens than a full RL run. Finally, it demonstrates that external rewards grounded in generation-verification asymmetries (e.g., self-verification on Countdown) escape the intrinsic confidence ceiling by providing signals that do not degrade as the model's distribution shifts.

## Results

Intrinsic URLVR universally follows a rise-then-fall pattern: majority-voting training initially matches or exceeds ground-truth RLVR on AIME24/AIME25/AMC23, but collapse occurs at ~1,000 steps even under the most stable hyperparameter settings. Training on ≤128 samples (e.g., DAPO-32) prevents collapse entirely, with KL divergence from the reference model reaching only 0.057 after 600 steps versus 2× higher for DAPO-512. Model Collapse Step requires only 1.19B inference tokens (5.6× fewer than a 6.66B-token standard RL run) while matching or surpassing pass@k in predicting RL trainability across 7 models from 3 families. On the Countdown task, Self-Verification achieves higher validation accuracy than Trajectory-Level Entropy on both Qwen3-1.7B-Base and 4B-Base, with Reward Accuracy recovering above 0.5 after an initial dip around step 200.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between intrinsic URLVR (majority voting, entropy-based, probability-based rewards) and standard supervised RLVR with ground-truth labels: intrinsic methods initially match or exceed supervised performance but inevitably diverge, with the proxy reward rising while validation accuracy falls (reward hacking). Among intrinsic methods, no hyperparameter choice (temperature, mini-batch size, KL coefficient, rollout count) prevents collapse—only the timing shifts. In the self-verification experiment (Section 7), Self-Verification clearly outperforms Trajectory-Level Entropy (EM-RL) on Countdown validation accuracy for both 1.7B and 4B models, while Oracle Supervision remains the upper bound; Self-Verification's Reward Accuracy recovers and stabilizes above 0.5, indicating genuine learning rather than reward hacking. As a trainability predictor, Model Collapse Step correlates more strongly with actual GT Gain from supervised RL than pass@k does, and unlike pass@k it is robust to multiple-choice questions where pass@k saturates toward 1.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on TTRL (Zuo et al., 2025) and the broader wave of intrinsic-reward URLVR methods (entropy minimization, self-certainty, majority voting) that have reported encouraging early gains. Rather than proposing yet another reward variant, it provides a unifying theoretical lens showing that all intrinsic methods—despite diverse formulations—reduce to sharpening the model's initial distribution, and proves geometric convergence to a deterministic policy under stable majority. It departs from prior empirical characterizations by establishing that the rise-then-fall collapse pattern is universal across methods and hyperparameters, reframing the problem from 'which reward works best' to 'when does the model prior permit sharpening to help.'

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes a fundamental ceiling on intrinsic URLVR: performance is bounded by the model's initial confidence-correctness alignment, and no engineering choice can escape this. This reframes the field's direction toward external rewards (generation-verification asymmetries, unlabeled-data signals) as the only path to scalable unsupervised training. The Model Collapse Step metric offers a practical, label-free diagnostic for RL trainability that is 5.6× cheaper than a full RL run, potentially changing how practitioners select base models. Open questions include whether the self-verification results on Countdown generalize to open-ended domains, and whether hybrid intrinsic-external reward schemes can combine the safety of small-dataset sharpening with the scalability of external signals.
