## Problem

Equivariant machine learning for tensors has been largely limited to low-dimensional rotation groups (SO(2), SO(3)) via Clebsch-Gordan coefficient decompositions, with no general recipe covering arbitrary tensor orders, indefinite orthogonal (Lorentz) groups, or symplectic groups. This paper asks: can one characterize *all* equivariant tensor-to-tensor functions for these classical Lie groups in a form directly usable as a neural network architecture?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263278/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The architecture accepts n input vectors (or, in the symmetric-matrix variant, a single symmetric 2-tensor) in R^d and outputs a k-tensor in R^d. The output is structured as a linear combination of at most ⌊k/2⌋+1 terms, each being a permutation of a tensor product of (k−2t) input vectors and t copies of the group's invariant bilinear form (Kronecker delta for O(d), I_{s,d−s} for O(s,d−s), or J_d for Sp(d)). The scalar coefficients are learned MLPs whose inputs are all pairwise inner products ⟨v_i, v_j⟩_G, so the network's inductive bias is that only these G-invariant scalars can modulate the tensor structure.

## Method

The core result (Theorem 1) shows every O(d)-equivariant polynomial tensor map decomposes into tensor products of the inputs with O(d)-isotropic tensors followed by Einstein-summation contractions; Corollary 1 specializes this to vector inputs, yielding a sum over permutations σ ∈ S_k and index tuples J of terms q_{t,σ,J}(⟨v_i,v_j⟩) · (v_{J_1}⊗…⊗v_{J_{k−2t}}⊗δ^{⊗t})^σ. The isotropic tensors are characterized by Lemma 3 (Jeffreys 1973) as linear combinations of Kronecker-delta and Levi-Civita tensor products, eliminating the need for Clebsch-Gordan coefficients. Theorem 2 and Corollary 3 extend the construction to O(s,d−s) and Sp(d) by replacing δ with the group's invariant bilinear form and using the corresponding G-isotropic tensor characterization. In practice, the q_{t,σ,J} functions are shared MLPs, and for the symmetric-matrix case (Corollary 2) the problem reduces to a permutation-equivariant function on eigenvalues handled by a graph network. This invariant-theory parameterization is group- and dimension-agnostic, unlike Clebsch-Gordan-based methods restricted to SO(2)/SO(3).

## Results

On the TFENN stress-strain tensor dataset, the proposed equivariant model achieves a squared Frobenius error of 4.057e-6 at n=5,000 samples, outperforming the TFENN equivariant baseline (5.3e-5) by over 10× and the MLP baseline (1.586e-4) by nearly 40×. On the path signature estimation task, the method achieves errors of 0.002 (O(d)) and 0.005 (Lorentz), beating the best MLP-augmented baseline (0.007 and 0.186 respectively) by 3.5× and 37×. For sparse vector estimation, the equivariant model achieves ⟨v, v̂⟩² values of 0.938–0.957 under Random and Bernoulli-Rademacher sampling, substantially outperforming the MLP baseline (0.241–0.923) while matching or exceeding SoS methods when their theoretical assumptions are violated.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against TFENN (Garanger et al., 2024), the proposed method achieves 10–40× lower squared Frobenius error on the stress-strain tensor task across all dataset sizes, demonstrating that the invariant-theory-based parameterization (Corollary 2) is more sample-efficient than the prior equivariant architecture. Against SoS methods (Hopkins et al., 2016; Mao & Wein, 2022) on sparse vector estimation, the learned equivariant model wins decisively when the SoS theoretical assumptions are violated (Random or Diagonal noise covariance), achieving ⟨v, v̂⟩² of 0.938–0.957 versus 0.241–0.526 for SoS, while SoS retains an edge under the Identity covariance setting where its guarantees hold. Against all MLP baselines (same width, same parameter count, and data-augmented variants), the equivariant model consistently achieves the lowest error across all three experiments, with the gap most dramatic on the Lorentz path signature task (0.005 vs. 0.186 for the best MLP) and the sparse vector problem under Random sampling (0.938 vs. 0.241). The results collectively support the paper's central claim that enforcing O(d), Lorentz, and symplectic equivariance via invariant-theory parameterizations improves both accuracy and generalization over non-equivariant alternatives.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the invariant-theory-based equivariant ML framework of Villar et al. (2021) from O(d)-invariant scalar functions to full O(d)-, O(s,d-s)-, and Sp(d)-equivariant tensor-to-tensor polynomial maps, using Kronecker deltas and Levi-Civita symbols rather than Clebsch-Gordan decompositions. It is closest in spirit to Kunisky et al. (2024) on O(d)-invariant tensor polynomials, but generalizes to equivariant (not just invariant) maps, to multiple groups, to arbitrary tensor orders and parities, and to learning applications. The Clebsch-Gordan-based approaches (e3nn, escnn, Domina et al. 2025) achieve similar expressivity for SO(d)/O(d) in low dimensions but require explicit representation-theoretic computation; this paper's invariant-theory parameterization is group-agnostic and avoids that overhead at the cost of higher memory for large intermediate tensor orders.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is, to the authors' knowledge, the first general recipe for equivariant ML on tensors that simultaneously covers the orthogonal, indefinite orthogonal (Lorentz), and symplectic groups with arbitrary input/output tensor orders and parities, making it a practical toolkit for physics-informed learning across relativity, classical mechanics, and Euclidean geometry. The consistent outperformance of non-equivariant baselines across three structurally different applications (hyperelastic constitutive modeling, path-signature estimation, sparse vector recovery) validates the inductive-bias argument, and the sparse-vector results show learned equivariant models can operate in regimes where sum-of-squares theoretical guarantees do not apply. Open questions include the computational cost for high-rank output tensors (the k′! factor in Corollary 1), whether the polynomial characterization extends to all continuous equivariant maps, and how the framework scales to higher dimensions where the number of isotropic tensor basis elements grows rapidly.
