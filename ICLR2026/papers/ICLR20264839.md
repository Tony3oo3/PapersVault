## Problem

Whether transformers can perform noise-robust learning — learning a boolean function from training data with noisy features that generalizes to noiseless inputs — was largely unexplored, despite prior work establishing transformers' robustness to noise at evaluation time. This paper shows that transformers' simplicity bias causes them to fail at this task for a broad class of boolean functions (random k-juntas), even while achieving near-optimal validation accuracy on noisy data, and identifies the mechanism behind this failure.

## Input / Output

Models receive length-n binary bitstrings corrupted by iid bitflip noise (each bit flipped independently with probability p) as features, paired with a boolean label Y = f(X) computed from the noiseless input. The key evaluation metric is noiseless generalization error Pr_X(g(X) ≠ f(X)) — whether the learned function correctly predicts on clean inputs after training exclusively on noisy (Z, Y) pairs. Functions studied include sparse parity PARITY(n,k), sparse majority MAJ(n,k), and random k-juntas over n=10 bits with k ∈ {5,6,7}, with bitflip rates ranging from 0 to 0.49.

## Method

The paper combines empirical experiments with a theoretical result (Proposition 2) proving that for uniformly random boolean functions, the Bayes-optimal noisy predictor f_N* = sign(T_{1-2p} f) has lower average sensitivity than f, meaning the optimal solution for noisy data is structurally simpler than the target. Since transformers exhibit a well-documented bias toward low-sensitivity functions, they converge to f_N* rather than f when the two differ, achieving near-optimal noisy validation error while failing on noiseless evaluation. To isolate this mechanism, the authors construct a controlled 'trap' function where err_f(f_N*) ≈ err_f(f) but I[f_N*] ≪ I[f], demonstrating that transformers are trapped at the simpler solution while LSTMs fail via overfitting for entirely different reasons. They further show that adding a sensitivity penalty term (−λI[f̂]) to the cross-entropy loss allows transformers to escape the trap and recover f, confirming simplicity bias as the root cause of the failure.

## Results

Across 300 training runs per configuration, the median transformer (SAN) reliably outperforms the best LSTM on MAJ(20,5), MAJ(40,5), and MAJ(50,3) at all tested bitflip rates, approaching the information-theoretic optimal (f*_N). For PARITY(20,4), transformers achieve ≥95% noiseless accuracy in a significantly higher fraction of runs than LSTMs, even when both architectures perform comparably at zero noise. However, across 3200 randomly-generated k-juntas (k∈{5,6,7}, n=10 bits), transformers fail at noise-robust learning as the sensitivity gap I[f]−I[f*_N] grows, despite achieving near-optimal noisy validation error with minimal overfitting. A controlled trap-function experiment confirms that transformers converge to the simpler f*_N rather than the target f, and that adding a sensitivity penalty (−λI[f̂]) to the loss allows transformers to escape this trap for a narrow range of λ.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares self-attention network transformers (SANs) against LSTMs as the primary baseline across all experiments. On sparse majority functions with odd k (MAJ(20,5), MAJ(40,5), MAJ(50,3)) and sparse parity (PARITY(20,4)), the median SAN outperforms the best-of-300 LSTM at every nonzero bitflip rate, with the gap widening as noise increases; LSTMs are practically incapable of noise-robust learning for PARITY(20,4) beyond zero noise. On random k-juntas, both architectures fail at noise-robust learning but for fundamentally different reasons: SANs converge to the lower-sensitivity f*_N (simplicity bias), while LSTMs overfit to training data and perform no better than memorization. On MAJ(30,4) (even k, where f≠f*_N and f*_N is heavily biased), the performance gap between median SAN and best LSTM almost completely disappears, as both struggle to learn the imbalanced target. The sensitivity-penalty variant (SAN+λ) demonstrates that a complexity bias can rescue transformers from the trap, but only for a narrow λ range and only when err_f(f*_N)≈err_f(f); when f*_N is substantially more accurate than f (e.g., MAJ(30,4) at p=0.32), even strong complexity bias cannot recover the target function.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the simplicity-bias literature for transformers (Bhattamishra et al., 2023b; Hahn & Rofin, 2024) by studying a qualitatively different setting: training on noisy features rather than evaluating on noisy inputs. It builds on the self-predicting function framework of Weinberger & Shayevitz (2018) to explain why PARITY and MAJ are special cases where transformers succeed, and uses the Fourier-spectral regularization idea of Gorji et al. (2023) to construct a sensitivity penalty that can escape the simplicity trap. The paper also repositions next-token prediction from a source-coding/compression perspective (Deletang et al., 2024) to a noisy-channel-coding one (Shannon, 1948), arguing that feature noise in training data is fundamentally different from label noise or evaluation-time perturbation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that transformers' simplicity bias, which confers robustness at evaluation time, actively prevents them from learning sensitive functions when training data contain feature noise—has direct implications for LLMs learning arithmetic or discrete reasoning from noisy text. The paper identifies a fundamental tension: the optimal predictor for noisy data is typically simpler than the target function, so risk minimization on noisy data will converge to the wrong solution. Open questions include whether these results extend to correlated or non-iid noise, to natural language tasks, and whether more sophisticated regularization (beyond the narrow sensitivity penalty demonstrated) can resolve the mismatch. The synthetic boolean-function setting, while clean, limits direct transferability to real-world NLP.
