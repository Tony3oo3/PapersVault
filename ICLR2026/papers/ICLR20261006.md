## Problem

Prior spatial reasoning benchmarks for VLMs rely heavily on synthetic scenes, template-generated questions, or narrow task scopes (2–14 categories), which oversimplify spatial structure and mask failures under real-world visual noise, occlusion, and multi-step relational complexity. SPATIALAB addresses this by introducing a benchmark of 1,400 manually authored QA pairs spanning 30 fine-grained task types across six spatial categories, drawn from diverse real-world imagery, to expose the true gap between current VLM capabilities and human spatial reasoning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261006/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each evaluation item pairs a real-world RGB image (averaging 21.48 objects, 11.88 partially visible, across 3.23 depth layers, with multi-step spatial reference chains of 2.07 links) with a spatial question. In the MCQ format, the model selects one of four options; in the open-ended format, it generates a free-form answer scored by an LLM judge (Gemini-2.5-Flash, validated at Cohen's κ = 0.738 against human majority vote). Images are profiled along six meta-dimensions—lighting, texture, edge complexity, dominant spatial relation, material type, and gravity constraints—to ensure coverage of in-the-wild variability absent from synthetic datasets.

## Method

SPATIALAB is constructed through a three-source image pipeline (web crawling, targeted retrieval, manual snapshots) followed by three-phase annotation (training, QA authoring, dual-format encoding) and a three-tier quality-control review yielding 1,400 validated items. The benchmark organizes tasks into six principal categories (Relative Positioning, Depth & Occlusion, Orientation, Size & Scale, Spatial Navigation, 3D Geometry), each decomposed into five subcategories for 30 distinct task types, with ≥25 questions per subcategory and ≥200 per category. Evaluation covers 25+ VLMs across four groups (proprietary, open-source, reasoning-tuned, spatial-specialist) in both MCQ and open-ended settings, benchmarked against human annotators. The paper further probes performance improvement strategies—SFT on Qwen-VL2.5-3B, CoT prompting, CoT with self-reflection, and a multi-agent pipeline (SPATIOXOLVER adapted from Xolver)—revealing that reasoning scaffolds help orientation and alignment tasks but fail to correct fundamental perceptual grounding deficits in depth, occlusion, and multi-step navigation.

## Results

On SPATIALAB-MCQ (1,400 questions, 6 categories, 30 subcategories), the best model InternVL3.5-72B achieves 54.93% overall accuracy, far below the human baseline of 87.57% (random choice = 25%). On SPATIALAB-OPEN, the best model GPT-5-mini scores 40.93% versus 64.93% for humans, with an average MCQ-to-open-ended performance drop of 23.0% (σ=5.5%) across 25 models. Spatial Navigation, Depth & Occlusion, and Size & Scale are the hardest categories for all models, while Orientation and 3D Geometry see the highest model scores (several exceed 50%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among proprietary models, GPT-5-mini (54.29%) and Gemini-2.5-Flash (48.29%) lead, while Claude 3.5 Haiku (42.93%) and GPT-4o-mini (46.50%) trail; GPT-5-mini's advantage is concentrated in Orientation (60.40%) and Relative Positioning (62.74%). Among open-source models, InternVL3.5-72B (54.93%) is the overall best performer, outscoring Qwen-VL2.5-72B (48.86%) and Llama-3.2-90B (50.36%), with a particularly strong Relative Positioning score of 66.04%. Reasoning-tuned models (o4-mini-medium: 53.21%, Gemini-2.5-Flash-Thinking: 52.93%) approach but do not surpass the best open-source model, and their gains are uneven—o4-mini drops to 38.89% on Size & Scale. Spatially specialized models (SpaceOm: 41.36%, SpaceThinker: 40.64%, SpaceQwen: 40.14%) underperform general-purpose VLMs by 10–15 points, indicating that task-specific spatial pretraining does not transfer to diverse real-world scenes. In the open-ended setting, GPT-5-mini (40.93%) leads all models, with o4-mini-medium (37.86%) and Gemini-2.5-Flash-Thinking (32.77%) following, while spatial specialists collapse to 10–13%, and the human baseline (64.93%) remains 24 points above the best model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of spatial reasoning benchmarks for VLMs (CLEVR, GQA, EmbSpatial-Bench, Space3D-Bench, BLINK-Spatial, RoboSpatial, VSI-Bench, OmniSpatial) by moving from synthetic, template-driven, or narrow-scope evaluations to a real-world, unconstrained setting with 30 fine-grained task types across six categories. It departs from prior work in three key ways: (1) using in-the-wild photographic imagery with controlled complexity dimensions (lighting, texture, depth layers, material) rather than rendered or LLM-generated scenes, (2) supporting both multiple-choice and open-ended evaluation to separate discriminative from generative spatial competence, and (3) incorporating a multi-agent spatial reasoning pipeline (SPATIOXOLVER) adapted from Xolver (Hosain et al., 2025) as one of several intervention strategies. The paper also positions itself against the growing family of spatial-specialist VLMs (SpatialVLM, SpaceOm, SpaceThinker) by testing whether architectural specialization actually translates to broader spatial competence.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SPATIALAB provides a much-needed diagnostic tool that exposes a persistent 30–45 point gap between state-of-the-art VLMs and humans on spatial reasoning, with the gap widening further in open-ended generation. The finding that spatially specialized models (SpaceOm, SpaceThinker, SpaceQwen) perform at or near the level of general-purpose small models challenges the assumption that domain-specific training is sufficient, while the observation that CoT prompting often degrades rather than improves spatial accuracy suggests that step-by-step verbal reasoning cannot compensate for missing perceptual grounding. Open questions include whether richer embodied training signals (action-conditioned feedback, physics-aware supervision) or architectural mechanisms for explicit geometric reference-frame tracking can close the gap, and whether the MCQ-to-open-ended performance drop (~23%) reflects a fundamental limitation in generative spatial description or merely a calibration issue. The benchmark's moderate scale (1,400 pairs) limits statistical power for subcategory-level analysis, and the reliance on an LLM judge for open-ended scoring introduces a potential circularity that the authors partially address with human agreement analysis.
