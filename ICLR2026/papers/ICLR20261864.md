## Problem

Existing data-driven global weather forecasting methods suffer from two fundamental limitations: (1) they train independent single-interval models that ignore multi-scale temporal correlations and treat the Earth as a flat 2D image, failing to capture the circular longitude structure; and (2) they rely on inflexible autoregressive rollout schemes (naive fixed-interval or greedy largest-interval) that cannot adapt to the non-uniform nature of atmospheric evolution, struggling to balance error accumulation against the need for fine-grained dynamics during rapid transitions.

## Input / Output

The model takes an initial weather state X_0 ∈ R^{V×H×W} on a 128×256 equirectangular grid (1.40625° resolution) comprising 13 pressure-level variables (Z, Q, T, U, V at 50–1000 hPa) and 4 surface variables (T2m, U10, V10, TCC), along with a target lead time. It predicts weather changes Δ_δ = X_δ − X_0 rather than absolute states, and produces the final forecast by following an adaptive autoregressive trajectory where the time interval (6h, 12h, or 24h) at each step is dynamically selected by the AR Scheduler based on the current predicted weather state and temporal position in the trajectory.

## Method

ARROW comprises two stages. Stage 1 pre-trains a Multi-Interval Forecasting Model (MIFM) that unifies 6h/12h/24h forecasting in a single Transformer with 16 layers, using a Shared-Private Mixture-of-Experts (1 shared + 9 private FFNs, top-3 routing) where interval-conditioned noise perturbs gate scores to route tokens to interval-specific experts, regularized by two auxiliary cross-entropy losses enforcing expert specialization and global load balance; Ring Positional Encoding replaces standard 2D positional encoding to encode the circular longitude structure via sin/cos terms with periodicity matching the grid width. Stage 2 introduces an Adaptive Rollout Scheduler formulated as a Deep Q-Network that selects the optimal time interval at each autoregressive step given the current weather embedding and temporal position, with reward defined as negative latitude-weighted RMSE plus a trajectory-length penalty; because fine-tuning the MIFM under the learned policy changes the environment, the paper solves the resulting bi-level optimization via alternating optimization between DQN temporal-difference updates and multi-step fine-tuning of the MIFM prediction head.

## Results

On the WeatherBench benchmark (ERA5, 128×256 grid), ARROW achieves state-of-the-art performance across six meteorological variables (T2m, U10, V10, TCC, Z500, T850) at lead times from 5 to 14 days. On average, ARROW improves RMSE by 9.3% and ACC by 10% over the second-best data-driven model across all variables and lead times. At the 14-day lead time, ARROW's advantage is most pronounced, e.g., T2m RMSE of 2.99 vs. 4.15 (Stormer) and 3.58 (FourcastNet), and Z500 RMSE of 887.37 vs. 996.46 (Stormer) and 1,063.54 (FourcastNet).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ARROW outperforms all seven baselines on average across the six evaluated variables and four lead times. Against the strongest data-driven competitors, FourcastNet and Stormer, ARROW achieves the largest gains at longer lead times: at 14 days, ARROW reduces T2m RMSE from 3.58 (FourcastNet) / 4.15 (Stormer) to 2.99, and Z500 RMSE from 1,063.54 / 996.46 to 887.37. Against Pangu-weather (which uses a greedy multi-interval rollout), ARROW consistently wins, e.g., T2m 14-day RMSE 2.99 vs. 3.54, confirming that adaptive rollout surpasses fixed greedy scheduling. Against GraphCast (multi-scale GNN), ARROW leads on all variables at 9-day and 14-day, with T2m RMSE 2.50 vs. 3.24 at 14 days. Keisler (bipartite GNN) is the weakest baseline, with 14-day T2m RMSE of 5.84 versus ARROW's 2.99. IFS (ECMWF's operational NWP) is competitive at 5-day (e.g., T2m RMSE 1.83 vs. ARROW's 1.66) but degrades rapidly beyond 10 days, while ARROW maintains skill through 14 days. Climatology serves as a trivial lower bound and is outperformed by all learned methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

ARROW extends the iterative-prediction paradigm for data-driven weather forecasting (FourCastNet, Pangu-weather, Stormer) in two orthogonal directions. Architecturally, it replaces the standard practice of training independent single-interval forecasting models with a unified Multi-Interval Forecasting Model that uses a Shared-Private Mixture-of-Experts to capture both common and interval-specific atmospheric dynamics, plus a Ring Positional Encoding that respects the Earth's longitudinal circularity—addressing the 'flat image' simplification in prior Transformer-based models. Strategically, it introduces the first reinforcement-learning-based adaptive rollout scheduler for weather forecasting, formulating interval selection as a DQN decision problem and solving the resulting bi-level optimization (policy vs. environment) via alternating updates, a departure from the fixed or greedy rollout strategies used by all prior data-driven methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The adaptive rollout concept is genuinely new for weather forecasting and offers a principled way to balance error accumulation against fine-grained atmospheric resolution, with the learned policy demonstrably correlating with real meteorological regime shifts (seasonal transitions, typhoons). The unified multi-interval model also reduces the computational and maintenance overhead of training and serving separate models per interval. Open questions include whether the RL scheduler generalizes beyond the WeatherBench/ERA5 setting, whether the bi-level optimization admits stronger convergence guarantees, and whether physics-guided constraints could further improve the learned rollout policy. The method is currently validated on a single benchmark at 1.4° resolution, leaving open its behavior at higher resolutions or for regional forecasting.
