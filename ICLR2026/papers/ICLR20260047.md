## Problem

The Fisher Information Matrix (FIM) is the unique metric tensor on the neuromanifold, yet existing estimators lack tight theoretical guarantees: the empirical FIM is biased with error that can be arbitrarily large under adversarial labels, and the Monte Carlo FIM has unbounded coefficient of variation when the input distribution is heavy-tailed. No prior method simultaneously provides deterministic bounds with controlled gap and an unbiased random estimator with provably bounded variance.

## Input / Output

The method consumes the network parameters θ, a batch of inputs x, and the corresponding output logits z(x,θ) (from which probabilities p(y|x,θ) are derived via softmax). It produces (i) deterministic lower and upper bounds of the full FIM in the Loewner partial order, expressed in terms of the singular values of the parameter-output Jacobian ∂z/∂θ and the order statistics of the probability vector, and (ii) a single-sample unbiased rank-1 random estimate of the FIM (or its diagonal) requiring exactly one additional backward pass per batch. The Hutchinson estimate is model-agnostic and architecture-independent, applicable to any differentiable classifier.

## Method

The paper first analyzes the FIM in the low-dimensional 'core space' (the probability simplex Δ^{C−1}), establishing its spectral structure (Theorem 1) and identifying tight envelopes: a rank-1 lower bound λ_C v_C v_C^⊤ and a diagonal upper bound diag(p) (Lemma 2). These core-space envelopes are then lifted to the neuromanifold via the Gauss-Newton form F(θ) = Σ_x (∂z/∂θ)^⊤ I(z) (∂z/∂θ), yielding deterministic Loewner-order bounds (Proposition 5) whose tightness is characterized by the trimmed probability vector and Jacobian singular values (Propositions 7–8). The key stochastic contribution is a Hutchinson-based estimator: a scalar probe function h = Σ_{x,y} √p̃(y|x,θ)·ℓ_{xy}(θ)·ξ_{xy} is constructed with Rademacher probes ξ and stop-gradient on p̃, its gradient ∂h/∂θ is computed via a single AD backward pass, and the outer product (∂h/∂θ)(∂h/∂θ)^⊤ gives an unbiased rank-1 sample of F. Theorem 11 proves Var(F_ii) ≤ 2F_ii², bounding the coefficient of variation by √2, in contrast to the unbounded CV of MC estimators. Variants for diagonal-core (multi-label) and low-rank-core (top-k eigenvectors via power iteration) settings are also provided, with the low-rank variant offering improved accuracy on fine-tuned models where the core FIM is approximately low-rank.

## Results

The paper evaluates FIM estimators using Relative Mean Absolute Error (RelMAE) against ground-truth diagonal FIM entries across six tasks. Hutchinson's unbiased estimate ($\mathbb{F}_{ii}$) achieves the best overall RelMAE, ranging from 0.11 (CIFAR-100) to 0.22 (DBpedia), corresponding to roughly ±10–20% relative deviation. On fine-tuned NLP models (SST-2, MNLI), the low-rank estimators $\mathbb{F}_{ii}^{\mathrm{LR}}$ and $\mathbb{F}_{ii}^{\mathrm{LR}(2)}$ are most accurate (RelMAE 0.05–0.12), while the empirical FIM $\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{ii}$ is the least accurate, with RelMAE as high as 56.8 on SpeechCommands and 53.9 on MNLI. All estimators require a single backward pass per batch, with Hutchinson's estimate matching the empirical FIM in speed (×1.00–×1.07 speedup).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares five single-backward-pass FIM estimators: the empirical FIM ($\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{ii}$, from Le Roux et al. 2007), Hutchinson's unbiased estimate ($\mathbb{F}_{ii}$), a diagonal-core estimate ($\mathbb{F}_{ii}^{\mathrm{DG}}$), and two low-rank estimates ($\mathbb{F}_{ii}^{\mathrm{LR}}$, $\mathbb{F}_{ii}^{\mathrm{LR}(2)}$). Hutchinson's estimate dominates the empirical FIM on every task, reducing RelMAE from 53.9 to 0.16 on MNLI and from 56.8 to 0.17 on SpeechCommands — a >300× improvement where the eFIM is most biased. On CIFAR-100 (randomly initialized heads), Hutchinson's estimate and the diagonal-core estimate tie at RelMAE ≈ 0.11, both outperforming the eFIM (0.17). On fine-tuned NLP models (SST-2, MNLI), the low-rank estimators are most accurate (RelMAE 0.05–0.12) because the core FIM is approximately low-rank after fine-tuning, but they are ~4–5× slower than Hutchinson's estimate on CIFAR-100 due to power-iteration overhead. The diagonal-core estimate $\mathbb{F}_{ii}^{\mathrm{DG}}$ is consistently the worst or second-worst, with RelMAE of 341 on SST-2, confirming that the upper bound in Proposition 5 is a poor point estimate.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper applies Hutchinson's stochastic trace estimator (Hutchinson, 1990; Skorski, 2021) to the Fisher Information Matrix in a way that prior work had not: instead of estimating only the trace (Jastrzebski et al., 2021) or the Hessian diagonal (Yao et al., 2021), it constructs an unbiased rank-1 matrix estimator of the full FIM with a single backward pass. The deterministic bounds build on the Gauss-Newton/pullback structure of the FIM (Martens et al., 2010; Sun, 2020) and new spectral analysis of the simplex FIM, departing from prior coarse bounds (Soen & Sun, 2024) by exploiting the order statistics of output probabilities. The paper positions itself against both the biased empirical FIM (Le Roux et al., 2007) and the unbounded-variance Monte Carlo estimator, showing that Hutchinson's method uniquely combines unbiasedness, bounded CV, and single-backward-pass efficiency.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The FIM underpins natural gradient, pruning, transfer learning, and generalization theory, yet reliable estimation at scale has been a persistent bottleneck; this paper provides a drop-in replacement for the widely-used but biased eFIM with provable guarantees (CV ≤ √2) and comparable computational cost. The deterministic bounds on the neuromanifold FIM, derived through the low-dimensional core space, offer theoretical tools for information geometry that go beyond existing monotonicity results. Key limitations include the static (fixed-θ) nature of the analysis, the rank-1 structure of individual Hutchinson samples requiring moving averages in practice, and the absence of integration into downstream optimizers or variance-reduction techniques, which the authors flag as future work.
