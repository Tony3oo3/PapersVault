## Problem

While short-term synaptic plasticity rules (Hebbian, STDP) are well characterized, the principles governing circuit-level reorganization over hours to weeks under repeated stimulation remain unknown. Existing machine learning approaches absorb slow cross-session change into high-dimensional parameters, entangling fast neural activity with slow adaptation and making the underlying plasticity law unidentifiable and non-generalizable to unseen stimulation protocols. The paper asks: can long-term plasticity be formalized as a low-dimensional, stimulus-conditioned dynamical law that is directly inferable from longitudinal recordings?

## Input / Output

The method takes D ordered sessions of length-T population neural recordings (N units) along with their associated stimulation protocols u^k(t), where a session is a contiguous block over which connectivity is assumed stationary. The stimulation protocol is aggregated into a summary vector ū^k for the slow timescale. Outputs include within-session neural activity forecasts, per-session plasticity embeddings z^k, motif scales c^k that parameterize session-specific connectivity via a rank-R CP decomposition (with optional E/I sign constraints), the learned plasticity recurrence g_θ, and counterfactual predictions of connectivity and activity under novel stimulation schedules not seen during training.

## Method

STEER is a dual-timescale structured model that disentangles fast within-session dynamics from slow across-session plasticity. At the fast timescale, a low-rank RNN generates neural activity using session-specific connectivity W^k = Σ_r c_r^k a_r b_r^T, where the connectivity tensor is factorized via a rank-R CP decomposition with identifiability constraints (unit-norm factors, orthogonality penalties, and optional Dale's-principle sign masks). At the slow timescale, a plasticity encoder maps each session's recording to an embedding z^k, which evolves under a learnable stimulus-conditioned recurrence z^{k+1} = g_θ(z^k, ū^k) — this recurrence is the inferred plasticity rule. Training jointly optimizes a composite objective comprising H-step autoregressive within-session prediction, cross-session law consistency, and motif-scale smoothness, with a forward-in-time temporal holdout (first 60% train / last 40% test) to evaluate out-of-distribution generalization. The key architectural insight is that the shared low-rank motif structure (fixed a_r, b_r across sessions) forces all cross-session variability into the low-dimensional coefficient vector c^k, making the slow plasticity dynamics identifiable as a separate dynamical object rather than unstructured parameter drift.

## Results

On the Lorenz benchmark, STEER achieves a Dynamical Similarity Analysis (DSA) score of 0.169 (lower is better) and predicts unseen systems with explained variance (EV) of 0.961 and 0.974. On the BCM plasticity benchmark, all models achieve competitive within-session EV > 0.9 (no significant difference, t=0.4221, p>0.05), but STEER most faithfully tracks the ground-truth |W|₂ evolution across sessions and its inferred motif scales significantly correlate with the ground-truth sliding threshold PCs. On the PD-DBS longitudinal dataset, the PD-DBS group exhibits significantly larger plasticity change magnitude (Δc_mag) during the DBS intervention window (w4→w5) compared to both PD and Sham groups (p < 0.05), consistent with stimulation-evoked slow network reconfiguration.

## Baselines & Comparisons

STEER is compared against two baselines: MD-SSM (Vermani et al., ICLR 2025) and an adapted hierarchical PLRNN (Brenner et al., ICLR 2025). On the fast timescale (within-session EV), all three methods perform comparably (EV > 0.9 in BCM; no significant difference, p > 0.05), confirming that STEER does not sacrifice short-term predictive accuracy. On the slow timescale, STEER clearly outperforms both baselines: in the Lorenz experiment it achieves the lowest DSA (0.169) and highest EV on unseen systems; in BCM, MD-SSM systematically deviates from the ground-truth |W|₂ trajectory while STEER closely tracks it, and the hierarchical PLRNN produces much weaker modular structure in inferred weights. The key distinction is that MD-SSM treats sessions as effectively independent (meta-learning trial-level variability), discarding inter-session structure essential for identifying the slow plasticity law, whereas STEER's explicit dual-timescale factorization with a shared low-rank motif preserves this structure. On the task-learning benchmark, STEER with 5-session-ahead forecasting demonstrates the best weight similarity to ground truth, outperforming both baselines in replicating the expected connectivity dynamics.

## Relation to Prior Work

STEER sits at the intersection of neural system identification (low-rank RNN surrogates, SINDy-style equation discovery) and plasticity rule inference (Hebbian/STDP/BCM), but departs from both by elevating the slow across-session process from an unstructured parameter drift to a first-class, identifiable latent dynamical law z_{k+1}=g_θ(z_k, u_k). Prior ML approaches (MD-SSM, hierarchical PLRNN) allow session-to-session variability but absorb it into high-dimensional parameters, entangling fast activity with slow adaptation; STEER instead enforces a structured CP-decomposition of connectivity and a low-dimensional recurrence for the plasticity embedding, making the slow rule the primary inference target. The conceptual lineage draws on low-rank recurrent dynamics (Mastrogiuseppe & Ostojic, Valente et al.) and tensor-based neural dynamics (Pellegrino et al.), but the explicit dual-timescale factorization with stimulus-conditioned slow evolution and motif-level interpretability is new.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By turning long-term plasticity from a hidden confound into a falsifiable, low-dimensional dynamical object, STEER provides a principled path toward counterfactual design of stimulation protocols (DBS, TMS) and mechanistic biomarkers of neuromodulatory control. Key limitations include the small number of longitudinal time points (4 weeks), limited behavioral readout linking plasticity embeddings to motor outcomes, and validation confined to a single disease model and species. Open questions include whether the learned rule generalizes across modalities (optogenetics, TMS), whether safety-aware constraints on the reachable set of z and c can be formalized, and whether richer biological structure (cell-type-specific gains, homeostatic set points) can be embedded directly into g_θ.
