## Problem

Parallel LLM inference scaling samples N independent responses for a single prompt, partitioning compute and leaving potentially useful information from one generation untapped by others. Unlike sequential chain-of-thought scaling where past computation informs all future steps, independent parallel generations cannot leverage cross-sequence information, limiting the performance ceiling. The paper seeks a fully parallel method that introduces interdependence among N simultaneous generations without requiring extensive post-training or funneling all outputs into a single response.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single text prompt and produces N parallel reasoning traces (text responses) that are mutually informative during generation. The generation width N is fully flexible at test time and can differ from the training width (e.g., trained at width 4, evaluated at width 8 or 16). Because Bridge operates purely at the generation level, any post-generation aggregation technique (majority vote, self-consistency, LLM-based synthesis) can be applied to the N outputs without modification.

## Method

Bridge inserts small attention-like blocks after each feedforward layer that perform attention along the batch axis of the 3D hidden-state tensor (B×S×D), allowing tokens at the same position from different sequences sharing the same prompt to exchange information. Three key design choices distinguish Bridge from standard self-attention: (1) an attention mask restricts interaction to same-prompt, incomplete generations only; (2) no positional encoding is used, preserving sample-position invariance and enabling arbitrary generation widths; (3) the Markovian design (attending only to the current timestep's cross-sequence features) eliminates the need for a key-value cache. Training proceeds in two stages: an optional SFT warm-up on correctness-filtered GSM8K traces (freezing all original parameters) followed by RLVR using GRPO with token-level normalization on DeepScaleR. The total parameter overhead is only 2.8%–5.1%, and the architectural change makes the GRPO ratio and KL terms inherently inter-sample dependent, intertwining gradients across all sequences in a group.

## Results

On DS-Qwen-7B, Bridge with SFT warmup + RLVR achieves 39.40 average accuracy across 7 math benchmarks, a 5.85-point improvement over the original model (33.55), which is 39% greater than the next best method's improvement (RLVR only: +4.20). On DS-Qwen-1.5B and DS-Llama-8B, Bridge achieves 31.25 and 32.47 average accuracy respectively, outperforming both RLVR-only and the parameter-matched P-Match baseline. Bridge also improves set-level quality (G-Pass@8_τ) across nearly all τ values, indicating both higher coverage and greater consistency of correct responses.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares Bridge against three baselines: the original model, RLVR-only (standard GRPO training without architectural changes), and P-Match (parameter-matched 2-layer MLPs inserted in the same positions as Bridge blocks, trained with the identical SFT warmup + RLVR pipeline). Bridge outperforms all baselines on average accuracy across all three models, with the largest relative advantage on DS-Qwen-7B where its 5.85-point gain over the original is 39% larger than RLVR-only's 4.20-point gain. P-Match is notably inconsistent: on DS-Qwen-7B it actually underperforms RLVR-only (36.68 vs. 37.75 avg), confirming that Bridge's gains stem from inter-sample information sharing rather than merely added parameters. On non-math tasks (XSum, CNN/DailyMail, GPQA, ZebraLogic, Countdown), Bridge also achieves the highest scores in most cases despite being trained exclusively on math data. At the set level, Bridge achieves higher G-Pass@8_τ values for nearly all τ, indicating both greater coverage (at least one correct response) and higher consistency (multiple correct responses) compared to independent sampling.

## Relation to Prior Work

Bridge repurposes the axial attention concept (Ho et al., 2019) from computer vision to the LLM decoding context, applying attention along the batch axis of the hidden states tensor rather than the sequence axis. It departs from prior mid-generation synthesis methods (Hogwild! Inference, Group Think, ParScale) that funnel N parallel processes into a single output, instead maintaining N simultaneous interdependent outputs. The tensor perspective on hidden states draws from the high-order tensor literature, but the specific application to enabling cross-sample information flow during autoregressive decoding is new. The training pipeline (SFT warm-up followed by GRPO-based RLVR) follows standard practice, with the key novelty being entirely architectural.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Bridge opens a more general mode of parallel scaling where compute is pooled rather than partitioned across generations, and it is compatible with any post-generation aggregation technique (self-consistency, best-of-N, synthetic data generation). The low parameter overhead (2.8–5.1%) and width flexibility make it practically attractive, though it still requires architectural modification and retraining of the full model. Open questions include whether Bridge blocks help during pretraining or mid-training, whether Pass@k or other global objectives further amplify the benefit, and how the method behaves on tasks with lower output homogeneity. The paper is limited to math reasoning for training and primarily evaluates on math benchmarks, though non-math generalization is encouraging.
