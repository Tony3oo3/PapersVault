## Problem

Prior interpretability work on protein language models has focused on how individual sequences are transformed into latent representations, leaving the global geometric structure of the representation space—how proteins relate to one another across the full sequence space—largely unexplored. This paper asks what the shape space of PLM representations looks like as a function of model depth and size, and at which context lengths three-dimensional protein structure is most faithfully encoded in those representations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261143/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis takes 1377 protein sequences (up to 200 per class) from eight SCOPe structural classes along with their PDB coordinates, and pushes them through four ESM2 model sizes and the Ankh model. Rather than using the standard mean-pooled vector, each protein's full per-residue representation tensor (amino acids × model dimension) is treated as an ordered point cloud in R^m and interpolated with quadratic splines at 1000 points to form a curve. Outputs are per-layer Fréchet radius and effective dimension of the SRV shape space, plus per-layer, per-class graph filtration moments tracked across k-neighbor levels.

## Method

The paper introduces two complementary geometric analyses. First, it maps both 3D structures and PLM representations to square-root velocity (SRV) curves, quotients out rotations via SVD to obtain a Riemannian shape space, and computes the Fréchet radius (spread) and effective dimension (via tangent PCA) at every transformer layer, revealing a characteristic expansion-contraction pattern in dimensionality that is more pronounced in larger models. Second, it constructs k-nearest-neighbor graphs from both the 3D structure and the PLM embedding for each protein, compares their adjacency matrices via L1 distance, and normalizes by distances to random point clouds to obtain a graph filtration moment as a function of k. This filtration reveals a bimodal encoding pattern: PLM representations best preserve 3D structure at very short context (~2 neighbors) and at a secondary scale (~8 neighbors), with the optimal structural encoding occurring near but before the final layer rather than at it.

## Results

Across 1377 proteins from SCOPe, the Fréchet radius of PLM shape spaces decreases with layer depth and is substantially smaller than the 3D protein structure baseline, yet remains roughly constant across ESM2 model sizes (35M–3B). The effective dimension of the SRV shape space exhibits a characteristic expansion-contraction pattern: larger models (650M, 3B) show pronounced dimension expansion in early layers followed by contraction toward a low-dimensional subspace in later layers, while smaller models (35M, 150M) stay closer to the 3D structure baseline. Graph filtration moments reveal a bimodal encoding pattern: PLM representations best match 3D protein structure at ~2 nearest neighbors (immediate local context) and again at ~8 neighbors, with the optimal structural encoding occurring in layers just before the final layer rather than in the last layer itself.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an interpretability/analysis paper rather than a method-comparison study, so there are no competing methods to benchmark against. The primary 'baseline' is the 3D protein structure shape space itself: PLM representations consistently achieve lower Fréchet radii (tighter shape clusters) than the 3D baseline, and their effective dimensions deviate from the 3D baseline in a size-dependent way—larger ESM2 models expand dimensionality well above the 3D baseline in early layers before contracting below it. Among ESM2 model sizes, the 3B and 650M models show the most pronounced expansion-contraction pattern, while the 35M model tracks the 3D baseline more closely. The Ankh model reproduces the same qualitative patterns but with more severe dimension reduction in later layers. Across protein classes, Alpha/Beta proteins show the highest graph filtration similarity to their 3D structure, while Small and Designed proteins are the least structurally faithful, suggesting class-dependent encoding quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the PLM interpretability literature (Zhang et al. 2024, Simon & Zou 2024, Gujral et al. 2025), which has focused on how individual sequences are transformed, to study the geometric structure of the *collective* representation space. It repurposes the square-root velocity (SRV) shape-analysis framework from Srivastava et al. (2010) and graph filtration learning from Hofer et al. (2020) — tools previously applied to protein structure alignment and graph learning respectively — to define a Riemannian metric on PLM representation shapes and a scale-resolved comparison between 3D structure and embedding geometry. The observed expansion-contraction pattern in effective dimensionality connects to the broader language-model literature on intrinsic dimensionality (Aghajanyan et al. 2020; Cheng et al. 2024; Valeriani et al. 2023), but the paper's contribution is in applying these ideas to the shape space of protein representations rather than the raw embedding space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that 3D protein structure is optimally encoded in layers immediately preceding the last (rather than in the final layer) suggests a concrete, testable strategy for improving folding-model initialization by selecting intermediate PLM layers. The bimodal context-length sensitivity (peaks at ~2 and ~8 neighbors) reveals a structured, multi-scale encoding of local structure that is not captured by standard per-residue or mean-pooled analyses. However, the practical implication remains unverified — the authors note that initial attempts to train folding models on intermediate layers failed to generalize — and the analysis is limited to 1,377 proteins from two model families (ESM2, Ankh), leaving open whether the patterns hold at larger scale or for other architectures. A key open question is what biological or geometric feature underlies the ~8-neighbor peak, which is less pronounced in Beta-class proteins.
