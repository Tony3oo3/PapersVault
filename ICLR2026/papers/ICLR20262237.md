## Problem

For BERT-style encoders, contrastive learning (CL) has been the dominant training objective for reranking, but for LLMs the more natural supervised fine-tuning (SFT) paradigm—predicting 'yes'/'no' tokens—appears competitive, yet no systematic analysis has determined which objective is intrinsically better for LLM-based reranking or why. Additionally, multimodal reranking research remains largely confined to single datasets or narrow task definitions, lacking a unified benchmark for evaluating generalization across diverse modalities.

## Input / Output

The reranker takes a task-specific instruction, a query, and a document candidate—each of which can be text, an image, or a text-image combination—formatted into a chat template with a maximum input length of 3,200 tokens. In the SFT setting, the relevance score is the normalized probability of the 'yes' token logit over the two-token vocabulary {'yes','no'}; in the CL setting, it is the raw 'yes' logit. The model is built on Qwen2.5-VL-Instruct (3B or 7B) with LoRA (rank 16) and is trained with 16 negatives per positive, using a 1:1 mix of random and hard-mined negatives.

## Method

The paper introduces a unified reranking loss (URL) framework that decomposes both CL and SFT objectives into a scalar weight (controlling gradient magnitude) and a vector direction (controlling update direction), enabling controlled ablation of each component. The key finding is that SFT's weight is intrinsically larger than CL's because SFT's softmax denominator involves only the current instance's two logits, whereas CL's denominator sums over all negatives, making CL's gradients smaller and more vulnerable to numerical precision errors. The direction component differs in that SFT uses the difference of 'yes' and 'no' token embeddings (M_y − M_n) while CL uses only M_y, and experiments show SFT's native direction is near-optimal while CL benefits from learning a random-initialized projection matrix. Building on these insights, the authors train GMR-3B and GMR-7B using SFT on 1.5M diverse multimodal pairs and introduce the MRB benchmark (40 datasets spanning single-, cross-, and fused-modal retrieval) on which both models achieve state-of-the-art results.

## Results

On the MRB benchmark (40 datasets, NDCG@10), GMR-7B achieves a 63.85 average, outperforming GMR-3B (61.40), the retrieval backbone GME-2B (52.54), Jina-rerank-m0 (54.36), and MonoQwen (44.20). On the text-only T→T subset, GMR-7B reaches 61.08, surpassing the 4B-parameter Qwen3-Reranker (60.49). On the MRMR Knowledge benchmark (multi-image setting), GMR-7B achieves 74.22 NDCG@10 versus 59.93 for the retrieval model and 48.05 for the best reranking baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Qwen3-Reranker (4B, text-only), GMR-3B (3.75B) trails slightly on T→T (59.22 vs. 60.49) but GMR-7B (8.29B) surpasses it (61.08 vs. 60.49); more importantly, GMR dominates all multimodal categories where Qwen3 cannot operate. Against MonoQwen (2.21B, visual-document specialist), GMR-3B wins on T→VD (72.38 vs. 71.29) and GMR-7B extends the lead (72.94), while GMR's advantage is far larger on fused-modal tasks (e.g., IT→T: 48.81/53.29 vs. 14.35; IT→IT: 79.08/82.19 vs. 35.83). Against Jina-rerank-m0 (2.21B), both GMR models achieve substantial gains across every category, with the largest improvements on fused-modal tasks (IT→T: +21.0/+25.5; IT→IT: +27.5/+30.7) and I→T (+7.6/+11.2). On the multi-image MRMR Knowledge benchmark, GMR-7B (74.22) outperforms the best reranking baseline (48.05) by over 26 points, and both GMR models improve when scaling from top-25 to top-100 reranking depth, whereas Jina-rerank-m0 shows a slight drop (−0.08%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on two threads: (1) the LLM-based point-wise reranking lineage initiated by Nogueira et al. (2019, 2020), where contrastive learning (InfoNCE) and supervised fine-tuning (yes/no token prediction) emerged as the two dominant training objectives, and (2) the universal multimodal retrieval (UMR) framework from Zhang et al. (2025b, GME), which unifies diverse modalities under a single retrieval model. The key departure is analytical: rather than treating CL and SFT as separate paradigms, the authors decompose both losses into a shared 'weight' (gradient magnitude) and 'direction' (update vector) structure, enabling controlled probing of which component drives the empirical SFT advantage. This unified loss framework (URL) is the paper's conceptual contribution, bridging the BERT-era CL literature and the LLM-era SFT practice under one algebraic lens.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides practitioners with a clear, mechanistically grounded answer to a question that has been empirically observed but not explained: SFT should be preferred over CL for LLM-based reranking because its per-instance weight assignment produces larger, more robust gradient signals, while the direction component is nearly equivalent. The MRB benchmark (40 datasets spanning single-, cross-, and fused-modal tasks) fills a notable gap in multimodal reranking evaluation, and the open-source GMR-3B/7B models offer strong baselines. Open questions include whether the weight-magnitude advantage persists for list-wise or set-wise reranking formulations, whether it holds at substantially larger model scales, and whether the findings transfer to non-generative encoder architectures where CL historically dominated.
