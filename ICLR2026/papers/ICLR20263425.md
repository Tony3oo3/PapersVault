## Problem

Latent reasoning language models like Huginn-3.5B represent intermediate reasoning steps as sequences of hidden states rather than natural language, making them computationally efficient but lacking interpretability and supervision. It is unclear whether these latent thoughts encode meaningful signals that distinguish correct from incorrect reasoning, and whether external reward signals can be applied directly in the latent space to improve thinking quality without the overhead of verbal chain-of-thought.

## Input / Output

The LRM takes a sequence of t latent thoughts (each a matrix in R^{L×d}, where L is the number of output tokens and d is the hidden dimensionality) as input and outputs a scalar correctness probability via a 2-layer Transformer with mean pooling over the token dimension. LTO then takes N candidate latent thinking trajectories sampled from the base policy, scores each with the LRM, and applies acceptance-rejection sampling with a KL-regularized acceptance probability φ_i = exp((r_i − r_max)/β) to produce M filtered trajectories that are more likely to yield correct answers. The final natural-language answer is decoded from the last latent thought of each accepted trajectory.

## Method

The paper first demonstrates that latent thoughts leading to correct versus incorrect answers exhibit distinct geometric and information-theoretic patterns (higher entropy, lower effective rank, higher anisotropy, and higher intrinsic dimension for correct trajectories), and that a lightweight 2-layer Transformer classifier trained with binary cross-entropy can predict answer correctness from partial latent trajectories with near-perfect ROC-AUC on math tasks. Building on this, LTO formulates latent thinking improvement as a KL-regularized reward maximization over latent policies (Eq. 2), derives a closed-form optimal policy π_r(z_i|x) ∝ π_ref(z_i|x)·exp(r(x,z_i)/β) (Theorem 1), and implements sampling from this policy via an acceptance-rejection algorithm (Algorithm 1) that avoids explicit density estimation. The KL regularization with weight β prevents collapse to degenerate policies and mitigates exploitation of LRM errors, reducing to best-of-N sampling only in the limit β→0. The approach generalizes to standard LLMs (OLMo, Llama, Mistral) by treating their multi-layer hidden states as latent chain-of-thought, and the LRM demonstrates cross-domain transferability, enabling a single generalist reward model to improve performance across math, commonsense, and code generation tasks.

## Results

On Huginn-3.5B, LTO achieves the highest answer correctness rate across all five datasets: 0.385 on GSM8K, 0.305 on GSM-Symbolic, 0.538 on SVAMP, 0.517 on CommonsenseQA, and 0.299 on MBPP, all statistically significant (p < 0.05) over the base model (0.326, 0.265, 0.517, 0.500, 0.278 respectively). When applied to general LLMs (Table 2), LTO yields improvements of up to 103% over the base model (e.g., Llama-2-13B on SVAMP: 0.791 vs. 0.521) with a modest sampling budget of N=20. The latent classifier (LRM) achieves near-perfect ROC-AUC (~1.0) on SVAMP and ~0.8 on MBPP, confirming that latent thoughts encode strong correctness signals.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LTO outperforms all four baseline categories on every dataset. Against Majority Voting (Wang et al., 2023), LTO gains +0.052 on GSM8K (0.385 vs. 0.333) and +0.021 on MBPP (0.299 vs. 0.288), while majority voting provides negligible or negative gains on SVAMP (0.511 vs. base 0.517). Against Self-Correction with Confidence Score (Ren et al., 2023b), LTO leads by +0.043 on GSM8K (0.385 vs. 0.342) and +0.011 on MBPP (0.299 vs. 0.288). Self-Correction with Verbal Evaluation (Manakul et al., 2023) actually degrades performance below the base model on GSM8K (0.262 vs. 0.326) and MBPP (0.226 vs. 0.278), highlighting that verbal self-evaluation is poorly suited for latent thinking. The latent-space baselines CoE-R and CoE-C (Wang et al., 2025c) also underperform LTO across all datasets, with LTO achieving +0.055 on GSM8K over CoE-R (0.385 vs. 0.330) and +0.019 on MBPP over CoE-C (0.299 vs. 0.280), confirming that the LRM-based probabilistic optimization is more effective than heuristic latent-space scoring.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Huginn-3.5B (Geiping et al., 2025), a latent reasoning language model that replaces verbal chain-of-thought with sequences of hidden-state latent thoughts, and on the broader process reward model literature (Wang et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2024) that scores intermediate reasoning steps in natural language. The key departure is moving reward modeling from the verbal space into the latent space: the authors show that a lightweight classifier can predict answer correctness from latent trajectories, and they cast latent thinking improvement as a KL-regularized policy optimization problem solved via acceptance-rejection sampling (Algorithm 1), borrowing the theoretical machinery from KL-regularized RL (Jaques et al., 2017; Rafailov et al., 2023). They further extend the approach to general LLMs by reinterpreting multi-layer hidden states as a 'latent chain of thoughts' (following Wang et al., 2025c), positioning their method as a parameter-free, domain-agnostic alternative to verbal process reward models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central insight—that latent representations of reasoning LLMs encode separable correctness signals exploitable by a simple classifier—opens a path to supervised test-time scaling without generating or parsing natural language, which is both cheaper and avoids overthinking. The cross-domain transferability of the LRM (e.g., a CommonsenseQA-trained LRM improving GSM8K) suggests latent reward signals may capture domain-agnostic reasoning structure, pointing toward a generalist latent reward model. However, the evaluation is limited to relatively small models (≤13B) and moderately difficult tasks; whether the approach scales to frontier models or highly complex multi-step reasoning remains open. The 'latent chain of thoughts' interpretation for general LLMs is also somewhat heuristic, and the LRM's imperfect accuracy means the theoretical guarantees (Theorem 3) depend on a bounded-error assumption that is not tightly characterized in practice.
