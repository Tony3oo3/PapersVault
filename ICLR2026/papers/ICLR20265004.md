## Problem

Multimodal large reasoning models (MLRMs) suffer from a fundamental imbalance: they overthink on simple problems, generating unnecessarily lengthy reasoning traces, while under-exploring on challenging ones, leading to missed solutions. Existing adaptive reasoning methods either degrade accuracy when suppressing verbosity or over-encourage exploration on hard problems without delivering significant performance gains, leaving open the questions of when a model should explore and how much reasoning effort to allocate.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20265004/cropped_page3_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The model accepts a text prompt (mathematical or logical problem) optionally paired with an RGB image (diagram, chart, or figure) and produces a chain-of-thought reasoning trace followed by a final answer. The key distinguishing feature is that the reasoning trace length is not fixed but adaptively modulated: easy problems yield concise traces (~300 tokens on GSM8K) while hard problems elicit extended exploratory chains (~22K tokens on AIME25). The system operates at 3B and 7B parameter scales built on Qwen2.5-VL, with a maximum sequence length of 20K tokens (4K prompt + 16K response) during RL rollouts.

## Method

ARES introduces a two-stage training pipeline. Stage 1 (Adaptive Cold-Start, AdaCS) fine-tunes the model on a curated 224K-sample dataset where reasoning trace length is explicitly correlated with problem difficulty via a pass-rate-based target length formula, instilling initial difficulty awareness. Stage 2 (Adaptive Entropy Policy Optimization, AEPO) is the core RL contribution: it uses high window-entropy (HWE) tokens—sliding-window averages of token-level entropies exceeding a batch-adaptive 95th-percentile threshold—as triggers to decide *when* to explore (branching additional trajectories at sustained high-uncertainty regions), and a difficulty-aware hierarchical reward with a closed-form Lagrange multiplier to decide *how much* to explore (penalizing over-exploration on easy problems, stabilizing medium, and encouraging deeper exploration on hard ones). A dynamic KL mechanism further allocates the thinking budget token-wise, relaxing KL constraints inside validated high-entropy windows while tightening them elsewhere, all without introducing additional hyperparameters beyond the base GRPO/DAPO formulation.

## Results

On multimodal reasoning benchmarks (Table 1), ARES-7B achieves an average of 55.9, surpassing the best open-source 7B model (Vision-G1, 46.5) by +9.4 points, with particularly large gains of +19.0 on MathVision and +11.5 on MMMU-Pro. On textual reasoning (Table 4), ARES-7B reaches 61.7 on AIME25 and 65.0 on AIME24, where most 7B open-source baselines score below 3.3, yielding a +27.2 average improvement over the best 7B open-source competitor. At the 3B scale, ARES-3B achieves 46.1 average multimodal accuracy (+8.4 over the best 3B baseline) and 45.8 average textual accuracy (+20.7). The adaptive training strategy also improves efficiency: ARES-RL-7B shortens responses on easy tasks (e.g., GSM8K from 358.8 to 278.9 tokens) while extending them on hard ones (e.g., AIME25 from 16361.6 to 22618.8 tokens).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ARES-7B decisively outperforms all open-source 7B baselines on multimodal benchmarks, achieving +9.4 average over Vision-G1 (46.5), +9.9 over MM-Eureka-Qwen-7B (46.0), and +11.6 over Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct (43.3), with the largest single-benchmark margin being +19.0 on MathVision (51.9 vs. 32.9 for ThinkLite-7B-VL). On textual reasoning, the gap is even more dramatic: ARES-7B scores 61.7 on AIME25 versus 0.0–3.3 for all 7B open-source baselines, and 95.2 on MATH500 versus 64.4–69.0. At the 3B scale, ARES-3B leads all 3B open-source models by +8.4 average on multimodal and +20.7 on textual benchmarks. Against closed-source systems, ARES-7B (55.9 multimodal avg) still trails Gemini-2.5-Pro-Thinking (73.8) and Doubao-1.5 (73.2) by roughly 18–18 points, but narrows the gap to GPT-4.1 (61.8) to within 5.9 points and to Claude-4-Sonnet (62.6) to within 6.7 points, all at a fraction of the inference cost. The ablation against GRPO and DAPO baselines (Table 3) confirms that ARES's hierarchical reward and dynamic KL design each contribute +4.5 and +4.0 average points respectively over the cold-start-only baseline, with the full ARES-7B reaching 55.7 average versus 53.4 (GRPO) and 54.1 (DAPO).

## Relation to Prior Work

ARES builds directly on the GRPO (DeepSeek-R1) and DAPO policy-optimization frameworks, adapting their clipped surrogate objectives with a token-level entropy-shaped advantage and a window-adaptive KL regularizer. It extends the adaptive-reasoning line of work (Wang et al. 2025d, Huang et al. 2025a, Shen et al. 2025b) that curates difficulty-stratified cold-start data or applies difficulty-aware RL penalties, but departs from these by using high-window-entropy tokens as an explicit exploration trigger rather than relying solely on response-length penalties or pass-rate-based oversampling. The cold-start stage is inspired by Wang et al. (2025d) but replaces pass-rate-based sample selection with a length-proportional-to-difficulty curation strategy. The paper positions itself as the first framework to bring this adaptive, entropy-guided reasoning paradigm to the multimodal setting, where most prior adaptive-reasoning work has been text-only.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The entropy-difficulty interaction finding—that suppressing high-entropy exploration improves accuracy on easy problems while encouraging it is essential for hard ones—provides a clean, empirically grounded principle for allocating reasoning compute, and the window-entropy trigger offers a practical, low-overhead signal that practitioners can adopt in existing RLVR pipelines. The two-stage ARES pipeline (difficulty-aware cold-start + AEPO) is a reusable recipe that could be integrated into other multimodal reasoning training stacks. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond the Qwen2.5-VL family and to non-mathematical multimodal tasks, whether the GPT-dependent semantic token filtering can be replaced with a learned criterion, and whether the 95th-percentile threshold and window size (4–8 tokens) remain optimal at larger model scales or in longer-horizon reasoning settings.
