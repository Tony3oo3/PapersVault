## Problem

Training deep models within resource-constrained budgets remains challenging because backpropagation incurs high computational cost (two full-rank matrix multiplications per layer) and requires storing full-rank activations in memory. Prior SVD-based activation compression methods add substantial per-iteration overhead, while gradient compression approaches like LBP-WHT are restricted to low-frequency data (images) and achieve only modest compression ratios. The paper addresses the gap of simultaneously reducing both backward-pass FLOPs and activation memory without the computational penalty of per-step SVD or the data-type limitations of fixed-basis transforms.

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard image batches (32×32 CIFAR, variable-size Flowers/Food-101/Pets) or text sequences (up to 512 tokens for BERT/DistilBERT) with no modification to the forward pass or model architecture. During training, instead of storing the full-rank activation x ∈ R^{L×C_x} for backpropagation, it stores a compressed version x̂ ∈ R^{R_x×C_x} where R_x ≪ L (e.g., R_x=8 vs L=512 for BERT). At inference time, the compressed representations P, Q, and x̂ are discarded entirely, so there is zero overhead compared to vanilla inference.

## Method

INSTANT periodically (every N_t steps) constructs two orthonormal projection matrices—P for activations and Q for output gradients—by performing SVD on the auto-correlation tensors E[x·x^T] and E[g_y·g_y^T] accumulated over a calibration window, then truncating singular vectors at an energy threshold ε=95% plus p oversampled vectors to absorb distribution drift. In the forward pass, the activation is projected to x̂=P·x and stored in place of x, reducing memory from O(L·C_x) to O(R_x·C_x). In the backward pass, the weight gradient is computed as g_w≈(g_y^T·P^T)·(P·x) in the low-rank space, and the input gradient is recovered via three low-rank multiplications through Q, replacing the two full-rank products of vanilla backpropagation. The key empirical insight is that activation gradients are inherently low-rank (only ~6 ranks retain 95% of energy in BERT), enabling aggressive compression with negligible accuracy loss. An energy-offset normalization (dividing by the square root of the sum of kept singular values) compensates for the energy discarded at truncation, and the calibration is designed to be memory-neutral via on-policy processing and smaller batches.

## Results

On the GLUE benchmark with BERT (fine-tuning last block), INSTANT-15 achieves 73.06% mAcc vs. 73.79% Vanilla (0.73% drop) while reducing FLOPs from 14,495 to 1,018 MFLOPs (14.2×) and activation memory from 13.50 MB to 0.42 MB (32.1×). On SwinV2-Small (CV, fine-tuning last block), INSTANT-7 reaches 91.58% mAcc vs. 91.67% Vanilla (0.09% drop) with 5.1× FLOPs reduction (530 vs. 2,718 MFLOPs) and 6.8× memory reduction (0.33 vs. 2.25 MB). In full fine-tuning of BERT, INSTANT-25 achieves 79.65% mAcc vs. 80.76% Vanilla with 8.4× FLOPs reduction and 19× memory reduction. On edge devices (Raspberry Pi 5), INSTANT-0 reduces backward time by up to 12.55× compared to Vanilla.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Gradient Filtering, INSTANT-7 (BERT) achieves 72.33% mAcc vs. 68.15% (4.18% higher) at similar FLOPs (565 vs. 226 MFLOPs) and memory (0.21 vs. 0.21 MB), showing that gradient filtering discards too much information. Against LBP-WHT, INSTANT-7 outperforms LBP-WHT-2 by 2.48% mAcc (72.33% vs. 69.85%) while using 75% of the FLOPs (565 vs. 732) and 33% of the memory (0.21 vs. 0.63 MB); INSTANT-15 further narrows the gap to Vanilla (73.06% vs. 73.79%) at 14.2× lower FLOPs. Against GaLore, INSTANT-15 matches or exceeds accuracy (84.68% QNLI, 91.63% SST-2 vs. 84.37%, 91.28% for GaLore-32) while reducing FLOPs by 14× and memory by 32×, since GaLore does not reduce activation memory or backward FLOPs. Against CompAct-32, INSTANT-15 achieves comparable accuracy (84.68% vs. 84.37% QNLI) at 8.2× lower FLOPs (1018 vs. 8355 MFLOPs) with similar memory (0.42 vs. 0.44 MB). Against LoRA, INSTANT is orthogonal and complementary: combining LoRA+INSTANT-7 reduces FLOPs to 449 (vs. 896 for LoRA alone) and memory to 0.34 MB (vs. 1.95 MB) while maintaining 94.38% CIFAR-10 accuracy. Overall, INSTANT consistently wins on the FLOPs/memory–accuracy trade-off across all baselines, with the largest margins over Gradient Filtering and LBP-WHT.

## Relation to Prior Work

INSTANT builds directly on ESPACE's theoretical foundation (SVD of auto-correlation matrices yielding MSE-optimal projection subspaces) and extends it from activation-only compression to joint compression of both activations and output gradients during backpropagation. It departs from LBP-WHT's Walsh-Hadamard-based approach, which is restricted to low-frequency data (images) and achieves modest compression ratios, by using adaptive SVD-based projections applicable to arbitrary data distributions. Unlike optimizer-state compression methods (GaLore, CompAct) that leave backpropagation computation untouched, INSTANT targets the backward pass itself, and unlike Gradient Filtering's patch-pooling strategy, it constructs per-layer calibrated subspaces with energy-threshold truncation and oversampling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The demonstrated 15× FLOP reduction and 32× activation memory savings with ~1% accuracy loss offer a practical path for training on resource-constrained hardware (edge devices, single GPUs), and the method's orthogonality to LoRA, GaLore, and CompAct means it can be stacked with existing compression techniques. However, the GPU latency results (Table 8) reveal that FLOP reductions do not translate to wall-clock speedups on modern GPUs—backward time is actually 1.4× vanilla—limiting immediate practical impact on high-end training infrastructure. Open questions include whether the low-rank gradient property holds for very large or novel architectures, how calibration frequency should scale with model size, and whether the method can be made GPU-friendly through kernel-level optimization.
