## Problem

Thinking LLMs solve complex reasoning tasks but waste hundreds of tokens overthinking simple queries, while non-thinking LLMs are fast but underthink on hard problems, forcing users to manually select between separate model variants. Prior work has studied overthinking and underthinking in isolation on disparate benchmarks with incompatible metrics, making it impossible to track whether a model achieves an optimal balance of efficiency and accuracy across the full spectrum of query difficulty.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260313/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes text queries spanning 72 domains and four answer types (numeric, MCQ, short-phrase, open-ended) for OverthinkingBench, and procedurally generated reasoning tasks (games, algorithms, graphs, arithmetic, geometry, logic) plus competition math for UnderthinkingBench. For each query, the system records the model's answer, the number of thinking tokens generated, and correctness (via LLM-as-a-Judge for general questions, math-verify for math, and code-based verifiers for reasoning tasks), producing per-sample OAA_t values, an aggregated AUC_OAA score, underthinking accuracy, and a unified F1_otb score. The maximum thinking token threshold t_max is fixed at 1000 for AUC computation.

## Method

OptimalThinkingBench unifies two complementary sub-benchmarks under a single F1 metric. OverthinkingBench is built via a constrained generation pipeline (prompting an LLM with domain+answer-type constraints) followed by a 100%-agreement filtering step (8 samples from a separate LLM must all match the reference answer), ensuring questions are simple, unambiguous, and correct. UnderthinkingBench is constructed by selecting reasoning tasks where a small thinking model (Qwen3-1.7B) outperforms a large non-thinking model (Qwen3-235B-A22B) by more than λ=0.1, guaranteeing that thinking is genuinely necessary. The key evaluation metric, AUC_OAA, computes the area under the Overthinking-Adjusted Accuracy curve (accuracy restricted to responses using fewer than t thinking tokens, integrated over t from 0 to t_max), penalizing both incorrect answers and excessive token usage simultaneously. The final F1_otb score combines AUC_OAA with UnderthinkingBench accuracy, requiring models to excel on both sub-benchmarks to achieve high scores.

## Results

On OptimalThinkingBench, o3 achieves the best unified F1_otb score of 71.1%, with 97.5% accuracy and 235 thinking tokens on OverthinkingBench (AUC_OAA = 78.6%) and 65.0% accuracy on UnderthinkingBench. Among open-weight models, GPT-OSS-120B leads at 68.3% F1_otb, generating only 154 thinking tokens on simple queries (AUC_OAA = 83.3%) while achieving 57.9% on UnderthinkingBench. All other open-weight models score below 50% F1_otb, and no model simultaneously excels on both sub-benchmarks. Thinking models generate 100–3300+ tokens on simple queries (vs. 0 for non-thinking models), while non-thinking models achieve only 5–32% accuracy on complex reasoning tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates five methods for improving optimal thinking against their base models. AdaptThink (Zhang et al., 2025a) is the most effective, reducing OverthinkingBench tokens by 82% (1172→211) while slightly improving UnderthinkingBench accuracy (+1.8%), yielding the largest F1_otb gain (+13.8). VeriThinker (Chen et al., 2025b) reduces tokens by 41% but drops UnderthinkingBench accuracy by 4.2%, resulting in a modest +2.9 F1_otb gain. L1 (Aggarwal & Welleck, 2025) reduces tokens by only 12% and decreases UnderthinkingBench accuracy by 1.8%, actually lowering F1_otb by 3.7 points. Model Merging (Wu et al., 2025) cuts tokens by 36% with a small 1.2% UnderthinkingBench drop, improving F1_otb by 13.9. The Arch-Router (Tran et al., 2025) improves F1_otb by 20.4% over the best individual Qwen3 mode (46.9 vs. 24.3) but still trails the oracle router by ~15 points (61.2 vs. 70.5 for Qwen3-235B), indicating routing remains an open challenge.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by unifying two previously isolated research threads—overthinking analysis (Chen et al. 2025a, Cuadron et al. 2025, Kumar et al. 2025) and underthinking analysis (Wang et al. 2025b, Muennighoff et al. 2025)—into a single benchmark with a shared F1 metric. Prior efficient-reasoning methods (L1, AdaptThink, VeriThinker, model merging) and underthinking interventions (adaptive injection decoding, forced longer generation) each used bespoke evaluation setups and metrics, making cross-method comparison difficult. The paper repurposes existing datasets (MATH, AIME, HMMT, Reasoning Gym) and a synthetic generation pipeline with LLM-as-a-Judge filtering to construct a contamination-resistant, extensible benchmark, while introducing AUC_OAA as a thinking-budget-adjusted accuracy metric that had no direct predecessor.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that no model among 33 evaluated (including o3, GPT-OSS-120B, Sonnet-4) achieves optimal thinking, and that efficiency methods consistently degrade underthinking in 5 of 6 configurations—establishes a fundamental trade-off that the field has not yet resolved. The 15-point gap between a trained router and an oracle router on Qwen3 models suggests that adaptive compute allocation remains an open challenge, and the near-linear overthinking increase with irrelevant MCQ distractors (42 tokens/option, R²=0.94) reveals that current models allocate compute based on superficial cues rather than task complexity. The benchmark's synthetic generation recipe is designed to evolve with model capability, positioning it as a long-term tracking tool. Open questions include whether a single model can learn to adaptively allocate thinking tokens, whether the over/underthinking trade-off is fundamental or an artifact of current training objectives, and whether the LLM-as-a-Judge evaluation introduces systematic biases.
