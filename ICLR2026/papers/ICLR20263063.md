## Problem

Current medical benchmarks like MedQA and HealthSearchQA evaluate LLMs on exam-style or consumer-searched questions but do not test whether models can detect and correct false presuppositions (patient-held misconceptions) embedded in real patient inquiries. This gap is critical because 32.6% of patients now consult LLMs for medical advice, and failing to challenge a patient's misconception—such as believing no treatment exists for advanced lymphoma—can lead to delayed or forgone effective care.

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes naturalistic cancer patient questions (585 adversarial examples in Cancer-Myth, 150 clean examples in Cancer-Myth-NFP) that embed one of seven types of false presuppositions (e.g., 'No Treatment,' 'Inevitable Side Effect,' 'Causal Misattribution') within complex patient narratives including age, family context, and prior medical history. Models produce free-text responses evaluated zero-shot by a GPT-4o verifier on a 3-point scale (−1 = presupposition ignored, 0 = partially addressed, 1 = fully corrected), yielding two metrics: Presupposition Correction Score (PCS, mean score) and Presupposition Correction Rate (PCR, fraction of fully correct answers).

## Method

The Cancer-Myth dataset is constructed via a multi-stage adversarial generation pipeline: 994 cancer myths are collected from NCI and public sources, then an LLM generator (run separately with GPT-4o, Gemini-1.5-Pro, and Claude-3.5-Sonnet) produces patient questions embedding each myth as a false presupposition with diverse patient details, guided by in-context valid/invalid examples. An LLM responder answers each question and a GPT-4o verifier scores whether the response identifies and corrects the presupposition; failures are retained as adversarial examples while successes feed back into the generator's prompt. Three hematology-oncology physicians validate all 888 candidates, yielding 585 confirmed adversarial questions and 150 no-false-presupposition controls, balanced across seven misconception categories via a category-wise sampling algorithm. For mitigation, the paper tests GEPA reflective prompt optimization (trained on 5 examples per benchmark) and a monitoring agent within the MDAgents multi-agent framework, finding that while GEPA raises Gemini-2.5-Pro's Cancer-Myth PCR to 88%, it simultaneously causes 41% false-positive presupposition flagging on Cancer-Myth-NFP and a ~10% relative drop on standard medical benchmarks.

## Results

No frontier LLM corrects false presuppositions in cancer patient questions more than 43% of the time. GPT-5 achieves the highest Presupposition Correction Rate (PCR) at 42.1%, followed by Gemini-2.5-Pro (41.4%) and Claude-4-Sonnet (40.0%), while GPT-4o scores only 5.8% PCR. GEPA prompt optimization raises Cancer-Myth PCR to 88% for Gemini-2.5-Pro but causes a 28-point drop on Cancer-Myth-NFP (96→68) and 5–15% relative drops on standard medical benchmarks (MedQA, PubMedQA, SymCat, Medbullets, Craft-MD). Multi-agent MDAgents orchestration actually worsens performance, with GPT-4o w/ MDAgents scoring only 2% PCR versus 12% for standalone GPT-4o.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among frontier models, GPT-5 (42.1% PCR) narrowly outperforms Gemini-2.5-Pro (41.4%) and Claude-4-Sonnet (40.0%), while Gemini-1.5-Pro lags at 27.2% and GPT-4o drops to just 5.8%. Public/open-weight models perform substantially worse: Gemma-2-27B leads at 17.3% PCR, while DeepSeek-R1 (13.7%), Qwen-2.5-72B (7.0%), and LLaMA-3.1-70B (6.3%) all fall below 20%. Thinking/reasoning models do not close the gap—O3 achieves 34.8% PCR and O4-mini only 13.0%, confirming that increased test-time compute does not mitigate sycophancy toward false premises. MDAgents multi-agent collaboration (GPT-4o-based) performs worse than standalone GPT-4o (2% vs. 12% PCR), and adding a monitoring agent raises Cancer-Myth to 81% but crashes Cancer-Myth-NFP to 35%, indicating severe over-caution. GEPA optimization on Gemini-2.5-Pro achieves the best Cancer-Myth score (88%) but at the cost of a 28-point NFP drop and 5–15% degradation across all five standard medical benchmarks, demonstrating that prompting alone is not a reliable remedy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on false-presupposition benchmarks (CREPE, (QA)², FRESHQA, Pregnant Questions) from general or single-domain settings to the cancer-care domain, where patient-specific narratives embed medically dangerous misconceptions. It also builds on adversarial data generation pipelines (Bartolo et al., Fu et al.) but introduces a physician-in-the-loop verification step that prior LLM-generated adversarial benchmarks lack. The evaluation of multi-agent medical frameworks (MDAgents) and prompt-optimization strategies (GEPA) positions the work at the intersection of medical AI safety and LLM sycophancy research, showing that techniques effective on exam-style benchmarks do not transfer to the presupposition-correction task.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

With 32.6% of patients now consulting LLMs for medical advice, the finding that no frontier model corrects false presuppositions more than 43% of the time—and that standard mitigations (GEPA prompting, multi-agent orchestration) create a harmful over-caution trade-off—exposes a critical and currently unaddressed safety gap in medical AI deployment. The Cancer-Myth benchmark provides a concrete, physician-verified measure of this gap that prior medical benchmarks (MedQA, HealthSearchQA) cannot capture. Open questions include whether fine-tuning or architectural changes (rather than prompting) can resolve the detection-vs-over-caution trade-off, and whether the findings generalize beyond cancer to other high-stakes medical domains.
