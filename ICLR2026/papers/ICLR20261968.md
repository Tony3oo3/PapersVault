## Problem

It is widely assumed that Transformer language models are inherently lossy: non-linear activations, LayerNorm, and many-to-one attention are expected to collapse distinct inputs into identical hidden states, making exact input recovery impossible. This paper challenges that view by proving that standard decoder-only Transformers are in fact almost-surely injective (different prompts yield different last-token representations) and by providing the first algorithm that exploits this property to reconstruct the exact input from internal activations.

## Input / Output

SIPIT takes as input the full per-position hidden-state matrix H^(ℓ) ∈ R^{T×d} at a chosen layer ℓ of a pre-trained decoder-only Transformer, along with the model's vocabulary V, and outputs the exact original token sequence s = ⟨s₁,…,s_T⟩. The algorithm is training-free and operates on any fixed layer; it does not require access to model weights beyond forward-pass evaluation, and it tolerates bounded perturbation in the hidden states (e.g., from FP4/INT8 quantization) as long as the noise stays below half the minimum pairwise token-separation margin at each position.

## Method

The theoretical core shows that the map (s, θ) ↦ r(s; θ) from prompts to last-token representations is real-analytic in the parameters, so the collision set {θ : r(s;θ) = r(s';θ)} for any s ≠ s' has Lebesgue measure zero; standard continuous initializations avoid this set with probability one, and each gradient-descent step is itself a real-analytic map with non-vanishing Jacobian determinant, preserving absolute continuity of the parameter law and hence injectivity throughout training. The algorithmic contribution, SIPIT, exploits the causal structure of Transformers: at position t, given the already-recovered prefix π, the one-step map v ↦ h_t(π ⊕ v) is almost-surely injective, so the true token s_t is the unique vocabulary entry whose predicted hidden state matches the observed h_t. SIPIT searches candidates using a gradient-guided policy (rather than uniform random order), verifies each by forward-passing the candidate prefix and comparing the resulting hidden state to the target within a tolerance ε, and appends the first match before advancing to t+1. This yields a provable worst-case bound of T|V| forward passes (linear in sequence length and vocabulary size), while in practice the gradient heuristic explores less than 0.22% of the vocabulary per position, achieving 100% exact recovery on GPT-2 Small, Mistral-7B, and Llama-3.1-8B in seconds rather than the hours required by brute-force search.

## Results

SIPIT achieves 100% token-level accuracy in exact prompt recovery from hidden states, completing inversion in 28.01±35.87 seconds on GPT-2 Small (20-token prompts), compared to 3889.61±691.17 s for the brute-force ablation and 6132.59±104.61 s for HARDPROMPTS (which achieves 0% accuracy). Across ~5 billion pairwise comparisons on 100k prompts from four datasets, no collisions were observed in any of six state-of-the-art models; minimum last-token ℓ₂ distances range from 0.001 (Llama-3.1-8B, layer 1) to 9.020 (Phi-4-mini, final layer), all far above the 10⁻⁶ collision threshold. On FP4-quantized Mistral-7B and Llama-3.1-8B, SIPIT still recovers all tokens with 100% accuracy while exploring less than 0.22% of the vocabulary on average.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SIPIT is compared against HARDPROMPTS (Wen et al., 2023), a gradient-based discrete optimization method for prompt discovery. HARDPROMPTS completely fails to recover the input (0% token accuracy) while taking ~6133 s per prompt, whereas SIPIT achieves 100% exact recovery in ~28 s—a 219× speedup with a qualitative difference in correctness. The paper also includes a BRUTEFORCE ablation that replaces SIPIT's gradient-guided candidate policy with uniform random sampling; BRUTEFORCE eventually succeeds (100% accuracy) but requires ~3890 s, making SIPIT roughly 139× faster. The paper notes that other inversion approaches (Morris et al., 2023a,b; Nazir et al., 2025) operate in a different setting (black-box logprob access with trained inverters) and produce only approximate reconstructions, so they are not directly comparable to SIPIT's training-free, exact inversion from hidden states.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant intuition that Transformer components (LayerNorm, softmax, attention) make the architecture inherently many-to-one, and from prior theoretical work (Dong et al. 2021, Yang et al. 2018) that highlights rank decay and the softmax bottleneck. It also extends Sutter et al. (2025), who prove injectivity at random initialization with respect to the full hidden-state matrix, by showing injectivity at the task-relevant last-token state persists under gradient-based training. On the inversion side, it supersedes prior prompt-recovery methods (Morris et al. 2023, Nazir et al. 2025, Thomas et al. 2025) that rely on trained inverters, operate on logprobs or encoder logits, or lack exactness guarantees, by providing a training-free algorithm with provable linear-time exact recovery from decoder-only hidden states.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result reframes Transformer hidden states as lossless encodings of the input, with direct consequences for interpretability (probes and causal analyses now have a sound foundation), privacy (any system storing or transmitting hidden states is effectively handling verbatim user text), and regulatory compliance (GDPR-style obligations may apply to KV-caches and intermediate representations). Key limitations include the requirement of access to all per-position states at a given layer (not just the final embedding), the finite-horizon training guarantee, and the open question of whether the result extends to multimodal architectures or survives aggressive quantization. The paper also leaves open the design of an efficient algorithm for the final-embedding-only setting, which is the most realistic threat model.
