## Problem

Existing causal inference methods adjust confounders to balance treated and control group distributions, but this risks the over-balancing issue where discriminative information needed for outcome prediction is removed. The paper asks whether adjusting the outcome prediction model itself—rather than the covariates—can achieve cross-group generalization without sacrificing predictive signal.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262732/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard observational tabular data: covariate vectors x, binary treatment indicators t ∈ {0,1}, and factual outcomes y. It outputs two potential outcome prediction models h_0(x) and h_1(x) that each generalize across both groups (not just their training group), from which the CATE τ̂(x) = h_1(x) − h_0(x) is computed. The intermediate groups generated by optimal transport carry per-sample weights γ*_{ij} from the transport plan, which are used as sample weights during self-training.

## Method

G-learner solves the large distribution shift between treated and control groups by constructing a sequence of K intermediate groups along the 2-Wasserstein geodesic (i.e., the OT barycenter path) between the two empirical distributions, computed once via a linear program. The outcome model trained on the control group is then gradually transferred toward the treated group (and vice versa) through consecutive intermediate groups using a self-training paradigm: at each step, the previous model annotates the next group's generated samples, and the current model is trained on those pseudo-labeled samples. A dropout-based uncertainty filter retains only the top-r fraction of generated samples with lowest prediction variance, reducing label noise. The paper provides a theoretical upper bound on PEHE that decomposes into the initial group prediction errors plus a term depending on the accumulated Wasserstein distance across intermediate steps, sample size, model depth, and filtering ratio.

## Results

On the out-sample real-world benchmarks, G-learner achieves the best ε_ATE on News (0.2451), the best √ε_PEHE and ε_ATE on Twins (0.3200 and 0.0084), and the best R_POL and ε_ATT on Jobs (0.1691 and 0.0596). On the simulation dataset, G-learner consistently attains the lowest √ε_PEHE across all confounding bias levels (m_c = 0.5 to 1.4), ranging from 0.20 to 0.36, substantially outperforming the next-best method (DragonNet at 0.27–0.46). The method also shows the smallest standard deviations on Twins and Jobs, indicating stable performance across random splits.

## Baselines & Comparisons

G-learner wins on 5 out of 6 real-world metrics: it achieves the best ε_ATE on News (0.2451 vs. ESCFR's 0.4255), the best √ε_PEHE and ε_ATE on Twins (0.3200/0.0084 vs. DKLite's 0.3207/0.0086), and the best R_POL and ε_ATT on Jobs (0.1691/0.0596 vs. DKLite's 0.1730/0.1470). The only metric where G-learner is not first is News √ε_PEHE, where TARNet (2.1524) and ESCFR (2.7435) outperform it (2.8681), though G-learner still ranks third. On the simulation dataset, G-learner dominates all four confounding levels, achieving √ε_PEHE of 0.20–0.36 versus DragonNet's 0.27–0.46 and TARNet's 0.54–0.55, with the gap widening as confounding bias increases. Among balanced-representation methods (CFR_Wass, CFR_MMD, ESCFR, BNN), G-learner consistently outperforms on ε_ATE and ε_ATT, supporting the paper's claim that adjusting the prediction model avoids the over-balancing issue. Classical methods (OLS, BART, PSM, k-NN) are substantially worse on News (√ε_PEHE > 4) and degrade sharply on simulation data as m_c increases.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in causal inference of adjusting covariates (via reweighting or balanced representation learning) to address confounding bias, instead proposing to adjust the outcome prediction model itself. It builds on two threads: (1) optimal transport theory, where prior work (Yan et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2023) uses OT to align group distributions or learn sample weights, whereas here OT is repurposed to generate intermediate groups via the Wasserstein geodesic; and (2) gradual domain adaptation (He et al., 2024), whose self-training paradigm over intermediate domains is adapted to the causal inference setting. The key conceptual shift is from 'make the data look similar' to 'make the model generalize across dissimilar data,' thereby sidestepping the over-balancing problem that plagues representation-learning approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By reframing the confounding-bias problem as a model-adjustment problem rather than a data-adjustment problem, G-learner offers a principled alternative that preserves all discriminative features for outcome prediction, addressing a well-recognized limitation (over-balancing) of CFR-style methods. The theoretical error bound (Theorem 1) provides a clear characterization of how the estimation error depends on sample size, number of intermediate groups, and model complexity. Open questions include scalability to high-dimensional or continuous-treatment settings, sensitivity to the choice of OT cost function, and whether the Wasserstein geodesic interpolation remains meaningful when group distributions have very different support structures. The moderate but consistent empirical gains across multiple benchmarks suggest practical utility, though the method's advantage over the strongest baselines (e.g., ESCFR, CFR-Pro) is not overwhelming on all datasets.
