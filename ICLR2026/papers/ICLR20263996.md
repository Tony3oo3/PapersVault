## Problem

Data curation pipelines use a small private target dataset to guide selection of valuable public data, with the implicit assumption that the resulting model—trained only on public data—is privacy-preserving. No prior work has systematically analyzed whether the curation process itself leaks membership information about the private target set. This paper demonstrates that every stage of the pipeline (curation scores, selected subsets, and the final trained model) can reveal which samples were in the private curation target, exposing a previously overlooked privacy risk.

## Input / Output

The adversary observes one of three progressively restricted signals: continuous curation scores over the full public pool (e.g., 12.8M samples from CommonPool), a binary selection mask indicating which pool samples were curated, or black-box query access to the final trained model. For end-to-end attacks, the adversary additionally injects a small number of fingerprinted samples (as few as 5) into the public pool before curation, exploiting the fact that web-scraped pools are susceptible to data injection. The output is a scalar membership inference score per target sample, where higher values indicate stronger evidence that the sample belonged to the private curation target set T_sel.

## Method

The paper designs seven membership inference attacks across three pipeline stages. For score-based attacks, they adapt LiRA by replacing shadow models with shadow curation runs and selecting the single most informative public sample per target via signal filtering; they also exploit Image-based curation's deterministic nearest-neighbor structure through a voting scheme that reverse-engineers which target produced each pool score, and solve a least-squares system to recover the membership mask from TRAK's linear score decomposition. For subset attacks, they model binary selection as Bernoulli distributions (Binary LiRA) and design an iterative voting algorithm that refines a target-set hypothesis until the reconstructed curation matches the observed selection via Jaccard similarity. For end-to-end model attacks, they insert fingerprinted samples into the public pool whose curation scores are selectively sensitive to individual targets (using mislabeled captions for Image-based, or orthogonal caption suffixes for TRAK), then detect whether these fingerprints were selected to infer membership. The key finding is that Image-based curation's max-similarity mechanism creates high vulnerability for samples with non-zero influence, while TRAK's gradient averaging provides partial protection that degrades sharply for small target datasets—the very scenario motivating curation in sensitive domains.

## Results

For score-based attacks, the custom Voting attack on Image-based curation achieves AUC ≈ 0.95, matching the oracle upper bound, while adapted LiRA reaches only AUC ≈ 0.6; TRAK's gradient averaging yields near-random performance (AUC ≈ 0.5) for both attacks. For end-to-end model attacks, Image-based curation leaks up to 21.4% TPR at 1% FPR (RESISC45, |T|=100), while TRAK exhibits strong size-dependent vulnerability that diminishes as |T| grows. Differential privacy mitigations reduce TPR at 1% FPR from 98.4% to 5.4% (Image-based) and from 100% to 33.2% (TRAK) at ε=100, and to near-baseline (1.1% and 1.7%) at ε=10.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary baseline is the adapted LiRA likelihood-ratio attack (Carlini et al., 2022), which serves as the principled, method-agnostic reference across all three pipeline stages. On Image-based curation scores, the custom Voting attack substantially outperforms LiRA (AUC ≈ 0.95 vs. ≈ 0.6) by exploiting the deterministic nearest-neighbor structure, and matches the oracle attack that has perfect knowledge of the curation mechanism. On TRAK scores, both LiRA and compressed-sensing variants (OMP, IHT) achieve only near-random AUC ≈ 0.5, indicating that TRAK's averaging provides inherent protection that no tested attack can overcome at scale. For subset selection, the Iterative Voting scheme (Image-based) successfully recovers all non-zero-influence targets, outperforming Binary LiRA which is limited by the coarser binary signal. The end-to-end fingerprinting attacks have no direct LiRA equivalent (since they require active pool poisoning), but their success is bounded by the influence sparsity shown in Figure 2, where 98.28% of PatchCamelyon targets and 34.16% of STL-10 targets have zero influence under nearest-neighbor curation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the Likelihood Ratio Attack (LiRA) framework from Carlini et al. (2022a) to the curation setting by replacing shadow models with shadow curation runs, and borrows the signal-filtering technique from Jagielski et al. (2023) to counteract score dilution across large public pools. It applies these adapted attacks to two well-studied curation methods—Image-based nearest-neighbor scoring (Gadre et al., 2023) and TRAK gradient attribution (Park et al., 2023)—and further draws on compressed-sensing formulations (OMP, IHT) for recovering sparse membership signals from TRAK's averaged scores. The end-to-end model attack builds on the practical feasibility of poisoning web-scale training data demonstrated by Carlini et al. (2024), extending that threat model to the curation-specific setting where fingerprinted samples must be selectively triggered by individual target membership.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic demonstration that data curation pipelines leak private target-set membership at every stage—scores, selected subsets, and the final model—challenging the widespread assumption that training exclusively on public data is inherently privacy-preserving. The practical impact is significant given that curation is now central to industry ML pipelines (DataComp, RedPajama, FineWeb) and a growing data-curation-as-a-service market. Key open questions include the full privacy-utility tradeoff of DP curation (the paper shows DP works but does not characterize the accuracy cost at useful ε values), whether the vulnerabilities generalize to other curation methods beyond the two studied, and how to design curation algorithms with built-in privacy guarantees that do not sacrifice utility. The finding that TRAK's averaging provides natural protection only for large target sets—precisely the opposite of the small, sensitive datasets motivating curation in healthcare and finance—highlights a tension that remains unresolved.
