## Problem

Estimating conditional average potential outcomes (CAPOs) over time from observational data requires proper adjustment for time-varying confounding, yet existing neural methods either target the wrong estimand (CRN, CT, TE-CDE) or rely on fragile strategies: RMSNs divide by products of propensity scores that approach zero, while G-Net and G-transformer must estimate the full distribution of all future confounders via Monte Carlo sampling. No prior neural method performs fully regression-based iterative G-computation in an end-to-end architecture.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a patient's observed history as three parallel time series—outcomes Y_t ∈ R^{d_y}, covariates X_t ∈ R^{d_x}, and treatments A_t ∈ {0,1}^{d_a}—along with a specified interventional treatment sequence a_{t:t+τ-1} for τ future steps. It outputs a single CAPO estimate E[Y_{t+τ}[a_{t:t+τ-1}] | H_t = h_t], i.e., the expected outcome at horizon τ under the chosen treatment sequence, conditioned on the full observed history. The multi-input transformer backbone processes each modality through a dedicated sub-transformer before sharing information, and the final G-computation head g_0^φ produces the CAPO from the encoded history and the first treatment in the sequence.

## Method

IGC-Net reframes the nested G-computation formula as a recursion of conditional expectations over pseudo-outcomes G_{t+δ}^{ā}, where G_{t+τ}^{ā} = Y_{t+τ} is the only observed quantity. Training alternates between a Generation step (the G-computation heads predict intermediate pseudo-outcomes, detached from the computation graph) and a Learning step (all heads regress on the generated pseudo-outcomes plus the observed final outcome via squared-error loss, updating the shared transformer backbone and heads jointly). This iterative scheme requires only τ regressions of a d_y-dimensional outcome (first moments of a low-dimensional variable) rather than estimating all moments of a (τ−1)×(d_x+d_y)-dimensional confounder distribution, and avoids inverse propensity weighting entirely. The architecture pairs a multi-input transformer backbone (three encoder-only sub-transformers for Y, X, A with cross-attention) with τ G-computation heads, and the entire pipeline is trained end-to-end so that information is shared across time steps and the optimization of heads propagates from g_{τ−1}^φ (anchored on ground-truth Y_{t+τ}) backward to g_0^φ (the CAPO estimator).

## Results

On the synthetic tumor growth model (τ=2), IGC-Net achieves RMSE of 3.13–3.71 across confounding strengths γ=10–20, outperforming the best baseline (G-Net) by 6.4%–17.4%. On semi-synthetic MIMIC-III data (d_x=25 covariates), IGC-Net achieves RMSE of 0.24–0.48 across sample sizes N=1000–3000 and prediction windows τ=2–6, outperforming the best baseline by up to 26.7%. IGC-Net also demonstrates robustness to overlap violations (up to 20.4% improvement) and unobserved confounding (up to 12.8% improvement) compared to all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

IGC-Net outperforms all six baselines across every experimental setting (synthetic, semi-synthetic, overlap sensitivity, and unobserved confounding). Against the no-adjustment baselines (CRN, TE-CDE, CT), IGC-Net achieves 6–17% lower RMSE on synthetic data and 9–27% lower RMSE on MIMIC-III, while also exhibiting far lower variance (e.g., ±0.14–0.30 vs. ±0.55–1.67 for CRN/CT). Against RMSNs (IPW-based), IGC-Net avoids the variance explosion from products of inverse propensity scores, achieving 10–25% lower RMSE on MIMIC-III, with the gap widening as prediction horizon τ increases. Against G-Net and G-transformer (both G-computation-based but relying on Monte Carlo sampling of full confounder distributions), IGC-Net achieves 10–27% lower RMSE, with G-transformer being the weakest baseline overall (RMSE 5.3–6.7 on synthetic data vs. IGC-Net's 3.1–3.7). The advantage of IGC-Net grows with confounding strength, prediction horizon, and covariate dimensionality, confirming that its regression-based iterative approach scales better than distributional estimation or IPW.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the classical G-computation framework (Robins, 1999; Bang & Robins, 2005) for adjusting for time-varying confounding, and on the line of neural methods for estimating conditional average potential outcomes (CAPOs) over time, including CRN, Causal Transformer, TE-CDE (which lack proper adjustments), RMSNs (which use inverse propensity weighting), and G-Net/G-transformer (which estimate full future confounder distributions via Monte Carlo sampling). The key departure is reframing the nested expectations of G-computation as a sequence of iterative pseudo-outcome regressions that can be trained end-to-end within a single neural architecture, avoiding both the variance explosion of IPW products and the curse of dimensionality of full distribution estimation. The multi-input transformer backbone is inspired by the Causal Transformer architecture, but the iterative generation-and-learning training loop is the core architectural novelty.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first neural end-to-end implementation of regression-based iterative G-computation for CAPOs, filling a gap between theoretically sound causal adjustment and practical deep learning for sequential treatment decisions in medicine. The consistent 6–27% RMSE improvements across synthetic, semi-synthetic, and overlap-violation settings demonstrate that proper adjustment strategy matters more than backbone choice, and the IGC-LSTM ablation confirms the method's generality. Limitations include reliance on sequential ignorability (no unobserved confounding), the real-world MIMIC-III evaluation serving only as a factual-prediction sanity check rather than a true counterfactual benchmark, and the iterative training scheme adding implementation complexity. Open questions include scalability to very long prediction horizons, integration with sensitivity analysis for unobserved confounding, and extension to continuous-time settings.
