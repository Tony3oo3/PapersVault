## Problem

While VLA models increasingly leverage pretrained VLMs as policy backbones, the field has focused almost exclusively on network architecture, training paradigms, and action decoding, leaving a fundamental question unaddressed: how does the choice and specific competence of the underlying VLM actually translate to downstream robotic control performance? This paper systematically investigates whether general VLM capabilities, embodied-specific skills, or particular architectural components predict VLA success, revealing that standard VLM benchmarks are poor predictors of embodied control.

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes a single-view RGB image (standardized to 224×224 before any model-specific resizing) and a text instruction, deliberately excluding proprioceptive state, tactile feedback, or multi-view inputs to isolate the VLM's intrinsic contribution. The output is a continuous action chunk (relative end-effector position plus discrete end-effector status) decoded from a single learnable action query token's hidden state through a small MLP head, with chunk size varying by environment (10 for Calvin, 4 for Simpler/Libero).

## Method

VLM4VLA is a minimal plug-in that wraps any general-purpose VLM into a VLA policy by appending a single learnable action query token to the VLM's native input sequence; the token's last hidden state is decoded into an action chunk via a compact MLP head (two linear layers with ReLU, totaling <1% new parameters). Training uses a deterministic maximum-likelihood imitation objective (MSE for continuous position, BCE for discrete status) rather than diffusion or flow-matching losses, eliminating inference stochasticity and enabling fair cross-model comparison. All VLM parameters—including the vision encoder, LLM, and word embeddings—are fine-tuned with identical hyperparameters across every backbone. The empirical study then evaluates 17 VLM configurations across Calvin ABC-D, SimplerEnv-Bridge, and Libero-Long along three axes: (1) correlation between general VLM benchmark scores and VLA performance, (2) whether fine-tuning on seven auxiliary embodied tasks (pointing, spatial QA, depth/segmentation generation, etc.) improves downstream control, and (3) the effect of freezing versus fine-tuning the vision encoder, which is identified as the primary performance bottleneck.

## Results

On Calvin ABC-D, Qwen2.5VL-7B with VLM4VLA achieves 4.057 average completed tasks (out of 5), outperforming pi0 (3.509) and OpenVLA (2.548). On SimplerEnv Bridge, the smallest model Kosmos-2 (1.7B) achieves the highest success rate of 60.4%, matching pi0 and surpassing all larger VLMs. On Libero-Long, InternVL3.5-4B leads among VLM4VLA models at 62.8%, though ThinkAct (70.9%) remains the overall best. A key finding is that VLM general-purpose QA benchmark scores show strong correlation with Calvin performance (r=0.919) but no meaningful correlation with SimplerEnv (r=-0.321) or Libero (r=0.381), indicating general VLM capability is a poor predictor of downstream VLA success.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against OpenVLA (Llama-2, 7.7B), VLM4VLA models dominate on Calvin ABC-D (best: 4.057 vs 2.548) and SimplerEnv Bridge (best: 60.4% vs 4.2%), but OpenVLA remains competitive on Libero-Long (53.7% vs best VLM4VLA 62.8%). Against pi0 (Paligemma-1 + flow-matching, 3.1B), VLM4VLA with Qwen2.5VL-7B wins on Calvin (4.057 vs 3.509), while Kosmos-2 ties pi0 on SimplerEnv (60.4% vs 60.4%); on Libero, VLM4VLA models range from 43.0% to 62.8% versus pi0's 46.0%. Against ThinkAct (Qwen2.5VL-7B + RL + proprioceptive state, 7.4B), ThinkAct substantially outperforms all VLM4VLA models on Libero-Long (70.9% vs 62.8%), likely due to its use of proprioceptive input, but underperforms on SimplerEnv (43.8% vs 47.9–60.4%). Notably, pi0's flow-matching action expert provides no benefit over the base Paligemma-1 on Calvin (3.509 vs 3.506), suggesting the VLM backbone itself is the binding constraint rather than the action decoding scheme.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant VLA research trajectory of designing increasingly sophisticated action decoders (diffusion heads, flow-matching experts, discrete tokenization) by instead isolating the VLM backbone as the variable of interest. It builds on the VLA paradigm established by RT-2, OpenVLA, and pi0, but deliberately strips away architectural complexity to create a minimal <1%-parameter plug-in (action query token + MLP head) that enables fair cross-VLM comparison. It also engages with the growing line of work on enhancing VLMs with embodied auxiliary tasks (RoboBrain, Robo2VLM, RoboPoint, Vica-332k) by empirically testing whether those gains transfer to downstream control, and extends the limited VLM comparison in RoboVLMs (Liu et al., 2025) to a broader set of 17 models under a unified protocol.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that standard VLM benchmark scores are poor predictors of VLA performance, and that fine-tuning on embodied auxiliary tasks consistently degrades rather than improves control, directly challenges the field's working assumption that a stronger VLM backbone automatically yields a better robot policy. The identification of the vision encoder as the primary bottleneck (freezing it causes 20–42 point drops) provides a concrete, actionable direction for future VLA training. Key limitations include simulation-only evaluation (no physical robot validation) and the use of a single deterministic MLP head, which may not capture findings that would emerge with stochastic decoders. The open question of what visual representations actually matter for embodied action-planning—beyond what current VLM pretraining objectives provide—remains unresolved and is likely to drive the next wave of VLA research.
