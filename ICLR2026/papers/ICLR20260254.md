## Problem

Existing geometric reasoning benchmarks for VLMs suffer from three critical gaps: reliance on textbook-sourced problems creates test-data contamination risk, evaluation focuses narrowly on final-answer correctness while ignoring the logical reasoning process, and there is no diagnostic framework to pinpoint whether failures stem from weak spatial perception, poor planning, incorrect theorem selection, or inability to detect and correct reasoning errors.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260254/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each benchmark instance pairs a geometric diagram (rendered from TrustGeoGen's formal constructions) with a natural-language problem description and four multiple-choice options (one correct, three systematically constructed distractors). The six tasks probe distinct reasoning sub-skills—from extracting a single numerical or structural premise from the image to identifying the first divergent step in a deliberately flawed proof path. Evaluation produces per-task accuracy scores (compared against a random-choice baseline) and Spearman rank correlations between each sub-task score and final-answer accuracy on GeoBench-solving, GeoQA, and Geometry3K.

## Method

GeoBench is built on a four-level hierarchical framework inspired by the van Hiele model of geometric thinking, operationalized as six multiple-choice tasks: Numerical Perception and Structural Perception (Level 1), Irrelevant Premise Filtering and Sub-Goal Decomposition (Level 2), Theorem Selection (Level 3), and Faulty Branch Localization (Level 4). All 1,021 samples are generated via TrustGeoGen, a rule-driven formal engine that produces geometric problems with formally verified reasoning graphs G = (P, S, R, →), where premises, derived states, and deductive rules are explicitly tracked. Each task is constructed directly from the reasoning graph structure: distractors are generated by numerical/label perturbation, inverse negation of geometric relations, sampling from unused premises or rules, and introducing divergent reasoning branches that share upstream nodes but reach an erroneous terminal state. This formal-verification pipeline eliminates the contamination risk of textbook benchmarks and ensures every ground-truth label is provably correct, while the hierarchical task design enables fine-grained diagnosis of which specific reasoning capability a model lacks.

## Results

On GeoBench's six tasks spanning four hierarchical levels, OpenAI-o3 achieves the strongest overall performance, reaching 91.00% on Sub-Goal Decomposition (S.D.), 74.81% on Structural Perception (S.P.), and 70.00% on Irrelevant Premise Filtering (I.P.F.). However, performance degrades sharply at Level 4 (Self-Reflective Backtracking): even the best model, OpenAI-o1, scores only 27.89% on Faulty Branch Localization (F.B.L.), barely above the 25.71% random baseline. Spearman correlation analysis reveals that I.P.F. (ρ=0.979), S.D. (ρ=0.888), and T.S. (ρ=0.830) are the strongest predictors of final geometric problem-solving accuracy, while F.B.L. shows the weakest correlation (ρ=0.496).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Reasoning MLLMs consistently outperform general MLLMs across all four levels: OpenAI-o3 leads on S.D. (91.00%) and S.P. (74.81%), while Gemini-2.5-pro achieves the best I.P.F. score (74.00%) among all models. Among general MLLMs, Qwen2.5-VL-72b is the strongest, reaching 77.00% on S.D. and 47.42% on T.S., but still trails reasoning models by 14–20 points on these tasks. The LLaVA-1.5 series (7b/13b) performs at or below random chance on most tasks (e.g., 19.00% on S.D. vs. 24.55% random), indicating near-absent geometric reasoning. The CodePlot agent, which generates Python plots during reasoning, underperforms all commercial MLLMs, scoring only 19.30% on T.S. and 15.65% on F.B.L. Critically, no model exceeds 27.89% on F.B.L. (Level 4), a task where even human experts achieve only 52.94%, highlighting self-reflective backtracking as the dominant unsolved bottleneck in current geometric reasoning systems.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on TrustGeoGen (Fu et al., 2025) as its formal data-generation engine, repurposing its verified reasoning graphs to construct diagnostic sub-tasks rather than final-answer problems. It departs from the dominant benchmark paradigm (GeoQA, Geometry3K, GeoEval, GeomVerse) that scores only final-answer correctness by introducing a four-level hierarchical evaluation grounded in the van Hiele model of geometric thinking, covering perception, planning, theorem application, and self-correction. The synthetic-data approach follows GeomVerse and TrustGeoGen in avoiding textbook contamination, but the key conceptual departure is treating geometric reasoning as a decomposable multi-skill process rather than a single end-to-end prediction task.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GeoBench provides the first systematic diagnostic tool for pinpointing *where* in the reasoning chain a model fails (perception vs. planning vs. theorem selection vs. error correction), which is critical for targeted model improvement rather than opaque score-based comparison. The finding that Chain-of-Thought prompting degrades performance on Faulty Branch Localization challenges the near-universal assumption that CoT universally aids reasoning, suggesting that misleading intermediate steps in prompts can actively interfere with error detection. Key limitations include restriction to 2D planar geometry, a modest sample size (1,021), and reliance on a single generation engine (TrustGeoGen), leaving open whether the hierarchical difficulty gradient and CoT findings generalize to 3D geometry, open-ended proof tasks, or other problem domains.
