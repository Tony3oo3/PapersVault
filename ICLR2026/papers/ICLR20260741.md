## Problem

Robotic table tennis demands millisecond-level perception of a fast-moving ball and precise motor control to strike it into specific target regions, yet existing approaches either depend on expensive high-precision hardware vision or suffer from sim-to-real gaps that degrade performance on physical robots. No prior system combines rapid physics-based trajectory prediction with learned neural calibration to correct for unmodeled effects (spin, air resistance, sensor noise) while simultaneously enabling strategic ball placement through imitation learning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260741/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At deployment, the system consumes a 60 Hz RGB-D stream (Intel RealSense D455, 640×480) for ball detection and a 6-DOF robot joint state, producing a corrected hittable position and a 3-dimensional joint adjustment vector for the ABB IRB-120 arm. The spike camera (Spike M1K40-H2-Gen3, 1000×1000 at 20 kHz) is used exclusively during training data collection to capture the exact ball-paddle contact moment and compute ground-truth deviation vectors; it is not required at inference time. The IMPACT module additionally takes a one-hot 4-dimensional target-region label and outputs joint angle perturbations on three critical axes (shoulder, elbow, wrist) to direct the return.

## Method

The core architecture is a Fast-Slow system inspired by Kahneman's dual-process theory. System 1 (Fast) runs YOLOv4-tiny ball detection at up to 150 Hz, feeds an EMA filter (α_x=0.15, α_y=0.15, α_z=0.25) for state estimation, and applies a closed-form ballistic model with coefficient-of-restitution rebound handling to predict the hittable position in under 1 ms. System 2 (Slow) is a Transformer-MLP regressor trained with MSE loss on 1k spike-camera-annotated samples to predict a 2D deviation vector that corrects System 1's systematic errors from unmodeled spin and aerodynamics; at deployment it runs as a lightweight feedforward network without spike input. The IMPACT module is a separate Transformer encoder that maps ball trajectory history, current joint configuration, and desired landing region to 3-DOF joint adjustments, trained via imitation learning on 2k demonstrations generated by randomly perturbing three joints (±10° shoulder, ±10° elbow, ±20° wrist) around the Fast-Slow-predicted intercept pose and retaining only successful returns. This decomposition yields a 0.407 ms end-to-end inference time versus 25.18 ms for Diffusion Policy, enabling real-time control at 2.4 kHz.

## Results

SpikePingpong achieves a 92% average success rate (30 cm zone) and 70% (20 cm zone) for single-target returns across four court regions, substantially outperforming the human average of 53%/33% and all learned baselines (ACT: 19%/7% without vision; Diffusion Policy: 6%/2% without vision). For sequential 100-shot target execution, the system reaches 78% overall success versus 45% for humans and 15% for ACT. Inference latency is 0.407 ms, roughly 17× faster than ACT (7.15 ms) and 62× faster than Diffusion Policy (25.18 ms). Out-of-distribution generalization (relocated launcher) retains 74% at 30 cm, and ultra-high-precision 10 cm targeting reaches 31%, far exceeding the 8% reported by Büchler et al.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SpikePingpong decisively outperforms all four baselines on every reported metric. Against ACT (Zhao et al., 2023), the gap is largest: 92% vs. 19% (30 cm, no vision) for single-target returns and 78% vs. 15% for sequential execution, while inference is 17× faster (0.407 ms vs. 7.15 ms). Diffusion Policy (Chi et al., 2023) performs worst among learned baselines (6% single-target, 2% sequential, 25.18 ms inference), with vision inputs actually degrading performance. The Elman RNN (1990) trajectory-prediction baseline achieves 22.80 MAE / 23.73 RMSE on hittable-position prediction versus SpikePingpong's 12.34 / 13.85, and in the ablation (Table 7) yields only 67% single-target success versus 92% for the full Fast-Slow system. For ultra-high-precision 10 cm targeting (Appendix F), SpikePingpong reaches 31% average, nearly 4× the 8% reported by Büchler et al.'s HYSR system, confirming the advantage of the spike-camera-calibrated neural correction over purely physics-based or RL approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of neuromorphic vision and robotic manipulation by repurposing Kahneman's dual-system cognitive architecture as a concrete engineering design pattern: a fast physics-based predictor (System 1) paired with a slow neural calibrator (System 2) trained on spike-camera ground truth. It departs from prior robotic table tennis work (control-based pipelines like Acosta 2003, learning-based RL like Gao 2020, and imitation learning like GoalsEye/Ding 2022) in two key ways: (1) spike cameras are used exclusively for high-fidelity training label collection rather than real-time inference, enabling deployment with conventional RGB-D hardware; and (2) the IMPACT module learns strategic striking via demonstration-based imitation on real-world data, avoiding the sim-to-real gap that plagues prior RL approaches. The method family (MLP + Transformer encoder for regression) is standard, but the system-level integration of physics modeling, neuromorphic sensing, and imitation learning into a unified Fast-Slow pipeline is the primary conceptual contribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The system demonstrates that millisecond-level inference (0.407 ms) is the critical enabler for high-speed manipulation, and that spike-camera data can serve as a training-time oracle to calibrate conventional-camera systems without requiring expensive neuromorphic hardware at deployment. However, the baselines are notably weak (Diffusion Policy at 3–6%, ACT at 12–19%), partly because diffusion-based methods are architecturally ill-suited to sub-millisecond decision loops, making the comparison less informative about the upper bound of learning-based approaches. Open questions include whether the unmodeled spin dynamics (identified as the primary source of the 79% near-miss failures) can be addressed with richer physics or learned corrections, whether the 31% zero-shot transfer to unseen human players is sufficient for practical human-robot interaction, and whether the Fast-Slow decomposition generalizes beyond table tennis to other high-speed interception tasks.
