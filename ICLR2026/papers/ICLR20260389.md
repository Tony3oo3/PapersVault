## Problem

LLM-based multi-agent systems suffer from high failure rates (up to 86.7%), yet identifying which agent and which step caused a failure remains largely manual. State-of-the-art reasoning LLMs achieve below 10% accuracy on existing failure-attribution benchmarks, and those benchmarks themselves are tiny (127–200 trajectories), leaving both a data gap and a methodology gap for automated, scalable failure localization.

## Input / Output

The tracer consumes a complete multi-agent execution trajectory (the full sequence of states, actions, and agent assignments) together with environmental feedback such as code execution errors or tool-call failures; ground-truth solutions are optional and the method is evaluated both with and without them. Output is a structured pair — the failure-responsible agent identifier and the decisive error step index — preceded by a chain-of-thought reasoning trace, all wrapped in a strict <think>… / <answer><agentID>|<stepID></answer> format that enables automated parsing. The 'decisive error' is formally defined as the earliest step whose oracle correction would flip the trajectory outcome from failure to success, distinguishing it from merely suboptimal actions.

## Method

AgenTracer first builds a 2,000+ trajectory dataset (TracerTraj-2.5K) through two complementary annotation strategies: counterfactual replay, where an analyzer LLM proposes a minimally invasive corrected action at each step of a failed trajectory and re-simulates to find the earliest step whose fix restores success, and programmatic fault injection, where a perturbation operator corrupts a single step in a known-good trajectory to create a synthetic failure with the error location known by construction. The failure tracer AgenTracer-8B is then trained from Qwen3-8B via online GRPO reinforcement learning (no KL penalty, dynamic clipping schedule B_s that decays from broad exploration to tight exploitation). The reward is multi-granular and gated: a binary format-compliance indicator multiplies a weighted sum of a binary agent-level accuracy term and a Gaussian-kernel step-level term that gives partial credit for temporal proximity to the true error step, producing a smoother optimization landscape than pure binary rewards.

## Results

On the Who&When benchmark, AgenTracer-8B achieves 69.10% agent-level and 20.68% step-level accuracy (w/ ground truth) on the handcrafted subset, and 69.62% agent-level and 42.86% step-level accuracy on the automated subset. In the more realistic w/o ground-truth setting, it maintains 63.82% and 20.68% (handcrafted) and 63.73% and 37.30% (automated), consistently leading all baselines. On the TracerTraj held-out test sets, AgenTracer-8B reaches 72.95% agent-level and 57.63% step-level accuracy on the math domain (w/ G), surpassing the next-best model (Claude-Sonnet-4) by 26.92% at the step level. When deployed as a feedback module, AgenTracer-8B boosts off-the-shelf agentic systems (MaAS, OWL-Workforce, MetaGPT) by 4.8–14.2% over three self-correction rounds.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AgenTracer-8B (8B parameters) outperforms all proprietary LLM baselines across both Who&When subsets. On the handcrafted subset (w/ G), it leads GPT-4.1 by +26.0% and Gemini-2.5-Pro by +17.4% in agent-level accuracy, and surpasses Claude-Sonnet-4 by +12.2%. On the automated subset, it exceeds DeepSeek-R1 by +2.95% (agent-level) and +11.54% (step-level), and beats Claude-Sonnet-4 by +1.69% (agent-level) and +2.21% (step-level). Notably, even without ground-truth access (w/o G), AgenTracer-8B retains a clear lead over all baselines, whereas several larger models degrade substantially (e.g., DeepSeek-R1 drops 9.21% on TracerTraj-math). In the self-correction experiments (Figure 3), AgenTracer-8B consistently improves agentic systems across three iterations, while CRITIC (GPT-4.1) often degrades performance (e.g., −4.9% at iteration 2 on MaAS+GAIA) and Self-Refine provides only marginal gains. The 8B model also far exceeds its own backbone Qwen3-8B (e.g., 20.68% vs. 1.72% step-level on handcrafted w/ G), confirming that the RL training and multi-granular reward design are the primary drivers of the improvement.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly addresses the gap identified by Who&When (Zhang et al., 2025c), which demonstrated that even state-of-the-art reasoning LLMs achieve below 10% accuracy on multi-agent failure attribution, and by MAST (Cemri et al., 2025), which first characterized fourteen failure patterns in multi-agent LLM systems. Rather than building on a single prior method, AgenTracer repurposes two ideas from different domains—counterfactual replay from causal inference and programmatic fault injection from software testing—to construct the first automated annotation pipeline for multi-agent failure trajectories. The training side applies GRPO-based reinforcement learning with a novel multi-granular reward (combining Gaussian step-level proximity and binary agent-level correctness), which is a new application of RLVR to the failure-localization task rather than an extension of a specific prior RL formulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

With multi-agent LLM systems exhibiting failure rates up to 86.7%, the inability to automatically identify which agent and which step caused a failure is a critical bottleneck for debugging, data efficiency, and self-improvement. AgenTracer-8B's ability to outperform 100B+ proprietary models while remaining lightweight makes failure attribution practically deployable in production agentic pipelines, and the demonstrated 4.8–14.2% downstream gains on MetaGPT, MaAS, and OWL-Workforce suggest a path toward self-correcting multi-agent systems. Open questions include whether the counterfactual replay annotation approach scales to trajectories with hundreds of steps or highly stochastic environments, whether the 8B model generalizes to entirely unseen agent architectures beyond the six systems used for training, and how attribution quality degrades when multiple agents contribute to a compound failure rather than a single decisive error.
