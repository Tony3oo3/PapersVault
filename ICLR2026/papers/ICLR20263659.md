## Problem

Two prominent late-phase training phenomena—grokking (sudden generalization long after training loss plateaus) and the information bottleneck compression phase (progressive discarding of task-irrelevant input information)—remain poorly understood in their mechanisms and mutual relationship. No prior work provided a unified geometric explanation linking both phenomena to a common underlying quantity or to the training dynamics that produce them.

## Input / Output

The analysis takes a standard DNN (MLP, CNN, or Transformer) trained via gradient descent with weight decay on K-class classification tasks (MNIST, CIFAR-10, Fashion-MNIST), and produces theoretical upper bounds on generalization error and redundant mutual information expressed in terms of a scale-invariant population within-class variance (rescaled by B_g = sup‖g(x)‖₂). Empirically, the method tracks the rescaled NC1 (RNC1) score and NC2 condition number over training steps, alongside test accuracy and MI estimates (via Kozachenko-Leonenko and nHSIC), revealing how these quantities evolve under different weight decay strengths.

## Method

The paper establishes that the population within-class variance of learned representations is the unifying quantity: it upper-bounds both the generalization error (Theorem 3.2, via Cantelli's inequality on the margin) and the redundant information I(Z;X|Y) in the IB framework (Theorem 3.4, via Gaussian entropy bounds). A uniform convergence result (Theorem 4.1) then bridges the population variance to its empirical counterpart on the training set, justifying analysis of the rescaled NC1 (RNC1) metric. The key dynamical result (Theorem 4.3, building on Jacot et al. 2025) shows that under gradient descent with weight decay λ, the training loss converges on a time scale O((1/η) log(1/ε₁)) independent of λ, while RNC1 converges on a slower scale O((1/(λη)) log(1/ε₂)) that grows inversely with λ. This time-scale separation—where neural collapse lags behind training-set fitting—directly explains grokking (generalization improvement delayed until RNC1 decreases) and the IB compression phase (redundant information reduction synchronized with RNC1 decrease), with stronger weight decay accelerating both transitions.

## Results

The paper's headline results are theoretical: Theorem 3.2 bounds the generalization error by a function of the population within-class variance of representations, Theorem 3.4 bounds the redundant information I(Z;X|Y) ≤ (1/2σ²)·E[‖g̃(X)−E[g̃(X)|Y]‖²], and Theorem 4.3 establishes that the training loss converges on time scale τ₁ = Ω((1/η)log(1/ε₁)) while RNC1 converges on the slower time scale τ₂ = Ω((1/(λη))log(1/ε₂)), with the gap τ₂/τ₁ growing as weight decay λ decreases. Empirically, on MNIST with a 4-layer MLP, test accuracy jumps from 15% (overfitting phase, τ=16,000) to 88% (convergence phase, τ=100,000), and the RNC1 score decrease is synchronized with this generalization improvement rather than with training-loss convergence. Stronger weight decay (λ=0.3 vs. λ=0.01) accelerates both grokking onset and the IB compression phase, consistent with the 1/λ dependence in Theorem 4.3.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against competing methods; its contribution is a unified theoretical explanation validated empirically. The key empirical 'comparison' is across weight-decay settings (λ ∈ {0.01, 0.1, 0.3}) on the same MLP/MNIST setup: stronger weight decay consistently narrows the gap between training-loss convergence and the onset of grokking (test accuracy improvement) and the IB compression phase (redundant information decrease), matching the predicted 1/λ time-scale dependence. The RNC1 score tracks test accuracy improvement more closely than training accuracy, and the NC2 score (condition number of class-mean vectors) converges toward 1 in parallel, corroborating the neural-collapse interpretation. For IB dynamics, both MI-based estimates (via Kozachenko-Leonenko) and nHSIC-based estimates of redundant information decrease in lockstep with RNC1, confirming Theorem 3.4's prediction that within-class variance contraction drives the compression phase.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate lines of research—grokking (Power et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2022b) and information bottleneck dynamics (Shwartz-Ziv & Tishby, 2017)—under the geometric lens of neural collapse (Papyan et al., 2020). It builds directly on Jacot et al. (2025), who first proved that neural collapse emerges under gradient descent with weight decay in parameterized networks, by extending their time-scale analysis to explain why generalization lags behind training-loss convergence. The key departure from prior work is the introduction of RNC1 (rescaled NC1), which isolates within-class variance from class-mean separation, allowing the authors to pinpoint the specific geometric quantity responsible for both grokking and IB compression rather than conflating multiple collapse properties as in the NCC metric used by Han et al. (2025).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unified explanation via within-class variance contraction provides a single mechanistic account for two phenomena that had resisted a common theoretical framework, and the time-scale separation (τ₁ ≪ τ₂) gives a concrete, testable prediction about when grokking should emerge relative to training-loss convergence. Practically, tracking RNC1 during training could serve as an early indicator that continued training will yield generalization gains, and the weight-decay dependence offers a simple knob to accelerate the transition. Limitations include reliance on pyramidal architectures, smooth activations, and specific initialization conditions for the dynamics theorem, and experimental validation is concentrated on MNIST with MLPs (with CNN/Transformer results relegated to appendices). Open questions include whether the time-scale analysis extends to non-pyramidal architectures, whether neural collapse can emerge without explicit weight decay, and whether the framework generalizes to tasks beyond classification.
