## Problem

While preference alignment has been explored for text-to-image generation, research on aligning image inpainting outputs with human aesthetic preferences remains largely unexplored. This paper revisits foundational questions: how effective are off-the-shelf reward models for constructing preference training data, how scalable is such data, and how do reward model biases lead to reward hacking in the inpainting setting.

## Input / Output

The method takes a partially masked RGB image (with user-specified regions to fill) and a text prompt describing the desired content, and produces a complete inpainted image. Preference pairs for DPO training are constructed by generating 16 candidate inpainting results per prompt with varied random seeds, scoring them with reward models, and selecting the highest- and lowest-scoring samples as preferred and dispreferred pairs. The base inpainting models operate at standard resolutions and use either U-Net (BrushNet) or Transformer (FLUX.1 Fill) architectures.

## Method

The paper applies Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) to align inpainting models with human preferences, using nine public reward models (CLIPScore, Aesthetic, ImageReward, PickScore, HPSv2, VQAScore, UnifiedReward, Perception, HPSv3) to score candidate inpainting results and construct preference pairs. The key technical contribution is a simple ensemble strategy that averages the rank of each candidate across all reward models to select preferred/dispreferred pairs, which mitigates individual model biases in brightness, composition, and color scheme that cause reward hacking. Experiments are conducted on two architecturally distinct base models—BrushNet (U-Net + DDPM) and FLUX.1 Fill (Transformer + Flow Matching)—across two benchmarks (BrushBench, EditBench), with GPT-4 and Qwen3-VL serving as additional evaluators. The ensemble approach yields state-of-the-art results without modifying model architectures or introducing new datasets, and the paper provides systematic analyses of candidate scaling, sample scaling, and the specific mechanisms by which reward model biases interact with base model characteristics to produce reward hacking.

## Results

On BrushBench, FluPA (FLUX.1 Fill + Ensemble DPO) achieves the best GPT-4 score of 87.609, HPSv2 of 28.735, and Aesthetic of 6.546, outperforming the previous best (HD-Painter: 85.016 GPT-4, 27.931 HPSv2, 6.480 Aesthetic). On EditBench, FluPA leads with GPT-4 72.307, HPSv2 25.972, and Aesthetic 5.681. BruPA (BrushNet + Ensemble DPO) similarly surpasses its baseline BrushNet by +3.66 GPT-4 points on BrushBench (83.054 vs. 79.391) and +4.14 on EditBench (61.186 vs. 57.046), while FluPA improves over FLUX.1 Fill by +3.67 (BrushBench) and +5.33 (EditBench) GPT-4 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FluPA achieves the best score on every metric across both benchmarks, setting new state-of-the-art results. Against the strongest prior method HD-Painter, FluPA gains +2.59 GPT-4 points on BrushBench (87.609 vs. 85.016) and +3.22 on EditBench (72.307 vs. 69.087), while also leading on HPSv2 (+0.80/+2.02), PickScore (+0.26/+0.54), and Aesthetic (+0.07/+0.04). BruPA beats its own baseline BrushNet on all metrics (e.g., +3.66 GPT-4 on BrushBench) but trails HD-Painter on GPT-4 (83.054 vs. 85.016) and PickScore, though it leads on CLIPScore and HPSv2 (BrushBench). StrDiffusion and ASUKA are substantially weaker, scoring 34.255 and 75.140 GPT-4 on BrushBench respectively, indicating they struggle with general inpainting quality. Among the remaining baselines (SDI, CNI, BLD, PowerPaint, PrefPaint), FluPA leads on all metrics by margins of 1–13 GPT-4 points, with the largest gap over PrefPaint on EditBench (72.307 vs. 60.815). User studies confirm these quantitative findings: FluPA is preferred over HD-Painter 62% of the time and over ASUKA 58% of the time in blind pairwise comparisons.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the DPO-based preference alignment framework from image generation (Diffusion-DPO, Wallace et al. 2024) to the specific task of image inpainting, using two architecturally distinct baselines (U-Net/DDPM BrushNet and Transformer/Flow-Matching FLUX.1 Fill). Rather than proposing a new alignment algorithm, it repurposes nine publicly available reward models as data constructors and systematically studies their effectiveness, scalability, and failure modes. The key methodological departure from prior inpainting-alignment work (e.g., PrefPaint, which relies on a single human-annotated reward model) is the use of a simple rank-averaging ensemble across multiple reward models to mitigate individual model biases.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that a trivially simple ensemble of off-the-shelf reward models outperforms any single model and prior SOTA inpainting methods across all metrics, GPT-4 assessments, and human studies—without architectural changes or new datasets—providing a practical, reproducible baseline for the field. The identification of systematic biases (brightness, composition, color scheme) in reward models and their interaction with baseline model characteristics (e.g., HPSv2 causes reward hacking on BrushNet but not FLUX.1 Fill) is a cautionary finding relevant to all preference-alignment work beyond inpainting. Limitations include confinement to 2D images and DPO; the authors note extensions to video, 3D, and RLHF as natural next steps, and the reliance on GPT-4 as a 'fair' evaluator remains an untested assumption.
