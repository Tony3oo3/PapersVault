## Problem

Current training data attribution methods assign a single, static influence score to each training sample, assuming influence is a fixed property of the data. This breaks down for neural networks, whose degenerate loss landscapes (non-isolated minima, rank-deficient Hessians) make the classical influence function theoretically ill-defined, and whose stagewise development means the same sample can be helpful at one stage and harmful at another. The paper asks: how does influence between samples actually evolve over the course of training, and can we predict and detect the phase transitions that drive these changes?

## Input / Output

The method consumes model checkpoints at multiple training times (not just the final model), a training dataset, and query samples or observables. It produces influence as a trajectory over training time rather than a single scalar: per-sample influence curves in the toy model, and per-token influence matrices in language models (where computing at token granularity incurs no extra cost over sequence-level). A key output is the identification of phase transition points where influence peaks or flips sign, which can be used to pinpoint when specific data drives learning. The BIF is estimated locally at each checkpoint via SGLD sampling, with a localization strength parameter γ that controls the temporal window of influence.

## Method

The paper introduces a stagewise data attribution framework grounded in Singular Learning Theory (SLT), which predicts that neural networks undergo first-order phase transitions between qualitatively distinct solutions. The core tool is the Bayesian Influence Function (BIF), defined as the negative posterior covariance between a sample's loss and an observable, which is Hessian-free, distributional, and well-defined at any point in training (unlike the classical IF that requires an invertible Hessian at a stable minimum). The BIF is estimated via RMSProp-preconditioned SGLD sampling from each checkpoint, with a localization term anchoring the posterior near the current weights. The key theoretical insight is decomposing the BIF via the Law of Total Covariance into within-phase and between-phase components: the between-phase term is maximized when posterior mass is evenly split across two phases (π_U ≈ π_V ≈ 0.5), predicting that influence peaks at transitions and can flip sign when within-phase covariances differ. This is validated analytically and empirically in a 2-layer deep linear network on a hierarchical semantic dataset (Saxe et al.), where influence sign flips and peaks align with the progressive learning of animal→mammal→dog distinctions, and confirmed at scale in Pythia language models where token-level influence for induction patterns and delimiters exhibits non-monotonic dynamics matching known developmental transitions.

## Results

In the toy hierarchical semantic model (Saxe et al. dataset, N=8, H=3), the BIF, analytical influence derivation, and Leave-One-Out retraining all agree that influence between samples changes non-monotonically over training, with sign flips and sharp peaks precisely at the branching points of the learned hierarchy (animal/plant → mammal/bird → dog/cat). In Pythia-14M language models trained on The Pile, token-level BIF reveals that induction-pattern influence begins rising at ~128 training steps, peaks around 30k steps (matching the known induction circuit formation), and then diminishes. The BIF-LOO Pearson correlation is maximized at hyperparameters β=1000, ε=1e-3, γ=5e+3, confirming the BIF as a faithful proxy for true causal influence. A time-specific ablation experiment shows that ablating a sample during its peak-influence window induces the largest loss difference, validating the temporal localization of influence.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the proposed Bayesian Influence Function (BIF) against classical influence functions (Cook 1977; Koh & Liang 2020) in the toy model setting (Appendix C.4, Figures 9–10). The classical IF, which requires an invertible Hessian at a stable minimum, is shown to be theoretically ill-defined for the overparameterized deep linear network (rank-deficient Hessian with multiple near-zero eigenvalues) and requires a dampening term γ*I to be computable. When measured with damped Hessian and damped Gauss-Newton-Hessian approximations, the classical IF produces qualitatively similar non-monotonic patterns to the BIF but is less stable across checkpoints, particularly at intermediate (unconverged) training times where the Hessian is most degenerate. The BIF, being Hessian-free and defined via posterior covariance, remains well-defined at every checkpoint without ad-hoc regularization, and its predictions are validated against the gold-standard LOO retraining experiment. The paper does not frame this as a competitive accuracy comparison but rather as a theoretical correction: the classical IF's regularity assumptions are violated by neural networks, making the BIF the principled tool for studying influence dynamics in singular models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the training data attribution (TDA) literature by connecting classical influence functions (Cook 1977; Koh & Liang 2020) to the stagewise development predicted by singular learning theory (Watanabe 2009). It adopts the Bayesian Influence Function (BIF) from Kreer et al. (2025) as its measurement tool, replacing the Hessian-based classical IF that is ill-defined on degenerate neural network loss landscapes. The theoretical framework follows the Developmental Interpretability pipeline (Lehalleur et al. 2025): model SGD with an idealized Bayesian process, apply SLT to predict phase transitions, then verify empirically. The paper positions itself against unrolling-based methods (TracIn, HyDRA, SOURCE) by arguing that integrating influence over time risks cancellation effects when influence flips sign, and instead treats the influence trajectory itself as the object of study.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central prediction—that influence between two data points can flip sign and peak sharply at developmental phase transitions—challenges the field's foundational assumption that influence is a static, global property of a training sample. If correct, this means data selection, curriculum design, and model debugging must account for *when* a sample is influential, not just *whether* it is. Key open questions include the theoretical gap between the Bayesian SLT framework and actual SGD dynamics, the computational cost of BIF estimation via SGLD limiting scalability, and the need to move from behavioral (loss-based) to mechanistic (circuit-level) accounts of influence. The time-specific ablation experiment in the toy model provides causal validation, but at scale the evidence remains correlational with known transitions like induction head formation.
