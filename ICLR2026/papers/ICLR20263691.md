## Problem

Existing Chain-of-Thought (CoT) approaches—few-shot in-context learning and CoT-annotated fine-tuning—are costly, require long prompts or large amounts of high-quality reasoning traces, and offer limited gains for models that already possess CoT abilities. While task vectors have been shown to compactly encode task-specific knowledge for simple adaptation scenarios, it remains unclear whether this paradigm can be extended to complex multi-step reasoning. The paper asks whether the general 'problem-solving mindset' of a task can be distilled into a compact, reusable, and efficiently injectable representation.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text question as input and produces a step-by-step reasoning chain followed by a final answer. During acquisition, a support set of (Question, CoT, Answer) triplets is used to derive a single task-general CoT Vector (a single vector per layer, ~4.2K parameters for the learnable variant). At inference, the vector is added as an additive shift to the hidden state at one chosen layer during every autoregressive forward pass, with no increase in input context length and negligible runtime overhead.

## Method

The paper formalizes the effect of CoT as a 'CoT Shift'—a consistent additive perturbation in attention outputs—providing a theoretical basis for compressing reasoning into a single vector. Two acquisition strategies are proposed: (1) Extracted CoT Vectors, computed as the mean activation gap at layer l between CoT and non-CoT forward passes across the support set, and (2) Learnable CoT Vectors, optimized end-to-end via a teacher-student framework where a frozen teacher processes (Q, CoT, A) and a student processes (Q, A) with the vector injected, trained with a combined representation-alignment (KL) loss and cross-entropy loss. The learnable vector is initialized as a learnable parameter and updated while all LLM weights remain frozen. At test time, the vector is injected at a single layer (shallow layers preferred) during each forward pass of autoregressive generation, steering the model's latent reasoning without modifying model weights or increasing sequence length.

## Results

On Qwen2.5-Math-7B, Learnable CoT Vectors achieve an average accuracy of 53.6% across six benchmarks, improving over the zero-shot CoT baseline (50.5%) by +3.1 points while using only 4.2K trainable parameters. On LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct, Learnable CoT Vectors reach 60.6% average accuracy (+1.9 over the 58.7% baseline). The method outperforms LoRA (10.0M/13.6M parameters) on average for both models (53.6 vs. 53.4 on Qwen; 60.6 vs. 60.4 on LLaMA) with roughly 2,400× fewer trainable parameters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the zero-shot CoT baseline (Kojima et al., 2022), Learnable CoT Vectors consistently improve average accuracy by +3.1 points on Qwen2.5-Math-7B (53.6 vs. 50.5) and +1.9 points on LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct (60.6 vs. 58.7), with the largest gains on StrategyQA (+5.4 on Qwen, +4.2 on LLaMA) and MATH-Hard (+1.8 on Qwen, +1.8 on LLaMA). Against LoRA (Hu et al., 2022), Learnable CoT Vectors achieve a marginal but consistent average advantage (53.6 vs. 53.4 on Qwen; 60.6 vs. 60.4 on LLaMA) while requiring only 4.2K trainable parameters compared to LoRA's 10.0M–13.6M—a reduction of roughly 2,400×. On individual benchmarks, LoRA edges out Learnable CoT Vectors on GSM8K for Qwen (79.0 vs. 78.2) and on MATH-E for Qwen (70.4 vs. 72.0 is actually Learnable winning), but the overall pattern shows Learnable CoT Vectors matching or exceeding LoRA on most datasets. The paper attributes LoRA's limited gains to the fact that instruction-tuned LLMs already possess strong CoT priors, leaving little room for additional parameter updates to improve reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the task vector paradigm (Ilharco et al., 2022) from simple classification and in-context learning tasks to complex multi-step chain-of-thought reasoning, grounding the extension in the attention-shift formalization of He et al. (2021). It departs from prior task vector work in two key ways: (1) it reveals that the layer-wise effectiveness pattern for reasoning is fundamentally different (U-shaped, with shallow and deep layers effective but middle layers harmful) compared to the middle-layer peak observed in simpler tasks, and (2) it introduces a learnable, teacher-student-optimized vector that actively distills reasoning knowledge rather than passively averaging activation gaps. Unlike implicit CoT methods that modify model architecture or require intensive post-training, CoT Vectors keep the model architecture untouched and operate as a plug-and-play external vector.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides both a practical, extremely parameter-efficient tool (4.2K trainable parameters vs. 10–13.6M for LoRA) for enhancing LLM reasoning and a conceptual lens—the three-stage perception-reasoning-expression organization—that explains why naive activation extraction fails in middle layers. The U-shaped layer pattern and the finding that model pre-training structure (e.g., Qwen's more structured latent space vs. LLaMA's) modulates vector effectiveness offer testable hypotheses about how LLMs internally organize multi-step reasoning. Key limitations include modest absolute gains (1–4 points), evaluation on only two 7–8B models, and the fragility of learnable vectors in mid/deep layers where over-steering collapses diverse reasoning paths. Open questions include whether finer-grained or adaptive vectorization can capture intra-task diversity, and whether the three-stage organization holds at larger scales or in non-mathematical reasoning domains.
