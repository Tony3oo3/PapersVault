## Problem

State-of-the-art reward models for RLHF are vulnerable to reward hacking in out-of-distribution (OOD) settings, where training and test prompt-response pairs come from different distributions—a scenario largely overlooked by prior work that focuses on in-distribution evaluations. While multi-objective reward models (MORMs) that score fine-grained attributes can mitigate this issue, their performance is bottlenecked by the limited availability of high-quality multi-attribute annotations, making them weaker than single-objective reward models (SORMs) trained on large-scale preference data.

## Input / Output

The model takes a concatenated prompt-response text pair (x ⊕ y) and passes it through a pretrained decoder-only LLM backbone (without the original output linear layer), extracting the final hidden state as a d-dimensional feature vector. Two linear heads are attached: a single-objective head (w_S ∈ R^{d×1}) producing a scalar BT preference score, and a multi-objective head (w_M ∈ R^{d×k}) producing a k-dimensional vector of attribute scores (e.g., helpfulness, correctness, coherence, verbosity). At inference, three strategies are supported: SMORM-F uses only the scalar head, SMORM-L averages the multi-attribute scores, and SMORM-M averages both heads. Notably, the two heads can be trained on entirely different prompt-response datasets without requiring shared pairs.

## Method

SMORM jointly trains a Bradley-Terry single-objective head and a multi-objective regression head over a shared embedding space, optimizing a combined loss of BT log-likelihood on preference pairs D_S and MSE regression on multi-attribute labels D_M. The key theoretical contribution establishes that the BT loss is upper-bounded by the MSE of the regression objective (Lemma 1), and that joint training yields strictly lower asymptotic MSE for both heads compared to training either in isolation (Theorem 2). A second theorem (Theorem 1, the Implicit Multi-Attribute Effect) proves that under positive correlation between the single-objective score and aggregated attribute scores, the multi-attribute score is lower-bounded by a constant times the single-objective score, explaining why the BT head implicitly guides the weaker regression head. This mutual benefit means the regression task refines the embedding space to improve the BT head's OOD robustness against reward hacking, while the BT task corrects response positioning to boost the multi-objective head's scoring accuracy—enabling a 7B model to outperform a 70B baseline using only 20K multi-attribute samples.

## Results

On RewardBench, SMORM-F achieves 72.8 average (400K UnifiedFeedback/UltraFeedback) and 71.0 average (40K UnifiedFeedback/HelpSteer2), outperforming all baselines including GRM w/ sft (71.5/69.5) and Ensemble (71.0/65.2). For multi-objective scoring, SMORM-L gains +13.9 on RewardBench and +12.4 on RM-Bench over the baseline MORM with Mistral-7B-Instruct and 40K data. In the headline comparison against advanced multi-objective models, SMORM-L 7B (89.0 avg) surpasses the 70B Llama-3-70B-RM (88.8 avg), and SMORM-L 8B (90.4 avg) matches ArmoRM-Llama3-8B-v0.1 (90.4 avg) despite using 15.9× less training data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SMORM-F outperforms all single-objective baselines on RewardBench: it beats GRM w/ sft by +1.3 (400K) and +4.2 (40K) in average score, Ensemble by +1.8 and +5.8, and the plain BT baseline by +4.6 and +6.8. In PPO/BoN reward-hacking experiments (Fig. 2), SMORM-F and SMORM-M show consistent gold-score increases as proxy scores rise, while GRM's gold scores decrease under BoN and ODIN's gold scores drop during PPO, confirming that neither length disentanglement nor generation regularization adequately mitigates OOD reward hacking. Against advanced multi-objective models (Table 3), SMORM-L 7B (89.0) surpasses the 70B Llama-3-70B-RM (88.8) and matches ArmoRM-Llama3-8B-v0.1 (90.4) with 15.9× less data, though it trails the 340B Nemotron-4-340B-RM (93.7). In OOD generalization benchmarks (Table 6), SMORM-F consistently outperforms all BT variants (BT-Norm, BT-Hinge, BT-DR, BT-BSR) across prompt-disjoint, response-disjoint, and mutual-disjoint settings, with gaps of 3–6 points over the strongest baseline BT-BSR.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of Bradley-Terry preference modeling and multi-objective reward regression, two paradigms that prior work has treated as separate. It departs from ODIN (which applies BT loss to both quality and length heads) by introducing a theoretically grounded BT + MSE regression joint objective, and from GRM (which regularizes via text generation) by showing that multi-attribute regression supervision is a more effective and stable regularizer. The key conceptual departure is from sequential training approaches (e.g., Wang et al. 2025b), which initialize one head from the other; SMORM instead co-trains both heads simultaneously and proves this yields mutual improvement rather than one-directional transfer. The paper also extends the reward-hacking literature, which has focused almost exclusively on in-distribution evaluation, by demonstrating that OOD generalization is where existing methods break down.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The first theoretical connection between BT preference loss and multi-objective regression provides a principled justification for joint training that was previously ad hoc, and the OOD experiments expose a critical blind spot in the reward-hacking literature that practitioners deploying RLHF in production will encounter. The practical result—a 7B SMORM outperforming a 70B multi-objective baseline with 15.9× less data—suggests that architectural synergy can substitute for scale in reward modeling. Open questions include whether the positive-correlation assumption (Theorem 1) holds for adversarially constructed or highly domain-specific attributes, how the framework scales to dozens of attributes, and whether the OOD robustness gains persist when the policy model itself is much larger than the reward model.
