## Problem

Whether LLMs engage in latent planning—implicitly representing and acting on future tokens without verbalizing a plan—remains unclear because prior evidence is largely observational (probing or decoding future tokens from activations), which the authors argue is insufficient to establish causal planning. This paper introduces a causal framework for latent planning requiring both forward planning (a representation causes the planned token's output) and backward planning (it causes a licensing context), and asks how these abilities emerge as model scale increases.

## Input / Output

The method takes Qwen-3 model weights (0.6B–14B, with 32B for behavioral-only analyses), per-layer transcoder models (ReLU, hidden dim 163840) trained on each MLP, and text prompts for planning tasks or couplet first lines. It produces per-example transcoder feature circuits—weighted acyclic digraphs with exact direct-effect edge values computed under frozen attention patterns and layer-norm denominators, limited to the top 7500 most influential feature nodes—and from these, identified planning features, causal intervention effects (zero-ablation and 5×-boosting on planning features), and behavioral metrics such as per-class article recall and rhyming accuracy.

## Method

The core technique is transcoder feature circuit tracing: per-layer transcoders replace each MLP with a sparse, monosemantic feature decomposition plus an input-specific reconstruction-error term, yielding a local replacement model that is exactly equivalent on the given input. With attention patterns and layer-norm denominators detached, all remaining computations are linear, enabling exact computation of each node's direct effect on every downstream node via a single backward pass per target. The authors then define planning features programmatically (active at the prompt's last position and upweighting or semantically containing the planned word) and verify their causal role through two intervention types: all-effects interventions (zeroing or 5×-boosting planning features) and direct-effects interventions (same interventions but with all other features frozen to block second-order mediation). For rhyming couplets, they additionally perform steering experiments—downweighting original rhyme features and upweighting features from a different rhyme group—to test whether the model's intermediate context adapts to license the new rhyme, thereby separating forward from backward planning.

## Results

On the a/an planning task, Qwen-3 14B achieves >0.8 recall on both the majority class 'a' and minority class 'an', while 0.6B–1.7B models always predict the majority class and 4B–8B show smooth intermediate improvement. On the is/are task, models ≥8B score perfectly on the minority class 'is', whereas 4B scores below chance. On rhyming couplets, Qwen-3 14B reaches 0.60 perfect-rhyme accuracy (0.80 with assonant rhyme), and 8B models produce a valid assonant rhyme in >70% of cases. Causal interventions confirm planning features are relevant: ablating them harms 'an' prediction, while 5× upweighting improves it, with effects concentrated on the minority class.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper is a scaling study within a single model family (Qwen-3) rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so there are no external baselines to beat or match. The key comparison is across model sizes: planning ability (both forward and backward) emerges between 4B and 14B parameters, with 14B being the smallest model that consistently succeeds on all simple planning tasks. The paper contrasts its causal, circuit-based approach to identifying planning with prior observational/probing-based work (Dong et al. 2025; Pochinkov 2025; Pal et al. 2023), arguing that decodability of future tokens is insufficient evidence for planning; their Appendix G probing experiment on TinyStories confirms that probed animal representations are largely not causally relevant (<10% exact-match intervention success). The closest prior causal work is Lindsey et al. (2025) on Claude Haiku's rhyming, which this paper replicates and extends to open-weight Qwen-3 models, finding that Qwen-3 exhibits forward planning in couplets but lacks the strong backward planning observed in Claude.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the circuit-tracing methodology (Ameisen et al., 2025; Dunefsky et al., 2024) to study latent planning across model scale, building directly on Lindsey et al.'s (2025) causal framework for planning in LLMs, which first demonstrated that rhyming features in Claude causally control both the rhyme and its preceding context. It departs from prior observational work (Pal et al., 2023; Pochinkov, 2025; Dong et al., 2025) that equated decodability of future tokens with planning, arguing instead that causal evidence—specifically both forward and backward planning—is required. The paper positions itself as the largest-scale feature circuit study on open models to date, applying transcoder feature circuits systematically across the Qwen-3 family (0.6B–14B) to trace how planning mechanisms emerge.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a rigorous, causally-grounded framework for measuring latent planning that distinguishes it from mere decodability, which is important for AI safety monitoring of potential scheming behavior. Key open questions include whether planning mechanisms generalize across model families (currently limited to Qwen-3), whether more complex planning tasks (e.g., multi-step reasoning, chain-of-thought unfaithfulness) exhibit the same forward-before-backward scaling pattern, and why backward planning is so costly that even 14B models rarely engage in it for non-trivial tasks. The finding that teacher forcing may reduce pressure to plan suggests that RL-trained models could exhibit stronger latent planning, making this an area to monitor as models scale.
