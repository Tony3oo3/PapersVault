## Problem

Obtaining numerical predictive distributions from LLMs requires repeated autoregressive sampling, where each real-valued output spans multiple tokens and multiple forward passes, making uncertainty quantification computationally prohibitive. This paper asks whether the LLM's internal hidden states already encode the full numerical prediction and its distributional properties before any token-by-token generation begins, and whether lightweight probes can recover this information in a single forward pass.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264599/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The probe takes as input a concatenated vector of the last token's hidden states from a fixed set of LLM layers (e.g., last 8 layers of Llama-2-7B, yielding a 32,768-dim vector), extracted from a single forward pass over the serialized time series. For point prediction, it outputs a magnitude class probability vector and a scaled regression value, combined via top-K marginalization (K=3) to yield the expected prediction. For uncertainty, it outputs S=7 quantile levels (0.025 through 0.975), each with its own magnitude classifier and regression head, enabling direct construction of IQRs and calibrated confidence intervals without any autoregressive sampling.

## Method

The core contribution is a magnitude-factorised regression probe that decomposes numerical prediction into an order-of-magnitude classifier (predicting ⌊log₁₀|y|⌋) and a scale-invariant value regressor conditioned on the predicted magnitude, addressing the wide spread of target magnitudes that breaks standard MSE or log-scaling approaches. For point prediction (mean, median, greedy), a two-phase training procedure first trains the classification head with cross-entropy, then the regression head with MSE on the scaled target. For quantile regression, S parallel magnitude-classification and pinball-loss regression heads are trained jointly. The probe is a lightweight MLP (1 hidden layer, dim 512, GELU) trained on frozen LLM representations, requiring only ~34M FLOPS per statistic versus ~70B FLOPS per LLM sample, and achieves comparable accuracy to 20–25 autoregressive samples while producing well-calibrated confidence intervals across multiple LLM architectures.

## Results

On the synthetic time series dataset (scale 1.0), the magnitude-factorised probe achieves MSE of 0.006 for both mean and median prediction, and 0.015 for greedy prediction, substantially outperforming simple baselines (series mean: 0.035–0.041, last value: 0.085–0.087, dataset mean: 0.256–0.273). The probe achieves >90% accuracy in recovering the order of magnitude across scales spanning 10⁻³ to 10⁴. Predicted confidence intervals are well-calibrated, with empirical coverage of 52.0±0.4% (target 50%), 90.9±0.3% (target 90%), and 95.5±0.2% (target 95%) on the scale-1.0 dataset. Inference with the probe takes 0.034±0.006 s, roughly 47× faster than generating a single LLM sample (1.59±0.08 s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The probe outperforms all three simple baselines by a wide margin: it reduces MSE by 42–98% relative to the series mean, last value, and dataset mean baselines across all three targets (mean, median, greedy). Against the LLM's own sampled predictions (Table 3), the probe achieves comparable error on the one-step-ahead prediction task (e.g., 0.0562 MSE for mean vs. 0.0555 for the LLM), confirming it captures essentially all the predictive information available in the hidden states. The probe also outperforms a Gaussian Process baseline (0.0717 MSE) on the same task. In terms of sample efficiency, the probe matches or beats the LLM's empirical mean for all sample sizes up to 20–25, meaning a single forward pass through the probe replaces dozens of autoregressive generations. A vanilla MLP probe (without magnitude factorisation) achieves 33–42% higher MSE, confirming the importance of the proposed architecture for handling multi-scale numerical targets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM probing literature (Alain & Bengio, 2018; Kossen et al., 2024; Orgad et al., 2025), which has primarily targeted binary or categorical properties of hidden states, to the continuous regression setting of numerical predictive distributions. It builds directly on the LLM-as-time-series-forecaster framework of Gruver et al. (2024) for serialising inputs and extracting hidden states, and on prior evidence that LLMs encode future token information in their representations (Pal et al., 2023; Lindsey et al., 2025). The key methodological departure is the magnitude-factorised probe, which decomposes regression into order-of-magnitude classification and scale-invariant value prediction to handle the wide dynamic range of numerical targets—a challenge absent from prior probing work focused on bounded or categorical outputs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: a single forward pass through the LLM plus a lightweight MLP probe replaces 100+ autoregressive sampling calls, yielding a ~47× inference speedup while matching or exceeding the accuracy of empirical sampling for up to 20–25 samples. Conceptually, the results suggest that much of the LLM's numerical 'reasoning' is completed during input encoding, with autoregressive decoding merely surfacing already-determined values—a finding with implications for efficient deployment of LLMs in regression and uncertainty-aware prediction. Key limitations include the need for white-box access to hidden states, model-specific retraining for each architecture/tokeniser, and degradation in calibration on out-of-distribution real-world data. A critical open question is whether a single universal probe can be trained that transfers across LLM architectures and diverse numerical domains without retraining.
