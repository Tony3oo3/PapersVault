## Problem

The DRL-for-imperfect-information-games literature has largely dismissed generic policy gradient methods (e.g., PPO) in favor of complex FP-, DO-, and CFR-based approaches, based on the assumption that naive self-play diverges in adversarial settings. However, this conclusion rests on small-game benchmarks and head-to-head metrics that obscure true relative strength, and no prior work has conducted a rigorous, large-scale exploitability comparison across algorithm families on games with millions of information states.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20265319/cropped_page7_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Each algorithm consumes a discrete game observation (information state) from one of five large IIGs (2D5F Liar's Dice, 3x3 Dark Hex, Phantom Tic-Tac-Toe, and their abrupt variants) and outputs a policy mapping information states to distributions over legal actions. Evaluation produces exact exploitability scores computed via sequence-form representations (~1000× more compact than explicit game trees) with dynamic payoff matrix generation, as well as head-to-head expected returns. The games range from ~6M to ~27M information states, orders of magnitude larger than the Leduc Hold'em (936 infostates) typically used in prior DRL-IIG benchmarks.

## Method

The paper contributes two main technical components: (1) an open-source Python package (exp-a-spiel) implementing exact exploitability and best-response computations for five large IIGs using sequence-form treeplex representations, multi-threaded gradient computation (18 buffers for 81 opening sequences), and dynamic payoff matrix materialization, achieving 30–90 second runtimes on commodity hardware; and (2) the largest-ever exploitability comparison of DRL algorithms for IIGs, spanning 7,000+ training runs across 350 hyperparameter configurations and 345,000+ CPU hours, evaluating NFSP, PSRO, ESCHER, R-NaD, MMD, PPO, and PPG. A critical finding is that entropy coefficients of 0.05–0.2—far exceeding the 0–0.01 defaults in standard DRL libraries—are essential for PG methods to perform competitively in adversarial IIGs. Over all runs, FP-, DO-, and CFR-based approaches (NFSP, PSRO, ESCHER) fail to outperform the generic PG methods (MMD, PPO, PPG), which achieve rough parity with one another.

## Results

Across 7,000+ training runs (350 hyperparameter configurations, 345,000+ CPU hours) on five large imperfect-information games (millions of infostates each), generic policy gradient methods (MMD, PPO, PPG) consistently achieved the lowest exploitability and dominated head-to-head comparisons. In head-to-head play, PG methods defeated NFSP by 0.08–0.30, ESCHER by 0.19–0.52, and PSRO by 0.21–0.49 in expected return across the five games, while maintaining rough parity among themselves (differences ≤ 0.03). ESCHER was uniformly noncompetitive, PSRO was largely noncompetitive (finding Nash equilibria only sporadically in DH3), and NFSP/R-NaD typically underperformed PG methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates three generic PG methods (MMD, PPO, PPG) against four baselines spanning FP (NFSP), DO (PSRO), CFR (ESCHER), and a non-standard PG method (R-NaD). All three PG methods consistently outperform every baseline in both exploitability and head-to-head play across all five games. ESCHER (CFR-based) is the weakest baseline, losing to all PG methods by 0.19–0.52 in head-to-head return and showing uniformly high exploitability. PSRO (DO-based) is also largely noncompetitive, losing to PG methods by 0.21–0.49, with the notable exception of sporadic Nash-equilibrium solutions in DH3. NFSP (FP-based) is the closest non-PG competitor, losing to PG methods by 0.08–0.30, and in ADH3/PTTT/APTTT the PG methods actually have negative head-to-head values against NFSP (i.e., NFSP wins those specific matchups), though NFSP still has higher exploitability. R-NaD approaches PG-method performance in select cases but typically underperforms them. Among the PG methods themselves, MMD, PPO, and PPG are roughly on par (head-to-head differences ≤ 0.03), supporting the paper's central claim that generic PG methods are competitive with or superior to FP-, DO-, and CFR-based DRL approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in deep RL for imperfect-information games, which has focused on adapting tabular game-theoretic algorithms (fictitious play, double oracle, counterfactual regret minimization) to neural networks. It builds directly on Sokota et al. (2023), who showed that magnetic mirror descent (MMD), a specialized policy gradient method, achieves competitive performance with CFR in IIGs, and extends this observation by arguing that the success of MMD is not due to its specific reverse-KL formulation but to the shared design ethos (value maximization, entropy regularization, update-size control) present in generic PG methods like PPO and PPG. The exploitability computations rely on sequence-form representations from von Stengel (1996) and Koller et al. (1996), applied here at a scale not previously accessible for these games.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

If the policy gradient hypothesis is confirmed more broadly, it would substantially simplify the DRL toolkit for adversarial IIGs, replacing complex multi-component algorithms (NFSP, PSRO, ESCHER) with a single well-understood PG method, with practical implications across autonomous vehicles, network security, radar, and language model alignment. The released exact exploitability computations for five large games (millions of infostates) address a critical evaluation gap that has forced the field to rely on small games or intransitive head-to-head metrics. Key open questions include whether the result generalizes beyond these five (partly homogeneous) games, whether newer variants of FP/DO/CFR (dozens exist, especially for PSRO) might close the gap, and whether longer training horizons shift the relative performance landscape.
