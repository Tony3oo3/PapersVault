## Problem

The Pass@k inference setting—where an LLM generates N candidate responses and submits up to k of them—lacks a theoretical characterization of optimal scaling with both k and the sampling budget N. The paper shows that neither majority voting nor Best-of-N is scaling-monotonic (performance degrades as N grows) and neither achieves the correct 1/√k regret scaling, leaving a fundamental gap in understanding how inference compute should be allocated.

## Input / Output

Given a text prompt x, the method draws N i.i.d. responses from a reference policy π_ref and scores them with an imperfect reward model r̂ (bounded squared estimation error ε_RM). A frequency threshold α (set to 3/(4C*) where C* is the coverage coefficient) partitions the N responses into a high-frequency subset, from which the top-k by r̂ are returned. The output is a set of k responses; success is measured by the Pass@k metric, i.e., whether at least one of the k is the unique correct answer, with regret defined as the gap to the optimal reward.

## Method

Best-of-Majority (BoM) applies a pessimism principle: it first computes the empirical frequency of each response among the N samples, then discards all responses whose frequency falls below a threshold α = 3/(4C*(x)), retaining only candidates the reference policy assigns sufficiently high probability to. From this filtered set, it selects the top-k responses by the estimated reward r̂. This two-stage design—frequency filtering followed by reward-based selection—ensures the reward model is only trusted in regions where it has adequate supervision, bounding the per-candidate estimation error by √(ε_RM²/(αk)). The resulting regret is O(ε_opt + √(ε_RM² C*/k)) with sample complexity N = Õ(C*), matching a minimax lower bound and making BoM the first minimax-optimal and scaling-monotonic Pass@k inference strategy.

## Results

On MATH-500 (Qwen3-4B, N=500), BoM with α=0.005 achieves Pass@1 of 86.8%, Pass@3 of 89.6%, and Pass@10 of 90.2%, consistently outperforming both BoN (83.6% at Pass@1) and majority voting (86.4% at Pass@1). On GSM8K and AIME24, BoM shows large improvements over majority voting and outperforms BoN for small k. When varying N on AIME24 (N from 100 to 2000), BoM maintains stable or improving performance for N≥200, while BoN degrades and majority voting remains flat at a low level, confirming the scaling-monotonicity property.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against majority voting, BoM (α=0.005) wins at every k on MATH-500 (e.g., 86.8 vs. 86.4 at Pass@1; 89.2 vs. 88.0 at Pass@2), and the gap widens at intermediate k. Against BoN, BoM achieves a substantial 3.2-point gain at Pass@1 (86.8 vs. 83.6) and 2.0 points at Pass@2 (89.2 vs. 87.2), but the advantage narrows to 0.2 points at Pass@10 (90.2 vs. 90.0), consistent with the theory that the frequency filter matters most when k is small. Against SBoN (soft BoN with and without reward calibration), BoM is included as a baseline in the experiments but the paper's headline claims focus on the comparison with majority voting and BoN. Critically, when N is scaled from 100 to 2000 on AIME24, BoN's performance degrades (reward overoptimization), majority voting stays flat at a low level (constant regret), while BoM remains stable or improves, empirically validating the scaling-monotonicity property that the other two methods provably lack.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes two well-studied inference strategies—majority voting (Wang et al., 2022) and Best-of-N (Lightman et al., 2023)—into a new algorithm, Best-of-Majority (BoM), specifically for the Pass@k setting where k responses are submitted rather than one. The theoretical framework builds directly on Huang et al. (2025a), adopting their coverage coefficient, reward estimation error assumptions, and the concept of scaling-monotonicity, but extends the analysis from k=1 to general k. The authors explicitly position the Pass@k inference optimization problem as unexplored in prior literature, noting that while the Pass@k metric (Li et al., 2022) is widely used for evaluation, no prior work has studied the algorithmic problem of optimally selecting k responses from N samples with theoretical guarantees.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical implication is that BoN—a widely deployed inference strategy—can actually degrade in performance as the sampling budget N increases when the reward model is imperfect, challenging the common assumption that more samples always help. BoM provides a simple, minimax-optimal alternative that is scaling-monotonic, making it the preferred strategy when practitioners scale up inference compute. Open questions include whether instance-dependent (rather than minimax) bounds can be established, how to handle multiple optimal responses, and whether the 1/√k scaling gap for BoN identified in the lower bound is inherent or an artifact of the analysis. The empirical validation is limited to a single 4B model on mathematical reasoning, leaving open whether the results generalize to larger models and non-verifiable tasks.
