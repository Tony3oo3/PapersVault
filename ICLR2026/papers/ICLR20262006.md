## Problem

Existing white-box knowledge distillation methods for LLMs rely on divergence-based output distribution matching, which propagates teacher prediction errors and bias to the student, limiting its generalization capacity. This problem is compounded in cross-tokenizer settings (mismatched vocabularies and sequence lengths) and when distilling from Mixture-of-Experts teachers to dense students, where no prior method simultaneously addresses teacher imperfection, token misalignment, and expert knowledge utilization.

## Input / Output

The method consumes teacher hidden states from intermediate layers, student hidden states, and ground-truth text sequences. Teacher hidden states are first compressed into a fixed low-dimensional space (d_A=64) via pretrained projectors before being used in distillation; in cross-tokenizer settings, the student and teacher sequences have different lengths, so a cross-model attention matrix of shape n_S × n_T is computed to align them. The output is a fine-tuned student model (full or LoRA) that produces next-token predictions, evaluated via Rouge-L on instruction-following benchmarks.

## Method

The framework operates in two stages. In Stage 1, linear projectors P^{T→A} and P^{A→T} form an autoencoder that compresses teacher hidden states into a 64-dimensional space and reconstructs them, optimized via a next-token prediction cross-entropy loss; P^{T→A} is then frozen. In Stage 2, residual learning computes residual hidden states as h_i^S − β·h_i^{(T→A)→S} only at positions where the teacher's top-1 prediction is wrong (an accuracy mask), with β adaptively scaled by the dimension ratio √(d_S/d_A) and the mean token-wise norm ratio between student and projected teacher states. The student is trained to predict next tokens from these residual states, combined with standard SFT loss (λ=0.5). For MoE teachers, a scaled dot-product self-attention mechanism enriches each expert's output with information from all other experts before the original top-k router aggregation, enabling single-pass utilization of all expert knowledge. For cross-tokenizer distillation, a cross-model attention mechanism computes normalized dot-product similarities between student and teacher hidden states in the shared low-dimensional space A, producing a soft alignment matrix that replaces the need for explicit token-matching rules.

## Results

On same-tokenizer KD, the proposed method achieves an average Rouge-L of 30.68 for Mixtral-8×7B-Instruct→Mistral-7B, matching the teacher's zero-shot performance (30.67) and outperforming the best baseline MultiLevelOT (29.16) by +1.52. For LLaMA2-7B→TinyLLaMA-1.1B, it reaches 25.17 average Rouge-L, surpassing the best baseline DSKD (24.55) by +0.62. Under cross-tokenizer settings, the method achieves 25.09 (Mixtral→TinyLLaMA-1.1B) and 20.01 (Mixtral→GPT2-120M) average Rouge-L, outperforming the strongest baseline DSKD by +1.20 and +1.61 respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method consistently outperforms all five baselines across both same-tokenizer and cross-tokenizer settings. Against MultiLevelOT (Cui et al., 2025), the strongest same-tokenizer baseline, the method gains +1.52 average Rouge-L on Mixtral→Mistral-7B (30.68 vs. 29.16) and +3.52 on LLaMA2→TinyLLaMA (25.17 vs. 21.65). Against DSKD (Zhang et al., 2025b), the best cross-tokenizer baseline, improvements are +1.20 (Mixtral→TinyLLaMA: 25.09 vs. 23.89) and +1.61 (Mixtral→GPT2-120M: 20.01 vs. 18.40). ULD (Boizard et al., 2024) and ALM (Minixhofer et al., 2025) trail significantly, with ULD achieving only 28.41 and ALM 20.53 average Rouge-L in their respective best settings. ABKD (Wang et al., 2025) is competitive in the same-tokenizer setting (29.86 on Mixtral→Mistral-7B) but still falls short of the proposed method by 0.82 points. Notably, the proposed method matches the teacher's zero-shot performance (30.68 vs. 30.67) in the Mixtral→Mistral-7B setting, a result no baseline achieves.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant divergence-based paradigm in white-box KD (KL, reverse KL, JS, optimal transport) by introducing residual learning, where the student is trained on the difference between its own hidden states and projected teacher states specifically at positions where the teacher errs. It builds directly on DSKD's cross-model attention mechanism for cross-tokenizer alignment and on the self-reconstruction autoencoder idea for compressing teacher representations, but reframes the distillation objective around teacher errors rather than distribution matching. For MoE teachers, it replaces the stochastic expert sampling and router-probability adjustment of Kim et al. (2025) with a single-pass scaled dot-product attention over all expert outputs, avoiding multiple forward passes while still leveraging full expert knowledge.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The residual learning mechanism offers a principled way to mitigate two under-addressed failure modes in KD—teacher hacking and teacher bias—by explicitly using teacher errors as corrective signals rather than forcing the student to replicate imperfect distributions. The framework's generality across same-tokenizer, cross-tokenizer, and MoE-to-dense settings makes it broadly applicable as LLM deployment increasingly demands diverse model pairs. However, evaluation is limited to instruction-following benchmarks with ~11K training samples and a single metric (Rouge-L), leaving open whether the approach transfers to reasoning-intensive or code-generation tasks, and whether the gains persist at larger training scales or with more capable student models.
