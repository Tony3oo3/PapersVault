## Problem

Cloud et al. (2025) demonstrated that language models can inherit hidden biases from a teacher during distillation even when training data is semantically unrelated (e.g., number sequences), a phenomenon called subliminal learning. However, the mechanism behind this transfer remained unclear: concurrent work attributed it to token entanglement and statistical logit leakage, but it was unknown whether these factors are actually necessary. This paper asks: when and how does subliminal learning actually occur, and what specific signals in the training data carry the hidden bias?

## Input / Output

The method takes a biased teacher model (bias induced via a system prompt, e.g., 'You love owls'), generates unrelated prompt-completion pairs (number sequence continuations) using either greedy or temperature sampling, and a student model initialized from the same base architecture. The key outputs are: (1) divergence token positions—token indices where the factual teacher's argmax differs from at least one counterfactual teacher's argmax under teacher forcing; (2) per-layer importance scores computed via attribution patching on divergence token pairs; and (3) bias transfer rates measured by the percentage of evaluation responses containing the target animal word. The unusual aspect is that the training data contains no explicit mention of the bias, yet a small subset of tokens (~5–18%) causally determines whether the bias transfers.

## Method

The paper first rules out logit leakage and token entanglement as necessary conditions by showing that greedy sampling (eliminating stochastic logit leakage) and removal of the top-50 entangled tokens both still permit bias transfer. It then introduces divergence tokens: positions where a factual-bias teacher and a counterfactual-bias teacher produce different argmax predictions under teacher forcing. Loss-masking experiments show that training only on divergence tokens (as few as 4.7% of all tokens) preserves or amplifies bias transfer, while masking them out suppresses it to near-baseline levels. For mechanistic analysis, the authors apply attribution patching with integrated gradients on divergence token pairs to compute per-layer causal importance, revealing that early layers (0 and 7) are critical—finetuning a single early layer with LoRA suffices for subliminal learning, while later layers yield negligible transfer. Finally, the paper demonstrates fragility: meaning-preserving prompt paraphrasings (even generated by the biased teacher itself) and mixing data from multiple teachers (even with the same bias) typically suppress the effect, consistent with the rarity and context-dependence of divergence tokens.

## Results

Divergence tokens—positions where a factual-bias teacher and a counterfactual-bias teacher disagree under greedy decoding—constitute only 4.7% (Qwen) to 18.3% (Gemma) of finetuning tokens, yet masking them from the loss suppresses subliminal bias transfer to near Base/Control levels, while training exclusively on them preserves or even amplifies transfer. Finetuning a single early layer (layer 0 or 7) with LoRA is sufficient to reproduce subliminal learning, sometimes exceeding all-layer finetuning, whereas finetuning later layers (14, 21, 27, 33) yields negligible transfer. Subliminal learning is fragile: meaning-preserving prompt paraphrasings (even by the biased teacher itself) and mixing in just 25% data from an unbiased teacher typically suppress hidden bias transfer while maintaining task performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a mechanistic analysis paper rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so the 'baselines' are ablation conditions: Base (unfinetuned student), Control (finetuned on unbiased teacher data), and various masking/sampling variants. Greedy sampling (eliminating logit leakage) does not suppress transfer and sometimes increases it (e.g., 'dog' for Qwen transfers under greedy but not under temperature sampling). Removing all samples containing the top-50 entangled tokens still allows bias transfer, ruling out token entanglement as a necessary mechanism. The critical comparison is divergence-token masking: training only on divergence tokens (as few as 4.7% of all tokens) preserves or amplifies transfer, while training only on non-divergence tokens suppresses it to Base/Control levels. For layer ablation, finetuning layer 0 or 7 alone matches or exceeds all-layer LoRA finetuning, while layers 14, 21, 27, and 33 show negligible transfer. Finally, mixing 25% unbiased teacher data nearly eliminates transfer, and a 50/50 mix of two same-bias but architecturally different teachers (Qwen + Gemma) weakens transfer for most animals, though an unexpected cross-model transfer of 'eagle' from Gemma to Qwen was observed.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Cloud et al. (2025), who first demonstrated that hidden biases transfer during distillation even with semantically unrelated data (subliminal learning), and on concurrent work by Zur et al. (2025), who attributed the phenomenon to token entanglement and statistical logit leakage. The key departure is showing that neither mechanism is necessary: subliminal learning persists under greedy sampling (eliminating logit leakage) and when entangled tokens are removed. Instead, the paper introduces divergence tokens—rare positions where biased and unbiased teachers disagree—as the causal driver, and leverages mechanistic interpretability tools (attribution patching with integrated gradients, following Nanda 2023 and Wang et al. 2023) to localize the effect to early transformer layers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first mechanistic account of subliminal learning, shifting the question from 'does it happen?' to 'when and how does it happen?' with actionable implications for AI safety: simple interventions like meaning-preserving prompt paraphrasing or mixing data from multiple teachers typically suppress hidden bias transfer without sacrificing task performance. Open questions remain about why certain architectures (e.g., Phi-4) are susceptible while others (Llama-3.2, Ministral, Falcon3) are not, whether cross-model transfer (observed for 'eagle' and 'penguin') indicates a more general vulnerability, and whether divergence tokens fully explain all cases (exceptions like 'penguin' persist). The fragility finding is practically reassuring but also suggests that more adversarial or systematic data constructions could potentially make subliminal learning more robust.
