## Problem

Video VLMs process thousands of patch tokens per frame even when consecutive frames share nearly identical backgrounds and predictable motions, incurring massive superfluous computation. Existing efficiency methods rely on attention maps, fine-tuned selectors, or architectural modifications that are not always available in deployed systems. What is missing is a training-free, backbone-agnostic signal of temporal predictability that can decide on-the-fly which tokens carry genuinely novel information worth computing over.

## Input / Output

SURGE operates on raw patch embeddings produced by a ViT vision encoder (before projection into the LLM), consuming a sequence of T frames each partitioned into m spatial cells of dimension d. It outputs a binary spatio-temporal mask M_{u,j} that is applied after positional encodings and before the language model, retaining only the top-ρ fraction of tokens by surprise score. In the SURGE* variant, a CLIP ViT-B/32 pass over peak frames additionally produces a Top-K event ranking that further restricts the mask to query-relevant intervals, with a small context floor maintained outside selected events.

## Method

SURGE defines surprise as the prediction error of each token from a causal constant-velocity model (z_t ≈ z_{t-1} + (z_{t-1} − z_{t-2})), then removes global spatially-coherent drift by fitting a 3-coefficient affine function of token coordinates to the displacement field via least squares. The residual prediction error is variance-normalized using an exponential moving average to yield a calibrated scalar surprise score per token. A global percentile threshold across the entire sequence produces a binary mask, and aggregating retained-token counts over time forms a surprise curve whose peaks (found with minimum separation and prominence) segment key events. Optionally, CLIP similarity between the text query and peak-frame embeddings ranks events, concentrating computation on the Top-K most relevant intervals. The entire pipeline is training-free, requires no access to internal attention maps, and is compatible with any ViT-based backbone.

## Results

At the default retention ratio ρ=0.25, SURGE retains only ~26–27% of visual tokens (~4× fewer) while keeping benchmark scores within ±1 point of full-token baselines across five video QA benchmarks. With CLIP Top-5 event focusing (SURGE*), token counts drop further to ~14–16% (~7× fewer), and on long-context benchmarks such as LongVideoBench and MLVU, SURGE* actually surpasses the full-token baseline (e.g., +0.9 on LVB, +0.25 on MLVU G-Avg for InternVL-3.5-VL). Prefill FLOPs drop by 86% at ρ=0.25 and over 98% at ρ=0.01, with total visual tokens reduced by 72–96%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against FastV (attention-based token pruning), SURGE is markedly more reliable at the same token budget: on Video-LLaVA-Qwen at ~3.3k tokens, SURGE* achieves V-MME 64.5 vs. FastV's 58.1 and MLVU 72.7/3.20 vs. 52.3/3.11, indicating that temporal surprise is a stronger pruning criterion than attention magnitude. Against AKS (adaptive keyframe selection), the two methods operate at different granularities—AKS selects fewer frames while preserving all tokens within them, whereas SURGE prunes tokens within all frames—so they are complementary rather than directly competing; combining them (AKS w/ SURGE*) yields the lowest token counts (e.g., 2,390 for InternVL-3.5-VL) while matching or slightly exceeding the full-token baseline on most metrics. On long-context benchmarks (LVB, MLVU), SURGE* consistently matches or surpasses both the full-token baseline and AKS alone, while on short-form QA (V-MME) all methods stay within ±1 point. Random pruning, included as a sanity check, degrades sharply beyond 75% token removal (e.g., V-MME drops to 53.6 at ρ=0.25 and 29.8 at ρ=0.01 on Qwen2.5-VL), whereas SURGE remains stable within ±1.1% across all pruning levels.

## Relation to Prior Work

SURGE carves new ground by repurposing the prediction-error (surprise) signal from cognitive science and RL curiosity modules as a token-level pruning criterion for video VLMs, rather than relying on attention maps, learned selectors, or architectural modifications. It departs from attention-based pruning (FastV, SparseVLM, IVTP) and token merging (ToMe, vid-TLDR, TempMe) by requiring no internal model access or retraining, and from temporal keyframe selection (AKS, SeViLA) by operating within frames at the patch level. The constant-velocity predictor with affine drift correction borrows from motion compensation in video stabilization, while the variance-normalized surprise score draws on predictive coding theory, but the specific combination as a training-free, backbone-agnostic spatio-temporal mask for VLM inference is novel.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SURGE offers a drop-in, training-free mechanism that reduces prefill cost by 86–98% and extends the practical video length on fixed hardware by over an order of magnitude, making long-context video VLMs viable on commodity accelerators without retraining or architectural changes. Its composability with keyframe selection (AKS) and query-aware CLIP focusing suggests a modular efficiency stack for deployed systems. Key limitations include the extra CLIP pass and sensitivity to the Top-K parameter and query phrasing in SURGE*, and the method has only been validated on three model families. Open questions include whether lighter relevance models can replace CLIP, whether adaptive event selection can reduce the K sensitivity, and whether the constant-velocity prior generalizes to highly non-linear or discontinuous video content.
