## Problem

Existing neural routing solvers assume simple graphs with a single edge between each node pair, making them unable to handle multigraphs where multiple competing edges (e.g., differing in travel time and distance) exist between the same nodes. This limitation is twofold: transformer-based encoders like MatNet cannot represent multigraph structures, and standard node-based decoders cannot select which parallel edge to traverse. The paper addresses this gap in the multi-objective setting, where competing objectives naturally give rise to competing edges, and in the non-Euclidean asymmetric setting that is more practically relevant.

## Input / Output

The method takes a multigraph with N nodes and M parallel edges per node pair, where each edge carries a multi-dimensional cost vector (e.g., distance and travel time), plus a preference vector λ ∈ ℝ^m (summing to 1) that controls the objective trade-off. The encoder is preference-agnostic and run once per instance; only the decoder is conditioned on λ via an MLP hypernetwork. The output is a Pareto front obtained by solving 101 scalarized subproblems (linearly spaced λ values), yielding routes as sequences of edges (GMS-EB) or nodes on a pruned simple graph (GMS-DH). For GMS-DH, the output is structured as a two-stage decision: a non-autoregressive edge selection (one edge per node pair) followed by an autoregressive node permutation.

## Method

Both models use the GREAT (Graph Edge Attention Network) encoder, which produces edge embeddings via attention over outgoing and incoming edges with residual connections that preserve distinctions between parallel edges. GMS-EB autoregressively selects the next edge from the current node using an edge-based multi-pointer decoder that scores all outgoing edges to unvisited nodes, scaling as O(MN⁴). GMS-DH instead inserts a selection-decoder after L₁−1 GREAT layers that non-autoregressively prunes one edge per node pair (using a multi-pointer score over parallel edges), then feeds the pruned graph through a final GREAT layer and a standard node-based multi-pointer routing decoder, scaling as O(N³). Training uses REINFORCE with Chebyshev scalarization reward and POMO baselines; for GMS-DH, the selection-head is trained collaboratively using the best tour found by the routing-head as a proxy for the optimal tour on the pruned graph. Curriculum learning progressively increases instance size, and for GMS-DH the routing-head is pre-trained on simple graphs to stabilize the reward signal for the selection-head.

## Results

On the MOTSP (TMAT50), GMS-EB achieves a normalized Hypervolume (HV) of 0.57 with a 1.50% gap to LKH (0.58), while GMS-DH reaches 0.57 with a 2.36% gap. On the MOCVRP (TMAT50), GMS-EB (augmented) attains the best HV of 0.47 (0.00% gap), outperforming MBM (aug) at 0.47 (0.99% gap). On the multigraph MGMOTSP (FLEX2-50), GMS-DH achieves HV 0.89 (1.84% gap to LKH's 0.90), while on the MGMOCVRP (FLEX2-50), HGS leads with HV 0.89 and GMS-EB (aug) follows at 0.86 (3.55% gap). Inference times for GMS-DH range from 4–20 seconds for 50–100 node instances, compared to 6–32 minutes for LKH.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LKH3 (Helsgaun, 2017) remains the strongest baseline on MOTSP and MGMOTSP, with GMS-EB and GMS-DH trailing by only 1.1–2.4% HV gap at 50 nodes and 1.1–2.8% at 100 nodes. HGS (Vidal et al., 2012) is the best method on MGMOCVRP, with GMS-EB (aug) within 3.55% and GMS-DH (aug) within 3.86% on FLEX2-50. OR-Tools (Perron & Furnon, 2019) is consistently outperformed by both GMS variants across all four problems, with gaps of 4–6% HV. The MOEAs (MOEA/D, NSGA-II, NSGA-III, MOGLS) are substantially outperformed: MOEA/D trails GMS-EB by 17.5% HV on MGMOTSP FLEX2-50 and 14% on MOCVRP TMAT50, while NSGA-II lags by over 20% on MGMOCVRP. The neural baseline MBM (MatNet + MP decoder) is competitive on MGMOTSP (within 2–5%) but degrades sharply on XASY100 MOCVRP (24.47% gap vs. GMS-EB's 1.23%), highlighting the robustness advantage of the GMS architectures. The pre-pruning ablation (GMS-DH PP) confirms that explicit multigraph-aware encoding is critical: it loses 14–16% HV to the full GMS-DH on multigraph problems despite using the same pruning strategy as MBM.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the neural routing literature (GREAT encoder, multi-pointer decoder, POMO training, hypernetwork preference conditioning) to a structural setting—multigraphs—that all prior learning-based solvers have ignored. While OR heuristics (LKH3, HGS, MOEAs) and exact solvers have long handled multigraph VRPs, no prior neural method could encode or decode on graphs with parallel edges. The dual-head GMS-DH architecture, combining non-autoregressive edge pruning with autoregressive route construction under a shared encoder, departs from both the purely autoregressive paradigm (GMS-EB) and the purely non-autoregressive heatmap approaches, drawing a conceptual parallel to divide-and-conquer NAR+AR hybrids (Ye et al., 2024) but applied to edge selection rather than graph partitioning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Multigraph routing is directly relevant to real road networks where multiple parallel routes between intersections carry distinct time/distance profiles, and the paper demonstrates that learned pruning substantially outperforms even theoretically optimal pre-pruning under linear scalarization—suggesting the multigraph structure encodes information that any fixed pruning rule destroys. Limitations include the O(MN⁴) scaling of GMS-EB, slow training times (1.7h/epoch on multigraphs), and the fact that GMS-DH's edge choice is context-free (must be fixed before rollout). Open questions include whether alternative decomposition strategies (beyond pruning) can further improve scalability, and whether the framework extends to stochastic or hard-constrained variants.
