## Problem

Prior game benchmarks for LLMs/VLMs entangle perception, reasoning, and memory simultaneously, making it impossible to attribute model failures to specific capabilities, and existing benchmarks are either too hard for current models to show progress (VideoGameBench) or already saturated (parts of LMAct). This paper asks: can we build a game benchmark that is challenging yet discriminative, and that allows selective probing of individual model capabilities through controlled scaffolding?

## Input / Output

The benchmark streams game states s_i (either raw RGB screenshots or, when the perception module is active, backend-derived textual tables like 'Box at (2,3)' or o3-generated scene descriptions) along with a trajectory of the latest N turns and an optional memory reflection R_{i-1}, formatted as [{J_{i-N:i-1}}, R_{i-1}, s_i] → a_i. The model outputs a single discrete action per turn (e.g., a direction in 2048, a tile placement in Tetris, a dialogue choice in Ace Attorney). Performance is measured as a continuous raw score per game (e.g., cumulative horizontal distance in Mario, total merged tile sum in 2048, count of correct courtroom actions in Ace Attorney), averaged over three runs, with both progression and long-horizon reward structures.

## Method

LMGame-Bench builds a unified Gym-style API over six games (Super Mario Bros, Tetris, Sokoban, Candy Crush, 2048, Ace Attorney) and introduces a modular gaming harness with three independently toggleable components: a perception module that converts visual game states into symbolic text (backend reads for grid games, o3 VLM descriptions for Mario and Ace Attorney), a memory module combining transient history (past N states/actions) with a self-reflection prompt that encodes lessons to avoid repeated failures, and a reasoning module that enables or disables long chain-of-thought. To ensure robustness, the benchmark applies data-contamination mitigation (vision-level frame-reordering tests for Mario; entity masking, paraphrasing, and enforced reasoning for Ace Attorney) and a two-stage prompt standardization using DSPy's SIMBA optimizer that reduces prompt variance by 33.8–63.5%. Evaluation of 13 models is complemented by Spearman correlation with 20 external benchmarks and low-rank matrix factorization of the model–benchmark performance matrix, decomposing each game into a sparse combination of four latent abilities (language/multi-task knowledge, coding, symbolic/puzzle-solving, physical reasoning).

## Results

Across 13 state-of-the-art models on 6 games, o3 and o1 achieve top-2 performance in all games, followed by Gemini-2.5-Pro and Claude-3.7-Sonnet; among non-reasoning models, GPT-4.1 leads. Without the gaming harness, 40% of game runs fail to beat a random-play baseline, whereas with the harness 86.7% do. Paired-sample t-tests confirm statistically significant harness gains (p<0.05) for 5 of 6 games, with the largest improvement in Candy Crush (+217.50 points, +224.8%) and Sokoban (+1.97 points, +537.5%). Glass's δ analysis shows harnessed runs average δ=3.334 vs. 0.750 for unharnessed, pulling scores substantially farther from randomness.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baselines are random play and human performance (3 graduate students). Without the harness, most models score at or near random levels: over three-quarters score zero on Sokoban and Ace Attorney, and Tetris/Candy Crush scores cluster around the random baseline (e.g., random Tetris = 10.2, random 2048 = 100.4). With the full harness, o3 reaches 8.0 on Sokoban (vs. human 9.7), 42.0 on Tetris (vs. human 353.3), and 647.0 on Candy Crush (vs. human 283.3, surpassing humans), while o1 achieves 16.0 on Ace Attorney (vs. human 17.3). The harness ablation (Table 2) shows that the perception module is most impactful for reasoning models on visually complex games (Sokoban: 0.9→4.0 for reasoning models; Tetris: 13.4→26.4), while the memory module primarily benefits non-reasoning models on temporally extended games (2048: 57.6→102.5; Candy Crush: 36.1→97.6). Correlation analysis links individual games to established benchmarks: Sokoban aligns with math/coding (AIME, BigCodeBench), Tetris/2048 with pattern recognition (EnigmaEval, NYT-Connections), and Ace Attorney with long-context language (LiveBench-Language), confirming that the games probe distinct, non-redundant LLM capabilities.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the growing line of game-based LLM/VLM benchmarks (BALROG, LMAct, VideoGameBench, GameArena, GameBench) by introducing a modular harness architecture with toggleable perception, memory, and reasoning modules, enabling controlled isolation of individual capabilities rather than evaluating them entangled. It departs from prior work in three specific ways: (1) the harness design allows ablation of perception aids and memory/reflection while holding the game constant, (2) explicit data contamination detection and mitigation (entity masking, paraphrasing, enforced reasoning) for games with publicly available assets, and (3) a two-stage prompt standardization pipeline using DSPy's SIMBA optimizer to reduce evaluation variance. The benchmark builds on the Gymnasium interface and the long tradition of games as AI testbeds (TD-Gammon, AlphaGo, OpenAI Gym), but positions itself in the gap between VideoGameBench (too hard, 3D) and LMAct (partially saturated) by selecting games with inherent difficulty scaling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LMGame-Bench provides a practical, modular framework for diagnosing *why* LLMs fail at interactive tasks, not just *that* they fail, which is valuable for both model developers and agentic system designers. The correlation and low-rank factorization analysis offering a quantitative mapping from individual games to latent capabilities (symbolic reasoning, spatial reasoning, long-context language, physical reasoning) is a useful interpretive tool that could inform targeted model improvements. Key limitations include high variance in partially observable games (Super Mario Bros), substantial computational cost of agentic evaluation, and the fact that the harness modules (especially perception) essentially offload capabilities the models should ideally possess natively. Open questions include whether the benchmark will saturate as models improve, whether harness-internalized capabilities transfer to unscaffolded settings, and whether the modular design generalizes to 3D or real-time games.
