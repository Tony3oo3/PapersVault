## Problem

Diffusion-based image restoration faces a fundamental trade-off: conditional generation struggles to balance fidelity and realism, inversion-based methods accumulate errors, and diffusion posterior sampling (DPS) requires a precisely known forward measurement operator that is rarely available for heterogeneous real-world degradations like haze, shadows, and blur. This paper eliminates the dependency on an explicit forward operator by learning the posterior correction directly.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single degraded RGB image (hazy or shadowed) at resolutions of 256×256 (ISTD) or 512×512 (dehazing, FaceShadow) and produces a restored clean RGB image at the same resolution. It operates entirely in pixel space without a pretrained VAE, using only 5 sampling steps at inference. The degraded image serves dual roles: as a residual prior (R = y − x₀) during training and as the initialization anchor at the optimal sampling step T′ during inference.

## Method

LearnIR integrates two components on top of the RDDM residual diffusion backbone. The Dynamic Resolution Module (DRM) applies non-trainable bilinear interpolation to downsample images in early high-noise stages (t ≥ T/2) for global structure capture and restore native resolution in later stages for fine texture, eliminating the need for a VAE. The Diffusion Posterior Sampling Correction (DPSC) replaces DPS's explicit forward-operator gradient with a lightweight learned network that predicts the Gaussian mean and variance of the discrepancy between the model's reverse prediction and the true forward posterior state (Theorem 1). A consistency loss regresses the predicted mean toward the analytically computed mean derived from the closure property of Gaussian distributions, explicitly aligning the learned reverse dynamics with the true forward posterior. Training uses a two-stage strategy: first learning DPSC at fixed resolution, then fine-tuning with DRM enabled at dynamic resolutions.

## Results

On the three dehazing benchmarks at 512×512, LearnIR achieves 27.70 dB PSNR / 0.832 SSIM / 0.055 LPIPS on O-HAZE, 27.32 / 0.905 / 0.065 on HazyDet, and 25.43 / 0.795 / 0.085 on REVIDE, setting new state-of-the-art on all three. On the ISTD shadow-removal benchmark (256×256), LearnIR scores 29.57 dB PSNR and 0.927 SSIM, the best among mask-based methods. On the newly constructed FaceShadow dataset (512×512), LearnIR reaches 28.87 dB PSNR and 0.970 SSIM on the synthetic split, outperforming the next-best method (DiFaReli) by 2.44 dB in PSNR.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On dehazing, LearnIR decisively outperforms all seven baselines across all three datasets: it gains +2.27 dB PSNR over MB-TaylorFormerV2 (25.43) on O-HAZE, +1.65 dB over ConvIR (25.67) on HazyDet, and +0.18 dB over MB-TaylorFormerV2 (25.25) on REVIDE, while also achieving the lowest LPIPS in every setting. On ISTD shadow removal, LearnIR (29.57 dB PSNR) is the best mask-based method, surpassing ShadowRefiner (28.75) by 0.82 dB, but trails the strongest mask-free methods—ShadowFormer (30.47) and ResFusion (30.09)—by roughly 0.5–0.9 dB in PSNR. On the FaceShadow dataset, LearnIR dominates all four competitors with a 2.44 dB PSNR margin over DiFaReli (26.43) and a 4.42 dB margin over ResFusion (24.45) on the synthetic split, and similarly leads on the real-world test set (27.93 vs. 26.22 for ResFusion). The ablation confirms both DRM and DPSC contribute substantially: removing either drops PSNR by ~1.3 dB, and removing both causes a 5.66 dB collapse.

## Relation to Prior Work

LearnIR sits at the intersection of two diffusion-based restoration paradigms: residual-guided diffusion (ResFusion, RDDM) and diffusion posterior sampling (DPS). It inherits ResFusion's residual formulation and optimal sampling step T′, and uses RDDM as its backbone, but departs from DPS's core requirement of a known forward measurement operator A by replacing the analytic gradient with a learned Gaussian correction (DPSC). The Dynamic Resolution Module draws conceptual inspiration from Matryoshka Diffusion Models and PixelFlow, but applies them in pixel space via non-learnable bilinear interpolation, explicitly avoiding a pretrained VAE. The key conceptual move is showing (Theorem 1) that the DPS gradient is proportional to the difference between the forward and reverse posterior means, which—by the closure property of Gaussians—can be modeled as a learnable Gaussian offset.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By eliminating the need for an explicit forward operator, LearnIR makes diffusion posterior sampling applicable to real-world degradations (haze, shadows) where no clean analytical model exists, bridging a practical gap that has limited DPS to synthetic or well-characterized inverse problems. The DRM's ability to replace a VAE with simple interpolation while outperforming a fully fine-tuned Stable Diffusion VAE (Table 7) suggests a lighter-weight path for pixel-space restoration pipelines. However, the method still struggles with strongly color-tinted shadows (Fig. 9), and the FaceShadow dataset, while useful, is synthetic and built on a prior relighting method, leaving open the question of generalization to truly uncontrolled real-world lighting. The reliance on a two-stage training protocol (DPSC first, then DRM fine-tuning) adds practical complexity that may limit adoption.
