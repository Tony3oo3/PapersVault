## Problem

The LLM-as-a-judge paradigm has been validated primarily on static, well-defined tasks, but its reliability in open-ended domains requiring continuous interaction with a dynamic environment remains unexplored. Web development is an ideal stress test because assessment depends not just on static code but on real-time interaction, and the task is intrinsically open-ended with no single absolute answer. This paper asks whether current LLM, MLLM, and agentic evaluators can approximate expert human preferences in such complex settings, and if not, what fundamental limitations cause the gap.

## Input / Output

Each benchmark instance is a quadruple (Q, W_a, W_b, l_p): a web development query, two paired React/Next.js implementations, and a human-annotated preference label. Evaluators receive the query, source code, and optionally a rendered screenshot (for MLLMs), and must output a preference (a wins / b wins / tie). The rubric tree decomposes each query into ~30 binary leaf nodes across three dimensions (intention, static quality, dynamic behavior), enabling per-criterion pass/fail scoring. For agentic evaluation, the executor (UI-TARS-1.5) additionally navigates a live deployed web environment, consuming interaction trajectories rather than static observations. Performance is measured as agreement rate with expert human preferences, with a human baseline of 84.56%.

## Method

The paper introduces a structured annotation protocol using query-grounded rubric trees—hierarchical decompositions of requirements into verifiable binary leaf nodes—which raises inter-annotator agreement from 65% (raw labels) to 92% (human-written rubrics) and 90% (LLM-generated rubrics). Evaluation is conducted under two paradigms (pairwise comparison and single-answer grading) with three guidance mechanisms (direct, Likert scale, rubric-based), across 15+ LLM/MLLM evaluators and an agentic planner-executor-summarizer pipeline using UI-TARS-1.5 as the GUI executor. A key diagnostic contribution is WebDevJudge-Unit, a 502-task dataset for feasibility verification that reveals complementary weaknesses: static LLM evaluators achieve high recall (90–93.5%) but low precision (~70%), while agentic evaluators show the opposite pattern (high precision ~82%, low recall ~70%). The paper further proposes a gated ensemble (Agent ∨ LLM for dynamic tasks) that leverages these complementary strengths, and identifies two fundamental bottlenecks: failure to recognize functional equivalence across diverse implementations, and systematic positional bias that persists despite explicit debiasing instructions.

## Results

On WEBDEVJUDGE, the best-performing LLM judge (GPT-4.1 under pairwise comparison) achieves only 70.34% agreement with expert human preferences, compared to the human baseline of 84.56%—a gap of approximately 14.2%. Pairwise comparison consistently outperforms single-answer grading by over 8% in average agreement rate across all models. The agentic workflow (planner-executor-summarizer with UI-TARS-1.5) achieves 60.55% under single-answer grading with combined rubrics, failing to outperform any vanilla LLM due to compounding errors across pipeline stages.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4.1 leads all LLM judges at 70.34% pairwise agreement, narrowly ahead of Claude-4-Sonnet (70.18%) and Claude-3.7-Sonnet (68.96%), but all remain ~14% below the human expert baseline of 84.56%. Among non-reasoning models, GPT-4.1 outperforms GPT-4o (67.74%) by 2.6 points and DeepSeek-V3-0324 (67.13%) by 3.2 points in pairwise agreement. The agentic workflow (planner-executor-summarizer using UI-TARS-1.5) underperforms all vanilla LLMs: its best configuration (combined rubrics) reaches only 60.55% single-answer agreement, trailing GPT-4.1's single-answer score (60.86%) and far below its pairwise score (70.34%), attributed to brittle planning and faulty execution errors compounding across stages. Pairwise comparison dominates single-answer grading across every model by an average of >8%, with the gap largest for weaker models (e.g., Qwen2.5-14B: 54.74% pair vs. 44.95% single). On the WebDevJudge-Unit feasibility verification task, LLM evaluators (GPT-4.1: 75.1% acc, DeepSeek-V3: 73.7% acc) achieve high recall (90–93.5%) but low precision (69.6–72.1%), while the agentic evaluator UI-TARS-1.5 shows the opposite pattern (82.4% precision, 70.3% recall), revealing complementary weaknesses that a gated ensemble partially addresses (improving GPT-4.1 from 65.0% to 66.2% and DeepSeek-V3 from 66.3% to 67.6% agreement).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM-as-a-judge paradigm (established by MT-bench and Chatbot Arena) from static, well-defined text tasks to dynamic, interactive web development evaluation. It adapts the rubric-tree annotation methodology from Mind2Web 2 (Gou et al., 2025) and the planner-executor-summarizer agentic architecture from Bian et al. (2025), but applies both to a novel domain where evaluation requires continuous interaction with a live web environment rather than one-shot assessment of final outputs. Unlike prior meta-evaluation benchmarks (JudgeBench, AgentRewardBench) that focus on text-based or pre-scripted trajectories, WEBDEVJUDGE uniquely supports both static code/screenshot analysis and real-time interactive assessment of deployed web applications.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals a persistent ~15% gap between the best LLM judge (GPT-4.1 at 70.34%) and human experts (84.56%), and identifies two fundamental bottlenecks—failure to recognize functional equivalence and complementary weaknesses in feasibility verification (static LLMs: high recall/low precision; interactive agents: high precision/low recall)—that point toward architectural rather than prompting solutions. The counterintuitive finding that agentic workflows underperform vanilla models due to error accumulation across planning and execution stages challenges the assumption that multi-stage pipelines are inherently better for interactive evaluation. Open questions include whether the gated ensemble strategy (combining LLM code analysis with agent-based dynamic verification) can close the gap, and whether these limitations generalize beyond web development to other complex interactive domains.
