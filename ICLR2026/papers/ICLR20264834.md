## Problem

Unbiased language model watermarks are guaranteed to preserve the output distribution only in expectation over random keys, yet no prior work quantifies the distributional drift that accumulates when the same prompt is queried repeatedly under a fixed key. Additionally, robustness evaluations in the literature are fragmented and inconsistent, with paraphrasing-based attacks exhibiting high variance that leads to misleading comparisons across methods.

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes a language model (Llama-3.2-3B, Mistral-7B, or Phi-3.5-mini), a set of text prompts, a watermark key (secret key plus context key such as n-gram index or token position), and a watermarking method configuration (e.g., δ for KGW, α for DiPmark, list length for MCmark). It produces three normalized scores in [0,100]—unbiasedness (combining one-shot task metrics and SPMG multi-generation gaps), detectability (weighted TPR at FPR ∈ {5%, 1%, 0.1%}, median p-value, and AUROC), and robustness (weighted TPR under DIPPER paraphrasing and random token replacement at 10/20/30% edit budgets)—alongside the watermarked text itself for downstream inspection.

## Method

UWBENCH combines three theoretical contributions with a standardized empirical protocol. First, it proves an impossibility result (Theorem 4.1) showing that any detectable watermark scheme necessarily diverges from the original LM distribution under repeated queries of the same prompt with a fixed key, using Bhattacharyya coefficient decay under product measures. Second, it introduces the SPMG (Single-Prompt Multi-Generation) metric, which measures the mean absolute deviation of a bounded per-generation surrogate (e.g., perplexity) between the watermarked and original model across m generations per prompt, calibrated against an i.i.d. clone to factor out sampling noise, with McDiarmid concentration bounds providing finite-sample detection thresholds. Third, it formalizes robustness via token effect regions: a single edit at position i affects at most R_max downstream token scores, yielding a worst-case Lipschitz bound S(x)−S(x′) ≤ b·R_max·B that certifies detection survival under b-edit attacks. Empirically, the benchmark evaluates seven unbiased and two biased watermarking methods across three LLMs and five datasets, demonstrating that random token modification attacks produce roughly four times lower p-value variance than DIPPER paraphrasing, making them the preferred robustness probe.

## Results

Across three language models (Llama-3.2-3B, Mistral-7B, Phi-3.5-mini) and four datasets, SynthID achieves the highest detectability with 99.03% TPR@5%FPR, 97.29% TPR@1%FPR, and 94.66% TPR@0.1%FPR (AUROC 0.995), closely followed by MCmark(n=10) at 98.51%/97.09%/94.57% (AUROC 0.993). Among unbiased methods, ITS-Edit and EXP-Edit show the weakest detection (41.67% and 67.14% TPR@0.1%FPR respectively), while biased baselines KGW(δ=2.0) and Unigram(δ=2.0) reach 94.04% and 94.66% TPR@0.1%FPR. On robustness (TPR@1%FPR under 30% random token replacement), Unigram(δ=2.0) dominates at 95.63%, whereas unbiased methods like MCmark(n=10) drop to 39.26% and ITS-Edit to just 2.40%, revealing a fundamental detectability–robustness trade-off for distortion-free watermarks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among unbiased watermarks, SynthID and MCmark(n=10) clearly lead in detectability, achieving 94.66% and 94.57% TPR@0.1%FPR respectively, outperforming γ-reweight (66.43%), DiPmark (58–65%), STA-1 (59.4%), and EXP-Edit (67.14%) by 28–53 percentage points. ITS-Edit is the weakest unbiased method overall, scoring only 41.67% TPR@0.1%FPR and 0.804 AUROC, trailing even the biased KGW(δ=0.5) baseline. On robustness under 30% random token replacement (TPR@1%FPR), the biased Unigram(δ=2.0) achieves 95.63% and KGW(δ=2.0) reaches 62.11%, while the best unbiased method MCmark(n=10) drops to 39.26% and ITS-Edit to 2.40%, confirming that distortion-free watermarks sacrifice substantial robustness. In the SPMG unbiasedness evaluation (Table 2), MCmark(n=50) and STA-1 show the smallest distributional drift (ΔPerplexity ≈ 0.28 and 0.15), whereas EXP-Edit (ΔPerplexity = 2.00) and ITS-Edit (ΔPerplexity = 1.49) exhibit the largest repeated-prompt bias, consistent with the paper's impossibility theorem. The paper further demonstrates that DIPPER paraphrasing produces roughly 4× higher variance in p-values than random token replacement, making the latter a more reliable robustness metric for benchmarking.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a dedicated evaluation space for unbiased (distortion-free) watermarking, a sub-line of statistical watermarking that began with Aaronson's Gumbel-max construction and was developed through inverse-sampling (Christ et al., Kuditipudi et al.), permute-reweight (Hu et al.), and multi-bit extensions (MCmark, SynthID). It departs from existing watermarking benchmarks (WaterBench, MarkMyWords, MarkLLM) by (i) restricting scope to unbiased methods, (ii) introducing a multi-generation distributional-bias metric (SPMG) motivated by a new impossibility theorem, and (iii) replacing paraphrasing-based robustness evaluation with token-level modification attacks. The theoretical tools (Bhattacharyya-based separation amplification, McDiarmid concentration) are repurposed from standard probability theory but applied here for the first time to watermarking distributional guarantees.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The impossibility result (Theorem 4.1) formalizes a fundamental tension: any detectable unbiased watermark necessarily accumulates distributional drift under repeated queries of the same prompt, meaning the 'unbiased' label is only a one-shot guarantee. This reframes how practitioners should interpret unbiasedness claims and motivates the SPMG metric as a more honest evaluation. The finding that paraphrasing-based robustness evaluation (DIPPER) exhibits roughly 4× higher variance than random token replacement challenges the field's default adversarial protocol and suggests prior robustness comparisons may be unreliable. Open questions include whether the impossibility can be mitigated by key rotation or adaptive schemes, and whether the SPMG metric generalizes beyond the bounded-surrogate setting assumed in the concentration bound.
