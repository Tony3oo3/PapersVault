## Problem

Neural network cognitive models, including LLMs fine-tuned via supervised methods like Centaur-style SFT, achieve strong predictive accuracy on human behavioral data but offer no interpretable account of the cognitive mechanisms they capture. This paper asks whether reinforcement learning with outcome-based rewards can elicit LLMs to simultaneously produce accurate predictions of human risky choices and generate explicit chain-of-thought reasoning that verbalizes the underlying cognitive processes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261016/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a natural-language description of a two-option risky choice problem (e.g., 'Option A offers a 100% chance to win $27; Option B offers a 90% chance to win $25 and a 10% chance to win $92') and must produce a step-by-step reasoning trace followed by a JSON object with integer percentages for option_A and option_B that sum to 100. The RL model is allowed up to 1,024 tokens for its completion to accommodate elaborate reasoning, whereas SFT and Centaur-style baselines are restricted to 30 tokens since they are trained to emit the JSON prediction directly without intermediate reasoning.

## Method

The paper post-trains Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct via LoRA (rank 32, ~80.7M trainable parameters) using GRPO with an outcome-based reward: at each step the model generates 12 candidate completions (CoT + JSON prediction) per problem, and each is scored as 1 minus the absolute difference between its predicted probability for option B and the empirical human choice proportion, plus a format reward (up to 0.5) for well-structured JSON placed after reasoning tokens. A critical design choice is omitting the standard GRPO advantage normalization by group standard deviation, which the authors show prevents mode collapse to a degenerate 50%-50% prediction. The reward operates on the numerical prediction values rather than their tokenized forms, distinguishing it from SFT/Centaur-style training that relies on next-token prediction over the JSON string. The approach is validated against SFT and Centaur-style SFT on the choices13k dataset, with control experiments using synthetic expected-value-maximizer data and a weaker Gemma-2-2B backbone to test data adaptivity and model-capacity dependence.

## Results

On the held-out test set of choices13k (1,462 problems), all three post-training methods achieve comparable predictive accuracy: SFT reaches MSE = .0144 (SE = .0006), Centaur-style SFT reaches MSE = .0155 (SE = .0006), and RL (GRPO) reaches MSE = .0148 (SE = .0006), with no statistically significant differences among them (all p > .19). For reference, the best neural network model from Peterson et al. (2021) (Mixture of Theories) achieved MSE = .0113, while the neurally augmented prospect theory model achieved MSE = .0204. The RL model additionally generates chain-of-thought reasoning traces that human raters judged more reasonable than the backbone model's in 61.5% of trials (p < .05), and a CoT-swapping experiment confirms that RL-generated CoTs causally improve prediction accuracy (reducing backbone MSE from .0694 to .0212).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is Centaur-style SFT (Binz et al., 2025), which selectively masks all tokens except human choice proportions during training. On predictive accuracy (MSE), the proposed RL method ties with both Centaur-style SFT (.0148 vs. .0155) and standard SFT (.0148 vs. .0144), with no statistically significant differences (p = .43 and p = .56, respectively). The critical distinction is that RL uniquely produces interpretable chain-of-thought reasoning: a CoT-swapping experiment shows that feeding the RL model's CoT to the backbone LLM reduces its MSE from .0694 to .0212, while feeding SFT- or Centaur-generated CoTs to the backbone yields no improvement (MSE ≈ .073–.079, comparable to the backbone's original .0694). In the model control experiment with the weaker Gemma-2-2B-Instruct backbone, RL underperforms both SFT (MSE .0526 vs. .0162) and Centaur-style SFT (.0526 vs. .0163), indicating that the RL approach's advantage in generating useful CoTs depends on backbone model capacity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Centaur model (Binz et al., 2025), which uses supervised fine-tuning to adapt LLMs for predicting human behavior, by replacing SFT with reinforcement learning (GRPO) and outcome-based rewards to elicit interpretable chain-of-thought reasoning alongside predictions. The RL training algorithm builds directly on GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) and its 'Done Right' variant (Liu et al., 2025), with a key modification of omitting advantage normalization to prevent mode collapse. Conceptually, the paper departs from the prediction-only framing of prior LLM cognitive models by treating the generated CoT as a verbalized cognitive theory, positioning the work at the intersection of RL post-training and computational cognitive science.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates a practical path toward cognitive models that are simultaneously predictive and interpretable, with control experiments showing that elicited CoTs adapt to the generative structure of training data (e.g., shifting from risk-aversion to expected-value reasoning when data are synthetic). A key limitation is the strong dependence on backbone LLM capacity: the weaker Gemma-2B model fails to elicit the critical expected-value mechanism under RL, while SFT still works, suggesting RL and SFT have fundamentally different generalization profiles. Open questions include whether RL can discover genuinely novel theories beyond what is latent in the pretrained model (the elicitation hypothesis), how to effectively combine RL and SFT, and whether the approach generalizes beyond risky choice to other cognitive domains.
