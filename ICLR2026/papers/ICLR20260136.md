## Problem

Prior evidence of VLM bias relied on adversarial Y/N questions that embed biased statements directly in the prompt, making it impossible to disentangle whether failures stem from the image content or the text framing. This paper asks whether VLMs' memorized prior knowledge about well-known subjects (e.g., 'Adidas has 3 stripes,' 'chickens have 2 legs') causes them to fail on neutral, objective visual counting and identification tasks when presented with counterfactual images whose ground truth contradicts common knowledge.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260136/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each test image is a counterfactual modification of a well-known subject (e.g., a 4-striped Adidas logo, a 5-legged puma) presented at three resolutions (384, 768, 1152 px on the longest side), paired with a neutral, descriptive text prompt such as 'How many legs does this animal have?' The VLM outputs a number in curly brackets for counting questions or Yes/No for identification questions. Beyond raw accuracy, the paper reports a 'bias rate' — the proportion of responses matching the memorized prior (e.g., answering '3' for the 4-stripe logo) — to quantify reliance on prior knowledge versus actual visual analysis.

## Method

VLMBias is a semi-automated benchmark spanning 7 domains of decreasing popularity (animals, logos, flags, chess pieces, game boards, optical illusions, patterned grids) with 1,392 counterfactual images. Photo-realistic images (animals, logos) are generated and modified using SOTA text-to-image models (Gemini-2.0 Flash, GPT-4o), while abstract images (flags, chess, game boards, illusions, grids) are created programmatically via Python scripts and SVG code; all images undergo human quality review. The key design choice is using neutral prompts with biased images rather than biased prompts with normal images, isolating visual bias from textual adversarial framing. To pinpoint the source of bias, the authors conduct linear probing on SigLIP vision encoder features from LLaVA-OneVision-S, showing that visual information is sufficiently encoded (95.26% accuracy before projection) but gets overridden by the language model's memorized priors (full VLM drops to 49.71%). Additional ablations include background removal via LangSAM segmentation or SVG manipulation, in-image text injection of subject names, and analysis of reasoning-token length versus accuracy for thinking models.

## Results

On the VLMBias benchmark, all five SOTA VLMs achieve only 17.05% mean accuracy on counterfactual counting images, compared to 100% on unmodified originals. The best-performing model, o4-mini, reaches just 20.25% task-mean accuracy, while GPT-4.1 scores the lowest at 13.88%. Critically, 75.70% of the time VLMs output the pre-defined biased answer (e.g., '3 stripes' for a 4-striped Adidas logo), confirming they default to memorized prior knowledge rather than visual analysis. Removing image backgrounds nearly doubles accuracy (+21.09 points to 38.14%) and reduces bias rate by 40.58 points, revealing that background cues strongly trigger biased responses.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All five SOTA VLMs (Gemini-2.5 Pro, Sonnet-3.7, GPT-4.1, o3, o4-mini) fail similarly on VLMBias, with task-mean accuracy ranging from 13.88% to 20.25%—no model exceeds 21%. Thinking models (o3: 18.50%, o4-mini: 20.25%) marginally outperform non-thinking models (GPT-4.1: 13.88%, Sonnet-3.7: 16.59%), but the gap is small. Newer models show mixed results: GPT-5 improves to 30.72% (largely driven by 84.72% on chess), while Grok-4 underperforms at 17.71%. Open-source VLMs exhibit inverse scaling—Qwen2.5-VL-72B (13.87%) scores lower than Qwen2.5-VL-7B (16.62%), and Pixtral-Large (13.60%) underperforms Pixtral-12B (12.15%) only slightly. Small counting-specific VLMs (Moondream-2B: 33.27%, Molmo-72B) substantially outperform SOTA generalist VLMs, with 2.1× lower bias rates. Compared to prior VLM bias benchmarks (PhD, VLind-Bench, ViLP, HallusionBench), VLMBias uniquely uses neutral prompts with counterfactual images rather than adversarial text injection, and its top leaderboard score (o4-mini: 20.25%) is dramatically lower than prior benchmarks (GPT-4o: 81–89%), confirming the severity of the visual bias failure mode.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the VLM bias literature (PhD-ccs, VLind-Bench, HallusionBench, ViLP) by replacing adversarial Y/N prompts with neutral, objective counting questions on counterfactual images, isolating visual bias from text-prompt bias. It builds on Rahmanzadehgervi et al. (2024) for the linear-probing methodology that dissects where in the VLM pipeline the failure occurs, and on Makowski et al. (2021) for parametric optical illusion generation. The key departure is the systematic, semi-automated construction of 1,392 counterfactual images across 7 domains of decreasing popularity, enabling controlled comparison of how prior-knowledge strength modulates bias.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The linear-probing result (95.26% vision-encoder accuracy vs. 49.71% full-VLM accuracy on 5-leg vs. 4-leg animals) provides a clean mechanistic explanation: the visual information is present but the language model actively overrides it with memorized priors. This reframes the problem from 'VLMs can't see' to 'VLMs see but choose not to trust what they see,' with practical implications for architecture design and prompting. Open questions include whether the bias is fundamental to the LLM backbone or addressable through training, whether the inverse-scaling trend (larger models more biased) persists as models grow, and whether the overthinking-then-decline pattern in reasoning tokens can be mitigated. The benchmark is a practical tool for tracking progress on this failure mode as new models are released.
