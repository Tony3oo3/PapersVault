## Problem

Existing causal discovery methods for multivariate Hawkes processes assume causal sufficiency, i.e., all subprocesses are fully observed. In practice, many real-world systems (neuroscience, finance, social networks) are only partially observable, with unmeasured subprocesses acting as latent confounders that create spurious causal edges among observed ones. No prior principled framework can identify the existence, number, and causal connections of such latent subprocesses without specifying them in advance.

## Input / Output

The method takes continuous-time event sequences (or pre-binned event counts) from a multivariate Hawkes process as input, with a bin width Δ chosen small relative to the excitation function's effective support. It produces a summary causal graph over both observed and latent subprocesses, including directed edges, self-loops, and cycles, with latent confounder nodes explicitly identified. The only data requirement is second-order statistics (cross-covariance matrices) of the discretized variables; no likelihood fitting or parametric kernel specification is needed beyond the separable excitation assumption φ_ij(s) = a_ij·w(s).

## Method

The core insight is that as the bin width Δ→0, a stationary multivariate Hawkes process converges to a linear autoregressive model (via multivariate INAR convergence, Theorem 4.1), yielding a discrete-time window causal graph that is a DAG even when the summary graph contains cycles and self-loops. This DAG structure enables d-separation-based rank tests on cross-covariance matrices of observed discretized variables (Lemma 4.2). Proposition 4.3 identifies observed parent-cause sets via minimal rank conditions, while Proposition 4.5 detects latent confounders by a characteristic rank deficiency (rank = 2m+1 rather than 2m) arising from the separable excitation kernel aligning latent contributions across lags. Theorems 4.7 and 4.8 extend identification to arbitrary causal relations involving latent subprocesses by using observed surrogates in place of unobserved nodes. A two-phase iterative algorithm (Algorithm 1) alternates between Phase I (identifying causal relations among known subprocesses via rank tests) and Phase II (discovering new latent confounders by exhaustively checking pairs), requiring no prior knowledge of latent component count.

## Results

The paper evaluates causal graph recovery using average F1-scores over ten runs on six synthetic graph families (one fully observed, five with latent confounders) and a real-world cellular network dataset (~35k events, 18 alarm types). The proposed method consistently outperforms all five baselines across every synthetic case, with the gap widening for latent-confounder structures that require larger sample sizes due to attenuation of causal influence along latent paths. On the real-world cellular network subgraph (five alarm types, one treated as latent), the method successfully identifies the latent subprocess (Alarm_id=7) and its primary causal influences, quantitatively outperforming representative baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The method outperforms all five baselines on F1-score across all synthetic and real-world cases. Among Hawkes-specific baselines, SHP (Qiao et al., 2023) and THP (Cai et al., 2022) are likelihood-based methods for binned event counts, while NPHC (Achab et al., 2018) is a cumulant-based method for continuous-time data; all three assume full observability and cannot identify latent subprocesses without prior knowledge of their existence or number. Among causal-discovery baselines, Hier. Rank (Huang et al., 2022) and RLCD (Dong et al., 2023) are rank-based methods designed for i.i.d. linear latent models that rely on hierarchical structure and cardinality constraints (|children| > |parents|) incompatible with the dense autoregressive structure of discretized Hawkes processes. LPCMCI (Gerhardus & Runge, 2020) handles time series with exogenous latent confounders but assumes weak autocorrelation, a condition violated by the dense cross-lag dependencies inherent in Hawkes dynamics. The proposed method's advantage is most pronounced on latent-confounder cases (Figs. 2a, 3a–3d), where all baselines fail to recover the hidden subprocesses, while the rank-based approach with time-aware constraints correctly identifies both the latent nodes and their causal edges.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate literatures: Hawkes process structure learning (which has relied on likelihood-based fitting under causal sufficiency, e.g., Xu et al. 2016, Qiao et al. 2023, Cai et al. 2022) and rank-based causal discovery with latent variables (Huang et al. 2022, Dong et al. 2023, which assume i.i.d. data and restrictive cardinality conditions). The key conceptual move is Theorem 4.1, which shows that a stationary multivariate Hawkes process converges to a linear autoregressive model as the bin width shrinks (via INAR convergence), thereby making second-order rank statistics applicable to Hawkes dynamics. The paper departs from prior rank-based methods by introducing a time-aware 'symmetric path situation' (Definition 4.4) and observed-surrogate machinery that handle endogenous latent confounders and dense cross-lag dependencies inherent to Hawkes processes—assumptions that are explicitly violated by i.i.d. rank-based approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first principled framework that identifies latent subprocesses in Hawkes processes without prior knowledge of their existence or number, which is critical for domains like neuroscience and finance where partial observability is the norm. However, identifiability relies on a separable excitation function (Assumption 1: φ_ij(s) = a_ij·w(s)) and the symmetric path condition, both of which limit generality; relaxing these (e.g., node-specific decay rates) is identified as future work. The two-phase iterative algorithm's computational complexity grows with graph density and the number of latent components, and empirical validation is limited to small synthetic graphs and a single real-world cellular network subgraph, leaving open questions about scalability to larger, sparser real systems.
