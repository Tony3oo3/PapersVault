## Problem

A long-standing hypothesis suggests that flat minima in the loss landscape confer adversarial robustness, but this connection has remained informal and incomplete, with no rigorous formalization linking parameter-space curvature to input-space perturbation sensitivity. This paper resolves the disconnect by showing the intuition is only partially correct: flatness guarantees local but not global adversarial robustness, and adversarial examples can settle in flat, high-confidence regions where the model is confidently wrong.

## Input / Output

The method operates on trained CNN classifiers (ResNet-18, WideResNet-28-4, DenseNet-121, VGG-11) applied to 32×32 RGB images from CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100, using cross-entropy loss. Rather than requiring retraining, the analysis consumes the penultimate-layer weight matrix and feature representations of a converged model, producing a closed-form relative sharpness value, a certified perturbation radius δ as a function of loss tolerance ε, and a geometric decomposition of the loss landscape into confidence-driven basins. The adversarial examples are defined via a loss-change criterion (Definition 4) rather than the standard prediction-flip criterion, enabling continuous analysis of how perturbations propagate through the loss surface.

## Method

The paper derives a closed-form Hessian for cross-entropy loss at the penultimate layer, H(w) = (diag(ŷ) − ŷŷᵀ) ⊗ φφᵀ, yielding the relative sharpness κ_Tr(w) = ‖w‖₂ Σⱼ ŷⱼ(1−ŷⱼ) Σᵢ φᵢ², which decomposes into confidence, feature scale, and weight magnitude components. It then shows that for ReLU networks all curvature originates at the output layer and propagates backward through affine maps, justifying analysis at the penultimate layer alone. By representing input perturbations as weight perturbations via a Lipschitz feature extractor (Lemma 7) and applying a second-order Taylor expansion, the paper derives a certified robustness radius δ ∝ ε^{1/3}/(L·κ_Tr(w))^{1/3} that saturates as sharpness approaches zero. Empirically, the authors scale penultimate-layer weights post-hoc to control sharpness without retraining, revealing that flatter networks form wider loss basins around clean inputs but that adversarial attacks traverse sharp decision boundaries before descending into the 'Uncanny Valley'—vast flat regions of high-confidence misclassification where standard flatness measures are uninformative about correctness.

## Results

On ResNet-18/CIFAR-10, the standard model achieves 90.33% robust test accuracy under PGD-ℓ2 (25 steps, ε=0.025). Scaling the penultimate layer by a factor s systematically reduces loss increase under adversarial perturbations: at s=50, loss increases approach zero over the full attack trajectory, confirming the theoretical bound δ ∝ ε^{1/3}/κ_Tr(w)^{1/3}. For the adversarially trained model (PGD-ℓ∞, ε=8/255), the basin width is approximately 20× larger than the standard model, yet local curvature (Tr(H)) remains comparable (0.6 clean vs. 1.0 adversarial), with the difference explained by lower model confidence. The Uncanny Valley phenomenon is observed: along PGD-ℓ∞ trajectories, relative sharpness peaks at the decision boundary then drops to near-zero while loss continues to increase, revealing that adversarial examples settle in flat, high-confidence misclassification regions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external baselines; instead, it validates theoretical predictions through controlled scaling experiments on the same architecture. The key comparison is between standard and adversarially trained ResNet-18: the adversarially trained model exhibits ~20× larger basin widths under a stronger attack (PGD-ℓ2, ε=0.5 vs. ε=0.025), confirming that adversarial training widens the certified robustness radius. Within the scaling experiments, larger scaling factors (flatter networks) consistently produce smaller loss increases and wider basins, with basin width strongly correlating with sharpness measured at the clean input (Figure 2d). The paper also contrasts the standard model's behavior with the adversarially trained model's: while both show the same qualitative scaling pattern, the adversarially trained model's lower confidence (visible in the κ_Tr formula's ŷ_j(1−ŷ_j) term) accounts for its larger effective basin despite similar Hessian trace values. The central empirical finding—that flatness is locally protective but globally insufficient—is demonstrated by showing that even at s=50, a sharp 'take-off' point exists beyond which loss increases rapidly, meaning no finite scaling eliminates adversarial examples entirely.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the relative flatness framework of Petzka et al. (2021) from generalization guarantees to adversarial robustness, deriving a closed-form Hessian expression for cross-entropy loss at the penultimate layer and using it to bound loss variation under input perturbations. It builds on the broader flatness-for-robustness line (Stutz et al. 2021, Wu et al. 2020, Zhao et al. 2020) but departs from the informal intuition by rigorously showing that flatness yields only local, pointwise robustness guarantees rather than global ones. The loss-based reformulation of adversarial examples (Definition 4) is a methodological departure from the standard prediction-flip criterion, enabling direct geometric analysis of the loss landscape.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper formalizes a long-suspected but previously informal connection between parameter-space flatness and input-space adversarial robustness, and in doing so exposes a critical limitation: flatness guarantees are inherently local and cannot prevent adversarial examples that traverse the loss landscape globally. The 'Uncanny Valley' finding—that adversarial examples settle in flat, high-confidence regions where the model is confidently wrong—challenges the implicit assumption that flatness indicates correctness and suggests that current flatness-optimization methods (SAM, SWA) may be insufficient for adversarial robustness. A key open question is how to develop flatness measures that disentangle feature-extractor geometry from classifier confidence, which the paper identifies as the core confound in Tr(H)-based metrics.
