## Problem

Current LLM safety safeguards (e.g., Llama Guard) treat safety as a binary block/allow decision, completely rejecting any response containing even minor risky content. This blunt approach exacerbates overrefusal on benign but sensitive prompts and provides no nuanced guidance for dual-use queries, failing to simultaneously reduce unsafe responses and overrefusals. The paper asks whether safety alignment can be reframed as a collaborative, positive-sum process where a dedicated feedback agent improves responses rather than discarding them.

## Input / Output

The system takes a single user prompt as input. The conversation agent produces a natural-language response, while the feedback agent outputs a structured JSON containing a reasoning trace, two binary labels (unsafe, overrefuse), and a free-text feedback string. Only the feedback string is passed back to the conversation agent for revision; the reasoning and labels remain private to the feedback agent. At inference, the feedback agent engages adaptively—triggering only when it predicts the response is unsafe or overrefusing—keeping latency low on safe queries (6.7% trigger rate on AlpacaEval). Experiments use T_max=1 feedback round, yielding at most two conversation agent responses per prompt.

## Method

WALTZRL formulates safety alignment as a positive-sum multi-agent game and jointly trains a conversation agent and a feedback agent via multi-agent reinforcement learning. The key mechanism is the Dynamic Improvement Reward (DIR), which rewards the feedback agent by the difference in the conversation agent's reward before and after incorporating its feedback, causing the reward signal to evolve as the conversation agent policy updates. Training follows a two-stage recipe: Stage 1 freezes the conversation agent so the feedback agent learns correct format and label prediction; Stage 2 enables collaborative co-evolution with both agents updated each step using a REINFORCE++-based clipped surrogate objective with KL regularization. The conversation agent is trained on a mixture of initial-response trajectories (learning to produce safe responses directly) and post-feedback trajectories (learning to incorporate useful revisions). At inference, both agents are deployed jointly, so an adversarial attack must jailbreak both to succeed, and the feedback agent's adaptive stopping condition preserves helpfulness and latency on safe prompts.

## Results

WALTZRL achieves an average Attack Success Rate (ASR) of 3.7% and average Over-Refusal Rate (ORR) of 7.6% across five safety/overrefusal datasets, substantially outperforming the Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct baseline (26.5% ASR, 25.7% ORR). On WildJailbreak, unsafe responses drop from 39.0% to 4.6%, and on OR-Bench-Hard-1K, overrefusals drop from 45.3% to 9.9%. On general capability benchmarks (AlpacaEval, IF-Eval, GPQA, MMLU, TruthfulQA), WALTZRL shows little to no degradation despite using no helpfulness data during RL training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to the Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct baseline, WALTZRL reduces average ASR from 26.5% to 3.7% (a 7.2× reduction) and average ORR from 25.7% to 7.6% (a 3.4× reduction), winning on both safety and helpfulness metrics simultaneously. Against the Llama Guard 4 safeguard applied on top of the baseline, WALTZRL achieves lower ASR (3.7% vs. 9.0%) while also achieving lower ORR (7.6% vs. 29.8%), demonstrating that the safeguard actually worsens overrefusal by 4.1 percentage points on the baseline. Even against the stronger single-model RL + safeguard combination (5.3% ASR, 14.9% ORR), WALTZRL wins on both metrics (3.7% ASR, 7.6% ORR), showing that the multi-agent collaborative approach outperforms the best single-model defense. The oracle label-converted feedback baseline (which has access to ground-truth safety labels) still underperforms WALTZRL on ORR (16.6% vs. 7.6%), confirming that rich, detailed feedback is crucial for converting overrefusals into helpful responses.

## Relation to Prior Work

WALTZRL extends the REINFORCE++ algorithm to a two-agent collaborative setting and departs from prior multi-agent safety work (Liu et al. 2025, Zheng et al. 2024) that frames safety as a zero-sum attacker-defender game and deploys only the defender at inference. It also contrasts with standalone safeguard models (Llama Guard, Constitutional Classifiers) that binary-reject unsafe content, instead training a feedback agent to produce constructive, contextual suggestions that the conversation agent can incorporate. The positive-sum game formulation and the Dynamic Improvement Reward—which conditions the feedback agent's reward on the conversation agent's actual improvement—distinguish WALTZRL from both debate-based safety approaches (Irving et al. 2018) and deliberative alignment (Guan et al. 2025), which operate within a single model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that the helpfulness-harmlessness trade-off can be improved simultaneously by replacing blunt rejection with collaborative revision, and provides the empirical finding that safeguards exacerbate overrefusal more severely when the base model already has low overrefusal—a practical warning for deployment. The oracle baseline underperforming WALTZRL shows that detailed, contextual feedback is necessary to convert overrefusals into helpful responses, not merely knowing the correct label. Key limitations include evaluation on a single 8B model, English-only datasets, a single feedback round, and static (non-adaptive) attacks, leaving open whether the approach generalizes to larger models, multilingual settings, and adaptive jailbreaks.
