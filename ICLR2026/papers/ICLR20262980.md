## Problem

Existing protein-ligand docking benchmarks predominantly evaluate self-docking (reproducing known co-crystal poses), which is impractical for real drug discovery where novel ligands are docked into non-native protein conformations. Prior benchmarks also suffer from limited method coverage (1–7 methods), heavy evaluation frameworks requiring training, and lack of physics-based post-processing. PoseX addresses these gaps by introducing a cross-docking benchmark covering 23 methods across three categories, a curated dataset free of training-data overlap, and a well-designed relaxation protocol.

## Input / Output

The 23 evaluated methods consume inputs differently by category: physics-based methods require a protein 3D structure, ligand 3D structure, and explicit binding pocket; AI docking methods take protein 3D structure plus ligand SMILES (with optional pocket specification); AI co-folding methods accept only protein amino acid sequence and ligand SMILES. The PoseX dataset constrains ligands to 100–900 Da with ≤2000-residue proteins, drawn exclusively from PDB entries released between January 2022 and January 2025 to avoid training-data overlap with all evaluated models. Outputs are assessed on three axes: geometric accuracy (RMSD < 2 Å), physicochemical validity (PoseBuster's 20-metric PB-Valid suite covering stereochemistry, intra- and intermolecular clashes), and interaction fingerprint recovery (PLIF-Valid).

## Method

PoseX curates two evaluation subsets—PoseX-SD (718 entries) for self-docking and PoseX-CD (1,312 entries) for cross-docking—through a 20-step filtering pipeline over RCSB PDB structures enforcing resolution ≤2 Å, ligand quality metrics (real-space R-factor ≤0.2, correlation ≥0.95), and sequence/structure clustering to prevent redundancy. The benchmark evaluates 23 methods spanning five physics-based tools (Glide, Discovery Studio, MOE, AutoDock Vina, GNINA), eleven AI docking models (SurfDock, UMD V2, DiffDock, Interformer, etc.), and seven AI co-folding models (AlphaFold3, Boltz-1x, Chai-1, Protenix, etc.), reporting results both with and without a novel relaxation module. The relaxation module, built on OpenMM 7.7, applies PDBFixer for structural repair, Amber ff14SB for protein parameterization, OpenFF 2.1.0 for ligand parameterization, and constrained Langevin MD (300 K, 0.004 ps timestep) with backbone restraints to resolve steric clashes while preserving predicted poses. A public real-time leaderboard (dock-lab.tech) enables community submission and ranking. Key findings show AI methods consistently outperform physics-based methods in cross-docking, relaxation resolves most clashes but not chirality errors, and explicit pocket specification substantially boosts performance particularly for co-folding methods.

## Results

On the PoseX-CD benchmark (the more realistic cross-docking scenario), SurfDock achieves the highest success rate of 77.0% (RMSD < 2Å & PB-Valid with relaxation), followed by UMD V2 (69.2%) and AlphaFold3 (68.8%). On PoseX-SD, SurfDock leads at 78.0%, while on the Astex benchmark UMD V2 and SurfDock both reach ~94%. Across all three benchmarks, AI-based methods (both docking and co-folding) consistently outperform the best physics-based method (GNINA at 54.1% on PoseX-CD), with 9 AI approaches surpassing GNINA in cross-docking versus only 3 in self-docking, highlighting a widening AI advantage under conformational variability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SurfDock (Cao et al., 2024) and UMD V2 (Alcaide et al., 2024) dominate across all three benchmarks, with SurfDock achieving 77.0–78.0% success on PoseX-CD/SD and UMD V2 reaching 94.1% on Astex, both surpassing the best physics-based method (GNINA at 54.1% on PoseX-CD) by over 20 percentage points. Among AI co-folding methods, AlphaFold3 (Abramson et al., 2024) leads in blind-docking (68.8% on PoseX-CD) but is outperformed by SurfDock and UMD V2 in pocket-given settings; Boltz-1x (Wohlwend et al., 2024) achieves the highest PB-Valid pass rate among all AI methods (61.2% on PoseX-CD) due to its physics-inspired inference-time steering, while other co-folding methods (Chai-1, Protenix, AlphaFold3) suffer persistent chirality errors that relaxation cannot fix. In cross-docking specifically, the gap between AI and physics-based methods widens dramatically: 9 AI approaches beat GNINA versus only 3 in self-docking, and physics-based methods (Glide 38.4%, MOE 33.3%, AutoDock Vina 28.1%) degrade far more than AI methods under conformational shift. Pocket specification provides substantial gains (DiffDock-Pocket 58.5% vs. DiffDock 47.2% on PoseX-CD), and the relaxation post-processing step improves PB-Valid by 10–30 percentage points for most AI docking methods, though it cannot correct stereochemical (chirality) errors.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a comprehensive successor to existing protein-ligand docking benchmarks (PoseBuster, PoseBench, PLINDER), which it criticizes for focusing exclusively on self-docking, limited method coverage (1–7 methods), and lack of cross-docking evaluation. Rather than proposing a new docking algorithm, it carves new ground by introducing cross-docking as a more realistic evaluation scenario, curating a novel dataset (PoseX) from post-2022 PDB entries to avoid training-data leakage, and assembling 23 methods spanning physics-based, AI docking, and AI co-folding categories. The relaxation post-processing module draws on established molecular dynamics tools (OpenMM, OpenFF) but is specifically designed as a bridge between AI-generated poses and physical validity, a combination not systematically explored in prior benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark fills a critical gap by evaluating methods under cross-docking, which mirrors real drug-discovery workflows where novel ligands are docked into known protein structures. Key open questions remain: whether AI co-folding methods can resolve their persistent chirality errors without physics-inspired steering (as in Boltz-1x), whether pocket-conditioned generation can reduce the strong dependence on pocket similarity revealed by the generalizability analysis, and how these methods perform on binding affinity prediction and multi-ligand systems. The public leaderboard and open-source pipeline lower the barrier for fair, reproducible comparison, which the authors argue prior benchmarks lacked.
