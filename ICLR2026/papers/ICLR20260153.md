## Problem

Fine-grained sparse computation (e.g., Mixture-of-Experts) promises higher parametric capacity at roughly constant per-token FLOPs, but suffers from training instability due to non-uniform expert routing, load-balancing objectives that interfere with the primary loss, and growing all-to-all communication overhead as expert counts increase. Existing token-indexed alternatives like Hash Layers eliminate routing but lack context adaptivity and can degrade quality. The paper asks whether a static, token-indexed mechanism can achieve the capacity benefits of fine-grained sparsity while remaining stable to train and simple to deploy.

## Input / Output

The model takes a standard token sequence and, at each STEM layer, additionally uses the raw token ID to index a row from a per-layer embedding table of shape V × d_ff (vocabulary size by FFN hidden width). The embedding table is decoupled from the matmul weights and can be offloaded to CPU memory, with rows asynchronously prefetched to GPU based on the token IDs in the current batch. Output is the standard next-token probability distribution; the key operational difference is that parameter traffic scales with the number of unique tokens in the batch rather than with batch size, enabling predictable and deduplicable memory access.

## Method

STEM replaces the dense up-projection matrix W^u in the SwiGLU FFN with a token-indexed embedding lookup U_ℓ[t] from a per-layer table, while keeping the gate projection W^g and down-projection W^d dense and shared across all tokens. This static, token-indexed design eliminates runtime routing entirely, enabling CPU offload of the embedding tables with asynchronous prefetch and removing cross-device all-to-all communication. The paper shows that replacing the up-projection (rather than the gate) is critical: the gate must remain input-dependent to provide context-aware modulation, whereas the up-projection's role as a learned affine address can be substituted by a direct token-indexed vector. The learned STEM embeddings exhibit large angular spread (near-zero pairwise cosine similarity), which reduces representational interference and increases effective knowledge-storage capacity. A hybrid variant STEM† retains the up-projection and additively adds the token embedding, but does not improve over the pure replacement design.

## Results

At 350M scale, STEM (1/3 FFN replacement) achieves 50.90 average downstream accuracy with 0.70 GFLOPs per token, outperforming the dense baseline (49.72 avg, 0.74 GFLOPs) and Hash-MoE (50.58 avg, 0.74 GFLOPs), yielding a 1.08× training ROI. At 1B scale, STEM reaches 56.63 average accuracy with 2.83 GFLOPs versus the dense baseline's 55.82 and 3.00 GFLOPs. After mid-training, the 1B STEM model further improves on reasoning benchmarks: GSM8K 46.4 vs. 44.2 and MMLU 32.38 vs. 29.92 over the dense baseline. On long-context Needle-in-a-Haystack, the STEM advantage grows from 8.4% at 8k to 13% at 32k context length.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the dense baseline (MobileLLM-350M / Llama-3.2-1B), STEM consistently wins on average downstream accuracy at both scales (50.90 vs. 49.72 at 350M; 56.63 vs. 55.82 at 1B) while using strictly fewer per-token FLOPs (0.70 vs. 0.74 GFLOPs; 2.83 vs. 3.00 GFLOPs). The largest individual gains appear on knowledge-intensive tasks: ARC-Easy improves by +5.35 points at 350M and OpenBookQA by +6.06 points at 1B. Against Hash-Layer MoE (matched total parameter count, top-1/16 routing), STEM achieves higher average accuracy (50.90 vs. 50.58) with lower FLOPs (0.70 vs. 0.74) and, critically, exhibits no training loss spikes that plague the MoE variant. After mid-training at 1B, STEM extends its lead to reasoning benchmarks: GSM8K 46.4 vs. 44.2 (+2.2) and MMLU 32.38 vs. 29.92 (+2.46), confirming that the embedding-module design benefits transfer beyond pretraining. On long-context evaluation (Needle-in-a-Haystack and LongBench), STEM's advantage widens with sequence length, reaching a 13-point NIAH gap at 32k tokens and consistent 2–8 point LongBench gains across all context-length bins.

## Relation to Prior Work

STEM extends the token-indexed sparsity paradigm introduced by Hash Layers (Roller et al., 2021) and the Per Layer Embeddings in Gemma-3n (Google DeepMind, 2024), but departs from both by surgically replacing only the FFN up-projection with a layer-local embedding table rather than routing entire expert FFNs or merely modulating FFN outputs. This positions STEM as a middle ground between dense transformers and fine-grained MoE: it inherits the static, routing-free compute path of hash-based selection while avoiding the expressivity loss of full-module token-indexed routing. The design also draws on the key-value memory interpretation of FFNs (Geva et al., 2021) to motivate why the up-projection is the right component to sparsify, and contrasts with PKM-style parametric memory by using a much simpler direct-index lookup instead of product-key retrieval.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

STEM offers a practically attractive path to scaling parametric memory without the training instability, auxiliary loss tuning, and all-to-all communication overhead that plague fine-grained MoE, making it a candidate for on-device and memory-constrained deployment where CPU offloading is natural. The emergent long-context capacity scaling (more distinct parameters activated as sequence length grows) and the interpretability of token-indexed knowledge editing are notable secondary benefits. However, all experiments are limited to 350M and 1B parameter scales with at most one-third of FFN layers replaced; whether the angular-spread property and stability advantages persist at 7B+ scale, and whether the approach composes well with other architectural innovations (e.g., multi-head attention variants, different activation functions), remain open questions that will determine its broader impact.
