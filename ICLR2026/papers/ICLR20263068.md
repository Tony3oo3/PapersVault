## Problem

LLM leaderboards built on Bradley-Terry models are widely treated as definitive rankings, but it is unknown whether they are stable under the removal of even a tiny worst-case fraction of existing preference data. Unlike prior work on adversarial vote injection, this paper asks a data-analysis robustness question: can dropping a very small subset of already-collected human or LLM-judge preferences flip the top-ranked model, and if so, which specific preferences are responsible?

## Input / Output

The method takes a pairwise preference dataset (each record specifying two competing models and a win/loss/tie label), a target rank k, and a drop fraction α, and produces a binary robustness verdict plus, when non-robustness is found, the specific model pair whose rankings flipped, their BT-score differences before and after removal, and the exact indices of the dropped preferences. The procedure operates on the full-data BT fit and its influence-function derivatives, requiring no retraining during the search phase; a single exact refit is performed only to verify the candidate subset, guaranteeing no false positives.

## Method

The core reduction (Proposition B.1) shows that top-k robustness is equivalent to pairwise robustness for all (i,j) pairs with i inside and j outside the top-k set, enabling a greedy search ordered by increasing BT-score gap. For each candidate pair, the paper applies the Approximate Maximum Influence Perturbation (AMIP) framework: it reformulates the weighted Bradley-Terry model as a logistic regression with a structured design matrix, computes per-datapoint influence scores via the Hessian-inverse gradient formula, and selects the ⌊αN⌋ data points whose joint removal most decreases the score difference θ̂ᵢ − θ̂ⱼ. The AMIP approximation identifies a candidate drop set in O(N log(αN) + NP² + P³) time, after which an exact BT refit on the reduced dataset confirms or rejects the predicted ranking flip. This two-stage design (fast approximation for search, exact refit for verification) makes the check tractable on ~50k-evaluation arenas in under three minutes on a laptop, while the exact verification step ensures every reported non-robustness result is a definitive witness.

## Results

The headline finding is that dropping just 2 out of 57,477 human preferences (0.00348%) on Chatbot Arena is sufficient to flip the top-ranked model from GPT-4-0125-preview to GPT-4-1106-preview. Across all LLM arenas tested, the fraction needed to change the top-1 ranking ranges from 0.003% (Chatbot Arena, human) to 2.74% (MT-bench, human), with MT-bench being the sole arena robust at the 1% level. The method runs in under 3 minutes on a personal computer for a 50,000-evaluation dataset, and all identified non-robustness claims are verified by exact refitting (9/9 surfaced subsets confirmed to flip rankings).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against competing robustness-auditing methods; instead, it benchmarks the sensitivity of different evaluation platforms against each other. Among LLM arenas, MT-bench is markedly more robust (requiring 2.74% of data to be dropped for human judges) than all other platforms, which the authors attribute to its use of expert annotators and carefully constructed multi-turn prompts. For human vs. LLM-as-a-judge comparisons, neither is systematically more sensitive: on Chatbot Arena, human annotations are slightly more sensitive for k ∈ {1,5,10,20} while LLM annotations are slightly more sensitive for k=3; on MT-bench, LLM annotations are more sensitive for all k. A key contrast is between worst-case and uniform data-dropping: dropping 1% of preferences uniformly at random leaves the top-ranked model unchanged in 77–100% of 100 trials across all arenas, whereas the worst-case subset requires only 0.003% on Chatbot Arena, demonstrating that the fragility is driven by a tiny set of specific influential preferences rather than general noise.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Approximate Maximum Influence Perturbation (AMIP) framework from Broderick et al. (2020) and the broader data-dropping robustness literature (Kuschnig et al., 2021; Moitra & Rohatgi, 2023; Freund & Hopkins, 2023; Shiffman et al., 2023) to Bradley-Terry-based LLM ranking systems. It departs from the adversarial-attack literature on leaderboards (Min et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025b) by targeting a fundamentally different failure mode: non-robustness at the data-analysis stage that requires no adversarial intent, only the removal of a worst-case tiny subset of already-collected preferences. The closest prior work is Shiffman et al. (2023), which applied AMIP to p-value rankings in gene set enrichment; here the authors reformulate the weighted BT model as a logistic regression to make influence-function computation tractable and scalable to arenas with tens of thousands of matchups.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that removing just two preferences (0.003%) from Chatbot Arena's 57,477 evaluations can swap the top-ranked model is a stark warning that widely-cited LLM leaderboard positions may rest on a handful of idiosyncratic human judgments rather than robust signal. The method is computationally cheap (under 3 minutes on a laptop for ~50k evaluations) and produces exact, verifiable witnesses of fragility, making it practical for arena operators to audit before publishing results. Key limitations include the possibility of false negatives due to masking effects (where jointly influential points appear individually uninformative), and the fact that the method only certifies non-robustness when a flip is found—it cannot certify robustness. Open questions include whether step-wise greedy variants would surface additional fragile subsets, whether design changes (richer feedback, discriminative prompts, expert annotation) can close the BT-score gaps that drive sensitivity, and whether the same fragility extends to other ranking paradigms beyond pairwise BT models.
