## Problem

Existing graph generation models (diffusion and flow-based) construct probability paths by linearly interpolating individual nodes and edges independently in disjoint spaces, which breaks the interconnected relational structure of graphs. This disentangled interpolation produces non-smooth, irregular probability paths with sharp transitions, leading to poor velocity estimation, unstable training dynamics, and failed sampling convergence. The paper addresses this by asking: can we construct a theoretically optimal, smooth probability path that respects the joint, non-Euclidean geometry of graph components?

## Input / Output

The method takes a reference (noise) distribution over graphs and a dataset of target graphs, both represented as node feature matrices and weighted adjacency matrices. During training, pairs of source and target graphs are converted into GraphMRF parameters (colored Gaussian means and Laplacian matrices), interpolated in the MRF domain, and converted back to graph form for the graph transformer to predict the clean target. At inference, the model iteratively applies the learned velocity field starting from noise to produce generated graphs or 3D molecules with coordinates, with the key constraint that both source and target graphs must share the same node count and feature dimensionality (same embedding space V).

## Method

BWFlow models graphs as Graph Markov Random Fields (GraphMRF), where node features follow a colored Gaussian parameterized by the graph Laplacian and edges follow a Dirac delta on the adjacency matrix. The core theoretical contribution is a closed-form Bures-Wasserstein distance between two graph distributions, derived by extending the OT formula between Gaussians to the Kronecker-product structure of the GraphMRF covariance, yielding a distance that decomposes into a Frobenius norm on means and a trace term on pseudo-inverse Laplacians. The probability path is then constructed as the OT geodesic: node features follow linear interpolation while Laplacians follow a geodesic in the space of positive semi-definite matrices (L_t† = L_0^{1/2}((1-t)L_0† + t(L_0^{†/2}L_1†L_0^{†/2})^{1/2})²L_0^{1/2}), ensuring smooth co-evolution of structure and features. Closed-form velocity fields are derived from this interpolation, and the framework is extended to discrete flow matching where node features use Categorical distributions and edges use Bernoulli distributions with a derived discrete velocity. Training uses x-prediction with a graph transformer backbone, and the entire pipeline is simulation-free, requiring only matrix operations on the Laplacian at each step.

## Results

On plain graph generation (Table 1), BWFlow achieves the best A.Ratio (lower is better) across all three benchmarks: 2.4±0.9 on Planar, 1.3±0.2 on Tree, and 2.3±0.5 on SBM, outperforming the next-best methods (DeFoG at 3.5/1.9/3.4 and Cometh at 3.0/2.0/4.7). BWFlow also attains the highest V.U.N. on Planar (84.8%) and SBM (84.5%), though it trails DeFoG (83.5%) and Cometh (84.5%) on Tree V.U.N. On 3D molecule generation (Table 2), BWFlow achieves 96.45% V.U.N. and 97.84% Mol.Stab. on QM9, and 87.75% V.U.N. and 46.80% Mol.Stab. on GEOM-DRUGS, substantially surpassing MiDi and FlowMol on all metrics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BWFlow dominates on the A.Ratio metric (distribution fidelity) across all three plain graph benchmarks, beating the strongest competitors DeFoG (3.5/1.9/3.4) and Cometh (3.0/2.0/4.7) by 1.1–2.4 points. On V.U.N., BWFlow wins on Planar (84.8% vs. 80.5% Cometh, 77.5% DeFoG) and SBM (84.5% vs. 85.0% DeFoG, 77.5% Cometh), but loses on Tree (81.5% vs. 84.5% Cometh, 83.5% DeFoG), which the authors attribute to tree graphs' wider spectral spread being less compatible with the GraphMRF prior. Among diffusion baselines, DiGress and DisCo are substantially outperformed on all metrics (e.g., DiGress A.Ratio of 9.9 on Planar vs. BWFlow's 2.4). On 3D molecule generation, BWFlow outperforms both MiDi and FlowMol on every metric: on QM9 it achieves 96.45% V.U.N. vs. 93.13% (MiDi) and 87.53% (FlowMol), and on GEOM-DRUGS it reaches 46.80% Mol.Stab. vs. 32.42% (MiDi) and 36.90% (FlowMol). In the small-step sampling test (30 steps vs. 1000), BWFlow retains 77.0% V.U.N. on Planar while DeFoG without path manipulation drops to 24.5% and Cometh to 17.0%, highlighting BWFlow's sampling efficiency advantage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the flow-matching framework for graph generation (Lipman et al., 2023; Qin et al., 2024) by replacing the standard linear interpolation in disjoint node/edge spaces with a Bures-Wasserstein (BW) interpolation derived from a Markov Random Field (MRF) parameterization of graphs. The BW distance between graph distributions builds directly on the Gaussian OT formula (Dowson & Landau, 1982) and the recent Bures-Wasserstein means of graphs (Haasler & Frossard, 2024), while the MRF formulation draws on statistical relational learning (Zhu et al., 2003; Qu et al., 2019). Unlike DeFoG (Qin et al., 2024) and Cometh (Siraudin et al., 2024), which rely on heuristic path manipulations (time distortion, target guidance, predictor-corrector) to partially compensate for the suboptimality of linear paths, BWFlow constructs a theoretically grounded smooth path that respects the non-Euclidean, interconnected geometry of graphs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first closed-form OT displacement for graph distributions parameterized as MRFs, offering a principled alternative to the ad-hoc linear interpolation that underpins most current graph generative models. This could shift the field's thinking from 'how to fix a bad path with heuristics' to 'how to construct the right path from first principles.' Key limitations remain: the O(N³) matrix operations in path construction limit scalability to large sparse graphs, the framework does not yet handle multiple edge types, and the MRF prior is less effective on graphs with wide spectral spread (e.g., trees). Open questions include whether the MRF representation can be learned end-to-end (analogous to latent diffusion), how to extend BW interpolation to heterogeneous/multi-relational graphs, and whether the spectral bias of GMRF can be mitigated for broader graph families.
