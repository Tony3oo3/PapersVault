## Problem

Graph Neural Simulators (GNSs) trained with one-step losses and rolled out autoregressively accumulate error rapidly over long horizons, and their message-passing cores suffer from over-smoothing, over-squashing, and gradient degradation that prevent effective long-range information propagation across the spatial domain. Existing multi-step training objectives only work over short windows because standard GNN updates are dissipative, causing information to vanish after a few steps. The paper asks whether a simulator architecture can be designed that inherently preserves information flow across the graph while still capturing the full range of non-conservative physical dynamics.

## Input / Output

The model receives an initial node-state matrix X̄ ∈ R^{n×d} (split into generalized coordinates q and momenta p), the graph/mesh topology G=(V,E), and static geometric edge features encoding displacement vectors and Euclidean distances between connected nodes. It outputs the full trajectory of node states {q̂^{(t+τ)}, p̂^{(t+τ)}} for τ ∈ [1,K] over a fixed supervision window T, with the multi-step loss supervising every intermediate step simultaneously rather than only the next state. The model operates on irregular meshes of varying sizes (e.g., 19×19 to 29×29 grids) and must generalize across different force configurations and boundary conditions.

## Method

IGNS structures its latent evolution around a parameterized port-Hamiltonian system: a Hamiltonian H_θ built from message-passing over the graph (with block-diagonal weight matrices separating q and p components), plus damping and external forcing terms, integrated via the symplectic Euler method to preserve the energy-conserving structure. A warmup phase performs l rounds of message passing at initialization to broadcast global context before rollout begins, and a geometric encoding module injects spatial displacement/distance information into the forcing term as a static prior rather than updating edges at every step. Training uses a multi-step loss that supervises all intermediate q and p predictions over a window of K steps, enabling PDE matching where the integrated port-Hamiltonian trajectory aligns with ground-truth trajectories. The key theoretical guarantee is that the Hamiltonian core induces a divergence-free vector field, so the sensitivity matrix ∂x(t)/∂x(s) is lower-bounded by 1, preventing the vanishing gradients that cripple standard message passing and allowing gradients from distant time steps to remain informative during multi-step training.

## Results

Across six physical simulation benchmarks, IGNS (time-varying) achieves the lowest test MSE on 5 of 6 tasks. The most dramatic gain is on Impact Plate, where IGNS reaches 67.86 MSE versus 3095.75 for MGN (a ~46× improvement) and 646.81 for HCMT (~10×). On Wave Balls, IGNS attains 0.37×10⁻³ MSE, outperforming the next-best A-DGN (0.70×10⁻³) by nearly 2×. On Kuramoto-Sivashinsky, IGNS scores 1.20×10⁻³ MSE versus 10.76×10⁻³ for MGN, while GraphCON (0.49×10⁻³) remains the strongest single method on that chaotic task.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the main competitor MGN (Pfaff et al., 2021), IGNS wins on 5 of 6 tasks with the largest margins on Impact Plate (67.86 vs. 3095.75 MSE, ~46×), Wave Balls (0.37 vs. 1.78×10⁻³, ~4.8×), and Kuramoto-Sivashinsky (1.20 vs. 10.76×10⁻³, ~9×); MGN edges out IGNS only on Plate Deformation (1.27 vs. 1.34). GraphCON (Rusch et al., 2022) is the strongest baseline on static/chaotic tasks—winning on Plate Deformation (1.20 vs. 1.34) and KS (0.49 vs. 1.20×10⁻³)—but fails catastrophically on dynamic tasks, with its loss diverging on Impact Plate and yielding 1.62×10⁻³ on Wave Balls versus IGNS's 0.37×10⁻³. A-DGN (Gravina et al., 2023) is outperformed by IGNS on all six tasks, most notably on Impact Plate (3974.56 vs. 67.86) and Wave Balls (0.70 vs. 0.37×10⁻³). MP-ODE (Iakovlev et al., 2021) is evaluated on only four tasks and is dominated by IGNS everywhere, with particularly large gaps on Wave Balls (97.04 vs. 0.37×10⁻³) and KS (59.55 vs. 1.20×10⁻³). On the dedicated comparisons in Table 2, IGNS beats HCMT (Yu et al., 2024) on Impact Plate by ~10× (67.86 vs. 646.81) and FNO-RNN (Li et al., 2021) on KS by ~5.6× (1.20 vs. 6.69×10⁻³). GCN+LN (Luo et al., 2024) is the weakest baseline overall, losing to IGNS on every task by margins ranging from 1.6× (Sphere Cloth) to 59× (Impact Plate).

## Relation to Prior Work

IGNS extends the port-Hamiltonian architectural bias for GNNs introduced by Heilig et al. (ICLR 2025) from a static long-range propagation setting into a full physics simulator, adding a symplectic integrator, warmup phase, geometric encoding, and a multi-step PDE-matching loss. Its universality proof directly builds on the neural oscillator universality theorem of Lanthaler et al. (NeurIPS 2023), casting the port-Hamiltonian dynamics into the mass-spring-damper form to inherit the approximation guarantee. The paper departs from standard GNSs (MeshGraphNets, MP-ODE/FEN) that rely on dissipative message passing and one-step autoregressive training, and from oscillatory baselines like GraphCON, by grounding the entire rollout in energy-conserving dynamics with explicit damping and forcing terms.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The near-order-of-magnitude improvement on Impact Plate (67.86 vs. 646.81 MSE for HCMT) and the catastrophic ablation result (2388.6 vs. 25.3 MSE without multi-step loss) demonstrate that the combination of a non-dissipative core and trajectory-level supervision is not merely helpful but essential for long-horizon physical simulation. The three new benchmarks (Plate Deformation, Sphere Cloth, Wave Balls) fill a gap in the evaluation landscape by specifically targeting long-range spatial propagation and oscillatory dynamics under external forcing. Key limitations remain: the model operates in an open-loop causal setting without closed-loop feedback, and the time-varying weight parameterization, while effective, adds complexity that may limit interpretability. Future work on inverse design and control tasks is a natural extension given the preserved gradient flow.
