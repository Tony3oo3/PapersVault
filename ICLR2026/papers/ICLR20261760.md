## Problem

Existing LLM evaluations for legal judgment focus on whether the generated output is correct, but do not examine whether the internal inference patterns behind a seemingly correct judgment are actually faithful to legal domain knowledge. This paper asks: even when an LLM produces the right verdict, how many of the interaction patterns it relies on represent misleading, irrelevant, or entity-mismatched logic that would be unacceptable to a human judge?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261760/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a legal case text segmented into ~10 informative phrases (identified by senior legal experts), an LLM to audit, and a human annotation partitioning each phrase into relevant (R), irrelevant (I), or forbidden (F) based on legal domain knowledge. The input is consumed by enumerating all 2^n masked states of the phrase set to compute the LLM's output score on each, from which the AND-OR interaction decomposition is derived. Outputs include the full set of salient AND-OR interaction patterns with numerical weights, their decomposition into reliable and unreliable effects, and two aggregate metrics: s^reliable (fraction of total interaction strength aligned with domain knowledge) and s^conflict (fraction of interaction strength cancelled by opposing-sign effects).

## Method

The core mechanism is an AND-OR logical model h(·) that exactly matches the LLM's output score v(·) on all 2^n masked input states (Theorem 1, universal matching property), where AND interactions (Harsanyi dividends) capture conjunctive phrase co-occurrence effects and OR interactions capture disjunctive effects. A LASSO-like sparsity objective over learnable parameters {γ_T} extracts only the O(n^p) salient interactions (1.5 ≤ p ≤ 2.0), yielding a concise surrogate model. The key novelty is decomposing each interaction weight I_S into a reliable component R_S and unreliable component U_S using the human phrase annotations: for AND interactions, reliability requires S to contain at least one relevant phrase and no forbidden phrases; for OR interactions, the effect is proportionally allocated by the fraction of relevant phrases in S. This yields the s^reliable metric quantifying what fraction of the LLM's total inference strength is grounded in legally valid reasoning versus spurious or entity-mismatched patterns.

## Results

The headline metric is the ratio of reliable interaction effects $s^{\text{reliable}}$, which measures the proportion of interaction effects aligned with human legal domain knowledge. Across all evaluated LLMs and datasets, over half of the interaction patterns used for legal judgment were unreliable (i.e., $s^{\text{reliable}} < 50\%$ in most settings). In the case study on incorrect entity attribution, SaulLM-7B-Instruct achieved only $s^{\text{reliable}} = 41.5\%$ while BAI-Law-13B reached $44.5\%$. Additionally, more than 60% of total interaction effect magnitude was mutually cancelled between positive and negative interactions, indicating substantial internal ambiguity in the LLMs' reasoning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper audits four LLMs rather than proposing a new method to outperform baselines. Among the two legal-specific LLMs, BAI-Law-13B marginally outperforms SaulLM-7B-Instruct on the incorrect-entity case ($s^{\text{reliable}}$ = 44.5% vs. 41.5%), but both remain well below the 50% threshold, meaning the majority of their reasoning relies on forbidden or irrelevant phrases. On the semantically-irrelevant-phrases case, both legal LLMs perform nearly identically (76.9% vs. 77.0%), suggesting that when the case is simpler, both models extract more reliable patterns. The general-purpose LLMs (Qwen2.5-14B-Base and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B) also fail to achieve reliable interaction ratios above 50% on the ECtHR, Learned Hand Crime, CAIL2018, LeCaRD, and LEVEN benchmarks, indicating that legal-domain fine-tuning does not substantially fix the underlying representational flaws. A consistent finding across all four models is that more than 60% of interaction effects are mutually cancelled, with larger/more reliable models exhibiting somewhat lower cancellation levels.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the interaction-based explanation theory established by Ren et al. (ICLR 2024), Li & Zhang (ICML 2023), and Ren et al. (CVPR 2023), which proved that DNN outputs can be faithfully decomposed into sparse AND-OR interaction patterns with universal matching guarantees. The key conceptual departure is repurposing these interactions from a descriptive explanation tool into a *diagnostic audit mechanism*: rather than asking 'which features matter,' the paper asks 'do the interaction patterns that matter align with domain expert knowledge?' It also leverages Zhou et al. (AAAI 2024) for the LASSO-based sparse extraction procedure. The paper explicitly positions itself against accuracy-based LLM evaluation, framing interaction reliability as a complementary (and in their view, more revealing) axis of assessment analogous to the distinction between procedural and outcome fairness.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that over half of salient interaction patterns in legal LLMs are unreliable—even when the final judgment is correct—provides the first quantitative evidence that 'right answer, wrong reasoning' is not a rare edge case but the majority regime in high-stakes LLM deployment. This has direct implications for regulatory adoption of legal AI and for the broader question of whether chain-of-thought prompting genuinely produces sound reasoning or merely plausible-sounding token sequences. Key limitations include the restriction to 10 informative phrases per case, 100 samples per dataset, and a three-way phrase annotation scheme the authors acknowledge is non-exhaustive; extending the audit to longer documents, more granular phrase decompositions, and broader domains (medicine, finance) remains open. The paper also raises but does not resolve how to *remediate* unreliable interactions (e.g., via interaction-consistency training or reliability-weighted SFT), leaving the path from diagnosis to fix as future work.
