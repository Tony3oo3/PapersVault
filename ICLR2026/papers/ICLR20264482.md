## Problem

Current cross-modal representation learning methods (CLIP, ImageBind, DCCA, ALIGN) optimize purely statistical objectives such as contrastive alignment or correlation maximization, which can introduce spurious dependencies or eliminate critical mediators through nonlinear mappings — a failure the authors term representation-induced structural drift. No existing method provides verifiable guarantees that learned representations preserve conditional independencies, Markov boundaries, or causal effect identifiability conditions (backdoor, frontdoor, instrumental variable) required for reliable causal inference in shared representation spaces.

## Input / Output

The framework accepts heterogeneous inputs spanning tabular variables (treatment T, mediator M, covariates X, outcome Y) and RGB images (mediator fundus images I^M, outcome proxy images I^Y), supporting three configurations (IM, IY, DUAL) based on which image modalities are available. A critical constraint is that in the DUAL configuration, the two image modalities are never jointly conditioned within the same loss term or discovery-phase conditioning set to prevent collider bias. Outputs include shared representations in Z ⊂ R^d, a CPDAG discovered via the PC algorithm with a surjective variable-tracing map back to original inputs, and decomposed causal effects (total, natural direct, natural indirect) with mediation proportions for each pathway.

## Method

CARL implements the Causal Structure Preservation (CSP) principle through three jointly optimized losses: a conditional independence loss estimating CMI via the NLL difference between two independently parameterized neural predictors (following Poole et al.'s variational approach), a Markov boundary retention loss using InfoNCE to prevent mediator representation collapse, and a monotonic alignment consistency loss maximizing Spearman correlation between semantic amplitude differences and L2 representation distances via differentiable soft-rank (Blondel et al.). Encoders are deterministic, Lipschitz-constrained mappings (MLPs for tabular, CNNs for images) with spectral normalization for stability. After training, the PC algorithm with Fisher's z-test and Benjamini-Hochberg FDR control recovers the CPDAG in representation space, which is projected to variable-level via a surjective tracing map. Theoretical guarantees establish that the empirical risk minimizer satisfies ε-CSP with error bounded by encoder approximation error plus O_P(n^{-1/2}) + O_P(K^{-1/2}) + O_P(n^{-1/3}) terms, and that causal query estimates in representation space deviate from ground truth by at most κ·ε + δ_cal under cross-fitting.

## Results

On the synthetic benchmark, CARL achieves a Causal Structure Index (CSI) of 1.0 across all sample sizes (n=500–5000) with structural accuracy stable at 0.61–0.75, while CLIP drops to CSI=0.25 and ImageBind to Structural=0.33. Under increasing noise (σ=0.1→0.5), CARL's CSI remains at 1.0 while MAC degrades from 0.89 to 0.42 and MBRI from 0.77 to 0.63. On the real-world Human Phenotype Project, CARL recovers the blood pressure→CVD pathway with TE=0.486, NDE=0.271, NIE=0.215 (44.24% mediation), and identifies five additional mediation pathways (e.g., AHI→PRV→CVD at 20.17%, gut microbiome→metabolism→CVD at 21.52%) consistent with independent clinical literature.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CARL is compared against seven baselines: CLIP, ImageBind, DCCA, CausaIVAE, ALIGN, IRM, and DEAR, plus a feature-concatenation baseline. On the synthetic structure-preservation benchmark, CARL dominates on the Causal Structure Index (CSI=1.00 vs. CLIP's 0.25 and DCCA's comparable low score) and Structural Accuracy (0.61 vs. ImageBind's 0.33), confirming that purely statistical objectives (contrastive, CCA, pivot-based) cannot preserve conditional independence patterns. The alignment-only configuration (closest to CLIP-style training) reproduces the same failure mode: high MAC (0.89) but low CSI (0.25) and Structural (0.32), showing that semantic alignment alone is insufficient without explicit causal constraints. CausaIVAE and DEAR, which incorporate some causal structure in unimodal settings, do not address the cross-modal information bottleneck or monotonic alignment challenges and thus underperform CARL on the multi-metric evaluation. IRM, designed for invariant risk minimization rather than structure preservation, similarly fails to maintain Markov boundary information. The ablation further confirms that removing any single CARL loss component degrades specific metrics (e.g., removing L_CI drops CSI from 1.0 to 0.25; removing L_MBR drops MBRI from 0.63 to 0.46), validating that the three losses target complementary aspects of causal structure that no single baseline captures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two well-developed but previously disconnected lines of research: cross-modal representation learning (CLIP, ImageBind, ALIGN, DCCA) and causal representation learning (CausaVAE, IRM, Schölkopf et al.). Prior cross-modal methods optimize purely statistical objectives (InfoNCE correlation, geometric alignment) without any structural verification mechanism, while prior causal representation learning has been predominantly unimodal. CARL departs from both by explicitly embedding causal structure preservation constraints—conditional independence transfer, Markov boundary retention, and monotonic alignment consistency—into the cross-modal alignment objective, drawing on variational CMI estimation (Poole et al.) and InfoNCE lower bounds (van den Oord et al.) as its information-theoretic backbone. The PC algorithm with variable tracing for causal discovery in the representation space extends standard constraint-based methods to the latent setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first formal ε-CSP guarantee linking cross-modal representation learning to causal query identifiability, which is a meaningful theoretical contribution for anyone attempting causal inference from multimodal data. However, the practical impact is tempered by strong assumptions (Gaussianity for PC consistency, faithfulness, realizability of predictors, Lipschitz-bounded encoders) and the HPP validation primarily confirms known biomedical pathways rather than discovering novel causal structures. Open questions include whether the framework scales to high-dimensional, partially observed causal graphs with unobserved confounders, whether the Gaussian faithfulness assumption for the PC algorithm holds for real biomedical data, and how the method performs when the true causal structure is unknown and must be jointly learned with the representations.
