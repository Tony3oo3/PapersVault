## Problem

State-of-the-art image captioning models rely on Supervised Fine-Tuning with expensive, non-scalable human or proprietary annotations, causing models to memorize specific ground-truth captions rather than learning general visual concepts. Applying Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) to captioning is challenging because the task is inherently open-ended and subjective, making it difficult to design an objective reward function that avoids reward hacking (e.g., exploiting verbosity or brevity biases in reward models).
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260446/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image (natural scenes, charts, documents, infographics, or user interfaces) as input and produces a free-form, dense text caption with no fixed structural template. The caption is unconstrained in length and format, and its quality is evaluated indirectly: a separate vision-free LLM must answer multiple-choice questions about the image using only the caption as context. The QA pairs used for reward computation are curated to be strictly visually-grounded, ensuring the LLM cannot answer from external knowledge or question cues alone.

## Method

CapRL introduces a decoupled two-stage pipeline for RLVR-based captioning: in Stage 1, an LVLM policy model (Qwen2.5-VL-3B) generates candidate captions for an image; in Stage 2, a vision-free LLM (Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct) answers curated multiple-choice questions about the image using only the caption as context, and its exact-match accuracy serves as the objective reward. Training uses GRPO with a KL-divergence penalty, where the reward for each caption is the average accuracy over N sampled questions with shuffled answer options to eliminate LLM choice bias. A three-stage QA curation pipeline (diverse image collection → Qwen2.5-VL-72B QA generation → strict filtering via Eq. 4 ensuring questions require image context) yields ~75k training images with visually-grounded QA pairs. The trained CapRL-3B model is then used to annotate 5M images, producing the CapRL-5M dataset for LVLM pretraining, which outperforms ShareGPT4V-1M and DenseFusion-1M across 12 benchmarks.

## Results

In the Prism Framework evaluation, CapRL-3B achieves an average score of 48.3, matching Qwen2.5-VL-72B (48.3) while outperforming Qwen2.5-VL-3B (39.9) by 8.4% and Qwen2.5-VL-7B (44.9) by 3.4%. In the pretraining setting, CapRL-5M yields the highest average across all three architecture configurations: 62.0 (Qwen2.5-3B), 63.8 (Qwen2.5-7B), and 62.2 (InternLM2.5-7B), surpassing the strongest baseline DenseFusion-1M by 4.9%, 3.6%, and 4.8% respectively. CapRL-3B also substantially outperforms both LVLM-as-a-Judge reward baselines (UnifiedRW-as-Judge-3B at 38.4 and Qwen2.5VL-as-Judge-3B at 42.5) in the Prism setting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Qwen2.5-VL series in the Prism setting, CapRL-3B (48.3 avg) matches Qwen2.5-VL-72B (48.3) exactly and surpasses Qwen2.5-VL-7B (44.9) by 3.4% and Qwen2.5-VL-3B (39.9) by 8.4%, with particularly large gains on chart/infographic benchmarks (e.g., +14.9% on ChartQA, +24.6% on InfoVQA over the 3B baseline). Against the two LVLM-as-a-Judge reward baselines, CapRL-3B wins decisively: UnifiedRW-as-Judge-3B (38.4) actually degrades below the untrained Qwen2.5-VL-3B baseline due to reward hacking toward brevity, while Qwen2.5VL-as-Judge-3B (42.5) suffers from verbosity exploitation, both falling 5.8–9.9% below CapRL-3B. In the pretraining setting (Table 1), CapRL-5M outperforms both ShareGPT4V-1M and DenseFusion-1M across all 12 benchmarks and all three architecture settings, with the largest margins on fine-grained perception tasks (e.g., +6.8% over DenseFusion on InfoVQA, +3.6% on ChartVQA under Qwen2.5-3B). The ablation in Table 2 confirms that the advantage stems from caption quality rather than image diversity, as CapRL-annotated versions of the same images still outperform the original captions by >2%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RLVR paradigm (established for verifiable tasks like math reasoning in DeepSeek-R1 and Tulu 3) to the inherently subjective and open-ended task of image captioning, where no natural verifiable reward exists. It departs from the dominant SFT-based captioning pipeline (ShareGPT4V, DenseFusion, ALLaVA) that relies on expensive human or proprietary-model annotations, and from earlier attempts to use LVLM-as-a-Judge or reward models for caption RL, which the paper shows are vulnerable to reward hacking. The key conceptual move is borrowing the decoupled VQA structure from the Prism evaluation framework and repurposing it as a training reward signal, creating a bridge between verifiable-reward RL and subjective generation tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CapRL provides a scalable, low-cost alternative to SFT for generating high-quality captions, demonstrated by the CapRL-5M dataset that improves LVLM pretraining across 12 benchmarks with a 3B annotator. The finding that LVLM-as-a-Judge rewards actively degrade caption quality (via verbosity or brevity bias) is a useful negative result that cautions against naive reward-model approaches for open-ended generation. Open questions include whether the MCQ-based reward fully captures caption quality beyond factual accuracy (e.g., coherence, style), whether the QA curation pipeline generalizes to new image domains without re-filtering, and whether the approach extends to video captioning or other sequential generation tasks.
