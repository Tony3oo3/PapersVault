## Problem

Random variate generation from arbitrary distributions is a core yet computationally expensive bottleneck in probabilistic machine learning, with high time and energy costs. Existing exact samplers (e.g., Knuth–Yao trees, FLDR, ALDR) require exponential memory or expensive multi-step searches, while widely used approximate samplers (Alias, inversion-based routines in NumPy/PyTorch/JAX) rely on the real-RAM model, lack controllable error bounds, and consume 32–64 bits per sample regardless of the distribution's entropy. No prior method simultaneously achieves exact sampling, controllable precision, near-optimal entropy cost, and low energy consumption for arbitrary finite discrete distributions.

## Input / Output

The method takes a finite discrete distribution specified by n outcomes x_1,…,x_n (of arbitrary type) and n probabilities p_1,…,p_n, which are quantized to b-bit precision via a sum-preserving rounding scheme to yield integer frequencies f_i = round(p_i · 2^b) summing to 2^b. Continuous or infinite-support distributions are first discretized (e.g., to IEEE 754 16-bit representable values) before table construction. Sampling consumes i.i.d. Bernoulli(0.5) bits from an entropy source and produces a single variate S ∈ X per call via one table lookup; the expected bit cost per sample is b − r + 2 − 2^{−(r−1)}, approaching the Shannon entropy H(p) for high-entropy distributions.

## Method

The cLUT approach represents the distribution as a two-dimensional compressed lookup table of (r+1) rows and 2^c columns (with r + c = b), where row i encodes a frequency weight of 2^{r−i}. A value x_i is placed in row j if and only if the j-th bit of its frequency f_i is 1, yielding a lossless compression ratio ρ = 2^r/(r+1) that grows exponentially with r. A rectification step redistributes entries from higher to lower rows so that all rows have uniform width 2^c, enabling a single flat-array index lookup. Sampling draws a row index I from a truncated geometric distribution (via a loop of i.i.d. bits, expected 2 − 2^{−(r−1)} bits) and a column index J uniformly (c bits), then returns compressedTable[I, J] in one memory access. This eliminates the conditional branching, multi-table searches, and pointer chasing that plague prior compressed-table and rejection-based samplers, reducing both transistor switching energy and cache-miss stalls while preserving exact, rejection-free sampling with provable KL-divergence bounds on the discretization error.

## Results

In C implementations, cLUT achieves 15.5 ns per sample (n∈[10³,10⁴)) and 33.0 ns (n∈[10⁷,10⁸)), outperforming ALDR (22.4 ns / 102.8 ns), Alias (26.8 ns / 55.9 ns), and FLDR (21.3 ns / 101.4 ns) in wall time, while consuming 199 nJ / 450 nJ of energy versus 263–320 nJ / 888–1224 nJ for competitors. Against Python ML libraries, cLUT delivers a 10× speedup for n∈[10⁴,10⁵) (0.037 s vs. 0.36–0.67 s for 10⁷ samples) and over 100× for n∈[10⁶,10⁷) (0.10 s vs. 0.80–9.62 s). In the TrueSkill application, cLUT reduces total execution time by 72% and energy by 34% compared to NumPy's discrete sampler.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the three Python ML library samplers (NumPy RandomGenerator.choice, PyTorch multinomial, JAX random.choice), cLUT achieves a 10× speedup at n≈10⁴–10⁵ and over 100× at n≈10⁶–10⁷, while also producing exact (not approximate) samples. In the C-level SOTA comparison, cLUT consistently outperforms all three exact samplers—ALDR, FLDR, and the Alias method—on both wall time (30–40% faster) and energy (25–50% less), with the advantage growing for larger distributions (e.g., 3.5× less energy than ALDR at n∈[10⁷,10⁸)). The Alias method, while having the lowest preprocessing cost, is the slowest and most energy-hungry at large n; cLUT's higher preprocessing overhead is offset after a break-even point of fewer than 10³ samples in energy terms. cLUT also matches or approaches the information-theoretic entropy lower bound (H(p) + 2 bits) in bit consumption, whereas the approximate methods (Alias, NumPy, PyTorch, JAX) typically consume a fixed 32 or 64 bits per sample regardless of distribution entropy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Marsaglia et al.'s (2004) compressed lookup tables for discrete sampling, but departs from their design by replacing conditional branching and multi-table searches with a single compressed table indexed via a truncated geometric row draw and a uniform column draw. It positions itself against the exact-sampling lineage (Knuth–Yao generating trees, FLDR, ALDR, the interval algorithm) by achieving near-entropy-optimal bit consumption while eliminating the expensive binary searches those methods require at each sampling step. On the approximate-sampling side, it contrasts with the widely deployed Alias method and inversion-based samplers in NumPy/PyTorch/JAX by providing exact, controllable-precision sampling with clear theoretical guarantees rather than uncontrolled floating-point deviations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Sampling is a pervasive bottleneck across variational inference, contrastive learning, diffusion models, and Bayesian skill estimation, and the paper demonstrates that a single direct memory lookup can cut both wall-clock time (30–40% vs. SOTA C samplers; 10–100× vs. Python libraries) and energy (up to 50%) without sacrificing exactness. The energy savings are particularly relevant as ML workloads scale to data centers and edge devices, and the RAPL-based measurements ground the claims in physical power draw rather than proxy metrics. Limitations include a higher preprocessing cost than the Alias method (offset only after a distribution-size-dependent break-even point), the requirement that table size be a power of two, and the fact that the reference implementation is not yet vectorized or parallelized, leaving GPU/SIMD gains (partially shown via JAX integration) as future work.
