## Problem

Existing vision-language benchmarks evaluate models on offline reasoning where the full video and question are provided simultaneously, leaving a critical gap in assessing whether AI systems can genuinely converse face-to-face in real time. No prior dataset tests the combined challenges of situated visual understanding, deictic reference resolution, audio-visual integration, and knowing *when* to speak during a live interaction.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262406/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each QIVD instance is a ~5-second, 30 FPS, 640×382 video clip where the question is spoken aloud (embedded in the audio channel) rather than typed, so the system must transcribe it from raw microphone input. The output is a free-form text answer plus a when-to-answer timestamp that marks when sufficient visual context has accumulated to answer correctly—often well after the spoken question ends (mean 81.5% of video duration). The dataset contains 2,900 clips with raw audio, human transcriptions, ground-truth answers, and annotated answer timestamps across 13 semantic categories.

## Method

The paper introduces QIVD, collected via a contemporaneous question-asking protocol where crowd workers record videos while speaking a question, ensuring authentic temporal uncertainty and situational context. For evaluation, a streaming baseline pairs Streaming-Whisper (LocalAgreement algorithm, 0.25 s chunks) to transcribe the question and detect its end-of-speech, then feeds the trimmed video, audio, and transcribed question to a Video-LMM backbone. To address the when-to-answer challenge, they fine-tune Qwen2.5-Omni in a streaming format by feeding 1-second audio-visual chunks and training it to emit a special listening token until the appropriate moment, at which point it generates the answer. They also fine-tune VideoLLaMA2.1-7B-AV on QIVD using 5-fold cross-validation (freezing the SigLIP vision encoder, training the Qwen2-7B LLM backbone and BEATs audio tower), demonstrating that audio-visual fine-tuning yields the largest gains on action counting (+16.96%) and audio-visual tasks (+17.39%) while revealing a persistent ~20-point performance drop from static to temporal reasoning categories across all models.

## Results

On QIVD, the best open-source model (Qwen3-VL-8B) achieves 60.07% correctness (LLM judge) in the offline/GT setting, while GPT-4o reaches 58.76% and Gemini-2.5-Flash 58.07%. A non-expert human annotator achieves 87.33%, revealing a ~27-point gap between the best AI system and human performance. In the streaming setup (ASR-derived questions and timestamps), performance drops by 4–7 points across all models, with Qwen3-VL-8B at 53.72% and VideoLLaMA3-7B at 50.59%. Fine-tuning VideoLLaMA2.1-7B-AV on QIVD yields the largest gains in action counting (+16.96%) and audio-visual tasks (+17.39%), though absolute performance on action counting remains low (29.91%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

All 20 evaluated LMMs fall substantially below the human baseline of 87.33% correctness. Qwen3-VL-8B (60.07%) edges out GPT-4o (58.76%) and Gemini-2.5-Flash (58.07%) by 1–2 points in the GT setting, making it the best-performing model overall. Among open-source video-specific models, VideoLLaMA3-7B (56.38%) and VideoLLaMA2-72B (50.83%) lead, while VideoChat (3.52%) and Video-LLaVA (15.00%) perform near-randomly. The streaming-to-GT gap is consistent: every model loses 4–7 correctness points when relying on ASR-derived questions and timestamps rather than ground truth, confirming that transcription and timing errors compound with visual reasoning failures. Fine-tuning VideoLLaMA2.1-7B-AV on QIVD narrows the gap most in temporal and audio-visual categories (action counting +16.96%, audio-visual +17.39%) but yields minimal gains in static object tasks (+1.24%), indicating that situated temporal reasoning remains the dominant bottleneck. The when-to-answer experiment shows that Stream-Qwen-Omni (Δt = 0.52s) substantially outperforms Whisper-Streaming (Δt = 0.83s), and using the more accurate timestamp improves overall answer correctness, confirming that timing is a first-order failure mode.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant offline video QA paradigm (Video-MME, NExT-GQA, MVBench) by introducing an online, real-time question-answering setup where both the question and the answer evolve as the video unfolds. It builds on streaming ASR (Streaming-Whisper with LocalAgreement) for detecting the when-to-answer moment and on Qwen2.5-Omni for concurrent audio-visual processing, but positions itself as fundamentally different from prior situated QA work (SQA3D, Ego4D) by requiring face-to-face interaction with deictic references, pointing gestures, and audio-visual integration in an uncontrolled, in-the-wild setting. The paper also extends the streaming video literature (VideoLLM-Online, Flash-VStream) by incorporating audio and the timing problem, which prior streaming models entirely ignore.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a large and consistent gap between human performance (87.3%) and the best LMM (60.1% for Qwen3-VL-8B), with the most dramatic failures in temporal reasoning (action counting: 7.6% for GPT-4o vs. 85.7% for humans) and audio-visual integration (4.4% for GPT-4o vs. 100% for humans). The finding that all models suffer a >19% performance drop from static to temporal categories while humans remain consistent (~2% gap) suggests a fundamental architectural limitation in current LMMs rather than a data or scale issue. Open questions include whether the when-to-answer problem can be solved without explicit ASR-based triggering, whether fine-tuning on larger audio-visual conversational data can close the temporal reasoning gap, and how these findings generalize to multi-turn, multi-question interactions that the single-QA-per-video design deliberately excludes.
