## Problem

Existing preference datasets for LLM alignment generate candidate responses via independent temperature sampling, which produces an 'algorithmic monoculture'—all 21 state-of-the-art LLMs cluster in the same secular-rational, self-expression quadrant, covering only 41% of human preference variation. This homogeneity means standard alignment methods (SFT, DPO, GRPO, prompt-steering) cannot learn heterogeneous preferences even along the two most salient Inglehart-Welzel value dimensions, even when using the most diverse existing dataset (PRISM).

## Input / Output

The method takes everyday chatbot text prompts and a base language model (Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct) as input, and produces the Community Alignment dataset: 233,319 preference comparisons from 3,603 annotators across five countries (US, France, Italy, Brazil, India), with 66% non-English comparisons, 44% including comparison-level natural language explanations, and 2,582 prompts annotated by at least 10 annotators. Each comparison presents four negatively-correlated candidate responses for a 2–4 turn conversation, with the median annotator completing 26 conversations. The dataset also supports downstream training of aligned language models via standard preference-optimization methods.

## Method

The core technical contribution is negatively-correlated (NC) sampling: instead of independently drawing candidate responses from one or many models at a fixed temperature, a single model is prompted to simultaneously generate four responses that represent diverse values (e.g., 'Generate four responses that represent diverse values. Each response should start with ### to demarcate where one begins and the other ends'). This simple prompt-based technique induces negative correlation within each candidate set, yielding Pareto improvements in coverage of all four Inglehart-Welzel value poles (traditional and survival coverage jumps from 15%/30% to 60%/53%). The paper demonstrates that NC sampling with one model substantially outperforms temperature sampling from 21 models, and that all four tested alignment methods (prompt-steering, SFT, DPO, GRPO) achieve Pareto improvements in win rates—jumping from near-random to 70–90%—when trained on NC-sampled candidates versus temperature-sampled ones. The Community Alignment dataset is then collected using this NC sampling strategy, with quality controls including training phases, attention tests, and human audits of free-form explanations.

## Results

In the large-scale human study (N=15,000, 5 countries), LLM responses align with only 41% of human preferences, revealing an 'algorithmic monoculture' across all 21 tested LLMs. Temperature sampling yields mean coverage of only 15% for traditional values and 30% for survival values in candidate sets, whereas NC sampling increases these to 60% and 53% respectively. In preference learning experiments, all four alignment methods (prompt-steering, SFT, DPO, GRPO) achieve win rates near random chance with temperature-sampled candidates, but jump to 70–90% with NC-sampled candidates. Remarkably, NC sampling from a single model (Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct) substantially outperforms temperature sampling from 21 different LLMs (the original PRISM setup).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is PRISM (Kirk et al., 2024b), the most diverse existing open-source preference dataset, which uses temperature-sampled candidates from 21 LLMs. The paper demonstrates that even PRISM's candidate sets are too homogeneous for standard alignment methods to learn Inglehart-Welzel value dimensions: all four tested methods (prompt-steering, SFT, DPO, GRPO) achieve win rates near random chance on PRISM data. In contrast, NC sampling from a single model (Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct) yields Pareto improvements across all methods and all four IW values, with fine-tuning win rates jumping from near-random to 70–90%. Community Alignment also surpasses PRISM in scale (233,319 vs. 27,172 comparisons), linguistic diversity (66% vs. 1% non-English), annotator count (3,603 vs. 1,500), per-annotator data volume (median 26 vs. 6 conversations), and uniquely provides prompt-level annotator overlap (2,582 prompts with ≥10 annotators vs. 1 annotator per prompt in PRISM) and comparison-level natural language explanations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of pluralistic alignment (Sorensen et al., 2024; Prabhakaran et al., 2022), preference dataset construction (Stiennon et al., 2020; Kirk et al., 2024b), and the algorithmic monoculture literature (Kleinberg & Raghavan, 2021; Bommasani et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2024). It departs from prior work in two key ways: first, it challenges the implicit assumption in preference learning that temperature-sampled candidates from multiple models provide sufficient diversity to learn heterogeneous preferences, showing instead that all 21 SOTA LLMs cluster in the same value quadrant; second, it introduces negatively-correlated (NC) sampling as a data collection strategy, drawing an analogy to determinantal point processes and diverse committee selection in social choice, but implementing it via a simple prompt to a single model. The paper also departs from prior LLM evaluation work that uses social science surveys (e.g., World Values Survey) as proxies for LLM preferences, instead conducting a direct human study where participants choose between actual LLM responses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Community Alignment dataset (233K comparisons, 5 countries, 66% non-English) is the largest open-source multilingual preference dataset and the first with prompt-level annotator overlap, directly enabling research on social-choice-based and distributional alignment that has been constrained by data scarcity. The NC sampling technique is practically important because it is trivially implementable (a single prompt to one model) yet substantially outperforms the standard multi-model temperature-sampling pipeline, suggesting a simple change to data collection protocols could improve pluralistic alignment broadly. Open questions include whether NC sampling generalizes beyond the two Inglehart-Welzel dimensions (which the authors acknowledge do not capture all value variation), whether the GPT-4o-based judge introduces systematic biases that confound the learnability results, and whether the 60-prompt human study is representative enough to support the strong negative result about algorithmic monoculture.
