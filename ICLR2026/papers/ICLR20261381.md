## Problem

Existing Byzantine-robust federated learning methods detect and remove malicious clients but then assign uniform weights to the remaining benign clients, which fails to adapt to heterogeneous data distributions where label imbalances persist among honest participants. This paper asks whether aggregation weights can be treated as learnable parameters—jointly optimized with the global model—so that the defense mechanism is embedded directly in the learning objective rather than applied as a post-hoc filtering step.

## Input / Output

At each round the server receives mini-batch gradients and scalar loss values from all n clients (up to b_f of which may be Byzantine), along with the current global model θ_k ∈ ℝ^d. The method requires two communication sub-rounds per epoch: first to collect gradients at θ_k, then to collect losses and gradients at a tentative update point θ̃_{k+1}. The output is an updated global model θ_{k+1} and a weight vector w_{k+1} constrained to the sparse unit-capped simplex Δ_{t,ℓ0}^+ (non-negative, summing to 1, each entry ≤ t, at most s non-zero entries), which simultaneously encodes which clients to exclude and how to weight the rest.

## Method

FedLAW formulates a joint optimization min_{θ,w} Σ w_i f_i(θ) over the sparse unit-capped simplex and solves it via a bilevel (nested) decomposition: the inner problem over θ yields a standard gradient step θ_{k+1}(w) = θ_k − αG_k w, while the outer problem over w becomes min_{w∈Δ_{t,ℓ0}^+} Σ w_i f_i(θ_k − αG_k w). The outer problem is non-convex in both objective and constraint set, so it is solved by a prox-linear step: a quadratic surrogate of the outer objective is minimized subject to the simplex constraint, reducing the update to a projection of h_k = w_k + αβG_k^T G̃_{k+1} w_k − βf̃_{k+1} onto Δ_{t,ℓ0}^+. This projection is decomposed into three efficient steps—select the s largest entries of h_k, fix the support, then project onto the unit-capped simplex via a KKT-based algorithm. The key mechanism is that the quadratic form w^T G_k^T G_k w in the expanded objective decomposes into pairwise gradient distances, so Byzantine clients whose gradients misalign with the honest cluster are naturally penalized and driven to zero weight by the sparsity constraint, while the non-uniform remaining weights adaptively balance heterogeneous benign data.

## Results

On MNIST under the inverse-gradient attack with 40% malicious clients and high heterogeneity (q=0.9), FedLAW achieves a test accuracy 3.6 percentage points higher than the next-best defense. On CIFAR-10, under label-flipping with q=0.6 and 40% adversaries, FedLAW reaches 70.5% accuracy, an +8.3 pp gain over the best baseline (Bulyan at 62.2%); under inverse-gradient with q=0.9 and 40% adversaries, it achieves 59.38% vs. 56.24% for the best baseline (+3.1 pp). Under the combined double attack, competing methods such as RFA, RFA-bucketing, CClip, and CClip-bucketing degrade by over 31% or diverge entirely, while FedLAW maintains stable accuracy across all attacker fractions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FedLAW consistently outperforms all seven baselines across MNIST and CIFAR-10 under five attack types (label-flipping, inverse-gradient, backdoor, double, LIE). Against Bulyan (Guerraoui et al., 2018), the gap is most pronounced on CIFAR-10 label-flipping (70.5% vs. 62.2%, +8.3 pp). Against Krum (Blanchard et al., 2017) and Trimmed Mean / CwMed (Yin et al., 2018), FedLAW leads by several percentage points under high heterogeneity and large malicious fractions, while the gap narrows at low attack rates. RFA (Pillutla et al., 2022) and CCLIP (Karimireddy et al., 2020) and their bucketing variants deteriorate sharply as the attacker fraction grows—under the double attack, RFA drops by over 31% and CClip diverges—whereas FedLAW exhibits graceful degradation. The Huber aggregator (Zhao et al., 2024) is competitive at low attack rates but falls behind FedLAW under gradient-based attacks. FedAvg (McMahan et al., 2017), the undefended baseline, is substantially outperformed in all adversarial settings. Under the LIE attack, where Byzantine gradients are aligned with honest ones, FedLAW performs on par with the strongest baselines, confirming that the attack itself is inherently weak when gradients are coherent.

## Relation to Prior Work

FedLAW departs from the dominant 'detect-then-uniform-weight' paradigm in Byzantine-robust FL (Krum, Bulyan, RFA, CCLIP), where malicious clients are identified and removed before applying equal weights to the remaining honest clients. Instead, it formulates aggregation weight selection as a joint optimization problem with the global model, drawing on bilevel/nested optimization (Dempe, 2002) and proximal methods for the sparse unit-capped simplex constraint. The weight update step leverages the capped-simplex projection from Wang & Lu (2015), and the algorithmic structure is positioned as a refinement over BSUM (Razaviyayn et al., 2013), which the authors show is a suboptimal approximation that ignores gradient-loss coupling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical insight is that uniform weighting after filtering becomes suboptimal under high data heterogeneity, where label distributions among surviving honest clients become imbalanced; learnable weights adapt to this imbalance and consistently yield 3–8 percentage-point accuracy gains over strong baselines. A notable limitation is the two-communication-round-per-epoch overhead relative to standard FL, which the authors partially mitigate by showing faster convergence in total rounds. Open questions include scalability to very large client populations (the O(dn) memory and O(n·min(s,log n)+s²) computation per round), applicability to asynchronous or partially synchronous settings, and whether the approach extends naturally to more complex architectures or larger datasets beyond CIFAR-10/100.
