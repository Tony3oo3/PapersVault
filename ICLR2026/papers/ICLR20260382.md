## Problem

Prior work has identified theoretical limitations of single-vector embeddings on the number of realizable top-k document subsets, but the community has assumed these difficulties stem from unrealistic queries and can be overcome with better training data or larger models. This paper demonstrates that the fundamental combinatorial limit on representable top-k sets is encountered even with extremely simple, realistic queries, and that no amount of training data or model scale can resolve it within the single-vector paradigm.

## Input / Output

The LIMIT dataset pairs simple natural language queries of the form "who likes X?" (where X is a single attribute such as "Hawaiian pizza") against a corpus of 50k documents, each a short description of a person's liked attributes (e.g., "Jon likes Apples, sports cars, ..."), requiring the model to return exactly the 2 documents sharing the queried attribute. A smaller variant (LIMIT-small) restricts the corpus to the 46 documents relevant to at least one of the 1000 queries. Evaluation uses recall@k metrics, and a synonym variant replaces all attribute terms with non-overlapping synonyms to test lexical robustness.

## Method

The paper proves a lower bound on embedding dimension via a sphere-packing volume argument: if all C(n,k) top-k subsets are realizable with score margin 2γ, the corresponding unit query vectors must be pairwise 2γ-separated, yielding d ≥ log(C(n,k))/log(1+1/γ). This bound is validated empirically through "free embedding" optimization, where unit document and query vectors are directly optimized with InfoNCE loss and Adam on the test qrel matrix, revealing a critical-n threshold (the maximum corpus size solvable at a given dimension) that follows a cubic polynomial in d. The LIMIT dataset instantiates the theory in natural language by mapping document combinations to a 1850-attribute "likes" system with 46 relevant documents embedded in a 50k corpus, creating a task where even state-of-the-art models (Gemini Embeddings, Qwen3, GritLM) fail to achieve full recall@2 despite the trivially simple query-document structure. The paper further shows that in-domain training does not help (ruling out domain shift), while test-set overfitting partially recovers performance, and that alternative architectures (cross-encoders, multi-vector ColBERT, sparse BM25) each have distinct trade-offs in addressing this limitation.

## Results

On the full LIMIT dataset (50k documents, 1000 queries, k=2), even state-of-the-art embedding models struggle to reach 20% recall@100. On the LIMIT-small variant (46 documents), no single-vector model can solve the task even at recall@20, with the best achieving less than 60% recall@2. The free-embedding optimization (best-case, directly optimizing vectors on the test set) shows critical-n values of 500k (d=512), 1.7M (d=768), 4M (d=1024), 107M (d=3072), and 250M (d=4096), confirming the theoretical lower bound. A cross-encoder (Gemini-2.5-Pro) solves all 1000 LIMIT-small queries with 100% recall in a single forward pass, in stark contrast to the <60% recall@2 of the best embedding models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All six single-vector embedding models (GritLM, Qwen3 Embeddings, Promptriever, Gemini Embeddings, Arctic Embed Large v2.0, E5-Mistral Instruct) fail on LIMIT, with performance scaling positively with embedding dimensionality (1024→4096). Promptriever, trained on more diverse instructions, performs best among single-vector models, suggesting broader training allows more of the embedding space to be utilized. BM25 achieves near-perfect scores on LIMIT due to its very high effective dimensionality, but drops over 89% on the synonym variant (LIMIT-small-synonym), falling below most neural models (Qwen3 drops only 38.9%). GTE-ModernColBERT (multi-vector) significantly outperforms single-vector models on LIMIT-small but still cannot solve the task. The cross-encoder Gemini-2.5-Pro achieves 100% recall on all 1000 LIMIT-small queries in one generation, demonstrating that the limitation is specific to the single-vector paradigm rather than the retrieval task itself. Training an off-the-shelf model on the LIMIT training set yields negligible improvement (recall@10 from ~0 to 2.8), ruling out domain shift as the cause of failure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes classical sphere-packing and high-dimensional geometry results (Vershynin, Conway et al.) to establish a formal lower bound on embedding dimension for representing all top-k document subsets, connecting them to the Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma and margin-based learning theory. It departs from prior empirical observations of embedding limitations (Reimers & Gurevych 2020; Ormazabal et al. 2019) by providing a provable, architecture-agnostic bound rather than dataset-specific observations. The work also positions itself against the growing instruction-following retrieval literature (QUEST, BRIGHT, FollowIR), arguing that the community's assumption that better training data and larger models will overcome representational limits is unfounded for single-vector embeddings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates a fundamental, inescapable ceiling on single-vector retrieval: no amount of training data, model scale, or optimization can overcome the combinatorial explosion of top-k subsets relative to embedding dimension. As instruction-following and reasoning-based retrieval push models to connect previously unrelated documents, this limit becomes increasingly relevant, and the LIMIT dataset provides a concrete, reproducible stress test. Open questions include extending the theoretical bounds to multi-vector and sparse architectures, characterizing which specific combinations fail (not just that some do), and developing new single-vector techniques (e.g., hyperencoders) that might circumvent the bound. The finding that cross-encoders solve LIMIT trivially while all single-vector models fail underscores the need for hybrid or alternative architectures in production systems.
