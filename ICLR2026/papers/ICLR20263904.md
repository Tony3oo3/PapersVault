## Problem

Existing learning-based methods for imaging inverse problems fall into two unsatisfying camps: iterative algorithms (PnP, diffusion) that are computationally expensive and often suboptimal, and unrolled architectures that are problem-specific, memory-heavy, and require full retraining for minor changes like channel count. No lightweight, non-iterative model exists that incorporates forward-operator knowledge while generalizing across diverse imaging tasks (MRI, CT, deblurring, inpainting, super-resolution) and modalities (grayscale, color, complex) in a single architecture.

## Input / Output

The model receives a measurement vector y, the forward operator A (e.g., Fourier mask for MRI, Radon transform for CT, blur kernel for deblurring), and noise parameters (σ for Gaussian, γ for Poisson gain). It handles arbitrary spatial sizes and channel counts (1 for grayscale, 2 for complex-valued, 3 for RGB) with only the first and last convolutional layers and KSM encode/decode blocks changing per modality. The output is a single-pass reconstructed image; no iterative refinement is required. For self-supervised finetuning, only noisy measurements {y_i} are needed—no ground-truth references.

## Method

RAM builds on a DRUNet backbone and introduces three key modifications: (1) a proximal estimation initialization module that computes a regularized middle-ground between A^T y and A†y, parameterized by a learnable λ scaled to the input SNR; (2) Krylov Subspace Modules (KSM) inserted at multiple scales, which decode feature maps to the image domain, stack power iterations of the scaled operator A_s^T A_s applied to both the current estimate and A^T y, and re-encode via a 3×3 convolution, thereby learning the linear combination coefficients of a Krylov-space solution without explicit unrolling; (3) multiscale operator conditioning where A is applied on progressively coarser grids (via Kaiser-windowed sinc upsampling) to stabilize the ill-posed fine-scale problem. The model is trained simultaneously on G tasks (deblurring, inpainting, denoising, MRI, CT, SR) with an L1 loss weighted by per-task SNR, using DRUNet pretrained weights for initialization. All biases are removed for scale equivariance, and noise levels σ and γ are injected as constant channel maps. Self-supervised finetuning on a handful of measurements (down to one image) uses SURE, splitting, equivariant-imaging, or multi-operator losses to adapt to unseen problems or distributions without ground truth.

## Results

On in-distribution tasks, RAM achieves 34.39 dB PSNR on MRI×4 and 31.50 dB on MRI×8 (fastMRI), outperforming uDPIR-tied (34.14/30.86 dB) and DPIR (30.54/25.28 dB). On CT (LIDC-IDRI), RAM reaches 28.83 dB PSNR vs. 28.35 dB for uDPIR-tied. On the out-of-distribution Poisson CT task, RAM achieves 28.83 dB PSNR, dramatically surpassing uDPIR-tied (14.67 dB) and PDNet (13.20 dB). RAM uses only 36M parameters and is 3.7× faster at inference than uDPIR and 120× faster than DDRM.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RAM outperforms all baselines on MRI reconstruction (×4 and ×8), achieving 34.39/31.50 dB PSNR vs. 34.14/30.86 dB for uDPIR-tied and 30.54/25.28 dB for DPIR. On CT, RAM leads with 28.83 dB vs. 28.35 dB (uDPIR-tied) and 23.09 dB (PDNet). On deblurring (Table 1), RAM is competitive with uDPIR-tied (e.g., 25.64 vs. 25.61 dB on motion blur hard/CBSD68) but slightly trails DDRM at very low noise (σ=0.01) on large images. On super-resolution ×4, RAM (26.04 dB) is marginally behind the task-specific SWINIR (26.16 dB) but ahead of DPIR (25.67 dB). The most striking result is on out-of-distribution Poisson CT, where RAM achieves 28.83 dB while uDPIR-tied drops to 14.67 dB and PDNet to 13.20 dB, demonstrating strong zero-shot generalization to unseen noise models. On multi-coil MRI (out-of-distribution), uDPIR-tied slightly edges RAM (36.06 vs. 35.62 dB), but both far exceed the UNet baseline (28.50 dB).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the DRUNet backbone (Zhang et al., 2017a) and departs from two dominant paradigms in computational imaging: iterative methods (PnP, diffusion-based DDRM) that are computationally expensive, and unrolled architectures (uDPIR, PDNet) that are task-specific and parameter-heavy. The key conceptual move is to extract the mathematical structure of iterative solvers—proximal initialization and Krylov subspace iterations on AᵀA—and embed them as lightweight conditioning modules within a standard feedforward convolutional network, rather than unrolling an optimization loop. This reconciles the interpretability of unrolled architectures with the efficiency and generality of established UNet-style backbones, while also extending the self-supervised finetuning literature (SURE, splitting losses, equivariant imaging) to the setting of a pretrained multi-task foundation model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

A single 36M-parameter model achieving state-of-the-art zero-shot performance across MRI, CT, deblurring, inpainting, and super-resolution—while being 3.7× faster than unrolled counterparts and 120× faster than diffusion methods—challenges the prevailing assumption that reconstruction networks must be specialized per task. The ability to self-supervisedly finetune on a single real measurement (without ground truth) for out-of-distribution problems like Cryo-EM or satellite compressed sensing is a major practical advance for domains where reference data is scarce or nonexistent. Open questions include whether the approach extends to genuinely nonlinear or blind forward operators beyond the Gerchberg-Saxton-style decomposition shown for phase retrieval, and whether the low-distortion focus can be reconciled with perceptual quality objectives without sacrificing the efficiency gains.
