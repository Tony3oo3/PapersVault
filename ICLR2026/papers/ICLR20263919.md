## Problem

Computing the Pareto front for multi-objective optimization remains difficult at scale: evolutionary algorithms, scalarization, and multiple-gradient descent methods struggle in high-dimensional or resource-constrained settings, while existing generative approaches (flow-matching, preference-guided diffusion) either lack a principled convergence guarantee or require separate preference classifiers. SPREAD addresses this by unifying a conditional diffusion backbone with an adaptive, MGD-inspired guidance mechanism and an explicit diversity force into a single framework that operates across online, offline, and Bayesian MOO settings.

## Input / Output

In the online setting, SPREAD takes a differentiable multi-objective problem (decision space X ⊆ R^d, m objectives) and produces n = 200 approximate Pareto-optimal points; in the offline setting it consumes a pre-collected dataset D of 60,000 (x, F(x)) pairs and outputs 256 solutions; in the Bayesian setting it receives an expensive black-box F with a budget of 100 evaluations (100 initial + 20 iterations × 5 new points). The training data is generated via Latin hypercube sampling over the decision space, and the conditioning signal is the objective vector F(x) augmented by a strictly positive shift Ξ, while at inference the model is conditioned on the unshifted F(x_T) of each random initialization.

## Method

SPREAD trains a DiT-MOO noise-prediction network (a 3-block Diffusion Transformer with multi-head cross-attention on objective-value conditions, ~800k parameters) via the standard DDPM denoising loss on LHS-sampled points, but with a key conditioning trick: training labels are F(x) + Ξ with Ξ > 0, so Theorem 1 proves that sampling conditioned on F(x_T) yields a point that dominates x_T with probability ≥ 1 − τ. At each reverse diffusion step, the standard denoising update is augmented with a guidance term h̃_t that solves a sub-problem balancing (i) alignment with the MGD common-descent direction g_t^i and (ii) minimization of a Gaussian RBF repulsion Γ_t over pairwise objective-space distances, solved by 10 gradient-descent iterations with a fixed penalty ν_t = 10. A random perturbation δ_t scaled by per-sample factors γ_t^i (chosen via Theorem 2 to preserve common descent) is added for stochasticity, and step sizes are set by Armijo backtracking. The final Pareto set is the top-n non-dominated points across all timesteps, selected by crowding distance; the framework extends to offline MOO by replacing F with a learned surrogate and to Bayesian MOO by using GP surrogates with SBX-based local-optima escape.

## Results

On the online MOO setting (Table 1), SPREAD achieves the best or tied-best hypervolume on 9 of 11 problems, with particularly large margins on real-world tasks: RE21 (70.10 vs. 48.14 for PMGDA), RE33 (133.76 vs. 43.06), RE34 (243.15 vs. 210.07), and RE41 (1008.75 vs. 901.90). On the offline MOO benchmark (Table 4), SPREAD attains the best average rank in both task groups—3.50 on Synthetic and 1.83 on Real-World—outperforming the next-best generative method PGD-MOO (4.58 / 8.75) and all DNN-based baselines. In the Bayesian MOO setting, SPREAD achieves the lowest final log-hypervolume difference (LHD) on most of the nine benchmarks, converging particularly rapidly on DTLZ2, DTLZ5, and the 4-objective Car Side Impact (RE41) problem.

## Baselines & Comparisons

In the online setting, SPREAD matches PMGDA, HVGrad, and MOO-SVGD on the synthetic ZDT1–ZDT3 problems but decisively outperforms all four gradient-based baselines on real-world engineering tasks (e.g., RE21: 70.10 vs. 48.14 PMGDA, 43.65 HVGrad, 20.43 MOO-SVGD, 19.07 STCH; RE41: 1008.75 vs. 901.90 PMGDA, 936.17 HVGrad, 579.53 MOO-SVGD, 506.33 STCH). SPREAD also leads on Δ-spread (diversity) for most problems, indicating better Pareto front coverage. In the offline setting, SPREAD achieves the best average rank (3.50 Synthetic, 1.83 RE), beating the closest generative competitor PGD-MOO (4.58 / 8.75) and ParetoFlow (7.50 / 6.83), as well as all DNN-based methods (MM-IOM 5.67/4.33, MM-ICT 6.50/3.83, MM-RoMA 6.08/7.75, MM-COM 8.00/7.42, MM-TriMentoring 8.17/4.58, MH-PcGrad 5.75/6.92, MH-GradNorm 9.58/11.50). In the Bayesian MOO setting, SPREAD consistently outperforms CDM-PSL (the other diffusion-based method) and all acquisition-based (PDBO, qPOTS) and Pareto-set-learning-based (PSL-MOBO, SVH-PSL) baselines, achieving the lowest final LHD on most of the nine benchmarks including the 4-objective Car Side Impact problem.

## Relation to Prior Work

SPREAD sits at the intersection of two research threads: generative modeling for optimization (ParetoFlow's flow-matching, PGD-MOO's preference-guided diffusion, CDM-PSL's diffusion-based Bayesian MOO) and gradient-based Pareto set discovery (MGD, MOO-SVGD, HVGrad, STCH). Its core departure is integrating MGD descent directions as step-wise adaptive guidance signals *inside* the DDPM reverse diffusion process, rather than using them as a standalone optimizer or a separate classifier-based guidance module. The conditioning strategy—training the diffusion model on objective values shifted by a positive vector Ξ and then sampling conditioned on the unshifted values—provides a clean theoretical dominance guarantee (Theorem 1) that prior generative MOO methods lack. Architecturally, it adapts the DiT transformer backbone with multi-head cross-attention on objective conditions, and borrows the SBX escape mechanism and data augmentation from CDM-PSL for the Bayesian setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SPREAD offers a single, unified diffusion-based framework that handles online, offline, and Bayesian MOO with the same core sampling procedure, which is practically valuable given that these settings often require entirely different algorithms. The theoretical dominance guarantee (conditioning on F(x_T) yields a dominating sample with probability ≥ 1−τ) is a clean result, though it relies on a total-variation approximation assumption that may be hard to verify in practice. Key open questions include: (i) constraint handling, which the authors explicitly leave for future work; (ii) whether the training phase (1000 epochs) is a practical bottleneck compared to gradient-free baselines in truly expensive settings; and (iii) whether the MGD guidance + RBF repulsion combination generalizes to problems with discrete or mixed-variable decision spaces beyond the continuous benchmarks tested.
