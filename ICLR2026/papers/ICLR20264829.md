## Problem

Conventional fMRI encoding models flatten 3D brain volumes into 1D vectors, treating each voxel as an independent output. This discards the local spatial smoothness of neural activity and ties every model to a single subject's voxel grid, making cross-subject transfer impossible and data efficiency poor in realistic low-data regimes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264829/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a natural scene image (processed through CLIP ViT-B/16 to extract multi-scale features from transformer layers 3, 6, and the final embedding) and a 3D spatial coordinate in standardized MNI space, producing a single scalar fMRI response at that location. Because the response is defined as a continuous function over ℝ³, the trained model can be queried at arbitrary spatial resolutions (e.g., 0.5 mm grids) independent of the 1.8 mm acquisition grid, and training batches sample 2000 voxels per image from the ~13,000–15,000 available.

## Method

NRF models fMRI activity as an implicit neural representation: a two-component architecture where a CLIP-based image encoder G(M) produces a fused multi-scale embedding, and an 8-layer MLP predictor P (hidden dim 4096) takes the concatenation of G(M) with Fourier-encoded spatial coordinates γ(x) to output the scalar response at (x,y,z). Training uses a convex combination of MSE and cosine similarity loss, with Adam at lr=3e-3. For cross-subject adaptation, a pretrained NRF is fine-tuned end-to-end on a new subject's limited data, then K fine-tuned base models are combined via per-voxel least-squares regression (voxel-wise ensemble) to preserve subject-specific variability. The key mechanism is that conditioning on MNI coordinates lets the model exploit local smoothness within a subject and anatomical alignment across subjects, enabling transfer with as few as 20 images.

## Results

On the full NSD dataset (~9k training images), NRF achieves the best voxel-level Pearson correlation (0.358) and lowest MSE (0.345) among all compared methods, outperforming fWRF (0.343 / 0.361), MindSimulator-5 (0.355 / 0.385), and Linear Regression (0.323 / 0.353). In the critical low-data regime, NRF trained on only 200 images surpasses baselines trained on 800+ images, demonstrating substantial data efficiency from its continuous anatomical formulation. For cross-subject adaptation, the finetune-ensemble strategy with just 200 images (≈40 min scanning) achieves a Pearson of 0.227 on the held-out subject, approaching the 0.269 obtained from a full-data model and substantially exceeding the 0.180 from training from scratch.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NRF outperforms all three baselines on the primary voxel-level metrics: Pearson correlation of 0.358 vs. 0.343 (fWRF), 0.355 (MindSimulator-5), and 0.323 (Linear Regression), and MSE of 0.345 vs. 0.361, 0.385, and 0.353 respectively. On semantic-level decoding metrics, fWRF scores higher (e.g., Alex(2) 96.9% vs. NRF's 91.6%), but the authors attribute this to decoder bias—fWRF's less neurally accurate outputs happen to align more closely with MindEye2's learned feature distribution—and explicitly caution against using these as primary comparison criteria. The most striking advantage appears in the low-data regime: with only 200 training images, NRF exceeds the performance of fWRF and Linear Regression trained on 800+ images. For cross-subject transfer, the finetune-ensemble strategy with 200 images (Pearson 0.227) beats training from scratch (0.180) by 26% and recovers most of the gap to full-data performance (0.269), a capability none of the discrete baselines support. Statistical analysis (paired permutation tests, BH-FDR corrected) confirms NRF's superiority over both baselines is significant (p < 0.05) across all subjects and training sizes, with medium-to-large effect sizes (Cohen's d up to 0.76 vs. Linear Regression).

## Relation to Prior Work

NRF adapts the implicit neural representation (INR) paradigm from computer vision and graphics (NeRF, SIREN, DeepSDF) to fMRI encoding, replacing the standard 1D flattened-voxel formulation with a continuous function over MNI coordinates. It builds directly on the voxel-wise regression ensemble strategy from Gu et al. (2022) for personalized encoding, and uses Fourier positional encodings (Tancik et al., 2020) to inject spatial coordinates into an MLP predictor conditioned on CLIP image features. The key conceptual departure from prior encoding models (fWRF, BrainDIVE linear regression, MindSimulator) is treating anatomical position as a first-class input rather than an implicit index, enabling the model to exploit local smoothness and cross-subject alignment that discrete voxel-wise models discard.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NRF demonstrates that grounding encoding in continuous anatomical space yields substantial data efficiency (200 samples outperforming baselines trained on 800+) and enables practical cross-subject transfer with as few as 200 images (~40 min scanning), which is critical for real-world settings where large-scale fMRI collection is infeasible. The resolution-agnostic querying capability (trained at 1.8 mm, queried at 0.5 mm) opens a path toward unified multi-resolution brain models. Limitations include evaluation on only 4 subjects from a single dataset (NSD), restriction to visual cortex ROIs, and reliance on a frozen CLIP backbone; whether the continuous formulation scales to whole-brain encoding, non-visual stimuli, or larger subject pools remains open. The observation that fWRF outperforms NRF on some semantic decoding metrics despite lower voxel-level accuracy highlights the need for careful interpretation of decoder-based evaluation.
