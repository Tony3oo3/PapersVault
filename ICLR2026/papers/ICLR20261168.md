## Problem

Despite LLMs being actively deployed in clinical settings for evidence synthesis, their ability to replicate the individual conclusions of expert-written systematic reviews—when provided with the same underlying studies—remains uncharacterized. Prior evaluations either tested internal medical knowledge (fact recall without source access) or required costly expert review of long-form generated summaries, leaving a gap in scalable, controlled assessment of multi-document evidence reasoning and scientific skepticism.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261168/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each question takes the form 'Is [medical outcome] higher, lower, or the same when comparing [intervention] to [control]?' and is paired with the full-text (when open-access) or abstracts of the specific source studies the original SR authors used for that conclusion. The model must output exactly one of five fixed classes: higher, lower, no difference, uncertain effect, or insufficient data. Critically, the model never sees the systematic review itself—only the raw source articles—making the task a faithful reproduction of the SR authors' reasoning from primary evidence.

## Method

MedEvidence is a 284-question benchmark curated through a four-stage human pipeline: (1) selecting 100 Cochrane SRs (2014–2024) whose sources are all indexed in PubMed, (2) converting individual 'Main Results' statements into closed-form QA pairs with GRADE evidence-certainty labels, (3) identifying the specific studies contributing to each conclusion via the SR's analysis section, and (4) validating answerability by requiring ≥75% of meta-analytic weight to come from studies with explicit numerical data. Twenty-five LLMs (7B–671B, reasoning and non-reasoning, general and medical-finetuned) are evaluated zero-shot and few-shot under two prompt variants (basic and expert-guided with explicit study-design grading instructions), with multi-step refinement for inputs exceeding context windows. Key findings include: reasoning models do not consistently outperform non-reasoning counterparts, medical fine-tuning degrades accuracy relative to base models, performance degrades monotonically with token length, all models exhibit overconfidence and a failure to apply scientific skepticism toward low-quality or conflicting evidence, and accuracy gains plateau beyond ~70B parameters.

## Results

On MedEvidence (284 questions from 100 Cochrane systematic reviews), the best-performing frontier models achieve relatively low accuracy: DeepSeek V3 reaches 62.40% (95% CI: 56.35–68.45) and GPT-4.1 reaches 60.40% (95% CI: 54.30–66.50), both well below the best expert clinical accuracy threshold of ~75%. No model matches or surpasses expert performance, with frontier LLMs failing to replicate SR conclusions in at least ~37% of cases. Performance degrades monotonically with increasing token length, and models show the lowest recall on the 'uncertain effect' class, reflecting overconfidence and a lack of scientific skepticism toward low-quality evidence.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 25 LLMs, with the most informative comparisons being: (1) DeepSeek V3 (62.40%) outperforms its reasoning counterpart DeepSeek R1, showing that increased test-time compute does not reliably improve multi-document evidence synthesis; (2) GPT-4.1 (60.40%) trails DeepSeek V3 by ~2 percentage points, indicating no single frontier model dominates; (3) LLaMA 3.3 70B distilled from DeepSeek R1 outperforms the LLaMA 3.3 70B base model, suggesting that reasoning distillation can transfer some benefit even when the full reasoning model does not help; (4) medically fine-tuned models (HuatuoGPT-o1, OpenBioLLM) consistently underperform their base generalist counterparts, with fine-tuning degrading rather than improving accuracy on MedEvidence; (5) within model families, scaling from 7B to 70B yields substantial gains, but returns diminish sharply beyond 70B parameters (e.g., Llama 4, Qwen3 at larger sizes show minimal improvement); (6) all models, regardless of size or reasoning capability, exhibit similar failure modes—overconfidence, reluctance to select 'uncertain effect,' and inability to apply scientific skepticism to low-quality or conflicting sources—placing them uniformly below the expert threshold.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior LLM-for-systematic-review work (Wallace et al., O'Doherty et al., Reason et al.) that evaluated open-ended summary generation requiring expert oversight, and from medical fact-verification benchmarks (MedREQAL, HealthFC) that test internal knowledge recall rather than multi-source reasoning. It also extends conflict-handling benchmarks (ConflictingQA, ClashEval, KNOT) by grounding evaluation in peer-reviewed medical literature with expert-assigned evidence quality (GRADE) and source-level concordance. MedEvidence carves new ground by reformulating SR conclusion replication as a closed-form QA task where the model receives the exact source studies used by Cochrane reviewers, enabling automated, scalable evaluation without expert annotation of outputs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Given that LLM-based research assistants (Deep Research, Elicit, Open Evidence) are already deployed in clinical and regulatory settings (including an FDA pilot), the finding that frontier models fail to match expert SR conclusions in ~37% of cases—and that all models uniformly lack scientific skepticism toward low-quality evidence—raises urgent reliability concerns. The consistent degradation from medical fine-tuning and the absence of gains from reasoning or scale beyond 70B parameters suggest that current scaling paradigms are insufficient for evidence-critical synthesis. Open questions include whether architectural changes (beyond prompt engineering) can instill calibrated skepticism, whether the benchmark's selection bias (requiring all sources to be available) limits generalizability, and whether multi-expert consensus or temporal updates would shift the difficulty landscape.
