## Problem

Existing OOD detection methods are unreliable and inconsistent across models, datasets, and training seeds, with little theoretical guidance on when their assumptions hold. The paper identifies two gaps: (1) a lack of principled spectral OOD detectors with per-sample guarantees, and (2) an underexplored dependence of detector performance on feature quality (pretrained vs. non-pretrained representations), which explains many inconsistencies in prior comparisons.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20265198/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

GradPCA requires a trained neural network and its ID training set with class labels during an offline phase to compute C class-mean gradient vectors (with respect to a configurable parameter subset, defaulting to the last hidden layer). At inference, a single test image is forwarded through the network and its gradient is computed; the method outputs a continuous projection score in [0,1] (the fraction of centered gradient norm preserved by the principal subspace) and a binary OOD decision via a threshold δ. The method is formulated for scalar-valued outputs, so a max-logit aggregation is applied across the C output heads by default.

## Method

GradPCA exploits the NTK alignment phenomenon, under which gradients of ID inputs in well-trained networks concentrate in a low-rank subspace spanned by C class-specific directions, making the empirical NTK approximately block-diagonal. Instead of computing the full P×P gradient covariance, the method performs PCA on the C×C Gram matrix of centered class-mean gradients, lifting the top-k eigenvectors to obtain the principal components of the gradient covariance. At inference, the test input's centered gradient is projected onto this subspace, and the cosine of the angle between the gradient and its projection serves as the OOD score. The paper provides a theoretical framework (Theorems 4.1–4.5) establishing sufficient and necessary conditions for spectral OOD detection, a robust OOD certificate via Davis-Kahan perturbation bounds, and a sample complexity result, distinguishing GradPCA from prior gradient-based detectors that lack formal justification for their spectral choices.

## Results

GradPCA achieves the highest average AUC across six benchmarks (CIFAR-10/100 and ImageNet with both pretrained BiT-M and non-pretrained TIMM/BiT-S models), ranking within the top three in virtually every setting. On CIFAR-100 with pretrained features (BiT-M), GradPCA and other PCA-based methods (CoRP, Revisited PCA) lead in both FPR95 and AUROC, while on the non-pretrained TIMM model, the abnormality-based GAIA method excels. On ImageNet, GradPCA ranks first in the pretrained (BiT-M) setting and third in the non-pretrained (BiT-S) setting, where DICE performs best. GradPCA also ranks first on the ViT-B/16 benchmark, and its inference speed is on par with fast logits-based methods like MSP and ODIN on CIFAR.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GradPCA outperforms or matches all 13 baselines in average performance across the six main benchmarks, with the key differentiator being consistency: while individual methods like GAIA, DICE, or GradOrth may win on specific model/dataset combinations, GradPCA stays in the top three everywhere. On pretrained models (BiT-M for CIFAR-100, BiT-M for ImageNet), GradPCA leads or ties with other regularity-based methods (Mahalanobis, KNN, CoRP, Revisited PCA), while abnormality-based methods (ODIN, MSP, Energy, ReAct) degrade noticeably. On non-pretrained models (TIMM for CIFAR-100, BiT-S for ImageNet), the landscape flips: GAIA and DICE take the top spots, and GradPCA drops to third on ImageNet but remains competitive. Gradient-based baselines GradOrth and Projected Gradients (Wu et al.) show inconsistent performance—GradOrth is strong on BiT-S but underperforms on BiT-M—whereas GradPCA's NTK-based formulation provides more stable behavior. The paper's key finding is that no single method dominates across all settings; feature quality (pretrained vs. non-pretrained) is the primary factor determining which detector class succeeds, with regularity-based methods (including GradPCA) favored by pretrained features and abnormality-based methods favored by task-specific training.

## Relation to Prior Work

GradPCA bridges classical spectral analysis (PCA, kernel PCA) with the modern NTK alignment literature, applying PCA to gradient class-means as an efficient surrogate for the full gradient covariance. It departs from prior gradient-based OOD detectors (GradOrth, Wu et al.) by using the empirical NTK—the true dual of the gradient covariance—rather than ad hoc approximations, and from activation-space spectral methods (Mahalanobis, KNN) by operating in the higher-dimensional gradient space where NTK alignment induces a robust low-rank structure. The paper also introduces a taxonomy (regularity-based vs. abnormality-based) that repositions existing methods and clarifies why they succeed or fail under different feature-quality regimes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first per-sample OOD certificates for spectral detectors (sufficient and necessary conditions), a theoretical guarantee largely absent from the predominantly empirical OOD literature. The finding that feature quality (pretrained vs. non-pretrained) systematically determines which detector class succeeds—regularity-based methods thriving on pretrained features while abnormality-based methods do better from scratch—offers practical guidance that helps reconcile long-standing inconsistencies in OOD benchmarking. Open questions include scalability to very large class counts, extension beyond classification, and whether NTK alignment theory can yield even tighter detection guarantees.
