## Problem

Existing mathematical reasoning benchmarks (MathQA, MathVista, ChartQA) evaluate models on static images or text, while existing video benchmarks focus on perceptual or narrative understanding without the precision of multi-step mathematical inference. No prior benchmark captures the 'needle-in-a-multimodal-haystack' challenge of temporally extended mathematical reasoning, where critical cues are dispersed non-linearly across evolving diagrams, spoken explanations, and textual content over durations from seconds to over an hour.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264857/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each benchmark instance pairs a video (10s to 1hr, sourced from YouTube educational content) with a five-option multiple-choice question, and the model receives sampled video frames (32–768 depending on model), aligned subtitles, and optionally audio narration. The expected output is a selected answer option plus a chain-of-thought rationale; the benchmark additionally provides 4–10 expert-annotated reasoning steps per question (2,945 total) with temporal localization, enabling step-wise scoring (0–10) of model rationales against ground-truth inference traces. The Multi-Binary (MBin) evaluation variant decomposes each MCQ into four independent binary choices to reduce guessing and better discriminate model capability across scales.

## Method

VideoMathQA is a 420-sample benchmark constructed through a three-stage expert annotation pipeline (video selection, question-answer crafting, step-wise reasoning) requiring ~2–2.5 hours per sample by graduate-level science experts, with independent annotators at each stage for quality control. Questions are categorized along three reasoning types—Problem Focused (direct solving), Concept Transfer (applying demonstrated methods to novel problems), and Deep Instructional Comprehension (multi-step reasoning over extended partially-worked explanations)—and span ten mathematical domains (geometry, calculus, statistics, graph theory, chart reading, etc.). The evaluation framework employs four complementary strategies: standard MCQ, Multi-Binary decomposition, CoT vs. direct answering, and step-wise reasoning evaluation using Qwen-3-4B as an LLM judge that scores model rationales against annotated steps while crediting valid alternative reasoning paths. The paper evaluates 30 models (5 proprietary, 25 open-source across 5B–80B parameters) and finds that even the best model (GPT-o4-mini at 44.8% CoT MBin) trails human performance (80.7%) by ~36 points, with the largest gaps in visually demanding categories like topology and chart reading.

## Results

On VideoMathQA, GPT-o4-mini achieves the highest overall accuracy at 44.8% (CoT MBin +Sub), with a step-wise reasoning score of 6.9/10, while the best open-source model Qwen2.5-VL-72B reaches 28.6% (CoT MBin +Sub) with a step score of 5.0/10. Human annotators achieve 80.7% accuracy, confirming the benchmark is solvable but substantially harder than current models. Under direct answering (MCQ), the best model (GPT-o4-mini) scores 61.4% with subtitles, while random guessing yields only 17.4%, and all 30 evaluated models remain far below human performance across all ten mathematical domains and three video duration categories.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-o4-mini dominates all proprietary and open-source models, achieving 44.8% CoT MBin +Sub accuracy—16.2 points above the next-best proprietary model (Gemini-2.0-Flash at 24.8%) and 16.2 points above the best open-source model (Qwen2.5-VL-72B at 28.6%). Among open-source models, Qwen2.5-VL-72B and InternVL3-78B are the strongest, both outperforming several proprietary models including Claude-3.7-sonnet (19.3%) and GPT-4o (22.9%) in CoT MBin +Sub. Model size consistently improves performance: InternVL3 rises from 20.0% (8B) to 25.0% (38B) to 27.9% (78B) in CoT MBin +Sub, and Qwen2.5-VL improves from 18.3% (7B) to 27.1% (32B) to 28.6% (72B). However, newer smaller models can surpass older larger ones—InternVL3-38B (25.0%) outperforms LLaVA-Video-72B (18.6%) and LLaVA-OV-72B (18.1%). Subtitles provide consistent gains for reasoning-capable models (GPT-o4-mini: 42.1→44.8%; Qwen2.5-VL-72B: 24.5→28.6%) but minimal or inconsistent improvements for smaller models (<9B). In step-wise reasoning evaluation, GPT-o4-mini leads at 6.9/10, followed by Qwen2.5-VL-72B (5.0) and InternVL3-78B (4.9), while most 7B open-source models score between 2.7 and 3.9, indicating a substantial gap in multi-step mathematical reasoning fidelity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of two well-developed but previously separate benchmark lineages: video question answering (ActivityNet-QA, MVBench, Video-MME, EgoSchema, LongVideoBench) and multimodal mathematical reasoning (MathVista, MMMU, ChartQA, DynaMath). Prior video benchmarks target perceptual or narrative comprehension over short-to-long clips, while prior math benchmarks are confined to static images or single-turn text queries. VideoMathQA deliberately occupies the unexplored middle: temporally extended, cross-modal (vision + audio + text) mathematical problem solving with step-wise reasoning annotations, a setting that neither lineage has addressed. The multi-binary evaluation protocol and step-wise CoT scoring are methodological contributions that extend standard MCQ evaluation to better diagnose intermediate reasoning fidelity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a substantial and persistent gap: even the strongest proprietary model (GPT-o4-mini) achieves only 44.8% on the multi-binary setting versus 80.7% for humans, and performance collapses on visually demanding domains like topology (20%) and chart reading (40%). The step-wise reasoning annotations and error taxonomy enable fine-grained diagnosis that goes beyond final-answer accuracy, exposing that the dominant failure mode is problem misunderstanding rather than computation. Key limitations include the small dataset size (420 samples) and the high annotation cost (~2–2.5 expert-hours per sample), which constrain scalability; the authors note that semi-automatic annotation pipelines are needed to grow the benchmark. Open questions include whether the observed medium-video > short-video > long-video performance pattern reflects a fundamental attention bottleneck or can be addressed with better temporal modeling.
