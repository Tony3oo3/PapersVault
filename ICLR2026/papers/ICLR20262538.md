## Problem

Prior task vector (TV) research relies on opaque extraction procedures (hidden-state differencing, attention-head ablation) that are sensitive to injection layer, dependent on ICL representation quality, and offer no mechanistic explanation of how injected TVs actually influence computation. The paper addresses both the practical limitation of extraction-based TV construction and the fundamental gap in understanding the low-level (which components interact with TVs) and high-level (how TVs propagate through layers) mechanisms underlying TV effectiveness.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262538/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a frozen LLM and a zero-shot (or ICL) text prompt, and injects a learned d-dimensional task vector θ additively into residual-stream hidden states at user-specified layers L and token positions P. Training minimizes the negative log-likelihood of the correct label over a small training split (≤100 examples per epoch, up to 10 epochs with early stopping), optimizing only θ with AdamW (lr=0.001, weight decay=0.01). At inference, the single (or |L|×|P|) vector(s) are added to the corresponding hidden states and the model produces its standard output — a classification label or generated text — with no other architectural modification.

## Method

Instead of extracting TVs from ICL hidden states, the paper directly trains Learned Task Vectors (LTVs) by adding θ to hidden states at chosen layers/positions and optimizing via gradient descent, eliminating dependence on ICL representation quality and enabling injection at arbitrary positions, multiple layers, and even into ICL prompts. Mechanistically, the paper shows that at the low level, TVs steer predictions primarily through attention-head OV circuits (W_O^T W_V θ), with a small subset of 'key heads' — identified by saliency scoring and characterized by reduced attention-sink behavior and focus on final positions — driving most of the effect. At the high level, despite Transformer nonlinearities, TV propagation through subsequent layers is largely linear: a fitted transport matrix W_TV,(l) maps the injected TV to final-layer hidden-state changes, and its polar decomposition W_TV,(l) = Q_(l) Σ_(l) reveals that early-layer TVs are rotated toward task-relevant label subspaces while late-layer TVs are predominantly scaled in magnitude, with rotation attenuating and stretch dominating as injection depth increases.

## Results

On SST-2, LTV achieves 92.89% accuracy with only d trainable parameters, outperforming Prefix Tuning (85.67%, d params) and LoRA (91.63%, 2d params) while also being the fastest to train (0.049s mean latency, 503.87 GB FLOPs). In the primary TV comparison (Table 2), LTV at the baseline setting (P={-1}, L={16}) reaches 83.49% dataset-average accuracy, nearly doubling Vanilla TV (37.80%) and FV (37.30%), and matches or exceeds 8-shot ICL performance. LTV also generalizes to complex generation: on the Myopic dataset, LTV-rated responses score higher than both ICL and extracted TV baselines on a 10-point myopic-choice scale.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LTV decisively outperforms both extracted TV baselines: Vanilla TV (Hendel et al., 2023) and FV (Todd et al., 2024) achieve only ~37% dataset-average accuracy at the standard middle-layer injection, while LTV reaches 83.49%—a gap of over 45 percentage points. The advantage widens in late layers where extracted TVs degrade severely, and LTV remains the only method that benefits from multi-position or multi-layer injection (e.g., 86.43% with 5 positions vs. 31.88% for FV). As a PEFT method on SST-2, LTV (92.89%) beats Prefix Tuning (85.67%) by 7.2 points and LoRA (91.63%) by 1.3 points, using fewer or equal parameters and the lowest training FLOPs. In the complex Myopic generation task, LTV surpasses not only both extracted TV baselines but also 8-shot ICL itself, a feat neither Vanilla TV nor FV achieves. In Appendix D.4, LTV also outperforms the more complex State Vector and I2CL methods across all layers on SST-2, while requiring substantially less training time.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the task vector (TV) framework introduced by Hendel et al. (2023) and the function vector (FV) formulation of Todd et al. (2024), which extract TVs from ICL hidden states or attention head outputs. The key departure is replacing opaque extraction procedures with direct gradient-based training of Learned Task Vectors (LTVs), drawing on the LLM steering literature where vectors are optimized rather than distilled. Mechanistically, the paper builds on the OV circuit formalism from attention-head interpretability work and connects to the growing body of evidence for linear representations in Transformers (Marks & Tegmark, Park et al., Razzhigaev et al.), applying polar decomposition to characterize TV propagation as rotation plus stretch.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LTVs offer a simple, efficient alternative to complex TV extraction that matches or exceeds ICL accuracy and doubles as a competitive PEFT method with only d parameters, potentially lowering the barrier to task-specific adaptation. The mechanistic analysis—showing OV circuits as the primary low-level channel and linear rotation/stretch as the high-level propagation mode—provides the first systematic account of how injected TVs actually shape predictions, moving the field beyond the 'injection works' observation. Open questions include whether the linear propagation finding extends to multi-token generation tasks beyond SST-2, whether the rotation/stretch decomposition holds for tasks with richer label geometries, and whether LTV compositionality (demonstrated for translation/gender) generalizes to broader semantic operations.
