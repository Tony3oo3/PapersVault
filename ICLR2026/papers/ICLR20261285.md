## Problem

Offline bandit evaluation of generative models relies on publicly shared reward models (e.g., on Hugging Face) to score candidates, yet the adversarial robustness of this pipeline has been unexplored when the attacker perturbs the reward model weights *before* bandit training begins. Prior bandit attack literature focuses on training-time reward poisoning or data manipulation, leaving open whether a weak adversary with only access to logged data and reward model weights can hijack the bandit's arm-selection trajectory. This paper shows that in high-dimensional settings, even imperceptibly small weight perturbations suffice to completely redirect the bandit away from the optimal arm.

## Input / Output

The adversary receives K logged datasets {D_i} (i.i.d. samples from each arm's distribution P_i over R^d), the reward model parameters (a weight vector w ∈ R^d for the linear case, or a parameter vector θ ∈ R^D for a ReLU network), and the bandit configuration (K arms, horizon T). It produces a single perturbation vector δ (in R^d or R^D) of minimal ℓ2 norm such that, when added to the reward model, the bandit's trajectory is hijacked—either to a fully prescribed sequence of arms, or simply to avoid the optimal arm at specified timesteps. The logged data is consumed sequentially (the t_i-th pull of arm i reveals sample X_{t_i}^{(i)}), and the adversary cannot modify these samples or interfere with the bandit's execution; its only lever is the pre-training weight perturbation.

## Method

The core mechanism reduces the attack to a convex quadratic program: minimize ‖δ‖₂² subject to linear inequality constraints δᵀT_{i,t} > R_{i,t} that enforce the desired UCB score ordering at each targeted timestep. For linear reward models this reduction is exact; for ReLU networks, Neural Tangent Kernel theory guarantees that sufficiently wide networks are approximately linear in their parameters, yielding the same QP structure with D variables. Three attack variants trade off constraint count for flexibility: Full Trajectory imposes O(TK) constraints to force an exact arm sequence, Trajectory-Free uses O(T) constraints to merely block the optimal arm, and the proposed Online Score-Aware (OSA) heuristic iteratively adds a constraint only when the optimal arm would otherwise be selected, empirically reducing the effective constraint count to O(log T) and cutting attack time by orders of magnitude. Theoretically, the paper proves feasibility whenever d > (T−K)(K−1) and establishes that the optimal perturbation norm shrinks as Õ(d^{−1/2}), explaining why high-dimensional inputs like images are especially vulnerable. A simple defense—randomly shuffling a fraction of the logged data before bandit execution—partially mitigates the attack by breaking the deterministic sample-to-timestep correspondence the adversary exploits.

## Results

All three attack variants (OSA, Trajectory-Free, Full-Trajectory) achieve 100% attack success rate (ASR) on UCB across both linear and nonlinear reward models. The key high-dimensional effect is confirmed: the ℓ₂-norm of the required perturbation decreases as Õ(d^{-1/2}) with input dimension (Theorem 3.4), meaning smaller perturbations suffice in higher dimensions. On real-world Hugging Face evaluators (Image Reward and Aesthetic Model), there is approximately an 80% probability that ASR falls between 90–100% for a randomly selected prompt. The OSA heuristic reduces the number of effective constraints to O(log T), dramatically cutting attack computation time while preserving near-perfect success.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its pre-training reward-model attack against two corruption-robust bandit algorithms that assume an active adversary modifying rewards during training. Against the Fast-Slow algorithm (Lykouris et al., 2018), the paper's attack achieves 100% ASR with an ℓ₂-norm perturbation of only ≈0.3, for K∈{3,5}, T=1000, and d=1000, using a corruption parameter of 0.5 (a typical setting in the original paper's experiments). Against the ε-contamination algorithm (Niss & Tewari, 2020), a robust UCB variant, the attack also achieves 100% ASR with an even smaller ℓ₂-norm perturbation of ≈0.2, at a reasonable contamination level of ε=0.15, for T∈{100,1000}, K∈{3,5}, and d=1000. The critical distinction is that both robust baselines are designed to defend against an adversary who is online and actively corrupting reward feedback during the bandit interaction, whereas this paper's threat model requires the adversary to only perturb the reward model's weights before training begins—a strictly weaker adversary assumption. Despite this weaker assumption, the pre-training attack completely defeats both robust algorithms, demonstrating that their robustness guarantees do not extend to reward-model poisoning in high-dimensional offline settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the established line of adversarial attacks on bandits (reward poisoning by Jun et al., 2018; Liu & Shroff, 2019; context poisoning by Ma et al., 2018; Garcelon et al., 2020), which all assume an active adversary modifying rewards or data during online training. Here, the threat model is fundamentally different: the adversary perturbs the reward model's weights before any bandit training begins, in an offline setting with logged data. The reduction of the neural network attack to a convex QP relies on NTK theory (Jacot et al., 2018), which shows that sufficiently wide networks are approximately linear in their parameters. The paper also positions itself against robust bandit algorithms (Lykouris et al., 2018; Niss & Tewari, 2020) designed for active reward corruption, showing they remain vulnerable to this pre-training threat.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical stakes are significant: reward models used for evaluating generative models (Image Reward, Aesthetic Predictor) are publicly shared on Hugging Face, and the paper demonstrates that small, imperceptible weight perturbations can completely hijack bandit-based model selection. The theoretical result that perturbation norm decreases as O(d^{-1/2}) with input dimension is a striking high-dimensional effect with broad implications for any system combining high-dimensional data with learned reward functions. However, the proposed defense (shuffling logged data) is only partial, and a complete defense remains an open problem. The finding that robust bandit algorithms designed for active adversaries are still 100% vulnerable to pre-training attacks highlights a gap in the robustness literature that needs addressing.
