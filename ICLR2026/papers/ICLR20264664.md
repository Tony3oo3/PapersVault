## Problem

When multiple sensitive attributes are present, the number of intersectional subgroups grows exponentially (2^q), causing severe data sparsity in small subgroups and prohibitive computational cost for enforcing fairness across all of them. Existing subgroup fairness methods either fail to guarantee marginal fairness (fairness on each individual sensitive attribute) or require computationally expensive per-subgroup fairness computations, making them impractical for large q.

## Input / Output

The method takes tabular or text input features x (e.g., census demographics, DistilBERT embeddings) along with q binary sensitive attributes s ∈ {0,1}^q, and outputs a scalar prediction score in [0,1] via a single-layered MLP with sigmoid activation. The sensitive attributes can be non-binary in practice (e.g., sex with 3 levels in CIVILCOMMENTS), which are binarized into indicator subgroups. The model f takes both x and s as input, allowing group-conditional predictions.

## Method

DRAF introduces subgroup-subset fairness, enforcing distributional fairness (via supIPM) only on a pre-selected collection W of sufficiently large subgroup-subsets (unions of subgroups), which simultaneously covers active intersectional subgroups and marginal subgroups. The key technical contribution is the Doubly Regressing R² (DR²) surrogate: instead of computing IPM separately for each W ∈ W (requiring |W| discriminators), DR² uses a single discriminator g and a weight vector v on the unit sphere S^M to jointly regress the discriminator output g(f_i) against the weighted subgroup indicator v^T c_i, yielding a single differentiable fairness penalty. The DRAF algorithm alternates between gradient descent on f (minimizing classification loss + λ·z-DR²) and gradient ascent on g and v (maximizing z-DR²), where the Fisher z-transformation ensures numerical stability. Theoretically, the DR gap is proven to upper-bound supIPM, and empirically the learned v converges to a simplex vertex, making DR² nearly equivalent to supIPM in practice.

## Results

On the four main datasets (ADULT q=4, DUTCH q=2, CIVILCOMMENTS q=3, COMMUNITIES q=18), DRAF achieves competitive fairness-accuracy trade-offs on less-sparse datasets (ADULT, DUTCH, CIVILCOMMENTS) and clearly outperforms baselines on the extremely sparse COMMUNITIES dataset (1,175 of 1,180 subgroups have ≤1% of samples). For the equalized odds extension on ADULT, DRAF-EO achieves TPR(1)=0.0155 and FPR(1)=0.0010, substantially better than FairICP (0.0342 and 0.0150) at the same accuracy of 0.804. For multi-class classification on COMMUNITIES (5 classes), DRAF reaches 0.380 accuracy with MP(1)=0.054, outperforming Denis et al. (2024) which achieves 0.365 accuracy at MP(1)=0.056. The DR gap is proven to upper-bound supIPM (Eq. 3), and empirically the learned weight vector v converges to a simplex vertex, making DR² ≈ supIPM in practice.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against REG (marginal fairness regularization), DRAF performs comparably on ADULT and DUTCH but clearly outperforms on COMMUNITIES for both subgroup fairness (SP) and first-order marginal fairness (MP(1)), confirming that optimizing only marginal fairness is suboptimal when subgroups are sparse. Against GerryFair (GF, worst-case subgroup disparity), DRAF outperforms on COMMUNITIES for MP(1) and is comparable on SP; on CIVILCOMMENTS, DRAF slightly outperforms GF at small MP(1) values. Against Sequential (SEQ, post-processing barycenter mapping), DRAF dominates across all datasets since SEQ cannot control the fairness level (shown as fixed points rather than trade-off curves). For the equalized odds extension, DRAF-EO outperforms FairICP (Lai & Guan, 2025) on every metric: TPR(1) is 0.0155 vs 0.0342, FPR(1) is 0.0010 vs 0.0150, and subgroup TPR/FPR (STPR/SFPR) are 0.0056/0.0004 vs 0.0154/0.0043, all at identical accuracy of 0.804. For multi-class classification, DRAF achieves higher accuracy (0.380 vs 0.365) with marginally better fairness (MP(1)=0.054 vs 0.056) compared to Denis et al. (2024).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the adversarial fairness learning paradigm (Edwards & Storkey, 2016; Madras et al., 2018) by replacing the standard non-differentiable misclassification loss with a differentiable regression-based R² surrogate (DR²), enabling a single discriminator to handle all subgroup-subsets simultaneously. It builds on IPM-based distributional fairness measures (Kim et al., 2022, sIPM) and the fairness gerrymandering problem (Kearns et al., 2018a,b), but departs from prior subgroup fairness methods (GerryFair, Bayesian approaches) by introducing 'subgroup-subset fairness' as a computationally tractable middle ground between full intersectional fairness (exponential in q) and marginal-only fairness. The key conceptual departure is the Theorem 4.1 identity linking IPM to a modified R², which provides the mathematical foundation for the doubly-regressing surrogate.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in scalability: DRAF requires only a single discriminator regardless of the number of subgroup-subsets, making it feasible for settings with q=18 sensitive attributes (COMMUNITIES) where prior methods become intractable. The theoretical guarantee that the DR gap upper-bounds supIPM, combined with the empirical finding that the learned weight vector converges to a simplex vertex (making DR² ≈ supIPM in practice), provides both a safety net and practical interpretability. Limitations include the need to manually tune γ (minimum subgroup size threshold) and the fact that fairness is only guaranteed for 'active' subgroups, leaving tiny subgroups unprotected. The authors suggest ANOVA-like decomposition of subgroup fairness into low-order components as a promising future direction for improved stability and interpretability.
