## Problem

Current RLVR approaches for LLM reasoning depend on human-curated problem-answer pairs and domain-specific reward engineering, creating a scalability bottleneck that limits the diversity of reasoning challenges models can learn from. While self-play on games has historically driven breakthroughs in AI (TD-Gammon, AlphaGo), applying multi-turn, multi-agent self-play to develop transferable reasoning in LLMs remained largely unexplored, with prior attempts limited to simple word games, offline updates, or single-turn tasks.

## Input / Output

At each turn, the model receives a structured text observation of the complete game state (with historical actions concatenated for partially observable games like Kuhn Poker), a player role indicator via system prompt, and the game identity. It generates a single multi-token response formatted as <think>reasoning<answer>action</answer>, where the reasoning trace externalizes chain-of-thought and the action is extracted to update the game state. The final output is a fine-tuned model whose improved reasoning transfers to out-of-domain benchmarks (math, science) without any domain-specific training data.

## Method

SPIRAL uses a single shared policy π_θ to play both roles in two-player zero-sum games (TicTacToe, Kuhn Poker, Simple Negotiation), with role conditioning via system prompts enabling distinct strategies per position. Training employs a fully online, multi-turn, multi-agent RL system with a distributed actor-learner architecture (built on Oat/IMPALA) where parallel actors sample self-play trajectories using vLLM and TextArena, while a centralized learner performs synchronous policy updates. The key algorithmic contribution is Role-conditioned Advantage Estimation (RAE), which maintains separate exponential-moving-average baselines b_{G,p} for each game G and role p to reduce the high variance of multi-agent REINFORCE, preventing a failure mode called 'thinking collapse' where models abandon reasoning traces after ~200 steps. Rewards are sparse and zero-sum (only at game termination: R_0 = ρ(s_T), R_1 = −R_0), forcing robust strategy development. Multi-game training combines complementary cognitive skills (spatial, probabilistic, strategic) synergistically, achieving up to 10.5% improvement across 8 reasoning benchmarks while outperforming SFT on 25,000 expert trajectories.

## Results

Multi-game SPIRAL training (TicTacToe, Kuhn Poker, Simple Negotiation) achieves up to 10.5% absolute improvement across 8 reasoning benchmarks without any domain-specific training data. On Qwen3-4B-Base, average performance rises from 34.0% to 44.5% (+10.5%), and on Qwen3-8B-Base from 39.5% to 49.6% (+10.1%). Gains are consistent across model families: Octothinker-8B-Base improves +8.0% (25.8%→33.8%), Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct +2.0% (23.9%→25.9%), and even the already-RLVR-trained DeepSeek-Distill-Qwen-7B gains +1.4% (60.4%→61.8%). SPIRAL consistently outperforms supervised fine-tuning on 25,000 expert game trajectories across all 8 benchmarks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SPIRAL-Multi outperforms SFT on 25,000 expert game trajectories (generated by Qwen3-32B) across all 8 reasoning benchmarks for every model tested, with the largest gaps on Qwen3-4B-Base (44.5% vs. 39.7% average) and Qwen3-8B-Base (49.6% vs. 46.1%). Fixed-opponent training against Mistral-Small-3 or Gemini-2.0-Flash-Lite yields substantially smaller gains (e.g., Qwen3-4B-Base drops to 29.6% with Mistral and 33.4% with Gemini on Kuhn Poker alone), and Table 3 shows fixed-opponent win rates rising from 0% to 62.5% (exploitation of static strategies) while self-play maintains a stable 50–52% win rate, confirming that the adaptive curriculum—not merely game mechanics or spurious rewards—drives genuine reasoning improvement. Doubling SFT data to 52k trajectories produces no additional gain (39.7% vs. 39.7% on Qwen3-4B-Base), confirming that SPIRAL's advantage stems from the RL dynamic rather than dataset size. On out-of-distribution games, multi-game SPIRAL achieves 59.5% average win rate against Gemini-2.0-Flash, outperforming all single-game specialists (best: 52.9%) and the base model (18.7%). Even for the already-RLVR-trained DeepSeek-Distill-Qwen-7B, SPIRAL improves the average from 60.4% to 61.8% while SFT causes a regression to 58.3%.

## Relation to Prior Work

SPIRAL extends the RLVR paradigm (DeepSeek-R1, OpenAI o1) by replacing human-curated problem-answer pairs with self-play on zero-sum language games, building on the distributed actor-learner architecture from OAT/IMPALA and the REINFORCE policy gradient. It departs from prior LLM self-play work (SPAG, Absolute Zero, SPC) which was limited to offline updates, single-turn tasks, or LoRA adaptations, by implementing fully online, full-parameter, multi-turn multi-agent RL with a shared policy playing both roles. The key methodological novelty is Role-conditioned Advantage Estimation (RAE), a variance-reduction technique that addresses the non-stationarity inherent in self-play by maintaining role-specific baselines, which the authors show is critical to prevent a 'thinking collapse' failure mode absent from single-agent RL literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SPIRAL offers a path toward autonomous reasoning development that eliminates the scalability bottleneck of human-curated training data, with the open-source framework making the approach reproducible. The finding that even simple games (TicTacToe, Kuhn Poker) develop transferable cognitive patterns (case-by-case analysis, expected value calculation, pattern recognition) that improve math benchmarks by up to 10.5% suggests a new axis for reasoning training beyond domain-specific RLVR. Key limitations include reliance on hand-designed game environments, substantial compute requirements (8 H100 GPUs for ~25 hours), and the open question of whether gains plateau or extend to more complex, sparse-reward environments. The paper also raises the question of whether the observed transfer reflects genuine reasoning development or exploitation of benchmark-specific patterns, since evaluation is strictly zero-shot on academic benchmarks.
