## Problem

The rapid pace of graph machine learning research has led to the premature consolidation of universal claims about oversmoothing, oversquashing, homophily/heterophily, and long-range tasks. These beliefs conflate distinct phenomena and are stated as inescapable truths, creating conceptual ambiguities that prevent researchers from formulating precise research questions and cause widespread misunderstandings in the field.

## Input / Output

The empirical component takes standard graph inputs (adjacency matrix and node feature matrix from the Cora dataset) and runs multiple DGN architectures (GCN, GAT, GIN, SAGE) under two feature-transform variants (W and 2W) across 50 random seeds. The outputs are Dirichlet Energy and Rayleigh Coefficient measurements tracking embedding collapse as a function of depth, alongside formal counterexamples (toy bipartite graphs, grid graphs, star graphs) that refute each of nine identified common beliefs.

## Method

The paper identifies and groups nine common beliefs into three categories (OSM, OSQ, homophily-heterophily) and refutes each with simple yet formally sufficient counterexamples. For OSM, it demonstrates that the phenomenon is neither universal (GIN's DE explodes rather than collapses) nor uniquely measurable (DE and RQ yield contradictory trends for the same model), and that a minor rescaling from W to 2W can reverse collapse behavior across architectures. For OSQ, it formally separates the ambiguous term into 'computational bottlenecks' (architecture-intrinsic, defined as the multiset size of the computational tree |M_v^K| counting all distinct walks) and 'topological bottlenecks' (graph-intrinsic, measured via curvature or spectral gap), showing via grid graphs that low Jacobian sensitivity can arise without any topological bottleneck. For homophily, it constructs a fully heterophilic bipartite graph where a single-layer sum-based DGN achieves perfect classification via degree information, and a highly homophilic graph where the task inherently requires long-range propagation, breaking the assumed homophily-performance link.

## Results

The paper's primary empirical finding (Figure 1, Cora, 50 seeds) demonstrates that oversmoothing (OSM) is neither universal nor metric-independent: GCN's Dirichlet Energy (DE) collapses under the vanilla AXW update but explodes under a simple 2W rescaling, while its Rayleigh Quotient (RQ) remains essentially flat in both cases, indicating rescaling rather than true collapse. GIN's DE explodes instead of collapsing in the vanilla setting, and GAT/GraphSAGE show RQ decay at roughly linear rates where DE behavior is less clear. These observations, combined with conceptual counterexamples (e.g., a fully heterophilic bipartite graph perfectly classified by a 1-layer DGN, a grid graph with no topological bottleneck yet rapidly growing computational bottleneck), refute all nine common beliefs catalogued in Table 1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines; instead, it uses existing architectures as illustrative cases to challenge universal claims. GCN (Kipf & Welling, ICLR 2017) is shown to exhibit DE collapse under vanilla message passing but not under a 2W rescaling, and its RQ stays flat throughout, undermining the narrative that OSM is an inevitable property of all DGNs. GIN (Xu et al., ICLR 2019) is highlighted as a counterexample where DE actually increases (explodes) rather than decreases, demonstrating that changing the aggregation function can reverse the apparent OSM trend. Together, these two architectures illustrate that conclusions about OSM depend heavily on the specific model, the choice of stability metric (DE vs. RQ), and seemingly innocuous hyperparameters such as the feature-transform scaling, rather than reflecting a universal phenomenon.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper does not build on a single prior line of work but rather positions itself as a critical survey of the entire landscape of oversmoothing, oversquashing, homophily/heterophily, and long-range dependency research in graph neural networks. It departs from the prevailing narrative by showing that universal claims (e.g., 'OSM is a property of all DGNs,' 'oversquashing equals topological bottleneck') rest on restrictive assumptions or conflate distinct phenomena. The key conceptual repurposing is splitting the overloaded term 'oversquashing' into two fundamentally different notions—computational bottlenecks (intrinsic to the message-passing architecture) and topological bottlenecks (intrinsic to graph connectivity)—and demonstrating via simple counterexamples (grid graphs, star graphs, bipartite graphs) that they do not always coexist. Empirically, the paper reuses standard architectures (GCN, GAT, GIN, GraphSAGE) and metrics (Dirichlet Energy, Rayleigh Quotient) under slightly different hyperparameters (W vs. 2W) to show that OSM observations are metric- and architecture-dependent rather than universal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This paper has the potential to redirect a significant portion of GNN research effort by challenging the default narrative that oversmoothing causes performance degradation and that oversquashing is a single, well-defined problem. The practical implication is that researchers should focus on node-embedding separability rather than OSM metrics per se, and should design separate benchmarks for computational versus topological bottlenecks rather than conflating them under 'oversquashing.' Limitations include the reliance on toy counterexamples and a single real dataset (Cora) for the main empirical demonstration, and the paper does not propose new architectures or training procedures. Open questions include: how to formally define and measure separability in a label-aware way, how to construct controlled benchmarks that isolate computational from topological bottlenecks, and whether the task-dependent view of homophily can be operationalized into a general design principle for DGNs.
