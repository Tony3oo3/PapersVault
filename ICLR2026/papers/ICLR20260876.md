## Problem

Vision-proprioception policies in robotic manipulation exhibit inconsistent generalization: some studies report clear improvements from adding proprioception, while others (e.g., Octo) find that policies trained with proprioception perform worse than vision-only policies. This paper identifies the root cause: during motion-transition phases where the robot must locate targets, the vision modality is suppressed because concise proprioceptive signals yield faster loss reduction and dominate gradient optimization, leaving the policy unable to generalize to new object positions.

## Input / Output

The policy consumes a 5-timestep observation history (H=5) consisting of RGB images (from wrist-mounted or front cameras) and 7-dimensional proprioceptive signals (6D Cartesian gripper pose plus a continuous gripper opening value in [0,1]). It outputs a sequence of 9 actions (L=9) encoding gripper position, orientation, and opening commands. For dual-arm setups, each arm's policy is trained independently with its own proprioceptive stream and camera observations.

## Method

GAP (Gradient Adjustment with Phase-guidance) operates in two stages. First, it segments expert demonstration trajectories into motion-consistent phases using Change Point Detection on a custom distance metric combining position, orientation, and gripper-opening consistency, then trains an LSTM on temporal proprioceptive differences to predict a continuous transition probability ρ for each timestep. Second, during behavior cloning, it scales the proprioception chunk's backward gradient by λ·(1−ρ), so that timesteps with high transition probability receive reduced proprioceptive gradient updates, forcing the network to learn visual features for target localization. The adjustment is applied only during the first 50 of 100 training epochs to avoid destabilizing convergence. The approach is architecture-agnostic, working with MLP, diffusion (UNet), and transformer policy heads, and is compatible with VLA models like Octo by applying gradient modulation to the proprioception feature extractor before the transformer.

## Results

On Meta-World, GAP achieves average success rates of 94.2% (pick-place), 94.2% (assembly), 91.4% (disassemble), 73.6% (push-wall), and 70.4% (bin-picking), outperforming the vision-only baseline by 2.4–10.4 points across tasks. On RoboSuite, GAP reaches 91.0% (put hammer into drawer), 77.6% (stack), and 53.0% (threading), improving over vision-only by 5.2–9.8 points. In real-world settings, GAP achieves 20/20, 17/20, 13/20 (one-arm) and 18/20, 16/20, 15/20 (dual-arm), consistently surpassing both vision-only and all baselines. Under out-of-distribution object placement shifts, GAP maintains 88% (assembly) and 67% (bin-picking) versus 62% and 32% for simple concatenation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GAP outperforms all four main baselines (MS-Bot, Aux, Mask, and simple Concatenation) across every simulated and real-world task. MS-Bot (Feng et al., 2024), which uses semantic stage tokens for dynamic fusion, is the strongest competitor but still trails GAP by 3–10 points on tasks with frequent motion transitions (e.g., push-wall: 66.2% vs. 73.6%; threading: 51.2% vs. 53.0%). The Aux baseline (Fu et al., 2024), which adds a next-frame prediction loss to strengthen vision, underperforms GAP by 12–22 points on tasks requiring proprioceptive precision (e.g., threading: 45.4% vs. 53.0%; put hammer into drawer: 71.2% vs. 91.0%). Mask (Liu et al., 2024), which randomly drops proprioceptive input, only marginally improves over concatenation and loses to GAP by 5–15 points. For trajectory decomposition, GAP's CPD-based approach (Table 6) surpasses both HDBSCAN (Sun et al., 2024) and CoTPC (Jia et al., 2024) by 4–15 points, and even slightly exceeds human-labeled transitions. For fusion architectures (Table 4), GAP applied to Concatenation, Summation, and FiLM (Perez et al., 2018) all outperform their unadjusted counterparts and the vision-only baseline, confirming method-agnostic applicability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of modality temporality in manipulation (Feng et al. 2024, He et al. 2025) and multi-modal optimization analysis (Huang et al. 2022, Fan et al. 2023), but carves new ground by providing a mechanistic, optimization-level explanation for why vision-proprioception policies underperform vision-only policies during motion-transition phases. Rather than treating the inconsistent observations in the literature (improvement vs. degradation) as unexplained, the paper identifies proprioception's gradient dominance as the specific cause and proposes a targeted fix. It departs from prior dynamic-fusion approaches like MS-Bot (which uses semantic stages) and from auxiliary-loss or masking strategies by operating at the gradient level with fine-grained, phase-conditioned modulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a clear diagnostic framework (intervention experiments + gradient analysis) that practitioners can use to understand when and why multi-modal manipulation policies fail, and GAP provides a simple, architecture-agnostic plug-in that consistently recovers and exceeds vision-only performance across simulated and real-world settings, one-arm and dual-arm, and both conventional and VLA models. The limitation to single-embodiment training and the open question of how gradient adjustment interacts with cross-embodiment or large-scale pre-training datasets remain important gaps. Broader implications include whether similar phase-conditioned gradient modulation could address modality competition in other multi-sensory settings (e.g., vision-tactile, vision-force).
