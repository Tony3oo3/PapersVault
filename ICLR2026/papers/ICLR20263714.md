## Problem

Existing LMM inference frameworks deploy multimodal models as monolithic workloads on a single accelerator, ignoring the inherently modular structure of LMMs (vision encoder, projector, language backbone, audio) and the heterogeneous accelerators (NPU, GPU, DSP) available on modern edge SoCs. These frameworks also inherit server-side assumptions about separate CPU/GPU memory, are inefficient under unified memory architectures, and rarely account for the tight power budgets of battery-powered devices, leaving hardware underutilized and energy consumption unoptimized.

## Input / Output

The system consumes RGB images pre-processed to fixed resolutions (448×736 for Qwen2-VL, 384×384 for LLaVA-OneVision) to satisfy NPU static-shape constraints, with multi-frame inputs merged via average temporal pooling; in on-demand low-power mode only a single frame is captured. Audio waveforms are processed by a standalone Whisper-base model for speech-to-text, and text prompts drive the LLM decoder. Outputs are text responses from the language model and synthesized speech via Piper TTS, with only minimal intermediate artifacts (embedding vectors or short text strings) passed between pipeline stages.

## Method

NANOMIND decomposes each LMM into independently executable modular bricks and dynamically offloads each to its optimal accelerator: the SigLip vision encoder runs on the NPU (in RKNN format, FP16 or INT8), while the quantized LLM (W4A16 GGUF) decodes on the GPU using custom fused dequant-GEMM OpenCL kernels and a linear-attention variant that eliminates explicit T×T attention matrix construction. A Token-Aware Buffer Manager (TABM) coordinates a shared ring buffer in unified DRAM, enabling zero-copy embedding transfer from NPU to GPU without CPU-managed copies or redundant memory movement. A three-state battery-aware scheduler (driven by a dedicated PMU) arbitrates between full parallel acceleration, proportional throttling of frame rate and memory bandwidth, and a critical On-Demand Cascade mode where each module follows a load→execute→release lifecycle triggered by events, reducing average power to 0.375 W and enabling ~18.8 hours of operation on a 2000 mAh battery. The hardware platform is a custom PCB around an RK3566 SoC with parallel LPDDR4x modules and a dedicated power management unit, though the framework is designed to be SoC-agnostic.

## Results

NANOMIND reduces end-to-end energy consumption by 42.3% compared to mainstream edge frameworks and devkits. In its on-demand low-power mode, the prototype runs LLaVA-OneVision-Qwen2-0.5B with a camera for 18.8 hours on a single 2,000 mAh battery at an average draw of 0.375 W. For Qwen2-VL-2B-Instruct (4-bit), NANOMIND achieves throughput comparable to Jetson Nano running NanoVLM with CUDA (~35.7 tok/s) while reducing end-to-end latency by 36.2% relative to the Orange Pi 5 Ultra using the official rkllm framework.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against llama.cpp, NANOMIND achieves substantially lower memory usage and CPU utilization during embedding transfer (Figure 7a), and avoids the memory overhead of layer-wise GPU offloading that inflates memory by up to 90% (Table 1). Against the official Rockchip rkllm on Orange Pi 5 Ultra (RK3588), NANOMIND on the weaker RK3566 SoC reduces end-to-end latency by 36.2% through cross-accelerator offloading (NPU for vision, GPU for LLM). Against NanoVLM on Jetson Nano, NANOMIND matches throughput (~35.7 tok/s) despite using a significantly less powerful SoC. In the fused dequant-GEMM kernel comparison (Figure 7c), NANOMIND's custom OpenCL kernel achieves the highest throughput, with PowerInfer-2 close behind, while MLC-LLM lags—likely due to weaker OpenCL support on Qualcomm GPUs. Overall, NANOMIND wins on energy (42.3% reduction), latency (36.2% vs. rkllm), and memory efficiency, while matching peak throughput of the more powerful Jetson Nano platform.

## Relation to Prior Work

NANOMIND builds on llama.cpp's GGUF format and OpenCL kernel infrastructure but fundamentally restructures the execution model: rather than treating an LMM as a monolith on a single accelerator (as llama.cpp, MLC-LLM, and PowerInfer-2 do), it decomposes the model into vision, projector, language, and audio modules and maps each to its best-suited accelerator (NPU, GPU, CPU) under a unified-memory architecture. This departs from prior heterogeneous-offloading work (llm.npu, PowerInfer-2) that partitions at the operator or neuron level within a single LLM, and from server-oriented frameworks that assume separate CPU/GPU memory spaces. The hardware-software co-design dimension—custom PCB with parallel LPDDR4x, a dedicated PMU, and fused low-bit GEMM kernels—has no direct counterpart in the software-only frameworks it compares against.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates a practical, reproducible path to fully offline multimodal inference on a sub-$12 SoC with ~19 hours of battery life, which is directly relevant to the growing demand for private, low-connectivity AI on wearables and small devices. However, the evaluation is limited to small models (0.5B–2B parameters) and primarily Rockchip hardware; Qualcomm support is in-progress and Apple's closed ecosystem is excluded, leaving open whether the modular cross-accelerator strategy generalizes to larger LMMs or to SoCs with different NPU/GPU capabilities. A key open question is whether future LMMs trained end-to-end (rather than with separately fine-tuned components) will still admit the clean modular decomposition that NANOMIND exploits.
