## Problem

Conventional entropy regularization, a standard technique in policy-gradient RL (PPO, SAC, A3C), provides strong gains in robotic and games tasks but yields weak to no improvements in LLM-RL training. The paper identifies the root cause theoretically: LLMs operate over an extremely large token vocabulary (~100k+) with highly sparse optimal responses, so the entropy bonus introduces a large bias term O(H log(|A|/|A*_H|^{1/H})) that overwhelms its optimization benefits. This creates a gap between the theoretical justification for entropy regularization and its observed ineffectiveness in LLM post-training.

## Input / Output

The method takes text queries (math problems from datasets like MATH, OpenR1-math, or DeepMath) as input and produces free-form text solutions as output, with rewards assigned based on verifiable correctness of the final answer. The LLM policy operates over a vocabulary of hundreds of thousands of tokens per step, with maximum response lengths of 3072–7168 tokens. A key hyperparameter is the clamping percentage p (e.g., 0.25–0.33), which determines the fraction of the vocabulary excluded from entropy computation, effectively reducing the token space to the top-probability tokens at each decoding step.

## Method

AEnt modifies the standard entropy-regularized RL objective in two ways. First, it replaces the full-vocabulary entropy with a clamped entropy computed over a re-normalized policy defined on a size-reduced, input-dependent token space A(s) consisting of the top (1−p) percent probability tokens, which smooths the policy only over plausible responses and reduces the entropy-induced bias from the vast non-optimal token space. Second, it adaptively adjusts the entropy coefficient λ during training via a projection-based update rule that increases λ when clamped entropy falls below a lower bound and decreases it when entropy exceeds an upper bound, preventing both entropy collapse and entropy blow-up. The full objective is L_AEnt(θ;λ) = L_PO(θ) + λ·H̃(π_θ), where L_PO is a standard policy optimization objective (GRPO in experiments). Theoretical analysis shows that without entropy control, policy entropy collapse implies learning stagnancy (Proposition 1), and that conventional entropy regularization's bias grows with response space size and optimality sparsity (Proposition 2), motivating the clamping mechanism.

## Results

AEnt consistently outperforms both GRPO and EntReg (conventional entropy regularization) across two training settings (Qwen2.5-math-1.5b on MATH, and DeepSeek-R1-distilled-Qwen-1.5b on OpenR1-math) and six math-reasoning benchmarks. In setting (a), AEnt achieves 0.552 on MATH-Hard, 0.217 on AIME24, and 0.581 on AMC, surpassing GRPO (0.524, 0.192, 0.550) and EntReg (0.546, 0.167, 0.562). In setting (b), AEnt reaches 0.813 on MATH-Hard, 0.392 on AIME24, and 0.825 on AMC, again leading both baselines. AEnt wins on 5 out of 6 benchmarks in both settings, with the only exception being MATH-500 in setting (a) where it scores 0.750 vs. GRPO's 0.756.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares AEnt against two baselines: GRPO (the standard group-relative policy optimization without entropy control) and EntReg (GRPO augmented with a fixed-coefficient conventional entropy bonus). AEnt outperforms GRPO on 5 of 6 benchmarks in both settings, with the largest gains on AIME24 (0.217 vs. 0.192 in setting a; 0.392 vs. 0.367 in setting b) and AMC (0.581 vs. 0.550; 0.825 vs. 0.769). Against EntReg, AEnt also wins on 5 of 6 benchmarks, most notably on AIME24 where EntReg actually underperforms GRPO (0.167 vs. 0.192 in setting a), while AEnt achieves 0.217. The sole exception is MATH-500 in setting (a), where GRPO edges out AEnt by 0.006 (0.756 vs. 0.750). The paper also reports that AEnt reaches the GRPO score threshold faster in wall-clock time (35h vs. 57h in setting a; 186h vs. 237h in setting b), indicating that the entropy control accelerates early learning despite a slightly higher per-step compute cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classical entropy-regularized RL lineage (Williams & Peng 1991; A3C; SAC) into the LLM-RL setting, where it has been largely neglected in favor of plain GRPO/PPO-clip variants. The key departure is a theoretical diagnosis: the entropy bias term scales as H·log(|A|/|A*_H|^{1/H}), which is prohibitively large for LLM vocabularies (~10^5 tokens) with sparse optimal responses, explaining why the standard bonus provides negligible gains. The proposed AEnt method modifies the entropy computation by re-normalizing the policy over a top-p token subset (clamping) and adaptively adjusting the coefficient, building on the policy-gradient convergence theory of Mei et al. (2020) and Klein et al. (2023) while addressing a gap those analyses leave unaddressed for finite-horizon, concatenation-transition MDPs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first clear theoretical explanation for why entropy regularization—a cornerstone of deep RL—fails in LLM training, and offers a simple, plug-and-play fix (clamped entropy + adaptive coefficient) that consistently improves over GRPO and standard EntReg across six math benchmarks. However, the empirical gains are modest (typically 1–3 percentage points), the method is validated only on 1.5B–7B models and math reasoning, and the authors acknowledge the absence of a theoretical analysis for the clamped entropy itself. Open questions include whether the clamping percentage and token-space selection generalize to non-math tasks, larger models, and whether representation-space entropy (rather than token-level) could further reduce the dimensionality problem.
