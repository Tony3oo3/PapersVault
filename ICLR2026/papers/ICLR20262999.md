## Problem

Existing code similarity metrics—whether token-based, structure-based, embedding-based, or execution-trace-based—evaluate surface-level syntax or output equivalence rather than the underlying algorithmic logic. In the LLM-AAD paradigm, where core design ideas are implicitly encoded in generated code, this limitation makes it impossible to distinguish genuine algorithmic innovation from mere syntactic variation, undermining both search diversity and systematic analysis of AI-generated algorithms.

## Input / Output

The method consumes two algorithm code implementations by actually executing them on sampled problem instances from a distribution P^I and from sampled starting points S_I, recording the sequence of intermediate (partial) solutions at each iteration. The output is a scalar behavioral similarity score in [0,1] computed as the expected DTW-normalized alignment between the two resulting PSTrajs. When applied to a population of algorithms, it yields a pairwise similarity matrix that supports hierarchical clustering by problem-solving behavior. The method handles both discrete (permutation, categorical) and continuous solution types, with distance defined as normalized edit distance or Euclidean distance respectively.

## Method

BehaveSim defines a problem-solving trajectory (PSTraj) as the ordered sequence of intermediate solutions x_0, x_1, …, x_T produced by an iterative algorithm on a given instance, then measures similarity between two PSTrajs via Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) over pairwise solution distances, normalized by the shorter trajectory length. The key insight is that DTW captures local behavioral alignment even when trajectories differ in length or contain temporal shifts, unlike mean pairwise distance or cosine similarity which capture different aspects. The final BehaveSim score is an expectation over both the problem-instance distribution and the starting-point distribution, making it robust to instance-specific artifacts. For integration into LLM-AAD, BehaveSim is used either as a niche-assignment criterion in FunSearch's multi-island database (placing new algorithms on the island with highest average behavioral similarity) or as a secondary objective in EoH's dominance-dissimilarity parent selection, both promoting behavioral diversity during evolutionary search.

## Results

On the code similarity benchmark, BehaveSim achieves a perfect 1.00 similarity score on Type-3 pairs (syntactically different but behaviorally identical algorithms), while all existing metrics range from 0.38 to 0.99. On the three LLM-AAD tasks, FunSearch+BehaveSim reduces the TSP top-1 gap from 25.22% to 17.37% and the ASP top-1 gap from 5.84% to 5.24%, while EoH+BehaveSim further improves TSP top-1 to 16.55% and ASP top-1 to 5.33%. On CPP, both augmented methods achieve the best top-1 sum of radii (2.6293 and 2.6263 vs. 2.6259 for FunSearch and 2.5724 for EoH).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two categories of baselines. For code similarity measurement, BehaveSim is evaluated against 10+ existing metrics (ROUGE, BLEU, CrystalBLEU, AST, CodeBLEU, CodeBERTScore, Jina-Code-Embedding, Qwen3-Embedding, execution-result checking, and execution-trace methods). BehaveSim decisively outperforms all on Type-3 (behaviorally similar, textually different) pairs, scoring 1.00 versus 0.38–0.99 for others, and correctly assigns low similarity (0.46–0.73) to Type-1/2/4 pairs where existing methods often score above 0.80. For LLM-AAD, both FunSearch+BehaveSim and EoH+BehaveSim consistently outperform their original counterparts across all three tasks. The most dramatic gain is on TSP, where FunSearch+BehaveSim reduces the top-1 gap by ~7.9 percentage points (25.22→17.37) and EoH+BehaveSim achieves the best overall at 16.55%. On ASP, both augmented methods improve top-1 by ~0.6–0.75 points. On CPP, gains are smaller but consistent, with FunSearch+BehaveSim achieving the best top-1 (2.6293) and top-10 (2.6249) scores.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the established code similarity literature (token-based, structure-based, embedding-based, and execution-trace-based metrics) by arguing that none of these capture the underlying problem-solving logic of algorithms. It introduces a new behavioral perspective—measuring similarity through sequences of intermediate solutions (PSTrajs) compared via DTW—that is orthogonal to both syntactic and output-level equivalence. On the LLM-AAD side, it builds on FunSearch's multi-island database and EoH's evolutionary search, replacing their code-level or fitness-based diversity mechanisms with behavioral diversity management, analogous to how AlphaEvolve uses MAP-Elites or MEoH uses AST distances for diversity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As LLM-AAD ecosystems generate increasingly large populations of AI-designed algorithms, the inability to distinguish genuine algorithmic innovation from syntactic reimplementation becomes a bottleneck for both search efficiency and scientific analysis. BehaveSim provides a lightweight, interpretable, and execution-based tool that addresses this gap, with demonstrated utility in both improving search (notably ~30% relative gap reduction on TSP) and enabling behavioral clustering of generated algorithms. Key limitations include its restriction to iterative (not one-shot) algorithms, its focus solely on behavioral similarity (excluding complexity, generality, or robustness dimensions), and the computational overhead of executing algorithms across multiple instances and starting points. Open questions include how to extend the framework to stochastic algorithms without seed-fixing bias, whether behavioral similarity generalizes to non-iterative or parallel algorithms, and how to integrate additional algorithmic dimensions (time/space complexity, scalability) into a unified novelty metric.
