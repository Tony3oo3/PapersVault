## Problem

Standard imitation learning from demonstrations (e.g., SFT for LLMs) relies on assuming the demonstrator belongs to a low-complexity policy class, which motivates maximum likelihood estimation. This paper shows that a strictly weaker assumption—bounding the complexity of the underlying reward class rather than the demonstrator class—is sufficient for learning, and that MLE provably fails under this weaker assumption even for trivial binary reward classes with always-correct demonstrators. The gap is both conceptual (reward maximization vs. distribution matching) and practical: the demonstrator's exact generative process is far harder to model than what makes an answer correct.

## Input / Output

The learner receives m offline demonstration pairs (x_i, y_i) where x_i ~ D are contexts and y_i are answers drawn from an expert policy supported on correct actions; crucially, the learner never observes whether its own prediction was correct (unobserved rewards) and never sees the reward function directly—only the reward class R is known. The output is a randomized policy mapping contexts to actions, constructed as a uniform mixture over the policies used at each step of a one-pass online algorithm. The action space Y can be arbitrarily large (e.g., all possible text completions), and the guarantee has no dependence on |Y| or the size of optimal action sets.

## Method

The core algorithm (Algorithm 1) maintains multiplicative weights over the finite reward class R. At each round, it predicts the action maximizing the weighted sum of rewards across all r in R, then updates weights: rewards inconsistent with the observed demonstration are zeroed (or down-weighted for suboptimal demonstrators), while rewards under which the prediction was suboptimal are up-weighted. For always-correct demonstrations, this yields at most log|R| mistakes; for suboptimal demonstrators, a softer update controlled by a step-size hyperparameter γ gives an online regret bound of O(sqrt((J* - J~) · log|R|)). An online-to-batch conversion (Algorithm 2) produces a statistical learner with an optimistic rate: O(log|R|/ε) samples when the demonstrator is optimal, degrading to O(log|R|/ε²) in the worst case. Unlike Syed & Schapire's multi-pass batch approach, this is a simple one-pass online algorithm that also handles arbitrarily adaptive demonstrations.

## Results

The paper is purely theoretical, with no empirical experiments. The headline result (Theorem 5) shows that the proposed learner achieves value suboptimality O(log|R|/m) with optimal demonstrations (sample complexity O(1/ε)) and O(√(Δ log|R|/m) + log|R|/m) in general (sample complexity O(1/ε²)), where Δ is the demonstrator's suboptimality. The online algorithm (Theorem 3) makes at most log|R| mistakes with always-correct demonstrations. Crucially, Theorems 1 and 2 prove that MLE fails to learn even for |R|=|Y|=2 with an always-correct demonstrator, establishing that the reward class assumption is strictly weaker than the demonstrator class assumption.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MLE (Foster et al., 2024), the paper proves a strict dominance: MLE fails to generalize under the reward class assumption even for trivial binary reward classes with |R|=2 (Theorems 1 and 2), while the proposed learner achieves optimal O(log|R|/m) sample complexity. Against Syed & Schapire (2007), the proposed method achieves a faster optimistic rate of O(1/ε) for optimal demonstrators versus O(1/ε²) in the general case, and is a simpler one-pass online algorithm rather than a multi-pass batch approach. Against Moulin et al. (2025), the proposed learner avoids any dependence on log|Y| (the action space size), which is significant for autoregressive generation where |Y| scales with response length; Moulin et al.'s dual-update approach incurs an O(ε⁻⁴ log|Y| log(|R|/ε)) sample complexity. The proposed method also enjoys adversarial robustness guarantees (regret minimization for arbitrary adaptive demonstrations) that the other approaches do not explicitly provide in the contextual bandit setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the game-theoretic apprenticeship learning framework of Syed & Schapire (2007), adapting their multiplicative-weights approach to the single-step contextual bandit setting and obtaining faster optimistic rates (1/ε vs. 1/ε² for optimal demonstrators). It deliberately departs from the dominant MLE/behavior-cloning line of work (Foster et al., 2024) by replacing the low-cardinality demonstrator class assumption with a strictly weaker low-cardinality reward class assumption, and shows that this shift fundamentally changes which learning rules succeed. The paper also positions itself relative to Moulin et al. (2025), who use dual (policy-space) multiplicative updates, whereas this work uses primal (reward-space) updates in a simpler one-pass online algorithm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean theoretical argument that the standard SFT objective (log-loss minimization / MLE) can fail catastrophically even with a two-element reward class and an always-correct expert, motivating a shift in how we think about supervised fine-tuning of LLMs. The key open question is practical: the proposed algorithm requires maintaining weights over all reward functions, which is exponential in the number of parameters for continuous reward classes, so a tractable implementation for real LLMs remains unresolved. The paper also leaves open what reward class to use in practice, how pretrained model knowledge interacts with reward-class-based learning, and whether this approach can genuinely replace behavior cloning in the SFT pipeline.
