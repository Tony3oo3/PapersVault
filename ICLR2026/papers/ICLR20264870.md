## Problem

Unified multimodal models are designed to process vision and language within shared representation spaces, yet it remains unclear whether knowledge acquired in one modality reliably transfers to the other. This paper identifies and systematically characterizes 'modal aphasia,' a dissociation in which models accurately generate visual content from memory but fail to articulate the same knowledge in text, raising both capability and safety concerns for current multimodal architectures.

## Input / Output

The same text prompt (a movie title, a fictional person's name, or a combination of made-up concept words) is independently used to query both the image-generation and text-description capabilities of a unified model, and the two outputs are compared for factual consistency. For real-world evaluation, outputs are graded against a unified modality-independent rubric (positive and negative requirements) generated by a frontier VLM judge; for controlled experiments, image accuracy is measured via attribute extraction (VLM-judge for faces, computer vision for abstract shapes) while verbal accuracy is measured via multiple-choice questions with an LLM-judge fallback for malformed responses. The safety case study additionally produces binary refusal/compliance decisions and forced image generations evaluated by a VLM for content correctness.

## Method

The paper combines frontier-model observation with controlled fine-tuning experiments on two open-weight unified autoregressive models (Janus-Pro 7B, which generates images as discrete token sequences, and Harmon 1.5B, which uses masked iterative image-embedding generation) to isolate modal aphasia from architecture-specific artifacts. Critically, fine-tuning updates only the LLM backbone while freezing all image encoders and decoders, ensuring that all memorized knowledge resides solely in the language model's parameters. Two synthetic datasets are constructed: a faces dataset (600 portraits covering the full combinatorial space of 4 primary attributes, paired with random names) for pure memorization, and an abstract concepts dataset (840 images defined by 4 concept types with made-up names, split 80/20 for train/test) to test generalization to unseen concept combinations. A two-stage safety case study first fine-tunes Janus-Pro to associate images of feet with a rare expression ('secondary balance units'), then applies text-only refusal alignment, demonstrating that the unsafe visual concept remains accessible through the rare expression despite successful text-level refusal. Across all settings, models achieve near-perfect image generation while verbal descriptions hover near random-guessing accuracy, confirming modal aphasia as a robust, architecture-independent property.

## Results

On nine iconic movie posters, ChatGPT-5's textual descriptions fail 45% of rubric requirements on average, while its image generations fail only ~6%—a 7× error-rate gap. Major hallucinations (fabricated characters/objects) appear almost exclusively in text (~95% of anticipated major hallucinations), while images contain only minor detail errors at 5× lower rates. In controlled synthetic-face experiments, fine-tuned Janus-Pro and Harmon generate near-perfect portraits but achieve only ~20% description accuracy (random-guessing level). In the safety case study, a text-aligned Janus-Pro refuses 89% of explicit 'feet' prompts but only 24% of the rare expression 'secondary balance units,' while still generating valid feet images.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical study of a failure mode rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so the key contrasts are cross-modal (image vs. text within the same model) and across architectures. ChatGPT-5's image generation achieves near-perfect poster reproduction (94% rubric accuracy) while its text descriptions drop to 55%—a 7× gap that no single architectural choice explains, as the phenomenon persists in both Janus-Pro (autoregressive discrete tokens) and Harmon (masked iterative embeddings). In the synthetic-face setting, both models generate correct portraits (>90% attribute accuracy) yet verbal descriptions hover at the ~20% random-guessing baseline, with no correlation between a model's image accuracy and its text accuracy for individual attributes. For abstract concepts, Janus-Pro generalizes visually to unseen combinations (~97% position accuracy) but underperforms random (25%) when verbally stating positions, while it slightly exceeds random (23% vs. 14%) for shapes despite worse visual shape generation. In the safety case study, the text-only alignment reduces refusal from 100% to 89% for the real word 'feet' but leaves the rare expression 'secondary balance units' at only 24% refusal, demonstrating that unimodal safeguards cannot suppress concepts stored in the image modality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by identifying and naming a specific cross-modal dissociation (modal aphasia) in unified multimodal models, building on but departing from several adjacent threads. It extends the memorization literature (Carlini et al. for both diffusion and language models) from single-modal extraction to a systematic comparison of what a model can produce versus articulate across modalities. It is conceptually related to the reversal curse (Berglund et al., 2024) and to Wen et al. (2025) on cross-modality recall gaps, but distinguishes itself by focusing on generation (not just recall) in natively unified architectures where image and text share a single embedding space. The cognitive science parallels (optic aphasia, verbal overshadowing) provide a theoretical framing that prior ML work had not drawn upon.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that unified training does not yield unified understanding challenges a core assumption behind the current generation of multimodal foundation models, suggesting that architectural changes (e.g., allowing models to visualize during reasoning, as in Chern et al., 2025) may be necessary rather than incremental data or training fixes. The safety implications are particularly pressing: the demonstration that text-only alignment can be circumvented through rare expressions that trigger image generation exposes a concrete vulnerability in how multimodal safeguards are currently designed. Open questions include whether modal aphasia persists at larger scale, whether it is specific to autoregressive unified architectures, and whether explicit visual reasoning can close the gap without sacrificing efficiency.
