## Problem

Classical normalizing flows (NFs) used in Boltzmann Generators for molecular equilibrium sampling provide exact one-step likelihoods, but their conventional maximum likelihood training is numerically unstable and computationally expensive, causing architectures like RealNVP and Jet to fail entirely on peptide systems. Modern continuous normalizing flows (CNFs) trained with flow matching are more expressive but require costly numerical ODE simulation for both sampling and likelihood evaluation. The paper asks whether a regression-based training objective can unlock stable, scalable training of classical NFs while preserving their exact one-step likelihood advantage.

## Input / Output

The method consumes Gaussian prior samples paired with pre-computed molecular conformation targets (3D Cartesian coordinates of peptide atoms, center-of-mass subtracted and scaled) obtained either from a pretrained DiT-based CNF (reflow, up to 10.4M pairs) or from an offline-computed optimal transport map. The output is a single forward pass of the NF that simultaneously yields a generated conformation and its exact log-likelihood via the change-of-variable formula, which feeds into self-normalized importance sampling to produce unbiased Boltzmann-distributed samples and free energy differences without any energy function evaluation during training.

## Method

REGFLOW replaces the MLE objective with a simple L2 regression loss that trains an invertible NF to match pre-computed noise-to-data pairings from a known invertible map (a pretrained CNF or an OT plan), eliminating the need to compute Jacobian determinants during training. Two regularization strategies prevent numerical collapse: a log-determinant penalty (free for the tested architectures) and a novel forward-backward self-consistency loss that enforces cycle consistency at the output level without requiring the Jacobian. The key theoretical insight (Proposition 1) is that pointwise convergence of the learned flow to the target invertible map guarantees correct push-forward of the prior, while Proposition 2 bounds the Wasserstein-2 distance to the data distribution in terms of the CNF approximation error and the regression residual. For autoregressive flows like NSF, training and inference both proceed in the fast noise-to-data direction, yielding up to 34× inference speedup over MLE-trained counterparts and ~27–35% faster convergence to competitive performance.

## Results

On alanine dipeptide (ALDP), REGFLOW-trained NSF achieves E-W1 = 0.501 and T-W2 = 0.951, compared to 13.797 and 1.243 for NSF(MLE) — a ~27× improvement in energy distribution fidelity. Res-NVP and Jet trained with MLE completely fail on all three peptides (ESS < 1e-4, E-W1 > 1e3, T-W2 > 30), while their REGFLOW counterparts achieve competitive or superior performance (e.g., Res-NVP(REGFLOW) on ALDP: E-W1 = 2.104, T-W2 = 0.812). REGFLOW also delivers a 34× inference speedup over MLE-trained NSF for likelihood computation (8.18s vs 277s for 200k points) and ~27–35% faster training time to reach equivalent or better performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against NSF(MLE), REGFLOW wins decisively on E-W1 (e.g., 0.501 vs 13.797 on ALDP, a 27× reduction) and T-W2 (0.951 vs 1.243), while slightly underperforming on ESS (0.035 vs 0.055) — the authors attribute this to mode collapse in MLE artificially inflating ESS. Against Res-NVP(MLE) and Jet(MLE), REGFLOW wins overwhelmingly: both MLE variants completely fail on all three peptides (ESS < 1e-4, E-W1 > 1e3, T-W2 > 30), whereas REGFLOW achieves usable performance (e.g., Res-NVP(REGFLOW) on ALDP: E-W1 = 2.104, T-W2 = 0.812). Compared to the CNF DiT baseline (trained with flow matching), REGFLOW-trained NSF is ~34× faster for likelihood computation (8.18s vs 26969.80s for 200k points), and the CNF is ~450× more expensive than the slowest NF (Jet) and ~7700× more expensive than the fastest (Res-NVP). In the Targeted Free Energy Perturbation task, REGFLOW achieves comparable free energy estimates to the DiT CNF while requiring only sample-based training (no energy evaluations) and completing in a fraction of the time.

## Relation to Prior Work

REGFLOW draws a direct conceptual parallel between flow matching's regression-based training of continuous normalizing flows and the MLE-based training of classical discrete normalizing flows, proposing that the same regression philosophy can be transplanted to one-step invertible maps. It builds on the Boltzmann Generator framework (Noe et al., 2019) for molecular equilibrium sampling, on reflow (Liu, 2022) and optimal transport (Tong et al., 2023) as sources of invertible coupling targets, and on one-step generative models (shortcut models, IMM) as motivation for single-step inference. The key departure is from the MLE objective itself: by fixing a coupling via a known invertible map and regressing in L2, REGFLOW avoids the Jacobian determinant computation and the simultaneous learning of forward and inverse maps that makes MLE training of NFs numerically unstable and architecturally restrictive.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

REGFLOW unlocks NF architectures (Res-NVP, Jet) that completely fail under MLE training (ESS < 1e-4) and delivers a ~34× inference speedup for autoregressive flows, making exact-likelihood Boltzmann Generators practical for molecular systems where CNF-based alternatives are prohibitively expensive. The new TFEP application—training a flow between metastable states using only samples and OT targets, with no energy evaluations during training—opens a capability unavailable to MLE-trained NFs. Limitations include proposal quality that remains below SOTA CNFs, and the theoretical gap between learned likelihoods and those of the base coupling. Open questions include identifying optimal couplings for specific architectures and datasets, scaling to larger molecular systems, and whether the regression framework extends to conditional or equivariant settings.
