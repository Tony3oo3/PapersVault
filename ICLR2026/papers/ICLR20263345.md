## Problem

Pretraining large LLMs is computationally prohibitive, yet the community has produced many small pretrained models at significant cost that remain underutilized. Existing approaches to leveraging smaller models for larger-model training—such as reverse knowledge distillation (RKD) or model growth—either require the teacher to be only modestly smaller, impose architectural constraints, or underperform the baseline when the size gap is large. This paper asks whether small pretrained models (up to 10× smaller) can be used in an architecture-agnostic way to accelerate and improve the pretraining of much larger LLMs.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a text token sequence (from The Pile, ~20B tokens, sequence length 1280) and a frozen small pretrained model T (e.g., SmolLM-135M, OPT-125M, Pythia-160M, or SmolLM-1.7B) whose final-layer hidden states serve as alignment targets. It produces a pretrained LLM M (1.4B or 7B parameters, LLaMA architecture with RMSNorm and SwiGLU) trained with a combined NLL + projection loss. When M and T have different hidden dimensions, a linear-interpolation projection maps M's early-layer hidden states into T's dimensionality before computing cosine similarity; the alignment weight λ decays linearly to zero by step S_stop, after which training reverts to standard causal LM.

## Method

LET introduces two coupled mechanisms: (1) late-to-early-layer learning, which aligns the k-th (early) layer hidden states of the target model M with the final-layer hidden states of the small pretrained model T via a negative cosine-similarity loss, and (2) late-to-early-step learning, which applies this alignment only during early training steps with a linearly decaying weight λ = λ₀·max(0, (S_stop − s)/S_stop), so the target model gradually regains full autonomy. The total loss is L_NLL + λ·L_proj, where L_proj is computed after L2-normalizing (and optionally projecting) the hidden states. A key design choice is aligning with early layers rather than middle or late layers: this leaves the remaining layers free to adapt and refine the guided representations through normal learning dynamics, making the approach robust even as M surpasses T in capability. A theoretical analysis on a deep linear network shows that early-layer alignment yields a smaller Frobenius-norm upper bound on the Hessian of the projection loss, implying a smoother optimization landscape compared to deeper alignment.

## Results

On 1.4B-parameter models trained on The Pile, LET achieves 43.6% average accuracy across nine downstream tasks, outperforming the standard training baseline (41.6%) by 2.0 points. At 7B scale, LET reaches 45.5% average versus 43.3% for the baseline (+2.2 points). Notably, LET trained for only 67% of the total steps (LET-67%) already exceeds the full-step baseline on both scales (42.5% vs 41.6% at 1.4B; 43.9% vs 43.3% at 7B), yielding up to a 1.6× speedup in convergence while using a teacher model up to 10× smaller than the target.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LET outperforms all three baselines on average downstream accuracy at both model scales: at 1.4B it beats Baseline (43.6 vs 41.6), SALT (43.6 vs 42.9), and RKD (43.6 vs 41.4); at 7B it beats Baseline (45.5 vs 43.3), SALT (45.5 vs 44.7), and RKD (45.5 vs 42.2). RKD, which applies reverse knowledge distillation on output logits, actually underperforms the standard training baseline on both scales (41.4 vs 41.6 at 1.4B; 42.2 vs 43.3 at 7B), confirming that logit-level distillation from a much smaller teacher is harmful. SALT, which uses a two-stage KD-then-standard-training schedule, is the strongest baseline but still trails LET by 0.7 points (1.4B) and 0.8 points (7B). The most striking result is that LET trained for only 67% of the total steps already surpasses the full-step baseline on both scales, translating to a 1.6× convergence speedup even when the teacher (SmolLM-135M) is 10× smaller than the 1.4B target model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper inverts the classical knowledge distillation paradigm by using a much smaller pretrained model (up to 10× fewer parameters) to guide the early training of a larger target model, rather than the conventional large-teacher-to-small-student direction. It departs from SALT (Rawat et al., 2024), which demonstrated that smaller models can bootstrap larger ones but only at a modest 1.87× size gap with heavy data preprocessing, and from model-growth methods (Du et al., 2024; Samragh et al., 2024) that require deliberate architectural modifications. The key conceptual departure is the 'late-to-early' framing: aligning the final-layer representations of a small model (its 'late' training phase) with the early layers of the target model during its early training steps, making the approach architecture-agnostic and robust to the smaller model's limited capacity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LET offers a practical, low-overhead mechanism to accelerate LLM pretraining by repurposing the vast computational investments already embedded in publicly available small models, achieving 1.6× convergence speedup with ~5% downstream accuracy gains at 1.4B scale. However, empirical validation is limited to 1.4B–7B parameters and 20B tokens, leaving open whether the paradigm scales to 70B+ models and trillion-token datasets where the smaller teacher's representations may become increasingly uninformative. The finding that reverse knowledge distillation (RKD) actively degrades performance below baseline when the teacher is much smaller is a useful cautionary result, and the slight throughput reduction (~1.5%) means the speedup is purely from faster convergence rather than per-step efficiency.
