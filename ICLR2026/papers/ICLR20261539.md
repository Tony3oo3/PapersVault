## Problem

Cosine similarity between a layer's input and output vectors has become the standard proxy for assessing layer relevance in pre-trained LLMs, underpinning both mechanistic interpretability analyses and structured pruning strategies. However, this paper demonstrates that cosine similarity is a poor indicator of actual performance degradation: a layer can exhibit an arbitrarily low cosine similarity score while its removal catastrophically reduces model accuracy, and empirically the correlation between the two is weak to moderate across multiple LLMs.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained decoder-only Transformer (e.g., LLaMA-3-8B, Mistral-7B, OLMo, Pythia) and a calibration dataset (task-specific training split or a mixed set of benchmark samples). For each layer, it produces a scalar relevance score computed by removing that layer, re-evaluating accuracy on the calibration set, and normalizing the drop relative to the full model's accuracy above chance level. When applied to pruning, the output is a structurally smaller model with the least relevant layers removed, evaluated on held-out test sets across eight benchmarks.

## Method

The paper provides a formal proof (Theorem 1) constructing a decoder-only Transformer where a specific layer has an arbitrarily small cosine similarity score (ε) yet its removal drops accuracy from perfect to zero, via a snowball effect where a subtle perturbation is amplified by downstream layers alongside irrelevant embedding dimensions that inflate other layers' scores. To replace cosine similarity, the authors propose an accuracy-based relevance score: for each layer l, the score is 1 minus the ratio of the pruned model's accuracy (above chance) to the full model's accuracy (above chance), yielding values where positive indicates performance loss, zero indicates no change, and negative indicates improvement upon removal. Empirically, this metric reveals that layer relevance is highly task-dependent (contradicting prior cosine-similarity-based conclusions of task-independence), and when used to guide iterative structured pruning of 25% of LLaMA-3-8B layers, it outperforms Taylor approximations, cosine similarity, output-based metrics, perplexity-based scores, and SliceGPT in both task-dependent and task-independent settings. The approach is computationally expensive (N×T forward passes vs. T for cosine similarity), averaging ~4.6 hours to prune 25% of LLaMA-3-8B, but the authors show it remains competitive with other performance-based baselines in runtime while yielding superior pruned-model accuracy.

## Results

On LLaMA-3-8B with 25% layer pruning, the proposed accuracy-based relevance score achieves a mean accuracy of 67.49% across eight benchmarks in the task-dependent setting, outperforming the best baseline (Slice-GPT, 61.76%) by 5.7 points and cosine similarity (59.54%) by 8.0 points. In the task-independent setting, the method reaches 65.04% mean accuracy versus 60.1% for cosine similarity and 58.53% for Slice-GPT. Notably, on BoolQ the pruned model (84.04%) even surpasses the unpruned original (82.02%), and the method retains over 75% accuracy on HellaSwag after removing 22% of layers, challenging prior claims that all layers are essential for reasoning tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed accuracy-based score consistently outperforms all six baseline families in both task-dependent and task-independent settings. Taylor approximations (Ma et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024) are the weakest baseline, achieving only 52.39% mean in the task-dependent setting—15.1 points below the proposed method—largely due to catastrophic MMLU collapse (25.03% vs. 62.97%). Cosine similarity (He et al., 2024; Gromov et al., 2025) reaches 59.54%, trailing the proposed method by 8.0 points; it is particularly weak on reasoning tasks like ARC-C (45.73% vs. 49.57%) and MMLU (44.05% vs. 62.97%). The output-based metrics from FinerCut (Zhang et al., 2024b) and related work (Yang et al., 2026; Sieberling et al., 2024) cluster between 57.4–59.5%, all below the proposed method. Perplexity-based pruning (Kim et al., 2024; Song et al., 2024; Zhong et al., 2025) performs poorly at 54.88%, with severe degradation on ARC-E (53.11%) and HellaSwag (58.92%). Slice-GPT (Ashkboos et al., 2024), the strongest baseline at 61.76%, is still 5.7 points below the proposed method and notably weaker on BoolQ (75.75% vs. 84.04%) and MMLU (48.74% vs. 62.97%). In the task-independent setting, the gap narrows slightly (65.04% vs. 60.1% for cosine similarity), and the method's performance becomes sensitive to calibration set choice—pruning with C4 drops mean accuracy to 50.23%, while a mixed 10%-per-benchmark calibration set preserves 65.04%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper challenges the foundational assumption underlying a large body of mechanistic interpretability and structured pruning work: that cosine similarity between a layer's input and output is a reliable proxy for that layer's importance. Rather than building on a specific prior method, it repurposes the straightforward idea of layer ablation (remove a layer, measure accuracy drop) as a primary interpretability metric, positioning it against the cosine-similarity-based approaches of He et al. (2024), Gromov et al. (2025), and Men et al. (2025), as well as against Taylor-approximation, perplexity-based, and output-divergence pruning methods. The theoretical contribution (Theorem 1) provides a formal construction showing the failure mode is not merely empirical but can be arbitrarily severe, which no prior work had established.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that cosine similarity misestimates layer relevance in over 90% of cases has broad implications for the many interpretability and pruning papers that rely on it, potentially invalidating conclusions about which layers perform which functions. The accuracy-based metric, while computationally expensive (N×T forward passes vs. T for cosine similarity), offers a more faithful picture of layer importance and yields state-of-the-art structured pruning results. Key open questions include: how to reduce the computational cost to make the metric practical for 70B+ models, what properties a calibration set must have for task-independent pruning to generalize, and whether the 'snowball effect' (subtle changes amplified by downstream layers) is a common or rare phenomenon in pre-trained LLMs.
