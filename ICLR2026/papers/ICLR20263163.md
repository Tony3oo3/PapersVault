## Problem

Prior work on competitive model markets has focused on two-layer settings where developers also operate delivery platforms, leaving the increasingly common three-layer structure (model providers → platforms → users) unformalized. This paper asks: under what conditions do pure Nash equilibria exist in a three-layer model-platform-user game, how do market structure, user welfare, and diversity depend on the number of models and platforms, and how can a model provider strategically design an entrant model for platform adoption?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263163/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a finite pool of generative models (DDPM variants fine-tuned via LoRA on CIFAR-10 class subsets), a discrete set of user types with class-preference weight vectors and population fractions, and a platform count N. A frozen ResNet-20 classifier (92.6% Top-1) computes per-class posterior probabilities to define the reward function r_θ(x) = Σ_c θ_c · p_acc(c|x), with 2000 samples per score evaluation. Outputs include equilibrium strategy profiles (or best-response cycles), coverage value / user welfare, HHI and support diversity metrics, and for the provider-side contribution, a trained entrant model with adoption-weighted quality.

## Method

The core technical contribution is a utility decomposition (Proposition 3.3) showing each platform's expected utility equals (1/N)(T_{f_i} + δ_{f_i}), where T is the model's average score across user types and δ is a deviation advantage capturing localized wins/losses against competitors. This yields closed-form equilibrium conditions (Lemma 3.4): a fully differentiated equilibrium exists iff the average-score gap between any two models is bounded by their mutual deviation advantages, while a homogeneous equilibrium arises when one model's average advantage exceeds all deviation terms. Corollary 3.5 further shows that a dominant user type with fraction π* ≥ 1 − ρ/(ρ+2Γ) forces homogeneous equilibrium regardless of minority-type preferences. For strategic entry, the paper designs two training schemes: (1) a resampling method that reweights training data by adoption probability σ_θ = σ(β(S_φ(θ) − S̄(θ))) computed via a Bradley-Terry soft gate, and (2) a direct-gradient method optimizing L(φ) = ℒ(φ) − λF(φ) where F is the adoption-weighted quality objective, using either pathwise or REINFORCE gradient estimators for ∇_φ S_φ(θ).

## Results

The paper's primary contributions are theoretical: it proves that pure Nash equilibria may not exist (Proposition 2.2), characterizes conditions for differentiated vs. homogeneous equilibria (Lemma 3.4), and shows that equilibrium welfare W is always ≤ social optimum W_opt (Lemma 4.2). In the illustrative example (Example C.4), the differentiated equilibrium (g₂, g₃) achieves W_eq = 0.7389 versus W_opt = 0.7526, a 1.9% welfare gap. In the algorithmic entry experiments on CIFAR-10 (DDPM + LoRA), the direct-gradient method (M_grad) converges in ~20 iterations and outperforms the resampling method (M_samp, ~500 iterations) in both welfare and HHI diversity, successfully displacing the best incumbent model M₅.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against external baselines; its comparisons are internal. In the strategic entry experiments (Fig. 6), the direct-gradient method (M_grad) dominates the resampling method (M_samp) on all reported metrics: M_grad achieves higher equilibrium welfare, greater HHI diversity, and replaces the best original model M₅ in the equilibrium, while M_samp only approaches M₅'s performance and reduces welfare. M_grad also converges ~25× faster (~20 vs. ~500 iterations). In the discrete best-response simulations (Fig. 5), adding sufficiently distinct models (M2, M3) increases diversity, while adding a close substitute (M5) homogenizes the market; adding platforms consistently improves both welfare and diversity, though welfare never reaches the social optimum. The theoretical counterexamples (Fig. 4, Example C.5) demonstrate that expanding the model pool can decrease welfare (0.85 → 0.84) and that adding platforms can induce best-response cycles that lower welfare (0.2148 → 0.2097).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the competitive model-market literature from two-layer settings (where model developers directly serve users, as in Jagadeesan et al. 2023b and Einav & Rosenfeld 2025) to a three-layer model-platform-user game, reflecting the reality that platforms like Azure, Bedrock, and Canva act as intermediaries between model providers and end users. The equilibrium characterization builds on the utility decomposition and deviation-advantage framework, while the provider-side training schemes repurpose the Bradley-Terry model (Bradley & Terry, 1952) as a differentiable adoption gate combined with REINFORCE and pathwise gradient estimators. Unlike prior generative-market analyses (Taitler & Ben-Porat 2025; Raghavan 2024) that focus on welfare under two-layer competition, this work introduces the platform-intermediation layer and the strategic-entry perspective for model providers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that expanding the model pool or adding platforms can reduce user welfare and diversity (a Braess's-paradox analog) provides a formal basis for the homogenization trends observed in current generative AI markets, with direct implications for antitrust policy and platform governance. The provider-centric training schemes (direct-gradient and resampling) offer a practical toolkit for model developers entering competitive markets, though their validation is limited to CIFAR-10 with LoRA fine-tuning. Key open questions include extending the static, single-model-per-platform, complete-information framework to dynamic multi-round settings, partially overlapping markets, and multi-model platform strategies, all of which the authors identify as future work.
