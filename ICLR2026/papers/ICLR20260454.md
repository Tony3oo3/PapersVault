## Problem

LLMs prompted with demographic information consistently fail to capture the true diversity of individual political opinions, exhibiting representativeness, steerability, and consistency failures. While individual-level persona modeling has been explored via interview transcripts, this approach is impractical at scale. The paper asks whether structured reasoning, trained via reinforcement learning on survey responses, can improve opinion alignment for individual political profiles.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260454/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives only a policy question and a country label in the system prompt—no explicit persona encoding or demographic features are provided. The output is a fixed two-part schema: a free-text reasoning trace enclosed in <reasoning> tags followed by a single stance token (Yes, No, or Neutral) in <answer> tags. Alignment to a specific individual is achieved entirely through the training signal (matching that person's survey answers), not through any input-side conditioning.

## Method

The paper trains one model per individual (or party/candidate) using GRPO, a group-relative variant of PPO that normalizes rewards within sampled output groups rather than learning a value function. A composite reward function combines format compliance (correct tag placement, weight 0.25), length penalty (symmetric deviation from a target trace length, weight 0.01), and answer correctness (binary match to the ground-truth survey stance, weight 1.0). Optionally, a supervised fine-tuning warm start is performed on synthetic chain-of-thought demonstrations generated by Llama 3.1 70B to pre-train the output format and provide a reasonable political reasoning initialization before RL. The key design choice is that the model must generate a justification that logically implies the correct stance, effectively treating opinion formation as a reasoning problem rather than a classification task. Experiments use Llama 3.1 8B, Qwen3 8B, and Magistral 24B as backbones with 4-bit quantization, evaluated on Swiss candidates (smartvote), German parties (Wahl-O-Mat), and U.S. voters (ANES 2020).

## Results

The best configuration (SFT+GRPO on Magistral 24B) achieves macro-F1 of 70.73% on smartvote (Swiss candidates), 53.21% on Wahl-O-Mat (German parties), and 45.43% on ANES 2020 (U.S. voters). SFT+GRPO consistently outperforms all baselines across datasets and models, with the largest gains on WoM where it surpasses the icl baseline by over 25 points (53.21 vs. 26.19). Performance is highest on the binary smartvote dataset and lowest on ANES, which includes a Neutral class that is particularly difficult to predict.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares SFT+GRPO against two external baselines: in-context learning (icl, following Haller et al. 2025) and ORPO (following Hong et al. 2024 / Stammbach et al. 2024). SFT+GRPO decisively outperforms icl on all three datasets for every model, with the largest margin on WoM (e.g., Magistral 24B: 53.21 vs. 26.19 macro-F1, a +27-point gap) and ANES (45.43 vs. 19.23 for Magistral 24B). Against ORPO, SFT+GRPO also wins across the board, though ORPO is competitive on WoM for Llama 3.1 8B (43.29 vs. 52.53) and ANES (34.84 vs. 40.66). Notably, GRPO alone underperforms SFT+GRPO on most settings, indicating that the supervised warm-start is important for training dynamics. The icl baseline performs surprisingly well on smartvote (66.16 for Magistral 24B) but collapses on WoM and ANES, suggesting that in-context learning struggles with the larger, more diverse question sets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant demographic-persona prompting paradigm (Santurkar et al., 2023; Argyle et al., 2023) by training individual-level agents directly on survey responses rather than relying on demographic proxies. It repurposes the GRPO reinforcement learning framework (Shao et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025), previously applied to mathematical reasoning, for the novel domain of political opinion alignment, framing stance prediction as a structured reasoning task. The closest prior work is Park et al. (2024), which models individual personas from interview transcripts, but this paper substitutes the prohibitively expensive interview data with structured survey responses (ANES, smartvote, Wahl-O-Mat), and Haller et al. (2025), which uses in-context learning for bias identification rather than opinion modeling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes the first multi-country benchmark for individual-level political opinion alignment from survey data, providing a concrete baseline for the digital-democracy research agenda. Its systematic analysis reveals that reasoning improves alignment but does not eliminate ideological bias—right-wing and center profiles remain consistently harder to learn, and the Neutral class poses a particular challenge—highlighting that current LLMs cannot yet serve as faithful political digital twins. Open questions include whether a single model can condition on compact persona representations instead of per-agent training, how to mitigate the observed ideological disparities, and whether the learnability gap between left and right profiles reflects model bias or intrinsic properties of the preference data.
