## Problem

The de facto standard for evaluating graph generative models—Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) on hand-crafted descriptors—lacks an absolute scale, is sensitive to kernel and descriptor parametrization, and cannot be compared across different feature spaces. At typical benchmark sizes (20–40 graphs), MMD estimates also suffer from substantial bias and variance, making model rankings unreliable. The paper asks whether a classifier-based evaluation can provide an interpretable, unit-bounded, and descriptor-comparable metric for graph generation.

## Input / Output

The method takes a multiset of reference graphs and a multiset of generated graphs, featurized by a collection of K graph descriptors (degree histograms, clustering coefficients, Laplacian spectrum, 4- and 5-node orbit counts, GIN embeddings; for molecules, Morgan fingerprints, ChemNet/MolCLR embeddings, topological and Lipinski descriptors). It produces a single PGD score in [0,1]—the square root of the maximum log-likelihood across descriptors, serving as a tight lower bound on the Jensen-Shannon distance—along with per-descriptor subscores. Data are split into equal fit and test halves; descriptor selection uses 4-fold stratified cross-validation on the fit set only, and the final score is computed on the held-out test set to prevent leakage.

## Method

PGD reframes distribution comparison as binary classification: a probabilistic discriminator (TabPFN v2.5, a transformer-based tabular foundation model) is trained to distinguish reference from generated graphs using a single graph descriptor, and the classifier's data log-likelihood on held-out data provides a variational lower bound on the Jensen-Shannon divergence (Eq. 2). Because the JS divergence is inherently bounded to [0,1] and independent of kernel bandwidth or feature scaling, resulting scores are directly comparable across descriptors. To obtain the tightest bound, PGD performs 4-fold stratified cross-validation on the fit set for each of K descriptors, selects the descriptor with the highest average validation log-likelihood, retrains a fresh discriminator on the full fit set, and evaluates on the test set. The max-reduction over descriptors yields the final PGD while identifying the most informative descriptor. TabPFN is chosen over alternatives (logistic regression, kernel logistic regression) because it is probabilistic, hyperparameter-free, fast, and empirically produces tighter bounds by modeling non-linear decision boundaries.

## Results

PGD achieves a Pearson correlation of 99.29 with graph validity when varying denoising steps in DiGress on PLANAR-L, substantially outperforming the best MMD metric (GIN RBF at 82.52). During training, PGD's Spearman correlation with training iterations reaches 93.64 on PLANAR-L and 83.33 on LOBSTER-L, while MMD metrics show weak or even negative correlations (e.g., Orb.RBF at −7.35 on LOBSTER-L). In the model benchmark (Table 4), AutoGraph achieves the lowest PGD (best) on PLANAR-L at 34.1±1.7 and on SBM-L at 6.8±1.2, while DiGress excels on LOBSTER-L at 4.0±3.1, with PGD scores ranging from 0 to ~100 across models and datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Thompson et al. (2022) RBF-kernel MMD metrics, PGD consistently achieves stronger correlations with both validity and training progress. On denoising experiments, PGD's Pearson correlation with validity is 99.29 versus 82.52 for the best MMD (GIN RBF); on training experiments, PGD's Spearman correlation with iterations is 93.64 (PLANAR-L) and 83.33 (LOBSTER-L) versus 86.36 (Clust.RBF) and 68.14 (Clust.RBF) respectively, while several MMD metrics show negative correlations (e.g., Orb.RBF at −7.35 on LOBSTER-L). Against Liao et al. (2019) Gaussian TV-based MMD, the comparison highlights PGD's core advantage: MMD values span orders of magnitude (e.g., 6.541e-05 to 1.905e-01 on LOBSTER-L) with no intrinsic scale, making cross-descriptor or cross-model interpretation impossible, whereas PGD scores are bounded in [0,1] and directly comparable. PGD also wins on robustness: in perturbation experiments, PGD maintains strong rank correlation across all perturbation types including edge-swapping (which preserves vertex degrees), while degree-based and GIN-based MMD metrics fail to detect this perturbation. The max-reduction over descriptors in PGD further provides a principled way to identify the most informative descriptor, something MMD cannot offer without arbitrary kernel choices.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transplants the classifier two-sample test (C2ST) paradigm from Lopez-Paz & Oquab (2017) into graph generation, where it had not previously been explored. Theoretically, it builds on the variational formulation of f-divergences from Nguyen et al. (2010), instantiating the Jensen-Shannon divergence as a classifier log-likelihood objective. Practically, it departs from the de facto MMD-based evaluation standard (You et al. 2018; Liao et al. 2019; Thompson et al. 2022) by replacing kernel-based distances with a hyperparameter-free TabPFN discriminator, yielding bounded [0,1] scores that are comparable across descriptors. The paper also extends the MMD critique from O'Bray et al. (2022) and Krimmel et al. (2026) with systematic evidence that standard MMD estimators are unreliable at typical benchmark sizes (20–40 graphs).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PGD addresses a critical bottleneck in graph generative model research by providing an absolute, descriptor-comparable evaluation metric, and the accompanying larger datasets (PLANAR-L, SBM-L, LOBSTER-L) are necessary to avoid the high bias and variance that plague current MMD-based comparisons. Key limitations include the fact that PGD is a lower bound on the divergence between descriptor distributions (not raw graph distributions), its dependence on several hundred samples for stable estimates, and its current restriction to undirected graphs. Open questions include extending PGD to directed, temporal, and heterogeneous graphs, exploring optimal feature selection for tighter bounds, and whether the multi-descriptor max-reduction strategy can be generalized to other domains (e.g., mitigating FID's sensitivity to InceptionV3 initialization).
