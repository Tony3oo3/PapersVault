## Problem

Predicting downstream task performance of large LLMs from smaller models during pre-training is hindered by two phenomena: emergent capabilities that appear abruptly at critical scales, and heterogeneous scaling patterns where different task samples within the same evaluation set follow distinct performance-compute trajectories. Existing methods either assume a uniform scaling pattern across all samples or rely on loss as an intermediate proxy that mismatches out-of-domain generalization, leading to unreliable predictions with high worst-case error.

## Input / Output

The method consumes per-sample pass rates (averaged over 100 stochastic evaluations with top_p=0.7, temperature=1.0) from 8 smaller models (122M–12B) on each task in the evaluation set, forming a 9-dimensional difficulty vector per sample, along with the target compute budget. It outputs a single predicted accuracy for the 70B target model on the full evaluation set, plus intermediate artifacts: cluster assignments, a binary extrapolatable/non-extrapolatable label per cluster, and a fitted cubic smoothing spline mapping the predictable-subset metric to the full-set metric. The method also supports cross-architecture transfer by reusing clusters derived from dense models to predict MoE model performance.

## Method

COD is a four-stage framework: (1) an improved MeanShift algorithm with adaptive radius and minimum cluster size (K=10) clusters task samples by their difficulty feature vectors, minimizing intra-cluster variance while automatically determining the number of clusters; (2) a theoretically derived performance scaling law, y(C) = g + (1−g)·exp(−aC^(−b) − c), is fitted per cluster, where the variance correction term σ²_L/(2μ_L) from a Taylor expansion of E[exp(−L)] motivates the clustering step; (3) clusters are filtered into extrapolatable (a>1, b>0.1, 0≤c<1) and non-extrapolatable groups, and only extrapolatable clusters are projected to the target compute; (4) a cubic smoothing spline, calibrated with anchor points from an external model (e.g., Qwen2-72B), maps the weighted-average predictable-subset accuracy to the full evaluation set accuracy. The key insight is that difficulty-aware clustering reduces the loss variance within each group, making the geometric-to-arithmetic mean approximation tight and enabling accurate per-cluster extrapolation that a single global curve cannot achieve.

## Results

COD achieves a mean absolute prediction error of 1.55% and a maximum error of 2.68% when predicting the 70B model's performance across eight benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH, BBH, TriviaQA, MBPP, AGIEval, DROP, MMLU-pro), using only evaluation results from eight smaller models (122M–12B). The best individual benchmark prediction is BBH at 0.47% error, while the worst is GSM8K at 2.68%. The ablation variant COD (w/o mapping) achieves 2.24% mean error, confirming that the subset-to-full mapping stage contributes an additional ~0.7% improvement.

## Baselines & Comparisons

COD outperforms all four baselines on both mean and maximum prediction error. The closest competitor is End-to-end(exp) (Xiao et al., 2024), which achieves 3.10% mean error—still 2× worse than COD's 1.55%—and fails on AGIEval (6.00%) and GSM8K (4.00%). Loss-intermediate (Chen et al., 2024) performs worst on GSM8K (9.39%) and MATH (6.95%), reflecting the fundamental mismatch between training loss and downstream accuracy. End-to-end(passrate) (Achiam et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2024) struggles on MATH (8.80%) and MBPP (7.34%), while End-to-end(BNSL) (Caballero et al., 2022) exhibits the largest maximum error (13.05% on BBH), indicating that piecewise power-law decomposition cannot handle emergent capability inflection points. COD's advantage is most pronounced on mathematically challenging sets (MATH: 0.79% vs. 3.86–8.80% for baselines) and reasoning sets (BBH: 0.47% vs. 0.64–13.05%), where difficulty heterogeneity within the evaluation set is highest.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the power-law loss scaling framework of Kaplan et al. (2020), deriving a new performance scaling law (Theorem 1) that connects answer loss to task accuracy via a Taylor-expansion bridge between arithmetic and geometric means. It departs from two dominant lines of prior work: loss-intermediate methods (Chen et al. 2024, Gadre et al. 2024) that assume a consistent loss-to-performance mapping, and end-to-end performance-compute extrapolation (Achiam et al. 2023, Hu et al. 2024, Caballero et al. 2022) that assume uniform scaling across all evaluation samples. The key conceptual departure is the Clustering-On-Difficulty (COD) framework, which treats heterogeneous intra-set scaling as the central obstacle and addresses it by partitioning tasks into difficulty-homogeneous clusters using an improved MeanShift algorithm (Fukunaga & Hostetler, 1975), then performing cluster-wise extrapolation before mapping back to the full set.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The COD framework provides LLM training teams with a practical, theoretically grounded tool for monitoring downstream performance during pre-training, achieving 1.55% average prediction error on a 70B model across eight diverse benchmarks. The finding that the subset-to-full mapping function transfers across architectures (enabling use of external models like Qwen2-72B as anchors) suggests that difficulty-based clustering captures task-intrinsic properties rather than model-specific artifacts. However, open questions remain: the method's theoretical foundation does not extend to chain-of-thought evaluations where answer uniqueness is violated, prediction accuracy degrades for smaller target models (~10B) where proxy models are too small to resolve difficulty features, and the multi-hyperparameter pipeline (K, Q, U, a, b, c, T) raises deployment concerns for novel prediction scenarios.
