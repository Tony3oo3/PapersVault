## Problem

Prior work proved that finding an optimal compression-based tokeniser is NP-complete, but all such results assumed unboundedly large alphabets. In practice, tokenisers operate over fixed-size alphabets (bytes, Unicode characters, or bits), leaving open whether the hardness is an artifact of large alphabets. This paper closes that gap by showing that tokenisation remains NP-complete and APX-hard even over binary and unary alphabets.

## Input / Output

The problems take as input a dataset of character-strings over an n-ary alphabet (n ∈ {1, 2}), a vocabulary size budget K, and a target compressed length δ, and ask whether a vocabulary (direct) or merge sequence (bottom-up) exists that compresses the dataset to at most δ symbols. The objective is compressed length (total number of subword tokens across all strings), and the key structural constraint is that the alphabet is bounded to a constant size, with strings constructed using carefully chosen multiplicities to enforce specific tokeniser structures. For the unary case, strings are represented as powers of a single symbol a^ℓ, and the problem is shown to be strongly NP-complete (hard even when lengths are given in unary).

## Method

The paper proves hardness via polynomial-time reductions from 3-OCC-MAX2SAT (for binary direct and bottom-up tokenisation) and from vertex cover (for unary direct tokenisation). The central construction encodes Boolean variables as strings of 0s of distinct lengths (x_j^T = 0^{2j-1}, x_j^F = 0^{2j}) and builds four subdatasets with carefully chosen multiplicities (f = 63, f' = 21, f'' = 7 for direct; f = 575, f' = 115, f'' = 23, f''' = 4 for bottom-up) that force any optimal tokeniser to be 'sat-compliant'—its vocabulary must contain specific tokens corresponding to a Boolean assignment. APX-hardness is established via L-reductions (with constants α = 444, β = 1 for the direct case), and explicit hardness-of-approximation constants (ρ < 1.000002 for direct, ρ < 1.0000001 for bottom-up) are derived from gap-preserving reductions using Berman and Karpinski's inapproximability results for 3-OCC-MAX2SAT.

## Results

The paper proves that binary direct tokenisation is APX-hard with a hardness-of-approximation constant of 1.000002 (i.e., no polynomial-time algorithm can achieve an approximation ratio below 1.000002 unless P = NP). Binary bottom-up tokenisation is shown to be APX-complete with an even tighter constant of 1.0000001. Additionally, unary direct tokenisation is proven strongly NP-complete via a reduction from vertex cover, and unary optimal pair encoding (OPE) tokenisation is shown to be (at least) weakly NP-complete via a reduction from addition chains. These results establish that computational intractability of tokenisation persists even over the smallest possible alphabets (binary and unary).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or experimental comparisons. The results extend and sharpen prior NP-completeness proofs by Whittington et al. (2025) and Kozma and Voderholzer (2024), which assumed unboundedly large alphabets. The key advance is showing that hardness is not an artifact of large alphabets: both binary direct and bottom-up tokenisation are APX-hard (not merely NP-hard), and unary direct tokenisation is strongly NP-complete. The paper also provides explicit hardness-of-approximation constants (1.000002 for direct, 1.0000001 for bottom-up) via gap-preserving reductions from 3-OCC-MAX2SAT, going beyond the prior work which only established APX-hardness without explicit constants. The authors note that whether binary direct tokenisation admits any constant-factor approximation (i.e., whether it is APX-complete) remains open.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the NP-completeness results of Whittington et al. (2025) and Kozma and Voderholzer (2024), which established hardness of compression-optimal tokenisation but only for unboundedly large alphabets. By introducing the n-ary tokenisation problem and proving hardness for binary (and even unary) alphabets, the authors close a gap that prior work explicitly left open. The reduction strategy builds on 3-OCC-MAX2SAT (for binary cases) and vertex cover (for the unary case), with the L-reduction framework from Papadimitriou and Yannakakis (1991) used to establish APX-hardness and explicit inapproximability constants.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

These results explain why practical tokenisation algorithms (BPE, UnigramLM) are necessarily heuristic: even the simplest possible alphabets (binary, unary) make optimal tokenisation intractable and non-approximable beyond a tiny constant. The paper identifies several open questions, including whether binary direct tokenisation admits any constant-factor approximation (i.e., whether it is APX-complete), and whether standard (non-OPE) unary bottom-up tokenisation is hard. For the broader NLP community, this motivates investment in provably good approximation algorithms or alternative objective functions that may be more tractable.
