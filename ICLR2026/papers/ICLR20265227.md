## Problem

Generating cardinally-minimal sufficient explanations for neural networks requires, in the worst case, an exponential number of NP-hard verification queries, making the task computationally infeasible even for small models. Existing methods settle for the weaker subset-minimal notion, which can yield much larger and less informative explanations. The paper asks whether the additive structure of Neural Additive Models (NAMs) can make the stronger cardinally-minimal guarantee tractable.

## Input / Output

The method takes a trained NAM (a sum of univariate ReLU networks plus an intercept), a specific tabular input vector x, and a perturbation radius ε_p defining an ℓ_p-norm ball around x. It outputs a cardinally-minimal subset of feature indices S such that fixing those features to their values in x provably preserves the prediction for all perturbations within the ball, along with a formal sufficiency certificate. Verification queries in Stage 1 operate on individual univariate components f_i (far smaller than the full model), while Stage 2 queries the full NAM f only O(log n) times.

## Method

The algorithm exploits the additive decomposition f(x) = β₀ + Σ f_i(x_i) in two stages. Stage 1 (Parallel Interval Importance Sorting) runs in parallel across all n univariate components: for each f_i, it uses binary search with α-β-CROWN verification queries to tighten bounds [l_i, u_i] on the extremal value of f_i within the perturbation ball, stopping once all n intervals are non-overlapping, yielding a total ordering of features by their 'importance' (derivation toward the decision boundary). Stage 2 (Logarithmic Feature Selection) then performs a binary search over this sorted ordering, querying the full NAM only O(log n) times to find the smallest prefix of the ordering that is a sufficient explanation. The key insight is that the non-overlapping sorted intervals guarantee that any sufficient explanation must be a prefix of the ordering, making binary search valid and ensuring cardinal minimality. Overall complexity is O((n/p)·log(max(β_i−α_i)/ξ_i)) parallelized univariate queries plus O(log n) full-model queries, versus O(2^n) for general networks.

## Results

On Breast Cancer, the proposed algorithm produces cardinally-minimal explanations of average size 4.00 in 35.60 s, compared to 16.58 (Lexicographic) and 16.27 (Sensitivity) for subset-minimal baselines that take ~635 s. On CREDIT, the method achieves size 3.76 in 132.67 s versus 12.42 in 473.63 s (Lexicographic) and 3.82 in 407.93 s (Sensitivity). On FICO HELOC, it yields size 5.59 in 317.92 s versus 15.60 in 146.16 s (Lexicographic) and 9.45 in 250.09 s (Sensitivity). Against the sampling-based NAM interpretation, the method achieves 100% verified sufficiency on both CREDIT and FICO HELOC, whereas sampling yields only 31.37% and 25.49% verified sufficiency respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Lexicographic baseline (greedy subset-minimal search with default feature ordering, from Ignatiev et al. 2019), the proposed method wins decisively on explanation size across all three datasets (4.00 vs 16.58 on Breast Cancer, 3.76 vs 12.42 on CREDIT, 5.59 vs 15.60 on FICO HELOC) and on runtime for Breast Cancer (35.6 s vs 634.9 s) and CREDIT (132.7 s vs 473.6 s), though it is slower on FICO HELOC (317.9 s vs 146.2 s). Against the Sensitivity baseline (reverse-sensitivity ordering from Wu et al. 2023), the method achieves comparable or better size on all datasets (3.76 vs 3.82 on CREDIT, 5.59 vs 9.45 on FICO HELOC, 4.00 vs 16.27 on Breast Cancer) while being substantially faster on Breast Cancer (35.6 s vs 636.8 s) and CREDIT (132.7 s vs 407.9 s), though slightly slower on FICO HELOC (317.9 s vs 250.1 s). Crucially, the proposed method solves the strictly harder problem of cardinally-minimal explanations, whereas both baselines only guarantee the weaker subset-minimality. Against the standard sampling-based NAM interpretation (Agarwal et al. 2021), the method produces larger explanations (3.76 vs 2.67 on CREDIT, 5.59 vs 3.04 on FICO HELOC) and is slower (132.7 s vs 5.1 s), but achieves 100% verified sufficiency versus only 31.37% and 25.49% for sampling, meaning most sampled explanations are actually insufficient and potentially misleading in safety-critical settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the sorting-and-selecting paradigm for cardinally-minimal sufficient explanations from binary linear threshold models (Barceló et al., 2020) to continuous-input Neural Additive Models, where the additive structure over univariate components enables independent per-feature analysis. It builds on the sufficient explanation framework and greedy subset-minimal search of Ignatiev et al. (2019) and the neural network verification backbone (α-β-CROWN) used by recent formal XAI work (Wu et al., 2023; Bassan & Katz, 2023), but departs from them by exploiting NAM-specific structure to reduce the query complexity from exponential (cardinal) or linear (subset) to logarithmic. The key conceptual departure is that certifying sufficiency is harder for NAMs than for monotonic networks (Harzli et al., 2023), but minimality becomes easier due to the additive decomposition.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first method providing certifiable, cardinally-minimal explanations for NAMs, which are widely deployed in safety-critical domains (healthcare, finance). The reduction from exponential to logarithmic verification queries makes provable explanations practically feasible where they were previously intractable, and the demonstration that sampling-based NAM interpretation can yield misleading (insufficient) explanations underscores the need for formal guarantees. Key limitations include dependence on complete verifiers with numeric tolerances and timeouts, and the approach is restricted to NAMs; the broader open question is whether similar structural exploitation can be applied to other interpretable architectures (e.g., monotonic networks, tree ensembles) to achieve comparable efficiency gains.
