## Problem

A prevailing view, reinforced by Sutton's bitter lesson, holds that explicit inductive biases like rotational symmetry are unnecessary because sufficiently large models can learn them from data. This paper challenges that assumption for geometric tasks by showing that equivariant architectures exhibit fundamentally different (larger) power-law scaling exponents in compute, data, and model size, meaning the performance gap between equivariant and non-equivariant models widens rather than closes as scale increases.

## Input / Output

The model takes a point cloud of n atoms, each specified by an integer atomic number z_i and a 3D position x_i ∈ R³, and predicts a scalar potential energy e(X) invariant to global rotations/translations plus n per-atom force vectors f_i(X) that are rotation-equivariant. Training uses direct force prediction (rather than backpropagating through energy) for scalability, with a combined loss of per-atom MAE on energy and MSE on forces. Experiments use the OpenMol neutral-molecule subset (34M samples, ~9.2×10⁸ atom-tokens) in a single-epoch regime, with geometric graphs built at a 6 Å cutoff (10 Å for GemNet-OC) and up to 30–50 neighbors per atom.

## Method

The paper conducts a systematic scaling-law study across four message-passing architectures ordered by symmetry expressivity: an unconstrained MPNN (no symmetry), GemNet-OC (E(3)-invariant, 4-body, tensor order ℓ=0), MC-EGNN (E(3)-equivariant, 2-body, ℓ=1), and eSEN (SO(3)-equivariant, 2-body, ℓ≥2 with frame-aligned sparse tensor products). For each architecture, they fit power laws L(C) = L_∞ + F_c·C^(−γ_c) in both FLOPs and wall-clock GPU-hours, and a sum-power-law L(N,D) = L_∞ + A·N^(−α) + B·D^(−β) across model sizes (up to ~80M params) and data fractions, using scheduler-free AdamW optimizers and μP for hyperparameter transfer across widths. Key findings: scaling exponents γ_c, α, β all increase monotonically with equivariance degree (e.g., γ_c goes from 0.142 for MPNN to 0.403 for eSEN); compute-optimal allocation yields a≈b≈0.5 across all architectures (scale N and D in tandem); symmetry-loss regularization shifts the frontier but does not change exponents; and multi-epoch training on 1% of data preserves the same power laws when data augmentation is applied to unconstrained models.

## Results

The paper reports architecture-dependent power-law scaling exponents for neural network interatomic potentials trained on the OpenMol dataset (34M molecules, ~9.2×10⁸ tokens). The compute-scaling exponent γ_c increases monotonically with equivariance degree: 0.142 (unconstrained MPNN), 0.173 (MC-EGNN), 0.255 (GemNet-OC), and 0.403 (eSEN), meaning the performance gap between architectures widens with increasing compute. The parameter-scaling exponent α follows the same trend (0.276 → 0.817), as does the data-scaling exponent β (0.311 → 0.753). Across all architectures, the compute-optimal allocation yields a ≈ b ≈ 0.5, indicating model size and data size should scale in roughly equal proportions (consistent with Chinchilla).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four message-passing architectures of increasing equivariance degree on the OpenMol neutral-molecule dataset. eSEN (high-order spherical tensors, ℓ≥2) achieves the best compute-scaling exponent (γ_c = 0.403), nearly 3× that of the unconstrained MPNN (γ_c = 0.142), and its parameter-scaling exponent (α = 0.817) is ~3× that of MPNN (α = 0.276). GemNet-OC (four-body invariants, ℓ=0) and MC-EGNN (Cartesian vectors, ℓ=1) occupy intermediate positions, with γ_c of 0.255 and 0.173 respectively. Crucially, the performance gap between architectures *widens* with increasing compute, meaning equivariant models pull further ahead at scale rather than converging. The compute-optimal allocation (a ≈ b ≈ 0.5) is consistent across all four architectures, so the architectural choice affects the slope of the frontier but not the optimal N/D ratio. Symmetry loss regularization on the unconstrained MPNN shifts the frontier rightward (by ~0.14 log₁₀(6)) but does not change the scaling exponent, confirming that architectural equivariance is fundamentally different from loss-based symmetry enforcement.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the compute-optimal scaling framework of Hoffmann et al. (2022) and the power-law methodology of Kaplan et al. (2020) to the geometric domain of neural network interatomic potentials, using μP (Yang et al., 2021) for stable width scaling and intermediate-checkpoint fitting (Choshen et al., 2025) for robustness. It departs from prior geometric scaling studies (Batzner et al., 2022; Brehmer et al., 2025) that characterized architecture-dependent exponents only with respect to dataset size, by providing a complete and comparable picture across compute (both FLOPs and wall-clock GPU-hours), parameters, and data simultaneously. Conceptually, it positions itself against the 'bitter lesson' narrative (Sutton, 2019) and recent non-equivariant models (Orb, Neumann et al., 2024) that suggest symmetry inductive biases become dispensable at scale, arguing instead that equivariance changes the fundamental scaling exponents rather than merely shifting the loss curve by a constant factor.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that equivariant architectures exhibit dramatically larger scaling exponents (γ_c ≈ 0.40 for eSEN vs. 0.14 for unconstrained MPNNs) challenges the widely held assumption that inductive biases like symmetry are outcompeted by scale, and provides concrete guidance that model and data sizes should scale in tandem (a≈b≈0.5) regardless of architecture. Key limitations include the single-epoch, academic-scale setting on one dataset (OpenMol neutral molecules), the absence of denoising pretraining, and the exclusion of architecture-agnostic equivariant methods (frame averaging, canonicalization). Open questions include developing a theoretical explanation for why symmetry changes exponents (not just coefficients) when the rotation group is only 3-dimensional relative to ~3n input degrees of freedom, and whether these findings persist at frontier scale with multi-epoch training and diverse molecular types.
