## Problem

Proactive agents must decide not only what to say but also whether and when to intervene, yet current systems rely on brittle heuristics or indiscriminate long reasoning that offers little control over the benefit-burden tradeoff. Acceptance optimization is typically decoupled from timing control, and slow reasoning is applied universally even to obvious cases, leading to over-intervention and wasted compute. The paper asks: can a principled, cost-sensitive gate paired with selective slow reasoning yield proactive agents that are precise, efficient, and tunable without retraining?

## Input / Output

At each timestep t the agent receives a context X_t drawn from a user event stream (a sequence of tuples (E_t, A_t, S_t) encoding the observed event, user activity, and environment state) along with deployment cost parameters C_FA and C_FN. It outputs two calibrated probabilities (p_need, p_accept), a binary intervene/silence decision from the cost-sensitive gate, and—only when intervening—a proactive proposal P_t. The slow reasoning pass is triggered only when the fast model's acceptance estimate falls within a configurable margin δ_slow of the adaptive threshold, so the output structure is identical across all cases but the internal compute path differs.

## Method

PRISM couples a Bayes-risk-derived cost-sensitive gate, τ(p_need) = C_FA / (C_FA + p_need·C_FN), with a dual-process (festina lente) inference architecture: a fast model produces initial (p_need^F, p_accept^F), and a single slow reasoning pass is invoked only when |p_accept^F − τ(p_need^F)| ≤ δ_slow, concentrating expensive computation on ambiguous, high-stakes cases. Training uses Decision-Consistent Curation (RDC) distillation, where a teacher running the full PRISM pipeline generates dense supervision on unlabeled interaction traces; a ranking score R_DC rewards accepted interventions and penalizes miscalibrated probability estimates, and the student (Qwen3-8B) is fine-tuned on the top-ranked subset with an objective L = L_need + L_acc + L_burden that includes inverse propensity scoring to correct selection bias. Post-hoc temperature scaling (T_need=0.5, T_accept=0.7) further aligns the calibrated probabilities with true risk, and the same costs and margins used at inference shape the training signal, closing the simulation-to-production gap.

## Results

On PROACTIVEBENCH (233 held-out clips), Qwen3-8B-PRISM achieves an F1 of 86.61% and a false-alarm rate of 22.94% under LLM-as-Judge evaluation, surpassing its DeepSeek-R1 teacher (F1 83.28%, FA 27.64%) and the prior SOTA Qwen2-7B-Proactive (F1 66.47%, FA 50.22%) by +20.14 F1 points and a 54% relative reduction in false alarms. Under human expert evaluation, PRISM maintains its lead with F1 84.85% vs. DeepSeek-R1's 82.05%. The slow-on-margin inference strategy (δ=0.1) routes only ~11% of cases to expensive reasoning, adding just 20 ms P95 latency over fast-only inference while gaining +5.06 F1 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PRISM decisively outperforms both proprietary frontier models and the prior SOTA Proactive Agent pipeline. Against GPT-4o (F1 64.60%, FA 51.85%), PRISM gains +22.01 F1 points and halves the false-alarm rate (22.94% vs. 51.85%), indicating that raw model capability without cost-sensitive gating leads to severe over-intervention. Against the Proactive Agent baselines (Lu et al., ICLR 2024) instantiated as LLaMA-3.1-8B-Proactive (F1 66.25%) and Qwen2-7B-Proactive (F1 66.47%), PRISM improves F1 by over 20 points while cutting false alarms from ~50% to 22.94%. Notably, PRISM also surpasses its own teacher DeepSeek-R1 (F1 83.28%) in Precision (+4.70 pp) and False-Alarm rate (−4.70 pp), with both differences statistically significant (p < 0.001), confirming that the RDC distillation and dynamic gating contribute beyond what the teacher's raw reasoning provides. Human expert evaluation corroborates these gains, with PRISM achieving F1 84.85% vs. DeepSeek-R1's 82.05% and a lower false-alarm rate (25.91% vs. 29.70%).

## Relation to Prior Work

PRISM sits at the intersection of cost-sensitive selective prediction (Elkan 2001; Geifman & El-Yaniv 2017, 2019), probability calibration (Guo et al. 2017), and dual-process reasoning inspired by chain-of-thought (Wei et al. 2022) and metareasoning (Russell & Wefald 1991). It builds directly on the Proactive Agent/ProactiveBench formulation (Lu et al. 2024) of acceptance-supervised intervention timing, but departs from prior proactive pipelines by decoupling the intervention gate from the response policy and by using the same cost parameters and slow-margin at both train and inference time (gate-aligned distillation via RDC). The knowledge-distillation backbone follows Hinton et al. (2015) and self-instruct-style synthesis (Wang et al. 2023), while the selective slow pass operationalizes the 'allocate computation where expected value is high' principle from metareasoning in a concrete, threshold-triggered form.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a compact 8B student, trained on less than one-third of the data via RDC filtering, can surpass a much larger reasoning teacher (DeepSeek-R1) on the precision and false-alarm metrics that matter most for user trust in proactive systems. The slow-margin gating result—achieving a +5 F1 gain over fast-only inference with only ~20 ms P95 latency overhead and ~11% slow rate—offers a practical recipe for deploying 'System 2' quality at 'System 1' cost in latency-sensitive settings. Open questions remain around time-varying costs (e.g., rising C_FN near deadlines), online recalibration under distribution shift, and ensuring utility-based gating does not suppress critical safety warnings; the authors acknowledge these as active limitations. The AUDBC metric and the interpretable cost/margin control surface could become useful standard tools for evaluating and tuning any proactive or selective-intervention agent.
