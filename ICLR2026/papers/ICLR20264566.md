## Problem

The softmax bottleneck limits the set of probability distributions an LLM can produce, since the output projection matrix maps a low-dimensional embedding space into a low-dimensional subspace of the full logit space. Prior work argued this handicaps LLM performance, but it remained unclear whether the bottleneck actually restricts the model's ability to assign accurate probabilities to the most likely tokens — the ones that matter for nucleus sampling and beam search at inference time.

## Input / Output

The analysis takes a d-dimensional embedding vector x, an N×d output projection matrix A (either randomly initialized with i.i.d. Gaussian entries or a trained OPM from a specific model), a target set of m tokens, and optionally specific target probability values for those tokens. The output is a theoretical lower bound on the maximum m for which any such token set can be assigned the desired probabilities with near-certainty, plus LP feasibility determinations that verify whether a concrete embedding x exists satisfying the logit constraints. The LP formulation constrains A_m x = y_m (the top-m logits) while requiring all remaining N−m logits to fall below a threshold, and the embedding may include a component orthogonal to the row space of A_m to further suppress the tail logits.

## Method

For random OPMs, the paper restricts the embedding to the row space of A_m and shows that the bottom N−m logits are approximately Gaussian with variance governed by the Inverse Wishart distribution of (A_m A_m^T)^{-1}, yielding a closed-form lower bound Φ^{N−m}(1/√(mv)) on the probability that the top-m tokens dominate. A scaling argument then extends this to specifying exact probability ratios among the top-m tokens while driving the tail probability to zero. For the upper bound on trained OPMs, the paper reduces the question to the sign rank of a combinatorial indicator matrix and, using results from Alon et al., proves that the minimal embedding dimension d* satisfies 2m ≤ d* ≤ 2m+2, showing that optimally trained matrices can specify far more top-m tokens than random ones. Empirically, linear programming is used to find feasible embeddings for both random and trained OPMs across GPT-2, GPT2-XL, TinyLlama, T5-Large, and Llama2-7B, confirming that trained matrices perform comparably to random ones and that m reaches ~95 for GPT-2 and ~1070 for Llama2-7B.

## Results

The paper derives a theoretical lower bound showing that a randomly initialized OPM can assign any desired probabilities (summing to ~1) to the top-m tokens with probability near 1. For GPT-2 (N=50,257, d=768), this bound gives m≈26; for GPT-3-175B (d=12,288), m≈418; and for Llama2-7B (d=4,096), m≈143. Linear programming experiments show that both random and trained OPMs can actually specify top-m probabilities for much larger m: ~95 for GPT-2, ~400 for TinyLlama, and ~1,070 for Llama2-7B. The sign-rank analysis further shows the best possible trained OPM can specify top-m for up to m≈383 (GPT-2 size) and m≈1,023 (TinyLlama size), far exceeding random matrix performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between random OPMs and trained OPMs (GPT-2, GPT-2-XL, TinyLlama, T5-Large, Llama2-7B). LP-based experiments show that trained matrices perform essentially identically to same-sized random matrices in their ability to specify top-m token probabilities: GPT-2 (trained) and a random 50,257×768 matrix both achieve m≈95, while Llama2-7B (trained) reaches m≈1,070, matching its random counterpart. This indicates that training neither improves nor degrades the OPM's capacity to express high-probability token tuples. The theoretical sign-rank upper bound (Corollary 3: 2m ≤ d* ≤ 2m+2) shows the best possible OPM can specify top-m for m≈383 at GPT-2 size, roughly 4× the random matrix theoretical bound of m≈26, but the LP experiments close much of this gap empirically. The paper also compares uniform-distribution target probabilities against natural-language probabilities (from GPT-2 on NYT articles) and finds the results are nearly identical, confirming the robustness of the findings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a re-examination of the softmax bottleneck identified by Yang et al. (2018) and analyzed in depth by Kanai et al. (2018), Chang & McCallum (2022), and Godey et al. (2024), rather than proposing yet another architectural fix. It extends the m=1 LP feasibility analysis of Grivas et al. (2022) to arbitrary top-m tokens, and draws on Alon et al.'s (2015) sign-rank machinery to derive tight upper bounds on the embedding dimension needed for a trained OPM to specify any m-token subset. The key departure from prior work is the shift from asking 'which distributions are inexpressible?' to the more practical question of 'can the OPM accurately specify probabilities for the most likely tokens?', using random matrix theory (Inverse Wishart) for lower bounds.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges the working assumption that the softmax bottleneck is a significant practical limitation requiring architectural modifications (mixture of softmaxes, sigsoftmax, linear-monotonic-softmax, etc.), showing that even random OPMs handle top-m probability specification well for m values far exceeding what nucleus sampling typically requires (~200 tokens). The empirical finding that trained OPMs perform similarly to random ones on this metric is notable and raises the question of whether training is even optimizing this aspect of the output layer. However, the analysis is restricted to inference-time relevance; whether the bottleneck constrains gradient flow or representation learning during training remains an open question the paper explicitly flags but does not resolve.
