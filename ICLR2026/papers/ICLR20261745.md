## Problem

Prior work showed that a contaminated teacher can pass test-set knowledge to a clean student via knowledge distillation ('data laundering'), but its prevalence, magnitude relative to direct contamination, and underlying mechanisms remained unexplored. This paper asks whether data laundering is a pervasive threat to benchmark integrity or a minor, condition-dependent effect, and whether knowledge distillation can actually serve as a decontamination technique.

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes text classification samples (up to 512 tokens) and teacher model outputs (soft logits at temperature τ=2.0 or hard labels) as input, producing class predictions from a DistilBERT student. Critically, the student is always trained on the original clean training set; any test-set knowledge must enter exclusively through the teacher's distillation signal. Contamination is implemented by injecting the full test set into the teacher's training data while removing an equal number of original samples to keep size constant, and all experiments are repeated over five random seeds.

## Method

The paper conducts a three-part empirical study: (1) a large-scale comparison across eight classification benchmarks quantifying ΔAcc for direct contamination (baseline and teacher models) versus laundering (student models), showing KD reduces contamination gains by an order of magnitude; (2) a sample-level analysis computing Pearson correlations between per-sample laundering and contamination effects, revealing only weak correlations (r ≈ 0.1–0.3) that establish laundering as a mechanistically distinct phenomenon rather than 'contamination-lite'; and (3) controlled experiments on emotion and rotten_tomatoes where training data is stratified into five quintiles by semantic similarity to the test set, systematically widening the train-test distributional gap and demonstrating that laundering becomes statistically more significant as the gap increases, while direct contamination remains stable. The distillation protocol uses soft-label forward KL divergence (α=1, τ=2.0) as the primary configuration, with robustness verified across reverse KL, hard-label, and mixed-objective variants.

## Results

Across eight classification benchmarks, direct contamination yields large and highly significant accuracy gains for both Baseline and Teacher models, ranging from +4.89% (Emotion) to +25.66% (Tweet Sentiment) for Baselines and up to +29.01% for Teachers. In stark contrast, data laundering via knowledge distillation produces substantially smaller student gains, ranging from +0.56% (Rotten Tomatoes) to +5.77% (SNLI), with the difference being statistically insignificant for three of eight benchmarks (Banking77, Rotten Tomatoes, SNLI). On the most affected benchmark (Tweet Sentiment), the 25.66% direct contamination gain is reduced to just 3.25% after distillation, confirming KD as a strong decontamination bottleneck. Controlled experiments on Emotion and Rotten Tomatoes further show that systematically widening the train-test distributional gap increases the statistical significance of laundering, while direct contamination remains stable across gap levels.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between two leakage mechanisms: direct contamination (training directly on test-set-contaminated data, measured as ΔAcc_B for Baselines and ΔAcc_T for Teachers) versus data laundering (distilling from a contaminated teacher, measured as ΔAcc_S for Students). Direct contamination dominates across all eight benchmarks: Baseline gains range from +4.89% to +25.66%, while Student laundering gains are at most +5.77% (SNLI) and are statistically insignificant for Banking77 (+2.23%), Emotion (+0.66%), and Rotten Tomatoes (+0.56%). Even in the two cases where laundering is significant (AG News +0.65%, Tweet Sentiment +3.25%), the effect is 10–40× smaller than the corresponding direct contamination gain. Sample-level Pearson correlations between laundering and contamination effects are weak (r ≈ 0.12–0.32), confirming they are mechanistically distinct rather than one being a scaled version of the other. The authors also validate generality using Llama-3.2-1B and Qwen3-0.6B as teachers, where student laundering gains remain minimal (e.g., +1.91% on Tweet Sentiment vs. +25.80% for the teacher), reinforcing that KD acts as a consistent decontamination filter regardless of teacher architecture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper systematically extends Mansurov et al. (2025), who first formalized 'data laundering'—the transfer of test-set knowledge from a contaminated teacher to a clean student via knowledge distillation. While Mansurov et al. demonstrated the phenomenon exists, their experiments used a trimmed 2-layer BERT student and did not compare laundering against direct contamination or identify when it arises. This work departs by using properly pretrained student models, benchmarking laundering against direct contamination across eight diverse classification tasks, performing sample-level mechanistic analysis, and conducting controlled experiments that isolate the train-test distributional gap as the key driver. The broader data contamination literature (Magar & Schwartz 2022; Balloccu et al. 2024) provides the motivation, but this paper specifically addresses the KD-mediated leakage vector rather than direct memorization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway—that knowledge distillation generally functions as an effective decontamination filter rather than a leakage amplifier—provides a principled basis for using KD to mitigate risks of direct data exposure in model development pipelines. The identification of train-test distributional gap as the enabling condition for laundering has direct implications for benchmark design, motivating the release of multiple test splits at varying distributional distances. Key limitations include the restriction to English classification tasks with BERT-family encoder students, leaving open whether findings generalize to generative tasks, decoder-only LLM students, multilingual settings, or the partial/noisy contamination patterns that occur in practice. The privacy-utility trade-off between hard-label (safer) and soft-label (higher utility) distillation remains an unresolved design tension.
