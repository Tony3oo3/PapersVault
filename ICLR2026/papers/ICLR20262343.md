## Problem

Existing MLLM benchmarks either isolate narrow human-related tasks (action, face recognition) or report only coarse-grained scores, lacking the fine-grained probing power to diagnose nuanced human-centric visual understanding. A more critical gap lies in reasoning evaluation: current video benchmarks rarely require models to integrate multiple disparate visual cues or proactively seek implicit evidence beyond what is explicitly referenced in the question, leaving a key reasoning faculty untested.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262343/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Human-P&C takes 1–6 images or video clips (avg. 35.4 s, max 584 s) paired with multiple-choice questions (~5 options each), while Human-R takes longer video clips (avg. 469 s, max 5,225 s) paired with open-ended questions. For P&C, the output is macro-averaged accuracy per task, dimension, and level; for R, the output is an open-ended answer scored by an LLM judge (o3-mini) against expert-annotated Chain-of-Thought rationales that enumerate all key visual evidence. Each R question is guaranteed to require at least two distinct visual evidence items, at least one of which must be proactively extracted rather than referenced in the prompt.

## Method

HumanPCR organizes evaluation into three hierarchical levels—Perception (5 dimensions, 17 tasks), Comprehension (4 dimensions, 17 tasks), and Reasoning (11 domains, 5 reasoning types: Causal, Prediction, Counter-Factual, Assessment, Planning)—spanning 34 tasks and 9 dimensions. P&C questions are generated at scale (6,176 items) by combining template-based and LLM-based generation over existing dataset annotations, followed by LLM blind filtering and human verification. Human-R (442 items) is manually curated by domain experts with a multi-stage review pipeline (annotator → reviewer → meta-reviewer) enforcing that each question demands multi-evidence integration and at least one proactive visual cue, yielding a final acceptance rate below 20%. Evaluation uses Direct Answer prompting for multiple-choice and CoT prompting for open-ended questions, with o3-mini as an LLM judge validated against 4,000 human annotations (Pearson > 0.64, pairwise agreement ~0.85). The key empirical finding is that models predominantly fail at proactive evidence extraction rather than referred-evidence retrieval, and that scaling frame counts or applying heuristic context-extraction techniques offers only marginal gains, whereas reasoning-enhanced models (e.g., o3) reduce missed proactive evidence and achieve consistent improvements.

## Results

On HumanPCR, the best open-source model (InternVL3-78B) achieves 62.77% macro-average accuracy on Human-P&C, while the best proprietary model (o4-mini) reaches 62.28%, both far below the human baseline of 81.95%. On the challenging Human-R reasoning level, o4-mini leads proprietary models at 53.39%, while the best open-source model (InternVL3-78B) reaches only 37.56%, and the human baseline is 73.17%. Most open-source models remain below 30% on Human-R, and even the strongest models show significant deficits in fine-grained perception (Posture, Contact) and high-level understanding (Procedure, Relation).

## Baselines & Comparisons

On Human-P&C (perception and comprehension), open-source models rival proprietary ones: InternVL3-78B (62.77%) marginally surpasses o4-mini (62.28%), while Gemini-2.5-Flash (60.02%) and InternVL2.5-78B (60.58%) are competitive. However, on Human-R (reasoning), proprietary models dominate: o4-mini (53.39%) and o3 with thinking (59.28%) substantially outperform the best open-source model InternVL3-78B (37.56%) by over 15 points, and all proprietary models exceed 30% while most open-source models remain below 30%. GPT-4o (41.40% on Human-R) and Gemini-2.5-Flash (43.44%) show intermediate reasoning performance. All models lag the human baseline by 15–35 points across levels, with the largest gap on Human-R (73.17% human vs. 53.39% best model). The paper further shows that advanced video understanding techniques (token selection, memory-based retrieval) that work on Video-MME see significant performance drops on Human-R, confirming that multi-evidence proactive reasoning is a distinct challenge not addressed by existing context-extraction methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

HumanPCR carves new ground at the intersection of human-centric visual understanding benchmarks and video reasoning benchmarks, two lines of work that have largely developed in parallel. On the human-centric side, it departs from narrow single-task benchmarks (MotionBench, Face-Human-Bench, ActionAtlas) and broader but shallow evaluations (HumanVBench) by introducing a 9-dimension, 34-task hierarchical taxonomy spanning perception, comprehension, and reasoning. On the video reasoning side, it extends prior multi-evidence benchmarks (Video-Holmes, MMR-V) by adding the explicit requirement of *proactive* evidence seeking—where the question does not reveal which visual cues are needed—combined with open-ended questions and expert-annotated CoT rationales, a combination absent from all prior benchmarks it surveys.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a concrete and actionable failure mode: models treat the question as a retrieval shortcut and fail to proactively extract implicit visual evidence, a gap that neither frame scaling nor standard context-extraction techniques (token selection, memory retrieval) can close. This reframes the video reasoning problem from one of context length to one of evidence-seeking strategy, with implications for how future models should be trained. Limitations include the small Human-R set (442 questions, 20% acceptance rate), reliance on LLM-as-judge for open-ended scoring, and dependence on academic datasets that may not cover all professional domains. Open questions include whether reasoning-enhanced models (o3, Video-R1) will close the proactive-evidence gap and how to efficiently scale annotation for the reasoning level.
