## Problem

Mutual information skill learning (MISL) methods in RL exhibit wildly varying performance, yet the role of the learned representation and the specific mutual information parametrization remains unexplained theoretically. This paper provides the first identifiability guarantee for representation learning in RL, formally establishing when and why MISL recovers ground-truth environment states, and explaining the practical differences between design choices such as inner product vs. other critic parametrizations and different MI formulations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260737/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes observations o (either low-dimensional state vectors or high-dimensional pixel images) and skill vectors z drawn uniformly from the unit hypersphere S^{d-1}. It produces an encoder φ mapping observations to features on the hypersphere, a skill-conditioned policy π(a|o,z), and a contrastive critic q(z|φ(o),φ(o')). For linear identifiability to hold, the feature dimensionality must match the ground-truth state dimensionality d; a lower-dimensional latent space creates an information bottleneck that prevents linear decoding of all state components, though task transfer can still succeed.

## Method

The paper proves that CSF's learned features identify ground-truth POMDP states up to a linear transformation by recasting the MISL problem as a nonlinear ICA identifiability problem, where diverse skills act as auxiliary variables. The key mechanism is the interplay between three components: (1) a finite set of skills forming an affine generator system of R^d, (2) a contrastive loss equivalent to cross-entropy with an inner product parametrization [φ(o')−φ(o)]^T z, and (3) feature differences following a von Mises-Fisher distribution conditioned on the skill. The proof applies the cross-entropy identifiability technique of Reizinger et al. (2024a) by showing the CSF loss has the same log-linear structure, then uses skill diversity to construct an invertible matrix that recovers the linear map A relating learned features to true states. The analysis further explains why I(s,s';z) is geometrically superior to I(s;z) (the former enforces distinctness of consecutive embeddings while the latter permits collapse) and why maximum-entropy policies break the skill-dependence required for identifiability.

## Results

The paper proves that CSF features identify ground-truth POMDP states up to a linear transformation (Prop. 1 and 3), and validates this empirically across five MuJoCo/DMC environments. The coverage-dependent R² score (CDR²) shows strong positive correlation with oracle return in state-based environments (0.93 for Half Cheetah, 0.94 for Ant, 0.97 for Quadruped State), confirming that identifiability predicts zero-shot task transfer. In pixel-based environments (Quadruped Pixel, Kitchen, Robobin), the correlation is weaker but the agent still explores and recovers ground-truth states. Ablations confirm that a minimum number of diverse skills and sufficient latent dimensionality (≥ state dimension) are necessary for identifiability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical identifiability paper without direct algorithmic baseline comparisons; the 'competing' methods are design choices within the MISL family. The paper contrasts three mutual information formulations—$I(s,s';z)$ (CSF), $I(s_0,s;z)$, and $I(s;z)$—showing that only the feature-difference formulation $I(s,s';z)$ avoids representation collapse by enforcing that consecutive state embeddings are close but distinct (Fig. 2). On skill diversity, a small fixed set of skills (e.g., 4 or 8) fails to achieve identifiability or coverage in the Ant environment, while the original continuous sampling (effectively infinite skills) succeeds (Fig. 4). On latent dimensionality, reducing the feature space below the true state dimension (29) prevents linear identifiability (R² drops sharply), though zero-shot task transfer degrades more gracefully (Fig. 5). The maximum-entropy policy is shown to be suboptimal (Prop. 4) because it breaks skill dependence, making the discriminative critic unable to infer skills from state pairs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges mutual information skill learning (MISL) in RL with the nonlinear ICA identifiability framework from self-supervised learning. It takes the CSF method (Zheng et al., 2025) as a prototypical MISL algorithm and shows that its design choices—inner product parametrization of the critic, diverse skill-conditioned policies, and contrastive loss—precisely satisfy the assumptions required by the cross-entropy identifiability theory of Reizinger et al. (2024a), which itself builds on the auxiliary-variable ICA framework of Hyvarinen et al. (2019) and Zimmermann et al. (2021). Prior MISL work documented empirical performance differences between objectives and parametrizations without theoretical explanation; this paper provides the first formal identifiability guarantee in RL, recasting skills as auxiliary variables in the ICA sense and explaining why certain architectural choices succeed while others (e.g., maximum-entropy policies) fail.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As the first identifiability result in RL, this work gives a principled explanation for why MISL methods work and provides actionable design guidance: inner product parametrization is not merely a convenient choice but is necessary for linear identifiability, and entropy regularization actively undermines skill diversity. The coverage-dependent R² metric (CDR²) addresses a practical gap in evaluating representation quality in RL by distinguishing well-explored from collapsed scenarios. Open questions include whether the technical assumptions (vMF concentration, uniform marginals, injective generator) can be relaxed for broader environments, whether the results extend beyond CSF to other MISL variants, and the non-trivial relationship between identifiability scores and downstream task performance, which the paper notes can be at odds in some settings.
