## Problem

Flow map models (e.g., MeanFlow) enable 1-4 step image generation by predicting average velocity between timesteps, but their training typically requires architectural modifications—specifically, injecting the next-timestep embedding throughout the entire diffusion transformer. This limits compatibility with pretrained flow models and raises the question of whether the next-timestep information is actually needed in the encoder, or only in the decoder where future-state prediction occurs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262193/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model operates in the latent space of a pretrained Stable Diffusion VAE (8× downsampling, 4 channels), so a 256×256 image becomes a 4×32×32 latent and a 512×512 image becomes a 4×64×64 latent. Input is a Gaussian noise sample in this latent space plus an optional class label (with 10% dropout for unconditional branches), and output is a single generated image decoded from the latent. Generation requires only 1–4 network function evaluations (NFE) via Euler or restart samplers, compared to 400+ NFE for the underlying flow model.

## Method

Decoupled MeanFlow (DMF) partitions the diffusion transformer into an encoder (first d layers) conditioned on the current timestep t and a decoder (remaining layers) conditioned on the next timestep r, reusing the same timestep embedding layer for both. This decoupling means any pretrained flow model can be directly repurposed as a flow map without architectural changes, and the paper shows the converted model often outperforms the original flow model even without fine-tuning. Training combines a flow matching loss (for boundary conditions) with a MeanFlow loss (for average-velocity prediction) using an adaptive weighted Cauchy loss to handle high-variance MF gradients, model guidance to enable high-quality 1-step generation without doubling inference cost, and a modified logit-normal timestep proposal that biases r toward zero to improve 1-step performance. A two-stage strategy—first training a flow model, then fine-tuning as DMF—proves more compute-efficient than training flow maps from scratch, with stronger base representations (e.g., from REPA alignment) accelerating convergence.

## Results

On ImageNet 256×256, DMF-XL/2+ achieves a 1-step (NFE=1) FID of 2.16 and a 4-step (NFE=4) FID of 1.51, surpassing all prior few-step diffusion/flow models and even GANs (StyleGAN-XL, FID=2.30) and normalizing flows (STARFlow, FID=2.40) at 1 step. On ImageNet 512×512, DMF-XL/2+ reaches 1-step FID=2.12 and 4-step FID=1.68, outperforming the prior best few-step method sCD-XXL (FID=2.28 at 1 step). The 4-step FID of 1.51 on 256×256 nearly matches the full flow model SiT-XL/2+REPA (FID=1.37) while requiring over 100× fewer function evaluations (4 vs. 434 NFE).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DMF-XL/2+ at 1 step (FID=2.16) outperforms all prior few-step flow/diffusion models: MF-XL/2+ (FID=2.20 at 2 NFE), IMM-XL/2 (FID=2.51 at 2×4 NFE), and Shortcut-XL/2 (FID=10.60 at 1 NFE), as well as strong 1-step baselines StyleGAN-XL (FID=2.30) and STARFlow (FID=2.40). At 4 steps, DMF-XL/2+ (FID=1.51) approaches the quality of the full flow model SiT-XL/2+REPA (FID=1.37) while using ~100× fewer NFE (4 vs. 434). On ImageNet 512×512, DMF-XL/2+ (1-step FID=2.12) beats sCD-XXL (FID=2.28), sCD-XL (FID=2.40), and AYF-S (FID=3.32) at 1 step, and at 4 steps (FID=1.68) slightly edges out AYF-S (FID=1.70). Against MeanFlow (MF) of the same architecture size, DMF consistently achieves lower FID with fewer training FLOPs: DMF-XL/2 at 200 epochs (FID=3.02) beats MF-XL/2 at 240 epochs (FID=3.43), and DMF-XL/2 at 240 epochs (FID=2.83) further widens the gap. The paper also shows that converting a pretrained flow model into DMF without fine-tuning already outperforms the original flow model at matched NFE, validating the architectural decoupling hypothesis.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends MeanFlow (Geng et al., 2025a), which introduced a principled training objective for learning flow maps (average velocity between timesteps), by proposing an architectural decoupling: the next timestep r is fed only to the decoder rather than both encoder and decoder as in prior flow map designs. The decoupling is motivated by the implicit representation-learning perspective on diffusion transformers (Yu et al., 2025; Li et al., 2023), where the encoder extracts semantic features that are timestep-agnostic, while the decoder is where future-state prediction actually occurs. The method is designed to be a drop-in conversion of any pretrained SiT-style flow model (Ma et al., 2024) into a flow map, building on the REPA representation-alignment framework (Yu et al., 2025) for strong base representations, and incorporating model guidance (Tang et al., 2025) and adaptive Cauchy loss weighting (Karras et al., 2024b) for stable few-step training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: any pretrained flow model can be repurposed as a flow map with zero architectural changes, enabling 1–4 NFE generation at >100× inference speedup while matching or exceeding the quality of the original multi-step flow model. The finding that investing compute in flow model training (rather than flow map training) yields better scaling is a useful resource-allocation insight for practitioners. Limitations include visual artifacts at 1-step, evaluation restricted to ImageNet with a VAE latent space, and training instability at 512×512 requiring QK-norm. Open questions include whether DMF transfers to text-to-image and video generation, how post-training alignment methods (DPO, GRPO) interact with flow maps, and whether inference-time scaling strategies (searching over intermediate noise states) can further exploit the restart sampler's stochasticity.
