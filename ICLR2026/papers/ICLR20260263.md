## Problem

When fine-tuning LLMs on multi-domain SFT data (math, code, reasoning, instruction), naively mixing datasets produces imbalanced capabilities and cross-domain interference, yet the optimal mixture proportions remain poorly understood. Existing data-mixing methods (DoReMi, DOGE) are pretraining-centric or rely on costly global weight searches, leaving a principled, SFT-specific framework for resolving data conflicts across domains as an open problem.

## Input / Output

The method takes a base LLM (e.g., Llama3.1-8B), a multi-domain SFT training set partitioned into n domain-specific subsets, and a small independent reference validation set (500 held-out samples) strictly excluded from training. It produces a domain-level adjustment vector β ∈ ℝⁿ where positive values trigger upsampling (data repetition) and negative values trigger downsampling (random subsampling) of each domain's subset, yielding a reweighted training set and a fine-tuned multi-capability model. The β magnitudes are normalized by a dynamic scaling factor γ so that the maximum absolute adjustment is capped at a hyperparameter m (default 0.15), keeping perturbations small relative to the original distribution.

## Method

IDEAL formulates data mixture optimization as a bi-level problem: the inner loop trains the model to convergence on the current mixture, while the outer loop computes the gradient of reference-set loss with respect to each domain's volume parameter βⱼ via the influence function (inverse Hessian times gradient, Eq. 4–5). To make this tractable for billion-parameter LLMs, the Hessian is approximated using K-FAC: each MLP layer's Hessian block is decomposed as a Kronecker product of input and error-gradient covariance matrices, further reduced via eigendecomposition to retain only low-variance (stable) layers. A dynamic scaling vector γ normalizes the resulting β to a fixed magnitude m, and the algorithm iterates (train → compute β → upsample/downsample domains → retrain) for T rounds. Unlike prior influence-function work that operates at the sample level, IDEAL applies second-order information at the domain level, directly controlling data volume ratios rather than selecting individual examples, and requires no proxy model or reinforcement learning.

## Results

On the primary 4-benchmark evaluation (GSM8K, HumanEval, BBH, IFEval) with Llama3.1-8B, IDEAL achieves an overall average of 57.87 (Epoch=1) and 59.23 (Epoch=3) after 2 iterations, outperforming uniform Joint SFT (54.79 / 55.35) by approximately 5.6% and 7.0% respectively. The largest single-benchmark gain is on HumanEval, where IDEAL reaches 50.61 Pass@1 (Epoch=1) versus 41.26 for Joint SFT, a +9.35-point improvement. In the extended 8-benchmark evaluation (adding MATH, ARC_C, MBPP, TruthfulQA), IDEAL achieves 55.20 overall versus 54.03 for Joint SFT, confirming consistent gains across more diverse tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

IDEAL consistently outperforms both DoReMi and DOGE across all four benchmarks in both Epoch=1 and Epoch=3 settings. Against DoReMi (best run D2), IDEAL D2 gains +2.70 points overall at Epoch=1 (57.87 vs 55.17) and +2.98 at Epoch=3 (59.23 vs 56.25), with the largest margin on HumanEval (+6.71 at Epoch=1). Against DOGE (best run D1 at Epoch=1, D2 at Epoch=3), IDEAL D2 gains +3.16 points overall at Epoch=1 (57.87 vs 54.71) and +2.36 at Epoch=3 (59.23 vs 56.87), again with the most substantial advantage on HumanEval (+8.37 and +4.10 respectively). IDEAL also surpasses the uniform Joint SFT baseline by +3.08 (Epoch=1) and +3.88 (Epoch=3) overall, and notably exceeds the best single-task Specific SFT model (Instruction, 47.17 at Epoch=1) by a wide margin, demonstrating that optimized multi-domain mixing can outperform domain-specific specialization. The paper further validates via a controlled experiment (Table 3) that IDEAL's advantage is not merely due to increased total data volume: when DoReMi and DOGE are upsampled to match IDEAL's total data size, they still trail IDEAL by 2.5 and 4.1 points respectively.

## Relation to Prior Work

IDEAL adapts the influence function framework (Koh & Liang, 2017) from sample-level data selection to domain-level data volume control, and leverages K-FAC (Martens & Grosse, 2015) to make the required inverse-Hessian computation tractable for large LLMs. It departs from pretraining-centric data mixing methods like DoReMi and DOGE, which rely on proxy models and group DRO, by formulating a bi-level optimization directly on the SFT model's second-order information. Unlike LESS or MATES, which select individual samples, IDEAL operates at the domain granularity, adjusting up/down-sampling ratios via a single scalar β per domain. The paper also contrasts with empirical mixing laws (Ye et al., 2024) that require costly global searches, positioning gradient-guided iterative refinement as a more scalable alternative.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

IDEAL provides a principled, model-aware mechanism for resolving cross-domain data conflicts in multi-capability SFT, a problem that has largely been addressed by heuristic reweighting or proxy-model approaches. The finding that data distribution matters more than raw volume (D0 vs. D0(FULL) showing near-identical performance) reinforces that SFT quality is governed by composition rather than scale. Limitations include the reliance on full fine-tuning (no LoRA/PEFT experiments), K-FAC approximation error that may degrade with very deep architectures, and the computational cost of two full training iterations (~88 hours on 8×A100 for 70B). Open questions include whether the approach extends to more than five domains, whether it works with parameter-efficient fine-tuning, and how sensitive the β updates are to the choice of reference set.
