## Problem

Linear classifiers offer interpretability and reduced overfitting but face a persistent tradeoff: high-performing methods like LDA and SVM require cubic time complexity, making them impractical for large-scale data, while faster methods like the minimum distance classifier sacrifice predictive accuracy. Additionally, no unified geometric framework existed to explain how centroid-based linear classifiers relate to one another or to systematically derive new ones.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an N×M labeled data matrix (flattened images, SMILES-tokenized chemical formulas, or gene expression vectors) in original values without standardization, and produces a unit-length discriminant vector plus an optimal operating point (bias) found by scanning N−1 midpoints between sorted projections. For multiclass tasks, one-versus-one binary classifiers are combined via error-correcting output codes with hinge loss. The discriminant is always normalized to unit length, so only its direction carries meaning; the bias is determined post hoc by a performance-score search over projected midpoints.

## Method

The paper introduces the Geometric Discriminant Analysis (GDA) framework, which decomposes any centroid-based linear discriminant into a Centroid Discriminant Basis 0 (CDB0, the unit vector between class centroids) plus geometric correction terms, showing LDA is a special case where the correction is a covariance-dependent rotation. Building on GDA, Centroid Discriminant Analysis (CDA) iteratively refines CDB0 through performance-guided rotations on 2D planes: at each step, sample weights are shifted toward points near the decision boundary to compute a second CDB, the two vectors span a plane, and Bayesian optimization searches for the rotation angle maximizing a composite performance score (F-score + AC-score). This yields quadratic time complexity O(NM + N log N) versus cubic for LDA/SVM, with convergence guaranteed by monotonicity of the bounded performance sequence. A kernel extension replaces the Gram matrix to enable nonlinear classification while retaining the same rotation-based training procedure.

## Results

Across 27 real-world datasets (standard images, medical images, chemical properties), CDA achieved a top-2 multiclass AUROC on 17/27 datasets and the best average ranking (~3.3) among all linear classifiers. On large-scale data (N×M > 10⁷), CDA's quadratic training complexity yields substantially faster runtimes than cubic-complexity LDA and SVM. On the 1.3M-cell mouse brain dataset, CDA-Fibonacci outperforms fast SVM on both AUROC and single-core training time as sample size grows. In the kernel (nonlinear) setting, nCDA achieves 0.777 AUROC on SVHN, 0.625 on ClinTox, and 0.625 on FractureMNIST3D, substantially improving over linear CDA (0.615, 0.567, 0.518 respectively).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LDA (Fisher's), CDA wins on scalability (quadratic O(NM + N log N) vs. cubic O(NM² + M³) training complexity) and achieves a better average AUROC ranking (~3.3 vs. higher) across 27 datasets; on the kernel setting, nCDA is competitive with nLDA (0.777 vs. 0.786 AUROC on SVHN; 0.625 vs. 0.605 on ClinTox; 0.625 vs. 0.608 on Fracture3D). Against SVM (original, Liblinear fast, and SVM-SGD variants), CDA clearly wins on training time for large datasets (N×M > 10⁷) and on the 1.3M-cell mouse brain benchmark where CDA-Fibonacci surpasses fast SVM in both AUROC and speed; on the kernel setting, nCDA outperforms nSVM on ClinTox (0.625 vs. 0.500 AUROC) and Fracture3D (0.625 vs. 0.608) but is slightly below nSVM on SVHN (0.777 vs. 0.736 — actually nCDA wins here too). Against logistic regression (Liblinear fast LR), CDA achieves a better average ranking on the 27-dataset benchmark. Against PLSDA (Barker & Rayens 2003), the paper reports in Section T that CDA remains competitive, though detailed numbers are deferred to the supplement. Overall, CDA consistently outperforms or matches all linear baselines in performance while being faster, and its kernel extension closes the gap with kernel LDA/SVM on challenging nonlinear tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper introduces the Geometric Discriminant Analysis (GDA) framework, which reframes centroid-based linear classifiers as geometric rotations of a centroid discriminant basis (CDB0). It demonstrates that both Fisher's LDA (1936) and the Minimum Distance Classifier (Duda et al., 2001) are special cases of GDA, with LDA corresponding to a covariance-driven correction and MDC to the zero-correction limit. From this framework, the authors derive Centroid Discriminant Analysis (CDA), which replaces LDA's closed-form covariance correction with iterative 2D-plane rotations searched via Bayesian optimization, achieving quadratic rather than cubic training complexity. The approach departs from prior discriminant methods by making the optimization target explicitly performance-dependent at each rotation step rather than relying on a single variance-ratio criterion.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The quadratic time complexity of CDA (vs. cubic for LDA and standard SVM) is practically significant for large-scale applications such as single-cell genomics, where the paper demonstrates CDA outperforming fast SVM on a 1.3-million-cell dataset. The GDA framework offers a principled template for designing new classifiers under different geometric constraints, though the paper acknowledges this generative potential is not yet fully explored. Key limitations include the absence of a native multiclass formulation (relying on ECOC instead), the restriction to centroid-based classifiers (excluding margin-based methods like SVM from the framework), and the preliminary nature of the kernel extension. Open questions include whether GDA can be extended to non-centroid-based discriminants and whether the Bayesian optimization step can be replaced by cheaper alternatives without sacrificing performance.
