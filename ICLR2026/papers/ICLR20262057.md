## Problem

When LLMs are used both to synthesize training data and to evaluate student models trained on that data, the relatedness between generator and judge introduces a systematic evaluation bias that inflates scores for the related student. This 'preference leakage' is distinct from previously studied biases (egocentric bias, data leakage) because it operates through subtle stylistic and formatting features inherited via synthetic data, making it harder to detect and more pervasive in real-world LLM-as-a-judge pipelines.

## Input / Output

The evaluation pipeline takes pairwise responses from two student models (each fine-tuned on synthetic data from a different generator LLM) and prompts from Arena-Hard (500 questions) or AlpacaEval 2.0 (805 questions). A judge LLM performs pairwise comparisons, and the resulting win-rates are aggregated into the Preference Leakage Score (PLS), a single scalar per model pair that measures the degree to which each judge favors its related student over the unrelated one. For real-world impact analysis, the output is a ranking difference between LMArena and AlpacaEval 2.0 positions for known distillation pairs.

## Method

The paper formally defines preference leakage through three relatedness conditions between generator M_G and judge M_J (same model, inheritance, same family) and proposes the PLS metric, which computes the relative deviation of each judge's win-rate toward its related student from the average win-rate across judges. Empirically, the authors fine-tune Mistral-7B and Qwen-2.5-14B on 30K synthetic responses from GPT-4o, Gemini-1.5, and LLaMA-3.3, then evaluate all model pairs under each judge to quantify bias. Further analyses vary data mixing ratios (10–70% synthetic), learning methods (SFT, DPO, ICL), student model size, and question type to characterize severity, while a recognition experiment shows judges perform near-randomly at identifying their student's outputs, confirming the bias is driven by subtle surface-level features (style, format) rather than explicit recognition. A BERT classifier achieves 82.4% accuracy in distinguishing student generations, and ablation of spurious features (style, format, wording) confirms that stylistic and formatting artifacts are the primary conduits of leakage.

## Results

On Arena-Hard and AlpacaEval 2.0, the Preference Leakage Score (PLS) ranges from −1.5% to 37.1% across model pairs, with the strongest leakage observed for Qwen-2.5-14B under the GPT-4o & Gemini-1.5 judge pair (37.1% on Arena-Hard, 18.6% on AlpacaEval 2.0, avg 27.9%). For Mistral-7B, the same pair yields 28.7% / 18.4% (avg 23.6%). The GPT-4o & LLaMA-3.3 pair shows near-zero leakage (−0.1% to 1.7% avg), confirming that bias is tied to the degree of relatedness between generator and judge. Smaller student models (e.g., Qwen-2.5-3B at 35.4% avg PLS) exhibit higher leakage than larger ones (Qwen-2.5-14B at 27.9%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against a single competing method but instead compares preference leakage across different relatedness settings and learning methods. Among relatedness types, the same-model setting produces the highest average PLS (23.6%), followed by inheritance with different instructions (22.3%) and inheritance with same instructions (19.3%); same-family models in the same series yield 8.9%, while different-series models drop to 2.8%. Among learning methods, SFT shows the strongest leakage (23.6% avg PLS), DPO reduces it to 5.2%, and ICL eliminates it entirely (−2.7%), indicating that pairwise optimization and context-only learning substantially mitigate the problem. In the real-world impact analysis, preference leakage produces a ranking difference of 1.33 on AlpacaEval 2.0 for Vicuna models, exceeding the 1.00 difference from egocentric bias for GPT-4 Preview, suggesting preference leakage has a larger practical effect on leaderboard rankings. For mitigation, contextual calibration reduces the Error Bias metric from 17.8 to 7.3, outperforming prompting (18.3), CoT (15.6), paraphrasing (18.7), and auto-calibration (20.7).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM-as-a-judge evaluation paradigm (Zheng et al., 2023) and the line of work on evaluator biases (egocentric bias in Panickssery et al., 2024) by identifying a new contamination vector: preference leakage arising from relatedness between the synthetic-data generator and the judge LLM. Unlike egocentric bias, where the judge IS the generator, preference leakage operates through a third party (the student model trained on the generator's outputs) and through three distinct relatedness types (same model, inheritance, same family). The paper also draws a conceptual analogy to data leakage in pre-training, but shifts the contamination from content overlap to preference/style overlap transmitted via synthetic data, making it harder to detect since LLMs do not disclose training data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Preference leakage is shown to be pervasive in widely-used leaderboards (AlpacaEval 2.0, Arena-Hard, LMArena) and to cause ranking distortions comparable to or exceeding egocentric bias, yet it remains undetected because judge LLMs perform at near-random accuracy when asked to recognize their own student models. The finding that smaller student models are MORE susceptible (opposite of data contamination) and that subjective questions are disproportionately affected highlights that the problem is not easily mitigated by simply using larger models or objective benchmarks. Open questions include scalable detection methods (the BERT classifier achieving 82% accuracy suggests the signal is detectable by simpler models), whether preference leakage compounds in iterative RLHF/synthetic-data loops, and whether the limited mitigation via contextual calibration (reducing Error Bias from 17.8 to 7.3) is sufficient for production evaluation pipelines.
