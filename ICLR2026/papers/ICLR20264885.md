## Problem

As Speech Language Models (SLMs) move from single-user personal devices to shared multi-user environments (smart homes, in-car assistants), they must distinguish between speakers to prevent one user's confidential information from leaking to another. Existing SLM benchmarks test conversational ability but are speaker-agnostic, multi-speaker benchmarks analyze who said what without assessing whether the model adapts its response, and privacy benchmarks target globally sensitive data (passwords) rather than contextually sensitive information (a private appointment shared in conversation). No prior benchmark systematically evaluates whether an SLM can enforce speaker-conditioned access to information in asynchronous multi-user spoken dialogues.

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes multi-speaker audio dialogues (32.86 hours, bilingual English/Chinese, synthesized via CosyVoice2 from 200-speaker pools drawn from AISHELL-2 and WenetSpeech) structured as three-turn sequences: secret disclosure → confidentiality instruction → third-party probe. The model must produce a speaker-aware text response that either withholds or discloses the secret depending on whether the querier's voice matches the original speaker. Evaluation is reference-free, using an LLM-as-judge (Deepseek-V3 and Gemini-2.5-Pro, majority-voted over 3 inferences) that classifies each response into three categories: valid-safe (A), valid-leaked (B), or invalid (C), from which Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1 are derived.

## Method

VoxPrivacy introduces a three-tier task structure of increasing cognitive difficulty: Tier 1 tests obedience to an explicit non-disclosure command, Tier 2 requires using the querier's voice as a biometric key for conditional access, and Tier 3 demands proactive inference of sensitivity from content alone without any explicit instruction. The dataset is built via a four-stage pipeline: multi-LLM text generation (Deepseek, Gemini, ChatGPT) across eight secret categories, automated deduplication and human-verified refinement, assembly into structured dialogues with owner/third-party speaker conditions, and TTS synthesis with DNSMOS and WER quality filtering. To demonstrate a path forward, the authors fine-tune Kimi-Audio (updating its Whisper-large-v3 encoder and adaptor) on a 4,000-hour training set that mixes privacy dialogues with ~1,500 hours of general tasks (ASR, SER, ASC, AQA, Voice-Chat) at a 30% general-to-privacy ratio to prevent catastrophic forgetting. The paper further validates findings on a 586-utterance human-recorded subset (Real-VoxPrivacy), diagnoses failures via a non-sensitive control experiment revealing a Speaker Continuity Bias, and stress-tests robustness against needle-in-the-haystack, jailbreaking, and voice-spoofing attacks.

## Results

On the VoxPrivacy benchmark (32.86 hours, 7,107 utterances), most open-source SLMs perform at or near random chance (~50% accuracy) on the conditional privacy tasks (Tier 2 and Tier 3), indicating a fundamental inability to bind speaker identity to privacy rules. The proposed fine-tuned model (Kimi-Audio-sft) achieves 83.93% accuracy and 82.65% F1 on Tier 2 (EN) and 77.57% accuracy and 77.83% F1 on Tier 3 (EN), surpassing the strongest closed-source baseline Gemini-2.5-pro (76.05% / 66.28% accuracy) on both tiers. The text-only LLM upper bound reaches 88.37% (Tier 2) and 85.21% (Tier 3) accuracy, revealing a persistent gap between speech-based and text-based privacy reasoning. On the human-recorded Real-VoxPrivacy subset, the same performance ordering is confirmed, validating that the failures are not TTS artifacts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The fine-tuned Kimi-Audio-sft outperforms all eight open-source baselines by a wide margin on both Tier 2 and Tier 3: e.g., on Tier 2 (EN) it reaches 83.93% accuracy versus 48–50% for Qwen2.5-Omni, MiniCPM-o2.6, Kimi-Audio (unfine-tuned), Qwen2Audio, Voxtral3B, Baichuan-Omni-1.5, and GLM4Voice. Against the closed-source Gemini-2.5-pro, the proposed model wins on both tiers (Tier 2: 83.93% vs 76.05% accuracy; Tier 3: 77.57% vs 66.28% accuracy), with the gap widening on the harder Tier 3 inference task. Gemini-2.0-flash is consistently weaker than Gemini-2.5-pro and is outperformed by the proposed model on all metrics. The text-only LLM upper bound (Gemini-2.0-flash with explicit speaker ID tags) still leads by 4–8 points, indicating that the residual gap is attributable to speech processing and biometric-semantic integration rather than pure reasoning. On the Chinese subset, the proposed model maintains its lead over all baselines (e.g., Tier 3 ZH: 82.88% accuracy vs 68.58% for Gemini-2.5-pro), though the absolute scores are slightly lower than English, reflecting higher ASR error rates and weaker Chinese commonsense reasoning in the backbone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a new evaluation axis at the intersection of multi-speaker awareness and privacy in Speech Language Models. Prior multi-speaker benchmarks (MSU-Bench, Song et al.) test whether a model can identify who said what, but stop at input analysis without assessing whether the model generates a speaker-conditioned response. Existing privacy benchmarks for SLMs (AudioTrust, SafeDialBench) focus on globally sensitive data like passwords, while text-based interactional privacy work (Confaide, PrivacyLens) cannot address the speech-specific challenge of inferring 'to whom' from acoustic properties. The paper grounds its task in Nissenbaum's Contextual Integrity theory and uses Kimi-Audio as the architectural base for its fine-tuned model, but the benchmark itself is a novel contribution that no prior work addresses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that interactional privacy is a largely unsolved problem: most open-source SLMs perform at random chance on conditional privacy decisions, and even the strongest closed-source model (Gemini-2.5-pro) drops from ~76% on Tier 2 to ~67% on Tier 3, exposing a fundamental 'inference gap' where models cannot autonomously determine what is sensitive. The Speaker Continuity Bias finding—that open-source models make disproportionately more errors when the speaker switches—suggests current training paradigms are heavily single-speaker weighted, pointing to a concrete data-level fix. Open questions include whether reinforcement learning or other paradigms can close the remaining gap to the LLM upper bound, how privacy norms vary across cultures, and whether the synthetic-data limitation affects generalization to genuinely paralinguistic contexts (emotion, stress) that might modulate privacy expectations.
