## Problem

Existing theoretical analyses of transformer factual recall rely on idealized assumptions such as orthogonal (one-hot) embeddings or infinite data, which do not reflect practical training with finite samples and random non-orthogonal embeddings. This paper asks whether one can characterize the optimization and sample complexity of gradient-based learning in a transformer with non-orthogonal embeddings on a factual recall task, and whether the resulting scaling between vocabulary size, sequence length, embedding dimension, MLP width, and sample size is fundamentally statistical.

## Input / Output

The model receives a length-L sequence of one-hot tokens drawn uniformly from a vocabulary of size V, where a single random position ℓ is designated as informative and marked by an added trigger vector z_trig in the embedding space; all other positions are noise. The target label is the informative token passed through a fixed random permutation matrix Π*, so the task is to both locate ℓ via attention and recall the correct permuted label. Embeddings are random Gaussian matrices of dimension d < V (non-orthogonal), and the model outputs a softmax distribution over V tokens, with argmax decoding used for accuracy evaluation.

## Method

The paper analyzes a single-layer transformer (attention + optional MLP) with random Gaussian embeddings trained by a 3-step gradient descent procedure: first update the value matrix V, then the key-query matrix W_KQ, then V again, with a small learning rate for the first step and a large one for the remaining two. The core technical contribution is decomposing the post-training attention scores into a Signal term (alignment between the trigger and learned W_KQ) and three noise terms (gradient noise from finite-sample concentration, mean bias from nonzero token means, and MLP noise from finite width), yielding Theorem 1: successful learning requires Signal ≳ 1/(VL²) to dominate all noise terms, producing a multiplicative scaling in (V, N, d, L, m). For the Attention-only model this reduces to d² ≳ max{V, V^{2/3}L^{8/3}/N^{4/3}}, while the Attention-MLP model introduces additional phases governed by the interplay of width m and embedding dimension d. A statistical lower bound (Theorem 2) shows that any method accessing only the initialized attention outputs requires N ≳ V·min{1, L/d²}, confirming the multiplicative trade-off is inherent rather than an artifact of the specific 3-step algorithm. Empirical experiments with both the 3-step procedure and full Adam training (with layer normalization) validate the predicted multiplicative scalings across varying V, d, L, N, and m.

## Results

The main result (Theorem 1) establishes that successful gradient-descent learning of factual recall requires the signal term 1/(VL²) to dominate three noise terms (gradient noise, mean bias, MLP noise), yielding a multiplicative dependence on (V, N, d, L, m). For the Attention-only model, Corollary 1 reduces this to d² ≳ max{V, V^{2/3}·L^{8/3}/N^{4/3}}, showing that increasing sample size N can compensate for smaller embedding dimension d. For the Attention-MLP model, Corollary 2 identifies three distinct regimes (MLP-noise-dominated, gradient-noise-dominated, mean-bias-dominated) depending on the relative sizes of m, d, V, and L. Empirically, the predicted multiplicative scaling is confirmed: for short sequences (L ≍ V^{0.25}), the model achieves the optimal capacity md ≍ V, while for longer sequences (L ≍ V), the required parameter size grows with slope ≈ 2 in d vs. V, and increasing N from V log V to V^{1.5} reduces the fitted slope from ~2.02 to ~1.42 (theory: 1.32).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper and does not benchmark against competing methods in the traditional sense. Instead, it compares its upper bound (Theorem 1) against a statistical lower bound (Theorem 2), which shows that any method accessing only the noisy attention-output queries {h_i, h_i h_i^⊤} fails when N ≲ V·min{1, L/d²}. The upper bound is slightly stronger than the lower bound (the gradient-noise term in Theorem 1 is tighter than the lower bound), leaving a gap that the authors attribute to the specific 3-step GD procedure rather than an inherent statistical limitation. Relative to prior work, the paper's key advance over Nichani et al. (2025) is moving from population-level analysis with orthogonal embeddings to finite-sample gradient descent with random (non-orthogonal) embeddings, revealing that the optimal capacity md ≍ V is achievable only for short sequences, while longer sequences force a trade-off between embedding dimension (capacity) and sample size (statistical efficiency). The results also contrast with the orthogonal-embedding assumption used in most prior transformer learning analyses (Tian et al. 2023, Chen et al. 2024, Ghosal et al. 2024), showing that non-orthogonality introduces token interference that fundamentally changes the sample complexity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the factual-recall analysis of Nichani et al. (2025), which established that non-orthogonal (random) embeddings enable near-parameter-count storage in transformers, by removing two key idealizations: infinite data (population dynamics) and orthogonal embeddings. The training procedure follows the 3-step gradient descent framework of Oymak et al. (2023) and the early-phase analysis lens of Bietti et al. (2023), but applies them to a setting with finite samples and random Gaussian embeddings where token interference creates intricate optimization dynamics. The key departure from prior work is the simultaneous treatment of both finite-sample effects and non-orthogonality, yielding explicit multiplicative scaling laws across five parameters (V, N, d, L, m) that were previously only available under orthogonal or population-level assumptions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper formalizes a fundamental trade-off: smaller embedding dimensions increase superposition and thus storage capacity, but simultaneously make the learning problem harder by requiring more samples or parameters. The statistical lower bound (Theorem 2) confirms this trade-off is intrinsic to any method using gradient information from the initialized transformer, not merely an artifact of the specific 3-step algorithm. Open questions include whether the trade-off can be mitigated by structured data distributions (e.g., shorter sequences during training, as in long-context extension), whether results extend to multi-layer architectures, and whether the polynomial activation assumptions in Assumption 3 are truly necessary or artifacts of the short training horizon. The empirical observation that full GD training with Adam and layer normalization preserves the multiplicative scaling suggests the findings may generalize beyond the simplified theoretical setting.
