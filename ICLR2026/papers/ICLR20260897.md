## Problem

Causal transformers scale memory and compute linearly with sequence length during inference, motivating constant-complexity RNN alternatives. However, existing linear RNNs (DeltaNet, Mamba2, xLSTM) approximate test-time learning with a single gradient step per token, and the previously proposed Mesa layer—which solves the in-context regression to optimality at every step—was only implementable sequentially, making it infeasible to train at scale. This paper asks: can optimal test-time training be made parallelizable, numerically stable, and competitive with transformers at the billion-parameter scale?

## Input / Output

The model consumes a sequence of token embeddings (byte-level BPE tokens from GPT-2) of length up to 2048 during training and up to 32k during evaluation, and produces standard next-token probability distributions. Unusually, the sequence-mixing layer at each position solves a linear system (H_t + Λ)⁻¹q_t via conjugate gradient, so the per-token compute is not fixed but depends on how many CG iterations are needed to meet a tolerance ε—making inference cost data-dependent rather than purely length-dependent. The recurrent state consists of two matrices (G_t ∈ R^{n_v×n_a} and H_t ∈ R^{n_a×n_a}), roughly doubling the state size relative to single-matrix RNNs but adding less than 1% to total model memory.

## Method

The Mesa layer defines a cumulative, forget-weighted squared-error loss over all past key-value pairs plus a quadratic regularizer, and at each time step computes the exact minimizer Φ_t = argmin L_t(Φ) rather than taking a single gradient step as in DeltaNet. The closed-form solution reduces to solving (H_t + Λ)⁻¹q_t, which is approximated via conjugate gradient; crucially, each CG matrix-vector product has the same algebraic form as a gated linear attention (GLA) update, enabling a chunkwise-parallel training algorithm with O(T) complexity that leverages existing hardware-efficient GLA kernels. At inference, the layer runs recurrently with O(1) per-token memory, and a dynamic stopping criterion on the CG residual allows the model to allocate more or fewer iterations per head, per token, and per sequence. The full MesaNet stacks N residual blocks of a SwiGLU MLP and the Mesa layer in a standard Llama2-style decoder-only architecture, trained self-supervised on SlimPajama up to 1B parameters, and is shown to match or exceed transformer perplexity while outperforming all other RNN baselines on global reasoning and in-context recall benchmarks.

## Results

On 1B-parameter models trained on 50B SlimPajama tokens, MesaNet achieves an average per-token PPL of 13.79, matching the Transformer baseline (13.79) and outperforming all recurrent baselines (best RNN: Gated DeltaNet at 13.87). The hybrid Hawk-Mesa model slightly exceeds the Transformer at 13.75 average PPL. On downstream benchmarks, MesaNet leads all RNNs on global reasoning (45.03% acc), in-context recall (41.79% acc), and few-shot word scrambling (10.10% acc), though it remains behind the Transformer on in-context recall (41.79 vs 52.27) and translation (8.17 vs 13.61 BLEU). Notably, MesaNet surpasses the Transformer on word scrambling tasks (10.10 vs 6.98).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MesaNet outperforms all recurrent baselines on average PPL: it beats Gated DeltaNet (13.79 vs 13.87), DeltaNet (13.79 vs 14.05), GLA and xLSTM (13.79 vs 14.03), Hawk (13.79 vs 14.43), and Mamba2 (13.79 vs 14.58). Against the Transformer baseline, MesaNet ties on average PPL (13.79) but wins on few-shot word scrambling (10.10 vs 6.98) while losing on in-context recall (41.79 vs 52.27) and translation (8.17 vs 13.61 BLEU). Among RNNs, MesaNet leads on global reasoning (45.03 vs Gated DeltaNet's 44.84), in-context recall (41.79 vs DeltaNet's 40.46), and scrambling (10.10 vs Gated DeltaNet's 8.90). The SWA-1024 control (13.97 avg PPL) remains a surprisingly strong baseline, outperforming most RNNs on PPL and matching MesaNet on some long-context benchmarks, highlighting that PPL alone may not fully capture long-range dependency modeling. The Hawk-Mesa hybrid (13.75 avg PPL) marginally beats both MesaNet and the Transformer, combining Hawk's strong short-sequence modeling with Mesa's long-sequence advantages.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the fast weight programming lineage (Schmidhuber, 1992) through the test-time training unification of modern linear RNNs (DeltaNet, Mamba2, xLSTM, GLA), which all perform a single gradient descent step on an instantaneous loss. The key departure is the Mesa layer, which instead solves the cumulative regularized least-squares problem to optimality at every time step using conjugate gradient, making it a second-order online learner rather than first-order. Technically, the chunkwise parallelization builds directly on GLA's hardware-efficient training (Yang et al., 2024b), exploiting the equivalence between CG matrix-vector products and gated linear attention to enable O(T) parallel training alongside O(1) recurrent inference. The original Mesa layer (von Oswald et al., 2024) was sequential and numerically unstable with forgetting; this work resolves both issues.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that RNNs (including MesaNet) systematically outperform transformers on early tokens but underperform on later tokens — despite matching or beating average perplexity — is a useful diagnostic that reframes how we should evaluate sequence models. The dynamic test-time compute allocation (reducing from 30 to ~9 CG steps with minimal loss via a stopping criterion) offers a principled compute-performance tradeoff that aligns with broader test-time scaling trends. However, the Mesa layer costs roughly k times more flops than linear attention baselines, and a SWA-1024 transformer remains a surprisingly strong long-context baseline, limiting the practical advantage. Open questions include whether warm-starting CG across time steps or hybrid parallel-recurrent inference can close the compute gap, and whether the token-position performance asymmetry persists at larger scales.
