## Problem

Diffusion LLMs (dLLMs) promise dramatic inference speedups through parallel decoding, but their conditional independence assumption causes tokens to be generated without capturing mutual dependencies, inevitably degrading quality. Existing evaluations on standard benchmarks (math, coding) fail to expose this degradation, and no systematic analysis or benchmark exists to quantify the fundamental speed-quality trade-off of parallel decoding.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263040/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes structured text prompts as input—lists of customer names for queue operations (Copy, Shuffle, Replace, Insert, Remove), sets of 4 words for sentence construction, or 4×4 grid configurations for Sudoku/Latin Square—and produces generated text evaluated by exact-match accuracy (Waiting Line, Puzzles) or grammar scores via LanguageTool (Text Writing). The number of items n (3–6 in main experiments, up to 24 in extended) serves as a controllable difficulty knob, and the key evaluation axis is how quality degrades as the number of tokens decoded in parallel per timestep increases from 1 (one-by-one) to n (fully parallel).

## Method

The paper provides a three-part contribution: (1) an information-theoretic analysis proving that T-step parallel decoding in dLLMs incurs a minimum KL divergence lower-bounded by the sum of conditional total correlations C(S_i | X, S_{<i}) across steps, with the bound monotonically decreasing as T increases; (2) case studies on analytically tractable synthetic list operations (Copy, Replace Index, Replace Random, Shuffle) showing that C(Y|X) ranges from 0 to ∞ depending on the task, and that achievable accuracy under Top-k and Threshold unmasking strategies varies dramatically (e.g., Shuffle accuracy → 0 for any k > 1 as n → ∞); (3) PARALLELBENCH, a 17-task benchmark across Waiting Line, Text Writing, and Puzzles categories that spans the full spectrum of parallel decoding difficulty, revealing that dLLMs like LLaDA 1.5 and Mercury suffer severe quality degradation on tasks with C(Y|X) > 0 (Shuffle, Replace Random, W2S-hard, Latin Square) while maintaining accuracy on C(Y|X) = 0 tasks, and that no existing unmasking strategy (Top-k, Threshold, Factor-based, Semi-AR, SlowFast, DUS, WINO, APD) can adaptively balance speed and quality across this spectrum.

## Results

On PARALLELBENCH, LLaDA 1.5 achieves near-perfect accuracy on Copy regardless of parallelism degree, but Replace Random degrades from ~100% (one-by-one) to near-zero under parallel decoding, and Shuffle shows the steepest collapse. Mercury (closed-source dLLM) maintains near-perfect accuracy on Reverse but fails on Shuffle as n increases (n=5–20), while autoregressive LLMs (Llama 3.1 8B, Qwen2.5 7B, Claude 3.5 Haiku) show the opposite pattern—higher accuracy on Shuffle than Reverse. The oracle (per-sample optimal threshold) achieves both the best accuracy and a significant speedup over the best fixed-threshold method, demonstrating substantial room for improvement in adaptive parallelism.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Mercury (Inception Labs, 2025) is the primary dLLM compared against autoregressive LLMs (Llama 3.1 8B, Llama 3.2 3B, Qwen2.5 3B/7B, Qwen3 4B, Claude 3.5 Haiku) on Waiting Line tasks with n=5–20: Mercury maintains near-perfect accuracy on Reverse but collapses on Shuffle, while all AR LLMs score higher on Shuffle than Reverse—the exact opposite trend, confirming that Mercury cannot adaptively reduce parallelism for high-dependency tasks. Among unmasking strategies on LLaDA 1.5, Confidence Threshold outperforms Top-k at conservative thresholds but degrades rapidly at aggressive ones; no single unmasking method dominates across all tasks (e.g., Random Top-k beats Confidence Top-k on Shuffle and Replace Random where C(Y|X)>0, while the reverse holds for Copy and Replace Index where C(Y|X)=0). Fine-tuning LLaDA 1.5 on Waiting Line raises one-by-one accuracy to ~100% for all tasks (including Replace Index, which improves from <50% to ~100%), but Replace Random and Shuffle still degrade under parallelism, confirming the theoretical limit. Additional decoding methods—SlowFast Sampling, DUS, WINO, and APD—all remain far below the oracle speed-quality curve, with WINO showing the best trade-off among them. Remasking samplers (RCR, ReMDM) and Chain-of-Thought prompting (which uses 8× more tokens) provide no meaningful improvement to the speed-quality trade-off.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the one-step generation lower bound of Huang et al. (2022) to T-step parallel decoding (Theorem 1), formalizing how conditional total correlation imposes fundamental limits on any factorized dLLM. It departs from prior dLLM evaluation work (Wu et al., 2025; Feng et al., 2025; Gong et al., 2025), which assessed parallel decoding only on standard benchmarks like GSM8K and HumanEval, by arguing these benchmarks are insufficient to expose parallel decoding's vulnerability to token dependencies. The paper also builds on the masked diffusion framework (Sahoo et al., 2024; Nie et al., 2025) and the unmasking strategy taxonomy (top-k vs. threshold) established by recent dLLM systems, but reframes the evaluation question around the speed-quality trade-off rather than absolute generation quality.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PARALLELBENCH is the first benchmark purpose-built to stress-test parallel decoding in dLLMs, and its finding that even trivially simple tasks (list shuffling, word-to-sentence construction) suffer near-total accuracy collapse under parallelism is a sobering signal for the dLLM community. The oracle gap analysis demonstrates that current adaptive unmasking methods (threshold, factor-based) leave substantial performance on the table, pointing to a clear research direction for per-sample parallelism adaptation. Key open questions include whether the C(Y|X)-driven degradation can be mitigated by architectural changes (e.g., uniform transition matrices, remasking) or whether it is an inescapable consequence of the factorization assumption, and whether the benchmark's short-output focus generalizes to the long-sequence settings where dLLMs are most practically relevant.
