## Problem

Classical influence functions were designed for pointwise (M-estimator) losses and cannot be directly applied to contrastive learning, where each sample's information is entangled across every term in the batch loss as both a positive and a negative sample. This makes it impossible to isolate a single sample's contribution using the standard up-weighting framework, and existing data valuation methods (Shapley, TracIN, TRAK) either require expensive retraining or fail to capture the dual positive/negative role of samples in contrastive objectives.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a trained CLIP-style encoder with parameters θ, a training set of text-image pairs organized into batches, and a validation/test set for task-related scoring. The output is a dual influence vector (positive-IF, negative-IF) per sample, a closed-form parameter edit θ̃ = θ − positive-IF − negative-IF that approximates retraining without that sample, and derived scores (task-related IS, relative-IS) used to rank samples by harmfulness, value, or relevance to a specific misprediction. Unlike scalar influence scores in pointwise settings, ECIF produces a two-component influence that must be jointly considered to assess a sample's net effect.

## Method

ECIF decomposes a sample's influence into two analytically separable components. For the positive role, the sample's pairing loss L_T2I + L_I2T is up-weighted by ε and the influence is derived via first-order Taylor expansion of the optimality condition, yielding positive-IF = −H⁻¹∇Pos. For the negative role, the key insight is that removing a sample from the softmax denominators of all other pairs is equivalent to driving its similarity to −∞; this is parameterized by a scalar ζ that scales the sample's exponential term, and a Taylor expansion at ζ=1 isolates the negative influence as Neg = Σ_k (Σ_j e^{S_kj})/e^{S_kn} (plus the symmetric I2T term). Both components are combined with the regularized inverse Hessian H⁻¹ = (Σ∇²L_Batch + δI)⁻¹ to produce a closed-form parameter update. The paper further derives a constrained optimization (Proposition 5.3) that reduces the misprediction trace-back problem to a simple argmax over relative-IS scores, and provides an upper bound on the approximation error between ECIF-based editing and actual retraining under convexity assumptions.

## Results

After removing 10% of the most harmful samples identified by ECIF, the edited model achieves 23.02% accuracy on FGVC-Aircraft, 84.90% on Food101, 68.30% on Flowers102, and 73.00% on CIFAR-100, closely matching full retraining (23.50%, 84.83%, 68.00%, 72.83%) while reducing runtime by over 2× (e.g., 456 s vs. 1174 s on FGVC-Aircraft). ECIF's accuracy gap versus retraining is at most 0.5% across all datasets. In the brittleness test on Food101, removing the top-30% most valuable data identified by ECIF drops accuracy from 84.7% to 84.1%, whereas random removal at the same ratio actually increases accuracy, confirming ECIF's ability to distinguish genuinely useful from noisy samples.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ECIF decisively outperforms all three baselines across every dataset. On FGVC-Aircraft, ECIF reaches 23.02% accuracy after harmful-sample removal, exceeding IF-EKFAC (19.84%), TRAK (18.27%), and TracIN (19.48%) by 3.2–4.8 percentage points. The gap is even more pronounced on CIFAR-100, where ECIF achieves 73.00% while the baselines drop to 58.67–61.67%, a margin of 11–14 points. Notably, all three baselines perform *worse* than the original fine-tuned model without any data removal (e.g., 19.84% vs. 22.18% on FGVC-Aircraft), indicating they fail to identify truly harmful samples and instead remove useful data. The paper attributes this failure to the baselines being designed for pointwise loss and thus unable to capture the dual positive/negative role of each sample in contrastive learning. ECIF's results are within 0.5% of the retraining ground truth, confirming that its closed-form influence approximation faithfully captures the effect of data removal without requiring costly retraining.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classical influence function framework of Koh & Liang (2017), originally designed for pointwise M-estimator losses, to the fundamentally different structure of contrastive (noise-contrastive estimation) loss. The key conceptual departure is recognizing that in contrastive learning, each data pair plays a dual role—as a positive sample in its own pairing loss and as a negative sample in all other pairing losses within the batch—requiring a dual-perspective (positive-IF + negative-IF) formulation rather than the single up-weighting term used in prior work. The paper also leverages LOGRA-style low-rank gradient projection to address the computational intractability of Hessian inversion in high-dimensional contrastive models, and positions ECIF against pointwise-loss baselines (IF-EKFAC, TRAK, TracIN) that it shows are inapplicable to contrastive objectives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As CLIP-style contrastive models become the backbone of multimodal AI, the inability to diagnose which training pairs cause misalignment or hallucination is a critical transparency gap; ECIF offers a retraining-free mechanism to address this. However, the validation is limited to relatively small datasets (FGVC-Aircraft, Food101, Flowers102, CIFAR-100) and a single model architecture, leaving open whether the linear-superposition assumption and first-order Taylor approximation remain accurate at the scale of billion-parameter CLIP models trained on hundreds of millions of pairs. The approximation error bound (Theorem E.7) is derived under convexity assumptions that do not hold for real contrastive losses, and the practical scalability of the Hessian computation even with LOGRA at true CLIP scale remains untested.
