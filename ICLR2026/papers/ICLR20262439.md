## Problem

Embedding model benchmarks like MTEB report scores against theoretical maxima (e.g., MAP=1.0) without any human performance reference, making it impossible to interpret whether a model's 0.85 MAP represents genuine semantic understanding or mere fitting to noisy annotation labels. This interpretability gap risks 'blind optimization,' where R&D resources are spent replicating annotation artifacts rather than achieving meaningful progress, and where apparent 'superhuman' model performance on ambiguous tasks masks fundamental dataset quality issues.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes downsampled subsets (20–50 instances) from 16 MTEB datasets spanning reranking, classification, clustering, and STS across English, Arabic, Russian, Norwegian, and Danish. Human annotators (NLP practitioners with native or near-native proficiency) label these via task-specific Argilla interfaces—binary relevance for reranking, categorical labels for classification, free cluster ID assignment for clustering, and 0–5 similarity scores for STS—producing performance scores computed with the exact same metrics used for model evaluation (MAP, Accuracy, V-Measure, Spearman) alongside inter-annotator agreement metrics (Fleiss' κ, ARI, Spearman ρ). Nine LLMs are also run on identical instances to assess their viability as scalable annotation proxies.

## Method

HUME establishes a reproducible human evaluation protocol aligned directly with MTEB's model evaluation pipeline: 2–3 culturally diverse annotators independently label downsampled task subsets without access to ground truth or model predictions, and human scores are computed using the same metric implementations as MTEB. Statistical significance is assessed via 95% confidence intervals using metric-appropriate methods (Wilson Score Intervals for accuracy, Fisher z-transformations for correlations, empirical annotator ranges for clustering/reranking), identifying 14 of 26 tasks where models fall outside human CIs. The key diagnostic insight is that human performance variation correlates with task quality rather than human limitation: tasks with high inter-annotator agreement (reranking ρ=0.64–0.85, WikiCities ARI=0.91) provide reliable benchmarks, while low-agreement tasks (Emotion κ=0.39, ArXiv ARI=−0.001) reveal ambiguous specifications where model 'superhuman' scores likely reflect consistent reproduction of arbitrary label patterns. The framework further benchmarks nine LLMs (GPT-5, GPT-4.1, Gemini, Mistral, Qwen3 variants) as annotators, finding they achieve 76.1% average versus 81.2% for humans, with a notable 10-point gap on reranking where human agreement is highest.

## Results

Across 16 MTEB datasets (26 task-language pairs), human annotators achieve an average score of 77.6%, ranking 4th among 13 embedding models, trailing only jasper_en_vision_language_v1 (80.1%), SFR-Embedding-Mistral (78.3%), and e5-mistral-7b-instruct (78.2%). Humans outperform all models on non-English sentiment classification (Arabic: 95.0 vs. 77.5 best; Russian: 92.5 vs. 81.2) and Arabic STS (67.5 vs. 40.9), but fall well short on reranking (87.2 vs. 96.4 best) and ArXiv clustering (49.2 vs. 84.6 best). Nine LLMs evaluated as annotators peak at 76.1% (GPT-4.1-mini) versus 81.2% for humans, with the largest gap on reranking (78.0% best LLM vs. 88.3% human). Models perform outside human 95% confidence intervals in 14 of 26 tasks, often on low-agreement datasets where 'superhuman' scores likely reflect artifact fitting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the 13 embedding models, humans (77.6%) rank 4th overall, beaten by jasper_en_vision_language_v1 (80.1%), SFR-Embedding-Mistral (78.3%), and e5-mistral-7b-instruct (78.2%), but outperforming 10 other models. Humans decisively win on culturally-grounded tasks: Arabic sentiment (95.0 vs. 77.5 best model, +17.5 pts), Russian sentiment (92.5 vs. 81.2, +11.3 pts), Norwegian sentiment (85.0 vs. 75.0, +10.0 pts), and Arabic STS (67.5 vs. 40.9, +26.6 pts). Models dominate on reranking (best 96.4 vs. 87.2 human) and clustering (best 85.1 vs. 67.4 human), with the largest human-model gap on ArXiv clustering (49.2 vs. 84.6) where inter-annotator agreement is near zero (ARI = −0.001). Among the 9 LLMs tested as annotators, GPT-4.1-mini leads at 76.1% average, still 5.1 points below humans (81.2%); the gap is most severe on reranking (best LLM 78.0% vs. human 88.3%, a 10.3-point deficit) while classification is near-parity (GPT-5: 78.9% vs. human 79.1%). The paper concludes that LLMs should augment rather than replace human annotation, particularly for high-agreement tasks where the semantic distinctions are most well-defined.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on MTEB and MMTEB as the evaluation substrate, introducing human performance baselines that these frameworks explicitly lack. It departs from prior human evaluation work in NLP (TREC relevance judgments, GLUE/SuperGLUE human baselines, MERA) by converting human annotations into model-comparable scores under identical metrics (MAP, V-Measure, Spearman) rather than treating them as gold standards. The LLM-as-annotator component extends the growing literature on using LLMs for data labeling, but applies it specifically to discriminative embedding tasks (reranking, STS) where prior LLM-annotation work has focused primarily on generative or classification tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reframes how the community should interpret embedding model scores: 85% on emotion classification (185% of human performance, κ=0.39) is fundamentally different from 85% on reranking (97% of human performance, ρ=0.75), yet both appear identical on a leaderboard. It identifies specific MTEB datasets (emotion classification, ArXiv clustering, STS22-Russian) that should be deprecated due to quality issues, and demonstrates that LLMs cannot yet replace human annotators for benchmark development, particularly on reranking. Open questions include how to design tasks that balance meaningful challenge with unambiguous specification, how to incorporate natural human disagreement into evaluation rather than collapsing it to single gold labels, and whether the observed human advantages in low-resource languages reflect genuine model limitations or dataset-specific artifacts.
