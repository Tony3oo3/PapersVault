## Problem

Full calibration guarantees that best-responding to a forecaster is the optimal decision policy, but full calibration is intractable to achieve in high-dimensional outcome spaces. Weaker, more tractable calibration guarantees (e.g., decision calibration, self-orthogonality) are available in practice but lack clear decision-theoretic semantics: it is unknown how a conservative decision maker should map such partially calibrated forecasts to actions. This paper asks: given a forecaster promised to satisfy a specific H-calibration guarantee, what is the minimax-optimal decision rule that maximizes worst-case expected utility over all distributions consistent with that guarantee?

## Input / Output

The method consumes a forecast vector v = f(x) ∈ [0,1]^d, a finite-dimensional calibration test class H = span{h_1,...,h_k}, a utility function u(a,v) that is linear in v (risk-neutral expected utility over d outcomes), and a finite action set A. It produces two objects: a worst-case belief q*(v) ∈ [0,1]^d (the adversarially tilted conditional expectation) and a robust action a_robust(v) ∈ A (the best response to q*(v)). The computation is pointwise in v: after solving a finite-dimensional concave dual for multipliers λ*, each forecast requires only a small convex minimization over p ∈ [0,1]^d followed by a best-response lookup, without constructing the full policy mapping.

## Method

The paper formulates robust decision making as a minimax problem max_a min_q E[u(a(f(X)), q(f(X)))] over the ambiguity set Q of all conditional expectations consistent with H-calibration, then applies Sion's minimax theorem and Lagrangian duality to obtain a closed-form saddle-point characterization (Theorem 3.1): the worst-case belief q*(v) is the pointwise minimizer of val(p) + p·s*(v) where val(p) = max_a u(a,p) and s*(v) = Σ h_i(v)λ_i*, and the robust action is the best response to q*(v). The key theoretical result is a sharp transition (Theorems 4.1–4.2): once H contains the |A| decision-calibration indicators {1_{R_a}}, the adversarial tilt collapses (q*(v) = v a.e.) and the minimax-optimal robust rule coincides exactly with the plug-in best response, upgrading prior swap-regret guarantees to full minimax optimality. For weaker guarantees that fall short of decision calibration—specifically self-orthogonality induced by squared-loss training with a linear head (Proposition 4.4) and bin-wise calibration from post-hoc recalibration (Proposition 4.5)—the robust rule remains efficiently computable via the same dual structure, yielding a scalar or piecewise-constant correction to the forecast. Empirically, on Bike Sharing and California Housing datasets with MLP regressors, the robust policy outperforms plug-in best response under calibration-preserving distribution shift while incurring only mild cost under i.i.d. conditions.

## Results

On both UCI Bike Sharing and California Housing, the robust policy (derived from self-orthogonality under squared loss) achieves higher mean utility than the plug-in best response under adversarial evaluation: 0.412 vs. 0.393 (Bike Sharing) and 0.166 vs. 0.155 (California Housing) under the plug-in-tuned adversary, and 0.410 vs. 0.402 and 0.164 vs. 0.160 under the robust-tuned adversary. Under nominal i.i.d. evaluation, the plug-in rule slightly outperforms the robust policy (0.474 vs. 0.463 and 0.216 vs. 0.207), confirming that the cost of robustness is mild when no distribution shift is present.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central empirical comparison is between the plug-in best response (a_BR) and the minimax-optimal robust policy (a_robust) derived from the self-orthogonality calibration guarantee of squared-loss regression. Under the adversary tailored to harm the plug-in rule, the robust policy wins by a clear margin: +0.019 on Bike Sharing (0.412 vs. 0.393) and +0.011 on California Housing (0.166 vs. 0.155). Under the adversary tailored to the robust policy, the robust policy also dominates: +0.008 on Bike Sharing (0.410 vs. 0.402) and +0.004 on California Housing (0.164 vs. 0.160), consistent with the saddle-point guarantee. Under nominal i.i.d. conditions, the plug-in rule edges out the robust policy by 0.011 (Bike Sharing) and 0.009 (California Housing), indicating that the robustness overhead is small when the calibration promise holds exactly. No external baseline methods are compared; the evaluation validates the theoretical prediction that the robust policy dominates under adversarial shift while incurring only a mild cost under ideal conditions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the H-calibration / weighted multicalibration framework of Gopalan et al. (2022) and the decision calibration notion of Zhao et al. (2021) and Noarov et al. (2023), but departs from the regret-based guarantees (swap regret, external regret) that prior work provides for downstream decision makers. Instead, it imports a minimax/robust-optimization lens (rooted in Gilboa & Schmeidler, Hansen & Sargent, Duchi & Namkoong) to ask a qualitatively different question: what is the worst-case-optimal policy over all distributions consistent with a given calibration guarantee? It also extends Rothblum & Yona (2023), who studied bounded full-calibration error in the 1D binary case, to high-dimensional outcomes with structurally weaker (but tractable) calibration constraints.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The sharp-transition result—that the minimax-optimal policy collapses to plug-in best response the moment H contains the |A| decision-calibration indicators, and that no further enrichment of H changes the answer—gives practitioners a crisp, task-specific target for forecaster design and a clear certificate of trustworthiness that is far more tractable than full calibration. Key limitations include the assumption of linear (risk-neutral) utilities and finite action sets; extending to risk-averse or nonlinear utilities (e.g., variance-dependent) is flagged as open, as is the practical question of how to efficiently achieve decision calibration in high-dimensional multiclass settings. The empirical validation is limited to two small regression datasets with three actions each, so the practical gap between robust and plug-in policies under realistic distribution shift remains underexplored.
