## Problem

Existing benchmarks for image generation models are fragmented across isolated tasks (text-to-image, editing, personalization), cover narrow domains, and provide opaque scalar scores without explaining *why* a model fails. As unified models now handle generation and editing in a single framework, there is no systematic, explainable evaluation protocol that spans the full spectrum of real-world use cases and diagnoses specific failure modes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261428/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each of the 3.6K condition sets pairs a natural-language instruction with an optional source image and/or one or more reference images, spanning six task types (TIG, SRIG, MRIG, TIE, SRIE, MRIE) and six topical domains (Artworks, Photorealistic, Information Graphics, Textual Graphics, Computer Graphics, Screenshots) with 100 samples per task-topic cell. Evaluation outputs are four-criterion scores (Prompt Relevance, Aesthetic Quality, Content Coherence, Artifacts) on a 5-point Likert scale rescaled to [0,1], plus structured error annotations: object-level tags identifying missing or distorted entities, and segment-level tags marking specific image regions (via Set-of-Mark partitions) responsible for score deductions. Each image is independently rated by three human annotators, yielding 20K fine-grained annotations in total.

## Method

ImagenWorld introduces an explainable human evaluation schema that goes beyond scalar scoring: for object-level issues, a VLM (Gemini-2.5-Flash) pre-generates a list of expected objects from the instruction and reference images, and annotators flag which are missing, incorrectly rendered, or distorted; for segment-level issues, each output image is partitioned using Set-of-Mark (SoM) and annotators select regions containing visual flaws or inconsistencies. Automated evaluation follows the VIEScore paradigm, using Gemini-2.5-Flash to produce VLM-based scores aligned with the same four criteria, complemented by CLIPScore and LPIPS as auxiliary metrics. The benchmark evaluates 14 models spanning diffusion, autoregressive, and AR+diffusion hybrid architectures, including four unified models (GPT-Image-1, Gemini 2.0 Flash, BAGEL, OmniGen2) and ten task-specific baselines. Inter-rater reliability is quantified via Krippendorff's alpha, and VLM-human agreement is measured through Spearman rank correlation and Kendall's accuracy, revealing that VLM judges achieve Kendall accuracies up to 0.79 for relative ranking but under-penalize fine-grained visual artifacts that humans consistently flag.

## Results

Across 14 models evaluated on ImagenWorld's 3.6K condition sets, GPT-Image-1 achieves the highest overall human scores, reaching 0.91 on Text-guided Image Generation (TIG) and 0.82 on Text-guided Image Editing (TIE), outperforming Gemini 2.0 Flash by approximately 0.1–0.2 points on average. Editing tasks (TIE, SRIE, MRIE) consistently score ~0.1 lower than their generation counterparts (TIG, SRIG, MRIG), with the gap widening for open-source unified models. Text-heavy domains (Screenshots, Information Graphics) are the hardest, averaging ~0.55, while Artworks and Photorealistic Images reach ~0.78. VLM-based evaluation (Gemini-2.5-Flash via VIEScore) achieves Kendall's accuracy of 0.79 on Prompt Relevance, closely matching human–human agreement (0.77), but under-penalizes visual artifacts (positive bias of +0.06).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-Image-1 (closed-source) leads all six tasks, outperforming Gemini 2.0 Flash by 0.1–0.2 points on average, with the gap most pronounced in editing (e.g., SRIE: 0.80 vs. 0.59; MRIE: 0.79 vs. 0.62). Among open-source unified models, BAGEL and OmniGen2 trail closed-source systems by 0.15–0.35 points on editing tasks, while Flux.1-Krea-dev and Qwen-Image are competitive on TIG (0.82 and 0.87 respectively, vs. GPT-Image-1's 0.91). Qwen-Image is a notable exception in text-heavy domains, outperforming even closed-source models on Textual Graphics (0.94) and Artworks (0.97) for TIG, attributed to its synthetic text-rich data curation pipeline. Dedicated editing models (IC-Edit, Step1X-Edit, InstructPix2Pix) score 0.45–0.50 on TIE, well below unified models, but exhibit far lower rates of generating entirely new images (0.6–3.4%) compared to AR-diffusion hybrids like OmniGen2 (17%). VLM-based scoring (Gemini-2.5-Flash) achieves Spearman ρ of 0.70 and Kendall accuracy of 0.79 on Prompt Relevance—matching or exceeding human–human agreement—yet shows a positive artifact bias (+0.06), indicating it under-penalizes visual defects that humans consistently flag.

## Relation to Prior Work

ImagenWorld extends the line of image generation/editing benchmarks (ImagenHub, MMIG-Bench, ICE-Bench, Gecko) by being the first to simultaneously cover all six task types—text-guided and reference-guided generation and editing, with single and multiple references—across six topical domains. Methodologically, it builds on VIEScore's VLM-as-judge paradigm for automated scoring and on Set-of-Mark prompting for segment-level error localization, but departs from prior work by introducing a structured explainable evaluation schema that tags object-level and segment-level failures with textual descriptions and masks, going beyond scalar scores. The paper explicitly positions against the fragmentation of existing benchmarks (Table 1), where no prior work checks all six properties (generation+editing, single/multi-reference, human rating, topic variety, explainable trace) simultaneously.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark provides the most comprehensive diagnostic picture to date of where 14 state-of-the-art image models succeed and fail, revealing that editing remains a fundamental bottleneck (models either regenerate entirely new images or return inputs unchanged) and that text-heavy/symbolic domains (screenshots, information graphics) are persistent weak points across all architectures. The finding that Qwen-Image's targeted synthetic data curation allows it to outperform closed-source systems on textual graphics suggests data design is as critical as architectural scale, offering a practical path forward. Open questions include whether the collected human scores and object-level tags can be leveraged for preference optimization or self-corrective generation, and whether VLM-based evaluators can be trained to match human-level fine-grained error attribution rather than just relative ranking.
