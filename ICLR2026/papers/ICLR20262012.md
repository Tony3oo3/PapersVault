## Problem

Existing video generation models are trained for a single task (typically T2V) using large amounts of costly high-quality annotations, while other tasks like I2V, video inpainting, and colorization require separate fine-tuned models. This paper asks whether a single model can be trained from scratch to handle 10+ visual generation and manipulation tasks simultaneously, and whether the multi-task data actually improves core video generation quality rather than merely adding capabilities.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262012/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

All 3D conditions (pixel data, depth maps, binary masks) are concatenated channel-wise and passed through a shared lightweight adapter that downsamples them to match the video VAE latent resolution (8×8 spatial, 4× temporal compression), then additively injected into the latent features. Task identity is disambiguated by appending a task name (e.g., 'text-to-video', 'video super-resolution') to the text prompt, since different tasks can share the same mask structure. The model outputs 97-frame videos at resolutions up to 720×1280, with inference taking ~4 minutes for the 8B model at full resolution using 30 diffusion steps.

## Method

MfM is a DiT with 3D full attention trained via Rectified Flow, where the key architectural contribution is a lightweight convolutional adapter that unifies all 3D conditions (pixel, depth, mask) into a single latent-space representation, eliminating the need for separate processing pipelines per condition type. The transformer uses 3D RoPE (allocating 2/8, 3/8, 3/8 of hidden channels to temporal and two spatial dimensions), QK-Norm for training stability, and AdaLN to inject timestep and motion score. Training follows a progressive multi-resolution schedule (128px → 360px → 720px → multi-resolution via NaViT) with a joint image-video strategy that starts from pure text-image pairs and gradually introduces video data. At each training step, a random qualified task is assigned to the sampled video (with 3× higher probability for T2V/I2V/T2I), and the corresponding conditional input is constructed on the fly, enabling the model to learn from 160M images and 120M videos across 16 tasks without requiring task-specific architectures.

## Results

On VBench-T2V, MfM (8B) achieves the best average rank of 2.86 among all compared models, outperforming Sora (3.27), Wan2.1 (3.77), and HunyuanVideo (5.73), while using only ~10% of the training data of these larger models. On VBench-I2V, MfM also achieves the best average rank of 3.33, surpassing Magi-1 (3.50) and Hunyuan-I2V (3.67). On the MovieGen Benchmark, MfM (8B) attains the best average rank of 2.2 with the highest dynamic degree score (0.7188), closely matching or surpassing much larger models like Wanx (14B) and Hunyuan (13B). Across four video manipulation tasks (inpainting, outpainting, FLF2V, colorization), MfM achieves FID improvements of 16.4%–72.9% over specialized baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MfM (8B) achieves the best average rank on both VBench-T2V (2.86) and VBench-I2V (3.33), outperforming all open-source and commercial baselines including Sora, Wan2.1 (14B), HunyuanVideo (13B), Gen-3, and CogVideoX1.5. Specifically, MfM leads in appearance style (0.251) and overall consistency (0.277) on T2V, and in imaging quality (0.718) on I2V, while remaining competitive across all other dimensions. On the MovieGen Benchmark, MfM (8B) achieves the best dynamic degree (0.7188) and best average rank (2.2), surpassing Wanx (14B, rank 3.4) and Hunyuan (13B, rank 2.4) despite using far fewer training samples (120M videos vs. 1.5B+). For video manipulation tasks, MfM consistently outperforms specialized models: on video inpainting it achieves FID 51.03 vs. VACE (67.51) and ProPainter (119.93); on outpainting FID 44.15 vs. VACE (54.34) and M3DDM (174.61); on FLF2V FID 31.98 vs. Wanx (38.24) and Hunyuan (118.18); and on colorization FID 76.54 vs. Colormnet (77.08) and TCVC (82.42). In the I2V user study, MfM achieves a 64.29% Top-1 preference rate, far exceeding Wanx-I2V (20.00%) and all other competitors. The key advantage is that MfM accomplishes all of this with a single unified model trained from scratch on ~10% of the data used by competing T2V foundation models, rather than requiring separate specialized models per task.

## Relation to Prior Work

MfM builds directly on the DiT + Rectified Flow architecture popularized by SD3/Flux and adopted by recent video models (CogVideoX, HunyuanVideo, Wan2.1), incorporating GoKu's transformer block design and extending RoPE to 3D for joint spatial-temporal position encoding. Its key departure from prior work is the unified multi-task training paradigm: whereas SORA, Wan2.1, and HunyuanVideo train a T2V model first and then fine-tune separate models for I2V, V2V, or editing tasks, and VACE (Jiang et al., 2025) builds an all-in-one model on top of a pre-trained T2V backbone, MfM trains a single model from scratch using a lightweight adapter that unifies heterogeneous 3D conditions (pixel, depth, mask) across 16 tasks. The joint image-video progressive training strategy and the use of depth maps as an explicit 3D-space condition are additional design choices not present in the compared baselines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: an 8B open-source model competitive with 13–30B commercial and open-source systems while using roughly 10% of the video training data, and a single checkpoint covering 10+ generation and manipulation tasks without per-task fine-tuning. The ablation evidence that multi-task training *improves* primary T2V/I2V quality (rather than merely enabling auxiliary capabilities) is a useful finding for the field, suggesting that diverse supervisory signals act as regularizers for video generation. Open questions include whether the framework scales to long-duration (beyond 97-frame) video generation via streaming or chunk-wise pipelines, whether explicit multimodal fusion (e.g., via vision-language encoders) can replace the current implicit self-attention-based fusion of text and 3D conditions, and whether the depth-map conditioning generalizes to other geometric cues (normal maps, optical flow).
