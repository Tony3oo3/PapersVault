## Problem

Existing Poisoned Sample Detection (PSD) methods degrade significantly under weak backdoor attacks (low poisoning ratios or weak trigger patterns), yet the underlying cause has not been systematically characterized. This paper reveals a strong positive correlation (Pearson r = 0.73) between the strength of the backdoor effect, measured by Top-k Trigger Activation Change (TAC), and detection performance, showing that weak backdoor effects reduce feature-space separability between clean and poisoned samples and thereby undermine detection.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264183/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a potentially poisoned RGB image training dataset (CIFAR-10 at 32×32, Tiny ImageNet at 64×64, GTSRB at 30×30) along with a small clean reference set of 250 samples per class drawn from the test set. It outputs per-sample detection labels identifying poisoned entries, yielding a cleansed training dataset. The framework is model-agnostic (validated on ResNet-18, VGG19-BN, DenseNet-161) and attack-independent, requiring no knowledge of the trigger, poisoning ratio, or target label.

## Method

The core contribution is a three-stage SAM-enhanced PSD pipeline that amplifies the backdoor effect during training to improve downstream detection. Stage 1 trains a model on the suspicious dataset using Sharpness-Aware Minimization, whose bi-level optimization (min-max over a perturbation ball of radius ρ) selectively increases the pre-activations of top-TAC backdoor neurons while suppressing unrelated neurons, as formalized in a theoretical decomposition of the SAM update for a two-layer ReLU network. Stage 2 extracts backdoor-related features via PCA projection followed by Mahalanobis scaling with a dynamically filtered reference set, serving as a surrogate for the true Top-k TAC neuron activations that the defender cannot access. Stage 3 feeds these enhanced features into any off-the-shelf PSD detector (Spectre, SCAn, SS, AC, Beatrix). The result is a plug-and-play module that achieves an average TPR gain of +34.3% over conventional PSD across 13 attack types, with particularly large improvements under weak-attack settings (0.5%–1% poisoning).

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with ResNet-18 (5% poisoning ratio), SAM-enhanced PSD achieves an average TPR gain of +34.3% across 13 attacks and 5 detection methods. The largest improvements are for Beatrix (+87.4% TPR, from ~5% to ~92%) and Spectre (+30.6% TPR, from ~35% to ~66%), while FPR remains low or decreases (e.g., Spectre FPR drops from 5.9% to 4.2%). On GTSRB, average TPR gains reach +24.3% for AC and +52.7% for Beatrix. Under weak attacks (0.5% poisoning), gains remain substantial: SS achieves +47.0% and AC achieves +85.4% average TPR improvement.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new detector but enhances five existing PSD baselines (Spectre, SCAn, SS, AC, Beatrix) by replacing vanilla SGD training with SAM. SAM-enhanced PSD wins on TPR for all five methods across nearly all 13 attacks on CIFAR-10, with the most dramatic gains for Beatrix (average +87.4% TPR, e.g., Blended: 5.0% → 99.8%) and Spectre (average +30.6% TPR, e.g., Adap-Blend: 24.1% → 65.9%). SCAn already achieves near-perfect TPR (>92%) under most attacks, so the marginal gain is small (+3.2% average), though it helps under WaNet (66.3% → 90.1%). SS and AC show strong improvements (+20.5% and +29.8% average TPR respectively), particularly on weak-trigger attacks like Blended and Adap-Blend where base methods fail badly (e.g., AC on Blended: 2.3% → 98.8%). FPR generally stays low or decreases, confirming the gains are not from over-flagging. On GTSRB, the pattern holds: Beatrix gains +52.7% TPR and AC gains +24.3%, while SCAn's improvement is modest (+4.7%) due to its already-high baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a plug-and-play enhancement layer for existing pre-training poisoned sample detection (PSD) methods rather than a new detector. It builds on SAM (Foret et al., 2021) as an optimization technique and adapts the PCA-based feature extraction pipeline from Spectre (Hayase et al., 2021) to approximate Top-k TAC neuron features. Conceptually, it inverts the use of SAM seen in FT-SAM (Zhu et al., 2023), which suppresses backdoor effects during post-training repair on clean data, by instead amplifying backdoor effects during pre-training on the poisoned dataset to aid detection. The TAC metric from Zheng et al. (2022) provides the quantitative bridge between backdoor effect strength and detection performance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The established correlation between backdoor effect strength (Top-k TAC) and PSD performance offers a useful diagnostic lens for understanding why weak attacks evade detection, and the SAM-based amplification provides a simple, model-agnostic remedy that requires no knowledge of the trigger or poisoning ratio. Limitations include degraded performance at extremely low poisoning ratios (0.1%, where ASR itself drops to ~32%) and reduced gains on very deep architectures like VGG19-BN where backdoor signals disperse across high-dimensional feature spaces. Open questions include whether the approach transfers to non-CNN backbones (transformers, diffusion models), whether it generalizes beyond RGB image classification, and whether adaptive attackers who can influence the training process (beyond just the data) can circumvent the defense.
