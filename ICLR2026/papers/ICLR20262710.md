## Problem

The training dynamics of deep ReLU networks from small initialization remain poorly understood, particularly the structure of escape directions from the saddle at the origin. Prior theoretical analyses of saddle-to-saddle dynamics in deep nonlinear networks relied on equivalence to linear networks (via differentiable nonlinearities or sign-preserving ReLUs), uniformly predicting that all layers are rank-one in the first escape. This paper asks whether deep ReLU networks instead exhibit a more complex, depth-dependent rank structure in their escape directions.

## Input / Output

The analysis takes a training data matrix X ∈ R^{d_in × N}, the loss gradient at the origin G = ∇C(0) ∈ R^{n_L × N}, and a fully-connected bias-free ReLU network of depth L with widths n_0, …, n_L initialized with small weights (σ_0 → 0). The key output is a per-layer bound: for all layers ℓ > c², the ratio of the sum of squared singular values beyond the first to the total is O(ℓ^{-1/2}), and the ReLU nonlinearity effect ‖Z_ℓ^σ − Z_ℓ‖_F²/‖Z_ℓ‖_F² is similarly bounded. The constant c = (‖X‖_F ‖G‖_F / s*)·√(2 log(‖X‖_F ‖G‖_F / s*)) depends on the data and the optimal escape speed s*, which is proven non-decreasing in depth, ensuring the bound applies to all but finitely many initial layers.

## Method

The paper analyzes the localized loss L_0(θ) = Tr[G^T Y_θ] near the origin, which is L-homogeneous, and decomposes gradient flow into independent dynamics of the parameter norm and of the normalized direction on the sphere. Escape directions are defined as critical points of L_0 on the sphere ‖θ‖² = L, and the optimal escape direction minimizes L_0 subject to this constraint. The main proof (Theorem 3.1) combines two intermediate results: a 'weak control' (Proposition 3.4) showing that at least (1−p)L layers have approximately rank-one activations when the escape speed is large, and a 'strong control' (Proposition 3.3) showing that if one layer's activation is approximately rank-one with non-negative entries, then all subsequent layers must also be approximately rank-one. The combination guarantees that for any layer ℓ > c², the ℓ-th layer is approximately rank-one with a bound that strengthens as ℓ^{-1/2}. A counterexample on a unit-circle dataset (N=8, alternating gradients) demonstrates that the optimal escape direction can be exactly rank-two in the first layer followed by rank-one layers, confirming that 'approximately rank-one' is the correct notion rather than exact rank-one.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 3.1) shows that at the optimal escape direction of a depth-L ReLU network, for all layers ℓ > c², the weight matrix W_ℓ and activation Z_ℓ^σ are ℓ^(-1/4)-approximately rank-1: the ratio of the sum of squared singular values (i ≥ 2) to the total is bounded by O(ℓ^(-1/2)), meaning the first singular value dominates by at least a factor of ℓ^(1/4). Proposition 3.2 proves the optimal escape speed s*(L) is non-decreasing in depth, ensuring the low-rank condition holds for all but finitely many initial layers. A counterexample (Example 1) on a unit-circle dataset with N=8 shows the optimal escape direction can be exactly rank-two in the first layer (speed s₂ = 1/2) while the best rank-one direction achieves only s₁ = √2 − 1 ≈ 0.414. Empirically, on MNIST and CIFAR-10 with 6-layer MLPs (1000 hidden units, small initialization), a single dominant singular value emerges in every layer after the first saddle escape, with the effect most pronounced in deeper layers (4–6), and a second singular value appears only after subsequent saddle escapes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing methods in the standard benchmark sense; it is a theoretical analysis with empirical validation. The key contrast is with prior saddle-to-saddle results: for deep linear networks (Jacot et al., 2022; Li et al., 2020) and deep networks with differentiable nonlinearities (Bai et al., 2022), all layers are exactly rank-one in the first escape direction, whereas this paper shows deep ReLU networks exhibit a more complex structure where only the deeper layers are approximately rank-one and the first few layers can be higher-rank. The counterexample in Section 4 demonstrates that the optimal escape direction is not always exactly rank-one (rank-two in the first layer beats rank-one by a speed ratio of 0.5/0.414 ≈ 1.21), distinguishing ReLU networks from the linear and differentiable-nonlinearity settings. The empirical MNIST/CIFAR-10 results qualitatively confirm the theoretical prediction: deeper layers show a stronger low-rank bias than earlier layers, and the rank increases across successive saddle escapes, consistent with the conjectured BN-rank incremental learning dynamics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the saddle-to-saddle dynamics framework from deep linear networks (Saxe et al., 2014; Jacot et al., 2022) to deep ReLU networks, which had previously only been analyzed under restrictive assumptions that reduce them to linear networks (e.g., differentiable nonlinearities or fixed ReLU sign patterns). The escape direction and homogeneous loss machinery from Jacot et al. (2022) is carried over directly, while the bottleneck rank concept from Jacot (2023a) provides the interpretive lens for the resulting sparsity structure. The key departure from prior work is the finding that the low-rank bias is non-uniform across depth in ReLU networks, in contrast to the uniform rank-one structure in all layers observed for linear networks and shallow ReLU networks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first rigorous characterization of escape directions in deep ReLU networks without reducing to the linear case, filling a significant gap in the theory of implicit bias. The non-uniform rank structure (deeper layers more rank-one) and the counterexample showing a genuinely rank-two optimal escape in the first layer challenge the simple 'all layers rank-one' picture inherited from linear network theory. Major open questions remain: whether GD actually finds the optimal escape direction in practice, whether the approximate rank-one layers persist until the next saddle, and whether the full saddle-to-saddle dynamics implement a greedy low-BN-rank algorithm as conjectured. The empirical validation is limited to small MLPs on MNIST and CIFAR-10, leaving the theory's applicability to larger, more realistic architectures untested.
