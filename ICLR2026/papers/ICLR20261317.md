## Problem

Existing rewrite-based detectors for LLM-generated text rely on fixed, hand-crafted distance functions (e.g., N-gram overlap, Levenshtein, BERTScore) that fail to generalize across different target LLMs, datasets, and unobserved prompts. The paper also lacks a principled geometric explanation for why rewrite-based detection works and why an adaptive distance should outperform a fixed one.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261317/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a candidate text X and a surrogate LLM (Gemma-2-9B) that produces K stochastic rewrites of X. The learned distance function d_φ, parameterized by a fine-tuned language model, computes the absolute difference in per-token log-probabilities between X and each rewrite. The final output is a binary label: X is classified as LLM-generated if the average reconstruction error across K rewrites falls below a predetermined threshold, and as human-authored otherwise.

## Method

L2D parameterizes the distance as d_φ(X₁, X₂) = |log p_φ(X₁)/len(X₁) − log p_φ(X₂)/len(X₂)|, where p_φ is a language model initialized from a pre-trained LLM and fine-tuned (via LoRA or final-layer updates) to maximize the gap in reconstruction errors between a human corpus D_h and an LLM-generated corpus D_m. The key insight, formalized in Proposition 3, is that the optimal bounded distance assigns zero to LLM–LLM pairs and the maximum M to human–LLM pairs; the learned p_φ acts as a soft relaxation of this binary indicator, assigning low probability to human text and near-uniform per-token probability to LLM text. A geometric framework in a Hilbert space (Propositions 1–2) proves that human text lies outside the LLM subspace M, so its projection-based reconstruction error is strictly larger, and that this gap persists under prompt-induced distribution shifts as long as the rewrite perturbation remains small. At inference, K rewrites are generated and their average distance to the original determines the classification.

## Results

On 21 diverse datasets for detecting GPT-3.5-Turbo-generated text, L2D achieves an average AUC of 0.948, outperforming the strongest baseline ImBD (0.890) by 52.5% relative gain. Across three proprietary LLMs (Claude-3.5, GPT-4o, Gemini-2.5-Flash) and three prompt types (rewrite, polish, expand) on Wiki/Story/News benchmarks, L2D achieves near-perfect AUC (0.984–1.000) with relative gains of 54.3%–75.4% over the best baseline. In the ablation study, learning the distance function yields a 97.1% average improvement over using a fixed (pre-trained) distance, confirming the core methodological contribution.

## Baselines & Comparisons

L2D outperforms all 12 baselines on the majority of settings. Against the strongest logits-based ML method ImBD, L2D wins on 16–19 out of 21 datasets (Table 1), with relative gains up to 89.4% (TechnicalWriting) and 87.0% (TravelTourism); ImBD is the only method that occasionally matches or slightly exceeds L2D (e.g., EducationMaterial 0.996 vs. 0.973). Against the strongest rewrite-based baseline RAIDAR, L2D achieves substantially higher AUC on nearly every dataset (e.g., 0.948 vs. 0.812 on AcademicResearch; 0.968 vs. 0.785 on NewsArticle), confirming that learning the distance function is more effective than RAIDAR's fixed Levenshtein-based distance. The zero-shot logits-based method FDGPT is consistently the weakest among strong baselines (average AUC 0.639), highlighting the advantage of ML-based and rewrite-based approaches. In the unseen-prompt experiments (Table 2), L2D achieves near-perfect AUC (≥0.98) across all 27 settings while ImBD and RAIDAR show more variability, with relative gains of 54.3%–75.4% over the best baseline depending on the target LLM. Under adversarial attacks (paraphrasing and decoherence), L2D's AUC remains stable or even increases on News, whereas RAIDAR and ImBD degrade substantially, demonstrating superior robustness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the rewrite-based detection paradigm introduced by RAIDAR (Mao et al., 2024) and related work (Zhu et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024), which compare original text to its LLM reconstruction using a fixed distance (N-gram overlap, Levenshtein, BERTScore). The key departure is replacing this fixed metric with an adaptively learned distance function, parameterized by a fine-tuned language model, motivated by a new geometric framework (Hilbert-space projection) that formally proves rewrite-based methods work and characterizes the optimal distance as model-specific (Proposition 3). The paper also positions itself against the broader taxonomy of passive detectors—logits-based (DetectGPT, Fast-DetectGPT, ImBD) versus rewrite-based—arguing that the learned-distance rewrite approach outperforms both families under unseen prompts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The geometric framework provides the first formal justification for why rewrite-based detection works and why it generalizes to unseen prompts, filling a theoretical gap that prior empirical work left open. The ~96% relative improvement from learning versus fixing the distance (ablation) and the 54–75% gains over the strongest baselines across 100+ settings make this a practically strong detector, and its robustness to paraphrasing and decoherence attacks suggests real-world deployability. However, the computational cost of generating multiple rewrites per text remains a limitation, and open questions include whether high-order reconstruction errors can further boost detection and whether the method can segment LLM-generated portions within human-LLM co-authored texts.
