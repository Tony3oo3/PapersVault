## Problem

Special unitary matrices SU(2) are underexplored in rotation estimation despite their structural advantages over SO(3), and existing rotation representations for neural networks suffer from discontinuity in minimal dimensions or suboptimal gradient properties. This paper bridges the gap by showing that SU(2)-based reformulations of Wahba's problem yield linear constraints on quaternion parameters, which can be leveraged both for classical optimization and for designing new continuous rotation representations for learning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264030/cropped_page5_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

For Wahba's problem, the method consumes weighted pairs of 3D unit vectors (or their stereographic projections onto the complex plane) and outputs the optimal rotation as a quaternion or SU(2) matrix. For the learning representations, 2-vec takes a 6D network output interpreted as two predicted 3D axis directions and maps them to SO(3) via balanced Wahba-optimal alignment, while QuadMobius takes a 16D output arranged as a Hermitian gain matrix, eigendecomposes it, and maps the resulting Möbius transformation to the nearest SU(2) element. Both maps are continuous and differentiable, enabling backpropagation through the rotation parameterization.

## Method

The core contribution is reformulating Wahba's problem in complex projective space via SU(2), which produces linear constraints on quaternion parameters (unlike the standard quadratic eigenvalue formulations). Three equivalent solutions are derived: a stereographic-plane formulation (G_P), a Möbius-transformation approximation (G_M), and a direct 3D-sphere formulation (G_S), all solvable as smallest-eigenvalue problems. From these, two new rotation representations are introduced: 2-vec, which maps a 6D vector to SO(3) by optimally balancing error from both predicted axes (unlike Gram-Schmidt's greedy orthogonalization), and QuadMobius, a 16D two-stage map that first learns a stable latent Möbius transformation via eigendecomposition of a learned Hermitian matrix, then projects to SU(2) via SVD or a closed-form algebraic formula. The two-stage structure of QuadMobius decouples gradient informativeness from input-noise robustness, and the linear constraint structure enables efficient residual computation for iterative solvers and closed-form two-point Wahba solutions.

## Results

On ModelNet10-SO3 3D shape alignment, QMAlg achieves the best mean angular error on Sofa (14.336°) and QMSVD on Toilet (6.026°), while 2-vec leads on Chair (12.544°), all outperforming Gram-Schmidt (13.606°/15.015°/6.586°) and SVD (13.061°/14.967°/6.135°). On Cambridge Landmarks camera pose estimation, QMAlg achieves the lowest mean error on King's College (2.631°) and Shop Facade (6.317°), beating SVD (3.292°/7.117°) and QCQP (3.204°/6.802°). For Wahba's problem, the SU(2)-based solvers (G_P, G_S) match Davenport's Q-method accuracy exactly (7.4676e-4° median at ε=1e-5, n=3) but are slower (4.084 μs vs 3.583 μs), while the Möbius approximation (G_M) trades accuracy for speed (0.917 μs). The two-point SUPER method requires roughly one-third the multiplications of existing solvers in the unweighted case while achieving identical error percentiles.

## Baselines & Comparisons

For Wahba's problem, the SU(2) stereographic (G_P) and 3D sphere (G_S) solvers match Davenport's Q-method and QUEST accuracy exactly (7.4676e-4° median at ε=1e-5), while the Möbius approximation (G_M) degrades to 1.2614e-3° under the same noise, confirming it is an approximation. The two-point SUPER method ties QUEST and Markley's Fast 2 Vec on all error percentiles but uses only 29 multiplications vs 89 for QUEST in the unweighted case. For learning, 2-vec consistently outperforms Gram-Schmidt (Zhou et al., 2019) at the same 6D dimensionality, e.g. 12.544° vs 13.606° mean on Chair and 3.085° vs 3.298° on King's College. QMAlg and QMSVD outperform both QCQP (Peretroukhin et al., 2020) and SVD (Levinson et al., 2020) on most metrics: on King's College, QMAlg achieves 2.631° mean vs 3.204° (QCQP) and 3.292° (SVD); on Shop Facade, QMAlg reaches 6.317° vs 6.802° (QCQP) and 7.117° (SVD). The only case where a baseline wins is QMSVD on Shop Facade 75th percentile (8.947° vs 8.957° for 2-vec), a negligible difference.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper re-examines Wahba's problem (Wahba, 1965) through the lens of SU(2) matrices, deriving linear constraints on quaternion parameters that unify several classical solutions (Davenport, QUEST, etc.) under a single algebraic framework. For rotation representations in neural networks, it positions itself as a direct successor to the Gram-Schmidt map (Zhou et al., 2019) via the 2-vec representation, and as a synthesis of the SVD-based (Levinson et al., 2020) and QCQP-based (Peretroukhin et al., 2020) approaches via QuadMobius, which introduces a Möbius transformation as a learned intermediate latent space before projecting to SU(2). The key departure from prior work is the use of stereographic projection and complex projective geometry to obtain these linear constraints, a perspective not previously exploited for rotation estimation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The linear constraint formulation provides a principled, drop-in mechanism for efficient residual computation in iterative rotation optimization (IRLS, robust losses) and enables closed-form two-point solutions that are roughly 3× cheaper in multiplications than existing methods. QuadMobius's two-stage structure (learned Möbius latent → SU(2) projection) offers a useful conceptual decomposition that decouples noise robustness from gradient informativeness, potentially generalizable to other structured-output learning problems. Open questions include whether the Möbius approximation's noise sensitivity can be mitigated without sacrificing gradient quality, and whether the SU(2) framework extends naturally to higher-dimensional rotation groups or analytical camera pose estimation.
