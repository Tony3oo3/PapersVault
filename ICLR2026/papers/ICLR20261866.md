## Problem

Intelligent systems must simultaneously support broad generalization (recognizing similarity across stimuli) and precise identification (distinguishing individual items), yet the mathematical structure of this tension has remained informal. While Frankland et al. (2021) proposed that finite precision in similarity computation creates a fundamental tradeoff, no closed-form characterization of the resulting Pareto front existed, and it was unclear whether the constraint is universal across architectures or merely an artifact of specific model designs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261866/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The theoretical framework takes an abstract metric stimulus space (M, d, ν) equipped with a probability measure, a similarity function g parameterized by a resolution threshold ε (and optional noise level Δ), and a number of simultaneous items n, and produces closed-form expressions for the probability of correct generalization p_S and identification p_I. The key structural output is a universal Pareto curve in the (p_S, p_I) plane parameterized solely by the ball measure b(ε) = ν(B_ε(p)), independent of the specific geometry of M. Empirically, the framework is instantiated with bird species images (ResNet-50), year-based text prompts (LLMs), and geometric shape images (VLMs), where the resolution ε is operationalized as a distance threshold on learned feature embeddings.

## Method

The paper defines a constant similarity function g_{ε;Δ}(x,y) = 1_{B_ε(x)}(y) + Δ·1_{M\B_ε(x)}(y) that captures finite semantic resolution, then derives closed-form expressions for p_S and p_I via integration over pushforward distance measures (Theorems 1–3). The central result is that in homogeneous spaces, both p_S and p_I are functions of the single scalar b(ε), yielding a universal Pareto front with three regimes: low ε (perfect identification, chance generalization), medium ε (optimal generalization at b(ε)=1/2), and high ε (degradation of both). For n items, identification decays as 1/(b(ε)·n), predicting a sharp capacity collapse. The theory is validated in a minimal ReLU autoencoder (Elhage et al. toy model) where a resolution boundary self-organizes during training and learned linearly-decaying similarity functions match Proposition 1's predictions, and in ResNet-50 fine-tuned on bird phylogenies, LLMs performing year-similarity judgments, and VLMs performing spatial proximity tasks, all exhibiting the predicted resolution-limited behavior.

## Results

The paper derives closed-form expressions (Theorems 1–3) showing that for any model with finite semantic resolution ε, the probability of correct generalization p_S and identification p_I are linked by a universal Pareto front parameterized by the ball measure b(ε). For n-item processing, identification performance decays as p_I^n(ε) ≈ (b(ε)·n)^{-1}, predicting a sharp 1/n collapse in multi-item capacity. Empirically, a ResNet-50 fine-tuned on Caltech-UCSD Birds-200-2011 shows up to 25% improvement in phylogenetic generalization at the cost of 10–20% identification accuracy when the similarity loss weight α is increased. LLMs (Gemma-2B, Llama-3.2-3B, Qwen2.5-7B) exhibit an emergent finite resolution of approximately 70–80 years on temporal similarity tasks, and VLMs (Gemma-3-12B, Qwen2.5-VL-7B) show analogous spatial resolution limits, confirming the tradeoff is architecture-independent.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is primarily a theoretical paper that does not benchmark against competing methods; instead, it validates its derived universal laws across a hierarchy of architectures. The toy ReLU autoencoder (m=10, l=50) trained on a circular metric space produces (p_S, p_I) trajectories that closely track the theoretical curve for linearly decaying similarity (Proposition 1), with the ReLU-induced resolution boundary emerging spontaneously during training. The ResNet-50 CNN on bird species confirms the tradeoff is tunable via the loss weight α: at α=0 (pure identification), generalization is near chance, while at α≥0.8, generalization improves substantially but identification drops. For LLMs, all three models show performance degrading as probe displacement increases, consistent with exponentially decaying similarity plus noise, and the n-item similarity task shows identification decreasing gracefully (better than 1/n chance) while similarity performance is capped below 0.7. The VLM spatial task similarly reveals model-specific resolution scales beyond which accuracy drops toward chance. No direct head-to-head comparison against prior methods is performed; the contribution is the theoretical framework itself, with empirical results serving as validation that the predicted constraints are universal rather than model-specific artifacts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper formalizes and extends the conceptual generalization-identification tradeoff proposed by Frankland et al. (2021), who argued that processing limits in cognitive systems reflect the 'curse of generalization.' While Frankland et al. identified the tension qualitatively and through simple models, this work derives closed-form Pareto front expressions for arbitrary metric spaces, extends the analysis from 2-item to n-item processing (yielding a sharp 1/n capacity collapse), and incorporates noise and spatial heterogeneity effects. The theoretical framework draws on Shepard's Universal Law of Generalization, the Luce choice model, and the binding problem literature, while the empirical validation connects to superposition in neural networks (Elhage et al., 2022) and topological processing limits (Petri et al., 2021).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a rare architecture-agnostic theoretical explanation for why both humans and billion-parameter neural networks exhibit striking multi-object processing limits, positioning finite semantic resolution as an information-theoretic constraint rather than an implementation artifact. The 1/n identification collapse prediction offers a concrete, testable benchmark for evaluating representational quality across systems. Key limitations include the focus on non-compositional representations (excluding hierarchical syntax, analogical reasoning), and the fact that the full tradeoff is only directly demonstrated in toy and CNN models, while VLM/LLM evidence is more suggestive (showing finite resolution but not the complete Pareto trajectory). Open directions include extending to compositional coding, validating resolution bounds in neural population data via fMRI, and developing resolution-based diagnostic tools for architecture design.
