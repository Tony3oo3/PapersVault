## Problem

Existing AI research agent benchmarks evaluate narrow, isolated skills (code accuracy, parameter tuning) in simplified, resource-constrained environments that do not support long-horizon or distributed training, and frame success as replicating known results rather than generating novel solutions. This limits the ability to assess whether agents can orchestrate coherent, end-to-end research workflows that mirror real LLM development practice.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261239/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each task provides a structured description (motivation, objective, data paths, constraints, evaluation metrics, environment info, and helper scripts) alongside a writable starter workspace containing a pre-built conda environment, complete training/validation/test datasets, model checkpoints, and a code repository with the paper's key novelty removed. The agent interacts via 42 primitive tool calls (terminal commands, file operations, web search/browse, multimodal parsing) and submits outputs through a Kaggle-style eval interface with up to 4 scoring opportunities; evaluation runs externally to the workspace to prevent answer hacking, with scores calibrated between a baseline (~0) and a hidden reference solution (~80). Tasks span 2–36 hours of wall-clock time and may require 8×80 GB GPUs across multiple nodes.

## Method

InnovatorBench comprises 20 tasks derived from 14 influential papers across six LLM research domains (data construction, filtering, augmentation, loss design, reward design, scaffold construction), each requiring the agent to propose creative methods, implement them, iterate based on results, and produce runnable artifacts. ResearchGym is the supporting platform that provides a rich action space (Command, File, Parse, Web Search, Web Browse families), multi-computer control via HTTP for distributed training, asynchronous command execution to prevent decision blocking during multi-hour jobs, and snapshot saving/loading with branching for pausing and resuming experiments. A lightweight ReAct agent with context summarization is deployed on top of frontier LLMs (Claude Sonnet 4, GPT-5, GLM-4.5, Kimi-K2, Qwen3-32b) to establish baselines. The key design distinction from prior benchmarks is the combination of innovation-oriented objectives (surpassing a hidden reference rather than replicating it), multi-GPU/multi-node infrastructure, asynchronous long-horizon execution, and multi-dimensional scoring (correctness, performance, output quality, and uncertainty metrics like post-RL entropy).

## Results

On InnovatorBench's 20 LLM research tasks, Claude Sonnet 4 achieves the highest weighted average score (24.01 final / 24.54 best), leading on 4 of 6 research domains. GPT-5 follows with 12.04/12.52, excelling notably in Scaffold Construction (60.07), while GLM-4.5 scores 11.85/13.35 and Kimi-K2 5.35/5.45. Qwen3-32b scores 0 across all tasks due to its limited context window. All models score substantially higher on data-related tasks (Construction, Filtering, Augmentation) than on algorithm-related tasks (Loss Design, Reward Design), and agents require over 11 hours to reach their best performance, roughly 6.5× longer than on PaperBench.

## Baselines & Comparisons

InnovatorBench is positioned against 8 prior benchmarks (SWE-bench, ScienceAgentBench, RExBench, RE-Bench, EXP-Bench, PaperBench, ML-Bench, MLE-bench) primarily on structural dimensions rather than direct score comparison. InnovatorBench is the only benchmark supporting multi-GPU/multi-node execution, snapshot save-and-restore, and explicit creativity evaluation, while also allowing up to 4 evaluation submissions (vs. 1 for most others). In terms of difficulty, Figure 5 shows agents require ~11 hours to reach peak performance on InnovatorBench versus ~1.75 hours on PaperBench (~6.5× longer), indicating substantially greater task complexity. Among the evaluated agents, Claude Sonnet 4 dominates 4 of 6 domains, GPT-5 wins Scaffold Construction by a wide margin (60.07 vs. 36.63), and GLM-4.5 edges out Claude on Data Augmentation (25.49 vs. 22.73). All models struggle with Loss Design and Reward Design, where even the best scores remain below 13, highlighting a persistent gap in algorithmic creativity versus data engineering.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of AI research agent benchmarks (PaperBench, ScienceAgentBench, RExBench, EXP-Bench) by shifting from narrow replication or single-skill evaluation to end-to-end, innovation-oriented LLM research tasks. It builds on the ReAct agent paradigm and OpenHands' action-space design but departs fundamentally in scale: ResearchGym introduces multi-machine control, asynchronous command execution, and snapshot-based pausing/resuming, none of which prior agent environments (MLGym, OpenHands) support. The benchmark's emphasis on surpassing a hidden reference solution rather than reproducing a known result distinguishes it from PaperBench and RExBench, which measure fidelity to existing work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

InnovatorBench provides the first systematic evaluation of whether frontier LLMs can conduct genuine LLM research (data construction, loss/reward design, scaffold building) end-to-end, revealing that even the best model (Claude Sonnet 4) achieves only ~24/100 and requires 11+ hours to saturate. The identified failure modes—impatience (killing valid training runs), resource contention, and template-based reasoning—offer concrete targets for improving research agents. Open questions include whether the benchmark generalizes beyond LLM research to other scientific domains, whether more sophisticated agent scaffolds (beyond ReAct) close the gap, and whether the 20-task scale is sufficient for reliable model ranking.
