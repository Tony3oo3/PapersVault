## Problem

Lower-resource languages lack both native-speaker instruction datasets and fluent instruction-tuned models capable of generating synthetic training data, forcing post-training to rely on machine-translated instruction data that introduces translationese artifacts and degrades fluency. This paper asks whether fluent preference-aligned models can be created for lower-resource languages without any instruction-tuning data in the target language, and whether a disfluent judge model can still guide a fluent policy.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263185/cropped_page4_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes user prompts in the target language (Norwegian Bokmål) and produces fluent text responses in that language. During training, prompts are drawn from the machine-translated No Robots dataset, but the model is only ever trained on its own sampled responses (8 per prompt), never on the translated reference responses. The judge model receives the prompt, the model's generated response, and the gold English response as a reference, then outputs a scalar quality score (1–10) used as the reward signal.

## Method

The paper proposes a three-stage post-training pipeline: (1) continual pretraining on the target language, (2) a brief SFT stage on 1,000 English LIMA examples (one epoch) to teach chat format without overwriting target-language fluency, and (3) on-policy reinforcement learning where the model is trained exclusively on responses it samples from itself. The RL objective is a REINFORCE-style policy gradient with a sample-mean baseline and sample-std normalization over G=8 responses, a length-bias-corrected token-level loss, and a Rao-Blackwellized KL divergence penalty (using full next-token distributions over the vocabulary) to prevent reward hacking. The reward signal comes from an LLM-as-a-judge (d-RLAIF) rather than a trained Bradley-Terry model, and the key finding is that even a disfluent judge (e.g., Mistral Nemo 12B with 67% fluency) can produce a substantially more fluent policy (92% fluency) because the policy never sees translated text and thus retains the fluent generation subspace learned during pretraining. The distributed training is fully synchronous, keeping samples nearly on-policy and eliminating the need for PPO-style clipping or importance sampling.

## Results

In native-speaker pairwise fluency comparisons (300 pairs, 5 annotators), the on-policy RL model achieves a 79.7% average win-rate, outperforming translated SFT (60.0%) and Mistral Nemo (10.3%). Specifically, on-policy RL is preferred over translated SFT in 67.5% of head-to-head comparisons and over Mistral Nemo in 91.8%. The automatic fluency scorer (trained on ASK-GEC + backtranslation data, 85.5% agreement with human consensus) confirms the ranking: 92.2% for on-policy RL, 85.7% for translated SFT, and 65.3% for Mistral Nemo.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three post-training approaches built from the same NorMistral 11B base model. On-policy RL (the proposed method) decisively outperforms both baselines on fluency: it wins 67.5% of pairwise comparisons against translated SFT (SFT on machine-translated No Robots data via Tower-Plus-72B) and 91.8% against Mistral Nemo 12B (the standard multilingual English-focused instruction-tuned model). The gap over translated SFT is attributed to residual translationese artifacts in the SFT outputs, while Mistral Nemo's poor fluency reflects its English-centric post-training. Notably, the on-policy RL policy (92.2% fluency) substantially exceeds the fluency of its own judge model, Mistral Nemo (67.0% fluency), demonstrating that a disfluent judge can still guide a fluent policy. Ablations confirm that even a single epoch of translated SFT (91.0% fluency) degrades performance relative to English-only SFT (94.2%), and that the choice of judge model has negligible impact on policy fluency (range: 92.1–94.2% across eight judges).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper combines three established components—REINFORCE-style policy gradient optimization (Williams, 1992), LLM-as-a-judge reward signals from the RLAIF literature (Lee et al., 2024), and Rao-Blackwellized KL divergence estimation (Amini et al., 2025)—into a unified on-policy post-training pipeline specifically designed for lower-resource languages. It departs from the dominant paradigm of supervised finetuning on machine-translated instruction data (Suzuki et al., 2023; Pipatanakul et al., 2023; Üstün et al., 2024) by arguing that any exposure to translated responses introduces translationese that degrades fluency. Conceptually, the paper connects to the weak-to-strong generalization framework (Burns et al., 2024) and the documented judge-policy capability disconnect in RLAIF (Zheng et al., 2023), but provides the first systematic empirical demonstration that a disfluent judge can bootstrap a fluent policy in a lower-resource language setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical recipe for post-training lower-resource languages without requiring native-speaker instruction data, which is a critical bottleneck for hundreds of languages. The finding that policy fluency is essentially independent of judge fluency (Pearson r = 0.067 across 8 judges) is actionable: it means the alignment phase does not require a fluent instruction-tuned model in the target language, only a judge with adequate comprehension. Key limitations include evaluation on a single language (Norwegian Bokmål, ~5M speakers, which is relatively well-resourced) and the prerequisite of a strong language-specific pretrained model. Open questions include whether the approach transfers to truly low-resource languages lacking good pretrained models, whether the fluency preservation holds for longer training runs or different reward criteria, and whether the method can be extended to multi-turn dialogue or domain-specific alignment.
