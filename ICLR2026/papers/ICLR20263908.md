## Problem

Large reasoning models trained with critic-free RL (e.g., GRPO) suffer from 'overthinking'—generating excessively long reasoning paths without performance benefit. Existing length-penalty approaches fail to balance efficiency and accuracy because of a fundamental misalignment between sequence-level rewards and token-level policy updates: they erroneously suppress essential high-entropy exploratory tokens while inadvertently rewarding partial redundancy in shorter sequences. The paper provides formal proofs (Theorems 1 and 2) showing these two flaws cause both performance degradation and suboptimal token reduction.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt (math, science, or coding problem) and produces a compressed chain-of-thought reasoning trace followed by a final answer. During training, 16 rollouts are generated per prompt with a maximum token limit of 16384 (8192 for 7B models). A lightweight 1.5B judge model segments each correct rollout's thinking tokens into chunks using discourse markers (e.g., 'Wait', 'However', 'Alternatively') and identifies the first chunk containing the correct answer, defining the NRP boundary that separates necessary from redundant tokens.

## Method

DECS introduces a decoupled token-level reward mechanism that assigns a fixed high reward (r+=1.1) to all NRP tokens while giving redundant post-NRP tokens a length-scaled reward between r0=1.0 and r+=1.1, ensuring that via GRPO's group-relative advantage computation, redundant tokens consistently receive negative advantages regardless of sequence length. A curriculum prompt scheduling strategy adaptively adjusts the proportion of easy prompts (where all G rollouts are correct) in each batch based on the observed NRP ratio, preventing the over-suppression of high-entropy tokens that Theorem 1 identifies as a failure mode. The NRP boundary is detected by a fine-tuned Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct model that classifies whether each reasoning chunk semantically contains the correct answer, adding only ~5% training overhead. Together, these components reduce reasoning tokens by over 50% across seven benchmarks while maintaining or improving Pass@1 accuracy.

## Results

On the DeepSeek-R1-Distill-1.5B base model, DECS reduces average reasoning tokens by 57.17% (9,340 → 4,000) while improving average Pass@1 accuracy by +2.48 points (45.21% → 47.78%), achieving the best AES score of 0.74. On the 7B model, DECS cuts thinking tokens by 49.50% (7,857 → 3,968) with a +0.8 point accuracy gain (61.57% → 62.48%) and an AES of 0.54. The method generalizes to Qwen3-4B with 54.80% token reduction and +1.32 Pass@1 improvement (AES 0.61), and extends to out-of-domain tasks (56.33% fewer tokens on GPQA-Diamond, 33.52% fewer on LiveCodeBench).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DECS outperforms all nine baselines on both efficiency and efficacy simultaneously. Against the strongest length-reward baseline ThinkPrune (Hou et al., 2025), DECS achieves comparable token counts (4,000 vs. 3,975 on 1.5B) but +1.95 points higher average Pass@1 (47.78% vs. 45.83%) and a substantially better AES (0.74 vs. 0.62), indicating ThinkPrune sacrifices necessary reasoning tokens. Against GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), DECS matches or exceeds accuracy while using ~55% fewer tokens (4,000 vs. 8,814). Against AdaptThink (Zhang et al., 2025b) and LC-R1 (Cheng et al., 2025), DECS wins on both accuracy (+5.0 and +4.9 points on 1.5B) and efficiency, as these methods either suppress necessary reasoning or fail to fully eliminate redundancy. Against TLMRE (Arora & Zanette, 2025), DECS achieves +1.0 point higher accuracy with 27% fewer tokens. In the extended comparison (Table 11), DECS also dominates MinD (Zeng et al., 2025b), VSRM-R++ (Yue et al., 2025), LAPO (Wu et al., 2025), and LASER-D (Liu et al., 2025) on both Pass@1 and token count across all seven benchmarks, with the largest margins on AIME2024 (+4.1 points over MinD) and GPQA-Diamond (+1.6 points over LASER-D).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the GRPO/RLVR framework established by DeepSeek-R1 and the DAPO filtering strategy, but departs from the dominant line of length-penalty methods (ThinkPrune, TLMRE, AdaptThink, LC-R1) by proving that sequence-level length rewards are fundamentally misaligned with token-level policy optimization. The key conceptual departure is the introduction of the Necessary Reasoning Prefix (NRP) as a token-level boundary that enables a decoupled reward: NRP tokens receive a fixed high reward while post-NRP tokens receive a length-scaled lower reward, ensuring redundant tokens are always penalized regardless of sequence length. The curriculum scheduling component extends standard RLVR training by adaptively controlling the proportion of easy prompts based on observed NRP ratios, addressing the suppression of high-entropy exploratory tokens identified in Theorem 1.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical diagnosis (Theorems 1 and 2) provides the RLVR community with a clear mechanistic explanation for why the seemingly straightforward approach of adding a length penalty to correctness reward degrades performance and fails to fully eliminate redundancy—this reframes how practitioners should think about efficiency optimization in reasoning models. The practical impact is significant: over 50% token reduction while maintaining or improving accuracy across seven benchmarks and two model scales, with generalization to out-of-domain tasks (science, coding) and a different backbone (Qwen3-4B). Open questions include whether the approach scales to 70B+ models, whether NRP detection can be integrated into the policy itself (eliminating the auxiliary 1.5B model and its ~5% training overhead), and whether the math-specialized NRP detector's near-perfect cross-domain accuracy (>97%) holds for more diverse reasoning tasks.
