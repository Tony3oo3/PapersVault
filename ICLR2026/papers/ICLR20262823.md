## Problem

Existing FP8 LLM training frameworks face a fundamental efficiency-accuracy tradeoff: per-group activation quantization preserves numerical fidelity but forces dequantization onto slow CUDA cores inside the GEMM main loop, negating Tensor Core speedups, while just-in-time weight scaling incurs costly max-reduction and repeated HBM reads/writes. No prior method simultaneously achieves high quantization precision and minimal runtime overhead for FP8 training on widely available hardware.

## Input / Output

The method consumes FP32/BF16 model weights, activation tensors, and gradient tensors during standard LLM training (pretraining or fine-tuning). It produces FP8-quantized activations carrying a two-level scale structure (one FP32 global per-tensor factor plus per-32-element E8M0 power-of-two local factors) and FP8-quantized weights with a single per-tensor FP32 scale whose value is predicted analytically from the learning rate and step count rather than computed via max-reduction. The dequantized FP32 partial sums are reconstructed only in the GEMM epilogue, keeping the main loop entirely on Tensor Cores.

## Method

MOSS introduces two complementary mechanisms. First, a two-level microscaling scheme for sensitive activations: a high-precision FP32 global scale is applied at coarse granularity (per-tensor), while compact E8M0 power-of-two local scales are applied at fine granularity (every 32 elements), so that the expensive FP32 multiplication is deferred to the GEMM epilogue on CUDA cores and the main loop executes purely on Tensor Cores with lightweight E8M0 scaling. Second, an automatic scaling mechanism for linear-layer weights that exploits the bounded-update property of Adam/AdamW (|Δt| ≤ η) to predict the weight scaling factor analytically as s_t = s_0 + η·t/Δ_max^FP8, eliminating per-step max-reduction entirely; periodic re-scaling at fixed intervals (default 500 steps) corrects drift at negligible cost. A theoretical SNR analysis proves that this two-level scheme achieves strictly higher quantization fidelity than both per-tensor and per-group quantization, and custom Triton GEMM kernels enable MXFP8 computation on Hopper GPUs that lack native microscaling Tensor Core support.

## Results

On OLMo-7B pretraining (22B tokens, 8×H800), MOSS achieves 45,374 tokens/s throughput (+34.2% over BF16's 33,805) while matching BF16 perplexity: 40.96 on WikiText-103, 30.63 on C4, and 25.08 on The Pile (vs. BF16's 39.59, 30.59, 25.18). On LLaMA-2-7B fine-tuning, MOSS delivers 241.8 samples/s (+43.8% over BF16) with downstream accuracy of 52.8% (Mathematics), 64.7% (GSM8K), and 59.4% (NumGLUE), matching or slightly exceeding BF16. MOSS also achieves a 1.8× reduction in peak activation memory (23.5 GB vs. 42.3 GB for BF16) and 83.4% compute-communication overlap.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against COAT (Xi et al., 2024), MOSS wins decisively on efficiency: 45,374 vs. 40,416 tokens/s end-to-end throughput (+12.3%), 0.77 ms vs. 3.73 ms average GEMM runtime (4.8× faster), and 23.5 GB vs. 28.6 GB peak activation memory. On model quality, MOSS is slightly better on C4 (30.63 vs. 30.89 PPL) and Pile (25.08 vs. 26.05 PPL) but marginally worse on WikiText-103 (40.96 vs. 40.62 PPL), all within noise. Against Transformer Engine (Nvidia, 2025), MOSS achieves comparable GEMM runtime (0.77 ms vs. 0.84 ms average) while providing finer-grained activation quantization that TE's per-tensor approach lacks. Against DeepGEMM (Zhao et al., 2025), MOSS is slower in raw GEMM (0.77 ms vs. 0.54 ms average), which the authors attribute to DeepGEMM's Hopper-specific hardware optimizations (Increasing Accumulation Precision) that MOSS's Triton-based implementation does not exploit. On the larger Qwen-3-14B/32B fine-tuning (Table 4), MOSS matches BF16 within ±0.5% on all five benchmarks (MATH500, GPQA-Diamond, C-Eval, AIME24, MMLU-Redux), confirming no meaningful degradation at scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

MOSS builds directly on the FP8 mixed-precision training lineage initiated by Transformer Engine and extended by FP8-LM, COAT, and DeepSeek-V3, which introduced per-group activation quantization to handle FP8's limited dynamic range. It departs from this line by replacing per-group scaling along the GEMM inner dimension—which the paper shows incurs prohibitive CUDA Core dequantization overhead—with a two-level microscaling scheme (FP32 global scale + E8M0 power-of-two local scales) that confines the main loop to Tensor Core operations. For weight scaling, MOSS moves beyond the just-in-time and delayed scaling strategies used by prior frameworks by exploiting AdamW's bounded-update property to predict scaling factors analytically, a conceptual departure from the data-driven max-reduction approaches in Transformer Engine and COAT.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MOSS demonstrates that FP8 training can be both faster and equally accurate without relying on hardware-native microscaling support (available only on Blackwell), making it immediately deployable on widely available Hopper GPUs. The 34% throughput gain over BF16 and 12.3% over COAT with lossless accuracy on 7B–32B models is practically significant for reducing large-scale training costs. However, the framework is limited to GEMM operations (non-linear layers remain in higher precision), has not been validated at 100B+ scale where gradient dynamics may differ, and its Triton-based GEMM kernel is outperformed by hardware-specific implementations like DeepGEMM on Hopper, leaving open whether the approach retains its advantage on next-generation hardware with native MX support.
