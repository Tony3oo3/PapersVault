## Problem

It remains unclear to what extent decoder-only language models engage in forward-looking planning (structuring computations over a receding horizon) and branch-aware computation (preserving information about alternative valid continuations) in their internal states, without external scaffolds like chain-of-thought prompting. Existing interpretability tools—probing classifiers and circuit discovery—suffer from confounding (probe artifacts, manual engineering) that make them ill-suited for quantifying these planning properties in an automated, scalable way.

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes collections of hidden-state blocks G_S (variable-sized tensors spanning layer and token axes, e.g., 16×11×768 for CFG prefixes or 70×11×768 for PF-Short) extracted from a frozen GPT-3 Small model, along with the corresponding prefix token sequences. It produces discrete codebook indices (K ranging from 64 to 1024 depending on block size) that serve as coarse summaries, from which empirical joint and marginal distributions yield mutual information and normalized MI values. All reported quantities are relative (nMI) rather than absolute, since the coarse-graining step inherently discards information and bounds MI by log(K).

## Method

The core pipeline trains a VQ-VAE (transformer encoder + discrete codebook + transformer decoder) on hidden-state blocks to map each variable-shaped G_S to a single discrete code Z_S ∈ [K], using a composite loss that combines reconstruction MSE, standard VQ commitment, a cosine-similarity penalty to spread codebook embeddings, and an entropy term to prevent codebook collapse. Mutual information is then computed between the learned discrete codes of two block collections (e.g., prefix computation summary vs. a future decision state) via empirical co-occurrence counting, avoiding the probe-induced confounding that plagues accuracy-based probing. The framework is applied to three analyses: (i) measuring nMI between the full prefix computation code and last-layer decision-state codes at increasing future offsets τ to characterize planning horizon; (ii) comparing nMI between prefix codes and alternative correct paths versus decoy paths to quantify branch awareness; and (iii) localizing decision-relevant information across layers and 16-token prefix blocks via a recency/depth heatmap. Models are trained with either standard NTP or multi-token prediction (MTP) loss (Γ=2–4 auxiliary heads) to assess how the training objective modulates these planning properties.

## Results

On the branch-awareness metric I(Z_H;Z_alt)/I(Z_H;Z_decoy), both NTP and MTP models yield ratios well above 1, confirming that prefix computations encode information about unused correct continuations: 7.60±0.78 (NTP) and 6.29±0.17 (MTP) on PF-Short, and 1.45±0.01 (NTP) and 1.82±0.27 (MTP) on PF-Long. On PF-Long, MTP training substantially improves path-finding accuracy from 0.60±0.01 (NTP) to 0.85±0.02 (MTP), while on PF-Short both models achieve high accuracy (0.92 vs. 0.88). The planning horizon is strongly task-dependent: on CFG, nMI between prefix summary codes and decision-state codes decays to roughly one-fifth of its initial value by τ=10, whereas on PF-Long the nMI remains elevated and can peak beyond τ=1, indicating non-myopic pre-output computation. Across all settings, a clear recency effect is observed: the last layers and most recent prefix blocks carry the highest nMI with the next-token decision state, with conditional nMI for earlier positions within the first block dropping to ~0.3.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external interpretability baselines (e.g., probing classifiers or circuit-discovery methods); instead, its primary comparison axis is the training objective (NTP vs. MTP) and the task domain (CFG vs. PF-Short vs. PF-Long). On the easier PF-Short task, NTP slightly outperforms MTP on both the branch-awareness ratio (7.60 vs. 6.29) and accuracy (0.92 vs. 0.88), suggesting that when the task is simple, the standard next-token objective already captures sufficient branch information. On the harder PF-Long task, MTP reverses this: it achieves a higher branch-awareness ratio (1.82 vs. 1.45) and a substantially higher accuracy (0.85 vs. 0.60), indicating that multi-token prediction helps the model maintain richer forward-looking and branch-aware computation when the planning horizon is longer. The authors note that the nMI curves for MTP on PF-Long are slightly more uniform across τ than for NTP, which they attribute to better prediction of earlier (harder) tokens along the generated path. Across all three datasets, the planning horizon is task-contingent rather than uniformly extended by MTP: on CFG, both objectives show rapid nMI decay consistent with short-range, locally syntactic planning, and MTP does not substantially extend the horizon. The paper positions its VQ-VAE–MI pipeline as an alternative to probing-based methods, arguing (with supporting experiments in App. D.2) that probe-based quantities can be confounded by probe capacity and marginal complexity, whereas MI between learned discrete codes is invariant under reparameterization and free from such artifacts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the VQ-VAE framework (Van Den Oord et al., 2017) for discrete representation learning, repurposing it as a compressor for transformer hidden states to enable scalable mutual information estimation. It departs from two dominant interpretability paradigms: circuit discovery (Elhage et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023), which requires heavy manual engineering, and probing classifiers (Hewitt & Liang, 2019; Pimentel et al., 2020), which conflate probe capacity with representation content. The information-theoretic lens connects to prior work on information bottleneck principles (Tishby & Zaslavsky, 2015; Shwartz-Ziv & Tishby, 2017) and MI-based probing (Voita & Titov, 2020), but the key distinction is computing MI between two learned discrete summaries rather than between a hidden state and an external target, avoiding the confounding that plagues probe-based metrics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The VQ-VAE-to-MI pipeline offers an automated, scalable, and confound-resistant tool for studying internal planning structure in LMs, filling a gap left by both manual circuit analysis and probe-based methods. The finding that planning horizon is strongly task-contingent (short in syntactic CFG, extended in path-finding) and that models implicitly retain information about unused correct continuations provides concrete evidence for non-myopic computation without external scaffolds. However, the analysis is limited to GPT-3 Small, captures only aggregate (average-case) phenomena rather than per-prompt behavior, and the lossy VQ-VAE compression means absolute MI values carry no direct interpretive weight. Open questions include whether these patterns persist at scale, in reasoning-tuned models, and whether architectural modifications can deliberately extend the planning horizon.
