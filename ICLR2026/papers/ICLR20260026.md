## Problem

While reinforcement learning has empirically enhanced the planning capabilities of large language models, the theoretical mechanisms explaining why RL outperforms supervised fine-tuning—and the fundamental limitations of current RL approaches—remain unexplained. This paper asks what SFT structurally fails to learn, how policy gradient achieves correct planning through exploration, and what pathologies (e.g., diversity collapse, Q-value bias) arise in different RL formulations for path planning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260026/cropped_page4_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a source node token and a target node token as input, representing a pair of nodes in an unknown directed graph, and must autoregressively generate a sequence of intermediate node tokens forming a valid directed path from source to target, terminated by an end-of-sequence token. The graph is abstracted from real planning domains (e.g., Blocksworld block configurations as nodes, valid single moves as edges), and the backbone is a one-layer, single-head Transformer with embedding dimension 120. During RL training, the model generates paths on-policy (or off-policy for Q-learning) and receives either a binary outcome reward (1 if the full path is valid) or per-step process rewards combining adjacency and target-reachability checks.

## Method

The paper provides a theoretical analysis of RL training dynamics for path planning under a graph abstraction, assuming the Transformer's next-token logits depend only on the (target, current) node pair. For SFT, they characterize the stable point as memorizing co-occurrence frequencies among (target, current, next) triples, explaining why SFT cannot exploit transitivity. For policy gradient, they prove equivalence to SFT restricted to correct paths, show convergence to 100% training accuracy, identify a diversity collapse where output diversity monotonically decreases even after perfect accuracy, and demonstrate that KL regularization preserves diversity at the cost of accuracy. For Q-learning, they show that outcome rewards cause Q-value bias (all logits collapse to a constant per target), while process rewards recover the full adjacency and reachability structure, preserve output diversity at convergence, and naturally support off-policy learning—validated on Blocksworld and Erdős-Rényi graphs.

## Results

This is primarily a theoretical paper with empirical validation on Blocksworld (73-node graph) and Erdős-Rényi graphs (100 nodes, edge probability 0.15). Key empirical findings: PG without KL regularization reaches 100% training accuracy but output diversity collapses to a single path per source-target pair; Q-learning with process rewards achieves comparable training accuracy and significantly better test accuracy than PG; Q-learning with outcome rewards collapses to near-zero accuracy on both training and test sets. On the four-way data split (Erdős-Rényi), PG with λ=10⁻⁴ achieves the best balance between generalization and training accuracy, while Q-learning with process rewards nearly recovers the complete adjacency structure of the graph.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three training paradigms — continual SFT, Policy Gradient (PG), and Q-learning — on path-planning over graph structures. PG outperforms continual SFT on test accuracy because its on-policy exploration generates new correct paths absent from the fixed SFT dataset (Takeaway 2), but PG without KL regularization suffers progressive diversity collapse, eventually producing only one path per pair and degrading test accuracy (Takeaway 3). Q-learning with process rewards dominates PG on test accuracy while preserving output diversity at convergence (Takeaway 6), and uniquely supports off-policy sampling with comparable performance to on-policy training. Q-learning with outcome rewards fails catastrophically, collapsing to near-zero accuracy due to Q-value bias (Theorem 5.1). KL regularization in PG acts as a diversity-preserving mechanism but caps training accuracy; the optimal λ=10⁻⁴ balances generalization gains against accuracy loss (Takeaway 4, Figure 7).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the graph-based planning abstraction and transformer training dynamics analysis of Wang et al. (2024b, 'Alpine'), which showed that SFT-based transformers fail to learn complete reachability structures. While Wang et al. characterized what SFT *cannot* learn, this paper provides the complementary structural characterization of what SFT *does* learn (co-occurrence memorization, Theorem 3.1) and then analyzes the gradient dynamics of two RL paradigms—policy gradient and Q-learning—on the same abstraction. It also provides a theoretical explanation for the empirical 'SFT memorizes, RL generalizes' observation of Chu et al. (2025) and formalizes the diversity-collapse phenomenon reported by Cui et al. (2025), positioning itself as the first systematic theoretical treatment of RL gradient dynamics in LLM planning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a principled explanation for why RL outperforms SFT in planning (exploration-driven data augmentation) and identifies a concrete, previously uncharacterized failure mode of policy gradient: diversity collapse that persists even after 100% training accuracy. The finding that Q-learning with process rewards preserves diversity and supports off-policy learning points toward a practical alternative to PG-based methods (PPO/GRPO) that are currently dominant in LLM RL training. Key limitations include reliance on Assumption 3.1 (model as a function of target and current node), validation only on 1-layer 1-head transformers and small graphs (≤100 nodes), and the fact that Q-learning for LLMs remains largely unexplored in practice. Open questions include whether these dynamics hold for deeper, multi-head architectures and whether the Q-learning advantages scale to the large batch sizes and quantized models used in production RLHF pipelines.
