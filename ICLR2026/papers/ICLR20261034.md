## Problem

Existing physics-integrated Gaussian splatting methods (e.g., PhysGaussian, GIC) handle moderate elastic deformations but fail under extreme mechanical collisions, producing two critical artifacts: unphysical adhesion of fractured solids to neighboring objects in MPM simulations, and visually broken rendering at fracture surfaces where Gaussian overlap is disrupted by particle separation. No prior framework simultaneously addresses both the simulation-level adhesion problem and the rendering-level fracture surface representation.

## Input / Output

The method takes multi-view RGB images (1024×1024) of collision objects along with manually specified material parameters (Young's modulus, Poisson's ratio, density, and NACC plasticity parameters α, β, ξ, M) for each component. Objects may be heterogeneous (e.g., a ficus with distinct leaf, branch, and pot materials). The output is a 100-frame dynamic fracture simulation rendered as a 3D Gaussian splatting video, where particle trajectories are computed via MPM and fracture surfaces are dynamically tracked and re-rendered each frame. The pipeline samples 200K surface and 100K interior particles per object from an SDF-constrained domain.

## Method

The framework has two core technical contributions. First, Collision-MPM augments MLS-MPM with momentum-conserving interface forces: at each grid node, normalized mass distributions of the two colliding objects define interface direction tendencies, and a collision force is applied only when relative velocity indicates penetration, computed from the difference in momenta divided by total mass and timestep. This eliminates the non-physical adhesion inherent in standard MLS-MPM. Second, Fracture Particles Gaussian Optimization (FPGO) uses the hardening parameter α from the NACC constitutive model to identify fractured particles each timestep; for each fractured particle, it locates intact neighbors within a search radius, computes the minimal-volume enclosing ellipsoid (MVEE) of the original Gaussian overlap regions, and generates replacement Gaussian particles that inherit optical properties (opacity, color) but receive recomputed spatial parameters (position, covariance) from the MVEE. An occlusion-aware sampling scheme ensures only the nearest optimized particle contributes to each pixel during rendering.

## Results

The paper evaluates Fracture-GS against PhysGaussian and GIC using PSNR, LPIPS, FID, and a user-study Fracture Simulation Fidelity (FSF) score on a 5-point scale. Fracture-GS achieves the best results across all metrics in the integrated comparison (Figure 5), with the authors noting that competing methods produce visible artifacts at fracture interfaces and collision adhesion regions. A momentum conservation experiment (Figure 7) confirms strict adherence to the conservation law, and an energy stability analysis (Figure 8) shows bounded total energy over 50 frames with no non-physical growth. Per-frame rendering times range from 6.13 ms (Teapot, no fracture tracking) to 62.78 ms (Ficus, with fracture tracking), indicating moderate computational overhead from the FPGO module.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Fracture-GS is compared against two Gaussian-splatting-based simulation frameworks: PhysGaussian (Xie et al., CVPR 2024) and GIC (Cai et al., NeurIPS 2024, forward-simulation component only). Both baselines use MLS-MPM without the proposed Collision-MPM or FPGO modules. Fracture-GS outperforms both on all reported metrics (PSNR, LPIPS, FID, and user-study FSF), with the advantage attributed to two factors: (1) Collision-MPM eliminates the non-physical adhesion artifacts that MLS-MPM produces at multi-body contact interfaces, and (2) FPGO reconstructs Gaussian attributes at fracture surfaces via MVEE-based interpolation, avoiding the rendering discontinuities that both baselines exhibit when directly using initial Gaussian attributes. The authors note that Fracture-GS's FID is still relatively high due to reliance on training-view interpolation for Gaussian restoration in hidden areas, a limitation shared by all compared methods. The user study (10 participants, 5-point scale) confirms that Fracture-GS receives the highest mean FSF rating, with participants identifying more realistic fracture propagation and natural separation of fragments.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two established lines of work: MPM-based fracture simulation (MLS-MPM by Hu et al. 2018, CD-MPM/NACC by Wolper et al. 2019) and 3D Gaussian splatting for real-time rendering (Kerbl et al. 2023). Recent efforts like PhysGaussian (Xie et al., 2024) and GIC (Cai et al., 2024) have begun coupling MPM with Gaussian representations, but they are limited to moderate elastic deformations. Fracture-GS extends this pipeline to extreme multi-body collisions with dynamic fragmentation by introducing two targeted modifications: a momentum-conserving collision force (adapted from fluid-solid MPM, Yan et al. 2018) to eliminate interpenetration/adhesion artifacts, and an MVEE-based Gaussian attribute reconstruction (FPGO) to maintain rendering continuity at newly exposed fracture surfaces.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap in the physics-to-rendering pipeline: existing Gaussian-splatting simulation frameworks produce visible artifacts (adhesion, broken rendering) precisely when objects fragment, which is the most visually dramatic and commonly requested effect in VFX and virtual prototyping. The momentum-conserving collision force is a clean, physically grounded fix, and the FPGO strategy avoids expensive post-processing. However, significant limitations remain: the method is not real-time (20–80% overhead from fracture tracking), material parameters must be set manually, and the evaluation relies on self-referencing image metrics (PSNR/LPIPS/FID against pre-fracture frames) rather than ground-truth fracture sequences, making quantitative claims harder to verify. Open questions include whether the approach scales to large multi-body scenes, whether material parameters can be inferred automatically, and whether dedicated fracture-specific evaluation metrics can be established.
