## Problem

Differentiable rigid-body simulators produce erratic gradient information—rugged gradients at contact and vanishing gradients at distance—that traps gradient-based optimizers in trivial local minima. The paper identifies the contact model as the root cause and shows that no existing formulation simultaneously guarantees intersection-free behavior, twice-differentiability, non-prehensile forces, and non-vanishing gradients at arbitrary separation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262155/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes triangle-mesh representations of articulated rigid bodies along with their kinematic states (vertex positions and velocities) and a sequence of control signals. It outputs a contact potential with analytically computable first and second derivatives, which drives a position-based dynamics optimizer to produce updated kinematic states. A key design constraint is that the potential must have global support (non-zero at any finite distance) while remaining twice differentiable, and the BSH hierarchy requires a layered (not wrapped) sphere construction to preserve the non-prehensile property.

## Method

The contact potential is built from pairwise separating-hyperplane terms between triangle pairs, using a globally-supported 1/(x)⁺ barrier instead of the locally-supported log-barrier in prior IPC-style models, which ensures non-vanishing gradients at arbitrary distance. To make evaluation tractable, the paper introduces a C² smooth blending operator that transitions from the exact pairwise potential to a closed-form centered potential (12[1+1/√‖x_I−x_J‖]²) as bounding-sphere centers separate. This blending is organized hierarchically over a Bounding Sphere Hierarchy (BSH) with a layered construction, where each internal node's sphere tightly encapsulates its children's spheres, enabling recursive replacement of child-pair sums by a single centered term. The paper proves by induction that all four well-behaved properties (Barrier-Form, Smoothness, Non-prehensile, Non-vanishing) are preserved through blending and hierarchical composition, and shows O(T) evaluation complexity on uniform grids. Friction is handled via a locally-supported variant of the pairwise potential that vanishes beyond a threshold distance, preserving non-vanishing normal gradients while enabling efficient BVH-based rejection.

## Results

The paper's primary evaluation is qualitative: across all five contact-rich tasks (Billiards, Push, Sort, Gather, Ant-Push), the proposed BSH-based contact model is the only method that converges to a solution from a trivial initialization where objects are far apart, while all four baselines (IPC, SDRS, MuJoCo, Gradient Bundle) fail to make progress even with random-sampling initialization. Physics accuracy is validated via a 20-plank book-stacking problem, where the system remains stable for μ < 1e-6 with margin errors in the millimeter range (e.g., 5.67 mm at μ=1e-6) and contact-force errors below 6.4e-4 N. The BSH-accelerated evaluation is substantially faster than brute-force pairwise computation but remains slower than IPC due to the nested 4D optimization per triangle pair.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four baselines: IPC (Li et al. 2020 / Huang et al. 2024), SDRS (Ye et al. 2025), MuJoCo (Tassa et al. 2012, finite-difference gradients), and the Gradient Bundle method (Suh et al. 2022b, randomized smoothing). On the Billiards task, all four baselines produce zero progress from trivial initialization due to vanishing gradients; even with random-sampling initialization they converge slowly or get stuck, while the proposed method converges fastest with or without sampling. On the Push task (receding-horizon, 200-frame trajectory), all baselines make no progress even with random sampling, whereas the proposed method discovers a multi-stage motion (rod moves around the box, then pushes it repeatedly). On Gather and Sort (10 cubes each), other methods make little to no progress while the proposed method completes both tasks using only gradient information. On Ant-Push (9-link, 16-DOF articulated robot), the proposed method again significantly outperforms all baselines. The SDRS baseline is the closest competitor since it satisfies all properties except Non-vanishing, but this single missing property is sufficient to cause complete failure from distant initializations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the IPC (Li et al., 2020) and SDRS (Ye et al., 2025) lines of barrier-based contact models for differentiable rigid-body simulation, and on the Barnes-Hut/Fast Multipole Expansion tradition of hierarchical N-body force evaluation. Its key departure is identifying that the two dominant gradient pathologies in differentiable physics—ruggedness at contact and vanishing at distance—both originate in the contact potential's design rather than in the optimizer or integrator. By replacing the locally-supported log-barrier with a globally-supported 1/x potential and introducing a formal four-property framework (Barrier-Form, Smoothness, Non-prehensile, Non-vanishing), the paper shows that no prior contact model satisfies all four simultaneously, and constructs one that does via separating-hyperplane optimization combined with BSH-based smooth blending.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The non-vanishing gradient property is the practically critical contribution: it enables gradient-based optimizers to discover complex, contact-rich motions from trivial initializations where objects are arbitrarily far apart, a regime where all prior differentiable simulators produce zero gradient and stall. This has direct implications for model-predictive control, robot co-design, and locomotion learning where initialization quality is often poor. Key limitations include restriction to rigid bodies (bounding spheres may not enclose deformed geometry), the nested 4D optimization per triangle pair adding overhead, and validation on a modest set of 2D/3D manipulation tasks. Extending the framework to deformable bodies and reducing the per-pair optimization cost are natural open questions.
