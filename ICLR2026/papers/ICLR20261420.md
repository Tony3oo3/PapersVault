## Problem

Classical Proper Orthogonal Decomposition (POD) provides energy-optimal linear bases for PDE model reduction but cannot capture nonlinear dynamics, while existing deep learning decomposition solvers lack physical grounding and suffer from mode collapse where learned modes become redundant. The paper identifies that POD's variance-maximization criterion is a special case of mutual information maximization under linear-Gaussian assumptions, and asks whether this information-theoretic principle can be generalized to the nonlinear regime to yield a principled, collapse-resistant decomposition framework.

## Input / Output

The method takes a PDE parameter function a(x) (encoding boundary conditions, coefficients, or source terms) and an initial condition field u(μ) defined over a spatial-temporal domain (e.g., 1024×200 for 1D problems, 64²×21 for 2D problems) as input, and produces the solution field Ŷ(μ') at a new parameter state μ'. Internally, the decomposition yields K global nonlinear basis functions {Φ_k} (high-dimensional fields) and their corresponding scalar coefficients {a_k(μ)}, which are evolved in a low-dimensional latent space before being recombined via linear superposition to form the final output.

## Method

OrthoSolver decomposes operator learning into three composed operators: a Basis Decomposition Operator E_θ that sequentially extracts K basis-coefficient pairs by maximizing mutual information between the current residual and the extracted coefficient (using F-FNO for basis extraction and MLP for coefficient extraction), a Solver Operator S_θ that evolves each coefficient to new parameter conditions via dedicated F-FNO networks, and a parameter-free Synthesis Operator D that reconstructs the solution. The MI objective is made tractable via the CLUB contrastive upper bound, and the optimization is reformulated as minimizing MI between the current field and the post-extraction residual. An orthogonality regularization (‖GᵀG − I‖²_F) enforces linear independence of the learned basis to prevent mode collapse, and the four loss terms (MI, reconstruction, orthogonality, prediction) are balanced end-to-end using Dynamic Weight Averaging.

## Results

OrthoSolver achieves the best Relative L2 error on all seven PDEBench datasets, with headline values of 0.0033 (1D Advection), 0.0150 (1D Burgers), 0.0157 (1D NS), 0.001372 (1D DiffSorp), 0.0008 (1D DiffReac), 0.0055 (2D NS), and 0.0172 (2D DiffReac). The largest gains over the next-best method are 39.56% on 2D NS and 27.27% on 1D DiffReac, while the smallest margin is 0.58% on 1D DiffSorp. The model uses only 1.1M parameters and 47.77 MB testing memory, making it substantially lighter than competitors like U-FNO (39.4M params) and U-NO (50.8M params).

## Baselines & Comparisons

OrthoSolver outperforms all 13 baselines on every one of the seven datasets. The closest competitors are Transolver (0.0036 on Advection, 0.001380 on DiffSorp) and F-FNO (0.0038 on Advection, 0.0091 on 2D NS), but OrthoSolver still edges ahead on each. The most dramatic wins are against decomposition-based methods on complex nonlinear problems: on 1D Burgers, OrthoSolver (0.0150) beats Transolver (0.0973) by 85% and Transolver++ (0.2892) by 95%, and on 2D NS it beats U-FNO (0.0130) by 58%. Against monolithic operators, the advantage is more moderate but consistent: FNO (0.0166 on Burgers, 0.0168 on 1D NS) and U-FNO (0.0192 on Burgers, 0.0130 on 2D NS) are the strongest non-decomposition baselines, yet OrthoSolver still leads by 9.6% and 58% respectively. Transformer-based methods (GNOT, Factformer, Erwin, UPT) and wavelet-based MWT are consistently far behind, with GNOT and MWT failing to converge on most datasets (errors >0.5). The paper attributes the large gaps on nonlinear problems (Burgers, NS) to mode collapse in baselines lacking orthogonality constraints, quantified by inter-mode correlation coefficients of 0.75–0.88 in Transolver versus 0.05–0.09 in OrthoSolver.

## Relation to Prior Work

OrthoSolver sits at the intersection of three research threads: classical Proper Orthogonal Decomposition (POD) for model order reduction, Factorized Fourier Neural Operators (F-FNO) as the architectural backbone, and information-theoretic representation learning (specifically the CLUB MI estimator). The key conceptual departure is from prior theoretical work (Chechik et al. 2003, Globerson & Tishby 2003, Burges et al. 2010) that established POD's variance maximization as equivalent to mutual information maximization under linear-Gaussian assumptions but never operationalized this insight. OrthoSolver is the first framework to leverage this equivalence as a design principle, replacing POD's linear variance criterion with a nonlinear MI objective while retaining the sequential residual-extraction structure. It also departs from existing decomposition-based neural solvers (Transolver, LSM) by grounding the decomposition in a physically principled objective and explicitly addressing their mode collapse pathology through an orthogonality constraint.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled information-theoretic justification for decomposition in PDE solvers, bridging a gap between classical reduced-order modeling and modern neural operators. The orthogonality constraint as a theoretically grounded remedy for mode collapse (reducing inter-mode correlation from ~0.78 to ~0.06) is a practical contribution applicable beyond this specific architecture. Open questions include whether the MI-based decomposition scales to 3D problems or industrial-scale geometries, whether the CLUB estimator remains tight in high-dimensional function spaces, and whether the training-time overhead (3727s vs. ~720s for FNO) is acceptable for time-critical applications. The framework also raises the question of whether other classical decomposition criteria (e.g., DMD, HPOD) admit analogous information-theoretic generalizations.
