## Problem

Existing MGT detection benchmarks evaluate detectors on generic, free-form prompts (e.g., 'Write an article about machine learning'), but real-world Wikipedia editors use LLMs for specific, contextually constrained tasks such as summarisation, paragraph continuation, and style transfer. These task-specific MGT instances are linguistically and semantically closer to human text, yet no prior benchmark has systematically assessed detector reliability under such realistic conditions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262603/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each detection instance is a text passage (paragraph, lead section, or sentence) paired with a binary label (0 = human-written, 1 = machine-generated). The generation context is task-specific: paragraph writing conditions on article/section titles plus retrieved web content via RAG; summarisation conditions on the full article body; TST conditions on a biased sentence or paragraph. The benchmark spans 3 languages (English, Portuguese, Vietnamese), 6 generators (GPT-4o, GPT-4o Mini, Gemini 2.0 Flash, DeepSeek, Qwen2.5-7B, Mistral-7B), and 3 tasks with subtasks, yielding 152,910 MGT samples alongside 25,485 human-written references. Detectors output a real-valued score thresholded into a binary prediction.

## Method

TSM-BENCH constructs a multilingual, multi-generator, multi-task benchmark by (1) defining editing tasks grounded in editor surveys, (2) selecting the highest-scoring prompt per task via automatic evaluation (Naive RAG for paragraph writing, one-shot for summarisation, five-shot for TST), and (3) generating MGT with six LLMs of varying sizes. Five experiments then benchmark 12 SOTA detectors across off-the-shelf, zero-shot, and supervised settings, testing out-of-domain generalisation to news and social-review domains, SHAP-based feature analysis, and cross-task transfer. The central empirical finding is a generalisation asymmetry: models fine-tuned on task-specific data generalise well to generic MGT both within and across domains, whereas models fine-tuned on generic data fail to transfer to task-specific data even within the same domain. SHAP analysis reveals that generic-data models overfit to superficial formatting artefacts (e.g., '==' or '#' section markers) rather than transferable linguistic patterns, explaining their strong in-domain but weak out-of-domain performance.

## Results

On TSM-BENCH, supervised detectors (mDeBERTa, XLM-RoBERTa) achieve the highest task-specific detection accuracy, averaging 85.2–90.8% across Introductory Paragraph, Paragraph Continuation, and Summarisation (excluding sentence-level TST), while zero-shot methods peak at 64.7% average accuracy. Off-the-shelf detectors that score >93% on generic MGT drop to 47–73% on task-specific data. Overall, detection accuracy decreases by up to 32% (zero-shot), 20% (off-the-shelf), and 10% (supervised) relative to prior generic-prompt benchmarks. A generalisation asymmetry is confirmed: mDeBERTa fine-tuned on task-specific data reaches 89.7% average accuracy on generic test sets, whereas fine-tuning on generic data yields at most 73.3% on task-specific data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Supervised transformers dominate: mDeBERTa achieves the highest average accuracy (86.4% Intro. Para., 86.6% Para. Cont., 90.7% Summarisation), narrowly outperforming XLM-RoBERTa (84.1%, 85.9%, 91.0%) on paragraph tasks but trailing it on Summarisation by 0.3 pp. Among zero-shot white-box methods, Binoculars leads (61.8% Intro. Para., 64.8% Summarisation), outperforming LLR (53.0%, 54.4%) and FastDetectGPT-WB (58.3%, 62.3%) by 4–11 pp. Among black-box methods, GECScore is the strongest on Introductory Paragraph (69.7%) and Summarisation (68.9%), while BiScope leads on Paragraph Continuation (64.0%); both substantially outperform Revise-Detect (53.3–54.8%) and FastDetectGPT-BB (55.4–61.6%). Off-the-shelf detectors (RADAR, Binoculars, Desklib, e5-small) that score >93% on generic Wikipedia articles collapse to 47–73% on task-specific data, confirming that prior benchmark evaluations overestimate real-world reliability. The paper's central finding is that no zero-shot detector exceeds 64.7% average accuracy, whereas supervised models maintain 79.7–91.8%, establishing a clear performance gap that persists across all three languages and six generators.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the MGT detection benchmarking literature (M4, MAGE, MULTITuDE, MGTBench) by shifting from generic free-form generation prompts to task-specific, context-conditioned generation that mirrors how Wikipedia editors actually use LLMs. It builds directly on the authors' prior WETBench work (Quaremba et al., 2025), which introduced the WikiPS and mWNC datasets, and on the Wikipedia Neutrality Corpus (Pryzant et al., 2020) for the Text Style Transfer task. The key conceptual departure is the formal distinction between generic generation (unconstrained prompts) and task-specific generation (constrained, context-conditioned prompts), which the authors argue produces text that is linguistically and semantically closer to human writing, thereby challenging the detection assumptions embedded in prior benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that detector accuracy drops by 10–40% on task-specific data compared to generic benchmarks suggests that the field has been systematically overestimating the readiness of MGT detectors for deployment on UGC platforms like Wikipedia. The generalisation asymmetry—where task-specific training transfers to generic data but not vice versa—challenges the implicit assumption that generic data is a more 'general' training signal and points toward a concrete recommendation: future detectors should be trained on diverse task-specific data. Open questions include how to optimally combine multiple tasks in training, whether the findings extend beyond the three tasks and three languages studied, and whether the poor cross-task generalisation can be addressed through multi-task learning or architectural changes.
