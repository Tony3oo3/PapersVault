## Problem

Existing transfer-based adversarial attacks are highly brittle: even minor disturbances such as JPEG compression, rotation, or Gaussian blur can completely nullify their effectiveness, especially in targeted settings. Prior robustness-enhancing methods like EOT rely on random disturbance sampling, which is computationally inefficient (requiring exponentially more samples in high dimensions) and does not adapt to the specific adversarial example being generated. The paper asks how to generate adversarial examples that remain effective under diverse, potentially unseen disturbances without prohibitive sampling costs.

## Input / Output

The method takes a clean RGB image (32×32 for CIFAR-10, 224×224 for ImageNet, 112×112 for CelebA face crops), a target class label, and a surrogate model (e.g., Inception-v3), and produces an L∞-bounded adversarial perturbation δ (ε=8/255 for CIFAR-10/CelebA, ε=16/255 for ImageNet) such that x+δ is misclassified into the target class even after additive disturbances (Gaussian blur, JPEG, contrast/brightness shifts) or non-additive disturbances (rotation, resizing, perspective distortion, combined transforms). The perturbation is updated via sign-based gradient steps with a per-pixel clamp, and the method requires N=20 disturbance samples per outer iteration over 100 total iterations.

## Method

IGSA employs an iterative two-step framework: (i) inverse gradient sampling, where each disturbance φ is drawn from a Gaussian prior N(0,σ²) and then refined via a single gradient-ascent step φ̂ = φ + μ·sign(∇_φ C^t(x+δ+φ)) to identify the most disruptive direction in the neighborhood of the current adversarial example; and (ii) disturbance-guided refinement, where δ is updated by gradient descent on the expected loss over the refined disturbances, using a score-function (REINFORCE-style) estimator to avoid second-order derivatives. Theoretically, IGS achieves ~10⁸× higher sampling efficiency than EOT for approximating the most destructive disturbance (Theorem 2), and the iterative procedure implicitly minimizes the Hessian trace of the loss (Theorem 4), which aligns the surrogate model's gradient with the natural data distribution P_D and thereby increases the data likelihood of the resulting adversarial examples—explaining their superior robustness under distribution mismatch and their enhanced transferability via flatter loss landscapes.

## Results

On ImageNet targeted attacks under disturbances, IGSA achieves the highest ASR across nearly all settings: 97.2% on ResNet34 under Gaussian blur (vs. 91.7% for PGD+EOT) and 50.8% under 10° rotation (vs. 22.9% for PGD+EOT). Against adversarially trained defended models (Table 2), IGSA reaches 27.30% targeted ASR on ResNet50(Dense) and 23.90% on ViT(Dense), substantially outperforming the best baselines (TIM at 18.60% and DTA at 11.20%, respectively). In transferability experiments (Table 3), combining IGSA with DIM yields 91.0% ASR on ResNet34 and 89.6% on ViT, surpassing standalone ILPD (86.0%/83.1%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

IGSA outperforms all 15 baselines on the majority of disturbance settings in Table 1. Against the strongest robustness-oriented baseline PGD+EOT, IGSA gains +5.5 pp on ResNet34 GSB (97.2 vs 91.7), +34.5 pp on ResNet34 RT (50.8 vs 22.9), and +4.6 pp on ViT GSB (92.2 vs 87.6). Transfer-based attacks (MI-FGSM, DTA, GRA, PGN, SMI-FGRM) suffer severe degradation under non-additive disturbances (e.g., MI-FGSM drops to 0.0% on ResNet34 RT), while IGSA maintains 50.8%. Against defended models (Table 2), IGSA's targeted ASR of 27.30% on ResNet50(Dense) exceeds the best baseline TIM (18.60%) by 8.7 pp, and 23.90% on ViT(Dense) exceeds DTA (11.20%) by 12.7 pp. In transferability (Table 3), IGSA as a plug-in module boosts DIM from 78.0% to 91.0% on ResNet34 (+13.0 pp) and from 66.4% to 89.6% on ViT (+19.0 pp), surpassing the standalone ILPD baseline (86.0%/83.1%). IGSA's average runtime (0.423 s) is comparable to PGD+EOT (0.461 s) and faster than PGN (0.659 s) and GRA (0.345 s).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the EOT (Expectation over Transformation) framework of Athalye et al. (2018), which improves adversarial robustness by sampling random disturbances during attack optimization. The key conceptual departure is replacing random disturbance sampling with inverse gradient sampling (IGS), where the gradient of the loss with respect to the disturbance itself identifies the most destructive direction, yielding an exponential efficiency gain in high dimensions (Theorem 2). The method also draws on the flat-minima/loss-landscape literature (Ge et al., 2023) by showing that IGS implicitly minimizes the Hessian trace of the loss, which the paper connects to maintaining high data-distribution likelihood of adversarial examples (Theorems 3–4). Unlike prior robustness-oriented attacks (DIM, TIM, BSR) that apply fixed transformations, IGSA adapts the disturbance to each specific sample and surrogate model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a well-recognized practical gap: transfer-based adversarial examples are brittle under even minor real-world disturbances, limiting their applicability in security-critical deployments. The plug-and-play design (IGSA can be layered onto DIM, DTA, SMI-FGRM, ILPD) makes it immediately useful for practitioners building robust black-box attacks. A key open question is whether the fixed mapping function h(φ, x+δ) can be replaced by a learnable module to handle truly adversarial or structured disturbances beyond the Gaussian prior. The theoretical efficiency claim (≈10⁸× over EOT) rests on convexity and uniform-distribution assumptions that may not hold for deep network loss landscapes, and the non-convex extension (Appendix C) is only partially developed.
