## Problem

Task attribution—quantifying how each training task influences downstream performance—is critical for multi-task learning, in-context learning, and multi-objective RL, but the ground-truth leave-one-out retraining is computationally infeasible at scale. Existing efficient surrogates are linear and cannot capture second-order task interactions such as synergy, antagonism, or XOR-type effects, while influence functions require expensive Hessian computations. The paper asks whether a more expressive surrogate can model these nonlinear interactions efficiently without repeated retraining.

## Input / Output

The method takes K training tasks encoded as binary subset vectors, a pretrained model from which logits and gradients are computed once at initialization W_0, a test dataset with a performance metric F(·), and a subset sampling distribution (e.g., Bernoulli with probability p). Rather than retraining on m sampled subsets, the algorithm consumes only the precomputed gradient features projected into a low-dimensional space via Gaussian random projection. The output is a kernel surrogate function g_θ(s) that predicts test performance for any task combination, from which per-task attribution scores are derived by averaging predictions over all sampled subsets containing that task; these scores then drive downstream selections such as top-k demonstrations for ICL or synergistic task groupings for RL.

## Method

The paper first establishes via a second-order Taylor expansion in the task-weighting space that linear surrogate coefficients are approximately equal to influence functions when the Hessian of the performance landscape is small (Proposition 3.1, Corollary 3.2), using the delta method on covariance statistics. To go beyond linearity, it replaces the linear surrogate with an RBF kernel ridge regression in an RKHS, where the kernel k(s^(a), s^(b)) = exp(−γ‖s^(a)−s^(b)‖²) encodes Hamming-distance locality over the {0,1}^K subset space. The key efficiency gain comes from a first-order Taylor approximation of model outputs around the pretrained initialization: f_W(x) ≈ f_{W_0}(x) + ⟨∇f_{W_0}(x), W−W_0⟩, which reduces each subset's training problem to a low-dimensional regularized logistic regression over projected gradient features. Gaussian random projection (Johnson-Lindenstrauss) compresses the d-dimensional gradients to k ≪ d dimensions, making the per-subset regression solvable in seconds on CPU. Empirically, the first-order approximation error stays below 2% relative error across models from small MLPs to 34B-parameter LLMs, and the resulting kernel surrogate achieves 25% higher correlation with LOO ground truth than linear surrogates and influence-function baselines.

## Results

KERNELSM achieves the highest Linear Datamodeling Score (LDS) across all five evaluation domains, ranging from 0.30 (modular addition) to 0.80 (Meta-World MT10), outperforming the strongest baseline (linear surrogate models) by 12–67% relative improvement. On the Coin Flip ICL task, KERNELSM reaches an LDS of 0.54 versus 0.43 for linear surrogates and 0.05 for influence functions. For downstream task selection, KERNELSM yields 40% lower cross-entropy loss in ICL demonstration selection (0.02 on Coin Flip vs. 0.05 for linear surrogates) and 15% higher target rewards in multi-objective RL (18.8 vs. 13.1 on MT10), while maintaining comparable runtime (~1–2 minutes).

## Baselines & Comparisons

KERNELSM outperforms all six baselines on every domain where they are comparable. Against influence functions (Koh & Liang, 2017), the gap is largest on modular arithmetic (0.30 vs. 0.03 on addition; 0.52 vs. 0.01 on quadratic) and ICL (0.54 vs. 0.05 on Coin Flip), reflecting the inability of first-order Hessian-based estimates to capture nonlinear task interactions. TracIn (Pruthi et al., 2020) and TRAK (Park et al., 2023) both trail KERNELSM by 12–42% relative LDS across all five domains, with the largest deficits on modular addition (0.30 vs. 0.17/0.14) and Coin Flip (0.54 vs. 0.32/0.23). SOURCE (Bae et al., 2024) is only evaluated on modular arithmetic and RL, where KERNELSM leads by 36% (0.30 vs. 0.22) and 196% (0.80 vs. 0.27) respectively. BIF (Kreer et al., 2025) is competitive on modular quadratic (0.42 vs. 0.52) but lags significantly on ICL (0.28 vs. 0.54 on Coin Flip) and RL (0.22 vs. 0.80). The closest competitor is the linear surrogate model (Li et al., 2023), which KERNELSM surpasses by 67% on modular addition (0.30 vs. 0.18), 18% on modular quadratic (0.52 vs. 0.44), 12% on SST-2 (0.37 vs. 0.33), 26% on Coin Flip (0.54 vs. 0.43), and 5% on Meta-World (0.80 vs. 0.76), confirming that the kernel's ability to model second-order interactions provides the largest gains in the most nonlinear settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate lines of data attribution research—linear surrogate models (datamodels, Li et al. 2023; Ilyas et al. 2022) and influence functions (Koh & Liang, 2017)—by showing via a second-order Taylor expansion that linear surrogate coefficients approximate influence functions up to third-order errors (Proposition 3.1, Corollary 3.2). It then departs from both by replacing the linear surrogate with an RBF kernel ridge regression model to capture second-order task interactions (synergy, antagonism, XOR-type effects) that neither prior approach can represent. The efficient estimation procedure builds on the first-order Taylor approximation of model outputs around initialization (Li et al., 2024b; Malladi et al., 2023) combined with Johnson-Lindenstrauss random projection, extending these tools from sample-level to task-level attribution without repeated retraining.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Task attribution is a prerequisite for practical multi-task learning, demonstration selection in ICL, and synergistic task grouping in RL, yet prior methods either require expensive retraining (LOO) or miss nonlinear interactions (linear surrogates, influence functions). The 25% correlation improvement over LOO ground truth and 40% downstream task-selection gain, achieved without retraining, make accurate attribution tractable at scale. A key limitation is that evaluation focuses on prediction tasks; the authors explicitly note that extending the framework to planning abilities and understanding SFT-vs-RL generalization gaps remains open. The Hessian regularization experiment also reveals a practical caveat: linear surrogates degrade sharply under standard SGD as the Hessian trace grows, suggesting kernel surrogates are not merely more expressive but more robust to training dynamics.
