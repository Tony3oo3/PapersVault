## Problem

VLMs frequently produce incorrect VQA answers even when the correct visual evidence is present in the image, yet it remains unclear whether these failures stem from an inability to perceive the evidence or from a failure to leverage it during reasoning. This paper systematically probes the internal attention dynamics of VLMs to disentangle perception from utilization, revealing a widespread 'seeing but not believing' phenomenon where deep layers reliably attend to correct evidence regions even when the final answer is wrong.

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image and a text VQA question as input. It performs only a single-token forward pass (not full answer generation) to extract attention maps from pre-identified visual-grounding layers, then constructs a patch-level evidence score vector that is reshaped into a 2D grid, denoised, and Gaussian-smoothed to pixel resolution. The output is an augmented image where evidence regions are preserved at full brightness while non-evidence regions are darkened (controlled by α=0.5), which is fed back into the VLM along with a modified prompt instructing it to focus on highlighted regions.

## Method

VEA (Visual Evidence Augmentation) is a training-free, inference-time intervention that makes the model's own deep-layer visual grounding explicit by overlaying it onto the input image. First, a one-time profiling step uses a 100-sample VisualCOT diagnostic subset to compute per-layer AUROC against ground-truth evidence bounding boxes, selecting the top 10% of layers as visual-grounding layers (L_VG). At inference, a single-token forward pass extracts and averages attention over L_VG to produce a patch-level evidence score vector. A neighborhood-based denoising step suppresses attention-sink artifacts by replacing any patch whose score exceeds 10× its 3×3 neighbors with the local average, and a Gaussian smoothing step (σ = 0.5 × shorter image side) ensures spatial coherence. The final mask is blended with the original RGB image via element-wise scaling (α + (1−α)·ē), preserving evidence pixels while darkening irrelevant regions, and the augmented image is paired with a prompt that explicitly directs the model to use words from the highlighted region.

## Results

VEA achieves the best average rank (1.12 for Exact Match, 1.22 for Token F1) across 8 VLMs from 4 families (LLaVA-Next, Qwen2.5-VL, Gemma3, InternVL3.5) on four evidence-based VQA tasks. Compared to base models, VEA delivers an average improvement of +5.67 EM (up to +11.1 on LLaVA-7B) and +6.83 Token F1 (up to +17.3 on LLaVA-7B). On evidence attribution quality, VEA attains the highest AUROC (83.6–85.2) and NDCG@all (58.4–68.6) across all models, outperforming static layer-selection baselines and GradCAM-based AGLA. Gains are most pronounced on smaller models (LLaVA-7B, Gemma-4B), suggesting VEA is particularly effective at compensating for weaker visual grounding in compact architectures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VEA consistently outperforms all four baselines (INST, CGR, VAR, AGLA) on both EM and Token F1 across every model family and scale. The strongest baseline is AGLA (GradCAM-based masking with ensemble), which achieves an average rank of 2.50 (EM) and 2.31 (F1), but VEA still leads by a clear margin (rank 1.12 / 1.22). CGR (two-step captioning-then-answering) and VAR (final-layer attention binary mask) occupy the middle tier with average ranks around 3.1–3.4, while simple instruction prompting (INST) provides negligible or even negative gains (e.g., −0.6 EM on Gemma-4B, −0.5 EM on InternVL-14B). The largest VEA advantage over AGLA appears on smaller models: on LLaVA-7B, VEA gains +3.2 EM and +5.5 F1 over AGLA, and on Gemma-4B it gains +0.8 EM and +1.9 F1. On evidence attribution quality (Table 2), VEA also dominates, achieving AUROC of 83.6–85.2 versus AGLA's 77.7–81.1 and VAR's 51.2–75.2, confirming that the adaptive per-model layer profiling yields more precise evidence localization than static layer spans or gradient-based saliency.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes insights from two research threads: (1) the growing body of work documenting VLMs' tendency to underutilize visual evidence in favor of linguistic priors (Tong et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2025; Kang-il et al. 2024), and (2) inference-time visual grounding interventions such as attention-based masking (VAR, Liu et al. 2025), GradCAM-based saliency (AGLA, An et al. 2025), and two-step captioning (CGR, Liu et al. 2025). It departs from these by showing that the bottleneck is not perceptual failure but a 'seeing but not believing' disconnect—deep layers already localize correct evidence even on wrong answers—and by using model-specific profiling to select intermediate deep layers (rather than final-layer attention or gradients) as the highlighting signal, with a denoising step that explicitly targets the attention-sink artifact (Kang et al. 2025; Darcet et al. 2024). The RAG context under-utilization literature (Garima et al. 2024; Fei et al. 2024) provides the conceptual framing that treating the image as 'retrieved context' whose salient parts need emphasis is a natural intervention.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 'seeing but not believing' diagnostic reframes VLM failure analysis from a perception problem to a reasoning/integration problem, suggesting that internal representations already contain the needed signal and that lightweight inference-time interventions can bridge the gap without retraining. The method's training-free, architecture-agnostic nature (validated across LLaVA, Qwen, Gemma, InternVL at multiple scales) makes it immediately deployable, and the strong robustness under noise/corruption (+16–26 points under extreme perturbations) hints at broader utility in low-quality visual settings. Key limitations include the requirement for internal attention access (ruling out API-only models), the relatively small gains from multi-round self-validation (suggesting current VLMs lack confidence calibration for productive reflection), and the fact that attention-based attribution, while strong, is not a perfect proxy for true evidence localization. Open questions include whether attention-guided cropping or zooming can extend this to agentic multi-step visual reasoning, and whether the approach transfers to tasks where evidence is distributed rather than localized.
