## Problem

In skill-based RL, mutual-information objectives that promote skill diversity tend to induce premature specialization and reduce exploration, while entropy-based exploration objectives sacrifice skill distinguishability and limit downstream utility. Existing methods that attempt to combine both (e.g., CeSD, ComSD) rely on ad-hoc weighting or heuristic regularization without a principled mechanism to resolve the resulting gradient conflicts, leading to suboptimal trade-offs between coverage and separation.

## Input / Output

During pretraining, the agent receives a raw environment observation (2D position in mazes; 24-dim, 78-dim, or 55-dim state vectors in URLB's Walker, Quadruped, and Jaco domains) concatenated with a uniformly sampled one-hot skill vector (16 skills by default), and outputs a continuous action via a DDPG policy. During fine-tuning, the skill vector is no longer random: a SAC-based selector network maps the observation to a discrete distribution over skills, and the policy conditioned on the selected skill produces the action. The skill selector uses an ε-greedy strategy with ε decaying from 1.0 to 0.01 over 20,000 steps, becoming fully greedy at evaluation.

## Method

AMPED formulates exploration and skill diversity as a multi-objective RL problem and resolves their gradient conflicts via PCGrad: at each update, if the exploration and diversity gradients have a negative inner product, one is randomly projected onto the orthogonal complement of the other (with environment-specific probability p) before summing. The exploration reward combines a particle-based entropy estimator (from CIC) with Random Network Distillation, while the diversity reward uses AnInfoNCE, an anisotropic contrastive objective that explicitly pushes apart skill-induced state distributions. A theoretical result (Theorem 1) shows that greater minimum pairwise skill separation δ reduces the sample complexity of a greedy skill selector as O(1/(δ−2ε)²). During fine-tuning, a jointly trained SAC skill selector adaptively picks the skill best aligned with the downstream task, replacing the uniform random sampling used by prior SBRL methods.

## Results

On the URLB benchmark (12 downstream tasks across Walker, Quadruped, and Jaco domains), AMPED achieves the highest median, IQM, arithmetic mean, and smallest optimality gap among all methods, using IQM as the primary metric. Specifically, AMPED surpasses BeCL by 17.96%, CIC by 15.02%, APT by 9.73%, CeSD by 20.91%, and ComSD by 35.01% in IQM. In the Tree Maze qualitative evaluation, AMPED is the only method that simultaneously achieves full state coverage and clearly separated skill regions, whereas DIAYN/BeCL leave coverage gaps and CIC/ComSD produce overlapping trajectories.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AMPED outperforms all seven baselines on URLB IQM. The largest margin is against ComSD (+35.01%), which uses a heuristic skill-based multi-objective weighting (SMW) that the paper argues lacks theoretical justification; CeSD (+20.91%) is also substantially beaten, with the paper attributing CeSD's weakness to its diversity regularizer saturating once skill supports become disjoint. Among skill-differentiation methods, BeCL (+17.96%) is the closest competitor but sacrifices state coverage for separation. Entropy-focused methods CIC (+15.02%) and APT (+9.73%) achieve good coverage but produce poorly disentangled skills. RND, used as a pure exploration baseline, is also outperformed. In the Tree Maze, AMPED uniquely combines full coverage with distinct skill boundaries, while DIAYN and BeCL leave gaps, CIC produces entangled trajectories, and ComSD shows more region mixing than AMPED.

## Relation to Prior Work

AMPED positions itself within the unsupervised skill discovery (URL) literature, building directly on DIAYN's MI-based diversity objective, CIC's particle-based entropy estimator, and BeCL's contrastive MI formulation. Its key departure is reframing the exploration–diversity tension as a multi-objective RL problem and resolving it with PCGrad-style gradient surgery (Yu et al., 2020), a technique previously applied to multi-task learning but not to SBRL. The paper also introduces AnInfoNCE (Rusak et al., 2024) as a skill-diversity estimator—previously unused in this context—and a SAC-based skill selector for adaptive fine-tuning, distinguishing it from prior methods (CeSD, ComSD) that combined exploration and diversity through heuristic weighting or clustering-based penalties without a principled conflict-resolution mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical result (Theorem 1) providing a clean sample-complexity bound linking skill diversity to greedy skill-selection accuracy gives the field a formal justification for why diversity matters beyond empirical observation, and the near-universal gradient conflict ratio (~99.97% in Walker/Quadruped) strongly validates the multi-objective framing. However, the skill selector—a central component—underperforms single-skill fine-tuning in several tasks (Table 4), revealing that adaptive selection remains an open problem; the paper itself flags the need for more robust selector training strategies. Broader implications include the potential transfer of gradient-projection techniques to other multi-signal learning settings, and the observation that increasing skill count does not monotonically improve diversity, pointing to open questions about principled skill-dimension selection.
