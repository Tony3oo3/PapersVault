## Problem

While RLVR has substantially improved LLM reasoning, the token-level mechanisms underlying these gains remain unclear. Existing analyses focus on aggregate metrics (accuracy, reward, response length) or coarse token-level entropy, leaving open how distributional shifts are structured across positions and contexts, how probability mass is reallocated across candidate tokens, and which specific token-level changes are actually responsible for downstream reasoning improvements.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264217/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis takes a base LLM and its RLVR-finetuned counterpart (e.g., Qwen2.5-32B with DAPO or SimpleRL) and evaluates them on math reasoning prompts (AIME 2024/2025, AMC, GPQA-Diamond). At each token position along RL-generated trajectories, the paper computes JS divergence between the two models' next-token distributions using top-p truncated distributions for divergence but full distributions for entropy, and conducts cross-sampling interventions that sequentially swap token choices at positions exceeding a divergence threshold under a fixed intervention budget. Outputs include divergence percentile curves, positional concentration profiles, entropy-divergence relationships, top-k overlap and rank-shift statistics, cross-sampling accuracy curves as a function of intervention count, and a divergence-weighted fine-tuned model.

## Method

The paper develops a three-part empirical framework: (1) characterizing the structure of token-level distributional shifts via JS divergence, entropy, positional concentration, and semantic identity analyses; (2) conducting forward and reverse cross-sampling interventions that selectively swap tokens between base and RL policies at positions exceeding a divergence threshold, using a sequential intervention budget to probe both the sufficiency (forward: inject RL tokens into base generations) and necessity (reverse: replace RL tokens with base tokens) of RL-induced changes; and (3) analyzing fine-grained mechanics including top-k candidate overlap, rank reordering, low-probability token promotion, and training-evolution dynamics. The central finding is that RLVR acts as a sparse, targeted refinement: over 83–98% of token distributions remain near-identical, while a small subset of high-divergence positions carries disproportionate functional importance, with fewer than 40 token substitutions sufficient to recover or erase RL-level performance. At high-divergence positions, RLVR primarily reallocates probability mass within the base model's existing top-k candidate set rather than introducing fundamentally new tokens. As a diagnostic intervention, the paper also explores divergence-weighted advantages that scale per-token learning signals by KL divergence from the old policy via a sigmoid weighting scheme, showing modest improvements over the DAPO baseline.

## Results

RLVR induces highly sparse token-level distributional shifts: under DAPO, over 83% of token positions exhibit near-zero JS divergence, while under SimpleRL this exceeds 98%. Cross-sampling experiments confirm functional criticality of these sparse shifts—injecting fewer than 4% RL-sampled tokens (≈38 effective substitutions) into base-model generations recovers SimpleRL-level accuracy on AIME 2024 (8.23% → >25%), while reverting ~5% of RL tokens collapses performance back to base levels. The divergence-weighted advantage variant improves over baseline DAPO on Qwen2.5-Math-7B, raising overall average accuracy from 42.48% to 45.05% (high-KL boost) across AIME 2024, AIME 2025, and AMC.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper analyzes two RLVR methods—DAPO and SimpleRL—as the primary comparison axes. DAPO (with clip-higher and no KL penalty) exhibits a broader divergence distribution and requires more cross-sampled tokens to recover or erase performance (7.8% forward / 10.1% reverse on AIME 2024) compared to SimpleRL (3.86% / 5.0%), reflecting DAPO's stronger fine-tuned performance (>44% vs. >25% on AIME 2024). Both methods confirm the sparsity thesis, but DAPO modifies even initially low-entropy (confident) base-model predictions, whereas SimpleRL concentrates divergence in higher-entropy regions. Against supervised fine-tuning (SFT), RLVR produces substantially sparser shifts: SFT exhibits a larger high-divergence token set, lower top-k overlap with the base model, and broader positional divergence across the entire response. The divergence-weighted advantage variant (high-KL boost) improves over baseline DAPO by +2.5 points on AIME 2024 (36.74 vs. 33.61), +1.25 on AIME 2025 (20.00 vs. 18.75), and +3.3 on AMC (78.40 vs. 75.08), with the low-KL boost yielding comparable gains (+2.29, +1.15, +3.89 respectively).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the growing line of work analyzing RLVR's effects on LLMs—prior studies have examined token-level entropy (Wang et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2025), parameter-level sparsity (Mukherjee et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025), and aggregate KL divergence (Rajani et al., 2025; Huan et al., 2025)—by providing the first systematic token-level distributional analysis using JS divergence and, crucially, connecting those shifts to functional performance via cross-sampling interventions. The cross-sampling framework draws conceptual inspiration from speculative decoding and BiLD (Leviathan et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2023), but repurposes the two-policy generation setup as a diagnostic tool rather than an inference acceleration method. The paper also departs from the 'scalpel vs. hammer' framing by showing that RLVR's sparsity is not merely a smaller-magnitude version of SFT's changes but a qualitatively different, more targeted form of refinement.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that fewer than 40 token substitutions per response (under 4% of tokens) suffice to recover or erase RLVR's performance gains provides a concrete, mechanistic account of why RLVR works and where its gains are concentrated, which could inform more targeted training objectives and diagnostics. The divergence-weighted advantage experiments offer a practical, if preliminary, path toward exploiting this sparsity during training, though the authors note that stabilizing such approaches may require model-specific tuning. Open questions include whether the sparse critical-token set can be predicted a priori (enabling cheaper inference or targeted fine-tuning), whether findings generalize beyond mathematical reasoning to broader domains, and whether the cross-sampling methodology can be adapted for other post-training paradigms such as DPO or process-reward models.
