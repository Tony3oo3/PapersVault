## Problem

Simulation-based inference (SBI) with neural posterior estimation requires large numbers of high-fidelity simulations to achieve reliable posterior estimates, making it impractical for computationally expensive simulators. While multifidelity modeling has been explored in Bayesian optimization and Monte Carlo settings, its potential for reducing simulation budgets in neural density estimation for SBI had not been realized. This paper asks whether transfer learning from cheap low-fidelity simulators can dramatically reduce the number of expensive high-fidelity simulations needed for accurate posterior inference.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a prior distribution p(θ), a cheap low-fidelity simulator p_L(x_L|θ) (e.g., a single-compartment neuron model or mean-field approximation), a costly high-fidelity simulator p(x|θ) (e.g., an 8-compartment neuron model or 5120-neuron spiking network), and a single empirical observation x_o. Both simulators must share the same observation domain, and the low-fidelity parameter space must be a subset of the high-fidelity one (extra high-fidelity parameters are treated as dummy variables during pre-training). The output is an amortized or non-amortized posterior density q_φ(θ|x_o) parameterized by Neural Spline Flows, with observations summarized via hand-crafted statistics or CNN embeddings depending on the task (time series, images, or spiking activity).

## Method

MF-NPE pre-trains a Neural Spline Flow density estimator on a large set of low-fidelity (θ, x_L) pairs, then fine-tunes the same network on a much smaller set of high-fidelity (θ, x) pairs, leveraging the learned feature representations to reduce high-fidelity sample complexity. The sequential variant MF-TSNPE extends this by iteratively drawing high-fidelity parameters from a truncated prior within the current posterior support, and MF-TSNPE-AF further augments each round with active samples selected by an acquisition function that maximizes the epistemic uncertainty (sample variance across a 5-network ensemble) of the posterior estimate. The key mechanism is that low-fidelity pre-training aligns the density estimator's feature space with the high-fidelity task, so that only a fraction of high-fidelity simulations are needed to refine the posterior. The approach requires up to two orders of magnitude fewer high-fidelity simulations than standard NPE or TSNPE while maintaining comparable posterior accuracy, as validated on four statistical benchmarks and two neuroscience simulators (multicompartmental neuron and recurrent spiking network with synaptic plasticity).

## Results

Across four benchmark tasks (SIR, SLCP, OU process, Gaussian Blob), MF-NPE and MF-TSNPE(-AF) consistently outperform NPE and TSNPE, with the largest gains at low high-fidelity simulation budgets (50–10³ simulations). On the multicompartmental L5PC neuron model, MF-(TS)NPE achieves comparable posterior accuracy to NPE with a total computational cost 4.44 ± 0.06× lower. On the recurrent spiking network (4096 E / 1024 I neurons, 24 plasticity parameters), MF-NPE improves NLTP over NPE and increases the proportion of posterior samples within target firing-rate bounds (1–50 Hz) by almost 30%. Overall, the method requires up to two orders of magnitude fewer high-fidelity simulations than standard NPE to reach comparable posterior quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against **NPE** (Greenberg et al., 2019), MF-NPE consistently achieves better C2ST/MMD on all four benchmarks at equal or lower HF budgets, with the gap largest at 50–10³ HF simulations; on the L5PC neuron model, MF-NPE matches NPE's NLTP/NRMSE while using 4.44× less total compute, and on the spiking network it yields better NLTP and ~30% more posterior samples within physiologically plausible firing-rate bounds. Against **TSNPE** (Deistler et al., 2022), MF-TSNPE outperforms TSNPE particularly in low-HF-budget regimes, and the acquisition-function variant MF-TSNPE-AF further improves on the OU process (though gains on SIR/SLCP are not significant). Against **MF-ABC** (Prescott & Baker, 2020), MF-NPE shows substantially higher C2ST/MMD performance across the OU process variants; the authors note that MF-ABC may benefit from more extensive hyperparameter tuning but that neural density approaches scale better to complex problems. In all comparisons, the multifidelity methods require up to two orders of magnitude fewer high-fidelity simulations to reach comparable posterior quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Neural Posterior Estimation (NPE) and its sequential variant TSNPE by introducing a multifidelity pre-training step via transfer learning, drawing on the multifidelity modeling tradition from Monte Carlo estimation, Bayesian optimization, and ABC (MF-ABC). The key conceptual move is repurposing transfer learning—previously applied to surrogate modeling and physics-informed networks but not systematically to SBI—as a mechanism to reduce high-fidelity simulation budgets. The sequential variant MF-TSNPE-AF further integrates epistemic-uncertainty-based acquisition functions from active SBI (Lueckmann et al., 2019) to adaptively select high-fidelity parameters. The closest prior multifidelity SBI method, MF-ABC (Prescott & Baker, 2020), uses rejection-based sampling and inherits ABC's known scalability limitations in high-dimensional spaces.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By reducing high-fidelity simulation requirements by up to two orders of magnitude, MF-(TS)NPE makes SBI tractable for simulators where even a single run takes minutes (e.g., multicompartmental neurons, large spiking networks), opening the door to Bayesian inference on previously infeasible scientific models. Key limitations include the dependence on low-high fidelity similarity (which domain experts can often assess a priori), inherited scalability constraints of normalizing flows in high-dimensional parameter spaces, and the ensemble training cost of the acquisition-function variant. Open questions include optimal allocation of low- and high-fidelity simulation budgets under fixed compute, extension to more expressive density estimators (e.g., diffusion models), and the development of standardized multifidelity SBI benchmarks.
