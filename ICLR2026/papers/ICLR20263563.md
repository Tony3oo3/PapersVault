## Problem

Existing network dismantling methods rely on global structural knowledge (e.g., all-pairs shortest paths for betweenness centrality), exhibit prohibitive running times on large networks, and overlook the network's latent geometry—a hidden geometric manifold known to govern the dynamics of complex systems. The paper asks whether latent geometry, estimated via simple local rules, can guide effective dismantling and whether such local estimators can match the performance of global methods like Node Betweenness Centrality while offering orders-of-magnitude speedups.

## Input / Output

The method takes an unweighted, undirected network (represented as an adjacency matrix) with no isolated components and produces a sequential node removal order that fragments the network until the largest connected component (LCC) drops below a 10% threshold. Edge weights are computed via local network automata rules (requiring only first-neighbor connectivity), and node strengths are the sum of incident edge weights; the process is dynamic, meaning all scores are recomputed after each removal. An optional reinsertion step can reduce the total number of physical removals. GPU acceleration reformulates the per-step computation as matrix operations (common-neighbor matrix A∘A²), enabling efficient scaling to networks with up to 23,000 nodes and 507,000 edges.

## Method

LGD-NA is a parameter-free framework that estimates a network's latent geometry using local network automata rules to assign dissimilarity weights to edges, then computes each node's strength as the sum of its incident edge weights; the highest-strength node is removed iteratively (dynamic dismantling). The key insight is that the Common Neighbor Dissimilarity (CND) rule, ν_ij = 1/(1+CN_ij)—merely the denominator of the Repulsion-Attraction RA2 rule—achieves near-state-of-the-art dismantling performance using only the count of shared neighbors between linked nodes, with no free parameters or training. The paper validates this on an ATLAS of 1,475 real-world networks across 32 domains, showing LGD-NA outperforms all non-latent-geometry methods including GNNs and spectral Laplacian approaches, with only NBC (a global latent-geometry estimator) ranking higher. GPU acceleration via Hadamard-product matrix operations yields a 130× speedup over CPU for RA2 and 63× over CPU-based NBC on the largest networks. The explainability of CND further enables a robustness-engineering strategy: adding links between adjacent nodes of the most critical nodes increases network resilience by 36–259%.

## Results

Across 1,475 real-world networks spanning 32 complex systems domains, NBC (Node Betweenness Centrality) achieves the best mean-field dismantling rank in every field, while the local LGD-NA methods (CND, RA2, RA2_num) consistently rank second in the majority of domains and outperform all non-latent-geometry baselines including GDM, GND, and CoreGDM. GPU-accelerated RA2 runs 130× faster than its CPU counterpart and 63× faster than CPU-based NBC on the largest network (23,000 nodes, 507,000 edges). The CND-based robustness engineering strategy increases network AUC by 36–95% with only 1% added links and by 59–259% with 10% added links, with domain-specific functional gains up to 363% (e.g., in the Drosophila Connectome and Terrorist Cell).

## Baselines & Comparisons

LGD-NA (specifically CND and RA2) outperforms all machine-learning-based dismantlers (GDM, CoreGDM) and spectral Laplacian-based methods (GND) across the mean-field ranking in 29 of 32 domains, losing the second-place slot only to Fitness Centrality (Foodweb), GDM (Infrastructure), and GND (Social). Against the global topological baseline NBC, LGD-NA methods are consistently second-best but never surpass it; however, CND matches NBC's efficacy while being orders of magnitude faster (63× speedup on GPU). Compared to other local methods like Collective Influence and CoreHD, LGD-NA achieves substantially better mean-field ranks, confirming that latent-geometry-driven local rules dominate purely topological local heuristics. Against centrality measures (Eigenvector, PageRank, Domirank, Fitness, Resilience), LGD-NA methods rank higher in the majority of fields, with CND specifically outperforming all of them in Internet, Biomolecular, Brain, and Covert domains. The GPU-accelerated implementation makes LGD-NA the only viable option for large-scale dismantling (>1,000 nodes), where NBC's O(Nm) per-step cost becomes prohibitive.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Repulsion-Attraction network automata rules (RA2) introduced by Muscoloni et al. (2017) for hyperbolic network embedding into a dismantling framework, making the conceptual leap from geometry estimation for embedding/community detection to geometry estimation for targeted node removal. It departs from the dominant dismantling paradigm—which relies on global shortest-path measures (NBC), spectral partitioning (GND), or trained GNNs (GDM)—by showing that purely local, parameter-free topological rules can approximate the same latent geometric distances that global methods capture. The CND rule, a single-term ablation of RA2 retaining only the common-neighbor denominator, is the paper's key methodological contribution, reducing the estimator to its simplest geometrically meaningful component.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that CND (1/(1+CN_ij)) nearly matches NBC across 1,475 networks and 32 domains while being orders of magnitude faster (and GPU-accelerable) provides a practical, interpretable alternative to the field's gold standard for large-scale dismantling. The ATLAS benchmark itself is a major resource, expanding the tested network collection from at most 57 (prior SOTA) to 1,475, and the constructive robustness-engineering application (adding links between neighbors of critical nodes) shifts the narrative from pure attack to defense. Open questions include whether the latent-geometry principle extends to directed, weighted, or temporal networks, whether it generalizes to link-level dismantling, and whether the geometric intuition holds for networks lacking clear latent structure (e.g., highly random or lattice-like topologies).
