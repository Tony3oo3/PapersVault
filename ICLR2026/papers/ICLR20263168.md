## Problem

Expressive performance rendering (EPR) and automatic piano transcription (APT) are inherently inverse tasks—mapping between symbolic scores and expressive performances—yet prior work has addressed them independently, with EPR systems relying on note-level alignment and explicit composer/performer labels that limit scalability and accessibility. No existing framework jointly models both directions while disentangling note-level score content from global performance style, nor does one automate style selection from the score alone.

## Input / Output

The model consumes two types of note-level sequences of approximately equal length: symbolic scores (8 discrete attributes per note including onset-in-bar, IOI, pitch, note value, measure length, hand/staff, trill, and grace) and expressive performance MIDI (4 attributes: pitch, IOI, duration, velocity). For EPR output, the model generates structured MidiTok token sequences (pitch, duration, velocity, and time-shift events) rather than the same 4-attribute format used at input, while APT output extends to 12 attributes including notation-specific cues like stem direction, accidentals, and voice number. A separate PSR module outputs a single global style embedding vector conditioned on score content, which is then broadcast-added to the content representation to condition the performance decoder.

## Method

The core architecture is a unified transformer-based Seq2Seq model with five components (Score Encoder, Performance Encoder, Style Encoder, Score Decoder, Performance Decoder), each a 6-layer, 8-head transformer with rotary positional encodings, pre-layer norm, and SwiGLU activations (d=512). Disentanglement is enforced architecturally: content is represented as a sequence of note-level latent vectors (z_x, z_y), while style is a single global vector (z_s) extracted via a BERT-style [CLS] token, regularized with a KL penalty toward a standard Gaussian prior. Four tasks are trained jointly—EPR, APT, and masked reconstruction of both score and performance from unpaired data—using only sequence-aligned supervision without note-level alignment. An independent DDPM-based PSR module, trained separately with velocity prediction, learns to generate diverse style embeddings from a frozen score content encoder, mimicking a pianist's ability to infer appropriate expressive style from a written score. The model is trained on 967 paired ASAP performances plus 75,913 unpaired MusicXML scores and transcribed YouTube piano covers, with input dropout (0.75 mask rate for APT decoder, 0.2 for EPR decoder) to encourage long-range structural learning.

## Results

On the ASAP dataset, the proposed joint model achieves competitive APT performance with a MUSTER average error of 12.48 (vs. 14.10 for the end-to-end baseline Beyer & Dai, 2024) and a ScoreSimilarity pitch-spelling error of 6.24 (vs. 14.31), while requiring no note-level alignment. For EPR, Ours(Target) achieves the lowest KL divergence for duration (5.51) and velocity (1.76) among all baselines, and Ours(PSR) attains the highest alignment rate (92.27%) and lowest insertion rate (3.77%) in the accuracy evaluation. Subjective listening tests confirm that Ours(Target) receives the highest ratings across dynamics, tempo, style, and overall human-likeness, with Ours(PSR) closely following.

## Baselines & Comparisons

For APT, the proposed model outperforms the strongest end-to-end baseline (Beyer & Dai, 2024) on 8 of 12 metrics, most notably reducing onset deviation (16.26 vs. 17.48), offset deviation (27.30 vs. 32.92), and pitch-spelling error (6.24 vs. 14.31), while slightly trailing on pitch edit distance (3.08 vs. 2.73) and missing-note rate. Against note-aligned methods (Liu et al., 2022; Shibata et al., 2021), the model achieves substantially lower onset/offset errors (16.26/27.30 vs. 68.28/54.11 and 25.02/29.21) at the cost of marginally higher pitch errors, reflecting the trade-off of alignment-free training. For EPR, Ours(Target) achieves the lowest KL divergence for both duration (5.51) and velocity (1.76) and the lowest MAE for duration (0.37) and velocity (10.33), outperforming DExter (KL_D=1.48 but MAE_D=0.88, KL_V=2.32, MAE_V=24.27) and VirtuosoNet (KL_D=5.72, MAE_D=0.48, KL_V=4.91, MAE_V=14.40). Ours(PSR) achieves the best alignment rate (92.27%) and lowest insertion rate (3.77%) in the accuracy evaluation, surpassing both DExter (91.27%, 5.11%) and VirtuosoNet (91.88%, 4.23%). The EPR-Only ablation (trained without APT or unpaired data) is consistently worse than the full joint model, confirming that multi-task learning and unpaired data improve EPR quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate MIR tasks—expressive performance rendering (EPR) and automatic piano transcription (APT)—into a single transformer-based Seq2Seq framework with disentangled content and style latents. It builds directly on Beyer & Dai's (2024) alignment-free Seq2Seq formulation for APT, extends it to EPR, and borrows the masked-autoencoder objective from He et al. (2022) to incorporate unpaired data. The PSR module repurposes DDPM (Ho et al., 2020) with velocity prediction (Salimans & Ho, 2022) to generate style embeddings from score content, while the performance output representation follows Huang & Yang's (2020) MidiTok tokenization. Conceptually, the paper departs from prior EPR work (VirtuosoNet, DExter, ScorePerformer) by eliminating the need for note-level alignment and composer/performer labels, and from prior disentangled music representation work (Zhang & Dixon, 2023) by targeting score-to-performance generation rather than performance-to-performance analysis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The alignment-free Seq2Seq formulation for EPR removes a significant data-preprocessing bottleneck (note-level alignment tools) that has constrained the scalability of expressive rendering research, and the PSR module makes style-aware rendering accessible to non-expert users who cannot specify expressive parameters manually. The joint EPR+APT formulation with mutual supervision is a clean architectural idea that could generalize to other inverse music tasks. However, the model's 188M parameter count (nearly 6× the APT baseline), its restriction to classical piano, and the 10-second EPR inference time limit immediate practical adoption. Open questions include whether the disentangled content/style split holds for genres with improvisation (jazz, pop), whether the PSR module generalizes beyond the four historical eras tested, and whether the heavy reliance on unpaired data (75K+ scores, YouTube transcriptions) introduces distributional biases that affect OOD performance.
