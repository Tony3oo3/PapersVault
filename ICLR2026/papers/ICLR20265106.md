## Problem

Human-centered assessment tasks (e.g., estimating worker workload, evaluating paper quality) require standardized scores when ground truth is costly, unobservable, or only available in the future. Existing approaches face a dilemma: human-only methods suffer from heterogeneous expertise and inconsistent rating scales, while model-only methods must learn from noisy proxy labels or incomplete features. No prior framework principledly combines the ordinal reliability of human comparisons with the metric consistency of model predictions.

## Input / Output

The method takes two inputs: (1) pairwise comparative judgments (i ≻ j) from m annotators over n items, where each annotator may judge different subsets, and (2) a model-generated score vector s_p ∈ R^n from any predictive model (supervised, unsupervised, or trained on proxy labels). The output is a calibrated score vector ŝ ∈ R^n that is nondecreasing with respect to the human-derived consensus ranking π̂ while remaining as close as possible to s_p in ℓ₂ distance, plus the consensus ranking itself. Crucially, only the ordinal structure of human judgments is used—raw rating values are discarded in favor of the induced ranking.

## Method

AtC is a two-stage pipeline. Stage 1 fits a Heterogeneous Thurstone Model (HTM) via maximum likelihood, where each annotator u has a precision parameter γ_u that captures individual reliability; the MLE yields consensus scores s* and a consensus ranking π̂. Stage 2 performs isotonic regression by computing the Euclidean projection of s_p onto the closed convex set of vectors nondecreasing under π̂, solved efficiently via the Pool-Adjacent-Violators algorithm. The key design insight is that humans are more reliable at comparing items than at absolute scoring (Weber-Fechner laws), so only ordinal information is extracted from human input while the model supplies the quantitative scale. Theoretically, the paper proves that heterogeneous aggregation is strictly more efficient than homogeneous (Theorem 3.3), that isotonic calibration enjoys risk bounds even under ranking misspecification (Theorem 3.5), and that the calibrated output is strictly closer to ground truth than the uncalibrated model with high probability (Theorem 3.9).

## Results

On the real-world Dots-activity dataset, AtC (with HRA-E Stage-1) achieves Kendall τ = 0.943, Wasserstein distance = 2.53, KL divergence = 0.861, and MSE = 9.59, substantially outperforming all three human-centered baselines (GPPL, Rank-SVM, BARCW) which score 61–65 on Wasserstein and 3800–4236 on MSE. On the semi-synthetic Reading Level dataset, AtC's calibrated output ŝ consistently dominates both human-only s* and model-only s_p across all four metrics; for example, with HRA-G, AtC achieves Kendall τ = 0.410 (vs. 0.375 for s* and 0.399 for s_p), Wasserstein = 0.839 (vs. 2.250 and 2.831), and KS = 0.163 (vs. 0.500 and 0.300).

## Baselines & Comparisons

AtC outperforms all five baselines on every reported metric. On the Dots dataset, AtC (HRA-E) achieves Kendall τ = 0.943, beating GPPL (0.931), Rank-SVM (0.923), and BARCW (0.940); the gap is most dramatic on Wasserstein distance (2.53 vs. 61–65) and MSE (9.59 vs. 3814–4236), where baselines are off by two to three orders of magnitude. On the semi-synthetic Reading Level dataset, the heterogeneous Stage-1 methods (CrowdBT, CrowdTCV from Chen et al. 2013) produce better calibrated AtC outputs than the homogeneous Bradley-Terry (BTL) and TCV baselines: for instance, AtC with CrowdBT achieves Wasserstein = 0.843 and MSE = 7.555, while AtC with BTL gives 0.894 and 8.097. The homogeneous TCV baseline is the weakest, yielding the lowest Kendall τ (0.338 for s*) and highest Wasserstein (2.343 for s*) among all Stage-1 methods. Notably, BARCW comes closest to AtC on Kendall τ (0.940 vs. 0.943) but still lags by a factor of ~25 on Wasserstein and ~440 on MSE, confirming that AtC's isotonic calibration step provides a decisive advantage in absolute score accuracy beyond ranking quality alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two well-studied but previously separate lines: heterogeneous rank aggregation (building directly on the Heterogeneous Thurstone Model of Jin et al., 2020) and isotonic/shape-restricted regression (drawing on oracle inequalities from Bellec, 2018 and risk bounds from Chatterjee et al., 2015). The key departure is that prior judgment aggregation work stops at producing a consensus score or ranking, while prior isotonic calibration work assumes a fixed ground-truth ordering; AtC bridges these by using the aggregated human ranking as a *random* constraint set for calibrating an arbitrary predictive model. Unlike human-AI complementarity methods (e.g., Pan et al., 2022; Li et al., 2022) that impose linear model constraints on both stages, AtC is fully modular: any Stage-1 aggregator and any Stage-2 predictor can be plugged in without modification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a principled, theoretically grounded recipe for the common practical scenario where ground truth is unobservable at decision time (workload estimation, paper review, product quality), and the three theorems (heterogeneous efficiency, robustness under misspecification, and asymptotic optimality) give practitioners clear conditions under which the two-stage pipeline is guaranteed to help. Limitations include the single-dimensional score assumption, the requirement for pairwise comparisons (not full rankings or ratings alone), and the Gaussian noise model in the theoretical analysis. Open questions the authors flag include multi-criteria assessment, expert polarization requiring multiple consensus rankings, and extension to LLM-as-a-judge settings where annotator heterogeneity maps to model diversity.
