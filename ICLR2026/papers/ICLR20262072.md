## Problem

Even after extensive RLVR training, LLMs still produce overthinking or underthinking CoTs, and once early reasoning steps are misguided, the model rarely recovers regardless of how many additional tokens it generates. Existing RLVR paradigms force a single-chain commitment where the model must always output a final answer within one trajectory, with no mechanism to abandon an unproductive path and restart. This paper asks whether reinforcement learning can teach models to flexibly re-solve problems when the current reasoning direction is flawed.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262072/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

During training, the model receives a math/science problem paired with a randomly truncated reasoning prefix (truncation ratio drawn uniformly from [0, 0.8]) and must generate a continuation that either produces a boxed final answer or emits the explicit redo phrase "It's better to redo the question." At inference time, whenever a sampled completion triggers a redo action, sampling is restarted from scratch until a valid final answer is produced, and the first such answer is taken as the model's output. A specialized prompt template instructs the model to continuously self-assess its solution path and use the redo phrase only when genuinely stuck, distinguishing it from internal self-reflection ("Do I need to redo the question?").

## Method

Re² introduces a three-way reward scheme within a DAPO-style group-based RL loop: continuations yielding a correct answer receive reward 1, incorrect answers receive 0, and redo actions receive the expected accuracy of re-solving from scratch, estimated from out-of-group completions via a finite geometric series that accounts for up to R=5 redo rounds. For each query, n full responses are sampled and randomly truncated to form diverse prefixes, and m continuations are generated per prefix; advantages are computed by normalizing rewards within each prefix group, and degenerate groups (all identical rewards) are filtered out. The policy is updated with a clipped PPO-style objective identical to DAPO, requiring no supervised fine-tuning. This reward design naturally incentivizes the model to commit to a final answer when the current trajectory is promising (high continuation reward) and to honestly signal a restart when the path is flawed (higher expected reward from re-solving), amplifying the rare redo behavior in vanilla models from 0.5% to over 30%.

## Results

Re² consistently outperforms DAPO across all five benchmarks and five model types (3B–14B parameters), with average accuracy gains of +2.7 to +5.8 points. The largest improvements appear on the hardest benchmarks: AIME24 improves by up to +10.3 points (Qwen2.5-14B Base: 18.2→28.5) and AIME25 by up to +12.7 points (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct: 8.6→21.2). On the out-of-domain GPQA-Diamond benchmark, Re² gains +5.1 to +7.1 points over DAPO, confirming generalization beyond in-domain math. Under test-time scaling, Re² continues to improve as the number of samples increases beyond 64, while DAPO's performance saturates.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is DAPO (Yu et al., 2025), the state-of-the-art open-source RLVR system. Re² outperforms DAPO on every benchmark and every model, with the largest gains on the hardest problems (AIME24: +5.2 to +10.3; AIME25: +2.3 to +12.7) and consistent but smaller gains on easier benchmarks (GSM8K: +0.3 to +2.6). For test-time scaling on AIME25, Re² is additionally compared against GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), DLER (Liu et al., 2025), and DeepConf (Fu et al., 2025); Re² achieves the best accuracy–compute trade-off, surpassing all three once the number of samples exceeds 64, while the others saturate earlier. The only setting where Re² underperforms DAPO is at very low sample counts (≤64) on hard problems, where the higher redo rate reduces the proportion of valid final answers in the sampled outputs. Overall, Re² wins on all five benchmarks across all five model configurations, with statistical significance (p < 0.05) confirmed via confidence intervals in Table 2.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RLVR training paradigm established by GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) and DAPO (Yu et al., 2025), retaining their group-based advantage computation and clipped policy update, but introduces a third action—re-solving—alongside the standard correct/incorrect outcomes. Prior attempts to address unproductive reasoning (e.g., self-backtracking by Yang et al., 2025b; confidence-based termination by Fu et al., 2025) relied on supervised fine-tuning or decoding heuristics, whereas Re² learns the redo decision purely through reinforcement learning with a reward that estimates the expected success rate of restarting from scratch. The paper positions itself as the first to frame re-solving as an RL-learnable action within the standard RLVR loop, rather than a post-hoc correction mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that CoT length negatively correlates with accuracy and that the first ~20% of a response largely determines the outcome provides a concrete diagnostic for why test-time scaling plateaus in current RLVR-trained models. The re-solving paradigm offers a principled escape from this bottleneck, and the demonstrated test-time scaling advantage (continuing to improve while DAPO saturates beyond 64 samples) suggests a new axis for inference-time compute allocation. Limitations include the absence of a mechanism to control redo frequency at inference time, the restriction to text-based math/science reasoning, and an ~11% increase in rollout time; extending the approach to multi-modal reasoning, tool use, and controllable inference budgets are natural next steps.
