## Problem

Existing diffusion-based image restoration methods either rely on UNet backbones that saturate under complex mixed degradations, or use small DiTs with heavy ControlNet-style adapters that underutilize backbone capacity. Adapting a large-scale DiT like Flux.1 (12B) to restoration is non-trivial: naive conditioning leaks artifacts, VLM-generated captions add latency and introduce degradation-related semantic bias (17–24% of captions contain such terms), and public datasets lack the structure-rich, quality-controlled scale that billion-parameter models require.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single low-quality RGB image as input and internally generates a lightly restored proxy (LRP) via a frozen SwinIR model, so no external text prompt or caption is needed at inference. Both the LQ and LRP are encoded into compact conditioning tokens at VAE latent resolution and injected into the Flux.1 backbone. The output is a photorealistic restored image at 1024×1024 resolution (4× upscale for super-resolution benchmarks), produced with 28 FlowMatch Euler sampling steps in FP16, with a total inference time of ~23.6 s on a single GPU.

## Method

LucidFlux freezes the entire 12B Flux.1 DiT backbone and trains only a lightweight dual-branch conditioner (DBC) plus a SigLIP connector (~1.6B trainable parameters). The DBC uses two separate two-block MMDiT modules—one on the LQ to anchor geometry and preserve high-frequency detail, one on the LRP to suppress artifacts—whose outputs are fused after a timestep- and layer-adaptive modulation (TLCM) that predicts per-channel scale and bias from sinusoidally encoded (t/T, l/L), aligning conditioning strength with the backbone's coarse-to-fine hierarchical roles. For semantic guidance, a frozen SigLIP encoder extracts features from the LRP and a lightweight Connector projects them into Flux.1's text-embedding space, eliminating all caption generation at both training and inference. Training uses a standard L2 latent loss for velocity prediction under flow matching, with Adafactor at lr 2e-5 on 8× A800 GPUs (~7 GPU-days), and a three-stage automated data curation pipeline (Laplacian-variance blur screening, Sobel-based flat-region filtering, CLIP-IQA top-20% selection) that yields 342K high-quality images and 1.36M degraded pairs via the Real-ESRGAN pipeline.

## Results

On the real-world RealLQ250 benchmark, LucidFlux achieves CLIP-IQA+ 0.7406, MUSIQ 73.01, MANIQA 0.5589, and NIQE 3.6742, outperforming all open-source baselines (best prior: SeeSR at CLIP-IQA+ 0.7034, MUSIQ 70.38). On DRealSR, it reaches CLIP-IQA+ 0.6748 and MUSIQ 66.68, again leading all competitors. On synthetic DIV2K-Val, LucidFlux attains the best perceptual scores (CLIP-IQA+ 0.7492, MUSIQ 73.90) but trails on pixel-fidelity metrics (PSNR 15.44 vs. DiffBIR's 20.04), a trade-off typical of generative restorers. Against commercial systems (Table 2), LucidFlux surpasses MeiTu SR (the strongest commercial baseline) on every IQA metric, e.g., CLIP-IQA+ 0.7406 vs. 0.6653 and MUSIQ 73.01 vs. 66.59 on RealLQ250.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LucidFlux consistently outperforms all seven open-source baselines on perceptual and semantic IQA metrics (CLIP-IQA+, Q-Align, MUSIQ, MANIQA, NIMA) across every benchmark. The largest margins are over DreamClear (e.g., RealLQ250 MUSIQ 73.01 vs. 67.08; DRealSR CLIP-IQA+ 0.6748 vs. 0.4461) and SUPIR (RealLQ250 CLIP-IQA+ 0.7406 vs. 0.6532), reflecting the advantage of the 12B Flux.1 backbone over smaller DiT or UNet backbones. Against SeeSR (the strongest UNet-based competitor), LucidFlux gains ~3.7 points in CLIP-IQA+ and ~2.6 in MUSIQ on RealLQ250. On synthetic DIV2K-Val, LucidFlux trails DiffBIR on PSNR (15.44 vs. 20.04) and SSIM (0.38 vs. 0.52), but leads on all no-reference perceptual metrics, which the authors argue better correlate with human judgment. Against commercial systems, LucidFlux beats MeiTu SR (the best commercial baseline) on all seven IQA metrics on RealLQ250 (e.g., MUSIQ 73.01 vs. 66.59, NIQE 3.67 vs. 5.41), and substantially outperforms Seedream 4.0 and Gemini-NanoBanana. A key practical advantage is caption-free inference: LucidFlux adds only 0.012 s of semantic-processing overhead versus 5.9–8.7 s for VLM caption generation in SeeSR/SUPIR/DreamClear, yielding a competitive total runtime (23.6 s) despite the 12B backbone.

## Relation to Prior Work

LucidFlux extends the diffusion-based image restoration lineage (SUPIR, SeeSR, DreamClear) by replacing UNet or small DiT backbones with the 12B Flux.1 MMDiT, while adopting the dual-branch conditioning paradigm from DreamClear (Ai et al., 2024) to decouple structure anchoring from artifact suppression. Its key conceptual departure is eliminating VLM-generated captions entirely in favor of SigLIP-based semantic alignment, and introducing a timestep- and layer-adaptive modulation schedule that routes conditioning signals according to the backbone's hierarchical roles—arguing that *how* to condition a large DiT matters more than *how much* to condition it. The paper also contributes the first publicly documented, fully automated three-stage IR data-curation pipeline, positioning itself against the unfiltered 20M-image SUPIR corpus and the manually curated LSDIR.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a frozen 12B DiT with only 1.6B trainable parameters can outperform smaller caption-based systems on perceptual and semantic IQA metrics while eliminating VLM inference latency, offering a practical recipe for caption-free restoration at scale. The data-curation pipeline (blur → flat-region → CLIP-IQA filtering) is reproducible and shows that 342K carefully selected images yield the largest single ablation gain, suggesting data quality over quantity is the binding constraint for large DiT training. Open questions include whether the approach extends to video/multi-frame restoration, whether the 12B backbone is truly necessary versus a mid-scale DiT, and how to reduce the >20 s inference cost for interactive use. The paper's own limitations section acknowledges high VRAM requirements and the need for >15 sampling steps, leaving real-time deployment unresolved.
