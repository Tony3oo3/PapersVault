## Problem

Existing multimodal retrieval benchmarks are limited in three ways: they target general-domain knowledge rather than expert-level domains, they emphasize semantic matching over deep reasoning (e.g., diagnosing microscopic slides or identifying violated rules), and they restrict queries and documents to single images with supplementary text. MRMR addresses all three gaps by introducing the first expert-level, reasoning-intensive, interleaved multimodal retrieval benchmark.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263407/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each query is an interleaved sequence of text segments and one or more images (averaging ~1.1 images per query), derived from MMMU-Pro expert-level VQA questions. The document corpus consists of interleaved image-text chunks scraped from web pages, theorem statements, or rule handbooks (averaging ~0.7 images per document for Knowledge, ~0.001 for Theorem). The system outputs a ranked document list scored by nDCG@10 (or Hit@1 for the four-candidate Negation subtask), where relevance is defined by whether a document supports the reasoning chain, conveys the needed theorem, or states a rule the query violates.

## Method

MRMR constructs 1,435 expert-annotated examples across 23 domains in three task types: Knowledge (retrieving web pages that support answering expert questions), Theorem (retrieving theorems needed to solve multimodal calculation problems), and Contradiction (retrieving rules or statements that conflict with a case description). Positive documents are collected via a semi-automated pipeline where GPT-Search generates candidate web pages, MonkeyOCR extracts interleaved content, and human domain experts verify relevance; negative documents are sampled from PIN-14M to build a 26K-document corpus. The novel Contradiction Retrieval task requires models to perform logical reasoning beyond semantic matching to identify conflicting concepts, instantiated through three subtasks: synthetic negation detection, vehicle design rule violations, and traffic case rule violations. The benchmark evaluates four retrieval paradigms—text retrievers with LLM-generated captions, CLIP-style two-stream models with vector fusion, MLLM-based multimodal embedders, and document-as-image models—across 14 frontier models, revealing that text embedding with captioning (Qwen3-Embedding, 54.1 nDCG@10) outperforms the best multimodal model (Ops-MM-Embedding, 48.1) and that all systems drop sharply on reasoning-intensive tasks.

## Results

On MRMR, the text retriever Qwen3-Embedding paired with LLM-generated image captions achieves the highest overall score of 54.1 nDCG@10 (Hit@1 for Negation), outperforming the best multimodal model Ops-MM-Embedding (48.1) by 6.0 points. Ops-MM-Embedding shows a dramatic reasoning gap: it scores 67.4 on Knowledge subtasks but drops to 37.4 on Theorem and 36.6 on Contradiction, while Qwen3-Embedding maintains more consistent performance (68.0, 47.3, 44.7 respectively). CLIP-style two-stream models (EVA-CLIP, SigLIP, OpenCLIP, JinaCLIP) perform poorly across all tasks, averaging only 11.6–18.0 nDCG@10, far below MLLM-based retrievers.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Qwen3-Embedding with LLM image captions (54.1) outperforms all 14 multimodal baselines, beating the strongest multimodal model Ops-MM-Embedding (48.1) by 6.0 points overall. Among multimodal models, Ops-MM-Embedding dominates: it surpasses MM-Embed (39.8) by 8.3 points, GME-Qwen2-VL (36.2) by 11.9 points, and E5-V (8.6) by a margin of 39.5 points. On Knowledge subtasks specifically, Ops-MM-Embedding (67.4 avg) is competitive with Qwen3-Embedding (68.0 avg), but the gap widens sharply on Theorem (37.4 vs. 47.3) and Contradiction (36.6 vs. 44.7), revealing that multimodal models' weakness is reasoning rather than domain knowledge. CLIP-style two-stream models (EVA-CLIP 11.6, SigLIP 12.8, JinaCLIP 14.0, OpenCLIP 18.0) are uniformly outperformed by MLLM-based retrievers by 20–35 points, confirming that contrastive text-image alignment is insufficient for expert-level retrieval. ColPali (25.2) and VLM2Vec (18.1) represent the weakest document-as-image and merged-image approaches respectively, while NV-Embed-v2 (44.8) is the second-best text+caption system, trailing Qwen3-Embedding by 9.3 points.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends three distinct lines of work into a unified benchmark: it repurposes expert-level questions from MMMU-Pro as retrieval queries, adopts BRIGHT's reasoning-intensive retrieval formulation (originally text-only) and its theorem corpus for the multimodal setting, and generalizes TIIR's interleaved text-image format from general-domain wikiHow tutorials to 23 expert domains. It departs from prior multimodal retrieval benchmarks (NIGHTS, ViDoRe, MMDocIR, OVEN, etc.) by requiring deep domain expertise, logical reasoning over images, and multi-image interleaved inputs rather than single-image semantic matching. The Contradiction Retrieval sub-task is explicitly framed as a novel formulation absent from prior literature, requiring models to identify documents that conflict with a query rather than support it.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MRMR is likely to become a standard evaluation for multimodal retrieval in expert and agentic settings, given its coverage of 23 domains, three distinct reasoning task types, and expert-verified annotations. The central finding—that a text retriever with LLM-generated captions (Qwen3-Embedding, 54.1) outperforms all 11 native multimodal models (best: Ops-MM-Embedding, 48.1)—challenges the assumption that unified multimodal encoders should dominate on multimodal queries and suggests that current multimodal models lack the reasoning depth needed for expert-domain retrieval. The ~30-point performance collapse from Knowledge to Theorem/Contradiction tasks, and near-random performance on Negation, highlight that reasoning ability (not domain coverage or visual matching) is the fundamental bottleneck. Open questions include whether test-time scaling via query expansion (which yields +14.8 with a 72B VLM) can close this gap, and whether native interleaved models (e.g., TIIR, not yet public) will change the landscape.
