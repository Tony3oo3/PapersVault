## Problem

Despite LLMs' success on complex reasoning tasks, they still fail on tasks trivial for humans, such as overlooking critical information or exhibiting stereotypical biases. The paper identifies the root cause as a fundamental mismatch: language is primarily a communication tool rather than a thinking tool, so next-token prediction on human language integrates expression biases into the model's reasoning. This 'language-thought gap' means that implicit expressions—phrasings that occur less frequently in training—cause LLMs to underutilize or misinterpret critical premises.

## Input / Output

The method takes a standard text question (e.g., a pronoun-resolution sentence, a math word problem, or a multi-hop QA prompt) and produces a reasoning response that first explicitly echoes and expands all relevant information from the question before performing the actual reasoning step. The LoT prompt is a single instruction line ('Please observe, expand, and echo all the relevant information based on the question') prepended to or combined with existing reasoning prompts like CoT, so the output structure is: echoed facts → expanded interpretations → step-by-step reasoning → final answer. No architectural changes or additional training are required; the intervention operates purely at the prompt level.

## Method

The paper constructs Structural Causal Models (SCMs) for next-token prediction, modeling latent thought variables X and their token expressions L with a random permutation of token order. Proposition 2.3 shows that when tokens appear in anti-topological order relative to the causal graph, the model learns a marginal distribution over the conclusion that ignores unobserved premises. Theorem 2.4 then provides a KL-divergence lower bound decomposing the reasoning error into a 'language-thought gap' term (how well the model recovers the latent variable from the token) and a variational-distance term (the cost of misunderstanding), proving that even a model with perfect causal knowledge can exhibit biased reasoning when expressions are implicit. To operationalize this, the authors propose LoT (Language-of-Thoughts), a prompt-level intervention with two components: 'echo' (restate key facts to refresh the context q, reducing q-implicitness) and 'expand' (generate additional explicit expressions from the latent variable's expression set, reducing L-implicitness). They validate the theory by constructing WinoControl datasets with controlled levels of L- and q-implicitness, and demonstrate consistent improvements across 11 reasoning tasks and 4 LLMs (DeepSeek-V3, GPT-4o-mini, Qwen2-72B, Llama-3.1-70B), including general benchmarks like GPQA, FOLIO, and HotpotQA.

## Results

On the WinoBias benchmark, LoT achieves the best or second-best consistency (Con) score across all four LLMs, e.g., 90.7 on DeepSeek-V3 vs. 86.9 for CoT and 68.4 for RaR. On the Alice math benchmark, LoT improves over CoT by 8% on GPT-4o-mini (8.5% vs. 0.5%) and by 43.5% on Qwen2-72B (40.5% vs. 9.0%). On the BBQ bias benchmark, LoT outperforms all five baselines in 11 out of 12 model-bias-type combinations. On general reasoning tasks (GPQA, FOLIO, CSQA, etc.), LoT brings consistent improvements over CoT of up to 20% on GPQA (e.g., 42.19 vs. 23.21 on Llama-3.1-70B).

## Baselines & Comparisons

LoT consistently outperforms CoT on the primary WinoBias consistency metric across all four LLMs (e.g., 90.7 vs. 86.9 on DeepSeek-V3; 72.5 vs. 71.5 on GPT-4o-mini), and the gap widens on the Alice benchmark where CoT scores near zero on GPT-4o-mini (0.5%) while LoT reaches 8.5%. Against RaR, LoT achieves higher consistency in 3 of 4 models (90.7 vs. 89.9 on DeepSeek-V3; 72.5 vs. 68.4 on GPT-4o-mini) and substantially higher accuracy on Alice (8.5% vs. 1.0% on GPT-4o-mini). Against LtM, LoT matches or exceeds consistency on DeepSeek-V3 (90.7 vs. 91.2, near-tie) and GPT-4o-mini (72.5 vs. 71.0), while LtM is stronger on Qwen2-72B (81.1 vs. 86.9 for LoT actually wins). On the BBQ benchmark, LoT beats all three baselines (CoT, RaR, LtM) in 11 out of 12 cases, with the largest gains on GPT-4o-mini (e.g., 76.9 vs. 58.5 for CoT on Age bias). On general reasoning tasks, LoT improves over CoT by up to 19 points on GPQA (Llama-3.1-70B: 42.19 vs. 23.21) and by 4–8 points on FOLIO and MUSR, while RaR and LtM show inconsistent or smaller gains in the same settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Language of Thought Hypothesis (Fodor, 1975) and recent neuroscience evidence (Fedorenko et al., 2024) that human thinking operates on mental representations distinct from written language, and extends this insight to LLMs by constructing Structural Causal Models of next-token prediction. It unifies several previously observed LLM failure modes—premise-order sensitivity (Chen et al., 2024), irrelevant-context distraction (Shi et al., 2023), and the reversal curse (Berglund et al., 2023)—under a single formal mechanism: the 'language-thought gap' where implicit expressions trigger biased reasoning. Methodologically, it departs from the CoT prompting lineage (Wei et al., 2022; RaR; LtM) by targeting the understanding stage rather than the reasoning stage, and from prior bias-mitigation work by providing a causal-theoretic lower bound (Theorem 2.4) rather than purely empirical observations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first formal SCM-based explanation for why LLMs fail on tasks that are trivial for humans, grounding the 'language-thought gap' in a KL-divergence lower bound that separates understanding failure from reasoning failure. The LoT prompting technique is remarkably simple (a single sentence) yet yields consistent gains across 11 tasks, 4+ LLMs, and even non-autoregressive models, making it a low-cost addition to existing reasoning pipelines. Key limitations include its dependence on instruction-following ability (small models benefit less), the strong assumptions in Theorem 2.4 (perfect knowledge, Markov property), and the fact that it is a prompt-level fix that does not address the root cause in pretraining. Open questions include whether training-time interventions (architecture changes, alternative objectives) can close the gap more fundamentally, and whether the SCM framework extends cleanly to non-autoregressive or diffusion-based models.
