## Problem

Most continual learning methods focus on preventing catastrophic forgetting by preserving all prior knowledge, but this indiscriminate retention causes negative transfer, where outdated or conflicting prior knowledge interferes with new task learning and degrades both forward and backward transfer. The paper addresses two underexplored questions: how to detect when negative transfer is occurring, and how to selectively unlearn only the interfering knowledge while preserving useful prior information.

## Input / Output

DEDUCE operates as a plug-in module on top of existing CL backbones (ResNet-18 or ViT-Base) processing standard RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR, 64×64 for Tiny-ImageNet, 128×128 for CORE-50). In task-incremental settings, the task identity is available at both training and test time; in class-incremental settings, the model must output a unified class label over all previously seen classes without task identity. The method additionally consumes a replay buffer of stored exemplars from prior tasks, which feeds both the gradient-conflict detection and the FIM-regularized unlearning update.

## Method

DEDUCE is a three-phase (Detect–Decide–Unlearn) framework. Detection uses two complementary strategies: a transferability bound that estimates the HΔH-divergence between source and target domains via a trained domain classifier and approximates the ideal joint error λ using the LEEP score, and a gradient conflict analysis that checks whether the inner product of the new-task gradient and the memory-buffer gradient falls below a tolerance ε. Upon detecting negative transfer, the Local Unlearning Module (LUM) is activated to perform a FIM-regularized parameter update that increases the cross-entropy loss on the current mini-batch (pushing parameters away from interfering prior knowledge) while protecting high-Fisher-importance parameters that encode useful information. In parallel, the Global Unlearning Module (GUM) continuously tracks per-neuron contribution scores (a running average of activation magnitude weighted by outgoing weights, gated by a normalized importance score) and periodically reinitializes the lowest-contributing mature neurons, reclaiming underutilized capacity to restore plasticity. The unlearning rate δ, global unlearning rate φ, and tolerance ε are the key hyperparameters, with moderate values yielding the best trade-off between interference removal and knowledge preservation.

## Results

DEDUCE is a plug-in framework applied on top of existing CL baselines, reporting Averaged Accuracy (ACC) and Backward Transfer (BWT) across four datasets in both CIL and TIL settings. The headline result is an average gain of up to 4.55% over state-of-the-art baselines. For the strongest baseline DER++ on CIFAR-100 CIL, DEDUCE (gradient-based, OUR(G)) lifts ACC from 36.8% to 39.8%, and on Tiny-ImageNet CIL from 15.6% to 22.1%. The most dramatic improvement is on HAL, where CIFAR-100 CIL ACC jumps from 11.6% to 24.8% (+13.2%) with OUR(G).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DEDUCE is not a standalone method but a plug-in enhancement applied to nine existing CL baselines (oEWC, A-GEM, ER, DER++, HAL, PCR, OnPro, MOSE, STAR), and it consistently improves every baseline it is combined with across all four datasets and both CIL/TIL settings. The largest absolute gains appear on HAL, where CIFAR-100 CIL ACC improves by 13.2% (11.6→24.8) and CIFAR-10 CIL by 22.1% (44.9→67.0) with the gradient-based variant OUR(G). For the strongest baseline DER++, DEDUCE adds 3.0% on CIFAR-100 CIL (36.8→39.8), 3.5% on CIFAR-10 CIL (71.1→74.6), and 6.5% on Tiny-ImageNet CIL (15.6→22.1). The gradient-conflict variant OUR(G) generally outperforms the transferability-bound variant OUR(B), attributed to its finer batch-level detection granularity, though both consistently beat the unmodified baselines. On CORE-50, where overlapping classes make task boundaries ambiguous, DEDUCE still delivers consistent gains, with DER++ improving from 40.1% to 41.2% (CIL) and 76.0% to 76.8% (TIL). BWT results confirm that DEDUCE also mitigates negative backward transfer, e.g. improving DER++ BWT by 6.4% on CIFAR-100 and 9.2% on Tiny-ImageNet.

## Relation to Prior Work

DEDUCE sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) domain-adaptation theory (Ben-David et al., 2010) and the LEEP transferability score (Nguyen et al., 2020), which are repurposed here as a *detection* signal for negative transfer rather than an offline evaluation metric; (2) machine unlearning, which has been used for privacy compliance or domain-specific feature removal, but never as a *conditional, interference-triggered* mechanism within a continual learning loop; and (3) the KL-divergence-based unlearning formulation of Wibisono (2018), adapted into the Local Unlearning Module. The key conceptual departure from prior CL work (EWC, A-GEM, DER++, HAL, etc.) is that instead of treating all prior knowledge as something to preserve, DEDUCE treats it as something to *selectively discard* when a transferability bound or gradient-conflict test signals interference. This contrasts with earlier negative-transfer mitigations like Batch Spectral Shrinkage or AFEC, which prune or shrink features reactively but lack an explicit detection-then-act decision gate.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper carves out a concrete, actionable sub-problem within CL—*when* to unlearn and *what* to unlearn—that prior work has identified as important but left underexplored. The plug-and-play design (DEDUCE can be layered onto ER, DER++, HAL, A-GEM, STAR, etc.) makes it practically useful for practitioners who already have a CL pipeline and want to reduce task interference without redesigning the base method. Limitations include the significant computational overhead of gradient-based detection (~5–6× per epoch), the transferability-bound variant's requirement for a full data pass (limiting strict online use), and the observed degradation of LUM on CORE-50 where overlapping classes make source/target separation ambiguous. The authors themselves flag the open question of how to explicitly *promote* beneficial transfer while suppressing interference, suggesting the current framework is necessary but not sufficient for optimal knowledge integration.
