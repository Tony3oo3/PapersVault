## Problem

RLVR training for reasoning LLMs typically samples prompts uniformly or applies coarse difficulty filters, wasting compute on trivially solved or consistently failed examples. No prior work provides a unified theoretical account of how both the prompt sampling distribution and the per-prompt rollout allocation jointly govern gradient efficiency, nor a practical method that optimizes both simultaneously with minimal estimation overhead.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260059/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a prompt dataset of math problems (Numina-Math, partitioned into T=15 non-overlapping subsets), an initial policy LLM (Qwen2.5-Math 1.5B/7B, Llama3.2-3B, or Phi-4-mini), and a binary verifiable reward function. Each training iteration begins with a lightweight parameter-estimation phase drawing N′ rollouts per prompt to initialize Beta-distribution difficulty estimates, followed by a large-scale training phase that samples m prompts per step with a total rollout budget of N=m×n. The output is a fine-tuned reasoning model evaluated on eight math benchmarks (MATH500, GSM8K, AIME 2024/2025, AMC 2023, Minerva, OlympiadBench, GaokaoEN 2023).

## Method

CurES derives two theoretical results from a KL-constrained gradient analysis: (1) the per-prompt loss reduction is upper-bounded by √(2δ·p(1−p)) via the Cramér-Rao inequality, yielding an optimal softmax sampling distribution ρ*(x) ∝ exp(√(p(1−p))/τ) that maximizes expected gradient progress under an entropy constraint; and (2) the optimal rollout allocation n_i ∝ σ_i (where σ_i is the per-prompt gradient variance) minimizes total estimator variance under a fixed budget. In practice, prompt difficulty p_θ(x_i) is tracked via a Beta-Binomial conjugate posterior that updates incrementally as rollouts are observed, requiring only N′ initial rollouts per prompt per iteration. The dataset is split into T non-overlapping subsets trained iteratively to counteract distribution shift, and within each iteration the sampling probabilities and rollout quantities are re-estimated at every step from the evolving posterior, producing a bell-shaped allocation that concentrates compute on moderately difficult prompts.

## Results

On Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, CurES-GRPO achieves an average score of 44.94 across eight math reasoning benchmarks, outperforming GRPO (41.64) by +3.30 points and the best prior sample-efficient method GVM-GRPO (42.82) by +2.12 points. On Qwen2.5-Math-7B, CurES-GRPO reaches 52.41 average, exceeding GRPO (47.59) by +4.82 points and GVM-GRPO (50.64) by +1.77 points. In terms of convergence speed, CurES-GRPO reaches the same peak accuracy as GRPO in 5.5× fewer training steps (1.5B) and 4.3× fewer steps (7B), while CurES-RPP converges 1.75× faster than RPP.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CurES-GRPO consistently outperforms all six baselines on the 1.5B model: it beats GRPO by +3.30 avg points, Speed-RL-GRPO by +3.60, GVM-GRPO by +2.12, DAPO by +2.14, and MoPPS by +0.96. On the 7B model, CurES-GRPO leads GRPO by +4.82, Speed-RL-GRPO by +3.57, and GVM-GRPO by +1.77, though CurES-RPP (49.62) slightly trails vanilla RPP (50.18) on 7B, suggesting the RPP advantage estimator is already strong at that scale. The most notable gap is against GVM, the closest prior sample-efficient method: CurES wins on every individual benchmark for 1.5B and on 7 of 8 for 7B, with the largest per-benchmark gains on MATH500 (+2.40, 1.5B; +3.20, 7B) and Minerva (+4.41, 1.5B; +5.15, 7B). In wall-clock efficiency, CurES-GRPO reaches GRPO's peak accuracy in 4.5 hours vs. 20.1 hours (1.5B, 4.51× speedup) and 10.8 hours vs. 41.8 hours (7B, 3.86× speedup). Ablations confirm both components matter: removing rollout allocation drops 1.5B avg from 44.94 to 43.71, and removing difficulty-aware prompt sampling drops it to 44.08.

## Relation to Prior Work

CurES unifies two previously separate threads in RLVR training acceleration: prompt sampling (as in Speed-RL, which filters for intermediate difficulty) and rollout allocation (as in GVM, which minimizes gradient variance per prompt). The key departure is a single theoretical derivation via the Cramér-Rao inequality that links the Fisher Information Matrix to the binary reward's variance, yielding both the optimal sampling distribution (a softmax over √(p(1-p))) and the optimal rollout allocation (proportional to per-prompt gradient variance) from the same analysis. The Bayesian Beta-posterior tracking of prompt difficulty is borrowed from active learning and prior work on online difficulty prediction (MoPPS), but is here integrated as the lightweight estimation backbone that feeds both the sampling and allocation decisions without requiring a separate pre-evaluation pass.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 5.5× convergence speedup over GRPO (and 4.5× in wall-clock time) is practically significant for anyone training reasoning models, as it directly reduces the dominant cost of rollout generation. The theoretical framework is clean and extensible, but its current scope is limited to binary verifiable rewards and mathematical reasoning; whether the Cramér-Rao-based difficulty proxy transfers to continuous or multi-dimensional reward settings remains open. The method's reliance on iterative subset training (resetting difficulty estimates each iteration) is a pragmatic workaround for distribution shift that may not scale gracefully to very large or streaming datasets. An open question is whether the Bell-shaped rollout allocation generalizes when the reward is not strictly 0/1, since the entire variance decomposition in Eq. (21) depends on the binary structure.
