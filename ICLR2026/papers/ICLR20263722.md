## Problem

Constraint-based causal discovery requires numerous conditional independence tests (CITs), but the per-test computational cost grows super-linearly with sample size, creating a fundamental bottleneck that limits practical applicability. Prior work has focused on reducing the number of CITs invoked, but the high time complexity of individual CITs (e.g., cubic for kernel-based methods) remains unaddressed. Since no single CIT is uniformly effective across all dependence structures, a general-purpose mechanism to reduce per-test cost while preserving statistical power is needed.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a dataset of n samples over variables (X, Y, Z) and an arbitrary base CIT method as input. The data is partitioned into K subsets of fixed size n_k (e.g., 400), so K = n/n_k grows linearly with total sample size while each subtest cost stays constant. The output is a single ensemble p-value p_e computed via stable-distribution aggregation, from which a reject/fail-to-reject decision is made at a chosen significance level α_e.

## Method

E-CIT applies a divide-and-aggregate strategy: it partitions data into K subsets, runs the base CIT independently on each to obtain p-values p_1,...,p_K, and combines them using a novel aggregation grounded in the closure property of stable distributions. Specifically, each p-value is transformed via the inverse CDF of a stable distribution S(α,β,γ,δ), the transformed values are averaged, and the result is mapped back through the CDF of S(α,β,γ',δ) where γ' = K^(1/α−1)γ, yielding a valid ensemble p-value. The stability parameter α (recommended values 1.75 or 2) controls tail heaviness and provides flexibility to adapt to different CIT methods and data distributions. Theoretical guarantees include validity (uniform p-value under H₀), admissibility, unbiasedness, and power consistency as K→∞ under mild conditions on subtest effectiveness (Theorem 2), without requiring parametric assumptions on the subtests.

## Results

On the Flow-Cytometry dataset (n=1755), E-CIT improves F1-score for most base CITs: E-KCIT reaches 0.695 vs. KCIT's 0.624, E-LPCIT reaches 0.741 vs. LPCIT's 0.691, and E-FisherZ reaches 0.767 vs. FisherZ's 0.737. On synthetic post-nonlinear data (n=1200, t-distributed noise df=2), E-RCIT improves test power from 0.548 to 0.623 and E-FisherZ from 0.510 to 0.561, while maintaining Type I error near the nominal 0.05 level. In the p-value combination comparison (Table 10), the stable-distribution method achieves the highest power (e.g., 0.919 under t-noise) while keeping Type I error at 0.033, outperforming all classical alternatives (Tippett, Edgington, Fisher, Pearson, Mudholkar). E-KCIT also outperforms RCIT and KCIT in causal discovery (F1-score and SHD) under heavy-tailed noise while maintaining comparable runtime to RCIT.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against KCIT (Zhang et al., 2011), E-KCIT achieves comparable or higher test power while reducing runtime from cubic to linear in sample size; under heavy-tailed Cauchy noise, E-KCIT shows more consistent power than both KCIT and RCIT. Against RCIT (Strobl et al., 2019), E-RCIT consistently improves power (e.g., 0.623 vs. 0.548 at df=2, n=1200) with Type I error remaining near nominal. Against FastKCIT (Schacht & Huang, 2025), E-KCIT achieves comparable runtime but superior power under heavy-tailed noise distributions. Against LPCIT (Scetbon et al., 2022), E-LPCIT improves power across all noise settings (e.g., 0.447 vs. 0.422 at df=2). Against CMIknn (Runge, 2018), the ensemble provides only marginal gains since CMIknn already achieves near-saturated power (~0.98–0.99). Against CCIT (Sen et al., 2017), the ensemble significantly reduces the severely inflated Type I error (0.450→0.336) at the cost of modest power reduction. Against FisherZ (Fisher, 1921), E-FisherZ improves power by 5–7 percentage points under heavy-tailed noise. In the p-value combination comparison (Table 10), the proposed stable-distribution method outperforms all five classical alternatives (Tippett, Edgington, Fisher, Pearson, Mudholkar) in power while maintaining valid Type I error control; notably, Edgington's method inflates Type I error to 0.289–0.463, making it unreliable.

## Relation to Prior Work

E-CIT is structurally a generalization of the Stouffer et al. (1949) p-value combination method, replacing the normal quantile with a stable distribution quantile to gain flexibility in tail behavior. It departs from prior p-value combination work (Liu & Xie, 2020; Ling & Rho, 2022) by making no parametric assumptions on subtest statistics, which is essential for CITs whose alternative-hypothesis p-value distributions vary widely across data-generating mechanisms. Unlike FastKCIT, which is specifically tailored to accelerate KCIT via Gaussian mixture partitioning of the conditioning set, E-CIT is a method-agnostic wrapper applicable to any base CIT. The divide-and-aggregate strategy draws on ensemble learning principles but is adapted to the unique statistical structure of conditional independence testing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

E-CIT addresses a fundamental scalability bottleneck in constraint-based causal discovery by linearizing the sample-size complexity of base CITs, making large-sample causal inference practically feasible. The theoretical consistency guarantee (Theorem 2) is notable because it requires only mild conditions on subtest effectiveness rather than strong assumptions on the data-generating process, potentially extending consistency to methods that lack such guarantees. Key open questions remain: handling correlated p-values from resampling, adapting to distribution drifts across heterogeneous environments, and determining theoretically optimal α values for specific CIT methods. The framework does not resolve the curse of dimensionality in high-dimensional conditioning sets, and its Type I error control can be slightly perturbed when subtest p-values deviate from exact uniformity.
