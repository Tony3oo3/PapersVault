## Problem

Converting spoken mathematical expressions into structured LaTeX remains underexplored: prior work (MathSpeech) relies on dual ASR transcriptions, handles only isolated equations, provides no public training data, lacks multilingual coverage, and omits end-to-end multimodal approaches. No large-scale, human-annotated dataset of spoken math exists, and no benchmark covers mathematical sentences (equations embedded in natural language).
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263643/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system takes 16 kHz spoken audio of either isolated equations or full mathematical sentences (in English or Russian) and produces the corresponding LaTeX string, with sentences containing inline formulas within surrounding text. For evaluation, predictions and references are normalized via KaTeX AST parsing to eliminate superficial formatting differences (e.g., brace placement, operator spacing), and metrics (CER, TeXBLEU) are computed on lowercased text to avoid case-sensitivity artifacts. The dataset spans 66k human-annotated and 571k TTS-generated audio samples from 33 speakers and 9+ synthetic voices, with equations ranging from 3 to 230 symbols and sentences containing up to 11 embedded equations.

## Method

Two complementary pipelines are evaluated. The ASR post-correction approach uses a frozen Whisper-Large v3 model to transcribe audio into text, then a fine-tuned LLM (Qwen2.5-0.5B/1.5B/7B or Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B) converts the transcription into LaTeX; the 7B variant uses LoRA (r=8, α=32) on attention projections. The end-to-end Audio-LLM approach (SALMONN-13B, Qwen-Audio, Gemma-3n, Audio Flamingo-3) bypasses explicit transcription: audio encoders (Whisper + BEATs for SALMONN) extract latent features, a Q-former adapter aligns them with LLM token embeddings, and a LLaMA-based decoder generates LaTeX directly from concatenated audio and text prompt tokens, also fine-tuned via LoRA. Few-shot prompting (5-shot, 25-shot) with pretrained Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct is evaluated as a zero-fine-tuning baseline. The key dataset contribution combines human crowd-sourced recordings with XTTSv2 synthetic speech, augmented by 400k TTS samples from MathBridge, enabling both disjoint-formula and source-type (human vs. synthetic) evaluation splits.

## Results

On the S2L-equations benchmark (English, disjoint split), SALMONN-13B achieves the best equation CER of 17.5%, while the best ASR post-correction model (Qwen2.5-0.5B, Mix-full training) reaches 27.2% CER. On the S2L-sentences benchmark, SALMONN-13B attains a 39.7% equation CER and 9.6% text CER, establishing the first baseline for mathematical sentence recognition. On the MathSpeech benchmark, the paper's models achieve CERs of 27.7% (SALMONN) and 30.0% (Qwen), comparable to MathSpeech's own 27.7%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's sole external baseline is MathSpeech (Hyeon et al., AAAI 2025), a 120M-parameter ASR post-correction model trained on ~8M TTS-generated samples. On the MathSpeech benchmark, the comparison is essentially a tie: SALMONN matches MathSpeech at 27.7% CER, while Qwen2.5-0.5B is slightly worse at 30.0%. However, on the paper's own S2L-equations benchmark, the gap is dramatic: MathSpeech scores 64.0% CER versus 27.2% for Qwen and 17.5% for SALMONN — a margin of over 36 percentage points even after normalizing LaTeX formatting artifacts (which improves MathSpeech from 92% to 64%). The authors note that MathSpeech's poor transfer is partly due to its reliance on dual ASR transcriptions and its training on TTS-only data without human speech, whereas the S2L dataset includes 66k human-annotated samples. For S2L-sentences, no prior baseline exists, so the paper establishes the first benchmark with SALMONN achieving 39.7% equation CER.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a direct extension of MathSpeech (Hyeon et al., 2025), which introduced an ASR post-correction pipeline for isolated spoken equations but provided no training data, no multilingual support, and no sentence-level context. Rather than building on a single prior S2L method, the paper repurposes existing components—Whisper for ASR, Qwen2.5/Qwen2.5-Math for post-correction LLMs, and SALMONN/Qwen-Audio/Audio Flamingo 3 for end-to-end Audio-LLMs—into a unified evaluation framework for the S2L task. The dataset construction draws on MathBridge, TextTeller, and Proof-Pile-2 as textual sources, combined with XTTSv2 for synthetic audio and crowd-sourced human annotation, filling a gap the authors identify as the absence of any large-scale, open-source, human-annotated spoken-math dataset.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The release of the first large-scale open-source S2L dataset (66k human + 571k synthetic samples in English and Russian) and the first benchmark for mathematical sentence recognition provide the field with essential infrastructure that was previously missing. The finding that end-to-end Audio-LLMs (SALMONN at 17.5% CER) substantially outperform post-correction pipelines (27.2%) for isolated equations, while sentence-level CER remains around 40%, highlights that contextual mathematical speech is a significantly harder and underexplored problem. Key open questions include whether audio-visual methods can resolve ambiguities inherent in spoken math, whether synthetic data can eventually replace costly human annotation, and how to handle real-world lecture conditions where equations are paraphrased, incomplete, or tied to visual content.
