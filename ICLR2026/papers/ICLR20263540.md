## Problem

State-of-the-art Temporal Graph Neural Networks (TGNNs) for future node affinity prediction are outperformed by trivial heuristics like Persistent Forecast and Moving Average, revealing a fundamental misalignment between TGNN inductive biases and the ranking nature of affinity tasks. The paper identifies four compounding causes: nonlinear memory cells cannot express even the simplest heuristic (proven theoretically), cross-entropy loss ignores ordinal structure, local neighborhood sampling misses global temporal trends, and batching or fixed-size buffers discard temporal information.

## Input / Output

Given a query node u, the full continuous-time dynamic graph (a stream of timestamped, weighted interactions) observed up to time t, and a target future time t+, NAVIS produces a normalized affinity score vector over all other nodes in the graph. In the 'previous labels' setting, the model additionally receives the most recent ground-truth affinity vector as input, enabling it to operate as a learnable generalization of moving-average heuristics. The output is a full ranking (not a binary edge prediction), and during training the loss is computed only over the top-20 affinities for constant-time cost.

## Method

NAVIS maintains a per-node state h and a virtual global state g, both in R^d (d = number of nodes), updated via a learnable linear state-space model with sigmoid-gated mixing: the state update blends the previous state and the input affinity vector through a data-dependent gate z_h, and the output blends the updated state and input through a second gate z_s conditioned on the global state. The virtual global state is computed by aggregating a buffer of recent affinity vectors across all nodes, capturing network-wide trends that local sampling misses. Training uses a Lambda Loss (pairwise rank-based objective approximating NDCG gradients) augmented with a pairwise margin regularization to prevent score collapse, replacing the suboptimal cross-entropy loss. The key theoretical insight is that Persistent Forecast, SMA, and EMA are all special cases of linear SSMs (Theorem 1), while standard RNN/LSTM/GRU cells provably cannot express even Persistent Forecast (Theorem 2), motivating the linear SSM architecture that generalizes heuristics while remaining more expressive.

## Results

On the TGB node affinity benchmark (NDCG@10, test set), NAVIS achieves 0.863 on tgbn-trade, 0.520 on tgbn-genre, 0.552 on tgbn-reddit, and 0.444 on tgbn-token, outperforming the best TGNN baseline TGNv2 by +12.8% on tgbn-trade (0.863 vs. 0.735) and by +51% on tgbn-token (0.444 vs. 0.294). NAVIS also surpasses the strongest heuristic (Moving Average: 0.777, 0.497, 0.480, 0.414) across all four TGB datasets. On converted link-prediction datasets (Wikipedia, Flights, USLegis, UNVote), NAVIS gains +13.9% to +20% over the second-best TGNN.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NAVIS outperforms all 11 compared baselines across every dataset and metric. The most striking finding is that the majority of TGNNs—JODIE, TGAT, CAWN, TCL, GraphMixer, DyGFormer, DyGMamba, and DyRep—score between 0.31 and 0.41 NDCG@10 on TGB, falling well below the simple Moving Average heuristic (0.48–0.78), confirming the paper's theoretical argument that standard RNN/LSTM/GRU memory cells cannot express even Persistent Forecast. TGNv2 is the strongest TGNN (0.735 on tgbn-trade, 0.507 on tgbn-reddit) but still trails NAVIS by 12.8–51% depending on the dataset. Against the best heuristic (Moving Average), NAVIS gains +8.6% on tgbn-trade (0.863 vs. 0.777) and +4.4% on tgbn-genre (0.520 vs. 0.497). On the S4 state-space baseline (Table 11), NAVIS leads by +7.7% on tgbn-trade (0.863 vs. 0.796) and +14.5% on tgbn-genre (0.528 vs. 0.461). On the four converted link-prediction datasets, NAVIS achieves 0.573 (Wikipedia), 0.499 (Flights), 0.347 (USLegis), and 0.952 (UNVote), beating TGNv2 by 32%, 67%, 37%, and 17% respectively.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of state space models (SSMs) and temporal graph neural networks (TGNNs), building directly on the formal equivalence between linear SSMs and moving-average heuristics established by Gu et al. (2021, 2022) and Eliasof et al. (2025). It departs from the dominant TGNN paradigm (TGN, DyGFormer, GraphMixer) by arguing that their nonlinear message-passing and standard memory cells are fundamentally misaligned with node affinity prediction, and instead proposes a learnable linear SSM architecture (NAVIS) that generalizes simple heuristics while adding a virtual global state for network-wide trends. The rank-based Lambda Loss replaces the cross-entropy objective inherited from link prediction, addressing a loss-mismatch problem the authors formalize theoretically.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clear diagnostic for why a whole class of sophisticated TGNNs underperforms trivial baselines on node affinity prediction, offering both theoretical proofs (Theorems 1–3) and a practical, efficient architecture that outperforms all prior methods across eight datasets. However, NAVIS remains elementary: it cannot model complex multi-hop dependencies, uses a basic recency-based global buffer, and is limited to linear combinations of input and state. Open questions include whether attention-based or non-deterministic aggregation over the global buffer, and hybrid linear/nonlinear state updates, can close the remaining gap to more expressive temporal models.
