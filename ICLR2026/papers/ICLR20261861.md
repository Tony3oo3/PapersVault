## Problem

Text-guided color editing in images and videos is an ill-conditioned problem that requires modifying only specific color attributes (albedo, light source color, ambient lighting) while preserving geometry, material properties, and light-matter interactions. Existing training-free editing methods provide broad applicability but struggle with precise color control and introduce visual inconsistencies in both edited and non-edited regions, making film-industry-level color changing an unresolved challenge.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261861/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a source image (or video) along with a source prompt and a target prompt that specify which objects or lights to recolor and the desired target color, and produces a color-edited output where only the intended regions are modified. It operates on MM-DiT-based models at 1024×1024 for images (SD3, FLUX.1-dev) and 720×480 with 49 frames for video (CogVideoX-2B), and also integrates with instruction-based editing models (Step1X-Edit, FLUX.1 Kontext dev) that take a real input image plus a natural-language editing instruction. For real-image editing, the source is first mapped into the model's latent space via UniEdit-Flow inversion before the attention-control pipeline is applied.

## Method

ColorCtrl is a training-free method that manipulates the self-attention maps and value tokens within MM-DiT blocks across all layers and all inference timesteps, requiring no manual layer or timestep selection. It consists of three components: (1) structure preservation, which copies the vision-to-vision quadrant of the source attention map into the target branch to lock geometry, material, and camera parameters; (2) color preservation, which extracts a binary spatial mask from the vision-to-text attention quadrant (threshold ε=0.1) and copies source value tokens V^vision from non-editing regions into the target branch to prevent unwanted color shifts; and (3) optional attribute re-weighting, which scales attention scores in the text-to-vision quadrant before softmax to enable word-level control over color intensity. Unlike prior attention-control methods (e.g., DiTCtrl) that operate on key/value tokens or require careful layer selection, ColorCtrl works directly on attention maps and value tokens uniformly across the entire network, and its only hyperparameter is the mask threshold.

## Results

On PIE-Bench with FLUX.1-dev, ColorCtrl achieves Canny SSIM of 0.9196, BG Preservation SSIM of 0.9936, and PSNR of 39.49, outperforming the best training-free baseline (UniEdit-Flow: 0.8498, 0.9777, 37.57) by a wide margin. Against commercial models, ColorCtrl surpasses FLUX.1 Kontext Max (Canny SSIM 0.7607, BG SSIM 0.9165) and GPT-4o (Canny SSIM 0.7431, BG SSIM 0.8755) on all structural-preservation metrics, though commercial models score slightly higher on CLIP similarity (28.36/28.84 vs. 27.34). On the video benchmark (CogVideoX-2B), ColorCtrl achieves Canny SSIM 0.8651 and BG SSIM 0.9916, again leading all baselines. A user study with 24 experts confirms ColorCtrl is preferred in 64.71% of pairwise comparisons.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ColorCtrl outperforms all six training-free baselines (FireFlow, RF-Solver, SDEdit, DiTCtrl, FlowEdit, UniEdit-Flow) on every metric across both SD3 and FLUX.1-dev, with the largest gaps in Canny SSIM (+0.05 to +0.10) and BG Preservation SSIM (+0.016 to +0.033). The closest competitor, UniEdit-Flow, trails by 0.07 in Canny SSIM and 0.016 in BG SSIM on FLUX.1-dev. Against commercial models, ColorCtrl wins decisively on structural preservation (Canny SSIM 0.9196 vs. 0.7607 for FLUX.1 Kontext Max and 0.7431 for GPT-4o) but scores slightly lower on CLIP similarity (27.34 vs. 28.36/28.84), which the authors attribute to commercial models applying over-saturated, unrealistic colors to maximize prompt alignment. On the VIEScore editing-quality metrics (SC and PQ), ColorCtrl achieves 7.100 and 9.025 on FLUX.1-dev, surpassing both commercial models (SC 8.525/8.900, PQ 7.625/7.050) in perceptual quality while matching or exceeding them in semantic consistency. For real-image editing, ColorCtrl outperforms FlowEdit and PnP-Inversion on all structural and background-preservation metrics without requiring any hyperparameter re-tuning, whereas those baselines need careful per-backbone layer and timestep selection.

## Relation to Prior Work

ColorCtrl extends the dual-branch attention-control paradigm introduced by Prompt-to-Prompt (Hertz et al., 2023) and adapted to MM-DiT by DiTCtrl (Cai et al., 2025), but departs in three key ways: it operates on attention maps rather than key/value tokens, applies control across all layers and timesteps without manual selection, and introduces a color-preservation mechanism via mask extraction from vision-to-text attention. The shift from U-Net cross-attention to MM-DiT's unified self-attention is what enables these finer-grained manipulations, as the four quadrants of the joint attention map (vision-to-vision, vision-to-text, text-to-vision, text-to-text) each serve distinct roles in structure preservation, mask extraction, attribute re-weighting, and text guidance. Unlike prior training-free editing methods that require careful tuning of which layers and timesteps to intervene on, ColorCtrl's design is parameter-free with respect to layer/timestep selection, requiring only a single mask threshold.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that precise, physically consistent color editing (preserving geometry, material, reflections, refractions, and light-matter interactions) is achievable without any training, and that a training-free method on open-source models can outperform commercial systems like FLUX.1 Kontext Max and GPT-4o on structural and background consistency. A notable finding is that CLIP similarity—a standard evaluation metric—can be misleading for color editing, as commercial models achieve higher scores by over-saturating colors to match prompts while sacrificing realism; this motivates the use of VIEScore (SC/PQ) and gradient-based structural metrics. Open questions include whether the method's reliance on the underlying model's text-guided localization limits its robustness (e.g., failing to distinguish 'red trees' from 'green trees'), and whether real-image editing can be improved as inversion techniques mature. The model-agnostic design and new ColorCtrl-Bench (300 pairs) provide practical tools for the community.
