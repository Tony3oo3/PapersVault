## Problem

Prior work measures LLM novelty solely as originality (fraction of unseen n-grams relative to training data), but highly original outputs can be incoherent or low-quality. Conversely, human-preference-based quality metrics may favor memorized text, making them unreliable novelty signals. The paper asks: how can we jointly measure originality and quality to identify truly novel LLM generations, and what factors (scaling, post-training, inference-time methods) shift the novelty frontier?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263400/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes LLM-generated text alongside the model's full pre- and post-training corpus (indexed via WIMBD API and Infinigram for Dolma, Pile, and related datasets) to compute the fraction of unseen n-grams at n=4, 5, and 6. Quality is scored by o3-mini using task-specific additive 5-point rubrics (validated against human annotations with Spearman correlations of 0.50–0.52), averaged over 5 sampling runs and normalized to [0,1]. The final novelty score is a single harmonic mean per generation, reported as both average and top-10% values across three open-ended creative tasks: story completion (TinyStories), poetry writing (CoPoet), and creative tool use (MacGyver).

## Method

The core contribution is a novelty metric defined as the harmonic mean of two components: (1) originality, computed as the proportion of n-grams (n=4,5,6) in a generation that do not appear verbatim in the model's training corpora, and (2) task-specific quality, scored by an LLM-as-a-judge (o3-mini, 5-sample average) using additive rubrics that reward coherence, constraint adherence, and logical progression. The harmonic mean ensures that a generation must score well on both dimensions to achieve high novelty, unlike prior metrics that optimize only one axis. The paper then applies this metric to systematically ablate factors affecting novelty across OLMo, OLMo-2, and Pythia model families: scaling (1B→32B), post-training stages (base→SFT→DPO/RLVR→Instruct), base-model improvements at fixed scale (OLMo→OLMo-2, Pythia→Pythia-DDP), and inference-time interventions (sampling temperature 0.5–2.0, novel in-context examples, explicit novelty prompting, and iterative denial prompting that blocks previously used high-level concepts). Key findings show that scaling and post-training improve novelty primarily through quality gains, while base-model improvements drive originality gains, and inference-time methods yield only marginal novelty shifts by trading small originality increases against quality losses.

## Results

The paper introduces novelty as the harmonic mean of n-gram originality (fraction of unseen n-grams in training data) and LLM-as-a-judge quality (o3-mini, 5-sample average, normalized 0–1). On TinyStories, OLMo-1B falls below the human-written baseline (−0.096* at n=5), while OLMo-2-7B-Instruct exceeds it by +0.378* (n=5). On CoPoet, OLMo-2-7B-Instruct achieves the largest gain at +0.409* (n=5), and on MacGyver, OLMo-2-32B-Instruct reaches +0.198* (n=5). Scaling from 1B to 7B within a family improves novelty primarily through quality gains, while moving from OLMo to OLMo-2 at the same scale improves novelty through higher originality (e.g., n=5 originality jumps from 0.374 to 0.758 on TinyStories).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is human-written reference text from each dataset (scored against Dolma for OLMo models or the Pile for Pythia models). Small base LLMs (OLMo-1B, OLMo-7B) produce less novel output than human text on all three tasks, with the gap largest on MacGyver (−0.416 at n=5 for OLMo-1B). Post-training closes and reverses this gap: OLMo-7B-Instruct surpasses the baseline on TinyStories (+0.044*) and CoPoet (+0.231*) but still lags on MacGyver (−0.168). The OLMo-2 family, benefiting from a stronger base model, exceeds the baseline on all tasks even without post-training at 7B (e.g., +0.225* on TinyStories), and the Instruct variants achieve the largest margins (up to +0.409* on CoPoet). Inference-time interventions (novel ICL examples, asking for novelty, denial prompting) yield only marginal novelty changes (typically <10% relative), often trading a small originality gain for a quality loss, in stark contrast to the 20–50% gains from scaling or post-training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the n-gram originality measurement framework established by McCoy et al. (2023) and Merrill et al. (2024), and the creativity-index approach of Lu et al. (2024a), by introducing a joint metric that combines originality (unseen n-gram fraction) with task-specific quality via a harmonic mean. Prior work treated these as separate axes—originality-only metrics (RAVEN, Rusty-Dawg, Creativity Index) or quality-only metrics (Chatbot Arena human preference)—without a unified score. The denial-prompting technique from Lu et al. (2024b) is repurposed here as an inference-time baseline rather than a core contribution. Operationally, the paper leverages WIMBD and Infinigram indexing tools to ground originality in actual training corpora (Dolma, Pile), enabling cross-family comparison across OLMo, OLMo-2, and Pythia.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical, reproducible metric for tracking LLM novelty over time and across model families, and releases 5000+ scored generations to support future research. A key open question is extending the framework to black-box frontier models, where the authors suggest providers could report aggregate originality scores without exposing training data. The finding that inference-time interventions (temperature, novel ICL, denial prompting) barely move the novelty frontier is practically important: it suggests that improving creative output requires better training or post-training rather than cleverer prompting, motivating further research into elicitation strategies. Limitations include restriction to open-data models, integer quality scores (1–5), and only three creative tasks.
