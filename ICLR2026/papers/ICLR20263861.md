## Problem

The standard retrieve-and-rerank pipeline is bottlenecked by initial retrieval quality: LLM-based rerankers are too expensive to scan beyond a small top-k, so accuracy is capped by how well the embedding model surfaces relevant documents in the first stage. The paper asks: given a fixed reranker budget, how should we strategically select which documents to rerank to maximize retrieval accuracy on complex, reasoning-intensive queries?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263861/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a search query (text, image, or both), pre-computed document embeddings, and a DiskANN proximity graph over the corpus. A listwise LLM reranker (Gemini-2.0-Flash) is called in sliding windows of size w=10 to reorder candidate documents, with a hard budget of k total documents seen (k=100/300/500). The output is a ranked list of documents from which the top-10 are used to compute NDCG@10. For multimodal inputs (M-BEIR), CLIP text and image encoders produce separate embeddings that are combined via element-wise addition.

## Method

Reranker-Guided-Search (RGS) replaces sequential reranking with a greedy beam search over a DiskANN proximity graph, guided by reranker scores rather than embedding similarity. Starting from seed documents found by first-stage ANN search, RGS iteratively picks the highest-ranked unexpanded document, appends all of its graph neighbors to the search list, and reorders the list using a sliding-window listwise reranker. The key insight is the clustering hypothesis: documents similar to a reranker-approved document are likely relevant, so expanding the neighborhood of top-ranked documents concentrates the reranker budget on promising regions. This bi-metric design—traversing a graph built under embedding similarity while ranking by reranker score—means the embedding model only needs to preserve document-document structure, not query-document relevance, making RGS robust to poor query embeddings. The total reranker call complexity is O(L²/w) where L is the search list size, and the algorithm terminates when the reranker budget is exhausted.

## Results

On BRIGHT (reasoning-intensive retrieval), RGS achieves NDCG@10 of 28.8 at Reranker@100 and 33.0 at Reranker@500, improving over sequential retrieve-and-rerank (25.3 / 27.7) by +3.5 and +5.3 points respectively. On M-BEIR (multi-modal retrieval), RGS reaches 31.0 and 38.3 NDCG@10 at Reranker@100/500, a +5.1 and +8.2 point gain over the sequential baseline. On FollowIR (instruction following), RGS achieves 63.3 and 64.2 NDCG@10, a more modest +2.9 and +2.6 point improvement, consistent with the observation that 87% of ground-truth answers already appear in the top-100 embedding-similarity results on this benchmark.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RGS outperforms both baselines on all three benchmarks at every reranker budget tested. Against Retrieve-and-Rerank (RR), RGS gains +3.5 NDCG@10 on BRIGHT and +5.1 on M-BEIR at Reranker@100, with the gap widening to +5.3 and +8.2 at Reranker@500, indicating that the advantage of strategic document selection grows with budget. Against SlideGAR, RGS achieves a clear margin on BRIGHT (28.8 vs. 25.4 at Reranker@100; 33.0 vs. 26.9 at Reranker@500) and M-BEIR (31.0 vs. 28.6; 38.3 vs. 29.9), while on FollowIR the gap is smaller (63.3 vs. 62.5 at Reranker@100) but widens at Reranker@500 (64.2 vs. 59.8), where SlideGAR actually degrades below RR. The paper attributes RGS's advantage to its greedy graph traversal that explores all neighbors of the most promising document, whereas SlideGAR only retains w/2 graph-frontier candidates per round, potentially discarding relevant documents whose embeddings are not close to the query.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the bi-metric search framework of Xu et al. (2024), which showed that a proximity graph built under one distance metric can be used to search under a different metric, by applying it to the reranking problem: the graph is built on embedding similarity while the search is guided by reranker scores. It also builds on DiskANN's greedy traversal over proximity graphs, repurposing the ANN search machinery for document reranking. The paper departs from prior graph-based reranking methods (GAR, RAR, SlideGAR) by maintaining a globally up-to-date reranker-ordered list and performing full neighborhood expansion of the most promising document, rather than alternately sampling from a shortlist and a graph frontier. Unlike those prior works, which were evaluated only on classical TREC data, RGS is tested on reasoning-intensive, multi-modal, and instruction-following benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that under a fixed reranker budget, *how* you select documents to rerank matters as much as *which* reranker you use, and that a graph-guided greedy search can substantially outperform sequential scanning on complex retrieval tasks. A notable finding is that at high reranker budgets, final accuracy is determined primarily by the reranker's alignment with ground truth rather than by embedding quality, and that RGS remains robust even when the query embedding carries no useful signal—suggesting the first-stage ANN search is not essential. The main limitation is that adaptive, sequential document selection may hinder parallelization of reranker calls, and the approach's dependence on a well-structured proximity graph (it degrades on KNN graphs and fails on random graphs) constrains its applicability. Open questions include whether the method extends to iterative or multi-hop retrieval settings and whether the reranker-budget evaluation protocol (Reranker@k) becomes a standard way to compare retrieval pipelines.
