## Problem

Scaling generative AI models is bottlenecked by the scarcity of high-quality training data, and naively fine-tuning a model on its own synthetic outputs leads to model collapse (MAD) with rapid degradation in quality and diversity. Existing workarounds—external verifiers, auxiliary discriminators, negative guidance, or likelihood-based discrimination—add significant computational overhead, are restricted to specific architectures, or require complex iterative training. The paper asks whether the degradation signal from self-training can itself be harnessed as a corrective direction without any of these additional resources.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263515/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained base generative model (diffusion, flow matching, autoregressive, or few-step) and a small set of synthetic images (as few as 1k) generated from that model's own inference procedure, requiring no additional real training data, no access to the original training set, and no auxiliary models. It produces improved model parameters via a single linear merge θ_Neon = (1+w)θ_r − wθ_s, where w is a scalar extrapolation strength tuned on a validation set. The entire procedure uses less than 1% of the original training compute and preserves the base model's architecture and inference pipeline unchanged.

## Method

Neon exploits a key theoretical insight: mode-seeking inference samplers (temperature < 1, top-k/top-p sampling, classifier-free guidance, finite-step ODE solvers) create a predictable anti-alignment between the synthetic-data gradient and the real-data population gradient, formalized as a negative preconditioned inner product s = ⟨r_d, Pr_s⟩ < 0. The procedure is three steps: (i) generate a synthetic dataset S from the base model's inference routine, (ii) briefly fine-tune on S (using <1% of the original training budget) to obtain a degraded model θ_s, and (iii) negatively extrapolate via the parameter merge θ_Neon = (1+w)θ_r − wθ_s, moving away from the degradation direction. The paper proves (Theorems 1–2) that for any model near its optimum, mode-seeking samplers guarantee cos φ < 0 between the model error and sampler bias, ensuring s < 0 and thus that the linear term in the Taylor expansion of the data risk is negative. Empirically, the improvement mechanism operates as a precision-recall trade-off: synthetic fine-tuning concentrates probability mass on over-represented modes, and reversing this direction redistributes mass to under-represented regions, boosting recall and net FID. The method is architecture-agnostic (validated on EDM-VP, flow matching, VAR, xAR, and IMM) and the degradation signal is even transferable across architectures, enabling synthetic data from one model family to improve another.

## Results

On ImageNet-256, Neon elevates xAR-L from FID 1.28 to a new state-of-the-art 1.02 with only 0.36% additional training compute, surpassing the previous best (REPA-UCGM, FID 1.06). On CIFAR-10, Neon+EDM-VP improves conditional FID from 1.78 to 1.38 (1.75% extra compute) and Neon+Flow Matching improves unconditional FID from 3.50 to 2.32 (3.2% extra compute). On FFHQ-64, Neon+EDM-VP reduces unconditional FID from 2.39 to 1.12 (0.85% extra compute). For few-step IMM on ImageNet-256, Neon achieves FID 1.69 at 4 steps (nearly matching the base model's 8-step FID of 1.98) with <0.005% additional compute.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Neon's headline result is xAR-L+Neon achieving FID 1.02 on ImageNet-256, surpassing the previous SOTA REPA-UCGM (FID 1.06) by 0.04 FID with only 0.36% additional compute. Against the closest post-hoc competitor DDO, Neon wins decisively on autoregressive models: VAR-d16+Neon (FID 2.01) beats VAR-d16+DDO (FID 2.54) by 0.53 FID, and VAR-d30+Neon (FID 1.70) beats VAR-d30+DDO (FID 1.79) by 0.09 FID. On diffusion models, Neon is competitive but slightly behind: EDM+Neon (FID 1.38) trails EDM+DDO (FID 1.30) on CIFAR-10 conditional and EDM+SIMS (FID 1.33) on CIFAR-10 unconditional, though Neon uses far less compute (1.75% vs. 12% for DDO) and fewer NFE (35 vs. 70 for SIMS). On FFHQ-64, Neon (FID 1.12) is outperformed by one-step D2O-F (FID 0.85) and SIMS (FID 1.04), but Neon preserves the base model's 79 NFE sampling cost. Neon's key advantage is universality: it works across diffusion, flow matching, autoregressive, and few-step architectures with a single parameter-merge operation, whereas DDO cannot apply to likelihood-free models (flow matching, IMM) and SIMS is diffusion-specific.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the model collapse / MAD literature (Alemohammad et al., 2024a; Shumailov et al., 2024), which established that self-training on synthetic data predictably degrades generative models. It departs from prior post-hoc improvement methods (SIMS, DDO, Discriminator Guidance) that require architecture-specific machinery—external verifiers, auxiliary discriminators, likelihood computations, or inference-time modifications—by reframing the degradation itself as a usable signal. The key conceptual departure is the negative extrapolation: rather than preventing or correcting collapse through additional models or iterative training, Neon simply reverses the parameter displacement caused by a brief self-training step via a single linear merge. The theoretical contribution (Theorems 1–2) that mode-seeking samplers guarantee the required gradient anti-alignment is new and unifies the explanation across diffusion, flow matching, autoregressive, and few-step architectures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Neon's practical appeal is its extreme simplicity: a single parameter merge requiring no new real data, no auxiliary models, no inference changes, and less than 1% additional compute, yet it achieves a new SOTA FID of 1.02 on ImageNet-256 and consistent gains across four model families and three datasets. The cross-architecture transferability (e.g., using flow-matching synthetic data to improve a diffusion model) broadens its applicability when generating from the target model is costly. Open questions the authors raise include whether diversity-seeking samplers could enable direct (non-inverted) self-improvement, and whether one can actively synthesize optimal 'bad' datasets that maximize the corrective signal. A limitation is that the method requires a reasonably trained base model and a tuned extrapolation weight w, though ablations show robustness across a wide quality spectrum and synthetic-data quality range.
