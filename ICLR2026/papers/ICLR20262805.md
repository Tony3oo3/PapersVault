## Problem

Language-adapted LLMs (via continual pretraining or instruction tuning) suffer catastrophic forgetting of general factual knowledge, a problem especially acute in multilingual settings where adaptation overrides pretrained world knowledge with language-specific patterns. Existing mitigations either require access to the original pretraining data for retraining or apply generic decoding tweaks that do not explicitly recover the factual knowledge lost in the pretrained model's weights. This paper asks whether pretrained factual knowledge can be selectively reintroduced at inference time, without any additional training or pretraining data access.

## Input / Output

At inference, LGCD consumes a text prompt and a language-adapted model (LAM) whose FFN layers have been augmented with pre-computed LoRA matrices (rank-32, derived once offline via SVD of the PTM–LAM weight difference). The confidence threshold τ is fixed at 0.9 for multiple-choice QA but is language-specific for long-form generation, calibrated by OSCAR corpus word counts (e.g., τ=0.8 for German/Chinese, τ=0.1 for Korean/Indonesian/Swahili). Output is a generated text sequence or a multiple-choice answer, produced token-by-token with Top-K=100 sampling at temperature 0.7.

## Method

LGCD is a training-free, three-stage decoding framework. First, it performs a one-time offline LoRA extraction: for each FFN layer ℓ, it computes ΔW_ℓ = W^PTM_ℓ − W^LAM_ℓ, applies SVD, and retains the top-r singular components to form low-rank matrices A_ℓ, B_ℓ such that the approximated pretrained weight is W^aPTM_ℓ = W^LAM_ℓ + A_ℓB_ℓ; only FFN layers are modified (validated in ablation against QV-only and all-layer variants). Second, at each decoding step it measures token-level confidence c_t = max(softmax(l_t^LAM)) and applies a binary gate: if c_t ≥ τ the LAM decodes alone, otherwise contrastive decoding is triggered. Third, when triggered, LGCD computes aPTM logits via the LoRA correction, restricts the candidate set to the LAM's Top-K tokens, and scores each candidate i as l_t^LAM[i] + β·(l_t^aPTM[i] − α·l_t^LAM[i]) (with β=1.0, α=0.1), setting all other tokens to −∞. This correction term prioritizes the aPTM's factual signal while gently penalizing overconfident LAM predictions, and the Top-K constraint prevents the contrastive mechanism from selecting tokens that are low-probability in both models.

## Results

On Global MMLU across 12 language-adapted LLMs covering 9 languages, LGCD achieves 0.477 average accuracy in 0-shot and 0.498 in 5-shot settings, outperforming the best baseline (TIES at 0.449/0.488) by +2.8 pp and +1.0 pp respectively. On Multilingual TruthfulQA, LGCD reaches 0.376 (0-shot) and 0.435 (5-shot), surpassing the next-best method by +4.5 pp and +6.5 pp. On Multi-FAct, LGCD improves factual consistency by +0.040 average over LAMs, with the largest gain in Indonesian (+0.212). In long-form medical QA, GPT-4o judges prefer LGCD over PTM in 63.1% of pairwise comparisons and over LAM in 53.5%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LGCD is compared against five baselines: the pretrained model (PTM) and language-adapted model (LAM) both using Nucleus Sampling, the DoLa layer-contrastive decoding strategy, and two model-merging methods (TIES and SLERP). On Global MMLU 0-shot, LGCD (0.477) outperforms TIES (0.449, +2.8 pp), SLERP (0.445, +3.2 pp), PTM (0.448, +2.9 pp), LAM (0.441, +3.6 pp), and DoLa (0.439, +3.8 pp); in 5-shot it leads TIES (0.488) by +1.0 pp and LAM (0.481) by +1.7 pp. On Multilingual TruthfulQA, LGCD's advantage is even larger: 0.376 vs. LAM 0.334 (+4.2 pp) and TIES 0.331 (+4.5 pp) in 0-shot, and 0.435 vs. TIES 0.370 (+6.5 pp) in 5-shot. DoLa consistently underperforms LGCD across all benchmarks, suggesting that layer-contrastive decoding within a single model is less effective than explicitly recovering pretrained FFN knowledge. Model-merging baselines (TIES, SLERP) are competitive in 5-shot Global MMLU but fall behind LGCD in 0-shot and on TruthfulQA, indicating that static weight interpolation cannot match dynamic, confidence-gated knowledge injection. In long-form medical QA, LGCD is preferred over TIES in 65.3% of GPT-4o pairwise comparisons and over SLERP in 51.9%, while human evaluators rate LGCD above PTM (52.0%) and DoLa (45.8%). The one setting where LGCD does not clearly dominate is Indonesian 5-shot Global MMLU, where the LAM already outperforms the PTM (0.576 vs. 0.529) and LGCD (0.566) slightly trails the LAM, suggesting the method is most beneficial when adaptation has degraded pretrained knowledge.

## Relation to Prior Work

LGCD sits at the intersection of two research threads: contrastive decoding (Li et al., 2023), which reshapes token probabilities by contrasting a 'good' model against a 'bad' one, and the FFN-as-key-value-memory literature (Geva et al., 2020; Qiu et al., 2024), which identifies feed-forward layers as the primary repository of factual knowledge in transformers. The key departure from prior contrastive decoding work (e.g., DoLa, which contrasts layers within a single model) is that LGCD contrasts two distinct models—the language-adapted model and a LoRA-approximated version of the original pretrained model—enabling explicit recovery of knowledge lost during adaptation. Unlike static model-merging approaches (TIES, SLERP, task arithmetic), LGCD is dynamic: a confidence-gated mechanism decides token-by-token whether to invoke the pretrained knowledge, preserving domain fluency when the adapted model is confident and correcting it when uncertain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that LGCD offers a training-free, inference-time remedy for catastrophic forgetting in language-adapted LLMs without requiring access to original pretraining data at deployment time—only a one-time offline LoRA extraction from the PTM weights. This is particularly relevant for the growing ecosystem of publicly released language-specific LLaMA-3 variants where the adaptation process is opaque and the original pretraining corpus is unavailable. Key limitations include the dependency on PTM weights for the initial extraction (ruling out fully closed-source scenarios) and the reliance on logit-based confidence, which can be miscalibrated in overconfident low-resource models. Open questions include whether the FFN-only approximation generalizes to non-LLaMA architectures, whether adaptive confidence thresholds (explored in Appendix A.11) can fully replace the heuristic language-resource-based calibration, and whether the approach extends to domain-adapted (rather than language-adapted) models where the 'factual knowledge' boundary is less clear.
