## Problem

Prior work established that verifier generation capability correlates with verification accuracy, but the broader dynamics of verification—how problem difficulty, generator capability, and verifier capability jointly determine verification success—remain largely unexplored. This gap risks misallocating computational resources by defaulting to expensive frontier models as verifiers when simpler alternatives might suffice, and obscures the non-linear regimes where scaling verifiers provides no benefit.

## Input / Output

The generative verifier receives a problem x and a candidate response r (consisting of a chain-of-thought solution and a final answer a(r)), and produces a verification CoT followed by a binary verdict token ('Correct' or 'Incorrect'). For test-time scaling evaluation, K=64 responses are sampled per problem from the generator, the verifier filters them, and performance is measured as the conditional pass rate of the retained pool. Problem difficulty is defined model-agnostically as the average pass rate across 15 diverse generators, enabling a shared difficulty axis independent of any single model's capability.

## Method

The paper conducts a systematic empirical study across 12 benchmarks (mathematical reasoning, knowledge QA, NL reasoning) using 14 open-source models (2B–72B) and GPT-4o, varying three controlled factors: problem difficulty (quartile-binned by model-agnostic pass rate), generator capability, and verifier generation capability. Verification performance is decomposed into TPR (correctness recognition) and TNR (error detection) to isolate which factor drives which aspect. Locally weighted regression with nonparametric and linear fits characterizes the verifier capability–verification accuracy relationship, revealing three distinct regimes: threshold-limited (hard problems), linear (medium problems), and saturated (easy problems). Large-scale LLM-as-judge analysis (367K verification CoTs, 98K generator responses) provides mechanistic explanations: verifiers generate faulty reference solutions on hard problems (driving FNs), and strong generators produce internally consistent but incorrect reasoning that resists detection (driving TNR decline). TTS experiments demonstrate that weak generators paired with a strong verifier can close 75.7% of the performance gap to stronger generators, and that weak verifiers match strong ones at difficulty extremes and with strong generators.

## Results

Across 12 benchmarks (2,347 math, 1,196 knowledge, 901 NL reasoning problems) and 15 models (2B–72B + GPT-4o), the paper identifies three core verification dynamics: (1) TPR rises steadily as problem difficulty decreases, while TNR shows no systematic difficulty trend; (2) TNR drops sharply as generator capability increases (e.g., Qwen2.5-72B verifier TNR on Math falls from 0.68 for Llama-3.1-8B solutions to 0.17 for Qwen3-32B solutions); (3) verifier generation capability correlates with balanced accuracy linearly on medium problems (r > 0.9) but shows threshold-limited saturation on hard problems and a threshold effect near capability 0.9 on easy problems. In TTS, pairing a weak generator (Gemma2-9B) with GPT-4o as verifier closes 75.7% of the performance gap to Gemma2-27B (10.3% → 2.5%), and 39.1% of false-negative verifications on hard problems are attributable to the verifier's own solving mistakes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical study rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so the 'comparisons' are systematic ablations over three axes. On the generator axis (RQ4), weak-to-medium generators (e.g., Gemma2-9B, Llama-3.2-3B) achieve the highest verification gain Δp_V when paired with GPT-4o, nearly matching the post-verification pass rate of much stronger generators (Gemma2-27B, Qwen3-32B); the gap shrinks by 30–50% across most weak/strong pairs on full-domain evaluation. On the verifier axis (RQ5), the verification gain gap between GPT-4o and Qwen2.5-7B narrows at both difficulty extremes (easiest and hardest quartiles) and when evaluating strong generators, though in all these convergence regimes both verifiers yield minimal absolute gains (≤ 0.1). On the difficulty axis (RQ1), TPR increases monotonically from hardest to easiest quartiles across all three domains and all 15 verifiers, while TNR remains flat. Reasoning models (Qwen3-8B/32B-Thinking) preserve the TPR-difficulty pattern but additionally show TNR improving on easier problems, unlike instruction-tuned models. On hard NL Reasoning problems, verifiers fall below the 0.5 random baseline due to a solve-and-match failure mode in small answer spaces (3-way MCQs).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the generative verification paradigm (Zhang et al., 2024/2025; Mahan et al., 2024), where verification is framed as CoT reasoning followed by a binary verdict, by systematically studying the dynamics that govern verification success. Prior work (Chen et al., 2025c; Krumdick et al., 2025; Tan et al., 2025) established a positive correlation between a verifier's generation capability and its verification accuracy, but this paper reveals that relationship is non-linear and difficulty-dependent, and identifies two previously unexplored factors—problem difficulty and generator capability—that critically shape verification performance. It also connects to the test-time scaling literature (Snell et al., 2025; Brown et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2025) by translating its findings into practical guidance for generator-verifier pairing in TTS pipelines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The findings have direct practical implications for TTS deployment: weak generators can nearly match strong ones post-verification (75.7% gap reduction for Gemma2-9B vs. 27B), and in certain regimes (easy/hard problems, strong generators) a 7B open-source verifier performs comparably to GPT-4o, suggesting significant cost savings are possible. However, the paper also identifies fundamental limits—scaling verifiers alone cannot overcome verification challenges when errors are subtle or problems are extremely hard—pointing to open questions about how to design verifiers that detect high-level reasoning failures. The work is limited to verifiable problems with objective answers and instruction-tuned models (plus two reasoning models), leaving open whether these dynamics hold in open-ended generation or with future model architectures.
