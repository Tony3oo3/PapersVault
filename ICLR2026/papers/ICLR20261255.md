## Problem

Single-policy long-CoT approaches for inference-time scaling suffer from long-horizon plan failures, where the implicit plan drifts from valid strategies—especially for small LMs with limited capacity. Additionally, outcome-reward RL provides sparse, delayed feedback over trajectories of several thousand tokens, making credit assignment difficult and training unstable. No existing method combines structured multi-level planning with scalable process-level supervision for long-CoT generation.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text query and produces an alternating sequence of high-level step descriptors (specifying reasoning mode and semantic subgoal) and low-level detailed reasoning content, terminated by a final answer. A lightweight summarizer compresses each step's detailed content into a compact summary that conditions the next high-level descriptor, keeping the planner's context short. High-level trajectories are roughly 10–20% the length of low-level ones, and the entire generation is capped at 16,384 tokens.

## Method

MLR reformulates long-CoT generation as a two-level stochastic process: a high-level planner (a lightweight LoRA adapter on the base LM) generates step descriptors specifying reasoning mode and subgoal, while the low-level executor (the base LM with LoRA disabled) produces detailed reasoning conditioned on those descriptors, forming an alternating plan-execute loop that allows mid-course plan adaptation. For training, the paper introduces iterative Step-DPO with process-level preferences constructed via Twisted Sequential Monte Carlo (TSMC), which assigns importance weights to partial trajectories based on estimated future solvability, upweighting continuations more likely to reach a correct answer. The key insight is that taking log-increments of TSMC weights converts multiplicative updates into an additive utility that directly matches the pairwise preference objective of Step-DPO, eliminating the need for a separate process reward model. A small fine-tuned LM serves as the rollout policy to keep TSMC computation efficient, and a margin threshold δ filters unreliable preference pairs. The two policies are trained with an interleaved update scheme that alternates mini-batches of high-level and low-level preference pairs while keeping parameter updates modular.

## Results

MLR achieves the best average Pass@1 across all three base models: 54.2 on Qwen-2.5-1.5B, 66.2 on Qwen-2.5-MATH-7B, and 66.1 on Llama-3.1-8B, outperforming the strongest RL baseline (SFT+GRPO) by 5.8, 6.3, and 6.1 points respectively. On the hardest benchmark (AIME24 Cons@32), MLR reaches 47.4, 80.5, and 73.3 for the 1.5B, 7B, and 8B models, surpassing DeepSeek-R1-Distill (43.3, 78.0, 70.0) and SFT+GRPO (36.0, 66.2, 61.0). All results are obtained under a reduced data budget of ~10% SFT and ~5% preference data relative to the full DeepSeek-R1 distillation setup.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MLR consistently outperforms all six baselines across every benchmark and all three base models. Against the strongest distillation baseline (DeepSeek-R1-Distill), MLR gains +2.2 to +5.0 points on MATH500, +2.3 to +3.4 on AIME24 Pass@1, and a particularly large +16.0 to +21.0 on BoardGameQA-Hard, indicating the structured planner is most beneficial for logical reasoning. Against the strongest RL baseline (SFT+GRPO), MLR leads by +4.0 to +5.0 on MATH500, +9.2 to +11.2 on AIME24 Pass@1, and +3.8 to +5.8 on GPQA-Diamond. Against SFT+Step-DPO (the closest preference-optimization baseline), MLR gains +4.7 to +5.8 average Pass@1, confirming that the multi-level architecture contributes beyond step-level supervision alone. The SFT-only variant of MLR (without iterative Step-DPO) trails the full method by 10–24 points, underscoring the importance of the TSMC-based process preferences. All self-trained baselines use the identical data budget (~80K SFT examples, ~10K preference prompts), ensuring a controlled comparison.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the single-policy long-CoT paradigm established by OpenAI o1 and DeepSeek-R1 by introducing a hierarchical planner-executor architecture inspired by hierarchical reinforcement learning, where a lightweight LoRA module generates step descriptors and the base LM produces detailed reasoning in an alternating loop. For training, it extends Step-DPO (Lai et al., 2024) by repurposing Twisted Sequential Monte Carlo from the particle-filtering literature to construct step-level preferences without a separate process reward model, addressing the sparse-reward credit-assignment problem that plagues outcome-reward RL (GRPO, PPO) on long trajectories. The approach also contrasts with plan-then-execute prompting (Plan-and-Solve), which is brittle to mid-course failures, by allowing the high-level plan to adapt dynamically based on execution progress.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical, scalable alternative to expensive outcome-reward RL for training long-CoT reasoning in small LMs, eliminating the need for a separate PRM while delivering stepwise supervision throughout trajectories. The finding that structured multi-level reasoning degrades more slowly under long-horizon evaluation (concatenated multi-question prompts) suggests that explicit planning is a key ingredient for robustness, not just accuracy. Limitations include reliance on DeepSeek-R1 traces for SFT initialization, the added summarizer component, and evaluation only on models up to 8B parameters; whether the hierarchical structure continues to help at larger scales or in tool-augmented settings remains open.
