## Problem

Existing data filtering methods for instruction fine-tuning rely on predefined reward models or manually designed quality metrics, creating a mismatch between the filtering criteria and the actual needs of the specific LLM being fine-tuned. This paper asks whether the target LLM's own internal representations (hidden states) can serve as a more aligned signal for identifying which training samples will be most beneficial.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262114/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes instruction-response triplets (Instruction, Input, Response) from datasets such as Alpaca_GPT4 (52K entries), Reasoning-DeepSeek (146K entries), GSM8K, and Magicoder-Evol-Instruct-110K. Each triplet is fed through the target LLM, and only the hidden states corresponding to the Response portion are retained as feature vectors. The output is a per-sample CPQS probability (0–1) produced by a trained CNN, from which the top-K highest-scoring samples are selected for downstream fine-tuning.

## Method

The core insight is that LLM hidden states linearly separate high-quality from low-quality data (validated with AUC = 1.00 on a logistic probe). High-quality samples are GPT-4-generated Alpaca_GPT4 entries; low-quality samples are regenerated responses from small models (Llama-3.2-1B, Qwen2.5-1.5B) on the same instructions. A CNN with 2D and 1D convolutions, adaptive max pooling, and fully connected layers is trained as a binary classifier on these hidden-state vectors using a 1:2 positive-to-negative ratio and cross-entropy loss (Adam, lr=1e-4, AMP). At inference, the CNN outputs the probability of the positive class as the CPQS, which ranks all samples; the top-K are retained for LoRA or full-parameter fine-tuning. The approach is model-specific: swapping datasets selected by one LLM for another degrades performance, confirming that the hidden-state signal encodes each model's own quality perception.

## Results

On Alpaca_GPT4 with Llama2-7B, selecting only 5k samples (~10% of the 52k dataset) yields a macro-average of 54.40% across MMLU, ARC, TruthfulQA, and HellaSwag, and a 59.94% AlpacaEval win-rate, both surpassing the full-dataset model (54.37% / 59.81%). On Reasoning-DeepSeek with Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, a 10k filtered subset achieves a 65.45% average across GSM8K, Math_500, HumanEval, and GPQA, exceeding the full 146k dataset (60.92%) by 4.53 points. In downstream tasks, the method delivers an average gain of 3.6 percentage points over the strongest baseline on GSM8K, HumanEval, and HumanEval-Plus.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CPQS consistently outperforms all three baselines—Alpagasus, MoDs, and Superfiltering—across every dataset and model tested. On Alpaca_GPT4 at 1k samples, CPQS leads by 0.76 points in average score over Alpagasus (52.68 vs. 51.92) and by 6.33 points in AlpacaEval win-rate (55.98 vs. 49.65); at 2k, the AlpacaEval gap over MoDs widens to 4.48 points (57.90 vs. 53.42). On Reasoning-DeepSeek, CPQS at 10k (65.45 avg) surpasses Superfiltering at 20k (60.00) by 5.45 points and Alpagasus at 113k (61.04) by 4.41 points, while also beating the full 146k dataset (60.92) by 4.53 points. In downstream math (GSM8K), CPQS leads by 4.17 points on Llama2-7B-Chat (25.25 vs. 21.08 best baseline) and 3.61 points on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (81.12 vs. 77.51). In code generation, CPQS achieves a 14.95% average pass@1 on Llama2-7B-Chat, outperforming Superfiltering (9.55%) by 5.4 points and Alpagasus (11.3%) by 3.65 points; on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct the margin narrows to 1.47 points over Superfiltering (77.2 vs. 76.25). Notably, CPQS is the only method whose filtered subset at 5k or 10k scale exceeds the full-dataset training result, confirming that model-perception-based selection captures quality signals that external reward models and difficulty-based filters miss.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in instruction-tuning data selection—where external reward models (ALPAGASUS), multi-criteria scoring (MoDS), or weak-model difficulty estimation (Superfiltering) serve as the filtering signal—by instead reading the target LLM's own hidden states as an implicit quality assessment. It repurposes the broader observation that LLM hidden states encode task-relevant information (Xie et al., 2022; Servedio et al., 2025; Qian et al., 2025) for a new application: training a lightweight CNN to discriminate high- from low-quality samples from the model's internal perspective. The quality-tier construction (GPT-4 outputs as positive, small-model outputs as negative) follows the teacher-student data generation pipeline established by Alpaca and subsequent work, but the filtering criterion itself is model-specific rather than externally defined.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway—that under 10% of a large instruction dataset can outperform full-dataset training, and that the 'best' subset is model-specific (swapping selected data between Llama-2-7B-Chat and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct degrades performance)—offers a concrete efficiency lever for practitioners with limited compute. However, the approach requires white-box access to hidden states, limiting applicability to open-weight models, and the quality-tier construction assumes a clean GPT-4-vs-small-model gap that may not hold for all domains. Open questions include whether the CNN selector generalizes to substantially larger or architecturally different models without retraining, how sensitive CPQS is to the specific small models used for negative labels, and whether the method extends naturally to multi-turn or multimodal instruction data.
