## Problem

Molecular geometries in widely used ML datasets (QM9, QMugs, OMol25) are generated by computational chemistry codes that do not randomize pose, resulting in systematic orientation biases that are easily overlooked by visual inspection. This bias can artificially inflate performance of non-equivariant or approximately equivariant models and introduce systematic errors in grid-based tasks like electron density prediction, yet the extent and consequences of this bias had not been systematically quantified for the ML community.

## Input / Output

The method consumes 3D atomic coordinates and atom types in point-cloud format, with no bond graph required. Orientations are extracted as rotation matrices in SO(3) via normalized principal components of centered atom positions (with sign conventions to resolve eigenvector ambiguity), and the key outputs include: a binary label (canonical vs. randomly rotated), pairwise geodesic distance matrices over SO(3), scalar non-uniformity scores (KL divergence and KS statistic), and Mollweide-projection visualizations where each molecule's orientation appears as a triplet of colored points (one per principal axis). For the property-prediction experiment, the input is reduced to just the first two normalized principal components (6 scalars per molecule) fed into an MLP.

## Method

The paper introduces a multi-pronged analysis framework. First, a deliberately rotation-dependent 3-layer geometric message passing network (using Bessel/Gaussian embeddings of relative distances, max aggregation, 10 Å cutoff) is trained via binary cross-entropy to distinguish canonical from uniformly SO(3)-rotated molecules, remaining accurate even under 1 Å Gaussian noise and up to 90° pre-rotation. Second, a quantitative orientation analysis maps each molecule to SO(3) via its normalized principal components, computes all pairwise rotation angles θ(R₁,R₂) = arccos((tr(R₁ᵀR₂)−1)/2), applies a 1D Gaussian KDE at θ=0 to identify the most common orientation, and compares the resulting distance histogram against the theoretical Haar-uniform density p(θ) = (2/π)sin²(θ/2). Third, non-uniformity is summarized in a single number via a manifold-corrected Kozachenko-Leonenko KL divergence estimator that uses the exact geodesic ball volume V(θ) = 8π(θ − sin θ) on SO(3), complemented by a one-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test against the known CDF F(θ) = (θ − sin θ)/π. Finally, the paper demonstrates practical impact by showing that a simple MLP regressing molecular properties from normalized principal components alone significantly outperforms the test-mean baseline on canonical data but not on randomly rotated data, proving that networks can and do exploit the orientation bias.

## Results

A simple geometric message passing network distinguishes canonical from randomly rotated molecules with very high accuracy, even when atom positions are perturbed with 1 Å Gaussian noise and pre-rotated by up to 90°. The estimated KL divergence to the uniform SO(3) distribution is 0.90 for QM9, 1.76 for QMugs, and 1.04 for OMol25, confirming strong non-uniformity. Most strikingly, a simple MLP using only normalized principal components as input achieves a 23% lower MSE than the test-mean baseline for ZPVE on QM9 (0.6204 vs. 0.8107) and a 5.3% lower MSE for total energy on QMugs (843.48 vs. 890.54 Eh), while performing at the mean level on randomly rotated data—demonstrating that orientation bias is directly exploitable for property prediction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an analysis paper rather than a method comparison, so there are no external method baselines. The key internal comparison is between canonical (unrotated) and randomly rotated orientations. On canonical data, the MLP using only principal components as input consistently beats the theoretical best for uninformative features (the test mean) by 2–23% in MSE across all three datasets and all tested properties. On randomly rotated data, the same MLP performs at or slightly above the test-mean level, confirming that the improvement is entirely attributable to the orientation bias rather than any geometric signal. The paper further shows that a non-equivariant Graphormer trained on canonical QM9 achieves a lower MSE (0.000045) than one trained on randomized orientations (0.000062) when both are evaluated on canonical data, illustrating the critical scenario where orientation bias artificially inflates performance. For QMugs nuclear repulsion energy, the randomized training actually helps (MSE 66,544 vs. 77,127), suggesting the bias can also hinder learning of informative patterns.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Lawrence et al. (2025a), who first proposed detecting orientation bias in molecular datasets via a classifier two-sample test (Lopez-Paz & Oquab, 2017). Here, the authors introduce a complementary toolkit—PCA-based orientation mapping, Mollweide-projection visualizations, a manifold-corrected Kozachenko-Leonenko KL-divergence estimator, and a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test—to systematically quantify and visualize the bias across QM9, QMugs, and OMol25. The work also situates orientation bias within the broader framework of distributional symmetry breaking (Wang et al., 2023a) and connects it to the growing trend of relaxed or approximate equivariance in molecular ML architectures (Pertigkiozoglou et al., 2024; Elhag et al., 2024; 2025).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides actionable best practices (random-rotation test sets, data augmentation for non-equivariant models) that are likely to be widely adopted, given that QM9, QMugs, and OMol25 underpin a large fraction of molecular ML benchmarks. A key open question is how to properly handle the bias in equivariant models trained on grid-based targets (e.g., electron density), where the canonical orientation introduces a systematic grid-alignment bias even when the architecture is exactly equivariant. The analysis is limited to a handful of datasets, and the degree to which orientation bias interacts with other data-generating artifacts (e.g., conformer selection, DFT functional choice) remains unexplored.
