## Problem

Continual RL lacks principled theoretical foundations: existing algorithms are motivated by heuristics from disparate perspectives, with no unified way to quantify when knowledge transfer is beneficial, how to measure catastrophic forgetting, or how to set explicit optimization objectives. This gap hinders principled algorithm development and makes it difficult to balance plasticity (rapid adaptation) against stability (knowledge retention) across sequential tasks.

## Input / Output

The method consumes environment observations (210×160 RGB frames for Atari/MinAtar pixel-based tasks, or low-dimensional state vectors for Meta-World robotic manipulation) and outputs actions (discrete for pixel-based, continuous Gaussian-parameterized for control). It operates under semi-continual RL assumptions: known task boundaries, shared state and action spaces across tasks, and a fixed model size. The fast learner produces actions during online interaction, while the meta learner produces a consolidated Q-function or policy that serves as the warm-up source for the next task's fast learner.

## Method

FAME decomposes continual RL into two coupled roles inspired by hippocampal-cortical dynamics: a fast learner that rapidly adapts to a new task via an adaptive meta warm-up (a one-vs-all hypothesis test selecting among the meta learner, the previous fast learner, or random reset, with behavior cloning regularization for value-based or direct parameter initialization for policy-based transfer), and a meta learner that incrementally integrates knowledge by minimizing a formally defined policy-based catastrophic forgetting objective. The key theoretical contribution is showing that minimizing this forgetting reduces to a maximum-likelihood / policy-distillation update under KL divergence (Proposition 1, FAME-KL) or a closed-form weighted-average update under squared 2-Wasserstein distance for Gaussian policies (Proposition 2, FAME-WD), both expressible as incremental rules that only require the preceding meta learner and the current fast learner. A compact meta buffer storing ~1–2% of each task's state-action pairs provides the empirical weights for these updates, keeping memory fixed regardless of the number of tasks.

## Results

On MinAtar (DQN), FAME achieves the highest average performance across all three games (Breakout: 14.54, SpaceInvaders: 18.72, Freeway: 1.69) while simultaneously attaining the lowest forgetting score (0.72 vs. 1.25–1.65 for baselines) and competitive forward transfer (0.16). On Meta-World (SAC), FAME-WD reaches 0.767 average performance with near-zero forgetting (0.023), substantially outperforming Reset (0.093) and PackNet (0.491). On Atari (PPO), FAME achieves 0.90 average performance on Freeway and 0.96 on SpaceInvaders, exceeding all baselines including Finetune (0.21/0.61) and PackNet (0.41/0.47).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FAME decisively outperforms PT-DQN (Anand & Precup, NeurIPS 2023) on MinAtar: while PT-DQN's permanent value function retains almost no knowledge (avg. perf. ≈ 0.00–0.39), FAME achieves 14.54/18.72/1.69 across the three games with 45% lower forgetting (0.72 vs. 1.64). Against PackNet (Mallya & Lazebnik, CVPR 2018), FAME wins on both average performance and forward transfer on Meta-World (0.767 vs. 0.491 avg. perf.; −0.003 vs. −0.194 FT) and on Atari (0.90/0.96 vs. 0.41/0.47 avg. perf.), though PackNet trivially achieves zero forgetting by storing separate parameter masks per task. On Atari, FAME also surpasses ProgressiveNet (Rusu et al., 2016) in both average performance (0.90 vs. 0.39 on Freeway; 0.96 vs. 0.61 on SpaceInvaders) and forward transfer (0.68 vs. 0.21; 0.63 vs. 0.06), demonstrating that the adaptive meta warm-up and incremental knowledge integration provide stronger transfer than simply appending new network columns.

## Relation to Prior Work

FAME carves new ground by providing formal theoretical foundations for continual RL—specifically, definitions of MDP distance and catastrophic forgetting—that prior work lacked, rather than building on a single predecessor. Conceptually, it repurposes ideas from complementary learning systems theory (hippocampal-cortical interplay) into a dual-learner architecture, and shows that minimizing its policy-based catastrophic forgetting objective is mathematically equivalent to policy distillation (Rusu et al., 2015) and standard multi-task RL formulations (Teh et al., 2017), thereby bridging continual and multi-task RL. The adaptive meta warm-up via a one-vs-all hypothesis test is a novel mechanism that departs from the binary reset-vs-finetune choices common in prior continual RL work, and the Wasserstein-distance variant for continuous actions extends the framework beyond the KL-divergence setting typical of policy distillation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers practitioners a principled, fixed-size dual-learner framework that simultaneously addresses both plasticity (via adaptive warm-up) and stability (via incremental knowledge integration) without the growing model costs of PackNet or ProgressiveNet, validated across discrete and continuous action spaces. The theoretical unification—showing that catastrophic forgetting minimization reduces to MLE/policy distillation—gives a clear optimization objective and explains *why* the method works, which the authors argue has been missing in the fragmented continual RL literature. Key limitations include the assumption of known task boundaries and identical state/action spaces across tasks; extending to fully online continual RL without task boundaries or to heterogeneous state spaces remains open, as does replacing the meta learner with a learned latent representation for more scalable knowledge consolidation.
