## Problem

Current LLM reasoning suffers from redundant verbose content, unstable performance across sampling temperatures, and poor interpretability of internal step dependencies. Existing RL-based efficiency methods (length penalties, perplexity-based pruning) inadequately assess which reasoning steps are truly important, and no framework explicitly models the structural relationships between steps to guide optimization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263605/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a text question paired with a structured reasoning prompt that enforces a fixed vocabulary of ~24 domain-agnostic step tags (e.g., <rephrase>, <decompose>, <verify>, <consequence>) at the start of each sentence. It produces a tagged reasoning chain and final answer, while internally computing a step-to-step attention matrix (n×n, where n is the number of reasoning steps) that encodes the directed dependency graph. Evaluation is conducted under hard token-length caps (0.5k–8k) to measure efficiency, and across temperatures 0.1–1.0 to measure stability.

## Method

The paper proposes a three-stage pipeline: (1) supervised structured tuning on a small (~500-sample) dataset where a teacher model (DeepSeek-R1) generates tagged reasoning chains, teaching the student model to emit explicit step labels; (2) layer-wise step-dependency tracing that aggregates per-head attention scores over token spans to build a normalized n×n step attention matrix, validated against gold-standard entailment-tree annotations (71% alignment vs. 28% for shuffled controls); (3) structure-aware RL extending GRPO with two complementary rewards. The Max-Flow reward thresholds the attention matrix (τ=0.05) into a sparse directed graph, computes single-source single-sink max-flow from question to answer, and scores each step by the flow reduction when that node is removed—rewarding balanced, non-redundant step contributions. The LCS reward extracts step-label sequences from multiple sampled completions, computes pairwise longest common subsequences with a length-suppression factor to prevent token inflation, and rewards alignment with correct completions while penalizing similarity to incorrect ones. Together, these rewards prune redundant steps and reinforce high-quality reasoning sub-paths without requiring external step-level annotations.

## Results

On 7B models (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B), MaxFlow achieves 61.17% average Pass@1 accuracy at 8K max length, outperforming GRPO (58.33%), the base model (58.05%), and Light-R1 (57.62%). At the training length of 4K, MaxFlow reaches 55.97% vs. GRPO's 52.49%, a 3.5-point gap. For efficiency, LCS achieves 35.06% average at just 1K tokens, surpassing GRPO (29.19%) by nearly 6 points. On stability, MaxFlow shows the lowest variance across temperatures 0.1–1.0 (±0.48 on MATH500, ±0.50 on OlympiadBench for 7B), compared to ±0.92 and ±0.70 for GRPO.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MaxFlow consistently outperforms GRPO across all context lengths for both 1.5B and 7B models, with the largest gap at 8K (61.17% vs. 58.33% avg for 7B; 48.89% vs. 43.28% for 1.5B). Against the base DeepSeek-R1-Distill models, both LCS and MaxFlow yield substantial gains (e.g., +3.12 avg for MaxFlow over 7B base at 8K), while LCS shows particular strength at short lengths (35.06% at 1K vs. 24.01% base for 7B). Light-R1, a curriculum-based 7B method, is outperformed by MaxFlow at 4K (55.97% vs. 50.95%) and 8K (61.17% vs. 57.62%), though Light-R1 slightly edges out LCS at 8K (57.62% vs. 57.65%). FastCuRL and DeepScaleR (1.5B baselines) are surpassed by both proposed methods at all lengths; notably, MaxFlow trained at 4K matches or exceeds DeepScaleR trained at 24K/32K. In the broader 1.5B comparison (Table 10), MaxFlow (52.6% avg) is competitive with FastCuRL (52.9%) and DeepScaleR (53.0%) while using fewer reasoning steps (~9.2 vs. ~10–11). LCS trails MaxFlow at 8K for 7B (57.65% vs. 61.17%) but leads at 1K (35.06% vs. 32.32%), suggesting LCS is more effective under tight token budgets while MaxFlow benefits from longer contexts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) with two structure-aware reward mechanisms—Max-Flow and LCS—that treat reasoning as a directed acyclic graph over explicit step tags, departing from prior efficiency-focused RL methods (L1, O1-Pruner, DAST, ThinkPrune) that rely on length penalties or perplexity-based step scoring. The conceptual framing draws on cognitive science dual-process theory and neurosymbolic AI to justify explicit step-level structure, while the Max-Flow reward repurposes classical network flow theory (Ford & Fulkerson, 1956) for evaluating step importance in a way the authors show outperforms perplexity-based alternatives. The attention-based step-dependency tracing builds on standard transformer attention analysis but applies it at the reasoning-step granularity rather than the token level.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, interpretable alternative to ad-hoc length penalties for improving reasoning efficiency, and the stability finding (structured models maintain accuracy across temperatures 0.1–1.0 with variance as low as ±0.29, versus ±2.94 for baselines) has practical implications for deployment reliability. However, the evaluation is limited to 1.5B and 7B distilled models and predominantly mathematical benchmarks, leaving open whether the graph-based rewards and structured tags generalize to larger models, non-mathematical reasoning, or multilingual settings. The training-free structured guidance on DeepSeek-R1 671B (reducing token length ~46% while preserving accuracy) suggests the approach may transfer to frontier models, but this remains a single-model demonstration without systematic validation.
