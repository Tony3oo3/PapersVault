## Problem

Off-policy reinforcement learning for LLMs—where stale rollout data from past policies is reused for training—suffers from unstable optimization, rapid entropy collapse, and even training failure as data staleness increases. The paper identifies two root causes: negative-advantage samples dominate the policy gradient (suppressing useful behaviors and risking gradient explosions), and the fixed symmetric clipping in PPO-like objectives systematically blocks entropy-increasing updates, driving the policy toward over-exploitation.

## Input / Output

BAPO consumes text prompts from a training dataset, generates G rollout responses per prompt via a (potentially stale) behavior policy, and computes per-token advantages from a reward function. The key operational detail is that the rollout policy π_θ_rollout can lag behind the target policy π_θ by up to E staleness steps (via experience replay or partial rollout), and the method must remain stable under this distribution mismatch. The output is an updated LLM policy that produces longer, more accurate chain-of-thought reasoning responses, evaluated on mathematical and logical reasoning benchmarks.

## Method

BAPO replaces the fixed symmetric clipping bounds in the PPO/GRPO surrogate objective with dynamically adjusted asymmetric bounds c_low and c_high that are re-computed at every update step. For each batch, the algorithm iteratively increases c_high (step δ₁) and c_low (step δ₂) until the ratio of positive-token contribution to total policy-gradient loss reaches a target threshold ρ₀ (default 0.4), thereby incorporating previously clipped low-probability positive tokens while filtering out excessive low-probability negative tokens. This mechanism is grounded in the derived Entropy-Clip Rule, which proves that entropy change under PPO is governed by the covariance between log-probabilities and advantages of unclipped tokens, and that fixed clipping systematically excludes the low-probability positive tokens that would increase entropy. The result is a smoother output distribution, preserved exploratory capacity, and stable training across varying staleness levels without manual hyperparameter tuning.

## Results

On AIME 2024 and AIME 2025, the 32B BAPO model (BP-Math-32B_APO) achieves 87.1 and 80.0 respectively (average 83.5), surpassing Qwen3-32B (81.4/72.9) by 5.7 and 7.1 points, and SkyWork-OR1-32B (82.2/73.3) by 4.9 and 6.7 points. The 7B BAPO model (BP-Math-7B_APO) scores 70.8 and 62.5 (average 66.7), outperforming SkyWork-OR1-7B (70.2/54.6) by 7.9 points on AIME 2025. Against proprietary systems, the 32B model outperforms o3-mini-medium (79.6/76.7) and Gemini-2.5-Flash-Thinking (82.3/72.0), while the 7B model matches Gemini-2.0-Flash-Thinking (73.3/53.5). In head-to-head RL algorithm comparisons on the same 7B backbone, BAPO (70.8/62.5) beats DAPO (68.3/60.8), TOPR (68.8/60.4), DCPO (68.8/57.9), and GRPO (69.2/59.2).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among 32B open-source models, BP-Math-32B_APO leads all competitors: it beats Qwen3-32B by 5.7 (AIME24) and 7.1 (AIME25) points, SkyWork-OR1-32B by 4.9 and 6.7, and QwQ-32B by 7.6 and 14.7. Against proprietary systems, the 32B model surpasses o3-mini-medium by 7.5 and 3.3 points and Gemini-2.5-Flash-Thinking by 4.8 and 8.0 points, though it trails o3-mini-high (87.3/86.5) and Qwen3-235B-A22B (85.7/81.5). At 7B scale, BP-Math-7B_APO outperforms SkyWork-OR1-7B by 0.6 and 7.9 points, AceReason-Nemotron-7B by 1.8 and 8.9, and AReaL-boba-RL-7B by 8.9 and 14.2. In direct RL-algorithm comparisons on the identical BP-Math-7B backbone (Table 4), BAPO (70.8/62.5) consistently outperforms DAPO (68.3/60.8), TOPR (68.8/60.4), DCPO (68.8/57.9), and the GRPO baseline (69.2/59.2), with the largest margin over DCPO on AIME25 (+4.6). The 7B BAPO model also matches or exceeds Gemini-2.0-Flash-Thinking (73.3/53.5) on AIME25, demonstrating competitiveness against commercial systems at a fraction of the scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

BAPO builds directly on the PPO/GRPO surrogate objective used in mainstream LLM RL (Schulman et al., 2017; Shao et al., 2024), extending the fixed symmetric clipping mechanism to adaptive, asymmetric bounds. It is conceptually closest to DAPO's Clip-Higher (Yu et al., 2025), which manually raises the upper clipping bound, and to DCPO (Yang et al., 2025b), which adjusts clipping per-token based on prior probabilities; BAPO departs by deriving the Entropy-Clip Rule to show *why* fixed clipping causes entropy collapse, then using a global positive-contribution ratio to dynamically set both bounds per update step. The off-policy RL setting connects to TOPR (Roux et al., 2025a) and asymmetric REINFORCE (Arnal et al., 2025), but BAPO operates at the clipping-bound level rather than modifying the reward or advantage structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Entropy-Clip Rule provides a clear theoretical account of why PPO-like objectives systematically drive entropy down, reframing a widely observed but previously heuristic phenomenon as a direct consequence of the clipping mechanism. Practically, BAPO's simplicity (two dynamically adjusted scalars per step) makes it easy to drop into existing GRPO pipelines, and its demonstrated robustness across staleness levels, partial rollout, and multiple model families (7B, 32B, Llama-3B) suggests broad applicability as off-policy and asynchronous RL infrastructures become standard. Open questions include whether the fixed target ratio ρ₀=0.4 generalizes to non-mathematical domains or very long-horizon agentic tasks, and whether the adaptive clipping interacts favorably with other recent stabilization techniques (e.g., dynamic sampling, entropy regularization) in a unified framework.
