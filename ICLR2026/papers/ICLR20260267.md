## Problem

Molecular generative models frequently produce synthetically inaccessible candidates, and existing synthesizable projection methods struggle to navigate the exponentially large combinatorial space of synthesizable molecules, suffering from poor coverage and limited reasoning over synthetic pathways. The paper addresses this by asking how to effectively explore synthesizable chemical space through iterative, multi-view pathway refinement rather than single-pass generation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260267/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a target molecule SMILES (which may be unsynthesizable) as input and produces a synthetic pathway as a sequential token sequence over a unified vocabulary of 272 tokens (153 SMILES tokens, 115 reaction tokens, and special delimiters). The pathway is represented as concatenated blocks where molecular blocks are full SMILES strings bounded by [MOL:START]/[MOL:END] tokens and reaction blocks are single tokens, enabling both bottom-up (post-order) and top-down (reverse post-order) traversal of the same synthetic tree. The final output is the end-product molecule obtained by executing the pathway, which is guaranteed to be synthesizable from the 211,220 Enamine building blocks and 115 reaction rules.

## Method

ReaSyn integrates three components in an iterative refinement cycle: (1) bottom-up autoregressive decoding from building blocks toward the target, (2) top-down decoding that re-predicts a randomly selected subtree, and (3) holistic editing via Edit Bridge, a discrete flow model that refines the entire pathway using insertion, deletion, and substitution operations. A single encoder-decoder Transformer (166M params) handles both traversal directions by randomly switching between BU and TD sequences during training and biasing the first-token logits at inference, eliminating the need for separate models. Edit Bridge (174M params) is trained with a Bregman divergence loss using a novel coupling between the autoregressive model's output and the data distribution, achieving a 70.6% alignment rate versus 0% or 2.6% for empty/uniform couplings, which reduces required edit operations from ~95–143 to ~30 per sequence. The pathway representation directly uses SMILES instead of molecular fingerprints and a flat unified vocabulary instead of hierarchical type-then-feature prediction, avoiding information loss and error accumulation.

## Results

On synthesizable molecule reconstruction, ReaSyn achieves 95.0% reconstruction rate on Enamine, 31.7% on ChEMBL, and 87.9% on ZINC1k (with unseen building blocks), substantially outperforming SynFormer (66.3%, 19.7%, 18.0%) and SynNet (25.2%, 7.9%, 12.6%). ReaSyn also achieves the highest pathway diversity (0.118) and building block diversity (0.753) on Enamine. In synthesizable goal-directed optimization on 13 TDC oracles, Graph GA-ReaSyn achieves an average AUC top-10 of 0.633, matching unconstrained Graph GA (0.633) while guaranteeing synthesizability, and outperforming all synthesis-constrained baselines (best: Graph GA-SF at 0.612).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ReaSyn decisively outperforms both SynNet (Gao et al., 2021) and SynFormer (Gao et al., 2025) on all reconstruction metrics across all three datasets, with the largest gap on ZINC1k (87.9% vs. 18.0% reconstruction rate), which tests generalization to unseen building blocks. On the ChEMBL test set from Luo et al. (2024), ReaSyn achieves 33.0% reconstruction rate vs. 19.5% for SynFormer and 13.4% for ChemProjector. In goal-directed optimization (Table 3), Graph GA-ReaSyn (0.633 avg AUC) matches unconstrained Graph GA (0.633) and beats all synthesis-constrained baselines including Graph GA-SF (0.612), SynthesisNet (0.608), and SynNet (0.545). On sEH optimization (Table 4), Graph GA-ReaSyn achieves the best sEH score (0.96), lowest SA (2.05), highest QED (0.75), and highest AiZynthFinder success rate (0.97) among all methods, using only 5k oracle calls vs. 300k for baselines. In JNK3 hit expansion (Table 5), ReaSyn achieves 75.7% analog rate, 11.8% improve rate, and 8.8% success rate, roughly doubling SynFormer's 31.4%/6.0%/4.1%. Against AiZynthFinder (Table 9), ReaSyn wins on Enamine (95.0% vs. 79.2%) and ZINC250k (87.9% vs. 20.5%) but loses on ChEMBL (31.7% vs. 44.3%), likely because AiZynthFinder's larger reaction/building-block space is more compatible with ChEMBL molecules.

## Relation to Prior Work

ReaSyn builds on the synthesizable projection line of work (SynNet, SynFormer, ChemProjector) that generates synthetic pathways to project input molecules into a defined synthesizable space, and on the Edit Flow framework (Havasi et al., 2025) for discrete sequence generation via edit operations. It departs from prior pathway generation methods in two key ways: (1) it unifies bottom-up and top-down tree traversal in a single autoregressive model using a simple sequential SMILES-based representation (replacing hierarchical fingerprint-based encodings), and (2) it introduces Edit Bridge, which modifies Edit Flow's coupling to start from a pretrained model's output rather than an empty or uniform source, dramatically reducing the number of edit operations needed. The bidirectional iterative refinement cycle, framed as a Gibbs-sampling-like test-time search, is a novel combination of these two components that has no direct precedent in prior synthesizable generation or retrosynthesis planning literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The near-5× reconstruction rate gap on the out-of-distribution ZINC1k benchmark (87.9% vs. 18.0%) demonstrates that prior methods fundamentally under-explore the synthesizable space, and ReaSyn's multi-view refinement strategy addresses this gap in a way that is modular and pluggable into any molecular optimization pipeline (e.g., genetic algorithms). The Edit Bridge concept—bridging a learned distribution to a data distribution through learned edit operations—is a generalizable idea that could extend beyond synthetic pathways to other structured sequence generation tasks. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger reaction sets and building block catalogs, how to incorporate reaction selectivity and functional group compatibility, and whether the iterative cycle's compute cost is justified in high-throughput screening settings where thousands of molecules must be processed.
