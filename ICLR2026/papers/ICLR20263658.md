## Problem

There is a substantial gap in gradient complexity between monotone zero-sum games (optimal O(L/√(μν)·log(D²/ε))) and monotone general-sum games (long-standing O(L/min{μ,ν}·log(D²/ε)) from Tseng 1995), and the zero-sum assumption is often too restrictive for practical scenarios involving transaction fees or semi-cooperation. This paper introduces an intermediate class of monotone near-zero-sum games, parameterized by a smoothness parameter δ on the coupling part, that naturally interpolates between the two extremes and admits faster-than-general-sum convergence rates.

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes gradient queries of the two utility functions u₁ and u₂ over compact convex strategy spaces X×Y (with diameters D_X, D_Y), starting from an initial point z₀. It produces an ε-accurate Nash equilibrium (‖z−z*‖²≤ε) or, in the non-strongly monotone case, an ε-approximate Nash equilibrium (utility-based). The key structural constraint is that the coupling part g=−½(u₁+u₂) must be δ-smooth and jointly convex, while the zero-sum part h=½(−u₁+u₂) must be μ-strongly convex in x and ν-strongly concave in y; the algorithm is a black-box reduction that accepts any optimal zero-sum solver as its inner-loop oracle.

## Method

The Iterative Coupling Linearization (ICL) algorithm performs a black-box reduction from near-zero-sum games to zero-sum games: at each outer iteration t, it linearizes the coupling part g at z_t and adds a quadratic regularization, transforming the non-zero-sum problem into a strongly convex-concave saddle point problem φ_t(x,y) that can be solved by any off-the-shelf zero-sum solver (e.g., Catalyst-based methods). Convergence is driven by a potential function Δ(z) that decomposes into the jointly convex coupling and convex-concave zero-sum parts; a descent lemma shows geometric contraction of ‖z_t−z*‖² with rate θ=min{μ,ν}/(η⁻¹+min{μ,ν}) when η≤1/δ. The overall gradient complexity is Õ((L/√(μν)+L/min{μ,ν}·min{1,√(δ/(μ+ν))})·log²(D²/ε)), which recovers the optimal zero-sum rate when δ=0 and improves upon Tseng's general-sum bound whenever min{μ,ν}+δ=o(max{μ,ν}). A convex reformulation technique is also provided to handle cases where the coupling part is not jointly convex, as in regularized matrix games with transaction fees.

## Results

In the main numerical experiment (n = m = 10000, μ = 10⁻⁴, ν = 1, ε = 10⁻⁷), ICL requires 9.1k gradient queries at zero transaction fee (pure zero-sum) and scales to 123.8k at 0.18% fee, while OGDA stays flat at ~94k and EG at ~133k across all fee levels. ICL outperforms both baselines when the transaction fee ρ ≤ 0.12% (e.g., 75.7k vs. 93.9k for OGDA at ρ = 0.12%), consistent with the theoretical prediction that ICL is faster when ρ‖abs(M)‖ ≪ √(μν) = 1%. Theoretically, ICL achieves Õ((L/√(μν) + L/min{μ,ν}·√(δ/(μ+ν)))·log²(D²/ε)) gradient queries, improving upon the long-standing O(L/min{μ,ν}·log(D²/ε)) bound of Tseng (1995) for non-zero-sum games when δ is small.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares ICL against two classic variational inequality methods: Optimistic Gradient Descent Ascent (OGDA, Popov 1980) and Extra-Gradient (EG, Korpelevich 1976). ICL decisively outperforms both baselines when the game is sufficiently near-zero-sum: at ρ = 0.12%, ICL requires 75.7k gradient queries versus 93.9k for OGDA (19% fewer) and 132.9k for EG (43% fewer). At the pure zero-sum setting (ρ = 0%), ICL needs only 9.1k queries, roughly 10× fewer than OGDA and 14.6× fewer than EG. However, as the transaction fee increases beyond 0.12%, ICL's advantage erodes: at ρ = 0.15% and 0.18%, ICL (113.7k and 123.8k) becomes slower than OGDA (94.0k) though still faster than EG (132.9k). Notably, OGDA and EG show essentially identical performance across all fee levels (93.9–94.0k and 132.9k respectively), confirming that these classic methods do not exploit the near-zero-sum structure, whereas ICL's query count scales gracefully with the near-zero-sum parameter δ.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of two well-studied but disconnected threads: the optimal O(L/√(μν)·log(D²/ε)) rates for monotone zero-sum games (Lin et al. 2020; Kovalev & Gasnikov 2022; Lan & Li 2023) and the long-standing O(L/min{μ,ν}·log(D²/ε)) variational inequality bounds for monotone general-sum games (Tseng 1995; Nemirovski 2004). It introduces the intermediate class of monotone near-zero-sum games, parameterized by the smoothness δ of the coupling part g, and develops the Iterative Coupling Linearization (ICL) algorithm as a black-box reduction that leverages any optimal zero-sum solver as an inner-loop oracle. Conceptually, ICL departs from the Catalyst framework (Lin et al. 2018; Carmon et al. 2022) that underpins recent zero-sum advances, since Catalyst's regularized minimization transforms non-zero-sum problems into Stackelberg games whose solutions deviate from Nash equilibria—a key technical obstacle the paper explicitly identifies and circumvents through coupling linearization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first gradient complexity improvement over Tseng's (1995) long-standing bound for non-zero-sum monotone games, achieved by exploiting the near-zero-sum structure (small δ) to interpolate between the optimal zero-sum rate and the general-sum rate. Practical relevance is demonstrated through matrix games with transaction fees and competitive games with small cooperation incentives, where the near-zero-sum structure arises naturally. Key limitations include a log²(D²/ε) dependence (vs. single log for zero-sum), the absence of matching lower bounds for the new class, and restriction to two-player settings. The multiplayer extension is identified as highly non-trivial, since three-player zero-sum games are inherently as hard as two-player general-sum games via von Neumann–Morgenstern reductions, suggesting that significantly stronger structural assumptions will be needed.
