## Problem

Existing inference-time reasoning techniques (Chain-of-Thought, Tree-of-Thoughts, Graph-of-Thoughts) rely on manually predefined, task-agnostic logical structures that are applied uniformly across all reasoning tasks. These fixed frameworks cannot adapt to the diverse characteristics of different domains (mathematics, STEM, commonsense) nor dynamically adjust to the evolving problem-solving state during multi-step reasoning, limiting their effectiveness on challenging problems.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264029/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, the method takes a reasoning problem as input and produces a final answer guided by a dynamically constructed sequence of logic blocks. During the reasoning process, the LLM's current step is evaluated via a structured self-assessment prompt across 7 granular aspects (correctness of modeling/calculation, clarity, complexity, completeness), each scored 1–3, forming a 7-dimensional state vector that feeds the navigator. The navigator (a 3-layer MLP with only 2,566 parameters) outputs one of five discrete actions per step, and the full episode yields a task-specific logical structure (e.g., Reason→Refine→Debate) that orchestrates the LLM's multi-step reasoning.

## Method

RLoT models long-sequence reasoning as a Markov Decision Process where the action space consists of five human-cognition-inspired logic blocks (Reason one step, Decompose, Debate, Refine, Terminate) and the state is a low-dimensional self-evaluation vector extracted by prompting the LLM itself. A lightweight navigator model—a 3-layer MLP with Dueling Network architecture—is trained via Double-Dueling-DQN to sequentially select and combine these blocks, constructing task-specific logical structures without modifying the LLM's parameters. Training uses a Process Reward Model (Math-Shepherd) to score intermediate reasoning steps as per-action rewards, and only hard questions (those the LLM fails on direct prompting) are used, keeping the training set small. The key insight is that a sub-3K-parameter RL agent, trained once on a single LLM-task pair, can transfer to unseen LLMs and tasks, making sub-10B models competitive with ~10× larger counterparts while avoiding the search-and-trial overhead of tree/graph-based methods.

## Results

RLoT consistently outperforms all inference-time baselines across 7 reasoning benchmarks and 5 LLMs. The average accuracy improves from 62.94% (CoT-SC, best baseline) to 69.19% with Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct, and from 64.89% to 71.70% with Llama3.1-8B-Instruct. The largest single-task gain is +13.4% on GPQA with Llama3.1-8B-Instruct (46.88% vs. 33.48% for CoT-SC). With DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, RLoT reaches 82.92% average, surpassing the best baseline (CoT-SC at 78.38%) by 4.54 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RLoT outperforms all 19 baselines on the majority of benchmarks. Among single-round methods (Direct QA, Zero-shot CoT, Few-shot CoT), RLoT leads by 5–10 points on average across all LLMs. CoT-SC is the strongest baseline overall, but RLoT still surpasses it by 4.5–6.8 points in average accuracy (e.g., 69.19 vs. 62.94 for Qwen2.5-14B; 71.70 vs. 64.89 for Llama3.1-8B). ToT, despite its more complex tree structure, is consistently outperformed by RLoT and often underperforms even simple CoT variants. In the extended comparison (Table 10), RLoT beats all tree-search methods (rStar: 70.80, AFlow: 70.28, Q*: 69.30, Litesearch: 67.90) and fixed-pattern methods (Self-Refine: 66.75, DeAR: 66.13, Least-to-Most: 66.94) on the 4-benchmark average, achieving 73.54. On math-specific baselines (Table 12), RLoT (73.32 avg on MATH+GSM8K with Llama3.1-8B) outperforms all inference-time methods (HiAR-ICL: 72.85, LLaMA-Berry: 72.30, LLM2: 68.30) and approaches fine-tuning methods (AceMath: 77.44) at a fraction of the computational cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two well-developed lines of work: inference-time reasoning structures (CoT, ToT, GoT) and RL-based LLM enhancement (RLHF, DeepSeek-R1, GLoRE). It departs from the former by replacing manually designed, task-agnostic logical structures with an RL-learned policy that dynamically composes five cognition-inspired logic blocks, and from the latter by applying RL not to fine-tune the LLM itself but to train a <3K-parameter navigator that operates externally at inference time. The MDP formulation and use of a Process Reward Model for training signals draw on standard RL and PRM literature, while the avoidance of search-and-trial (unlike MCTS-based methods such as rStar, AFlow, and LiteSearch) is the key architectural distinction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is notable: a 2,566-parameter MLP navigator can close most of the performance gap between sub-10B and ~100B LLMs on reasoning benchmarks, and the trained navigator transfers across unseen LLMs and task domains without fine-tuning, suggesting that reasoning-strategy selection is partially LLM- and domain-agnostic. Limitations include reliance on the LLM's self-evaluation for state extraction (82% accuracy in manual checks), hand-designed logic blocks that may not cover all reasoning patterns, and diminishing returns on already-strong models like DeepSeek-R1. Open questions include whether the five logic blocks can be discovered or expanded automatically, whether the approach extends to multi-modal or tool-use reasoning, and how it interacts with the growing trend of natively reasoning LLMs (o1, R1) that internalize these strategies.
