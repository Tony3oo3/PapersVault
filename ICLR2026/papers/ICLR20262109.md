## Problem

While prior work has identified linear activation directions encoding metadata like knowledge awareness and data frequency, no study had established whether language models linearly encode the temporal order in which they were trained on different information. This paper asks whether a single linear direction in activation space captures training-order recency, and whether models can actually access this information.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262109/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes post-residual activations (all layers × all token positions) from a sequentially fine-tuned LLM on position-aligned test prompts about aliased named entities, where all aliases share the same token count so activations are token-aligned across samples. It produces a difference-of-means recency axis (average of c₁−c₆ centroid vectors across runs), 2D centroid projections showing collinear stage ordering, logistic-regression probe accuracies for pairwise stage discrimination, and a fine-tuned model that outputs the training stage label for a given alias.

## Method

The core procedure sequentially fine-tunes Llama-3.2-1B on six disjoint alias-entity QA datasets (D₁…D₆, 5 epochs each) with randomized entity-to-stage assignment per independent run, then collects test activations using a held-out entity-attribution template. Per-stage activation centroids are projected onto a 2D subspace whose x-axis is the average (c₁−c₆) difference-of-means vector, revealing that centroids lie on a straight line in exact training order and that lines from independent runs are roughly parallel. Logistic regression probes trained on 80:20 entity splits distinguish stages with >90% accuracy on held-out entities, and an auxiliary fine-tune lets the model explicitly report an unseen alias's training stage at ~80% accuracy. The paper further shows the axis tracks recency (re-exposure repositions centroids), persists through 30 epochs of mixed-data training, is not explained by activation magnitudes, logit confidence, or multi-token statistics (via joint-distribution balancing), and replicates across Qwen2.5 models up to 32B with LoRA.

## Results

Linear probes trained to distinguish entities from the earliest (D₁) vs. latest (D₆) fine-tuning stages achieve >90% accuracy on held-out entities, with performance increasing monotonically with temporal distance between stages. After 30 additional epochs of mixed-data training (which provides no training-order signal), probe accuracy decays only to ~63% (synthetic) and ~60% (natural), remaining well above the 50% chance level. A model fine-tuned to explicitly report an unseen entity's training stage reaches 79.8% accuracy, confirming the information is directly accessible to the model's own computations. The effect replicates across Llama-3.2-1B and Qwen2.5 models up to 32B parameters, and with both full fine-tuning and LoRA.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a discovery paper without traditional method-vs-baseline comparisons; instead, it systematically rules out alternative explanations. The training-order encoding is not attributable to activation magnitude differences (at token position 12, L₂ norms are identical between D₁ and D₂ yet probes still reach ~70% accuracy), nor to model confidence (balancing 7 logit statistics simultaneously has minimal impact beyond random downsampling). Among optimizers, vanilla SGD fails to produce the encoding even after 25 epochs per stage, while Adafactor and Lion are strongest and AdamW/RMSprop reach comparable strength with additional epochs. The signal persists when all entity attributes are shuffled across entities (decoupling fine-tuning data from pre-training knowledge), confirming it is not driven by pre-training familiarity. The encoding also survives 30 epochs of mixed-data training, suggesting gradient descent lacks pressure to remove distinctions that do not interfere with the training objective.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on linear activation directions encoding metadata (Ferrando et al.'s binary known/unknown entity detection, Merullo et al.'s frequency encodings, Krasheninnikov et al.'s reliability cues) by showing that training-order recency itself is a standalone linear feature in activation space. The method for extracting the direction follows difference-of-means steering (Panickssery et al., 2023), and the finding that the 'seen vs. unseen' axis also recovers full training order connects directly to Ferrando et al.'s knowledge-awareness result. Concurrent work by Kuditipudi et al. (2025) shows models remember later training examples more strongly at the output level; this paper provides the activation-space mechanistic underpinning for why such order-based signals exist. The path-dependence of representations noted by Lampinen et al. (2024) provides a broader theoretical context, but no prior work had isolated a continuous training-order direction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that models linearly encode and can explicitly access their own training history has direct implications for belief-editing techniques (Wang et al., 2025): if a model can detect that a belief was recently implanted, it may be able to resist or flag such modifications, with consequences for alignment faking scenarios. The signal's persistence through 30 epochs of mixed-data training (decaying only to ~63%) suggests that training-order information is a robust, hard-to-erase property of fine-tuned models. Key open questions include whether this encoding emerges during from-scratch pretraining on real data, whether models can access it without explicit auxiliary fine-tuning, and what mechanistic process underlies the directional drift (the paper rules out activation magnitudes and confidence but leaves the mechanism unresolved). Limitations include reliance on templated datasets and language models only.
