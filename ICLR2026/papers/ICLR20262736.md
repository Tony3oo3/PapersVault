## Problem

Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM) is widely credited with improved generalization, which would naively suggest reduced memorization and thus better membership privacy. However, this paper reveals the opposite: SAM-trained models are consistently more vulnerable to Membership Inference Attacks (MIA) than SGD-trained models across diverse datasets and attack methods, despite achieving lower test error. The paper investigates the mechanism behind this paradox—how a model that generalizes better can simultaneously leak more membership information—and provides both empirical and theoretical explanations.

## Input / Output

The empirical experiments train CNNs on RGB image datasets (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, Texas-100, EyePacs) and tabular data (Purchase-100), then evaluate MIA vulnerability using both direct threshold attacks (confidence, entropy, NN-based) and shadow-model attacks (LiRA, RMIA) with 256 random half-splits for shadow model training. The theoretical analysis operates on overparameterized linear models (d ≫ n) with Gaussian data, producing closed-form variance characterizations. Outputs include per-sample memorization scores (via Leave-One-Out), influence scores on test predictions, a novel entropy-based typicality metric for test points, and MIA metrics (AUC, attack accuracy, TPR@0.1% FPR).

## Method

The paper's core mechanism is a variance-shrinkage effect: SAM's sharpness penalty (modeled as minimum-G-norm interpolation with G_η = I + ηΣ) suppresses the model's reliance on a single majority feature, forcing it to distribute weight across diverse subclass-specific features. This reduces the variance of non-member prediction outputs while leaving member outputs fixed (due to interpolation), thereby sharpening the separation between member and non-member confidence distributions and amplifying MIA advantage. Empirically, the paper demonstrates this through memorization score analysis (SAM concentrates memorization in the mid-range, capturing atypical sub-patterns rather than pure noise), influence score analysis (SAM amplifies the influence of mid-to-high memorization samples on test predictions), and a novel entropy-based metric that shows SAM's generalization gains come specifically from correctly predicting atypical test samples that depend on a handful of memorized training points. Theoretically, Theorems 1–3 prove that under the sharpness-aware geometry, non-member output variance strictly decreases, guaranteeing higher MIA advantage for both confidence-threshold and likelihood-ratio attacks at every threshold.

## Results

Across five datasets (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, Purchase-100, Texas-100, EyePacs), SAM consistently achieves higher test accuracy than SGD (e.g., 81.60% vs 80.30% on CIFAR-100; 96.72% vs 96.00% on CIFAR-10) while simultaneously incurring higher membership inference attack accuracy across all four direct threshold attacks (NN, Confidence, Entropy, M-entropy). For shadow-model attacks (RMIA, LiRA), SAM also shows higher AUC and attack accuracy (e.g., LiRA AUC 93.7% vs 92.6% on CIFAR-100; TPR@0.1%FPR 31.0% vs 27.0%). The paper further shows that SAM's memorization scores are concentrated in the mid-range (0.6–0.85), indicating structured memorization of atypical sub-patterns rather than pure noise, and that SAM's generalization gains come predominantly from correctly classifying atypical test samples that depend on a handful of memorized training points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between SAM and standard SGD as training optimizers, evaluated on membership inference vulnerability. SAM wins on test accuracy across all five datasets (gains of 0.5–1.7 percentage points, e.g., 81.60% vs 80.30% on CIFAR-100, 75.41% vs 73.67% on EyePacs), confirming its known generalization advantage. However, SAM loses on every MIA metric: confidence attack accuracy is 1.5–4.1 points higher for SAM (e.g., 79.10% vs 77.19% on CIFAR-100; 61.64% vs 59.37% on CIFAR-10), and shadow-model attacks (LiRA, RMIA) show similar gaps (e.g., LiRA AUC 93.7% vs 92.6%, TPR@0.1%FPR 31.0% vs 27.0% on CIFAR-100). The paper also compares other sharpness-aware optimizers (ASAM, GSAM) and a custom 'Sharp' optimizer that explicitly seeks sharper minima; ASAM and GSAM replicate SAM's pattern (better generalization, worse privacy), while the Sharp optimizer shows the inverse (worse generalization, better privacy), confirming the link between loss-landscape flatness and MIA vulnerability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on three main threads: the SAM optimization framework (Foret et al., 2020), the memorization/influence score methodology of Feldman & Zhang (2020), and the variance-gap analysis of overparameterized models for membership inference from Tan et al. (2022). It departs from the standard narrative that better generalization implies better privacy (formalized by Yeom et al., 2018) by showing that SAM's sharpness-regularization mechanism—while improving test accuracy—actually increases MIA vulnerability through a variance-shrinkage effect on non-member predictions. The theoretical analysis extends Tan et al.'s framework by introducing a curvature-aligned geometry G_η = I + ηΣ that models SAM's implicit bias, going beyond the Euclidean or ridge-regularized settings previously studied.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work serves as a cautionary tale for the widespread adoption of SAM and other flat-minima-seeking optimizers in privacy-sensitive deployments, directly challenging the 'flatter minima = good' heuristic from a privacy standpoint. The variance-shrinkage mechanism provides a concrete, theoretically grounded explanation for why SAM's geometric bias simultaneously improves generalization and amplifies membership signals, unifying previously separate observations about memorization, influence, and prediction confidence. Open questions include whether effective MIA defenses exist that preserve SAM's generalization gains, whether the effect persists in non-linear deep networks beyond the linear theoretical regime, and how it interacts with other privacy mechanisms like differential privacy. The theoretical analysis is limited to a perfectly interpolating linear model, and the empirical memorization/influence scores are computed only for CIFAR-100 due to computational cost.
