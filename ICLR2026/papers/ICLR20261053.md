## Problem

MLLMs struggle with hour-long videos due to limited context windows, and existing solutions either require expensive retraining (context extension, token compression) or rely on image-text retrieval that misses temporal and action cues critical for video understanding. Among training-free retrieval-based methods, prior work selects keyframes but lacks mechanisms to adaptively allocate sampling density and spatial resolution to the most relevant segments, leaving redundant information unfiltered and critical details under-represented.

## Input / Output

The method takes a long video pre-segmented into 16-second clips, a natural-language question, and optionally multiple-choice answer options. The video is encoded at 1 fps by a video-text retrieval model (PerceptionEncoder PE-G/14) to produce segment embeddings. For LLaVA-Video, up to 64 frames at fixed 384×384 resolution are fed to the MLLM; for Qwen2.5-VL, up to 768 frames with variable resolution (84–644 px, stride-constrained to multiples of 28) under a total token budget of 20480×28×28. The output is a single text answer (option letter or short phrase) generated by the MLLM from the adaptively sampled and resolution-allocated frames.

## Method

The framework has three coupled components operating in a training-free pipeline. First, Video-Query-Options Similarity (VQOS) prompts the MLLM to generate candidate answer options, concatenates each with the question to form multiple text queries, and computes the maximum cosine similarity between each video segment embedding (from PerceptionEncoder) and all option-question embeddings, yielding a refined relevance score per segment. Second, Adaptive Frame Sampling (AFS) selects the top-k segments and partitions them into L₁ levels via a linear-programming solver (Pulp), assigning more frames to higher-similarity segments (c₁ > c₂ > … > c_{L₁}) while keeping the total frame count fixed. Third, Dynamic Resolution Allocation (DRA) assigns higher spatial resolution to frames from more relevant segments under a uniform per-level token budget, so that key frames retain fine-grained detail while less relevant frames are downsampled. The entire pipeline mirrors human cognition—hypothesis generation, focused verification, irrelevance filtering—and requires no fine-tuning of the underlying MLLM.

## Results

On five long video benchmarks (LVBench, MLVU, LongVideoBench, VideoMME, VideoEval-Pro), the proposed training-free framework achieves average accuracy gains of +5.3% (LLaVA-Video-7B) and +5.0% (Qwen2.5-VL-7B) with generated options (Ours-GO), and +3.6% and +3.2% at 72B scale. On the most challenging hour-long benchmarks LVBench (67 min) and VideoEval-Pro (38 min), the 7B models achieve substantial improvements of 8.5% and 8.3% over their respective baselines. The best configuration (Ours-PO + AdaReTake on Qwen2.5-VL-7B) reaches 59.9% average accuracy, surpassing all open-source training-based long-video MLLMs (VideoChat-Flash 55.2%, InternVideo2.5 54.6%, Video-XL-2 55.4%) without any fine-tuning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the two backbone MLLMs, Ours-GO improves LLaVA-Video-7B from 50.6% to 55.9% average (+5.3%) and Qwen2.5-VL-7B from 52.8% to 57.8% (+5.0%); at 72B the gains are +3.6% and +3.2% respectively. Compared with the SOTA training-free retrieval method AKS (CLIP-based), Ours-GO lags by ~0.4% on shorter-video benchmarks (MLVU, LongVideoBench, VideoMME) but surpasses it by ~3.0% on the longer LVBench and VideoEval-Pro, where query-focused retrieval matters more than uniform temporal coverage. Against AdaReTake (token-level KV compression), Ours-GO outperforms by 0.6% on shorter videos and 2.8% on longer ones, and the two methods are complementary—combining them (Ours-GO+AdaReTake) yields an additional +1.3% over Ours-GO alone. All training-based long-video MLLMs (VideoChat-Flash 55.2%, InternVideo2.5 54.6%, Video-XL-2 55.4%) are outperformed by Ours-GO without any fine-tuning. At 72B scale, Ours-PO on Qwen2.5-VL reaches 62.5% average, exceeding GPT-4o (57.6%) by 4.9% but remaining below the 200B proprietary Seed1.5-VL (67.7%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the retrieval-based training-free paradigm for long video understanding (AKS, BOLT) by replacing image-text retrieval on individual frames with video-text retrieval on temporal segments (PerceptionEncoder), thereby capturing actions and temporal dynamics that static frames miss. It departs from prior retrieval methods by not merely selecting keyframes but adaptively allocating both frame density (AFS) and spatial resolution (DRA) according to segment-level relevance scores. The VQOS mechanism—prompting the MLLM to generate candidate options and fusing them with the query for similarity computation—is a novel twist that mirrors human hypothesis-verification cognition, distinguishing it from prior work that relies solely on the raw question for retrieval. The method is explicitly complementary to token-level compression approaches like AdaReTake, operating at the input frame-sampling stage rather than the internal KV-cache level.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The training-free design means the framework can be immediately applied to any new MLLM architecture without retraining, addressing a key practical bottleneck as MLLMs evolve rapidly. A notable negative finding is that incorporating global textual summaries consistently degrades performance, suggesting that textual abstraction is lossy for video and that visual coverage via frame selection is more effective than textual context injection. Limitations include weaker gains on holistic reasoning and summarization tasks (where the retrieval-based, locally-focused design is inherently constrained), dependence on a separate VTR model (though the SigLIP-LLaVA variant partially mitigates this), and the observation that the method's advantage grows with video length, leaving shorter-video scenarios less differentiated. Open questions include whether the VQOS option-generation loop can be made more efficient for real-time streaming and whether the approach generalizes to video generation or editing tasks.
