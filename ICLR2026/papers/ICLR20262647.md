## Problem

While semantic similarity between text expressions is naturally measured through shared meanings, no method existed to quantify how well text-conditioned diffusion models' internal semantic representations align with human-annotated similarity. Existing approaches either compare text in language space (word co-occurrence, LLM continuations) or evaluate image quality/diversity, but none grounded meaning in the visual imagery a generative model actually produces from a prompt.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262647/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes two free-form text prompts (y1, y2) and a pre-trained text-conditioned diffusion model (Stable Diffusion v1.4 with classifier-free guidance at scale 7.5). It outputs a scalar Jeffreys divergence score measuring semantic distance, plus the intermediate denoising image sequences (in 64×64 latent space, decoded to 512×512 pixels for visualization) that make the comparison interpretable. Only T=10 denoising steps and k Monte-Carlo samples are needed, with each step taking ~2 seconds on a single RTX 4090.

## Method

The paper defines semantic similarity between two text prompts as the Jeffreys divergence (symmetrized KL) between the reverse-time SDEs each prompt induces in image space. Using Girsanov's theorem, the KL divergence between two SDEs is expressed via the Radon-Nikodym derivative as a stochastic exponential; under Novikov's condition, the stochastic integral term vanishes as a martingale, reducing the divergence to an expectation of the squared L2 difference between the two score functions s_θ(x,t|y1) and s_θ(x,t|y2) along the denoising trajectory. This is computed via Monte-Carlo by sampling initial Gaussian noise, denoising it under both prompts simultaneously, and accumulating the squared score differences at each of T timesteps with uniform weighting (g(t)=1). The resulting distance is interpretable: the intermediate image sequences visually reveal *how* the model transforms one concept into the other (e.g., converting leopard spots to tiger stripes), providing a visual explanation alongside the numerical score.

## Results

On the STS benchmarks (STS-B, STS12–16) and SICK-R, Conjuring Semantic Similarity achieves an average Spearman correlation of 65.4±5.3 with human-annotated similarity scores, outperforming all diffusion-model baselines (best: Final Timestep Prediction at 56.9±7.7) and all zero-shot encoder-based models (best: BERT-mean at 54.8±8.3). The method rivals autoregressive LLMs up to 33B parameters (LLaMA-33B: 66.6±8.5) while remaining zero-shot and requiring only ~2 seconds per Monte-Carlo step on a single RTX 4090. It trails contrastive-trained embedding models (SimCSE-BERT: 76.3±4.6, CLIP-ViTL14: 67.0±4.3), which are specifically optimized for semantic comparison tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against contrastive-trained embedding models, the method loses: SimCSE-BERT (76.3 avg) and CLIP-ViTL14 (67.0 avg) both outperform Conjuring Semantic Similarity (65.4 avg), though the gap to CLIP is narrow (~1.6 points). Against zero-shot encoder-based models, the method clearly wins: it outperforms BERT-mean (54.8), RoBERTa Large-mean (52.8), ST5-Enc-mean 11B (58.0), and BERT-CLS (29.2) by 10–36 points on average Spearman correlation. Against autoregressive LLMs using the 'meanings as trajectories' framework, the method is competitive: it slightly trails LLaMA-33B (66.6) and Falcon-7B (65.7) by ~1 point, but substantially outperforms GPT-2 (49.3) and GPT-2-XL (56.5). Among the diffusion-model-specific baselines (Initial Timestep, Final Timestep, Direct Output), the full symmetrized Jeffreys divergence method wins by 8.5–14.1 points over the best baseline (Final Timestep at 56.9), confirming that integrating over all timesteps and symmetrizing the divergence captures richer semantic structure than any single-timestep or output-only comparison.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the SDE-based formulation of diffusion models from Song et al. (2020b) and the Girsanov-theorem-based KL divergence derivation from Song et al. (2021) to a new purpose: measuring semantic distance between text prompts by comparing the reverse-time SDEs they induce. Conceptually, it departs from the long line of text-space semantic similarity methods (Word2Vec, CLIP, BERT embeddings) and from Liu et al. (2023), who defined meaning for autoregressive LLMs as the distribution over continuations, by instead grounding meaning in the distribution over conjured images. The Jeffreys divergence between two conditional SDEs, simplified via the martingale property under Novikov's condition, provides a closed-form Monte-Carlo-estimable distance that requires no additional training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first method to quantify and visualize semantic alignment between text-conditioned diffusion models and human annotators, opening a new evaluation axis beyond FID/CLIP-score that targets the *meaning* a model has learned rather than generation quality. The visual interpretability (seeing exactly which image features change between two prompts) is a genuine advantage over opaque embedding distances. Key limitations include the text-encoder bottleneck (CLIP constrains what the diffusion model can represent), inability to handle abstract or non-visual concepts, and the finding that verb and adjective semantics deteriorate significantly after diffusion training. Open questions include whether the framework extends to non-diffusion generative models and whether the text-encoder bottleneck can be circumvented with native text conditioning.
