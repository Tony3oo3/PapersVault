## Problem

Existing two-state methods for articulated object reconstruction require a priori knowledge of the number of parts and rely on cross-state geometric correspondence between start and end states. When the end state reveals regions absent in the start state (e.g., the interior of a refrigerator), this correspondence breaks down, causing severe degradation in part segmentation and articulation estimation. The paper asks whether continuous motion cues from an interaction video can replace two static states to enable prior-free, robust part mobility analysis.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262965/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a multi-view RGB scan of the object in its closed start state (100 views) plus a monocular RGB interaction video (200 frames for 2–3 part objects, 500 for complex multi-part objects) showing parts being moved. No depth sensor, part-count annotation, or joint-type label is required. It outputs a dual-Gaussian 3D representation (separate static and moving Gaussian sets), per-part 3D segmentation masks, per-part articulation parameters (joint type, axis direction, axis position, rotation angle or translation distance), and a 3D mesh via TSDF volume and marching cubes.

## Method

The pipeline has three stages. Stage I reconstructs a vanilla 3DGS of the start state. Stage II introduces a dual-Gaussian representation: the start-state Gaussian set is jointly optimised with a deformable Gaussian set (MLP-based deformation field tracking the motion video), where during initial training the static set's opacity is progressively pruned for moving regions, achieving clean dynamic-static disentanglement; a static-during-motion detection (SDMD) module reassigns newly revealed but stationary Gaussians back to the static set. Stage III applies an optimisation-free sequential RANSAC with a Kabsch solver to the clean time-parameterised trajectories of the moving Gaussians, clustering them into rigid parts and directly extracting per-part articulation parameters (axis, angle, translation, joint type) without any structural prior or iterative optimisation. The key architectural insight is that separating static and dynamic Gaussians into two explicit sets—rather than assigning a (near-zero) deformation to every Gaussian as in standard deformable 3DGS—eliminates static noise from trajectories, making the subsequent RANSAC-based clustering robust and prior-free.

## Results

On the PartNet-Mobility benchmark, AIM achieves the best 3D IoU on almost all objects in both two-part and multi-part settings, with a +27.11% improvement over prior SoTA on the complex Storage object (6 moving parts). For articulation estimation on complex objects, AIM reduces axis-angle error from 12.78° (ArtGS) to 0.58° on Storage and from 33.19° to 1.19° on Table, while part motion errors drop to 0.02 m (prismatic) and 10.56° (revolute). For mesh reconstruction of dynamic parts, AIM achieves CD-m of 8.36 mm on Storage vs. 71.17 mm (ArtGS) and 4.99 mm on Table vs. 51.40 mm (ArtGS).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DTA (Weng et al., 2024), AIM achieves substantially lower articulation errors on complex objects (e.g., Storage D₀ axis-angle: 0.56° vs. 58.63°) and competitive or superior 3D IoU on two-part objects, while DTA struggles with joint-type misclassification on objects with newly revealed interiors (Fridge, Oven). Against ArtGS (Liu et al., 2025), AIM dramatically outperforms on complex objects across all metrics (axis-angle 0.58° vs. 12.78° on Storage; CD-m 8.36 vs. 71.17 mm) and matches or exceeds it on two-part objects, where ArtGS frequently fails joint-type prediction (marked 'WT' in Table 3). Against PARIS (Liu et al., 2023), AIM reduces part motion error from 167.60° to 6.76° on the Fridge, reflecting PARIS's instability in articulation recovery. Against Video2Articulation (Peng et al., 2025), a pre-trained segmentation-driven method, AIM wins on all four metrics (Axis Ang, Axis Pose, CD-m, CD-s) across all tested objects, while Video2Articulation fails 100% on the Washer object due to unreliable pre-trained segmentation masks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on 3D Gaussian Splatting (Kerbl et al., 2023) for static geometry and Deformable 3DGS (Yang et al., 2024) for time-varying motion, but departs fundamentally from the dominant two-state paradigm (PARIS, DTA, ArtGS) that requires multi-view captures of a start and end articulation state plus a known part count. The key conceptual shift is replacing cross-state geometric correspondence with continuous motion trajectories from a single interaction video, and replacing optimization-based part segmentation with a non-learned sequential RANSAC (Magri & Fusiello, 2016) applied to clean dynamic-static separated trajectories. The dual-Gaussian representation and SDMD module are novel architectural contributions that enable this disentanglement without structural priors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Removing the known-part-count requirement and the two-state input assumption significantly broadens the practical applicability of articulated object analysis, since real-world objects often have unknown part counts and their end states reveal geometry absent from the start state (e.g., refrigerator interiors). The limitation that the method requires a video capturing all degrees of freedom and that reconstructed geometry is restricted to visible surfaces (requiring full disassembly for internal parts) remains an open challenge for building fully interactive digital twins. The real-world validation, while promising with Meta Aria glasses, is limited to two objects, leaving open questions about robustness to occlusion, non-rigid deformation, and more complex multi-object scenes.
