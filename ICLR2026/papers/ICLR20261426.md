## Problem

Human-AI teams frequently underperform the AI alone, yet it remains unclear which information each agent exploits and where complementary value exists. Without a principled way to quantify the decision-relevant information an AI prediction adds beyond what a human already uses (or vice versa), it is difficult to select models, design explanations, or target interventions to improve team performance.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes a formal decision problem (payoff function S, state space Ω, decision space D), an information model specifying a joint distribution π over basic signals (instance features, human decisions, AI predictions) and the payoff-relevant state, plus observed realizations of those signals. It outputs a global Agent-Complementary Information Value (ACIV) scalar per signal, an Instance-Level ILIV for individual predictions, and ILIV-SHAP feature importance scores that rank which data features drive the complementary information in a specific AI prediction. Unlike standard SHAP, the importance scores are computed over counterfactual ILIV evaluations rather than raw model outputs, so they reflect contribution to decision-relevant value over the agent's existing information.

## Method

The paper builds a Bayesian decision-theoretic framework where the value of a signal V is defined as the expected payoff gain of a rational (Bayes-optimal) decision-maker who observes V in addition to a baseline information set, minus the payoff from the baseline alone. ACIV (Definition 3.2) measures this marginal gain of a signal over an agent's decisions in expectation over the data-generating process, while ILIV (Definition 3.3) conditions on a specific signal realization to capture instance-level complementary value. ILIV-SHAP (Definition 4.1) adapts the Shapley value by replacing the model-output function g_f with a counterfactual ILIV evaluation, so each feature's importance score quantifies its marginal contribution to the decision-relevant information the AI prediction adds over the human's judgment. In practice, a predictive algorithm A estimates posterior beliefs over the state given signals and agent decisions, and the rational decision rule is the argmax of expected payoff under those posteriors; the regret of this estimator is provably bounded by twice the expected calibration error of A (Theorem I.5). The framework is validated through a preregistered between-subjects experiment on house-price prediction (showing ILIV-SHAP+SHAP yields 6.94% APE reduction vs. 5.88% for SHAP alone with the higher-ACIV model) and demonstrated on chest X-ray diagnosis and deepfake detection tasks.

## Results

In a preregistered experiment with 421 participants on house price prediction, the AI model with higher agent-complementary information value (AI1, ACIV = 4.61% in MAPE) reduces human-AI team absolute percentage error (APE) by 6.94% [6.50%, 7.38%] when paired with ILIV-SHAP+SHAP explanations, compared to 5.88% [5.47%, 6.28%] for SHAP alone and 5.96% [5.50%, 6.42%] for no explanation. The lower-ACIV model (AI2, ACIV = 2.00% in MAPE) shows a smaller benefit from ILIV-SHAP (5.31% vs 6.22% for SHAP), confirming that ILIV-SHAP's advantage is conditional on the AI actually containing complementary information. The interaction between model ACIV and explanation type is highly significant (p < 0.001).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is vanilla SHAP explanations, which communicate each feature's average contribution to the AI's prediction. When the AI model contains genuine complementary information over human judgments (AI1, trained on all six features including two human-uninterpretable ones), ILIV-SHAP+SHAP reduces APE by 6.94%, significantly outperforming SHAP alone (5.88%, p = 0.002) and no explanation (5.96%, p = 0.010). However, when the AI model lacks complementary information (AI2, trained only on the four human-interpretable features), ILIV-SHAP+SHAP actually underperforms SHAP alone (5.31% vs 6.22%), suggesting the explanation is most effective when the AI truly has information the human does not. The cross-model comparison under ILIV-SHAP+SHAP is highly significant (p < 0.001), confirming that the framework's ACIV metric correctly identifies which model will yield the best human-AI team performance. In the chest X-ray and deepfake detection demonstrations, the framework further shows that all five deep-learning models provide complementary information over radiologist reports, and that AI predictions capture ~65% of total information value in deepfake detection while human decisions capture only ~15%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Bayesian decision-theoretic framework for evaluating human-AI collaboration (Guo et al., 2024; Kleinberg et al., 2015) by introducing complementary information value metrics (ACIV, ILIV) that quantify how much additional decision-relevant information a signal provides beyond what is already encoded in agent decisions. It adapts the SHAP explanation framework (Lundberg & Lee, 2017) by replacing model-output contributions with information-value contributions, yielding ILIV-SHAP. The information model formalization follows Blackwell (1951), and the robustness analysis over proper scoring rules draws on the V-shaped scoring rule decomposition from Li et al. (2022) and Kleinberg et al. (2023). Unlike prior work that primarily measures whether human-AI teams outperform individual agents, this paper focuses on *why* and *where* complementarity exists or fails, providing actionable diagnostics for intervention design.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a principled, decision-theoretic basis for answering practical questions that prior empirical studies could not: which AI model to deploy (by comparing ACIV), which features carry complementary information (via ILIV-SHAP), and how much 'room' remains for improvement (via the gap between achieved and full information value). The deepfake detection demonstration revealing that human-AI teams capture only 30% of available information value despite the AI alone capturing 65% quantifies the integration failure in a new, actionable way. Key limitations include the experiment being designed for complementarity by construction (uninterpretable features), and the need for validation in real deployment settings where information asymmetry is more complex. Open questions include extending the framework to multi-agent settings with private information, handling high-dimensional signals without the Algorithm 1 approximation, and understanding how ILIV-SHAP interacts with cognitive load in sustained decision workflows.
