## Problem

Standard sparse autoencoders (SAEs) trained on aligned multimodal embedding spaces (e.g., CLIP, CLAP) suffer from a 'split dictionary' phenomenon where most learned features activate only for a single modality, destroying the cross-modal alignment that makes these embedding spaces useful. This limits SAEs' utility for cross-modal interpretability, steering, and retrieval tasks, since manipulating a concept in one modality's sparse code cannot influence another modality's output.

## Input / Output

The method consumes paired multimodal embeddings (512-dim, unit-normalized) from a frozen encoder—e.g., CLIP ViT-B/16 image+text pairs from CC3M or LAION CLAP music+text pairs from JamendoMaxCaps—and produces an 8192-dim sparse code (K=32 active via TopK) plus a reconstructed embedding. Both modalities in a pair pass through the same shared encoder/decoder weights (with modality-specific bias terms) and a shared random mask, so the sparse codes are structurally encouraged to overlap. The learned dictionary (16× overcomplete) is the primary output used for concept naming, linear probe interpretation, and cross-modal steering.

## Method

The proposed Masked Group-Sparse Autoencoder (MGSAE) adds two mechanisms to a standard TopK SAE: (1) a group-sparse L2,1-norm loss applied to the paired sparse codes (z_x, z_y) that penalizes them from having disjoint supports, directly encouraging shared sparsity structure across modalities; and (2) a cross-modal random mask (probability p) applied identically to both modalities before the TopK operation, forcing the sparsification to select from the same coordinate subset at each step. The total loss is the sum of per-modality reconstruction errors plus λ·L_gs(z_x, z_y). A supporting theorem proves that any modality-split dictionary on an aligned embedding space can be improved to a non-split dictionary with strictly better cross-modal alignment, arguing that the split-dictionary bias is an artifact of reconstruction-only training rather than a fundamental limitation of the Linear Representation Hypothesis. The approach is validated on CLIP (image/text) and CLAP (audio/text) embeddings, showing fewer dead neurons, higher multimodal monosemanticity scores, and substantially better zero-shot cross-modal classification and retrieval compared to standard SAE, BatchTopK, and Matryoshka variants.

## Results

On zero-shot image/text classification, MGSAE achieves 84.2% on CIFAR-10, 55.4% on CIFAR-100, and 37.3% on ImageNet, substantially outperforming the standard TopK SAE (65.7%, 41.8%, 30.3%) and approaching the dense CLIP ViT-B/16 reference (91.6%, 68.7%, 68.6%). On zero-shot audio/text tasks, MGSAE reaches 67.2% on GTZAN genres and 35.4% on NSynth instruments, while GSAE achieves 70.5% on GTZAN—both far exceeding the standard SAE (37.6% and 26.5% respectively). The MGSAE also reduces dead neurons and increases the fraction of neurons co-activating across both modalities compared to all baselines, as measured by the proposed Multimodal Monosemanticity Score (MMS).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MGSAE outperforms the standard TopK SAE by approximately 18.5 percentage points on CIFAR-10 (84.2% vs. 65.7%), 13.6 points on CIFAR-100 (55.4% vs. 41.8%), and 7.0 points on ImageNet (37.3% vs. 30.3%). The advantage over BatchTopK SAE is even larger on CIFAR-100 (55.4% vs. 27.7%) and ImageNet (37.3% vs. 17.8%), where BatchTopK degrades most severely. Matryoshka SAE performs worst overall (58.7% CIFAR-10, 16.6% CIFAR-100, 18.5% ImageNet), with MGSAE exceeding it by 25.5, 38.8, and 18.8 points respectively. On the audio/text setting, GSAE achieves 70.5% on GTZAN (vs. 37.6% for SAE-TopK, nearly matching the dense CLAP reference of 71.0%), while MGSAE leads on NSynth (35.4% vs. 26.5%) and FMACaps retrieval (0.061 vs. 0.023 MRR). Both GSAE and MGSAE consistently reduce dead neurons and increase cross-modal co-activation relative to all three baselines across CLIP, CLAP, SigLIP2, and AIMv2 embedding spaces.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the sparse autoencoder (SAE) framework for neural network interpretability—originally developed for language models (Cunningham et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2024)—to the multimodal setting, where prior work (Papadimitriou et al., 2025; Pach et al., 2025; Costa et al., 2025) identified a 'split-dictionary' failure mode in which learned features activate for only one modality. Rather than applying post-hoc fixes (cross-modal transformations or co-activation pair identification), the authors propose a first-principles training modification: a group-sparse L2,1 loss on paired sparse codes combined with cross-modal random masking, drawing on classical group LASSO and multi-task learning regularization (Yuan & Lin, 2006; Argyriou et al., 2008). A key theoretical contribution (Theorem 1) proves that any split dictionary on an aligned embedding space can be improved to a non-split one, arguing the split phenomenon is an artifact of reconstruction-only training rather than a fundamental limitation of the Linear Representation Hypothesis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that sparse codes from the MGSAE on CLAP embeddings match or exceed the original dense CLAP embeddings on zero-shot tasks—despite being 16× more sparse—suggests that properly aligned sparse decompositions can preserve cross-modal utility while adding interpretability, a property that split dictionaries fundamentally cannot provide. The theoretical guarantee that split dictionaries are always improvable reframes the problem from 'is it possible?' to 'how do we train toward it?', opening the door to more principled multimodal SAE design. Open questions include extension to more than two modalities, integration with hierarchical SAE variants, and whether the approach generalizes to settings with large amounts of unpaired data (the authors sketch but do not fully investigate this). The first application of SAEs to audio/text (CLAP) embeddings also opens a new interpretability frontier for music understanding.
