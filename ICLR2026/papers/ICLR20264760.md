## Problem

Existing Koopman-based generalization bounds explain why high-rank weight matrices generalize well, but they rely on Sobolev spaces that require smooth, unbounded activation functions and unbounded data spaces, excluding realistic models using ReLU-type, sigmoid, or tanh activations on bounded domains. Moreover, the Koopman operator norm factors in those bounds are difficult to evaluate, obscuring the role of activation functions. This paper resolves both limitations by reformulating the analysis in L² function spaces via an RKHS construction.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes a deep model's weight matrices (including their singular values and determinants), activation functions, a data space specification (which may be bounded), and a sample size S. It produces a Rademacher complexity bound of the form O(∏ G_l ‖K_{σ_l}‖_{L²_l} / (√S · det(W_l*W_l)^{1/4})), where the determinant in the denominator means larger singular values (higher rank) yield tighter bounds. Unlike prior norm-based bounds that penalize large singular values, this bound rewards them, and the L² operator norms ‖K_{σ_l}‖ are explicitly computable for tanh, sigmoid, and Leaky ReLU via closed-form Jacobian or partition-based estimates.

## Method

The paper introduces an algebraic representation of L-layered deep models as compositions of group representation operators (for constant-width, invertible networks) or Koopman operators (for non-constant-width networks) acting on a Hilbert space, then defines a positive definite kernel on the parameter space via the inner product of model outputs. This kernel yields an RKHS that is isometrically isomorphic to the model's function space, enabling the use of the reproducing property to bound Rademacher complexity purely through operator norms of the activation and weight operators. The critical shift from Sobolev to L² spaces removes smoothness and unboundedness requirements, and for non-injective weight matrices the standard Koopman operator is replaced by a weighted Koopman operator that decomposes the integral over the kernel and its orthogonal complement. The framework is instantiated for invertible networks (affine group), non-constant-width networks (injective and general weight matrices), and CNNs (where convolution eigenvalues are Fourier components), with numerical validation on MNIST regression and classification tasks including LeNet.

## Results

This is primarily a theoretical paper, so the main contribution is a new Rademacher complexity bound of the form O(∏_l G_l‖K_{σ_l}‖_{L_l} / (√S · det(W_l*W_l)^{1/4})), which explains why high-rank weight matrices generalize well. Empirically, on a synthetic regression task over [-1,1]^3, the generalization error tracks the proposed bound throughout training (Figure 4a). On MNIST classification with dense layers, regularization derived from the proposed bound yields better test accuracy than regularization from the existing Koopman-based bound of Hashimoto et al. (2024) (Figure 4b). On LeNet/MNIST, adding the proposed bound as a regularization term improves test accuracy over training without regularization (Figure 4c).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the existing Koopman-based bound from Hashimoto et al. (2024, ICLR), which uses Sobolev-space operator norms ‖K_{σ_l}‖_{H_l}. The proposed bound replaces these with L²-space norms ‖K_{σ_l}‖_{L_l}, making it applicable to nonsmooth activations (Leaky ReLU), bounded activations (tanh, sigmoid), and bounded data spaces — all of which the existing bound cannot handle. On MNIST with a 4-layer dense network (784→1024→2048→10→10), regularization derived from the proposed bound achieves higher test accuracy than regularization from the Hashimoto et al. (2024) bound (Figure 4b), though the paper notes the small sample size (S=1000) makes high accuracy challenging. For LeNet on MNIST, the existing Koopman-based bound is entirely inapplicable because it requires smooth, unbounded activation functions, whereas the proposed bound successfully regularizes the tanh/softmax LeNet and improves test accuracy over the unregularized baseline (Figure 4c). The key practical advantage is that the Koopman operator norms in the proposed bound are explicitly evaluable via Lemmas 2.3–2.5 (e.g., ‖K_{tanh}‖ ≤ (∏ sup 1/(1−x²))^{1/2}), whereas the corresponding factors in the existing bound are described as 'extremely challenging' to evaluate.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends the Koopman-based generalization bound of Hashimoto et al. (2024), which was the first to explain why high-rank weight matrices generalize well via a determinant factor in the denominator. The key departure is replacing Sobolev spaces (which required smooth, unbounded activation functions and unbounded data spaces) with L² function spaces and an RKHS constructed from a positive definite kernel on the parameter space. The algebraic framework draws on group representation theory (affine group for invertible networks, Heisenberg group for new model structures) and Koopman/weighted Koopman operators for non-constant-width networks, with Schur's lemma and the von Neumann double commutant theorem establishing universality. This positions the work as a bridge between the abstract Koopman-operator analysis and practical architectures (CNNs, networks with tanh/sigmoid/Leaky ReLU) that prior norm-based and compression-based bounds could not address.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that the bound becomes evaluable for realistic models: the Koopman operator norms for tanh, sigmoid, and Leaky ReLU are explicitly bounded (Lemmas 2.3–2.5), and the framework handles bounded data spaces and non-constant widths. However, the bound remains invalid for exact ReLU (whose derivative vanishes on a domain), and the empirical validation is limited to MNIST with small networks (784→1024→2048→10) and a 2-layer regression toy problem. Open questions include whether weighted Koopman operators can extend the framework to piecewise-constant activations, whether the regularization terms derived from the bound are competitive with standard techniques at scale, and whether the tradeoff between the regularizer E(c) and approximation fidelity (Remark 4.3) is well-characterized in practice.
