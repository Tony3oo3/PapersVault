## Problem

Sparse-reward goal-conditioned RL suffers from a credit assignment problem where agents receive no intermediate feedback, rendering standard exploration ineffective. Existing intrinsic motivation methods (curiosity, surprise, novelty) either lack theoretical guarantees, require expensive pre-training via behavioral cloning, or depend on explicit model-based formulations. This paper seeks a principled, on-policy intrinsic reward with provable convergence benefits that works from scratch without pre-training.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a LiDAR observation vector s_t ∈ [0,1]^N (32 dimensions per object category, normalized so that 1 indicates extreme proximity and 0 indicates far-away readings) as the egocentric state representation. It produces a continuous navigation action via a PPO policy network π_θ(a_t|s_t) and a dense intrinsic reward signal r_int(t) computed from the change in effective resistance between the agent and goal nodes in a per-timestep graph. The extrinsic reward is sparsified to fire only every K=25 steps, and the total reward combines both via r_total = r_ext + α·r_int.

## Method

At each timestep, the LiDAR state vector is transformed into a weighted undirected graph G_t = (V_t, E_t, W_t) where nodes represent the agent, goal, and clustered objects, and edge weights encode proximity. The intrinsic reward is defined as the negative change in Effective Resistance R_eff(A, g; G_t) between the agent and goal nodes, computed via the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse of the graph Laplacian, with large ±β penalties for goal visibility loss/recovery. The key theoretical insight is that decreasing effective resistance is mathematically linked to increasing algebraic connectivity (Lemma 1), and that r_int acts as a state-dependent variance reduction baseline for the policy gradient, yielding a sample complexity of O(U(2−2ρ)) where ρ is the correlation between the Q-function and −α·R_eff. The method is trained entirely on-policy with PPO, requires no pre-training, and provides (ε,δ,T)-robustness guarantees for the learned navigation policy.

## Results

On the hardest difficulty (Level 2) across Safety-Gymnasium environments, the proposed Effective Resistance intrinsic reward achieves a 55.5% success rate on Navigation (vs. 38.7% for the strongest baseline GCPO), 88.4% on Building (vs. 55.7% GCPO), and reduces median timesteps to goal by up to 56% (242 vs. 467 on Navigation-2, 168 vs. 384 on Building-2). Median normalized reward improves by up to 4× (0.0068 vs. 0.0017 on Navigation-2). Training convergence is also substantially faster, with loss plateauing at ~576 iterations vs. ~1636 for PPO on Building-Level-0, consistent with the theoretical variance reduction bound U_total = O(U(2−2ρ)).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method consistently outperforms all six baselines across all three environment families and difficulty levels, with the largest gaps at the hardest (Level 2) settings. GCPO (Xudong et al., 2024), the strongest baseline, is surpassed by 16.8 percentage points in success rate on Navigation-2 (55.5% vs. 38.7%) and by 32.7 points on Building-2 (88.4% vs. 55.7%), while also halving the median timesteps (242 vs. 467 and 168 vs. 384 respectively). The model-based Surprise RL variants (Achiam & Sastry, 2017) perform poorly in the hardest settings, with median timesteps of 769–881 on Navigation-2 and near-zero or negative rewards, reflecting their reliance on explicit transition dynamics. MEGA (Pitis et al., 2020) and NGU (Badia et al., 2020) struggle most in the Fading environment where the goal disappears, hitting the 1000-step cap, whereas the proposed method reaches the goal in a median of 248 steps. Notably, GCPO requires expensive Behavioral Cloning pre-training, while the proposed method trains from scratch in a fully on-policy manner, yet still achieves superior performance. Vanilla PPO and PPO+Entropy (Schulman et al., 2017) fail to learn meaningful policies in Level 2 settings, with success rates near zero and rewards at or below zero, underscoring the critical role of the intrinsic reward signal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by importing effective resistance from spectral graph theory into the intrinsic reward formulation for goal-conditioned RL, departing from the dominant information-theoretic approaches (novelty, curiosity, surprise) that dominate the intrinsic motivation literature. It positions itself against off-policy methods like HER and MEGA, model-based surprise methods (Achiam & Sastry), and the recent on-policy GCPO (which requires behavioral cloning pretraining), offering an on-policy-from-scratch alternative that requires no pretrained policy or explicit dynamics model. The variance reduction baseline interpretation connects the intrinsic reward to the classical policy gradient baseline literature, providing a theoretical justification that most prior intrinsic motivation methods lack. Unlike quasimetric learning approaches that learn distance metrics from data, this work leverages the fixed inductive bias of spectral graph theory for immediate structural interpretability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a rare combination of theoretical guarantees (variance reduction, robustness, sample complexity bounds) with strong empirical results for intrinsic motivation in sparse-reward RL, addressing a gap where most prior methods offer only empirical validation. The finding that effective resistance naturally serves as a state-dependent variance reduction baseline is an elegant conceptual contribution that could inform future reward shaping designs. However, the experimental scope is limited to 2D Safety-Gymnasium navigation tasks, the graph construction algorithm involves several ad hoc design choices (clustering threshold, inter-cluster connectivity), and the theoretical results rely on approximations (e.g., focusing on the Fiedler vector term in the spectral decomposition). Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to higher-dimensional continuous control, how sensitive performance is to the graph construction heuristics, and whether the variance reduction argument holds rigorously for PPO rather than just vanilla policy gradient.
