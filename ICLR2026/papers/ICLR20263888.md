## Problem

Scientific time series remain largely unexplored for LLMs: current multimodal LLMs handle them by serialising as text (excessively long sequences) or rasterising as images (loss of numerical precision), while existing unified time series models specialise in narrow task types like forecasting and their effectiveness on heterogeneous, non-periodic scientific signals is unclear. No comprehensive benchmark or LLM-compatible framework exists for unified scientific time series understanding and generation across diverse domains.

## Input / Output

The method accepts a time series signal (univariate or multivariate, up to 58 channels, lengths from 1 to 10^7 points, frequencies from 10^-5 to 10^7 Hz) concatenated with a natural-language task prompt. For understanding tasks (classification, anomaly detection, MCQ), the output is a discrete text token sequence; for generation tasks (forecasting up to 720 steps, imputation, event localisation, synthesis), the output is a continuous time series of variable length, with a predefined set of regression heads selected by closest target length and truncation applied as needed.

## Method

TimeOmni couples a time series encoder with a Qwen3-8B LLM backbone. The encoder uses a router that selects from a family of patch experts based on the flattened signal length (ensuring 100–200 output tokens), applies 1-D convolution to patchified segments, and then a patch reprogramming module that cross-attends the patch embeddings against a projected subset of the LLM's vocabulary embeddings to align temporal representations with the language model's embedding space. For understanding tasks the prompt is appended after the time series encoding (prompt-as-suffix); for generation tasks it precedes it (prompt-as-prefix). The LLM produces text via a standard softmax head or a time series via a flattening-and-linear regression head. The model is initialised from Qwen3-8B pretrained weights and fine-tuned with DoRA (rank 8, alpha 32) at learning rate 2×10^-5 for 10 epochs, jointly optimising understanding and generation tasks.

## Results

On the SciTS benchmark (54,023 instances, 43 tasks, 12 disciplines), TimeOmni achieves the best average ranking of 1.9 on understanding tasks (F1) and 4.1 on generation tasks (swMAPE), outperforming all 16 baselines. The strongest LLM baseline, Gemini-2.5-Flash (text input), ranks 5.5 on understanding and 4.6 on generation. TimeOmni's per-discipline F1 scores range from 45.9% (Physiology) to 96.4% (Economics), while its swMAPE on generation tasks ranges from 2.2% (Earth Science) to 656.5% (Mathematics). Specialized forecasting models (Moirai, TimeMoE, Chronos) achieve the lowest swMAPE on supported forecasting tasks (e.g., Moirai: 1.8% on Economics) but fail on imputation, event localisation, and QA tasks entirely.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TimeOmni decisively outperforms all 16 baselines on understanding tasks, achieving an average rank of 1.9 versus 5.5 for the best LLM (Gemini-2.5-Flash, text input) and 7.9 for the best specialized time-series model (UniTS). The gap is most dramatic in scientific domains: TimeOmni scores 73.2% F1 on Astronomy vs. 42.3% (GPT-5-mini) and 58.1% on Bioacoustics vs. 10.7% (GPT-5-mini), where all LLMs and specialized models struggle. On generation tasks, TimeOmni (AvgRk 4.1) narrowly edges out Gemini-2.5-Flash text input (4.6), with particularly large margins on Astronomy (2.8% vs. 55.4% swMAPE) and Earth Science (2.2% vs. 24.6%), though specialized forecasters like Moirai (1.8% swMAPE) and Chronos (41.5%) still lead on narrow forecasting sub-tasks they support. A key finding is that general-purpose LLMs (GPT-4.1-mini, Gemini-2.5-Flash, DeepSeek-V3) generalize better to unseen scientific domains than specialized time-series models (Moirai, TimeMoE, Chronos), which collapse to bottom rankings in most disciplines due to limited task coverage. Representing time series as text outperforms image input for generation tasks (where numerical precision matters), while image input helps understanding tasks by compressing long sequences—TimeOmni avoids both trade-offs by natively encoding temporal signals.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Time-LLM's (Jin et al., 2024) patch reprogramming mechanism as a core architectural component of TimeOmni's encoder, adapting it to route across multiple patch experts for scale-adaptive processing. It departs from two dominant paradigms: (1) multimodal LLMs that serialize time series as text or rasterize them as images, which the authors argue suffer from excessive sequence length and numerical precision loss respectively, and (2) unified time series foundation models (Moirai, TimeMoE, Chronos, UniTS) that typically specialise in forecasting or a narrow task set and lack compatibility with general-purpose LLM training. The SciTS benchmark itself extends prior time series benchmarks (Time-MQA, Time-MMD, MTBench, SFE) by introducing scientific-domain signals with extreme heterogeneity in frequency (10⁻⁵ to 10⁷ Hz), length (10⁰ to 10⁷ points), and dimensionality (up to 58 channels), alongside both understanding and generation tasks in a unified prompt-based format.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SciTS fills a clear gap as the first large-scale benchmark dedicated to scientific time series, and its 54k instances across 12 disciplines will likely become a standard evaluation suite for time series LLM research. The finding that general-purpose LLMs outperform specialised time series models on most scientific domains challenges the assumption that domain-specific architectures are necessary, suggesting that broad pretraining knowledge transfers better to unseen scientific signals than narrow multi-domain time series training. However, the evaluation is limited to zero-shot settings, TimeOmni is a single working example with modest ablations, and the finetuning experiments (Qwen2.5-VL, TimeMoE) are small-scale, leaving open whether the architectural insights generalise to larger models or longer training. A key open question is whether the patch-expert routing and reprogramming design choices are truly necessary or whether simpler explicit temporal encoders could achieve comparable results at lower cost.
