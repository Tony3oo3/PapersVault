## Problem

Learned branching for CDCL SAT solvers has been limited to either instance-level signals that exert no influence once the branching loop begins (SATformer) or reinforcement learning agents that suffer from sparse rewards, delayed feedback, and unstable exploration (Graph-Q-SAT). Since unit propagation accounts for roughly 89% of CDCL wall-clock time, a branching policy that directly reduces propagation is critical, yet no prior method provides dense, step-level supervision aligned with the prefix-conditioned structure of branching.

## Input / Output

The model receives a single serialized token sequence: the CNF formula in DIMACS integer format, a [SEP] delimiter, the KeyTrace prefix (each surviving decision literal followed by its implied unit-propagation assignments, with level fields omitted), and a trailing [D] probe marker. It outputs one signed variable (±1 to ±n) as the next branch decision. At inference the policy is queried under a small budget (3–5 calls per instance); if the predicted literal is illegal (already assigned or out of range) or the budget is exhausted, the solver immediately falls back to the native VSIDS heuristic, preserving completeness.

## Method

ImitSAT has two core components. First, KeyTrace construction scans a full CDCL trail left-to-right, appending decisions and propagations while trimming all suffix events above the backtrack level, thereby collapsing detours into a compact sequence of surviving root-to-current decisions; replaying this KeyTrace on the same instance is nearly conflict-free, using only ~4% of the original propagations. Second, a Perceiver-AR autoregressive model (12 blocks, 16 heads, single latent token) is trained by behavior cloning to predict the next signed variable given the serialized CNF + KeyTrace prefix, with O(N) per-query cross-attention cost. Training employs variable-permutation augmentation to prevent overfitting to variable identities and a staged curriculum that expands the variable range from 5–15 to 61–100, yielding stable convergence and strong generalization to UNSAT and non-k-SAT families without retraining.

## Results

On held-out random 3-SAT test sets, ImitSAT with 3 model queries achieves a Median Relative Propagation Percentage (MRPP r̃) of 0.73–0.78 across all variable ranges (5–15 through 61–100), meaning it reduces propagation counts by 22–27% relative to MiniSAT. The one-percent win rate (W₁%) reaches 0.60–0.69, indicating the method reduces propagations by ≥1% on the majority of instances. On structured SAT families (Table 2), ImitSAT achieves MRPP r̃ as low as 0.30 on PARITY and 0.42 on PRET, with a perfect W₁% of 1.00 on PRET. On wall-clock time (Table 12), ImitSAT outperforms MiniSAT on the hardest 61–100 range (6.11 s vs. 6.44 s per 50 instances) while remaining competitive on smaller ranges.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Graph-Q-SAT (GQSAT), ImitSAT consistently wins on both MRPP and W₁% across all random 3-SAT ranges: with 3 calls, ImitSAT achieves r̃ = 0.73–0.78 versus GQSAT's 0.89–1.15, and GQSAT actually *increases* propagation on the 61–100 range (r̃ = 1.15). Against SATformer, ImitSAT wins on MRPP in 5 of 6 ranges (e.g., 0.75 vs. 1.00 on 5–15; 0.74 vs. 0.88 on 50) and ties on 61–100 (0.78 vs. 0.78), while also achieving higher W₁% in most settings. Against pure MiniSAT (the reference solver), ImitSAT reduces propagations (r̃ < 1) in all ranges with 3 calls, and on wall-clock time it beats MiniSAT on the hardest 61–100 range (6.11 s vs. 6.44 s) but is slower on the easier 16–30 range (0.85 s vs. 0.53 s) due to model-query overhead. When integrated with CaDiCaL, ImitSAT further reduces MRPP to 0.63–0.75 on random 3-SAT and achieves W₁% of 0.62–0.73, confirming the policy transfers to a modern solver. On the SATCOMP industrial subset, ImitSAT matches SATformer and clearly outperforms GQSAT on the hardest instance groups in wall-clock time.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the two dominant paradigms for learned CDCL branching—reinforcement learning (Graph-Q-SAT) and instance-level signal prediction (SATformer)—by adopting imitation learning with dense, decision-level supervision. The KeyTrace construction, which collapses backtracks from a full CDCL trail into a compact sequence of surviving decisions, is the paper's central conceptual contribution, providing clean, near-conflict-free training targets that neither RL nor GNN-based initialization can offer. The autoregressive formulation draws on the Decision Transformer's framing of sequential decision-making as next-token prediction, and the Perceiver-AR architecture is borrowed from long-context sequence modeling, but the application to CDCL branching with prefix-conditioned supervision is new. The authors explicitly distinguish their setting from MIP branch-and-bound imitation learning, noting that SAT CDCL's non-monotone search with restarts and clause learning creates fundamentally different dynamics than MIP's monotone tree with node-local feedback.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Since unit propagation accounts for ~89% of CDCL wall-clock time, any method that reliably reduces propagation has direct practical impact on solver speed. ImitSAT's ability to generalize from random 3-SAT training to structured SAT/UNSAT families (PARITY, PRET, PHOLE) and SATCOMP instances without retraining suggests the learned branching policy captures transferable structural knowledge rather than overfitting to the training distribution. Key limitations include the restriction to ≤100 variables (constrained by text-based serialization and 4×V100 training budget), the use of a Python MiniSAT reimplementation rather than production solvers, and the modest query budget (3–5 calls) that limits the policy's influence on later search stages. Open questions include whether the approach scales to industrial-scale instances, whether hybrid imitation-reinforcement schemes could extend the policy beyond the expert's coverage, and whether richer expert sources (e.g., multiple solvers or portfolio runs) could improve imitation quality.
