## Problem

Existing redaction benchmarks evaluate removal of predefined PII categories or surface-level entity spans, failing to capture whether contextually inferable sensitive information survives redaction under policy-defined constraints. There is no standardized framework to quantify the trade-off between security (removing sensitive content) and utility (preserving non-sensitive content) across diverse domains and policy types, leaving practitioners without a rigorous way to assess whether LLM-based redaction provides genuine privacy protection rather than a 'false sense of privacy.'

## Input / Output

The redaction system receives a source text (drawn from individual, corporate, or government documents) paired with one or more natural-language security policies as input, and produces a redacted text as output. Evaluation is proposition-based: each source text is decomposed into minimal factual propositions (8,053 total) that include not only explicitly stated facts but also contextually inferable information (e.g., inferring organizational affiliation from a meeting location). An LLM judge (GPT-4.1-mini) then determines whether each proposition remains inferable from the redacted text, yielding a confusion matrix from which security (TN/(TN+FP)) and utility (TP/(TP+FN)) scores are computed.

## Method

The core contribution is a proposition-based evaluation framework that decomposes each text into atomic factual units and tracks their inferability after redaction, rather than checking for removal of specific entity spans. Three redaction strategies are evaluated: token-level masking (keyword/pattern matching), adversarial redaction (adapting Staab et al. 2025's two-step approach where the model first identifies policy-violating content, then rewrites by generalizing rather than masking), and iterative redaction (repeatedly applying the redaction model to its own output). The benchmark comprises 514 human-authored texts from three domains (individual, corporate, government) paired with 187 security policies spanning micro-level (e.g., instructor names) to macro-level (e.g., strategic business plans) sensitivity. Experiments across 11 LLMs reveal that while more capable models and iterative strategies improve security, they consistently degrade utility, and even the best automated system (GPT-5-mini with two AR iterations: 80.9% security) lags substantially behind manual redaction (up to 72.6% security at 57.5% utility), indicating significant headroom for future work.

## Results

On RedacBench, the best security score is 80.9% (GPT-5-mini with 2 iterations of adversarial redaction), but this comes at a severe utility cost of only 37.6% non-sensitive information preserved. Masking achieves a consistent ceiling of roughly 36–52% security across all models regardless of capability, while adversarial redaction shows clear model-dependent variation. Claude-Sonnet-4 achieves one of the most favorable security-utility balances (68.5% / 55.8% at AR iter2), and the open-source Qwen3-4B-2507 model reaches performance between GPT-4.1 and GPT-4.1-mini (75.8% / 44.4% at AR iter2).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates three redaction strategies (token-level Masking, single-pass Adversarial Redaction, and Iterative Adversarial Redaction) across eleven LLMs. Masking reaches a performance ceiling of ~36–52% security regardless of model size, confirming that keyword-based removal cannot capture semantically sensitive content. Adversarial Redaction (iter1) improves security substantially (46–72%), with reasoning-enhanced models (GPT-5, GPT-4.1) outperforming smaller variants by 10–20 points. Iterative redaction (iter2) further boosts security (57–81%) but consistently degrades utility by 5–20 points relative to iter1, illustrating the core security-utility trade-off. Among models, GPT-5-mini achieves the highest absolute security (80.9%) at AR iter2 but the lowest utility (37.6%), while Claude-Sonnet-4 offers the best balance (68.5% / 55.8%). Notably, the open-source Qwen3-4B-2507 (75.8% / 44.4%) outperforms GPT-4.1-mini (60.2% / 62.9%) on security, and GPT-4.1-mini after 7 AR iterations slightly surpasses GPT-5 with only 2 iterations, suggesting iterative refinement can partially compensate for model-scale differences.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the adversarial anonymization framework of Staab et al. (2025) from PII-focused anonymization to a broader policy-conditioned redaction task, where sensitivity is defined by arbitrary security policies rather than fixed entity categories. It departs from prior text sanitization benchmarks (Albanese et al., 2023; Asthana et al., 2025; Garza et al., 2025) that evaluate entity-span removal or keyword matching, instead introducing a proposition-based evaluation that tests whether sensitive information remains *inferable* after redaction. The benchmark also draws on the inference-based privacy threat literature (Staab et al., 2024; Beltrame et al., 2024; Gusain & Leith, 2025) to motivate why surface-level removal is insufficient, but provides the first concrete, multi-domain benchmark operationalizing this concern across individual, corporate, and government texts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RedacBench fills a critical gap: no prior benchmark systematically evaluates whether LLM-based redaction actually prevents inference of policy-defined sensitive information while preserving text utility. The results reveal that even the strongest models (GPT-5-mini) achieve only 80.9% security at the cost of retaining just 37.6% utility, and the ceiling analysis shows manual redaction substantially outperforms all automated methods, indicating the task is far from solved. Key open questions include whether the security-utility tradeoff is fundamental or an artifact of current prompting strategies, whether iterative refinement can reliably compensate for model scale, and how to incorporate formal privacy guarantees without destroying fluency. The benchmark and its interactive playground provide a practical foundation for future work in this area.
