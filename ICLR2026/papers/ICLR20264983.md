## Problem

State-of-the-art diffusion distillation methods have reached a practical floor of ~4 sampling steps, and further step reduction yields diminishing returns. The paper identifies an underexploited axis of efficiency: existing few-step models operate at a fixed latent resolution throughout the diffusion process, even though spectral analysis shows that high-frequency components are already suppressed by noise at high timesteps, making full-resolution computation redundant at intermediate steps.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264983/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt and produces a 1024×1024 image (or 81×480×832 video) in 4–6 sampling steps. Unusually, the generation is progressive: the model starts at a lower latent resolution (e.g., 32×32 or 64×64 for images, 6×20×34 for video) and upsamples to the target resolution at each subsequent step, so the effective compute per step decreases for early timesteps. All models are distilled exclusively on synthetic data generated by their own teacher, avoiding external data biases.

## Method

SwD unifies multi-scale generation within a single few-step model by pairing each timestep t_i with a progressively increasing latent resolution s_i. At each step, the model predicts a clean sample x̂₀ at the current scale, which is upsampled (bicubic spatially, adjacent-frame blending temporally) and renoised to the next noise level—preserving correct noise statistics and avoiding the out-of-distribution artifacts of directly upscaling noisy latents. The training objective combines a novel patch-level MMD loss (MSE between per-image spatial token means extracted from the teacher's middle transformer block after noising both real and fake samples) with standard DMD and GAN losses; the MMD term requires no additional trainable models and serves as a competitive standalone distillation objective. The student is initialized from the teacher with LoRA adapters and trained for ~3K iterations on teacher-generated synthetic data, with pixel-space downscaling before VAE encoding to produce multi-resolution training pairs.

## Results

On text-to-image generation, SwD models achieve state-of-the-art HPSv3, ImageReward, and GenEval scores within their model families while being ~2× faster than the fastest full-resolution distilled counterparts. For example, FLUX-SwD (4 steps, 0.72 s/img) reaches HPSv3 14.6 and GenEval 0.71 on MJHQ-30K, surpassing FLUX-Schnell (4 steps, 1.41 s/img) at HPSv3 11.2 and GenEval 0.69. On text-to-video, 4-step SwD on Wan2.1-1.3B achieves VBench-2.0 Overall 53.2 and Video Reward 6.27 at 1.8 s/video, outperforming both the 50-step teacher (51.6, 5.43 at 137 s/video) and the 3-step CausVid baseline (52.3, 6.21 at 4.2 s/video).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against full-resolution few-step distillation baselines, SwD consistently wins on both speed and quality: FLUX-SwD (0.72 s) is ~2× faster than FLUX-Schnell (1.41 s) while scoring +3.4 HPSv3 (14.6 vs 11.2) and +0.02 GenEval (0.71 vs 0.69) on MJHQ-30K; SD3.5-L-SwD (0.39 s) is ~1.6× faster than SD3.5-L-Turbo (0.63 s) with +2.8 HPSv3 (12.8 vs 10.0) and +0.27 IR (1.20 vs 0.93). Against distribution-matching distillation baselines, SDXL-SwD (0.11 s) is ~1.8× faster than SDXL-DMD2 (0.20 s) and achieves higher HPSv3 (12.4 vs 12.0) and IR (0.95 vs 0.87) on COCO-30K. For text-to-video, 4-step SwD (1.8 s/video) is ~2.3× faster than 3-step CausVid (4.2 s/video) while improving Vision Reward (0.064 vs 0.042), Video Reward (6.27 vs 6.21), and VBench-2.0 Overall (53.2 vs 52.3). SwD also outperforms its own teacher models: FLUX-SwD exceeds the 30-step FLUX teacher in HPSv3 (14.6 vs 12.4) and GenEval (0.71 vs 0.66) at 14× lower latency, and Wan2.1-SwD surpasses the 50-step Wan2.1 teacher in all three video metrics at 72× lower latency. The only notable trade-off is a slight increase in image defects for FLUX-SwD compared to the much slower FLUX and Hyper-FLUX baselines, attributed to the 4-step budget.

## Relation to Prior Work

SwD sits at the intersection of two research threads: (1) distribution matching distillation (DMD, DMD2, ADD) that compresses diffusion models into few-step generators, and (2) progressive/coarse-to-fine generation, previously explored in cascaded diffusion models and next-scale prediction architectures (Switti, Infinity). The paper departs from cascaded approaches by unifying multi-scale generation within a single model and single diffusion process, and from prior progressive DMs by leveraging the stochastic renoising mechanism of consistency-style few-step samplers to handle scale transitions. The MMD-based patch-level loss is a novel adaptation of feature-matching ideas from GAN training (Salimans et al., 2016) into the diffusion distillation setting, using a pretrained DM as a fixed feature extractor rather than a learnable discriminator.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: SwD delivers ~2× speedup for text-to-image and ~3× for text-to-video at matched quality, approaching the latency of just two full-resolution steps while outperforming them under the same compute budget. The MMD loss is a useful, lightweight addition to the distillation toolkit that requires no extra trainable models and can serve as a competitive standalone objective, potentially simplifying distillation pipelines. Open questions include whether the approach extends to 1–2 step generation, whether the MMD loss can fully replace DMD (which degrades when teachers struggle at low resolutions, as with SDXL and Wan2.1), and whether the scale/timestep schedules can be learned rather than hand-tuned. The spectral analysis of VAE latents, while confirmatory, provides a useful empirical foundation for future work on resolution-adaptive diffusion.
