## Problem

Existing medical QA benchmarks emphasize multiple-choice or fact-based tasks, leaving open-ended answers to real patient questions underexplored. This gap is especially critical in mental health, where patient queries mix symptoms, treatment concerns, and emotional needs, and where even small errors (speculative claims, unsafe recommendations, dismissive tone) can have immediate negative consequences. No large-scale, clinician-grounded benchmark existed for systematically evaluating and stress-testing LLMs on authentic, open-ended mental health QA.

## Input / Output

COUNSELBENCH-EVAL takes 100 real patient questions from the CounselChat forum (spanning 20 mental health topics) and evaluates four answer providers (GPT-4, LLaMA-3.3, Gemini-1.5-Pro, and online human therapists) on each, yielding 2,000 expert-annotated QA pairs. Each annotation includes six-dimension scores (5-point Likert for overall quality, empathy, specificity, toxicity; 4-point for factual consistency; binary yes/no for medical advice), span-level text extractions, and written rationales (median 576.5 words per survey). COUNSELBENCH-ADV takes 120 clinician-authored adversarial questions and produces 1,080 LLM responses (9 models × 120 questions), each annotated with a yes/no/not-sure classification for whether a targeted failure mode (medication advice, therapy suggestion, symptom speculation, judgmental tone, apathetic tone, or unsupported assumptions) is present.

## Method

The benchmark is constructed in two complementary stages. First, 100 licensed mental health professionals (recruited via Upwork with credential verification) independently rate each of the four responses per question across six clinically grounded dimensions, with span-level annotations and written rationales, yielding 2,000 evaluations with Krippendorff's alpha ≥ 0.72. Second, 10 of those experts author 120 adversarial questions specifically designed to trigger fine-grained failure modes identified from the first stage (e.g., GPT-4's tendency to recommend specific medications, LLaMA-3.3's symptom speculation, Gemini's apathetic tone), and five additional experts classify whether each of 1,080 LLM responses exhibits the targeted failure. A key empirical finding is that LLM-as-judge experiments (nine models) systematically overrate response quality, assign near-perfect factual consistency scores, uniformly assign lowest toxicity scores, and fail to detect problematic spans, revealing a critical misalignment between automated and human expert evaluation in high-stakes mental health settings.

## Results

On CounselBench-Eval (2,000 expert evaluations across 100 questions), LLaMA-3.3 achieves the highest overall quality score (4.29/5), leading on five of six dimensions, while online human therapists score lowest overall (2.60/5). However, LLaMA-3.3 has the highest rate of unauthorized medical advice flags (14%), and GPT-4 responses include safety disclaimers in ~1/3 of cases. Inter-rater reliability is strong (Krippendorff's α ≥ 0.72 across all ordinal dimensions). In the LLM-as-judge experiment, all nine LLM judges systematically overrate responses and uniformly assign near-zero toxicity scores, with Gemini-1.5-Pro paradoxically ranked above GPT-4 by every LLM judge despite being rated lowest by human experts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four answer providers—GPT-4, LLaMA-3.3, Gemini-1.5-Pro, and online human therapists—on identical patient questions. LLaMA-3.3 outperforms all other providers on Overall Quality (4.29 vs. 3.28/3.26/2.60), Empathy (4.22), Specificity (4.63), and Toxicity (1.36, lower is better), but carries the highest safety risk with 14% of responses flagged for unauthorized medical advice (vs. 7–8% for GPT-4/Gemini). GPT-4 differentiates itself through safety disclaimers, with roughly one-third of outputs explicitly declining to answer and recommending human consultation. Gemini-1.5-Pro scores lowest on Empathy (2.76) and is the model most frequently criticized for lacking emotional attunement (44.1% of low-rated responses). Online human therapists, despite being the 'gold standard' reference, score lowest on Overall Quality (2.60) and highest on Toxicity (2.56), with 46.7% of low-rated responses flagged for overgeneralization. In the adversarial benchmark (CounselBench-Adv, 1,080 responses from 9 LLMs), failure modes show clear family-level patterns: GPT-5 is most vulnerable to therapy suggestions (85%) and medication advice (47%), while GPT-3.5-Turbo is most apathetic (70%), and LLaMA models consistently speculate about symptoms (45%). LLM-as-judge evaluations diverge sharply from human expert rankings, with the best LLM judge (Claude-3.7-Sonnet) achieving only 0.50 F1 on failure-mode detection.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of medical QA benchmarks (MedQA, MedMCQA, MultiMedQA, HealthBench) that emphasize multiple-choice or fact-based accuracy, instead targeting open-ended mental health QA where responses must balance empathy, safety, and contextual sensitivity. It extends the LLM-as-judge paradigm by stress-testing its reliability in a high-stakes, subjective clinical domain, and moves adversarial evaluation beyond predefined, literature-driven failure modes to empirically-identified, model-specific vulnerabilities extracted from expert-annotated rationales. The benchmark is conceptually grounded in clinical psychology literature (therapeutic alliance, person-centered therapy, clinical boundaries) rather than purely NLP evaluation criteria, positioning it as a practitioner-anchored resource rather than a purely computational one.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As LLMs are increasingly deployed in mental health platforms (CounselChat, NOCD, EHR messaging) where even small errors—speculative claims, unsafe recommendations, dismissive tone—can have immediate negative consequences, this benchmark provides the first large-scale, clinician-validated framework for consistent model comparison on authentic patient questions. The finding that LLM judges systematically overrate quality and uniformly miss safety concerns (assigning lowest toxicity scores even when humans flagged harmful content) has broad implications for any automated evaluation pipeline in healthcare. Open questions include whether the identified adversarial failure patterns persist across model generations, whether the single-turn evaluation generalizes to multi-turn therapeutic dialogue, and whether the US-centric annotator pool limits cross-cultural applicability.
