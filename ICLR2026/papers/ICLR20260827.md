## Problem

Iteratively retraining generative models on their own synthetic outputs is known to cause model collapse, but prior theoretical analyses assumed unfiltered synthetic data or idealized verifiers. This paper asks whether a realistic, imperfect verifier that filters low-quality synthetic samples via binary accept/reject feedback can prevent collapse, and characterizes both the short-term gains and long-term convergence behavior of such verifier-guided retraining.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260827/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an initial real dataset (e.g., 500 MNIST images or 12.5% of XSUM training set), a generative model (CVAE, SmolLM2-135M, or OLS estimator), and a verifier that provides only binary Yes/No judgments based on whether each synthetic sample is consistent with a knowledge set B_r(θ_c) — a spherical ball of radius r centered at θ_c. The verifier does not reveal θ_c or r directly; it simply accepts or rejects each (x_i, y_i) pair. The output is a sequence of retrained model parameters {θ̂^k} that, in the long run, converge to the verifier's knowledge center θ_c rather than the true parameter θ*, with the convergence rate governed by the verifier's selectivity r and the synthetic sample size n_k.

## Method

The paper formalizes verifier-based synthetic retraining as a generate-verify-retrain loop in the linear regression setting, where synthetic covariates are aligned with the right singular vectors of the initial real data matrix to diagonalize the Markov transition operator. The key theoretical insight is that the verifier's filtering rule transforms the retraining update from an identity mapping (which causes collapse) into a contraction mapping T(·) with contraction factor ρ < 1, guaranteeing convergence to the verifier's knowledge center θ_c (Theorem 4.1). For the one-step case, a new bias-variance decomposition (Theorem 3.1) shows that filtering reduces estimation variance by a factor m_{2,j} < 1 along each singular direction but injects a systematic bias proportional to the verifier's offset Δ = ‖θ* − θ_c‖, yielding strict improvement when the variance reduction outweighs the injected bias. Empirically, the framework is validated on CVAEs trained on 500 MNIST images (showing FID improvement over 40 retraining rounds with a discriminator verifier) and SmolLM2-135M fine-tuned on XSUM (showing monotonic ROUGE-1 gains with top-12.5% ROUGE-filtered synthetic summaries), confirming that the bias-variance trade-off and convergence-to-verifier-center dynamics generalize beyond the linear model.

## Results

On MNIST (CVAE initialized with 500 real images), verifier-filtered synthetic retraining over 40 iterations achieves a FID of 21.17, approaching the 17.56 FID of a CVAE trained on all 60K real images, while unfiltered retraining causes severe degradation. On XSUM (SmolLM2-135M, 15 retraining rounds), filtered retraining (top 12.5% by ROUGE-1) yields monotonic early ROUGE-1 improvements before stabilizing, whereas unfiltered retraining fluctuates around its initial score with no meaningful gain. In the linear regression simulation, the estimator converges to the verifier's knowledge center θ_c under a biased verifier (Δ=1) and to the true parameter θ* under an unbiased verifier (Δ=0), with convergence rate governed by the contraction constant ρ<1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between verifier-filtered synthetic retraining and unfiltered synthetic retraining (the standard model-collapse setting). Filtered retraining consistently outperforms unfiltered retraining across all three experimental settings: on MNIST, unfiltered retraining leads to severe FID degradation and mode collapse, while filtered retraining steadily improves; on XSUM, unfiltered retraining shows no significant ROUGE-1 gain over 15 rounds, while filtered retraining improves monotonically in early iterations. A second comparison is against the full-data upper bound: the best filtered CVAE (FID 21.17) approaches but does not reach the 60K-image baseline (FID 17.56), with the gap attributed to the verifier's inherent bias and limited diversity preservation. Stronger verifiers (trained on more real data) produce larger FID improvements, while weaker verifiers cause early plateau or even degradation, confirming the theoretical prediction that verifier quality governs both short-term gains and the long-run convergence point.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the model collapse literature (Dohmatob et al., 2024a; Gerstgrasser et al., 2024), which analyzes iterative retraining on unfiltered synthetic data in linear regression, by introducing an external verifier that performs binary accept/reject filtering on synthetic samples. It departs from the few prior works on filtered retraining (Amin et al., 2025; Feng et al., 2025) by allowing the verifier to be imperfect—possessing both bias and variance—rather than assuming a perfectly correct labeler or highly structured i.i.d. noise. The theoretical framework reduces the multi-dimensional OLS retraining to independent one-dimensional truncated-normal mean estimation problems via a block-orthogonal covariate design, then applies contraction mapping and supermartingale arguments to characterize both short-term bias-variance trade-offs and long-term convergence dynamics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first principled characterization of when verifier-based synthetic retraining helps versus hurts, yielding a practical caution: even with verification, a biased verifier will eventually steer the model toward its own knowledge center rather than the ground truth, so early gains necessarily plateau or reverse. This has direct implications for LLM training pipelines that rely on LLM-as-a-judge or human filtering of synthetic data, suggesting that verifier quality is the binding constraint on sustained improvement. Key limitations include the restriction to linear regression theory (with a global ground-truth parameter), the idealized block-orthogonal design, and the fact that real generative objectives (diversity, perceptual quality) are not captured by a single MSE metric. Open questions include generalization to nonlinear models, alternative synthetic designs, and understanding verifier dynamics in large-scale LLM training.
