## Problem

Computing the graph edit distance is NP-hard, and existing ML-based approaches either regress a scalar GED value without producing an interpretable matching, or rely on linear assignment that computes all node-pair costs independently and thus cannot capture interdependencies between matches. This paper addresses the gap by formulating GED as a sequential decision process where each match is predicted conditioned on all previous ones, enabling the model to exploit structural dependencies that non-sequential methods miss.

## Input / Output

The model takes a pair of labeled graphs (source and target) as input, encodes them as a single disjoint-union graph with src/trg node labels and match edges, and autoregressively outputs a node matching that constitutes a valid upper bound on the true GED. At inference, an ensembling strategy with k=32 top-scoring starting pairs is used to explore multiple search branches, and the matching with the lowest total edit cost is returned. The autoregressive loop terminates once all nodes of the smaller graph are matched.

## Method

GELATO casts GED as a sequential decision process: at each step, a GINE-based GNN (5 layers, d=128, with batch norm and residual connections) acts as a link predictor over the graph-encoded instance, scoring all unmatched source-target node pairs and selecting the highest-scoring pair as the next match. Two key design choices distinguish it from prior work: (1) an instance-reduction step that removes nodes whose neighbors are all already matched, provably preserving optimality while shrinking the state space; and (2) a training procedure that handles automorphic matches by treating entire pair-automorphism classes as positive links, avoiding contradictory supervision. The model is trained with cross-entropy loss on sub-instances sampled from optimal matchings (computed via MIP-F2), and at inference uses greedy decoding with top-k ensembling to mitigate sensitivity to the first match.

## Results

GELATO achieves near-optimal solution quality across all six benchmarks, with nMAE ≤ 0.7% and EHR ≥ 91.1% on every dataset. On the most challenging datasets (ZINC-16, molhiv-16, code2-22), GELATO's nMAE is roughly one order of magnitude lower than the best baseline (GRAIL: 12.7, 8.5, 5.8 respectively). Inference time is 3–5 ms per graph pair on GPU, two orders of magnitude faster than GEDGNN (~1.4–2.8 s) and GEDHOT (~1.0–4.0 s). GELATO also generalizes to graphs up to 30 nodes (trained on ≤22), with only a gradual EHR decline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GELATO outperforms all eight baselines on every dataset and both metrics. The largest margins are on ZINC-16 (nMAE 0.7 vs. GRAIL's 12.7, a ~18× improvement) and molhiv-16 (0.5 vs. 8.5, ~17×), where edge labels and molecular structure make the problem hardest for non-sequential methods. On code2-22, GELATO achieves 0.6 nMAE vs. GRAPHEDX's 6.0 and GRAIL's 5.8. On the smaller, more saturated benchmarks (LINUX, IMDB-16), GELATO matches or slightly exceeds GRAIL (0.1 vs. 0.0 nMAE), while classical heuristics like BRANCH and REFINE remain competitive due to high automorphism in those datasets. Among ML-based matching methods, GEDHOT is the strongest baseline on LINUX/IMDB-16 but degrades sharply on larger or labeled graphs (nMAE 27.5 on ZINC-16), whereas GELATO maintains sub-1% nMAE uniformly. In terms of speed, GELATO (3–5 ms/pair) is ~300× faster than GEDGNN and ~400× faster than GEDHOT, which rely on expensive linear-assignment solvers and ensembling.

## Relation to Prior Work

GELATO departs from the dominant linear-assignment paradigm in ML-based GED (GEDGNN, GEDHOT, GOTSIM) by framing graph matching as an autoregressive sequential decision process, drawing on the neural combinatorial optimization literature (TSP, CVRP) where solutions are constructed step-by-step. It also differs from the closest sequential method, RGM (Liu et al., 2023a), by using supervised link-prediction training rather than reinforcement learning, incorporating a provably sound instance-reduction step to shrink the state space, and targeting GED cost minimization rather than affinity maximization. The paper further introduces clean, leakage-free benchmarks that address known isomorphism-pollution issues in prior GED datasets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GELATO addresses two practical bottlenecks in GED research simultaneously: the NP-hardness of computing ground-truth matchings (it remains effective with only 1k noisy training pairs) and the computational cost of inference (two orders of magnitude faster than competing ML methods). The new clean benchmarks and the demonstrated generalization to unseen graph sizes provide a more rigorous evaluation framework for the field. Open questions include whether beam search or self-improvement/RL can further close the optimality gap on larger graphs, and whether GELATO can be integrated into branch-and-bound exact solvers to reduce time-to-optimality.
