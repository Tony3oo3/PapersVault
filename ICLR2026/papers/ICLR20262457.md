## Problem

Reinforcement learning compute for LLMs has scaled dramatically (e.g., 100K+ GPU-hours for DeepSeek-R1-Zero), yet the field lacks predictive scaling methodologies comparable to those established for pre-training. Without a principled framework, practitioners cannot evaluate which RL design choices will scale reliably to large compute budgets, making progress dependent on expensive large-scale experimentation. This paper asks: can we fit a predictive curve to early RL training that accurately forecasts performance at 10× or more compute, and which algorithmic decisions actually shift the performance ceiling versus merely adjusting convergence speed?

## Input / Output

The method takes a base LLM (8B dense or 17B×16 Llama-4 Scout MoE) and verifiable math/code prompts from the Polaris-53K dataset (or Deepcoder for code), where each prompt yields a binary reward (±1) via automated checkers (SymPy, Math-Verify, or unit-test execution). Training uses a generator-trainer GPU split (64 generators / 16 trainers on 80 GB200s) with 48 prompts × 16 generations per batch and a 14K-token generation budget (12K thinking + 2K answer). The output is a trained reasoning LLM plus fitted sigmoidal scaling parameters (A, B, C_mid) on a 1,000-prompt held-out validation set, enabling extrapolation of pass rate to arbitrary future compute budgets.

## Method

The paper introduces a sigmoidal compute-performance model (Eq. 1) that fits pass rate against log(compute) with three interpretable parameters: asymptotic reward A, scaling exponent B (efficiency), and midpoint C_mid, and validates that extrapolation from the first 50% of a run predicts the full trajectory. Guided by this framework, the authors conduct a 400K+ GPU-hour ablation study across six design axes (off-policy setup, loss type, loss aggregation, advantage normalization, precision, data curriculum) to separate choices that raise the asymptotic ceiling from those that only modulate efficiency. The resulting SCALERL recipe combines PipelineRL-8 (streaming asynchronous off-policy training), CISPO loss (truncated importance-sampling REINFORCE with stop-gradient), prompt-level loss averaging, batch-level advantage normalization, FP32 computation at the LM head, zero-variance prompt filtering, No-Positive-Resampling (permanently dropping prompts with historical pass rate ≥ 0.9), and forced length interruptions. Leave-one-out ablations at 16K GPU-hours confirm each component contributes positively, and a 100K GPU-hour run on the 8B model validates that the sigmoidal fit from the first 50K hours accurately predicts the final performance, establishing SCALERL as a predictably scalable recipe.

## Results

SCALERL achieves an asymptotic pass rate of A=0.61 on the 8B dense model and A=0.71 on the 17B×16 Llama-4 Scout MoE, with the sigmoidal scaling curve fitted at 50k GPU hours accurately extrapolating to the full 100,000 GPU-hour run. The 17B×16 MoE outperforms the 8B dense model using only 1/6 of its RL training compute. SCALERL surpasses all compared recipes (DeepSeek/GRPO, Qwen2.5/DAPO, Magistral, MiniMax-M1) in both asymptotic performance and compute efficiency (B=1.92), and maintains predictable scaling across batch size (up to 2048), generation length (up to 32k tokens), and multi-task (math+code) settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SCALERL outperforms all four main baselines in Figure 2, achieving the highest asymptotic reward A=0.61. Against DeepSeek (GRPO), SCALERL wins decisively: the GRPO recipe destabilizes after ~6k GPU hours due to rising truncation rates, while SCALERL remains stable through 100k GPU hours. Against Qwen2.5 (DAPO) and Magistral, SCALERL achieves a higher asymptotic ceiling, with DAPO's token-level clipping and Magistral's PipelineRL setup both yielding lower A values. Against MiniMax-M1 (which also uses CISPO and FP32 precision), SCALERL achieves a higher asymptote while maintaining comparable stability; both are the only two recipes whose extrapolated curves align with extended training. In the loss-type comparison (Figure 4b), CISPO (used in SCALERL) substantially outperforms DAPO in asymptotic pass rate and is more robust to the clipping hyperparameter ε_max, whereas GSPO—while competitive—suffers mid-training divergence on larger models like Scout, making CISPO the preferred choice for stable large-scale training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as the RL analogue of pre-training scaling laws (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022), arguing that while pre-training has converged to predictable algorithmic recipes, RL for LLMs remains 'more art than science.' It builds directly on GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) as the base policy-gradient formulation, DAPO's asymmetric clipping (Yu et al., 2025), PipelineRL's streaming off-policy architecture (Piche et al., 2025), and CISPO's truncated importance-sampling loss (MiniMax et al., 2025), but departs from prior work by introducing a sigmoidal compute-performance fitting framework to systematically evaluate and extrapolate RL scaling rather than reporting ad-hoc training recipes. Unlike ProRL (Liu et al., 2025a) or LitePPO (Liu et al., 2025c), which focus on stability heuristics or small-scale ablations, this work operates at 6× the compute budget and explicitly targets predictability as the evaluation criterion.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic scaling study for RL training of LLMs (400K+ GPU-hours), providing both a predictive methodology (sigmoidal curve fitting enabling extrapolation from ~1.5K to 100K GPU-hours) and a validated SOTA recipe (SCALERL). The finding that most commonly-discussed design choices (loss aggregation, advantage normalization, curriculum) primarily modulate compute efficiency rather than asymptotic performance reframes how practitioners should prioritize their engineering effort. Open questions include whether the sigmoidal framework generalizes to multi-turn/agentic RL, structured or dense rewards, and whether in-distribution validation performance reliably predicts downstream generalization across all algorithmic choices.
