## Problem

Empirical studies show that standard ERM can rival or outperform state-of-the-art OOD generalization methods, and that using all available covariates (including non-causal ones) improves OOD performance—findings that contradict the prevailing causal-invariance paradigm. The paper identifies hidden confounding shifts as the root cause: when an unobserved variable U causes both covariates and the outcome, shifts in P(U) across environments simultaneously violate the label-shift and covariate-shift assumptions that underpin existing OOD methods, making it unclear what the optimal generalization strategy should be.

## Input / Output

The framework operates on tabular data with covariates partitioned into nested subsets (causal ⊆ arguably causal ⊆ all), environment labels identifying training/test domains, and a target variable. Mutual information terms are estimated non-parametrically via the KSG estimator (NPEET toolbox) on 20,000 random samples from ID and OOD test sets. The primary analytical output is a six-term decomposition of I(Y; Ŷ) into conditional informativeness, variation, label shift, feature shift, concept shift, and residual, alongside a sign-consistency metric that quantifies how reliably each term's change aligns with accuracy gains when expanding the covariate set.

## Method

The core contribution is an information-theoretic decomposition of predictive information I(Y; Ŷ) that isolates the effect of hidden confounding on OOD generalization. Theorem 4.1 decomposes I(Y; Ŷ) into six terms (conditional informativeness, variation, label shift, feature shift, concept shift, residual) for the general causal structure X↔Y. Theorem 4.2 then shows that under hidden confounding (U→X, U→Y) with either X→Y or Y→X, the decomposition collapses to conditional informativeness minus residual, because the variation/feature-shift and label-shift/concept-shift pairs cancel via d-separation inequalities. Proposition 4.1 proves that adding non-causal but informative covariates X_I (satisfying Y ⊥̸ X_I | X, E) strictly increases conditional informativeness and feature shift while decreasing concept shift, providing theoretical justification for why all-covariate models outperform causal-only models. Empirically, the paper validates these results on eight TableShift benchmark datasets using XGBoost, MLP, IRM, VREX, and Group DRO, showing that the conditional-informativeness-minus-residual gap correlates with ID/OOD accuracy (Spearman ρ = 0.93/0.80) and that XGBoost's superior performance stems from its higher sign consistency on conditional informativeness.

## Results

Across 8 real-world tabular datasets, XGBoost (a standard ERM method) achieves the highest OOD test accuracy with all covariates (72.90%), outperforming specialized OOD methods IRM (62.75%), VREX (65.21%), and Group DRO (65.87%). The sign consistency metric reveals that XGB excels at maximizing conditional informativeness (0.92) compared to MLP (0.65), GDRO (0.69), IRM (0.71), and VREX (0.52). The difference (conditional informativeness − residual) is positively correlated with ID test accuracy (Spearman ρ = 0.93) and OOD test accuracy (ρ = 0.80), confirming the theoretical prediction from Theorem 4.2. All 8 datasets exhibit significant concept shift (1.88–2.13 bits), confirming the prevalence of hidden confounding shift in real-world tabular data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates five methods (XGB, MLP, GDRO, IRM, VREX) on 8 TableShift benchmark datasets, using both ID and OOD test accuracy. XGBoost (ERM) achieves the best OOD accuracy with all covariates (72.90%), beating MLP (66.93%), GDRO (65.87%), VREX (65.21%), and IRM (62.75%) by 6–10 percentage points. The invariance-based methods IRM and VREX perform worst on ID accuracy (64.67% and 62.75% with all covariates respectively), trailing XGB by ~18 points, suggesting that enforcing invariance is counterproductive under hidden confounding shift. The sign consistency analysis explains this: XGB's high CI consistency (0.92) means it reliably increases conditional informativeness when adding covariates, while VREX's low CI consistency (0.52) and high residual (0.58) indicate it fails to capture environment-specific predictive information. Notably, using all covariates (A) consistently outperforms causal-only (C) covariates across all methods, with the gap largest for XGB (82.31% vs 78.91% ID; 72.90% vs 64.35% OOD), supporting the paper's theoretical claim that informative non-causal covariates reduce concept shift and improve generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the information-theoretic framework of Federici et al. (2021) and the informativeness/invariance analysis of Ye et al. (2021) to the setting of hidden confounding shifts, deriving a predictive information decomposition (Theorem 4.1) that simplifies under confounding to conditional informativeness minus residual (Theorem 4.2). It positions itself as a theoretical explanation for empirical findings from Nastl & Hardt (2024) showing all-covariate models Pareto-dominate causal-only models, and for the observation that ERM rivals invariance-based methods like IRM and VREX. The work connects to proxy variable adjustment literature (Landeiro & Culotta, Alabdulmohsin et al., Tsai et al.) but reframes the question: rather than proposing adjustment methods, it explains why informative non-causal covariates naturally mitigate hidden confounding by reducing concept shift.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled theoretical account for why the field's dominant invariance paradigm (IRM, VREX, Group DRO) can fail under hidden confounding, learning incorrect relationships via Simpson's paradox, while simple ERM with all covariates succeeds. This reframes covariate selection from a causal-purity question to an informativeness question, with practical implications for tabular ML pipelines. However, the paper explicitly does not propose a solution to hidden confounding, and key open questions remain: how to identify informative covariates without knowing the confounder structure, the cost-accuracy tradeoff of acquiring additional variables, and whether these insights extend beyond tabular data to vision or language settings.
