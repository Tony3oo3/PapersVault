## Problem

Standard conformal prediction guarantees only marginal coverage, producing intervals that are overly wide in low-variance regions and too narrow in high-variance ones when data exhibit latent multi-domain heterogeneity. Existing localized conformal methods (RLCP, PCP) weight calibration points by raw covariate proximity or by fitting mixture models on conformity scores, which may fail to identify the true latent domain structure and are computationally expensive. The paper asks whether the soft domain assignments already learned by a Mixture-of-Experts gating network can directly guide conformal calibration to yield domain-adaptive, valid, and tighter intervals without explicit domain labels.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a trained MoE model (K expert networks plus a softmax gating network), a held-out calibration set with precomputed absolute-residual nonconformity scores and per-point gating vectors, and a single test covariate X_{n+1}. It produces a prediction interval of the form {y : |y − μ̂(X_{n+1})| ≤ Q_{1−α}} where Q_{1−α} is a weighted empirical quantile, along with the gating probability vector π(X_{n+1}) as an interpretable soft domain assignment. A key unusual detail is the multinomial randomization of the test gating vector (with temperature τ) that is required for the validity proof and simultaneously controls the sharpness of the similarity weights.

## Method

MoE-CP trains a standard Mixture-of-Experts regressor (K experts + softmax gate) on the training set, then uses the learned gating vectors as a similarity metric in gating space to weight calibration residuals. For a test point, its gating vector is randomized via a Multinomial(τ, π(X_{n+1})) draw; each calibration point receives weight w_i = exp(−τ·D(π̃(X_{n+1}), π(X_i))) where D is a divergence (KL, cross-entropy, Euclidean, etc.), and weights are normalized over the calibration set plus the test point. The (1−α) quantile of the resulting weighted empirical distribution of nonconformity scores (with a δ_{+∞} mass at the test point) defines the interval half-width. Theoretically, KL or cross-entropy divergences yield exact finite-sample marginal validity conditional on the randomized gating vector, other divergences sharing the same minimizer give asymptotic validity, and under a finite-mixture representation of the score distribution the oracle version achieves conditional-on-X coverage. This differs from RLCP (which weights by raw covariate distance) and PCP (which refits a mixture on scores for every candidate y) by computing all weights from a single pre-trained gating network, making the procedure both adaptive to latent domains and computationally efficient at inference time.

## Results

On synthetic three-domain data (n=500, α=0.1), MoECP achieves the shortest prediction intervals among all six methods while maintaining nominal marginal coverage of 0.90, outperforming both quantile-regression baselines (CQR, CHR) and mean-regression baselines (SCP+CC, RLCP, PCP). On real-world Bike-Sharing and Temperature datasets (n=1500, α=0.1), MoECP again produces the shortest intervals, with worst-slice coverage approximately 0.90 across all methods. Ablation studies confirm robustness: marginal coverage and interval length remain stable across divergence measures (KL, Rényi, Jeffreys, Hellinger, Euclidean, Cosine) and temperature values τ∈[50,300].

## Baselines & Comparisons

MoECP outperforms all five baselines on interval length (efficiency) across both synthetic and real-data experiments while maintaining nominal marginal coverage. Against CQR and CHR (quantile-regression-based), MoECP achieves shorter intervals despite relying on mean regression, and its intervals adapt more effectively under domain shifts (e.g., X∈[−3,−2]∪[2,3] in the synthetic setting). Against SCP+CC, RLCP, and PCP (all mean-regression-based), MoECP produces noticeably tighter intervals; RLCP and PCP are also slightly conservative in marginal coverage on real data, whereas MoECP hits the nominal level. In conditional coverage (local average over 100 nearest neighbors), MoECP is closest to the nominal 0.90 level, while CQR and CHR fail to adapt in high-variance regions. The key differentiator is that MoECP's gating-space weighting identifies latent domain structure without explicit labels, whereas RLCP uses raw covariate proximity and PCP refits a mixture per candidate y, making MoECP both more adaptive and computationally efficient.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two well-developed lines of work: split conformal prediction (Lei et al., 2018) and Mixture-of-Experts architectures (Jacobs et al., 1991). Its closest conceptual neighbors are localized conformal prediction methods that weight calibration points by proximity—RLCP (Hore & Barber, 2025) uses Euclidean distance in raw covariate space, and PCP (Zhang & Candès, 2024) fits a mixture model on conformity scores. MoE-CP departs from both by defining similarity in a learned latent domain space (the MoE gating simplex), which requires no explicit domain labels and avoids the per-candidate refitting cost of PCP. The randomization step (multinomial sampling of the gating vector) directly borrows the validity-preserving mechanism from Hore & Barber (2025) and Zhang & Candès (2024), transplanted from covariate space to gating space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MoE-CP offers a practical, interpretable route to domain-adaptive prediction intervals in settings where heterogeneity is latent and unlabeled—a common scenario in healthcare, sensor networks, and multi-site data. The theoretical guarantees are clean (exact marginal validity under KL/cross-entropy, asymptotic validity for a broad class of divergences, and approximate conditional coverage under a mixture representation), but the conditional coverage result is only as strong as the MoE's ability to recover true domain structure, which is an unverified assumption in practice. Open questions include extension to non-exchangeable or sequentially dependent data, settings with known domain labels, and scaling to very large MoE architectures (e.g., LLM-scale gating). The computational cost of training the MoE is comparable to other neural conformal baselines, so the method is deployable, but the benefit over simpler weighted conformal approaches may diminish when domain structure is weak or the number of experts is misspecified.
