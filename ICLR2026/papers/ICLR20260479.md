## Problem

Distributed data-parallel training of billion-parameter language models is bottlenecked by full-precision gradient all-reduce at every step, demanding expensive high-bandwidth interconnects and geographically co-located clusters. Existing gradient compression methods (sparsification, quantization) either degrade model quality or require additional GPU memory for explicit error-feedback accumulators. The paper asks whether the momentum state itself can serve as a compressible, information-rich surrogate for raw gradients, enabling orders-of-magnitude communication reduction without sacrificing convergence.

## Input / Output

Each worker consumes its local stochastic gradient (computed on a microbatch of 8 sequences of length 2048) and its own evolving momentum buffer, producing a set of top-k DCT coefficients per 64×64 chunk (or 64-element chunk for vectors) that is transmitted to the parameter server. The server reconstructs a global momentum tensor via inverse DCT and broadcasts only the sign of that tensor (a binary update) back to all workers for the parameter step. Notably, the local momentum buffer doubles as the error-feedback accumulator—no separate memory allocation is needed, and the subtraction coefficient α (default 0.2) controls how aggressively previously communicated values are decayed.

## Method

DeMo replaces the standard DDP gradient all-reduce with a three-stage pipeline operating on the momentum buffer: (1) each worker updates its local momentum independently via M_t = βM_{t-1} + G_t without any cross-worker synchronization; (2) the momentum tensor is partitioned into small chunks, each chunk undergoes a separable multilinear DCT projection, and only the top-k largest-magnitude coefficients per chunk are retained and sent to the server; (3) the decoded (inverse-DCT) sparse update is subtracted from the local momentum buffer in-place, so uncommunicated residual information accumulates and is transmitted in subsequent steps—this momentum subtraction acts as a built-in error-feedback mechanism that requires no extra memory. The server aggregates the sparse coefficients across workers, applies inverse DCT to reconstruct a global momentum, and performs a sign-based parameter update (compatible with Lion/Signum-style optimizers). The DCT transform is critical: it converts the sparse coefficient selection into dense parameter-space updates, avoiding the harmful sparse update patterns that plague direct gradient sparsification, while its orthonormality guarantees norm-preserving reconstruction and its fast FFT-based implementation keeps computational overhead negligible.

## Results

On OLMo-300M (100B tokens), DeMo with k=8 achieves 0.38 HellaSwag, 0.47 ARC-Easy, and 0.67 PIQA normalized accuracy while transmitting only 7.49 MB per GPU per step, compared to AdamW-DDP's 636.9 MB/step (85× reduction) with 0.35/0.46/0.65 accuracy. At the 1B scale, DeMo k=16 reaches 0.47 HellaSwag, 0.53 ARC-Easy, and 0.70 PIQA at 55.16 MB/step versus AdamW-DDP's 2416.6 MB/step (44× reduction) with 0.43/0.51/0.68. Even at the most aggressive sparsity (k=1), DeMo retains competitive accuracy (0.35/0.45/0.65 on 300M) while transmitting just 0.93 MB/step—a 685× reduction over AdamW-DDP.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against AdamW-DDP, DeMo matches or exceeds accuracy on all three benchmarks at both 300M and 1B scales while reducing per-GPU communication by 2–3 orders of magnitude (e.g., 85× at k=8 on 300M, 44× at k=16 on 1B). Against Muon-DDP (which itself outperforms AdamW-DDP), DeMo underperforms at equal update counts but provides substantially lower communication cost, making it the preferred choice when bandwidth is the bottleneck. Against DiLoCo, DeMo consistently achieves lower validation perplexity at comparable compression ratios (e.g., at ~128× data-transmission reduction, DeMo k=32 with 64×64 chunks beats DiLoCo across all tested communication frequencies). Against PowerSGD (rank-32, ~26× compression), DeMo also shows superior final perplexity, with PowerSGD performing similarly to DiLoCo but remaining slightly below DeMo. Overall, DeMo wins on the communication-efficiency frontier: it achieves the best accuracy-per-GB-transmitted trade-off among all compared methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

DeMo extends the gradient-compression literature (top-k sparsification, quantization, error feedback) by shifting the compression target from raw gradients to the momentum buffer, which is a more compressible surrogate due to its temporal smoothing. It departs from prior sparsification methods (Lin et al., 2018b; Stich et al., 2018) that operate directly on gradients and suffer from sparse update patterns, by applying a DCT transform before top-k selection to preserve update uniformity. The momentum-subtraction-as-error-feedback mechanism is a conceptual departure from explicit error-feedback schemes (Karimireddy et al., 2019) that require a separate accumulator, and from decoupled-optimization approaches like DiLoCo that reduce synchronization frequency rather than per-step bandwidth.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DeMo's 85× communication reduction with maintained accuracy directly relaxes the hardware requirement for high-bandwidth interconnects (InfiniBand, NVLink), enabling large-scale training over standard Ethernet or even across geographically distributed datacenters—a significant cost and accessibility win. The drop-in compatibility with existing momentum-based optimizers (SGD-momentum, Lion, Muon) lowers adoption barriers. Key limitations include download bandwidth scaling linearly with worker count (inherent to top-k sparsification), and the method is primarily validated for a small number of inter-center links; interaction with FSDP or pipeline parallelism at scale remains unexplored. Open questions include whether the approach extends to very large worker counts, non-i.i.d. data splits, and whether the fixed DCT basis remains optimal for all tensor shapes and model architectures.
