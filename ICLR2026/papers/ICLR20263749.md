## Problem

Existing adversarial defenses for vision-language models (e.g., adversarial training via TeCoA or FARE) achieve robustness at the cost of significant clean-performance degradation (3–9% drops) and high computational overhead. This paper identifies an overlooked source of vulnerability: function words (articles, prepositions, auxiliary verbs) carry minimal semantic content yet are highly frequent, making them natural targets for cross-modal adversarial perturbations that exploit the semantic gap between images and text. The gap is that no prior work has shown that selectively reducing attention to these low-information tokens can tighten vision-language alignment and yield robustness essentially for free.

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard RGB images (processed by ViT-B/32) paired with natural-language text queries (processed by BERT), producing retrieval rankings (R@1/5/10) for image-text retrieval or bounding boxes for visual grounding. Unusually, FDA introduces no new parameters or architectural modules: it inserts a parallel attention-computation branch inside existing fusion-encoder heads that reads the same Q/K/V projections but restricts the query side to a masked subset of function-word tokens (91 words from a shortlisted stopword dictionary). The output is a modified attention tensor that replaces the original cross-attention before the value-weighted sum, so downstream layers and task heads remain unchanged.

## Method

Function-word De-Attention (FDA) adds a parallel pipeline within each selected fusion-encoder attention head that computes the cross-attention between function-word features (extracted via a binary mask over the text embedding) and visual features, yielding a 'distraction' score matrix. Softmax is applied along both the visual-token and text-token dimensions of this matrix to isolate the most misleading visual regions and the most misleading text positions, and both are multiplied by the value projection. A control gate G scales these distraction terms, which are then subtracted from the original cross-attention; the final attention is the element-wise minimum of the two subtraction results, ensuring a conservative correction. Inspired by differential transformers, this subtraction tightens the cross-modal embedding geometry (verified via t-SNE and similarity-score analysis), making the model less susceptible to perturbations that exploit function-word channels. FDA is applied to the first one or two fusion layers and the first six of twelve attention heads (L^{0-1}, H^{0-5}), and can be combined additively with adversarial fine-tuning methods like TeCoA or FARE as a plug-and-play robustness booster.

## Results

On Flickr30k retrieval (ε=2/255), FDA achieves an average ASR drop of 22.26% (ALBEF), 14.29% (TCL), and 51.60% (BLIP) across PGD/APGD/MAPGD, with clean R@1 drops of only 0.30%, 0.50%, and 0.70% respectively. On Visual Grounding (RefCOCO+, ε=2/255), FDA achieves a 93.16% ASR drop while actually improving clean accuracy by 0.9% over the vanilla model (66.80% vs 65.90% on Test A). The method scales with model size: BLIP (124M pre-trained images) benefits most with a 51.60% ASR drop, versus ~15% for ALBEF/TCL (14M images).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FDA consistently outperforms both TeCoA (Mao et al., 2022) and FARE (Schlarmann et al., 2024) across all three models and both tasks. On ALBEF retrieval (Flickr30k, ε=2/255), FDA achieves a 22.26% ASR drop versus −3.02% for TeCoA (which actually worsens robustness) and 5.36% for FARE, while maintaining 95.60% T2IR R@1 compared to 91.20% (TeCoA) and 91.10% (FARE). The gap widens substantially on BLIP, where FDA reaches 51.60% ASR drop versus 15.70% (TeCoA) and 3.09% (FARE), with clean performance of 96.50% versus 90.30% and 89.70% respectively. On Visual Grounding, FDA achieves a near-complete 93.16% ASR drop, dwarfing TeCoA's 21.21% and FARE's 12.08%, while also improving clean accuracy over the vanilla model. Both baselines suffer 3–9% clean performance drops on retrieval, whereas FDA's drops remain below 1% across all models, confirming the 'free robustness' claim.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant adversarial-training paradigm for VLM robustness (TeCoA, FARE) by reframing the defense problem as an attention-alignment issue rather than a training-objective issue. Its core mechanism—differentially subtracting a targeted attention component from the full attention—is directly inspired by differential transformers (Ye et al., 2024) and differential amplifiers, but applied to a novel target: function-word cross-attention. The paper also builds on the observation that cross-modal adversarial perturbations exploit semantic gaps, but identifies function words (high-frequency, low-semantic tokens) as the specific locus of vulnerability, a perspective absent from prior VLM defense literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FDA offers a lightweight, plug-and-play defense that requires no adversarial training and preserves (or even slightly improves) clean performance, making it attractive for practitioners who cannot afford the compute cost or accuracy penalty of adversarial fine-tuning. However, its applicability is currently limited to models with explicit fusion encoders (ALBEF, TCL, BLIP), excluding CLIP and projector-based architectures like LLaVA and Qwen-VL, which are increasingly dominant. Open questions include whether the function-word dictionary is truly optimal, whether FDA transfers to PEFT-based fine-tuning, and whether the approach generalizes to larger, more heterogeneous VLMs where attention head specialization may differ substantially.
