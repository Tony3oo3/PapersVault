## Problem

Most individual attention heads in Transformer models exhibit polysemantic or uninterpretable attention patterns, making it difficult to identify the atomic computational units responsible for specific behaviors like induction or successor prediction. The authors hypothesize that this stems from attention superposition, where multiple interpretable attention units are distributed across and within MHSA heads, analogous to feature superposition in MLP neurons. No prior method had successfully extracted these sparse, individually comprehensible attention units from the superposition.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262640/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Lorsa takes the same residual stream token sequence as the original MHSA layer and is trained via MSE to reproduce the MHSA output. Each Lorsa head produces a scalar activation z (the 1D weighted sum of values) and a rank-1 output vector z·w_o; only the top-K heads by |z| are summed per token position, yielding a sparse approximation of the full attention output. The QK dimension is kept at the original MHSA head dimension (64 for Pythia, 128 for Llama), while the OV dimension is collapsed to 1, and QK weights are shared across groups of G = d_QK heads to control parameter count.

## Method

Lorsa replaces each MHSA layer with an overcomplete set of heads (500–1000× more than the original) that each implement a rank-1 OV circuit: a 1D value projection w_v and a 1D output projection w_o, so each head reads and writes only a single residual stream direction. QK circuits are shared across groups of G heads (G = d_QK by default), making each QK-sharing group nearly identical to an original MHSA head except for the per-head sparsity constraint on the OV dimension. At each token position, only the top-K heads by scalar activation z are selected (Top-K sparsity, analogous to TopK-SAEs), and their rank-1 outputs are summed. Training minimizes MSE between Lorsa output and the frozen MHSA output on 800M tokens, with the key architectural insight that restricting OV rank to 1 forces each head to encode a single interpretable feature–feature interaction, while maintaining full-rank QK preserves the multi-dimensional attention pattern needed for faithful reconstruction.

## Results

Lorsa reconstructs MHSA outputs with per-layer FVU that correlates strongly with SAE FVU trends across both Pythia-160M (18M params, K=64) and Llama-3.1-8B (512M params, K=128). In automated interpretability (autointerp) scoring with GPT-4o, Lorsa heads achieve comparable monosemanticity to SAE features: 6 wins, 3 losses, and 15 ties at α=0.05 across 24 layer-wise comparisons. Lorsa rediscovers known attention mechanisms (induction heads, successor heads, attention sinks) at finer granularity and identifies novel head types including subtoken induction heads and a family of arithmetic-specific heads in Llama-3.1-8B.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against TopK-SAEs (Gao et al., 2024), Lorsa shows a notable FVU gap under the same parameter budget and sparsity, especially at large K, since Lorsa must learn both QK and OV circuits to predict attention output from hundreds of activations while SAE uses a standard dictionary learning setting with identical input and output. However, in automated interpretability scoring, Lorsa achieves parity with SAE features (6 wins vs. 3 losses across 24 comparisons), and the paper reports that Lorsa exhibits superior circuit discovery properties. Against attention-output SAEs (Kissane et al., 2024), Lorsa's z-pattern attribution is more direct (single rank-1 OV circuit + shared QK circuit without multi-head aggregation), enabling cleaner QK circuit attribution for attention units distributed across multiple MHSA heads. The paper notes that ~25% of learned Lorsa attention units are spread across multiple MHSA heads, providing direct evidence of attention superposition that SAE-based methods can only partially capture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the sparse autoencoder (SAE) and transcoder lineage (Templeton et al., 2024b; Ge et al., 2024; Dunefsky et al., 2024) from decomposing hidden states or MLP outputs to decomposing the attention mechanism itself. While attention SAEs (Kissane et al., 2024) apply dictionary learning to attention outputs post-hoc, Lorsa replaces the MHSA computation with an overcomplete set of rank-1 OV circuits, directly disentangling the QK and OV pathways. The concept of attention superposition (Jermyn et al., 2024) motivates the design, and the paper builds on the superposition hypothesis (Elhage et al., 2022) by hypothesizing that individual attention units are spread across multiple MHSA heads. The theoretical contribution (Theorem 1) formally bounds the behavioral equivalence between MHSA heads and their Lorsa decompositions under sparsity and approximation assumptions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Lorsa provides the first practical tool for extracting atomic, interpretable attention units, revealing finer-grained behaviors (subtoken induction heads, arithmetic-specific operand heads, theme anchors) that were invisible at the MHSA head level. Key open questions include: the shared QK circuit limitation prevents fully independent attribution, the majority of Lorsa heads remain inactive (wasting computation), cross-layer attention superposition is unaddressed, and the observed correlation between Lorsa and SAE reconstruction errors suggests a fundamental 'dark matter' limit to sparse dictionary learning. The paper's early success in fully sparsifying a 2-layer Transformer hints at a path toward end-to-end circuit tracing, but scaling this to production models remains unresolved.
