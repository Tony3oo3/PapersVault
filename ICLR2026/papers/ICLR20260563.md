## Problem

Existing competitive programming test datasets (CodeContests, TACO, HardTests) suffer from high false positive and false negative rates, meaning incorrect solutions pass weak tests while valid solutions are rejected on malformed inputs. This distorts evaluation landscapes and pollutes reinforcement learning signals. The paper asks whether LLMs can reliably perform the full problem-setting pipeline—generating adversarial test cases and authoring novel contest-quality problems—rather than merely solving them.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260563/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For test case generation, the system consumes a problem statement and an accepted reference solution, then produces a complete test suite comprising a Validator program (constraint checker), a Checker program (verdict issuer), an Interactor program (for interactive problems), and a diverse set of input-answer pairs spanning exhaustive small cases, randomized extremes, and TLE-inducing adversarial inputs. For problem generation, the input is a seed Codeforces problem (difficulty < 2200), and the output is a new problem statement with two solutions: an efficient reference (std.cpp) and a deliberately slower brute-force (brute.cpp), which together enable automated correctness verification without human intervention.

## Method

AutoCode employs a closed-loop multi-role architecture where the LLM simultaneously plays Generator, Validator, and Checker roles. A key design choice is multi-candidate selection: the LLM emits three distinct Validator (or Checker/Interactor) programs, and the most robust one is chosen by benchmarking against 40 targeted evaluation cases (10 valid, 30 near-valid). The Generator uses three complementary strategies—exhaustive small-case enumeration, randomized extreme data targeting overflows and hash collisions, and structured TLE-inducing inputs—to minimize false positives. For interactive problems, a novel mutant-based discrimination process creates slightly incorrect variants of the reference solution and selects the Interactor that best separates correct from mutant programs. For problem generation, a dual-verification protocol requires that the brute-force solution (ground truth) and the efficient reference solution produce pairwise-consistent outputs across all test cases, filtering out 27% of error-prone problems and raising reference-solution correctness from 86% to 94%.

## Results

On the 7,538-problem benchmark, AutoCode achieves 91.1% consistency with official judgments, substantially outperforming the best prior method (HardTests at 81.0%). AutoCode reduces the False Positive Rate to 3.7% (vs. 7.7–12.1% for baselines) and the False Negative Rate to 14.1% (vs. 25.8–46.3%), representing roughly a 50% reduction in both error rates over the state of the art. On a more challenging 720-problem Codeforces benchmark including interactive tasks, AutoCode reaches 98.7% consistency with only 1.3% FPR and 1.2% FNR.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AutoCode outperforms all four baselines on every metric. Against CodeContests (72.9% consistency, 46.3% FNR), the gap is largest in FNR, where AutoCode's 14.1% is more than 3× lower. Against CodeContests+ (79.9%, 8.6% FPR) and TACO (80.7%, 11.5% FPR), AutoCode nearly halves the FPR to 3.7%. Against the strongest baseline HardTests (81.0%, 12.1% FPR, 25.8% FNR), AutoCode gains 10.1 points in consistency while cutting FPR by nearly 7 points and FNR by over 11 points. A controlled comparison in Appendix K (same LLMs, same environment) confirms the gains are pipeline-driven rather than model-driven: with o3, AutoCode reaches 91.1% consistency versus HardTests' 80.0%, and with GPT-4o, AutoCode still edges out HardTests (79.5% vs. 78.4%) with a markedly lower FPR (7.5% vs. 12.2%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on automated test case generation for competitive programming (CodeContests, TACO, HardTests, CodeContests+) by introducing a multi-role Validator-Generator-Checker-Interactor architecture where LLMs play distinct, validated roles rather than acting as a single monolithic generator. It departs from prior methods in two key ways: (1) a candidate-selection mechanism that generates multiple Validator/Checker/Interactor programs and picks the most robust via targeted evaluation, and (2) a dual-verification protocol (brute-force solution as ground truth + reference solution) that enables fully automated generation of *new* problems, a task not addressed by any prior test-case-generation pipeline. The paper explicitly unifies three previously separate research threads—benchmark construction, verdict reliability, and solver/data curation—into a single closed-loop system.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ~10-point consistency gap over prior SOTA (91.1% vs 81.0%) and the halving of both FPR and FNR have direct practical implications for RL training data quality, where malformed test inputs (high FNR) punish valid reasoning and weak adversarial coverage (high FPR) rewards shortcuts. A critical open question highlighted by the paper is the near-zero correlation (0.07–0.11) between LLM self-evaluations and human expert judgments of problem quality and novelty, meaning the pipeline still depends on human experts for final quality screening—a bottleneck to fully scalable problem generation. The finding that ~4.2% of generated problems are solvable by others but not by the generating LLM itself opens an intriguing but underexplored path toward self-improvement, while the preliminary RL experiment (2.3-point Pass@1 gain on Qwen3-8B) is too small-scale to confirm utility at scale.
