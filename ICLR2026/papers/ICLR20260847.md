## Problem

Deployed time-series forecasters degrade under non-stationarity and distribution shift, and existing test-time adaptation (TTA) methods for time series rely on dual input/output adapters that indirectly calibrate data distributions, making their effect on the frozen base model hard to analyze. This paper asks whether a minimal, direct output-space correction can match or exceed these indirect approaches while remaining architecture-agnostic and computationally lightweight.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260847/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

COSA consumes the frozen base model's L-step prediction Y_t^(0) ∈ R^L (generated from a look-back window X_t ∈ R^W) and a context vector C_t ∈ R^K built by stacking the K most recent batch-wise mean aggregations of previously revealed ground truth. The adapter input is the concatenation [Y_t^(0) || C_t] ∈ R^(L+K), and the output is a corrected L-step forecast Ŷ_t = Y_t^(0) + tanh(g)·H_t. A leakage-free streaming protocol ensures adaptation at time t uses only ground truth revealed before t, never current or future labels.

## Method

COSA is a single linear layer (W ∈ R^{L×(L+K)}, b ∈ R^L) that computes a residual H_t, modulated by a learnable scalar gate g through tanh(g) ∈ [-1,1] to bound correction magnitude. The context vector encodes recent level/scale drift from observed targets, letting the linear layer learn scale and level corrections directly from the MSE error signal. At test time, only {W, b, g} are updated under a cosine-adaptive learning-rate schedule (CALR) that anneals within S gradient steps and dynamically adjusts the rate based on short-term loss trends—halving on spikes, increasing on stable decrease—while gradient clipping and L2 regularization prevent parameter drift. This design is architecture-agnostic (compatible with Transformer, linear, and MLP base models), adds negligible inference overhead (1.25 ms/batch vs. 10–13 ms for prior TTA), and achieves 13.91–17.03% MSE reduction over no-TTA baselines and 10.48–13.05% over SOTA TTA methods across six datasets and four horizons.

## Results

COSA achieves the best test MSE across all 6 datasets, 4 horizons, and 6 base architectures, improving over no-TTA baselines by 13.91–17.03% and over SOTA TTA methods (TAFAS, PETSA) by 10.48–13.05%. Gains are largest at the 720-step horizon, where COSA-F and COSA-P reduce MSE by 32.24% and 26.33% respectively versus the frozen baseline. Inference time per batch is 1.25 ms for COSA versus 10.96 ms (TAFAS) and 12.63 ms (PETSA), an 88.59–90.10% speedup. COSA also outperforms SOLID (a fine-tuning method) on accuracy while running ~41× faster (7.44 s vs. 306.55 s wall-clock).

## Baselines & Comparisons

COSA outperforms both dual-adapter TTA methods—TAFAS (Kim et al., 2025) and PETSA (Medeiros et al., 2025)—across every dataset, horizon, and base model, with average MSE reductions of 10.48–13.05%. Against SOLID (Chen et al., 2024), which fine-tunes the base model's prediction layer, COSA achieves lower MSE in all 24 settings (3 models × 4 horizons × 2 representative datasets) while running ~41× faster (7.44 s vs. 306.55 s). COSA also beats DynaTTA (Grover & Etemad, 2025) on every configuration in Table 10, with particularly large margins at L=720 (e.g., .4460 vs. .8790 on Exchange Rate with iTransformer). In the normalization comparison (Figure 3), COSA paired with basic standard normalization achieves lower mean MSE than both RevIN (Kim et al., 2021) and DDN (Dai et al., 2024), and when stacked on top of either normalizer it further reduces MSE by ~16.8–16.9%, confirming compatibility rather than redundancy.

## Relation to Prior Work

COSA departs from the dominant dual-adapter paradigm in time-series TTA (TAFAS, PETSA, DynaTTA), which calibrates distributions at both the input and output ends of a frozen base model. Instead, it performs correction exclusively in the output space via a single linear residual layer modulated by a scalar gate, drawing on the PAAS scheduling mechanism from TAFAS for its adaptive batch-size variant (COSA-P). It also contrasts with SOLID, which similarly operates in output space but directly fine-tunes the base model's prediction layer rather than maintaining a frozen model with a lightweight adapter. The broader vision TTA literature (Tent, SHOT, CoTTA) informs the general test-time adaptation framework, but COSA explicitly leverages the time-series-specific property that ground truth becomes sequentially observable, enabling direct MSE-based adaptation under a leakage-free protocol.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

COSA offers a practically attractive deployment story: it is architecture-agnostic, adds negligible inference overhead (1.25 ms/batch vs. ~11–13 ms for competitors), and requires no changes to the base model's training pipeline. The finding that a single linear layer with a context vector outperforms more complex dual-adapter and multi-layer alternatives challenges the assumption that input-side distribution calibration is necessary for effective TTA in time series. Key limitations include reliance on full ground truth (blocking real-time use with partial observations), a fixed context length K that may not suit all periodicity structures, and the restriction to linear corrections that may underperform on highly nonlinear distribution shifts. Open questions include whether adaptive K/B selection, masked updates for partial targets, and hybrid linear/nonlinear adapters can close the remaining gap on the most challenging non-stationary scenarios.
