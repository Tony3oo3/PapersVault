## Problem

Existing Automatic Stage Lighting Control (ASLC) methods reduce the task to classifying music into coarse categories (style, emotion, chord) and mapping them to predefined light patterns, producing formulaic outputs that lack the nuance of human-designed lighting. The paper argues that ASLC is fundamentally an artistic generation task, not a rule-driven mapping process, and that no public dataset or end-to-end generative model existed to support this reframing.

## Input / Output

The model takes a music audio sequence as input, processed via OpenL3 into 512-dimensional embeddings at 10 Hz (1-second windows, 0.1-second hop), with a maximum sequence length of 1024 frames (~102 seconds). It auto-regressively generates a per-frame lighting sequence where each frame is a pair of discrete tokens: hue (0–179, treated as a circular variable) and value/intensity (0–255). Lighting ground truth is extracted from video in HSV space with saturation fixed at 255; hue is taken as the mode of the per-frame hue histogram (to respect circularity) and value as the weighted mean of the value histogram, with low-intensity pixels filtered by a configurable threshold.

## Method

Skip-BART adapts the BART encoder-decoder architecture: the encoder processes OpenL3 music embeddings, while the decoder generates light tokens using discrete embedding layers (chosen over MLPs to handle hue's cyclic nature). The key architectural innovation is a skip connection that directly concatenates the music embedding at position i−1 with the light embedding at position i before feeding the decoder, explicitly encoding the one-to-one frame correspondence that the attention mechanism alone struggles to learn. Training proceeds in two stages: (1) an MLM-based pre-training phase where the decoder recovers masked music tokens (with Gaussian-distributed [MASK] tokens) under a GAN discriminator for realism, and (2) supervised fine-tuning with cross-entropy on hue and value classification using adaptive loss weights. Transfer learning is achieved by initializing from PianoBART's backbone, combining its fine-tuned parameters across five downstream tasks via the DARE method, and applying LoRA for efficient adaptation. At inference, a Restricted Stochastic Temperature-Controlled (RSTC) sampler enforces per-frame distance thresholds on hue and value to prevent physically implausible jumps while preserving diversity.

## Results

On the RPMC-L² dataset, Skip-BART achieves RMSE of 36.13 (Hue) and 60.74 (Value), substantially outperforming the rule-based baseline (48.67 and 93.39 respectively), with MAE of 28.72 (Hue) and 51.27 (Value) versus 43.43 and 86.55. In the human evaluation (7-point scale), Skip-BART scores 4.35±0.87 overall, statistically indistinguishable from Ground Truth (4.51±0.88, p=0.724), while the rule-based method scores only 2.67±1.29 (p<0.001 vs. Skip-BART). In cross-domain evaluation on folk, R&B, and jazz, Skip-BART maintains a 4.34 overall score versus 3.34 for the rule-based method.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against a single external baseline: a rule-based method reproducing Hsiao et al. (2017), which classifies music into emotion categories via a bidirectional LSTM trained on the DEAM dataset and maps emotions to lighting patterns using Nijdam's (2009) color-emotion framework. Skip-BART decisively outperforms this baseline on all quantitative metrics: RMSE improves by 25.8% (Hue) and 35.0% (Value), MAE by 33.7% (Hue) and 40.6% (Value). In the human evaluation, Skip-BART's overall score (4.35) is significantly higher than the rule-based method (2.67, p<0.001) and not significantly different from Ground Truth (p=0.724), suggesting near-human performance. The rule-based method's only relative strength is in the emotion-matching metric (3.12 vs. 4.69 for Skip-BART in-domain), which the authors attribute to the limited empirical link between hue and emotion. In cross-domain settings (folk, R&B, jazz), Skip-BART again significantly outperforms the rule-based method across all six evaluation dimensions (p<0.001 on most metrics).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs fundamentally from the dominant ASLC paradigm of classifying music into discrete categories (emotion, style, chord) and mapping them to predefined light patterns (Hsiao et al. 2017; Stanescu et al. 2018; Mabpa et al. 2021). Instead, it reframes the task as end-to-end sequence generation, building on the BART encoder-decoder architecture and initializing from PianoBART's pre-trained symbolic music backbone, combined with DARE parameter merging across multiple downstream tasks and LoRA for efficient adaptation. The skip-connection mechanism that explicitly aligns music and light frame embeddings is a novel architectural addition not present in prior BART-based music generation work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper introduces the first dedicated stage lighting dataset (RPMC-L², 699 samples) and demonstrates that a generative model trained on limited data can match professional lighting engineers (p=0.72) while significantly outperforming rule-based methods (p<0.001), challenging the field's assumption that emotion-to-color mapping is a viable paradigm. Key limitations include restriction to offline single-light control, a narrow genre range (rock/punk/metal/core), and a small evaluation cohort (38 participants, 3 tracks). Open questions include real-time multi-light control, cross-venue generalization, and whether the generative framing extends to richer modalities (rhythm, lyrics, musical sections).
