## Problem

Existing smart home benchmarks treat the home as a static system: they neither model how device operations continuously affect environmental variables (temperature, humidity, illuminance, air quality) over time nor support time-accelerated evaluation of workflow scheduling tasks. This makes it impossible to evaluate whether agents can monitor ongoing environmental changes, respect operational dependencies between device commands, or correctly coordinate multi-device actions across time.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261371/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The agent receives a natural-language user query in a pre-configured home environment (multiple rooms, each with up to 17 device types and four environmental variables) and must produce a sequence of Matter-protocol tool calls (execute_command, write_attribute, schedule_workflow, etc.) followed by a final natural-language response. Evaluation is dual-track: episodes involving physical state changes are verified by the simulator comparing final device/environment states against structured goals, while episodes requiring natural-language accuracy (state inquiry, infeasible-request explanations) are scored by an LLM-as-a-Judge with majority voting over three calls. A key structural detail is that schedule_workflow returns only a registration confirmation without validating whether the scheduled commands will succeed at execution time, mirroring real-world smart home platforms.

## Method

SimuHome is a tick-based (0.1 s) deterministic simulator with three components: a configurable Smart Home Environment (rooms, devices, environmental variables), a Real-Time State Update Mechanism that computes additive per-device effects on environmental variables with exponential decay toward room baselines at every tick, and an Agent-Simulator Interface exposing 16 Matter-protocol tools. The benchmark comprises 600 manually validated episodes across 12 categories (six query types—state inquiry, implicit intent, explicit device control, and three forms of workflow scheduling—each with feasible and infeasible variants), generated through a three-step pipeline of dependency-aware state randomization, goal/prerequisite-action construction, and GPT-5-mini query synthesis with human review (Cohen's κ = 0.92). The central empirical finding is that workflow scheduling is the most persistent failure mode across 18 evaluated models, driven by the absence of corrective feedback after scheduling; the paper demonstrates that neither alternative agent frameworks (HiAgent), multi-turn interaction, self-review, nor supervised fine-tuning on 204 GPT-5.1 trajectories resolve this gap, pointing to a fundamental reasoning bottleneck rather than a framework limitation.

## Results

Across 18 LLM agents evaluated on 600 SimuHome episodes, GPT-5.1 (extended reasoning) achieves the highest success rates, reaching 100% on QT1-F and QT4-1-IF, 92% on QT3-IF, and 72% on QT4-2-F, while the best non-reasoning model (GPT-4.1) peaks at 98% on QT1-F and 88% on QT3-IF but drops to 34% on QT4-3-F. Workflow scheduling (QT4) is the most persistent challenge for all models: even GPT-5.1 scores only 56% on coordinated scheduling (QT4-3-F), and non-reasoning models fall below 50% on all QT4 feasible variants. Reasoning models (GPT-5.1, Gemini-2.5-Pro) outperform non-reasoning counterparts by 10–30 percentage points on QT2 and QT4, but at 3–5× the inference time (e.g., GPT-5.1 requires ~121 s vs. GPT-4.1's ~27 s on QT4-1-F).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among small open-source models (<7B), Llama3.2-1B-it scores 0% across all categories, while Gemma3-4B-it achieves limited success on QT1 (44% F) and QT3 (28% F) but near-zero on all QT4 variants. Llama4-Maverick and Qwen3-235B-A22B represent the strongest open-source baselines, with Llama4-Maverick reaching 88% on QT3-F and Qwen3-235B-A22B achieving 38% on QT4-2-F, yet both remain well below closed-source models on workflow scheduling. GPT-4.1 (non-reasoning) leads on QT1-F (98%) and QT3-IF (88%) but lags GPT-5.1 by 14–48 points on QT4 categories; Gemini-2.5-Pro narrows this gap on QT4-1-IF (94%) and QT4-2-IF (76%) but still trails GPT-5.1 on QT4-3-F (46% vs. 56%). The HiAgent framework (replacing ReAct) improves QT4-2 and QT4-3 but underperforms on QT4-1, yielding mixed results and confirming that the bottleneck is model reasoning rather than agent architecture. Supervised fine-tuning on GPT-5.1 trajectories improves infeasible-request detection by up to 26 points (e.g., QT1-IF for Gemma3-4B-it) but yields no gains on feasible coordinated scheduling (QT4-3-F), indicating that dynamic temporal reasoning cannot be captured by imitation learning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from existing smart home agent benchmarks (HomeBench, Sasha, SAGE) that treat the home as a static system by grounding the simulator in the Matter protocol and modeling continuous environmental dynamics (temperature, humidity, illuminance) that evolve in response to device operations. It also extends the evaluation scope beyond single-turn command execution to include time-accelerated workflow scheduling, a capability absent from prior benchmarks. Conceptually, it bridges the gap between embodied agent benchmarks (AI2-THOR, ALFRED) that focus on physical navigation and smart home benchmarks that focus on API-level device control, by requiring agents to reason about both device operational dependencies and their ongoing effects on the environment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that workflow scheduling remains the most persistent failure mode across 18 models, alternative frameworks (HiAgent), and supervised fine-tuning suggests a fundamental reasoning bottleneck rather than a framework or data issue. The structural insight that deferred feedback (schedule_workflow returns only a registration confirmation with no execution-time validation) prevents agents from self-correcting is a useful diagnostic for the broader tool-use literature. The time-accelerated simulator opens a practical path for pre-validating agent plans before real-world deployment, and the paper identifies trial-and-error learning inside the simulator as a promising direction beyond imitation of recorded trajectories. Open questions include whether the Matter-protocol grounding transfers to non-Matter platforms and whether the benchmark's 17 device types and single-home scope adequately capture real-world complexity.
