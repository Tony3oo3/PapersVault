## Problem

Prior benchmarks study LLM reasoning (math, coding) and world knowledge (commonsense QA) in isolation, leaving the interplay and potential conflicts between these capabilities unexplored. This paper asks whether LLMs can perform genuine contextual causal reasoning over narratives—using the specific causal structure expressed in the text—rather than defaulting to memorized associations, and characterizes the systematic failure modes that emerge when reasoning and parametric knowledge conflict.

## Input / Output

The LLM receives a causal narrative (generated from a ground truth causal graph with controlled properties) and is asked either to judge whether one event causes another (Yes/No) or to reconstruct the full causal graph from the narrative. Narratives are constructed with two independently controlled difficulty axes: topological ordering (forward vs. reverse) and parametric knowledge alignment (causal vs. anti-causal), plus varying graph complexity (simple chains vs. graphs with forks and colliders). Evaluation tests every possible ordered pair of variables against the ground truth graph, and human raters confirmed >98% narrative-to-graph faithfulness.

## Method

The paper constructs a controlled experimental framework spanning synthetic, semi-synthetic (CauseNet-based), and real-world narratives, systematically varying world knowledge conflict and graph reasoning complexity. Five prompting strategies are compared: Standard QA, Chain-of-Thought, In-Context Learning, Explicit Causal Graph Extraction (LLM extracts the full causal graph, then deterministic graph traversal answers pair queries), and Narrative-Augmented Graph Extraction (both narrative and extracted graph provided jointly). The key finding is that explicit graph extraction forces the LLM to engage in long-term structural reasoning, avoiding both the topological ordering bias (assigning cause to earlier-mentioned events) and the parametric knowledge shortcut (ignoring narrative specifics in favor of memorized causal associations). Critically, when the narrative is re-provided alongside the extracted graph, the accuracy gains dissipate, indicating the narrative reactivates the shortcut. Results are validated across GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Llama 3.1 8B/70B, and GPT-5 Thinking, with the reasoning model (GPT-5 Thinking) showing the most robust performance across all failure modes.

## Results

On semi-synthetic narratives from CauseNet, when cause-effect pairs agree with the LLM's parametric knowledge, all prompting strategies achieve >90% accuracy (Standard 99.8%, CoT 99.6%, Graph 99.9%). However, when the narrative's causality conflicts with parametric knowledge, Standard drops to 67.2% and CoT to 73.1%, while explicit causal graph extraction maintains 98.7% accuracy. On real-world narratives, the gap widens: Standard falls to 52.1% and CoT to 57.6% under conflict, whereas Graph extraction retains 93.2%. These findings hold across GPT-4o and Llama 3.1 8B, with Llama showing slightly lower absolute numbers (e.g., 48.8% Standard vs. 52.1% for GPT-4o under real-world conflict).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares five prompting strategies (Standard QA, Chain-of-Thought, In-Context Learning, Explicit Graph Extraction, and Narrative-Augmented Graph) rather than external methods. The dominant finding is that explicit causal graph extraction (Graph) dramatically outperforms all narrative-based strategies under parametric knowledge conflict: 98.7% vs. 67.2% (Standard) and 73.1% (CoT) on semi-synthetic data, and 93.2% vs. 52.1% (Standard) and 57.6% (CoT) on real-world data. CoT and In-Context Learning provide negligible improvement over Standard QA in both conflict and non-conflict settings. The Narrative-Augmented Graph strategy (which feeds both the extracted graph and the original narrative back to the LLM) loses the benefits of pure graph extraction, suggesting the narrative re-introduces the shortcut. Across models (GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Llama 3.1 8B/70B, GPT-5 Thinking), the failure modes persist, though GPT-5 Thinking narrows the forward/reverse gap, indicating that explicit reasoning capability partially mitigates positional bias.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of causal reasoning benchmarks and narrative understanding. Prior causal reasoning work for LLMs (Jin et al. 2023, Joshi et al. 2024b) tests models on formulaic or single-sentence inputs, while common-sense causal benchmarks (Gordon et al. 2012, Ho et al. 2023) can be solved by pure retrieval of memorized associations. The authors' key departure is systematically decoupling two difficulty axes—graph reasoning complexity (chains vs. forks/colliders) and parametric knowledge conflict (narrative causality vs. pretraining priors)—within long-form narratives in everyday language, a setting they identify as unexplored. The experimental design draws on CauseNet for real-world causal edges and on controlled synthetic generation to isolate specific failure modes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of two concrete, reproducible failure modes (topological-ordering bias and parametric-knowledge shortcut) that persist across model families and are not fixed by CoT or ICL provides a clear diagnostic framework for practitioners deploying LLMs in causal decision-making. The finding that explicit graph extraction is a simple, effective mitigation—but that re-introducing the narrative alongside the extracted graph destroys the benefit—offers an immediately actionable prompt-engineering insight. Open questions include whether these failure modes extend to counterfactual reasoning, whether targeted finetuning on the identified failure patterns can close the gap, and whether the parametric-knowledge conflict worsens with model scale (the paper hints it does, as larger models perform worse on anti-causal narratives).
