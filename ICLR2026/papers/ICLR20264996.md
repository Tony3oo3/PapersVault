## Problem

Curriculum-based LLM pretraining, which sorts data in ascending order of quality, has consistently shown only marginal improvements over random shuffling in prior work. This paper identifies the overlooked cause: the standard decaying learning rate schedule (cosine or WSD) is fundamentally incompatible with ascending data quality ordering, because high-quality data is placed at the end of training precisely when the LR has decayed to its minimum, suppressing the model's ability to learn from its most valuable samples.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264996/cropped_page1_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretraining text corpus (e.g., 30B tokens from DCLM-Baseline or WebOrganizer) along with pre-computed per-sample quality scores (DCLM fastText or PreSelect) that are used solely for data ordering rather than filtering. The output is a final model obtained by weight-averaging (EMA, SMA, or WMA) the last several checkpoints saved during training, rather than simply taking the last checkpoint. Training is conducted at the 1.5B-parameter scale (Qwen2.5-1.5B architecture) with a 4096 sequence length and batch size of 512.

## Method

The paper's core insight is that the learning rate acts as an implicit importance weight on each data sample: a decaying LR shrinks the parameter update magnitude on high-quality data placed late in the curriculum, counteracting the curriculum's intended benefit. To resolve this, the authors propose two strategies. First, Curriculum Model Averaging (CMA) replaces LR decay entirely with a constant LR and applies weight averaging (default: EMA with α=0.2 over the last 6 checkpoints) to stabilize parameters without reducing update magnitude. Second, Curriculum with LR Decay Model Averaging (CDMA) uses a moderate LR decay (ending LR ≈ 1/3 of peak, e.g., 1×10⁻³ instead of 1×10⁻⁵) combined with weight averaging. A key finding is the synergy between curriculum and weight averaging: EMA/SMA (which assign higher weights to later, higher-quality checkpoints) outperform WMA under a curriculum, and the combination of curriculum + averaging outperforms either strategy alone. A simplified quadratic-loss theoretical model (Section 4) confirms that ascending ordering with SWA or moderate decay breaks the Ω(L²) loss lower bound that ascending ordering with aggressive WSD decay cannot.

## Results

On the DCLM-Baseline 30B-token pretraining (1.5B Qwen2.5 model), the best CMA configuration (SMA + Ascending order + Constant LR) achieves a Core benchmark score of 47.02 and an average downstream score of 50.94, improving over the WSD+Uniform baseline (46.21 / 50.56) by +0.81 and +0.38 respectively. In the mid-training setting (WebOrganizer + DCLM-Baseline), CMA (EMA + All-Together ascending + Constant LR) achieves a Core score of 43.82 and average of 48.69, improving over the WSD+U,U baseline (41.61 / 47.49) by +2.21 and +1.20. The combined CDMA strategy (moderate LR decay + EMA + curriculum) achieves up to +1.68% average improvement over the standard aggressive-decay baseline in mid-training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the DCLM standard WSD+Uniform baseline (Li et al., 2024), CMA (SMA+Ascend+Const) wins on Core (+0.81) and Avg (+0.38), while the naive WSD+Ascend combination actually loses (−0.76 Core), confirming the paper's central claim that aggressive LR decay negates curriculum benefits. Against the folding curriculum strategies from Dai et al. (2025) and Zhang et al. (2025), the paper shows that under a cosine schedule, folding reduces validation loss relative to simple ascending sort but still underperforms the uniform baseline; under a constant LR, simple end-to-end ascending sorting outperforms folding entirely, indicating the folding workaround is a symptom of the LR-decay conflict rather than a superior curriculum design. In the mid-training setting, CMA (EMA+A-T+Const) achieves +2.21 on Core and +1.20 on Avg over the WSD+U,U baseline, substantially outperforming the WSD+A-T variant (+1.12 Core, +0.52 Avg), demonstrating that the synergy between curriculum and weight averaging is more pronounced when high-quality data is sparse.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three threads: instance-level curriculum learning for LLM pretraining (Wettig et al., 2024; Dai et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025), which reported only marginal gains; standard LR schedule design (WSD, cosine), which has been optimized for uniform data ordering; and weight averaging for LLM training (Izmailov et al., 2018; Li et al., 2025c; Tian et al., 2025), which was recently shown to be comparable to LR decay for uniform data. The key departure is identifying that prior curriculum learning work was confounded by an aggressive LR decay that suppressed the very high-quality data the curriculum was designed to emphasize. The paper is the first to systematically study the interaction between data ordering and LR schedule, and to combine weight averaging with a quality-based data curriculum rather than applying either in isolation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a simple, overlooked interaction (aggressive LR decay nullifying curriculum benefits) explains why prior curriculum learning studies reported limited gains reframes a seemingly dead-end direction as one that was simply misconfigured. The proposed fix—moderate LR decay plus model averaging—is trivially implementable and requires no additional data or compute, making it immediately actionable for practitioners doing multi-stage or mid-training pretraining. Limitations include validation at only 1.5B parameters / 30B tokens with a single architecture (Qwen2.5), and the optimal ending LR and averaging hyperparameters may shift at larger scales. Open questions include whether the 1.64% gain persists at 7B+ scale, how to co-design curricula across multiple domains simultaneously, and whether the theoretical quadratic-loss model captures the relevant dynamics of real LLM training.
