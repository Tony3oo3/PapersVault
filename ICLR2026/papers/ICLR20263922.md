## Problem

Existing interpretable ML methods suffer from two fundamental limitations that restrict their scientific applicability: they are tool-centric modifications of standard architectures rather than frameworks grounded in scientific theory (e.g., optimization or conservation laws), and they are typically non-identifiable, meaning multiple distinct parameterizations can explain the same data, undermining reliable recovery of ground-truth mechanisms. The paper asks whether one can design an interpretable ML framework that simultaneously achieves high predictive performance, intrinsic interpretability, and parameter identifiability while being scientifically grounded.

## Input / Output

The method takes contextual features x ∈ R^d and a response y that may be discrete, continuous, or hybrid (y^disc, y^cont), and produces a conditional Gibbs distribution p(y|x) ∝ exp(BL(x,y)/τ) parameterized by a compositional utility function. Unusually, the output is dual-purpose: it serves both as a predictive/generative distribution and as a symbolic optimization problem (a UMP or hierarchical composition of UMPs) whose objective, inequality constraints, and equality constraints are each represented by explicit polynomial expressions, making the model directly readable as a scientific explanation. The temperature τ controls the stochasticity of the response, recovering deterministic best-response behavior as τ→0.

## Method

BL parameterizes a compositional utility function from intrinsically interpretable modular blocks, each of which is a penalty-based reformulation of a Utility Maximization Problem (UMP): B(x,y) = λ₀ᵀtanh(U) − λ₁ᵀReLU(C) − λ₂ᵀ|T|, where U, C, T are polynomial feature maps representing the objective, inequality constraints, and equality constraints respectively. These blocks are composed hierarchically in three architectural variants—BL(Single), BL(Shallow), and BL(Deep)—to model increasingly complex optimization structures, with the overall function inducing a conditional Gibbs distribution for prediction and generation. Training uses a hybrid objective combining cross-entropy for discrete responses and denoising score matching for continuous responses, avoiding intractable partition function computation. The Identifiable BL (IBL) variant replaces ReLU and |·| with smooth monotone functions (softplus and elementwise square) to guarantee parameter identifiability under mild conditions, along with consistency and universal consistency guarantees. The key theoretical insight (Theorem 2.2) is that any optimization problem is equivalent to a UMP, making BL a general-purpose framework for data-driven inverse optimization across scientific domains.

## Results

On standard prediction tasks across 10 datasets, BL attains first-tier predictive performance, with BL(Shallow) surpassing MLP and ranking second-to-third in mean F1-Macro rank among all 12 methods tested. On high-dimensional inputs (Table 1), BL matches or exceeds E-MLP in-distribution accuracy: e.g., on Yelp Polarity, BL(depth=1) achieves 91.53% vs. E-MLP's 89.86%, and on AG News, 89.29% vs. 86.05%. BL also generally improves OOD detection (AUROC), reaching 91.17% on MNIST and 91.89% on Fashion-MNIST at depth 1, versus 88.72% and 90.57% for E-MLP. On the constraint enforcement test, BL enforces a 64-dimensional energy-conservation constraint within 10⁻² error at λ=25 and τ=0.01.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary quantitative comparison is against E-MLP (energy-based MLP) on four high-dimensional datasets at matched parameter counts. BL wins on in-distribution accuracy for text datasets (AG News: 89.29% vs. 86.05% at depth 1; Yelp: 91.53% vs. 89.86%) and on Fashion-MNIST (89.26% vs. 88.79%), while E-MLP retains a slight edge on MNIST (98.15% vs. 97.97%). For OOD detection, BL outperforms E-MLP on MNIST (91.17% vs. 88.72% AUROC at depth 1), Fashion-MNIST (91.89% vs. 90.57%), and Yelp (84.31% vs. 79.35%), but E-MLP is stronger on AG News (87.95% vs. 83.15%). On the broader standard prediction benchmark (10 datasets, 10 baselines spanning neural networks, tree-based, gradient boosting, Bayesian, and linear methods), BL(Shallow) surpasses MLP and ranks second-to-third in mean F1-Macro rank, with no statistically significant gap to state-of-the-art models. The paper also notes that BL achieves better calibration (lower ECE and NLL) than E-MLP while using comparable parameter counts and only slightly higher training time, which the authors frame as a 'downward shift of the Pareto frontier' between performance, interpretability, and cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

BL is constructed from the Han-Mangasarian exact penalty reformulation of constrained optimization (Theorem 2.1), which provides the mathematical foundation for each modular block's penalty-based UMP structure. For training, it borrows the Gibbs distribution parameterization and denoising score matching objective from the energy-based model literature (LeCun et al., 2006; Vincent, 2011), but replaces the black-box energy function with intrinsically interpretable polynomial-based UMP blocks. Conceptually, it departs from prior interpretable ML (additive models, concept-based models, rule lists) by grounding the architecture in a scientific paradigm (utility maximization) and adding identifiability guarantees via the IBL variant, and from inverse reinforcement learning by being structure-free and not requiring policy matching.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

BL offers a principled path toward scientifically credible interpretable modeling by simultaneously addressing the performance-interpretability trade-off and the identifiability gap that plagues most existing interpretable methods. The universal approximation and M-estimation guarantees (consistency, universal consistency) provide theoretical backing that the learned optimization structures can recover ground-truth mechanisms under correct specification. Key open questions include whether identifiability holds in large-scale over-parameterized regimes, how to handle high-order polynomial instability, and whether the framework extends to genuinely generative settings (image/video/LLM) while preserving block-level interpretability. The empirical validation, while covering diverse domains, is limited in scale and does not yet stress-test identifiability claims on realistic misspecified models.
