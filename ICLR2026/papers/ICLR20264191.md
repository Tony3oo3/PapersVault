## Problem

The theoretical understanding of gradient descent with large (near-critical) step sizes remains limited, even in simple non-convex settings. Two fundamental questions are unresolved: what is the exact critical step size for convergence, and what implicit biases (if any) are induced near criticality? Prior rigorous analyses of chaotic gradient descent dynamics were restricted to univariate losses, leaving high-dimensional matrix factorization and neural network training uncharacterized.

## Input / Output

The analysis takes a diagonal target matrix Y (obtained via SVD reparametrization of an arbitrary target), initial parameters (U, V) ∈ R^{d×d_y}, a constant step size η, and an ℓ₂ regularization coefficient λ. Beyond the converged minimizer or divergence, the method produces geometric and topological invariants of the optimization landscape: the exact critical step size formula, the fractal dimension of the convergence boundary (box-counting dimension ≈ 1.249 for the projected boundary), the topological entropy of the GD map (≥ log 3), and the self-similarity degree of the boundary. The convergence region itself is characterized as a set in parameter space whose boundary can be fractal (with regularization) or smooth almost everywhere (without).

## Method

The paper derives the exact critical step size for scalar-vector factorization and shows that near criticality, infinitesimal perturbations of the initialization can send trajectories to minimizers with arbitrarily large norm, sharpness, or imbalance, or to a saddle point. A key technical tool is a quotient map T(u,v) = (uᵀv, ‖u‖²+‖v‖²) that reduces the high-dimensional GD dynamics to a planar map F, revealing that the convergence boundary is self-similar with degree three and that GD acts as a 3-covering (folding) map on a region containing the boundary. This folding mechanism explains both the fractal geometry and the sensitivity to initialization: the boundary is invariant under GD and the map is transitive (mixing) on it. The results extend to general matrix factorization via an orthogonal-initialization subspace where column-wise dynamics decouple into independent scalar factorizations, and to general polynomial neural networks where GD is shown to be a covering map outside a measure-zero set. Empirical validation on a depth-3 ReLU network trained on CIFAR-10 confirms the two-regime behavior (predictable EoS vs. chaotic) and fractal basin boundaries in parameter space.

## Results

The paper derives the exact critical step size for convergence in scalar-vector factorization (Theorem 1, Eq. 3), showing that for η|y| < 1 the convergence region is almost everywhere an ellipsoid. The topological entropy of the gradient descent system is proven to be at least log 3, and the system admits periodic orbits of every positive integer period. With ℓ₂ regularization, the projected convergence boundary T(∂D''η) is self-similar with degree three and has an estimated box-counting dimension of 1.249 (uncertainty exponent 0.751). Empirically, on a depth-3 ReLU network trained on CIFAR-10 (MSE loss, 5000 iterations), two distinct regimes emerge: in the EoS regime the final sharpness tracks 2/η smoothly, while in the chaotic regime the final sharpness spans nearly all values below 2/η and becomes highly sensitive to both step size and initialization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary empirical comparison is against the Edge of Stability (EoS) prediction of Cohen et al. (2021), which predicts final sharpness ≈ 2/η. The paper confirms this prediction holds in the small-to-moderate step size regime (EoS regime), but shows that at larger (near-critical) step sizes the prediction breaks down: the final sharpness no longer clusters near 2/η but instead spans almost the entire interval (0, 2/η), with the specific value depending sensitively on the exact step size and initialization (Figure 4). The paper also extends the convergence characterization of Wang et al. (2022), who proved convergence only for step sizes η < 1/(3|y|) and initializations in a strict subset of the convergence region, to the full spectrum of step sizes and the complete convergence region (up to a measure-zero set). In the regularized setting, the paper contrasts the small-step-size distance-minimization bias (Theorem 5: convergence to the closest minimizer p⁻) with the near-critical behavior where infinitesimal perturbations can switch the selected minimizer from the closest to the farthest global minimizer (Theorem 4), a phenomenon absent in all prior small-step-size analyses.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on large-step-size gradient descent dynamics (Edge of Stability, catapult phenomena) to the near-critical regime where prior convergence guarantees break down. It builds directly on Wang et al. (2022), who characterized convergence only for η < 1/(3|y|) in a strict subset of the convergence region, by deriving the exact critical step size for the full spectrum. The chaos analysis extends the rigorous Li-Yorke chaos results of Kong and Tao (2020) and Chen et al. (2024b) from univariate losses to high-dimensional matrix factorization, adding sensitivity of the limiting point to initialization and a fractal convergence boundary. The fractal geometry draws on classical dynamical systems theory (Grebogi, McDonald, Yorke) but departs from it by handling non-invertible, non-compact, and unbounded settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that near-critical step sizes place gradient descent in a genuinely chaotic regime where no simple implicit bias (balancedness, minimum norm, flatness) survives, and where convergence itself becomes unpredictable due to a fractal boundary. This challenges the practical assumption that large learning rates reliably select flat or balanced minima. Key open questions include whether these phenomena persist for general (non-orthogonal) initializations, deep networks, and nonlinear activations—the paper provides only empirical evidence for the latter. The covering-map mechanism (Proposition 6) suggests a general principle for polynomial networks, but rigorous characterization remains future work.
