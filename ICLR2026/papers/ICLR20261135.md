## Problem

Online RL for instruction-guided image editing is severely hindered by the absence of a high-fidelity, efficient reward signal: proprietary VLMs are too expensive to query at scale, and even the largest open-source VLMs (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL-72B) fail to provide accurate enough scores to guide stable policy optimization, often causing training instability or collapse. The community also lacks a dedicated benchmark to systematically evaluate reward models on editing quality.

## Input / Output

EditScore takes a triplet of (editing instruction, input image, edited output image) and produces a chain-of-thought reasoning paragraph followed by two scalar scores in [0,25]—Semantic Consistency (instruction following + preservation) and Perceptual Quality (photorealism, artifact absence)—combined via geometric mean into a final reward. The self-ensemble variant runs K=4 stochastic forward passes and averages the scalar scores, exploiting shared KV-cache prefilling so that latency grows sublinearly with K. For the RL pipeline, the policy model (OmniGen2) consumes only the instruction and input image to generate candidate edits, which are then scored by EditScore to compute group-normalized advantages.

## Method

The paper introduces EditReward-Bench (3,072 expert-annotated preference pairs across 13 subtasks from 11 editing models, scored on Prompt Following, Consistency, and Overall Quality via a two-annotator discussion protocol with tiered rankings) and EditScore, a family of 7B–72B reward models fine-tuned from Qwen2.5-VL with LoRA on 70K curated samples using the VIEScore two-axis scoring framework. A key design choice is reasoning-before-scoring with an expanded [0,25] score range, which yields a +0.038 accuracy gain over direct scoring. An inference-time self-ensemble strategy (K=4 stochastic passes, arithmetic mean of scores) further boosts accuracy, with EditScore-72B (Avg@4) surpassing GPT-5 on the benchmark. For RL, the authors derive a forward-time SDE from OmniGen2's flow-matching ODE (noise at t=0 → data at t=1) to define a stochastic policy, then optimize it with GRPO using EditScore as the terminal reward, demonstrating that reward signal variance—not absolute annotator accuracy—is the dominant factor for successful policy optimization.

## Results

On EditReward-Bench, EditScore-72B with self-ensemble (Avg@4) achieves 0.763 overall pairwise accuracy, surpassing GPT-5 (0.755) and GPT-4.1 (0.705), while even the compact 7B variant (Avg@4) reaches 0.727—far exceeding the 10× larger Qwen2.5-VL-72B (0.621). In the downstream RL experiment on GEdit-Bench, using EditScore-7B (Avg@4) as the reward signal yields an overall VIEScore of 6.68 (SC=7.20, PQ=7.46), a substantial improvement over the no-RL baseline (6.28) and competitive with GPT-4.1 (6.73). On the out-of-distribution EmuEdit benchmark, the EditScore-trained policy achieves CLIP-I of 0.900, surpassing GPT-4.1 (0.884).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against proprietary VLMs, EditScore-72B (Avg@4) surpasses GPT-5 on Overall (0.763 vs. 0.755) and Consistency (0.735 vs. 0.669), though GPT-5 retains a slight edge on Prompt Following (0.777 vs. 0.755). EditScore-72B (Avg@4) clearly outperforms GPT-4.1 across all three dimensions (0.755/0.735/0.763 vs. 0.673/0.602/0.705) and Gemini-2.5 Pro (0.703/0.560/0.722). Against open-source baselines, the improvement is dramatic: even EditScore-7B (Avg@4) exceeds Qwen2.5-VL-72B by +0.182 on Overall accuracy (0.727 vs. 0.621), and Qwen2.5-VL-72B actually performs below random chance on Consistency (0.435). In the RL setting (Table 4), EditScore-7B (Avg@4) is competitive with GPT-4.1 on GEdit-Bench (O: 6.68 vs. 6.73) while significantly outperforming Qwen2.5-VL-72B (O: 6.68 vs. 6.42), and it surpasses GPT-4.1 on the OOD EmuEdit benchmark (CLIP-I: 0.900 vs. 0.884; DINO: 0.870 vs. 0.843). The paper also shows that substituting Flow-GRPO with TempFlow-GRPO under the same EditScore reward further boosts the overall score from 6.68 to 7.21, confirming algorithm-agnostic generality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RL-for-generation paradigm (Flow-GRPO, DanceGRPO) from text-to-image to instruction-guided image editing, identifying the missing reward signal as the critical bottleneck. It builds on VLM-based reward modeling (VIEScore's SC/PQ decomposition, Qwen2.5-VL as backbone) and adapts test-time compute scaling (Snell et al.) into a self-ensemble strategy for generative reward models. The key departure from prior editing reward models (HIVE, ADIEE) is the explicit targeting of current SOTA editors and the demonstration that off-the-shelf VLMs—even at 72B—fail as RL reward signals, necessitating task-specific fine-tuning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first end-to-end, open-source toolkit (benchmark + reward model + RL pipeline) for online RL in image editing, unblocking a direction that was previously infeasible due to the absence of a reliable reward oracle. The finding that reward signal variance can outweigh absolute annotator accuracy for RL optimization is a practically important insight for reward model design. Limitations remain for highly subjective tasks (portrait beautification) and complex compositional edits, and the approach's transfer to video editing or other generative domains is unexplored.
