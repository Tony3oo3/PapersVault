## Problem

Prediction with expert advice under local differential privacy (LDP) was largely unexplored, with prior work almost exclusively assuming a trusted central curator (central DP). Additionally, existing private expert algorithms restricted experts to data-independent static actions, ignoring the practical need for experts that are themselves adaptive learning algorithms whose advice must be acted upon without leaking privacy. This paper addresses both gaps by designing LDP-satisfying algorithms that exploit the structure of the classical RW-FTPL random-walk algorithm and extend it to handle data-dependent learners.

## Input / Output

At each time step t, the algorithm receives a gain vector g_t ∈ [0,1]^n (representing the reward of each of n experts) that has been locally perturbed with Gaussian noise N(0, η²I_n) before sharing, where η = Δ/μ and Δ ≤ √n is the sensitivity. For RW-Meta, the input additionally includes m learner functions f_i that map the shared noisy gain history to actions in the probability simplex. The output at each step is an action x_t from the n-dimensional probability simplex (equivalently, which expert to follow), and for RW-Meta also a selection of which of the m learners to act on. The algorithms are fully non-interactive: all randomness is sampled locally at the data source, requiring no trusted server or multi-round communication.

## Method

Both algorithms build on RW-FTPL, which maintains a Gaussian random walk over cumulative noisy gains and plays the argmax vertex of the probability simplex. RW-AdaBatch exploits the limited-switching property of this random walk: when the gap between the top two experts is large, the leader is stable for many steps (characterized by Theorem 1 via Borell-TIS and Gaussian process extrema bounds). The algorithm adaptively batches data during these stable intervals, making it permutation-invariant within batches and yielding shuffle-model-style privacy amplification that grows stronger on easier data, at only a (1+α/2)× regret cost. RW-Meta extends the framework to m data-dependent learners by maintaining a Gaussian estimate of each learner's cumulative gain from the same shared noisy vectors, then applying a decorrelation step (projecting out the all-ones component from the data-dependent covariance matrix) before sampling a perturbation to select the best learner. This noise-sharing scheme ensures the entire meta-algorithm satisfies μ-GLDP by post-processing invariance, with regret scaling as O(√(Tnm log m)/μ) and tightening when learners are more independent (captured by the leading eigenvalue of the empirical covariance).

## Results

On the DHHS COVID-19 hospitalization data (predicting which hospital reports the highest density of COVID patients each week), RW-Meta achieves average cumulative gains of 40.14 (New Mexico), 43.70 (Pennsylvania), and 59.36 (California) at no privacy (μ=∞), compared to 26.48, 27.42, and 31.12 for the best CDP baseline (Agarwal, Min Noise). At the highest privacy level (μ=0.25), RW-Meta still achieves 22.46, 25.44, and 33.34 respectively, outperforming the CDP baseline by 1.5–1.9×. RW-Meta reaches approximately 90% of the best linear model's performance in every setting, and is the only method that achieves negative static regret at low and moderate privacy levels.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares RW-Meta against the state-of-the-art CDP experts algorithm of Agarwal & Singh (2017), evaluated under two parameterizations: 'Min Noise' (minimum noise to satisfy μ-GCDP) and 'Min Regret' (noise chosen to optimize worst-case regret bounds). RW-Meta outperforms Agarwal (Min Noise) by 1.5–1.9× in average cumulative gain across all states and privacy levels (e.g., 40.14 vs. 26.48 in New Mexico at μ=∞; 33.34 vs. 17.93 in California at μ=0.25). Against Agarwal (Min Regret), the margin is even larger, reaching up to 3.5× (59.36 vs. 16.68 in California at μ=∞). RW-Meta is the only method that achieves negative static regret at low and moderate privacy levels, a feat the authors note is 'essentially impossible to match using data-independent experts, regardless of noise scale.' The comparison is caveated by the authors: Agarwal & Singh's algorithm satisfies CDP (not LDP) and was not designed for dynamic environments, so the gap partly reflects the local-vs-central privacy tradeoff and the benefit of data-dependent learners rather than a pure algorithmic improvement.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the non-private RW-FTPL algorithm of Devroye et al. (2013), showing it naturally satisfies local differential privacy, and then develops two extensions: RW-AdaBatch exploits the algorithm's limited-switching property to achieve shuffle-model-style privacy amplification, while RW-Meta introduces a noise-sharing scheme for privately selecting among data-dependent learners. It departs from the dominant CDP-focused line of work (Agarwal & Singh 2017; Asi et al. 2023) by targeting the local model, which eliminates the need for a trusted curator, and from prior LDP expert work (Gao et al. 2024) by addressing oblivious adversaries in a non-federated setting. A key conceptual departure is handling data-dependent experts (learners that adapt to the data stream), whereas all prior private expert algorithms implicitly assume data-independent experts that consume no privacy budget.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that limited switching under LDP yields essentially free privacy amplification (stronger on easier data) is a novel connection between online learning stability and the shuffle model, with potential applicability beyond the experts problem. RW-Meta addresses a practical gap: prior work can identify the best expert but cannot act on data-dependent experts without violating privacy, and the paper shows this can be done at no additional privacy cost. However, the authors note that LDP requires gain vectors with sensitivity smaller than their dimension for reasonable regret, limiting applicability to high-dimensional settings, and RW-Meta's linear gain assumption prevents straightforward extension to general online convex optimization. The 1.5–3× empirical improvement over CDP baselines on COVID-19 hospital data is encouraging but explicitly caveated as a comparison of different paradigms rather than a head-to-head algorithmic contest.
