## Problem

Post-hoc explainability methods for time series (gradients, SHAP, attention weights) suffer from reference-state sensitivity, treat time points independently, or produce unfaithful attributions due to model non-linearities. No existing framework provides inherently faithful, scale-invariant positive and negative temporal attribution scores for multivariate time series while maintaining state-of-the-art predictive accuracy.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260010/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model accepts a multivariate (or univariate) time series x ∈ R^{L×v} of arbitrary length L and v channels, and produces a class label over C categories along with per-time-point positive (φ⁺) and negative (φ⁻) attribution scores. The core representation P ∈ R^{(n·v)×q} is sequence-length independent, where n is the number of symbolic bins and q is the latent dimension, so the number of trainable parameters does not grow with L. Attribution scores are computed non-parametrically from the internal segment representations after training, requiring no additional forward passes or sampling.

## Method

TimeSliver decomposes each input into overlapping temporal segments of size m and processes them through three modules: (I) a 1D CNN produces latent segment embeddings Q ∈ R^{κ×q}; (II) each variate is discretized into n symbolic bins (SAX-style), one-hot encoded, and average-pooled over each segment to form a symbolic composition matrix Z ∈ R^{κ×(n·v)} encoding the normalized frequency of each symbolic pattern per segment; (III) the linear product P = ZᵀQ aggregates global segment interactions into a sequence-length-independent representation that is projected by a single linear layer to class logits. Temporal attribution is then extracted by decomposing each P entry back into its κ segment-level contributions, using the sign of the logit gradient to route each contribution into a positive or negative score via ReLU, yielding scale-invariant scores that satisfy completeness, sensitivity, and symmetry axioms. The symbolic (frequency-based) encoding of Z is the key design choice that prevents high-magnitude but semantically irrelevant segments from producing spurious attributions, and the architecture supports multiple segment sizes stacked into a 3D tensor for capturing patterns at different temporal scales.

## Results

On four synthetic datasets with known ground-truth salient segments, TimeSliver achieves AUPRC scores of 0.94 (FreqSum), 0.97 (SeqComb-UV), 0.94 (SeqComb-MV), and 0.99 (LowVar), representing an average 18% improvement over the strongest baseline (COLOR). On three real-world datasets (EEG, Audio, FordA), TimeSliver leads in positive attribution with I(20) values of 14.52, 11.35, and 14.99 respectively, and achieves a 60% higher logit increase (0.26) when masking top negatively-attributing time points on EEG. For predictive performance on 26 UEA multivariate datasets, TimeSliver achieves 75.6% overall accuracy, within 2% of the best baselines (RLPAM and InceptionTime at 74.3%), with an average rank of 7.00 and 6 top-1 finishes across all 26 tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TimeSliver decisively outperforms all 12 explainability baselines on every synthetic dataset: it beats the strongest competitor COLOR by 41% on FreqSum (0.94 vs 0.53), 7% on SeqComb-UV (0.97 vs 0.90), 30% on SeqComb-MV (0.94 vs 0.72), and 3% on LowVar (0.99 vs 0.96). Gradient-based methods (Grad-CAM, DeepLift, Integrated Gradients) and attention-based methods (Attention Tracing, Grad-SAM) consistently underperform, with AUPRC scores typically below 0.70, confirming the paper's motivation that non-linearities in these approaches lead to unfaithful attributions. On real-world positive attribution, TimeSliver edges out Integrated Gradients (the strongest baseline) by ~0.8 in I(100) on EEG (83.99 vs 83.19) and by ~0.2 in I(20) on FordA (14.99 vs 14.76). For negative attribution on EEG, TimeSliver's logit increase of 0.26 after masking top 2% negatively-attributing points is 60% higher than Integrated Gradients (0.16); on Audio and FordA, all methods yield near-zero values, indicating few negatively-attributing segments exist. On predictive performance across 26 UEA datasets, TimeSliver (75.6% overall) surpasses RLPAM (74.3%) and InceptionTime (74.3%) in aggregate but trails InterpGN (98.3%) on Coord. datasets and InceptionTime (96.4%) on Motion datasets, while leading on Bio. signals (66.9% vs 66.75% for RLPAM) and achieving the best average rank (7.00) and most top-1 finishes (6/26) among all 17 methods compared.

## Relation to Prior Work

TimeSliver repurposes symbolic time series discretization (SAX, ABBA, SFA) not as a classification feature but as a scale-invariant encoding that enables direct, non-parametric temporal attribution. Conceptually, it extends the compositional linear operation framework from COLOR (Pandey et al., 2025) for univariate categorical protein sequences to multivariate continuous time series, and draws on the Bag-of-Words intuition from SAX-VSM (Senin & Malinchik, 2013) to construct a sequence-length-independent representation. It departs from post-hoc attribution methods (Grad-CAM, DeepLift, SHAP, Integrated Gradients) and attention-based methods (Grad-SAM, Attention Tracing) by making explainability an architectural property: the linear composition P = Z^T Q ensures attribution scores are directly decomposable from internal representations rather than requiring gradient backpropagation or baseline selection.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TimeSliver provides a practical, inherently explainable classifier that simultaneously delivers competitive predictive accuracy (within 2% of SOTA on 26 UEA datasets) and faithful positive/negative temporal attributions, addressing a gap where prior methods either sacrifice accuracy for interpretability or produce unfaithful attributions. The scale-invariance property and satisfaction of the completeness axiom offer theoretical grounding that post-hoc methods lack. Open questions include whether the masking-based evaluation protocol generalizes to imbalanced or streaming settings, whether the linear composition can be extended to feature-level (not just temporal-level) attribution, and whether human-in-the-loop validation (e.g., EEG sleep staging) confirms the clinical utility of the attribution scores.
