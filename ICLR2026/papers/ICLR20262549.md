## Problem

Mixture-of-Experts models lack explicit constraints ensuring that the router's token-assignment decisions align with the actual capabilities of the experts it routes to. Without such coupling, routers learn routing strategies through trial-and-error, producing misrouted tokens whose gradients interfere with expert specialization. Prior approaches that enforce this coupling (e.g., Autonomy-of-Experts) incur substantial computational and memory overhead that scales with the number of tokens, making them impractical at scale.

## Input / Output

The ERC loss is a training-time-only auxiliary loss that consumes the router parameter matrix R ∈ R^{n×d} and each expert's gate projection W_g^j ∈ R^{d×D}, producing a single scalar loss value. It operates on n² intermediate activations (one per expert-proxy pair), a fixed cost independent of batch size, unlike prior coupling methods that scale with the number of tokens (often millions per batch). At inference, the model behaves identically to a vanilla MoE with zero additional overhead.

## Method

The ERC loss treats each row R[i] of the router matrix as a cluster center for tokens routed to expert i, then perturbs it with bounded multiplicative noise δ_i ~ U(1−ε_i, 1+ε_i)^d (where ε_i is set to half the nearest-neighbor cluster distance divided by ‖R[i]‖) to create a proxy token R̃[i]. Each proxy is passed through all n experts' W_g projections, yielding an n×n activation-norm matrix M where M[i,j] = ‖R̃[i]·W_g^j‖. The loss then imposes two hinge constraints: (1) each proxy must activate its own expert more than any other (expert specialization), and (2) each expert must respond most strongly to its own proxy (precise routing), with a hyperparameter α ∈ [0,1] controlling coupling strength. Minimizing L_ERC = (1/n²)Σ_{i≠j}(max(M[i,j]−αM[i,i],0) + max(M[j,i]−αM[i,i],0)) jointly ensures router embeddings faithfully represent expert capabilities while experts specialize in their assigned token clusters, at a fixed O(n²) computational cost.

## Results

On 15B-parameter MoE models (n=256 experts, K=8), the ERC loss yields consistent improvements across all eight challenging benchmarks: MMLU improves from 63.2 to 64.6, C-Eval from 67.5 to 69.0, AGI-Eval from 42.0 to 44.2, and TriviaQA from 47.2 to 49.1. At the 3B scale (n=64, K=8), the ERC-augmented MoE achieves stable gains over vanilla MoE while narrowing the performance gap with the more expensive AoE baseline. The ERC loss adds only 0.2–0.8% training overhead and introduces zero inference cost, whereas AoE requires 1.6× more training hours and 1.3× higher memory.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the vanilla MoE baseline (OLMoE architecture), the ERC loss produces consistent gains of +0.4 to +2.2 points across all eight 15B-scale benchmarks, with the largest improvements on AGI-Eval (+2.2) and TriviaQA (+1.9). Compared to AoE (Lv et al., 2025), the ERC-augmented MoE narrows the performance gap at the 3B scale while retaining vanilla MoE efficiency; AoE requires 1.6× more training hours and 1.3× more memory, and could not be trained at the 15B scale at all due to prohibitive cost. Against the router orthogonalization loss from ERNIE 4.5 (Baidu-ERNIE-Team, 2025), the ERC loss delivers substantially larger improvements, as the baseline MoE's router embeddings are already nearly orthogonal (average absolute cosine similarity of 0.15), making further orthogonalization largely redundant. The paper concludes that weak expert–router coupling, not imperfect router orthogonality, is the more critical issue, and the ERC loss directly addresses this gap.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the activation-norm-as-capability-alignment insight from Geva et al. (2021) and the routing-by-norm mechanism of Autonomy-of-Experts (Lv et al., 2025) into a lightweight auxiliary loss that operates on only n² proxy tokens rather than all T input tokens. It departs from prior coupling methods (AoE, Pham et al. 2024) by decoupling the coupling signal from token count, achieving a fixed computational cost independent of batch size. The paper also explicitly contrasts with contrastive/orthogonality approaches (ERNIE 4.5, Guo et al. 2026), arguing that router orthogonality alone is insufficient because the fundamental issue is the weak alignment between router embeddings and expert parameters, not the geometry of the router space itself.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ERC loss is a practical, drop-in auxiliary loss with negligible overhead (0.2–0.8%) that consistently improves MoE pre-training from 3B to 15B parameters, making it immediately adoptable by practitioners training large MoE-LLMs. Beyond performance, it provides a quantitative and controllable tool (via α and ε) for studying expert specialization, revealing a trade-off where extreme specialization degrades performance—a finding that challenges the 'more specialization is always better' narrative derived from small-scale fine-tuning experiments. Open questions include how to determine the optimal α for a given architecture (n, K, shared experts), whether the specialization trade-off persists at even larger scales, and whether the proxy-token formulation generalizes to non-SwiGLU expert structures.
