## Problem

LLMs deployed in healthcare may inappropriately rely on patient race to inform clinical predictions, yet clinicians consuming these outputs have no visibility into such reliance. This paper asks whether Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs), which decompose model activations into interpretable latents, can both reveal problematic race-related associations inside LLMs and serve as a causal intervention tool to mitigate them in clinical tasks.

## Input / Output

The method consumes clinical discharge summaries (MIMIC-III) and brief hospital courses (MIMIC-IV) as text inputs, which are passed through gemma-2-2B-it or gemma-2-9B-it to extract hidden states at a fixed middle layer (layer 12 or 20). These hidden states are projected through GemmaScope SAEs (width 16K) to yield per-token latent vectors, which are max-aggregated across tokens into a single vector per document. Outputs include: (i) a ranked list of race-predictive latents from a logistic regression probe, (ii) steered model generations (belligerence risk, patient vignettes, pain-management decisions) after modifying the Black latent activation, and (iii) bias metrics (Δlogitdiff, FLDD) comparing Black vs. white patient inputs before and after latent ablation.

## Method

The pipeline has three stages. First, a logistic regression probe with L1 regularization is trained on max-aggregated SAE latent activations to identify the most race-predictive latents; latent descriptions are then re-interpreted on clinical text using Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct because existing Neuronpedia labels are domain-inappropriate (e.g., 'vehicle maintenance' becomes 'surgical valve replacement'). Second, causality is established via activation steering: the Black latent's activation is increased by α·z_max at the target layer, the modified latent vector is projected back to hidden-state space, and the model's downstream output (e.g., belligerence risk) is measured; this reveals that increasing 'Black-ness' raises predicted belligerence risk while CoT explanations remain unfaithful (never mentioning race). Third, for bias mitigation, race-related latents (7 for 2B, 9 for 9B, selected via re-interpreted descriptions) are zero-ablated and the effect is quantified via Fractional Logit Difference Decrease (FLDD), compared against an anti-bias prompting baseline. The key finding is that SAE ablation reduces bias in simple vignette-generation tasks (~30% reduction) but has minimal effect (≤3% FLDD) on more realistic clinical risk-prediction and pain-management tasks, suggesting race information is entangled with clinical concepts in complex settings.

## Results

On the simple vignette generation task, zero-ablating the Black SAE latent reduces the fraction of Black patient vignettes by ~30% on average (e.g., cocaine abuse: 0.88→0.46 for gemma-2-2B), outperforming anti-bias prompting (~18% reduction). On more realistic clinical tasks (risk prediction, pain management), SAE ablation yields minimal improvement: FLDD values range from 0.01% to 2.9%, and SAE interventions reduce Δlogitdiff in only 2 of 5 task-model combinations. Steering with the Black latent causally increases predicted belligerence risk by +0.51 (2B) and +0.80 (9B), while the corresponding white latent produces negligible change (−0.01, +0.09). CoT explanations generated under steering contain no race-related terms, confirming unfaithful reasoning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is an anti-bias prompting strategy that appends instructions like 'Avoid generating demographics that solely reflect stereotypes' or 'Do not make assumptions about the patient based on their race.' On the controlled vignette generation task, SAE ablation clearly outperforms prompting, reducing the Black patient fraction by ~30% versus ~18% for prompting across all three conditions. However, on more realistic clinical tasks (diagnosis evidence retrieval and Q-Pain), the comparison reverses: prompting significantly reduces Δlogitdiff in 4 of 5 task-model settings, while SAE ablation achieves meaningful reduction in only 2 (uterine fibroids and gestational hypertension, with marginal gains of 0.05 and 0.03 respectively). For Q-Pain on gemma-2-2B, SAE ablation is essentially ineffective (FLDD = 0.01%), and for cocaine abuse, prompting actually over-corrects by shifting toward 'Yes' for white patients. The paper concludes that SAEs are a useful diagnostic tool for revealing racial associations but their utility for bias mitigation does not generalize beyond simplified settings, where careful prompting remains more effective.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes Sparse Autoencoders (Cunningham et al., 2023) from general-domain interpretability to the clinical healthcare setting, adopting the steering formalism of Arad et al. (2025), the latent-effect computation of Marks et al. (2025), and the logistic-regression probe approach of Movva et al. (2025). It extends the growing literature on racial bias in clinical LLMs (Zack et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2024; Poulain et al., 2024) by introducing SAEs as a diagnostic and intervention tool, and by reinterpreting latent descriptions on clinical text (via Paulo et al., 2024) to obtain domain-specific semantics. The key conceptual departure is treating SAE latents as a more trustworthy window into model behavior than Chain-of-Thought explanations, which the paper shows to be unfaithful in clinical contexts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that CoT reasoning chains omit race as a factor even when the model is demonstrably using it to alter risk assessments has direct implications for clinician trust in LLM-assisted decision support. However, the negative result—that zero-ablating race latents yields negligible bias reduction (FLDD ≤ 2.9%) on realistic clinical tasks, while simple anti-bias prompting outperforms SAE ablation—suggests that race is too entangled with clinical concepts for single-latent interventions to be effective. This leaves open the question of whether more sophisticated SAE-based strategies (e.g., multi-latent, multi-layer, or circuit-level interventions) could close the gap, and whether SAEs are better suited as detection tools than as mitigation tools in high-stakes clinical settings.
