## Problem

Existing RLVR data scheduling methods rank queries using path-based metrics like final-answer accuracy, which conflates initial performance with true learning difficulty. A query with low accuracy but a simple, concentrated error structure may be far easier to optimize than one with moderate accuracy but a fragmented, diffuse error structure. The paper asks how to directly quantify a query's structural learnability from its reasoning tree rather than from flat path statistics.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20265244/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a set of training queries with verifiable ground-truth answers (DAPO-Math-17K math problems) and a base LLM, and additionally requires an offline pass where the base model samples multiple solution trajectories per query to build an approximate k-ary reasoning tree (k=4, d=4, l=200 tokens per branch, using KV-cache for shared prefixes). The output is a trained policy plus a per-query dynamic weight ω(q,t) that is recomputed each epoch, shifting emphasis from structurally simple to structurally complex queries over the course of training.

## Method

Re-Schedule frames RLVR training as a node-editing process on each query's reasoning tree and introduces the r-score: the maximum accuracy gain achievable by editing at most M non-conflicting nodes (each edit selects a node's single best child branch and prunes the rest). The query-level r-score is the optimal sum of node-level r-scores under this budget, so a high r-score indicates that few critical corrections yield large accuracy gains (structurally simple), while a low r-score indicates diffuse errors requiring many edits (structurally complex). During training, a scheduling function γ(t) (linear or sigmoid over T epochs) blends the weight toward high-r-score queries early and toward low-r-score queries later, with the final per-query weight mapped through rank to a [ω_min, ω_max] range and multiplied into the standard GRPO objective. This easy-to-hard structural curriculum consistently outperforms accuracy-based and gradient-based scheduling baselines by up to 3.2% average accuracy across six math benchmarks.

## Results

On Qwen2.5-Math-7B, Re-Schedule (sigmoid) achieves an average accuracy of 48.5% across six math-reasoning benchmarks (AIME24, AIME25, AMC23, MATH-500, Minerva, OlympiadBench), outperforming the strongest baseline ACC-sigmoid (46.6%) by 1.9 points and standard GRPO (44.3%) by 4.2 points. On Qwen2.5-7B, Re-Schedule (sigmoid) reaches 44.5% average accuracy, a 3.2-point gain over ACC-sigmoid (41.3%) and 3.8 points over GRPO (40.7%). The method also generalizes to code generation, improving pass@4 by 2.4 points over GRPO on LiveCodeBench v5.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Re-Schedule (sigmoid) outperforms all seven baselines on both model backbones. Against the closest scheduling baseline ACC-sigmoid, it gains +1.9 points on Qwen2.5-Math-7B (48.5 vs. 46.6) and +3.2 points on Qwen2.5-7B (44.5 vs. 41.3). Compared to classical RLVR methods, the advantage is larger: +4.2 over GRPO, +3.9 over SimpleRL-Zoo, +2.0 over OPO, and +5.7 over Eurus-PRIME on Qwen2.5-Math-7B. Among scheduling methods, Re-Schedule also beats LPPO by +3.5 points and Seed-GRPO by +3.1 points on Qwen2.5-Math-7B, with even wider margins on Qwen2.5-7B (+4.8 over LPPO, +6.5 over Seed-GRPO). The gains are most pronounced on harder benchmarks (AIME24, AIME25, Minerva), suggesting the r-score-based curriculum is particularly effective for structurally complex reasoning problems.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the GRPO framework (Shao et al., 2024) as its RLVR backbone, but its core contribution is a new data scheduling strategy that departs from the dominant paradigm in the RLVR curriculum learning literature. Existing scheduling methods (LPPO, Seed-GRPO, ACC-based curriculum) all rely on path-based or outcome-based proxies—accuracy, gradient magnitude, semantic entropy—to estimate query difficulty, effectively treating the solution space as a flat sequence. The paper's conceptual departure is to model the solution space as a reasoning tree and define difficulty via the tree's structural complexity (the r-score), arguing that accuracy alone conflates 'hard to solve' with 'hard to learn.' This positions the work at the intersection of curriculum learning theory and the emerging tree-structured reasoning literature (TreeRPO, Wang et al. 2025e).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a widely held assumption in RLVR scheduling—that final-answer accuracy is a reasonable proxy for learning difficulty—and provides empirical evidence (the 'Stagnant vs. Potential Samples' experiment) that this assumption is systematically wrong. The practical impact is a consistent 3.2–4.0% average accuracy gain over the best scheduling baselines across six math benchmarks and two model families, with a modest code-generation generalization. Key limitations include the ~48% additional computational overhead for offline tree construction at the default k=4, d=4 setting, validation primarily on math reasoning (with only a small-scale code experiment), and the reliance on a static r-score computed from a base model. Open questions include whether the tree-structural insight transfers to open-ended or multi-step agentic tasks where the 'tree' is less well-defined, and whether dynamic r-score updates (which show only marginal gains) become more important for longer training runs or larger models.
