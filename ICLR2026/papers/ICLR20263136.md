## Problem

Recent work has suggested that inference scaling through repeated resampling against verifiers (e.g., unit tests) could allow weaker models to match stronger ones by simply generating more candidates until one passes. This paper shows that when verifiers are imperfect—having a non-zero false positive rate—this approach is fundamentally bounded: weaker models produce false positives at higher rates, so no amount of additional compute can close the gap to a sufficiently strong model's single-sample accuracy.

## Input / Output

The method takes a coding task prompt and generates multiple candidate solutions, which are checked against standard unit tests serving as the verifier. Solutions that pass the standard tests are then evaluated against extended hidden test suites (HumanEval+/MBPP+) to classify them as true or false positives. Beyond correctness, the method also measures code quality metrics (naming conventions, line length, commenting) on passing solutions, revealing that false positives are lower quality across all models and metrics.

## Method

The paper combines empirical analysis with a theoretical model. Empirically, they generate 200–1000 samples per task across models of varying capability (GPT-4o, Llama-3.1, Code Llama, Command, etc.), measure false positive rates using extended test suites, and model the expected reward as a function of sample count K under different cost-benefit ratios to identify the optimal number of resampling attempts. The theoretical model (Section C) formalizes this with a two-type task structure (easy/hard) and Bayesian belief updates: after each verifier rejection, the posterior shifts toward harder tasks where false positives are more likely, causing the false positive rate to increase with K despite sampling being memoryless. This bimodal task difficulty distribution explains why inference scaling curves bend downward, with optimal K often below 10 and sometimes zero when the cost of a false positive outweighs the benefit of a correct solution.

## Results

The paper demonstrates that weaker models produce false positives (solutions passing limited unit tests but failing comprehensive ones) at significantly higher rates than stronger models, creating a fundamental ceiling on resampling-based inference scaling. Specifically, at a cost-benefit ratio of 4, the optimal number of resampling attempts K is ≤5 for all four tested model families; at a ratio of 10, K=0 for almost all models, meaning the expected cost of a false positive outweighs any benefit. The generalization gap is stark: models whose single-sample accuracy falls below GPT-4o's Pass@1 cannot match GPT-4o's performance through any amount of resampling, regardless of compute budget. Additionally, false positive solutions exhibit measurably worse code quality (naming conventions, line length, commenting) across all models and metrics tested.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new method to compare against baselines; instead, it empirically compares model families of varying capability (GPT-4o, Llama-3.1 8B/70B, Code Llama 7B/13B, Command Light/Light, Vicuna 7B, Mistral 7B, CodeT5p 16B, CodeGen/CodeGen2) on HumanEval+ and MBPP+ to characterize how false positive rates scale with model strength. The key finding is a consistent inverse relationship: less capable models (e.g., Code Llama 7B, Command Light) produce false positives at substantially higher rates than stronger models (e.g., GPT-4o, Llama-3.1 70B), and this relationship holds linearly across model families. GPT-4o, with the highest single-sample accuracy, shows nearly flat inference scaling curves (minimal benefit from additional samples), while weaker models show steeper initial gains that quickly reverse due to accumulating false positives. The Llama-3.1 family exhibits a particularly dramatic increase in false positive rate with K, whereas Code Llama and Command families show more gradual increases, resulting in higher optimal K values for the latter at low cost-benefit ratios.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a critical analysis of the resampling-based inference scaling paradigm popularized by Brown et al. (2024) 'Large Language Monkeys,' which demonstrated predictable scaling laws for repeated sampling with oracle verifiers. It departs from that optimistic framing by showing that the key assumption—availability of a perfect verifier—fails in practice for coding (unit tests) and reasoning (LM judges), and that the resulting false positives impose a hard ceiling on achievable accuracy. The theoretical model in Section C builds on the verifier-based judge setup from Davis et al. (2024), while the code quality evaluation in Section 5 reuses the prompts and implementation from Zheng et al. (2024). Conceptually, the paper extends the well-studied inference scaling task by introducing a cost for false positives and analyzing the generalization gap between standard and extended test suites, a dimension prior resampling work largely ignored.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that weaker models cannot match a single invocation of a sufficiently strong model through any amount of resampling (when the verifier has non-zero false positive rate) directly challenges a widely-held assumption in the LLM community and has immediate practical implications for anyone deploying resampling-based inference or training reasoning models via rejection sampling. The paper also reveals that standard coding benchmarks (HumanEval, MBPP) used simultaneously as verifiers and evaluation targets produce misleading model comparisons, urging the field to treat verifier quality as a first-class concern. Open questions include how findings change with LLM-generated unit tests, whether the results generalize to non-coding domains (math, planning, agents), and what mitigation strategies (e.g., post-verification refinement, diversity-inducing sampling) can close the gap. The practical takeaway—that optimal sample counts are often below 10 and can be zero for high-stakes applications—should reshape how practitioners allocate inference compute budgets.
