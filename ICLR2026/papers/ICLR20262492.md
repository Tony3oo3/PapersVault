## Problem

RLHF provides broad coverage of response quality but suffers from low interpretability and reward hacking because human judgments lack explicit criteria, while RLVR offers precise, interpretable verification but is restricted to correctness-based tasks. No existing paradigm combines the versatility of human-driven preferences with the precision of rule-based verification, leaving a gap in reward modeling that can capture nuanced quality dimensions beyond mere correctness.

## Input / Output

The reward model takes a prompt, a response, and a user-specified principle (e.g., 'accuracy of information', 'includes inline comments') and outputs a binary Yes/No judgment. The reward score is computed as log P(Yes) − log P(No), requiring only a single generated token for the ScalarRM (<0.1 s/task) or a full reasoning chain for the GenRM (>10 s/task). Principles are not fixed at training time; users can swap in arbitrary principles at inference to customize the evaluation focus, a capability absent in Bradley-Terry models.

## Method

RLBFF reformulates reward modeling as an entailment task: given a prompt, response, and principle, the model predicts whether the response satisfies the principle. Training data is derived from HelpSteer3-Feedback by extracting binary principles from natural-language human feedback using DeepSeek V3-0324, then filtering via evidence-citation (rapidfuzz partial_ratio > 60), removing partially-fulfilled principles, and enforcing cross-annotator consensus through Qwen-3-8B embedding cosine similarity > 0.8, yielding ~33k high-precision samples across 1,414 unique principles. The ScalarRM (Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct) is trained with supervised fine-tuning to emit a single Yes/No token, while the GenRM (Qwen3-32B) is trained with GRPO to reason step-by-step before judging. For model alignment, the GenRM serves as the reward signal in a GRPO loop that trains the Qwen3-32B actor to generate responses conforming to the principles, achieving performance comparable to o3-mini and DeepSeek R1 at <5% of their inference cost.

## Results

The Flexible Principles GenRM achieves state-of-the-art performance on RM-Bench (86.2 overall) and JudgeBench (81.4 overall, #1 on leaderboard as of 24 Sep 2025), outperforming all baseline Generative RMs and Scalar RMs. The Flexible Principles ScalarRM achieves 83.6 on RM-Bench and 76.3 on JudgeBench while requiring only 1 generated token per inference (<0.1s/task), compared to >10s/task for GenRMs. On the newly introduced PrincipleBench, the ScalarRM scores 91.6 overall, surpassing all baselines. For model alignment, RLBFF-trained Qwen3-32B reaches 55.6 on Arena Hard v2 and 70.33 on WildBench, matching or exceeding o3-mini and DeepSeek R1 at <5% of their inference cost.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against RewardAnything-8B-v1 (Yu et al., 2025), the paper's Flexible Principles GenRM wins decisively on JudgeBench (81.4 vs. 62.6) and RM-Bench (86.2 vs. 83.1 corrected), while the ScalarRM also outperforms it on JudgeBench (76.3 vs. 62.6) at 100x lower inference cost. Against RM-R1 (Chen et al., 2025), the GenRM leads on JudgeBench (81.4 vs. 66.0) and RM-Bench (86.2 vs. 83.9), with the gap driven primarily by JudgeBench Coding (90.5 vs. 73.8). Against R3 (Anugraha et al., 2025), the GenRM achieves 81.4 vs. 60.9 on JudgeBench and 86.2 vs. 84.9 on RM-Bench. In the alignment experiment (Table 5), RLBFF-trained Qwen3-32B slightly trails DeepSeek R1 on Arena Hard v2 (55.6 vs. 57.4) but substantially outperforms it on WildBench (70.33 vs. 64.24) and matches it on MT-Bench (9.50 vs. 9.49), all at 24x lower inference cost. The paper also shows that baseline GenRMs suffer severe position bias on JudgeBench (e.g., RewardAnything drops from 77.1 chosen-first to 65.1 rejected-first), whereas the individual-response scoring design of RLBFF avoids this issue entirely.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions RLBFF as a bridge between RLHF (Bradley-Terry preference models trained on pairwise comparisons) and RLVR (rule-based verifiers with binary correctness signals), drawing on KTO's binary good/bad formulation but grounding it in explicit, extractable principles. It departs from prior principle-following reward models (RewardAnything, R3, LMUnit) by deriving 1,000+ fine-grained principles directly from human-written natural language feedback (HelpSteer3-Feedback) rather than from fixed rubrics or synthetic generation, and by making principles user-specifiable at inference time. The training pipeline builds on the HelpSteer3 data family and the GRPO algorithm, while the reward definition (log-prob difference of Yes/No) follows the generative verifier line of Zhang et al. (2024) and Kadavath et al. (2022) but extends it to flexible, multi-principle evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The fully open-source recipe to align Qwen3-32B to match or exceed o3-mini and DeepSeek R1 at under 5% of their inference cost is practically significant for teams constrained by compute budgets. The introduction of PrincipleBench fills a gap in reward model evaluation by testing adherence to specific quality axes beyond correctness, and the finding that Scalar RMs outperform GenRMs on this benchmark challenges the assumption that reasoning is always beneficial for reward modeling. Open questions include whether the principle-extraction pipeline generalizes to feedback formats other than HelpSteer3's paragraph-length annotations, whether the 0.8 cosine-similarity consensus threshold is optimal across domains, and whether the approach scales to even more diverse principle spaces without the high-precision/low-recall filtering that currently retains only ~1.27 principles per feedback.
