## Problem

The standard 'pretraining + fine-tuning' paradigm for open-source LLMs contains a previously unrecognized vulnerability: the model provider can embed a backdoor during post-training that enables later extraction of private downstream fine-tuning queries using only black-box access to the fine-tuned model. Unlike model distillation (which replicates output behavior) or pretraining data extraction (which targets pretraining corpora), this attack recovers the actual training queries themselves—proprietary, curated prompts that are far more sensitive and costly to regenerate.

## Input / Output

The attacker's input is a crafted extraction instruction Q(w) specifying an opening word w, sent as a standard user prompt to the downstream fine-tuned model M2' via black-box API access. The model outputs verbatim training queries from the downstream dataset D2 that begin with w, or a refusal string if no such query was seen during training. The attacker iterates over ~1,386 candidate opening words aggregated from public instruction-tuning datasets (UltraFeedback, Alpaca, Dolly), sampling N completions per word and applying a scoring heuristic (combining refusal frequency and repetition rate) to classify which opening words are valid before collecting extracted queries.

## Method

The attack operates in two stages. During backdoor training (before model release), the provider constructs SFT pairs (Q(w), x) mapping extraction instructions to actual training queries, plus negative pairs (Q(w'), R(w')) for invalid opening words, then trains via SFT or GRPO (with a longest-common-prefix reward) while mixing in the original instruction-tuning data to preserve general performance. The critical mechanism is that computing loss on input queries during fine-tuning forces the model to memorize them, and the backdoor instruction creates a persistent shortcut from assistant-mode outputs to user-mode (query-like) outputs that survives downstream fine-tuning—the model automatically shifts from reproducing D1 queries to D2 queries as the query distribution evolves. At extraction time, the attacker simply prompts M2' with Q(w) for candidate opening words, samples multiple completions, and uses a scoring function to filter valid words before collecting the extracted queries, achieving up to 76.3% verbatim query recovery in practical settings and 94.9% under ideal conditions.

## Results

On the Dolly dataset (5,000 samples), SFT-based backdoor training achieves a Mean Match Ratio of 29.8–49.2% and Max@10 of 63.5–81.3% across four models (Qwen2.5-7B/32B, Llama3.2-3B, Llama3.1-8B), compared to only 11.6–18.7% Mean for the Raw baseline without backdoor training. GRPO-based training further improves Mean Match Ratio to 33.2–43.5% and Max@10 to 68.7–77.9%. In practical settings where opening words are unknown, up to 76.3% of fine-tuning queries can be perfectly extracted; under ideal conditions (all opening words known), the Query Extraction Ratio reaches 94.9% for Qwen2.5-32B. Critically, backdoor training causes no measurable degradation in general performance (AlpacaEval 2 win rate and MMLU accuracy remain unchanged or slightly improve).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper has no external baselines, as the authors note that prior data extraction methods (e.g., PreCurious, membership inference, differentiation-based extraction) are inapplicable under their strict black-box, query-only setting. The internal comparison is between three conditions: Raw (standard fine-tuned model prompted with the extraction instruction, no backdoor), SFT-based backdoor training, and GRPO-based backdoor training. Both backdoor methods dramatically outperform Raw across all models and datasets: on Dolly, SFT roughly doubles Mean Match Ratio (e.g., 29.8 vs. 13.4 for Qwen2.5-7B) and more than doubles Max@10 (63.5 vs. 27.4), while GRPO provides a further 3–5 point improvement over SFT. On the Finance dataset, the same pattern holds (e.g., Qwen2.5-7B: SFT 40.9 vs. Raw 18.6 Mean Match Ratio). GRPO consistently edges out SFT on Match Ratio and Opening Word Identification F1, but the gap is modest (1–5 points), and SFT is simpler to implement. A clear scaling effect is observed: larger models (Qwen2.5-32B, Llama3.1-8B) yield substantially higher extraction accuracy than smaller ones (Qwen2.5-7B, Llama3.2-3B), amplifying the severity of the threat as model scale grows.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from two main lines of prior work. First, conventional backdoor attacks on LLMs inject triggers tied to fixed harmful outputs, whereas here the backdoor is designed to adapt during downstream fine-tuning, reproducing whatever queries the model newly memorizes rather than a predetermined response. Second, prior training-data extraction work (Carlini et al., Nasr et al.) targets pretraining data via membership inference or divergence attacks, while fine-tuning data extraction studies (Feng & Tramber, Liu et al., Li et al.) require white-box access, continuous text sequences, or pre-known queries/responses. This paper carves new ground by targeting downstream fine-tuning queries under strict black-box access, where the adversary observes only assistant-mode outputs and has no prior knowledge of the fine-tuning data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This reveals a fundamental and previously unrecognized vulnerability in the now-standard 'pretrain + fine-tune' paradigm: open-source model providers can covertly steal proprietary downstream fine-tuning data with only black-box access, and the attack is nearly undetectable (no general performance degradation) and extremely difficult to defend against (DP-SGD causes 67–91% utility loss, and more fine-tuning epochs actually strengthen the backdoor). The practical stakes are high because fine-tuning queries often encode expensive, proprietary, or user-specific knowledge. Key open questions include whether effective, utility-preserving defenses exist, whether the attack scales to larger models or more diverse downstream distributions, and how the ecosystem should structurally mitigate trust in open-source model provenance.
