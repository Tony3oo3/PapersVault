## Problem

Existing molecular LLMs tokenize molecules at the atom level (SMILES, SELFIES), producing structures that are frequently invalid, unsynthesizable, and incompatible with automated laboratory platforms. This creates a fundamental gap between in-silico molecular generation and physical-world synthesis, as atom-level tokenization lacks the semantic granularity needed to capture functional knowledge and guarantees of makeability.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a 'code-switched' sequence where natural language tokens (instructions, property descriptions) are interleaved with molecular building block tokens—graph substructures encoded by a GNN and projected into the LLM's embedding space. At inference, mCLM outputs a sequence of synthesis-guaranteed building blocks (drawn from a constrained vocabulary of 582 blocks in the main experiments) that can be directly assembled on robotic platforms via three predefined coupling reactions. The iterative reasoning loop takes a current molecule, evaluates its ADMET properties, and produces a single-block modification targeting the weakest property.

## Method

mCLM tokenizes molecules into functional building blocks using a synthesis-guaranteed tokenizer that only severs bonds formable by three automated reactions (amide coupling, Suzuki-Miyaura coupling, Buchwald-Hartwig coupling), ensuring every generated molecule is makeable by design. Each building block is encoded by a GNN and projected through a linear adapter into the embedding space of a Qwen2.5 decoder-only transformer backbone, where it is concatenated with natural language embeddings at entity-name positions to form a bilingual 'code-switched' sequence. Training uses a unified categorical cross-entropy loss over the combined vocabulary of text tokens and molecular block tokens, applied to ~1M instruction examples drawn from 36M synthetic and real data points covering property prediction, molecule generation, and scaffold-constrained optimization. A critical chemical reasoning loop (Algorithm 1) iteratively evaluates generated molecules against oracle ADMET predictors, selects the property requiring most improvement, and prompts mCLM to propose a minimal single-block modification, repeating until convergence or a maximum iteration count.

## Results

On 122 FDA-approved drugs composed of synthesis-guaranteed blocks, mCLM achieves a 15.0% average improvement across six ADMET properties (AMES, BBBP, CYP3A4, DILI, HIA, PGP), substantially outperforming the best baseline Gemini-2.5-Flash (6.98%). mCLM also achieves 100% molecular validity and 98.23% makeability (verified by Allchemy retrosynthesis), exceeding even the FDA drug baseline (98.11%) and far surpassing MoleculeSTM (85.39%) and FineMolTex (84.96%). In a broader test on 430 FDA drugs without synthesis guarantees, mCLM still achieves 12.87% average improvement on 5 out of 6 properties despite 796 unseen blocks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

mCLM outperforms all seven text-based baselines on average property improvement, with the largest gap over Gemini-2.5-Flash (15.0% vs. 6.98%) and GPT-4o (15.0% vs. 2.45%). Notably, GPT-5 (−0.82%), FineMolTex (−0.73%), and LDMol (−3.07%) actually degrade properties on average, while mCLM improves all six properties simultaneously. On synthesizability (Table 2), mCLM achieves 100% validity and 98.23% makeability via Allchemy retrosynthesis, compared to MoleculeSTM's 93.80% validity / 85.39% makeability and FineMolTex's 94.20% / 84.96%; the ablation mCLM(NoSynth.Tokenizer) drops to 73.65% makeability, confirming the synthesis-guaranteed tokenizer is critical. The paper also reports comparisons with DGAE and HierVAE (Appendix H), where mCLM again leads on both property scores and synthetic accessibility. In the 'fallen angels' case study, mCLM successfully repairs Evobrutinib and TNG348 through multi-step reasoning, while MoleculeSTM produces syntactically invalid molecules at intermediate steps.

## Relation to Prior Work

mCLM draws a direct analogy to protein language models, which tokenize at the amino-acid level, and transplants this principle to small molecules by tokenizing at the level of synthesis-friendly building blocks (graph substructures) rather than atoms or SMILES characters. It builds on the Burke lab's block chemistry framework (iterative Suzuki-Miyaura, Buchwald-Hartwig, and amide couplings) to define a vocabulary that is, by construction, compatible with automated robotic synthesis, and on Qwen2.5 as a pretrained LLM backbone. The 'code-switching' encoder that interleaves GNN-encoded molecular blocks with natural-language tokens departs from prior cross-modal molecule-language models (MoleculeSTM, FineMolTex, LDMol) that operate on SMILES or atom-level representations, and from VQ-based discrete molecule encoders (DGAE, HierVAE) that lack natural-language alignment and synthesis guarantees.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper closes a critical gap between in-silico molecule generation and physical synthesis: mCLM's outputs are 100% valid and 98.2% synthesizable (matching or exceeding FDA-approved drugs), while simultaneously achieving a 15% average ADMET property improvement over the original drugs—far ahead of GPT-5 (−0.82%) and other strong baselines. The 'fallen angels' case study demonstrates multi-step iterative reasoning to rescue clinically failed candidates, suggesting a path toward AI-guided drug optimization in automated laboratories. Key limitations include the small evaluation set (122 synthesis-guaranteed or 430 full FDA drugs), the restriction to only three coupling reactions and 1,000 training blocks, and the acknowledgment that drug discovery is a many-objective problem where improving one property may compromise others not yet captured.
