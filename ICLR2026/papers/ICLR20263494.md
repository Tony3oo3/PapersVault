## Problem

Conventional multi-task codecs ignore the substantial overlapping information shared between vision tasks, producing redundant and inefficient representations. While the Gray-Wyner Network offers a principled information-theoretic framework for separating common from task-specific information, it has not been made learnable for practical image coding, and the lossy setting introduces a fundamental transmit-receive rate tradeoff that has no closed-form solution. This paper bridges that gap by deriving bounds on lossy common information and proposing a learnable architecture with an optimization objective that explicitly navigates this tradeoff.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image (3-channel, or 2-channel for the synthetic ablation) as input and produces a three-channel compressed bitstream: one common channel shared by both tasks and two private channels, each coded with its own entropy model. The common representation is conditioned on the private channels' entropy models, and the synthesis transforms concatenate the common and respective private representations before feeding them into frozen pre-trained task heads (e.g., DeepLabV3+, Faster R-CNN) to produce segmentation masks, depth maps, detection boxes, or keypoint coordinates. In the synthetic and MNIST experiments, the input is a single source X shared by both tasks rather than two distinct sources.

## Method

The proposed codec uses two parallel ResNet-based analysis transforms that both process the full input, each splitting its quantized output into a private tensor and a common tensor; the two common tensors are combined element-wise via a matching operation (averaging where they agree, zeroing otherwise) with an auxiliary L2 loss encouraging agreement. Three entropy models code the channels, with the private channels' entropy models conditioned on the common representation to exploit cross-channel redundancies. The training objective is a Lagrangian relaxation of the Gray-Wyner optimization (Theorem 2) with a hyperparameter β that interpolates between optimizing the total transmit rate (β=1) and the total receive rate (β=2), with β=3/2 balancing both. Theoretically, the paper proves bounds (Theorem 1) relating Wyner's and Gács-Körner lossy common information through interaction information, and a Rademacher-complexity-based compatibility measure (Theorem 4) that explains why the shared-branch architecture outperforms separated or combined alternatives by reducing the generalization gap between representations that must be jointly decoded.

## Results

On the synthetic two-task dataset, the proposed Shared architecture achieves a transmit rate of 4.651 BPP and receive rate of 5.742 BPP at η=0.1 (β=1), outperforming the Separated (4.957/6.341) and Combined (4.904/6.134) alternatives. Across three real-world computer vision task pairs (Cityscapes segmentation+depth, COCO detection+keypoints), the proposed method achieves an average BD-rate advantage of −81.58% in transmit rate compared to independent single-task codecs. On colored MNIST, the method correctly adapts: under a Dependent PMF it concentrates information on the common channel (R₀=15.84 BPP), while under an Independent PMF it minimizes the common channel rate, matching the theoretical zero-mutual-information bound.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The Shared architecture consistently outperforms both the Separated and Combined GWN alternatives on both transmit and receive rates across all β values tested (β=1, 3/2, 2). At β=1 and η=0.1, Shared achieves 4.651 BPP transmit rate versus 4.957 (Separated) and 4.904 (Combined), while also achieving a lower receive rate of 5.742 versus 6.341 and 6.134 respectively. Against the Joint baseline (single common channel), Shared has a slightly higher transmit rate (4.651 vs 4.230) but a substantially lower receive rate (5.742 vs 8.460), reflecting the tradeoff of distributing information across private channels. Against the Independent baseline (two private channels, no common), Shared has a higher transmit rate (4.651 vs 4.136) but achieves lower distortion (0.128/0.126 vs 0.152/0.151) at the same rate budget, demonstrating the benefit of the common channel. The headline result is the average −81.58% BD-rate advantage in transmit rate over independent single-task codecs on the Cityscapes and COCO experiments, with the method remaining close to the Joint (oracle) upper bound.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the two-channel coding-for-humans-and-machines framework (Choi & Bajic, 2022) to a full three-channel Gray-Wyner Network with a common channel and two private channels, enabling explicit separation of information shared between two tasks. Theoretically, it generalizes Wyner's (1975) lossless common information bounds to the lossy setting (Theorem 1), connecting Gács-Körner and Wyner common information through interaction information, building directly on Viswanatha et al. (2014). Architecturally, it departs from prior multi-task learned codecs (Chamain et al., 2021; Feng et al., 2022) that use only common channels without private ones, and from the two-channel human-machine codecs, by introducing a 'Shared' analysis-transform design where each branch produces both a common and a private representation, justified via a Rademacher-complexity-based compatibility measure (Appendix C).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The work provides a principled, information-theoretic recipe for distributed inference: a sender can transmit a common bitstream once and then send only task-specific increments, with the transmit-receive tradeoff (controlled by β) letting practitioners tune for bandwidth vs. storage. The -81.58% average BD-rate advantage over independent single-task codecs on Cityscapes and COCO is substantial and validates the GWN formulation in practice. Key open questions include extending to three or more tasks (where the channel count scales exponentially), closing the gap between empirical and theoretical rate-distortion curves, and determining whether the GK–Wyner gap is exploitable in high-dimensional continuous sources beyond the discrete synthetic experiments.
