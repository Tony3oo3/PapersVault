## Problem

Existing layer-wise sparsity allocation methods for LLM pruning (OWL, DLP) rely on Wanda-style weight scoring combined with empirically chosen reduce functions (mean or median) to estimate layer importance, and they enforce identical sparsity across all projections within a layer. This paper asks whether a superior reduce function is truly necessary and whether non-uniform sparsity can be applied at finer granularity (block or projection level) without the catastrophic performance degradation that OWL and DLP exhibit.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262578/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes pre-trained LLM weights and a small calibration set (128 sequences of 2048 tokens sampled from C4) to compute per-layer Hessian matrices H = X^T X. It outputs a sparse LLM with non-uniform sparsity ratios assigned per layer, per block (self-attention vs. FFN), or per individual projection (q/k/v/o/gate/up/down), while preserving the overall target sparsity ratio. The sparsity allocation is pre-computed and can be cached, so the actual pruning step (e.g., SparseGPT or Wanda) runs with per-layer sparsity targets rather than a single global value.

## Method

LSA measures each layer's redundancy by computing the minimal linear reconstruction error (LRE) under the assumption that 50% of its least important weights are removed, using a greedy submatrix-selection algorithm over S = H ⊙ (W^T W) that avoids exhaustive search. Layers with lower LRE are deemed more important (more prone to containing critical weight outliers) and receive lower sparsity; the importance score is I^l = 1 − E^l / ΣE^i, rescaled by a hyperparameter β to keep per-layer sparsity within [pr−β, pr+β]. Crucially, LSA eliminates the need for weight-wise scoring and any reduce function entirely, since the LRE directly captures the cumulative error of removing multiple weights simultaneously. The method extends naturally to block-wise and projection-wise granularity by computing LRE per projection and adjusting sparsity to account for differing parameter counts, achieving competitive or superior performance compared to layer-wise allocation without the degradation seen in OWL and DLP.

## Results

At 70% unstructured sparsity on WikiText, LSA combined with SparseGPT achieves the lowest perplexity across all tested models: 17.57 on LLaMA1-7B (vs. 17.78 DLP, 18.98 OWL, 27.18 Uniform), 12.45 on LLaMA1-13B (vs. 12.87 DLP, 14.15 OWL), and 12.56 on LLaMA2-13B (vs. 13.39 DLP, 14.31 OWL). On seven zero-shot commonsense tasks, LSA achieves the best mean accuracy in nearly all settings, e.g., 44.77% for SparseGPT on LLaMA1-7B (vs. 44.26% DLP, 44.23% OWL, 42.75% Uniform). At 70% sparsity, LSA also yields >3.0× inference speedup via DeepSparse, and block-wise granularity further improves perplexity (e.g., 32.94 vs. 39.56 for layer-wise on LLaMA3-8B with SparseGPT).

## Baselines & Comparisons

LSA consistently outperforms Uniform sparsity by large margins (e.g., 17.57 vs. 27.18 perplexity for SparseGPT on LLaMA1-7B) and also beats both OWL and DLP on perplexity in most model-size/pruning-method combinations, with the largest gaps over OWL (up to ~1.7 PPL on LLaMA1-7B SparseGPT). Against DLP, LSA's advantage is smaller but consistent (e.g., 12.45 vs. 12.87 on LLaMA1-13B SparseGPT; 15.36 vs. 16.80 on LLaMA2-13B Wanda), and LSA additionally generalizes to block- and projection-wise granularity without the catastrophic degradation that DLP and OWL suffer (Table 1: DLP projection-wise jumps to 23.26 PPL vs. LSA's 19.46 on LLaMA1-7B). On zero-shot tasks, LSA achieves the best mean accuracy in nearly all settings, improving over DLP by 0.51% (SparseGPT, LLaMA1-7B) and over OWL by 1.37% (Magnitude, LLaMA2-7B). LSA also outperforms AlphaPruning and ATP on LLaMA2-13B (Table 17: 12.56 vs. 13.19/14.31 PPL for SparseGPT), integrates with ADMM-Grad to yield the best perplexity (27.97 vs. 28.26 DLP, 29.20 Uniform; Table 11), and when combined with GPTQ quantization at 4-bit, reduces WikiText perplexity from 112.65 (Uniform) to 25.25 (LSA) at 70% sparsity (Table 14). The one setting where LSA does not clearly win is Wanda on LLaMA2-7B, where DLP achieves 22.85 vs. LSA's 22.88 PPL—a negligible 0.03 difference.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the greedy linear reconstruction error minimization from structured pruning (ThiNet, Luo et al. 2017) to unstructured LLM pruning, repurposing it as a layer-importance metric rather than a weight-selection criterion. It departs from the dominant OWL/DLP paradigm, which scores individual weights via Wanda-style magnitude×activation products and then applies an empirical reduce function (mean-with-multiplier or median) to derive layer importance. LSA replaces this two-step scoring-and-reducing pipeline with a single direct error computation, and inherits the β-rescaled sparsity allocation formula from OWL. The paper also extends the allocation granularity from layer-level to block-level and projection-level, a dimension that OWL and DLP do not support without severe degradation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By eliminating the need for weight-wise scoring and hand-tuned reduce functions, LSA offers a more principled and architecture-agnostic way to allocate sparsity, which is practically useful as LLM architectures diversify (GQA, MoE, etc.). The demonstration that projection-wise allocation is feasible without catastrophic degradation opens a new axis of compression that prior methods could not exploit. However, the empirical gains over DLP are modest (typically <1 perplexity point at 70% sparsity), the β hyperparameter still requires per-sparsity tuning, and validation is concentrated on LLaMA-family models. Open questions include whether the 50% error-computation ratio generalizes across architectures and whether the reconstruction-error metric captures importance signals that diverge from weight-score-based proxies in very large or multimodal models.
