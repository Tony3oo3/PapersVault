## Problem

Grokking—the phenomenon where test accuracy stagnates near chance for arbitrarily many epochs after training error has already reached near-zero—lacks a principled account of why the delay occurs and how to control it. This paper identifies that ill-conditioned gradient spectra, where singular values span orders of magnitude, cause the stagnation by making optimization progress along slow principal directions exponentially slower than along fast ones, and asks whether a simple gradient modification can remove the plateau without sacrificing final generalization.

## Input / Output

At each optimization step, EGD consumes the raw gradient matrix G (of shape m×p, where m is the layer's fan-out and p is its fan-in) for a chosen layer (e.g., the hidden layer of a two-layer ReLU network) and produces a preconditioned gradient G̃ = (GG^T)^(-1/2)G whose singular values are all exactly 1 while its left and right singular vectors are preserved. In practice, the SVD can be replaced by a truncated randomized SVD with a tunable rank (typically 30–128) to reduce per-epoch cost from O(m²p) to O(mpr), and EGD is automatically disabled once a validation-accuracy threshold is reached to avoid unnecessary overhead.

## Method

EGD applies the per-layer transformation G̃ = (GG^T)^(-1/2)G, which via the SVD G = USV^T reduces to G̃ = UV^T—preserving all principal directions while equalizing every singular value to 1, so no direction evolves faster or slower than any other. The paper proves on a solvable toy problem that vanilla GD's plateau length scales as 1/ε (where ε is the smallest eigenvalue of the feature covariance), while EGD's convergence rate becomes independent of ε, collapsing the plateau to a few iterations. EGD is shown to be a whitened version of natural gradient descent (EGD = F^{1/2} · NGD) and inherits Grokfast's low-pass filtering inductive bias as a by-product, but without Grokfast's gradient buffer or hyperparameters. The method is hyperparameter-free, introduces no extra memory beyond the current gradient, and is optimizer-agnostic (validated with SGD, Adam, AdamW, RAdam, and RMSprop across MLPs, CNNs, and transformers).

## Results

On Modular Addition (p=97), EGD with exact SVD groks in 116 epochs versus 5,392 for vanilla SGD (46.5× speedup), and EGD with RSVD (rank 128) achieves 139 epochs (38.8×) with lower per-epoch cost. On MNIST (3-layer MLP, AdamW), EGD with RSVD (rank 4) reaches 85% validation accuracy in 500 steps (9.2 s) versus 1,800 steps (28.7 s) for Grokfast and 43,100 steps (660 s) for vanilla AdamW, while also achieving the highest final validation accuracy (91.5%). On the Sparse Parity problem (k=2, n=400), EGD with RSVD (rank 40) groks in 42 epochs (187× faster than vanilla SGD's 7,873 epochs), outperforming even EGD with exact SVD (109 epochs).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is Grokfast (Lee et al., 2024), which amplifies slow gradient components via a low-pass EMA filter. On MNIST with a 3-layer MLP and AdamW, EGD with RSVD (rank 4) reaches 85% accuracy in 500 steps and 9.2 s, outperforming Grokfast (1,800 steps, 28.7 s) by 3.6× in steps and 3.1× in wall-clock time, while also achieving higher final validation accuracy (91.5% vs. 91.0%). On Modular Multiplication (p=97) with a 2-layer transformer and Adam, Grokfast slightly edges out EGD with exact SVD (910 vs. 1,170 steps to 95%), but EGD with RSVD (rank 36) narrows the gap to 980 steps (129.3 s) versus Grokfast's 910 steps (114.0 s), a difference of only ~13%. A key practical advantage of EGD over Grokfast is that it requires zero additional memory (no gradient buffer or EMA state), whereas Grokfast must store a copy of gradients for its filtering mechanism—important for large models. On the toy arithmetic tasks (Modular Addition/Multiplication, Sparse Parity) where Grokfast is not directly compared, EGD achieves 40–190× speedups over vanilla SGD in epochs to 95% accuracy, with column normalization (a simplified, SVD-free variant) providing 12–25× speedups at minimal computational overhead.

## Relation to Prior Work

EGD is positioned as a whitened variant of natural gradient descent (EGD = F^{1/2} · NGD), sharing the Fisher information matrix structure but replacing the full inverse with a square-root preconditioner to equalize singular values rather than fully precondition. It is also framed as a simplified, memory-free alternative to Grokfast (Lee et al., 2024), inheriting the same low-pass filtering inductive bias but achieving it through spectral normalization rather than gradient buffering. At the geometric level, EGD converges to the same polar/orthogonal update structure as the Muon optimizer (Jordan et al., 2024), though derived from a spectral theory of grokking rather than empirical design. The theoretical motivation draws on singular learning theory and the observation that ill-conditioned gradient spectra produce slow modes that stall generalization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

EGD offers a hyperparameter-free, zero-extra-memory drop-in modification that provably removes the ε-dependence of plateau length in a toy model and empirically eliminates or drastically shortens grokking plateaus across modular arithmetic, sparse parity, MNIST, and CIFAR-10. The main limitation is that the theoretical analysis is confined to a 2D linear model; extending convergence guarantees to MLPs and transformers (sketched via spiked random matrix theory) remains open. Practical concerns include the per-step SVD cost (mitigated by randomized SVD, which introduces a rank hyperparameter) and the observation that EGD can be unstable with exact SVD on transformer architectures, requiring the RSVD variant for robustness. Whether gradient spectral normalization improves sample efficiency in RL post-training or large-scale LLM pretraining is an open and potentially high-impact question.
