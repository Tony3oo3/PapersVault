## Problem

Deriving a perceptual distance metric from unlabeled data alone remains a fundamental open problem: supervised approaches trained on human judgments are costly, noisy, and mathematically opaque, while information-theoretic quantities are agnostic to the geometry of the data distribution. The paper tackles the specific challenge of how a scalar probability density can induce a valid global distance function and local Riemannian metric that adapt to the anisotropic structure of the data support.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260209/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pair of 256×256 RGB images and a pre-trained denoiser (an HDiT diffusion model trained on ImageNet-1k) that provides score estimates at each SNR level γ. The IEM is computed by numerically integrating the expected squared difference of score vector fields over γ ∈ [0, Γ], where Γ is a hyperparameter controlling the finest resolution at which the metric adapts to the density. The generalized variant IEM_f additionally accepts a scalar weighting function f (identity, quadratic, or a learned causal network) that modulates the contribution of each SNR level based on the log-probability ratio magnitude.

## Method

The IEM is constructed from the pointwise I-MMSE formula and the Tweedie-Miyasawa identity, which together decompose the log-probability of a signal into denoising error vectors proportional to the score (gradient of log-density) of the Gaussian-blurred density at each SNR level. The distance between two signals is defined as the square root of the integral of the expected squared difference of their score vector fields over a range of blur levels, which the paper proves satisfies all four metric axioms. For computation, a neural denoiser approximates the MMSE estimator, and the integral is solved numerically. The second-order Taylor expansion yields a local Riemannian metric G(x,Γ) tied to the Hessian of the blurred log-density, which behaves as a locally adaptive Mahalanobis metric—most sensitive in regions of high log-density curvature and to perturbations that change probability the most. For Gaussian priors the IEM reduces exactly to the Mahalanobis distance, while for complex distributions it adapts to the global geometry of the data support.

## Results

The primary evaluation metric is Spearman's rank correlation coefficient (SRCC) between predicted distances and human mean opinion scores (MOS) on four full-reference image quality benchmarks. The unsupervised IEM (Γ=1/4), computed solely from a denoiser trained on ImageNet without any human labels, achieves SRCC values competitive with or exceeding state-of-the-art supervised methods (LPIPS, DISTS, TOPIQ) on TID2013, LIVE, and CSIQ. On the TQD texture dataset, the unsupervised IEM_sq (Γ=10⁶) outperforms all baselines except DISTS (which was explicitly designed for textures). The learned variant IEM_f_ω, which only trains a small causal network f_ω on labeled data, achieves strong SRCC across all four datasets simultaneously, demonstrating the flexibility of the generalized IEM framework.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On TID2013, LIVE, and CSIQ, the unsupervised IEM (Γ=1/4) is competitive with or outperforms all classical hand-crafted metrics (SSIM, VIF, MAD, FSIM, GMSD, NLPD) and is on par with deep supervised methods (LPIPS, DISTS, TOPIQ), despite using no human labels. On TQD (texture similarity), the standard IEM struggles as do most methods, but IEM_sq (Γ=10⁶) outperforms all baselines except DISTS, which was explicitly designed for texture images. The learned IEM_f_ω, which only trains a small causal network on labeled data (keeping the denoiser fixed), achieves strong SRCC on both natural-image and texture benchmarks simultaneously, outperforming individual unsupervised variants. In the maximum differentiation competition (Fig. 5), minimizing the IEM at a fixed PSNR=10 dB constraint produces artifact-free, perceptually similar images, whereas minimizing DISTS or other supervised metrics yields unrealistic images with noticeable artifacts even at higher PSNR (25 dB). The paper also notes that VIF, FSIM, PieAPP, and LPIPS can assign smaller distances to perceptually distinguishable image pairs, while the IEM correctly detects the difference; conversely, MAD and DISTS tend to disagree with the IEM on perceptually noticeable distortions (e.g., DISTS favors noise over blur, while IEM shows the opposite preference).

## Relation to Prior Work

The IEM is constructed from the pointwise I-MMSE formula (Venkat & Weissman, 2012) and the Tweedie-Miyasawa identity (Robbins, 1956; Miyasawa, 1961), which together decompose log-probability into denoising error vectors tied to the score of the blurred density. This departs from information geometry (Fisher information metric), which depends on an observation model rather than the prior directly, and from recent score-based Riemannian metrics (Saito & Matsubara, 2025; Azeglio & Bernardo, 2025), which are defined at a single blur level and used to compute geodesics. The IEM instead integrates over a range of SNR levels to produce a global distance from which a local Riemannian metric is derived, and it reduces to the Mahalanobis distance for Gaussian priors—a property the competing approaches do not share.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The IEM offers a principled, unsupervised route to perceptual distance: a metric derived entirely from the geometry of the data distribution (via a learned denoiser) that is competitive with supervised methods on standard image quality benchmarks. Key open questions include the computational cost of the numerical integral (limiting use as an optimization objective), the lack of a systematic principle for choosing the hyperparameter Γ and the generalizing function f, and whether the integral can be evaluated in a single forward pass. The framework also opens avenues for unsupervised clustering, information retrieval, and evaluating generative models via the mismatched IEM, but its practical adoption will depend on resolving the efficiency bottleneck.
