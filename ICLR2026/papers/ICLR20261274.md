## Problem

Existing DR-submodular maximization algorithms rely on exact or stochastically unbiased gradient oracles, yet practical gradient estimators (smoothing, zero-order sampling) inherently produce biased gradients. No systematic Lyapunov-based framework existed to characterize and handle the adverse effects of bias and noise in this setting, and the approximability of non-monotone DR-submodular maximization under convex sets with a largest element (arising in resource allocation) remained open.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a DR-submodular function accessible only through a value oracle (no gradient oracle), a convex constraint set (general, down-closed, or with a largest element), and a stochastic biased gradient oracle satisfying bounded bias and noise assumptions. The quantum variant requires a β-approximation function value oracle F_β(x) with β ≤ σ²/(288πL₁d(8⌈ln(36L₀d/σ)⌉+1)), while the classical zero-order variant uses two-point smoothing over a unit sphere. The output is a feasible solution x(T) with a guaranteed approximation ratio (e.g., 1/e for the largest-element constraint) and an explicit iteration complexity bound.

## Method

The paper extends the Lyapunov framework from Du (2022) by incorporating stochastic biased gradients g(x) = ∇F(x) + b(x) + n(x,ξ), deriving a continuous-time variational problem and a discrete sequence problem that jointly maximize the approximation ratio while minimizing accumulated bias/noise errors. For the novel convex-set-with-largest-element constraint, the framework yields a 1/e approximation for non-monotone functions, surpassing the 1/4 hardness for general convex sets. As a direct application, two zero-order gradient estimators are introduced: a quantum algorithm based on an improved Jordan algorithm with quantum state tomography achieving O(ε^{-1}) iteration complexity (matching classical first-order methods), and a classical algorithm combining Hazan-style smoothing, two-point estimation, and a momentum-based variance reduction technique (extending Mokhtari et al. 2020 to biased gradients) achieving O(ε^{-3}) iterations. The quantum acceleration is validated numerically at d=3 across three constraint settings and three benchmark problem classes.

## Results

The paper's quantum zero-order algorithm achieves O(ε^{-1}) iteration complexity across all four constraint settings (monotone/convex, non-monotone/convex, non-monotone/down-closed, non-monotone/convex with largest element), matching the best classical first-order methods while requiring only function value queries. The classical zero-order algorithm achieves O(ε^{-3}) iteration complexity, matching prior stochastic unbiased gradient results. For the novel convex-set-with-largest-element constraint, the paper establishes a ‖1−x(0)‖_∞/e ≈ 0.368 approximation ratio for non-monotone DR-submodular maximization, surpassing the 1/4 = 0.25 hardness bound for general convex constraints. Numerical experiments (d=3) confirm the quantum zero-order algorithm converges faster than the classical zero-order algorithm while achieving solution quality comparable to the classical first-order algorithm.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Bian et al. (2017b), which achieves (1−e⁻¹) with O(ε⁻¹) deterministic first-order complexity for monotone DR-submodular maximization, this paper's quantum zero-order method matches the same approximation ratio and O(ε⁻¹) complexity but requires only function value queries rather than gradient access. Compared to Zhang et al. (2022), which achieves (1−e⁻¹) with O(ε⁻²) random first-order complexity via boosting and non-oblivious functions, the quantum zero-order method improves the iteration complexity to O(ε⁻¹) at the cost of requiring quantum hardware. For non-monotone cases, Du (2022) achieves ‖1−x(0)‖_∞/4 with O(ε⁻¹) deterministic first-order complexity; this paper matches that ratio with quantum zero-order at O(ε⁻¹) and extends the analysis to stochastic biased gradients. Pedramfar et al. (2023) achieve the same 1/4 ratio with O(ε⁻³) random first-order complexity; the classical zero-order method here matches that O(ε⁻³) rate while the quantum zero-order improves to O(ε⁻¹). For down-closed convex constraints, Chen et al. (2023a) achieve 0.385 with O(ε⁻¹) deterministic first-order complexity; this paper's quantum zero-order matches both the ratio and complexity, while the novel convex-set-with-largest-element constraint yields a strictly better 1/e ≈ 0.368 ratio (vs. 1/4 for general convex) with no prior baseline to compare against.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Lyapunov framework of Du (2022) from exact gradients to stochastic biased gradients, a setting previously handled only indirectly via smoothing transformations (Hazan et al., 2016) or studied in convex optimization (Ajalloeian & Stich, 2020; Driggs et al., 2022). It builds on the Frank-Wolfe variant algorithms for DR-submodular maximization (Bian et al., 2017a,b; Chen et al., 2023a; Pedramfar et al., 2023) and introduces a new constraint class—convex sets with a largest element—that naturally arises in resource allocation. As a direct application, it combines the improved quantum Jordan algorithm (van Apeldoorn et al., 2023) with the Lyapunov framework to obtain quantum zero-order DR-submodular maximization, a combination not previously explored in this domain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The direct analysis of biased stochastic gradients removes the need for the smoothing transformation that distorts the objective function, providing a cleaner theoretical foundation for practical gradient estimators that are inherently biased (e.g., due to finite-qubit approximations or sampling). The 1/e approximation for convex sets with a largest element closes a gap relative to the 1/4 hardness for general convex sets, though the constraint's practical scope remains to be validated. The quantum zero-order result (O(ε^{-1}) vs. O(ε^{-3}) classical) is theoretically compelling but currently limited to d=3 in experiments due to simulation costs; the paper itself notes that state preparation depth depends on the specific value oracle, leaving open whether the quantum advantage persists at practical problem scales.
