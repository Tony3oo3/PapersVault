## Problem

Existing evaluation of LLM mathematical reasoning relies almost exclusively on final-answer metrics like PASS@k, leaving it unclear whether a correct answer was reached through rigorous logical inference or through exploratory search and heuristic shortcuts. Prior graph-based formalizations of CoT (deterministic subgraph matching, reversible Markov chains, linear solution trees) fail to capture the goal-directed, absorbing, and cross-branch dependency structure of real mathematical derivations. The paper asks whether CoT can be formalized as a principled stochastic process over instance-specific DAGs, and whether this yields evaluation metrics that distinguish genuine reasoning from search-based answer-finding.

## Input / Output

The method takes a mathematical problem prompt plus 4 few-shot DAG-MATH demonstrations as input. The LLM must produce a structured CoT trajectory where each step is an explicit triple (Edge justification → Parent step IDs → Node conclusion), which is then parsed into a trajectory-specific DAG. Evaluation outputs include the Perfect Reasoning Rate (PRR), AUC scores parameterized by logical-closeness threshold (0%–100%), and five graph-level statistics (#Nodes, #Edges, density, max in-degree, max out-degree). The accompanying benchmark provides 2,894 gold-standard DAGs from Omni-MATH (difficulty 1–5) constructed via a three-stage LLM prompting pipeline with SymPy and LLM-as-Judge validation.

## Method

The framework operates in two phases: Phase 1 defines a task-specific DAG G(x_in) whose nodes are intermediate derivation states partitioned into source (problem givens), intermediate, and sink (final answer) nodes, with edges encoding logical dependencies; Phase 2 models CoT generation as an autoregressive stochastic process over this DAG where each transition is conditioned only on nodes whose parents have all been visited (Eq. 1), and the process is absorbing upon reaching any sink. The key evaluation concept is logical closeness: a trajectory DAG is logically closed if every non-sink node has out-degree ≥ 1, meaning no step is left dangling or unused. Perfect reasoning requires both logical closeness and a correct sink, yielding the PRR metric and its AUC relaxation. The DAG-MATH format makes the logical structure explicit through the Edge→Parents→Node ordering per step, enabling automated DAG extraction and closeness checking. The benchmark is constructed in reverse order (Node set → minimal parent dependencies → edge justifications) to minimize error propagation, and the framework is shown to be universal via its correspondence to Lean 4 proof terms, where leaves are hypotheses, internal nodes are rule applications, and the root is the goal proposition.

## Results

Across AIME 2025, BRUMO 2025, and HMMT 2025, all five evaluated LLMs (Gemini-2.5-Flash, Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite, GPT-4.1, GPT-4.1-mini, Qwen3-30B) exhibit a statistically significant gap between PASS@1 and Perfect Reasoning Rate (PRR), with the gap reaching 36% for Gemini-2.5-Flash (52.4% PASS@1 vs. 17.0% PRR without thinking). While PASS@1 varies widely across model families, PRR remains relatively stable, indicating that search/exploration inflates raw accuracy but inherent perfect-reasoning ability is broadly comparable. AUC scores under logical closeness range from 23.8% to 25.5% across models, and even correct-answer trajectories achieve at most 80% logic-closed nodes. Enabling thinking mode boosts both metrics (e.g., Gemini-2.5-Flash: 52.4%→64.0% PASS@1, 17.0%→35.9% PRR) but the gap persists.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper introduces an evaluation framework (DAG-MATH with PRR and AUC metrics) rather than a new model, so the 'comparison' is across five LLMs on the new metrics. Gemini-2.5-Flash achieves the highest PASS@1 (52.4% without thinking, 64.0% with thinking) on AIME 2025, but its PRR (17.0%/35.9%) is only modestly above Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite (15.9%/24.5%), GPT-4.1, GPT-4.1-mini, and Qwen3-30B, whose PRR values cluster in a narrow band. The key finding is that the model with the best PASS@1 does not have a proportionally better PRR, revealing that search-based exploration inflates accuracy without improving rule-consistent derivation. Graph-level analysis further shows that incorrect trajectories across all models exhibit higher maximum out-degree (branching) than correct ones, indicating failure stems from speculative expansion rather than insufficient aggregation. The framework's AUC curves (Figure 4) confirm that all models degrade similarly under stricter logical-closeness thresholds, reinforcing that perfect reasoning ability is a shared bottleneck rather than a differentiator among current frontier LLMs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper formalizes Chain-of-Thought as a rule-based stochastic process on instance-specific directed acyclic graphs, building directly on Ye et al.'s (2025) empirical observation of latent dependency graphs in LLMs and extending it into a rigorous, evaluable framework. It departs from Dziri et al. (2023) by replacing deterministic subgraph matching with stochastic transitions over absorbing DAGs, from Kim et al. (2025) by using acyclic, goal-directed structures rather than reversible Markov chains, and from Shalev-Shwartz & Shashua (2025) by capturing shared sub-derivations and cross-branch dependencies that linear trees cannot represent. The evaluation metric (logical closeness / Perfect Reasoning Rate) is positioned as a middle ground between free-form CoT scoring (PASS@k) and full formal verification via Lean 4, which requires expert-level problem formalization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides the first principled, graph-level diagnostic for distinguishing genuine logical inference from search-based exploration in LLM outputs, addressing a gap that PASS@k metrics leave entirely unaddressed. The finding that search inflates raw accuracy while perfect-reasoning ability remains broadly comparable across model families has direct implications for how the community interprets benchmark scores and designs training objectives. Open questions include whether the DAG structure is truly intrinsic to LLM reasoning or partly an artifact of the structured prompting format, whether the framework scales to IMO-level proofs without manual lemma-level annotation, and whether the 'difficulty boundary' identified via graph statistics can be exploited for targeted training or curriculum design.
