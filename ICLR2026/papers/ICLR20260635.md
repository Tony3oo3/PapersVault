## Problem

RLVR for mathematical reasoning requires tens of thousands of annotated queries, making training prohibitively costly. Classic active learning strategies (uncertainty-based, feature-space coverage) fail to outperform random selection in this setting because they select by subjective uncertainty alone, ignoring whether that uncertainty aligns with the objective binary reward signal. This paper asks whether fewer but more informative queries—selected by the consistency between subjective and objective uncertainty—can match or exceed full-dataset RLVR performance.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pool of mathematical reasoning queries (MATH, GSM8K, DAPO-MATH-17K) and, for each query, K model-generated responses with binary correctness labels from a rule-based reward function. It produces a p%-sized subset of queries for RL training: in the offline setting, queries are pre-selected before training begins; in the online setting, the top-p% queries are dynamically re-selected within each minibatch at every training step. The selected subset is fed into standard RLVR algorithms (GRPO, RLOO, DAPO, REINFORCE++) without modification, and the final output is the trained policy model evaluated by greedy-decoding Pass@1 on held-out test sets.

## Method

The core insight is that samples where subjective uncertainty (perplexity) and objective uncertainty (binary reward) are aligned—both high or both low—produce more stable policy gradients and are more valuable for RL training than samples chosen by high subjective uncertainty alone, which generate extreme outlier gradients that destabilize training. In the offline setting, alignment is measured via the Point-Biserial Correlation Coefficient (PBC) between per-response perplexity and correctness across K samples, and the p% queries with the most negative PBC are selected. For online training, where limited sampling and dynamically shifting output distributions make PBC intractable, the paper introduces r_pb_online, computed from normalized advantages and the current model's uncertainty with a balancing hyperparameter γ. Two theorems provide theoretical grounding: the online metric is strictly negatively correlated with the offline PBC (Cov < 0), and maximizing r_pb_online is equivalent to maximizing the decrease in sample uncertainty under gradient orthogonality and bounded gradient norm assumptions. Across three model families (Qwen2.5-7B/3B, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct) and two datasets, 30% consistency-selected data matches or exceeds full-dataset RLVR performance, while classic AL baselines show no improvement over random selection.

## Results

In the online setting with a 30% sampling ratio, the proposed r_pb^online metric achieves performance matching or exceeding full-dataset RL across all three models and both datasets. For Qwen2.5-7B, it reaches 91.7% on GSM8K (vs. 91.5% Full) and 72.9% on MATH (vs. 73.2% Full), while Random selection with the same 30% budget drops to 88.1% and 68.2% respectively. In the offline setting, r_pb selection yields 90.1% on GSM8K and 72.1% on MATH for Q-7B, outperforming all classic AL baselines (PPL: 88.9/71.0, ENT: 88.4/70.3, K-center: 88.1/70.5, AskLLM: 87.8/69.8) but still trailing Full (91.5/73.2). The method generalizes across model sizes (Q-3B, L-8B-I) and RLVR algorithms (GRPO, RLOO, DAPO, REINFORCE++), and remains effective on the harder DAPO-MATH-17K dataset (33.8% vs. 27.1% Random, 34.0% Full).

## Baselines & Comparisons

All five classic AL baselines are decisively outperformed by the proposed uncertainty consistency metric in both offline and online settings. In the offline setting (30% budget), r_pb selection beats PPL/ENT (Wang & Shang, 2014) by 1.2–1.7 points on GSM8K and 1.1–1.3 points on MATH for Q-7B, and the gap widens for smaller models (e.g., Q-3B MATH: 63.3 vs. 62.9 for ENT). Feature-space methods (K-center, Sener & Savarese, 2017) and LLM-prompting methods (AskLLM, Sachdeva et al., 2024) perform at or below Random, confirming that neither embedding coverage nor LLM-judged informativeness captures the right signal for RLVR. ICL-oriented strategies (Active Prompt, Diao et al., 2023; ZS-CAL, Du et al., 2024) are the weakest, losing 3–9 points to Full on MATH, indicating that prompt-selection heuristics transfer poorly to RL training. In the online setting, the proposed r_pb^online metric is the only method that matches or exceeds Full-dataset performance (e.g., Q-7B GSM8K: 91.7 vs. 91.5 Full; Q-3B MATH: 64.0 vs. 63.8 Full), while PPL and ENT online selection still trail Full by 1.1–1.3 points.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of RLVR (GRPO, DAPO, RLOO, REINFORCE++) and active learning, but departs from both in a specific way. Classic AL strategies—uncertainty-based (PPL, Entropy), feature-space (K-center, K-means), and LLM-prompting (AskLLM, Active Prompt, CAL)—are well-studied in supervised fine-tuning, ICL, and preference alignment, yet the paper shows they all fail to beat random selection when applied to RLVR query selection. The key conceptual departure is introducing 'uncertainty consistency' (alignment between subjective model uncertainty and objective reward correctness) as the selection criterion, formalized via the Point-Biserial Correlation Coefficient borrowed from statistics and extended to an online-computable variant with theoretical guarantees linking it to the offline metric.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: matching or exceeding full-data RLVR performance with only 30% of training queries directly reduces annotation and compute costs for reasoning RL, which currently requires tens of thousands of labeled queries. The finding that classic AL fails in RLVR because it ignores objective uncertainty challenges a working assumption in the AL community and provides a diagnostic for why uncertainty-based selection is insufficient in reward-based settings. Limitations include evaluation restricted to binary-reward math tasks (MATH, GSM8K, DAPO-MATH-17K) and three model families; open questions include whether the consistency criterion generalizes to non-binary or dense rewards, non-math reasoning domains, and whether the gradient-orthogonality assumption in Theorem 2 holds at larger scales.
