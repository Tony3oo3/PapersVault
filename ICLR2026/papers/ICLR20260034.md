## Problem

DNA foundation models trained on heterogeneous multi-species genomes fail to capture influenza-specific, genome-wide co-evolutionary constraints (e.g., compensatory mutations between HA and NA, polymerase-antigen co-adaptation), while site-wise evolutionary models like beth-1 fragment biological signals by treating mutations as independent events. This paper asks whether explicitly preserving the structural integrity of influenza's eight functional gene segments during pretraining can yield a DNA language model that outperforms both general-purpose genomic LMs and task-specific evolutionary predictors for antigen forecasting.

## Input / Output

For forecasting, the model consumes three consecutive monthly HA/NA nucleotide blocks (each HA ~1,700 nt, NA ~1,400 nt) delimited by sentinel tokens (<HA>, <NA>, <sep>) and autoregressively generates the next month's or next season's HA/NA nucleotide sequences one token at a time (one nucleotide per token, no BPE or k-mer). For classification, a single HA/NA pair is encoded and the final hidden state is projected to a subtype logit. The full pretraining input is the ~13,000-nucleotide concatenation of all eight segments in fixed order (PB2→PB1→PA→HA→NP→NA→MP→NS) from a single isolate, with no alignment gaps or structural tags.

## Method

AntigenLM is a compact GPT-2-style decoder-only Transformer (6 layers, 384 hidden dim, 6 heads, 13k position range) pretrained autoregressively on 54,512 complete influenza A genomes (~600M nucleotides) with all eight segments concatenated in a fixed biological order, preserving cross-segment co-evolutionary dependencies without artificial markers. The key architectural choice is a two-stage functional-unit encoding: pretraining maintains full-genome segment integrity to learn long-range constraints, while fine-tuning introduces sentinel tokens that delimit HA and NA regions to guide attention and constrain autoregressive decoding within each antigen. A dual-head design (LM head tied to embeddings for generation; linear classification head on sentinel-position hidden states for subtype discrimination) enables multi-task fine-tuning from a shared backbone. Ablations (segment-wise, incomplete-genome, antigen-only nucleotide/protein) confirm that disrupting segment order or truncating functional units severely degrades both perplexity and amino-acid mismatch, validating that structural preservation—not just sequence volume—is the critical factor.

## Results

On next-season HA/NA forecasting (post-2022 Japan data), AntigenLM achieves the lowest amino-acid mismatch across all subtypes, reducing mismatches by over 70% relative to the WHO current system (LBI) and by 50% compared to beth-1. On next-month forecasting, AntigenLM produces mean AA mismatches of 3–4 in HA (<1% of 566 AAs) and 1–2 in NA (<0.5% of 469 AAs), with token-level perplexity of 1.26 versus 3.55–4.56 for ablation variants. For subtype classification, AntigenLM achieves a micro-averaged F1 of 99.81% (only 1 misclassification out of 530 test strains). In geographic generalization (U.S. held out), AntigenLM correctly predicted clade transitions in 90/100 test cases (all 50 H1N1, 40/50 H3N2).

## Baselines & Comparisons

AntigenLM outperforms all four baselines on the primary next-season forecasting task. Against beth-1 (the strongest site-based evolutionary model), AntigenLM reduces amino-acid mismatch by approximately 50% on H1N1-HA and H3N2-NA, with statistical significance (p < 10⁻³); beth-1 exhibits higher variance and overpredicts single-site sweeps due to its independence assumption. Against the WHO/LBI tree-based system, AntigenLM achieves over 70% lower mismatch, and its forecasts are more seasonally stable, avoiding the abrupt clade switches seen in tree-based methods. General-purpose language models HyenaDNA and ProtGPT2 perform poorly: their outputs frequently have large length deviations (>150 nt) or misplaced sentinel tokens, making valid AA mismatch computation impossible for many samples, confirming that species-agnostic pretraining fails to capture influenza-specific structural constraints. In geographic generalization (U.S. held out), AntigenLM maintains substantially lower HA mismatch than beth-1 for both H1N1 and H3N2, though NA improvements are not statistically significant; no baseline model was evaluated on this OOD setting. For cross-subtype transfer to H7N9 (only 0.3% of fine-tuning data), AntigenLM generates predictions with mismatch rates comparable to major subtypes, a capability no baseline model supports.

## Relation to Prior Work

AntigenLM builds on the GPT-2 decoder-only Transformer architecture but departs from general-purpose DNA language models (DNABERT, Nucleotide Transformer, HyenaDNA, GROVER) by restricting pretraining to a single organism (influenza A) and enforcing fixed segment order and functional-unit integrity rather than training on heterogeneous multi-species corpora. It also contrasts with site-based evolutionary predictors like beth-1, which model mutations as independent per-site events, and with protein-level models (ESM, ProtGPT2, Hie et al.) that are blind to nucleotide-level mechanisms such as synonymous substitutions and noncoding regulatory elements. The key conceptual departure is the 'functional-unit-aware pretraining' principle: concatenating all eight segments in a fixed biological order so the autoregressive objective implicitly captures cross-segment co-evolutionary constraints, rather than relying on explicit structural tags or per-segment training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, well-ablated demonstration that preserving genomic architecture during pretraining is not merely a biological nicety but a measurable source of predictive power, with implications for how DNA foundation models should be constructed for any organism with known modular structure. The >50% reduction in amino-acid mismatch over beth-1 and the ability to generalize to unseen subtypes (H7N9) and geographies (U.S.) suggest a path toward more robust, data-efficient forecasting for under-sampled pathogens. Open questions include whether the structural-preservation principle transfers to other segmented or modular genomes (e.g., coronaviruses, rotaviruses), how the model performs under real-time deployment with noisy surveillance data, and whether the compact 6-layer architecture is sufficient as sequence diversity grows or as multi-season forecasting horizons extend.
