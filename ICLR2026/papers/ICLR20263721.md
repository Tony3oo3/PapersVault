## Problem

Post-hoc explanation methods (SHAP, LIME, PDP, PFI, Occlusion, RISE, etc.) frequently produce contradictory attributions for the same model prediction, and faithfulness metrics used to adjudicate between them are themselves inconsistent. This paper identifies the root cause of these disagreements as between-group feature interactions and shows that eliminating them yields a single explanation that is simultaneously optimal under all faithfulness metrics.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263721/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a black-box or white-box model, a set of input features (tabular columns or image pixels), a baseline distribution (data distribution for tabular, a single baseline image for pixels), and either the full data distribution (tabular) or a single image (pixels). It outputs a partition of features into disjoint groups, group-level importance attributions, and for images, a saliency map composed of path-connected patches. For tabular data the partition is computed once and reused for all data points; for images it must be recomputed per image since the model is only locally groupwise additive. The algorithm starts from a granular partition (individual features or 14×14 pixel patches) and iteratively fuses groups, with the constraint that image patches remain path-connected.

## Method

The paper unifies ArchAttribute, Occlusion, LIME, SHAP, and RISE through the Marginal Decomposition, proving that all methods agree on within-group main effects but disagree only on how to redistribute between-group interaction terms (f_{u,B} with |P(u)|≥2) among the involved groups. A key theorem shows that if the model is groupwise additive w.r.t. a partition, all explanation methods agree and all (un)faithfulness metrics are simultaneously minimized to zero. The AGREED algorithm exploits this by minimizing the L2 disagreement between PDP/ArchAttribute and PFI/Occlusion (the cheapest explainers to compute) via greedy group fusion: it starts from a granular partition, selects the group with the highest disagreement potential, computes its pairwise interactions with candidate groups using precomputed model-evaluation tensors, fuses it with the group of maximal interaction, and repeats until the objective falls below a threshold. For images, fusion is restricted to spatially adjacent patches to maintain path-connectivity, and the algorithm runs per-image with a null baseline. The overall complexity is O(d²N²) where N is the number of Monte Carlo samples used to estimate expectations.

## Results

On synthetic tabular data with known ground-truth partitions, AGREED recovers the exact optimal partition across all tested dimensions and Monte Carlo sample sizes, matching PAIRWISE and RECURSIVE (while IGREEDY fails to converge). On real tabular datasets (Marketing, Default-Credit, SPAM, NOMAO), AGREED reduces L2 disagreements between all explainer pairings and drives Sensitivity-1, INFD, and SWF unfaithfulness scores collectively toward zero as features are grouped, with runtimes of 10–100 seconds. On MiniImageNet (VGG16, ResNet18, ConvNext), AGREED achieves the most competitive disagreement-vs-patch-size tradeoff, and is the only method that consistently reduces disagreements and unfaithfulness on ConvNext, where QUICKSHIFT and SQUARE patches stagnate or worsen. On synthetic images, AGREED finds the optimal 4-patch partition and scales significantly better than PAIRWISE as image size W increases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against PAIRWISE (Tsang et al., 2020), AGREED matches on synthetic tabular data (both recover ground truth) but scales substantially better on images: PAIRWISE requires O(W⁴) pairwise interaction evaluations while AGREED exploits locality, and PAIRWISE was excluded from MiniImageNet experiments because its patches are not guaranteed path-connected. Against RECURSIVE (Sivill & Flach, 2023), RECURSIVE scales best with d and N on tabular data (Figure 3), but it is restricted to tabular settings and was not evaluated on images. Against IGREEDY (Xu et al., 2024), AGREED strictly wins on synthetic tabular data: IGREEDY fails to recover the ground-truth partition because its termination criterion assumes monotonic loss decrease, which only holds under feature independence. On MiniImageNet, AGREED outperforms both QUICKSHIFT segmentation (Ribeiro et al., 2016) and fixed W×W SQUARE patches (Zeiler & Fergus, 2014) across all three CNNs: it achieves lower L2 disagreement and lower INFD unfaithfulness at smaller average patch sizes. The advantage is most pronounced on ConvNext, where AGREED is the only method that consistently reduces disagreements as patches grow, while the other two baselines see disagreements stagnate or increase due to stronger feature interactions in that architecture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Functional Decomposition unification framework of Fumagalli et al. (2025) and Deng et al. (2024) from individual features to feature groups, identifying between-group interactions as the structural root cause of the XAI Disagreement Problem. It departs from Laberge et al. (2024), who reduced disagreement by restricting the baseline distribution to rule-based regions (tabular-only), by instead searching for an adaptive feature partition that makes the model approximately groupwise additive — a strategy that generalizes to images. The AGREED algorithm builds on the greedy fusion paradigm of IGREEDY (Xu et al., 2024) and PAIRWISE (Tsang et al., 2020) but uses L2 disagreement between PDP and PFI as its loss, which the paper proves satisfies desirable monotonicity properties that other disagreement metrics lack.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The clean theoretical result that eliminating between-group interactions simultaneously minimizes all faithfulness metrics and equalizes all explanation methods provides a principled target for XAI practitioners who are currently stuck choosing among inconsistent methods. However, practical limitations temper the impact: for images, AGREED must be rerun per image (no global partition exists for CNNs), and for tabular data, joint attributions of groups with more than three features remain difficult to interpret. Open questions include extending to overlapping patches (via concept-based explanations), other modalities like time-series and text, and automatic visualization of high-dimensional group effects.
