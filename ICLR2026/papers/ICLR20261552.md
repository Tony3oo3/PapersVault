## Problem

While overparameterized networks are known to memorize random labels, there is no practical tool to measure memorization at different network depths during normal training or to explicitly regularize it without disrupting the main task. Existing approaches require training entire networks on random labels, which neither reveals how memorization evolves across layers nor allows controlled study of its interaction with generalization. The paper further challenges the classical assumption that memorization is always detrimental, showing it can be beneficial for undersampled data.

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard image classification setups (ImageNet-1k at 224×224 with ViT-B/32; CIFAR-100 at 32×32 with WideResNet-16-4) and augments each training sample with a randomly assigned auxiliary label drawn from a pool of n labels (100,000 for ImageNet, 10,000 for CIFAR-100), fixed across all epochs. The network outputs both the standard class prediction and a random label prediction from the RLP-head; the resulting random label accuracy serves as the layer-wise memorization metric, while the RLP-regularizer produces feature representations with reduced sample-specific information. Only the RLP-head receives gradients from the random-label objective, leaving the classification head unaffected.

## Method

The core contribution is the Random Label Prediction Head (RLP-head): a single fully-connected layer with softmax, attached at arbitrary network depths (default: after the penultimate layer) in parallel with the standard classification head, trained with a cross-entropy loss on fixed random labels. Its accuracy is interpreted as an empirical proxy for Rademacher complexity, enabling layer-wise measurement of memorization during ordinary supervised training. For regularization, the paper introduces an inverted cross-entropy loss that penalizes confident random-label predictions, with gradients restricted to the feature extractor only—creating an adversarial setup where the RLP-head attempts to fit random labels while the feature extractor is pushed to produce less sample-specific representations. By attaching RLP-heads at every transformer block, the method reveals that memorization is concentrated in deeper, more abstract layers, and that regularizing only the final layer shifts both memorization and class-abstraction into earlier layers. Experiments show that suppressing memorization improves generalization on well-sampled datasets (ImageNet) but degrades it on undersampled ones (CIFAR-100), with the effect driven by sampling density rather than architecture.

## Results

On ImageNet with ViT-B/32, RLP-regularization (λ=10⁴) reduces random label accuracy from ~70% to near chance level while improving test accuracy by 1.5% (to 68.5%) and narrowing the train-test gap. On CIFAR-100 with WideResNet-16-4, the same regularizer suppresses memorization effectively but degrades test accuracy, revealing that memorization can be beneficial for undersampled datasets. A multi-head RLP variant (10 heads, λ=10⁴) pushes test accuracy further to 69.2% on ImageNet. Under adversarial attacks, RLP-regularization consistently improves robustness (e.g., PGD accuracy rises from 16.6% to 18.0%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to the Feldman & Zhang (2020) memorization score, the proposed random label accuracy shows very low correlation (Pearson's r = 0.08) on CIFAR-100, with distinctly different distributions (bimodal vs. approximately Gaussian). The authors argue this lack of correlation is expected: the Feldman score measures whether a model can generalize to a sample without seeing it (i.e., it conflates memorization with generalization), whereas random label accuracy isolates memorization independent of any label-sample relationship. Compared to the full-network random-label training paradigm of Zhang et al. (2021), the RLP-head achieves a strong correlation with full-network random-label training accuracy across varying WideResNet widths (Appendix A.17), validating it as a lightweight proxy that avoids retraining the entire network. The paper also demonstrates that standard regularizers (dropout, weight decay, label smoothing) all reduce random label accuracy, confirming the metric tracks model complexity, but none of them allow layer-selective control over memorization as the RLP-regularizer does.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the random-label training paradigm of Zhang et al. (2021) — which demonstrated that overparameterized networks can fit arbitrary labels — into a non-intrusive, layer-wise probe by attaching an auxiliary Random Label Prediction Head (RLP-head) in parallel with the standard classifier, rather than retraining the full network. The RLP-head's accuracy is interpreted as an empirical approximation of Rademacher complexity from PAC-learning theory (Mohri et al., 2012), bridging a gap between intractable theoretical complexity measures and practical deep learning diagnostics. The adversarial-style regularizer that penalizes RLP-head performance draws technical inspiration from bias-suppression methods in fair AI (Zhang et al., 2018a), while the layer-wise memorization analysis aligns with the localization perspective of Maini et al. (2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding that reducing memorization can either improve or impair generalization — depending on whether the dataset is sufficiently sampled — directly challenges the long-standing equivalence between overfitting and memorization, with implications for how practitioners should interpret and mitigate overfitting in data-scarce regimes. The RLP-head provides a lightweight, architecture-agnostic tool for diagnosing where and when memorization occurs during training, and the derived regularizer offers a new knob for explicitly controlling sample-specific feature learning. Open questions include whether the memorization-shift-to-earlier-layers phenomenon observed under RLP-regularization is truly beneficial or merely relocates the problem, and whether the dataset-sampling hypothesis generalizes beyond the two image classification settings studied. The partial confound from CIFAR-100 train-test duplicates (Appendix A.11) suggests the undersampled-dataset mechanism needs further isolation in cleaner experimental settings.
