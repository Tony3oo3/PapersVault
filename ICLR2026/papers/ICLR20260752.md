## Problem

Existing rs-fMRI foundation models rely on transformer backbones that are compute-intensive, require tokenization/patching ill-suited to continuous brain dynamics, and struggle to scale to long acquisition sequences (e.g., whole-night fMRI) or fine-tune on small clinical cohorts. This paper asks whether a dynamical-systems-based architecture grounded in multi-resolution analysis can match or exceed transformer-based approaches while being substantially more compute- and sample-efficient.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260752/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Input is parcellated rs-fMRI BOLD time series of shape T×450 (T=1200 for HCP, T=490 for UK Biobank), produced by a NIfTI→CIFTI→Schaefer/Tian parcellation pipeline with robust median/IQR normalization. During pre-training the model outputs a reconstructed (or masked-reconstructed) signal of the same shape; during fine-tuning, pooled latent features feed a lightweight MLP head that outputs class logits or a scalar regression value (age, trait score, diagnosis). The latent dynamics evolve in a compressed bottleneck of dimension 5, making the model tractable on a single A100 40 GB GPU.

## Method

MnemoDyn models latent brain dynamics as a continuous-time ODE/CDE, dz(t)/dt = F(z(t), u(t); θ), whose integral kernel K is parameterized as a linear combination of separable wavelet bases (db2) at multiple resolution levels, yielding a multi-resolution pseudo-differential operator. The wavelet-domain parameterization produces a block-diagonal sparse structure that is implemented via parallel convolution filters, avoiding expensive numerical solvers. A CP tensor decomposition further reduces parameter count across the high-dimensional spatial modes. The architecture stacks L=4 operator blocks at different wavelet scales with residual connections, and is pre-trained self-supervised via masked autoencoding (70% spatio-temporal masking) or denoising, then fine-tuned with frozen backbone plus a task-specific MLP adapter head.

## Results

On ADNI, MnemoDyn-Mask achieves 96.12% accuracy (95.98% F1) for NC/MCI classification and 95.27% accuracy (95.61% F1) for amyloid status prediction, substantially outperforming the strongest baseline Brain-JEPA (76.84% and 71.00% accuracy, respectively). On UK-Biobank, MnemoDyn-Mask achieves 0.44 MSE for age prediction and 88.40% accuracy for sex classification, matching or exceeding Brain-JEPA. On HCP-Aging, MnemoDyn-Mask reduces age prediction MSE to 0.90 (vs. 1.02 for Brain-JEPA), improves sex classification to 83.10% (vs. 79.17%), and nearly halves the Flanker task MSE (0.60 vs. 1.28). Reconstruction quality on held-out data reaches R² = 0.985 (UK-Biobank) and R² = 0.987 (HCP).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MnemoDyn decisively outperforms all five baselines on the ADNI tasks: NC/MCI accuracy jumps from 76.84% (Brain-JEPA) to 96.12% (+19.3 points), and amyloid prediction accuracy rises from 71.00% to 95.27% (+24.3 points). On UK-Biobank age regression, MnemoDyn-Mask achieves 0.44 MSE versus 0.50 for Brain-JEPA and 0.61 for BrainLM, while sex classification is essentially tied with Brain-JEPA (88.40% vs. 88.17% ACC). On HCP-Aging, MnemoDyn-Mask improves age MSE from 1.02 to 0.90, sex accuracy from 79.17% to 83.10%, and Flanker MSE from 1.28 to 0.60, all surpassing Brain-JEPA. The older standalone baselines (BrainNetCNN, BrainGNN, BNT) are consistently 10–35 points below MnemoDyn on classification tasks. The two foundation-model baselines (BrainLM, Brain-JEPA) are the closest competitors but are still outperformed by 15–25 points on ADNI and by 3–4 points on HCP-Aging, with the gap largest on the more challenging clinical classification tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

MnemoDyn departs from the dominant transformer-based paradigm for rs-fMRI foundation models (Brain-LM, Brain-JEPA) by framing brain signal modeling as learning a continuous-time dynamical operator rather than autoregressive attention over tokenized patches. It synthesizes ideas from Neural ODEs (Chen et al., 2018), Neural CDEs (Kidger et al., 2020), structured state-space models (Gu et al., 2021), and the operator-learning literature (Kovachki et al., 2023), but the key architectural novelty is the wavelet-domain pseudo-differential operator parameterization with CP tensor low-rank decomposition, which is motivated by multi-scale neuroscience (Friston, Vidaurre, D'Angelo & Jirsa) and the sparsity properties of wavelet-pseudo-differential operator interactions (Bey, 1991). The paper explicitly positions itself against attention mechanisms, arguing that tokenization/patching is a mismatch for continuous brain processes and that operator-based models offer better compute efficiency and sample efficiency for the small-cohort studies common in clinical neuroscience.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for the neuroimaging community: a 92M-parameter model trainable on a single A100 40GB GPU in ~3 hours (vs. 4 GPUs for transformer baselines) lowers the barrier to foundation-model-level rs-fMRI analysis, and the demonstrated ability to fine-tune effectively on small cohorts (~1000 subjects from HCP) addresses a real bottleneck in clinical and translational neuroscience. Open questions include whether the multi-scale operator structure has genuine neurophysiological correspondence (the authors explicitly disclaim this), whether the framework extends to voxel-level or multimodal inputs (EEG, PET), and whether longitudinal study designs can be accommodated. The very large accuracy gap on ADNI NC/MCI (96% vs. ~77%) is striking but rests on a small 189-participant cohort, and cross-modality generalization remains untested.
