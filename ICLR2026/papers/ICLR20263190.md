## Problem

Despite widespread use of generative models for data augmentation, it remains unclear which specific distributional properties of synthetic data actually improve classifier generalization. Existing approaches rely on heuristics such as CLIP-similarity filtering or center matching, but lack a principled characterization of what makes synthetic data useful. This paper asks: given a pool of synthetic samples, which selection criterion minimizes test error?

## Input / Output

The method takes a small set of real training images with class labels (e.g., 200 per class on CIFAR-10), a large pool of synthetic images generated per-class by a generative model (e.g., 6K–10K images from StyleGAN2-Ada or StableDiffusion), and a pretrained feature extractor (CLIP ViT-B or DINO) to map images into a 512-dimensional embedding space. It outputs a selected subset of synthetic images (e.g., 800 per class) whose CLIP-feature sample covariance best matches that of the real training set, computed in a 32-dimensional PCA subspace for efficiency. The selected subset is then unioned with the real data to train a downstream classifier (ResNet, ViT, or Swin-T).

## Method

The core theoretical contribution is a high-dimensional analysis of min-norm least squares regression trained jointly on real and synthetic data, showing via random matrix theory and rank-2 perturbation arguments that the asymptotic test error depends only on the covariance matrices Σ_t and Σ_s, not on the mean vectors μ_t and μ_s. This holds in both under-parameterized (Theorem 4.1) and over-parameterized (Theorem 4.4) regimes, and implies that covariance matching (Σ_s ∝ Σ_t) is optimal under trace normalization (Theorems 4.3, 4.5). In contrast, training on synthetic data alone yields a test error that does depend on both means and covariances (Proposition 4.2). Practically, the covariance matching criterion is implemented as a greedy algorithm that iteratively adds the synthetic sample minimizing the Frobenius norm distance between the running sample covariance of selected CLIP features and the real training covariance, requiring no labels for the synthetic pool and no access to the test distribution.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with StyleGAN2-Ada synthetic data, covariance matching achieves 54.00% (Scratch), 59.77% (Distillation), and 69.20% (Pretrained) top-1 accuracy, outperforming the best baseline DS3 (52.83%, 58.32%, 68.21%) across all three training paradigms. On ImageNet-100, covariance matching reaches 57.52% with truncated StyleGAN models, matching DS3 (57.47%). On the specialized RxRx1 microscopy dataset, covariance matching achieves 90.00% accuracy, the highest among all methods tested. The method consistently narrows the gap to the real-data upper bound (e.g., 61.08% Scratch vs. 54.00% for covariance matching vs. 44.36% without synthetic data).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Covariance matching outperforms DS3 (Hulkund et al., 2025), the strongest baseline, by 1.17% (Scratch), 1.45% (Distillation), and 0.99% (Pretrained) on CIFAR-10 with StyleGAN2-Ada data, and by 0.05% on ImageNet-100 with truncated models. Against He et al. (2023) Center matching, the gap is larger: 3.96% (Scratch), 5.94% (Distillation), and 2.19% (Pretrained) on CIFAR-10. All Lin et al. (2023) variants (Center sampling, K-means, Text matching, Text sampling) are consistently outperformed by 2–4% across paradigms. With T2I generative models (SANA-1.5, PixArt-α, StableDiffusion1.4), covariance matching ties or slightly trails DS3 in the Distillation setting (59.17% vs. 59.43%) but leads in Scratch (54.45% vs. 54.15%). On RxRx1, covariance matching (90.00%) edges out DS3 (89.67%) and K-means (89.00%), confirming generality beyond natural images.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the high-dimensional regression analysis of Yang et al. (2025) and Song et al. (2024) to non-zero-mean data, a critical departure since centering test data would require knowing its label. The key theoretical insight—that mean shift is irrelevant to test error when training on both real and synthetic data, unlike the synthetic-only setting of Hastie et al. (2022)—is new. On the practical side, it departs from the dominant CLIP-based filtering paradigm (center matching, text matching, clustering) by proposing covariance matching as a theoretically grounded selection criterion, building on the random matrix theory toolkit for characterizing min-norm least squares estimators.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a simple, principled criterion (match the covariance of synthetic data to real data) that consistently outperforms or matches recent baselines across GANs, diffusion models, ResNets, transformers, and multiple datasets, giving practitioners a theoretically justified alternative to heuristic CLIP-similarity filtering. Key limitations include the single-class analysis that ignores inter-class interactions, the linear-model theoretical foundation (with deep network results being purely empirical), and the over-parameterized optimality result being restricted to isotropic training covariance. Open questions include extending to multi-class Gaussian mixtures, analyzing model shift under covariance shift (a potential cause of model collapse), and studying other quantities like calibration and fairness within this framework.
