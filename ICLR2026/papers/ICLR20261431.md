## Problem

While adversarial training is the most reliable empirical defense against adversarial attacks, few studies have explored robustness from the perspective of the model's output probability distribution. Nasty training (NT) was originally designed for intellectual property protection and its probability sparsity property was linked only to hindering knowledge distillation, leaving its potential to enhance adversarial robustness unexplored and unexplained.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261431/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes standard RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 224×224 for ImageNet-100) and produces class labels, operating within a standard supervised classification pipeline. During training, it additionally consumes the outputs of an auxiliary adversary model (a vanilla-trained network of the same or different architecture) to compute the nasty regularization term; this adversary model is discarded at inference, so the deployed model has the same input/output interface as any standard classifier.

## Method

NAT extends standard adversarial training by adding a nasty regularization term: the target model minimizes cross-entropy on both clean and PGD-generated adversarial examples while simultaneously maximizing the KL-divergence between its softmax outputs and those of a frozen auxiliary adversary model. A Taylor expansion of the nasty loss reveals it acts as a high-order power optimization that, combined with the cross-entropy's 'single peak' pressure, drives the target model to redistribute probability mass from a uniform spread over non-target classes onto a small subset of semantically related classes, yielding a sparse output distribution. The paper further shows via spatial metric analysis that this sparsity translates into larger point-to-decision-boundary distances and greater shortest distances between classification hyperplanes, meaning adversaries must expend a larger perturbation budget to induce misclassification. The approach is architecture-agnostic (validated on ResNet, WideResNet, DenseNet, and ViT) and introduces only ~10% additional training time over standard AT.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with WRN-34-10, NAT (best model) achieves 89.15% clean accuracy and 65.88% average robustness across PGD-10/20/50, C&W, and AutoAttack, setting a new state-of-the-art that surpasses the previous best (LAS-AWP, 58.80% avg) by over 7 points. On CIFAR-100, NAT achieves 62.87% clean and 39.22% average robustness, remaining competitive with LAS-AWP (39.86% avg). On ImageNet-100 with ViT-Small, NAT improves average robustness from 54.38% (PGD-AT) to 58.78%, a gain of over 4 points. NAT also outperforms all baselines under adaptive attacks (e.g., 59.22% vs 54.64% avg on CIFAR-10 with WRN-34-10).

## Baselines & Comparisons

On CIFAR-10 (WRN-34-10), NAT achieves the best average robustness (65.88%) among all compared methods, outperforming LAS-AWP (58.80%), AWP (61.74%), LBGAT (59.99%), TRADES (60.28%), and PGD-AT (59.46%) by 4–10 points. NAT also leads on individual attacks PGD-10 (63.69%), PGD-20 (62.34%), PGD-50 (62.05%), and C&W (65.10%), though it trails LAS-AWP on AutoAttack (52.95% vs 55.52%). On CIFAR-100, LAS-AWP retains a slight edge in average robustness (39.86% vs 39.22%) and clean accuracy (64.89% vs 62.87%), but NAT matches or exceeds it on PGD-50 (36.22% vs 36.13%) and AutoAttack (30.85% vs 30.77%). Under adaptive attacks (where the attacker optimizes NAT's full loss), NAT still significantly outperforms PGD-AT (59.22% vs 54.64% avg on CIFAR-10, 32.33% vs 27.99% on CIFAR-100). Against explicit regularizers (LASSO, entropy minimization, negative norm regularization, mixup), NAT achieves the best average (63.85% on ResNet-18 CIFAR-10), confirming that probability sparsity via nasty regularization is not reducible to simpler output constraints.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes Nasty Training (NT), originally designed for intellectual property protection against knowledge distillation (Ma et al., 2021; 2022), as a regularization mechanism within the adversarial training (AT) framework of Madry et al. (2017). The key conceptual bridge is identifying that the probability sparsity induced by NT's nasty regularization—which was previously understood only as a distillation-blocking mechanism—also produces spatial metric benefits (larger decision-boundary margins and greater inter-class separation) that directly enhance adversarial robustness. Unlike prior AT variants that modify attack generation (LAS-AT, AGAIN) or loss weighting (TRADES, AWP), NAT introduces an auxiliary adversary model whose output divergence drives the target model toward sparse, semantically structured probability distributions, providing an interpretable regularizer orthogonal to the adversarial min-max objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NAT offers a simple, low-overhead (~10-15% extra training time) drop-in regularizer for adversarial training that achieves SOTA robustness on CIFAR-10/100 and ImageNet-100, with the added benefit of an interpretable spatial-metric explanation for why probability sparsity aids robustness. The finding that NAT partially resists distillation by student models (Table 10) opens an interesting but underexplored direction for protecting robust model IP. Key limitations include the absence of full ImageNet-1k evaluation, the reliance on a pre-trained adversary model (adding a training dependency), and the fact that the undistillable property is only partial—students still acquire considerable robustness. Whether the probability sparsity mechanism generalizes beyond classification to other tasks remains an open question, though preliminary results on ReID and object detection are encouraging.
