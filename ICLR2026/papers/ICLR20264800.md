## Problem

While RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR) and supervised fine-tuning (SFT) are the two dominant post-training methods for reasoning LLMs, their distinct roles in shaping the reasoning process remain largely unexplored beyond accuracy-based metrics like Pass@k. Prior work showed that RL models' Pass@k converges with base models at large k, but the underlying mechanism—how each training method restructures the space of reasoning paths—was not characterized. This paper asks: how do RL and SFT fundamentally reshape reasoning trajectories and step-level graph structures?

## Input / Output

The analysis takes M=256 sampled reasoning trajectories per problem (temperature 0.6, top_p 0.95, max 16000 tokens) from Base, RL, SFT, and SFT+RL models (1.5B, 7B, 14B) on AIME24, AIME25, AMC23, and HumanEval. At the trajectory level, outputs are compared via chrF character n-gram similarity and clustered with UPGMA (threshold 60) to count unique correct/incorrect paths. At the step level, trajectories are segmented into sentences, embedded with BGE-large-en-v1.5 (d=1024), and jointly K-means clustered (K=2000) across all four models into a shared node space, from which directed reasoning graphs are constructed. Outputs include unique trajectory counts, exponential decay rates β for visitation frequency/degree/betweenness centrality, eight global topology metrics, and 4-node graphlet proportions.

## Method

The paper introduces a two-granularity analysis framework. At the trajectory level, pairwise chrF similarities are computed over sampled outputs, UPGMA hierarchical clustering identifies unique reasoning paths, and the number of correct vs. incorrect clusters is compared across model variants, revealing that RL compresses incorrect trajectories while SFT expands correct ones. At the step level, sentences from all models are embedded into a shared BGE-large-en-v1.5 space and K-means clustered (K=2000) to define graph nodes, with directed edges encoding consecutive node transitions in each trajectory. The key quantitative finding is that rank plots of visitation frequency, degree, and betweenness centrality follow exponential decay laws, and the decay rate β increases ~2.5× under RL (concentrating hub functionality into fewer steps) while decreasing to ~1/3 under SFT (homogenizing functionality across many steps). Global topology analysis further shows RL transforms community-structured base graphs into hub-centralized structures (high Freeman centralization, low modularity), while SFT produces globally connected graphs with high algebraic connectivity and global efficiency. Both RL and SFT introduce local cyclic structures (increased G7/G8 graphlet proportions), but global topology differences explain the performance gaps between model variants.

## Results

At the trajectory level, RL dramatically reduces the number of unique incorrect trajectories (whether starting from Base or SFT models), while SFT increases the number of unique correct trajectories; the SFT+RL combination achieves the best Pass@1 by first expanding correct paths then compressing incorrect ones. At the step level, RL steepens the exponential decay rate β of node visitation frequency, degree, and betweenness centrality by approximately 2.5×, concentrating reasoning functionality into a small subset of hub nodes, whereas SFT flattens the decay rate to roughly one-third, distributing functionality homogeneously across many steps. Both RL and SFT convert local acyclic graphlet structures (G3, G4) into cyclic ones (G7, G8), but they produce distinct global topologies: RL yields high Freeman centralization and low assortativity (hub-dominated), while SFT yields low modularity, high global efficiency, and high algebraic connectivity (globally connected).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four model families—Base (Qwen2.5-Math 1.5B/7B, Qwen2.5-14B, Llama-3.1-8B), RL (Qwen2.5-Math-Oat-Zero, Qwen-2.5-14B-SimpleRL-Zoo, Llama-3.1-8B-SimpleRL-Zoo), SFT (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen 1.5B/7B/14B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B), and SFT+RL (Nemotron-Research-Reasoning-Qwen-1.5B, AceReason-Nemotron-7B/14B)—across AIME24, AIME25, AMC23, and HumanEval. RL models consistently reduce the number of unique incorrect trajectories relative to their Base counterparts and increase the exponential decay rate β, producing hub-centralized reasoning graphs with high Freeman centralization and low assortativity. SFT models (DeepSeek-R1-Distill) increase unique correct trajectories and decrease β, yielding globally connected graphs with low modularity and high algebraic connectivity, but they preserve incorrect trajectories with non-negligible probability mass, explaining why SFT alone does not guarantee Pass@1 gains. The SFT+RL models (AceReason-Nemotron, Nemotron-Research-Reasoning) combine both effects and achieve the highest Pass@1, confirming the two-stage recipe. Notably, the Base model's Pass@k eventually surpasses the RL model's at large k, consistent with RL's compression of the trajectory space. Results are consistent across model sizes (1.5B, 7B, 14B), model families (Qwen, Llama), and domains (math, code).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the reasoning graph analysis framework of Wang et al. (2024) and Minegishi et al. (2025), which constructed graphs from internal token representations for a single model, by embedding sentences into a shared external space (BGE-large-en-v1.5) to enable direct cross-model comparison. It also introduces a trajectory-level analysis (clustering full reasoning outputs via chrF + UPGMA) that is absent from prior step-level-only work. The paper positions itself against the Pass@k literature (Yue et al. 2025; Wu et al. 2025; Dang et al. 2025) that showed RL does not add capabilities beyond the base model, arguing that accuracy-based metrics alone cannot explain *how* RL and SFT differentially reshape the reasoning process.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The complementary squeeze/expand characterization provides a mechanistic explanation for why the SFT-then-RL two-stage recipe is the current best practice, and suggests practical directions such as applying RL only to functional (hub) steps for more efficient learning, or incorporating graph metrics as process rewards. A notable open question is whether exploration-bonus RL methods merely prevent collapse from over-squeezing or genuinely expand the graph in an SFT-like manner. Limitations include focus on verifiable domains (math, code) and lack of control for training dataset differences between RL and SFT, leaving distribution-shift effects unexplored.
