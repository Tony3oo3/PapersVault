## Problem

U.S. discrimination law can impose liability on firms that fail to adopt a less discriminatory alternative (LDA), and recent scholarship extends this to algorithmic decision-making, potentially obligating firms to search for models with lower disparate impact. Model multiplicity makes such searches plausible—retraining with different random seeds can yield comparably accurate models with materially different disparate impacts—but firms cannot retrain indefinitely. The central unanswered question is: when is the search sufficient to demonstrate good faith, and how can a firm statistically certify that further retraining is unlikely to yield meaningful improvements?

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes a fixed labeled dataset D, a pre-specified randomized training procedure A (which may randomize over algorithms, hyperparameters, or data splits), a cost-benefit threshold γ = c/b, a failure probability δ, and a loss function ℓ bounded in [0,1] (e.g., selection-rate disparity). It sequentially trains models one at a time, evaluating each on a held-out test split, and outputs a stopping time τ, the best model h_{i_τ} (selected by lowest empirical disparate impact), and an anytime-valid high-probability upper bound on the true marginal gain of training one additional model. The guarantee is conditional on the fixed dataset D and holds over the randomness of the training procedure and the finite-sample evaluation noise.

## Method

The paper formalizes LDA search as an optimal stopping problem and decomposes the expected marginal gain g(u) of a new model into a conditional expected improvement (CEI) μ(u) and an improvement probability p(u). The key technical contribution is a novel anytime-valid upper bound on p(u) (Lemma 3.2): a sequence p̄_t(α) such that with probability at least 1−α, the probability of observing a new minimum among iid draws never exceeds p̄_t at any time t, without requiring knowledge of the underlying distribution. Algorithm 1 combines this with a CEI bound (universally μ(u) ≤ u, or tighter under distributional assumptions) and stops at the first τ where μ̄(U_τ)·p̄_τ(δ) ≤ γ. For the finite-data regime, Theorem 3.5 shows that under a non-decreasing selection effect assumption (regression-to-the-mean is at least constant), the same algorithm applied to noisy empirical estimates still certifies that the true marginal gain is below γ with probability ≥ 1−δ. Empirically, on credit and housing datasets with logistic regression, random forests, and gradient boosted trees, the algorithm overshoots the full-information stopping time by only tens of models, and marginal gains drop below 0.01% per model after roughly 60 retrainings.

## Results

Across Adult, Folktables, and HMDA datasets with logistic regression, random forests, and gradient boosted trees, the paper finds approximately 20% spread between the highest and lowest disparate impact among retrained models, confirming meaningful model multiplicity. After roughly 60 retrained models, marginal gains in disparate impact reduction drop into the hundredths of a percent per new model; in several dataset-method combinations, gains become negligible after fewer than 10 models. The adaptive stopping algorithm (Algorithm 1) overshoots the full-information optimal stopping time by tens of models on average, with the gap being larger for logistic regression across all datasets and for all methods on HMDA.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against competing LDA search methods; instead, it benchmarks its adaptive stopping rule against the full-information optimal stopping time (the idealized case where the true marginal gain g(U_t) is known exactly). Algorithm 1's anytime-valid upper bound (pink line in Figure 1) consistently lies above the true marginal gain (brown line), meaning the algorithm trains additional models beyond what would be optimal with perfect information. The overshoot is on the order of tens of models in most settings but is notably larger for logistic regression on all three datasets and for all three ML methods on HMDA, suggesting the universal CEI bound μ̄(u)=u is particularly conservative in those regimes. The authors note that stronger distributional assumptions (A1–A3 in Appendix B) would yield tighter bounds and reduce this gap. A secondary comparison with the Fairlearn fairness-aware ML framework (Figure 5, Appendix A) and Algorithm 2 (Figure 4) is provided but discussed only in the appendix.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of the less-discriminatory-algorithm (LDA) legal-fairness literature (Black et al., 2024; Gillis et al., 2024), the model-multiplicity empirical literature (Marx et al., 2020; D'Amour et al., 2022; Rudin et al., 2024), and the classical optimal-stopping / Pandora's Box problem (Weitzman, 1978; DeGroot, 2004). It departs from the Pandora's Box formulation by assuming minimal knowledge of the model-performance distribution and by seeking high-probability marginal-return guarantees rather than total-utility maximization. The anytime-valid inference machinery (Waudby-Smith and Ramdas, 2024) is repurposed here to construct an adaptive, distribution-free upper bound on the probability of improving upon a running best sample, combined with a non-decreasing selection-effect assumption borrowed from the regression-to-the-mean literature (Stein et al., 1956; Andrews et al., 2024).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first concrete, certifiable procedure for a firm to demonstrate that its LDA search was sufficient, directly addressing a gap flagged by regulators and legal scholars who have noted that 'no constraints or limits on this search have been proposed.' The empirical finding that marginal gains collapse after roughly 60 retrained models (and sometimes fewer than 10) substantially alleviates the concern that LDA searches could be unbounded in cost. Open questions include extending the framework to adaptive search strategies where prior models influence subsequent training distributions, incorporating richer distributional assumptions for tighter bounds, and addressing the full qualitative dimensions of legal reasonableness that go beyond a single retraining loop.
