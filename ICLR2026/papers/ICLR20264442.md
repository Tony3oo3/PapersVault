## Problem

Existing architecture-based continual learning methods rely on dynamic network expansion or pruning to accommodate new tasks, making them hardware-unfriendly and highly sensitive to task order. While biological neural circuits achieve continual learning without structural growth through rich multi-synaptic connectivity, the cooperative interactions among multiple synapses and their role in continual learning remain largely unexplored in artificial systems.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264442/cropped_page1_idx4.webp)

## Input / Output

The method accepts grayscale images (28×28 for PMNIST) and color images (32×32 for CIFAR-100, 64×64 for TinyImageNet) presented sequentially as a stream of tasks, and outputs a class label per task via a multi-head architecture where task identity is known at both training and inference. In the SNN variant, inputs are encoded as spike trains processed by LIF neurons; in the ANN variant, standard forward passes are used. Each task introduces a disjoint set of classes, and the model must retain all previously learned tasks without access to their data.

## Method

MSCN replaces each single-weight connection with P parallel synapses (P≥1), each with a distinct decay time constant (SNN) or activation function (ANN), thereby increasing representational capacity within a fixed network topology. A local eligibility trace accumulates recent synaptic activity and drives a piecewise quadratic modulation function f_mod that scales and redirects gradient-based weight updates, enabling activity-dependent potentiation or depression at the synaptic level. This plasticity mechanism is combined with a mask-based subnetwork selection strategy: a shared learnable relevance score ranks synapses, and a binary mask activates the top-c% per layer for each task while an accumulated mask freezes previously allocated synapses. The result is a fixed-capacity network that dynamically routes task-relevant synapses and suppresses irrelevant ones, achieving zero backward transfer without any architectural growth.

## Results

On the ANN framework, MSCN achieves the highest average accuracy across all four benchmarks: 97.53% on PMNIST, 77.37% on 10-split CIFAR-100, 75.03% on TinyImageNet, and 93.69% on 5-Datasets, while maintaining zero backward transfer (BWT = 0.0) in every setting. On the SNN framework, MSCN reaches 96.34% (PMNIST), 79.54% (CIFAR-100), 73.22% (TinyImageNet), and 88.84% (5-Datasets), again with BWT = 0.0. Under a matched parameter budget (Table 3), MSCN also achieves the lowest AOPD (task-order robustness metric) on all four datasets, e.g., 0.23% on PMNIST vs. 0.29% for WSN and 2.23% for PackNet, while training 15–45% faster than baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MSCN outperforms all ANN baselines on average accuracy across every dataset: it leads WSN (96.41→97.53 on PMNIST; 76.38→77.37 on CIFAR-100; 71.96→75.03 on TinyImageNet; 93.41→93.69 on 5-Datasets) and Bayesian (96.74→97.53; 75.57→77.37; 73.93→75.03; 93.36→93.69) by 0.6–3.1 percentage points. Among zero-forgetting architecture-based methods (PackNet, SupSup, WSN), MSCN achieves the highest accuracy while matching their BWT = 0.0, with the largest margin on TinyImageNet (+3.07% over WSN, +15.57% over PackNet). Regularization-based baselines (EWC, GPM) suffer substantial forgetting (BWT as low as −25.47% for EWC on SNN TinyImageNet) and lower accuracy, while TAMiL, though competitive on PMNIST (96.87%), degrades with negative BWT (−3.15 to −4.72%) on all datasets. On the SNN side, MSCN trails the multi-task upper bound (MTL) by 0.18–5.40% but surpasses all continual-learning SNN baselines (EWC, HAT, GPM, HLOP) by 1.2–12.9% on TinyImageNet and 5-Datasets. Under a matched parameter budget, MSCN also achieves the best AOPD (task-order robustness) on all four datasets, e.g., 2.41% on CIFAR-100 vs. 2.59% for WSN and 5.36% for PackNet, confirming that the multi-synaptic cooperation mechanism provides both higher accuracy and greater order-independence than existing subnetwork or regularization approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

MSCN sits at the intersection of architecture-based continual learning and bio-inspired synaptic modeling. It adopts the mask-based subnetwork selection framework from Kang et al. (2022b) and the multi-head, layerwise-capacity-ratio protocol from Wortsman et al. (2020), but replaces the single-weight-per-connection paradigm with P parallel synapses per connection, each with independent decay constants or activation functions. The eligibility-trace modulation function is directly borrowed from Zhang et al. (2023), repurposed here to gate synaptic plasticity at the level of individual parallel synapses rather than whole connections. Conceptually, the paper departs from prior architecture-based methods (PackNet, SupSup, WSN) that achieve capacity through pruning or expansion by instead increasing representational diversity within a fixed topology via multi-synaptic redundancy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical contribution is demonstrating that multi-synaptic connectivity within a fixed architecture can match or exceed the accuracy of methods that dynamically expand or prune networks, while achieving zero backward transfer and substantially lower capacity overhead (CAP). The task-order robustness finding—MSCN shows markedly lower per-task accuracy variance across permutations than EWC, GPM, or WSN—addresses a known weakness of architecture-based methods and suggests the local activity-dependent modulation is a meaningful mechanism for stability. Limitations include the requirement for task IDs at inference (multi-head setting), the added parameter overhead from parallel synapses, and evaluation restricted to relatively small-scale benchmarks (up to 200 classes). Open questions include whether heterogeneous synapse counts per connection (as in biological systems) could further optimize capacity, and whether the approach scales to larger vision or language models.
