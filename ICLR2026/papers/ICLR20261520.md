## Problem

Existing LLM fingerprinting methods rely on post-hoc signatures (data patterns, optimization dynamics, hyperparameters) that only emerge after substantial training, causing them to fail during early pretraining and under distribution shifts. This contradicts the classical Galtonian notion of a fingerprint as an intrinsic, persistent identity present from the moment of a model's 'birth.' The paper asks whether a true fingerprint rooted in random initialization can enable lineage verification across the entire training lifecycle.

## Input / Output

The method takes two models (a base/reference model and a suspicious model) and a set of n=2000 random input sequences of length ℓ=1024 (uniformly sampled token IDs or isotropic Gaussian vectors). It produces a p-value from a one-sided z-test on Kendall-Tau correlations, with a binary same-lineage decision at significance level α=0.01. By default, final-layer hidden states (not logits) are used as model outputs for computational efficiency, since logits require substantially more random inputs to reliably estimate bias patterns as vocabulary size grows.

## Method

SeedPrints exploits the observation that random initialization seeds induce persistent, seed-specific bias patterns: certain output dimensions are systematically disfavored by untrained models, and these biases survive training. The algorithm (1) computes mean response vectors over random inputs for both models, (2) identifies 'identity dimensions' as the intersection of their bottom-m most-disfavored coordinates (m ≈ 0.1·d_out), providing mutual verification that suppresses spurious architectural correlations, (3) computes Kendall-Tau rank correlations on these identity dimensions across all random inputs, and (4) tests whether the mean correlation significantly exceeds a Gaussian null via a z-score. A key theoretical contribution proves that mean-based dimension extraction is stable under perturbations (exponential concentration in n), while per-sample argmin voting is inherently discontinuous and noise-sensitive, justifying the design choice. The analytical null avoids expensive empirical baseline construction by leveraging the known zero-mean Gaussian distribution of Kendall-Tau under independence, with weak softmax-induced dependencies mitigated by high-temperature normalization (T=10).

## Results

On the LeaFBench benchmark (65 models, 7 families, 6 post-training transformations), SeedPrints achieves an overall AUC of 0.992 and KS statistic of 0.986, matching the strongest baselines Intrinsic (AUC 0.994, KS 0.989) and ICS (AUC 0.994, KS 0.943) while substantially outperforming REEF (AUC 0.915, KS 0.739) and Gradient (AUC 0.801, KS 0.508). On OLMo-2-7B pretraining checkpoints spanning 5B to 3.9T tokens, SeedPrints achieves perfect lineage detection (p ≪ 0.001) from the very first checkpoint, whereas all baselines fall below the 0.8 similarity threshold within the first trillion tokens. Under large-scale fine-tuning (5M–700B tokens from LLaMA-2-7B), SeedPrints maintains p < 0.01 across all settings, while PCS and ICS drop below threshold on CodeLlama-7B (500B tokens) and Llemma-7B (700B tokens).

## Baselines & Comparisons

On LeaFBench, SeedPrints (AUC 0.992, KS 0.986) essentially ties with Intrinsic (AUC 0.994, KS 0.989) and ICS (AUC 0.994, KS 0.943), while decisively outperforming REEF (AUC 0.915, KS 0.739) and Gradient (AUC 0.801, KS 0.508). The critical differentiator is not post-training performance but early-stage and distribution-shift robustness: on OLMo-2-7B pretraining checkpoints, all four baselines (Intrinsic, REEF, PCS, ICS) fail to detect lineage before ~1T tokens, whereas SeedPrints detects it from the first checkpoint (p ≪ 0.001). Under continual training on The Stack (code corpus, a sharp domain shift from OpenWebText), all baselines misclassify true descendants as distractors (e.g., Intrinsic scores 0.489, REEF 0.557), while SeedPrints correctly attributes lineage (p ≈ 0). On large-scale fine-tuning, PCS and ICS drop below the 0.8 threshold on CodeLlama-7B (500B tokens: 0.6863, 0.3369) and Llemma-7B (700B tokens: 0.6682, 0.2905), while SeedPrints maintains p < 10⁻⁵ in both cases. Finally, all baselines fail to distinguish models initialized with different seeds (Tables 1–2: all similarity scores well below 0.8), confirming their signals are training-induced rather than initialization-born, whereas SeedPrints cleanly separates seeds at birth (p > 0.01 for cross-seed pairs) and confirms same-seed lineage after training (p < 10⁻²⁰).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the entire existing LLM fingerprinting literature (Intrinsic/PDF, REEF, PCS/ICS, Gradient) by identifying a fundamentally different signal source: rather than extracting signatures that emerge from training data, optimization dynamics, or hyperparameters, SeedPrints leverages biases present at random initialization that persist through training. Conceptually, it repurposes the recent theoretical observation (Li et al., 2026) that randomly initialized transformers exhibit seed-dependent representation contraction into a practical fingerprinting tool. Methodologically, it replaces the similarity-score paradigm of prior work with a statistical hypothesis test (Kendall-Tau correlation over identity dimensions with an analytical Gaussian null), providing calibrated p-values rather than heuristic thresholds.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is significant: existing fingerprinting methods are blind during early pretraining (the most vulnerable window for model theft), while SeedPrints provides verifiable lineage from the first checkpoint onward, surviving 3.9T tokens of heterogeneous pretraining and sharp domain shifts (text→code). The limitation is that the method requires white-box access to hidden states or logits, leaving the black-box/API-only setting open. A key open question is whether the initialization signal can be recovered from restricted outputs (e.g., top-k tokens or safety-filtered responses), and whether the seed-level distinguishability has meaningful legal standing for copyright claims given that initialization seeds are not typically considered intellectual property.
