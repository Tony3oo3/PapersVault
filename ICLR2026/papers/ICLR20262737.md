## Problem

Neuroscience has established that human brain activity synchronizes during social interaction (inter-brain synchrony), yet it remains unclear whether LLMs possess an analogous internal mechanism supporting social engagement. Prior work on LLM sociality has focused on behavioral evaluation or static single-model representations, leaving the temporal representational dynamics between genuinely interacting LLMs unexplored.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262737/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes hidden states extracted at the final token position of each interaction turn from two LLMs role-playing in SOTOPIA, where each agent has distinct profiles, goals, and secrets (i.e., the two models receive different contextual inputs with no shared correspondence). The output is a scalar Sync R² score per model pair, computed by averaging the best-predictive R² across all source layers in both directions, along with per-layer-pair synchrony heatmaps showing R² for every (l_A, l_B) combination.

## Method

For each turn in a SOTOPIA dialogue, hidden representations from all transformer layers of both agents are collected as temporally aligned pairs. Ridge regression with an intercept (λ=0.1) is trained to learn an affine transformation W, b mapping one agent's layer-l_A representations to the other's layer-l_B representations, and R² is evaluated on a held-out test set split by interaction (not by turn) to prevent leakage. For each source layer, the best-predicting target layer is selected, negative R² values are clamped to zero, and the mean across all source layers yields Sync R²(A→B); the bidirectional score averages both directions. Two control conditions validate the measure: a passive agent that only reads the dialogue (removing social engagement) and lagged pairing of representations k turns apart (removing temporal proximity), both of which significantly reduce synchrony. The approach is distinguished from standard representation similarity measures (CCA, CKA) by operating on non-corresponding inputs and explicitly modeling the temporal predictive relationship between agents.

## Results

Neural synchrony (Sync R²) between socially interacting LLMs shows strong positive correlation with behavioral-level social performance across all model family types: Mistral Family (Pearson r=0.88, p<0.05), Cross Family (r=0.89, p<0.001), and Llama Family (r=0.99, p<0.001). After controlling for instruction-following (IFEval) and long-context reasoning (MuSR) as confounds, partial correlations remain positive and significant (e.g., 0.92 for Mistral with MuSR control, 0.99 for Llama with MuSR control). Control experiments confirm that Sync R² drops substantially when social engagement is removed (passive agent) or when temporal alignment is broken (lag-k pairing), validating that the metric captures genuine interaction dynamics rather than static representational similarity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against competing synchrony or alignment methods (e.g., CCA, CKA) directly, as those require paired inputs which are unavailable in multi-agent social interaction. Instead, it validates Sync R² against two internal control conditions: (1) removing social engagement (passive agent) and (2) removing temporal proximity (lag-k pairing). In both controls, Sync R² drops significantly, with many layer pairs yielding negative R² values, confirming the metric is sensitive to genuine interaction dynamics. For the transformation choice, affine (ridge regression) is compared to a two-layer nonlinear MLP: affine achieves 0.75±0.01 for Llama-3-8B self-pairs versus 0.59±0.01 for nonlinear, while the two are comparable for cross-family pairs (0.55 vs. 0.60). Sensitivity analyses show that varying the top-k layer selection (k=1 to 32) keeps correlations above r>0.7, and that clamping negative R² to zero is essential for stable measurements (without clamping, standard errors inflate dramatically).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the neuroscience concept of inter-brain synchrony (IBS) for LLMs, but departs from all prior representation-alignment methods (CCA, CKA, CKNNA) that require paired inputs across models. While brain-LLM similarity work (Aw et al., Mischler et al., Doerig et al.) and internal ToM analyses (Zhu et al., Bortoletto et al.) examine single-model representations under static or third-person inputs, this paper is the first to study temporal representational dynamics between two LLMs engaged in multi-round social interaction with distinct, non-shared contextual inputs. The predictive affine transformation framework is borrowed from representation alignment literature but adapted to handle the temporal correspondence and asymmetric information structure inherent in social dialogue.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first empirical evidence that LLMs exhibit an internal mechanism analogous to human IBS, opening a representation-level lens for studying LLM sociality that complements existing behavioral evaluations. The strong correlation between Sync R² and social performance (r up to 0.99) suggests the metric could serve as a diagnostic tool for social agent design, though the causal relationship remains unestablished. Key limitations include the narrow model set (6 models, 21 pairs), single environment (SOTOPIA with 8-turn cap), and the fact that the correlation may partly reflect general model quality despite partial-correlation controls. Open questions include whether synchrony persists in longer, more open-ended, or multi-agent interactions, and whether it can be leveraged as a training signal for more socially intelligent agents.
