## Problem

Standard reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) for code generation optimizes solution correctness but provides no explicit incentive for the model to critique or reflect on existing solutions. This absence of a critique signal means the model never learns to judge whether a given solution is correct, limiting its reflective reasoning capabilities. The paper asks whether incorporating a dedicated critique reward into the RL loop can improve both code generation quality and general reasoning.

## Input / Output

The model operates in two modes within a single training run. In RL mode, it receives a coding problem specification and produces a code solution wrapped in a code block, preceded by a chain-of-thought reasoning trace. In CRL mode, it receives a concatenated question-solution pair [q; s] and produces a critique ending with a structured \conclusion{T} or \conclusion{F} token indicating whether the solution is correct. Training uses Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B in thinking mode with context lengths extended iteratively from 16k to 32k tokens, and evaluation uses temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95, top-k 20, and a 32,768-token max output length.

## Method

The core contribution is Critique Reinforcement Learning (CRL), where the model is prompted with a (question, solution) pair and rewarded with a binary signal (1 or 0) based solely on whether its final \conclusion{} judgment matches the ground-truth label, rather than on the quality of the critique text itself. This CRL signal is combined with standard RLVR (pass-rate reward on test cases) in a hybrid training set where 20% of samples are CRL and 80% are RL, both optimized jointly via GRPO. The advantage estimation in GRPO is thus jointly shaped by task-outcome rewards and critique-correctness rewards, aligning the policy with both execution correctness and reflective judgment. A two-phase context-lengthening schedule (16k → 32k) is used, with the CRL reward scaled by 0.8 during the shorter phase to prevent it from dominating the RL signal. Ablations confirm that 20% CRL is optimal: 100% CRL degrades performance below the RL-only baseline because the model over-specializes in judgment rather than solution generation.

## Results

CRITIQUE-CODER-4B achieves 59.0 on LiveCodeBench v5 (+4.8 over the Qwen3-4B baseline and +2.4 over the RL-only variant) and 86.5 on EvalPlus. CRITIQUE-CODER-8B reaches 60.8 on LiveCodeBench v5 and 35.6 on Aider-Polyglot (+7.2 over baseline), surpassing DeepCoder-14B (60.6) and GPT-o1 (59.5) on LiveCodeBench. On the BIG-Bench Extra Hard logic reasoning benchmark, CRITIQUE-CODER-4B gains +6.1 points over the baseline on average across four subtasks, demonstrating transferable reasoning improvements beyond coding.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the RL-only variants trained on identical data, CRITIQUE-CODER-4B wins on every benchmark (e.g., +2.4 on LiveCodeBench v5, +2.6 on Aider-Polyglot) and CRITIQUE-CODER-8B similarly dominates (e.g., +2.7 on BigCodeBench-I Hard, +1.2 on LiveCodeBench v5), confirming CRL as a consistent complement to RL. Compared to DeepCoder-14B, CRITIQUE-CODER-4B outperforms it across all reported benchmarks (59.0 vs 60.6 on LiveCodeBench is the closest gap; 86.5 vs 85.3 on EvalPlus; 24.4 vs 18.4 on Aider-Polyglot) while using only 28% of the parameters. CRITIQUE-CODER-8B edges out DeepCoder-14B on LiveCodeBench v5 (60.8 vs 60.6) and substantially on BigCodeBench-I Hard (27.0 vs 18.2). Against GPT-o1, CRITIQUE-CODER-8B surpasses it on LiveCodeBench v5 (60.8 vs 59.5) and BigCodeBench-I Hard (27.0 vs 28.4 is a near-tie), but trails significantly on Aider-Polyglot (35.6 vs 61.7), which the authors attribute to lack of non-Python optimization. On the BBEH logic reasoning benchmark, CRITIQUE-CODER-4B outperforms both the Qwen3-4B baseline (+6.1 avg) and the RL-only variant (+4.0 avg), with the largest gains on Time Arithmetic (+8.0) and Zebra Puzzles (+7.5).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RLVR (reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards) paradigm for code generation—pioneered by AceCoder, HardTests, and SWE-RL—by introducing a second reward signal based on the correctness of the model's own binary judgment over question-solution pairs. It departs from Critique Fine-Tuning (CFT) and Critique-Guided Distillation (CGD), which rely on teacher-provided critique traces or imitation, by instead using RL with a verifiable binary reward on the model's self-generated judgment, unifying both signals under GRPO. The two-phase context-lengthening training strategy is borrowed from DeepCoder, and the base models (Qwen3-4B/8B) and dataset (rStar-Coder) are shared with recent competitive coding RL work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical finding that a 20% CRL-to-RL mix ratio is optimal—and that 100% CRL degrades below the RL-only baseline—gives practitioners a concrete recipe for integrating critique learning into existing RL pipelines without sacrificing solution-generation ability. The transfer of critique-enhanced reasoning to non-coding logic tasks (BBEH, +6.1 over baseline) suggests that CRL may be a general-purpose reasoning booster, though this is demonstrated on a single 4B model and a single benchmark family. A key open question is whether CRL's benefits persist at larger scales or in domains where verifiable binary judgments are harder to define, and whether the inability to perform reliable post-hoc self-critique (confirmed in the parallel test-time scaling experiment) limits its applicability to iterative refinement settings.
