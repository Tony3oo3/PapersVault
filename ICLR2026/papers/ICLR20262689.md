## Problem

Transformer models are the de facto architecture for modern AI, but their training and inference costs make on-device fine-tuning on resource-constrained edge devices infeasible. Prior on-device learning work targeted compact CNNs, while existing low-rank methods for transformers either leave the architecture uncompressed at inference (LoRA), lack a principled link between truncation and performance loss (ASVD, FWSVD), or are restricted to LLMs with 3D activations (SVD-LLM). No prior method simultaneously compresses both model weights and activation maps into a stable low-rank subspace with controlled information loss, enabling efficient training *and* inference on edge hardware.

## Input / Output

WASI takes a pretrained transformer (ViT, SwinT, or TinyLlama) and fine-tunes it on downstream tasks: RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 224×224 for CUB/Flowers/Pets) mapped to class labels, or BoolQ natural-language questions mapped to yes/no answers. Internally, the method operates on the linear layers within MLP blocks (and optionally attention projections), where each layer's weight matrix W_i ∈ R^{O_i×I_i} and activation tensor A_i ∈ R^{B×N_i×I_i} are projected into low-rank subspaces of rank K_i before forward and backward computation. The external I/O interface is identical to vanilla training; the compression is entirely internal to the computation graph.

## Method

WASI (Weight-Activation Subspace Iteration) rests on the insight that a transformer's essential parameter information resides in a stable low-dimensional subspace throughout fine-tuning, so the subspace need only be identified once and then tracked cheaply. Weight Subspace Iteration (WSI) performs a full SVD at initialization to determine the essential subspace rank K_i via an explained-variance threshold ε, then replaces per-iteration SVD with a single warm-started Gram-Schmidt step (Algorithm 1). Activation Subspace Iteration (ASI) compresses 3D/4D activation tensors via Tucker decomposition with fixed per-mode ranks selected by a dynamic-programming search that minimizes memory under a perplexity constraint, and updates factor matrices via subspace iteration rather than expensive HOSVD. The forward pass becomes A_{i+1} = A_i R_i^T L_i^T and the backward pass computes gradients entirely in the low-rank space (Eqs. 9–11), yielding up to 62× training-memory reduction and 2× FLOP reduction while matching vanilla accuracy at ε = 0.9.

## Results

On ViT fine-tuned on CIFAR-10, WASI at ε=0.9 achieves 96.24% accuracy (vs. 97.32% vanilla) while reducing training memory from 2349 MB to 179.61 MB (~13×) and training FLOPs from 3.26×10¹² to 1.43×10¹² (~2.3×). On SwinT across multiple datasets, WASI at ε=0.9 matches vanilla accuracy while cutting memory by up to 62× and FLOPs by 1.5×. On TinyLlama (BoolQ), activation and weight memory drop by up to 953.86× and 30.12× respectively, with training and inference FLOPs reduced by 13.11× and 30.27×. On a Raspberry Pi 5, WASI at ε=0.9 completes training in 16.57 s vs. 23.87 s for vanilla (~1.4× speedup).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against ASI (Nguyen et al., 2025), WASI wins on both memory and FLOPs because it compresses weights in addition to activations; at ε=0.9, ASI's training FLOPs actually exceed vanilla training, while WASI remains ~2.3× below. On Raspberry Pi 5, WASI is consistently faster than ASI at every ε (e.g., 16.57 s vs. 25.52 s training at ε=0.9). Against SVD-LLM (Wang et al., 2024), WASI achieves up to 100× higher memory efficiency at comparable accuracy, since SVD-LLM's LoRA adapters force co-storage of frozen and adapter weights; at the lowest compression settings, SVD-LLM even consumes more memory than vanilla training. However, SVD-LLM achieves the lowest FLOPs among all methods due to its adapter-based design. Additionally, SVD-LLM's Truncation-Aware Data Whitening is undefined for 4D activation maps, making it inapplicable to SwinT, whereas WASI handles both 3D and 4D activations natively. Against vanilla training, WASI at ε=0.9 sacrifices only ~1% accuracy (96.24% vs. 97.32% on CIFAR-10) while delivering 13× less training memory and 1.4× faster on-device execution.

## Relation to Prior Work

WASI unifies two previously separate lines of low-rank compression: weight decomposition (LoRA, SVD-LLM, ASVD) and activation compression (AMC, ASI). It extends ASI (Nguyen et al., 2025) by adding a Weight Subspace Iteration (WSI) component that projects weight matrices into a stable low-rank subspace via warm-started subspace iteration (borrowed from PowerSGD), and by replacing ASI's fixed memory budget with a dynamic-programming rank selection under a perplexity constraint. The key departure from SVD-LLM and LoRA-based methods is that WASI compresses both weights and activations simultaneously into a joint low-rank representation, so inference also runs in the compressed space rather than merging adapters back into the full architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first method to make transformer fine-tuning feasible on commodity edge hardware (Raspberry Pi 5, Jetson Nano) by jointly compressing weights and activations, a setting previously dominated by compact CNNs. The 62× training memory reduction and 1.4× speedup are practically significant for privacy-preserving on-device learning. Limitations include evaluation on relatively small models (ViT, SwinT, TinyLlama with only 5 layers fine-tuned), reliance on a stability assumption that is validated empirically but not proven, and exclusion of attention-layer compression in main experiments. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger transformers, whether it applies to pre-training from scratch, and how it interacts with other compression techniques (quantization, sparsity).
