## Problem

Reasoning LLMs are trained exclusively for solo-reasoning, yet multi-model collaboration on shared trajectories requires them to assess and build upon off-distribution thinking tokens from other reasoners. No systematic evaluation exists for whether standard training pipelines (SFT, RL, distillation) yield the necessary off-trajectory reasoning capabilities, and it is unclear whether benchmark-optimized models can actually collaborate effectively.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263101/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The evaluation takes a math or coding question, a partial reasoning trajectory from the main model truncated at m tokens (0–80% of its full trajectory), and an off-trajectory steer from a collaborator truncated at n tokens, concatenated into a single shared trajectory placed inside the model's thinking block. The model produces a reasoning completion and final answer conditioned on the question and this stitched trajectory. For recoverability, the steer is a distractor sampled from the same model on a different question; for guidability, the steer is a partial correct trajectory from a stronger model placed at the very start (m=0) with no original reasoning, and the question is one the model cannot solve independently (solve rate ≤1/8).

## Method

The paper introduces a twin-test framework for off-trajectory reasoning: Recoverability (can the model backtrack from a misleading steer and resume its original correct reasoning?) and Guidability (can the model build upon a correct but incomplete steer from a stronger collaborator to solve problems beyond its solo capability?). Distractors are constructed by sampling from the evaluated model itself on a different question, guaranteeing incorrectness without external verification, while guides are partial trajectories from stronger models (DeepSeek-R1, Qwen3-235B, QwQ-32B) truncated at 20–80% of their full length. The authors evaluate 15 open-weight LLMs (1.5B–32B) across five math and four coding benchmarks, then conduct controlled experiments isolating three post-training factors: distillation teacher choice (showing teacher recoverability weaknesses transfer to students even with correct-only training data), RL training (GRPO on MATH8K substantially improves recoverability where SFT saturates), and data filtering strategy (LIMO-style aggressive filtering introduces high variance in off-trajectory robustness across checkpoints).

## Results

Across 15 open-weight LLMs (1.5B–32B), average recoverability on the shared math subset is 74.9% (a 25.1 pp drop from the original 100% solve rate), and 59.1% for coding. Counterintuitively, the top math model AM-Thinking-32B (82.6% benchmark) recovers only 33.4% of the time, while Qwen3-1.7B (59.9% benchmark) recovers 98.4%. On guidability, no model exceeds 9.2% on the shared math subset, and even after accounting for 18.6% of steers already containing the correct answer, the net gain from guidance remains under 8.1 pp on average.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an evaluation study rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so the key contrast is between model tiers. Low-benchmark models (e.g., Qwen3-1.7B at 59.9%, OpenThinker3-1.5B at 59.2%) achieve 95–98% recoverability, while the highest-benchmark models (AM-Thinking-32B at 82.6%, Qwen3-32B at 81.0%) drop to 33–72%, showing that benchmark optimization does not transfer to off-trajectory robustness. On guidability, even pairing each model with its own distillation teacher (e.g., Qwen3-1.7B guided by Qwen3-235B) yields no meaningful gain over other guides, confirming the ceiling is inherent to the student model. In the controlled study, AM-Distill models (trained on AM-Thinking-32B trajectories) show significantly lower recoverability than QwQ- or Qwen3-Distill counterparts (p≤0.005 after step 300) despite identical benchmark scores, demonstrating that teacher vulnerabilities propagate through distillation. RL (GRPO) applied after SFT saturation recovers 15.3–28.9% in recoverability, fully bridging the teacher-induced gap. Finally, LIMO-style aggressive data filtering (600–800 examples) introduces high variance in recoverability across checkpoints, whereas full-dataset training (8K–8.8K) yields stable scores at equivalent benchmark levels.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by formalizing 'off-trajectory reasoning' as a distinct capability and introducing the twin-test framework (Recoverability and Guidability) to evaluate it systematically. While closely related work has examined recovery from unhelpful thoughts (He et al., 2025a; Yang et al., 2025b) or robustness to noisy rationales (Zhou et al., 2024), those studies use hand-designed perturbations or single-model settings; here, distractors are model-specific (sampled from the evaluated model on a different question) and the guidability test is entirely new. The paper also departs from hybrid scaling and offloading systems (Akhauri et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025c; Pan et al., 2025) by asking a more fundamental question: can solo-trained models even handle interleaved off-distribution reasoning tokens, rather than designing new collaboration architectures around the assumption that they can.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that benchmark performance is largely orthogonal to off-trajectory robustness has direct implications for anyone deploying multi-model reasoning systems, agentic pipelines, or human-in-the-loop oversight: a model that scores 82% on AIME may be far less reliable as a collaborator than a 60% model. The controlled experiments showing that teacher vulnerabilities propagate through distillation (even with correct-only trajectories) and that RL can repair what SFT cannot provide actionable training guidance. Open questions include whether these limitations persist in closed frontier models, how to design collaboration protocols that work around the guidability ceiling, and whether the recoverability gap can be closed at scale without sacrificing benchmark performance.
