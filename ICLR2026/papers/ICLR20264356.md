## Problem

Controlling linear dynamical systems under adversarial disturbances requires competing with the best stationary policy in hindsight, but prior methods such as GPC scale polynomially with the inverse stability margin (1/γ) in their runtime, making them impractical for marginally stable systems where γ is small. This paper closes that gap by achieving polylogarithmic dependence on 1/γ in runtime while retaining optimal Õ(γ^{-4}√T) regret guarantees.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264356/cropped_page3_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes the known system matrices A and B, the current state x_t, and a window of m past disturbances w_{t-1},…,w_{t-m}, which are recovered online from observed states via w_t = x_{t+1} − Ax_t − Bu_t. It outputs a single control vector u_t ∈ ℝ^n at each step. The spectral filters that process the disturbance window are universal: they depend only on the assumed stability margin γ and memory length m, not on the particular system, initial state, or cost sequence.

## Method

The core contribution is a convex relaxation of the nonconvex linear control problem built on spectral filters derived from the top h eigenvectors of a Hankel matrix H with entries H_{ij} = (1−γ)^{i+j−1}/(i+j−1). At each step the control is a weighted sum of the past disturbance window multiplied by these filters, parameterized by h learnable matrices M_i, and training reduces to projected online gradient descent over a bounded convex set of such matrices. The key technical insight is that the Hankel eigenvectors form a universal low-dimensional feature map that approximates any diagonalizably stable linear policy to arbitrary accuracy using only polylogarithmically many parameters. Combined with fast online convolution, this yields a per-step runtime of Õ(log⁴(T/γ)), an exponential improvement over the polynomial O(γ^{-1} log T) of GPC, while the regret bound Õ(γ^{-4}√T) matches the best known guarantees.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 2.1) shows OSC achieves regret Õ(γ^{-4}√T) against the best diagonalizably stable linear policy, with per-step runtime O(log⁴(T/γ)) via fast online convolution. This improves upon GPC's Õ(γ^{-5.5}√T) regret and O(γ^{-1} log T) runtime, reducing the dependence on the stability margin from polynomial to polylogarithmic. Empirically, OSC uses only 15d parameters (vs. 50d for GPC) while matching or exceeding GPC's long-run performance across linear and nonlinear systems, various disturbance distributions, and stability margins.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GPC (Agarwal et al., 2019a), the primary comparable baseline in the fully adversarial setting, OSC improves the regret exponent on the stability margin from γ^{-5.5} to γ^{-4} and reduces per-step runtime from O(γ^{-1} log T) to O(log⁴(T/γ)) — a shift from polynomial to polylogarithmic dependence. Empirically, OSC matches GPC's long-run loss using only 15 Hankel eigenvectors (15d parameters) versus GPC's 50d parameters, and remains competitive even at h=5. Against Online LQ (Cohen et al., 2018), OSC operates in a strictly more general setting (adversarial disturbances, convex Lipschitz costs vs. i.i.d. noise, quadratic costs), though it incurs a worse γ-dependence (γ^{-4} vs. γ^{-2.5}) as a trade-off for generality. The classical LQR baseline is not directly comparable since it requires i.i.d. noise and known fixed quadratic costs; OSC subsumes this setting but with a √T regret rather than O(1). Across all ablations (state dimension, stability margin, disturbance type), OSC and GPC exhibit similar relative behavior, with OSC's spectral features providing a more compact and modular representation that integrates naturally with nonlinear heads.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the online nonstochastic control framework of Agarwal et al. (2019a) (GPC) by replacing its polynomial-in-1/γ parameterization with a spectral-filtering approach drawn from the sequence-prediction literature (Hazan et al., 2017; Marsden & Hazan, 2025). The key departure is the construction of a universal Hankel matrix whose eigenvectors serve as system-agnostic features, reducing the control problem to online convex optimization over a compact feature space. The polylogarithmic runtime is achieved by combining these spectral features with the fast online convolution technique of Agarwal et al. (2024a), a tool previously applied to generative modeling rather than control.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The shift from polynomial to polylogarithmic dependence on the inverse stability margin γ is practically significant: it makes online control of marginally stable systems (small γ, e.g., thermal or satellite dynamics) computationally feasible where prior methods were intractable. The universality of the spectral features—being independent of the specific LDS, initial state, and cost—positions them as a general-purpose representation that integrates naturally with nonlinear models (demonstrated with MLPs), opening a path toward hybrid spectral-deep architectures for large-scale control. Open questions include extending the framework to partial observation, unknown or time-varying dynamics, and bandit feedback, all of which remain active areas in the online control literature.
