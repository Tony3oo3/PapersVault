## Problem

Existing attention steering methods for prompt highlighting (e.g., PASTA) edit the attention score matrix after it is computed, requiring explicit storage of the full attention matrix and making them incompatible with memory-efficient implementations like FlashAttention. They also rely on costly, task-specific head searches to determine which attention heads to steer. This paper asks whether equivalent attention control can be achieved by intervening on key embeddings before attention computation, eliminating the need to materialise the attention matrix entirely.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt with user-specified highlighted tokens (delimited by ** markers) and operates on the key vectors extracted from selected KV heads at each transformer layer. During an offline phase, it consumes synthetic contrastive prompt triplets (neutral, positive, negative) to learn per-(layer, head) projection matrices via SVD of cross-covariance matrices. At inference, it produces modified key embeddings that are fed directly into the standard attention computation, yielding steered text generation. For AdaSEKA, the last token's query vector is additionally consumed to compute dynamic expert-routing coefficients, making the steering operator query-adaptive without any gradient-based training.

## Method

SEKA learns a 'relevance subspace' per KV head by computing cross-covariance matrices between neutral and positive/negative key embeddings from synthetic contrastive prompts, then extracting top (positive) and bottom (negative) left singular vectors via SVD to form projection matrices P⁺ and P⁻. At inference, highlighted key vectors are edited as k′ = k + (g⁺P⁺k + g⁻P⁻k)/2, which is algebraically equivalent to adding a low-rank, key-dependent bias to attention logits but operates entirely before the attention computation, preserving FlashAttention compatibility. Only KV heads whose positive-negative key embedding ℓ₂ distance exceeds a tunable threshold δ_min are steered, concentrating intervention on empirically relevance-sensitive heads in mid-to-late layers. AdaSEKA extends this by learning M task-specific expert projections (from CounterFact, BiasBios, HotpotQA, and a synthetic set) and, at inference, computing query-adaptive routing coefficients α_m via the weighted alignment of the last token's query with each expert's top-K singular vectors, then constructing a blended projection matrix on the fly. The entire pipeline is training-free: all projections are learned offline from a small number of contrastive samples, and inference adds only ~0.03 s latency per sample versus +1.03 s for PASTA.

## Results

On the CounterFact benchmark, SEKA achieves near-perfect Efficacy Scores (98.0–99.1%) across all five models (Qwen3-4B/8B/14B, Gemma3-4B/12B), substantially outperforming PASTA (68.3–97.2%) and SPA (65.2–93.9%). On Bias in Bios, SEKA and AdaSEKA secure the top two positions on most models (e.g., 92.42% accuracy for SEKA on Gemma3-4B vs. 82.58% for PASTA). On the Pronoun Changing task, AdaSEKA delivers SOTA performance (e.g., 99.52 A.P. Score on Qwen3-8B), with the advantage most pronounced on Gemma3-4B where the base model is less responsive to markdown emphasis. Inference overhead is negligible: SEKA adds only +0.03 s per sample and +0.03 GB peak memory, compared to PASTA's +1.03 s and +23.12 GB.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against PASTA (Zhang et al., 2024), SEKA achieves a clear and consistent advantage on CounterFact across all five models, with the gap widening for larger models (e.g., 98.92 vs. 76.84 ES on Qwen3-14B; 98.86 vs. 68.30 on Gemma3-12B). On Bias in Bios, SEKA/AdaSEKA outperform PASTA on four of five models, though PASTA retains a narrow edge on Gemma3-12B (94.72 vs. 93.04). On Pronoun Changing, the comparison is mixed: PASTA's best head-search configuration wins on Qwen3-4B (95.82 vs. 95.18 P.Score), but SEKA/AdaSEKA dominate on Qwen3-14B (98.66/99.88 vs. 90.98) and both Gemma3 models (e.g., 93.76 vs. 67.39 on Gemma3-4B). Against SPA (Tian & Zhang, 2025), SEKA and AdaSEKA win decisively on every task and model, with the largest margins on Bias in Bios where SPA scores as low as 37.02% (Qwen3-8B) versus 88.74% for SEKA. Overall, SEKA/AdaSEKA rank in the top two on 23 of 25 model–metric combinations, and the efficiency gap is stark: SEKA adds +0.03 s latency versus PASTA's +1.03 s and SPA's +5.32 s.

## Relation to Prior Work

SEKA adapts the Spectral Editing of Activations (SEA) framework (Qiu et al., 2024) from MLP-layer activation steering to token-level key-embedding steering, shifting the intervention point from post-attention matrix editing (as in PASTA, Zhang et al., 2024) to pre-attention key modification. This architectural repositioning is the paper's core departure: by editing key vectors before the QK^T computation, SEKA avoids materialising the full attention matrix and thus becomes compatible with FlashAttention, a constraint that renders all prior post-hoc steering methods (PASTA, positional calibration) infeasible in modern inference stacks. AdaSEKA further extends the single-projection design with a query-adaptive multi-expert routing mechanism, drawing on the contrastive SVD learning procedure but applying it across task-specific datasets to enable dynamic, intent-aware steering without per-task hyperparameter tuning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is substantial: SEKA's +0.03s overhead versus PASTA's +1.03s and SPA's +5.32s makes attention steering viable in production long-context pipelines that rely on FlashAttention, a constraint that has effectively blocked prior methods from deployment. The demonstrated U-shape inversion in the lost-in-the-middle setting shows that positional recall is not a fixed architectural limitation but a controllable property, opening the door to targeted attention interventions for specific context regions. Limitations include the need for per-model offline SVD computation, residual hyperparameter sensitivity (particularly δ_min and g+), and the fact that AdaSEKA's dynamic routing still incurs moderate memory overhead (+15.59 GB at batch 10). Open questions include whether the contrastive-prompt construction generalises to domains without clean positive/negative pairings, and whether the spectral relevance subspace remains stable under fine-tuning or domain shift.
