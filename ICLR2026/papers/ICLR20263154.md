## Problem

VLM-based embodied agents that perceive and plan directly from egocentric visual input open a new attack surface: visual backdoor attacks where a physical object in the scene triggers a multi-step malicious policy. Unlike textual triggers or static pixel patches, object-based visual triggers vary substantially across viewpoints and lighting, making reliable implantation and precise activation a fundamentally harder problem that has not been addressed for embodied agents.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263154/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At each timestep the agent receives an egocentric RGB frame (with bounding-box annotations in VAB-OmniGibson, without in EB-ALFRED), a fixed user instruction, and a textual interaction history of prior actions and environment feedback; it outputs a single discrete action from a predefined vocabulary (e.g., grasp, move, put_inside). The backdoor trigger is a physical object (knife or vase) placed in the scene whose appearance changes with viewpoint, and the malicious behavior is a coherent multi-step plan averaging 9 actions rather than a single-step output, requiring sustained policy-level control over the episode.

## Method

BEAT implants the backdoor via a two-stage fine-tuning scheme. Stage 1 (SFT) trains on a mixed corpus of benign trajectories and backdoor trajectories—where a rule-based agent executes the attacker's multi-step plan after the trigger first appears—giving the VLM competence in both benign and malicious behaviors. Stage 2 (Contrastive Trigger Learning, CTL) formulates trigger discrimination as a DPO-style preference-learning problem: for each trigger step, a paired contrastive example is constructed by replaying the same scene with and without the trigger object, yielding identical interaction histories that differ only in the visual input; the model is trained to prefer benign actions on trigger-free frames and attack actions on trigger-present frames. The CTL objective combines the standard DPO log-ratio term with an NLL regularization weighted by α to anchor the policy near the SFT reference and prevent catastrophic drift. This contrastive formulation is the key mechanism that sharpens the decision boundary around trigger presence, reducing false-triggering rates from up to 80% (naive SFT) to near zero while boosting attack success rates by up to 39% under limited backdoor data.

## Results

BEAT achieves attack success rates (ASR) up to 80.8% (InternVL3-8B on EB-ALFRED) and 77.9% (Qwen2-VL-7B on VAB-OmniGibson), while maintaining or improving benign success rates (SR) over Benign SFT (e.g., 23.0% vs. 19.0% for InternVL3-8B on VAB). The full BEAT pipeline achieves near-zero false triggering rate (FTR = 0.0%) across all settings, compared to FTRs of 7–81.3% for BEAT w/o CTL. The backdoor triggering F1 score (F1_BT) reaches 0.951 (InternVL3-8B on VAB) and 0.872 (InternVL3-8B on EB-ALFRED), demonstrating precise trigger-conditioned activation. Successful backdoor trajectories require an average of 9.0 steps, confirming coherent multi-step malicious plan execution.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares BEAT (full two-stage SFT + CTL) against three baselines: the Original pretrained model, Benign SFT (fine-tuned only on benign trajectories), and BEAT w/o CTL (SFT on benign + attack data without contrastive learning). Compared to BEAT w/o CTL, the full BEAT pipeline yields absolute ASR gains of up to 30.3% (Qwen2-VL-7B on VAB: 77.9% vs. 47.6%) and 19.0% (Qwen2-VL-7B on EB-ALFRED: 59.2% vs. 40.2%), while reducing FTR from 7–81.3% to 0.0% across all settings. Critically, BEAT w/o CTL degrades benign SR by up to 60% relative to Benign SFT (e.g., 11.0% vs. 19.0% for InternVL3-8B on VAB), whereas full BEAT actually surpasses Benign SFT in SR (23.0% vs. 19.0%), demonstrating that CTL preserves and even enhances benign competence. For GPT-4o, only SFT is applied (no CTL due to API limitations), resulting in lower ASR (32.4% on VAB) and non-zero FTR (10.0%), highlighting the importance of the contrastive stage. The F1_BT metric shows the largest relative improvement from CTL: from 0.562 to 0.951 for InternVL3-8B on VAB, confirming that preference-based trigger discrimination is the key differentiator.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the backdoor-attack-on-embodied-agents line initiated by Jiao et al. (2024), which targeted LLM-based agents with static textual triggers and single-turn outputs, to the VLM-based setting where triggers are physical objects with wide viewpoint and lighting variability and the malicious behavior is a multi-step policy. Methodologically, it repurposes DPO-style preference optimization (Rafailov et al., 2023) and the NLL-anchored variant from Pang et al. (2024) as a novel mechanism for sharpening trigger-discrimination boundaries, a use of preference learning that is, to the authors' knowledge, new in the backdoor-attack literature. Unlike prior visual backdoor work on VLMs (Ni et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024c) that employed static, low-variability triggers (corner patches, red balloons) to induce single-step misclassification, BEAT confronts the substantially harder problem of object-level triggers whose appearance changes with pose, occlusion, and scene context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By demonstrating that a fine-tuned VLM-based embodied agent can be covertly steered into executing multi-step malicious plans (up to 80% ASR) while maintaining near-zero false-triggering and even improving benign-task success over a benign-only SFT baseline, the paper exposes a concrete and previously unexplored attack surface as VLM-driven robots approach real-world deployment. Key open questions include whether effective defenses exist (the paper shows activation clustering and safety prompts are largely ineffective, while continual benign SFT works but is computationally prohibitive for end users), whether the results transfer to real-world (non-simulated) environments, and whether the CTL framework generalizes to more complex trigger types such as object co-occurrence or event-based triggers. The simulation-only evaluation and reliance on bounding-box annotations in one benchmark limit the immediate practical threat assessment.
