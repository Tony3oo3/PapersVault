## Problem

Multinomial logistic regression (MLR) is the standard framework for multi-class neural decoding but requires a finite set of discrete output classes, making it inapplicable to the many decoding tasks with continuous-valued outputs such as orientation, position, or velocity. Discretizing continuous outputs into bins to force-fit MLR reduces effective resolution, introduces quantization artifacts, and requires additional regularization to prevent overfitting. Standard regression models, while handling continuous outputs, yield only point estimates and cannot represent the multimodal, circular, or asymmetric conditional densities commonly observed in neural population data.

## Input / Output

The model takes a D-dimensional vector of neural population activity (spike counts or calcium imaging signals) and produces a full probability density over a continuous output variable y defined on a compact domain Ω (e.g., [0, 2π) for orientation, [0,1] for position, or [-30,30]² for 2D velocity). The output resolution is user-controlled via a decoding grid parameter J, allowing arbitrarily fine-grained densities; the density can be summarized by its posterior mean (for regression-style tasks) or mode (for classification-style tasks). For circular variables a periodic RBF kernel ensures continuity around the unit circle, and for multi-dimensional outputs an anisotropic RBF kernel assigns separate length scales per dimension.

## Method

CMLR generalizes MLR by replacing the discrete set of class weight vectors with D smooth weight functions w_d(y), each drawn from an independent Gaussian process prior with an RBF (or periodic RBF) kernel, so that the conditional density is p(y|x) ∝ exp(w(y)ᵀx). To make inference tractable, the weight functions are parameterized in the Fourier domain, exploiting the fact that the RBF kernel is diagonalized by a Fourier basis; spectral truncation to M ≪ N, T components reduces the infinite-dimensional inference problem to a finite one. Training proceeds via stochastic variational inference with mini-batches, where the normalizing constant is approximated by Riemann integration over T bins, the expected log-likelihood is estimated by Monte Carlo sampling, and the KL divergence admits a closed form under the fully factorized Gaussian variational family. The resulting algorithm scales linearly in the number of neurons D and only modestly in the number of samples N, with decoding performance largely insensitive to the number of Fourier components M beyond roughly 20.

## Results

On mouse V1 drifting grating orientation decoding, CMLR achieves a mean absolute circular error of 3.1° (median 2.1°), outperforming FlexCode (3.2°), Naive Bayes (4.9°), XGBoost (13.6°), and DNN (18.3°). On mouse hippocampal CA1 position decoding, CMLR achieves a mean absolute error of 0.15 (median 0.01), marginally beating FlexCode (0.16) and Naive Bayes (0.16) while clearly outperforming XGBoost (0.16, median 0.11) and DNN (0.18). On monkey motor cortex 2D velocity decoding, CMLR achieves R² = 0.53, approaching XGBoost (0.55) and DNN (0.58) while substantially outperforming FlexCode (0.35) and Naive Bayes (−0.43). CMLR also produces well-calibrated posteriors (ECE ≈ 0.02–0.04) compared to FlexCode's systematic miscalibration (ECE ≈ 0.03–0.09).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against FlexCode (Izbicki & Lee, 2017), the leading nonparametric CDE method, CMLR wins on mouse V1 (3.1° vs 3.2° mean circular error) and motor cortex (R² 0.53 vs 0.35), ties on mouse CA1 (0.15 vs 0.16), and is marginally behind on monkey V1 (14° vs 13°); CMLR also produces substantially better-calibrated posteriors (ECE 0.02–0.04 vs 0.03–0.09). Against XGBoost (Chen & Guestrin, 2016), CMLR achieves a large advantage on mouse V1 (3.1° vs 13.6°) and monkey V1 (14° vs 27°), a moderate advantage on CA1 (median 0.01 vs 0.11), but a small deficit on the large motor cortex dataset (R² 0.53 vs 0.55), where high-capacity tree ensembles benefit from the 25k-sample size. Against the DNN baseline (Glaser et al., 2020), CMLR wins decisively on mouse V1 (3.1° vs 18.3°) and monkey V1 (14° vs 26°), wins on CA1 (0.15 vs 0.18), and is slightly behind on motor cortex (R² 0.53 vs 0.58). The comparison with the correlation-blind Naive Bayes decoder (same GP priors but assuming conditional independence) highlights the importance of modeling noise correlations: CMLR outperforms NB on every dataset, with the largest gap on mouse V1 (3.1° vs 4.9°) and motor cortex (R² 0.53 vs −0.43). Overall, CMLR's advantages are most pronounced in low-data regimes and for circular/multimodal outputs, where its GP-based functional priors and full conditional density provide structural regularization that point-estimate regressors lack.

## Relation to Prior Work

CMLR extends multinomial logistic regression (MLR) for neural decoding by replacing discrete class weight vectors with smooth, output-indexed weight functions drawn from independent Gaussian process priors, yielding a continuous-limit generalization of the discrete MLR framework in Greenidge et al. (2024). The model is conceptually rooted in logistic Gaussian process density estimation (Tokdar et al., 2004; Riihimäki & Vehtari, 2014), but departs from those unconditional density models by introducing a conditional, feature-decomposed structure that preserves MLR's additive interpretability. For scalable inference, the paper adapts Fourier-domain sparse GP representations (Hensman et al., 2018; Keeley et al., 2020; Gondur et al., 2024) to the CDE setting, and positions itself against the leading nonparametric CDE method FlexCode (Izbicki & Lee, 2017) as a more structured, interpretable alternative.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CMLR is, to the authors' knowledge, the first CDE framework applied to neural decoding, providing full calibrated posterior densities (rather than point estimates) that naturally handle circular, multimodal, and asymmetric output distributions common in neuroscience. The well-evidenced finding that a simple GP-based additive model with only ~17–27 Fourier components matches or exceeds deep networks and XGBoost across four brain regions and two species—particularly in low-data regimes—suggests that principled inductive bias can substitute for model capacity in this domain. Key limitations include the absence of temporal or spatial smoothness priors on outputs, independent (non-shared) weight functions across neurons, and the additivity assumption that precludes higher-order feature interactions; integrating dynamical models, multivariate GP priors, or kernelized interactions are natural next steps.
