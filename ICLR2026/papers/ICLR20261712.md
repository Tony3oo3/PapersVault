## Problem

Standard AI alignment methods learn preference models from pairwise comparisons using hypothesis classes (linear models, neural networks, decision trees) that are agnostic to the cognitive processes humans actually employ, such as thresholding heuristics, tallying, and context-dependent feature interactions. This misalignment is especially problematic in high-stakes moral domains like healthcare, where stakeholders expect AI to justify decisions in a manner analogous to human reasoning, and where well-documented heuristics remain invisible or opaque within standard model classes.

## Input / Output

The method consumes individual-level pairwise comparison triples (x₁, x₂, r) where each option carries d tabular features and r ∈ {0,1} is the binary choice, and produces a two-stage model: per-feature editing functions h_inn^i mapping raw feature values to real-valued contribution scores (constrained monotonic), plus a fixed aggregation rule h_out (sigmoid or identity). The interpretable output is the set of editing functions, which reveal thresholding, diminishing returns, and conditional feature interactions governed by a context set ω, rather than a single opaque score vector.

## Method

The paper derives a two-stage hypothesis class from five axioms—complementarity, weak transitivity, codomain span, noninteractive compositionality (NC), and conditionally interactive compositionality (CIC)—that are strictly weaker than classical choice-theory axioms and do not assume linear utility or global transitivity. Stage 1 applies per-feature editing rules h_inn^{i, x^{ω_i}} that may be conditional on a context set ω, capturing structured feature interactions; Stage 2 aggregates via h_out over the sum of per-feature differences between the two options. Theorem 3.4 proves these axioms induce the two-stage factoring, while Theorem 3.6 shows that adding σ-linearity recovers logistic regression, monotonicity recovers monotonic models, and relaxing NC to CIC yields a hybrid decision-tree/GAM structure. Training minimizes cross-entropy or hinge loss over the editing functions, with the context set ω selected via cross-validation.

## Results

On the kidney allocation datasets, the proposed two-stage cognitively-faithful models achieve mean test accuracy of 0.90 (±0.06) on Study One, 0.90 (±0.05) with cross-entropy loss and 0.89 (±0.06) with hinge loss on Study Two, and 0.89 (±0.10) / 0.89 (±0.08) on the simulated decision-maker dataset. These results match or exceed all baselines across all three datasets, with the largest margins over Bradley-Terry (0.78 on Study Two, 0.77 on Simulated) and Decision Trees (0.79 on Study Two, 0.82 on Simulated). The learned editing functions additionally recover interpretable heuristics (thresholding, conditional interactions, feature irrelevance) that logistic and decision-tree baselines fail to surface.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Bradley-Terry (the standard probabilistic pairwise-comparison model), the proposed method ties on Study One (0.90 vs. 0.90) but substantially outperforms it on Study Two (0.90 vs. 0.78) and the simulated dataset (0.89 vs. 0.77), indicating that the rigid additive-utility structure of Bradley-Terry fails to capture the heuristic and threshold-based processing observed in real decision-makers. Against GAM with spline terms, the proposed method wins on Study One (0.90 vs. 0.87) and Study Two (0.90 vs. 0.84) while slightly trailing on Simulated (0.89 vs. 0.88), suggesting that the axiomatic two-stage structure provides a more faithful inductive bias than unconstrained spline flexibility. Against logistic regression, the proposed method ties on Study One (0.90 vs. 0.90) and Study Two (0.90/0.89 vs. 0.89) but clearly wins on Simulated (0.89 vs. 0.85), where the simulated decision-makers explicitly use non-linear threshold heuristics that a linear model cannot represent. Against decision trees, the proposed method consistently outperforms across all three datasets (e.g., 0.90 vs. 0.83 on Study One, 0.89 vs. 0.82 on Simulated) while also providing smoother, more interpretable feature-level editing functions. Overall, the proposed framework matches or surpasses every baseline in predictive accuracy while uniquely recovering the cognitive heuristics (thresholding, conditional feature interactions, feature irrelevance) that the baselines either miss or encode opaquely.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of axiomatic choice theory and cognitive science by deriving a two-stage hypothesis class (feature-level editing rules + dominance testing aggregation) from a set of deliberately weaker axioms than classical choice theory (no full transitivity, no IIA, no linear utility). It departs from standard preference-learning approaches (Bradley-Terry, logistic regression, neural networks) that are agnostic to cognitive processes, and from prior axiomatic work on pairwise comparisons (Noothigattu et al., 2020; Ge et al., 2024) whose axioms concern MLE estimation properties rather than structural constraints on how preferences must behave. The framework subsumes logistic regression, probit regression, and GAMs as special cases under additional domain-specific assumptions (linearity, monotonicity), positioning it as a generalization rather than a replacement.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

In high-stakes moral domains like kidney allocation, stakeholders expect AI to justify decisions in a manner analogous to human reasoning, making cognitive fidelity and interpretability essential for trust. The paper demonstrates that a structured, cognitively-motivated model can match or slightly exceed the accuracy of black-box baselines while revealing the specific heuristics (thresholding, conditional interactions, tallying) individuals actually use. A key open question the authors raise is whether their axioms characterize an individual's 'ideal' decision process versus their actual (potentially intransitive or inconsistent) behavior, and how to quantify deviations between the two. The interpretability of learned editing functions still requires validation through dedicated user studies to confirm that humans find the explanations meaningful and trustworthy.
