## Problem

The statistical sample complexity of Graph Neural Networks remains poorly understood: it is unclear how many labeled samples a GNN needs to generalize on a given graph, and how graph topology shapes this requirement. Prior theoretical work addresses only the inductive graph-level regime, while most widely used benchmarks (ogbn_arxiv, ogbn_products, Reddit) operate in the transductive node-level setting where node labels on a single slowly mixing graph exhibit substantial redundancy. This paper establishes the first minimax lower bounds for ReLU message-passing GNNs in both regimes, showing that graph structure can fundamentally alter the required sample size.

## Input / Output

The method operates on a graph G=(V,E) with adjacency matrix A and node feature vectors X_v ∈ R^d. In the inductive (graph-level) setting, each training example is an independent graph and the GNN outputs a single graph-level prediction ŷ ∈ R^q. In the transductive (node-level) setting, a single fixed graph is observed with a uniformly random subset S of n labeled nodes, and the model outputs a prediction vector ŷ_v for every node v ∈ V. The GNN architecture is constrained to L-layer ReLU message passing with input-independent permutation-invariant aggregation (sum or mean) and a uniform ℓ₁-norm budget ∑_ℓ(‖W^(ℓ)‖₁+‖B^(ℓ)‖₁) ≤ v_s on layer weights.

## Method

The core technical contribution is a minimax lower-bound analysis via Fano's inequality combined with Varshamov-Gilbert packing constructions. For the inductive regime (Theorem 1), a packing set is built by varying first-layer coordinate selectors on path graphs, yielding a worst-case risk of Ω(√(log d/n)) that matches classical ReLU network rates. For the transductive regime (Theorem 2), under a spectral-homophily condition (normalized Laplacian spectral gap λ₂ ≤ κ/log n), the proof identifies only K = Θ(log n) effectively independent blocks due to heavy overlap of message-passing neighborhoods on slowly mixing graphs, collapsing the effective sample size and tightening the minimax risk to Ω(d/log n). Empirically, the paper validates both bounds using ratio-based scaling diagnostics (Err(n)/√(log d/n) and Err(n)/(d/log n)) across GCN, GAT, and GraphSAGE on three real benchmarks and two controlled synthetic datasets, showing that real graphs fall in the spectral-homophily regime where the d/log n rate is empirically tight.

## Results

The paper establishes two minimax lower bounds for ReLU message-passing GNNs: Theorem 1 gives a worst-case inductive rate of Ω(√(log d/n)) on adversarial path graphs, while Theorem 2 proves a tighter transductive rate of Ω(d/log n) under the spectral-homophily condition λ₂ ≤ κ/log n. Empirically, ratio diagnostics Err(n)/(d/log n) remain stable (C* ≈ 15–25 for ogbn_arxiv, 18–22 for ogbn_products_50k, 10–20 for Reddit_50k, 8–12 for WorstCase_Bottleneck_20k) across two to three orders of magnitude in n, while Err(n)/√(log d/n) increases steadily, confirming the d/log n regime. All three real benchmarks satisfy the spectral-homophily condition (λ₂ values: 0.2112, 0.9201, 0.9683 with κ₀ ≤ 10.5), and the synthetic Theorem-1 construction follows √(log d/n) exactly, validating both bounds.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper, so the 'comparison' is between two candidate scaling rates rather than competing architectures. The primary empirical test is whether error decays as √(log d/n) (Theorem 1, classical deep-learning rate) or d/log n (Theorem 2, structure-limited rate). Across all three real benchmarks and three architectures (GCN, GAT, GraphSAGE), the d/log n ratio remains flat while the √(log d/n) ratio grows, unambiguously selecting Theorem 2's rate. The synthetic Theorem-1 dataset (Synthetic-FanoWorstCase) shows the opposite pattern: its √(log d/n) ratio is stable near 1 while d/log n diverges, confirming the two regimes are genuinely distinct. Among architectures, GCN sometimes shows curve fits closer to 1/n or 1/√n (e.g., R²=0.9589 for 1/√n on ogbn_arxiv), while GAT and GraphSAGE more consistently favor 1/log n fits, attributed to their adaptive mixing increasing neighborhood overlap. The paper explicitly notes curve fits are secondary evidence (1/log n is best-fit in only 3/13 dataset-architecture pairs) and that ratio diagnostics carry the primary evidential weight.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the minimax sample-complexity framework from feed-forward ReLU networks (Golestaneh et al., 2024) to message-passing GNNs, introducing graph topology as a first-class variable in the analysis. It is positioned as complementary to recent upper-bound work by Franks et al. (2024) and Pellizzoni et al. (2024), which characterize what is achievable under expressivity-enhancing augmentations; here the authors establish fundamental lower bounds for standard ReLU MPNNs without such augmentations. The key conceptual departure is the introduction of a spectral-homophily condition (small Laplacian spectral gap) that links graph mixing geometry to statistical efficiency, a dimension absent from prior GNN generalization analyses (VC-dimension, PAC-Bayesian, or covering-number approaches).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that practical GNN benchmarks (ogbn_arxiv, ogbn_products, Reddit) all fall in the spectral-homophily regime where the minimax rate is d/log n rather than the classical 1/√n challenges the assumption that GNNs inherit standard deep-learning generalization behavior, with the striking implication of exponential sample complexity in 1/ε². Open questions include whether architectures or sampling strategies can break the slow-mixing bottleneck, how the bounds extend to higher-order or attention-based models beyond the adjacency-masked setting, and whether the spectral-homophily condition is truly necessary or merely sufficient for the d/log n rate. The empirical validation, while consistent, relies on ratio-stability diagnostics across only three real datasets and three architectures, leaving the generality of the structural claim somewhat under-tested.
