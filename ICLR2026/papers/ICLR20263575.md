## Problem

Controlling stochastic nonlinear dynamics across multiple interacting objects is difficult because existing Koopman-based methods assume deterministic dynamics and ignore relational topology, while GNN-based predictors lack the linear structure needed for direct control synthesis. Compositional Koopman approaches that do incorporate graphs assume uniform neighbor influence (a probabilistically misspecified simplification) and provide no theoretical guarantees for stochastic settings or scalability to random topologies.

## Input / Output

The method consumes graph-structured multi-object observations (continuous-state vectors per node), a fixed binary adjacency matrix encoding pairwise interactions, and a predefined target observation feature in RKHS. A message-passing GNN encoder maps each object's history and future observation into finite-dimensional RKHS features, while actions use a fixed linear projection to keep them amenable to convex optimization. The output is an M-step optimal action sequence obtained by solving a quadratic LQR cost in feature space via convex optimization, along with predicted observation features at each step for rollout.

## Method

GCE embeds the conditional distribution P(O_t | a_t, h_t) of each object's next observation into an RKHS via characteristic kernel features, making stochastic dynamics evolve linearly under a conditional embedding operator. The global operator is decomposed into per-edge pair-wise operators C_{O^i|A^jH^j}, then the tensor product of history and action features is disentangled into a concatenated linear form to enable tractable sequential action optimization. A mean field approximation with adaptive Boltzmann-Gibbs weights (computed via a Nadaraya-Watson kernel estimator over history features) replaces O(N²) neighbor-specific history operators with a single shared operator, reducing sample complexity while preserving non-uniform interaction strengths. Training minimizes a forward loss (Hilbert-Schmidt norm between predicted and true observation features) plus a reconstruction loss through a GNN decoder, and control is synthesized by solving a finite-horizon LQR problem directly in the learned RKHS.

## Results

On the Swim environment (Table 3), GCE with Gaussian kernel achieves the best in-distribution control cost (383.7±77.8) and control error (0.41±0.08), outperforming CKO (389.1/0.42) and KPM (385.5/0.44). In few-shot validation on unseen graphs, GCE(Gaussian) achieves the lowest control cost (404.3±74.2) and error (0.41±0.09), beating CKO (421.0/0.44) and KPM (523.4/0.61). On the large-scale Power-Grid task with 100–150 objects and random topologies, GCE(Gaussian) achieves a noiseless control error of 0.21±0.005 versus 0.47±0.031 for CKO, and remains stable up to 20% noise (0.83) while KPM and GraphODE produce NaN (unstable) results above 2% noise.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GCE(Gaussian) outperforms all four baselines on both in-distribution and few-shot metrics in the Swim environment, with the largest gaps over VAE (−189 cost, −0.32 error in-distribution) and PCC (−129 cost, −0.27 error). Against the closest competitor CKO (Compositional Koopman Operator), GCE wins by a small margin in-distribution (383.7 vs. 389.1 cost) but pulls ahead more clearly in few-shot (404.3 vs. 421.0 cost; 0.41 vs. 0.44 error), consistent with the paper's argument that CKO's uniform-weight assumption is misspecified. KPM (Koopman Polynomial Model) is competitive in-distribution (385.5 cost) but degrades sharply in few-shot (523.4 cost, 0.61 error) because its polynomial features are not characteristic kernels. GraphODE, which lacks an explicit controllable embedding design, trails GCE by ~64 in cost and 0.11 in error in-distribution, and by ~289 in cost in few-shot. On the Power-Grid stress test, GCE(Gaussian) achieves 0.21 control error noiselessly (vs. 0.47 for CKO) and remains stable at 20% noise (0.83) while KPM and GraphODE fail entirely (NaN) above 2% noise.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate research threads: Hilbert space embeddings of conditional distributions (Song et al., 2009; Fukumizu et al., 2013) for stochastic dynamics, and graph neural network representations for multi-object interactions. It departs from compositional Koopman operators (Li et al., 2020), which the paper identifies as a misspecified special case (uniform-weight homogeneous form), by introducing stochastic handling via RKHS embeddings and non-uniform neighbor influence via mean field approximation with Boltzmann-Gibbs weights. The key conceptual departure is that prior controllable embeddings (Koopman, VAE-based locally linear) either ignore relational topology or lack stochastic rigor, while prior GNN-based multi-object methods lack explicit linear structure for control.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous theoretical framework (consistency, convergence, sample complexity bounds) for controllable embeddings in stochastic multi-object systems, filling a gap where prior methods lacked formal guarantees. The mean field approximation with adaptive weights offers a practical path to scaling to large random-topology graphs with provably low sample complexity, validated on power grids with 100–150 nodes. Limitations include restriction to pairwise relations (no hypergraphs), reliance on quadratic LQR costs, and the fact that the neural potential for energy functions proved unstable, leaving the framework somewhat dependent on kernel choices. Open questions include extending to richer relational structures, non-quadratic control objectives, and whether the homogeneity assumption on shared operators holds in truly heterogeneous systems.
