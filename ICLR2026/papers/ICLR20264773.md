## Problem

State-of-the-art probabilistic time series forecasting models rely on sampling to represent future uncertainty, which suffers from three inherent limitations: generated trajectories lack explicit probabilities (Probability Absence), finite sample sets may miss rare high-impact tail events (Coverage Inadequacy), and inference cost scales linearly with the number of samples required. This paper introduces the Probabilistic Scenarios paradigm, which directly produces a finite set of {Scenario, Probability} pairs in a single forward pass, eliminating the need for Monte Carlo approximation.

## Input / Output

The model takes a multivariate time series history of length L (set equal to the forecast horizon T: 24 for hourly datasets, 30 for daily) with D variates as input. It produces N = M×K future trajectory scenarios (each of shape T×D) formed by combinatorially adding M trend forecasts and K seasonal forecasts, along with a probability vector of length N summing to 1. Unlike sampling-based methods that require S separate forward passes, TimePrism generates all scenarios and their probabilities in one pass, making inference cost independent of the desired number of scenarios.

## Method

TimePrism is a proof-of-concept model composed of only three parallel linear layers operating on a trend-seasonal decomposition of the input. A moving-average filter splits the history into trend and seasonal components; two independent linear layers map these to M trend forecasts and K seasonal forecasts respectively, and the N = M×K scenarios are formed by all pairwise sums. A third linear layer takes the undecomposed history and outputs N logits, converted to probabilities via softmax. Training uses a two-term loss: a Winner-Takes-All reconstruction loss that only penalizes the single scenario closest to ground truth (with a relaxed variant at ε=0.01 to prevent parameter stagnation), and a Cross-Entropy probability loss that trains the model to assign highest probability to the winning scenario. The key insight is reframing the learning objective from continuous density estimation over R^{T×D} to a structured two-part problem—learning a discrete scenario basis and a categorical distribution over it—which reduces required model capacity enough that a purely linear architecture achieves 9 out of 10 state-of-the-art results across five benchmark datasets.

## Results

TimePrism achieves 9 out of 10 state-of-the-art results across five benchmark datasets on two primary metrics. On Weighted CRPS (measuring Probability Absence), TimePrism attains the best score on 4/5 datasets (Electricity: 0.133, Exchange: 0.468, Solar: 0.0852, Traffic: 0.111) and second-best on Wikipedia (0.506 vs. TACTiS-2's 0.484). On Distortion (measuring Coverage Inadequacy), TimePrism achieves the best score on all five datasets (Electricity: 0.211, Exchange: 0.595, Solar: 0.101, Traffic: 0.144, Wikipedia: 1.04). Inference cost is constant at 5.1×10⁵ FLOPs regardless of the number of scenarios, compared to 1.9×10⁹ for TimeGrad and 1.2×10⁸ for TACTiS-2 at S=100 samples.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TimePrism outperforms all six baselines on Distortion across all five datasets, with the largest margins against ETS (e.g., 0.211 vs. 1.24 on Electricity) and TempFlow (0.101 vs. 0.515 on Solar). On CRPS, TimePrism beats TimeGrad by 42–53% (e.g., 0.133 vs. 0.232 on Electricity, 0.0852 vs. 0.241 on Solar) and TACTiS-2 by 28–55% on four datasets, losing only on Wikipedia (0.506 vs. 0.484) where the high dimensionality (D=2000) and weak seasonality challenge the weight-sharing linear design. Against TimeMCL (the closest conceptual competitor), TimePrism wins on all five datasets for both metrics, with CRPS improvements of 28–64% (e.g., 0.133 vs. 0.370 on Electricity), confirming that explicit probability assignment via the cross-entropy loss resolves the Probability Absence trade-off that TimeMCL acknowledges. The efficiency gap is stark: TimePrism's 5.1×10⁵ FLOPs (constant) versus TimeGrad's 1.9×10⁹ and TACTiS-2's 1.2×10⁸ at S=100 samples represents a 1–5 order-of-magnitude reduction in inference cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Multiple Choice Learning (MCL) framework and its Winner-Takes-All loss (Guzmán-rivera et al., 2012; Rupprecht et al., 2017), extending them from scenario generation to explicit probability assignment over scenarios. It departs from the dominant sampling-based paradigm (DeepAR, TimeGrad, TempFlow, TACTiS-2) and from TimeMCL (Cortés et al., 2025), which produces discrete scenarios but lacks a valid probability distribution over them. The backbone draws on the simplicity of DLinear and FITS, but the core contribution is the reframing of the learning objective from continuous density estimation to a structured two-part task: discrete scenario representation plus categorical probability assignment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Probabilistic Scenarios paradigm offers a principled alternative to sampling that simultaneously addresses Probability Absence, Coverage Inadequacy, and Inference Cost, with the added benefit of interpretable scenario-probability pairs for decision-making. Key open questions include whether the paradigm scales to more complex backbones (Transformers, diffusion, flow matching), whether adaptive scenario counts can be learned, and whether decision-centric metrics (tail-risk, utility-based scores) further validate the approach. The current proof-of-concept relies on a decomposition backbone and weight-sharing that suit the benchmark datasets well, so generalization to trend-free or highly heterogeneous series remains untested.
