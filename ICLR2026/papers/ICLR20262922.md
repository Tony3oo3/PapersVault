## Problem

Current speech-language models treat speech understanding as a monolithic black box, aggregating outputs from isolated sub-tasks (ASR, emotion, intent) without modeling the intrinsic causal dependencies among speech components. This prevents genuine reasoning over how situational context, speaker affect, communicative function, and pragmatic goal interact, particularly under the sparse and partial supervision typical of real-world speech data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262922/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system consumes raw speech audio, which is decomposed into three feature streams: WavLM acoustic embeddings, openSMILE prosodic features (88-dim), and distilBERT text embeddings from the transcription. These are fused via a gated mechanism into a shared representation that conditions four discrete categorical state variables (WMA: 30 classes, ToM: 7 classes, SA: 24 classes, Prag: 14 classes). The final output is a structured pair: a 4–8 sentence causal reasoning trace explaining how the inferred states interact through the graph, followed by a contextually appropriate dialogue response.

## Method

The Speech World Model factorizes speech understanding into a four-node DAG (WMA → SA, ToM → SA, WMA → Prag, ToM → Prag, SA → Prag) where each node is a neural classifier conditioned on its causal parents' states and the fused multimodal input. Training uses edge-wise teacher forcing with a stochastic Bernoulli schedule, and a semi-supervised mechanism that disables teacher forcing on edges into unlabeled modules so that gradients propagate backward through causal edges, allowing unlabeled parents to be optimized via their labeled children. In the second stage, the inferred state vector is injected as explicit conditioning into an instruction-tuned LLM (Llama-3.1-8B for language-only, Qwen2-Audio for multimodal, both via LoRA), which generates the reasoning trace and response. The paper provides a theoretical analysis showing that the sparse causal Jacobian eliminates gradient redundancy present in fully-connected baselines, and that conditioning on the graph's low-entropy state vector acts as a control variate that reduces gradient variance in instruction tuning, yielding ~5× faster convergence over a random-graph baseline.

## Results

On the Model-as-Judge (M.J.) evaluation, SWM (Llama3.1-8B) achieves an overall score of 7.81 (Reasoning 7.84, Response 7.76) with 97.80% emotion mention rate and 66.26% emotion classification accuracy, while SWM (Qwen2-Audio) scores 7.59 overall with 71.02% emotion accuracy. The causal graph converges in 2.07 hours versus 10.39 hours for the Random Graph baseline (~5× speedup), and achieves an Average Causal Effect (ACE) of 23.57% and Intervention Consistency Score (ICS) of 43.29% under fully-supervised training. Total training cost is only ~20 GPU hours (19h language-only + 24.6h multi-modal on 4×A6000), dramatically lower than proprietary alternatives.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SWM (Llama3.1-8B) achieves an overall M.J. score of 7.81, surpassing GPT-4o (7.41) by 0.40 points and all open-source baselines by a wide margin (Qwen-Audio 2.70, Qwen2-Audio 2.63, Voxtral 2.92). In emotion classification accuracy (EA), SWM (Qwen2-Audio) reaches 71.02%, substantially exceeding GPT-4o (45.16%) and Gemini 2.5 Pro (51.29%), while SWM (Llama3.1-8B) achieves 97.80% emotion mention rate versus 68.20% for GPT-4o. Against the tuned CoT baseline (Qwen2-Audio fine-tuned with the same instruction data but without causal graph conditioning), SWM (Qwen2-Audio) improves the overall score from 5.18 to 7.59 and EA from 34.72% to 71.02%, isolating the causal graph's contribution. Gemini 2.5 Pro retains a slight overall edge (8.12 vs. 7.81), but SWM achieves this at roughly 20 GPU hours of training versus the undisclosed (and substantially larger) compute of proprietary models. The causal graph also converges ~5× faster than the unstructured Random Graph (2.07h vs. 10.39h) while maintaining stable ACE/ICS scores, whereas the Random Graph's information flow fluctuates chaotically with hyperparameters.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes three previously separate research threads—cognitive science accounts of speech perception (Hickok & Poeppel's dual-stream model, predictive coding), causal inference (Pearl's do-calculus and DAG factorization), and world models (Ha & Schmidhuber's latent dynamics, LeCun's JEPA)—into a unified causal-graph architecture for speech understanding. It departs from the dominant SLM paradigm (Qwen2-Audio, Voxtral, Audio Flamingo) that treats speech as a monolithic encoder-LLM pipeline, and from heuristic Chain-of-Thought prompting for audio (Audio-CoT, AudioReasoner), by grounding the reasoning structure in an explicit, cognitively-motivated DAG over four discrete latent states (WMA, ToM, SA, Prag). The authors explicitly position their causal graph as a 'third way' between implicit generative world models and symbolic autoregressive language models, providing structured forward dynamics that constrain the LLM's search space during instruction tuning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a small, structured causal prior (20 GPU hours total training) can match or exceed much larger open-source SLMs on speech reasoning, particularly in emotion recognition where it surpasses proprietary models like GPT-4o on the EA metric. The semi-supervised results showing logically localized degradation (only edges connected to an unlabeled module are affected) provide strong evidence that the modules learn genuinely disentangled representations, enabling inference of missing states from partial supervision—a practically important property given the cost of speech annotation. Key limitations include the fixed four-module graph (no learned structure), reliance on a Vicuna-13b label-generation pipeline whose errors propagate, and evaluation via GPT-4o-as-judge which introduces its own biases. Open questions include whether adaptive or learned causal structures can capture richer dependencies, whether the framework scales to more modules and domains, and whether the variance-reduction argument (Eq. 23) holds empirically at larger scale.
