## Problem

Kera et al. (2024) demonstrated that transformers can learn to compute Gröbner bases, but the quadratic cost of flat self-attention restricted them to at most 5 variables with heavily reduced polynomial density (ρ < 1). This paper addresses that scalability bottleneck by exploiting the inherent tree structure of multivariate polynomial systems to replace flat attention with a hierarchical mechanism that dramatically reduces effective sequence lengths.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260248/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a system of multivariate polynomial equations over a finite field (primarily F_7) as input, where each polynomial is tokenized term-by-term: each term is encoded as a coefficient token followed by n exponent tokens (n+2 tokens per term), terms are joined by a '+' token, and polynomials are separated by a '<sep>' token. The output is the unique reduced Gröbner basis in shape position—a triangular set consisting of one univariate polynomial in x_n and n−1 polynomials of the form x_i − g_i(x_n). The hierarchical structure imposes a 3-level nesting (tokens within terms, terms within polynomials, polynomials within the system) that the architecture explicitly mirrors.

## Method

The core contribution is a Hierarchical Attention Transformer (HAT) layer that replaces standard multi-head self-attention as a drop-in module. It operates in two phases: a bottom-up phase where local self-attention is computed at each tree level (leaf tokens → terms → polynomials → system) with pooling to aggregate child representations into parent embeddings, and a top-down phase where cross-attention propagates contextual information from parent nodes back to children. The architecture supports arbitrary tree depth and includes a detailed cost analysis showing that for a regular ℓ-ary tree the attention cost drops from O(L²) to O(L^{1+1/n}), enabling systems with up to 13 variables and degree 11. A learnable multi-axis positional embedding (sum of per-dimension embedding tables) encodes position in the tensor-shaped input. Combined with a Gaussian curriculum learning scheduler that progressively increases variable count and polynomial density during training, the method achieves 61% exact-match accuracy on 13-variable systems over F_7, outperforming classical algorithms STD-FGLM and Msolve which timeout on most instances.

## Results

On 13-variable polynomial systems over F₇, HATSolver-3 achieves 47–61% exact-match success rate across densities (peaking at 61.2% at 90% density), with support accuracy reaching 94% and per-token accuracy consistently above 99.7%. Inference runtime is 224–300 seconds, substantially faster than the 652–1129 s (STD-FGLM) and 588–1274 s (Msolve) runtimes for the small fraction of instances those classical algorithms complete within a 2-hour timeout. The model also demonstrates that curriculum learning lifts exact-match accuracy from 33.85% (no curriculum) to 61.2% at ρ=0.9, and that the hierarchical architecture alone (without curriculum) still captures meaningful structure at 14–34% accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HATSolver-3 decisively outperforms both classical Gröbner basis algorithms across all densities: at 90% density it achieves 61.2% exact-match success versus 6.1% (STD-FGLM) and 4.5% (Msolve), while also running 3–4× faster (292 s vs. 1012 s and 1232 s). The classical algorithms time out on 93.5% of full-density instances, whereas HATSolver-3 completes all evaluations. Against the flat-attention Transformer baseline of Kera et al. (2024), HATSolver-2 and HATSolver-3 converge faster and reach higher accuracy; on the hardest tested configuration (n=10, ρ=0.1), the baseline remains stuck at 0% accuracy while HATSolver-2 and HATSolver-3 reach 9% and 8% respectively. The Hi-Transformer architecture of Wu et al. (2021), adapted for polynomial systems, achieves 75% exact match on n=5 but completely fails to learn on n=13, and is slower than HATSolver-3 in both compute efficiency and learning speed. Curriculum learning applied to the Kera et al. baseline (without HAT) extends its reach from n=5/ρ=0.2 to n=7/ρ=1.0 (41% accuracy), confirming that both the hierarchical architecture and the training schedule contribute independently to scalability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Kera et al. (2024), which first demonstrated that a standard Transformer can learn Gröbner basis computation via supervised sequence-to-sequence training on backward-generated polynomial systems. The key architectural departure is replacing the flat multi-head attention layer with a Hierarchical Attention Transformer (HAT) that exploits the natural tree structure of multivariate polynomial systems (term → polynomial → system), reducing the quadratic attention cost to near-linear for regular trees. The approach also draws on the hierarchical attention literature from NLP (Yang et al. 2016, Wu et al. 2021 Hi-Transformer) and vision (Liu et al. 2024 H-MHSA), but generalizes to arbitrary tree depths and introduces a top-down cross-attention phase absent in prior hierarchical models. Curriculum learning is added as a training strategy, building on the standard curriculum learning paradigm but applied to progressively increasing variable counts and system densities.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that a neural network outperforms specialized classical algorithms (STD-FGLM, Msolve) on 13-variable polynomial systems—where those algorithms time out in over 93% of cases—suggests that learned solvers may eventually complement or replace symbolic algorithms in domains like post-quantum cryptography where Gröbner basis hardness underpins security. However, the ~60% exact-match accuracy (despite 99.8% per-token accuracy) reveals a significant gap: the model captures local structure well but struggles with global consistency, and all results are on backward-generated data rather than truly arbitrary systems. Open questions include generalization to unseen or non-prime finite fields, whether the model implicitly learns algorithmic primitives like S-polynomial reduction, and whether the approach scales beyond the 13-variable/degree-11 regime demonstrated here.
