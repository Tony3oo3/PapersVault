## Problem

The theoretical benefits of online interaction in adversarial imitation learning (AIL) and the role of system stochasticity in determining sample complexity remain poorly understood. Existing model-based IL analyses have not clarified why model-based approaches are advantageous under general function approximation, and the ε^{-1} (deterministic) vs. ε^{-2} (stochastic) gap has not been fully characterized for AIL as opposed to behavioral cloning.

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes N offline expert trajectories and K rounds of online environment interaction, both in a strictly reward-free paradigm where no reward signal is ever observed. It also requires three function classes (policy Π, reward R, transition model P) and operates under bounded cumulative reward (sum ≤ 1) and realizability assumptions. The output is a uniformly mixed imitation policy π̄ = Unif({π^k}) along with horizon-free, second-order sample complexity guarantees that scale with the variance of returns under the relevant policies.

## Method

MB-AIL decomposes policy learning into two parallel sub-procedures: (A) adversarial reward estimation via FTRL, which minimizes an empirical value-difference loss between the current policy and expert trajectories to identify the worst-case reward in R, and (B) transition model learning via maximum likelihood estimation within a version space, followed by optimistic policy planning over the version space. The key insight is that the policy class Π can be factored into the reward class R and model class P, enabling separate estimation with distinct sample complexity contributions. The regret analysis decomposes into reward error (bounded via Bernstein inequalities and union bounds over R) and model error (bounded via Eluder-dimension-based distribution transfer with Hellinger distance), each receiving a fine-grained variance-dependent treatment that yields horizon-free, second-order bounds. Combined with a newly constructed hard-instance family, the analysis establishes minimax-optimality for online interaction (up to log factors) and a log|R| gap for expert demonstrations.

## Results

On MuJoCo benchmarks with 64 expert trajectories, MB-AIL achieves near-expert cumulative reward on Humanoid (5816.4 vs. expert 5884.6), dramatically outperforming all baselines (BC: 342.5, GAIL: 1614.4, OPT-AIL: 2014.4). On Hopper, MB-AIL (3451.3) matches or slightly exceeds OPT-AIL (3438.6), while on Walker2d it is slightly below OPT-AIL (4169.7 vs. 4238.4). In terms of interaction complexity, MB-AIL requires 3–4× fewer environment steps than OPT-AIL across all three environments (e.g., ~60K vs. ~210K on Hopper, ~90K vs. ~220K on Humanoid), confirming the theoretical prediction that model-based approaches yield superior sample efficiency.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GAIL (Ho & Ermon, 2016), MB-AIL wins decisively on all three MuJoCo environments: on Humanoid the gap is enormous (5816.4 vs. 1614.4, a 3.6× improvement), and on Hopper and Walker2d MB-AIL exceeds GAIL by ~240 and ~390 reward points respectively. Against OPT-AIL (Xu et al., 2024), the most recent model-free AIL baseline with general function approximation, MB-AIL achieves comparable or better cumulative reward on Hopper (3451.3 vs. 3438.6) and a large margin on Humanoid (5816.4 vs. 2014.4, a 2.9× improvement), while being slightly lower on Walker2d (4169.7 vs. 4238.4, a ~1.6% gap). Crucially, MB-AIL requires substantially fewer online interaction steps than OPT-AIL across all environments—approximately 3× fewer on Hopper (~60K vs. ~210K) and Walker2d (~120K vs. ~320K), and ~2.4× fewer on Humanoid (~90K vs. ~220K)—validating the paper's theoretical claim that the model-based decomposition into reward learning and model learning yields superior interaction sample complexity.

## Relation to Prior Work

MB-AIL synthesizes two previously separate theoretical threads: the second-order, horizon-free analysis of model-based RL via Eluder dimension and Hellinger distance (Wang et al., 2024) and the adversarial no-regret reward learning framework for imitation learning (Xu et al., 2024; Ho & Ermon, 2016). The key departure is that prior model-based AIL analyses (Xu et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2021) either assumed deterministic experts or yielded horizon-dependent bounds, while prior AIL with general function approximation (Xu et al., 2024) was model-free. By decomposing the policy class into a reward class and a model class, the paper applies optimistic MLE for the transition kernel and FTRL for adversarial reward estimation in a unified algorithm, then proves that the resulting regret decomposes cleanly into reward-estimation and model-estimation errors, each admitting a variance-aware (second-order) bound.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first minimax-optimal characterization (up to log factors) of how much online interaction is needed in AIL when expert demonstrations are limited, and the first information-theoretic lower bound that simultaneously covers both expert and interaction sample complexity. The conceptual separation between BC and AIL regarding stochasticity—BC benefits from deterministic experts (ε^{-1}) while AIL does not, because AIL must match occupancy measures whose estimation inherently reflects transition stochasticity—clarifies a previously muddled distinction in the literature. Open questions include closing the remaining log|R| gap in expert sample complexity, extending the analysis to non-realizable or misspecified settings, and understanding whether the second-order gains translate to practical speedups in high-dimensional continuous control beyond the three MuJoCo tasks tested.
