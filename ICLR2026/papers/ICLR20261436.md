## Problem

Previous approaches to discovering neural mechanisms of cognition either trained RNNs for task optimality (missing characteristic behavioral errors like swap errors), relied on hand-crafted architectures with heuristic ablations, or used moment-matching criteria that cannot capture multimodal response distributions. No automated method existed to train biologically plausible RNNs to reproduce the full statistical richness of behavioral response distributions, including suboptimalities, which is necessary to uncover the neural dynamics underlying cognitive errors.

## Input / Output

The network receives stimulus features (location and colour on a circle) either as ordered scalars with an index cue (index-cued variant) or as conjunctive tuning curves with a continuous location cue (feature-cued variant), followed by a recall cue. The output is a 2D vector in a fixed orthonormal behavioural subspace, interpreted as a colour estimate via arctan, but critically the network is trained to produce samples from a trial-specific mixture of two Gaussians in R² (one mode at the cued colour, one at the distractor colour) rather than a single point estimate. The full n-dimensional neural activity trajectory is also tracked, with the behavioural nullspace carrying memory information that is not directly observable in behaviour.

## Method

The paper adapts denoising diffusion probabilistic model (DDPM) training to work within a leaky-integrator RNN with dendritic tree architecture. The key innovation is applying the DDPM denoising objective only to the 2D behavioural subspace projection (not the full activity vector) and only during the second delay period, with a trial-specific target distribution defined by a Bayesian non-parametric swap-error model (BNS) that generates synthetic data to overcome behavioral data scarcity. Teacher-forcing is used to keep the behavioural subspace on-distribution during sequential trial simulation, since the RNN's memory dynamics prevent the parallel, Markovian training of standard DDPMs. A base-distribution regulariser constrains the activity at cue offset to the unit normal, and L2 penalties control overall neural activity. By training the network to reproduce the full multimodal response distribution (including swap errors) rather than optimizing for task performance, the resulting neural dynamics spontaneously match qualitative features of macaque LPFC recordings—specifically the planar alignment of stimulus representations during cueing and the report-subspace geometry underlying item misselection—that were inaccessible to task-optimal or moment-matched networks.

## Results

RNNs trained with the DDPM-style criterion successfully reproduce the dependence of swap error rates on probe-feature distance across multiple target swap functions (Figure 3A), with the fitted BNS swap rates closely tracking the ground-truth dotted lines. Critically, only networks trained with a distance-dependent (smooth) swap probability capture the dynamic temporal evolution of representational plane alignment observed in macaque LPFC (Panichello & Buschman, 2021): the cosine similarity between stimulus representation planes sharply increases during cue exposure and remains elevated throughout the second delay. Table 1 (Appendix C) quantifies this: the smooth-swap network yields MSE differences between sigmoid and flat-line fits of 0.066, 0.0003, and 0.065 across three spatial configurations, far exceeding the near-zero values (≤0.0002) for constant-swap and task-optimal networks, confirming that only the distance-dependent training produces the biologically observed dynamic alignment. The trained networks also reveal a two-ring geometry in the report subspace (Figure 5) that discriminates swap from accurate trials independently of stimulus location, providing a candidate neural signature for item misselection.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four training strategies: (1) task-optimal MSE training, (2) DDPM training with a no-swap target, (3) DDPM training with a constant (probe-independent) swap probability, and (4) DDPM training with a distance-dependent (smooth) swap function. Task-optimal networks produce only unimodal response distributions and fail to generate any swap errors; naive post-hoc ablations (reducing probe distance or increasing process noise, Figure 3D–E) also fail to induce the characteristic second mode at the distractor colour. The constant-swap network produces swap errors but yields temporally static plane alignments (near-zero sigmoid-vs-flat-line MSE in Table 1), failing to match the sharp cue-triggered alignment increase seen in macaque data. Only the distance-dependent swap network (the paper's full method) simultaneously reproduces multimodal response distributions, the correct swap-rate dependence on probe distance, and the dynamic temporal profile of representational alignment matching biological recordings. The no-swap diffusion network partially captures alignment dynamics (intermediate Table 1 values) but lacks the swap-error-specific signatures entirely.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of training RNNs for task optimality (MSE, cross-entropy) or moment-matching by instead training them to reproduce the full distribution of behavioral responses, including characteristic errors. It builds on three pillars: DDPM training criteria (Ho et al., 2020) adapted to operate within a recurrent network's behavioral subspace, dendritic tree architectures for biological plausibility (Lyo and Savin, 2024), and a Bayesian non-parametric generative model of swap errors (Radmard et al., 2025) used to produce synthetic training data. The key conceptual departure is that the diffusion model criterion is applied only to the 2D behavioral output during the second delay period, with teacher-forcing to maintain in-distribution trajectories, rather than to the full network state as in prior RNN-diffusion integrations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that neural representational geometry (plane alignment in LPFC) matches macaque data only when networks are trained with distance-dependent swap errors, not with task optimality, no swaps, or constant-rate swaps—provides a concrete, testable link between the statistical structure of behavioral suboptimalities and the neural dynamics that produce them. This opens a path toward automatically discovering neural mechanisms by fitting to rich behavioral distributions rather than hand-crafting architectures. Key limitations include dependence on a generative behavioral model for training data (not available for all tasks), the narrow scope (2-item VWM, qualitative comparison to macaque data), and the absence of trial-by-trial dynamical analysis. Open questions include whether the method extends to other cognitive tasks with complex response distributions, and why the specific form of swap dependence (distance-dependent vs. constant) is what drives correct neural geometry.
