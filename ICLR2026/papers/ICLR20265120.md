## Problem

Test-time scaling via resampling methods (Best-of-N, Rejection Sampling) relies on verifiers to rank or filter candidate responses, yet prior theoretical analysis assumed perfect verifiers and characterized only pass@N. In practice, verifiers make both false-positive and false-negative errors, but the precise relationship between verifier imperfection and compute-accuracy scaling remained uncharacterized. This paper asks: how does the geometry of a verifier's ROC curve determine the scaling behavior of resampling methods at every compute level?

## Input / Output

Given a fixed query and a generator LLM, the framework consumes candidate responses sampled from the generator and a verifier score f: X → [0,1] (e.g., an LLM's chain-of-thought correctness assessment, averaged over 5 repetitions and normalized). It produces closed-form per-instance accuracy as a function of expected compute C (for RS) or sample count N (for BoN), fully determined by the generator's base accuracy π and the verifier's ROC curve T(F). The characterization is instance-level: each query has its own π and ROC curve, and the theory predicts accuracy at any compute budget without distributional assumptions beyond the score being discrete or absolutely continuous.

## Method

The paper proves that for a fixed query, the accuracy of both RS and BoN depends on the generator and verifier only through π and the ROC curve T(F), independent of implementation details. For RS, accuracy equals the precision of the thresholded classifier, yielding A(C) = C·π·T(F(C)); its derivative is governed by the local slope T'(F), so concave ROC curves guarantee monotone scaling, and the high-compute limit is determined solely by T'(0). For BoN, accuracy is an integral over the entire ROC curve: ACC(g_N) = 1 − (1−π)N∫₀¹ ψ(F)^{N−1} dF where ψ(F) = (1−π)(1−F) + π(1−T(F)), making BoN sensitive to global ROC geometry rather than local slopes. Key results show RS dominates BoN at fixed compute for concave ROC curves (Proposition 5), both methods converge to the same limit T'(0)·π / (T'(0)·π + 1−π) (Proposition 2, Theorem 1), and low-compute observations cannot predict high-compute performance (Propositions 3, 6). Experiments with Qwen3 and Llama models as generators and verifiers on GSM8K and MATH500 validate per-instance predictions to within one percentage point and confirm aggregate trends including the emergence of a 'Capped Rejection Sampling' hybrid that matches BoN accuracy at substantially lower compute.

## Results

The paper's primary contribution is theoretical: it proves that for concave verifier ROC curves, Rejection Sampling (RS) strictly outperforms Best-of-N (BoN) at any fixed compute budget (Proposition 5), while both methods converge to the same accuracy in the infinite-compute limit, determined solely by the ROC slope at the origin T′(0) (Theorem 1, Proposition 2). Empirically, on GSM8K and MATH500 with Qwen3-1.7B as generator and Qwen3-4B/32B or Llama-3.2-3B/Llama-4-17B as verifiers, RS consistently achieves higher accuracy than BoN at matched compute, with the gap vanishing at large N. A practical 'Capped RS' variant (RS with a 25-sample cap, falling back to BoN) matches Bo25 accuracy while requiring substantially fewer generated samples on average.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The central comparison is between Rejection Sampling (RS) and Best-of-N (BoN) as test-time scaling methods under imperfect verifiers. At any fixed compute budget N, RS outperforms BoN when the verifier's ROC curve is concave (Proposition 5), a result confirmed empirically on both GSM8K and MATH500 across multiple generator-verifier pairs (Figures 1, 3, 4). However, in the infinite-compute limit, both methods converge to the same accuracy T′(0)·π / (T′(0)·π + (1−π)) (Theorem 1), so the RS advantage is purely a finite-compute phenomenon. A second key comparison is between different verifiers: at low compute, verifiers with similar ROC geometry near F=1 produce nearly identical performance, but at high compute their performance diverges based on the ROC slope near F=0 (Propositions 2, 3, 6). This makes it impossible to extrapolate high-compute performance from low-compute observations. Finally, the 'Capped RS' hybrid (RS with a 25-sample cap) matches Bo25 accuracy on aggregate while using substantially fewer samples on average, offering a practical middle ground between the two methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the test-time scaling literature (pass@N, BoN, RS) by replacing the standard perfect-verifier assumption with a precise characterization via the verifier's ROC curve. It builds on the KL-regularized RL formulation of Rafailov et al. (2023) and the rejection-sampling-to-RL connection of Xiong et al. (2025) to show that the same fundamental accuracy limit applies to RL-trained generators. Prior theoretical work on BoN either assumed perfect verifiers (Brown et al., 2024; Schaeffer et al., 2025) or provided loose MSE-based bounds (Huang et al., 2025b); this paper gives exact per-instance formulas for both BoN and RS under arbitrary verifier quality, drawing on the classical ROC framework and an integral representation inspired by Scherlis (2021).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central practical implication is that early scaling behavior cannot be used to predict high-compute performance: two verifiers with identical accuracy at small N can diverge arbitrarily at large N, undermining the common heuristic of extrapolating from a few samples. The result that RS dominates BoN at fixed compute (for concave ROC curves) offers a concrete, implementable improvement, and the 'capped RS' hybrid provides a simple practical recipe. Open questions include whether these per-instance results aggregate cleanly under distributional assumptions on query difficulty, whether RL-trained models truly inherit the same fundamental limit, and whether verifiers can be trained to optimize different regions of the ROC curve for different compute budgets.
