## Problem

Current fMRI-to-image reconstruction methods, even those leveraging diffusion models, produce reconstructions that are visually plausible but unfaithful to the actual seen image, deviating in structural details (position, color) and often missing or distorting semantic content. This gap stems from how fMRI representations are extracted, mapped to image features, and incorporated into generative models—most approaches compress all voxels into a single global embedding, failing to exploit the distributed, localized nature of visual processing in the brain.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264225/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes fMRI voxel activations (~40K voxels from vision-related cortical areas, sampled to 15K per training example) and produces a 256×256 reconstructed natural image. Internally, the pipeline generates two complementary intermediate representations: 256 spatial OpenCLIP ViT-bigG/14 tokens for semantic conditioning and multi-layer VGG feature maps for structural initialization. The low-level branch first inverts VGG features through a Deep Image Prior to produce a coarse layout, which then serves as the noisy initialization for the diffusion-based semantic refinement stage.

## Method

Brain-IT introduces a Brain Interaction Transformer (BIT) that maps functionally similar brain-voxel clusters (128 shared clusters obtained via GMM on learned voxel embeddings) to localized image features, enabling direct information flow from functional brain regions to specific image locations. The BIT architecture comprises a Brain Tokenizer (per-voxel and per-cluster embeddings combined via graph attention to form Brain Tokens) and a Cross-Transformer module (self-attention across clusters plus cross-attention with query tokens that each predict one localized image feature). Two BIT models are trained: one predicts CLIP spatial tokens to condition an unCLIP-style SDXL diffusion model (semantic branch), and the other predicts multi-layer VGG features via InfoNCE loss, which are inverted through a Deep Image Prior to reconstruct a coarse image (low-level branch). At inference, the DIP-reconstructed coarse image initializes the diffusion process, which is then refined under semantic CLIP conditioning, combining structural fidelity with generative realism. All network weights are shared across subjects and clusters—only per-voxel embeddings are subject-specific—enabling transfer learning to new subjects with as little as 15 minutes of fMRI data.

## Results

On the NSD dataset (40 hours per subject, averaged across Subjects 1, 2, 5, 7), Brain-IT achieves the best score on 7 of 8 standard metrics: PixCorr 0.386, SSIM 0.486, Alex(2) 98.4%, Alex(5) 99.5%, Incep 97.3%, CLIP 96.4%, Eff 0.564, and SwAV 0.320. The only metric where it ranks second is CLIP (96.4% vs. NeuroVLA's 97.5%). In the transfer-learning setting with only 1 hour of subject-specific data, Brain-IT (PixCorr 0.331, SSIM 0.473, CLIP 93.0%) substantially outperforms MindEye2 and MindTuner trained on the same 1 hour, and approaches their 40-hour performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Brain-IT outperforms all 10 baselines on 7 of 8 metrics in the full 40-hour setting, with the sole exception being CLIP where NeuroVLA achieves 97.5% vs. Brain-IT's 96.4%. The largest margins are on low-level structural metrics: PixCorr (0.386 vs. 0.322 for MindEye2/MindTuner, a ~20% relative gain) and SSIM (0.486 vs. 0.431). Among the most recent competitors, Brain-IT surpasses MindEye2 (Scotti et al., 2024) and MindTuner (Gong et al., 2025) on every metric, and also beats NeuroPictor (Huo et al., 2024) and NeuroVLA (Shen et al., 2024) across the board except CLIP. In the 1-hour transfer-learning setting, Brain-IT's advantage widens dramatically: it achieves CLIP 93.0% and Incep 94.4% with only 1 hour of data, compared to 79.2%/81.2% (MindEye2) and 83.5%/84.8% (MindTuner) under the same budget, while remaining close to those methods' 40-hour scores (93.0%/95.4% and 93.8%/95.6% respectively). The paper also reports that Brain-IT generalizes to the out-of-distribution NSD-Synthetic dataset without any adaptation, and that even 15 minutes of subject-specific data yields meaningful reconstructions (CLIP 91.3%, Incep 90.7%).

## Relation to Prior Work

Brain-IT departs from the dominant paradigm in fMRI-to-image reconstruction, where prior methods compress all ~40K voxels into a single global embedding via a fully connected layer before predicting image features. Instead, it introduces a Brain Interaction Transformer (BIT) that operates on 128 functionally-defined voxel clusters (Brain Tokens) shared across subjects, enabling direct information flow from functional brain regions to localized image features. The semantic branch builds on the unCLIP-style SDXL diffusion model from MindEye2 (Scotti et al., 2024), while the low-level branch introduces a novel Deep Image Prior inversion of VGG features predicted by BIT. Cross-subject integration is voxel-centric (sharing weights across all voxels), inspired by the Universal Brain Encoder (Beliy et al., 2024), rather than the scan-level alignment used by prior multi-subject methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The most practically significant result is the data efficiency: with only 1 hour of fMRI from a new subject, Brain-IT matches prior methods trained on 40 hours, and meaningful reconstructions emerge from just 15 minutes—critical given the cost and time of fMRI data collection. The brain-inspired architecture (functional clusters, retinotopic organization) also provides interpretability through attention maps revealing spatial and semantic selectivity of brain tokens, which could inform neuroscience beyond reconstruction. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to other neural recording modalities, whether the functional clustering strategy transfers to non-visual tasks, and how to handle the remaining failures where either semantic or structural content is missed.
