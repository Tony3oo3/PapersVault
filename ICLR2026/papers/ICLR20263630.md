## Problem

Machine learning scaling has been dominated by parallel computation (wider models, more GPUs, parallelizable architectures), yet many important problems—mathematical reasoning, physical simulation, sequential decision-making—are inherently serial and cannot be efficiently parallelized. The paper identifies this as a critical blind spot: current parallel-centric architectures (Transformers, SSMs, diffusion models) are provably incapable of solving general instances of inherently serial problems, and the field lacks a formal framework to distinguish serial from parallel scaling.

## Input / Output

The paper operates on binary decision problems (N input tokens → yes/no output) as a universal framework, since any discrete-choice problem reduces to a sequence of yes/no questions. Concretely, it analyzes problem specifications including cellular automaton rules with initial states, MDP transition/reward structures, Newtonian many-body initial conditions, and math question token sequences. The outputs are complexity class membership proofs (TC^0, TC, or P-complete), formal seriality theorems, and limitation bounds on what each architecture family can compute in a single forward pass versus with serial inference procedures like Chain-of-Thought.

## Method

The paper formalizes the serial/parallel divide using the TC^i circuit complexity hierarchy, defining a problem as parallel if it lies in TC (polylog-depth, polynomial-width threshold circuits) and inherently serial otherwise. The key new theorem (Theorem F.2) proves that a diffusion model with a TC^0 backbone remains in TC^0 even with infinitely many denoising steps, by bounding the total variation between the target and sampled distributions via the intrinsic dimension of the solution manifold and then derandomizing the stochastic sampler via majority voting over fixed seeds. The paper further proves that certain MDPs require serial computation for approximately optimal policies, and that unbiased return estimation in policy gradient methods is inherently serial. Combined with prior results showing Transformers, SSMs, and MLPs all collapse to TC^0 in single forward passes, the paper establishes that only genuinely serial inference procedures (RNN recurrence, repeating layers, Chain-of-Thought) can escape TC^0 and solve inherently serial problems.

## Results

The paper's primary results are theoretical: Theorem 4.1 proves that diffusion models with a TC⁰ backbone remain in TC⁰ even with infinitely many denoising steps, meaning they cannot solve inherently serial problems regardless of step count. Theorem G.2 proves that certain MDPs require serial computation for approximately optimal policies. Empirically, the paper cites Aggarwal & Welleck (2025) showing sequential scaling (longer reasoning chains) consistently outperforms parallel scaling (majority voting) across AMC, MATH, AIME, and Olympiad-bench at matched token budgets, and Kevin et al. (2025) showing a 2× deeper network (8 layers, width 256) outperforms a 16× wider network (4 layers, width 4096) on the Humanoid locomotion task.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical position paper with no direct method-vs-baseline benchmark comparisons. Instead, it classifies existing architectures by their serial/parallel capabilities. Feedforward MLPs, Transformers, and SSMs (including Mamba) are all shown to collapse into TC⁰ and thus cannot solve inherently serial problems in a single forward pass. RNNs, repeating layers, and Chain-of-Thought inference are the only methods that genuinely add serial computation and can solve such problems. The paper's key empirical comparisons come from cited work: in Hex, MCTS expansion nodes (serial compute) yield consistent gains across all training regimes (Villalobos & Atkinson, 2023); in locomotion/manipulation, depth outperforms width by a large margin (Kevin et al., 2025); and in math/science QA, sequential scaling via longer reasoning chains beats parallel scaling via majority voting at equal token budgets (Aggarwal & Welleck, 2025; Muennighoff et al., 2025). The diffusion model result is novel: despite thousands of iterative denoising steps, the effective serial depth remains constant, explaining observed step-count plateaus in practice.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the 'parallelism tradeoff' line of work (Merrill & Sabharwal, 2023a,b) — which established that fixed-depth Transformers collapse into TC⁰ — into a broader, unifying framework called the Serial Scaling Hypothesis. It draws on classical complexity theory (P-completeness, depth-width tradeoffs, computational irreducibility) and applies these tools to new ML contexts: diffusion models, Markov decision processes, and real-world tasks like video prediction and math QA. The key new theoretical contribution is Theorem F.2, proving that diffusion models with TC⁰ backbones remain in TC⁰ even with infinite denoising steps, a result absent from prior expressivity analyses that covered only MLPs, Transformers, and SSMs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The SSH provides a principled lens for understanding why parallel-centric architectures plateau on reasoning, planning, and simulation tasks, with concrete implications for architecture design (need for deeper/serial structures), hardware investment (faster sequential processors beyond GPU parallelism), and benchmark construction (separating serial from parallel scaling). Key limitations include reliance on the unproven TC ⊊ P assumption, worst-case analysis only, and the absence of a training-time serial/parallel theory. Open questions include empirically quantifying seriality in practice, whether novel architectures (xLSTM, implicit gradients, test-time training) can efficiently provide scalable serial computation, and whether the diffusion result extends to backbones beyond TC⁰.
