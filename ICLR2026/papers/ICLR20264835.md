## Problem

Safety alignment of LLMs via reinforcement learning consistently degrades general capabilities (math, code, instruction-following), a phenomenon known as the alignment tax. Existing approaches treat this as a dual-objective balancing problem but do not explicitly address the geometric conflict between safety and general-capability gradients during training. The paper identifies the root cause as the safety gradient itself disrupting the parameter subspaces that encode general capabilities.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264835/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes two types of data: (1) safety preference data from PKU-SafeRLHF (only a 40% subset of ~11K samples is used for RL training, with Llama-Guard-4 12B providing safety rewards), and (2) a small set of 1,000 general-capability prompts (mixed from commonsense, math, and code domains) used solely to construct the projection matrix K via forward-pass hidden representations. The output is a set of updated LLM parameters that are safety-aligned while preserving the original general-capability mappings. Unlike prior methods that require large mixed safety+general training sets, NSPO trains exclusively on safety data.

## Method

NSPO modifies the GRPO policy gradient by geometrically projecting it into the null space of a general-capability representation matrix K. Specifically, K is formed from hidden representations of general prompts passed through the base model; its non-central covariance KK^T is decomposed via SVD, and the projection matrix P = ŨŨ^T (spanned by eigenvectors with near-zero eigenvalues) is applied to the policy gradient, yielding ∇_W J_NSPO = (∇_W J)·P. This ensures the parameter update Δ satisfies ΔK = 0, so the general-capability mapping W·K = V is exactly preserved after each step. The KL divergence term is removed since it pushes the policy toward an unsafe reference and conflicts with the safety objective. Two theoretical results are established: the projection is non-expansive (bounding gradient norm), and the projected gradient remains a valid descent direction for the safety objective, guaranteeing both capability preservation and effective safety optimization.

## Results

On Llama3-8B-Instruct, NSPO achieves the lowest Attack Success Rate (ASR) across nearly all 7 safety benchmarks, reducing ASR to 0.06% on AdvBench, 0.18% on HarmBench, and 16.81% on SORRY-Bench, while maintaining general capability scores within ~1% of the base model (e.g., MMLU 63.64% vs. 63.79% base, GSM8K 76.42% vs. 75.44% base). On Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, NSPO further reduces ASR to 0.52% on PKU-Safe, 0.50% on HarmBench, and 19.54% on SORRY-Bench, while general capability scores remain essentially unchanged (MMLU 71.74% vs. 71.71% base, MATH 75.05% vs. 74.80% base). Notably, NSPO achieves these results using only 40% of the PKU-SafeRLHF safety data (11K samples) without any mixed general-task training data, whereas baselines use the full dataset.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NSPO outperforms all five baseline families on safety metrics for both models. Against DPO variants (DPO-S, DPO-H, DPO-Mix), NSPO achieves substantially lower ASR on HarmBench (0.18% vs. 5.67% for DPO-S on Llama3; 0.50% vs. 18.33% for DPO-S on Qwen2.5) and SORRY-Bench (16.81% vs. 14.83% for DPO-S on Llama3, though DPO-S is competitive here). Against SafeRLHF, NSPO is dramatically superior, reducing ASR from 41.34% to 0.18% on HarmBench and from 44.77% to 16.81% on SORRY-Bench for Llama3. Against PeCAN and MoCAN, NSPO achieves lower ASR on most benchmarks (e.g., 0.18% vs. 8.83% on HarmBench for Llama3). Against W-DOOR, NSPO wins on most benchmarks except AdvBench on Qwen2.5 (0.29% vs. 0.00%) and SORRY-Bench on Qwen2.5 (19.54% vs. 15.00%), but dominates on PKU-Safe (0.52% vs. 3.72%) and ALERT (0.81% vs. 3.40%). Against BFPO, the closest competitor, NSPO achieves lower ASR on HarmBench (0.18% vs. 4.16% on Llama3; 0.50% vs. 11.33% on Qwen2.5), JailbreakBench (1.33% vs. 2.67%), and ALERT (1.00% vs. 7.22% on Llama3), while BFPO is slightly better on PKU-Safe for Llama3 (1.86% vs. 2.23%). Critically, on general capability benchmarks, NSPO matches or exceeds BFPO (which uses mixed safety+general data) while using only safety data, with performance drops of less than 1% on most tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

NSPO builds directly on GRPO (DeepSeekMath) by modifying its policy gradient with a null-space projection, drawing conceptual inspiration from null-space constrained knowledge editing (AlphaEdit, Fang et al., 2024). It departs from the dominant paradigm in safety alignment—dual-objective optimization with Lagrangian multipliers (SafeRLHF, PeCAN, MoCAN) or mixed-data training (W-DOOR, BFPO)—by instead imposing a geometric constraint that makes safety updates orthogonal to the general-capability subspace. The removal of the KL divergence term in favor of the projection as a regularizer is a notable departure from standard RLHF practice, justified by the argument that KL naturally conflicts with the safety objective by pulling toward an unsafe reference.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The alignment tax is a persistent practical barrier to deploying safety-aligned LLMs, and NSPO offers a clean geometric solution with formal guarantees (gradient stability, descent direction) that prior dual-objective methods lack. Its data efficiency—requiring only 40% of PKU-SafeRLHF and no mixed general-task data—reduces annotation and compute costs. Open questions include whether the static null-space (computed once from a fixed general dataset) remains valid as the model evolves during training, how the per-layer linear approximation interacts with non-linearities in deeper architectures, and whether the approach scales to 70B+ models where the projection matrix computation and memory overhead may become non-trivial despite the claimed O(d²) offloading.
