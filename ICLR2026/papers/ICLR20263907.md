## Problem

Reliable, fine-grained evaluation of instruction-based image editing remains a bottleneck: existing protocols either depend on paired reference images (inheriting biases from prior generators and covering only one realization of a valid edit) or rely on zero-shot VLMs that are poor at spatial reasoning, prone to object hallucinations, insensitive to subtle pixel-level changes, and miscalibrated for generative artifacts. Multi-turn editing compounds these issues, as no prior benchmark systematically evaluates sequential instruction following, cross-turn content preservation, and visual quality in a unified, reference-free framework.

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes a single real-world RGB image (572 images drawn from GEdit-Bench after filtering sensitive content) and produces three per-turn scores plus an aggregate: EdiVal-IF (instruction following, 0–1), EdiVal-CC (content consistency, 0–1), and EdiVal-VQ (visual quality via HPSv3, reported separately), combined into EdiVal-O = √(IF·CC). Editing instructions are not hand-written but auto-generated by GPT-4o from the object decomposition, spanning 9 types (subject add/remove/replace, color/material alter, text change, position change, count change, background change) across up to 3 sequential turns, yielding 1,716 instructions total. The multi-turn IF score is the logical AND of per-turn marginal successes along the chain, while CC is computed against the original base image using the evolving object pool.

## Method

EdiVal-Agent is a three-stage, object-centric agentic pipeline. Stage 1 (Decomposition) uses a VLM to extract per-object JSON (object, color, material, text, count, foreground) from the input image, then validates and localizes each object with Grounding-DINO, seeding three dynamically updated pools: P^all (all objects ever present), P^unch (objects not yet edited), and P^avail (currently editable objects). Stage 2 (Instruction Generation) samples a distinct instruction type per turn from the nine-type taxonomy, selects target objects from P^avail, emits a natural-language prompt via GPT-4o, and updates all three pools to reflect the intended edit. Stage 3 (Evaluation) splits instruction following into two paths: symbolically verifiable types (subject add/remove/replace, position, count) are checked by Grounding-DINO bounding-box geometry (e.g., center-x ordering for position_change), while semantically verifiable types (color, material, text, background) are judged by Qwen2-VL on detector-guided object crops. Content consistency (EdiVal-CC) computes DINOv3 feature similarity between the base and current images over the background region (Ω minus all object boxes) and over each unchanged object, averaged together. Visual quality (EdiVal-VQ) uses HPSv3, reported separately to avoid conflating beautification with fidelity. This hybrid symbolic-semantic design achieves 81.3% human agreement on instruction following, outperforming VLM-only (75.2%) and CLIP-directional (65.4%) baselines.

## Results

On EdiVal-Bench (1,716 instructions, 572 images, 3 turns), Seedream 4.0 achieves the best overall EdiVal-O score of 83.81/69.95/59.76 across turns 1–3, combining the strongest instruction following (75.93/55.58/41.59) with high content consistency (92.51/88.03/85.86) at 14.55 s/img latency. The proposed EdiVal-IF metric achieves 81.3% human agreement accuracy, significantly outperforming zero-shot VLM-only evaluation (Qwen2-VL: 75.2%) and thresholded CLIP_dir (65.4%). Among open-source models, Qwen-Image-Edit starts strong (EdiVal-O 78.36 at T1) but degrades sharply to 41.93 by T3, revealing pronounced exposure bias in multi-turn editing.

## Baselines & Comparisons

For evaluation methodology, EdiVal-IF (81.3% human agreement) outperforms both the VLM-only baseline (Qwen2-VL at 75.2%) and thresholded CLIP_dir (65.4%), with the gap driven primarily by superior performance on spatial-reasoning tasks (position_change) and object-existence tasks (subject_remove) where VLMs hallucinate. Among the 16 editing models evaluated, Seedream 4.0 ranks first overall, while GPT-Image-1.5 substantially improves over GPT-Image-1 by +13.49/+13.42/+12.99 points in EdiVal-CC and +7.33/+6.07/+5.74 in EdiVal-O across turns, at the cost of higher latency (35.55 vs. 26.47 s/img). Nano Banana 2 consistently outperforms Nano Banana in instruction following and overall score (EdiVal-O: 83.14/70.02/58.49 vs. 81.48/67.70/56.24) but at 2.6× the latency. Among flow-matching models, FLUX.2-max is the strongest (Rank 4), while Qwen-Image-Edit—despite being the best open-source model at T1 (EdiVal-O 78.36)—degrades most severely to 41.93 by T3, indicating strong exposure bias. The paper's rankings on the overlapping model set exactly match the Artificial Analysis human-vote leaderboard as of September 2025, validating the automated evaluation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from two dominant evaluation paradigms for instruction-based image editing: reference-based metrics (L1/L2, DINO, CLIP against ground-truth pairs from MagicBrush, UltraEdit, AnyEdit) and zero-shot VLM-as-judge protocols (VIEScore, GEdit-Bench, I2EBench, HQ-Edit, Complex-Edit). Rather than building on either, it introduces an object-centric agentic pipeline that repurposes existing specialist tools—Grounding DINO for open-vocabulary detection, DINOv3 for semantic similarity, Qwen2-VL for localized reasoning, and HPSv3 for aesthetic scoring—into a unified, multi-turn-aware evaluation framework. The paper also carves new ground by being the first to systematically compare in-context autoregressive multi-turn editors (GPT-Image, Nano Banana, Gemini) against context-free flow-matching and diffusion editors under a common protocol, and by introducing dynamic object pools that track scene state across sequential edits.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As multi-turn conversational editing becomes the dominant user-facing paradigm (GPT-Image, Nano Banana, Seedream), the field lacked a reliable, reference-free, fine-grained evaluation protocol; EdiVal-Bench and EdiVal-Agent fill this gap with 1,716 instructions across 9 types and 16 models. The exposure-bias finding for Qwen-Image-Edit (EdiVal-O dropping from 78.36 to 41.93 across three turns) and the multi-turn-vs-complex-prompt trade-off provide actionable diagnostics for model developers. Key limitations include the restriction to object-centric instructions (no style or narrative edits), the dependence on Grounding DINO's false-positive rate for symbolic checks, and the fact that evaluation scores are not yet fed back into model training (e.g., via Best-of-N or RL). Open questions include whether the object-centric decomposition generalizes to abstract or heavily compositional scenes, and whether the framework can be extended to video or 3D editing.
