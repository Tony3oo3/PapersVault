## Problem

Existing post-training backdoor defenses via model merging (e.g., WAG) require 3–6 homologous models to achieve effective suppression, depend on trusted auxiliary resources, and degrade to single-model performance under collusive attacks where multiple models share identical backdoors. This paper asks whether a more targeted fusion strategy can disrupt backdoor shortcuts with as few as two models, without trusted data, and remain robust even when some models are collusively compromised.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264272/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes multiple (as few as two) trojaned models sharing the same architecture (e.g., RoBERTa-large, ViT, ResNet) along with a small clean validation set of 20–50 samples per class—no poisoned data or trigger knowledge is required. It outputs a purified model constructed by selectively swapping weight modules (Q, K, V, O, I, P in Transformers; conv-bn pairs in CNNs) across source models, plus a reusable module-switching strategy (an index table mapping each module slot to a source-model index) that is architecture-specific but task-agnostic, so one search suffices for all models of the same structure.

## Method

The core insight is that backdoors function as localized spurious-correlation shortcuts within specific modules, and different backdoored models rarely implant them in the same location, so swapping corresponding modules severs these fragile pathways. The paper first proves in two-layer linear networks that module switching yields strictly greater backdoor divergence than weight averaging (Theorem 1) and guarantees at least one switched candidate exceeds WAG (Proposition 1). For deep models, it formulates strategy search as a discrete NAS problem scored by heuristic rules penalizing intra-layer, consecutive-layer, and residual adjacency violations (which could sustain backdoor propagation paths) plus a balance penalty and diversity reward. A modified regularized evolution algorithm (2M generations, no model training) searches this space to produce the optimal switching strategy. Finally, among the asymmetric candidates generated by applying the strategy, the best model is selected by maximizing mean cosine distance between its [CLS] (or GAP) features on clean non-target samples and a WAG-derived dummy feature for the detected suspect class, requiring only the small clean validation set.

## Results

On SST-2 with RoBERTa-large, MSD achieves an average ASR of 22.0% when merging BadNet+InsertSent models (vs. 31.9% for WAG) and 40.4% for BadNet+LWS (vs. 62.2% for WAG), while maintaining clean accuracy of ~96%. On CIFAR-10 with ViT, MSD reduces ASR to 18.5% for BadNet+PhysicalBA (vs. 39.6% for WAG) and 21.4% for BadNet+BATT (vs. 26.5% for WAG), with clean accuracy of ~98.7%. In the benign+compromised setting, MSD achieves only 12.2% ASR (BadNet) versus 39.3% for WAG, demonstrating effective blocking of backdoor transfer. The method also outperforms WAG under collusive attacks where multiple models share the same backdoor.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MSD consistently outperforms all model-merging baselines (WAG, TIES, DARE) across every attack combination and domain. Against WAG, MSD reduces ASR by 9.9 points on BadNet+InsertSent (22.0% vs. 31.9%), 12.3 points on BadNet+Hidden (34.1% vs. 46.4%), 21.8 points on BadNet+LWS (40.4% vs. 62.2%), and 27.1 points on Benign+BadNet (12.2% vs. 39.3%) on SST-2. TIES and DARE perform substantially worse than MSD in most settings (e.g., TIES reaches 77.1% ASR on BadNet+LWS vs. MSD's 40.4%), with DARE showing particularly poor results on vision (72.2% ASR on BadNet+PhysicalBA vs. MSD's 18.5%). Domain-specific baselines (Z-Def, ONION for text; CutMix, ShrinkPad for vision) are less effective against stealthy attacks: Z-Def achieves 97.3% ASR on LWS and ONION reaches 83.8% average ASR, while CutMix and ShrinkPad average 80.6% and 63.5% ASR respectively on CIFAR-10. MSD's advantage is most pronounced in the benign+compromised setting, where it blocks backdoor transfer far more effectively than any baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the model-merging line of backdoor defense initiated by WAG (Arora et al., 2024), which showed that averaging weights across homologous models suppresses backdoor triggers. Rather than continuous weight blending, MSD replaces individual modules (attention projections, FFN layers, conv-bn pairs) with counterparts from other models, framing the search as a discrete NAS problem solved via a modified regularized evolution algorithm (Real et al., 2019). The key conceptual departure is treating backdoors as localized 'shortcuts' that can be structurally severed by swapping modules, supported by a theoretical guarantee (Theorem 1) that module switching achieves strictly greater backdoor divergence than weight averaging. Unlike prior merging defenses that require 3–6 models or trusted auxiliary resources, MSD operates with as few as two models and only a small clean validation set.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MSD addresses a practical gap in post-training backdoor defense: it requires no training data, no trigger knowledge, and works with as few as two models, making it deployable in realistic model-sharing scenarios (HuggingFace, MoE, one-shot federated learning). The robustness against collusive attacks—where multiple models share identical backdoors and WAG degrades—is a practically important and previously underexplored threat surface. However, the current scope is limited to classification tasks; extending MSD to generative models and large language models with heterogeneous architectures remains open, and the one-time evolutionary search cost (2.6–4.3 hours) may be non-trivial for very deep or wide architectures.
