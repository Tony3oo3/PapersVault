## Problem

The Muon optimizer requires computing the polar decomposition of momentum matrices at every training step, but existing polynomial methods (Newton-Schulz) suffer from slow initial convergence while recent heuristic alternatives (Jordan, You) fail to converge at all. Deep learning demands GPU-friendly algorithms using only matrix-matrix products in low precision (bfloat16), which rules out the rational and QR-based methods from classical numerical analysis. The paper asks: what composition of low-degree odd polynomials gives the optimal worst-case approximation to the sign function over a given singular-value interval?

## Input / Output

The method takes a momentum matrix M ∈ R^{m×n} from the Muon optimizer, normalizes it by ‖M‖_F + 10^{-2}, and applies T precomputed odd-degree-5 polynomials (typically T=5 or 6) in bfloat16 using only matrix-matrix products and linear combinations. The polynomials are precomputed offline in float64 via a simplified Remez algorithm given a lower bound ℓ=10^{-3} and upper bound u=1 on the normalized singular values. The output is a semi-orthogonal matrix X_T ≈ polar(M) = UV^T, which serves as the descent direction in the Muon update W_{t+1} = W_t − λ·polar(M_t).

## Method

Polar Express constructs a composition p_T ∘ … ∘ p_1 of odd polynomials that is provably optimal in the minimax (L^∞) sense over the singular-value interval [ℓ, u]. The key theorem (Theorem 3.1) shows that the optimal composition can be found greedily: at each iteration t, solve the uniform approximation problem min_{p ∈ P_d^odd} max_{x∈[ℓ_t, u_t]} |1 − p(x)| to find p_t, then update the interval to [ℓ_{t+1}, u_{t+1}] = [p_t(ℓ_t), 2 − p_t(ℓ_t)]. For degree 5, each p_t is found via a simplified Remez algorithm (Algorithm 2) that exploits the equioscillation structure of odd quintics, requiring only a 4×4 linear solve per iteration. The resulting composition converges super-exponentially (cubically for d=5), and the method is strictly faster than Newton-Schulz even when the lower bound ℓ is misspecified. Finite-precision stability is ensured by a 1.01 safety factor on each polynomial and a cushion that prevents non-monotonic singular-value trajectories from causing sign flips in bfloat16.

## Results

On GPT-2-Large (774M params) trained on 1B FineWeb tokens, muon-PolarExp achieves a final validation loss of 3.340 (lr=0.02), outperforming muon-Jordan (3.398) and muon-You (3.399). On GPT-2-Small (124M params) with the same dataset, muon-PolarExp reaches 3.588 (lr=0.005) versus 3.639 for muon-Jordan, 3.629 for muon-You, and 4.197 for AdamW. At 10B tokens on GPT-2-Large with weight decay, the gap narrows but persists: muon-PolarExp (2.913) vs. muon-You (2.919) and muon-Jordan (2.921). In convergence experiments on synthetic matrices, Polar Express reaches a given error level roughly twice as fast as degree-5 Newton-Schulz and dominates all baselines at every iteration.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Polar Express consistently outperforms all three Muon baselines across every learning rate tested. Against Jordan's fixed-coefficient polynomial (Jordan et al., 2024b), Polar Express achieves a 0.058 validation loss improvement on GPT-2-Large (3.340 vs. 3.398) and 0.051 on GPT-2-Small (3.588 vs. 3.639); Jordan's method plateaus at ~0.3 error and never converges. Against You's six-step adaptive polynomial (Cesista et al., 2025), the gains are similar in magnitude (3.340 vs. 3.399 on GPT-2-Large), and You's method is only defined for six iterations. Against degree-5 Newton-Schulz (Higham, 2008), Polar Express converges approximately twice as fast in spectral norm error and dominates at every iteration count, whereas Newton-Schulz makes almost no progress for the first 17 iterations. Against AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019), muon-PolarExp achieves a substantially lower validation loss (3.588 vs. 4.197 on GPT-2-Small), confirming that the Muon framework with a better polar approximation is superior to standard adaptive gradient methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

Polar Express sits at the intersection of classical approximation theory and modern deep learning optimizers. It adopts the interval-adaptive update paradigm from Zolo-pd (Nakatsukasa & Freund, 2016) and the per-iteration polynomial rescaling idea from Chen & Chow (2014), but replaces rational functions with odd polynomials to remain GPU-friendly (only matrix-matrix products, no QR or inverses). The key conceptual departure is proving that the greedy per-iteration minimax polynomial selection is globally optimal for the composed polynomial (Theorem 3.1), a result that neither Chen & Chow nor the heuristic-search methods of Jordan and You establish. The paper also addresses bfloat16 finite-precision stability issues that are irrelevant in classical numerical analysis but critical for deep learning deployment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As Muon becomes a standard optimizer for LLM pretraining (NanoGPT speedrun, Kimi K2, multiple scaling-law studies), the quality of the polar factor approximation directly affects training efficiency. Polar Express provides a theoretically optimal, drop-in replacement that consistently improves validation loss across learning rates and model sizes, and has already been adopted into the NanoGPT speedrun. The ablation showing that 5–6 iterations suffice (even an exact SVD adds no benefit) is practically important, confirming that the coarse-approximation regime is the right target. Open questions include whether the minimax composition framework extends to other matrix functions (square root, inverse) used in Shampoo/Soap optimizers, and whether the fixed degree-5 choice remains optimal for all layer shapes and training regimes.
