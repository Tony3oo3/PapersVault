## Problem

LLMs in clinical settings frequently provide overconfident diagnoses despite incomplete patient information, because existing abstention methods rely on unreliable LLM self-assessment (confidence scores) rather than systematically identifying what specific medical evidence is missing. No prior approach grounds abstention decisions in structured external medical knowledge, leaving models unable to detect knowledge boundaries in multi-round diagnostic conversations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262761/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system receives an initial patient presentation (age, gender, chief complaint) and iteratively gathers additional information through multi-round dialogue, while querying a medical knowledge graph of 22k entities and 100k+ relationships derived from 300+ WHO guidelines. Each knowledge triplet is augmented with source text and document page images (base64-encoded) for multi-modal validation. At each round, the system outputs either a targeted follow-up question (abstention) or a free-text diagnostic answer, with the evidence pool maintained as a priority queue of K=6 triplets that evolves cumulatively across rounds.

## Method

KnowGuard introduces an investigate-before-abstain paradigm with two stages operating on a shared contextualized evidence pool. The Evidence Discovery stage performs dual retrieval: graph expansion via beam search (beam size 3, max hop depth 2) over high-priority entities, and direct retrieval using LLM-generated queries searched via FAISS. The Evidence Evaluation stage ranks candidates using five factors—embedding similarity (text-embedding-ada-002), LLM clinical relevance scoring, graph coherence (cumulative entity visit frequency), exponential temporal decay across rounds, and demographic-guided population weighting—combined into a weighted priority score. The final abstention decision is made by GPT-4 receiving the accumulated patient context, top-ranked evidence triplets with their multi-modal content, and a self-consistency check (2 iterations, threshold 3.5). The key distinction from prior work is replacing reactive confidence assessment with proactive, structured exploration of medical knowledge boundaries, enabling the model to identify specific evidence gaps rather than merely estimating internal certainty.

## Results

On the open-ended multi-round clinical reasoning benchmark (3,061 cases across ioAFRIMEDQA, ioMEDQA, ioCRAFT-MD), KnowGuard achieves the highest accuracy (68.70%, 70.98%, 66.47%) while maintaining the lowest ECE (0.050–0.065) and Brier scores (0.216–0.236) among all methods, with an average of ~5.2–5.4 conversation turns. In enhanced settings (with rationale generation and self-consistency), KnowGuard reaches 73.20%, 74.12%, and 71.96% accuracy. The method also reduces token usage by 95.7% and cost by 95.4% per case compared to the Long Context baseline, while achieving 28.5% faster per-round latency.

## Baselines & Comparisons

KnowGuard outperforms all six baselines across all three datasets on accuracy, ECE, and Brier score. Against the strongest confidence-based baseline (Scale Rating, Li et al. 2024), KnowGuard improves accuracy by 1.07–5.64 percentage points (e.g., 68.70 vs. 63.06 on ioAFRIMEDQA) while reducing ECE from 0.136–0.141 to 0.050–0.065. Against Long Context (Tu et al. 2024), which retrieves full medical documents, KnowGuard gains 8.58–11.10 percentage points in accuracy (e.g., 70.98 vs. 59.95 on ioMEDQA) while using 95.7% fewer tokens. Calibration-based methods (Temperature Scaling, Guo et al. 2017; Conformal Abstention, Yadkori et al. 2024) are overly conservative, deferring to the maximum 12-round limit with lower accuracy (0.626–0.715) compared to KnowGuard's 0.720–0.741 at only 5.3–6.5 rounds. Binary Decision (Srivastava et al. 2023) and Numerical Score (Tian et al. 2023) are consistently outperformed by 4.7–14.45 percentage points in accuracy, with Numerical Score also exhibiting the highest ECE (0.123–0.199) among methods that report calibration metrics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant confidence-based abstention paradigm (self-assessment scoring, calibration, self-consistency) by replacing internal LLM confidence with proactive external knowledge graph exploration. It builds on the interactive multi-round clinical QA framework introduced by MediQ (Li et al., 2024) and the knowledge-graph-augmented medical reasoning line (Gao et al., 2025; Pan et al., 2024), but repurposes graph traversal as a structured evidence-discovery mechanism rather than a static retrieval tool. The key conceptual shift is from asking 'how confident am I?' to 'what specific evidence am I missing?', operationalized through beam-search graph expansion and multi-factor evidence scoring on a WHO-guideline-derived knowledge graph.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a clinically critical safety gap—LLM overconfidence in diagnostic settings—by grounding abstention in verifiable external evidence rather than unreliable self-assessment, and the 95.7% token reduction versus long-context retrieval makes the approach far more deployable. The open-ended multi-round benchmark (3,061 cases) fills a real evaluation gap, as existing interactive clinical QA benchmarks are limited to multiple-choice formats that understate real diagnostic complexity. Open questions include generalization beyond WHO-guideline-derived knowledge to broader or region-specific medical corpora, performance with non-GPT-4 backbones, and whether the structured graph approach scales to highly specialized or rapidly evolving clinical domains.
