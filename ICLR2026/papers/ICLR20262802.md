## Problem

Prior active causal inference work has been narrowly focused on the conditional average treatment effect (CATE), while many practical causal questions involve other quantities such as ATE, ATT, or ATE under distribution shift. Standard active learning acquisition strategies (BALD, TVR) minimize posterior uncertainty over the unlabeled pool distribution, but CQ estimation requires reducing uncertainty over a potentially different target interventional or conditional distribution, creating a fundamental distribution mismatch that causes these methods to fail. This paper formalizes the broader task of Active Estimation of Causal Quantities (ActiveCQ) and provides a unified framework that automatically tailors acquisition to the specific CQ of interest.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a small labeled training set (treatment, effect modifiers, confounders, outcomes), a larger unlabeled pool of the same covariates without outcomes, a known causal DAG, and a user-specified set of treatment-effect-modifier pairs defining where estimation accuracy matters. At each batch round it outputs a set of pool indices to label (via greedy submodular selection) and a full posterior distribution (mean and covariance) over the target CQ, which is a GP over the treatment-effect-modifier space. A key structural detail is that the conditional distribution over confounders is never explicitly estimated as a density; instead it is represented as a conditional mean embedding in an RKHS, so the CQ estimator is computed entirely through kernel matrix operations rather than numerical integration or Monte Carlo sampling.

## Method

The framework unifies all target CQs (CATE, ATE, ATT, ATE-DS) as integrals of a regression function f(a,z,s) over a conditional or marginal distribution P*, then models f with a Gaussian Process using a product kernel over treatment, effect modifiers, and confounders. The central technical insight is representing the target distribution P* via Conditional Mean Embeddings (CMEs) in the same tensor-product RKHS as the GP, which converts the integral into a closed-form inner product and yields an analytic posterior GP for the CQ estimator without any explicit density estimation or Monte Carlo approximation. From this posterior, the paper derives two acquisition functions-information gain (minimizing the log-determinant of the CQ posterior covariance) and total variance reduction (minimizing its trace)-that directly target CQ uncertainty rather than pool-wide regression uncertainty. Batch selection uses a greedy submodular approximation to ensure diversity within each acquired batch, and the paper provides a convergence guarantee showing the marginal posterior variance of the CQ decays at rate O(gamma_{n_B}/sqrt(n_B)) plus an irreducible uncertainty floor.

## Results

The paper evaluates √AMSE (Average Mean Squared Error) as the primary metric across four causal quantities (CATE, ATE, ATT, ATE-DS) on simulation datasets and the semi-synthetic IHDP and Lalonde benchmarks, reporting mean ± std over 20 independent runs. The proposed CME-based methods (TVR-CME and IG-CME) consistently achieve the lowest √AMSE, with the largest gains on CATE and ATE-DS where substantial distribution shift exists between the target and pool distributions. On ATE (no distribution shift), all uncertainty-aware methods perform comparably, though IG-based variants can suffer numerical instability from determinant computation. The CME-based variants consistently outperform the MDN (mixture density network) sampling-based counterparts, attributed to CME operating directly in the GP's feature space.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against four baselines: Random, μ-BALD (Houlsby et al., 2011), TVR (Cohn et al., 1996), and Coresets/QHTE (Qin et al., 2021). On CATE estimation, both TVR-CME and IG-CME substantially outperform all baselines, with the gap widening as the number of acquired labels grows, because the proposed methods explicitly target the conditional distribution P(s|z) rather than the marginal pool distribution. On ATE-DS, where the target covariate distribution differs from the observational one, the proposed methods again significantly outperform μ-BALD, TVR, and Coresets, which all sample from the pool distribution and thus misallocate labeling budget. On ATE (no distribution shift), the baselines perform comparably to the proposed methods since all methods sample from the full population, though uncertainty-aware methods still beat Random. TVR (Cohn et al., 1996) is the strongest baseline overall but is outperformed by TVR-CME specifically because the CME representation aligns the variance reduction objective with the GP's kernel structure. The Coresets/QHTE baseline (Qin et al., 2021), designed for CATE with all covariates as effect modifiers, is outperformed when the effect modifier z is a proper subset of all covariates.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the active causal inference line initiated by CausalBALD (Jesson et al., 2021) and QHTE (Qin et al., 2021), which focused exclusively on CATE estimation, by formalizing a unified ActiveCQ task spanning ATE, ATT, CATE, and ATEDS. Methodologically, it builds on the kernel-based CQ estimation framework of Singh et al. (2024) and integrates Conditional Mean Embeddings (Song et al., 2013) with Gaussian Process regression to obtain closed-form posterior distributions for each CQ, replacing explicit conditional density estimation. The acquisition strategies (information gain, total variance reduction) are adapted from classical Bayesian AL (Houlsby et al., 2011; Cohn et al., 1996) but re-targeted to the CQ's posterior rather than the regression function's, aligning with the transductive active learning perspective of Hübotter et al. (2024). The paper explicitly distinguishes itself from ABCI (Toth et al., 2022), which requires interventional data and unknown causal structure, whereas ActiveCQ operates in a purely observational setting with a known DAG.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a principled, unified template for sample-efficient estimation of a broad class of causal quantities, with the CME-based approach offering a practical advantage over density-estimation baselines by operating in the same RKHS as the GP and enabling adaptive refinement after each acquisition round. The convergence guarantee (Theorem 2) is a meaningful theoretical contribution, bounding posterior variance by irreducible uncertainty plus a term decaying as O(γ/n_B). Key limitations include the GP's cubic complexity (mitigated only by noting modularity for sparse approximations), the assumption of no unmeasured confounders, and evaluation on small semi-synthetic datasets (IHDP, LaLonde) rather than large-scale real-world benchmarks. Open questions include extending to hidden confounders, instrumental variables, multivariate treatments, and scalable GP approximations that preserve the closed-form CME structure.
