## Problem

Prior work established that the contrastive learning (CL) loss approximates the Negatives-Only Supervised Contrastive Learning (NSCL) loss as the number of classes grows, but this loss-level similarity leaves open whether the two objectives also produce aligned representations throughout training. It remains unclear whether CL merely converges to a solution similar to NSCL or whether their representations remain coupled across the entire optimization trajectory, and under what conditions (class count, batch size, temperature) this alignment holds.

## Input / Output

The method takes RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 64×64 for Tiny-ImageNet, 224×224 for Mini-ImageNet) and produces two augmented views per sample via standard SimCLR augmentations (random crop, flip, color jitter, Gaussian blur). For supervised variants (NSCL, SCL, CE), class labels are additionally consumed. The output consists of learned embeddings from a ResNet-50 or ViT-Base encoder, from which pairwise cosine-similarity matrices are extracted to compute alignment metrics (linear CKA and RSA) between paired models trained under identical initialization, mini-batches, and augmentations.

## Method

The paper analyzes coupled SGD trajectories of CL and NSCL under shared randomness by working in similarity space rather than parameter space, since parameter drift grows exponentially under non-convex dynamics (Thm. 2). The key result (Thm. 1) proves that the Frobenius gap between CL and NSCL similarity matrices is bounded by a term that decreases with more classes, larger batch size, higher temperature, and smaller cumulative step size, yielding high-probability lower bounds on CKA and RSA (Cors. 1–2). The proof decomposes the per-step gradient mismatch into a reweighting error (controlled by class balance and batch concentration) and a stability term (controlled by the 1/(2τ²B)-Lipschitzness of the batch-gradient map), then unrolls the resulting recurrence via discrete Grönwall. Empirically, the authors train paired ResNet-50 and ViT-Base models on CIFAR-10/100, Mini-ImageNet, Tiny-ImageNet, and ImageNet-1K, confirming that NSCL aligns with CL more strongly than SCL or cross-entropy, and that alignment strengthens with scale and temperature as predicted.

## Results

The paper's headline finding is representational alignment: after 1,000 epochs on Tiny-ImageNet, the CL–NSCL linear CKA reaches 0.87, whereas CL–SCL CKA is only 0.043, confirming NSCL as the supervised objective most tightly coupled to CL in similarity space. In downstream accuracy (Table 1), NSCL outperforms CL on most datasets (e.g., CIFAR-10 NCCC: 94.47% vs. 88.37%; Mini-ImageNet LP: 72.60% vs. 65.30%), while SCL and CE achieve the highest absolute accuracies (e.g., Mini-ImageNet LP: 76.00% and 74.00% respectively). Alignment strengthens with more classes, higher temperature (τ=1.0 yields the highest CKA/RSA), and—under sub-linear learning-rate scaling—larger batch size, consistent with the theoretical bound in Thm. 1. In weight space, CL and NSCL diverge to 85.7° apart after 1,000 epochs, yet their representation-space vectors for a target class remain only 27.8° apart.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NSCL (Luthra et al., 2025) is the primary comparison target and the paper's central claim: it is the supervised objective whose representations remain most tightly aligned with CL throughout training (CKA ≈ 0.87 vs. ≈ 0.04 for SCL on Tiny-ImageNet after 1k epochs). In downstream accuracy, NSCL consistently beats CL (e.g., +6.1 pts NCCC on CIFAR-10, +7.3 pts LP on Mini-ImageNet) but trails SCL and CE, which achieve the highest absolute scores (SCL: 76.00% LP on Mini-ImageNet; CE: 74.00%). SCL (Khosla et al., 2020) wins on downstream accuracy across most datasets but is far less aligned with CL in representation space, because its stronger class-level constraint drives faster neural collapse that departs from CL's instance-level geometry. Cross-entropy (Shannon, 1948) sits between the two: it achieves competitive accuracy (e.g., 67.35% NCCC on CIFAR-100, the highest among all methods on that dataset) but shows intermediate alignment with CL, lower than NSCL and higher than SCL. The paper explicitly notes that NSCL is identified as the most tightly coupled supervised objective to CL—not as the strongest supervised baseline in top-1 accuracy—positioning it as a principled bridge between self-supervised and supervised representation learning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the loss-level coupling result of Luthra et al. (2025), which showed that the InfoNCE contrastive loss approximates the Negatives-Only Supervised Contrastive Learning (NSCL) loss as the number of classes grows, by asking whether this alignment persists at the representation level throughout training. It departs from the broader CL theory literature (Arora et al. 2019, Tosh et al. 2021, Saunshi et al. 2022) that typically relies on restrictive augmentation-connectivity assumptions to prove cluster recovery, instead working with label-agnostic, architecture-independent bounds on similarity-matrix drift. The empirical alignment observations of Grigg et al. (2021) are formalized here with high-probability CKA/RSA guarantees, and the analysis is positioned against the neural-collapse literature by showing that NSCL, not SCL or CE, most faithfully mimics CL's instance-level geometry.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical certification that CL and NSCL representations remain geometrically coupled (via CKA/RSA lower bounds) throughout training, even while their parameters diverge exponentially—a distinction that matters for tasks depending on representation geometry such as interpretability and segmentation. The identification of NSCL as the supervised objective most tightly coupled to CL (rather than the more 'natural' SCL) offers a principled bridge between self-supervised and supervised paradigms. Limitations include conservative worst-case bounds that are loose in practice, and the analysis is restricted to contrastive objectives; extending the framework to non-contrastive SSL (e.g., masked autoencoders, VICReg) and deriving data-dependent tighter constants are natural next steps.
