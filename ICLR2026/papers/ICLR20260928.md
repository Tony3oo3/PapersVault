## Problem

Existing LLM guardrail models are trained on general-purpose safety data and lack the domain-specific knowledge needed to detect harmful content embedded in technical jargon from high-stakes fields like finance, healthcare, and law. This gap allows adversarial prompts that exploit specialized terminology (e.g., 'haircuts' in asset valuation) to bypass detection, creating significant safety risks in professional deployments.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260928/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

EXPGUARD operates in a multi-task fashion: given only a user prompt, it predicts a binary safe/unsafe label for that prompt; given a prompt-response pair, it predicts harmfulness for both the prompt and the response simultaneously. The model is a 7B-parameter Mistral-based transformer fine-tuned for classification, and it is evaluated on both standalone prompts and prompt-response pairs across 13 harm categories plus an 'Unharmful' class.

## Method

The core contribution is a three-stage data construction pipeline (EXPGUARDMIX) that mines 2,646 domain-specific technical terms from Wikipedia via recursive crawling, Wikidata entity filtering, GPT-4o relevance screening, and human majority-vote verification, then uses those terms with GPT-4o to generate harmful and benign prompts grounded in real Wikipedia abstracts. A strict LLM-ensemble labeling protocol (Claude 3.7 Sonnet, Gemini 2.0 Flash, Qwen2.5-Max) with chain-of-thought rationales and exact-category majority voting filters 4.8% of ambiguous samples, followed by Sentence-BERT deduplication. EXPGUARD is then fine-tuned on the resulting 56,653-sample EXPGUARDTRAIN in a multi-task supervised setup, and the expert-annotated EXPGUARDTEST (2,275 samples, Cohen's Kappa 0.89–0.98) provides a gold-standard benchmark for domain-specific robustness evaluation.

## Results

On the novel EXPGUARDTEST benchmark (2,275 expert-annotated domain-specific examples), EXPGUARD achieves 93.3% F1 for prompt classification and 92.7% F1 for response classification, surpassing the previous state-of-the-art WildGuard by 8.9% and 15.3% respectively. Across eight established public safety benchmarks, EXPGUARD attains an average F1 of 85.7% on prompt classification (marginally ahead of WildGuard's 84.2%) and 78.5% on response classification (on par with WildGuard's 78.8%). API-based guardrails (Detoxify, Perspective, OpenAI Moderation) score near zero (0.3–0.6% F1) on EXPGUARDTEST, highlighting their inability to detect domain-specific harmful content.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EXPGUARD decisively outperforms all 13 baselines on EXPGUARDTEST, with the largest margins over WildGuard (+8.9% prompt, +15.3% response) and Aegis-Guard-Defensive (+10.4% prompt, +5.5% response). Among API-based tools, Detoxify, Perspective, and OpenAI Moderation are essentially non-functional on domain-specific content (≤0.6% F1), while Azure performs marginally better (14.1% prompt) but still far below LLM-based guardrails. On the eight public benchmarks, EXPGUARD achieves the highest average prompt F1 (85.7%) by a narrow 1.5% margin over WildGuard (84.2%), and matches WildGuard on response classification (78.5% vs. 78.8%). The gap over Llama-Guard3 is substantial on EXPGUARDTEST (+22.2% prompt, +8.5% response) but modest on public benchmarks (+6.8% prompt avg). ShieldGemma performs anomalously poorly on EXPGUARDTEST (17.3% prompt F1) despite reasonable public benchmark scores, suggesting a domain-specific failure mode. The retrained EXPGUARD+ variant, augmented with 270 AutoDAN-Turbo jailbreak prompts, further improves robustness against domain-specific adversarial attacks, outperforming all baselines in the jailbreak analysis.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the established line of LLM safety guardrail work (Llama-Guard, Aegis-Guard, ShieldGemma, WildGuard) by addressing a specific gap: existing models are trained on general-purpose and red-teaming data but lack the domain knowledge to detect harmful content concealed in technical jargon from finance, healthcare, and law. Rather than proposing a new architecture, the contribution is primarily in the data construction pipeline—recursive Wikipedia crawling, Wikidata entity filtering, GPT-4o-based prompt generation with domain-specific terms, and multi-LLM ensemble labeling with chain-of-thought rationales—which is then used to fine-tune a Mistral-7B backbone. The paper also draws on in-the-wild interaction data (LMSYS-Chat-1M, WildChat) and human-written safety datasets (HH-RLHF, Aegis 2.0) to maintain general moderation capability alongside domain-specific strength.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The near-zero F1 scores of widely deployed API-based moderation tools (Detoxify, Perspective, OpenAI Moderation) on domain-specific content highlight a practical safety gap that is likely underappreciated in industry deployments. The expert-annotated EXPGUARDTEST benchmark (2,275 samples) and the extensible data construction pipeline provide a reproducible template for building guardrails in other specialized domains, reducing reliance on expensive manual curation. Key open questions include whether the synthesized prompts adequately capture the full diversity of real adversarial queries, whether the approach generalizes beyond English and the three validated domains, and how the model performs as adversarial tactics evolve—limitations the authors acknowledge but do not fully resolve.
