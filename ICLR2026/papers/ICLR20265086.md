## Problem

Learned Sparse Retrieval (LSR) methods like SPLADE project token representations onto the fixed vocabulary of their backbone LLM, which constrains them to English-centric contexts and limits multilingual, cross-lingual, and out-of-domain generalization. This vocabulary dependence is a key reason LSR models have recently fallen behind dense embedding approaches on comprehensive benchmarks like MMTEB. The paper asks whether the semantically structured, language-agnostic latent features produced by Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) can serve as a superior representation space for sparse retrieval.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20265086/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

SPLARE takes raw text queries and documents as input and produces high-dimensional sparse vectors over the SAE's latent feature space (e.g., 131k dimensions for Llama Scope), where only a small subset of features is active. At inference, Top-K pooling caps the number of active dimensions (default k=40 for queries, k=400 for documents), yielding extremely compact representations. Relevance between a query and document is computed as a sparse dot product, enabling efficient exact retrieval via inverted index structures with ~5ms per-query latency on MS MARCO (8.8M documents).

## Method

SPLARE replaces the language modeling head in SPLADE with a pre-trained SAE (from Llama Scope or Gemma Scope) inserted at an intermediate transformer layer (~2/3 depth), mapping hidden states into a sparse latent vocabulary. During training, only the LLM backbone is fine-tuned via LoRA adapters (rank 64) while SAE parameters remain frozen, preserving the interpretability of the latent feature space. The training objective combines KL-divergence distillation from a cross-encoder teacher with FLOPS sparsity regularization, and bidirectional attention is enabled via Masked Next Token Prediction pretraining. At inference, Top-K pooling provides a tunable sparsity knob without retraining, and model averaging across multiple runs further boosts multilingual generalization. The key architectural insight is that SAE features are largely monosemantic and language-agnostic, providing a semantically grounded sparse space that overcomes the tokenization redundancy and vocabulary constraints inherent to SPLADE-style approaches.

## Results

SPLARE-7B (multilingual) achieves an average nDCG@10 of 62.3 on MTEB(Multilingual, v2) retrieval, making it the top-1 LSR model and placing it in the top-10 overall on the leaderboard. On the English split, it scores 59.3, and on XTREME-UP (cross-lingual retrieval from low-resource languages to English), it reaches 58.6 MRR@10—substantially outperforming most dense embedding models on this benchmark. The compact SPLARE-2B variant achieves 59.1 on MTEB(Multilingual, v2) and 41.6 on XTREME-UP, offering a strong efficiency-effectiveness trade-off. On MIRACL (18 languages), SPLARE scores 71.7 nDCG@10, surpassing the hybrid M3-Embedding (71.5) and achieving state-of-the-art results in 8 languages including the hidden test sets (German, Yoruba).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the vocabulary-based LSR baseline SPLADE-Llama (same Llama-3.1-8B backbone, identical training setup), SPLARE consistently wins: +0.4 on MTEB English (59.3 vs 58.9), +0.6 on MTEB Multilingual (62.3 vs 61.7), +2.4 on XTREME-UP (58.6 vs 56.2), and +1.8 on MIRACL (71.7 vs 69.9), with the gap largest on cross-lingual and multilingual tasks. Against other LSR methods, SPLARE outperforms Lion-SP-8B (Zeng et al., 2025) by +2.0 on MTEB Multilingual (56.3 vs 54.3 for the English-trained variant) and SPLADE-v3 by +8.2 on Multilingual (56.3 vs 38.1). Against dense embedding models, SPLARE trails the strongest systems—Qwen-3-Embedding-8B (70.9 multilingual) and gemini-embedding-001 (67.7 multilingual)—but significantly outperforms most open-source dense models on XTREME-UP, where it scores 58.6 versus 39.2 (voyage-3-large), 24.6 (Linq-Embed-Mistral), 18.7 (multilingual-e5-large), and 17.4 (gte-Qwen2-7B). On the English MTEB split, SPLARE (59.3) is competitive with SFR-Embedding-Mistral (59.4) and Linq-Embed-Mistral (60.1) but lags behind NV-Embed-v2 (62.8) and Qwen-3-Embedding-8B (69.4). The paper notes that SPLARE's advantage is most pronounced in multilingual and out-of-domain settings, while it underperforms SPLADE on the Code domain (63.0 vs 64.3), likely because the SAE features are not specialized to code semantics.

## Relation to Prior Work

SPLARE synthesizes two previously independent research threads: Learned Sparse Retrieval (SPLADE and its LLM-adapted variants like Lion-SP-8B) and Sparse Autoencoders (Llama Scope, Gemma Scope) developed for mechanistic interpretability. The key architectural departure is replacing the language-modeling head—which projects hidden states onto the fixed vocabulary space—with a pre-trained SAE encoder that maps to a learned latent feature space, effectively creating a 'latent vocabulary' unconstrained by tokenization. Unlike prior SAE-for-retrieval work (e.g., Park et al. 2025) that trains SAEs on top of an already-trained dense retriever, SPLARE uses SAEs pre-trained on the base LLM and fine-tunes the backbone via LoRA in a standard SPLADE-style distillation pipeline, enabling tighter integration of relevance learning and sparsity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SPLARE is the first LSR model to rival state-of-the-art dense retrievers on MMTEB multilingual retrieval (top-10, 62.3 avg) without synthetic data or pre-finetuning, while retaining the efficiency advantages of sparse retrieval (~5ms per query on 8.8M documents). The language-agnostic nature of SAE features directly addresses the fundamental multilingual limitation of vocabulary-based LSR, yielding consistent gains on cross-lingual benchmarks (XTREME-UP, MIRACL). A clear limitation is underperformance on the Code domain, where SAE features appear too generic; the authors suggest domain-specific SAEs as a natural extension. Open questions include whether wider SAEs (beyond 131k features) continue to improve retrieval, and whether the approach extends to multimodal settings.
