## Problem

Most existing LLM watermarking methods require privileged access to the model's decoding process (logits, sampling distributions), making them inapplicable when a third party—such as a conference organizer detecting AI-generated peer reviews—has no control over which LLM a user employs. This paper asks whether watermarks can be embedded into LLM outputs using prompt engineering alone, exploiting the model's in-context learning and instruction-following abilities, without any access to model internals.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261802/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

In the Direct Text Stamp (DTS) setting, the input is a watermarking instruction (encoding a secret key k and scheme τ) prepended as a system prompt to a normal user query; in the Indirect Prompt Injection (IPI) setting, the instruction is covertly embedded within a long document (e.g., white-text in a PDF) that the user feeds to the LLM. The LLM is treated as a pure black box accessed only via API. The output is the LLM's natural-language response, which carries the watermark; a separate detector D(y|k,τ) then computes a scalar score compared against a threshold η to decide whether the text is watermarked.

## Method

The paper proposes four ICW strategies at increasing granularity: (1) Unicode ICW inserts a zero-width space (U+200B) after every word; (2) Initials ICW biases the LLM toward words whose first letter belongs to a randomly selected 'green' letter set, detected via a z-statistic over green-initial frequency; (3) Lexical ICW instructs the LLM to overuse words from a green word list (restricted to adjectives, adverbs, and verbs, ~2,171 words at γ=20%), detected with a z-statistic analogous to Kirchenbauer et al.'s green/red list; and (4) Acrostics ICW constrains the initial letter of each sentence to follow a secret string, detected via Levenshtein distance with a z-statistic. Each strategy is paired with a tailored detection rule, and the IPI variant hides the instruction inside a document so that any LLM processing the document will produce watermarked output. The approach is entirely model-agnostic and requires no fine-tuning, weight access, or decoding control—only a carefully crafted natural-language instruction.

## Results

With GPT-o3-mini, all four ICW methods achieve ROC-AUC ≥ 0.995 in both the Direct Text Stamp (DTS) and Indirect Prompt Injection (IPI) settings, with Unicode and Acrostics ICW reaching a perfect 1.000. Detection performance is highly model-dependent: with the weaker GPT-4o-mini, Initials ICW drops to ROC-AUC 0.572 and Acrostics ICW to 0.590 in DTS, while Unicode ICW remains at 1.000 and Lexical ICW holds at 0.910. In the IPI setting (where watermarking instructions are covertly embedded in a full ICLR paper), GPT-o3-mini still achieves ROC-AUC 0.997–1.000 across all methods, confirming that LLMs can follow hidden instructions even in long-context scenarios. Text quality (LLM-as-a-Judge overall score) for ICW methods ranges from 4.28 to 4.81, comparable to unwatermarked text (4.99) and far above PostMark (3.00).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against YCZ+23 (post-hoc lexical substitution), ICW methods with GPT-o3-mini match or slightly exceed its DTS ROC-AUC of 0.998 (Unicode and Acrostics reach 1.000; Initials 0.999; Lexical 0.995), while the critical advantage is that ICW is the only approach applicable in the IPI setting where the reviewer has no incentive to post-process their own output. Against PostMark (black-box paraphrase-based watermark), ICW methods with GPT-o3-mini achieve higher DTS ROC-AUC (0.995–1.000 vs. 0.977) and dramatically better text quality (overall LLM-as-a-Judge score 4.28–4.81 vs. PostMark's 3.00), again with the added IPI applicability. Against GPTZero (post-hoc AI-text detector), all four ICW methods with GPT-o3-mini achieve TPR@4%FPR of 0.964–1.000, surpassing GPTZero's 0.890. The key differentiator across all comparisons is that ICW operates entirely in a black-box, prompt-only setting and is the sole method that functions in the IPI scenario, making it uniquely suited for detecting AI misuse in peer review.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of in-process LLM watermarking (e.g., Kirchenbauer et al. 2023, Zhao et al. 2023a), which requires direct control over the decoding process via logits perturbation or pseudo-random sampling, by showing that watermarks can be embedded purely through prompt engineering and in-context learning. It also contrasts with post-hoc black-box methods (PostMark, YCZ+23) that modify already-generated text, since ICW embeds the signal during generation without any model access. The theoretical false-alarm guarantees for Lexical and Initials ICWs are directly inherited from the green/red list analysis of Zhao et al. 2023a, while the IPI setting repurposes the indirect prompt injection attack literature (Greshake et al. 2023) with a reversed threat model where the benign party injects the instruction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ICW shifts watermarking control from LLM providers to third parties (e.g., conference organizers), addressing a practical gap where no one with motivation to watermark has access to the model's internals. The sharp capability dependency—near-perfect detection with GPT-o3-mini versus near-random performance with GPT-4o-mini—suggests ICW will become increasingly powerful as instruction-following improves, but also means it is currently fragile to model choice. Key open questions include robustness to adversarial removal when the scheme is known, whether ICW can be integrated into alignment training to make it more reliable, and whether the approach generalizes beyond the two proprietary models tested. The IPI application to academic peer review is a concrete, high-stakes use case that could drive adoption.
