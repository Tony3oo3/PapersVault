## Problem

Existing KV cache eviction methods for LLM inference rely on a 'stability assumption' that a fixed subset of cache entries remains consistently important throughout generation, and they aggregate multiple importance observations via simple averaging. This paper shows the stability assumption is inherently fragile—importance scores can shift abruptly during generation—and that mean aggregation is highly vulnerable in these extreme cases, producing severe quality drops. Prior work has focused almost exclusively on refining importance indicators (the scoring step) while defaulting to mean aggregation without questioning its robustness.

## Input / Output

The method operates during the prefilling stage and takes the full KV cache (K, V ∈ R^{n×d_h}), the output projection matrix W_O, and query vectors from m recent historical tokens (default m=32) as input. It produces a compressed KV cache (K̂, V̂) containing only the top-risk entries plus the most recent m tokens' cache, which replaces the full cache for all subsequent decoding steps. The entire eviction computation is linear-time in n (matching mean aggregation's cost) and introduces no additional memory overhead beyond the importance matrix already computed for scoring.

## Method

The core contribution is 'defensive aggregation,' a two-step, linear-time strategy that replaces mean aggregation with a worst-case risk control perspective. Step 1 (Worst-case Risk Estimation) computes R̃_i = max_j I_{j,i} for each cache entry, capturing the peak importance observed across all m historical token queries rather than their average. Step 2 (Adaptive Prior-Risk Correction) computes a head-level prior R̄ = (1/n)ΣR̃_i and sets R_i = max(R̃_i, R̄), acting as a Bayesian smoothing that prevents under-observation from limited historical windows from causing severe underestimation. DefensiveKV integrates this aggregation into the CriticalKV scoring pipeline (attention weights × projected value norms with SnapKV pooling), while Layer-DefensiveKV further normalizes projected value norms layer-wise and selects entries jointly across all layers for adaptive budget allocation. The paper provides a formal theoretical analysis (Theorems 1–2) proving that under a Bernoulli mixture model of fragile stability, the max-based estimator has strictly lower bias than mean aggregation for estimating true worst-case risk.

## Results

At 20% KV cache size on Llama-3.1-8B, DefensiveKV achieves an average LongBench score of 46.68 (4.8% quality loss vs. full cache) and Layer-DefensiveKV reaches 48.01 (2.6% loss), compared to the strongest baseline CriticalKV at 43.88 (11.1% loss) — representing 2.3× and 4.3× reductions in quality loss, respectively. On Mistral-7B at 20% cache, Layer-DefensiveKV achieves only 1.3% loss (46.91 vs. 47.50 full cache) versus CriticalKV's 9.7% loss. In Needle-in-a-Haystack retrieval at 10% cache, DefensiveKV and Layer-DefensiveKV score 194 and 193 on Llama-3.1-8B (near-lossless), while CriticalKV drops to 140; on Mistral-7B the gap widens to 139/161 vs. 28 (over 5× improvement).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DefensiveKV and Layer-DefensiveKV consistently outperform all six baselines across all three models and all task domains at 20% cache. Against the strongest baseline CriticalKV (43.88 avg on Llama-3.1-8B), DefensiveKV wins on 13/16 datasets and Layer-DefensiveKV on 15/16, with the largest gaps in summarization (GovRep: 30.99 vs. 28.35) and few-shot learning (TREC: 69.00 vs. 56.50). The training-based DuoAttention (39.52 avg) underperforms even SnapKV (40.21) despite its offline training cost, while the fixed-pattern StreamingLLM (34.72) suffers the largest degradation at 29% quality loss. On Mistral-7B, where long-context ability is weaker, the advantage is most pronounced: Layer-DefensiveKV (46.91) nearly matches full cache (47.50) while CriticalKV drops to 42.88. In the extreme 10% cache Needle-in-a-Haystack setting, the gap widens dramatically — on Mistral-7B, Layer-DefensiveKV scores 161 versus CriticalKV's 28, a 5.8× improvement.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper operates within the dominant scoring-aggregation framework for KV cache eviction (H2O, Scissorhands, SnapKV, CriticalKV, AdaKV, CAKE), where prior work has almost exclusively refined the scoring step (importance indicators) while defaulting to mean aggregation. The key departure is a conceptual reframe: rather than improving importance scores, the paper identifies the aggregation step as the critical vulnerability, arguing that mean aggregation is fundamentally blind to the fragility of the stability assumption. It introduces 'defensive aggregation'—a two-step worst-case risk control strategy (max-based estimation plus adaptive prior-risk correction inspired by Laplace smoothing)—and builds DefensiveKV on top of CriticalKV's scoring, with Layer-DefensiveKV adding layer-wise budget allocation orthogonal to existing strategies like PyramidKV and CAKE.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a widely-held implicit assumption in KV cache eviction (that mean aggregation is a safe default) and shows it produces outlier failures comparable to using a single unreliable token, with 65+ outlier instances in a single trial. The practical impact is substantial: a 4.3× reduction in quality loss over the strongest baseline at 20% cache, with negligible computational overhead (two linear-time operations), making it a drop-in replacement for any existing scoring-aggregation method. Open questions include whether other risk measures (e.g., CVaR) could further improve upon the max-based estimator, how the approach behaves under even more extreme compression or with very short observation windows, and whether the worst-case framing transfers to other compression paradigms (quantization, sparse attention) beyond eviction.
