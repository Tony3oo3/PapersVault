## Problem

While higher learning rates have been empirically observed to reduce shortcut reliance and improve robustness against spurious correlations, the underlying mechanism remains unexplained. Prior theoretical work treats gradient-based optimizers as black boxes converging to max-margin solutions, leaving the role of training hyperparameters—particularly batch size and learning rate—in modulating shortcut dependence poorly understood.

## Input / Output

Theoretically, the analysis operates on the four-point data model (a 2D feature vector [y, Bz] with binary labels) using linear classifiers under exponential loss, producing a weight vector whose spurious-component magnitude w_z measures shortcut reliance. Empirically, the method is applied to standard vision inputs (32×32 CMNIST, 64×64 Domino/Waterbirds, 112×112 CelebA/CIFAR-10) and text inputs (sentences from Multi-NLI and CivilComments), producing class labels evaluated via worst-group accuracy (WGA) and overall accuracy (ACC) across majority and minority subpopulations.

## Method

The paper identifies batch size as a critical additional factor and shows that SGD's implicit regularization—characterized via backward error analysis as an added penalty on the average squared mini-batch gradient norm—scales with the ratio ε/b and systematically suppresses gradient variance across mini-batches. This variance penalty shifts the optimal solution toward smaller w_z (less spurious feature reliance) and larger w_y (more invariant feature reliance), an effect absent in GD whose implicit regularization only penalizes the full-batch gradient norm. Theoretically, non-asymptotic guarantees are established in the four-point model proving that SGD reduces w_z by Θ(Bε/b) while GD increases it by Θ(ε), with an explicit upper bound on batch size b ≤ Õ(B/ρ(1−ρ)) for the benefit to hold. Empirically, the effect is validated on MLPs, ResNets, and BERT across eight benchmarks, showing that smaller batch sizes and larger learning rates (within the convergence regime) consistently improve WGA, and that combining small batches with explicit debiasing methods (DFR, AFR, EVaLS) yields 25–37% WGA gains on multi-level spurious datasets.

## Results

On biased datasets (ρ=5%), reducing batch size from 256 to 8 improves worst-group accuracy (WGA) by up to 15.9 points on Domino (59.3 vs 43.4), 11.5 points on Waterbirds (79.7 vs 68.2), and 11.4 points on CelebA (46.0 vs 45.0). On the multi-level spurious dataset Domino-CMF, applying small batch sizes to explicit debiasing methods (DFR, AFR, EVaLS) yields 25–37% WGA improvements over the same methods with large batches. For example, DFR jumps from 42.7% to 72.9% WGA, and EVaLS from 51.2% to 76.7% when switching from large to small batch sizes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its small-batch training strategy against two explicit shortcut-mitigation baselines: AFR (Qiu et al., ICML 2023) and EVaLS (Ghaznavi et al., 2025), as well as DFR and standard ERM. On single-level spurious datasets (Waterbirds, CelebA), AFR and EVaLS with large batches already achieve strong WGA (90.4% and 85.3% respectively), so the small-batch advantage is modest or even negative for AFR (77.8 vs 90.4 on Waterbirds). However, on the multi-level spurious dataset Domino-CMF, the gap reverses dramatically: EVaLS with small batch (76.7%) outperforms its large-batch counterpart (51.2%) by 25.5 points, and AFR improves from 40.3% to 67.3% (27 points). DFR with small batch (72.9%) also substantially beats its large-batch version (42.7%), and even surpasses EVaLS-large (51.2%). The key takeaway is that small batch sizes act as a complementary inductive bias that is especially critical when explicit methods fail to remove second-level spurious correlations, as in Domino-CMF.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the backward error analysis frameworks of Barrett & Dherin (ICLR 2021) for GD and Smith et al. (ICLR 2021) for SGD, which characterize how finite learning rates introduce implicit regularization terms into the effective loss landscape. It departs from prior shortcut-learning work (Puli et al., 2023; Nagarajan et al., 2021; Xue et al., 2024) that treated optimizers as black boxes converging to max-margin solutions and focused on data-dependent parameters (B, ρ), by instead analyzing how training hyperparameters—specifically the learning rate-to-batch-size ratio—modulate shortcut reliance through the structure of implicit regularization. The paper also draws a conceptual bridge to domain generalization methods (FISHR, gradient matching) that penalize gradient variance across domains, reframing each mini-batch as a sampled domain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous theoretical explanation for the empirically observed benefit of high learning rates on robustness, and identifies batch size as an equally critical but previously underappreciated factor. The practical takeaway—that combining small batch sizes with appropriately tuned higher learning rates introduces a favorable inductive bias toward robustness without explicit debiasing methods—could reduce the need for complex hyperparameter searches in shortcut-mitigation pipelines. Limitations include the theoretical analysis being restricted to linear models with exponential loss (though empirical results extend to ResNets and BERT), and the open question of whether the gradient-variance-suppression mechanism generalizes to multi-level or unknown spurious correlations beyond the controlled four-point setting.
