## Problem

Training scaling laws (generalization loss vs. data size) and inference-time scaling laws (pass@k vs. number of attempts) have been studied in isolation, with no solvable model analytically connecting how training progress shapes the inference-time scaling exponent. This paper asks: as a model's generalization error decreases with more training data, how does the pass@k failure curve steepen, and at what point does irreducible instance-level stochasticity cap further improvement?

## Input / Output

The model consumes frozen d-dimensional feature vectors (e.g., ResNet-18 penultimate-layer embeddings or LLM token representations) and, at inference, k fresh target realizations drawn from an instance-specific Gaussian whose variance is governed by a latent precision τ_x ~ Gamma(β/2, 1). The output is a scalar linear prediction, the pass@k failure rate L_inf(k; N) as a joint function of trial count and training set size, the effective inference exponent β_eff(N) extracted from local log-log slopes, and a closed-form compute-allocation rule specifying the optimal split between training samples and inference attempts under a fixed budget.

## Method

The Latent Instance Difficulty (LID) model formalizes last-layer ridge regression on frozen features where each instance carries a latent precision τ_x controlling target variance, with power-law feature spectra σ_j² ∝ j^{-(1+α)}. Training on single noisy realizations recovers standard generalization scaling (N^{-α} overparameterized, d/N underparameterized), while the key theoretical contribution is a two-tail mixture law for pass@k failure: L_inf(k;N) = C₁k^{-β} + C₂(N)k^{-γ(N)}, where the intrinsic tail exponent β comes from the heavy-tailed difficulty distribution and the finite-N tail exponent γ(N) ∝ 1/L_gen(N) encodes the model's residual bias. This yields β_eff(N) = min{β, γ(N)}, which monotonically increases with N and saturates at β, revealing that learning progressively 'shrinks' the hard tail of the error distribution. The paper further derives a compute-allocation rule (Proposition 4.5) showing that the training-dependent exponent introduces a logarithmic correction that shifts the optimal budget toward more training until β_eff saturates, after which additional inference attempts become the dominant gain. Predictions are validated in controlled simulations and two real-data proxies: CIFAR-10H (human label variance as instance difficulty) and GSM8K teacher-student distillation.

## Results

The LID model predicts a two-tail mixture law for pass@k failure: L_inf(k;N) = P̃ k^{-β} + P̃_N(N) k^{-γ(N)}, where the intrinsic exponent β is set by the difficulty distribution's tail and the finite-N exponent γ(N) = Θ(1/L_gen(N)) shrinks as training improves. The effective inference exponent β_eff(N) = min{β, γ(N)} increases monotonically with N and saturates at β, with the empirical fit β_eff(N) = β − Δ/(1 + c_β N^ν). On CIFAR-10H (frozen ResNet-18, d=512, linear head), the generalization loss exhibits the predicted 1/N tail for N ≫ d, and β_eff(N) rises then plateaus. On GSM8K distillation (Flan-T5-XL → Flan-T5-small, LoRA r=8, N ∈ [10, 6309]), the strict greedy MSE decreases with N while L_inf(k;N) steepens and β_eff(N) saturates, confirming the two-tail crossover.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper that does not benchmark against competing methods; instead, it validates its closed-form predictions against controlled simulations and two real-data proxies. The LID model extends the inference-scaling framework of Levi (2024), which analyzed pass@k in isolation, by making the exponent training-dependent through the generalization loss. It builds on the training-side scaling of Maloney et al. (2022) for high-dimensional linear regression with power-law spectra, recovering the N^{-α} (overparameterized) and d/N (underparameterized) regimes as the 'training' component. The key empirical validation is that both CIFAR-10H (human-label variance as instance difficulty) and GSM8K (teacher-student distillation) exhibit the predicted qualitative signature: pass@k curves steepen with more training data and the effective slope plateaus at an intrinsic limit, consistent with the two-tail mixture law. The compute-allocation analysis (Proposition 4.5) shows that the training-dependent β_eff'(N) introduces a logarithmic correction that shifts the optimal budget toward more training in the finite-N regime, a prediction confirmed by the contour plots in Fig. 2.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate lines of scaling-law research: the generalization-loss scaling of high-dimensional linear models (Maloney et al., 2022; Bartlett et al., 2020) and the inference-time pass@k scaling analyzed in isolation (Levi, 2024; Snell et al., 2024; Brown et al., 2024). The key departure is making the pass@k exponent a function of training progress rather than a fixed task property: prior inference-scaling models treat the power-law slope as constant, while here it grows with N and saturates at an intrinsic difficulty index β. The analytical toolkit (ridge regression, Marchenko-Pastur, Tauberian theorems) is standard; the novelty lies in the two-tail mixture structure that couples the finite-N bias tail to the heavy-tailed difficulty prior.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The LID framework gives a clean, closed-form criterion for when additional training data is more valuable than additional inference attempts, and when the reverse holds — a practically important question as inference-time compute (best-of-N, search) becomes a major cost center. The saturation prediction (β_eff → β) is directly testable and provides a diagnostic: if pass@k curves stop steepening with more training, the bottleneck is irreducible instance stochasticity, not model bias. Key limitations are the restriction to last-layer linear regression with scalar targets and the proxy-based empirical validation (CIFAR-10H human-label variance, small GSM8K distillation); extending the two-tail mechanism to multi-layer non-linear architectures, structured autoregressive outputs, and settings where the difficulty distribution itself shifts with model scale remain open.
