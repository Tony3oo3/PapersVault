## Problem

In standard InfoNCE-based audio-text contrastive learning, the pushing force from negative samples is generally not collinear with the pulling force from positive samples, producing a perpendicular component that causes optimization trajectory drift. This drift is a double-edged sword: it carries supplementary information from negatives but its uncontrolled, fluctuating magnitude destabilizes the optimization path, slowing convergence and degrading final alignment quality. No prior method selectively controlled this perpendicular component while preserving the useful negative-sample signal.

## Input / Output

The method consumes 16 kHz audio clips paired with text captions (English or one of seven translated languages) and produces L2-normalized audio and text embeddings in a shared d-dimensional space. During training, an auxiliary text support vector is constructed by displacing the text embedding toward its positive audio embedding by a learned semantic radius R; this support vector is used only in the SVR loss term and is discarded at inference, where retrieval and classification proceed identically to standard CLAP via cosine similarity ranking. For zero-shot classification, text labels are templated as 'This is a sound of {classlabel}' and matched by highest embedding similarity.

## Method

SupCLAP adds a Support Vector Regularization (SVR) term to the standard symmetric InfoNCE objective. The SVR term is an auxiliary contrastive loss computed between a text support vector t_sup = t⁺ + R·û (where û is the unit vector from text to positive audio) and the batch of audio embeddings. The key geometric insight is that the Jacobian of t_sup with respect to t⁺ acts as a projection that leaves the parallel component of the negative pushing force unchanged while scaling the perpendicular component by the factor (1 − R/‖a⁺−t⁺‖), thereby selectively suppressing trajectory drift without discarding negative-sample information. The semantic radius R is modeled unsupervised via two strategies: StaticSVR (a single globally shared learnable scalar) and DynamicSVR (a 3-layer MLP that predicts an instance-specific R from the batch similarity vector), with a ReLU-based constraint term penalizing R < 0 or R > ‖a⁺−t⁺‖ to prevent directional inversion or over-magnification of the perpendicular component. The final loss is L_orig + α·L_svr (+ β·L_cons), with bidirectional application to both text-to-audio and audio-to-text directions yielding the best results.

## Results

On monolingual AudioCaps retrieval, InfoNCE+bi-DynamicSVR achieves 44.16% T2A R@1 and 59.66% A2T R@1, outperforming the InfoNCE baseline (41.87% / 56.72%) by +2.29 and +2.94 points respectively. On Clotho, the same method reaches 19.75% T2A R@1 and 25.31% A2T R@1 versus 18.67% / 22.61% for InfoNCE. For zero-shot audio classification, bi-DynamicSVR attains 92.1% top-1 accuracy on ESC-50 and 83.74% on US8K, surpassing the InfoNCE baseline (89.6% / 81.63%). The method adds negligible overhead: peak GPU memory increases by only ~100 MB and training time by <2% relative to InfoNCE.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SupCLAP (bi-DynamicSVR) consistently outperforms all 12 baselines across retrieval and classification. On AudioCaps T2A R@1, it beats the strongest retrieval baselines—Cacophony (41.00), T-CLAP (39.70), and ReCLAP (37.10)—by 3.16, 4.46, and 7.06 points respectively, and surpasses SigLIP (36.74) by 7.42 points. On zero-shot classification (ESC-50), bi-DynamicSVR reaches 92.1%, exceeding COLLAT (84.0%), AudioCLIP (69.4%), and Wav2CLIP (41.4%) by 8.1, 22.7, and 50.7 points. In the multilingual setting, SupCLAP improves over ML-CLAP (37.20→39.75 T2A R@1) and ATRI-CACL (39.44→43.61 T2A R@1), confirming that SVR is orthogonal to and composable with existing advanced schemes. The method also generalizes under distribution shift (Clotho→AudioCaps A2T R@1: 29.62 vs. 26.68 for InfoNCE) and large-scale WavCaps data (A2T R@1: 21.30 vs. 20.60), with no additional inference cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the CLAP contrastive learning framework (Elizalde et al., 2023) by introducing a Support Vector Regularization (SVR) term that geometrically reshapes the gradient space. While prior SVM-inspired contrastive methods (Max-Margin CL, SV-Learner, T-MASS) use support vectors to handle hard negatives or noisy labels, SupCLAP repurposes the support vector concept to control a specific optimization instability—the perpendicular component of the negative pushing force—that the authors identify as the root cause of trajectory drift. The theoretical force-decomposition analysis (pulling vs. pushing, parallel vs. perpendicular) provides a new mechanistic lens on InfoNCE optimization that is orthogonal to existing loss-function modifications like SigLIP or temporal-enhanced variants.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The trajectory drift analysis offers a reusable diagnostic framework for understanding why contrastive learning converges slowly or suboptimally, potentially applicable beyond audio-text to vision-language and other contrastive settings. Practically, SVR is attractive because it requires no extra data, no inference-time computation, and adds negligible training overhead while consistently improving both InfoNCE and SigLIP baselines across monolingual, multilingual, large-scale, and distribution-shift scenarios. Open questions include whether the force-decomposition insight generalizes to non-symmetric or multi-positive contrastive objectives, whether the DynamicSVR predictor's reliance on a lightweight MLP limits its expressiveness in very high-dimensional or noisy embedding spaces, and whether the approach remains beneficial when batch sizes are extremely large (where the local perpendicular component from mini-batch stochasticity diminishes).
