## Problem

As AI systems become more capable and are entrusted with more complex, consequential tasks, it is unclear whether their failures will be dominated by bias (consistent pursuit of a wrong goal, i.e., systematic misalignment) or by variance (incoherent, inconsistent behavior that does not further any goal, i.e., a 'hot mess'). Prior AI safety work focused on systematic misalignment scenarios but did not quantify the relative contribution of incoherent failures, nor how this decomposition changes as model intelligence and task complexity increase.

## Input / Output

The method consumes ≥30 samples of model responses to the same question, varying both input randomness (different randomly sampled few-shot contexts) and output randomness (different autoregressive sampling seeds), and produces a per-question decomposition of total error into bias² and variance terms. For multiple-choice tasks the decomposition uses KL divergence with a geometric-mean reference distribution; for SWE-Bench it uses binary unit-test pass/fail vectors of variable length per task; for open-ended safety questions it uses variance of text-embedding vectors. The headline output, error-incoherence, is the ratio of summed variance to summed error across a question set, yielding a value in [0,1] that remains comparable across models with very different overall error rates.

## Method

The core contribution is adapting the classical bias-variance decomposition to a fixed-model, test-time setting: instead of averaging over training seeds, the expectation is taken over input (few-shot context) and output (sampling) randomness for a single model, using a KL divergence decomposition where bias is D_KL(y ‖ f̄) and variance is E_ε[D_KL(f̄ ‖ f_ε)], with f̄ the normalized geometric mean of log-probabilities across samples. They define error-incoherence as the fraction of total error attributable to variance, enabling cross-model comparison independent of absolute error level. To validate findings in a controlled setting, they train autoregressive transformers of varying sizes (47K–5M parameters) via decoding-based regression to predict steepest-descent update steps on an ill-conditioned quadratic (condition number 50), then measure bias and variance of the model's own rollout trajectories relative to the true optimum. They further analyze scaling across the Qwen3 family (up to 32B) with questions grouped by reference-model reasoning length as a complexity proxy, and test ensembling and reasoning-budget interventions as ways to reduce error-incoherence.

## Results

Across GPQA, MMLU, SWE-Bench, and Model-Written Evals, error-incoherence (the fraction of total error attributable to variance) consistently increases with reasoning length and action sequence length for all frontier models tested (Sonnet 4, O3-Mini, O4-Mini, Qwen3). On the Qwen3 family (up to 32B) evaluated on MMLU, easy questions show decreasing error-incoherence with model scale while the hardest questions show increasing error-incoherence, with variance scaling slopes falling below bias slopes for the hardest group. In the synthetic optimizer setting, larger transformers reduce bias substantially faster than variance, so final loss becomes increasingly variance-dominated with model size. Ensembling E samples reduces variance at the theoretical 1/E rate, and larger reasoning budgets slightly reduce error-incoherence, though natural variation in reasoning length has a much stronger effect on incoherence than budget changes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new method to compare against baselines; instead, it measures error-incoherence across model families, task difficulties, and reasoning conditions. Comparing frontier models on GPQA, O4-Mini shows higher baseline error-incoherence but a smaller slope with reasoning length, while O3-Mini has the largest slope but the lowest baseline. Within the Qwen3 family, all sizes show nearly identical incoherence levels and slopes on GPQA despite large performance differences, suggesting reasoning length (not model size) is the dominant driver. On MMLU, the hardest question group (longest reasoning) shows variance slopes that fall below bias slopes as models scale, meaning larger models remain constrained by incoherence on difficult tasks. The synthetic optimizer experiment provides a controlled comparison: smaller models reach a lower incoherence plateau after a tipping point where they can no longer follow the correct trajectory, while larger models continue reducing bias but accumulate more variance over longer rollouts. Ensembling (up to 32 samples) on O4-Mini/GPQA confirms the theoretical 1/E variance reduction, dropping error-incoherence substantially without affecting bias.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the classical bias-variance decomposition (specifically the KL-divergence formulation from Yang et al., 2020) as a lens for AI safety, operationalizing Sohl-Dickstein's (2023) 'hot mess theory' into a measurable quantity called error-incoherence. It connects to the inverse-scaling literature on reasoning length (Gema et al., 2025; Ghosal et al., 2025), which observed performance degradation with extended thinking but did not distinguish systematic from random errors. The synthetic optimizer experiment draws on decoding-based regression (Song & Bahri, 2025) and the mesa-optimizer framing (Hubinger et al., 2019) to create a controlled setting where bias and variance can be cleanly separated.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reframes the AI safety debate by providing empirical evidence that as models tackle harder tasks requiring longer action sequences, failures become increasingly variance-dominated (incoherent) rather than bias-dominated (coherent misalignment), suggesting the near-term risk profile resembles industrial accidents more than goal-directed adversarial behavior. This shifts the relative priority of alignment research toward reward misspecification and goal hacking (bias sources) over defending against coherent malicious optimization. Key open questions include: the mechanistic origin of increasing incoherence with trajectory length, whether the trend holds for truly open-ended goals where bias is ill-defined, and whether error-correction mechanisms (beyond ensembling) can scale to real-world action loops where state is irreversible.
