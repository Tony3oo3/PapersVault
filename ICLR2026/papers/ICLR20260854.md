## Problem

Existing spectrum-to-structure methods are confined to single spectroscopic modalities and treat spectra as fixed numerical features, making it impossible to integrate heterogeneous evidence (e.g., IR + NMR + MS) or incorporate experimental context. No prior approach unifies continuous (IR, Raman, UV-Vis, NMR) and discrete (MS) spectral types within a shared representation space for joint structural reasoning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260854/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model accepts one or more spectra per molecule, each converted into a natural-language prompt listing characteristic peak positions and normalized intensities (e.g., wavenumbers for IR/Raman, chemical shifts for NMR, m/z values for MS) along with experimental metadata such as ionization mode, collision energy, and instrument type. Multiple spectra are concatenated into a single prompt via a mapping function. The output is a single SMILES string generated autoregressively, with no modality-specific encoders or retrieval components in the pipeline.

## Method

SpectraLLM repurposes a frozen Qwen3-32B language model via LoRA fine-tuning, where all spectral modalities are serialized as structured textual peak descriptions in a shared linguistic space, eliminating the need for modality-specific encoders or handcrafted rules. The model is trained with standard cross-entropy loss on autoregressive SMILES generation over a corpus of 5.5M+ spectra paired with 940K+ molecules drawn from QM9s, the Multimodal Spectroscopic dataset, MassSpecGym, and MassBank. At inference, nucleus sampling (p=0.7) with temperature τ=0.4 balances structural accuracy and diversity. The key architectural insight is that expressing both continuous and discrete spectra as natural language enables the LLM to perform symbolic reasoning over complementary spectroscopic cues—capturing substructural patterns that are individually ambiguous but jointly constraining—without any cross-modal encoding artifacts that plague vision-language alternatives.

## Results

On the QM9s dataset, SpectraLLM achieves Tanimoto similarity of 0.1921 (IR), 0.2500 (Raman), and 0.4151 (NMR), more than doubling the strongest baseline in each modality. On the Multimodal Spectroscopic dataset, joint NMR+IR+MS input yields Tanimoto 0.4875, cosine 0.5973, and functional-group recovery of 0.8103. For mass spectrometry, SpectraLLM reaches 99.74% validity on MassSpecGym with cosine similarity 0.2558 and functional-group score 0.5003, while maintaining near-perfect validity (>98%) across all MS benchmarks where baselines drop to 23–79%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SpectraLLM outperforms all five baselines across nearly every metric and modality. Against IR-to-Structure and Spectra2Structure on vibrational/electronic spectra, SpectraLLM more than doubles Tanimoto similarity (e.g., 0.1921 vs. 0.0965 for IR; 0.2500 vs. 0.1089 for Raman) and reduces MCES by 30–40%. The NMR comparison is the most dramatic: SpectraLLM achieves Tanimoto 0.4151 and 98.92% validity versus NMR2Struct's 0.0433 and 47.62%, a roughly 10× gap in structural similarity. For mass spectrometry, SpectraLLM dominates Spec2Mol on all three MS benchmarks (e.g., Tanimoto 0.1844 vs. 0.0988 on Multimodal Spectroscopic) and surpasses DiffMS on validity (99.74% vs. 57.16% on MassSpecGym), cosine similarity, and functional-group recovery, though DiffMS edges it slightly on raw Tanimoto for MassSpecGym (0.1597 vs. 0.1533). The consistent pattern is that SpectraLLM's language-based representation yields substantially higher structural fidelity and near-perfect SMILES validity, whereas encoder-decoder and diffusion baselines suffer from decoding instability and lower substructural alignment.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of modality-specific encoders (CNNs for vibrational spectra, diffusion models for MS, contrastive learning for IR) by representing all spectral modalities—continuous (IR, Raman, UV-Vis, NMR) and discrete (MS)—as structured natural language prompts processed by a single frozen LLM (Qwen3-32B) fine-tuned via LoRA. It extends the single-spectrum-to-structure task (Spec2Mol, IR-to-Structure, NMR2Struct, DiffMS) to a unified multi-spectral setting, and contrasts its language-centric design against a vision-language model (VLM) pipeline that renders spectra as images, finding the latter consistently inferior. The work also connects to the broader trend of repurposing general-purpose LLMs for scientific reasoning, but is distinctive in treating raw physical measurements (wavenumbers, chemical shifts, m/z values) as first-class linguistic tokens rather than requiring domain-specific encoders.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a general-purpose LLM, with only lightweight LoRA adapters, can internalize spectrum-to-structure mappings across five spectroscopic modalities without any modality-specific architecture, suggesting a scalable template for other scientific inverse problems. The monotonic accuracy gains from multi-spectral fusion (e.g., Tanimoto rising from 0.2500 for Raman alone to 0.4875 for NMR+IR+MS jointly) mirror expert analytical practice and point toward practical deployment in laboratories where multiple instruments are available. Key limitations include the small-molecule focus (<500 Da), reliance on simulated spectra for most modalities, and a performance ceiling imposed by inherent spectral ambiguity (structurally distinct molecules sharing similar peaks). Open questions include whether the text-based representation is truly necessary versus a convenient interface, how the approach scales to larger or chiral molecules, and whether the VLM underperformance generalizes beyond the specific Qwen2.5-VL encoder tested.
