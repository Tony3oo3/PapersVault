## Problem

The formal stability-based memorization score of Feldman & Zhang (2020) requires training O(dataset size) models, making it infeasible at scale. Existing proxies (curvature, cumulative loss, influence functions) either lack a theoretical link to this definition or incur substantial computational overhead (7.8×–26× training cost). The paper asks whether a per-sample signal obtainable during standard training can serve as a theoretically grounded, near-free memorization proxy.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a standard supervised training pipeline (RGB images at 32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 224×224 for ImageNet) and, at each SGD iteration, computes the gradient of the per-sample loss with respect to the input vector via one additional matrix multiplication (backprop error × weight matrix). The primary output is a scalar CSG score per training sample (sum of squared input-gradient norms over all iterations), which can be thresholded to flag mislabeled examples or identify biased subpopulations. A secondary output is an early-stopping signal derived from the peak of the average sample gradient (equivalently, the weight norm), eliminating the need for a held-out validation set.

## Method

Cumulative Sample Gradient (CSG) accumulates the squared L2 norm of each sample's input-space loss gradient across all SGD iterations, yielding a single scalar per sample that is high for hard, late-learned (likely memorized) examples and low for easy, early-learned (likely generalized) ones. Theoretically, the paper proves two bounds: CSG is linearly bounded by learning time (Theorem 4.2) and by the Feldman–Zhang memorization score (Theorem 4.3), both under standard SGD convergence assumptions (Lipschitz smoothness, bounded gradient variance, β-stability, L-bounded loss). A key empirical observation is that the average sample gradient follows a rise–peak–decline trajectory whose peak coincides with the first minimum of the double-descent validation loss and with the peak of the network weight norm, enabling validation-set-free early stopping. Building on this, Sample Gradient Assisted Loss (SGAL) accumulates per-sample loss only until the gradient peak (typically 10–30% of total epochs), achieving 97–99% of the memorization correlation of full-training proxies at 0.1–0.3× the compute cost, and up to 140× faster than input-curvature methods.

## Results

CSG and SGAL achieve cosine similarity of 0.84/0.71 and 0.86/0.78 (CIFAR-100/ImageNet) with the Feldman & Zhang memorization score, at only 0.1–0.3× the compute cost of standard training. This represents up to 5 orders of magnitude speedup over the original memorization score, ~140× speedup over input curvature, and up to 10× speedup over CSL (the prior state-of-the-art proxy). For mislabeled-sample detection, CSG achieves AUROC of 0.9936 on CIFAR-100 at 30% label noise, surpassing all baselines including curvature (0.9932) and CSL (0.9903).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the prior state-of-the-art proxy CSL (Ravikumar et al., 2025a), SGAL achieves 97–99% of CSL's correlation (0.77 vs 0.79 on CIFAR-100) while requiring only 10–30% of the compute, and CSG is up to 10× faster with slightly lower correlation (0.72 vs 0.79). Against input curvature (Garg et al., 2024), CSG is ~140× faster and substantially more accurate (CS 0.84 vs 0.69 on CIFAR-100; Corr. 0.72 vs 0.49). Against TracIn (Pruthi et al., 2020) and GraNd (Paul et al., 2021), which require 26× and 7.8× compute respectively and are infeasible on ImageNet, CSG matches or exceeds their CIFAR-100 correlation at a fraction of the cost. Against VoG (Agarwal et al., 2022) and SAMIS (Agiollo et al., 2024), CSG achieves higher correlation (0.84 vs 0.80 and 0.53 CS) at lower or equal compute. For mislabeled-sample detection, CSG outperforms all baselines on CIFAR-100 across all noise levels (e.g., 0.9936 vs 0.9932 for curvature and 0.9903 for CSL at 30% noise), and on CIFAR-10 it matches curvature/CSL at 5–10% noise while surpassing them at 20–30% noise (0.9935 vs 0.9932/0.9906).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the memorization-estimation lineage initiated by Feldman & Zhang (2020), whose stability-based memorization score is the gold standard but requires O(dataset size) model retrainings. It builds directly on Ravikumar et al. (2025a) (cumulative sample loss, CSL) by replacing accumulated loss with accumulated input gradients, and on Ghadimi & Lan (2013) SGD convergence theory to derive formal bounds. Conceptually, it departs from prior proxies (curvature, VoG, influence functions, forgetting frequency) by providing the first clean theoretical bridge between a cheaply computable per-sample quantity and the Feldman-Zhang memorization definition, while also linking it to learning time. The discovery that the average sample gradient follows a rise-peak-decline trajectory mirroring weight norm, with the peak coinciding with the first double-descent minimum, is a new observation not present in prior memorization or optimization literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: CSG and SGAL enable memorization estimation at 0.1–0.3× the cost of standard training (vs. 2,000–30,000× for the gold standard), and the weight-norm-peak early-stopping criterion eliminates the need for a validation set while improving calibration and reducing privacy risk. Open questions include whether the theoretical bounds tighten under unbounded losses (e.g., cross-entropy), whether the no-skip-connection-at-first-layer assumption limits applicability to architectures like ResNet with early residual paths, and whether the CSG-based early stopping generalizes to non-vision tasks (NLP, tabular). The bias-discovery capability (e.g., color bias in CIFAR-100, contrast bias in FashionMNIST) suggests broader data-auditing applications beyond memorization.
