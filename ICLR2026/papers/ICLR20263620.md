## Problem

Existing image editing benchmarks and models prioritize semantic fidelity and instruction completion while largely ignoring the physical effects that accompany edits—such as correct shadows, reflections, refractions, deformations, and state transitions. This leaves a critical gap: we lack a systematic understanding of how far current models are from producing physically realistic edits, and we lack reliable evaluation tools sensitive to subtle physics violations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263620/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes a source image (resized to max 1024 px on the longer side) paired with a text editing instruction at one of three complexity levels (superficial, intermediate, explicit) and expects a physically consistent edited image. Evaluation is not a single scalar score: PICAEval decomposes each case into 4–5 spatially grounded yes/no questions anchored to human-annotated regions of interest (e.g., shadow zones, reflective surfaces), which a VLM (GPT-5 or Qwen2.5-VL-72B) answers after cropping and resizing the relevant region. A separate PSNR-based consistency metric measures preservation of non-edited pixels.

## Method

The paper contributes three components. First, PICABench is a 900-sample benchmark organized into three physical dimensions (Optics, Mechanics, State Transition) spanning eight checkable sub-dimensions, with instructions designed to implicitly probe specific physics. Second, PICAEval is a region-grounded VQA evaluation protocol: annotators mark physics-critical regions, GPT-5 generates targeted binary questions per region, and a VLM judges the edited image against those questions, yielding per-sub-dimension accuracy and a PSNR-based consistency score—substantially reducing VLM hallucination compared to general scoring prompts. Third, PICA-100K is a 105k-sample synthetic training dataset built by rendering diverse scenes with FLUX.1-Krea-dev, generating short physical-transformation videos with Wan2.2-14B-I2V, and extracting first/last frame pairs as (source, edited) supervision; fine-tuning FLUX.1-Kontext-dev or Qwen-Image-Edit via LoRA (rank 256, 10k steps, batch 64) on this data improves overall physical accuracy by ~1.7–3.8% and consistency by ~0.7–2.5 dB without sacrificing semantic quality.

## Results

On PICABench (superficial prompts, GPT-5 evaluator), the best closed-source model GPT-Image-1.5 achieves 67.05% overall physical accuracy and 21.73 dB consistency, while Nano Banana Pro reaches 66.16% accuracy. All open-source models remain below 60% overall accuracy, with the strongest being Qwen-Image-Edit at 58.29%. Fine-tuning on PICA-100K improves Qwen-Image-Edit to 62.13% (+3.84%) and Flux.1 Kontext to 50.64% (+1.71%), with consistency gains of +2.48 dB and +0.66 dB respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among closed-source models, GPT-Image-1.5 leads in overall accuracy (67.05%) and Nano Banana Pro is a close second (66.16%), both substantially ahead of GPT-Image-1 (61.08%) and Seedream 4.0 (61.91%). All open-source models trail the closed-source leaders by 8–30 points in accuracy; the strongest open-source baseline, Qwen-Image-Edit, reaches 58.29%, while unified architectures (Bagel 45.07%, OmniGen2 46.79%, UniWorld-V1 37.68%) underperform dedicated editing models despite their integrated understanding capabilities. Fine-tuning on PICA-100K yields consistent gains: Qwen-Image-Edit improves by +3.84% accuracy and +2.48 dB consistency, crossing the 60% threshold, while Flux.1 Kontext gains +1.71% accuracy. An ablation with real-video data (MIRA400K) actually degrades Flux.1 Kontext to 46.96% accuracy, confirming the synthetic video pipeline's superiority. Notably, consistency (non-edited region preservation) is highest for Bagel (28.42 dB) and Bagel-Think (26.88 dB), suggesting unified models better preserve context but sacrifice physical accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of instruction-based image editing and physical realism. While prior editing benchmarks (I2EBench, Kris-Bench, ImgeEdit) evaluate semantic fidelity and perceptual quality via VLM-as-a-Judge scoring, and recent scientific-plausibility benchmarks (Science-T2I, T2I-ReasonBench) probe domain-specific physics, none systematically evaluate the physical effects (shadows, reflections, deformations, state transitions) that accompany everyday editing operations. The training side draws on video-prior editing work (ByteMorph, UniReal, ObjectMover) but departs by using a fully synthetic T2I→I2V pipeline rather than real video, and by targeting implicit physical consistency rather than large non-rigid motions. The evaluation protocol (PICAEval) extends VLM-as-a-Judge with region-grounded, per-case binary QA to reduce hallucination, building on the modular verification idea from AuroraCap and VideoEspresso.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a substantial and previously unquantified gap: even the strongest closed-source models (GPT-Image-1.5, Nano Banana Pro) score only ~67% on physical accuracy, and all open-source models remain below 60%, indicating that physical realism is a first-order unsolved problem rather than a minor artifact. The finding that unified MLLMs with explicit world understanding do not outperform dedicated editing models on physical consistency challenges the assumption that understanding transfers to generation, pointing to an open coupling problem. Limitations include reliance on SFT (no RL post-training), single-image conditioning, and a synthetic data pipeline that may not capture the full diversity of real-world physics; future work on multi-image conditioning, RL-based training, and finer-grained temporal extraction from videos are natural next steps.
