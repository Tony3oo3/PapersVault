## Problem

Existing SSM compression techniques (knowledge distillation, post-training quantization, structured pruning) require a large model to be fully trained before compression, incurring the full cost of training at high state dimension. This paper asks whether principled in-training reduction of the SSM state dimension can achieve comparable or better performance at smaller dimensions while simultaneously accelerating the training process itself.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263794/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At each reduction checkpoint during training, the method extracts the discrete linear system matrices (A, B, C) from each SSM block (or per-channel for SISO architectures) and a user-specified energy retention threshold τ ∈ [0,1]. It produces a truncated balanced realization with reduced state dimension r, which replaces the original weights in-place. For diagonal A matrices (as in LRU, S5), Gramians are computed via closed-form entry-wise solutions rather than iterative Lyapunov solvers. The reduction is applied independently per SSM layer, agnostic to surrounding projections, non-linearities, convolutions, or skip connections.

## Method

COMPRESSM applies balanced truncation from classical control theory during training: it solves the discrete Lyapunov equations for controllability and observability Gramians, computes Hankel singular values (HSVs) as the square root of eigenvalues of PQ, and truncates the balanced realization to the smallest rank r retaining (1−τ) of total energy. The key theoretical justification is Weyl's theorem applied to the Hermitian matrix H = (P^{1/2}QP^{1/2})^{1/2}, which bounds per-step HSV changes by the operator norm of the perturbation δH, establishing that HSVs evolve continuously under gradient updates. Empirically, the relative ordering of HSVs stabilizes rapidly after a brief initial transient, and low-energy dimensions remain negligible throughout training, making early truncation decisions robust. Reductions are scheduled at fixed intervals during the learning-rate warm-up phase (e.g., four equidistant steps in the first 10% of training), with a pragmatic variant that uses validation-metric monitoring to automatically halt further reductions when performance degrades.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, COMPRESSM at tolerance τ=0.1 achieves 85.7% top-3 accuracy with a final state dimension of only 92.6 (vs. 384 for the full model), outperforming the baseline trained directly at that dimension (81.8%) by nearly 4 points while the full model reaches 86.5%. On sMNIST, COMPRESSM at τ=0.001 retains 97.2% accuracy at dimension 191.4, matching the full 256-dimension model (97.3%). On ListOps, compressed models at dimension ~82 achieve 51.8% versus 46.3% for the same-dimension baseline (full model: 49.7%). COMPRESSM also delivers a 1.5× training speedup on CIFAR-10 while preserving nearly full accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Hankel Nuclear Norm (HNN) regularization (Forgione et al., 2024; Schwerdtner et al., 2025), COMPRESSM dominates on both axes: on sMNIST at n=13, COMPRESSM achieves 95.9% accuracy with a 2.8× speedup, while HNN regularization yields only 91.7% and runs at 0.06× speed (roughly 16× slower) due to per-step Gramian-eigenvalue evaluations. Against Knowledge Distillation (Hinton et al., 2015), COMPRESSM matches or exceeds KD at comparable state dimensions (e.g., 84.4% vs. 79.4% at n=57 on CIFAR-10) and maintains performance at aggressive compression levels where KD degrades sharply; additionally, KD runs at 0.45–0.61× speed (slower than the uncompressed baseline) because it requires a forward pass through the teacher, whereas COMPRESSM achieves 1.03–1.58× speedup. The most striking comparison is against the natural baseline of training directly at the target smaller dimension: on CIFAR-10, COMPRESSM at dimension ~93 scores 85.7% versus 81.8% for the same-dimension baseline (a 3.9-point gap), and on ListOps the gap is 5.5 points (51.8% vs. 46.3%), demonstrating that in-training compression preserves task-critical structure that is lost when models are trained small from the start.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes classical control-theoretic balanced truncation (Antoulas, 2005) for the in-training compression of State Space Models, a setting where prior MOR work was either post-hoc (Ezoe & Sato, 2024, applying balanced truncation to S4 before retraining) or regularization-based (Hankel Nuclear Norm penalties from Forgione et al., 2024; Schwerdtner et al., 2025). The key conceptual departure is using Weyl's eigenvalue perturbation theorem to establish that Hankel singular values evolve continuously and rank-preservingly under gradient updates, thereby justifying truncation *during* training rather than after convergence. This positions COMPRESSM as the first principled in-training MOR method for SSMs, distinct from the broader in-training compression literature (dynamic pruning, QAT) which operates on weight sparsity or numerical precision rather than dynamical-system state dimension.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that models which start large and shrink during training outperform models trained directly at the smaller dimension (e.g., 85.7% vs. 81.8% on CIFAR10 at dimension ~92) challenges the default practice of choosing a fixed architecture size before training and offers a concrete ~1.5× training speedup with no accuracy loss. However, the benefit is dataset-dependent: on tasks where state dimension does not correlate with performance (AAN, Pathfinder), compression yields no gain. The Mamba case study reveals that SISO per-channel architectures dilute the expressivity advantage of larger states, limiting the method's applicability to selective SSMs. Open questions include extending COMPRESSM to MIMO selective models (Gated Linear Attention, Mamba2, Gated DeltaNet) and to language-scale tasks where state capacity is more clearly a performance driver.
