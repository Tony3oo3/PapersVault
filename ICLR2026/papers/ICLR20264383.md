## Problem

Classical Riemannian optimization requires explicit knowledge of the constraint manifold to perform projections, retractions, and tangent-space operations. In modern data-driven settings (generative design, data-driven control), the manifold is only implicitly given through finite samples from a distribution, making standard geometric operations unavailable. This paper asks: can one recover the essential manifold operations needed for optimization directly from the data distribution, without ever explicitly learning the manifold geometry?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264383/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pretrained score network s(x,σ) (trained via denoising score matching on samples from the data distribution) and an objective function f, requiring only forward passes and backpropagation with respect to the network's input (not its parameters) at each iteration. For the control application, it additionally takes a reference trajectory r and produces an optimized input sequence u* whose corresponding output y* tracks r while remaining close to the system behavior manifold. The output is an approximate minimizer with provable bounds on both its distance to the manifold (feasibility) and its Riemannian gradient norm (optimality), both scaling as Õ(σ) plus the score approximation error ε.

## Method

The key insight is a link function ℓ_σ(x) = ½‖x‖² + σ² log p_σ(x) whose gradient v(x) = x + σ²∇log p_σ(x) converges to the closest-point projection π(x) and whose Hessian I + σ²∇²log p_σ(x) converges to the tangent-space projection as σ→0 (Theorem 1, with Õ(σ) uniform error bounds). Since ∇log p_σ is exactly the score function learned in variance-exploding diffusion models, a pretrained score network provides all required geometric operations at inference time. Two algorithms exploit this: Denoising Landing Flow (DLF), a continuous ODE ẋ = −v′(x)∇f(v(x)) + η(v(x)−x) that combines approximate Riemannian gradient descent with a landing penalty, and Denoising Riemannian Gradient Descent (DRGD), a discrete update x_{k+1} = v(x_k − γ_k v′(x_k)∇f(x_k)) where v acts as approximate retraction and v′ as approximate tangent projection. Both achieve non-asymptotic convergence to approximate stationary points with explicit feasibility and optimality guarantees, and each iteration requires only a single forward-backward pass through the score network.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 1) establishes that the score-based link function approximates the closest-point projection and tangent-space projection with error O(σ|log σ|³) uniformly on tubular neighborhoods. Theorem 3 shows DLF converges to points with Riemannian gradient norm O(σ|log σ|³ + ε), while Theorem 5 gives DRGD an average gradient-norm bound of O(1/√N) plus an O(σ|log σ|³ + ε) residual. Numerically, on the orthogonal group O(n) (n=10,20), the method achieves objective values below the best point in the 20,000-point training set, with accuracy improving as σ→0. On the unicycle car and double pendulum reference-tracking tasks (N_h=100), the optimized trajectory tracks the reference significantly better than the best training-data trajectory, with small deviation from the true system behavior.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not include a standard multi-method comparison table; instead, it benchmarks against two natural references. First, on the orthogonal group, DLF is compared to the 'exact landing flow' (equation 10 with true projection π and π'), showing that the score-based approximation closely tracks the exact method for small σ (e.g., σ=0.05 for n=20) while requiring only network inference. Second, on the data-driven control tasks, the optimized output y* is compared to the best training-data trajectory y₀ (the argmin of f over D_train); the paper reports that y_true (obtained by back-testing u* on the true system) tracks the reference r substantially better than y₀, demonstrating generalization beyond the training set. Theoretically, setting σ=ε=0 in Theorem 5 recovers the classical O(1/√N) Riemannian gradient descent rate from Boumal (2023, Corollary 4.9), confirming that the proposed DRGD is a faithful generalization of the standard algorithm when the manifold is known exactly.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate literatures: classical Riemannian optimization (Boumal 2023; Absil et al. 2008), which requires explicit manifold operations like retraction and tangent-space projection, and score-based diffusion models (Song et al. 2020), which learn the gradient of the log-density of a Gaussian-smoothed data distribution. The key conceptual link is the 'link function' ℓ_σ whose gradient and Hessian recover the closest-point projection and tangent-space projection in the small-noise limit, generalizing Tweedie's formula (Alain & Bengio 2014) from full-dimensional densities to lower-dimensional manifold-supported measures. Unlike latent-space optimization via GANs/autoencoders (which requires learning an invertible chart and inverting a metric tensor) or graph-based manifold denoising (Wang et al. 2025, which is non-parametric and discrete), this approach uses a single pretrained score network as a universal provider of all needed geometric operations, requiring only forward-backward passes at inference time.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper opens a principled path to Riemannian optimization in settings where the constraint manifold is only accessible through data samples—covering airfoil design, data-driven control, and generative design—by repurposing pretrained diffusion model scores as geometric oracles without additional training. Non-asymptotic guarantees (Theorems 3 and 5) provide the first convergence analysis for score-based manifold optimization, though the rates are O(1/√N) and the L^∞ approximation assumption on the score is strong. Key open questions include: extending analysis to L^2 score errors (more realistic for finite-capacity networks), accelerating convergence via Newton or trust-region methods using the score's Jacobian, and scaling to high-dimensional problems where the current 3000-iteration budget and small test dimensions (n≤20, N_h=100) are far from practical. The practical promise of 'optimization for free' given a pretrained diffusion model is compelling but remains to be validated at scale.
