## Problem

DNA language models have demonstrated the ability to generate novel, experimentally verified bacteriophages, yet no systematic evaluation of their susceptibility to jailbreak attacks targeting human pathogenic viruses has been conducted. This gap leaves the dual-use biosecurity risk of these models unquantified, with no standardized benchmark or attack methodology to probe whether models can be steered into producing sequences homologous to regulated human pathogens.

## Input / Output

The attack prompt is constructed by concatenating a phylogenetic tag (e.g., D_VIRUS;P_SSRNA;O_RETROVIRIDAE;F_LENTIVIRUS;G_HIV-1), a few-shot block of retrieved non-pathogenic homologous sequences, and a short upstream genomic prefix of the target CDS. The target DNA language model then autoregressively generates L=640 nucleotides in chunks of C=128. Success is determined by nucleotide and protein BLAST against a curated pathogen database (≥90% identity threshold) combined with VADR functional annotation, yielding a binary jailbreak flag per trial and an aggregate Attack Success Rate across six viral categories.

## Method

GeneBreaker is a three-stage jailbreak framework. First, a ChatGPT-4o agent acts as a bioinformatics assistant to retrieve GenBank accession IDs of non-pathogenic sequences with high homology to the target pathogenic region (e.g., FIV for HIV-1 env), circumventing the manual curation burden of direct BLAST searches. Second, a beam search over the DNA language model's autoregressive outputs is guided by a composite scoring function f = PathoLM(x) + α·log p(x), where PathoLM provides a pathogenicity score and the average log-probability serves as a proxy for similarity to known pathogens (Pearson r = 0.75); at each round, K=8 candidate chunks are sampled and the top K'=4 are retained. Third, an evaluation pipeline applies nucleotide/protein BLAST and VADR annotation to classify outputs as successful jailbreaks. The key insight is that framing the prompt around non-pathogenic homologs (analogous to in-context learning) while using pathogenicity-aware scoring during generation effectively bypasses any implicit safety constraints in the DNA model, with attack success rates scaling monotonically with model size (up to 60% for Evo2-40B).

## Results

On JailbreakDNABench, GeneBreaker achieves up to 60% Attack Success Rate (ASR) on Evo2 (40B) for Small DNA viruses and Enteric RNA viruses, with 52% on Large DNA viruses and 37.7% on Positive-strand RNA viruses. ASR increases monotonically with model scale: Evo2 (1B) < Evo1 (7B) < Evo2 (7B) < Evo2 (40B), confirming that larger DNA language models are more vulnerable. Case studies demonstrate high fidelity: the jailbroken SARS-CoV-2 Spike protein achieves 92.77% nucleotide and 95.29% amino acid identity to the Wuhan-Hu-1 reference, while the HIV-1 Env core prediction has only 0.334 Å RMSD to the crystal structure (PDB 4RZ8). In the SARS-CoV-2 evolution study, 201 out of 10,000 generated sequences exceeded 99.9% nucleotide identity to Nextstrain reference variants spanning Alpha, Delta, and Omicron clades.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper explicitly states that GeneBreaker is the first systematic jailbreak study on DNA language models, so no prior attack baselines exist for direct comparison. Instead, the cross-model comparison serves as the primary axis: Evo2 (40B) dominates all other tested models (Evo2 1B, Evo1 7B, Evo2 7B, GENERator 1B, GenomeOcean 4B) across every viral category, with ASR gaps of 28–60 percentage points versus the smallest models. Ablation studies (Figure 3c) confirm that removing either the high-homology prompt or the PathoLM + log-probability beam search guidance drops ASR to zero, establishing both components as necessary. A simple PathoLM-based veto defense (rejecting chunks with pathogenicity score > 0.8) reduces ASR from 60% to 32% on SARS-CoV-2 and from 50% to 28% on HIV-1, but introduces 5% premature stop codons, indicating that existing safeguards remain insufficient. The scaling trend (ASR rising monotonically with parameter count) is the paper's central comparative finding, showing that model capacity amplifies dual-use risk rather than mitigating it.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transplants the LLM jailbreak paradigm (adversarial prompts that circumvent safety alignment) into the DNA language model domain, where the prompt space is restricted to nucleotide sequences and no explicit safety alignment exists. It builds directly on Evo2's beam search and phylogenetic tagging framework (Brixi et al., 2025) for autoregressive generation, and on PathoLM (Dip et al., 2024) as a pathogenicity scoring oracle, combining them with an LLM-agent-based prompt design step that retrieves non-pathogenic homologous sequences as in-context learning examples. The conceptual departure from LLM jailbreaks is fundamental: rather than crafting adversarial text to bypass RLHF-trained refusals, the attack exploits the model's learned sequence distributions through biologically informed prompts and guided decoding, making the 'jailbreak' closer to adversarial steering than to safety-mechanism circumvention.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic red-teaming evaluation of DNA language models for biosafety, and its central finding—that attack success rate scales monotonically with model size (up to 60% ASR for Evo2-40B) and that the model can reproduce evolutionarily distinct SARS-CoV-2 Spike variants spanning Alpha through Omicron clades—raises urgent questions about whether current training-data exclusion strategies are sufficient. The paper's defense experiments (PathoLM-based vetoing) show only partial mitigation, leaving open the design of robust, sequence-level safety alignment for genomic foundation models. A key limitation is that these models lack explicit safety alignment to begin with, so the 'jailbreak' framing somewhat overstates the vulnerability; the more precise concern is that capable generative models can be steered toward regulated pathogen sequences with modest guidance, and that this risk grows with scale.
