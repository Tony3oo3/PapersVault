## Problem

Contrastive representations trained with InfoNCE are empirically observed to be approximately Gaussian, and this property is exploited in downstream tasks like OOD detection and uncertainty estimation, yet no principled population-level explanation exists for why the InfoNCE objective itself induces Gaussian structure in representation space. The paper addresses this gap by providing a rigorous asymptotic characterization of the distributional law induced by the population InfoNCE objective.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261493/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a base data sample (an image or a high-dimensional vector) and produces two stochastic augmented views via a channel A(·|X₀), which are encoded by a Borel-measurable encoder f: X → ℝᵈ. The output is the d-dimensional representation vector z = f(X), and the paper's central result characterizes the distribution of its fixed-k projections: as d → ∞, √d·zₖ converges to a multivariate Gaussian N(0, r₀²Iₖ) for normalized and unnormalized representations respectively. The analysis operates at the population level (infinite batch limit N → ∞) and in the high-dimensional limit (d → ∞), with finite-dimensional empirical validation on 32–256 dimensional representations.

## Method

The paper establishes Gaussianity via two complementary routes. First, an alignment-plateau analysis introduces a novel bound (Proposition 1) showing that positive-pair alignment is upper-bounded by the squared HGR maximal correlation η₂ between the augmented view and the base sample, quantifying how augmentation strength constrains alignment. Under the assumption that alignment saturates at a plateau below this bound, the population InfoNCE objective reduces to minimizing the uniformity potential Φ(μ), whose unique minimizer is the uniform law on the sphere; the Maxwell-Poincaré spherical CLT then yields Gaussian k-projections, and a thin-shell concentration assumption extends the result to unnormalized representations. Second, a regularized route adds a vanishing convex regularizer (KL divergence to a Gaussian reference γ_λ^B) that jointly penalizes large norms and low entropy, proving via a Donsker-Varadhan variational argument that the uniform angular distribution is asymptotically optimal as d → ∞, yielding the same Gaussian limit without relying on training dynamics. Empirical validation on synthetic data, CIFAR-10 (MLP and ResNet-18), and pretrained foundation models (CLIP, DINO) confirms norm concentration, alignment saturation, and near-Gaussian coordinate distributions, with contrastive training producing significantly more Gaussian representations than supervised training under identical architectures.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with ResNet-18, InfoNCE (contrastive) training yields near-Gaussian representations (CV=0.09, AD avg=0.43 with 96.1% of coordinates passing, DP avg=0.39 with 94.5% passing), while supervised training on the same architecture produces strongly non-Gaussian representations (CV=0.5, AD avg=3.3 with only 6.2% passing, DP avg=0.041 with 3.9% passing). On synthetic data (Laplace, GMM, binary), linear encoders trained with InfoNCE achieve 100% coordinate compliance on both AD and DP tests with CV≈0.08. Among pretrained models on MS-COCO, self-supervised CLIP and DINO show AD averages of 0.44–0.53 with 94–97% coordinate compliance, whereas supervised ResNet-34 and DenseNet show AD averages of 2.98–10.01 with 0–42% compliance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central empirical claim is that InfoNCE training induces Gaussian structure, and it validates this by contrasting contrastive against supervised training on identical architectures. On CIFAR-10 with ResNet-18, contrastive (InfoNCE) training achieves 96.1% AD compliance and 94.5% DP compliance versus only 6.2% and 3.9% for supervised training—a gap of roughly 90 percentage points on both tests. Among pretrained models on MS-COCO, self-supervised DINO (AD avg 0.44, 97.0% compliant) and CLIP image encoder (AD avg 0.47, 96.8% compliant) both exhibit strong Gaussian signatures, while supervised ResNet-34 (AD avg 10.01, 0.0% compliant) and DenseNet (AD avg 2.98, 42.2% compliant) deviate substantially. CLIP's text encoder (AD avg 0.53, 94.0% compliant) and its performance on ImageNet-R sketch/painting domains (94–95% compliant) confirm the Gaussian structure generalizes beyond natural images. The key takeaway is that Gaussianity is a direct consequence of the contrastive objective rather than the data or architecture, since supervised training on the same ResNet-18 and CIFAR-10 produces non-Gaussian representations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper connects three previously separate threads: the population InfoNCE formulation and alignment/uniformity decomposition of Wang & Isola (2020), the classical Maxwell-Poincaré spherical central limit theorem of Diaconis & Freedman (1987), and HGR maximal correlation theory. Prior work (Baumann et al., Balestriero et al.) has empirically observed or exploited approximate Gaussianity in contrastive representations, but no prior work provided a principled population-level derivation of why the InfoNCE objective itself induces this structure. The novel HGR-based alignment bound (Proposition 1) is a new technical contribution that quantifies how augmentation strength constrains achievable positive-pair alignment, a quantity not previously formalized in contrastive learning theory.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This result provides a principled justification for the widespread practice of modeling contrastive representations as Gaussians in downstream tasks (likelihood scoring, OOD detection, uncertainty estimation), moving these choices from empirical heuristics to theoretically grounded approximations. The two complementary proof routes (alignment plateau + thin-shell concentration vs. vanishing regularizer) make the result robust to different assumptions about training dynamics. Key limitations are that the results are asymptotic (d→∞) and rely on idealized conditions (alignment saturation, norm concentration) that may not hold in all practical regimes; the paper does not analyze optimization dynamics or prove that finite-time training attains the population optima. Open questions include quantitative finite-dimensional convergence rates, extension to multi-modal or asymmetric contrastive objectives, and whether the Gaussian structure persists under distribution shift or domain adaptation.
