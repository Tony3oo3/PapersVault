## Problem

While LLM unlearning aims to remove specific knowledge from trained models, this paper reveals a previously unexplored vulnerability: unlearning leaves persistent, detectable 'fingerprints' in both model behavior and internal representations. The central question is whether an adversary can determine whether a model has undergone unlearning purely from its input-output behavior, even when queried with forget-irrelevant prompts, thereby enabling targeted relearning or jailbreaking attacks on identified unlearned models.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263782/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The detector consumes two types of observable model outputs: (1) discrete textual responses generated by the LLM, encoded into fixed-length embeddings via LLM2Vec, and (2) continuous pre-logit activation vectors recorded for each newly generated token, aggregated across the full response sequence (e.g., 409,600-dim for Zephyr-7B). The output is a binary label indicating whether the generating model is original or unlearned. Critically, detection is evaluated on forget-irrelevant prompts (MMLU, UltraChat) where behavioral differences are subtle, not just on forget-relevant WMDP prompts where incoherence is obvious.

## Method

The paper frames unlearning-trace detection as supervised binary classification, training lightweight two-layer MLP classifiers on either LLM2Vec-encoded text or raw pre-logit activations, using a 50/50 mixed training set of forget-relevant (WMDP) and forget-irrelevant (MMLU) responses. The key analytical contribution is identifying spectral fingerprints: SVD of centered activation matrices reveals distributional shifts along principal right singular vectors that distinguish unlearned from original models. For NPO-unlearned models, strong separation appears directly in final-layer RMS-normalized activations; for RMU-unlearned models, fingerprints are localized to intermediate FFN sublayers (D_PROJ, G_PROJ) and propagate nonlinearly to the final layer, where supervised UMAP exposes a low-dimensional separable manifold invisible to linear projections. The MLP classifier effectively learns this manifold, achieving >90% detection accuracy even on forget-irrelevant prompts, with larger models (Yi-34B) exhibiting stronger and more persistent traces than smaller ones (Zephyr-7B).

## Results

For RMU-unlearned models, text-based detection accuracy varies dramatically with model scale: Yi-34B achieves 95.77% on MMLU and 87.46% on UltraChat, while Zephyr-7B drops to 53.68% and 50.14% (near random). However, activation-based classifiers achieve near-perfect detection (98–100%) across all four models and all three test sets for RMU. For NPO-unlearned models, both text-based and activation-based classifiers achieve 99–100% accuracy across all models and prompt types, reflecting NPO's more aggressive behavioral disruption. Forget-data detection (identifying whether a specific response originates from the forget domain) exceeds 70% accuracy across all unlearned models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external detection baselines but instead contrasts two unlearning paradigms (RMU vs. NPO) and two signal types (text responses vs. pre-logit activations). NPO unlearning is dramatically easier to detect than RMU: even the smallest model (Zephyr-7B) achieves 99–100% text-based detection under NPO, whereas the same model drops to ~50% (random) under RMU on forget-irrelevant prompts. Activation-based classifiers close this gap for RMU, jumping Zephyr-7B MMLU detection from 53.68% (text) to 98.59% (activations), a >40-point improvement. Model scale matters for RMU text detection (Yi-34B: 95.77% vs. Zephyr-7B: 53.68% on MMLU) but not for NPO, where all models are trivially separable. Cross-domain generalization of activation-based classifiers is strong (82–99% off-diagonal in Table 2), except for LLaMA-3.1-8B-trained classifiers which fail to transfer to other model families.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the SVD-based spectral fingerprint analysis from backdoor detection (Tran et al., 2018) to a new forensic setting: detecting whether an LLM has undergone unlearning. It positions itself within the reverse engineering of deceptions (RED) paradigm, drawing a parallel between inferring an adversary's strategy from attack traces and inferring unlearning from model outputs. The work also connects to LLM model identity detection (Sun et al., 2025; Nikolic et al., 2025), but deliberately shifts from inter-model identification to intra-model comparison (original vs. unlearned variant of the same architecture). Methodologically, it relies on standard supervised classification (two-layer MLPs with LLM2Vec or raw activations) rather than introducing new detection algorithms.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that unlearning is detectable even on forget-irrelevant prompts undermines a core assumption in the unlearning literature: that a successfully unlearned model should be indistinguishable from its original on general tasks. If an adversary can identify which models have been unlearned, they can concentrate relearning or jailbreaking attacks on those specific models, amplifying the practical risk in safety-critical deployments. Open questions include whether trace-mitigation techniques exist (e.g., unlearning methods that avoid leaving spectral fingerprints), whether the phenomenon persists under iterative or multi-topic unlearning, and whether theoretical conditions can be derived for when traces become undetectable. The paper is purely empirical and offers no mitigation strategy or formal detectability bound.
