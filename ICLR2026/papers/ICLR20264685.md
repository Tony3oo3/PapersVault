## Problem

Recent open-source automatic evaluator work has converged on training task-specialized models at small data scales (tens of thousands of samples) using complex RL pipelines, failing to meet the twin demands of multi-task capability and multi-domain coverage needed as evaluators are integrated into every phase of the LLM development cycle. This paper asks whether a simple, data-driven scaling approach—rather than methodological novelty—can produce well-rounded evaluators that match or exceed specialized RL-trained models.

## Input / Output

The evaluator receives a structured input consisting of an evaluation protocol (specifying the task type—pairwise, step-level, reference-based/free verification, or single rating—and a rubric), the original question, and a set of model responses whose size and structure depend on the task (e.g., two responses for pairwise, one response decomposed into steps for step-level evaluation). The output is a natural language critique followed by a discrete judgment whose form is task-specific: a binary choice (A/B), a yes/no verdict, a step-level error annotation, or an integer score on a 1–5 scale. The model can also be prompted to omit the critique entirely and emit only the judgment, enabling low-latency use in reranking and RL rollout verification.

## Method

FARE is trained with iterative rejection-sampling SFT (RS-SFT) on a curated 2.5M-sample, multi-task, multi-domain dataset that combines existing high-quality annotations with synthetic data generated via programmatic error injection and a generate-then-grade strategy using 12 diverse generator models. At each training iteration, the current policy samples K=4 responses per input; only inputs with at least one correct response are retained (one correct response chosen at random), and the policy is updated via standard SFT on the filtered set—avoiding both teacher-model distribution shift and the computational brittleness of RL. Two key training design choices differentiate FARE: a continuous curriculum that sorts each batch by pass-rate (yielding large gains on step-level evaluation) and a fixed proportion of direct-judgment samples that strip the critique to isolate the judgment training signal. Multi-task training is shown to outperform single-task training, with step-level error detection improving pairwise accuracy by endowing the evaluator with finer-grained mistake detection. FARE-8B is initialized from Qwen3-8B-Base (cold-started with one SFT iteration) and FARE-20B from gpt-oss-20B, both trained with the same RS-SFT recipe at different rollout batch sizes.

## Results

FARE-20B achieves 64.29 on JudgeBench, 57.05 on ReasoningJudgeBench, 74.4 on PPE Correctness, 90.5 on RM-Bench, and 76.67 on When2Call, outperforming all 70B+ open-source evaluators (e.g., J1-70B at 60.00, EvalPlanner-70B at 56.60) while using ~20× fewer active parameters. On ProcessBench, FARE-20B reaches 84.4 overall F1, nearly matching GPT-5 (84.6) and surpassing the specialized Qwen2.5-Math-72B-PRM (78.3) by 6.1 points. On VerifyBench-Hard, FARE-20B scores 85.10 accuracy, beating GPT-4o (72.60) by 12.5 points. As a GRPO verifier, FARE-20B improves downstream model performance by 14.1% relative to string-matching verifiers, and as a test-time reranker it achieves near-oracle performance on MATH.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FARE-8B outperforms all comparably-sized RL-trained judges: it beats J1-8B by 13.71 points and RM-R1-14B by 8.85 points on JudgeBench, and surpasses CompassJudger-7B by 6.57 points. FARE-20B challenges 70B+ evaluators, exceeding J1-70B by 4.29 points and EvalPlanner-70B by 7.69 points on JudgeBench, while using roughly 20× fewer active parameters. On ProcessBench, FARE-20B (84.4) nearly matches GPT-5 (84.6) and beats the specialized Qwen2.5-Math-72B-PRM (78.3) by 6.1 points; FARE-8B (63.5) outperforms the RL-trained StepWiser-7B (61.9) by 1.6 points. On VerifyBench-Hard, FARE-20B (85.10) beats GPT-4o (72.60) by 12.5 points and gpt-oss-120B (88.30) is the only model that exceeds it. In downstream GRPO training, FARE-20B as verifier yields 14.1% relative gain over string-matching and 9.4% over the trained General-Verifier from Ma et al. (2025). FARE-20B-Code, after just 15K samples of continual training, outperforms gpt-oss-20B by 65% on test-case quality evaluation in CodingJudgeBench.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two lines of work on generative evaluators: early foundational evaluator training (SFR-Judge, CompassJudger, Atla Selene) that scaled data with offline SFT/DPO, and recent methodological advances using RL (J1, RM-R1, StepWiser) or inference-time scaling (DeepSeek-GRM) that typically operate on small, task-specific datasets. The key departure is adopting iterative rejection-sampling SFT (RS-SFT), inspired by STaR and RAFT, as a 'semi-online' middle ground that avoids teacher distribution shift (a problem with offline SFT) while remaining computationally stable compared to RL. The paper positions itself as a data-scaling contribution rather than a methodological one, arguing that 2.5M multi-task samples with a simple training loop can match or exceed RL-trained evaluators that use orders of magnitude less data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FARE provides the first open-source evaluator family that simultaneously handles five evaluation tasks across reasoning-centric domains at competitive scale, with FARE-20B (3.6B active params) surpassing 70B+ specialized evaluators. The practical downstream results—near-oracle reranking on MATH, 14.1% RL training gains over string-matching verifiers, and 65% improvement in code test-case evaluation after 15K-sample continual finetuning—demonstrate that a single well-trained evaluator can serve as a drop-in component across the LLM development lifecycle. Open questions include whether the gpt-oss CoT prior can be better handled (FARE-20B degrades when forced to skip CoT), whether the approach extends to truly unverifiable domains, and whether the cold-start SFT recipe for Qwen3 is optimal. The finding that positional bias emerges naturally with data scale (Fig. 6) suggests a simpler path to robustness than augmentation or RL-based debiasing.
