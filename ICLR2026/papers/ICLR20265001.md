## Problem

Existing activation-based safety monitors for LLMs rely on coarse misuse datasets (e.g., 'cybercrime,' 'hate speech'), leading to poor precision with many false positives, inflexible deployment that requires retraining for each new policy, and opaque detection that cannot explain which internal states triggered an alarm. This paper asks whether safety can instead be specified as explicit, composable, and interpretable rules over fine-grained model behaviors, analogous to rule-sharing practices in cybersecurity (Snort, YARA).

## Input / Output

At inference, the system consumes per-token attention outputs from a target LLM (concatenated across a contiguous mid-to-later layer range, e.g., layers 13–26 of Mistral-7B) in 5-token segments, alongside a user-configured set of Boolean predicates over named Cognitive Elements. It produces per-token multi-label CE probability scores (a K-dimensional vector where K is the number of defined CEs), which are aggregated over a temporal window into a binary presence vector and evaluated against each rule's predicate. When a rule fires, the system reports the specific predicate, the triggering CEs, and the exact tokens responsible, enabling transparent audit. The detector itself is a lightweight 3-layer GRU (256 units) requiring only ~150 MB of GPU memory and adding <1% latency overhead per token.

## Method

GAVEL decomposes LLM behavior into Cognitive Elements (CEs)—interpretable, mid-level primitives such as 'making a threat,' 'masquerade as human,' or 'payment tools'—and expresses safety policies as Boolean predicates over CE co-occurrence within a temporal window. For each CE, a text-only excitation dataset is constructed and passed through the target model using an Explicit Revision Instruction (ERI) prompt that contextualizes the model's attention on the target concept, yielding aligned attention-output activations. A multi-label RNN classifier is then trained on these per-token activations (one-hot labeled per CE) to predict simultaneous CE presence, generalizing to overlapping CEs in real dialogues despite being trained on isolated excitation data. Safety rules are written in a human-readable condition syntax (e.g., 'refuse if task:creating_content AND (directive:click OR directive:personal_information)') that compiles to a Boolean formula over the CE presence vector, decoupling activation engineering from policy configuration so that new rules can be added or modified without retraining any model or detector.

## Results

On Mistral-7B across nine misuse categories (cybercrime, psychological harm, scam automation), GAVEL achieves a mean AUC of 0.99, balanced accuracy of 0.96, and near-zero FPR (0.00 average) against both targeted benign conversations and natural dialogue benchmarks (0.088 FPR on UltraChat, 0.008 on DialogueSum). The closest competitor, a per-category activation classifier, reaches AUC 0.97 but with 0.07 FPR, while steering-based methods (CAST, RepBending) and surface-text moderators (Llama Guard 4, Perspective, OpenAI Moderator) trail by 0.1–0.5 AUC points. Under adversarial attacks (misdirection, goal hijacking, evasion), GAVEL maintains >85% TPR across all categories, whereas GPT-4 text-based detection collapses to as low as 0–15% TPR on several tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GAVEL outperforms all eight baselines on the primary AUC metric, with the largest margins over CAST (0.99 vs. 0.41 AUC; 0.00 vs. 0.60 FPR) and Perspective (0.99 vs. 0.53 AUC), both of which struggle with the fine-grained, multi-turn scam and psychological-harm scenarios. Against the closest activation-based competitor (per-category Activation Classifier), GAVEL gains +0.02 AUC and reduces FPR from 0.07 to 0.00, confirming that decomposing behaviors into compositional CEs avoids the overgeneralization that coarse misuse categories introduce. Steering methods (Circuit Breakers, RepBending) achieve moderate AUC (0.68–0.87) but cannot match GAVEL's precision, particularly on scam automation where they produce FPR up to 0.23. Surface-text moderators (Llama Guard 4, OpenAI Moderator) maintain low FPR but miss many harms in psychological-harm and scam domains (AUC 0.53–0.87), and their performance degrades sharply under adversarial misdirection and goal-hijacking attacks, whereas GAVEL's activation-level monitoring remains robust (>85% TPR). Legilimens, the only baseline evaluated on a different model (Llama2-7B), shows the worst FPR (0.70) due to its binary harmful/harmless framing, further illustrating the cost of coarse-grained detection.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the representation engineering and activation-monitoring line of work (Zou et al., 2023; CircuitBreakers; RepBending; CAST) but departs fundamentally from the dominant 'train a classifier on a broad misuse dataset' paradigm. Instead, it borrows the rule-sharing architecture from cybersecurity tools (Snort, YARA, OSSEC) and applies it to LLM activations: behaviors are decomposed into fine-grained, interpretable Cognitive Elements (CEs), and safety constraints are expressed as Boolean predicates over those CEs. The closest prior work, CAST (Lee et al., 2024), allows users to select a steering vector for a detected generic misuse category, but remains coarse-grained; GAVEL replaces the single generic detector with a compositional vocabulary of 23 CEs and user-authored logical rules, decoupling activation engineering from policy configuration.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GAVEL addresses three concrete gaps in current activation-based safety: poor precision from broad misuse datasets, inflexibility when policies change, and opacity when a detector fires. The rule-based formulation enables community sharing of CEs and rulesets (analogous to shared threat-intelligence feeds in cybersecurity), rapid policy updates without retraining, and transparent, token-level audit trails. Key open questions include: whether Boolean rules are expressive enough for all safety needs (e.g., graded or probabilistic violations), how the framework scales beyond the 23 CEs and 9 categories demonstrated here, and the adversarial robustness of the CE-level detection itself (a 'CE-level jailbreak' that suppresses activation signatures without changing surface text). The paper also notes that the approach is orthogonal to alignment and content moderation, positioning it as an additional high-precision governance layer rather than a replacement.
