## Problem

Data curriculums (e.g., annealing high-quality data in the final stages of LLM pre-training) are now standard practice, yet the principles governing optimal data placement remain unclear. The common heuristic of placing high-quality data at the very end of training—when the learning rate is near zero—is assumed to maximize effectiveness, but no principled diagnostic exists to determine where in the training trajectory data is actually best retained by the final model.

## Input / Output

The method consumes optimizer hyperparameters (peak learning rate η, weight decay λ, batch size B), the full learning-rate schedule η(t), and tokens-per-parameter (TPP) as inputs, and produces a predicted normalized TREC curve over training fraction t̂ ∈ [0,1] along with the optimal insertion window for a fixed-budget high-quality data segment. The TREC itself is defined retrospectively—evaluating the final model θ_T on the i.i.d. training sequence in order—but the prediction is made in advance from the AdamW EMA coefficient function c(t̂) and a fitted training-fraction exponent m, requiring no actual training run. The output is a shape (min-max normalized), not an absolute loss magnitude, and the optimal placement is identified as the TREC minimum (valley), which under step-decay or decay-to-zero schedules can occur well before the final steps.

## Method

The paper introduces the Training Re-Evaluation Curve (TREC), defined as L_re(t) = L(B_t; θ_T), the loss of the final model on each training batch in sequence order, and shows that placing high-quality data at the TREC minimum outperforms end-of-training placement across multiple LR schedules and model scales (111M–3.9B). The key mechanistic insight is that AdamW parameters are an implicit EMA over weight updates with timescale τ = B/(ηλD), so the EMA coefficient function c(t̂) = η_t λ ∏_{j>t}(1−η_j λ) quantifies each update's contribution to final weights and governs TREC shape. To account for minimizer drift (batch-specific loss surfaces shifting after gradients are computed), the authors propose the predictive form L̂_re(t̂) = 1 − c(t̂)^p · t̂^m with p fixed at 0.5, where the training-fraction exponent m follows a power law m* = C · TPP^{μ1} · τ^{μ2} fitted at 111M scale and validated up to 3.3B (r_p ≈ 98%). This enables proactive curriculum design: given any optimizer configuration and LR schedule, one can predict the TREC valley and insert high-quality data there without costly ablation sweeps, as demonstrated in a 3.9B continual pre-training experiment where mid-CPT placement at the TREC minimum outperformed end placement across three learning rates.

## Results

The paper studies 600 TRECs across models from 111M to 3.9B parameters and 20 to 1280 tokens-per-parameter (TPP). TREC shape prediction achieves Pearson correlation r_p ≈ 98% across scales, with the m* power-law fit (Eq. 4) achieving R² = 99% at 111M and 77% at 3.3B. In the 3.9B continual pre-training experiment (900B base tokens + 18B CPT tokens), TREC-guided mid-CPT placement consistently outperforms end-of-training placement across three peak learning rates. The analysis explains why Llama-3 405B gained no benefit from GSM8k annealing: the annealing phase spans only ~3 optimizer steps (40M tokens at 16M batch size), leaving EMA coefficients essentially zero.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares TREC-guided data placement against three baselines: (1) end-of-training placement (the standard heuristic used in Llama-3, OLMo-2, and others), (2) aggregate blend (uniform 90/10 HQ/general mixing across all steps), and (3) all-general-blend (no HQ data inserted). TREC-guided placement at the TREC minimum outperforms all three baselines across all three LR schedules tested (Step decay, 10× linear decay, and decay-to-zero); the aggregate blend is a strong baseline that only the TREC-optimal position beats. In the 3.9B CPT setting, mid-CPT placement (at the TREC minimum) beats end placement across all three peak LRs, though at the highest LR (0.015) even correctly placed data fails to match the original base model. The paper further validates its predictive framework against published recipes: Feng et al.'s empirically optimal 40% HQ onset aligns with the predicted TREC valley, and OLMo-2's 5.7% HQ window matches the narrower predicted TREC dip, confirming that TREC analysis can identify optimal placement without costly trial-and-error ablations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the AdamW-as-EMA perspective introduced by Wang & Aitchison (2024) and the time-varying EMA formulation from Bergsma et al. (2025b, 'Straight to zero') into a practical predictive framework for data curriculum design. While prior work (Pagliardini et al. 2024, Lesci et al. 2024, Bergsma et al. 2025b) measured re-evaluation loss on training batches as a retrospective diagnostic, this paper is the first to show that the resulting curve (TREC) can be predicted in advance from optimizer hyperparameters alone, enabling proactive rather than post-hoc curriculum decisions. It also connects to the data-curriculum literature (Llama-3, OLMo-2, DeepSeek) by explaining their annealing-phase choices through the TREC lens and identifying where those choices are suboptimal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that optimal high-quality data placement is at the TREC minimum—which can be well before the end of training—directly challenges the widespread 'anneal on HQ data at the end' heuristic used by Llama-3, OLMo, and others, and the predictive framework offers a principled alternative to costly ablation sweeps. Open questions include whether TREC absolute values generalize across optimizer configurations (the paper shows they do not), how the framework extends to optimizers without an implicit EMA (Adagrad, Adafactor, SGD), and how to jointly optimize placement and schedule selection. The paper also raises the tension between maximizing retention (memorization) and generalization (grokking), noting that TREC-guided placement may inadvertently favor memorization at high learning rates.
