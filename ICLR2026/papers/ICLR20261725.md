## Problem

Despite rapid progress in VideoLLMs for video question answering, their internal mechanisms for temporal reasoning remain largely unexplored. Prior work has focused on external design choices (data scaling, key-frame selection, token compression) but has not revealed where and how video and textual information is extracted, integrated, and propagated through the network layers to produce correct answers.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261725/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a video sampled as 8 frames at 336×336 resolution (yielding 8×12×12 = 1152 video tokens after patchification and spatial pooling) concatenated with a text question prompt containing multiple-choice options. The primary output is the selected answer, but the analytical output of this paper is a layer-wise characterization of effective information flow pathways: which layers handle cross-frame video interactions, which layers perform video-to-language integration on temporal keywords, and which layers drive final answer generation. Analysis is restricted to samples where the model produces the correct answer to ensure valid causal tracing.

## Method

The paper applies two mechanistic interpretability techniques to reverse-engineer VideoLLM internals: (1) Attention Knockout, which selectively sets attention mask entries to −∞ to disconnect specific token-to-token edges within a k=9 layer window and measures the resulting drop in correct-answer probability, and (2) Logit Lens, which projects video-token hidden states through the LM head at each layer to track when spatial vs. temporal concepts emerge. The key finding is a three-stage pipeline: cross-frame interactions among video tokens in early-to-middle layers (L6–15), selective video-to-language integration onto temporal reasoning keywords (option tokens) in middle layers (L6–20), and answer generation at the last token in middle-to-late layers (L16–25+). Validation shows that retaining only these effective pathways (≈42% of all attention edges in LLaVA-NeXT-7B-Video-FT) preserves VideoQA accuracy on TVBench and TOMATO, while randomly blocking the same fraction of edges causes severe degradation, confirming the pathways are both necessary and sufficient.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative finding is that VideoLLMs retain near-full VideoQA accuracy when only the identified effective information pathways are kept, while randomly pruning the same fraction of attention edges causes severe degradation. For example, LLaVA-NeXT-7B-Video-FT drops only 0.3 points on TVBench (51.5→51.2) when retaining 42% of attention edges via effective pathways, versus a 11.4-point drop (51.5→40.1) under random blocking at the same sparsity. Blocking cross-frame attention in the first half of layers causes accuracy drops of 18.0–60.8% across five TVBench tasks, while blocking the same edges in the second half causes only 0.5–1.7% drops, confirming that temporal reasoning is concentrated in early-to-middle layers. These patterns hold consistently across four models (4B–13B) with different vision encoders and LLM backbones.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an interpretability study rather than a method comparison, so the key 'baseline' is full causal attention, and the critical control is random blocking at the same sparsity level. Across all four models, effective pathways preserve within 0.5 points of full-attention accuracy on TVBench, while random blocking at identical sparsity causes drops of 11–33 points (e.g., VideoLLaMA3-7B: 55.2→57.2 effective vs. 55.2→22.2 random). The paper also contrasts video-fine-tuned models against their image-only counterparts (e.g., LLaVA-NeXT-7B vs. LLaVA-NeXT-7B-Video-FT), showing that video instruction tuning is what induces the sensitive cross-frame attention pattern in early-to-middle layers; the image-only model shows minimal probability change when cross-frame edges are blocked. Layer-wise ablations further confirm that the first half of layers carries the critical temporal signal (18–60.8% accuracy drop) while the second half is nearly redundant (0.5–1.7% drop), establishing a clear functional separation between temporal encoding and answer generation stages.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends mechanistic interpretability techniques—specifically Attention Knockout (Geva et al., 2023) and Logit Lens (nostalgebraist, 2020)—from image-based MLLMs to VideoLLMs, building directly on Zhang et al. (2025c) who characterized cross-modal information flow in static image-text models. While prior VideoLLM research has focused on external design choices (data scaling, key-frame selection, token compression), this work is among the first to reverse-engineer the internal temporal reasoning circuitry. The paper positions itself as a natural extension of the image-MLLM interpretability line (Neo et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025c) to the spatiotemporal setting, asking what novel mechanisms emerge from video instruction tuning beyond image-text pretraining.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic mechanistic blueprint of VideoLLM temporal reasoning, showing that the process decomposes into three well-defined stages (cross-frame interaction → video-language integration at temporal keywords → answer generation) with consistent layer ranges across architectures. The practical implication that 58% of attention edges can be pruned while retaining VideoQA accuracy opens a path toward efficient inference via early-exit or sparse-attention strategies. Open questions include whether pathway regularization during training could improve robustness, how to reduce the static-scene bias identified in failure cases, and whether the identified layer ranges generalize to longer videos or more complex temporal reasoning beyond the multiple-choice format studied here.
