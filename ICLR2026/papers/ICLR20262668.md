## Problem

Language models trained with different tokenizers have mismatched vocabularies, preventing them from cooperating at the next-token distribution level for tasks like ensembling, knowledge distillation, or speculative decoding. Prior work (Phan et al., 2025; Vieira et al., 2025) provided a theoretically guaranteed reduction only to the byte-level vocabulary (256 tokens), which is the most restrictive case and inherently increases inference cost since every byte must be predicted individually. This paper asks whether one can reduce to an arbitrary sub-vocabulary—larger than bytes but smaller than the full vocabulary—while preserving the exact text-generation distribution.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input an autoregressive language model with vocabulary V (accessed only through its next-token distribution p_V), a target sub-vocabulary V_sub ⊂ V with its own tokenizer, and the previously sampled sub-tokens y_{1:k} ∈ V_sub^k. It outputs the next-token distribution p_{V→V_sub}(y_{k+1} | y_{1:k}) over V_sub, which can be sampled to continue generation. Crucially, each step requires only a single forward pass of the original model (to obtain p_V(x_{t+1}|x_{1:t}) after re-tokenizing y_{1:k} in V), plus a lightweight bookkeeping loop over a cached relative covering set and a top-K subset of V (K=300 in experiments), making the per-step overhead negligible relative to the model's own computation.

## Method

The core mechanism is a new notion called nested tokenization: a text is first tokenized in V, then each V-token is re-tokenized in V_sub via its byte string, producing a sequence in V_sub. The vocabulary-reduced distribution p_{V→V_sub} is defined as the pushforward of p_V under this nested tokenization, and Theorem 3.1 proves it is lossless—the induced text distribution is identical to the original. To compute the next-token distribution in practice, the paper introduces the relative covering C_{V,V_sub}(y_{1:k}), a generalization of the minimal covering from byte-level reduction, and derives a recursive formula (Theorem 3.4) decomposing the marginal probability into two terms: (i) continuation of tokens already in the previous step's relative cover, and (ii) new tokens that complete the current sub-token boundary. Algorithm 2 (K-LVR) approximates the second term by restricting to the top-K highest-probability tokens in V, reducing the per-step cost from |V_sub|×|V| to O(|C| + K) operations while maintaining near-lossless accuracy in practice.

## Results

On the ensemble task (Qwen2.5-3B + Falcon3-7B via Product of Experts), K-LVR over the Maximal Common Vocabulary (MCV, 63,552 tokens) achieves 81.12% on GSM8K, 30.29% on MATH, 34.71% on ACP, and 42.00% on MMLU-Pro, substantially outperforming both the Union vocabulary baseline (27.60/19.57/25.86/1.93) and the Naive MCV restriction (24.94/21.71/25.71/2.07). For vocabulary reduction alone, K-LVR preserves accuracy across all sub-vocabulary sizes: e.g., Falcon3-7B scores 79.30% on GSM8K with 1-byte tokens versus 76.65% with the full vocabulary, while the Naive restriction drops to 0.00%. Inference speed for MCV ensemble is approximately 2× faster than byte-level ensemble (33.50 vs. 17.03 bytes/sec for Qwen2.5-3B & Falcon3-7B).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two ensemble baselines: (1) Union vocabulary (Yao et al., 2025), which extends each model's next-token distribution to the union of all vocabularies by zeroing out-of-vocabulary tokens, and (2) Naive MCV restriction, which simply zeros probabilities for tokens outside the maximal common vocabulary and renormalizes. Both baselines catastrophically fail under Product of Experts: Union scores only 27.60% on GSM8K and 1.93% on MMLU-Pro, while Naive MCV scores 24.94% and 2.07% respectively—far below either single model. In stark contrast, K-LVR (MCV) achieves 81.12% on GSM8K and 42.00% on MMLU-Pro, exceeding both single models and approaching the 1-byte K-LVR variant (82.49/41.21). The gap is most dramatic on MMLU-Pro, where baselines drop by ~40 points while K-LVR maintains performance within 0.7 points of the best single model. Additionally, K-LVR (MCV) is ~2× faster in inference than the byte-level ensemble (Phan et al., 2025), since it generates multiple bytes per step rather than one byte at a time.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes the byte-level reduction framework of Phan et al. (2025) and Vieira et al. (2025), which convert token-level language models to equivalent byte-level models, to arbitrary sub-vocabularies of any size. The key theoretical contribution is the notion of nested tokenization and the relative covering set, which extend the minimal covering concept from the byte-level setting. For the ensemble application, the paper departs from heuristic approaches (union vocabulary, string matching, embedding similarity) by providing a theoretically lossless reduction to the maximal common vocabulary (MCV), and contrasts with prior vocabulary-reduction work aimed at model compression rather than cross-model cooperation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The MCV-based ensemble achieves comparable accuracy to byte-level ensemble while being roughly 2× faster (Table 3), making cross-vocabulary model cooperation practically viable without the per-byte inference overhead. A notable limitation is that the top-K approximation (K=300 in experiments) introduces a small but nonzero loss, and greedy decoding can produce different texts from equivalent distributions, which may matter for downstream tasks sensitive to exact token sequences. Open questions include whether the framework extends to non-BPE tokenizers without the validity condition, how it scales to ensembles of many models with very small MCVs, and whether the relative covering set can grow problematically for certain tokenizer pairs.
