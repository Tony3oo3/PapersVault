## Problem

Adapting GRPO-style reinforcement learning to diffusion models is hindered by a fundamental mismatch: GRPO requires a stochastic policy, but the most efficient diffusion samplers are deterministic ODEs. Prior work forces SDE-based sampling to induce stochasticity, but this yields lower-quality rollouts, slow convergence from model-agnostic Gaussian noise, and expensive full-trajectory training. The paper identifies that GRPO's practical success stems from its use of fine-grained group-level relative preference information rather than the policy-gradient mechanism itself, motivating a method that retains the group information while discarding the stochastic policy requirement.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt as input and produces a generated RGB image via a diffusion model (SD3.5-M). During training, for each prompt the online model generates a group of G samples using a few-step (e.g., 10-step) deterministic ODE rollout to keep inference cost low; these samples are scored by a reward function and partitioned into positive and negative groups. A timestep clip strategy restricts training timesteps to [t_min, T] to prevent the model from overfitting to visual artifacts introduced by the low-step generation.

## Method

DGPO replaces the policy-gradient objective with a direct group-level preference optimization based on the Bradley-Terry model. For each prompt, G samples are generated via ODE rollouts, scored by a reward model, and partitioned into positive and negative groups using GRPO-style advantage normalization (mean/std). The group-level reward is parameterized as a weighted sum of per-sample rewards, where each sample's weight is the absolute value of its normalized advantage; this choice guarantees that the intractable partition function Z(c) cancels between the positive and negative sums. Applying Jensen's inequality to move the expectation outside the sigmoid yields a final loss (Eq. 17) that is a sigmoid-gated difference of denoising score matching losses between the current and reference models, evaluated at a single randomly sampled timestep rather than the full trajectory. This eliminates the need for stochastic policies, enables high-fidelity ODE sampling, and reduces per-iteration cost by avoiding trajectory-level training, resulting in ~20× faster convergence than Flow-GRPO.

## Results

On the GenEval benchmark, DGPO applied to SD3.5-M achieves an overall score of 0.97, surpassing Flow-GRPO (0.95), GPT-4o (0.84), and the base SD3.5-M (0.63). On visual text rendering, DGPO reaches 0.96 OCR accuracy versus 0.92 for Flow-GRPO, and on human preference alignment it scores 23.89 PickScore versus 23.31. Training is approximately 20× faster overall (and ~30× faster on GenEval specifically) compared to Flow-GRPO, while out-of-domain quality metrics (Aesthetic, DeQA, ImageReward, UnifiedReward) are maintained or slightly improved.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Flow-GRPO (Liu et al., 2025), the primary baseline, DGPO wins on every in-domain metric: GenEval overall (0.97 vs. 0.95), OCR accuracy for text rendering (0.96 vs. 0.92), and PickScore for human preference alignment (23.89 vs. 23.31). On out-of-domain quality metrics (Aesthetic, DeQA, ImageReward, UnifiedReward), DGPO matches or marginally exceeds Flow-GRPO, indicating no reward-hacking degradation. DGPO also trains roughly 20× faster than Flow-GRPO overall and ~30× faster on the GenEval task, owing to its use of deterministic ODE samplers and single-timestep training rather than full-trajectory SDE rollouts. Against Diffusion DPO (Wallace et al., 2024), DGPO outperforms in both online and offline settings, demonstrating that group-level preference optimization captures fine-grained relative reward information that pairwise DPO cannot exploit.

## Relation to Prior Work

DGPO sits at the intersection of two lines of work: DPO-style preference optimization (Rafailov et al., 2024; Wallace et al., 2024), which avoids stochastic policies by directly optimizing likelihood ratios, and GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), which extracts fine-grained relative preference signals from groups of samples. The paper's key conceptual departure is the argument that GRPO's effectiveness for diffusion models stems from its group-level relative information rather than its policy-gradient mechanism, allowing the authors to transplant GRPO's advantage-based weighting into a DPO-style objective. This eliminates the need for SDE-based stochastic rollouts (required by Flow-GRPO and DanceGRPO) while also overcoming Diffusion-DPO's restriction to pairwise comparisons, yielding a group-level Bradley-Terry objective with advantage-weighted samples.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ~20× training speedup over Flow-GRPO is a substantial practical win for anyone performing RL post-training of diffusion models, and the ability to use high-fidelity ODE samplers for rollouts (rather than lower-quality SDE samples) addresses a real quality bottleneck. The timestep clip strategy is a simple but important practical detail that prevents overfitting to few-step artifacts. Limitations include evaluation on a single base model (SD3.5-M) and a single resolution (512), leaving open whether the speedup and quality gains transfer to larger architectures, video generation, or different flow-matching formulations. The paper also acknowledges that offline DGPO underperforms the online setting, suggesting the online sampling loop remains important for full performance.
