## Problem

Existing physics benchmarks evaluate only final answers via multiple-choice or short-answer formats, obscuring the reasoning process and providing limited diagnostic value. Recent stepwise approaches rely on heuristic LLM-as-judge scoring (prone to hallucination and prompt sensitivity) or restrictive linear step-ordering assumptions that cannot capture the inherently non-linear, branching structure of physics derivations. This paper addresses the need for a fine-grained, interpretable, and theoretically grounded process-level evaluation framework for competition-level physics reasoning.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a student's solution (markdown text with LaTeX formulas, optionally accompanied by a problem diagram) and a pre-annotated reference DAG where each node is a canonicalized formula and each directed edge encodes a prerequisite dependency. It produces a step-level accuracy score computed as |Ach(M)|/|F| (the fraction of reference formulas in the ancestor closure of the matched set), a binary final-answer accuracy, and the explicit matched and achieved formula sets. The scoring is entirely deterministic and rule-based—no LLM is invoked during evaluation—making it reproducible and free of judge hallucination.

## Method

The core mechanism represents each reference solution as a directed acyclic graph (DAG) of formulas, where edges point from prerequisite formulas to their dependents, encoding causal derivation dependencies. Scoring uses an Ancestor Closure policy: once a formula is matched by the student's solution, all of its DAG ancestors (reverse-reachable prerequisites) are also credited, and the score is the fraction of total reference formulas covered. The paper proves (Theorem 1) that under singleton-minimal-justifier and causality assumptions, DAGs are in bijection with order-keeping justification systems, establishing the DAG as the minimal lossless encoding of formula dependencies, and (Theorem 2) that Ancestor Closure Scoring is the unique admissible policy satisfying matched inclusion, ancestor closure, and soundness. Formula equivalence is determined by a two-stage rule-based algorithm: (1) constant/variable substitution to normalize notation, and (2) iterative solution-set equivalence checking where random values are assigned to non-target variables and the target is solved in both equations over up to 40 trials with a 10^{-6} tolerance, avoiding any LLM-based judgment.

## Results

On PRISM-PHYSICS, the strongest model (GPT-5 with High reasoning effort) achieves only 29.36% final-answer accuracy and 54.13% step-level accuracy on average, with final-answer accuracy dropping below 10% on hard problems for most models. Step-level scores consistently exceed final-answer scores by 15–25 percentage points, revealing that models derive valid intermediate equations before failing at later stages. For evaluation framework alignment with human experts (IPhO Gold Medalist + Top-Tier Physics PhD), PRISM-DAG achieves Kendall's τ_b = 0.346 (p = 1.31×10⁻⁴), outperforming both LLM-as-Judge (τ_b = 0.294) and PSAS-S (τ_b = 0.213).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PRISM-DAG outperforms both baselines on human-annotation alignment. Against LLM-as-Judge (which follows the SeePhys evaluation protocol and assigns binary 0/1 scores), PRISM-DAG achieves a higher Kendall's τ_b (0.346 vs. 0.294) with a substantially lower p-value (1.31×10⁻⁴ vs. 6.90×10⁻³), indicating stronger and more statistically robust concordance with expert graders. Against PSAS-S (Zhang et al., 2025), which evaluates steps independently without modeling causal dependencies, PRISM-DAG improves τ_b by 0.133 (0.346 vs. 0.213) and reduces the asymptotic p-value by over 16×. The key advantage is that PRISM-DAG is fully rule-based (no LLM in the scoring loop) and explicitly propagates credit along DAG causal edges, whereas both baselines are LLM-based and either ignore process structure (LLM-as-Judge) or treat steps as independent (PSAS-S). On the benchmark itself, even the best frontier models (GPT-5 High, Gemini-2.5-Pro) achieve only ~29–30% final-answer accuracy, confirming that competition-level physics remains a significant open challenge.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from two dominant paradigms in LLM reasoning evaluation: (1) final-answer-only benchmarks for physics (OlympiadBench, SeePhys, PhyBench, GPQA) that ignore the reasoning process, and (2) LLM-as-judge scoring that is susceptible to hallucination and prompt sensitivity. The closest prior work, PhysReason (Zhang et al., 2025), attempts step-level scoring but assumes strictly linear step ordering and checks only expressions rather than full equations. PRISM-PHYSICS carves new ground by representing solutions as directed acyclic graphs that capture branching, merging, and reuse of intermediate results, and by replacing heuristic LLM judgment with a fully rule-based symbolic equivalence checker, backed by a proof that the DAG representation and ancestor-closure scoring policy are optimal under mild assumptions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a practical gap: final-answer scoring on hard physics problems yields near-zero accuracy for all tested models, making it impossible to distinguish models that complete 80% of a derivation from those that fail immediately. Step-level DAG scoring provides dense intermediate signals that are directly useful for RL-based fine-tuning and data curation. Key limitations remain: the equivalence checker cannot yet handle context-dependent equivalences (e.g., re-parameterized integrals), text-based answers fall outside its scope, and long-tail LaTeX parsing gaps persist. The framework is explicitly domain-agnostic, suggesting potential extension to mathematics, chemistry, and biology, and the authors plan to use the benchmark for post-training experiments with process-level reward signals.
