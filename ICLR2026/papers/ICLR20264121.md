## Problem

Given black-box access to a language model, can we extract a compact DFA that accepts (approximately) the same strings as the model's generative support? While Next Symbol Prediction (NSP) labels have been used empirically to evaluate sequence models, the formal learnability of languages under NSP supervision—and whether the richer per-prefix continuation labels overcome known computational hardness barriers for DFA learning—had not been established.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264121/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The learner receives positive strings from the target language, each annotated with NSP labels: for every prefix, a membership bit and a vector of |Σ| continuation bits indicating which next symbols admit an accepting continuation (total label size (|Σ|+1)(|x|+1) per example). In addition, the learner queries a language-model oracle via membership queries (accept/reject) and generative queries (sample a valid continuation conditioned on a prefix prompt). The output is a DFA approximating the model's truncated support, along with NSP error estimates and, when the extracted DFA differs from the target, concrete erroneous strings from the symmetric difference.

## Method

The core contribution is L_nsp*, a provable extension of Angluin's L* algorithm that handles counterexamples arising from continuation-label disagreements, not just membership disagreements. The key mechanism converts any NSP-label mismatch into an ordinary membership mismatch: if the target forbids a symbol but the hypothesis allows it, a BFS in the hypothesis finds a witnessing suffix; if the target allows a symbol but the hypothesis forbids it, a single generative query conditioned on that prefix yields a valid continuation that the hypothesis rejects. This reduction lets the standard L* update (Lemma G.3) proceed unchanged, maintaining the (Q,T) access/test-word invariant. The algorithm terminates after at most |A*| disagreements, and a standard Occam-style bound yields a PAC guarantee with respect to the teacher LM's distribution. The paper also proves that NSP labels are information-theoretically sufficient to identify minimal DFAs (Prop. 3.1) yet do not remove worst-case computational hardness (Theorem 4.1, via a padding construction that renders all but one continuation bit uninformative).

## Results

On bounded Dyck languages, L_nsp* recovers the target DFA with approximately 10 examples for DYCK-(2,2) and approximately 100 examples for DYCK-(2,4) and DYCK-(4,3), achieving near-perfect NSP accuracy. In contrast, classical L* with binary labels fails to recover the target automaton even with 10^3 samples on these same Dyck tasks. On the small Tomita grammars (2–5 states), both methods recover the target quickly (often within a second), and for Parity, L_nsp* identifies the teacher's larger support DFA while binary labels yield no additional states. Across all 11 languages, approximately 99.5% of sampled strings are positive, making binary classification accuracy uninformative and the extracted state count the meaningful signal.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares L_nsp* (NSP labels) against classical L* (binary membership labels only, Angluin 1987) on six languages. L_nsp* wins decisively on bounded Dyck languages: it recovers the target DFA with ~10 examples for DYCK-(2,2) and ~100 for DYCK-(2,4) and DYCK-(4,3), whereas L* fails to recover the target even with 10^3 samples. On the small Tomita grammars (2–5 states), both methods perform similarly since a single counterexample suffices to identify the target. On Parity, L_nsp* identifies the teacher's larger support DFA (enabling discovery of erroneous strings), while L* with binary labels produces no disagreements and thus no additional states beyond the trivial 2-state target. The advantage of NSP labels is most pronounced on languages with transitions to dead states (e.g., bounded Dyck), where continuation labels are heavily used to distinguish states, leading to substantially improved sample complexity over binary labels alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Angluin's L* algorithm for learning regular languages from membership and equivalence queries to the Next Symbol Prediction (NSP) setting, where each prefix carries continuation labels indicating which next symbols admit an accepting continuation. It builds on the Kearns–Valiant (1994) cryptographic hardness results for learning acyclic DFAs, showing that the richer NSP supervision does not circumvent these barriers. The work also connects to the automata-extraction-from-neural-models line (Giles et al. 1992; Weiss et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2024) by reframing the goal as learning the truncated support of a language model under a sampling rule (top-p, min-p), a formulation that prior empirical NSP evaluations of sequence models had not formalized from a learnability perspective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first formal learnability characterization for the NSP setting, establishing both identifiability (finite teaching sets exist) and a clean separation between what NSP labels can and cannot achieve computationally. The L_nsp* algorithm offers a practical, polynomial-time procedure for extracting interpretable DFAs from small-to-medium regular-language teachers, and the ablation showing that continuation labels are critical on languages with dead-state transitions (bounded Dyck) gives concrete guidance on when NSP supervision is most valuable. Key limitations are significant: the algorithm does not scale beyond a few thousand states or large vocabularies, and the support of real LLMs may not be regular, so the guarantees do not apply. The open question of exact learning with standard MQ+EQ oracles in the NSP setting, and the loose teaching-set bounds from Proposition 3.1, point to further theoretical work.
