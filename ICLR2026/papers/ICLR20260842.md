## Problem

Existing interventional causal discovery frameworks cannot distinguish true causal relations from post-treatment selection, because both produce the identical statistical signature: a variant marginal distribution p(effect) and an invariant conditional distribution p(effect|cause) after intervening on the cause. This gap is critical in practice (e.g., single-cell gene perturbation studies where only quality-controlled cells are retained), as unmodeled selection introduces spurious dependencies that mimic causal responses and distort structure recovery.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a collection of distributions {p^(k)}_{k=0}^K over observed variables X, where p^(0) is the pure observational distribution and p^(k) are interventional distributions with known hard or soft intervention targets I^(k). It outputs an F-PAG (Fine-grained Partial Ancestral Graph) over the observed variables, which extends the standard PAG with novel edge marks (▷, ◁, □) to encode whether each adjacency arises from direct causation, direct selection, latent confounding, or an equivalent inducing path — a finer distinction than the circle marks in a standard PAG.

## Method

The paper introduces an augmented DAG formulation that explicitly includes a post-treatment selection variable S alongside latent confounders L and intervention indicators ψ, then defines FI-Markov equivalence — a finer equivalence class than standard interventional Markov equivalence that distinguishes, e.g., a direct causal link from an inducing path with the same endpoint CI patterns. The F-FCI algorithm first recovers the skeleton from observational data via FCI, then orients edges between intervened node pairs by capturing four CI patterns (ψ⊥X_j, ψ⊥X_j|X_i, ψ⊥X_i, ψ⊥X_i|X_j) to assign tail, arrowhead, or square marks. Crucially, Step 2.3 resolves the remaining ambiguity of ▷ and ◁ edges by intervening on Type I inducing nodes (non-endpoint vertices with an incoming arrowhead into a square) along inducing paths, using the resulting marginal changes to confirm or reject a direct causal or selection link. Finally, standard FCI orientation rules and interventional invariance rules (see-see, do-see, do-do) orient all remaining edges. The algorithm is proven sound and complete under faithfulness.

## Results

On synthetic graphs with 10–25 variables, 2–3 latent confounders, and 2–3 selection variables, F-FCI achieves an average DAG Precision exceeding all baselines by more than 5 percentage points in most configurations, while also yielding lower DAG SHD. In the head-to-head comparison against FCI-INTERVEN (the strongest baseline handling latent confounders) on 20-variable graphs with 5–6 selection variables, F-FCI achieves 64.2% precision and 10.9 SHD under hard interventions (n=2000) versus 43.7% precision and 17.2 SHD for FCI-INTERVEN — a precision gain exceeding 15%. Under soft interventions at the same scale, F-FCI reaches 70.3% precision and 8.6 SHD compared to 48.1% and 14.4 for FCI-INTERVEN. On the real-world Norman single-cell gene perturbation dataset (5,045 genes, 105 perturbed genes), F-FCI identifies causal links consistent with prior biological evidence (e.g., IGDCC3→FEV, CEBPA→FEV, SET→FOXL2) and flags genes such as ZNF318, CDKN1C, CDKN1A, and RREB1 as affected by post-treatment selection.

## Baselines & Comparisons

F-FCI outperforms all six baselines (GIES, IGSP, UT-IGSP, JCI-GSP, FCI-interven, and CDIS) on both DAG Precision and DAG SHD across the majority of synthetic configurations (10–25 variables, varying sample sizes, hard and soft interventions). The largest and most consistent gap is against FCI-INTERVEN, the only baseline that also handles latent confounders: F-FCI achieves a precision advantage exceeding 15 percentage points (e.g., 64.2% vs. 43.7% under hard interventions at n=2000), and this advantage grows as the number of selection variables increases from 2 to 6. GIES, IGSP, UT-IGSP, and JCI-GSP, which do not model latent confounders or selection at all, show substantially lower precision and higher SHD than F-FCI in every tested configuration. CDIS (Dai et al., 2025), which handles pre-treatment selection but not post-treatment selection, is also outperformed by F-FCI, confirming that explicitly modeling post-treatment selection is the key differentiator. Notably, FCI-INTERVEN achieves higher recall than F-FCI in some settings (e.g., 77.8% vs. 66.4% under hard interventions at n=2000), indicating that F-FCI is more conservative in edge orientation — it trades some recall for substantially higher precision by correctly identifying spurious dependencies induced by selection rather than misclassifying them as causal edges.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the augmented DAG framework for interventional causal discovery (Kocaoglu et al. 2019; Hauser & Bühlmann 2012) and the FCI/MAG/PAG machinery for latent confounders (Zhang 2008b) to explicitly model post-treatment selection, a setting that prior interventional methods (GIES, IGSP, FCI-interven, CDIS) cannot handle because selection and causation produce identical CI patterns. It departs from Dai et al. (2025), which addressed pre-treatment selection via a twin-graph construction, by showing that post-treatment selection requires a fundamentally different treatment within the augmented DAG. The new FI-Markov equivalence and F-PAG representation refine the standard PAG by introducing novel edge marks (square, ▷, ◁) that encode information about inducing paths and Type I inducing nodes, going beyond what observational CI patterns alone can recover.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Post-treatment selection is pervasive in single-cell perturbation studies and clinical trials (QC filtering, per-protocol analysis), yet existing causal discovery tools silently misattribute selection-induced dependencies to causal edges, potentially leading to incorrect biological conclusions. The paper's key limitation is that identifying direct causal links or direct selection requires the presence of Type I inducing nodes along the path; inducing paths composed solely of Type II nodes remain ambiguous, leaving a gap for more complex topologies. A natural open question is how to distinguish post-treatment selection from biological constraints (e.g., cell-type-specific expression filters) that also condition the observed sample, a problem the authors flag but do not resolve.
