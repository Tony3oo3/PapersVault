## Problem

Mechanistic interpretability (MI) lacks a precise criterion for when two models share the same high-level algorithmic interpretation, and the many-to-many relationship between circuits and interpretations (Méloux et al., 2025) makes individual circuit-to-interpretation comparisons ill-defined. This paper introduces interpretive equivalence — determining whether two models implement the same interpretation without requiring an explicit description of what that interpretation is — as a tractable subproblem that enables reductions to simpler models or tasks.

## Input / Output

CONGRUITY takes two pre-trained models and a task input set (e.g., 200 IOI sentences or 100 C4 sentences), then generates parallel implementations of each model by ablating components identified as causally irrelevant via causal mediation analysis. It extracts hidden representations at each layer and computes bidirectional linear approximation error between representation spaces. The output is a scalar congruity score in [0,1] where values near 1 indicate interpretive equivalence (representation similarity cannot distinguish the models), with statistical significance assessed via bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals.

## Method

The core principle is that two interpretations are equivalent if and only if their sets of all possible implementations are equivalent, measured by Hausdorff distance over a representation-induced metric. The algorithm operationalizes this by generating implementations through causal interventions (activation patching) on non-circuit components, extracting hidden representations, and testing whether linear representation similarity can differentiate within-interpretation distances from cross-interpretation distances. The theoretical foundation specializes causal abstraction to define circuits, representations, and interpretations as nested abstractions, then proves that representation similarity provides both upper and lower bounds on interpretive equivalence (Main Results 1–2), with the CONGRUITY score directly related to these bounds (Main Result 3). The framework also introduces interpretive compression — the diameter of an interpretation's implementation set — as a measure of how much abstraction an interpretation imposes, revealing a duality where more compression simplifies interpretations but complicates equivalence testing.

## Results

On the 10-permutation detection task (6 hard-coded RASP interpretations, 100 implementations each, all achieving 96%+ accuracy), CONGRUITY yields high within-interpretation congruence (diagonal) and low cross-interpretation congruence (off-diagonal), with sorting-based interpretations (1–4) showing 0.43 average within-group congruence versus only 0.01 cross-group congruence with counting-based interpretations (5–6). On the IOI task across GPT2 and Pythia models (85M–2.9B parameters), within-family congruence is 0.73 (Pythia) and 0.92 (GPT2) versus only 0.13 cross-family, confirming that Pythia-2.8B is interpretively equivalent to Pythia-160M. For next-token prediction vs. parts-of-speech identification on GPT2, terminal punctuation and closing brackets/quotations show significantly higher POS congruence (medium effect size) compared to the all-token control (0.48), while articles and prepositions remain statistically indistinguishable from the control.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare CONGRUITY against competing interpretive-equivalence methods, as no prior method addresses this problem. Instead, validation is performed against known ground truth and prior circuit-discovery findings. On the toy permutation task, CONGRUITY correctly separates six manually designed interpretations into two algorithmic families (sorting vs. counting), matching the ground-truth structure. On IOI, CONGRUITY's cross-family score of 0.13 (Pythia vs. GPT2) aligns with the distinct circuits independently identified by Tigges et al. (2024) and Merullo et al. (2024), while the high within-family scores (0.73, 0.92) confirm that scale does not change the underlying interpretation. For the POS reduction, CONGRUITY's ability to distinguish syntactic tokens (terminal punctuation, closing brackets) from semantic tokens (articles, prepositions) provides a novel, quantitative signal that prior circuit analyses had not formally tested.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the causal abstraction framework of Geiger et al. (2025) to formalize interpretations, circuits, and representations as nested abstractions of a model's computation graph, and on the non-identifiability result of Méloux et al. (2025) showing a many-to-many mapping between circuits and interpretations. It departs from prior MI work (both top-down alignment methods like Geiger et al. 2024 and bottom-up circuit discovery like Conmy et al. 2023) by shifting the comparison target from individual interpretations or circuits to *sets of implementations*, using linear representation similarity (CKA, Kornblith et al. 2019) as the distance metric. The GETIMPL procedure for enumerating implementations via causal interventions draws on activation patching (Wang et al. 2022) and strict interchange intervention training (Gupta et al. 2024), while the POS circuit discovery leverages function vectors (Todd et al. 2024).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical guarantees (Main Results 1–3) that simultaneously relate a model's interpretations, circuits, and representations, establishing representation similarity as both necessary and sufficient for interpretive equivalence under stated assumptions. Practically, this could enable reducing interpretation of large models to smaller ones (e.g., Pythia-2.8B to Pythia-160M on IOI) or decomposing complex tasks like next-token prediction into simpler subtasks. Key limitations include reliance on linear representation similarity (which may fail for representations on complex manifolds), the need for some circuit knowledge to identify unimportant components, and experiments limited to relatively small models and synthetic tasks. Open questions include how to translate equivalence judgments into actual algorithmic descriptions, whether the framework generalizes to non-linear similarity measures, and how to handle the asymmetry in choosing which circuit to compare against.
