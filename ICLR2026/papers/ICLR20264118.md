## Problem

Standard RL fine-tuning for multimodal LLMs suffers from two underexplored pathologies: Advantage Collapsing, where most computed advantages cluster near zero and drown out informative gradient signals, and Rollout Silencing, where the fraction of rollouts contributing non-zero gradients steadily declines during training. Existing pipelines treat all sampled trajectories uniformly under a static paradigm, ignoring that signal quality varies and evolves over the course of training, leading to wasted computation and suboptimal updates.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264118/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes multimodal queries (an RGB image paired with a free-form text question) and produces a structured response containing a reasoning chain enclosed in think tags followed by a final answer in \boxed{} format. For each query, 2N=16 rollouts are generated at temperature 1.0, from which PTS retains only the top-M=4 contrastive pairs (α=0.5) before ABS reshuffles the global batch of 512 trajectories into S=8 weighted sub-sampling rounds. The reward is a verifiable weighted sum (0.1×format + 0.9×accuracy), and evaluation uses pass@1 averaged over 8 runs at temperature 0.5.

## Method

Shuffle-R1 introduces two data-centric modules layered on top of the GRPO policy-gradient objective without altering the loss function. First, Pairwise Trajectory Sampling (PTS) sorts the 2N rollouts per query by advantage, pairs the highest with the lowest (max-min pairing), and retains only the top-M pairs with the largest advantage contrast, concentrating gradient updates on the most discriminative positive-negative trajectory pairs to mitigate Advantage Collapsing. Second, Advantage-based Batch Shuffle (ABS) assigns each retained pair an importance weight equal to the sum of its two absolute advantages, normalizes these into a sampling distribution, and performs S rounds of weighted sub-sampling (with replacement across sub-batches) to reshape the training batch so that high-utility trajectories receive repeated exposure while low-signal ones are down-weighted, alleviating Rollout Silencing. The reshuffled batch preserves the original batch size and gradient update paradigm, adding only 4–7.7% wall-clock overhead while achieving comparable accuracy to GRPO in roughly half the training steps.

## Results

On the 30k-sample MM-Eureka training set, Shuffle-R1-Qwen-7B achieves 64.7% average accuracy across six visual reasoning benchmarks (MathVerse, MathVision, MathVista, WeMath, HallusionBench, ChartQA), surpassing all open-source 7B RL baselines and matching or exceeding GPT-4o and Claude-3.7-Sonnet on MathVerse (53.9 vs 50.8/52.0) and MathVista (77.0 vs 63.8/66.8). On the smaller Geometry3K dataset, the 3B model reaches 47.88% in-domain accuracy (vs. 42.64% GRPO, 45.09% DAPO, 43.22% GSPO), and the 7B model reaches 55.89%. Notably, Shuffle-R1 matches GRPO's final accuracy with roughly half the training steps while incurring only 4–7.7% additional wall-clock GPU time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Shuffle-R1 consistently outperforms all RL algorithm baselines (GRPO, DAPO, GSPO, RLOO, REINFORCE++) across both in-domain and out-of-domain benchmarks; on Geometry3K the 3B model gains +5.24 over GRPO, +2.79 over DAPO, and +4.66 over GSPO. Against open-source 7B RL models trained on similar data, Shuffle-R1 achieves the highest average (64.7%) by a clear margin over VL-Rethinker (62.1%), NoisyRollout (62.1%), and MM-Eureka (60.1%). On close-source comparisons, the 7B model surpasses GPT-4o on MathVerse (53.9 vs 50.8) and MathVista (77.0 vs 63.8), and beats Claude-3.7-Sonnet on MathVerse (53.9 vs 52.0) and MathVista (77.0 vs 66.8), though it trails on MathVision (30.0 vs 41.3 for Claude). The framework also generalizes beyond math: on Referring Expression Comprehension (RefCOCO/RefCOCO+/RefCOCOg), it improves over GRPO by 1.9–5.3 points, and on text-only LLM benchmarks (Math12K, AIME24, MATH500, GSM8K, GPQA, OlympiadBench) it outperforms GRPO by 2.8–6.6 points. In terms of efficiency, Shuffle-R1 reaches GRPO-equivalent accuracy in approximately half the training steps with only 4–7.7% extra wall-clock GPU time, and maintains a high token utilization rate throughout training, directly mitigating the Rollout Silencing problem.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) as its underlying policy gradient algorithm, adding two data-centric modules—Pairwise Trajectory Sampling and Advantage-based Batch Shuffle—on top of the standard RL pipeline. It departs from the broader line of RL improvements for MLLMs (DAPO, GSPO, RLOO, REINFORCE++) that modify the objective function, reward design, or importance sampling, instead targeting the data structuring layer: which trajectories receive gradient updates and how often. The conceptual framing—that 'what data the model updates on is as important as how it updates'—positions the work as complementary to algorithmic improvements rather than competing with them.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that a simple, low-overhead data restructuring (4–7.7% extra wall-clock time) can halve the number of training steps needed to match GRPO, which is meaningful for practitioners running expensive MLLM RL pipelines. The ablation showing that one-way positive or negative sampling degrades performance below the GRPO baseline provides useful evidence that contrastive pairing is not merely a heuristic but captures a genuine signal structure. Open questions include whether the introduced positive bias (acknowledged in Appendix H) causes issues at longer training horizons, whether the approach transfers to non-verifiable-reward settings, and whether the gains persist as model scale or data diversity increases beyond the tested 3B–32B range.
