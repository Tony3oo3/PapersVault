## Problem

Prior impossibility results for attention-only transformers (Peng et al., 2024; Sanford et al., 2024b) were conditional on bounds on embedding dimension and numerical precision, leaving open whether wider or higher-precision single-head models could overcome representational barriers. This paper establishes the first unconditional, dimension- and precision-independent impossibility result: no 1-head, 1-layer attention-only transformer can solve the Endpoint Selection Problem on any directed graph containing a cycle, regardless of how large the embedding space or how fine the arithmetic precision. It further shows that a mere second head suffices to solve the problem on all directed graphs with constant dimension and logarithmic precision, revealing that head count—not width—is the critical axis of expressivity.

## Input / Output

The model receives a fixed-length-4 token sequence (u, v, i, #) where u and v are vertex tokens representing a directed arc, i ∈ {1,2} is a selector token, and # is a fixed query token that prevents the model from exploiting direct key–query alignment between the selector and its target position. The output is a single vertex token: u if i=1, v if i=2, with the input distribution uniform over all 2m arc-selector pairs. Temperature is set to zero so the model is deterministic (argmax over output logits), and the task is evaluated as zero-error classification over the graph's arc set.

## Method

The impossibility proof (Theorem 2) reduces the attention computation to a geometric constraint: for each edge (a,b) in a cycle, the softmax-weighted context vector must lie in the a-dominant half-space for selector 1 and the b-dominant half-space for selector 2, yielding a system of dot-product inequalities that Lemma 2 shows is unsatisfiable for any cycle via an induction on cycle length that collapses two consecutive edges into one. The positive 2-head construction (Theorem 3) assigns each head a dedicated attention matrix that reads exactly one input position (position 1 or 2), while the selector token's embedding carries a large negative component that biases the softmax to up-weight the correct position; the value and output projection matrices then route the selected position's vertex embedding to the correct logit. A corollary achieves constant dimension d=5 by encoding vertices as well-separated unit vectors on a 2D circle, requiring only O(log n) precision to distinguish adjacent vertices. The paper also connects the optimal 1-head error to the Maximum Acyclic Subgraph problem, proving NP-hardness of even approximating the best 1-head solution within a 1.3606 factor.

## Results

The paper establishes an unconditional impossibility: no 1-head, 1-layer, attention-only transformer can solve the Endpoint Selection Problem (ESP) on any directed graph containing a cycle, regardless of embedding dimension or numerical precision (Theorem 2). In contrast, a 2-head, 1-layer, attention-only transformer solves ESP on arbitrary directed graphs with O(n) embedding dimension and O(1) precision (Theorem 3), or with constant dimension d=5 and O(log n) precision (Corollary 2). The optimal 1-head error on a graph with m edges and maximum acyclic subgraph of size MAS is exactly 1/2 − MAS/(2m), making the minimum-error problem NP-hard to approximate within a 1.3606 factor (Theorem 5). Empirically, 1-head models on complete digraphs plateau near the theoretical 75% accuracy bound, while 2-head models reach near-perfect accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between 1-head and 2-head attention-only transformers on the same ESP task. Prior impossibility results (Peng et al., 2024; Sanford et al., 2024b) required conditional assumptions bounding dimension d, precision p, or the product H(d+1)p < n log n; this paper's Theorem 2 removes all such conditions, establishing an unconditional barrier for 1-head models. The 2-head construction (Theorem 3) achieves zero error on all directed graphs with O(n) dimension and O(1) precision, while Corollary 2 further reduces dimension to a constant 5 at the cost of O(log n) precision. Empirically, on complete digraphs (the hardest case with |MFAS| = n(n−1)/2), 1-head models fail to reach the theoretical 75% accuracy ceiling even with d ≫ n, whereas 2-head models achieve near-perfect accuracy with sublinear dimension. The paper also shows that adding a bounded-width FFN to a 1-head model can match 2-head attention-only performance at comparable parameter counts (Appendix C.2), suggesting the bottleneck is specifically in the attention mechanism's single-head structure rather than total model capacity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of transformer impossibility results (Peng et al., 2024; Sanford et al., 2024b) by removing the critical assumption that embedding dimension and precision are bounded, establishing a dimension- and precision-independent lower bound for 1-head attention-only transformers. It also builds on the induction head literature (Olsson et al., 2022; Sanford et al., 2024c) by showing the newly introduced Endpoint Selection Problem (ESP) is a special case of the 2-hop induction head, thereby transferring the impossibility to that well-studied task. The graph-theoretic framing departs from prior sequence-based formulations (Index Lookup in Bhattamishra et al., 2024; path-finding in Wang et al., 2024a) by tying representational limits directly to the structural properties of the underlying directed graph (acyclicity, feedback arc set size).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes a clean, unconditional 1-head vs. 2-head separation for a natural task, suggesting that attention heads act as discrete units of computational power and opening the door to a fine-grained hierarchy of transformer architectures. The NP-completeness connection (optimal 1-head error is governed by Maximum Acyclic Subgraph) is a striking theoretical link that implies even finding the best 1-head weights is intractable, adding a computational complexity dimension to the representational analysis. Limitations include the attention-only, 1–2 head setting without FFN layers or residual connections, and the simplified 4-token input; whether the hierarchy extends to more heads, deeper stacks, or realistic LLM-scale tasks remains open. The empirical observation that gradient descent fails to reach the provably optimal 1-head solution on complete digraphs raises practical questions about trainability vs. representability gaps.
