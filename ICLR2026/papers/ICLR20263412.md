## Problem

Task-trained RNNs exhibit a strong simplicity bias that causes different networks trained on the same neuroscience-style task to collapse onto effectively the same minimal solution (e.g., fixed-point attractors, line attractors, ring attractors), a phenomenon termed dynamic collapse. This limits the diversity of computationally distinct hypotheses that can be generated for how biological neural circuits might implement the same computation, since standard means of varying solutions (seeds, initialization scales, architectures) still yield representationally similar dynamics.

## Input / Output

The method operates on rate-based RNNs (N=128, tanh nonlinearity) trained via BPTT on time-series tasks with noisy stimulus inputs and context cues. The key additional input is the firing-rate matrix of a previously trained reference RNN, which is projected into its readout nullspace before being used in the similarity penalty. The output is a new RNN whose activity trajectories are linearly unpredictable from the reference, yielding alternative dynamical solutions (e.g., oscillatory modes instead of attractor states) while still producing the correct task output.

## Method

INSD (Iterative Neural Similarity Deflation) adds a linear-predictivity penalty to the standard task loss for each subsequently trained RNN. Specifically, firing rates from both the new RNN and the reference RNN are projected into their respective readout nullspaces (to avoid penalizing the output-potent component necessary for task performance), and the penalty is the r² score measuring how well the reference activity can be linearly predicted from the new RNN's activity. This directional choice (penalized → reference) is critical: it prevents the trivial solution of inflating activity dimensionality with task-irrelevant noise, which would drive symmetric metrics like CKA or RSA to zero. The procedure is iterative—a third RNN is penalized against both prior RNNs—resembling a Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization in the space of RNN solutions. A small ridge regularizer (ρ=10⁻³) is added for numerical stability when computing the projection, and the penalty strength λ=0.05 is held constant across all tasks.

## Results

Across three neuroscience-style tasks (context-dependent integration, 3-bit flipflop, MemoryPro), INSD-generated RNNs produce solutions that are markedly less linearly predictive of standard RNNs (near-zero predictivity after readout-nullspace projection) and occupy distinct regions in DSA dissimilarity embeddings. These alternative solutions replace fixed-point attractors and slow manifolds with quasi-periodic oscillatory dynamics, and encode task variables more nonlinearly (e.g., context requires RBF-kernel decoding rather than linear decoding). Under out-of-distribution conditions, alt-2 RNNs moderately outperform the standard population in high-noise regimes for context-dependent integration and flipflop, while alt-1 RNNs significantly outperform standard RNNs on the most difficult MemoryPro trials (high noise + high memory load), though they underperform under low memory loads.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares INSD-generated RNNs (alt-1, alt-2) against a population of 40 standard RNNs (10 seeds × 4 initialization scales g ∈ {0.01, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5}) on each task. Under in-distribution conditions, standard RNNs consistently outperform INSD models, as expected since the similarity penalty trades some task accuracy for representational distinctness. However, in out-of-distribution sweeps (Fig. 5), INSD models win in specific regimes: for context-dependent integration, alt-2 moderately outperforms the standard population at high noise σ while remaining robust to lengthened trial durations; for 3-bit flipflop, alt-2 achieves a moderate but significant performance gain at high noise; for MemoryPro, alt-1 significantly outperforms standard RNNs when both noise and fractional memory load are high, but significantly underperforms at low memory loads. The alt-2 RNN for MemoryPro only matched or underperformed across all conditions, suggesting it failed to learn the task adequately. All standard RNNs are near-perfectly linearly predictive of each other regardless of initialization scale, confirming the dynamic collapse phenomenon that INSD is designed to break.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two active threads: (1) the neuroscience-style RNN task framework (Mante et al., 2013; Barak et al., 2013; Maheswaranathan et al., 2019) and (2) the recent recognition that task-trained RNNs exhibit a strong simplicity bias leading to dynamic collapse (Turner & Barak, 2023; Driscoll et al., 2024; Hazelden et al., 2025). Rather than building on a prior method for generating diverse RNN solutions, INSD carves new ground by repurposing a neural similarity measure—specifically, directional linear predictivity—iteratively as a training penalty, loosely analogous to Gram-Schmidt deflation in solution space. The approach draws conceptual inspiration from Barlow Twins (Zbontar et al., 2021) and linear adversarial concept erasure (Ravfogel et al., 2022), while the analysis toolkit (DSA, fixed-point topology, linear decodability) is inherited from the RNN interpretability literature (Ostrow et al., 2023; Sussillo & Barak, 2013).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The simplicity bias in RNNs is a genuine bottleneck for the neuroscience modeling program: if all trained networks collapse to the same attractor-based solution, they cannot generate the competing hypotheses needed to test against biological data. INSD provides a simple, practical mechanism to break this collapse, and the finding that the resulting oscillatory solutions can outperform standard attractor-based solutions in noisy or out-of-distribution regimes offers a concrete computational argument for why biological circuits might favor dynamic over persistent coding. Key limitations include the restriction to simple single-task settings with attractor-based standard solutions, the use of linear predictivity (an imperfect proxy for dynamical similarity) as the penalty, and the lack of an extensive sweep over penalty strength. Open questions include whether INSD generalizes to multi-task settings, tasks requiring transient dynamics, and whether the 'capacity' for distinct solutions scales predictably with task difficulty and network size.
