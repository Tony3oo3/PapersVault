## Problem

Standard RAG pipelines retrieve isolated text chunks based on literal or semantic similarity, failing to model the structural dependencies between pieces of information needed for multi-hop reasoning. Existing graph-based RAG extensions (GraphRAG, RAPTOR, HippoRAG) either rely heavily on LLMs during indexing—increasing cost and introducing noise—or require both NER and relation extraction, complicating the pipeline. BrowseNet addresses this by formulating multi-hop retrieval as query-specific subgraph exploration over a lexically connected graph, requiring only NER and a single LLM call for query decomposition.

## Input / Output

The system takes a multi-hop question and a corpus of Wikipedia passages (9K–12K chunks per benchmark) as input. The corpus is pre-processed into a graph-of-chunks where each node carries a semantic embedding (NV-Embed-v2) and named entities, and edges connect chunks sharing exact or synonymous entities (via ColBERTv2). At query time, the multi-hop question is decomposed into a DAG of single-hop sub-queries, and the system outputs a set of retrieved context subgraphs (preserving inter-chunk reasoning dependencies) along with a generated answer and step-by-step reasoning explanation. Unlike flat retrievers that return a ranked list of independent passages, BrowseNet returns structured subgraphs whose topology mirrors the query's dependency structure.

## Method

BrowseNet constructs a graph-of-chunks by running zero-shot NER (GLiNER) on each passage and linking chunks that share exact or synonymous entities (ColBERTv2 cosine similarity > 0.9). At retrieval time, GPT-4o decomposes the multi-hop query into a DAG of single-hop sub-queries, which are then processed in topological order. For initiator nodes (no predecessors), top-k chunks are retrieved via semantic search scoring each chunk by the maximum cosine similarity to either the original query or the sub-query. For non-initiator nodes, the candidate pool is restricted to graph neighbors of the predecessor chunks, and a beam-search-like scoring retains the top-k subgraphs weighted by inverse depth (earlier hops weighted more heavily to penalize error propagation). This two-pronged design—structural graph traversal for multi-hop linking plus dense semantic matching for relevance—achieves SOTA recall with only one LLM interaction per query, reducing cost by ~33× compared to HippoRAG-2.

## Results

BrowseNet achieves state-of-the-art multi-hop QA performance, reaching an average Exact Match of 55.90 and F1 of 68.76 across HotpotQA, 2WikiMQA, and MuSiQue, outperforming the previous SOTA (HippoRAG-2) by 4.3 EM and 3.46 F1 points. On retrieval, BrowseNet attains average Recall@5 of 87.87, surpassing HippoRAG-2 (86.87) and NV-Embed-v2 (80.74). The gains are most pronounced on MuSiQue (4-hop reasoning), where BrowseNet achieves 41.60 EM versus 35.00 for HippoRAG-2 and 36.90 for NV-Embed-v2. BrowseNet also achieves ~33× lower LLM cost than HippoRAG-2 across the full pipeline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BrowseNet outperforms all 13 baselines on average EM and F1 across the three benchmarks. Against the strongest competitor HippoRAG-2, BrowseNet gains +2.9 EM on HotpotQA (62.20 vs 59.30), +3.4 EM on 2WikiMQA (63.90 vs 60.50), and +6.6 EM on MuSiQue (41.60 vs 35.00), with the largest margin on the most challenging 4-hop dataset. On retrieval (Recall@5), BrowseNet leads HippoRAG-2 by 1.0 points on average (87.87 vs 86.87), with the gap widening on 2WikiMQA (93.30 vs 90.20); however, NV-Embed-v2 marginally edges out BrowseNet on HotpotQA R@2 (83.95 vs 83.85), a dataset noted as weaker for multi-hop retrieval due to spurious signals. Dense retrievers (NV-Embed-v2, GTE-Qwen2, Granite) trail BrowseNet by 6–16 EM points on average, confirming that pure semantic similarity is insufficient for multi-hop reasoning. Graph-augmented RAG methods (GraphRAG, LightRAG, SiReRAG, RAPTOR) perform substantially worse, with LightRAG achieving only 4.80 average EM, likely due to its reliance on LLM-generated summaries during indexing. Simple baselines (BM25, Contriever, GTR) are far behind, with average EM below 50, underscoring the difficulty of multi-hop retrieval without structural reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

BrowseNet carves new ground at the intersection of graph-based retrieval and associative memory, departing from standard RAG at every pipeline stage. It builds conceptually on brain-inspired retrieval (HippoRAG/HippoRAG-2) and graph-augmented RAG (GraphRAG, RAPTOR, LightRAG) but distinguishes itself by requiring only NER (not relation extraction) for graph construction and by minimizing LLM involvement to query decomposition and optional entity extraction. The core novelty is framing multi-hop retrieval as query-specific subgraph exploration over a lexically-connected graph-of-chunks, combining structural (entity co-occurrence) and semantic (embedding) similarity in a beam-search-like traversal that prior graph-RAG methods do not explicitly perform.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 33× reduction in LLM cost relative to HippoRAG-2 while matching or exceeding its retrieval and QA performance makes graph-based multi-hop retrieval substantially more accessible for production deployment. The consistent 7–10 point Recall@5 gains over naive dense retrieval across three different encoders (Table 5) suggest the graph traversal methodology is robust rather than encoder-dependent. However, error analysis reveals that nearly half of failures still originate in the semantic retrieval stage, and the system's dependence on LLM-generated query decompositions (only 68.5% isomorphic accuracy on MuSiQue) remains a key vulnerability. Open questions include whether the approach scales to heterogeneous or non-Wikipedia corpora, whether richer semantic relationships beyond entity co-occurrence can be encoded, and whether the combinatorial traversal can be further pruned for deeper (5+ hop) queries.
