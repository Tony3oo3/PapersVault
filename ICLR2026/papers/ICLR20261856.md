## Problem

Standard temporal-difference (TD) learning represents Q-functions monolithically, without the iterative computation and dense intermediate supervision that make modern generative models (transformers, diffusion) effective at capturing complex functions. This limits the ability to scale Q-function capacity in value-based RL, particularly in challenging offline settings where generalization is critical. The paper asks whether iterative computation with stepwise supervision can be brought to TD-based value estimation to enable compute scaling analogous to what works in language and vision.

## Input / Output

The method takes a state-action pair (s, a) and a scalar initial noise sample z(0) drawn from Unif[l, u], where [l, u] is chosen to overlap with the expected Q-value range. The velocity field v_θ(t, z|s, a) is conditioned on the state-action pair, the current interpolant z(t) (encoded as an N-bin categorical histogram via HL-Gauss), and time t (encoded as a 64-dimensional Fourier basis). The output is a scalar Q-value estimate obtained by K Euler integration steps of the velocity field, with the learned distribution converging to a Dirac delta around the true Q-value. A separate distilled one-step critic is trained to approximate the flow critic's output for efficient policy extraction.

## Method

floq parameterizes the Q-function as a time-dependent, state-action-conditioned velocity field over a 1D latent, trained with a linear flow-matching loss against TD-bootstrapped targets computed by integrating a target (EMA) velocity field. At each training step, a random t ~ Unif[0,1] is sampled, the interpolant z(t) = (1-t)·z(0) + t·y(s,a) is constructed, and the velocity is supervised to match the displacement (y - z(0))/(1-0). Two critical design choices prevent the flow from collapsing to a monolithic network: (1) setting the initial noise range [l, u] wide enough to produce curved (not straight) flow trajectories, ensuring the velocity field must meaningfully condition on z(t) and t; and (2) using HL-Gauss categorical encoding for z(t) and Fourier-basis encoding for t to handle non-stationary magnitudes and enable time-dependent behavior. Capacity is scaled by increasing the number of integration steps K at inference, which reuses the same velocity network parameters rather than adding new layers, and the dense supervision at every t provides the iterative compute benefit absent in ResNets or ensembles.

## Results

On the OGBench suite of 50 offline RL tasks (5 locomotion + 5 manipulation environments × 5 tasks each), floq (Best) achieves an average success rate of 59% across all environments and 38% on the 5 hardest environments, compared to 47% and 21% for FQL (2M steps), representing a ~1.8× improvement on hard tasks. On the 10 default OGBench tasks, floq (Default) reaches 64% average success rate versus 40% for FQL (1M) and 38% for DSRL, more than doubling DSRL's performance on hard tasks (45% vs. 21%). On the standard D4RL benchmark, floq matches FQL with nearly identical normalized returns, confirming robustness on easier tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

floq outperforms FQL (Park et al., 2025d), its primary baseline sharing the same flow-matching policy, by ~1.8× on hard OGBench environments (38% vs. 21% average success rate) and by 12–13 points on the full 50-task suite (59% vs. 47%). Against ReBRAC (Tarasov et al., 2023), the strongest monolithic-critic Gaussian-policy method, floq achieves 59% vs. 31% overall and 38% vs. 15% on hard tasks. On the 10 default OGBench tasks, floq (Default) surpasses DSRL (Wagenmaker et al., 2025) by over 2× on hard tasks (45% vs. 21%) and SORL (Espinosa-Dice et al., 2025) by a wide margin on the hardest environments (e.g., 52% vs. 20% on hmmaze-large). floq also outperforms the distributional RL baseline IQN (Dabney et al., 2017) on default tasks (64% vs. 45% average). On D4RL, floq matches FQL closely, indicating no performance regression on simpler benchmarks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes flow-matching — previously applied to policy representation in RL (FQL, diffusion policies) — for the Q-function critic, introducing iterative computation with dense stepwise supervision into value estimation. It builds directly on FQL (Park et al., 2025) as the base offline RL algorithm and on linear flow-matching (Lipman et al., 2023; Albergo & Vanden-Eijnden, 2023) for the training objective, while borrowing the HL-Gauss categorical encoding from Farebrother et al. (2024) to handle non-stationary scalar inputs. The key departure from prior Q-function scaling work (ResNets, ensembles, classification losses) is that floq's iterative computation is supervised at every integration step via the flow-matching loss, rather than only at the final output, which the paper identifies as the critical ingredient distinguishing it from ResNet-style sequential compute.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

floq introduces a new, orthogonal axis of scaling for value-based RL — the number of flow-integration steps — that is decoupled from network depth or width, and demonstrates that this axis yields substantially better capacity scaling than either ResNets or ensembles at matched compute. The finding that dense stepwise supervision (not just iterative architecture) is what unlocks the benefit of iterative computation for Q-functions has implications beyond RL for any setting where one wants to scale inference-time compute for a scalar prediction. Open questions include how to automatically select the optimal number of integration steps (performance degrades at very high K due to overfitting, a phenomenon shared with ResNets), whether the 'cascaded critic' property (one network yielding critics of varying capacity) can be exploited for model selection or efficient deployment, and whether the curved-flow-as-error-correction analogy to LLM reasoning can be formalized theoretically.
