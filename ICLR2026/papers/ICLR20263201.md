## Problem

Existing evaluation protocols for open-ended segmentation rely on embedding-based similarity scores (e.g., Sentence-BERT) to map free-form model outputs to target taxonomies, but these mappings diverge by 37.7% from human judgments on Cityscapes, systematically misestimating models' true semantic accuracy. Additionally, no multi-modal large language model had been trained with a contrastive objective to jointly align visual regions and textual descriptions for open-ended segmentation.

## Input / Output

The model takes an RGB image (H×W×3) as input and produces binary segmentation masks M_i ∈ {0,1}^{H×W} paired with free-form text descriptions T_i for each detected region. Unlike prior MLLM-based approaches that feed bounding boxes, OPAL uses pixel-level mask embeddings as visual prompts to the LLM, providing finer-grained region context. The evaluation protocol maps each free-form description to the target vocabulary via hierarchical lexical relationships (exact match → synonym → hyponym → meronym), producing a Lexical Alignment Curve whose area under the curve serves as the aggregate score.

## Method

OPAL builds on the Osprey architecture (CLIP vision encoder, visual projector, mask-aware visual extractor, and LLaMA fine-tuned with LoRA) and introduces a dual-loss training strategy. The generative loss performs standard next-token prediction conditioned on both image and mask embeddings, while the contrastive loss aligns the last language embedding with the mask embedding in a joint space, requiring only the textual description in the LLaMA prompt for a separate forward pass. This contrastive objective adds only 1% FLOPs overhead during training while reducing output variance by nearly 50% compared to Osprey. For evaluation, the paper introduces a lexical mapping function that uses an LLM to pre-compute, for each target category, lists of associated nouns organized into cumulative lexical levels (exact matches, synonyms, hyponyms, meronyms), then matches extracted description subjects against these lists to assign categories, yielding the Lexical Alignment Curve metric.

## Results

OPAL achieves state-of-the-art open-ended panoptic segmentation with 48.8 PQ / 38.9 mIoU / 38.4 AP on ADE20K and 52.8 PQ / 56.1 mIoU / 31.9 AP on Cityscapes, surpassing the previous best (Osprey-7B: 46.6 PQ / 50.2 PQ) by at least 2 and 0.6 absolute points across all tasks on ADE20K and Cityscapes, respectively. The Lexical Alignment Curve (LAC) evaluation further shows OPAL outperforming all baselines at every lexical level (exact, synonym, hyponym, meronym). Additionally, OPAL reduces output variance by nearly 50% compared to Osprey-7B, demonstrating that the contrastive training objective improves robustness.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OPAL outperforms all seven baselines across every metric on both ADE20K and Cityscapes. Against the closest competitor Osprey-7B (the architecture OPAL builds upon), OPAL gains +2.2 PQ on ADE20K and +2.6 PQ on Cityscapes, while also reducing output variance by ~50%. Among MLLM baselines, GPT4RoI-7B is the strongest (44.5 PQ on ADE20K, 39.7 PQ on Cityscapes), trailing OPAL by 4.3 and 13.1 PQ points respectively; Ferret-7B and Shikra-7B lag further behind. Among open-vocabulary segmentation methods, MasQCLIP is the best (38.6 PQ on ADE20K, 43.8 PQ on Cityscapes), still 10.2 and 9.0 PQ points below OPAL, while MaskCLIP and OVSeg are substantially weaker (27.1 and 26.2 PQ on ADE20K). The paper also introduces a simple MLLM+MasQCLIP baseline (42.9 PQ on ADE20K) that, while competitive, is outperformed by OPAL by 5.9 PQ, confirming that the contrastive alignment objective provides meaningful gains over a naive tag-then-segment pipeline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Osprey (Yuan et al., 2024) architecture by adding a contrastive alignment loss between region embeddings and the final language token, positioning OPAL as the first MLLM trained with a joint generative-plus-contrastive objective for open-ended segmentation. More fundamentally, it departs from the established evaluation paradigm that relies on embedding-based similarity scores (Sentence-BERT, CLIPScore) to map free-form descriptions to target categories, arguing that these mappings inherit biases from the underlying embedding models and systematically misassign descriptions. The paper situates open-ended segmentation as the natural successor to open-vocabulary segmentation in the progression from closed-set to fully generative visual recognition, while identifying the evaluation gap as the critical unsolved problem in this lineage.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The demonstration that Sentence-BERT mappings diverge from human judgments by 37.7% on Cityscapes is a significant finding that calls into question the validity of prior open-ended segmentation benchmarks and the relative rankings they produce. The proposed Lexical Alignment Curve (LAC) provides a more interpretable, multi-level evaluation that reveals whether models produce precise or generic descriptions, but its reliance on LLM-constructed noun lists introduces a new dependency that may limit generalizability to domains or vocabularies not well-covered by the mined noun set. Open questions include whether the lexical mapping approach scales to truly open taxonomies (beyond the 19 Cityscapes or 150 ADE20K categories), whether the contrastive loss benefit persists at larger model scales, and whether the evaluation protocol can be extended to video or 3D segmentation settings.
