## Problem

SMOTE is one of the most widely used techniques for handling class imbalance and generating synthetic tabular data, yet its privacy implications have been systematically overlooked. Prevailing evaluation practices—naive distinguishing classifiers and the Distance to Closest Record (DCR) metric—completely fail to detect any leakage, giving a false sense of security. This paper asks whether, why, and how much privacy leakage occurs when SMOTE is used as either a data augmentation method or a standalone synthetic data generator.

## Input / Output

Both attacks require only a single dataset (augmented D_aug for DistinSMOTE, synthetic D_syn for ReconSMOTE) plus knowledge that SMOTE was applied and its parameters k (number of neighbors) and r (imbalance ratio), which can be approximated from the released data. No shadow models, repeated queries, public reference data, or aggregate statistics are needed. DistinSMOTE outputs a binary label (real vs. synthetic) for every minority record in the augmented set, while ReconSMOTE outputs a set of reconstructed real minority records with perfect precision and recall that approaches 1 for r ≥ 20 and k = 5.

## Method

Both attacks exploit SMOTE's core geometric property: every synthetic point lies strictly on the line segment between two real minority endpoints, and under the global non-collinearity assumption no three real points are collinear. DistinSMOTE starts from the convex hull of minority records and iteratively searches for collinear triplets among neighbors; the middle point of any such triplet is pruned as synthetic, while endpoints are retained as real, achieving perfect precision and recall. ReconSMOTE operates solely on synthetic data by detecting lines from collinear point sets, computing their pairwise intersections, and retaining only intersections supported by at least three distinct lines—guaranteeing perfect precision since synthetic points lie on exactly one SMOTE line while real endpoints lie on three or more. Theoretical analysis (Theorems 1–4) proves perfect precision for both attacks and provides a lower bound on ReconSMOTE's expected recall that grows exponentially with the imbalance ratio, reaching 1 for k=5 and r≥20. Worst-case complexity is O(n²d + n(kr)²), and both attacks run in under three minutes on all experimental datasets.

## Results

DistinSMOTE achieves perfect precision and recall (1.00±0.00) in distinguishing real from synthetic minority records across all eight augmented datasets, regardless of imbalance ratio (r=8.6 to 130). ReconSMOTE achieves perfect precision (1.00±0.00) and an average recall of 0.85±0.01 when reconstructing real minority records from synthetic data, with recall reaching 1.00 for imbalance ratios r≥20. In stark contrast, the prevailing naive distinguishing approach yields only 0.01±0.01 precision, and the DCR-based naive metrics achieve just 0.16±0.10 accuracy, completely failing to detect the leakage. State-of-the-art MIAs achieve 0.68±0.07 AUC on augmented data and 0.93±0.02 AUC on synthetic data, confirming substantial but incomplete leakage detection.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its geometric attacks against four categories of prior approaches. Naive distinguishing (training a Random Forest to separate real from synthetic records) and DCR-based linkability (Giomi et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2021) both completely fail: they achieve 0.01 precision and 0.16 accuracy respectively, missing the leakage entirely because they capture only distributional differences, not record-level geometry. State-of-the-art MIAs (Shokri et al., 2017; Carlini et al., 2022) using MLP classifiers achieve 0.68 AUC on augmented data and 0.93 AUC on synthetic data (via GroundHog, Stadler et al., 2022), confirming substantial leakage but requiring ~20k–60k model trainings per dataset (up to 30 minutes). DistinSMOTE and ReconSMOTE dominate all baselines: they achieve perfect precision (1.00) with minimal assumptions (only access to one dataset plus knowledge of k and r), run in under 3 minutes, and require no shadow models, meta-classifiers, or auxiliary data. The 17% increase in MIA AUC when classifiers are trained on augmented vs. real data further validates that SMOTE amplifies minority-record sensitivity, a gap the naive metrics entirely miss.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the SMOTE algorithm (Chawla et al., 2002) but departs fundamentally from prior work that treated SMOTE as a utility-focused preprocessing step. While standard privacy evaluation tools—naive distinguishing classifiers and the Distance to Closest Record (DCR) metric from Zhao et al. (2021)—have been applied to SMOTE in the context of diffusion model baselines (Kotelnikov et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024; Pang et al., 2024; Mueller et al., 2025), this paper shows those tools are structurally incapable of detecting SMOTE's leakage. The key conceptual departure is replacing learned black-box attacks with a geometric analysis of SMOTE's deterministic linear interpolation, yielding perfect distinguishing (DistinSMOTE) and near-perfect reconstruction (ReconSMOTE) attacks that require only knowledge of the algorithm and its parameters, not shadow models or auxiliary data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that SMOTE is inherently non-private and that DCR-based evaluation gives a false sense of security undermines privacy claims in a recent line of top-tier generative-model papers that use 'SMOTE + DCR' as their privacy benchmark. More broadly, it highlights that the very records SMOTE aims to amplify—minority, often vulnerable individuals—are the most exposed, with regulatory implications (e.g., UK ICO guidance on protecting outlier records). Open questions include whether differential-privacy variants (DP-SMOTE, SMOTE-DP) adequately address the increased sensitivity of minority records, whether region-based variants like G-SMOTE and GISMOTE are truly resistant to MIAs, and how to design practical defenses that preserve SMOTE's utility while preventing geometric reconstruction.
