## Problem

Micro-benchmarking methods promise to reduce LLM evaluation cost by selecting small subsets of full benchmarks, but it remains unclear whether these subsets can reliably rank models as consistently as the full benchmarks they replace, or even outperform random sampling. Existing meta-evaluation measures (mean estimation error, Kendall's tau rank correlation) summarize reliability in the aggregate without revealing which specific model pairs a micro-benchmark can or cannot distinguish, leaving practitioners without guidance on the trade-off between subset size and ranking reliability.

## Input / Output

The method takes a full benchmark dataset (e.g., MMLU-Pro with 12,032 examples), a set of source models with cached per-example predictions on the full benchmark, and a set of target models to evaluate. It produces an agreement curve mapping each accuracy-difference bucket (at 0.5-point resolution, accuracy reported as 0–100%) to the probability that the micro-benchmark preserves the full benchmark's pairwise ranking, plus a single MDAD scalar summarizing the smallest accuracy difference at which agreement reaches 80%. MDAD computation takes on average 2.4 seconds per trial, and the full experimental protocol averages over 50 random partitions of data and models into source/target sets.

## Method

The paper introduces the Minimum Detectable Ability Difference (MDAD), a meta-evaluation measure inspired by statistical power analysis that identifies the smallest performance gap on the full benchmark at which a micro-benchmark correctly ranks model pairs at least 80% of the time. It first computes an agreement function (Eq. 4) that, for each bucket of pairwise accuracy differences on the full benchmark, measures the fraction of target model pairs whose relative ordering is preserved on the micro-benchmark; MDAD (Eq. 5) then reports the centroid of the smallest bucket where this agreement crosses the 0.8 threshold. The authors evaluate six selection strategies—Anchor Points (k-medoids on model-confidence correlations), tinyBenchmarks (IRT-based clustering), stratified sampling by confidence, a DPP-based diversity sampler, uniform random sampling, and subtask-stratified random sampling—across four benchmarks (MMLU, MMLU-Pro, BBH, GPQA) at subset sizes from 10 to 1000 examples. Key findings: at 10 examples no method reliably distinguishes models less than 3.5 points apart on MMLU-Pro; Anchor Points achieves the lowest MDAD at extreme reductions but stagnates at larger sizes due to imbalanced cluster sizes; and once ~250 examples are selected, random sampling matches all other methods in MDAD, making it a competitive and simpler alternative.

## Results

The paper introduces MDAD (Minimum Detectable Ability Difference), the minimum accuracy gap on the full benchmark at which a micro-benchmark correctly ranks a model pair ≥80% of the time. With only 10 examples, no method can reliably distinguish models differing by fewer than 3.5 points on MMLU-Pro, 3 points on MMLU, 6 points on BBH, or 6.5 points on GPQA. At 250 examples, all methods (including uniform random sampling) achieve MDAD ≤ 2, making random sampling competitive. For 32 instruction-tuned 8B models on MMLU-Pro with 25 examples, 51% of pairwise comparisons fall below the MDAD threshold and are unlikely to be preserved.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Anchor Points (Vivek et al., 2024) achieves the lowest MDAD at 10 examples across all benchmarks (e.g., MDAD 6 on BBH vs. ~15 for others) but paradoxically has the *highest* MDAD at 1,000 examples due to extreme cluster-size imbalance in its k-medoids clustering. tinyBenchmarks (Polo et al., 2024) is second-best at small sizes, with MDAD dropping from 16 to 1 on BBH as examples grow from 10 to 1,000, and matches random sampling (MDAD 2) at 500 examples on MMLU. Stratified sampling by confidence (Fogliato et al., 2024) shows similar Kendall's tau rank correlation to random sampling at ≤100 examples but achieves lower MDAD, revealing a finer-grained advantage invisible to aggregate rank correlation. The Diversity method (Bardenet et al., 2024) performs comparably to other selection methods but experiences a larger MDAD increase (1.12 pts) when generalizing to held-out subtask draws, versus only 0.37 pts for Anchor Points. BenTo (Zhao et al., 2025), which selects whole subtasks, achieves MDAD 1.5±0.5 on MMLU—identical to uniform random sampling (1.5±0.5)—confirming that even subtask-level selection offers no reliability advantage over random at that scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the micro-benchmarking evaluation literature (Vivek et al., 2024; Polo et al., 2024; Perlitz et al., 2024) by introducing MDAD, a meta-evaluation measure adapted from statistical power analysis (Card et al., 2020) that quantifies the minimum performance difference a micro-benchmark can reliably detect. Unlike prior meta-evaluations that use mean estimation error (pointwise) or Kendall's tau (aggregate rankings), MDAD provides a fine-grained, pairwise view of which model comparisons are preserved as a function of their accuracy gap. The paper positions itself as complementary to, rather than contradictory with, existing measures, and systematically compares four non-random selection methods (Anchor Points, tinyBenchmarks/IRT, stratified confidence sampling, DPP-based diversity) against two random sampling baselines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides practitioners with a concrete, interpretable criterion (MDAD) for choosing micro-benchmark size based on the granularity of model distinctions they need, rather than relying on one-size-fits-all recommendations. The finding that random sampling becomes competitive with all sophisticated selection methods at ~250 examples has direct cost implications for LLM evaluation pipelines. Open questions include extending MDAD to open-ended generation metrics, using it to actively guide data selection, and understanding whether the results hold for benchmarks with very different task structures or for models with substantially different architectures.
