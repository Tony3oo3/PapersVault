## Problem

Existing LLM-based autoformalization methods can produce syntactically valid formal code but lack reliable automated mechanisms to verify that the formalization semantically preserves the meaning of the original natural language statement. Moreover, prior approaches cannot iteratively refine their output using detected semantic inconsistencies as corrective feedback, leaving a gap between generating a formalization and ensuring it is both semantically faithful and syntactically correct without human intervention.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263008/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a natural language math problem, a set of 8–16 domain-specific few-shot examples, and the LoC-Decomp template as input, and produces structured Lean 4 code organized into six sequential declaration segments (environment, auxiliary types, mathematical system, auxiliary functions/predicates, constraints, and problem statement). The output is a predicate named `problem_statement` rather than a theorem with a `sorry` placeholder, accompanied by a three-level semantic consistency verdict (Fully Consistent, Consistent without Loss of Generality, or Inconsistent) and, if inconsistent, targeted rectification suggestions. A lightweight parser enforces template compliance beyond standard Lean 4 syntax, and the framework is adaptable to Coq and Isabelle with minor modifications.

## Method

Loc-Decomp integrates three modules in a closed-loop pipeline: (1) a CoT-inspired Lean 4 template that decomposes formalization into six semantically meaningful segments grounded in Discourse Representation Theory, with a theoretical expressiveness guarantee (Theorem 3.1); (2) an Automatic Semantic Consistency Checker (ASCC) that employs a divide-conquer-merge back-translation strategy—splitting the formal code into self-contained segments, translating each independently, then merging into a cohesive natural language description—to detect subtle semantic discrepancies at both segment-level and holistic level; and (3) a joint Syn-Sem iterative rectification loop that alternates between semantic correction (driven by ASCC discrepancy reports) and syntactic correction (driven by Lean 4 compiler errors), with inner semantic and syntactic loops forming Sem-Syn Iter Units nested within an outer loop of up to N units per round, repeated for up to M rounds. The entire pipeline is prompt-based with no parameter training, relying on in-context learning and structured feedback to progressively refine the formalization until it passes both semantic and syntactic checks consecutively.

## Results

Using LLM-as-a-judge evaluation, LoC-Decomp achieves 96.60% on MATH-500, 97.54% on miniF2F, 93.09% on PutnamBench, and 73.57% on ProofNet. The PutnamBench result represents an 18-percentage-point improvement over the previous SOTA SFT-based model (DeepSeek-Prover-V2-671B at 75.41%). On the ASCC-3-MV metric with iterative rectification (3 rounds), the method reaches 84.00% on MATH-500 and 90.16% on miniF2F with DeepSeek-V3, up from 63.20% and 77.25% at pass@1 without iteration. Human evaluation on the challenging MATH-Level5-50 subset yields an 84% pass rate after iterative refinement, compared to 52% for the basic baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DeepSeek-Prover-V2-671B (the strongest SFT-based baseline), LoC-Decomp wins on three of four datasets: MATH-500 (96.60 vs. 84.60, +12.0 pp), miniF2F (97.54 vs. 87.18, +10.4 pp), and PutnamBench (93.09 vs. 75.41, +17.7 pp), but loses on ProofNet (73.57 vs. 83.10, −9.5 pp). The ProofNet deficit is attributed to a low compilation pass rate (~74%) caused by unresolved Mathlib type-class instances, which the authors note is an orthogonal issue addressable via RAG augmentation. Against Goedel-Formalizer-V2-32B, LoC-Decomp wins on all four datasets, with the largest margin on PutnamBench (+14.4 pp) and the smallest on ProofNet (+2.2 pp). In the BEq (bidirectional equivalence) evaluation on miniF2F, LoC-Decomp with KIMI-K2 also outperforms Goedel-Formalizer-V2-32B (62.82 vs. 54.91), confirming the advantage under a stricter formal-verification metric.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM-based autoformalization line initiated by Wu et al. (2022) and Azerbayev et al. (2023), which established prompt-based and SFT-based approaches for converting natural language math into formal code. It builds on Li et al. (2024, NeurIPS) by replacing their holistic back-translation verification with a divide-conquer-merge strategy that decomposes Lean 4 code into semantically self-contained segments for finer-grained consistency checking. It also extends FIMO (Liu et al., 2023), which first integrated semantic and syntactic feedback but required manual involvement, by making the entire loop fully automated, and goes beyond KELPS (Zhang et al., 2025) by actually leveraging its concept-decomposition idea inside the semantic checker rather than only as an intermediate representation. The LoC-Decomp template, grounded in Discourse Representation Theory, provides a theoretical expressiveness guarantee (Theorem 3.1) that the structured decomposition does not reduce Lean 4's expressive power.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Autoformalization remains a critical bottleneck for trustworthy LLM-based mathematical reasoning, and this paper addresses two gaps that prior methods left open: reliable automated semantic consistency checking and iterative self-correction without human intervention. The 18-percentage-point improvement over the previous SOTA on PutnamBench (93.09% vs. ~75%) and the consistent gains across three different LLMs (DeepSeek-V3, KIMI-K2, Qwen3-235B) suggest the framework is model-agnostic and practically useful. However, the notably lower performance on ProofNet (73.57% vs. DeepSeek-Prover-V2's 83.10%) reveals a dependency on Mathlib retrieval that the current method does not handle, pointing to RAG integration as a natural next step. The small human-evaluation set (50 problems) and the acknowledged sensitivity to prompt design across models are limitations that temper the generalizability claims.
