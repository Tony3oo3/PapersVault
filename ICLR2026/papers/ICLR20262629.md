## Problem

Deep neural networks produce overconfident predictions on out-of-distribution inputs, yet existing training-time mitigation methods construct proxy OOD samples using heuristics (noise, corruption, class mixing) that lack theoretical grounding and fail to explicitly target the semantically ambiguous regions where overconfidence is most likely to arise. This paper identifies a principled geometric signal—singularities in semi-discrete optimal transport—that marks precisely these structurally unstable zones.

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard RGB images (28×28 for MNIST, 32×32 for CIFAR-10/100/SVHN/FMNIST, 224×224 for ImageNet) and produces class labels with calibrated confidence scores. During training, an autoencoder encodes images into a compact latent space (256-dim for small images, 1024-dim for ImageNet), where OTIS samples are synthesized near singular boundaries and decoded back to pixel space; each training batch mixes 50% ID samples (cross-entropy) with 50% OTIS (confidence suppression loss).

## Method

The framework first encodes ID training samples into a latent space via a convolutional autoencoder, then solves a semi-discrete optimal transport problem between a continuous base distribution (Gaussian or uniform) and the discrete set of latent embeddings, yielding a power-diagram partition of the latent space into Laguerre cells. Adjacent cell boundaries are scored by the angular deviation between their associated support points; the top-ranked boundaries (e.g., top 10%) are identified as transport singularities marking semantically ambiguous regions. For each selected boundary, the method computes inverse-distance-weighted interpolation between the Monte Carlo-estimated centroids of the two neighboring cells, applies a smoothed transport extension to reduce aliasing, and decodes the resulting latent vector back to the input space to produce an OTIS sample. These OTIS samples are then used during training with a uniform confidence suppression loss (encouraging equal softmax probability across all K classes), guiding the classifier to produce low-confidence, calibrated predictions in structurally uncertain regions without sacrificing ID accuracy.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, OTIS reduces OOD mean maximum confidence (MMC) to 13.18% on SVHN (vs. 84.22% for the untrained baseline and 72.48% for CEDA), while maintaining a competitive test error of 7.52% and ID MMC of 95.46%. On CIFAR-100, OOD MMC drops to 9.30% on SVHN (vs. 48.27% baseline), with TE of 23.56%. On ImageNet (ResNet-50), OTIS achieves the highest ID MMC (88.93%) and the lowest OOD MMC on most benchmarks (e.g., 31.01% on iNaturalist, 38.81% on Textures), with FPR95 of 49.16% on iNaturalist and 63.96% on OpenImage-O.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OTIS dramatically outperforms all five baselines on OOD MMC across nearly every ID/OOD configuration. On CIFAR-10→SVHN, OTIS achieves 13.18% OOD MMC versus 71.62% (CEDA/ACET), 82.16% (CCUs), 72.48% (CODES), 72.35% (VOS), 55.82% (OE), and 76.52% (CCUd) — a reduction of 58–69 percentage points over the strongest competitor. On CIFAR-100→SVHN, the gap is even wider: 9.30% for OTIS versus 52.57% for OE (the best baseline). On adversarial shifts, OTIS achieves 8.94% OOD MMC on CIFAR-100→Adv. Noise, far below VOS (77.58%) and CODES (27.85%), though ACET and CCUs reach 1.00% on this particular extreme case. On ImageNet, OTIS attains the highest ID MMC (88.93%) and the lowest OOD MMC on OpenImage-O (34.96%), iNaturalist (31.01%), and Textures (38.81%), outperforming CEDA, ACET, CODES, and VOS, at the cost of a modestly higher test error (26.43% vs. 23.87% baseline). The method also improves FPR95 substantially (e.g., 1.21% on CIFAR-10→SVHN vs. 59.51% baseline) and ECE on CIFAR-10 (1.88×10⁻² vs. 3.77×10⁻² baseline), confirming that confidence suppression on OTIS does not degrade in-distribution calibration.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research threads: training-time OOD overconfidence mitigation (CEDA, ACET, CCU, CODES, VOS, OE) and semi-discrete optimal transport geometry (An et al., 2020; Mérigot, 2011). Prior methods construct proxy OOD samples via heuristics—noise injection, input corruption, class mixing, or external datasets—without a principled account of *where* in input space overconfidence is most likely to arise. The key departure here is using the singular boundaries of a semi-discrete OT map (where the Brenier potential is non-differentiable) as a geometric criterion for identifying structurally ambiguous regions, then generating samples (OTIS) near those boundaries via an autoencoder. The confidence suppression loss and 50/50 batch training protocol are inherited from CODES (Tang et al., 2021) and CCU (Meinke & Hein, 2020), respectively, so the novelty is concentrated in the sample-construction mechanism rather than the training objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical link between transport singularities and OOD overconfidence, giving a geometric justification for *why* certain regions elicit unwarranted high confidence and *where* to place regularization pressure. The FID analysis (Table 3) is a useful diagnostic: OTIS samples are closest to the ID manifold (FID 2.45) among all compared OOD sample sets while still triggering high confidence, suggesting that overconfidence is not simply a function of distributional distance but of local geometric ambiguity. Open questions include whether the semi-discrete OT formulation scales efficiently to very high-dimensional latent spaces (the ImageNet experiment uses 1024-dim latents with a VGG-16 autoencoder, but the Monte Carlo volume estimation may become costly), whether the angular-deviation singularity score is the optimal criterion, and whether the approach extends naturally to non-convex or non-Euclidean latent geometries. The method also requires a pre-trained autoencoder, adding a dependency that simpler baselines avoid.
