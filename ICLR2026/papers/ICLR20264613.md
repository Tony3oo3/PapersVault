## Problem

How heterogeneous synaptic plasticity mechanisms across different synapse types cooperatively produce functional behavior in realistic cortical circuits remains poorly understood. Prior theoretical, data-driven, and meta-learning approaches have been restricted to small artificial networks, limited rule spaces (typically uniform or excitatory/inhibitory splits), and single-task or single-objective settings, leaving the broader landscape of biologically plausible, heterogeneous plasticity rules unexplored.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264613/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes visual stimuli (grating images with orientations in [60°, 120°] at 0.1° precision, or 8 natural images from ImageNet) presented at 60 ms with 90 ms inter-stimulus grey intervals, along with scalar reward signals that drive a population-shared reward prediction error trace. It produces mathematical formulations of synaptic plasticity rules—weighted sums over up to 25 candidate terms derived from a third-order Taylor expansion of five neural signals (pre/post spikes, eligibility traces, reward trace)—with over 2,645 optimizable parameters distributed across 289 synapse types, plus the resulting change-detection accuracy and six biological validity metrics.

## Method

The paper constructs an interpretable candidate space by expanding five fundamental neural signals (S_pre, S_post, X_pre, X_post, R) up to third order via Taylor expansion, yielding 25 candidate plasticity terms after removing redundancies. Each plasticity rule is a gated, weighted combination of these terms with synapse-type-specific coefficients, neuron-type-specific eligibility and reward trace decay constants, and binary selection gates shared across all synapse types. A custom multi-objective evolutionary algorithm (population of 4,000, 150 generations, built on JAX/EvoX) navigates this high-dimensional space by simultaneously optimizing six competing objectives: cross-domain task accuracy, rule complexity, maximum firing rate, asynchronous firing proportion, and two firing-rate distributional distances against mouse V1 recordings. The rules are applied to a biologically grounded mouse V1 spiking network (17 neuron types, 289 synapse types, 3,000 neurons, GLIF₃-based) with Dale's law and hard weight bounds enforced, and the resulting Pareto-optimal population reveals diverse families of structurally distinct yet functionally equivalent plasticity rules that achieve few-shot learning and scale across network sizes.

## Results

The evolutionary search (population N=4,000, 150 generations) yields 70 Pareto-optimal plasticity rules. The overall best-performing rule (Δw = X_post + S_pre·X_pre + S_post·X_pre + X_post² + X_post·R) achieves 71.86% cross-domain change-detection accuracy, matching biological mouse performance (~73% on natural images, ~60% on gratings). The most prevalent formulation (Δw = S_pre, 48.57% of filtered rules) reaches 65.18% average accuracy with only 1 active term. All discovered rules achieve comparable accuracy within 100 training trials, whereas the Adam baseline requires ~5,000× more samples, and the best rule generalizes across network scales from 1,000 to 5,000 neurons.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the Adam optimizer with a surrogate gradient function (Neftci et al., 2019), which uses global error signals and backpropagation through the spiking V1 model. On the grating change-detection task (Fig. 6), all three representative plasticity rules (Δw = S_pre, Δw = S_pre·X_post, and the overall best 5-term rule) reach their plateau accuracy within the first 100 training trials, while Adam requires approximately 5,000× more samples (~500,000 trials) to attain comparable performance levels. The same pattern holds on the natural-image change-detection task (Fig. S13). The comparison is framed as a data-efficiency analysis rather than a final-accuracy contest: both approaches can converge to similar asymptotic accuracy, but the evolved local plasticity rules achieve it with orders-of-magnitude fewer samples, which the authors interpret as evidence that evolutionary priors embed task-relevant inductive biases into the rule structure, enabling few-shot adaptation analogous to innate biological abilities.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the meta-learning/evolutionary discovery of plasticity rules (Confavreux et al. 2020, 2023, 2025a; Jordan et al. 2021; Najarro & Risi 2020; Tyulmankov et al. 2022) from small artificial networks with uniform or limited-heterogeneity rules to a biologically realistic mouse V1 model with 17 neuron types, 289 synapse types, and over 2,600 optimizable parameters per rule. It builds directly on the Allen Brain Atlas-based V1 model (Billeh et al. 2020; Chen et al. 2022) and the GLIF3 neuron model (Teeter et al. 2018), modifying the latter for plasticity compatibility. The key methodological departure is the use of truncated Taylor expansion to construct an interpretable candidate space of 25 plasticity terms, combined with a multi-objective evolutionary algorithm (via EvoX) that simultaneously optimizes task performance and multiple biological plausibility constraints, rather than the single-objective or manually constrained searches used in prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The discovery of computational degeneracy—multiple structurally distinct plasticity rules (from the trivial Δw=S_pre to complex 5-term formulations) achieving comparable behavioral performance—offers a potential explanation for contradictory experimental reports on synaptic mechanisms in the same cortical region. The ~5000× data efficiency advantage over gradient-based Adam (100 vs. ~500,000 training trials) provides a concrete computational account for how evolutionary priors embedded in plasticity rules could underpin innate abilities and few-shot adaptation. Key limitations include restriction to mouse V1 and visual change detection, absence of behavioral-timescale plasticity (BTSP), and evaluation of biological similarity only at the representation level rather than dynamics level, leaving open whether these findings generalize to other sensory modalities, cortical areas, or longer timescales.
