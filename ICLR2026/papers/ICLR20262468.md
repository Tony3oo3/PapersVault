## Problem

LLM-generated math proofs lack a reliable, fine-grained automated evaluator: existing benchmarks focus on final-answer verification, which is insufficient for proof problems where intermediate reasoning errors matter. While LLM-as-a-judge is a natural approach, its application to mathematical proofs is unsettled, and the key design factors—backbone model, available context, rubric construction, and prompting—are poorly understood, making it unclear how to build an evaluator that aligns with expert human grading.

## Input / Output

The evaluator consumes a competition-level math problem statement, a model-generated natural-language proof, and two contextual artifacts: one or more human-written reference solutions and a problem-specific marking scheme (an LLM-generated rubric with additive checkpoints, zero-credit items, and deduction rules). It outputs a single integer score on the 0–7 scale used by premier math competitions, where 0 means fully incorrect and 7 means fully correct. The marking scheme is generated by Gemini-2.5-Pro from the problem and reference solution, and experts treat it as a flexible guide rather than a rigid checklist, allowing valid alternative solution paths to be credited.

## Method

PROOFGRADER is an LLM-as-a-judge evaluator built on the O3 reasoning model, prompted with the reference solution and marking scheme (REF+MS) under a flexible NORM instruction that allows the model to map valid alternative derivations to equivalent checkpoints. Rather than training a dedicated reward model, the paper searches over a discrete design space—six backbone models, four context configurations, three instruction styles, and three workflow types (single-pass, ensembling, staged Binary+Errors→Fine-Grained)—and finds that a strong backbone with rich context plus simple median ensembling over five independent runs achieves the best alignment with expert scores (MAE 0.926). The key insight is that the marking scheme contributes the majority of the context gain over reference solutions alone, and that ensembling primarily reduces variance rather than shifting the mean. In a downstream best-of-n selection task at n=16, PROOFGRADER achieves an average expert score of 4.14/7, closing 78% of the gap between a naive binary evaluator (2.48) and the human oracle (4.62).

## Results

PROOFGRADER (O3 ensemble with REF+MS context and NORM instructions) achieves a MAE of 0.926 and WTA≤1 of 77.7% against expert 0–7 scores, outperforming the best single-pass O3 evaluator (MAE 0.964) and all other backbone/context combinations. In the downstream best-of-n selection task at n=16, PROOFGRADER selects proofs with an average expert score of 4.14/7, closing 78% of the gap between a naive binary evaluator (2.48) and the human oracle (4.62). The marking scheme (MS) contributes the majority of the context gain: O3 with MS alone (MAE 1.069) substantially outperforms O3 with no context (MAE 1.680), while adding the reference solution (REF+MS) provides a further small improvement for the strongest backbone.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the PairJudge RM knockout-tournament baseline (Liu et al., 2025b), PROOFGRADER consistently outperforms both Knockout (single-elimination) and Tournament (all-pairs) selection across all values of n in the best-of-n experiment (Figure 3b), while requiring only O(n) independent scoring calls versus O(n) or O(n²) pairwise comparisons. In the best-of-n task at n=16, PROOFGRADER achieves an average selected-proof score of 4.14/7, compared to 2.48 for a binary (correct/incorrect) O3 evaluator and 4.62 for the human oracle, closing 78% of the oracle gap. Within the single-pass evaluator comparison (Table 1), O3 with REF+MS dominates all other model–context combinations on MAE (0.964) and WTA≤1 (76.5%), with GPT-5+MS a close second (MAE 1.018). Ensembling five O3 runs further reduces MAE from 0.964 to 0.926 and raises WTA≤1 from 76.5% to 77.7% (Table 4). Staged Binary+Errors→Fine-Grained pipelines improve mid-tier backbones (O4-MINI MAE 1.367→1.245) but degrade the strongest O3 backbone (MAE 0.964→1.065), confirming that simple ensembling is preferable for strong models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the LLM-as-a-judge paradigm (Zheng et al., 2023; Sheng et al., 2025) for the specific domain of natural-language math proof evaluation, where prior work has relied on binary annotations (Dekoninck et al., 2025) or manual expert grading (Petrov et al., 2025). It departs from the reward-model literature (Gao et al., 2022; Frick et al., 2024) by targeting fine-grained 0-7 scoring rather than scalar reward prediction, and from comparison-based selection methods (Liu et al., 2025b) by showing that independent scoring with rich context outperforms pairwise tournament approaches. The paper carves new ground as the first systematic empirical study of the evaluator design space (backbone, context, instructions, workflow) for proof grading, rather than proposing a single novel architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PROOFBENCH and PROOFGRADER provide the field with the first reliable automated fine-grained proof evaluator, closing 78% of the gap to human oracle in best-of-n selection and opening a path toward using proof quality as an RL reward signal for training proof generators. Key limitations include reliance on closed-source backbones (O3), scope restricted to olympiad-style proofs (not research-level or educational), and assessment of mathematical correctness only (no readability or elegance). Open questions include whether the evaluator can be integrated into training loops, whether open-source models can close the gap, and whether the methodology extends to longer, multi-page research proofs.
