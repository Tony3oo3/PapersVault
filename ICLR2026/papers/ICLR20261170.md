## Problem

While base LLMs are known to exhibit next-token calibration, it remains unclear whether they can meaningfully assess confidence in the semantic meaning of their open-ended responses, and no principled mechanism existed to explain when or why such semantic calibration emerges from standard next-token-prediction pretraining. Empirical observations of calibration behavior across models, post-training procedures, and inference strategies lacked a unifying theoretical account.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261170/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a question prompt (with 5 few-shot examples) and a pretrained LLM, then samples M=50 responses at temperature T=1. A semantic collapsing function B—implemented via string parsing plus an LLM judge for semantic equivalence—maps each raw generation to a finite set of semantic classes, yielding an empirical distribution over classes. Semantic confidence is the probability of the plurality class and semantic accuracy is whether that class matches the ground truth; calibration is quantified via SmoothECE (smECE) and visualized as kernel-smoothed reliability diagrams.

## Method

The paper introduces B-calibration, a notion of calibration parameterized by a collapsing function B that groups semantically equivalent outputs into classes, transforming the LLM into a multi-class classifier. The core theoretical contribution establishes an equivalence between B-confidence-calibration and local loss optimality with respect to a specific perturbation family W_B (Thm. 6), then proves that if the LLM can predict its own distribution over semantic classes before generating (i.e., its intermediate B-confidences are easy to represent), then W_B is implementable as a shallow circuit on top of the model (Thm. 9). This yields the main heuristic: a base LLM trained with cross-entropy loss will be semantically calibrated when a small LoRA adapter can easily learn to predict the model's own semantic class distribution from the question alone. The theory produces three testable predictions—base models are calibrated, instruction-tuning (RLHF/DPO) breaks calibration, and chain-of-thought reasoning breaks calibration—all validated across 650+ experiments on Qwen, Gemini, Mistral, and Llama models from 0.5B to 72B parameters.

## Results

Across over 650 evaluation experiments (Qwen, Gemini, Mistral, Llama models from 0.5B to 72B, on 6 datasets), base LLMs in non-CoT settings (concise and sentence response styles) are remarkably well-calibrated, with low SmoothECE (smECE) values and reliability diagrams closely tracking the diagonal. Calibration does not depend significantly on model size: even models ≤1B are well-calibrated. In contrast, instruction-tuned models are typically overconfident (curves below the diagonal), and chain-of-thought reasoning breaks calibration for both base and instruct models on math datasets (GSM8K, OpenMathInstruct, MATH500). A LoRA learnability probe confirms the theoretical prediction: configurations where the model can easily predict its own semantic class distribution (low KL gap) also exhibit low calibration error.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external calibration methods but instead compares different model configurations and training paradigms to test theoretical predictions. Base models (trained with cross-entropy loss) are well-calibrated across all non-CoT settings, while instruction-tuned models (RLHF, DPO) are consistently overconfident, confirming that post-training with non-proper losses breaks calibration. Within the Mistral-7B lineage, the DPO-finetuned model (zephyr-7b-dpo-full) is significantly more miscalibrated than both the base model and the SFT-only model (zephyr-7b-sft-full), isolating DPO's improper loss as the culprit. Chain-of-thought reasoning breaks calibration even for base models on math tasks: base models are calibrated when answering immediately (concise/sentence) but become underconfident when allowed to reason step-by-step. The LoRA learnability probe (Figure 6) provides a quantitative validation: models with low KL gap to the optimal semantic distribution predictor (Qwen2.5-3B, 7B, 14B in concise/sentence settings) are well-calibrated, while high-KL configurations (CoT, 0.5B sentence) are miscalibrated, matching the theoretical prediction of Claim 10.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the calibration–local-loss-optimality connection established by Błasiok et al. (2023, 2024) from binary classifiers to the autoregressive LLM setting, using the weighted-calibration formalism of Gopalan et al. (2024) to handle the multi-class, sequence-level structure. It formalizes a new parameterized notion (B-calibration) that subsumes prior sampling-based confidence measures such as semantic entropy (Farquhar et al., 2024) and empirical semantic confidence (Lamb et al., 2025), while providing the first principled theoretical mechanism explaining *why* semantic calibration emerges from standard next-token pretraining. The work departs from prior empirical studies of LLM calibration (Kadavath et al., 2022; OpenAI, 2023) by moving from observation to explanation, and from next-token calibration to a semantic-level framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first unified theoretical account of when and why LLMs are semantically calibrated, yielding three concrete, experimentally validated predictions (base models are calibrated; instruction-tuning breaks it; CoT breaks it) that directly inform practitioners choosing models and prompting strategies for uncertainty quantification. The LoRA-based learnability probe offers a practical, low-cost diagnostic for predicting calibration before deployment. Key limitations include the restriction to confidence-calibration (not full calibration), the computational cost of sampling-based confidence estimation, evaluation on only six datasets, and the fact that the core mechanism relies on an informal 'easy-to-learn' assumption (Claim 16) that is only partially justified theoretically. Open questions include whether other calibration types (verbalized, top-label) emerge via different mechanisms, and how to make semantic confidence estimation computationally efficient enough for real-time use.
