## Problem

MoE LLMs rely on a superficial safety mechanism where harmful inputs are routed to specific safety-critical experts, but fine-tuning causes routing decisions for harmful inputs to drift substantially from the safety-aligned model, undermining safety even under benign fine-tuning. Existing defenses against harmful fine-tuning (HFT) attacks are designed for monolithic architectures and fail to prevent this routing-specific drift, leaving MoE LLMs particularly vulnerable.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a safety-aligned MoE LLM, a task-specific fine-tuning dataset (e.g., 5k SAMSum or SQL samples mixed with 500 harmful BeaverTails samples), and a small set of 100 harmful instructions used exclusively for the regularization objective. It produces a fine-tuned MoE LLM that preserves task utility (within ~1% degradation) while maintaining safety-aligned routing. The regularization is applied only to routing weights at the last token of each harmful instruction across all transformer layers, and the aligned model's routing weights are precomputed once to avoid redundant forward passes during training.

## Method

SAFEMOE introduces a KL-divergence regularization that penalizes the gap between the fine-tuned model's routing weight distribution and the safety-aligned model's routing distribution on harmful inputs, directly constraining safety routing drift during fine-tuning. A temperature parameter τ sharpens the routing distribution (smaller τ focuses regularization on top-ranked safety-critical experts), enabling tighter control over which experts process harmful tokens. To avoid the computational cost of joint optimization, SAFEMOE employs a bi-level greedy strategy that alternates between standard SFT gradient steps and periodic regularization steps every T_reg steps, incurring only ~2% training overhead. The approach is architecture-aware by design: rather than constraining generic parameter drift (as monolithic-LLM defenses do), it targets the specific MoE routing mechanism that underpins the model's safety, making it effective across diverse MoE configurations from 7B to 141B parameters.

## Results

On OLMoE fine-tuned on SAMSum, SAFEMOE reduces the harmfulness score (HS) from 62.0 (vanilla fine-tuning) to 5.0 while maintaining fine-tuning accuracy (FA) within 0.4% (48.9 vs. 49.3). On DeepSeek V2 (SQL task), HS drops from 72.0 to 4.0, and on Qwen1.5 MoE (SAMSum), HS reaches 0. Across all eight MoE LLMs tested (7B–141B parameters), SAFEMOE consistently achieves the lowest HS among all methods with ≤1% FA degradation and only ~2% training time overhead.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SAFEMOE outperforms all five baselines by a wide margin on harmfulness score across every model–task combination. On OLMoE (SAMSum), SAFEMOE achieves HS=5.0 versus SafeInstr (46.0), Lisa (21.0), SaLoRA (24.0), Antidote (40.0), and SafeDelta (13.0). The gap is especially stark on DeepSeek V2, where SaLoRA and Antidote fail almost entirely (HS=66.0 and 70.0 on SAMSum, 74.0 and 62.0 on SQL—comparable to or worse than vanilla fine-tuning), while SAFEMOE reduces HS to 1.0 and 4.0 respectively. SafeDelta is the closest baseline on OLMoE (HS=13.0 on SAMSum) but still trails SAFEMOE by 8 points and degrades FA more substantially. The paper attributes the baselines' failure to their monolithic-architecture assumptions: they constrain or prune all parameters uniformly, whereas MoE LLMs dynamically activate only a subset of experts, making parameter-level defenses ineffective at preserving the routing decisions that gate safety-critical experts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the safe fine-tuning literature (SafeInstr, Lisa, SaLoRA, Antidote, SafeDelta) from monolithic transformers to the MoE architecture, where safety is mediated by a routing mechanism rather than uniformly distributed parameters. It builds on the recent discovery that MoE LLMs rely on a small set of 'safety-critical experts' (Lai et al., 2025; Fayyaz et al., 2025) but departs from prior defenses by identifying routing drift as the specific failure mode and introducing a KL-divergence regularization on gating weights for harmful inputs. The bi-level greedy optimization strategy repurposes the standard alternating-update idea to keep the regularization overhead minimal without requiring simultaneous optimization of both losses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As MoE LLMs (gpt-oss, Llama 4, Qwen3, DeepSeek-R1) become the dominant architecture for frontier models and fine-tuning APIs proliferate, the finding that even benign fine-tuning disrupts safety-critical routing exposes a practical vulnerability that current defenses cannot address. The method's 2% overhead and compatibility with both LoRA and full fine-tuning make it immediately deployable in commercial fine-tuning services. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to non-MoE architectures, whether safety-critical expert identification remains stable across model updates, and how well it defends against adaptive attackers who can partially negate the regularization (the paper shows HS still rises to 32.0 under adaptive attack).
