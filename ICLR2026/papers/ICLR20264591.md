## Problem

Jailbreak prompts that bypass one LLM's safety training often also bypass others', but the underlying mechanism remains unclear: is transferability a fragile artifact of shared safety-tuning quirks, a family-specific coincidence, or a deeper property of how models represent information? This paper asks whether representational similarity between models causally drives jailbreak transfer, rather than merely correlating with it.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264591/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The evaluation pipeline consumes 10,329 adversarial inputs (33 jailbreak strategies × 313 harmful prompts from StrongREJECT) applied to 20 open-weight LLMs, sampling 10 responses per input at temperature 0.6 and scoring them with the StrongREJECT LLM judge to produce continuous harm scores in [0,1]. Model similarity is computed from hidden representations at layer ⌊0.8L⌋ over 10K Alpaca prompts using a mutual k-NN (k=100) Jaccard metric. The distillation protocol takes a teacher's benign Alpaca responses (52K) combined with the student's own refusal completions to AdvBench harmful prompts (5,120 pairs) as SFT training data, producing a single-epoch fine-tuned student model whose transferability is then re-measured against the original source model's jailbreaks.

## Method

The paper establishes a causal link between representational alignment and jailbreak transfer through a two-part design. First, it shows observational evidence that pairwise mutual k-NN similarity of hidden representations (measured on benign Alpaca prompts) predicts symmetric transfer AUROC across all 380 model pairs, with no high-similarity/low-transfer counterexamples. Second, it performs a causal intervention: benign-only distillation, where a student model is SFT-trained on a teacher's responses to benign Alpaca instructions plus the student's own refusal responses to harmful AdvBench prompts, deliberately increasing representational similarity without the student ever seeing the teacher's harmful outputs. This manipulation reliably increases jailbreak transfer from the teacher to the student across all three cross-family pairs tested, and even improves transferability of adaptive GCG attacks optimized on the distilled model. Qualitatively, persona-style jailbreaks (which exploit shared semantic geometry) transfer far more broadly than cipher-based attacks (which rely on idiosyncratic token-level quirks), and benign-only distillation can paradoxically increase a student's safety against certain persona attacks by inheriting the teacher's refusal behavior.

## Results

Across 20 open-weight models and 33 jailbreak strategies (10,329 adversarial inputs total), symmetric transfer AUROC increases monotonically with pairwise model representation similarity (mutual k-NN metric, k=100, ω=0.8), with no high-similarity model pair exhibiting low transfer. Benign-only distillation causally increases model similarity (e.g., Gemma2-27B → Qwen2.5-14B: 0.28 → 0.42, a 50% relative gain; other pairs gain 0.10–0.12) and systematically raises mean transfer scores across all strength thresholds τ ∈ [0.5, 0.9]. Persona-style jailbreaks (pair, aim, pap_*, evil_confidant, refusal_suppression) transfer to 47% of models and 52% of families at strength ≥ 0.3, versus only 8% and 9% for cipher-based attacks; at the stricter threshold ≥ 0.5, the gap narrows to 17% vs 4%. Smaller Qwen2.5 checkpoints (0.5–7B) are the most consistently jailbroken, averaging a mean jailbreak strength of 0.31 across all attacks versus a cross-family median of 0.12.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical study rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so there are no traditional baselines. The key comparative findings are: (1) persona-style jailbreaks are roughly 6× more transferable than cipher-based attacks at a low strength threshold (≥0.3) and ~5× at a moderate threshold (≥0.5), consistent with the hypothesis that natural-language attacks exploit shared representational geometry while cipher attacks rely on idiosyncratic quirks. (2) Benign-only distillation (no harmful responses from the teacher) reliably increases transferability of both passive (StrongREJECT) and active (GCG-based) jailbreaks across all three cross-family teacher-student pairs, with larger distilled models showing dramatically higher relative transferability gains. (3) Surprisingly, distilling from a safer teacher (Llama3.1-8B) into a less-safe student (Qwen2.5-7B) can reduce the student's vulnerability to specific persona-style attacks (e.g., AIM), making the distilled model safer than the teacher on those attacks while simultaneously increasing overall transferability. (4) In-family distillation (e.g., Llama3.1-8B → Llama3.2-3B) has almost no effect on model similarity, suggesting a representational capacity ceiling that cross-family distillation can partially overcome.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper connects two previously separate threads: the Platonic Representation Hypothesis (Huh et al., 2024), which posits that models converge to similar statistical representations regardless of architecture or training data, and the jailbreak transferability literature (Zou et al., 2023b; Meade et al., 2024; Schaeffer et al., 2024), which has demonstrated cross-model transfer of adversarial prompts without explaining its mechanism. The key methodological departure is the use of benign-only knowledge distillation as a causal intervention on representational similarity, repurposing a standard training technique to test whether alignment in representation space is sufficient to transfer vulnerabilities. The evaluation framework builds on StrongREJECT (Souly et al., 2024) for robust multi-sample judging, and the active attack pipeline combines refusal-direction ablation (Arditi et al., 2024) with GCG (Zou et al., 2023b).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By providing causal evidence that representational alignment—not incidental safety-training quirks—drives jailbreak transferability, this work reframes a practical security concern as a fundamental property of how LLMs encode information, with direct implications for defense design (e.g., consistency training on representation geometry, modular external classifiers). The double-edged finding that benign-only distillation can simultaneously increase transferability of some attacks while reducing vulnerability to others (e.g., AIM) complicates the safety calculus of model distillation and fine-tuning in practice. Key limitations include coverage of only three distillation pairs, a single training epoch, text-only decoder-only transformers, and one type of adaptive attack; open questions include whether the effect extends to vision-language models, MoE architectures, and whether consistency training can genuinely harden the shared representational geometry without degrading helpfulness.
