## Problem

CNNs are limited to local receptive fields and are sensitive to geometric variations, while Vision Transformers provide global connectivity but incur quadratic cost, lack spatial inductive bias, and are tied to a fixed patch size. Prior spectral operators (GFNet, FNO) parameterize filters on discrete FFT grids, breaking resolution invariance, and structured continuous operators (S4ND, SNO) rely on axis-aligned constructions that cannot capture oriented or anisotropic patterns in natural signals. The paper asks whether a single operator can simultaneously provide global receptive fields, orientation-aware spatial inductive bias, true resolution invariance, and parameter efficiency.

## Input / Output

SONIC consumes 2D RGB images (e.g., 64×64 SynthShape, 224×224 ImageNet) or 3D medical volumes (KiTS, ACDC, PI-CAI) and produces class labels, per-pixel segmentation masks, or lesion detection scores depending on the task head. The key operational detail is that the input is transformed to the frequency domain via a real FFT, modulated by the learned spectral operator, and inverse-transformed back; because the spectral filters are continuous functions of physical frequency, the same learned parameters are valid at any spatial resolution or pixel spacing without retraining.

## Method

SONIC parameterizes the spectral transfer function Ĥ(ω) as a low-rank superposition of M shared orientation-selective modes: Ĥ_{k,c}(ω) = Σ_m C_{km} T_m(ω) B_{mc}, where B and C are learned complex mixing matrices. Each mode T_m(ω) is a resolvent-type filter inherited from continuous-time LTI state-space models, parameterized by a unit orientation vector v_m, a spectral scale s_m, a complex coefficient a_m (controlling damping and oscillation), and a transverse penalty τ_m that sharpens directional selectivity. This construction yields global receptive fields with an inductive bias for oriented structure, and because all filters are defined as continuous functions of physical frequency (normalized by pixel spacing), the operator is truly resolution-invariant. The parameter count scales as O(M(C+K)) and is independent of spatial resolution, while the forward pass costs O((C+K)N log N + M(C+K)N) via FFT-based computation. A spatial skip projection and pointwise nonlinearity complete each SONIC block, and multiple blocks can be stacked for depth-wise expressivity.

## Results

On ImageNet-1K, SonicNet-S achieves 78.8% Top-1 accuracy with 15.0M parameters and 1.9 GFLOPs, surpassing ResNet-50 (76.1%, 25.6M params, 4.1 GFLOPs) by 2.7 points while using less than half the compute. In 3D medical segmentation, SonicNet matches nnU-Net ResEnc L on KiTS (DSC 88.55 vs. 88.98) and exceeds it on ACDC (DSC 92.02 vs. 91.40) using only 2.59M parameters—an order of magnitude fewer. On the SynthShape robustness benchmark, SonicNet achieves 0.93 under 1.5× rescaling (vs. 0.59 for ConvNet, 0.32 for S4ND) and solves the HalliGalli long-range dependency task perfectly (1.00).

## Baselines & Comparisons

In the drop-in ResNet-50 setting, SONIC (76.7% Top-1) outperforms GFNet (75.1%) by 1.6 points and dramatically surpasses S4ND (57.5%) by 19.2 points, confirming that orientation-aware spectral modes are far more effective than axis-aligned state-space or unstructured frequency masks. As a native backbone, SonicNet-S (78.8%) approaches DeiT-S (79.9%) and GFNet-S (80.0%) at roughly half the FLOPs (1.9 vs. 4.5–4.6 GFLOPs), though it trails by ~1–1.2 points in absolute accuracy. On 3D medical segmentation, SonicNet nearly matches nnU-Net ResEnc L (DSC 88.55 vs. 88.98 on KiTS) while using only 2.59M vs. 31.12M parameters, and exceeds it on ACDC (92.02 vs. 91.40). In external validation on Prostate158 and PROMIS, SonicNet improves AUROC over nnU-Net (0.841 vs. 0.814; 0.687 vs. 0.646) with ~12× fewer parameters, suggesting better out-of-distribution generalisation. On SynthShape, SonicNet is most competitive with NIFF under noise and rescaling but clearly outperforms GFNet (which degrades to 0.33–0.40 under rescaling) and S4ND (0.32–0.49), while both SonicNet and NIFF solve HalliGalli perfectly.

## Relation to Prior Work

SONIC sits at the intersection of three prior lines: discrete spectral operators (GFNet, FNO) that learn filters on a fixed FFT grid; unstructured continuous spectral operators (NIFF, Grabinski et al.) that use MLPs to define frequency responses; and structured state-space models (S4ND) that impose axis-aligned separability. The paper departs from all three by introducing a low-rank factorization through M shared, orientation-selective modes whose transfer functions inherit the resolvent structure of continuous-time LTI systems. This yields a truly resolution-invariant operator (unlike GFNet/FNO, whose coefficients are tied to specific frequency indices) while retaining the directional inductive bias that axis-aligned S4ND constructions lack. The authors explicitly show that restricting SONIC orientations to coordinate axes recovers the S4ND form, positioning their method as a strict generalization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is most visible in 3D medical imaging, where SonicNet matches nnU-Net segmentation quality with roughly 12× fewer parameters, and in high-resolution settings where SONIC's O(N log N) scaling avoids the quadratic cost of self-attention. Key limitations include the repeated FFT/IFFT overhead (nonlinearities must be applied in spatial domain), occasional initialization instabilities across datasets with different physical scales, and potential loss of very fine local structure inherent to global frequency representations. Open questions include whether hybrid spectral-spatial architectures can recover local detail, whether the orientation-aware modes transfer to video or other anisotropic modalities, and how to reduce the constant-factor compute overhead relative to plain convolutions.
