## Problem

Existing game benchmarks for LLM agents suffer from three critical gaps: they rely on text-only or 2D-grid simulators rather than complex real video games, they provide insufficient assessment of agentic modules (self-reflection, memory, tool use) essential to complex gameplay, and they lack fine-tuning datasets needed to adapt pre-trained LLMs into effective gaming agents. These limitations hinder both the evaluation of LLMs on realistic long-horizon decision-making and the practical deployment of LLM agents in the game industry.

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes structured text game states (pre-processed to hide irrelevant information, with object positions extracted via YOLOv11 for fighting games or 2D pattern matching for Super Mario) and optionally RGB screenshots, combined with agentic strategy prompts that specify the module sequence (e.g., reflection → planning → action). Outputs include discrete or code-based game actions, normalized scores per game, Elo ratings computed via Bradley-Terry optimization for competitive play, and an ~11k-sample fine-tuning dataset where each sample is a triple (X^a, S, Y^a) representing the agentic module prompt, game state, and LLM response, augmented 10× via GPT-4o paraphrasing.

## Method

Orak introduces a plug-and-play evaluation framework built on the Model Context Protocol (MCP), where each of 12 game environments and each agentic module (reflection, planning, skill-management, action inference) operates as an independent MCP server exposing callable tools, enabling LLMs to compose arbitrary agentic workflows without per-game code customization. The benchmark spans 6 major genres (action, adventure, RPG, simulation, strategy, puzzle) with 15 LLMs evaluated under default per-game strategies selected via GPT-4o ablation. A fine-tuning dataset of ~11k expert trajectories (from GPT-4o and o3-mini using reflection-planning-action sequences, filtered to top-scoring runs and augmented with paraphrases) is released for supervised fine-tuning. Evaluation dimensions include per-game leaderboards, pairwise battle arenas with Elo ratings, ablation studies of agentic modules and input modality, and three-tier generalization tests (intra-game, OOD-game, and non-game tasks like Math500 and WebShop) to measure transfer of gameplay meta-knowledge.

## Results

On Orak's 12-game benchmark with default agentic strategies, Gemini-2.5-pro achieves the best average rank of 3.5 across 15 LLMs, ranking first in 5 out of 12 games (e.g., Pokémon Red 83.3%, StarCraft II 100%, Slay the Spire 51.9%). GPT-5 and GPT-4o tie for second with an average rank of 3.6, with GPT-5 excelling in puzzle games (Baba Is You 100%, 2048 10.2%) and GPT-4o leading in StarCraft II (100%) and Darkest Dungeon (93.4%). Proprietary LLMs consistently outperform open-source models: the best open-source model (Llama3.3-70B, avg rank 7.3) trails the best proprietary model by over 3.8 rank positions, and all sub-8B open-source models score near zero on complex games (Pokémon, Minecraft, Stardew Valley, StarCraft II, Slay the Spire).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Orak evaluates 15 LLMs (8 open-source, 7 proprietary) as baselines on a unified 12-game benchmark. Gemini-2.5-pro achieves the best overall average rank (3.5), outperforming GPT-5 and GPT-4o (both 3.6) and o3-mini (4.0), with particular strengths in Pokémon Red (83.3% vs. GPT-5's 88.9% but GPT-4o's 38.9%), Slay the Spire (51.9% vs. GPT-4o's 23.6%), and StarCraft II (100%, tied with GPT-4o). GPT-5 dominates puzzle games, scoring 100% on Baba Is You and 10.2% on 2048, far ahead of all other models (next best: o3-mini at 73.3% and 25.3% respectively). Among open-source models, Llama3.3-70B (avg rank 7.3) and Qwen2.5-72B (8.8) are the strongest but remain 3.7–5.3 rank positions behind the top proprietary models; all sub-8B open-source models (Llama-3.2-1B/3B, Qwen-2.5-3B/7B, Minitron-4B/8B) score 0.0 on five complex games (Pokémon, Minecraft, Stardew Valley, StarCraft II, Slay the Spire). In the competitive Street Fighter III arena, Minitron-8B surprisingly achieves the highest Elo (1536.8), outperforming GPT-4o (1525.0) and Gemini-2.5-pro (1512.3), suggesting adversarial dynamics shift relative performance compared to single-agent evaluation. Fine-tuning Llama-3.2-3B on Orak trajectories yields non-game generalization gains on WebShop (0.0% → 12.6% on hard) and Math500 (29.6% → 36.1%), while Llama-3.2-1B shows no such transfer, indicating capacity-dependent generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of game-based LLM evaluation (Cradle, V-MAGE, DSGBench, LMGame-Bench) by being the first to cover all six major game genres with real video games, rather than the text-based or 2D-grid environments used by prior benchmarks. It departs from prior agentic workflow research (Voyager, Reflexion, ReAct) by introducing a plug-and-play MCP interface that decouples game environments and agentic modules into independent callable servers, enabling systematic ablation of reflection, planning, and skill-management strategies across games. The fine-tuning component builds on FireAct and AgentTuning for agent-oriented SFT, but is the first to release expert gameplay trajectories spanning diverse genres, encapsulating meta-knowledge on when and how to deploy agentic strategies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Orak fills a clear gap by providing the first unified benchmark with full genre coverage, agentic module ablation, and a fine-tuning dataset, making it a likely standard reference for evaluating and training gaming agents. Key open questions remain: whether the pre-processed text states (rather than raw visual inputs) limit ecological validity, whether the SFT-only fine-tuning approach can be extended to RL for more robust generalization, and whether the latency constraints in real-time games (where all models fail at hard difficulty) will be addressed by faster inference or speculative decoding. The finding that fine-tuning on gameplay transfers to non-game agentic tasks (WebShop, Math500) suggests games may serve as a general training ground for decision-making, but this is demonstrated only on small models and two tasks.
