## Problem

Riemannian zeroth-order optimization requires the exponential map to be defined at randomly perturbed points, but the natural Euclidean metric on many practical manifolds (probability simplex, positive definite matrices, mesh configuration spaces) is geodesically incomplete, so the exponential map is not globally defined. Existing convergence analyses assume geodesic completeness, leaving a fundamental gap: how to perform gradient-free Riemannian optimization when the canonical metric does not guarantee that perturbations stay within the domain of the exponential map.

## Input / Output

The method takes a smooth Riemannian manifold with a possibly geodesically incomplete metric g, a smooth objective function accessible only through function evaluations (no gradient access), and an initial point p₁ on the manifold. It outputs an ε-stationary point under the original metric g_E, with the guarantee that min_{1≤t≤T} E[||∇_{g_E} f(p_t)||²] ≤ ε² in O(d/ε⁴) iterations. A key constraint is that the perturbation stepsize μ must satisfy μ² ≲ √(d/T), and the retraction used in practice must satisfy a second-order regularity condition relative to the exponential map.

## Method

The paper constructs a structure-preserving metric g' = h·g (conformally equivalent to the original) that is geodesically complete while preserving the set of ε-stationary points, following a modified Nomizu–Ozeki construction. Under this complete metric, the classical symmetric two-point estimator (f(exp_p(μv)) − f(exp_p(−μv)))/(2μ) · v is analyzed purely intrinsically, yielding an MSE bound of (1 + μ²κ²)/d · ||∇f||² + O(μ²) that explicitly depends on the sectional curvature κ of the manifold. To sample perturbation directions uniformly from the g'-unit sphere (which is an ellipsoid in local coordinates, not a Euclidean sphere), the paper uses rejection sampling with an acceptance criterion based on the metric matrix A, proving the output is exactly uniform. The resulting SGD convergence analysis achieves O(d/ε⁴) complexity, matching the best-known rate in the geodesically complete setting, and transfers back to the original metric under a compactness condition on the ε-stationary set.

## Results

In the mesh optimization experiment (Helmholtz equation, 20×20 coarse mesh vs. 200×200 ground truth), the structure-preserving metric achieves the lowest final MSE over 20,000 optimization steps while maintaining stable convergence throughout. The unconstrained baseline exhibits unstable fluctuations (notably around step 16,000) due to mesh validity violations; the reversion baseline stalls after ~8,000 steps; and soft projection shows little improvement beyond ~14,000 steps. In the synthetic sampling experiment, rejection sampling (Algorithm 1) consistently outperforms rescaling sampling on both quadratic and logistic objectives, with rescaling even diverging under the logistic loss. The curvature experiment confirms that zeroth-order gradient estimation MSE decreases monotonically as sectional curvature decreases, matching the theoretical bound in Theorem 2.7.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four approaches in the mesh optimization task: (1) Unconstrained optimization, which frequently violates mesh validity and produces unstable loss fluctuations; (2) Soft Projection, which reduces the perturbation stepsize until the movement is valid but converges slowly with minimal improvement after ~14,000 steps; (3) Reversion, which reverts to the original position on invalid steps but stalls entirely after ~8,000 steps; and (4) the proposed Structure-Preserving metric, which twists the Riemannian geometry to guarantee perturbations stay within the domain and achieves the lowest final MSE with stable, monotonic convergence. In the sampling experiment, the proposed rejection sampling (Algorithm 1) is compared against the standard Gaussian-rescaling approach; rejection sampling wins on both quadratic and logistic objectives, while rescaling diverges on the logistic loss. The curvature experiment compares four conformally equivalent metrics with varying sectional curvature, confirming that flatter geometries yield lower estimation MSE as predicted by the (1 + μ²κ²)/d factor in Theorem 2.7.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Riemannian zeroth-order optimization literature (Li et al. 2023a,b; Wang et al. 2021a, 2023; He et al. 2024), which has uniformly assumed geodesic completeness of the underlying metric, to the practically common case where the metric is geodesically incomplete. The construction of structure-preserving metrics builds directly on the Nomizu-Ozeki (1961) existence theorem for complete metrics, modified to enforce conformal equivalence and one-directional ε-stationarity preservation. The convergence analysis adapts the standard Euclidean SGD framework of Mishchenko et al. (2020) to the Riemannian setting, with the key novelty being a purely intrinsic MSE bound for the symmetric two-point estimator that depends on sectional curvature rather than any ambient embedding. The rejection sampling approach for the non-Euclidean unit sphere departs from the standard Gaussian-rescaling technique used in all prior Riemannian zeroth-order work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine practical gap: many natural optimization domains (probability simplex, positive definite matrices, mesh configuration spaces) are geodesically incomplete under their canonical metrics, yet all existing convergence guarantees require completeness. The intrinsic analysis removes the need for the computationally infeasible Nash embedding, making the framework directly applicable. A key limitation is the one-directional ε-stationarity preservation: an ε-stationary point under the constructed metric g' is not automatically one under the original metric g, requiring additional compactness or completeness conditions (Corollary 2.10) to transfer the complexity bound. Open questions include whether the curvature-dependent error term can be tightened, whether the rejection sampling overhead becomes prohibitive in high dimensions, and whether the framework extends to nonsmooth or composite objectives.
