## Problem

Evaluating generalist robot manipulation policies (VLAs) at scale remains fundamentally constrained: real-world testing is labor-intensive, unsafe, and irreproducible, while existing simulation benchmarks like SIMPLER and BEHAVIOR rely on manual scene construction and limited task coverage. There is no scalable, automated, and continuously extensible benchmark that can stress-test policy generalization across diverse environments and controlled perturbations, analogous to how LMarena standardized LLM evaluation through crowdsourced pairwise comparisons.

## Input / Output

The pipeline requires only a single RGB frame from a static-camera robot demonstration video, per-frame joint angle annotations, a language task description, and the robot's URDF file—no multi-view captures, fiducial markers, or manual calibration are needed. It outputs a complete physics-based simulated environment (3D textured object meshes with inferred mass/friction, calibrated camera-to-robot pose, inpainted background, and tuned PD controller gains) that can be perturbed along background, color, and object-placement axes. For evaluation, the system produces per-frame VLM task progress scores (mean over the final 30% of frames) and a global Bradley-Terry policy ranking derived from over 8,500 crowdsourced pairwise human preference judgments.

## Method

The real-to-sim translation pipeline operates in five automated stages: (1) camera-robot calibration via differentiable rendering of a joint-angle-conditioned 3D Gaussian robot model, optimizing the 6-DoF camera pose by minimizing a composite loss of RGB pixel difference, optical flow consistency, and DINOv2 feature alignment; (2) object extraction using Gemini for segmentation, InvSR for super-resolution, and Hunyuan-3D for image-to-textured-mesh generation; (3) object pose recovery by rendering multiple synthetic views of each mesh, matching 2D keypoints via MINIMA, lifting to 3D using MoGE monocular depth calibrated against the robot's known geometry, and solving for the rigid transform via SVD; (4) background generation via LaMa inpainting; and (5) PD gain identification via simulated annealing in the Genesis physics engine to match simulated end-effector trajectories to the video. Evaluation couples automated VLM scoring (Gemini 2.5 Pro prompted with frames plus privileged object/robot state) with double-blind pairwise human preference comparisons aggregated through a Bradley-Terry model with sandwich variance confidence intervals, enabling both absolute progress measurement and relative policy ranking across in-distribution (BridgeSim) and out-of-distribution (DROIDSim, RH20TSim) environments.

## Results

Across 8,749 pairwise human preference comparisons in BridgeSim, the Bradley-Terry global ranking places π₀ and X-VLA at the top, followed by CogAct, RoboVLM, Octo, and SpatialVLA, with human and VLM-derived rankings matching exactly. In out-of-distribution RH20TSim, RoboVLM achieves 19.05% VLM progress score while X-VLA scores 0.00%, revealing severe cross-dataset generalization failure. Under systematic perturbations (background change, color shift, object pose randomization), all six policies degrade, with weaker-backbone models like Octo showing the largest drops. Compared to SIMPLER's four hand-crafted environments, all VLAs score substantially higher on SIMPLER than on BridgeSim's 70 environments, suggesting SIMPLER overestimates true policy performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates six open-source VLAs—π₀, X-VLA, CogAct, RoboVLM, Octo, and SpatialVLA—under a unified protocol of VLM progress scoring and crowdsourced pairwise human preferences. In the in-distribution BridgeSim setting, π₀ and X-VLA clearly outperform all other baselines on both VLM scores and human preferences, with the two rankings matching exactly. However, cross-dataset generalization is the critical differentiator: in RH20TSim, RoboVLM (19.05%) dramatically outperforms every other model including X-VLA (0.00%), while in DROIDSim all policies degrade relative to BridgeSim. Under perturbations, π₀ and X-VLA (with stronger VLM backbones) are more robust to color shifts than Octo, but all policies degrade under background and object-pose changes, indicating overfitting to training scene configurations. Compared to SIMPLER (Li et al., 2024c), which uses only four hand-crafted Bridge scenes, BridgeSim's 70 environments yield substantially lower scores for all policies, demonstrating that SIMPLER's limited coverage overestimates generalist capability. A single real-world validation task ('Put the carrot in the plate') confirmed sim-to-real consistency: RoboVLM and SpatialVLA succeeded in both domains while Octo failed in both.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the LMarena/Chatbot Arena paradigm (Chiang et al., 2024) of crowdsourced pairwise preference evaluation, transposing it from LLMs to robot manipulation policies, and on DR-Robot (Liu et al., 2024b) for differentiable rendering-based camera calibration. It departs from the closest prior sim-based evaluation efforts—SIMPLER (Li et al., 2024c) and BEHAVIOR (Li et al., 2024)—by fully automating the real-to-sim translation pipeline (using Gemini for segmentation, Hunyuan-3D for mesh generation, MoGE for depth, and MINIMA for pose estimation) rather than relying on manual scene construction, and by requiring only a single RGB view rather than multi-view captures. The systematic perturbation protocol (background, color, object pose) for stress-testing generalization is a novel addition not present in prior robot benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This benchmark addresses the fundamental scalability bottleneck in robot policy evaluation by replacing labor-intensive physical testing with automated simulation plus lightweight human preference judgments, enabling continuous, reproducible, and large-scale comparison of VLAs. The finding that current VLAs are highly dataset-specific rather than true generalists, combined with the 'Spatial Paradox' (implicit 3D priors from multi-view pretraining outperforming explicit 3D inductive biases), provides actionable guidance for the field. Key limitations remain: no wrist-camera support, difficulty modeling fine-grained contact dynamics, and the sim-to-real correlation validated on only one task, leaving open whether the benchmark's rankings faithfully reflect real-world performance at scale.
