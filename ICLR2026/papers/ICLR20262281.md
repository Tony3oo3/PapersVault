## Problem

Applying DPO to long-horizon LLM agents reveals a granularity mismatch: trajectory-level DPO provides stable but unresolvable credit assignment signals, while step-level DPO offers fine-grained supervision that is statistically noisy and data-inefficient under limited Monte Carlo rollouts. No existing method bridges this gap with a principled intermediate granularity that captures multi-step sub-task structure while remaining statistically tractable.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262281/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The agent receives a natural-language task instruction and a textual environment observation (e.g., 'On the cabinet 4, you see a mug 2') and outputs a textual action command (e.g., 'take mug 2 from cabinet 4'). Training operates entirely offline after a single frozen-policy exploration phase: the reference policy collects trajectories once, Monte Carlo rollouts (M=5) estimate rewards at each granularity, and all subsequent DPO optimization uses only these fixed preference datasets with no further environment interaction. The DPO loss consumes preference pairs at three granularities—full trajectories, single-step suffixes, and length-matched action groups—each conditioned on the shared history prefix.

## Method

HPL introduces a composite DPO objective that simultaneously optimizes at trajectory, step, and a novel action-group level, where groups are semantically coherent sub-task segments (e.g., 'navigate → open → take') produced by one of four segmentation strategies (fixed-length, uncertainty-based, or LLM-guided semantic partitioning). The key innovation is a dual-layer curriculum scheduler that organizes group-level training along two axes—group length (sub-task complexity) and reward gap ΔR (sample discriminability)—progressively expanding from easy short groups to hard long groups across three phases. A bias-variance proposition shows that choosing group length k = Θ(log(1/ε)) reduces variance by a factor Ω(T/log(1/ε)) relative to both trajectory- and step-level losses while incurring at most additive bias ε, formalizing why the intermediate granularity is statistically advantageous under limited rollout budgets.

## Results

On Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, HPL (Semantic) achieves an average score of 67.28 across ALFWorld, WebShop, and InterCode-SQL, surpassing the strongest baselines IPR (63.82) and ETO (63.47) by 3.46 and 3.81 points respectively. The advantage is most pronounced on ALFWorld unseen scenarios, where HPL (Semantic) reaches 84.08% success rate versus 78.11% for IPR. For the smaller Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct model, HPL (Semantic) achieves 59.44 average, outperforming IPR (55.49) and ETO (55.43) by approximately 4 points. All HPL variants (Fixed-N, Fixed-K, Uncertainty, Semantic) consistently outperform every single-granularity baseline across both model scales and all three benchmarks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HPL (Semantic) outperforms all three preference-based baselines across every benchmark and both model scales. Against ETO (trajectory-level DPO, Song et al. 2024), HPL gains 3.81 points in average score on the 7B model (67.28 vs. 63.47) and 4.01 points on the 1.5B model (59.44 vs. 55.43), with the largest margin on ALFWorld unseen (84.08% vs. 77.86%). Against IPR (step-level DPO, Xiong et al. 2024), HPL leads by 3.46 points on 7B (67.28 vs. 63.82) and 3.95 points on 1.5B (59.44 vs. 55.49), again with the most dramatic gap on ALFWorld unseen (84.08% vs. 78.11%, a 6-point improvement). Against RFT (rejection sampling fine-tuning, Yuan et al. 2023), HPL's advantage is even larger at 5.10 points (7B) and 5.65 points (1.5B), confirming that preference-based hierarchical optimization substantially exceeds imitation-learning-only approaches. Notably, even the simplest HPL variant (Fixed-N with N=3) surpasses both ETO and IPR, validating that the intermediate action-group granularity itself—regardless of segmentation quality—provides a meaningful inductive bias over the two extreme granularities.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the DPO-based agent alignment line initiated by ETO (trajectory-level) and IPR (step-level) by introducing an intermediate action-group granularity that bridges the credit-assignment gap between the two extremes. The core departure is the dual-layer curriculum scheduler, which organizes group-level preference pairs along sub-task complexity (group length) and sample discriminability (reward gap) — a structured training progression not previously applied to preference optimization for agents. The theoretical contribution (Proposition 1) formalizes the bias-variance trade-off of group-level DPO, showing that a group length of O(log(1/ε)) simultaneously reduces variance by a factor of Ω(T/log(1/ε)) while incurring at most additive bias ε, providing a principled justification for the intermediate granularity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The granularity mismatch problem is a practical bottleneck for anyone training LLM agents from offline data: trajectory-level signals are too coarse for credit assignment, while step-level signals are too noisy under limited rollout budgets. HPL offers a concrete, implementable solution that requires no online RL and only a one-shot exploration phase, making it accessible to practitioners with modest compute. Key limitations include dependence on segmentation quality (semantic segmentation requires a powerful external LLM), domain-specific curriculum hyperparameters, and potential bias inheritance from the teacher model. Open questions include whether self-supervised or learned segmentation can replace the GPT-4o segmenter, whether the curriculum can be adapted online, and whether the hierarchical approach extends to even longer horizons or continuous action spaces.
