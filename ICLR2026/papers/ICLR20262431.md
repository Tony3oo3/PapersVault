## Problem

Existing video-based robotic manipulation methods either rely on large-scale real-world video datasets that introduce domain gaps or require laborious physical demonstrations captured under tightly controlled conditions. This paper asks whether a single AI-generated video, conditioned on the specific scene and task, can serve as the sole source of supervision for real-world robotic manipulation without any physical demonstrations, robot-specific training, or additional supervision.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262431/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB-D image of the initial scene and a free-form language command (e.g., 'pour water on the plant') as input. The real depth map is used to resolve the scale-shift ambiguity of the monocular depth estimator applied to the generated video. The output is a continuous 6DoF end-effector trajectory, retargeted from the tracked object pose via a fixed end-effector-to-object transformation, executed in closed-loop with real-time FoundationPose tracking and automatic recovery when deviations exceed 3 cm or 20 degrees.

## Method

RIGVid generates a candidate video using an off-the-shelf video diffusion model (Kling v1.6) conditioned on the scene image and command, then automatically filters unsuccessful generations by prompting GPT-4o with four evenly sampled frames. A monocular depth estimator predicts per-frame depth, aligned to the real depth via an affine scale-shift transformation. The active object is identified through GPT-4o + Grounding DINO + SAM-2, and its 6D pose trajectory is extracted using FoundationPose (a render-and-compare tracker requiring a pre-computed mesh from BundleSDF). The trajectory is retargeted to the robot via a fixed end-effector-to-object transform, making the approach embodiment-agnostic. The key finding is that 6D object-level pose tracking substantially outperforms alternatives like point tracks, optical flow, feature fields, and VLM-based keypoint prediction (ReKep), achieving 85% success versus 50% for ReKep and 67.5% for Gen2Act, with filtered generated videos performing on par with real human demonstrations.

## Results

RIGVid achieves an overall 85% success rate across four real-world manipulation tasks (pouring, lifting, placing, sweeping) using filtered Kling v1.6 generated videos, matching performance with real human demonstration videos. Per-task success rates with filtered generated videos are: pouring 100%, lifting 80%, placing 90%, and sweeping 70%. Against the SOTA VLM-based trajectory prediction method ReKep, RIGVid achieves 85% vs. 50% success. Among alternative trajectory extraction methods, RIGVid's 6D object pose tracking (85%) substantially outperforms Gen2Act (67.5%), 4D-DPM (35.0%), AVDC (32.5%), and Track2Act (7.5%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

RIGVid outperforms all five compared baselines. Against ReKep (VLM-based relational keypoint constraints), RIGVid achieves 85% vs. 50% success, with failures attributed to ReKep's inaccurate keypoint predictions. Among trajectory extraction methods, RIGVid's 6D object pose tracking (85%) beats Gen2Act (67.5%) by 17.5 percentage points, with the gap widening on harder tasks (20–25% advantage on placing and sweeping). The remaining baselines trail significantly: 4D-DPM (35.0%) suffers from unstable and jerky tracking, AVDC (32.5%) accumulates optical flow errors across frames, and Track2Act (7.5%) fails because its learned track prediction network does not generalize to the generated video scenarios. The paper also shows that filtered generated videos perform on par with real human demonstration videos, and that video quality directly correlates with task success (Sora: 0%, Kling v1.5 unfiltered: moderate, Kling v1.6 filtered: 85% average).

## Relation to Prior Work

RIGVid builds on the visual imitation paradigm for robotics (Bahl et al., Dasari & Gupta) and the 6D pose tracking stack (FoundationPose, BundleSDF), but departs fundamentally from prior video-generation-for-robotics work (Gen2Act, Dreamitate) by eliminating all robot demonstration data: those methods still require offline robot trajectories to train policies, whereas RIGVid uses a generated video as the sole supervision signal, extracting a 6D object pose trajectory and retargeting it directly. It also positions itself against VLM-based trajectory prediction (ReKep), arguing that the dense pixel-level information in a generated video provides richer supervision than compact keypoint abstractions. The pipeline composes off-the-shelf components—video diffusion (Kling), VLM filtering (GPT-4o), monocular depth estimation, and 6D tracking—into a novel zero-demonstration manipulation system.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that filtered generated videos match real human demonstrations in imitation effectiveness suggests a path toward eliminating the laborious data collection bottleneck in robotic manipulation, with performance improving as video generation models advance. Key limitations include the requirement for a pre-computed object mesh (mesh-free tracking is too slow for real-time use), the sensitivity to monocular depth estimation errors (the primary failure source), and the substantial computational cost of video generation. Open questions include whether this approach scales to multi-object, long-horizon, or contact-rich tasks, whether the mesh requirement can be removed at real-time speeds, and whether future VLM-based methods might close the gap with full video generation.
