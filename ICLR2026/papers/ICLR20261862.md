## Problem

LLMs are deployed as conversational interfaces where users frequently issue underspecified instructions that are refined over multiple turns, yet evaluation has predominantly focused on single-turn, fully-specified settings. Prior multi-turn benchmarks treat conversations as episodic (each turn is a self-contained subtask), which overestimates model capability when information must be fused across turns. This paper asks: how much do LLMs actually degrade when the same task is delivered incrementally across a multi-turn conversation rather than all at once?

## Input / Output

The framework takes fully-specified instructions from six existing benchmarks (HumanEval, Spider, BFCL, GSM8K, ToTTo, Summary of a Haystack) and transforms them into sharded instructions—sets of 3–8 smaller shards that jointly preserve the original information. In the SHARDED setting, the LLM under test receives at most one shard per turn from a GPT-4o-mini user simulator that contextually selects which shard to reveal next. Outputs include per-turn task-specific scores (binary correctness or 0–100 scale), aggregated into averaged performance (P̄), aptitude (A⁹⁰, the 90th-percentile score), and unreliability (U⁹⁰₁₀, the 90th-minus-10th percentile gap), with the conversation's final score being the maximum over all answer attempts across turns.

## Method

The core contribution is a sharded simulation environment that repurposes single-turn benchmark instructions into multi-turn underspecified conversations via a semi-automatic sharding process (segmentation → decontextualized rephrasing → automated verification → manual inspection) governed by five validity properties (information preservation, clear initial intent, order insensitivity, maximal sharding, minimal transformation). The simulation loop instantiates three LLM-backed roles: the assistant (model under test), a user simulator (GPT-4o-mini) that holds the full sharded instruction and contextually selects which shard to disclose each turn, and a system module that classifies each assistant reply into one of seven strategies and extracts answer spans for task-specific scoring. Five simulation types are compared—FULL (single-turn baseline), SHARDED (one shard per turn), CONCAT (all shards concatenated in one turn as a rephrasing control), RECAP (sharded plus a final consolidated recap turn), and SNOWBALL (cumulative repetition of all prior shards each turn)—enabling decomposition of performance loss into aptitude versus reliability components. At scale (15 LLMs, 6 tasks, ~100 instances each, N=10 repeated simulations per configuration, yielding 200,000+ conversations), the framework reveals a uniform ~39% average performance drop in SHARDED versus FULL, driven primarily by a 112% increase in unreliability rather than a 16% drop in aptitude, with root causes identified as premature answer attempts, answer bloat from over-reliance on prior incorrect attempts, loss-in-middle-turns, and over-verbosity.

## Results

Across 15 LLMs and six generation tasks (Code, Database, Actions, Math, Data-to-text, Summary), all models exhibit a 39% average performance drop in multi-turn SHARDED conversations compared to single-turn FULL instructions (e.g., GPT-4.1 drops from 76.2 to 49.0; Gemini 2.5 Pro from 78.1 to 47.3). The CONCAT control (all shards in one turn) retains 95.1% of FULL performance on average, confirming the degradation stems from multi-turn underspecification rather than rephrasing. Decomposing the drop, aptitude (90th-percentile score) decreases only 16% on average, while unreliability (90th–10th percentile gap) skyrockets 112%, meaning models become far less consistent rather than fundamentally less capable. Reasoning models (o3, DeepSeek-R1) and lower temperature settings (T=0) do not mitigate the multi-turn reliability loss.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between three simulation settings (FULL, CONCAT, SHARDED) rather than between competing methods. SHARDED (multi-turn, one shard per turn) degrades 39% on average relative to FULL, while CONCAT (all shards concatenated in one turn) retains 95.1% of FULL performance, isolating multi-turn underspecification as the cause. No model escapes the effect: even the strongest models (Gemini 2.5 Pro, GPT-4.1, Claude 3.7 Sonnet) lose 30–40% in SHARDED, comparable to weaker models like Llama 3.1-8B. Smaller models show slightly larger CONCAT degradations (86–92% vs. 97–103% for larger models), indicating reduced robustness to paraphrasing. Agent-style mitigations (RECAP, SNOWBALL) partially recover performance (SNOWBALL gains 15–20% over SHARDED) but still fall well short of FULL. Temperature reduction to T=0 lowers single-turn unreliability but leaves multi-turn unreliability at ~30%, confirming that the lost-in-conversation phenomenon is structural rather than stochastic.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior multi-turn LLM evaluation work (e.g., MT-Eval, MathChat) by arguing that treating conversation turns as episodic, independently gradable subtasks overestimates model capability. It builds on Herlihy et al. (2024)'s taxonomy of LLM response strategies to underspecified queries (clarification, hedging, refusal, etc.) and on Choi et al. (2021)'s decontextualization framework to construct its sharding process. The key methodological contribution is the sharded simulation environment, which repurposes existing single-turn benchmarks (HumanEval, Spider, GSM8K, BFCL, ToTTo, Summary of a Haystack) into gradually-revealed multi-turn conversations, enabling direct single-turn vs. multi-turn comparison on identical tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that all 15 tested LLMs—spanning small open-weight to state-of-the-art closed models—suffer an average 39% performance drop in multi-turn underspecified settings, driven primarily by a 112% increase in unreliability rather than a 16% aptitude loss, reframes the central challenge of conversational AI from capability to consistency. This has direct practical implications for novice users who cannot fully specify instructions upfront, and for system builders who assumed agent-style recaps or temperature tuning would mitigate the problem (both shown insufficient). Open questions include whether the effect extends to creative/open-ended tasks, non-English languages, and multimodal settings, and whether the simulation-based findings (which the authors argue underestimate real-world degradation) hold under natural human-AI dialogue.
