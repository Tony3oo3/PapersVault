## Problem

Existing jailbreak methods manipulate only the input prompt (via optimization, rewriting, or templates), offering limited interpretability, requiring many queries, and degrading sharply under safety defenses. No prior attack intervenes directly on the model's internal causal circuitry in a geometry-aware, defense-resilient manner, leaving open whether safety alignment can be bypassed by targeting the specific attention heads that encode refusal behavior.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261123/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a standard text prompt (a jailbreak benchmark item) and the target model's attention out-projection weight matrices (W^O) at inference time; no gradient access, auxiliary prompts, or weight modification is required. It produces a single text completion (up to 128 tokens, temperature 0.7, top-p 0.95) that bypasses the model's safety alignment. The intervention is applied only at the final token position of each decoding step and is transient—masking and steering are re-applied per forward pass via context-managed hooks, leaving model weights on disk unmodified.

## Method

HMNS is a closed-loop, inference-time intervention with three coupled stages repeated up to 10 decoding attempts: (1) KL-divergence causal attribution scores every attention head by ablating its out-projection slice and measuring the shift in the next-token distribution, selecting a global top-K=10 heads; (2) dynamic masking zeros the corresponding column blocks in W^O for the current forward pass, silencing those heads' residual contributions; (3) nullspace steering computes a thin QR factorization of the masked write matrix M_ℓ, samples a random Gaussian vector, projects it into the orthogonal complement of span(M_ℓ), and injects an RMS-scaled perturbation δ_ℓ = α·RMS(a_ℓ)·u_ℓ into the residual stream after attention. The key geometric guarantee is irreproducibility: because u_ℓ lies in W_ℓ^⊥, no linear combination of the masked heads' outputs can reconstruct or cancel the injected direction, so the steering effect is locally protected from the very circuitry being suppressed. Closed-loop re-identification of causal heads at each attempt allows the method to adapt to shifting attribution patterns under defenses, and the entire pipeline requires no training, no gradient access, and only ~2 external queries per success.

## Results

Across four jailbreak benchmarks (AdvBench, HarmBench, JBB-Behaviors, StrongReject) and three open-weight LLMs (LLaMA-2-7B-Chat, Phi-3-Medium-14B, LLaMA-3.1-70B), HMNS achieves state-of-the-art Attack Success Rate (ASR) of 92–99% (GPT-4o grader) and 80–96% (GPT-5 grader), while maintaining an Average Query Count (ACQ) of approximately 1.8–2.2—roughly 3.5–4× fewer external queries than the strongest prompt-based baseline (ArrAttack, ACQ ≈ 7–8). Averaged over 12 model–dataset pairs, HMNS improves ASR by +5.9 pp (GPT-4o) and +5.0 pp (GPT-5) over ArrAttack. Under six defenses (SmoothLLM, DPP, RPO, Paraphrase, PAT, SafeDecoding), HMNS retains the highest ASR with average gains of +6–8 pp over ArrAttack on GPT-4o.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HMNS outperforms all eight baselines on every model–dataset pair by a wide margin. The closest competitor, ArrAttack (re-ranking-based optimization), achieves 93–94% ASR on LLaMA-3.1-70B with ACQ ≈ 7–8, while HMNS reaches 96–99% ASR with ACQ ≈ 1.8–2.1—a 5–6 pp ASR gap at roughly 4× fewer queries. Tempest (tree-search multi-turn) is the second-closest at 85–86% ASR but still trails HMNS by 13–14 pp. Prompt-rewriting methods (AutoDAN, PrisonBreak, MasterKey) cluster in the 68–78% ASR range with ACQ of 10–13, and multi-shot jailbreaking (MSJ) and adaptive optimization (ADC) fall in the 64–72% range. Foot-In-The-Door (FITD) performs worst across all settings, achieving only 40–48% ASR with the highest query counts (15–17). Under defenses, the gap widens: HMNS maintains 55–57% average ASR across six defenses on LLaMA-3.1-70B versus 49–50% for ArrAttack, confirming that the mechanism-level intervention is more robust to defense-induced routing changes than prompt-level attacks.

## Relation to Prior Work

HMNS departs fundamentally from the dominant prompt-level jailbreak paradigm (optimization-based suffixes like GCG/AutoDAN, template-based attacks like PrisonBreak/MasterKey, and rewriting-based methods like ReNeLLM) by intervening directly on internal Transformer circuitry. It repurposes tools from mechanistic interpretability—causal tracing via KL-based head ablation (Meng et al. 2022), activation steering (Turner et al. 2023), and function-vector geometry (Todd et al. 2023)—which had previously served understanding or alignment, and redirects them toward adversarial safety circumvention. The specific combination of out-projection masking with nullspace-constrained residual injection in a closed-loop re-identification cycle is, to the authors' knowledge, novel in the jailbreak literature, bridging a gap between interpretability-informed control and adversarial robustness evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that safety-aligned refusal behavior is concentrated in a sparse, localizable set of attention heads (top-K=10 globally) has direct implications for the alignment community: it suggests that fine-tuning-based safety may be structurally fragile against white-box adversaries and motivates distributed or redundant safety mechanisms. The compute-normalized metrics (FEP, IPC, FPS, LPS) and budget-matched baseline protocol address a real gap in how mechanism-level interventions are evaluated relative to prompt-only attacks. Key open questions include whether the steering vectors transfer in a black-box setting, whether the nullspace geometry can be repurposed defensively (e.g., to detect or resist such interventions), and whether the sparse-head concentration finding holds under more diverse alignment procedures or in multi-turn/long-context regimes where attribution patterns may shift more dramatically.
