## Problem

Existing video benchmarks primarily evaluate perception and understanding, requiring models to only locate question-mentioned frames and perceive a few adjacent frames, falling short of assessing deep multimodal reasoning. This gap is critical because real-world applications such as embodied intelligence and security monitoring demand the ability to mine and reason over evidence scattered across long-range, non-adjacent frames. MMR-V addresses this by posing questions that cannot be answered through direct perception and require inference over hidden, multi-frame visual evidence.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262306/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Each task pairs a video clip (7–3771 s, avg 277 s) with a text question and 7–11 multiple-choice options (avg 10); the model outputs a single selected option, scored by accuracy. Frame sampling is standardized per model (8–512 frames or 1 fps), and for full-modal models (e.g., Gemini-2.0-Flash) audio is optionally included as an additional input channel. Tasks typically require reasoning over ~12 frames spanning ~60% of the video duration, and evaluation is conducted under both zero-shot and zero-shot + CoT prompting settings.

## Method

MMR-V is built on three construction principles: multi-frame evidence (questions demand long-range, non-adjacent frames), deep reasoning (answers are not directly perceivable), and realism (correct answers cross-referenced with popular video comments to align with common user understanding). The 317 curated YouTube videos span six categories (Animation, Film, Philosophy, TV, Life, Art) and are selected to avoid linear, descriptive content in favor of creatively edited, thematically rich material. Tasks are organized into Implicit Reasoning (metaphor, theme, emotion, comment matching, implicit symbol) and Explicit Reasoning (causal, sequential structure, counterintuitive, cross-modal transfer, video type/intent) — 10 categories and 33 subcategories inspired by Kahneman's Dual Process Theory. Distractors are generated via three strategies (GPT-4o's own incorrect answers, GPT-4o-generated options, human-written options), with the first shown to yield the most confusable distractors (GPT-4o accuracy drops to 59% vs. 70% for strategy 2). All 1,257 tasks are manually annotated and quality-checked by five annotators using a structured checklist, and the benchmark is evaluated on 11 proprietary and 10 open-source MLLMs.

## Results

On MMR-V (1,257 multiple-choice tasks over 317 videos), the best proprietary model Gemini-2.5-pro (1 fps sampling) achieves only 64.3% overall accuracy, while the best open-source model Gemma-3-27b-it reaches 42.0%. Under a fixed 32-frame input setting, GPT-5 attains the highest score at 60.9%. Human annotators score 86.0%, revealing a 21.7-point human–model gap. Chain-of-Thought prompting yields only a 0.88% average gain, and 'Thinking' models improve just 2.4% over their base versions, indicating that current reasoning-enhancement strategies offer limited benefit for multimodal video reasoning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among open-source models, Gemma-3-27b-it (42.0%) leads, outperforming InternVL2.5-38B (39.9%) and Qwen2.5-VL-72B (39.1%) by 2.1 and 2.9 points respectively; the smallest models (LLaVA-Video 18.4%, NVILA-8B 25.5%) remain near random. Among proprietary models, Gemini-2.5-pro at 1 fps (64.3%) surpasses GPT-5 (60.6%) by 3.7 points and o3 (59.1%) by 5.2 points, while GPT-4o (52.8%) and o4-mini (52.5%) cluster in the mid-50s. A clear scaling trend is visible: Qwen2.5-VL-72B (39.1%) beats its 7B counterpart (30.1%) by 9 points, and GPT-4o (52.8%) beats GPT-4o-mini (34.8%) by 18 points. Models perform better on implicit tasks than explicit tasks by an average of +7.9%, with the largest gaps on Counterintuitive Reasoning, Sequential Structure Reasoning, and Comment Matching. Adding audio input improves Gemini-2.0-Flash by 1.4%, Gemini-2.0-Flash-Thinking by 1.0%, and Phi-4-multimodal-instruct by 1.0%, confirming that richer modality coverage yields modest but consistent gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by positioning video reasoning as fundamentally distinct from both video understanding (Video-MME, MVBench, TempCompass) and image-based multimodal reasoning (MathVista, Math-LLaVA). It departs from prior video reasoning benchmarks (VideoEspresso, Video-MMMU, VRBench, VideoReasonBench) by requiring purely manually annotated, open-domain tasks that demand long-range multi-frame evidence mining rather than perception of adjacent frames or specialized disciplinary knowledge. The conceptual framing via Kahneman's Dual Process Theory—splitting tasks into implicit (subtext, metaphor, cultural symbols) and explicit (causal, sequential, counterintuitive) reasoning—provides a principled taxonomy absent from prior video QA work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 21.7% human-model gap (86% vs 64.3%) and the finding that CoT/thinking strategies yield only 0.88%/2.4% gains challenge the assumption that text-domain reasoning enhancements transfer to multimodal video reasoning. The observation that visual analysis constitutes only ~10% of model CoTs points to a concrete failure mode: models default to text-based reasoning over question-frame perception rather than genuine multi-frame evidence mining. Open questions include how to architecturally integrate long-range visual reasoning into CoT, whether tool use (e.g., frame retrieval) can close the gap, and whether the benchmark's English-centric, manually-annotated design (317 videos) limits generalizability.
