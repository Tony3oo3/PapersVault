## Problem

Existing arbitrary-scale super-resolution (ASSR) methods based on implicit neural representations require repeated upsampling and decoding for every target scale, leading to high computational cost, while their coordinate-to-pixel mappings are inherently limited in modeling continuous high-resolution signals. The paper asks whether one can directly reconstruct a 2D continuous HR signal from an LR image in a single pass, then sample at any scale via fast rendering, thereby decoupling reconstruction quality from per-scale computation.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single low-resolution RGB image (e.g., 48×48 or 256×256 patches) and a target upsampling scale factor (tested from ×4 to ×48). It first constructs a 2D continuous Gaussian field with 8 parameters per kernel (2D position, 2×2 covariance, 3-channel RGB) in one forward pass through a SwinIR or HAT backbone, then renders the HR image at the requested scale via Gaussian splatting in approximately 1 ms. Memory usage remains nearly constant (~2.5 GB) across scales, unlike INR-based methods that OOM at higher scales.

## Method

ContinuousSR adopts a Pixel-to-Gaussian paradigm: a CNN backbone encodes the LR image, and three lightweight modules predict the parameters of N 2D Gaussian kernels that jointly represent the continuous HR signal. The key insight is the Deep Gaussian Prior (DGP), discovered by statistically analyzing covariance parameters across 40,000 natural images, which reveals that σ²ₓ, σ²ᵧ, and ρσₓσᵧ follow Gaussian distributions within narrow ranges (0–2.4, 0–2.2, −0.9–1.5). DGP-Driven Covariance Weighting (DDCW) samples 730 pre-defined covariance kernels from the DGP and learns content-adaptive softmax weights to combine them, avoiding the ill-posed direct optimization of unbounded covariance. Adaptive Position Drifting (APD) initializes kernel centers at LR pixel positions and adds a Tanh-bounded offset (range [−1,1]) predicted by a 5-layer MLP, allowing the model to densify kernels in texture-rich regions. A separate 5-layer MLP (Color Gaussian Mapping) predicts per-kernel RGB values. Training is supervised with L1 loss (plus frequency loss in the stronger HAT configuration), and the one-time Gaussian field construction is followed by scale-agnostic rendering, yielding a 19.5× speedup over GSASR across 40 scales.

## Results

On Urban100 ×4, ContinuousSR (SwinIR backbone) achieves 27.65 dB PSNR, surpassing the prior SOTA GSASR (27.56 dB) by 0.09 dB, while the stronger HAT-backed variant (Ours+) reaches 28.22 dB. On DIV2K ×4, Ours+ achieves 29.80 dB vs. GSASR's 29.63 dB, and on LSDIR ×4, 27.14 dB vs. 26.88 dB. The headline efficiency gain is dramatic: average inference time across 45 scales (×4 to ×48) is only 3.3–3.5 ms, compared to 89–434 ms for GSASR and up to 4962 ms for GaussianSR, yielding a 19.5× speedup over GSASR on Manga109.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ContinuousSR outperforms all nine baselines across every benchmark and scale tested. Against the closest competitor GSASR, it gains +0.07 to +0.09 dB PSNR at ×4 on Urban100, DIV2K, and LSDIR respectively, with the gap widening at higher scales (e.g., +0.18 dB on Manga109 ×4 per the abstract). The speed advantage is the most striking: at 3.3 ms average over 45 scales, ContinuousSR is ~27× faster than GSASR (89.1 ms on Urban100), ~103× faster than CiaoSR (341.5 ms), and ~1500× faster than GaussianSR (4962.8 ms on DIV2K). Against INR-based methods (LIIF, LTE, SRNO, MetaSR), the PSNR gains range from +0.63 to +1.45 dB at ×4, with the largest margin over GaussianSR (+1.45 dB on Urban100 ×4). The HAT-backed Ours+ variant extends these leads by an additional ~0.5 dB over the SwinIR-backed Ours, confirming that the framework benefits from stronger backbones. On the out-of-distribution BRATS medical dataset, Ours achieves 29.93 dB at ×4 vs. GSASR's 28.02 dB, demonstrating generalization beyond natural images.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant INR-based ASSR paradigm (LIIF, CiaoSR, LTE, SRNO) that learns coordinate-to-pixel mappings requiring repeated upsampling and decoding at each target scale. It also extends the Gaussian-based ASSR line (GaussianSR, GSASR) by reconstructing a single continuous 2D Gaussian field once and then rendering at arbitrary scales in ~1 ms, rather than regenerating scale-specific Gaussians or features. The key conceptual shift is treating SR as explicit continuous-signal reconstruction via 2D Gaussian Splatting (Huang et al., SIGGRAPH 2024) rather than implicit function evaluation, with the Deep Gaussian Prior providing a tractable initialization strategy that prior Gaussian-SR works lacked.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 19.5× speedup across 40 scales makes arbitrary-scale SR practically deployable in real-time applications where INR-based methods are prohibitively slow, and the near-constant memory footprint across scales removes a key scaling bottleneck. The DGP discovery—that Gaussian field parameters in natural images follow tight, Gaussian-distributed ranges—is a useful empirical prior, though the 700+ GPU-hour extraction pipeline limits its immediate reusability. Open questions include whether DGP generalizes to non-natural domains (medical, satellite), whether the approach extends to video SR, and whether the Gaussian-field construction can be made even lighter for edge deployment.
