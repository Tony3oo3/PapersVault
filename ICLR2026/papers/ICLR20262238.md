## Problem

Existing optimization-based jailbreak attacks (GCG, MAC, I-GCG) suffer from three interrelated failures: the model frequently appends refusal statements after the harmful prefix, produces pseudo-harmful outputs that pass the prefix check but fail strict harmfulness criteria, and rely on a dot-product token-ranking rule that conflates gradient direction with step magnitude, leading to misaligned and inefficient updates. TAO-Attack targets all three gaps simultaneously.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262238/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a malicious query string, a target harmful prefix (e.g., 'Sure, here is a script that can exploit vulnerabilities...'), and a refusal set R of K short refusal strings collected by querying the model with random suffixes. It produces a 20-token adversarial suffix initialized with a fixed symbolic string (or via easy-to-hard transfer from a simpler query) that, when concatenated to the query, elicits a genuinely harmful completion. The suffix is optimized in the discrete token space of the target LLM's vocabulary, with each iteration updating a single token position sampled from a temperature-scaled distribution over top-k candidates.

## Method

TAO-Attack combines a progressive two-stage loss with a direction-priority token optimization (DPTO) strategy. Stage One (refusal-aware loss) minimizes the negative log-likelihood of the harmful prefix while adding a penalty term α·log p(r_j | prompt ⊕ prefix) for each refusal response r_j in the set, optimized sequentially over K refusals until convergence. Once the generated prefix matches the target with Rouge-L ≥ τ, Stage Two (effectiveness-aware loss) activates, penalizing the observed continuation x_O to push the model away from pseudo-harmful completions; the process reverts to Stage One if refusal-like content persists for N consecutive steps. DPTO replaces GCG's single dot-product score with a two-step selection: first, candidates are filtered by cosine similarity between their embedding displacement and the negative gradient (ensuring directional alignment), then within the filtered set, tokens are sampled via a temperature-scaled softmax over the gradient-projected step size. This decoupling is supported by a theoretical analysis showing that the alignment threshold η_i bounds the variance of projected steps and provides a sufficient condition for guaranteed one-step expected loss decrease.

## Results

On AdvBench, TAO-Attack achieves 100% attack success rate (ASR) across all three evaluated models (Vicuna-7B-1.5, Llama-2-7B-Chat, Mistral-7B-Instruct-0.2), matching I-GCG and substantially outperforming all other baselines. Under a stricter fixed-initialization protocol (Table 2), TAO-Attack further differentiates itself: on Llama-2-7B-Chat it reaches 92% ASR in 305 iterations versus I-GCG's 68% in 604, and on Mistral-7B-Instruct-0.2 it achieves 100% ASR in just 86 iterations versus I-GCG's 80% in 406. For transferability to closed-source models, the universal suffix optimized on Vicuna-7B-1.5 yields 82% ASR on GPT-3.5 Turbo, compared to only 30% for both GCG and I-GCG.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TAO-Attack ties I-GCG at 100% ASR on all three standard AdvBench models but clearly outperforms it under the stricter fixed-initialization protocol: 92% vs. 68% ASR on Llama-2-7B-Chat (305 vs. 604 iterations) and 100% vs. 80% on Mistral-7B-Instruct-0.2 (86 vs. 406 iterations). Against GCG, TAO-Attack improves Llama-2-7B-Chat ASR from 54% to 100% and Mistral from 92% to 100%. MAC, AutoDAN, and Probe-Sampling all struggle on Llama-2-7B-Chat (56%, 26%, 56% respectively), while TAO-Attack reaches 100%. LLM-based methods PAIR and TAP are particularly weak on Llama-2-7B-Chat (10% and 4% ASR), and AmpleGCG and AdvPrompter trail significantly across all models. For cross-model transferability, TAO-Attack's universal suffix achieves 82% ASR on GPT-3.5 Turbo, more than doubling the 30% achieved by both GCG and I-GCG, and is the only method to elicit any harmful response from GPT-4 Turbo (8% vs. 0%).

## Relation to Prior Work

TAO-Attack extends the GCG (Zou et al., 2023) iterative suffix-optimization paradigm and the I-GCG (Jia et al., 2025) improvements (diverse templates, adaptive multi-coordinate updates, easy-to-hard initialization) by replacing the single-template loss with a two-stage objective that first suppresses refusals and then penalizes pseudo-harmful continuations. Its second key departure is the DPTO token-selection strategy, which decouples directional alignment (cosine similarity with the negative gradient) from step magnitude (projected descent), in contrast to GCG's and I-GCG's dot-product ranking that conflates the two. The paper explicitly ablates against I-GCG's harmful-guidance template and multi-coordinate updating, showing both are counterproductive within the two-stage framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in DPTO's demonstrated generality—it improves both GCG and I-GCG when grafted on independently—making it a reusable component for the broader gradient-based jailbreak literature. The ablation revealing that I-GCG's harmful-guidance template *reduces* ASR from 100% to 65% challenges a working assumption in the field that forcing the model to acknowledge harmfulness aids the attack. Open questions include whether the two-stage design extends to multi-turn conversations and multimodal inputs (acknowledged as future work), whether the modest transferability to closed-source models (82% on GPT-3.5 but only 8% on GPT-4) can be improved, and whether adaptive defenses like CAA/SCANS can be further strengthened to close the remaining gap.
