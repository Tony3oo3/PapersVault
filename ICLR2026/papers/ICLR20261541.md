## Problem

Existing LLM fingerprinting methods lack cryptographic unforgeability and fail under realistic adversarial settings where a stolen model is fine-tuned or configured with meta-prompts that alter its output style. This paper addresses the gap by requiring fingerprints to be both cryptographically verifiable (proving ownership without a trusted third party) and robust to output-distribution shifts caused by instruction-tuning and meta-prompt manipulation.

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of k=10 fingerprint questions (either 10-token random sequences or semantically valid natural-language queries), a fixed response set of 256 curated phrases, and a SHA-256 hash function as inputs, and produces a binary ownership verdict (pass if ≥2 of 10 questions yield the cryptographically determined response prefix) along with a continuous fingerprint strength score (cumulative token probability). Verification is strictly black-box: the claimant queries the suspect model via API and checks whether each response begins with the expected token sequence, requiring no access to weights or gradients.

## Method

Chain & Hash cryptographically binds each fingerprint question to one of 256 responses via SHA-256, where the hash input includes the full question set and response set, creating a chain such that altering any single question changes all response mappings and making forgery computationally infeasible without fine-tuning. Fingerprint embedding is achieved by fine-tuning the target model on augmented QA pairs, where augmentation includes GPT-4-generated meta-prompt variations (to override adversarial style instructions), random token padding (to resist post-fine-tuning drift), and multiple prompt templates (to survive instruction-tuning format changes). Training uses a combined loss of cross-entropy on fingerprint samples plus KL divergence on non-fingerprint samples to preserve utility, with adaptive termination once all fingerprints reach ≥90% verification probability. The framework extends to LoRA adapters and supports multi-model collusion resistance through overlapping question chains shared across model versions.

## Results

Chain & Hash achieves Fingerprint Strength of 93.8–100% across four models (Llama-3-8B, Llama-3-8B-Instruct, Phi-3-mini-instruct, Llama-2-13B-Instruct) with both random and natural question formats, rising from near-zero pre-fingerprint baselines (e.g., 1.6×10⁻⁵ for Llama-3-8B random). Ownership verification requires only 1 trial in all standard settings, and benchmark performance (HellaSwag, MMLU, TruthfulQA, Winogrande, IFEval, GSM8K) shows negligible degradation (mostly within ±1%). Under adversarial meta-prompts, random questions sustain >99% Fingerprint Strength while natural questions average 82.8%; after fine-tuning (Alpaca/ChatDoc), required trials remain ≤25 for random and ≤2 for natural questions on the Instruct model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Xu et al. (2024), the prior state-of-the-art black-box fingerprinting method, Chain & Hash decisively wins on robustness to meta-prompts: Xu et al.'s fingerprint maintains strong performance only under the default 'Helpful' prompt but drops to <0.04 Fingerprint Strength under 'Courteous' and <10⁻⁴ under other adversarial meta-prompts, whereas Chain & Hash with random questions sustains >99% across all seven tested meta-prompts. Against Nasery et al. (2025), the scalable fine-tuning-based approach, Chain & Hash again wins on adversarial robustness: Nasery et al.'s default configuration drops below 10% under stylistic meta-prompts (e.g., 'talk like a pirate'), and while enabling their use_chat_template option recovers ~90% robustness, it simultaneously degrades utility on normal prompts, a trade-off Chain & Hash avoids (all benchmark changes remain within ±1%). Both comparisons highlight that prior methods were not evaluated under realistic meta-prompt adversarial settings, which Chain & Hash explicitly addresses through meta-prompt diversification during fingerprint training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions Chain & Hash as a departure from prior LLM fingerprinting methods (Xu et al. 2024, Nasery et al. 2025) by introducing a cryptographic layer—SHA-256-based chain construction—that binds fingerprint questions to a fixed response set, providing unforgeability guarantees absent in earlier fine-tuning-based approaches. It also reframes fingerprinting as a 'benign backdoor' (drawing on Hubinger et al. 2024 and the broader backdoor literature) but explicitly targets the unforgeability and meta-prompt robustness dimensions that prior backdoor and fingerprinting work leaves unaddressed. Unlike white-box methods (Fernandez et al. 2024, Zeng et al. 2024), Chain & Hash operates entirely in a black-box setting, requiring only API-level access for verification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As LLMs become high-value commercial assets deployed in third-party infrastructure, a practical, black-box-verifiable fingerprinting method with cryptographic unforgeability addresses a real and growing IP-protection gap. The paper's demonstration that prior SOTA fingerprints collapse under trivial meta-prompt changes (e.g., 'answer like a pirate') highlights a previously underappreciated vulnerability in the field. Open questions include whether the approach scales to very large model families with multi-model collusion, whether it survives aggressive output paraphrasing in all regimes, and how it interacts with emerging safety-training pipelines that may inadvertently erase fingerprint signals.
