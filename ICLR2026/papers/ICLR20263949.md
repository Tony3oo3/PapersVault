## Problem

Existing DPO-like alignment methods operate on a single overall 'better-than' preference and cannot handle multiple fine-grained, aspect-specific preferences. Aggregating such fine-grained data introduces severe preference conflicts (nearly 30% of UltraFeedback samples exhibit explicit conflicts) and noise, causing training inefficiency and performance degradation. The paper asks: how can we effectively harness fine-grained preferences for robust LLM alignment in the presence of these inherent conflicts?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263949/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an aggregated dataset composed of κ disjoint sub-preference subsets (each sample annotated with a single binary preference under one specific fine-grained criterion, requiring no additional annotation overhead), an SFT reference model, and a small proxy LLM (e.g., Llama 3.2-3B) for reward modeling. It produces a curated subset (e.g., 30% of the full data) of high-consensus samples, which is then used for standard DPO training to yield the final aligned policy. The key constraint is that each sample carries only one aspect's label, so the method must cross-predict pseudo-rewards across aspects to estimate inter-aspect agreement.

## Method

The paper derives a Direct Fine-Grained Preference Optimization (DFPO) objective by extending DPO to multi-aspect settings, revealing a preference divergence (PD) term that implicitly re-weights samples by measuring consensus or conflict among fine-grained aspects. Rather than directly optimizing this complicated objective, the authors recast it as a data selection problem and prove (via loss-bound optimality) that selecting samples with the most negative PD values minimizes both upper and lower bounds of the DFPO loss. Practically, PD is estimated by training small proxy reward models (Bradley-Terry contrastive loss) per sub-preference, cross-predicting pseudo-reward gaps for samples from other aspects, applying quantile normalization, and mitigating length bias via balanced sampling and an explicit length penalty in the reward loss. The resulting curated subset is then aligned using standard DPO, achieving consistent improvements over full-data training with only 30% of the data.

## Results

On UltraFeedback (Llama3.1-8B), PD(ours) achieves the best AlpacaEval 2 win rate of 21.00 (LC 26.11) and average pairwise win score of 1.24±0.06, using only 30% of the data (18.6 GPU hours), outperforming full-data DPO (ALL: WR 15.18) and the DFPO variant (DMPO: WR 19.42, 42.1 GPU hours). On HelpSteer, PD(ours) reaches WR 7.55 (LC 12.13) and AW 1.19±0.04 with 50% of data, surpassing all full-data baselines. The method also demonstrates robustness across conflict levels (10%–30%), maintaining WR above 19.4 on UltraFeedback while full-data ALL degrades from 15.18 to 13.40.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary external baselines are the reward-model-based self-agreement filtering methods of Deng et al. (2025) and Lee et al. (2025), collectively represented as the RAF strategy. PD(ours) consistently outperforms RAF on both datasets: on UltraFeedback, WR improves from 19.64 to 21.00 (+1.36) and LC from 23.34 to 26.11 (+2.77); on HelpSteer, WR improves from 6.58 to 7.55 (+0.97) and LC from 10.46 to 12.13 (+1.67). PD(ours) also surpasses the full-data DFPO variant (DMPO) on UltraFeedback WR (21.00 vs. 19.42) while requiring less than half the GPU hours (18.6 vs. 42.1). Against the strongest full-data baseline (AVG.), PD(ours) gains +4.37 WR on UltraFeedback and +1.37 WR on HelpSteer. The PD(rati.) variant, which uses ground-truth discrete ratings instead of learned proxy rewards, achieves comparable performance (WR 18.85 on UltraFeedback) but requires costly multi-aspect rating annotations for every sample, whereas PD(ours) needs only a single binary preference per sample.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) to the multi-aspect setting by deriving the DFPO objective from the PPO formulation (Schulman et al., 2017), introducing a preference divergence (PD) term that captures inter-aspect conflicts. It departs from prior multi-objective preference optimization work (Ji et al., 2023; Rame et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2024), which seeks Pareto frontiers via weighted sub-preferences, by instead recasting the optimization as a data selection problem. Among data selection methods for alignment (Deng et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2025), which operate on overall preferences, this is the first to exploit fine-grained preference conflicts as the selection signal, grounded in a loss-bound optimality theorem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway—that a 30% subset selected by PD outperforms full-data DPO across models, datasets, and conflict levels—offers a concrete, low-overhead recipe for practitioners working with multi-aspect preference data. The theoretical loss-bound guarantee provides principled justification rather than heuristic filtering, distinguishing it from reward-model-based self-agreement baselines. Open questions include the availability of large-scale multi-aspect public datasets (only UltraFeedback and HelpSteer qualify), the sensitivity to proxy reward model quality, and whether the approach extends to iterative or online alignment settings where preferences evolve.
