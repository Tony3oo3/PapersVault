## Problem

When aggregating rankings from multiple evaluators (e.g., RLHF annotators, peer reviewers, or benchmarks), different aggregation rules can produce vastly different outcomes, yet there is no principled, data-driven way to select the most appropriate rule for a given dataset. Existing approaches either commit a priori to a set of axioms (which may be incompatible or leave many valid rules) or assume a generative noise model (which may be misspecified), leaving practitioners without a general framework for rule selection.

## Input / Output

The method takes a profile of evaluations (full or partial rankings, or numeric scores) over a set of alternatives, along with a set of candidate aggregation rules (e.g., positional scoring rules, MLEs of noise models, or any other SWFs). It outputs the rule (or set of tied rules) that minimizes expected disagreement between two random halves of the data. The framework is agnostic to evaluation format and output type, requiring only a distance function between rule outputs, making it applicable to aggregate rankings, single winners, or reward functions.

## Method

Aggregation by Consistency (AbC) randomly splits voters into two groups, applies each candidate rule independently to each group, and selects the rule minimizing the expected Kendall tau distance (with a 1/2 penalty for ties) between the two resulting rankings. For finite candidate sets, this is approximated via Monte Carlo sampling; for infinite sets like all positional scoring rules, it is solved via constrained optimization (Simulated Annealing over scoring vectors). The key insight is that consistency across random splits acts as a model-free proxy for quality, analogous to stability in clustering or minimum variance in estimation, without requiring a ground truth or generative model. The paper proves AbC satisfies reversal symmetry and plurality-shuffling consistency (both failed by all welfare-maximizing RPRs), while establishing impossibility results showing no anonymous RPR can simultaneously satisfy reversal symmetry, PSC, and union consistency, and that checking for perfect consistency over positional scoring rules is NP-complete.

## Results

On the astronomy peer review dataset (Table 1), the arithmetic mean aggregation achieves the lowest mean KT distance between random splits at 0.364±0.001, outperforming the max function (0.409±0.001), min function (0.444±0.001), median (0.371±0.001), and geometric mean (0.369±0.001). On synthetic Mallows and Plackett-Luce data (Figure 1), the model's MLE achieves the lowest disagreement between splits and the lowest distance to ground truth simultaneously, and AbC correctly selects it. On 25 real-world IRV elections, a rule achieving zero split-disagreement exists in 21/25 elections, but the identity of that rule varies across elections; AbC reliably identifies it in each case, while IRV itself is the worst-performing rule in several elections (e.g., elections 1, 16, 18).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparisons are against the MLEs of known generative models (Kemeny, Mallows, and Plackett-Luce) and standard positional scoring rules (plurality, veto, Borda). On Mallows-distributed data, the Mallows MLE achieves the lowest KT distance to ground truth and the lowest split-disagreement among all tested SWFs, and AbC selects it; similarly, on Plackett-Luce data, the PL MLE dominates and is selected by AbC. Kemeny MLE, while competitive, is outperformed by the model-specific MLE on both synthetic distributions. On the astronomy peer review data, AbC identifies the arithmetic mean as the most consistent aggregation function (0.364 KT), contradicting the common practice of prioritizing maximum scores (0.409 KT). On IRV election data, AbC outperforms the fixed IRV rule in multiple elections by selecting a different rule that achieves zero or near-zero split disagreement, demonstrating that no single rule is universally optimal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by formally introducing Rule Picking Rules (RPRs) as a distinct object of study, departing from both the axiomatic tradition (selecting criteria then designing rules) and the statistical tradition (assuming a noise model and computing MLE). It synthesizes the consistency-stability connection from clustering (von Luxburg, 2010), the minimum-variance-unbiased-estimator intuition from statistics, and the interpanel-agreement experiments from peer review to motivate a data-driven criterion. Unlike prior work on comparing voting rules (Sertel & Kara, 2003; Aldrich et al., 2014) that collects stated preferences over rules, AbC selects a rule purely from the data at hand without requiring voters to express meta-preferences or the analyst to commit to a generative model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a principled, model-agnostic way to select aggregation rules in settings where the 'right' rule is genuinely data-dependent (RLHF, peer review, AI agent evaluation), and the impossibility results show that the axioms AbC fails (union consistency, monotonicity preservation) are incompatible with axioms it satisfies, so no RPR can do better. Open questions include behavior under strategic voters, extension to other distance functions and output formats, and whether the consistency criterion remains well-calibrated when the data collection process is non-stationary. For practitioners, the practical takeaway is that the 'best' rule can differ substantially across datasets (e.g., 21/25 elections have a zero-disagreement rule, but it varies), making a fixed default rule suboptimal.
