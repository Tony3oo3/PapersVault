## Problem

Few-step generation from flow-matching models suffers from significant quality degradation because the self-consistency loss (SL) in shortcut models only penalizes trajectory misalignment at the current time step, ignoring how that immediate deviation propagates and compounds in subsequent steps. This paper identifies this limitation through an optimal control formulation and shows that SL is a degenerate special case of a cumulative objective that should account for downstream effects along the entire generation trajectory.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262323/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes random Gaussian noise (x₀ ~ N(0,I)), a scalar step-size parameter d that conditions the network on the desired generation budget, and optionally a class label for conditional generation. It operates in a 4×32×32 latent space (for 256×256 pixel images via the Stable Diffusion VAE encoder) and produces a single denoised image. A single DiT backbone handles all step budgets (1, 2, 4, or 128 steps) by simply changing the step-size conditioning, eliminating the need for separate models per generation budget.

## Method

The paper formulates few-step generation as a controlled ODE where the shortcut model's velocity field deviates from the base flow-matching drift by an error term u_θ, and shows that the standard self-consistency loss corresponds to an optimal control objective with a Dirac-delta cost (penalizing error only at the current time). The proposed Cumulative Self-Consistency Loss (CSL) replaces this delta with a full trajectory integral of ‖u_θ‖² from the current time to t=1, so the gradient contains both an immediate term and a cumulative term that backpropagates future misalignment into the current step. In practice, CSL is estimated with R=2 rollout terms: the model takes a 2d shortcut step, then evaluates the consistency error at the resulting next state, causing the second step's gradient to flow back into the first step's parameters. Training jointly optimizes a standard flow-matching loss (for the d=0 base model) and the CSL loss (for the shortcut model) on a shared DiT backbone, with a B:K ratio balancing flow-matching and bootstrap targets per batch.

## Results

On CelebA-256, ST-CSL achieves FID-50K of 10.96 (2-step) and 18.37 (1-step), improving over the shortcut baseline (ST) by 1.6 and 2.1 points respectively. On CIFAR-10, ST-CSL reaches FID-50K of 9.24 (2-step) and 17.76 (1-step), outperforming ST by 2.55 and 2.04 points. On the more challenging ImageNet-256 class-conditional task, ST-CSL (B:K=1:1) achieves FID 17.35 (2-step) and 31.66 (1-step), narrowing the gap to the 128-step base model (FID 15.21) to within 2.1 and 16.5 points, while ST at the same ratio yields FID 32.22 and 51.78.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the shortcut model (ST), ST-CSL consistently wins on all few-step settings: on CIFAR-10 it improves 2-step FID from 11.79 to 9.24 (−2.55) and 1-step from 19.80 to 17.76 (−2.04); on CelebA-256 the gains are 12.56→10.96 (2-step) and 20.46→18.37 (1-step). Versus two-phase distillation methods, ST-CSL outperforms Progressive Distillation (PD) on 2-step (10.96 vs 16.73 CelebA; 9.24 vs 11.80 CIFAR) and 4-step (8.98 vs 14.49 CelebA; 8.10 vs 10.75 CIFAR), and beats Reflow on 2-step (10.96 vs 14.48 CelebA; 9.24 vs 15.59 CIFAR) and 4-step (8.98 vs 13.77 CelebA; 8.10 vs 14.92 CIFAR), while requiring only a single training phase. Against single-phase baselines, ST-CSL dramatically surpasses raw Flow Matching (FM) on few-step generation (e.g., 10.96 vs 112.1 on 2-step CelebA) and beats Consistency Training (CT) on 1-step CelebA (18.37 vs 33.2). On ImageNet-256, ST-CSL (B:K=1:1) outperforms MeanFlow (5% bootstrap) by a wide margin on 2-step (FID 17.35 vs 36.61) and 1-step (31.66 vs 45.12), and also beats ST at the same ratio (17.35 vs 32.22 on 2-step; 31.66 vs 51.78 on 1-step). The only setting where ST-CSL does not clearly win is 128-step CelebA (7.88 vs 7.83 for ST), where both are essentially equivalent since the base flow-matching loss dominates.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on shortcut models (Frans et al., 2025), which introduced the self-consistency loss (SL) to align larger-step trajectories with a base flow-matching model. The key conceptual departure is reframing few-step generation as a controlled dynamical system, showing that SL corresponds to a degenerate optimal control objective (a Dirac-delta-restricted cost at the current time step) and generalizing it to a cumulative self-consistency loss (CSL) that penalizes misalignment across the full trajectory. The paper also draws an explicit connection to on-policy reinforcement learning, interpreting the cumulative cost as a value function, which had not been made in prior few-step generation work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The optimal control framing provides the first principled theoretical justification for the SL loss, which was previously introduced without formal grounding, and naturally motivates the CSL generalization. The consistent improvements across model sizes (33M–458M), datasets (CelebA, CIFAR-10, ImageNet-256), and step counts (1, 2, 4) with only 6–10% extra training time make CSL a practical drop-in replacement for SL. Open questions include whether the RL connection can be fully exploited (e.g., learned value networks, policy gradient methods), whether intermediate state costs g(X_t, t) can be incorporated to steer trajectories, and whether the R=2 approximation remains sufficient for very few-step regimes or higher-dimensional data.
