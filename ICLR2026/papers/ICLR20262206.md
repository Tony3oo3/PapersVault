## Problem

While most efficient-attention research targets the scaled dot-product computation, the linear projection layers that produce Q, K, V tensors are often overlooked despite their input activations consuming up to 20% of peak GPU memory in attention blocks during training. Prior compression methods (e.g., CompAct, GaLore) exploit redundancy along the hidden dimension, but the paper identifies a far more substantial source of redundancy: the sequence dimension, where token activations in transformer models are highly correlated due to repeated patterns and local contextual similarity.

## Input / Output

During the forward pass, PAMM receives the full activation tensor X ∈ R^{b×n} (b = batch_size × seq_length, n = hidden_dim) feeding a Q, K, or V projection and replaces it with a compressed representation: k = ⌈r·b⌉ generator rows C ∈ R^{k×n} (as few as k=1 in finetuning), an assignment vector f ∈ [k]^b, and scaling coefficients α ∈ R^b. During backpropagation, given the upstream gradient ∇Z ∈ R^{b×m}, it produces an approximate weight gradient ∇W̃ ∈ R^{n×m} without ever materializing the full X, while the input gradient ∇X = ∇Z·W^T is computed exactly as usual. The forward pass and all other layers remain completely untouched.

## Method

PAMM approximates the matrix product O = A^T B (where A is the activation and B is the gradient) by treating rows of A as points in R^n and replacing each with its projection onto the line spanned by the closest of k randomly sampled generator rows. The assignment is computed via maximum absolute cosine similarity (Lemma 1), reducing it to a single b×k matmul plus argmax. The key computational trick avoids reconstructing the full compressed matrix: B is first contracted into a small k×m matrix B̃ via a parallel index_add, and the final product is computed as Õ = C^T B̃, costing O(kn(b+m)) instead of O(bnm). A normalization factor β = b/(b−η) corrects for rows dropped by the neighborhood condition (though empirically ε→∞, i.e., no dropping, gives best results). Theoretical analysis (Lemma 2) shows k only needs to scale as O(log(b/δ)) under uniform sampling, explaining why compression ratios as extreme as 1/512 preserve perplexity. PAMM is applied exclusively to Q, K, V projections during backpropagation and composes with FlashAttention, gradient checkpointing, and LoRA without modification.

## Results

PAMM compresses Q,K,V projection activations by up to 512× while maintaining or improving model quality. In pretraining LLaMA-1B on C4, PAMM at r=1/512 achieves 15.36 perplexity vs. 15.56 for the full-rank baseline, while reducing activation memory from 3 GB to 24 MB (99.2% reduction). For LLaMA-7B, PAMM-512 reaches 13.57 perplexity at 150K steps versus 14.61 for the baseline. In GLUE finetuning of RoBERTa-base, PAMM at r=1/128 achieves 86.11 average score (vs. 86.28 baseline) while cutting Q,K,V memory from 288 MB to 6.75 MB. Throughput degradation is below 2.7% for the 1B model and 2.1% for the 7B model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks PAMM against CompAct (Shamshoum et al., 2025) and Uniform-CRS (a uniform column-row sampling method equivalent to PAMM with ε=0) on LLaMA-60M Q,K,V projections. PAMM with ε=∞ dramatically outperforms both baselines at low compression ratios: while PAMM maintains near-baseline perplexity even at r=1/512, CompAct and Uniform-CRS suffer pronounced perplexity degradation as r shrinks below 1/32. The key advantage is that PAMM exploits redundancy along the sequence/batch dimension (storing representative token rows), whereas CompAct compresses along the hidden dimension using random Gaussian projections, which is a less redundant axis. In GLUE finetuning, PAMM at r=1/128 achieves 86.11 average score versus 86.28 for full fine-tuning (a 0.17-point gap) while reducing Q,K,V memory by 97.7%, and at r=1/256 achieves 86.18 with 98.8% memory reduction. Throughput analysis shows PAMM introduces only 2.1–2.7% overhead for 1B–7B models, making it a practical drop-in replacement.

## Relation to Prior Work

PAMM carves new ground by identifying the sequence (token) dimension as the primary axis of redundancy in QKV projection activations, in contrast to prior compression methods (CompAct, GaLore, Performer) that exploit redundancy along the hidden/embedding dimension. It departs from approximate-backward-pass work (Adelman et al., Liu et al., Approx-BP) by using a data-dependent, cosine-similarity-based assignment rather than uniform random sampling of rows/columns, and from clustering-based approaches by showing that random selection of generators is sufficient. The method is explicitly positioned as complementary to efficient attention techniques (FlashAttention, GQA, Linformer) that target the scaled dot-product computation itself, rather than the linear projections that feed into it.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PAMM offers a practical, drop-in 512× reduction in QKV activation memory with negligible perplexity impact, composable with FlashAttention, LoRA, and gradient checkpointing—making it immediately useful for practitioners training large models under memory constraints. Open questions include extending PAMM to other layers (FFN, output projection), jointly treating the shared activation between FlashAttention and the output projection, and whether more strategic generator selection (beyond random sampling) could push compression ratios even further. A key limitation is that the method only addresses the QKV projection activations (~20% of attention-block peak memory), leaving the broader activation memory landscape partially unaddressed.
