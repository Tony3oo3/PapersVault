## Problem

Prior model merging research has focused on designing better merging strategies (TIEs, DARE, task arithmetic) but has largely overlooked how the training paradigm used to create the individual task-specific models affects merging outcomes. This paper asks whether RL-trained LLMs, compared to SFT-trained ones, are inherently more suitable for parameter-space merging, and if so, what mechanisms explain the difference.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260856/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes pairs of independently fine-tuned LLMs (sharing a common base model such as Llama-3.1-8B, Llama-3.2-3B, or Mistral-Small-3-24B) trained on different verifiable tasks (math, code, instruction following, logical puzzles, ranking) and produces a single merged model via pairwise parameter-space operations. The critical input distinction is whether each task-specific model was trained with SFT or with one of three RL algorithms (PPO, GRPO, REINFORCE++), and merging is evaluated under four strategies (averaging, TIEs, task arithmetic, DARE) across all task pairs.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a systematic empirical and theoretical analysis identifying three mechanisms by which RL mitigates task conflicts during merging. First, on-policy data sampling in RL produces substantially smaller parameter update norms (e.g., 0.78 vs. 6.5 for SFT on math), reducing the risk of overwriting other tasks' knowledge. Second, the RL objective's advantage-based scaling—bounded by √Var(r) and provably converging to zero (Theorems 1–2)—naturally attenuates update magnitudes as training progresses, unlike SFT's fixed-intensity gradient steps. Third, joint optimization over both positive and negative samples steers the model toward an unbiased task-specific parameter subspace, further reducing cross-task conflicts. The authors validate each mechanism through performance landscape visualization, a formal conflict-norm analysis, and a controlled RL-Pos ablation that zeroes out negative-sample advantages to isolate the contribution of joint positive/negative optimization.

## Results

Across five tasks (Math, Code, IF, Puzzle, Rank) and four merging strategies, RL-trained (GRPO) models retain an average of 60.1–67.1% performance after merging (7.1–17% relative drop), whereas SFT-trained models drop to 47.9–50.6% (18–22% relative drop). The gap is most pronounced on Instruction Following, where SFT loses 23–27% but RL loses only 0–6.2%, and on Puzzle, where SFT degrades by up to 65% versus 24–56% for RL. The advantage holds consistently across all four merging methods (Averaging, TIEs, Arithmetic, DARE) and across three RL algorithms (GRPO, PPO, REINFORCE++) and three base models (Llama-3.2-3B, Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-Small-3-24B).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new merging method but instead compares two training paradigms (SFT vs. RL) under four existing merging strategies. Against Averaging (Choshen et al., 2022), RL-trained models lose only 17% on average versus 22% for SFT; against TIEs (Yadav et al., 2023), the gap widens to 7.1% vs. 19%; against Task-Arithmetic (Ilharco et al., 2023), RL drops 8.3% vs. 18% for SFT; and against DARE (Yu et al., 2024), RL drops 7.1% vs. 19%. Among RL algorithms, GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) achieves the best post-merging retention (e.g., −0.3% on IF with TIEs), slightly ahead of PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) at −2.5% and REINFORCE++ (Ahmadian et al., 2024) at −2.6% on the same task. The advantage of RL over SFT is consistent across all base models tested, with the largest absolute gains observed on Mistral-Small-3-24B where SFT drops 17.7% on IF after TIEs merging while GRPO actually improves by +0.4%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two well-developed threads: model merging strategies (TIEs, DARE, Task Arithmetic, model averaging) and LLM post-training paradigms (SFT vs. RL). Prior merging work has focused on improving the merging operator itself—pruning conflicting parameters, sign-based resolution, Fisher-information weighting—while implicitly assuming SFT-trained inputs. The paper departs from this by shifting the question from 'how to merge better' to 'how to train for better merging,' and provides the first systematic comparison of SFT- vs. RL-trained models under identical merging protocols. The theoretical analysis draws on standard RL convergence results (variance decay of bounded rewards, Bhatia-Davis inequality) and recent empirical observations that RL produces sparser parameter updates (Shenfeld et al., 2025; Mukherjee et al., 2025), but connects them to the specific phenomenon of cross-task parameter conflict in a way that prior work has not.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

For practitioners building multi-task LLMs via model merging, the finding that RL-trained models degrade far less after merging (7–17% vs. 18–22% average) provides a concrete, actionable design choice: prefer RL over SFT when the end goal is a merged generalist model. The 'enough is as good as a feast' characterization of RL's advantage decay offers a new mechanistic understanding of why RL's optimization dynamics are inherently conflict-averse. Open questions include whether the result extends to non-verifiable tasks (open-ended generation, creative writing) where reward signals are noisier, whether the advantage holds for multi-model merging beyond pairwise settings at scale, and how it interacts with more sophisticated merging methods that explicitly model task subspaces. The persistent 56% degradation on the puzzle task even under RL+averaging suggests the effect is task-dependent and not a universal guarantee.
