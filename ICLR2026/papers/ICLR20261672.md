## Problem

Robustness evaluation in Text-Attributed Graph (TAG) learning remains fragmented: prior benchmarks either omit GraphLLMs, use only shallow text embeddings, or cover limited attack types, leaving the field without a systematic understanding of how textual and structural vulnerabilities interact across model families. In particular, no prior work reveals the fundamental text-structure robustness trade-off or provides a single defense that handles both attack modalities simultaneously.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261672/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For GNN-based methods, each node's sequential text is first encoded into a fixed-dimensional feature vector (via RoBERTa, MiniLM, or BoW) and combined with the binary adjacency matrix; for GraphLLMs, raw node texts and neighbor texts are fed directly into prompt templates. The output is a class label from C categories, and in SFT-auto an additional binary attack-detection flag (text_attacked vs. normal) is produced per node. Evaluation uses 10 datasets across academic, web, social, and e-commerce domains under both inductive (60/20/20) and transductive (10/10/80) splits, with structural perturbations at 20–30% edge budget and textual perturbations replacing 40–80% of node texts via LLM-generated rewrites.

## Method

SFT-auto is a supervised fine-tuning framework built on Mistral-7B that overcomes the text-structure robustness trade-off through multi-task training with three sample types: normal node-neighbor pairs, text-attacked samples (center text replaced with different-class content, labeled 'text_attacked'), and recovery samples (center text removed entirely, forcing reliance on neighbors). At inference, a three-stage pipeline first detects text attacks via an extended (|C|+1)-class LLM classification and structural attacks via cosine-similarity thresholding against neighbors, then adaptively recovers: text-attacked nodes bypass corrupted text and classify from neighbors alone, structure-attacked nodes use their own text with filtered low-similarity neighbors removed, and normal nodes proceed with standard neighbor-inclusive classification. The key insight is that LLMs' multi-modal reasoning enables unified anomaly detection and prediction within a single model, whereas GNNs lack the linguistic sophistication for text anomaly detection (AutoGCN shows 6.2–17.4× worse detection). The paper also establishes a unified evaluation protocol spanning classical GNNs, RGNNs, and GraphLLMs under PGD/GRBCD structural attacks, LLM-generated textual attacks, and Text-GIA injection attacks, revealing that simple methods like GNNGuard become near-SOTA when paired with advanced text encoders.

## Results

The paper evaluates 20+ defense methods across 10 datasets using rank-based evaluation (averaging ranks across datasets for both absolute accuracy and relative accuracy drop). Key findings: (1) SFT-neighbor (GraphLLM) outperforms most RGNNs against structural evasion attacks without any defense mechanism; (2) GNNGuard, a simple similarity-filtering RGNN, achieves top-tier structural robustness when paired with RoBERTa embeddings; (3) against textual evasion attacks, naive GCN/GAT outperform structurally-robust methods like GNNGuard and RUNG; (4) under textual poisoning (80% training text replaced), SFT-neighbor's accuracy drops by 25% on CiteSeer while most GNNs suffer only 5–10% decrease. SFT-auto achieves the most balanced robustness, uniquely avoiding the text-structure trade-off that polarizes all other baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against structural evasion attacks (inductive, 20% perturbation), SFT-neighbor and GraphGPT (instruction-tuned GraphLLMs) outperform most RGNNs, while GNNGuard achieves top-tier performance when using RoBERTa embeddings — a finding that reverses its poor ranking under BoW/TF-IDF used in prior work. Against textual evasion attacks (40% test text replaced), the ranking inverts: simple GCN and GAT become the most robust, while GNNGuard, RUNG, SFT-neighbor, and GraphGPT all suffer significant degradation. Under textual poisoning (80% training text replaced, transductive), GNNs maintain 5–10% accuracy drop while SFT-neighbor drops 25% on CiteSeer, revealing GraphLLMs' dependence on high-quality training text. SFT-auto, the paper's proposed method, uniquely achieves balanced robustness against both structural and textual attacks simultaneously, whereas noise-injection variants (e.g., GraphGPT-noise, SFT-noisefull) and similarity-filtering variants (SFT-simf) each defend only one attack modality. AutoGCN (the GNN analogue of SFT-auto's detection pipeline) shows 6.2–17.4× worse textual anomaly detection than SFT-auto, confirming that LLM reasoning is essential for the detection-recovery framework.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three previously fragmented research threads: (1) robust GNN evaluation (GRB, Gosch et al.) that relied on shallow embeddings like BoW/TF-IDF and focused almost exclusively on structural attacks; (2) early GraphLLM robustness studies (TrustGLM, Olatunji et al.) that covered limited attack settings and excluded GNN/RGNN baselines; and (3) the SFT-neighbor framework (Wang et al., 2025b) from which the proposed SFT-auto directly extends by adding a multi-task detection-recovery pipeline. Conceptually, the paper departs from prior work by treating text and structure as co-equal attack surfaces within a single unified threat model, rather than evaluating them in isolation, and by showing that the choice of text encoder fundamentally changes which RGNN architectures appear effective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The text-structure robustness trade-off is a practically important finding: it means deploying a single 'robust' model is insufficient in adversarial TAG settings, and practitioners must either accept vulnerability to one modality or adopt a detection-based approach like SFT-auto. The benchmark itself (10 datasets, 4 domains, 3 model families, 6+ attack types) fills a clear gap and will likely become the standard reference for TAG robustness evaluation. Open questions include whether the auto-detection paradigm can be made to work with GNN backbones (the paper shows AutoGCN fails), whether SFT-auto scales to larger LLMs or multi-hop neighborhoods, and how the trade-off manifests under adaptive or hybrid attacks at scale.
