## Problem

Prior agent-privacy research focuses on static threats (under-specified user instructions or malicious environmental elements), but real-world agent-agent interactions involve dynamic, multi-turn adversarial dialogues where attackers actively and adaptively attempt to extract sensitive information. This evolving attack surface makes it difficult to identify vulnerabilities through manual analysis or enumeration, and no systematic methodology exists for discovering long-tail privacy risks in such interactive settings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263331/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a privacy norm (e.g., 'the data sender must not share the data subject's sensitive information with the data recipient via email') and transforms it into a simulation configuration specifying three ReAct agents (data subject, data sender, data recipient) with contextual memories and optimizable instructions, operating within mock applications (Gmail, Messenger, Notion). The LLM optimizer consumes full multi-turn simulation trajectories (not just scalar scores) to understand failure modes and propose revised agent instructions. The output consists of natural-language attack and defense instructions that can be directly plugged into agent prompts, evaluated by a leak velocity metric that penalizes both whether and how quickly each sensitive item is leaked across K items.

## Method

The core contribution is an alternating search framework that co-evolves attack and defense strategies by using LLMs as optimizers reflecting on simulation trajectories. Attack search employs N parallel threads (default N=30), each independently refining its instruction through reflection on the highest-leak-velocity trajectories, with cross-thread propagation that broadcasts the globally best strategy to all threads whenever a new record is set, balancing exploration diversity with exploitation of breakthroughs. Defense search is single-threaded but evaluates the candidate defense simultaneously across all Q training scenarios, leveraging multi-task pressure to produce a universal defense that generalizes to unseen attacks. The leak velocity metric s = (1/K)Σ(1 − log(l_i)/(log(l_i)+1)) provides a fine-grained, differentiable-like signal that rewards both preventing leakage and delaying it, which is critical because many proposed attacks produce zero observable signal. The framework discovers a progression from direct requests → fake urgency/authority → multi-turn impersonation with consent forgery on the attack side, and from basic monitoring → rule-based consent verification → strict identity-verification state machines on the defense side.

## Results

With basic instructions, all backbone models exhibit nontrivial privacy leakage (31.2% average leak velocity for gpt-4.1-mini as both attacker and defender). The alternating search dramatically escalates risk: the discovered attack A1 raises leak velocity to 76.0% against the basic defense D0, while the evolved defense D1 reduces it to 2.5%. A second attack iteration (A2, using multi-turn impersonation and consent forgery) pushes leak velocity back to 42.2% against D1, and the final state-machine defense D2 brings it down to 7.1%. Cross-model transfer shows attacks generalize well across backbones (A1 outperforms A0 in most settings), while detailed defenses like D2 require strong instruction-following capability to transfer effectively to weaker models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baseline methods but instead establishes internal baselines through the search progression. Basic instructions (A0, D0) yield 3.4% leak velocity, while the searched attack A1 (fake urgency, invented authority, prosocial framing) increases this to 76.0%—a 22× escalation. The searched defense D1 (rule-based consent verification) reduces leak velocity to 2.5%, but the second-generation attack A2 (multi-turn impersonation with consent forgery) circumvents it, raising leak velocity to 42.2%. The final defense D2 (state-machine with identity verification) achieves 7.1%, substantially outperforming a directly generated comprehensive defense instruction that the authors tested in Appendix L (which achieves 46.3% leak velocity against A2, comparable to the unoptimized D0). Cross-model transfer reveals that attacks generalize well across backbones (A1 > A0 in nearly all settings), whereas the detailed D2 defense degrades on weaker models like gpt-4.1-nano (16.1% vs. 7.1%), suggesting strong instruction-following is required for complex protocol-based defenses.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the agent privacy evaluation line (PrivacyLens, ConfAIde, AgentDam) from static, single-turn, or environment-injection threat models to dynamic multi-turn adversarial agent-agent dialogues, where an attacker agent actively and iteratively attempts to extract sensitive information. Methodologically, it builds on LLM-as-optimizer (Yang et al., 2023) for reflection-based instruction refinement and on the LM-emulated sandbox (Ruan et al., 2023) for the simulation environment, but departs by framing privacy risk discovery itself as a search problem with parallel threads and cross-thread propagation, rather than relying on manually designed attack scenarios. The alternating attack-defense co-evolution draws on adversarial training intuitions but operates entirely in the prompt/instruction space without any gradient-based or weight-level updates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As LLM agents increasingly mediate interpersonal information exchange, the agent-agent adversarial privacy threat identified here is likely to become a first-order deployment concern, and this paper provides the first systematic, scalable methodology for surfacing such vulnerabilities before they are exploited in the wild. The discovered attack patterns (multi-turn impersonation, consent forgery) and defense mechanisms (state-machine identity verification) are directly actionable for agent developers, and the cross-model and cross-scenario transferability results suggest the framework can serve as a reusable red-teaming tool. Key limitations include the simulation-to-reality gap (only a 5-trial case study), the restriction to prompt-based defenses, and the assumption that the data subject never grants consent during simulation; open questions include whether the search methodology extends to agent architecture or training-objective optimization, and how defenses should behave when the data subject sometimes legitimately consents.
