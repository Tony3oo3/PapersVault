## Problem

Pass@k and its variants produce unstable, potentially misleading model rankings when the number of trials is limited, as is common on small or costly benchmarks like AIME. These metrics lack closed-form uncertainty estimates, offer no principled decision rule for distinguishing meaningful performance gaps from noise, and cannot naturally handle graded or rubric-based (non-binary) evaluation outcomes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20265041/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes an M×N results matrix R where each entry R_{αi} is an integer category label in {0, …, C} (binary correctness when C=1, or richer rubric levels for graded evaluation), a weight vector w = (w_0, …, w_C) defining the desired performance metric, and optionally an M×D prior results matrix R⁰ encoding evidence from related models. It outputs a closed-form posterior mean μ and standard deviation σ for the weighted performance metric, from which model rankings with 95% credible intervals are derived via a z-score threshold rule (two models share a rank if |μ−μ′|/√(σ²+σ′²) falls below the chosen z threshold). No iterative optimization or Monte Carlo sampling is required; the entire computation is O(M(N+D+C)).

## Method

The framework models each question's N trial outcomes as a (C+1)-category multinomial draw with a Dirichlet conjugate prior, yielding a Dirichlet posterior whose moments are computed in closed form via the moment-generating function expressed through the confluent Lauricella hypergeometric function, expanded to O(t²). This gives exact expressions for the posterior mean μ and variance σ² of any weighted rubric π̄ = (1/M)Σ_α w·π_α, valid for all finite M and N without relying on the Central Limit Theorem. A key theoretical result shows that under a uniform prior, μ is a positive affine transformation of the naive weighted average accuracy (avg@N), making their rankings identical at all finite N while the Bayesian form additionally provides well-calibrated credible intervals. The framework supports incorporating prior evidence from related models (e.g., an older or non-quantized version) through the R⁰ matrix, and naturally extends to multi-category rubrics (format-aware, confidence-penalized, efficiency-adjusted schemas) without ad hoc aggregation.

## Results

On all four math-reasoning benchmarks (AIME'24, AIME'25, HMMT'25, BrUMO'25), Bayes@N reaches Kendall's τ > 0.90 with only N=10 trials and approaches τ ≈ 1 by N=80, while Pass@k variants (k=2,4,8) and their extensions converge substantially more slowly. Mean convergence @n (the smallest trial count at which the ranking stabilizes to the 80-trial gold standard) is approximately 44.2 for HMMT'25 and 27.1 for BrUMO'25 under Bayes@N, compared to ~69.5 and ~48.5 for the best-performing Pass@k scores. On AIME'24 and AIME'25, the Pass@k family frequently fails to converge within 80 trials, whereas Bayes@N converges reliably (AIME'25 plateaus at τ ≈ 0.95 due to tight model separations). In biased-coin simulations with known ground truth, Bayes@N reaches τ = 1 far faster than all Pass@k variants, and incorporating 95% credible intervals correctly identifies statistically indistinguishable model pairs (e.g., LLM₉ vs LLM₁₀ with π̄ = 0.608 vs 0.6213 requires ~200 trials for 95% CI).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares Bayes@N against three families of Pass@k-based metrics: the original Pass@k (Chen et al., 2021), Pass^k (Yao et al., 2024), and G-Pass@k_τ̃ / mG-Pass@k (Liu et al., 2024). Bayes@N outperforms all three on every dataset for rank convergence (Kendall's τ vs. gold standard): at N=10, Bayes@N already exceeds τ > 0.90 while Pass@k variants remain well below, and the gap persists through N=80. On AIME'24 and AIME'25, Pass@k (k=2,4,8) and its variants frequently fail to converge to the gold-standard ranking within 80 trials, whereas Bayes@N converges in all cases. For mean convergence @n, Bayes@N requires ~44.2 trials on HMMT'25 and ~27.1 on BrUMO'25, compared to ~69.5 and ~48.5 for the best Pass@k scores—a 36–44% reduction in required samples. The only dataset where Bayes@N does not fully converge is AIME'25 (τ plateaus at ~0.95), which the authors attribute to extremely tight model separations (e.g., 0.608 vs 0.6213) requiring ~200 trials for 95% CI. Notably, avg@N is proven rank-equivalent to Bayes@N under a uniform prior, so the practical advantage of the Bayesian framework lies in its closed-form uncertainty quantification, categorical generalization, and prior incorporation rather than in a different point estimate.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reframes LLM benchmark evaluation as a Bayesian inference problem, building on the Dirichlet-multinomial conjugate framework (Hayden & Armitage, 2025) and the generalized Laplace rule of succession from Jaynes (2003). It departs from the dominant Pass@k paradigm (Chen et al., 2021) and its recent stricter variants (Pass^k, G-Pass@k_τ̃, mG-Pass@k) by replacing point-estimate metrics with posterior means and credible intervals. A key theoretical result is that under a uniform prior, the Bayesian posterior mean is order-equivalent to simple average accuracy (avg@N), which retroactively explains avg@N's empirical robustness while adding principled, closed-form uncertainty that CLT-based methods fail to provide in small-sample regimes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Pass@k is the de facto standard for reporting LLM reasoning performance, yet this paper shows it produces unstable rankings and fails to converge on small benchmarks like AIME within 80 trials, while the proposed Bayes@N converges reliably with far fewer samples. The practical finding that distinguishing two models with true accuracies of 0.608 vs. 0.6213 requires ~200 trials for 95% confidence underscores how much current leaderboards overstate precision. Open questions include practical guidelines for informative priors (e.g., from older model versions or base models), extension beyond math reasoning to open-ended tasks, and whether the categorical rubric framework generalizes to non-verifiable evaluations. The framework is limited in validation to four math benchmarks with up to 80 trials and 20 models, so broader applicability remains to be established.
