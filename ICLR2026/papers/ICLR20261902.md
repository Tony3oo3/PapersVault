## Problem

Dominant Generalized Category Discovery (GCD) methods enforce compact clustering as a universal objective, which is geometry-agnostic: it indiscriminately compresses intra-class variability, collapses token manifolds into a few principal directions, and produces anisotropic, low-rank feature distributions that mask fine-grained semantics and amplify open-world errors such as spurious class merges. The paper argues the bottleneck is not insufficient compactness but the loss of geometric quality, and that GCD requires geometric restoration of an over-flattened feature space rather than further compression.

## Input / Output

The method takes a labeled set of images from known classes (50% of known-class images) and an unlabeled set mixing known and novel classes, processed through a pretrained DINO ViT-B/16 encoder with a projection head. BIA operates on the [cls] token embeddings extracted per image, stacking them into a B×d batch matrix after ℓ2-normalization or LayerNorm. It produces cluster assignments for all unlabeled samples (evaluated via Hungarian matching into All/Old/New accuracy) and, when integrated with CMS, an estimated number of classes K with reduced estimation error.

## Method

BIA minimizes the Bures distance between the batch class-token Gram matrix Σ_B = ZZ^T and the identity, which under the mild trace constraint (tr(Σ_B) ≈ B from normalization) is equivalent to maximizing the nuclear norm ‖Z‖_* of the stacked class tokens. The implementation is two lines of PyTorch: compute the SVD of the batch class-token matrix and subtract λ·sum(singular_values) from the base GCD loss. This spectrum-shaping objective is Schur-concave, redistributing eigenvalue energy from dominant directions to collapsed ones, thereby homogenizing the eigen-spectrum, raising von Neumann entropy, and increasing effective rank of the class-token autocorrelation. BIA is architecture-agnostic and plug-and-play, requiring no changes to backbones, loss schedules, or training protocols, and adds less than 1.5% computational overhead. It is validated as a drop-in addition to contrastive (GCD, CMS) and prototype-based (SimGCD, SPTNet, SelEx) GCD frameworks across six benchmarks.

## Results

BIA is a plug-and-play loss that consistently improves strong GCD baselines across six benchmarks. On fine-grained datasets (Table 1), average gains over four baselines (SelEx, SimGCD, CMS, SPTNet) are +2.2% All / +3.2% New on CUB, +1.4% All / +1.1% New on Stanford Cars, and +1.8% All / +2.0% New on FGVC Aircraft. On coarse-grained datasets (Table 2), average gains are +1.5% All / +2.2% New on ImageNet100 and +0.3% All / +0.4% New on CIFAR100. Without ground-truth K (Table 3), CMS+BIA improves CUB All accuracy by +2.5% and CIFAR100 New by +3.8%, while also reducing class-number estimation error (e.g., ImageNet100 K error drops from 2% to 0%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

BIA is evaluated as an additive loss on top of four strong GCD baselines (SelEx, SimGCD, CMS, SPTNet) and compared against two isotropy-promoting SSL regularizers (VICReg, CorInfoMax). Against SimGCD, BIA yields +1.4 to +2.6% on novel-class accuracy across fine-grained datasets and +3.4% All on ImageNet100. Against CMS, the largest single gain is +4.0% All on CUB (67.1→71.1) and +3.7% New on CUB (63.2→66.9); on ImageNet100, CMS+BIA improves New accuracy by +2.9%. Against SPTNet, BIA gives the most consistent gains across all three fine-grained datasets (+1.3 to +3.1% All). Against SelEx, improvements are smaller but positive (+1.9% All on CUB, +1.0% All on Aircraft). In the head-to-head comparison with VICReg and CorInfoMax (Table 5), BIA outperforms both: on SimGCD+CUB, BIA reaches 62.1% All vs. 61.1% (VICReg) and 60.7% (CorInfoMax); on CMS+CUB, BIA reaches 71.1% All vs. 68.3% (VICReg) and 65.7% (CorInfoMax). GCD (Vaze et al.) and PrCAL (Zhang et al.) serve as lower-bound references in the tables but are not directly augmented with BIA in the main experiments.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself within the established Generalized Category Discovery (GCD) literature, where methods like SimGCD, CMS, and SPTNet rely on compact clustering objectives. Rather than building on a specific prior GCD method, it repurposes the Bures distance from quantum information theory as a geometry-restoring regularizer, drawing a conceptual parallel between representational completeness in quantum states and class-token manifold quality in GCD. The nuclear-norm equivalence under a trace constraint connects this to the broader representation-completeness literature (DirectCLR, whitening, Bregman divergence) but applies it specifically at the batch class-token Gram level where GCD decisions are made, distinguishing it from instance-level or coordinate-level regularizers like VICReg and CorInfoMax.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete geometric failure mode in GCD (over-compression causing dimensional collapse) and provides a minimal, architecture-agnostic fix that consistently improves novel-class accuracy and class-number estimation across six benchmarks. The Bures-to-nuclear-norm equivalence offers a clean theoretical bridge between information geometry and practical optimization, and the plug-and-play nature (two lines of code) makes it immediately adoptable. Limitations include marginal gains on low-quality embedding datasets (CIFAR100, Herbarium19) where the underlying features lack sufficient discriminative capacity for isotropy to help, and the method's benefit may diminish if future GCD frameworks already incorporate strong geometry-aware objectives. Open questions include whether the isotropic target is universally optimal or whether task-specific anisotropy could be beneficial, and whether the approach extends to video, multimodal, or continual GCD settings.
