## Problem

Deep learning models for protein-ligand binding affinity prediction require large volumes of 3D structural data paired with experimentally measured affinities, but existing datasets (PDBbind, PLINDER, CrossDocked) are limited in scale, lack complete affinity annotations, or exclude experimentally verified potency values. This scarcity of high-quality structure-affinity pairs constrains the training and evaluation of structure-based deep learning methods for drug discovery.

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes protein amino acid sequences (retrieved from UniProt by accession) and ligand SMILES strings (standardized via RDKit: salt removal, neutral-pH protonation, canonicalization) along with bioactivity values (IC50, Ki, Kd) from ChEMBL35 and BindingDB. It produces 5,244,285 predicted 3D structures (5 per complex, stored as .cif files) for 1,048,857 unique protein-ligand systems, each annotated with pIC50, Boltz-1x confidence metrics (iPTM, iPLDDT, interaction PTM), and PoseBusters validation flags. All complexes with experimentally solved PDB structures are explicitly excluded to prevent data leakage, and proteins exceeding 2000 residues or ligands exceeding 1250 Da are filtered out.

## Method

SAIR is constructed by curating bioactivity data from ChEMBL35 and BindingDB (applying filters for missing SMILES/potency, multi-target entries, inequality values, and a 1 pM–100 µM dynamic range), then computationally folding every protein-ligand complex using Boltz-1x, an MIT-licensed AlphaFold 3 implementation that adds a guiding potential to the diffusion process to reduce steric clashes. Each complex is folded with 5 independent samples, 3 recycling steps, and 200 diffusion sampling steps, with MSAs generated via MMseqs2 against UniRef30 and ColabFoldDB. The resulting structures are validated with PoseBusters (~3.17% structure-level failure, predominantly internal energy violations), and the dataset is benchmarked by evaluating empirical scoring functions (Vina, Vinardo via GNINA), a 3D CNN (OnionNet2), and a GNN (AEV-PLIG) on the synthetic structures, revealing that ML methods outperform scoring functions but none achieve high correlation with ground-truth pIC50, suggesting the need for fine-tuning on synthetic structure distributions.

## Results

Across all assay types, the GNN (AEV-PLIG) achieves the highest binding affinity prediction performance, followed by the 3D CNN (OnionNet2), with empirical scoring functions (Vina, Vinardo) performing worst. However, no method achieves a high correlation with ground-truth pIC50; the best Spearman correlations are comparable to the Boltz-1x interface confidence metric iPTM (r_s = 0.27) and the custom interaction PTM (r_s = 0.25). PoseBusters validation shows only ~3.17% of the 5.24M structures fail any check, with internal energy violations accounting for over half of all failures. External validation by GatorAffinity (Wei et al., 2025) confirms SAIR's utility: pre-training on SAIR reduces PDBbind RMSE from 1.343 to 1.293.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks four binding affinity prediction approaches on SAIR's synthetic structures: AEV-PLIG (GNN), OnionNet2 (3D CNN), Vina and Vinardo (empirical scoring functions via GNINA). AEV-PLIG consistently outperforms OnionNet2, which in turn outperforms both empirical scoring functions across all correlation metrics (Spearman, Pearson, Kendall) and AUC. However, the performance gap between the GNN and CNN is modest, and all methods achieve only moderate correlations—comparable to the raw iPTM confidence score from the folding model itself, which was not trained for affinity prediction. Filtering to high-confidence structures (Boltz-1x confidence > 0.8) improves performance for nearly all methods, confirming that structural quality is a key bottleneck. A family-balanced evaluation (inverse-frequency weighting) confirms the ranking is not an artifact of over-represented kinase targets. The authors note that both ML models were originally trained on experimental structures, so fine-tuning on SAIR's synthetic distribution would likely close the remaining gap.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the 'distillation' paradigm—using a powerful generative model to augment limited experimental data—previously applied to protein structure prediction (AlphaFold, Chai-1, NeuralPlexer) to the protein-ligand co-folding setting, leveraging Boltz-1x (an open-source AlphaFold 3 implementation) to generate 5M+ structures. It fills a specific gap in the landscape of protein-ligand datasets: CrossDocked offers scale but no affinity labels, PLINDER provides experimental structures with partial affinity data at ~450K scale, and DockGen offers synthetic structures without affinity. By pairing Boltz-1x-generated structures with curated IC50/Ki/Kd values from ChEMBL and BindingDB, SAIR is the first dataset to combine synthetic 3D structures with experimentally measured binding potencies at over one million complex scale, and it explicitly excludes PDB-verified systems to prevent data leakage.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SAIR addresses a fundamental bottleneck in structure-based drug discovery: the scarcity of paired 3D structures and binding affinities for training deep learning models. The key open question the paper surfaces is whether models trained on experimental structures can generalize to synthetic ones—the benchmarking shows they cannot without fine-tuning, and the independent GatorAffinity validation confirms that pre-training on SAIR materially improves downstream performance (RMSE 1.293 vs 1.343 on PDBbind). Limitations include inheriting Boltz-1x's inductive biases, the absence of ground-truth structures for RMSD validation (by design, to prevent leakage), and the monomeric-only treatment that excludes cofactors and quaternary structure. The dataset also opens avenues for inverse design and self-distillation of co-folding models, but the field still needs architectures specifically optimized for the synthetic structure distribution.
