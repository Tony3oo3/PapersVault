## Problem

Existing MLLM benchmarks emphasize STEM-style vertical reasoning (sequential logical deduction toward a single answer) and general knowledge, while overlooking the Humanities and Social Sciences, which demand horizontal, interdisciplinary reasoning and the ability to connect abstract cultural, historical, or economic concepts with their visual representations. This paper identifies that even state-of-the-art MLLMs fail to establish meaningful mappings between HSS-related images and the abstract concepts they encode, revealing a critical blind spot in current evaluation.

## Input / Output

Each sample pairs an RGB image with a text question in one of six UN official languages (English, Chinese, French, Russian, Spanish, Arabic). Questions are presented in two formats: multiple-choice (one correct answer among plausible distractors) and open-ended (no options provided). A key design constraint is strict multimodal dependency: the question cannot be answered from text alone, and the image alone cannot reveal the correct answer, ensuring both modalities are necessary. The benchmark comprises 13,152 samples across 6 categories (Geography, Economy, Culture, Social Sciences, History, Art) and 45 subtypes.

## Method

The core contribution is a three-stage VQA Generation Pipeline (VGP) that collaborates domain experts with a multi-agent LLM framework (using GPT-4o and GPT-4.1 with Chain-of-Thought prompting). Stage I (Preparation) collects and screens multimodal raw materials via both expert curation and a Networked Information-Aggregation Agent that retrieves and filters web data against discipline-specific keyword indices. Stage II (Construction) generates questions through two parallel paths: experts hand-craft and revise items, while a multi-agent pipeline (summarizer → extractor → question generator → image matcher) automates large-scale question formulation guided by human-authored exemplars. Stage III (Validation) enforces quality through agent-based deduplication and a critical multimodal-dependency check (rejecting questions answerable from a single modality), followed by cross-expert peer review for accuracy, ambiguity, and cultural sensitivity. Data that fails validation is iterated back to Stage II or discarded.

## Results

On HSSBench (English, COT prompting, multiple-choice), the best open-source model Qwen2.5-VL-72B-Instruct achieves 51.87% overall accuracy, while the best closed-source model GPT-4.1 reaches 42.66%. In the open-ended setting (no answer options), GPT-4.1 leads with 39.97%, nearly double that of GPT-4o (19.36%), while the best open-source model (Qwen2.5-VL-72B) drops to 19.73%. Human experts score 93.83% overall, and all MLLMs fall well below 60% accuracy, confirming HSSBench poses significant challenges. Economy is the hardest category (lowest average scores) while Geography is the easiest across all models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the 20+ MLLMs evaluated, Qwen2.5-VL-72B-Instruct achieves the highest multiple-choice accuracy (51.87% Ct.C.), outperforming GPT-4o (46.88%) and GPT-4.1 (42.66%) by 5 and 9 points respectively, suggesting open-source models can be competitive in HSS multiple-choice tasks. However, in the open-ended setting, GPT-4.1 dominates with 39.97% (Ct.O.), nearly doubling GPT-4o's 19.36% and far exceeding the best open-source model (Qwen2.5-VL-72B at 19.73%), indicating closed-source models retain a significant advantage when answer options are removed. GPT-4.1-mini (48.03% Ct.C.) also surpasses GPT-4o (46.88%) in multiple-choice, showing the 4.1 series generally improves over 4o. Compared to related benchmarks (CMMMU, MME, MMMU), HSSBench yields consistently lower model accuracy, confirming it is more challenging and better captures HSS-specific complexity. COT prompting does not uniformly help: some models (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL-72B) score higher with direct answers (54.17% Dr.C. vs. 51.87% Ct.C.), while GPT-4.1 benefits substantially from COT in open-ended tasks (39.97% vs. 25.38%), revealing that longer reasoning can introduce hallucinations in HSS contexts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a new evaluation domain (Humanities and Social Sciences) within the established multimodal VQA benchmark paradigm, departing from the STEM-centric 'vertical reasoning' framing that dominates prior work (MMMU, MathVista, ScienceQA). Conceptually, it repurposes the multi-agent LLM pipeline idea—previously used for general data generation—into a domain-specific construction framework where expert annotation logic is encoded into specialized agent roles (summarizer, extractor, question generator, image matcher). The 'horizontal reasoning' framing, emphasizing cross-disciplinary connections and multi-interpretation validity rather than single-answer logical deduction, positions HSS evaluation as fundamentally distinct from the sequential-reasoning tasks that prior benchmarks target.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

HSSBench fills a clear gap: prior multimodal benchmarks have largely ignored the HSS domain, leaving MLLMs' ability to connect abstract cultural, historical, and social concepts with visual representations unmeasured. The finding that even GPT-4.1 scores below 40% on open-ended HSS questions, and that COT prompting can degrade rather than improve performance, challenges the assumption that extended reasoning universally benefits multimodal tasks. The failure of RAG to consistently help suggests the bottleneck is not knowledge retrieval but the model's inability to internalize and transfer domain-specific visual-semantic mappings. Open questions include whether specialized HSS retrieval corpora or fine-tuning on cross-modal cultural data could close the gap, and whether the multilingual evaluation reveals language-specific reasoning weaknesses.
