## Problem

Spike cameras emit asynchronous, binary, high-throughput intensity streams that are fundamentally incompatible with frame-based stereo algorithms, and converting them to frames introduces temporal quantization errors and motion blur that negate the sensor's advantages. Unlike event cameras (which capture differential luminance changes), spike cameras use an integral sampling model, yet no specialized stereo algorithm or benchmark exists for estimating depth directly from raw spike streams.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260392/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes raw binary spike streams S ∈ {0,1}^{N×H×W} from a calibrated stereo pair of spike cameras (400×250 spatial resolution, 20,000 Hz temporal resolution), where each frame is a binary spiking pattern produced by threshold-based photon accumulation. It outputs a disparity map at 1/4 resolution refined over 16 iterative RSNN steps, then upsampled via convex combination to full-resolution depth. The synthetic dataset provides 76,500 spike stream pairs with pixel-wise depth ground truth from Blender-rendered scenes, while the real dataset offers 3,000+ stereo spike pairs annotated by a Kinect depth sensor.

## Method

SpikeStereoNet follows a RAFT-Stereo-style pipeline—multi-scale feature extraction, 4-level correlation pyramid, and iterative disparity refinement—but replaces the standard ConvGRU updater with a three-layer recurrent spiking neural network (RSNN) using a novel Adaptive Leaky Integrate-and-Fire (ALIF) neuron. The ALIF neuron introduces three input-dependent adaptive scalars (α for membrane potential retention, β for firing threshold, γ for soft reset) that dynamically modulate neuronal dynamics, drawing on neuroscience findings that these parameters vary with context in biological circuits. The RSNN combines recurrent intra-layer connections with feedforward inter-layer connections to expand receptive fields across scales, and at the finest resolution incorporates local cost volumes and current disparity estimates. Training uses BPTT with a sigmoid surrogate gradient and a composite loss combining weighted L1 disparity error, firing-rate regularization toward a target rate, and membrane-potential voltage regularization to enforce temporal sparsity. A Banach fixed-point analysis proves the RSNN update is a contraction mapping (Lipschitz constant < 1), guaranteeing linear convergence to a unique fixed point, and the model is trained on synthetic data then domain-adapted to real spike streams via fine-tuning.

## Results

On the synthetic spike dataset, SpikeStereoNet achieves the best performance across all metrics: 8.41% bad-1.0, 4.13% bad-2.0, 2.38% bad-3.0, and 0.42 px AvgErr, outperforming the strongest frame-based baseline Selective-Stereo (9.24/4.57/2.66/0.45) and the event-based ZEST (11.10/4.94/3.50/0.62). On the real stereo spike dataset, SpikeStereoNet attains 5.33% bad-2.0, 3.19% bad-3.0, and 0.56 px AvgErr, again the best among all compared methods. The model also maintains competitive efficiency with 12.15M parameters and 473B FLOPs, ranking second in FLOPs while using fewer parameters than most competitors.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SpikeStereoNet outperforms all 10 baselines on every reported metric on both the synthetic and real datasets. On the synthetic set, the closest competitor is Selective-Stereo (CVPR 2024), which SpikeStereoNet beats by 0.83 pp on bad-1.0, 0.44 pp on bad-2.0, 0.28 pp on bad-3.0, and 0.03 px on AvgErr; MonSter (CVPR 2025) is similarly surpassed by 0.82/0.51/0.34 pp and 0.04 px. Among event-based methods, ZEST (NeurIPS 2024) and StereoSpike (IEEE Access 2022) trail significantly, with AvgErr of 0.62 and 1.10 px respectively, confirming that temporal-contrast-based event stereo does not transfer well to integrated-intensity spike streams. On the real dataset, SpikeStereoNet leads Selective-Stereo by 0.17 pp on bad-2.0 and 0.24 pp on bad-3.0, and outperforms DLNR (CVPR 2023) by 0.31/0.19 pp and 0.05 px AvgErr. Notably, SpikeStereoNet achieves these gains with only 12.15M parameters and 473B FLOPs—substantially fewer than MonSter (388.69M / 1567B) and DLNR (57.83M / 1580B)—while also achieving the highest inference speed (1.91 FPS) among all compared methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

SpikeStereoNet adapts the iterative refinement paradigm of RAFT-Stereo (Teed & Deng, 2020; Lipson et al., 2021) to the spike-camera domain, replacing the standard ConvGRU update with a biologically-inspired adaptive leaky integrate-and-fire (ALIF) recurrent spiking neural network. It draws on the SNN literature—surrogate-gradient training (Neftci et al., 2019), adaptive recurrent SNNs (Yin et al., 2021), and Izhikevich-type neuron dynamics (Izhikevich, 2003)—while departing from event-based stereo methods (Zhou et al., 2021; Lou et al., 2024; Rançon et al., 2022) that operate on DVS temporal-contrast signals rather than integrated-intensity spike streams. The paper also builds on the broader spike-camera vision line (Zhang et al., 2022a; Zhao et al., 2024a) but is the first to formulate and benchmark stereo depth estimation directly from raw spike streams without frame conversion.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By providing the first dedicated synthetic and real-world benchmarks for spike-stream stereo, the paper opens a previously unaddressed evaluation space and demonstrates that spike cameras can support robust depth estimation in textureless and extreme-lighting regions where frame-based methods degrade. The data-efficiency result (competitive accuracy at 10% training data) suggests the ALIF-RSNN inductive bias may be particularly well-suited to the sparse, asynchronous structure of spike inputs, though this is validated only on a single synthetic domain. Open questions include scalability to outdoor and large-baseline scenes, real-time deployment on neuromorphic hardware, and whether the biological framing (adaptive thresholds, soft reset) provides genuine inductive advantages over well-tuned GRU/LSTM baselines beyond the modest gaps observed here.
