## Problem

Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM) approximates the worst-case loss direction by applying the gradient at a perturbed ascent point to the current parameters, yet a direct, intuitive explanation for why this nonlocal gradient operation is effective has been lacking. The paper further reveals that this approximation is often inaccurate, unstable across training, and degrades as the number of ascent steps increases, explaining the unexpectedly poor performance of multi-step SAM.

## Input / Output

XSAM takes the same inputs as SAM: current model parameters, a mini-batch of training data, and the loss function, plus hyperparameters for perturbation radius ρ, neighborhood radius ρ_m, and the number of ascent steps k. The key additional output is a dynamically estimated interpolation factor α* that specifies the optimal direction toward the local maximum within a 2D hyperplane; this factor is updated epoch-wise (or at a configurable frequency) and requires only 20–40 forward passes per update, adding less than 2.5% overhead over SAM. The final output is updated model parameters that converge to flatter minima, verified via Hessian spectrum analysis and loss-landscape visualization.

## Method

XSAM replaces SAM's implicit gradient-direction approximation with an explicit probe of the loss surface. After performing k≥1 gradient ascent steps to reach the final ascent point ϑ_k, XSAM defines a 2D hyperplane spanned by the normalized displacement vector v_0 = (ϑ_k − ϑ_0)/‖ϑ_k − ϑ_0‖ and the normalized gradient v_1 = g_k/‖g_k‖ at ϑ_k. Within this hyperplane, candidate directions are parameterized via spherical linear interpolation v(α), and the optimal α* is found by maximizing the loss L(ϑ_0 + ρ_m · v(α)) over a sampled range of α. The parameters are then updated along −v(α*) scaled by ‖g_k‖, directly escaping the estimated maximum. The 2D hyperplane definition is crucial: it guarantees the highest-known-loss point lies within the search space, unifies single-step and multi-step settings, and avoids the inaccuracy of directly applying g_k to ϑ_0. A theoretical proposition under second-order approximation confirms that the SAM gradient provably outperforms SGD in approximating the maximum direction, while also proving that better approximations exist beyond the SAM gradient, motivating the explicit search.

## Results

On CIFAR-100 with ResNet-18 (single-step), XSAM achieves 81.24% test accuracy, outperforming SAM (80.93%) and SGD (78.55%). On Tiny-ImageNet with ResNet-18, XSAM reaches 63.82% vs. SAM's 62.81%. In the multi-step setting (k=4) on CIFAR-100/ResNet-18, XSAM maintains 81.37% while SAM degrades to 80.65%. On larger-scale tasks, XSAM improves over SAM by 0.18% on ImageNet/ResNet-50 (77.22 vs. 77.04), 0.33 BLEU on IWSLT2014/Transformer (35.63 vs. 35.30), and 0.52% on CIFAR-100/ViT-Ti (68.32 vs. 67.80).

## Baselines & Comparisons

XSAM consistently outperforms SAM (Foret et al., 2020) across all datasets, architectures, and settings, with gains of 0.2–1.0% in single-step and up to 0.72% in multi-step (k=4) configurations. Against ASAM (Kwon et al., 2021), XSAM achieves 81.24% vs. 81.11% on CIFAR-100/ResNet-18, and the combination XSAM+ASAM further reaches 81.68%. In multi-step settings, XSAM surpasses both MSAM (Kim et al., 2023) and LSAM (Mordido et al., 2024) at all k values; for example, at k=4 on CIFAR-100/ResNet-18, XSAM (81.37%) beats MSAM (81.01%) and LSAM (80.74%). XSAM also outperforms VaSSO (Li & Giannakis, 2024) by 0.40% (81.24 vs. 80.84) and WSAM (Yue et al., 2023) by 0.29% (81.24 vs. 80.95) on CIFAR-100/ResNet-18. Notably, while SAM's performance degrades as k increases (80.93→80.65 from k=1 to k=4), XSAM remains stable or improves (81.27→81.37), demonstrating its superior handling of multi-step ascent.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on SAM (Foret et al., 2020), providing the first direct geometric interpretation of why SAM's nonlocal gradient (computed at the ascent point but applied to the current parameters) outperforms the local gradient: it better approximates the direction toward the maximum within the local neighborhood. Rather than treating SAM's effectiveness as an implicit regularization or bias phenomenon (as in Wen et al., 2023; Bartlett et al., 2023; Andriushchenko et al., 2023a,b), the paper reframes the problem as one of direction estimation and proposes XSAM, which explicitly probes the loss surface to find the true maximum direction. This positions XSAM as a reformulation of the sharpness-aware objective itself, distinct from prior update-step modifications (WSAM, GSAM, GAM) that add auxiliary regularization terms, and from perturbation-step improvements (ASAM, VaSSO, FSAM) that are largely orthogonal and composable with XSAM.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper resolves a long-standing conceptual gap in understanding SAM's mechanism and provides a clear geometric explanation for why multi-step SAM underperforms single-step SAM (gradient deviation from the original ascent ray). XSAM's unified formulation for single-step and multi-step settings, combined with negligible computational overhead (≤2.5%), makes it a practical drop-in replacement. Limitations include the restriction of the search to a 2D hyperplane and the assumption that the maximum lies on the neighborhood boundary; whether these constraints are sufficient in very high-dimensional or highly non-convex landscapes remains open. The modest but consistent improvements (0.3–1% over SAM) suggest the SAM gradient, while better than SGD, leaves meaningful room for improvement that XSAM partially captures.
