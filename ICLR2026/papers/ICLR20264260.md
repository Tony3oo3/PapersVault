## Problem

LLM-powered search and answer systems synthesize multiple web sources into single summaries, but current pipelines offer no incentive structure for sources to be accurate and are highly vulnerable to adversarial manipulation (prompt injections, strategic content steering). Unlike traditional ranked search where a manipulative source is contained to its own slot, summarization integrates sources into one narrative, allowing a single strategic actor to hijack the entire output. The paper addresses this gap by designing a mechanism that makes truthful, informative reporting the utility-maximizing strategy for sources, without requiring ground-truth labels.

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes a user query and a set of retrieved web documents (typically 6–7 sources) and produces a final factual summary generated exclusively from a filtered subset of reliable sources. A key structural detail is the leave-one-out (LOO) evaluation: each source is scored on atomic claims constructed from the *other* documents, so no source can tailor its content to the evaluation criteria. In practice, the LOO is approximated by splitting sources into two random groups A and B, where each group is evaluated on claims synthesized from the other, reducing computational cost from O(|C|²K) to O(|C|K).

## Method

TTS (Truthful Text Summarization) adapts multi-task peer prediction (Correlated Agreement) to LLM summarization in three ways: (i) a leave-one-out construction makes the held-out claim set exogenous to each scored source, preventing strategic gaming of evaluation criteria; (ii) an LLM extractor converts free-form documents into per-claim stances (support/contradict/abstain), which are shown to be strategically equivalent to signal-report strategies in classical peer prediction; and (iii) inclusion in the final summary (visibility and attribution) serves as the incentive rather than monetary payment. The scoring rule rewards on-task agreement between a source and its peers while subtracting off-task agreement (via a random permutation of claims), so that generic overlap is discounted and only informative corroboration earns credit. The paper proves asymptotic informed truthfulness, strong truthfulness against significant deviations (those flipping ≥ φ_min fraction of stances), and finite-K ε-informed truthfulness with exponential convergence in the number of claims K.

## Results

On Natural Questions (NQ), TTS achieves 72.3% answer accuracy and 76.1% claim-level precision, substantially outperforming the best baseline (Majority Claims) at 38.6% accuracy and 50.1% precision. On ClashEval, the gains are even more pronounced: TTS reaches 77.1% answer accuracy and 86.2% precision versus 38.4% and 63.6% for the strongest baseline. TTS also improves F1-score from 33.1% to 50.0% on NQ and from 35.4% to 56.1% on ClashEval, while maintaining competitive recall (37.2% and 31.5% respectively).

## Baselines & Comparisons

TTS is compared against three single-pass baselines: Initial Synthesis (standard LLM summary of all sources), Majority Prompt (LLM prompted to include only majority claims), and Majority Claims (decompose into atomic claims, keep majority-supported ones, re-summarize). TTS dominates all three baselines on every metric across both datasets. On NQ, TTS improves answer accuracy by 33.7 points over Majority Claims (72.3% vs. 38.6%) and by 47.2 points over Initial Synthesis (72.3% vs. 25.1%). On ClashEval, the gap widens further: TTS achieves 77.1% accuracy versus 38.4% for Majority Claims and 15.6% for Initial Synthesis. The precision gains are particularly striking (76.1% vs. 50.1% on NQ; 86.2% vs. 63.6% on ClashEval), indicating that TTS's peer-prediction scoring effectively filters out deceptive and adversarial sources before the final summarization step, whereas majority-based methods remain vulnerable to coordinated uninformative behavior.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts multi-task peer prediction (Dasgupta & Ghosh, 2013; Shnayder et al., 2016) — originally designed for crowdsourced judgment elicitation — to the problem of source selection in LLM-based multi-source summarization. It departs from the dominant RAG literature, which treats sources as a fixed input and optimizes summary quality, by instead modeling sources as strategic agents whose incentives are shaped by the summarization pipeline. Compared to concurrent LLM-based peer-informed scoring work (Lu et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2025; Wu & Hartline, 2024), TTS avoids the need for reference labels or token-level likelihood comparisons by constructing leave-one-out atomic claims and scoring sources via informative cross-claim agreement, with inclusion in the final summary serving as the incentive rather than monetary payment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As LLM-powered search (AI Overviews, Copilot Search, Perplexity) becomes the primary interface for information, the vulnerability of synthesized summaries to adversarial prompt injection and strategic manipulation is a growing practical concern that traditional ranked-search defenses do not address. TTS provides a principled, incentive-aligned mechanism with formal truthfulness guarantees, offering a blueprint for making truthful reporting the dominant strategy for content creators. Key limitations include reliance on synthetic (LLM-generated) sources for evaluation, a stylized binary-signal theoretical model, and non-trivial computational overhead (~174K input tokens per query). Open questions include extending to dynamic repeated-game settings with reputation accumulation, multi-hop and long-context regimes, and validating robustness against real-world adversarial behavior at scale.
