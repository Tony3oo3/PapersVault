## Problem

Standard SAE interpretability evaluation requires generating a natural language explanation for each latent and then scoring how well that explanation predicts activations or causal effects. This two-stage pipeline conflates the quality of the latent itself with the quality of the explanation generation process, introducing confounding hyperparameters (prompting strategy, number of examples shown, CoT vs. direct) that make cross-study comparison difficult. The paper asks whether interpretability can be assessed directly from activations, without any intermediate natural language description.

## Input / Output

For intruder detection, the evaluator receives five 32-token text snippets (four activating, one non-activating intruder) with active tokens highlighted via << >> markers; the number of highlighted tokens in the intruder matches the mean count in activating examples. For example embedding scoring, the method consumes sentence-level embeddings from a small model (stella_en_400M_v5, finetuned with Multiple Negatives Ranked Loss) rather than raw text, computing cosine-similarity-based AUROC between positive/negative query embeddings and their respective example sets. Outputs are per-latent accuracy or AUROC scores, reported both overall and stratified by activation decile (10 bins), enabling analysis of interpretability as a function of activation strength.

## Method

The paper introduces two explanation-free evaluation methods grounded in the principle that a latent is interpretable if a human can distinguish activating from non-activating examples without articulating a verbal explanation. Intruder detection presents four activating examples and one non-activating intruder (drawn from contexts that activate other latents) to a human or LLM judge, which must identify the odd-one-out by index; examples are sampled from a single decile of the activation distribution and scored by accuracy averaged across deciles. Example embedding scoring measures whether activating examples form a tighter cluster in embedding space than non-activating ones by computing the mean cosine-similarity difference between a positive query and its class versus the other class (and symmetrically for a negative query), then converting to AUROC. The embedding model is finetuned on Pythia-160m skip-transcoder latents using Multiple Negatives Ranked Loss to learn the highlighting convention. The authors validate the LLM evaluator against human judgments (Spearman ρ = 0.83 for Claude Sonnet 3.5) and show that example embedding scoring correlates strongly with both human and LLM intruder scores (r ≈ 0.77–0.78) while being orders of magnitude cheaper to compute.

## Results

Human intruder detection accuracy averages 64% across 105 latents, rising to 78% on the highest-activation deciles; one-third of latents exceed 80% accuracy while only one in seven falls below 30%. The Spearman correlation between Claude Sonnet 3.5 LLM scores and human scores is 0.83, exceeding prior SAE evaluation metrics. Example embedding scoring achieves a Pearson correlation of r=0.78 with human intruder detection and r=0.75 with LLM intruder detection, while correlating at r=0.87 with the Fuzzing baseline from Paulo et al. (2024).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is against the Fuzzing and Detection scoring methods from Paulo et al. (2024), which require generating natural language explanations before evaluation. Both proposed methods (Intruder Detection and Example Embedding) correlate more strongly with human judgment than Detection does: Intruder Detection (Human) correlates at 0.77 with Fuzzing but only 0.48 with Detection, while Example Embedding correlates at 0.87 with Fuzzing and 0.52 with Detection. Fuzzing emerges as the strongest existing baseline, maintaining high correlations (0.77–0.87) with both proposed methods, suggesting it captures similar latent structure. Detection is the weakest baseline, showing the lowest cross-method correlations (0.48–0.64), indicating it captures a different and less human-aligned signal. The key advantage of the proposed methods is that they achieve comparable or better alignment with human judgment (r=0.75–0.78) without the explanation-generation step, and Example Embedding additionally offers a computationally cheaper alternative using a small finetuned embedding model rather than a large LLM.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant SAE interpretability evaluation pipeline—which generates a natural language explanation per latent and then scores how well that explanation predicts activations—by eliminating the explanation step entirely. It adapts the word-intrusion paradigm from topic model evaluation (Chang et al., 2009) into a sentence-level intruder detection task, and repurposes the two-alternative-forced-choice separability idea from CNN feature-map evaluation (Borowski et al., 2020) into an embedding-space clustering metric. The embedding scoring metric is a direct modification of the explanation-based embedding scoring in Paulo et al. (2024), substituting activating examples for generated explanations as the positive class.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By removing explanation generation, the paper eliminates a major source of confounding hyperparameters (prompt format, number of examples, chain-of-thought, highlighting strategy) that have made SAE interpretability scores hard to compare across labs. The strong human–LLM agreement (Spearman 0.83) and the cross-architecture validation (SmolLM2, Gemma 2, Llama 3.1, Pythia) suggest these metrics could become a standardized, low-cost complement to explanation-based scoring. Open questions include whether the metrics capture causal interpretability (the paper acknowledges its correlational nature), whether they scale meaningfully to the millions of latents in production SAEs, and whether the below-random asymmetric decile-detection results reflect a genuine property of SAE features or an artifact of the small model sizes tested.
