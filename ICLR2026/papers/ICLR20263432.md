## Problem

Difficult examples (samples near class boundaries) are essential in supervised learning but contribute minimally or even negatively to unsupervised contrastive learning. While prior work observed that removing 20–40% of training examples could preserve or improve downstream accuracy, no theoretical explanation existed for why difficult examples hurt contrastive learning or how to systematically mitigate their negative effects.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263432/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes unlabeled RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 96×96 for STL-10, 64×64 for Tiny-ImageNet, 96×96 for ImageNet-1k) and produces a learned encoder representation evaluated via linear probing. Unusually, during training the method computes pairwise cosine similarities within each mini-batch using pre-projector features to dynamically identify difficult pairs, then applies selective margin and temperature modifications only to those pairs rather than uniformly across all samples.

## Method

The paper introduces a similarity graph framework with three similarity levels (α for same-class, β for different-class easy pairs, γ for different-class difficult pairs, where β < γ < α < 1) and derives linear probing error bounds proving that the presence of difficult examples strictly worsens generalization. Three interventions are theoretically shown to improve the bounds: (1) removing difficult samples reduces the bound to the no-difficult-example case with reduced sample size, (2) margin tuning adds a positive margin σ proportional to (γ−β) to difficult pair similarities to neutralize their excess similarity, and (3) temperature scaling applies a smaller temperature ρτ ∝ β/γ to difficult pairs to suppress their influence. Practically, a lightweight selection mechanism identifies difficult pairs by computing cosine similarity percentiles within each batch (using pre-projector features), requiring no pre-trained model or extra computational overhead, and the combined margin-plus-temperature approach yields up to 15% improvement on Tiny-ImageNet.

## Results

The combined method (margin tuning + temperature scaling on selected difficult pairs) achieves linear probing accuracy of 89.68% on CIFAR-10, 62.86% on CIFAR-100, 77.35% on STL-10, and 80.00% on Tiny-ImageNet, improving over the SimCLR baseline by 1.4%, 2.9%, 1.4%, and 10.4% respectively. On the real-world ImageNet-1k dataset, the combined method reaches 38.98% top-1 accuracy versus 37.62% for the SimCLR baseline. The largest gains appear on Tiny-ImageNet (+10.4%) and CIFAR-100 (+2.9%), where the higher class count amplifies the negative impact of difficult examples.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the SimCLR baseline (Chen et al., 2020), the combined method wins on all four primary datasets: +1.42% on CIFAR-10 (89.68 vs 88.26), +2.91% on CIFAR-100 (62.86 vs 59.95), +1.37% on STL-10 (77.35 vs 75.98), and +10.42% on Tiny-ImageNet (80.00 vs 69.58). Against MoCo (Chen et al., 2020), the combined method applied to the MoCo paradigm improves CIFAR-10 accuracy from 85.84% to 86.82% (+0.98%), confirming cross-paradigm scalability. Compared to the SAS coreset subsets from Joshi & Mirzasoleiman (2023) shown in Figure 1, the proposed difficult-example selection mechanism achieves comparable or superior linear probing gains across CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, STL-10, and Tiny-ImageNet without requiring a pre-trained model for sample selection. Notably, applying margin tuning or temperature scaling to all samples (rather than only selected difficult pairs) yields negligible or even negative gains, underscoring that the selection mechanism is critical to the method's effectiveness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the spectral contrastive loss and augmentation graph framework of HaoChen et al. (2021), extending it with a three-level similarity model (α, β, γ) that explicitly encodes difficult examples as high-similarity cross-class pairs. It takes the empirical observation from Joshi & Mirzasoleiman (2023) that removing difficult examples does not hurt (and sometimes helps) contrastive learning, and provides the first theoretical explanation via linear probing error bounds. The practical techniques—margin tuning and temperature scaling applied selectively to identified difficult pairs—adapt standard contrastive learning hyperparameters in a targeted way, departing from prior work that applies them uniformly across all sample pairs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper resolves a genuine conceptual puzzle: why do the most informative samples for supervised learning actively degrade unsupervised contrastive learning? The theoretical framework gives practitioners a principled criterion for identifying and handling difficult pairs without requiring a pre-trained model, and the combined margin-tuning-plus-temperature-scaling approach is simple to implement. Limitations include reliance on class-balanced data and a specific similarity structure (three discrete levels), and the selection mechanism's posHigh/posLow thresholds are dataset-dependent heuristics. Open questions include whether the framework extends to non-contrastive self-supervised methods, how it interacts with curriculum learning or active learning strategies, and whether the benefits persist at very large scale (e.g., full ImageNet training budgets).
