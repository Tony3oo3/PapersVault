## Problem

Existing graph canonicalization methods flatten graphs into a single sequence, which provably distorts graph distances (stretch and contraction bounded below by graph bandwidth) and limits expressivity to that of the node labeler, even with a universal downstream sequence model. Meanwhile, architecturally invariant MPNNs are capped at 1-WL expressivity and suffer from oversmoothing and oversquashing, leaving a gap between invariance and expressivity in graph representation learning.

## Input / Output

The method takes an undirected graph with node feature vectors and produces a permutation-invariant graph-level representation by decomposing the graph into K canonical spanning trees (K=4 for molecules, K=8 for proteins). Each tree is rooted at its center via a two-BFS routine, and the residual non-tree edges are processed separately by a GIN. The final output is a permutation-invariant aggregation (SUM) over per-tree TreeMPNN representations, yielding a fixed-size vector suitable for downstream classification via AUC or accuracy.

## Method

CTNNs construct a canonical spanning tree cover by iteratively extracting minimum spanning trees with coverage-aware edge weight updates: edges used in the previous tree receive an additive penalty τ, biasing subsequent trees toward uncovered edges, so that O(log m) trees recover all edges on sparse graphs. Each tree is encoded by a bidirectional child-sum Tree-LSTM (bottom-up and top-down passes), augmented with a GIN operating on the residual non-tree edges to capture local connectivity missed by the spanning tree. The per-tree representations are aggregated with a permutation-invariant SUM operator to yield the final invariant graph embedding. The approach supports both deterministic invariance (via canonical labelers like NAUTY) and probabilistic invariance (via inexpensive labelers like degree with random tie-breaking), and is provably strictly more expressive than sequence-based canonical GNNs and MPNNs, achieving universality on invariant graph functions when paired with universal tree encoders and full coverage.

## Results

CTNN achieves the best or near-best performance across all eight molecular and protein benchmarks. On molecular tasks, CTNN leads with AUC of 84.7 on ClinTox, 79.3 on BACE, 86.1 on BBBP, 75.2 on HIV, and 87.4 on PCBA. On protein tasks, CTNN achieves 72.1% accuracy on SCOP, 82.0 AUC on GO BIO, and 86.6 AUC on GO MOL. CTNN also achieves the lowest stretch (2.3–2.5 on molecules, ~18 on proteins) and optimal contraction (1.00) across all benchmarks, substantially outperforming sequence-based canonicalizations that exhibit stretch up to 196 (DGCNN on proteins) and contraction up to 16.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CTNN outperforms all architecturally invariant MPNNs (GCN, GAT, GIN) by large margins on nearly every benchmark; for example, on SCOP CTNN achieves 72.1% accuracy versus GIN's 68.0% and GCN's 63.4%. Against expressive GNNs beyond 1-WL, CTNN leads on all five molecular benchmarks (e.g., 84.7 vs. 63.7 for GSN on ClinTox; 87.4 vs. 83.7 for ESAN on PCBA) and on GO BIO (82.0 vs. 79.6 for ESAN), while RWSE is competitive on SCOP (74.5 vs. 72.1) and GO MOL (85.8 vs. 86.6). Among canonicalization baselines, CTNN dominates sequence-based methods: it beats DGCNN by 10–25 points on molecular tasks and 10–20 points on proteins, beats RCM by 14–24 points, and beats SMILES by 12–17 points on molecular benchmarks. The one notable exception is Fingerprint, which edges out CTNN on BACE (82.9 vs. 79.3) and ties on BBBP (86.2 vs. 86.1), though Fingerprint relies on domain-specific chemical descriptors and is not applicable to protein or general graph tasks. On protein benchmarks, CTNN also surpasses Primary Seq. (Alley et al.) by 9–12 points, confirming that tree-cover canonicalization generalizes beyond the molecular domain where sequence-based approaches are most common.

## Relation to Prior Work

CTNN carves new ground at the intersection of graph canonicalization (Bloem-Reddy & Teh; Kaba et al.), tree-structured recurrent encoders (Tai et al., 2015), and MST-based graph decomposition. It departs from prior canonicalization work (DGCNN/SortPooling, RCM, SMILES) by replacing the single canonical sequence with a canonical spanning-tree cover driven by coverage-aware edge reweighting, and from MPNNs by operating on expressive tree encoders rather than local message passing. The paper also formalizes and quantifies limitations of sequence-based canonicalization (bandwidth lower bound on distortion; expressivity equivalence to the node labeler) that prior work had only informally noted, providing the theoretical motivation for the tree-cover design.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The expressivity result—that a sequence-based canonical GNN with a universal downstream model is no more expressive than its node labeler—challenges the common assumption that powerful sequence models add value in canonicalization pipelines, and the bandwidth lower bound gives a concrete, computable metric for evaluating any sequence canonicalization. Practically, CTNN offers a one-time preprocessing step (K MSTs) that is efficient on sparse graphs and yields a unified framework interpolating between fully deterministic (NAUTY) and probabilistic invariance. The main open question is behavior on dense graphs, where the logarithmic coverage guarantee and low-distortion properties may degrade; the authors acknowledge this limitation and the NeuroGraph brain-graph experiment is a first step but does not fully resolve the dense regime.
