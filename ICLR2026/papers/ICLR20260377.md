## Problem

Generalized linear bandits (GLBs) assume the expected reward function (link function) is known to the agent, but in practice this parametric form is typically unknown, and even minor misspecification leads to linear regret for all existing GLB algorithms. This paper introduces the single index bandit (SIB) problem, where the reward is an unknown continuously differentiable function of a linear predictor, and develops the first algorithms with sublinear regret guarantees that require no knowledge of the reward function.

## Input / Output

At each round, the agent observes K feature vectors (arms) in R^d drawn i.i.d. from a known continuous distribution D, selects one arm, and receives a stochastic reward y_t = f(x_t^T θ*) + η_t where f is unknown and η_t requires only finite variance (no sub-Gaussian assumption). The algorithm outputs a selected arm per round and maintains a parameter estimate θ̂ computed via a single averaging operation over truncated score-weighted rewards (no iterative optimization), with O(d) memory and O(dT) total time. The key structural requirement is that the arm distribution D must be known to compute the score function S(x) = −∇log p(x), while the reward function f(·) remains entirely agnostic.

## Method

The core contribution is a Stein's-method-based estimator exploiting the identity E[y·S(x)] = μ*θ* to estimate the unknown parameter direction without knowing f(·), formulated as a truncated quadratic loss that admits a closed-form solution in O(nd) time. For monotonically increasing reward functions, STOR (Explore-then-Commit) achieves Õ(T^{2/3}) regret, while ESTOR improves to the near-optimal Õ(√T) regret via an epoch-based schedule with exponentially growing lengths and a novel density update p_i(x) = K·p(x)·F_i(x^T θ̂_i)^{K−1} that correctly accounts for the non-i.i.d. sampling induced by greedy arm selection within each epoch. The sparse high-dimensional extension adds an l1 regularizer, preserving the same regret rates with the ambient dimension d replaced by the sparsity level s without requiring knowledge of s. For arbitrary non-monotonic reward functions, GSTOR employs a double exploration strategy combining the Stein-based parameter estimator with kernel regression to approximate f(·), achieving Õ(T^{3/4}) regret under a Gaussian design assumption.

## Results

The paper's primary contributions are theoretical regret bounds: ESTOR achieves a nearly-optimal Õ_T(√T) regret for monotone reward functions (Theorem 3.5), STOR achieves Õ(d^(2/3) T^(2/3)) (Theorem 3.2), and GSTOR achieves O(d^(3/8) T^(3/4)) for general non-monotone functions (Theorem 3.9). In the sparse high-dimensional setting, the same bounds hold with ambient dimension d replaced by sparsity level s. Empirically, across four link functions (linear, Poisson, square, fifth-power) with T=10,000 over 20 repetitions, ESTOR matches or approaches the best baselines while being hundreds of times faster than UCB-GLM and thousands of times faster than GLM-TSL in wall-clock time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against four baselines: LinUCB and LinTS (for the linear case) and UCB-GLM and GLM-TSL (for the three generalized linear cases). ESTOR consistently achieves regret comparable to the best baseline across all four link functions (linear, Poisson, square, fifth-power) in Figure 1, while STOR shows the expected explore-then-commit pattern with initially growing then flattening regret. Under model misspecification (fitting UCB-GLM or GLM-TSL with the wrong link function, shown as dashed lines in cases 3 and 4), both GLB baselines suffer significant performance degradation and slower convergence, whereas ESTOR remains robust. Computationally, ESTOR and STOR are hundreds of times faster than UCB-GLM and thousands of times faster than GLM-TSL, owing to their closed-form estimator requiring only O(nd) time and O(d) space. On the real-world Forest Cover Type and Yahoo News datasets (Appendix M, Table 3), all proposed methods (STOR, ESTOR, GSTOR) consistently outperform state-of-the-art GLB methods, confirming the practical advantage of the agnostic approach when the true link function is unknown.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper introduces Single Index Bandits (SIBs), a new problem that extends Generalized Linear Bandits (GLBs) by removing the assumption that the reward (link) function is known. The core technical tool is a Stein's-identity-based estimator previously applied only to low-rank matrix bandits (Kang et al., 2022), here repurposed for the first time in linear/GLB settings. The paper explicitly demonstrates that all existing GLB algorithms (UCB-GLM, GLM-TSL, second-order Newton methods) and realizability-based contextual bandit approaches (FALCON+, Foster & Rakhlin) are fundamentally inapplicable when the link function is unknown, as their analyses rely on explicit knowledge of f(·) for concentration bounds or regression oracles. For the general non-monotonic case, GSTOR borrows kernel regression from the single index model literature (Härdle, 2004) to approximate the unknown link function.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a critical practical vulnerability: model misspecification of the link function causes linear regret for all existing GLB methods, yet in real applications the parametric form of the reward is typically unknown. The Stein's-method estimator is remarkably efficient (closed-form in low dimensions, O(nd) time, O(d) space, no iterative optimization), making it hundreds to thousands of times faster than UCB-GLM or GLM-TSL while achieving the same minimax-optimal Õ(√T) regret for monotone rewards. Key limitations include the i.i.d.-from-fixed-distribution arm assumption (not adversarial), the Gaussian design requirement for GSTOR, and the open question of whether √T regret is achievable for fully general non-monotonic reward functions. Extending to adversarial arm selection and removing distributional assumptions remains a challenging open direction.
