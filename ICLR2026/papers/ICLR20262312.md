limitations: The method assumes residuals have low-complexity structure (horizon-wise bias, scale miscalibration, calendar offsets), so gains may be limited when residuals are highly complex or when distribution shift is severe rather than gradual. surprise_factor: The consistent outperformance of input-side adapters (Ada-X) over output-side adapters (Ada-Y) across nearly all backbones and datasets is a useful practical finding, but not deeply surprising given that correcting inputs before nonlinear processing is more impactful than correcting outputs after. ## Problem

Deployed time series forecasters suffer from post-deployment condition drift, high performance variance, and inefficient retraining, yet existing testing-time adaptation methods rely on future labels (causing leakage) or complex modules that destabilize hardened systems. The paper asks whether a tiny, bounded post-hoc module can improve accuracy and uncertainty of a frozen forecaster without any retraining or interface changes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262312/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a frozen (possibly black-box) forecaster F, time series context windows X ∈ R^{L×d}, and a trust-region scalar δ ∈ (0,1) that caps every per-entry edit. It produces corrected point forecasts via additive or multiplicative input nudging and output residual correction, a sparse nearly-binary feature mask M ∈ [0,1]^{L×d} for interpretability, and calibrated prediction intervals (quantile offsets or conformal sets) with finite-sample coverage. All adapter outputs are bounded in magnitude by δ, ensuring the frozen model's predictions shift by at most O(δ).

## Method

δ-Adapter learns a tiny MLP or low-rank head at two minimal interfaces of a frozen forecaster: input-side nudging (softly editing covariates before inference) and output-side residual correction (refining predictions after inference), each in additive or multiplicative form bounded by a trust-region parameter δ. A key instantiation is a feature-selector adapter that outputs a sparse, horizon-aware mask via Gumbel-Sigmoid (Concrete) relaxation, trained end-to-end with sparsity, entropy, total-variation, and budget regularizers to expose the most consequential inputs. For uncertainty, a Quantile Calibrator learns monotonic horizon-wise quantile offsets with pinball loss and reliability regularization, while a Conformal Calibrator learns a heteroscedastic scale function for normalized-residual conformal prediction, yielding finite-sample coverage. The paper provides local descent guarantees (Propositions 2.1–2.2), O(δ) Lipschitz drift bounds (Proposition 3.1), and compositional stability for combined input+output adapters (Proposition 3.2), all under the assumption that F is Lipschitz and the adapter outputs are ∞-norm bounded by 1.

## Results

On the primary comparison (Table 2), δ-Adapter (Ada-X+Y) achieves the lowest MSE across all 8 datasets and all three backbones (DistPred, iTransformer, Autoformer). For example, on Autoformer/ELC it reaches 0.417 MSE versus 0.502 (SOLID), 0.510 (TAFAS), and 0.478 (LoRA); on iTransformer/Traffic it reaches 0.461 versus 0.475 (SOLID/TAFAS) and 0.468 (LoRA). On SOTA models (Table 3), Ada-X+Y yields consistent 2–15% MSE reductions, with the largest gains on Exchange (DistPred: 0.350→0.297, ~15%) and ETTm2 (FreTS: 0.335→0.285, ~15%). The feature-selector mask retains only 92–98% of inputs while still improving accuracy (Table 4), and the calibrators achieve the highest PICP among all interval methods tested.

## Baselines & Comparisons

δ-Adapter (Ada-X+Y) outperforms all tested baselines on every dataset across all three backbones. Against SOLID (Chen et al., KDD 2024) and TAFAS (Kim et al., AAAI 2025), the gains are modest on DistPred (1–5% MSE reduction) but substantial on Autoformer (e.g., ELC: 0.417 vs. 0.502/0.510, a ~17–18% improvement). LoRA (Hu et al., ICLR 2022) is the closest competitor, yet δ-Adapter still wins on all 24 dataset–backbone combinations (e.g., iTransformer/Exchange: 0.316 vs. 0.376, a 16% gap). The online methods OneNet (Wen et al., NeurIPS 2023) and FSNet (Pham et al., ICLR 2023) perform markedly worse—often 30–100% higher MSE—confirming that label-leakage-free post-hoc adaptation is more reliable than online ensembling. For calibration, both the Quantile Calibrator and Conformal Calibrator achieve the highest PICP (coverage) among CQR (Romano et al., 2019), EnbPI (Xu & Xie, 2021), and SPCI (Xu & Xie, 2023), with CC producing tighter intervals at comparable coverage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the NLP adapter paradigm (LoRA, prefix-tuning, MAD-X) to time series forecasting, but departs fundamentally by operating strictly at the input/output interface of a frozen forecaster rather than modifying internal layers. It builds on conformal prediction (Vovk et al., 2017; Romano et al., 2019) for its uncertainty calibrators, and positions itself against existing test-time adaptation methods for TSF (SOLID, TAFAS, PETSA, DynaTTA) by eliminating label leakage and providing Lipschitz-based stability guarantees that those methods lack. The key conceptual departure is the bounded-edit (δ) framework with explicit descent and drift theorems, unifying input nudging, output residual correction, feature masking, and distribution calibration under a single trust-region parameter.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The plug-and-play nature (no retraining, no interface changes, negligible compute) makes δ-Adapter immediately deployable alongside existing production forecasters, addressing a real operational gap that full fine-tuning or architectural changes cannot. The theoretical guarantees (local descent, O(δ) drift bounds, compositional stability) provide a safety argument that prior TTA methods for time series lack, which is important for high-stakes deployments. Open questions include how the method behaves under severe (non-gradual) distribution shifts, whether the low-complexity residual assumption holds for foundation-model-scale forecasters, and how the δ parameter should be selected adaptively rather than fixed. The 96% MSE improvement on Weather in Table 1 appears anomalous (possibly a data artifact) and should be interpreted cautiously.
