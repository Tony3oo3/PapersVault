## Problem

It has long been conjectured and empirically observed that neural networks preserve the clustering structure of their input data, but no formal characterization existed for *when* and *why* this occurs. This paper fills that gap by establishing precise sufficient conditions under which a network layer's output retains the subspace clustering structure of its input, and by deriving quantitative bounds on the extent of preservation. A secondary motivation is explaining why certain data types (images, audio, text) benefit more from deep learning than others.

## Input / Output

The method takes a data matrix X ∈ R^{m×n} whose columns lie near a union of K low-dimensional subspaces (the UoS model), a learned weight matrix W, and an activation function σ (e.g., ReLU). It produces a projection matrix P(Y) onto the r-dimensional principal row-space of the layer output Y = σ(WX), whose support pattern directly encodes cluster membership: the (i,j)-th entry is large if and only if columns i and j belong to the same subspace. The key constraint is a spectral-gap condition δ > η, where δ is the gap between the r-th and (r+1)-th singular values and η depends on noise level ‖Z‖, the transformation ‖WZ‖, and activation distortion ‖Y − WX‖.

## Method

The core contribution is a three-part theoretical proof built on the Davis-Kahan sin(Θ) theorem: (i) the projection operator P(X*) onto the principal row-space of clean data encodes the subspace clustering in closed form and can be estimated from noisy data X = X* + Z; (ii) this projection is invariant under arbitrary linear transformations W (so P(X*) = P(WX*) when rank(W) ≥ r); (iii) under a spectral-gap condition on the activation distortion ‖Y − WX‖, the non-linear output Y = σ(WX) preserves the same clustering structure. The surprising empirical finding is that ReLU (and GELU, SiLU) networks inherently learn weights W satisfying these conditions during standard gradient descent training—without any explicit regularization—while CNNs and Transformers, which use non-linear operators beyond a single linear-plus-activation layer, do not preserve the original projection structure. The multi-layer extension follows by a union bound over layers, and the practical implication is that nonnegative initialization of W (natural for nonnegative data like pixel intensities) immediately satisfies the preservation conditions.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative result is Theorem 3.1, which guarantees that the projection matrix of the network output Y is within ε/2 (in entrywise infinity norm) of the true data projection P(X*) whenever the spectral gap δ exceeds the noise-dependent threshold η = (√(2⁷r)/ε)·max(‖Z‖, ‖WZ‖, ‖Y−WX‖). On synthetic union-of-subspaces data (K=4 subspaces, r_k=4, m=400, n=400), the conditions of Theorem 3.1 hold with high probability for noise variance s² up to approximately 0.1, degrading gracefully as noise increases. Across 12+ real-world image datasets (MNIST, CIFAR-10, Fashion-MNIST, EuroSAT, etc.), 5-layer ReLU feedforward networks show monotonically decreasing projection distances over 200 epochs, confirming that the network inherently learns weights satisfying the theorem's conditions without explicit regularization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external baselines in the traditional sense; its goal is mechanistic analysis rather than benchmarking. Instead, it contrasts architectural choices on the same synthetic and real-data tasks. 5-layer ReLU feedforward networks and 3-layer LSTMs with ReLU activations both show decreasing projection distances over training, indicating inherent preservation of subspace clustering structure. In contrast, 3-layer CNNs and 4-layer Transformers exhibit large and persistent projection distances, showing that convolution and attention transformations do not inherently preserve the original clustering structure. Among activation functions, ReLU, GELU, and SiLU all demonstrate structure preservation in feedforward architectures, indicating the phenomenon is not exclusive to ReLU. The paper explicitly notes that all architectures achieve comparable clustering accuracy (ACC, NMI, ARI); the distinguishing factor is the mechanism—ReLU-type networks cluster by learning the closed-form projection structure, while CNNs and Transformers use a different mechanism that does not preserve the original subspace geometry.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper formalizes a long-standing empirical conjecture (Papyan, 2020; Arora et al., 2018) that ReLU-type networks preserve clustering structure, by deriving explicit sufficient conditions via the Davis-Kahan sin(Θ) perturbation theorem applied to the union-of-subspaces model (Vidal et al., 2005; Elhamifar & Vidal, 2013). It sits at the intersection of subspace clustering theory, matrix perturbation analysis, and neural network interpretability, building on the implicit-bias literature (Soudry et al., 2018) and activation-function expressivity work (Kou et al., 2024) but departing from them by providing a closed-form projection-operator characterization rather than analyzing optimization dynamics. The paper also contrasts its linear-plus-activation framework with convolutional and attention-based architectures, showing the latter do not share the same structural preservation property.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway—that nonnegative weight initialization trivially satisfies the preservation conditions and acts as a local optimum—offers a simple, interpretable strategy for improving clustering in ReLU networks without additional regularization. However, the spectral-gap condition in Theorem 3.1 is restrictive and may not hold uniformly across real-world data, limiting the theorem's direct applicability. A key open question is whether the preservation mechanism extends to more complex transformations (convolutions, attention), which the paper's own experiments suggest it does not, leaving the clustering mechanism in CNNs and Transformers unexplained by this framework. The finding that ReLU networks effectively 'learn the closed-form projection' also raises the question of whether this explains their particular strength on nonnegative, subspace-structured data (images, genomics, audio) versus other modalities.
