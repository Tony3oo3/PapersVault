## Problem

Automated interpretability methods produce natural language descriptions of LLM features that are verbose, inconsistent, and ambiguous, making them poorly suited for compositional reasoning tasks like detecting redundant features or quantifying feature complexity across layers. The paper asks whether a structured language can describe LLM features with the same accuracy as natural language while providing the precision and consistency that free-form text lacks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261772/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes SAE latent features (from Bloom SAE on GPT-2-Small with 24,576 features, or Gemma Scope on Gemma-2-2B with 16k/65k features) along with their top-N activating text examples (10 for semantic-regex, 32-token windows centered on the maximally activating token) retrieved via the Neuronpedia API. It produces a semantic regex description—a structured pattern composed of primitives ([:symbol X:], [:lexeme X:], [:field X:]) combined with modifiers for context (@{:context C:}), sequence/alternation composition, and zero-or-one quantification—rather than a free-form sentence.

## Method

The core contribution is the semantic regex language, designed via grounded theory from manual survey of thousands of features across models and layers on Neuronpedia. Three primitives capture different levels of abstraction: symbols match exact strings, lexemes match syntactic variants (tense, plurality), and fields match semantic category members. Three modifiers extend expressivity: context restricts matching to a domain, composition allows sequential or disjunctive patterns, and quantification adds optional components. The language is embedded into a standard automated interpretability pipeline by injecting its definition and few-shot examples into the max-acts prompt, with GPT-4o-mini serving as both explainer (generating descriptions) and evaluator (scoring them via detection, fuzzing, clarity, responsiveness, purity, and faithfulness metrics). The key finding is that this structural constraint does not reduce accuracy relative to natural language baselines, while enabling new analyses: the number and abstraction level of components in a semantic regex directly proxies feature complexity, revealing that features grow more complex and abstract with model depth.

## Results

Across GPT-2-RES-25k, Gemma-2-2B-RES-16k, and Gemma-2-2B-RES-65k (100 features per layer), semantic regexes achieve detection scores of 0.81, 0.79, and 0.79 respectively, matching or exceeding natural language baselines (max-acts: 0.86, 0.86, 0.85; token-act-pair: 0.86, 0.78, 0.77). On clarity (generation metric), semantic regexes score 0.68, 0.57, and 0.47, statistically non-inferior to max-acts (0.70, 0.59, 0.45) across all models. Beyond accuracy, semantic regexes are substantially more concise (median 41 characters vs. 139 for max-acts and 55 for token-act-pair) and more consistent, producing identical descriptions for the same feature 33.6% of the time versus 12.2% (token-act-pair) and 0.0% (max-acts).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares semantic regexes against two natural language baselines: token-act-pair (Bills et al., 2023) and max-acts (Paulo et al., 2024). Against token-act-pair, semantic regexes significantly outperform (p<0.05) on clarity across all three models and on detection, fuzzing, and responsiveness on GPT-2-RES-25k and Gemma-2-2B-RES-65k. Against max-acts, semantic regexes are statistically non-inferior (p<0.05) on clarity across all models, detection on GPT-2-RES-25k, and responsiveness on Gemma-2-2B-RES-65k, though they score lower on purity (e.g., 0.66 vs. 0.78 on GPT-2) and faithfulness (e.g., 0.37 vs. 0.52 on GPT-2). The paper frames these results as demonstrating that the structured language constraint does not reduce expressivity, while the structured form provides additional benefits: descriptions are 3.4× shorter than max-acts (41 vs. 139 median characters) and 3× more consistent for redundant features (33.6% vs. 12.2% identical-description rate). In a 24-person user study, participants scored as well or higher using semantic regex descriptions on 9 of 12 features when generating activating phrases and counterfactuals.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the automated interpretability pipeline established by Bills et al. (2023) and Paulo et al. (2024), which prompt an LLM to generate natural language descriptions of SAE features from their activating data. Rather than proposing a new feature extraction or prompting strategy, it replaces the output format: instead of free-form text, the explainer model produces descriptions in a structured language (semantic regexes) with primitives (symbols, lexemes, fields) and modifiers (context, composition, quantification). The evaluation framework draws on both Eleuther metrics (detection, fuzzing) and FADE metrics (clarity, responsiveness, purity, faithfulness), and the language design is grounded in empirical observation of feature activation patterns across Neuronpedia, aligning with prior findings that features encode concepts at varying levels of abstraction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical contribution is that a constrained structured language matches natural language accuracy while enabling model-wide analyses (e.g., quantifying feature complexity across layers) and improving consistency for detecting redundant features—capabilities that free-form text cannot support. Limitations include non-uniqueness of descriptions (multiple valid regexes for the same feature), occasional over-terse descriptions that lose nuance, and inability to handle high-degree polysemanticity. Open questions include whether style guides or validation loops can resolve ambiguity, whether structured languages face adoption barriers when models are biased toward natural language, and whether more expressive metrics (continuous scoring, readability) are needed to fully capture the trade-offs between description formats.
