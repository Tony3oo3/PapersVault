## Problem

It remains unclear whether reinforcement learning teaches LLMs genuinely new skills or merely reweights reasoning strategies already present in base models. Recent pass@k analyses suggesting RL only 'reranks' base model outputs may be confounded by evaluating on tasks where base models already achieve high accuracy, leaving no incentive for RL to learn new capabilities. This paper asks whether RL can teach new compositional skills, what training signals are needed to incentivize them, and whether such skills generalize beyond the training task.

## Input / Output

The model receives an input string (length 3–10) and a composition of deterministic string-transformation functions identified by meaningless labels (e.g., func_16, func_2) with all function definitions hidden. The model must produce the output string after applying the nested transformation, formatted as JSON. Difficulty is controlled by nesting depth: Level 1 is a single function application, Level 2 is a two-function composition, up to Level 8 in evaluation, with 256 held-out problems per level.

## Method

The paper introduces a controlled synthetic framework with 25 unique string-transformation functions (character manipulation, reordering, filtering, structural modification) assigned meaningless identifiers to prevent data contamination. Training follows a two-stage protocol: Stage 1 uses rejection fine-tuning (RFT) to acquire atomic skills, where function definitions are shown during data collection but removed during fine-tuning so the model internalizes each function's behavior; Stage 2 trains on compositional problems (Level 2) using either online RL (DAPO with binary correctness rewards, 16 on-policy rollouts per prompt, filtering all-correct/all-incorrect problems) or iterative RFT as a baseline. The key finding is that RL on Level-2 compositional data teaches a generalizable compositional skill—performance on unseen Level-3 problems rises from near-zero to ~30% and Level-4 to ~15%—while RFT on identical data fails to generalize beyond its training difficulty. This compositional skill further transfers cross-task: a model with Countdown atomic skills plus string-task compositional RL achieves 35% on unseen Level-3 Countdown problems without any Countdown RL training.

## Results

On held-out compositional string tasks, the RL Level 2 model achieves ~64% accuracy on Level 2 and generalizes to ~27–30% on Level 3 and ~15% on Level 4, whereas the RFT Level 2 baseline reaches only ~15% on Level 2 and never exceeds 2.6% on Level 3. The RL Level 1 model (atomic-only RL) peaks at ~90% on Level 1 but remains below 25% on Level 2 and near zero on Levels 3–6. For cross-task transfer to Countdown, Multi-Base + RL L1+2 achieves 35% Avg@32 at Level 3 (vs. ~17% for Multi-Base alone) and ~6% at Level 4 where other models fail. On pass@1024 at Level 5, the RL Level 1+2 model's advantage over the RFT base grows from 4% at pass@1 to ~25%, demonstrating genuine new skill acquisition rather than reranking.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between RL and RFT on identical Level 2 compositional data: RL Level 2 achieves 64% on Level 2 and 27% on Level 3, while RFT Level 2 reaches only 15% on Level 2 and never exceeds 2.6% on Level 3, showing that supervised exposure to compositional examples alone is insufficient. Against RL Level 1 (atomic-only RL), the compositional RL model generalizes dramatically to unseen higher nesting depths (Level 3: ~30% vs. ~0%; Level 4: ~15% vs. ~0%), confirming that explicit compositional incentive is necessary. On the Countdown transfer task, Multi-Base + RL L1+2 outperforms the Multi-Base baseline by 18 percentage points at Level 3 (35% vs. ~17%) and maintains a ~6% advantage at Level 4 where all other models fail. The String-Base + RL L1+2 control (compositional skill without Countdown atomic skills) fails completely, confirming that task-specific atomic skills are a prerequisite for compositional transfer. The pass@k analysis further shows that on hard problems (Levels 3–6), the RL model's advantage over the RFT base *widens* with more samples (e.g., 4% → 25% at Level 5), directly contradicting the 'reranking' narrative that applies only to easy problems where the base model already has high pass@k.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly challenges the recent pessimistic consensus (Yue et al. 2025; Wu et al. 2025a; Gandhi et al. 2025; Zhao et al. 2025) that RL merely reweights or amplifies reasoning patterns already present in base models, rather than teaching genuinely new skills. It draws on Anderson's (1982) cognitive science framework of skill acquisition through composition, and on the compositional reasoning literature (Fodor & Pylyshyn 1988; Lake et al. 2016; Andreas et al. 2015), to construct a controlled synthetic testbed where atomic and compositional skills are precisely defined. Methodologically, it builds on standard RL-for-LLMs infrastructure (GRPO/DAPO, rejection fine-tuning) but introduces a two-stage protocol that separates atomic skill acquisition (via RFT) from compositional skill learning (via RL), and contrasts RL against iterative RFT on identical data to isolate RL's specific contribution. The cross-task transfer experiment extends beyond prior work (Sun et al. 2025; Yin et al. 2025) by showing that compositional RL on one domain transfers to an unrelated task, provided atomic skills for the target domain are already in place.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first controlled, decontaminated evidence that RL can teach LLMs a genuinely new compositional skill that generalizes to higher difficulties and even transfers across tasks, reframing the 'reranking' narrative as an artifact of evaluating on tasks where base models already perform well. Practically, it suggests a concrete post-training strategy: ensure base models possess the necessary atomic skills (via pretraining or SFT), then use RL with compositional incentives to unlock generalizable composition. Key limitations include reliance on synthetic string tasks and a single 8B model, leaving open whether these findings hold in natural reasoning domains (math, code, science) where skill boundaries are less clean. An important open question the authors raise is whether RL can acquire both atomic and compositional skills simultaneously without supervised scaffolding.
