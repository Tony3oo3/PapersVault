## Problem

Existing video instruction-tuning tasks such as captioning and question answering primarily promote modality alignment and visual perception but neglect the temporal dimension that distinguishes videos from static images. These tasks also rely on human or strong-MLLM annotations, raising scalability concerns. The paper asks: what self-supervised learning task can effectively equip MLLMs with temporal reasoning over video inputs without requiring expensive external annotations?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261081/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives only a truncated past segment of a video (32 frames by default) rather than the full video, and must generate a free-form textual caption describing events in the unseen future segment. For FutureBench evaluation, the input additionally includes a multiple-choice question specifying an 'end anchor' (the final event), and the model must select the correct sequence of intermediate future events from distractors. The key distinction from standard video understanding is that the input is deliberately incomplete, forcing the model to go beyond perception into prediction.

## Method

Next-Event Prediction (NEP) splits each video at a causal pivot point (identified via LLM-based caption analysis) into past and future segments; the MLLM takes past frames as input and is trained to generate a caption describing the future, thereby integrating visual perception from the frozen encoder with commonsense knowledge in the LLM for causal inference. The authors construct V1-33K (~33K past-future pairs from diverse sources) using an automated pipeline of caption analysis, timestamp grounding, and optional reasoning/critique generation. Four training strategies are explored—SFT, critique fine-tuning (CFT), teacher distillation, and a mixed combination—alongside R1-style GRPO reinforcement learning, with the visual encoder frozen throughout. FutureBench (1,056 QA pairs) evaluates multi-hop temporal reasoning via extrapolation (1–3 hop) and interpolation subtasks, with GPT-4-generated distractors designed to be commonsense-plausible but logically inconsistent.

## Results

On Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct, NEP-trained models achieve the highest temporal average (T-Avg) of 53.5, outperforming the Instruct baseline (49.7), Captioning (49.7), MCQA (47.7), and OEQA (51.2), while also attaining the best general average (G-Avg) of 60.9. On the proposed FutureBench, NEP scores 61.3 vs. 52.6 for the untrained baseline. On TOMATO, NEP-Distill yields the largest overall improvement (+2.42 over the base model), and GRPO-based RL training on NEP further boosts FutureBench 1-hop accuracy from 67.6 (SFT) to 83.8, though at the cost of general benchmark performance (G-Avg drops from 59.7 to 58.2).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares NEP against three standard video instruction-tuning tasks—Captioning, MCQA, and OEQA—all trained on the same 3K video samples from LLaVA-Video-178K but using the full observed video. NEP (using only partially observed video) outperforms all three on temporal benchmarks: T-Avg of 53.5 vs. 49.7 (Captioning), 47.7 (MCQA), and 51.2 (OEQA), with the largest gap on SeedBench-R1 (39.5 vs. 33.6/33.0/35.4) and FutureBench (61.3 vs. 55.8/60.3/58.8). NEP also matches or exceeds the best general-benchmark score (G-Avg 60.9 vs. 60.4 for OEQA), showing no trade-off. In a separate reasoning-type comparison (Table 5), deductive NEP (T-Avg 53.5) beats inductive Video QA (51.2) and abductive Previous-Event Prediction (47.8), supporting the claim that forward causal prediction is the most effective temporal reasoning signal. On TOMATO, NEP-Distill achieves the highest overall score (29.31, +2.42 over base), while Captioning actually degrades performance (−0.07).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the LLaVA-style encoder-decoder MLLM architecture and the Qwen2.5-VL training recipe (CLIP pre-training → modality alignment → end-to-end fine-tuning), but departs from the dominant video instruction-tuning paradigm of observational tasks (captioning, QA) by introducing a predictive objective. It also extends the line of future-prediction work in computer vision (action anticipation, future-frame representation learning) from low-level, encoder-focused objectives to high-level, semantic natural-language prediction that targets the LLM and cross-modal projector while freezing the visual encoder. The GRPO-based RL training strategy is adapted from DeepSeekMath, applied here to multi-choice future-event prediction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NEP provides a scalable, self-supervised learning signal for temporal reasoning in MLLMs that avoids the annotation bottleneck of video QA, and the controlled comparison design (same videos, different tasks) cleanly isolates the effect of the task formulation. However, the gains are modest (~4 points on temporal benchmarks), NEP data saturates beyond ~5K samples due to restricted source diversity, and RL training degrades general video understanding—suggesting that practical recipes will need to combine NEP with other supervision types. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to longer-horizon or multi-scene videos, whether better data quality (beyond the current automatic pipeline) unlocks further scaling, and how to schedule NEP alongside QA and captioning in a full training pipeline.
