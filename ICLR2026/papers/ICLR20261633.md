## Problem

Training a single unified world model across heterogeneous tasks with diverse observation spaces and varying complexities suffers from gradient conflicts and plasticity collapse, where simpler tasks dominate learning signals and suppress progress on harder ones. Additionally, conventional architectures apply uniform computational resources to all tasks regardless of their learning progress, wasting interactions on already-converged tasks. These two intertwined obstacles—representational interference within a single iteration and static resource allocation across the training process—remain largely unexplored in the context of latent-space planning.

## Input / Output

The model consumes heterogeneous inputs depending on the benchmark: 64×64×3 RGB frames encoded by a from-scratch ViT (Atari), low-dimensional MuJoCo state vectors encoded by task-specific MLPs (DMC), and text observations encoded by a frozen BGE language model (Jericho), all augmented with action tokens and a learnable task embedding. The unified Transformer backbone jointly predicts four outputs per timestep: a next latent state (768-dim), a discretized reward, a policy distribution over actions, and a bootstrapped state value, which together drive a 50-simulation MCTS planner for action selection.

## Method

ScaleZero replaces the dense feed-forward networks in UniZero's Transformer backbone with sparse Mixture-of-Experts layers (8 routed experts + 1 shared expert, top-1 gating), creating conditional computation pathways that route task-specific representations to specialized sub-networks and thereby reduce gradient conflicts; a theoretical analysis proves the MoE layer's gradient conflict upper bound is strictly lower than its dense counterpart. The architecture pairs this MoE backbone with a ViT encoder and standard LayerNorm (replacing UniZero's SimNorm), validated through a systematic five-axis design-space exploration. To address static resource allocation, Dynamic Parameter Scaling (DPS) introduces a staged training protocol: after a warm-up phase on the shared backbone, new LoRA adapters (rank 64) are injected at progress- or budget-triggered transitions, with all previously learned parameters frozen to prevent catastrophic forgetting. Learnable scaling factors (constrained to [0.8, 1.2] via tanh reparameterization) modulate the contribution of the base model and each adapter, while an active-set mechanism restricts data collection and gradient updates to unsolved tasks only, yielding a 28.5% reduction in environment interactions on DMC.

## Results

On the Atari 100k benchmark (26 games), ScaleZero as a single multitask agent achieves a mean Human-Normalized Score of 0.39, surpassing the 26 independently trained single-task UniZero specialists (mean 0.38), while the multitask UniZero baseline drops to 0.31. On the 18-task DMControl suite, ScaleZero achieves a median score of 887.3 versus 875.1 for single-task UniZero, though its mean (769.7) trails the single-task mean (787.2). On Jericho (4 text-based games), ScaleZero matches single-task UniZero on 3 of 4 tasks and is competitive with CALM+OC. With Dynamic Parameter Scaling, ScaleZero-DPS reaches the same DMC performance target using only 71.5% of environment interactions (a 28.5% reduction).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the single-task UniZero baseline on Atari 100k, ScaleZero (MT) achieves a higher mean HNS (0.39 vs. 0.38), demonstrating positive knowledge transfer across 26 heterogeneous games, though its median (0.16) is lower than the single-task median (0.21), indicating a few hard-exploration games still lag. On DMControl, ScaleZero wins on median (887.3 vs. 875.1) but trails on mean (769.7 vs. 787.2), with notable gaps on finger-spin (574.2 vs. 810.7) and walker-stand (919.9 vs. 976.4), while it surpasses the baseline on acrobot-swingup (501.0 vs. 400.3) and hopper-hop (138.0 vs. 120.5). On Jericho, ScaleZero ties UniZero (ST) on Acorncourt (10), Omniguest (10), and Zork1 (44), but falls slightly short on Detective (280 vs. 295); it remains competitive with the stronger CALM+OC language-model baseline (288.5 on Detective, 38.0 on Zork1). The multitask UniZero baseline (MT) degrades substantially on Atari (mean 0.31 vs. 0.38 for ST), confirming the plasticity collapse diagnosis that ScaleZero's MoE backbone is designed to address.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the MuZero/UniZero line of work on MCTS-based planning in learned latent world models to the heterogeneous multitask setting, where prior unified world models were designed and validated only for single-task training. Architecturally, it repurposes sparse Mixture-of-Experts (originally developed for LLMs) as the backbone of a world model to create conditional computation pathways that mitigate inter-task gradient conflict, and adapts LoRA from its conventional offline fine-tuning role into an online, progress-triggered capacity expansion mechanism (DPS). The systematic five-axis design space exploration (task conditioning, encoder, normalization, backbone, optimization) positions the contribution as a principled ablation study that identifies MoE as the dominant factor, departing from prior MTRL work that has focused primarily on model-free or supervised settings rather than latent-space planning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that a single online RL agent matches or exceeds 26 individually trained specialists on Atari, 18 DMC tasks, and 4 Jericho games provides a concrete architectural blueprint for generalist world models, while the 28.5% reduction in environment interactions via DPS offers a practical efficiency lever for compute-constrained settings. Key limitations include the MoE configuration being acknowledged as a pragmatic rather than theoretically optimal choice, DPS being validated only on DMC, and the median Atari score remaining below the single-task baseline. Open questions include whether the approach scales to hundreds of tasks, whether pre-trained visual encoders (e.g., DINOv2) can be effectively integrated, and whether the theoretical gradient-conflict bounds for MoE layers hold under the non-stationary, online data regime that differs from the supervised settings in which MoE theory was developed.
