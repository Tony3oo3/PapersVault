## Problem

Neural LID estimators are typically validated on either trivially simple synthetic manifolds (with known LID but unrealistic geometry) or real datasets (with realistic complexity but unknown LID), making it impossible to reliably assess true performance. The paper demonstrates that accuracy on simple manifolds does not transfer across domains or under targeted stressors such as non-uniform density, curvature, boundaries, thin or nearby manifolds, and that state-of-the-art methods (LIDL, FLIPD, NB) exhibit clear failure modes that existing benchmarks fail to expose.

## Input / Output

The benchmark suite operates on image-domain data: grayscale 16×16 or 32×32 images (FMNIST-based) and RGB 32×32 images (Arrows dataset), with 100K training / 1K test samples for most datasets. Each LID estimation algorithm (ESS, NB, LIDL, FLIPD) is applied to the same manifold embedded in multiple domain representations via IDR, producing per-point LID estimates that are summarized as mean, standard deviation, MAE against ground truth, and qualitative H/M/L/O rankings across 11 distinct manifold-property aspects. For real-world datasets with unknown LID, the output is a before/after comparison of estimates under controlled transformations whose LID shift is known a priori.

## Method

The core contribution is a principled benchmarking framework built around five data-construction methods: (i) Inverse Domain Representation (IDR), which embeds an arbitrary manifold into the PCA subspace of FMNIST class-7 images (784-dim ambient space) via a linear map using the top-d principal component vectors, preserving all geometric properties while making the data visually resemble real images; (ii) Monotonic Embedding (ME), applying coordinate-wise diffeomorphisms (e.g., x→x^ℓ) that provably preserve LID; (iii) Ambient Space Extension (ASE), re-embedding via x↦(x,F(x)) with smooth inverse; (iv) Auxiliary Dimension Injection (ADI), adding known dimensions via parametric transformations with non-degenerate Jacobians; and (v) Manifold Synthesis (MS), rendering complex images (e.g., multi-arrow scenes with 6 DOF per arrow) with exactly known LID. The framework then stress-tests four algorithms across 11 targeted aspects—non-uniform density, curvature, boundaries, thin manifolds, nearby manifolds, architecture invariance, sample-size dependence, and three real-world transformations—revealing that convolutional architectures (Glow, U-Net) systematically underperform feedforward ones (MAF, MLP) on the same manifold, and that LIDL/FLIPD estimates drift toward ambient dimensionality regardless of the true LID.

## Results

Across 8 synthetic datasets with known LID (ranging from 1D to 20D), ESS achieves the lowest MAE on 6 of 8 datasets, with particularly strong results on low-dimensional manifolds (MAE 0.03–0.21 on Spaghetti, Moon, and Funnel). NB is competitive on low-dimensional tasks (MAE 0.25–1.12) but fails catastrophically on the Arrows dataset (MAE 440.82, mean estimate 455.85 vs. true LID ~6–48). LIDL and FLIPD, both neural-flow-based methods, show consistently poor performance on IDR-transformed datasets, with LIDL MAE ranging from 2.26 to 14.70 and FLIPD MAE from 2.80 to 17.43. On the challenging Arrows (MS) dataset, no algorithm produces estimates close to ground truth, with ESS (MAE 6.23) being the least inaccurate.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ESS (Johnsson et al., 2014), the classical non-parametric method, dominates on low-dimensional manifolds (1D–5D), achieving MAE below 0.25 on Spaghetti, Moon, and Funnel, and remains the only method that correctly tracks LID as a function of local geometry (e.g., funnel radius, spiral revolution). NB (Stanczuk et al., 2024), a diffusion-model-based method, is the strongest neural baseline on low-dimensional tasks (MAE 0.25–1.12) and is the only method that stabilizes with sample size on FMNIST, but it overestimates LID on curved manifolds (Spheres MAE 1.83) and collapses on the Arrows dataset (mean estimate 455.85). LIDL (Tempczyk et al., 2022) performs well on its original low-dimensional manifold (MAE 0.24–0.90 in Table 4) but degrades severely under IDR transformation into the 784-dim image space (MAE 9.31–14.70), revealing a critical sensitivity to ambient dimensionality and network architecture. FLIPD (Kamkari et al., 2024) is the weakest method overall, with MAE ≥ 2.80 on every dataset and convergence issues that make its 'knee' selection unreliable in practice. The key finding is that accuracy on simple synthetic manifolds does not transfer: methods that pass original benchmarks (LIDL, FLIPD) fail under domain transformation, while the simple ESS method remains robust across most geometric stressors.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper does not extend a single prior method but instead carves new ground as a meta-evaluation framework for the LID estimation literature (ESS, LIDL, NB, FLIPD). Its key conceptual tool, Inverse Domain Representation (IDR), repurposes PCA as a geometry-preserving embedding that maps synthetic manifolds into real-world image spaces, enabling like-for-like cross-architecture comparisons that prior benchmarks could not support. The controlled transformations (Monotonic Embedding, Ambient Space Extension, Auxiliary Dimension Injection) extend the standard benchmarking paradigm of 'known-LID synthetic data' and 'unknown-LID real data' into a middle ground where LID shifts are known even when absolute LID is not.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that accuracy on simple synthetic manifolds does not transfer across domain representations—challenges the field's default validation protocol and suggests that inductive biases of domain-specific architectures (CNNs, U-Nets) fundamentally alter LID estimates even when the underlying manifold is identical. Open questions remain: how to handle non-integer or heterogeneous LID, the effect of quantization and noise, scalability to higher-dimensional manifolds, and the need for principled scalar metrics to replace the current plot-based, qualitative evaluations. The paper also highlights that the δ/t hyperparameter selection in LIDL and FLIPD remains an unsolved practical problem, with estimates varying dramatically across values.
