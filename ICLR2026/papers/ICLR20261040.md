## Problem

Prior transfer learning results for Neyman-Pearson classification assume the source and target share the same class-0 distribution, with shift only in class-1. In practice, both distributions may shift, and a classifier satisfying the source Type-I constraint need not satisfy the target constraint, making it unclear how to leverage source data to simultaneously control both Type-I and Type-II errors without prior knowledge of source-target relatedness.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261040/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The procedure consumes i.i.d. samples from both classes in both source and target domains (n_{0,S}, n_{1,S}, n_{0,T}, n_{1,T} samples over a measurable space X) and outputs a parameterized binary classifier h_θ: X → R with decision rule 1{h_θ(x) ≥ 0}, subject to the constraint that the population Type-I error w.r.t. μ_{0,T} stays below a pre-specified threshold α. The hypothesis class is norm-bounded (‖θ‖ ≤ B) and the surrogate loss φ is L-Lipschitz, convex, and bounded, ensuring uniform concentration of empirical risks.

## Method

The paper proposes a two-stage adaptive transfer learning procedure. In the first stage, source class-0 samples are used to compute an empirical threshold α̂_S (the smallest α′ ≥ α such that the source feasible set intersects the target's near-optimal set), which restricts the hypothesis class to functions whose population Type-I error on the target is controlled. In the second stage, source class-1 samples further reduce Type-II error within this restricted set; if the source and target near-optimal sets intersect, a joint solution is selected, otherwise the procedure falls back to the target-only minimizer, thereby avoiding negative transfer. Computationally, the entire procedure is reduced to a sequence of convex programs solved via a stochastic gradient descent-ascent solver (CP-Solver) with a single projection step, yielding a polynomial gradient complexity bound that scales as O(1/ε²) in the target sample size.

## Results

The paper evaluates on two climate datasets for heavy rain detection: CLIMSIM (124 features) and NASA POWER (6 features), with α=0.1, fixed target samples n_{0,T}=n_{1,T}=40, and source samples n_{0,S}=n_{1,S} varying from 50 to 950 over 10 trials. Results are presented as figures (not tabular) showing Type-I and Type-II errors as a function of source sample size. On CLIMSIM (informative source), TLA keeps Type-I error close to the 0.1 threshold while substantially reducing Type-II error compared to the Only Target baseline. On NASA POWER (uninformative source), TLA adaptively matches Only Target performance, avoiding the high Type-II error exhibited by the Only Source method.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the proposed Transfer Learning Algorithm (TLA) against two baselines: Only Target (ignoring source data) and Only Source (ignoring target data). On CLIMSIM, where the source is informative, TLA outperforms Only Target on Type-II error while keeping Type-I error at the prescribed α=0.1 threshold; Only Target exceeds the Type-I threshold and has higher Type-II error. Only Source, which does not account for the target constraint, performs poorly. On NASA POWER, where the source is uninformative (distribution shift in both μ₀ and μ₁), TLA matches Only Target performance, confirming the no-negative-transfer guarantee, while Only Source suffers from a high Type-II error due to the distribution mismatch. The key finding is that TLA adaptively exploits the source when it is helpful and gracefully falls back to target-only performance when it is not, without requiring prior knowledge of source-target relatedness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the transfer learning framework for Neyman-Pearson classification established by Kalan & Kpotufe (2024b) and Kalan et al. (2025), which were restricted to the special case where source and target share the same class-0 distribution (μ₀,S = μ₀,T) and only μ₁ shifts. The key conceptual departure is handling distribution shift in both μ₀ and μ₁ simultaneously, which introduces the fundamental difficulty that a classifier satisfying the source Type-I constraint need not satisfy the target constraint. The computational guarantee builds on the SGDA-with-projection framework of Mahdavi et al. (2012), reformulating the two-stage adaptive procedure as a sequence of convex programs solvable in polynomial time.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

In practical applications like disease diagnosis, malware detection, and climate science, distribution shifts in both classes are the norm rather than the exception, making the general setting addressed here more realistic than prior work. The adaptive procedure's guarantee of avoiding negative transfer without requiring prior knowledge of source-target relatedness is practically valuable, as practitioners rarely know in advance whether auxiliary data will help. A key open question identified by the authors is establishing matching minimax lower bounds for the general both-shift setting, which would clarify the fundamental limits of transfer in NP classification. The empirical validation is limited to two small-scale climate datasets with fixed target sample sizes, leaving open questions about scalability to larger, more diverse domains.
