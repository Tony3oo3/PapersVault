## Problem

Specialized prover LLMs for formal theorem proving (e.g., Lean 4) solve substantially fewer problems than general-purpose reasoning LLMs: on PutnamBench, reasoning LLMs solve ~83% of problems informally while the best publicly available prover LLMs achieve only 13% with verified formal proofs. Existing agentic frameworks that combine informal reasoning with formal verification use only shallow, single-layer decomposition, breaking down on complex subgoals that remain too difficult to solve directly. The paper addresses this gap by introducing a recursive decomposition strategy that iteratively breaks down unsolved subgoals until they become tractable.

## Input / Output

The system takes a formal theorem statement in Lean 4 as input, along with access to the Mathlib library (v4.15.0) for semantic theorem retrieval via FAISS-indexed sentence-transformer embeddings. The output is a fully verified Lean 4 proof, checked by the Kimina Lean Server. Proofs can be extremely long—averaging 1,454 lines on PutnamBench with the longest exceeding 15,000 lines—far beyond what a single LLM context window can handle, since the agentic framework stitches together independently proven subgoals. The system uses `sorry` placeholders during decomposition and only produces a final proof once all subgoals are discharged.

## Method

HILBERT orchestrates four components in a multi-turn agentic loop: a general-purpose reasoning LLM (Gemini 2.5 Pro/Flash or gpt-oss-120b) for informal proofs and decomposition, a specialized prover LLM (DeepSeek-Prover-V2-7B or Goedel-Prover-V2-32B) for formal tactic generation, a Lean 4 verifier (Kimina Lean Server), and a semantic retriever over Mathlib. The core mechanism is recursive subgoal decomposition: the reasoner first generates an informal proof, then a Lean 4 proof sketch with `have` statements marked `sorry`; subgoals are extracted as independent theorems, each attempted first by the prover (4 candidates), then by the reasoner in a 'shallow solve' with retrieval-augmented formal proof writing (up to 6 correction passes, 30-line length cap), and finally by recursive decomposition up to depth D=5 if both fail. The reasoner also interprets compilation errors, validates mathematical correctness of subgoals, and assembles the final proof by replacing `sorry` placeholders with calls to proven subgoal theorems. This design exploits the complementary strengths of each model: the reasoner excels at decomposition and error interpretation while the prover excels at producing syntactically correct formal tactics for individual subgoals.

## Results

On MiniF2F (244 test problems), HILBERT with Gemini 2.5 Pro + Goedel-Prover-V2-32B achieves a 99.2% pass rate, failing on only 2 problems. On the substantially harder PutnamBench (660 problems), the same configuration solves 462/660 problems (70.0%), surpassing the proprietary SeedProver (50.4%) by nearly 20 percentage points and exceeding the best publicly available baseline (Goedel-Prover-V2-32B self-correction, 13.4%) by over 5×. Even with the open-source gpt-oss-120b as the informal reasoner, HILBERT reaches 90.8% on MiniF2F and 13.3% on PutnamBench, matching the best public prover baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HILBERT outperforms all 11 baselines on PutnamBench, with the most dramatic gap against the best public method: 70.0% vs. Goedel-Prover-V2-32B self-correction at 13.4% (a 5.2× improvement) and DeepSeek-Prover-V2-671B at 7.1%. Against the proprietary SeedProver (50.4%), HILBERT gains 19.6 percentage points. On MiniF2F, HILBERT (99.2%) surpasses all public baselines including Goedel-Prover-V2-32B (92.6% with self-correction), BFS-Prover-V2-32B w/ Planner (95.1%), and DeepSeek-Prover-V2-671B (88.9%), and also edges out the proprietary Delta Prover (95.9%) and SeedProver (99.6% is marginally higher). The open-source gpt-oss-120b variant of HILBERT (90.8% MiniF2F, 13.3% PutnamBench) still exceeds every standalone public prover, confirming that the agentic orchestration—not just model scale—drives the gains. Notably, HILBERT's advantage is largest on PutnamBench, where long multi-step proofs are required, suggesting the recursive decomposition and retrieval-augmented reasoning are most impactful on harder problems.

## Relation to Prior Work

HILBERT extends the line of agentic theorem-proving frameworks (COPRA, Prover-Agent, ProofCompass, DeltaProver) by introducing recursive subgoal decomposition, where previously only shallow single-layer decomposition was attempted. It also departs from the DSP/LEGO-Prover paradigm of using a general-purpose LLM to propose proof sketches with an ATP filling gaps, instead orchestrating a specialized prover LLM, a general-purpose reasoner, a formal verifier, and a semantic retriever in a structured multi-agent pipeline. A key conceptual departure from DeltaProver (which uses only an informal LLM) is the demonstration that prover LLMs become highly effective tools when embedded in an appropriately designed multi-agent framework rather than used in isolation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

HILBERT narrows the large gap between informal mathematical reasoning (~83% on PutnamBench) and formal proof generation (13.4% best public) to a much smaller one (70.0%), suggesting that orchestration and decomposition architecture—not raw model capability—was the primary bottleneck. The framework's ability to generate proofs exceeding 15,000 lines and consume 22.8M+ tokens (far beyond any single model's context window) demonstrates that agentic decomposition can effectively extend the practical reasoning horizon of LLMs. Key limitations include heavy dependence on Gemini 2.5 Pro for the headline result (open-source gpt-oss-120b achieves only 13.3%, comparable to the standalone prover), and the failure-mode analysis shows 44.8% of Gemini's failures occur at the shallow-solve stage, indicating the final verification loop remains a bottleneck. The authors propose a virtuous cycle where HILBERT-generated proofs train better prover and reasoner models, but this is left as future work.
