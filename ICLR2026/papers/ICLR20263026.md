## Problem

Human-in-the-loop (HITL) is widely invoked as a safety and accountability safeguard for automated decision-making systems, yet existing legal frameworks (GDPR Article 22, EU AI Act Article 14) rely on vague terms like 'meaningful' and 'effective' oversight without specifying what computational structure is actually required. There is no unified formal framework to distinguish the vastly different degrees of human involvement that HITL can entail, nor to connect those degrees to their distinct safety outcomes, failure modes, and legal-moral responsibility implications.

## Input / Output

The formalism takes an oracle machine T• (encoding the ADMS's algorithmic decision-making process) and a human oracle function f (representing human judgment at query points), and classifies the resulting HITL setup by the type of reduction between them. The classification hinges on the concept of a 'real query'—an oracle call where a fork exists in the computation tree and not all branches yield the same set of possible outputs—rather than merely counting oracle calls. The output is a three-way setup classification (trivial monitoring, endpoint action, involved interaction), a five-category failure mode taxonomy correlated with setup type, and a legal-moral responsibility analysis identifying an unavoidable explainability-responsibility trade-off.

## Method

The paper maps HITL setups onto oracle machines and reductions from computability theory: trivial monitoring corresponds to a total function (the human can only halt the machine, never influence its computation), endpoint action corresponds to a many-one reduction (the machine makes exactly one real query then halts, handing the final decision to the human), and involved interaction corresponds to a Turing reduction (the machine makes potentially unbounded real queries in a computational 'ping-pong' with the human). The key distinguishing mechanism is the 'real query' definition, which prevents tokenistic setups where the machine asks questions but ignores or inverts the answers. The authors then construct a five-category failure mode taxonomy (machine components, process/workflow, human-machine interface, human component, exogenous circumstances) and show how failure susceptibility correlates with setup type. Finally, they analyze UK/EU legal frameworks to show that current law only contemplates trivial monitoring or endpoint action, and identify an unavoidable trade-off: involved interactions increase explainability (by breaking the black box into smaller interpretable segments) but simultaneously obscure responsibility attribution due to human-machine entanglement across many decision points.

## Results

This is a purely theoretical paper with no quantitative experiments or benchmark results. The key contributions are: (1) a formal three-way classification of HITL setups—trivial monitoring (total function), endpoint action (many-one reduction), and involved interaction (Turing reduction)—unifying existing HITL/HOTL/HOOTL terminology; (2) a five-category failure mode taxonomy (machine components, process/workflow, human-machine interface, human component, exogenous circumstances) correlated with setup type; and (3) identification of an unavoidable trade-off between responsibility attribution and technical explainability, where stronger reductions (involved interactions) increase transparency but obfuscate causal responsibility. The paper demonstrates via case studies (Uber self-driving fatality, Notre-Dame fire, MCG security breach) that real HITL failures span multiple taxonomy categories simultaneously.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not perform empirical comparisons against baselines, as it is a theoretical formalisation. However, it positions its framework against several existing approaches: (1) Andersen & Maalej's (2024) 10 HITL design patterns, of which only three relate to deployment/inference—and all three map to trivial monitoring or endpoint action, with none capturing involved interaction or task abortion; (2) GDPR Article 22 and EU AI Act Article 14, which the paper argues only implicitly require endpoint-action-level oversight ('meaningful'/'effective') and do not distinguish among the three reduction types; and (3) the SCHUFA court case (C-634/21), where a nominally endpoint-action credit scoring system was deemed by courts to function as trivial monitoring, illustrating the practical difficulty of verifying setup type. The paper's formalism is presented as a tool to make these distinctions precise and to prevent 'tokenistic' HITL implementations that satisfy the letter but not the spirit of regulatory requirements.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes oracle machines and reductions from classical computability theory (Soare 1987; van Melkebeek 2000) to formalise HITL setups, a move not previously attempted in the AI safety or governance literature. It unifies the disparate qualitative terminology (HOTL, HOOTL, HIC, MHC) and design patterns (Andersen & Maalej 2024) under a single computational framework, and connects to the responsibility-gap literature (Matthias 2004; Sienknecht 2024) and the 'moral crumple zone' critique (Elish 2019). The failure-mode taxonomy draws on empirical oversight literature (Sterz et al. 2024; Sudeeptha et al. 2024) but reorganises it around the computational setup types rather than treating oversight as a monolithic concept.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a formal, non-circumventable framework for regulators to distinguish tokenistic HITL from genuinely involved human interaction, directly addressing omissions in GDPR Article 22 and EU AI Act Article 14. The unavoidable explainability–responsibility trade-off (more human involvement increases transparency but obscures causal attribution) is a key open problem for liability law that the paper flags but does not resolve. Practical limitations remain: determining a setup's reduction type requires deep computational analysis, intermediate reduction types are deferred, and the framework does not yet address resource-bounded human oracles or multi-agent HITL configurations.
