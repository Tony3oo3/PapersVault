## Problem

Scaling laws research has focused overwhelmingly on English, yet the most prominent AI models serve billions of international users. Existing multilingual scaling laws (Chinchilla, DCSL, MSL) fail to jointly account for multi-epoch data repetition, cross-lingual transfer beyond a target's language family, and the capacity-driven 'curse of multilinguality' where adding languages degrades per-language loss. This paper fills these gaps with the largest multilingual scaling study to date (774 experiments, 10M–8B parameters, 400+ training languages, 48 evaluation languages).

## Input / Output

ATLAS consumes model size N, per-language training tokens D_t, the full language mixture M (with per-language token counts), and the number of training languages K, and outputs a predicted vocabulary-insensitive loss for any target language. The effective data term D_eff is decomposed into three weighted components—target-language tokens, up to |K_t|=3 most co-sampled transfer languages, and an 'other' remainder—each passed through a shared saturation function S_λ(D;U) that smoothly caps returns after one epoch of unique tokens U. Beyond loss prediction, the method yields a 38×38 cross-lingual transfer matrix (Bilingual Transfer Scores), closed-form iso-loss scaling equations for expanding language coverage K→rK, and a compute crossover formula C = 10283128 × N^1.65 for deciding between pretraining from scratch and finetuning a Unimax checkpoint.

## Method

ATLAS modifies the Chinchilla power-law L = E + A/N^α + B/D^β by replacing D with a structured effective-data term D_eff that separately models monolingual target data, a small set of transfer languages (the 3 most co-sampled across mixtures), and an 'other' remainder, each weighted by learned coefficients τ_i initialized from empirical Bilingual Transfer Scores. A shared saturation function S_λ(D;U) = U[1+(1−exp(−λ(D/U−1)))/λ] for D>U handles multi-epoch repetition without the two-stage fitting required by DCSL, making ATLAS fit in one stage with fewer parameters. For the curse of multilinguality, the paper derives L(K,N,D_t) = L_∞ + A·K^φ/N^α + B·K^ψ/D_t^β (φ=0.11, ψ=−0.04), from which closed-form iso-loss equations give the exact N, D_tot, and compute multipliers needed to expand language coverage by a factor r without degrading loss. Cross-lingual transfer is quantified via two complementary scores: the Bilingual Transfer Score (relative data-efficiency of a 50/50 bilingual model vs. monolingual) and the Finetuning Adaptation Score (integrated loss change on all targets after finetuning a Unimax checkpoint on one source), together producing the 38×38 transfer matrix.

## Results

In the multilingual setting, ATLAS (with all three data terms: D_t + D_other + ∑K_t D_i) achieves R²=0.98 overall, with R²(N)=0.89, R²(D)=0.96, R²(C)=0.98, and R²(M)=0.82, substantially outperforming the Chinchilla Scaling Law (R²=0.64, R²(N)=−0.99) and the Multilingual Scaling Law of He et al. (R²=0.67, R²(N)=−0.65). In the monolingual setting, ATLAS achieves R²(N)=0.88 versus 0.68 for Chinchilla and 0.78 for the Data-Constrained Scaling Law. The paper further derives a 38×38 cross-lingual transfer matrix, quantifies the curse of multilinguality (φ=0.11, ψ=−0.04), and identifies a pretrain-vs-finetune crossover at 144B–283B tokens.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Chinchilla Scaling Law (Hoffmann et al., 2022), ATLAS achieves a dramatic improvement in the multilingual setting: overall R² rises from 0.64 to 0.98, and R²(N) goes from a deeply negative −0.99 to 0.89, meaning Chinchilla's law actively mispredicts loss for larger models in multilingual training while ATLAS generalizes well. Against the Data-Constrained Scaling Law (Muennighoff et al., 2024), ATLAS wins on the most critical generalization axis R²(N) in the monolingual setting (0.88 vs. 0.78), while overall R² is comparable (0.92 vs. 0.93). Against the Multilingual Scaling Law of He et al. (2024), ATLAS outperforms on every held-out dimension: R²(N) 0.89 vs. −0.65, R²(D) 0.96 vs. 0.73, R²(C) 0.98 vs. 0.67, and R²(M) 0.82 vs. 0.70. The ablation shows that the Transfer Language term (∑K_t D_i) is essential for the R²(M) gain over MSL (0.82 vs. 0.66 without it), confirming that explicit per-language transfer coefficients capture cross-lingual dynamics that family-level grouping alone cannot.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Chinchilla scaling law (Hoffmann et al., 2022) and the Data-Constrained Scaling Law (Muennighoff et al., 2024) to the multilingual setting, departing from the Multilingual Scaling Law (He et al., 2024) by explicitly decomposing effective data exposure into three independently-weighted terms: target-language data, a small set of transfer languages, and a residual 'other' term. Prior multilingual scaling work was limited to models under 1.2B parameters and did not account for multi-epoch data repetition or cross-lingual transfer beyond language-family grouping. ATLAS unifies these concerns in a single-stage fit with a saturation function for data repetition, and additionally introduces a new scaling law for the curse of multilinguality (modeling loss as a function of K languages, N, and D) and a pretrain-vs-finetune crossover formula that had no prior formulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

With 774 experiments spanning 10M–8B parameters and 400+ training languages, this is the most comprehensive multilingual scaling study to date, providing practitioners with directly actionable tools: a 38×38 transfer matrix for mixture selection, closed-form iso-loss equations for expanding language coverage, and a compute-budget formula for choosing between pretraining and finetuning. The finding that model size N mitigates the curse of multilinguality more effectively than additional data D (|∂S/∂log N| > |∂S/∂log D|) has concrete budget-allocation implications. Open questions include whether these laws hold at frontier scale (100B+), whether the Unimax-specific finetuning crossover generalizes to other checkpoint families, and why English exhibits the largest compute-efficiency tax despite being the most common transfer source.
