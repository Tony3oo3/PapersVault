## Problem

Despite the growing reliance on ultrasound for prenatal care and the global shortage of trained sonographers, no standardized benchmark exists to evaluate Vision-Language Models on fetal ultrasound interpretation. This gap stems from the modality's operator dependency, noisy imaging conditions, and the limited public availability of annotated fetal ultrasound datasets, leaving VLM capabilities in this clinically critical domain entirely unmeasured.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264322/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance pairs a single fetal ultrasound image (clinical or phantom) with a multiple-choice question drawn from one of five clinical tasks: anatomical plane identification, visual grounding (where a red bounding box is overlaid on the image and the model must name the enclosed structure), fetal orientation assessment, clinical view conformity, and clinical diagnosis. The MCQ format was deliberately chosen to enable objective, automated scoring and to reduce ambiguity and hallucination risks inherent in free-text generation. The benchmark contains 42,036 images and 93,451 question-answer pairs, with roughly 19k images sourced from anatomical phantoms.

## Method

Fetal-Gauge is constructed by aggregating 13 publicly available fetal ultrasound datasets and unifying their heterogeneous annotations—segmentation masks are converted to bounding-box visual grounding questions, image-level labels become classification MCQs, and vocabulary is normalized across sources (e.g., expanding abbreviations, harmonizing synonyms). A strict patient-wise splitting strategy prevents data leakage, and small datasets are reserved exclusively for the test set to probe generalization. The evaluation systematically benchmarks 15 state-of-the-art VLMs (six medical-specific, eight general-purpose, one commercial) under a unified accuracy-based protocol, with additional analyses on phantom vs. clinical performance and bounding-box size effects. To assess domain adaptation, two open-source models (Llama-3.2-11B and Qwen2.5-VL-7B) are fine-tuned via LoRA on the training split across multiple epochs, revealing that task-specific training lifts Llama-3.2 from 33% to 85% overall accuracy while GPT-5 remains the strongest zero-shot model at 55%.

## Results

On the Fetal-Gauge benchmark (93,451 QA pairs across 5 clinical tasks), GPT-5 achieves the highest overall accuracy at 55%, followed by Lingshu-32B (46%) and Lingshu-7B (40%), while most other models perform at or near the random-guess baseline of 26%. After LoRA fine-tuning on the training split, Llama-3.2-11B-Vision-Instruct improves dramatically from 33% to 85% overall accuracy, and Qwen2.5-VL-7B improves from 24% to 52%. GPT-5 leads on Plane Identification (66%), Visual Grounding (58%), and Clinical View Conformity (62%), but no model exceeds 49% on Clinical Diagnosis or Fetal Orientation Assessment without fine-tuning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-5 dominates all other models on overall accuracy (55% vs. 46% for Lingshu-32B and 40% for Lingshu-7B), leading on PI (66%), VC (62%), and VG (58%), though it trails HuatuoGPT-Vision-7B on Clinical Diagnosis (20% vs. 49%). Among open-source models, Lingshu-32B and Lingshu-7B are the strongest, attributed to their explicit training on adult ultrasound data; they outperform all other open models by 10–25 points on overall accuracy. Medical-specific VLMs (MedGemma-27B at 32%, MedVLM-R1 at 21%, HuatuoGPT-Vision-7B at 29%) show only marginal gains over random chance, confirming that training on adult CT/MRI does not transfer well to fetal ultrasound. General-purpose models (InternVL3, Qwen2.5-VL, Aya Vision, vip-llava) cluster between 19–33% overall, with none substantially exceeding the 26% random baseline. Fine-tuning Llama-3.2-11B with LoRA for 7 epochs yields 85% overall accuracy, surpassing GPT-5 by 30 points, while fine-tuned Qwen2.5-VL-7B reaches 52%, matching GPT-5's overall score but with much weaker PI (37–57%) and VG (43–52%) performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing the first VQA benchmark specifically for fetal ultrasound, a domain entirely absent from prior medical VQA efforts (VQA-Med, VQA-RAD, SLAKE, OmniMedVQA, CAREs) that have focused on adult modalities like CT, MRI, and X-ray. Conceptually, it repurposes the standard MCQ-VQA evaluation format and integrates 13 heterogeneous public datasets (originally designed for classification, segmentation, and detection) into a unified question-answering framework. On the model side, it positions itself against the emerging wave of medical VLMs (Lingshu, MedGemma, MedVLM-R1, HuatuoGPT-Vision) that have been trained on adult imaging but never systematically evaluated on fetal ultrasound, revealing that none of them were designed for this modality.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Fetal-Gauge fills a critical evaluation gap: without it, progress in fetal ultrasound VLMs cannot be measured or compared, and the global sonographer shortage (affecting 132M+ annual births) remains unaddressed by AI tooling. The finding that the best off-the-shelf model (GPT-5) reaches only 55% accuracy—far below clinical deployment thresholds—while a LoRA-fine-tuned 11B model jumps to 85% reframes the bottleneck as data availability rather than model architecture, pointing toward domain-specific training as the most promising near-term path. Open questions include whether MCQ evaluation captures the full complexity of clinical reasoning, how models will generalize across the wide inter-operator and inter-machine variability inherent to ultrasound, and whether phantom-trained models can safely transfer to real patients.
