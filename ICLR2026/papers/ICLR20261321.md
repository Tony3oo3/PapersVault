## Problem

Multimodal models suffer severe and uneven performance degradation when modalities are missing at inference, with the impact depending heavily on which modality is absent. The paper identifies the root cause as an implicit training bias: modalities rich in low-frequency information dominate gradient updates, causing other modalities to be under-optimized. Existing balancing methods operate in the spatial domain and have not fully resolved this imbalance.

## Input / Output

MWAM is a training-time-only, parameter-free module that consumes the raw multimodal inputs (e.g., 112×112 RGB/Depth/IR images, 80×80×80 MRI volumes, or video+optical flow frames) by applying DCT to 8×8 non-overlapping patches and extracting 2×2 low-frequency (top-left) and 2×2 high-frequency (bottom-right) blocks from each. At inference the module is fully detached, so the host model's output format (class labels, segmentation masks, or bounding boxes) and computational cost remain unchanged. The only runtime addition is a negligible FLOPs overhead during training (e.g., +0.0452G for MMANet).

## Method

The core insight is that modality dominance can be quantified in the frequency domain via a Frequency Ratio Metric (FRM), defined as the L1-norm of the element-wise ratio between low-frequency and high-frequency DCT components (with a small σ to handle zeros). Guided by FRM, the Multimodal Weight Allocation Module (MWAM) assigns each modality a dynamic weight through a reflected-sigmoid function of the modality's FRM relative to the batch mean, then applies this weight either by scaling per-branch gradients (parameter-free) or by weighting auxiliary-head losses. An FRM bank with exponential moving average (ω=0.5) smooths the metric across mini-batches to handle anomalous inputs. The theoretical justification rests on NTK analysis: eigenvectors with larger eigenvalues—corresponding to low-frequency functions—converge faster, so the modality richest in low-frequency content naturally dominates early gradient signals and suppresses optimization of weaker branches. MWAM is plug-and-play, requiring no architectural changes, and has been validated across CNN and ViT backbones on classification, segmentation, detection, and action recognition tasks.

## Results

On CASIA-SURF (multimodal classification), MMANet+MWAM achieves the best average accuracy of 97.03% and lowest PCR of 2.02%, surpassing the strongest baseline MMANet (96.06% Acc, 3.61 PCR) and CRMT-JT (94.44% Acc, 5.02 PCR). On BRATS2020 (brain tumor segmentation), GSS+MWAM reaches 87.56% Dice with 4.44 PCR, outperforming vanilla GSS (86.41% Dice, 5.30 PCR) and RFNet (85.41% Dice, 5.62 PCR). On NYU-Depth V2 (semantic segmentation), MMANet+MWAM attains 45.81% MIoU with 12.66 PCR, improving over vanilla MMANet (43.47% MIoU, 14.87 PCR). The module is parameter-free and adds negligible FLOPs (<0.07G) with zero inference overhead.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MWAM consistently improves every host model it is integrated into. On CASIA-SURF, SF-MD+MWAM (96.41% Acc, 2.97 PCR) surpasses the SOTA baselines CRMT-JT (94.44% Acc, 5.02 PCR) and MMFormer (94.07% Acc, 4.75 PCR), while MMANet+MWAM (97.03% Acc, 2.02 PCR) further extends the already-strong MMANet (96.06% Acc, 3.61 PCR). On BRATS2020, GSS+MWAM (87.56% Dice, 4.44 PCR) outperforms both RFNet (85.41% Dice, 5.62 PCR) and mmFormer (85.64% Dice, 5.21 PCR), and RFNet+MWAM (86.41% Dice) matches or exceeds the SOTA LS3M in PCR. On NYU-Depth V2, ESANet-MD+MWAM (44.47% MIoU, 13.31 PCR) and MMANet+MWAM (45.81% MIoU, 12.66 PCR) both substantially outperform their vanilla counterparts. On UCF-101 action recognition, MWAM (71.48% Acc, 18.65 PCR) beats both OGM-GE (69.19% Acc, 20.66 PCR) and LFM (70.83% Acc, 19.57 PCR) without task-specific tuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of modality-balancing work (OGM, CRMT, COM) that quantifies modality preference in the spatial/feature domain, arguing instead that the frequency domain offers a more principled and global basis for measuring which modality dominates training. It repurposes the frequency principle / spectral bias literature (Rahaman et al. 2019; Xu et al. 2020) and NTK convergence theory to provide a theoretical justification for why low-frequency-rich modalities are preferentially optimized, then translates this into a concrete metric (FRM) and a parameter-free training intervention (MWAM). The plug-and-play design is conceptually similar to gradient-modulation methods but is distinguished by its frequency-domain signal source and its ability to scale to arbitrary numbers of modalities without architectural changes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical appeal is strong: MWAM is parameter-free, incurs zero inference overhead, and can be bolted onto existing SOTA missing-modality methods (RFNet, mmFormer, GSS, MMANet) to push them past their own performance ceilings, which is a useful property for practitioners who do not want to redesign architectures. However, the method's reliance on DCT-based frequency decomposition is inherently tied to grid-structured image data, raising open questions about generalization to non-visual modalities (text, audio) or irregularly sampled inputs. The paper also notes that the frequency-sampling window size (q) has a narrow effective range, and the scaling factors in the weight-allocation function, while shown to be relatively insensitive, still require per-task tuning in extreme settings (e.g., batch size 1).
