## Problem

Existing inference-time hallucination detection methods rely exclusively on aleatoric (data) uncertainty captured through temperature-based sampling at the prediction layer, ignoring epistemic (model) uncertainty. The paper argues that standard next-token sampling is suboptimal for hallucination detection because it does not probe the model's sensitivity to parameter-level perturbations, which is the signal most indicative of whether the model truly knows the answer.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264355/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt (with in-context learning examples) and an off-the-shelf pre-trained LLM, requiring no fine-tuning or auxiliary model training. It produces K generated text samples and a hallucination detection score computed as answer entropy (or an alternative uncertainty metric) over those samples. The noise is injected into MLP activations of roughly the top third of transformer layers, with the same noise vector shared across all selected layers to prevent cancellation through skip connections; the perturbation is implemented via the pyvene library and introduces no measurable latency overhead.

## Method

The core contribution is a training-free Bayesian surrogate distribution q(ω) that approximates the posterior over model parameters by injecting non-negative uniform noise U(0, α) into MLP activations of upper transformer layers, which is mathematically equivalent to perturbing the MLP bias terms. This captures epistemic uncertainty, while temperature-based sampling at the prediction layer simultaneously captures aleatoric uncertainty; the two sources are shown to be complementary (Pearson correlation 0.58) and their combination yields the best detection. A key practical advantage is that activation-level noise injection allows K independent 'model samples' to be generated in parallel within a single batched forward pass, unlike parameter-level perturbation which would require K separate forward passes. The detection score is the answer entropy over K samples (exact string matching for formatted answers, BERT-embedding clustering for free-form answers), evaluated threshold-free via AUROC.

## Results

Noise-enhanced sampling consistently improves hallucination detection AUROC across all six evaluated models (Gemma-2B, Phi-3-mini, Llama-3.2-3B, Mistral-7B, Llama-2-7B, Llama-2-13B) and three datasets (GSM8K, CSQA, TriviaQA). The largest gains appear on GSM8K, where Phi-3-mini-4k-instruct improves from 65.86 to 72.51 AUROC (+6.65) and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct from 76.53 to 82.70 (+6.17). On CSQA, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 gains the most (76.52→79.55, +3.03), while TriviaQA shows smaller but consistent improvements (e.g., Llama-2-13B-chat: 73.39→75.10). The method is training-free, adds no measurable latency overhead, and does not degrade generation accuracy (majority-vote ACC).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is temperature-only sampling (no noise injection), which captures only aleatoric uncertainty. Noise-enhanced sampling outperforms this baseline on every model–dataset combination in Table 3, with the most substantial gains on GSM8K (up to +6.65 AUROC for Phi-3-mini) and CSQA (up to +3.03 for Mistral-7B), while TriviaQA shows smaller but consistent improvements (up to +1.71 for Llama-2-13B). The paper also compares against input perturbation (shuffling in-context learning examples, following Jiang et al. 2023b), which captures aleatoric uncertainty in the data. On Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct (Table 7), model noise injection alone (GSM8K: 82.70, CSQA: 72.83, TriviaQA: 78.49) outperforms input perturbation alone (76.10, 72.07, 78.33), and combining both yields the best results (82.84, 72.98, 79.20), confirming that epistemic and aleatoric data uncertainty are complementary. Across five uncertainty metrics on TriviaQA (Table 4), noise injection improves AUROC for every metric tested (e.g., Semantic Entropy: 75.70→77.21; selfCheckGPT-NLI: 75.80→77.53), demonstrating that the sampling improvement is metric-agnostic.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the multi-sample uncertainty-based hallucination detection line (Lin et al. 2022, 2024; Kuhn et al. 2023a; Chen et al. 2024; Malinin & Gales 2021) by arguing that standard next-token sampling is suboptimal because it captures only aleatoric (data) uncertainty, and proposing to additionally capture epistemic (model) uncertainty via a training-free Bayesian surrogate distribution. The surrogate is implemented as uniform noise injection into MLP activations, drawing conceptually on variational Bayesian deep learning (Gal & Ghahramani 2016b; Kendall & Gal 2017) but avoiding any retraining or dropout. The approach is positioned as complementary to input-perturbation methods (Jiang et al. 2023b; Gao et al. 2024), which address aleatoric uncertainty in the data rather than model uncertainty.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method is immediately deployable—training-free, requiring only a single noise-magnitude hyperparameter and no architectural changes—making it attractive for practitioners who need lightweight hallucination detection at inference time. However, the AUROC gains are modest (typically 2–6 points) and the Bayesian framing, while elegant, amounts to adding bounded uniform noise to a subset of activations, raising the question of whether the theoretical justification is necessary or merely post-hoc. Open questions include whether the approach transfers to much larger models (100B+), whether the specific choice of MLP bias perturbation is optimal versus other parameter subsets, and whether the per-model/per-dataset noise-magnitude tuning can be replaced by a more principled, scale-invariant rule.
