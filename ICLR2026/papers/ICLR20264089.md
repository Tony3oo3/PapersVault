## Problem

Federated clustering with an unknown total number of clusters K across privacy-constrained clients whose local cluster sets are heterogeneous but potentially overlapping remains unsolved. Existing federated methods either assume all clients share identical cluster sets, require prior knowledge of K, or (as with AFCL) leak enough information for the server to reconstruct raw client data. This paper tackles the setting where no client knows K, clusters overlap across clients, and raw data cannot be shared.

## Input / Output

Each client g holds N_g high-dimensional samples drawn from a local mixture with K_g known components and fixed weights π_kg; the global K is unknown and no client possesses all clusters (2 ≤ K_g < K). During training, clients transmit only a (maximizer, uncertainty-radius) tuple per component—never raw data—while the final aggregation radius ε_kg^final is a user-tuned hyperparameter. The server outputs per-client cluster center parameters, an estimated global cluster count K̂*, and the super-cluster membership assignment linking each local component to its global cluster.

## Method

FedGEM is a two-stage federated Generalized EM algorithm. In the collaborative training stage, each client runs local EM steps, then solves a semi-infinite optimization to obtain the radius of a Euclidean uncertainty ball around each component's M-step maximizer such that any point in the ball does not decrease the expected complete-data log-likelihood. The server performs pairwise closed-form distance checks between uncertainty balls to detect cross-client cluster overlaps, computes a shared update vector ν* lying within both balls, and aggregates to produce new parameters. In the final aggregation stage, the server merges all components whose estimates fall within a user-specified radius to infer K̂*. For isotropic GMMs, the radius problem admits a bi-convex 2-dimensional reformulation solvable by bisection in O(log(ε_tol^{-1})) time, and the paper proves first-order stability, contraction, and probabilistic convergence to a neighborhood of the true parameters, guaranteeing correct K estimation with high probability.

## Results

FedGEM is evaluated on 8 datasets (MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, EMNIST, CIFAR-10, Abalone, Frog A, Frog B, Waveform) using Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) as the primary metric. Among methods that do not require prior knowledge of the global cluster count K, FedGEM achieves the best ARI on every dataset, ranging from 0.138 (Abalone) to 0.552 (Frog A). Notably, FedGEM also outperforms several methods that *do* know K: it beats k-FED on MNIST (0.452 vs. 0.354), Abalone (0.138 vs. 0.100), and Waveform (0.335 vs. 0.277), and beats FFCM-avg1 on all 8 datasets. For cluster count estimation (Table 2), FedGEM estimates 13.63 clusters on MNIST (true K=10) and 58.67 on EMNIST (true K=47), far closer to ground truth than DP-GMM (110.20, 247.40) or AFCL (501.82, 575.39).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against AFCL (the only other federated method without prior knowledge of K), FedGEM wins decisively on all 8 datasets, e.g., MNIST ARI 0.452 vs. 0.038 and Waveform 0.335 vs. 0.157. Against DP-GMM (centralized, unknown K), FedGEM also wins on every dataset, with the gap widening on high-dimensional data (CIFAR-10: 0.286 vs. 0.068). Among methods that *do* know K, FedGEM beats k-FED on MNIST (0.452 vs. 0.354), Abalone (0.138 vs. 0.100), and Waveform (0.335 vs. 0.277), but loses on FMNIST (0.287 vs. 0.288, near-tie), CIFAR-10 (0.286 vs. 0.358), and Frog A (0.552 vs. 0.617). FedGEM ties FedKmeans on EMNIST (0.285) and beats it on Abalone and Waveform, but trails on MNIST (0.452 vs. 0.640) and CIFAR-10 (0.286 vs. 0.437). Against FFCM-avg2, FedGEM wins on MNIST (0.452 vs. 0.336), EMNIST (0.285 vs. 0.114), Abalone (0.138 vs. 0.102), and Waveform (0.335 vs. 0.268), but loses on CIFAR-10 (0.286 vs. 0.513), Frog A (0.552 vs. 0.720), and Frog B (0.468 vs. 0.645). Overall, FedGEM is the strongest method in the unknown-K setting and competitive with known-K methods, particularly on datasets where cluster heterogeneity across clients is high.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Generalized EM framework (Wu, 1983) into a federated setting, using the first-order stability (FOS) condition from Balakrishnan et al. (2014) as the backbone of its convergence analysis. It departs from prior federated clustering methods (k-FED, FFCM, FedKmeans) that all require the global cluster count K to be known in advance, and from AFCL (Zhang et al., 2025), the only other unknown-K federated method, which suffers from privacy vulnerabilities due to sharing arrays of the same size as raw data. The key conceptual departure is the use of per-component uncertainty sets (Euclidean balls around local M-step maximizers) whose intersections enable the server to detect cluster overlaps and infer K via closed-form computations, without requiring clients to share raw data or same-dimensional arrays.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FedGEM addresses a practically important gap: OEMs and other federated settings where the total number of fault classes or clusters is genuinely unknown and clients cannot share raw data. The theoretical contribution—probabilistic convergence guarantees and a tractable bi-convex reformulation of the radius problem for isotropic GMMs—provides a foundation that prior federated clustering work lacked. Limitations include overestimation of K on high-dimensional datasets (e.g., 37.72 vs. true 10 for CIFAR-10), the requirement for well-separated clusters in the convergence proof, and the restriction to isotropic GMMs for the tractable client-side computation. Open questions include extending the framework to anisotropic or non-Gaussian mixtures, reducing the K overestimation, and formalizing the privacy guarantees beyond the preliminary differential privacy discussion.
