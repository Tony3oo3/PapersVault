## Problem

LoRA and its variants impose a low-rank structural bottleneck on weight updates, confining adaptation to a small subspace that may be insufficient for complex, high-rank patterns. While high-rank alternatives like HiRA relax this constraint, they still rely on priors from the original weights, effectively restricting updates to a weight-dependent subspace. The paper asks whether parameter efficiency can be achieved without any low-rank constraint or dependence on pre-existing weight structure.

## Input / Output

The method takes a frozen pretrained linear layer W₀ ∈ R^{h×d} and produces an additive update ΔW of the same shape, constructed by broadcasting K learnable scalars over a fixed, randomized partition of the h×d weight indices. K is set to r(d+h) to match the exact parameter budget of a rank-r LoRA adapter, and the tessellation is shared across all layers of identical shape to keep metadata overhead constant regardless of network depth. The resulting ΔW is full-rank and can be merged into W₀ for zero-latency inference with no architectural modification.

## Method

MoSA partitions the entries of each target weight matrix into K disjoint groups (tesserae) via Balanced Random Tessellation (BRT), which randomly permutes all indices and splits them into near-equal contiguous blocks, then assigns one learnable scalar λ_k per group. The update is ΔW = Σ_k λ_k M_k where M_k is a binary mask, making every weight element modulated by exactly one scalar while the overall update remains full-rank. A key theoretical result (Theorem 1) proves via Schur-convexity that balanced group sizes minimize the expected squared deviation from the unconstrained gradient update, justifying BRT over skewed or spatially structured partitions. The non-local random grouping disrupts short-range correlations in the weight matrix, acting as an implicit regularizer against co-adaptation. For efficient training, a custom segmented-reduction backward kernel aggregates per-group gradients in a single pass using a stable permutation, achieving up to ~9,500× speedup over PyTorch autograd at small K while requiring only O(N) metadata storage.

## Results

On the 8-dataset commonsense reasoning benchmark with Llama-3-8B, MoSA (LoRA r=32 equivalent budget, 0.7002% params) achieves 87.63% average accuracy, outperforming the strongest baseline HiRA (86.72%) by +0.91% and LoRA (80.79%) by +6.84%. On the out-of-distribution GSM8K task (trained on MetaMathQA), MoSA reaches 78.00% exact-match accuracy, a +7.19% margin over HiRA (70.81%). On ConvAI2 open-domain dialogue, MoSA scores 50.14% average across six metrics (BLEU-4, BERTScore P/R/F1, METEOR, ROUGE-L), surpassing HiRA (47.80%) by +2.34%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MoSA outperforms all 11 baselines across every main evaluation. Against the closest competitor HiRA (Huang et al., 2025), MoSA leads by +0.91% average on commonsense reasoning (Llama-3-8B), +2.34% on ConvAI2, and a striking +7.19% on OOD GSM8K (78.00% vs. 70.81%). Against LoRA and DoRA (the most widely used PEFT baselines), MoSA gains +6.84% and +2.43% respectively on commonsense reasoning with Llama-3-8B. MoRA, despite its high-rank design, collapses on HellaSwag (43.53% vs. MoSA's 96.57%), suggesting its square-matrix parameterization is unstable; MoSA avoids this failure mode. Prompt Tuning and P-Tuning are far behind (36.96% and 18.11% average on Llama-3-8B commonsense), confirming that input-space methods are insufficient for this task. In the supplementary comparison (Table 8, Llama-2-7B), MoSA beats PiSSA (59.8 vs. 53.22 GSM8K), HD-PiSSA (59.8 vs. 52.92), LoRA-GA (59.8 vs. 53.60), and LoRA-Pro (59.8 vs. 57.57) on GSM8K, and also leads on HumanEval (24.39 vs. 22.97 for LoRA-Pro). Against HydraLoRA (Table 9), MoSA achieves a near-tie (87.63% vs. 87.60% average) while using only ~0.70% of parameters versus HydraLoRA's ~3.60%, a 4.5× parameter reduction.

## Relation to Prior Work

MoSA repurposes the randomized parameter-sharing principle from hashing-based model compression (Chen et al., 2015) as a PEFT strategy, applying it to the fine-tuning update ΔW rather than the full pretrained weight matrix. It departs from the low-rank bottleneck that defines LoRA and its descendants (DoRA, AdaLoRA, PiSSA) and from the Hadamard-product mechanism of HiRA, instead constructing full-rank updates via broadcasting a small set of learned scalars over a fixed random tessellation. The theoretical contribution—a Schur-convexity argument proving balanced group sizes minimize update-direction error—is novel within the PEFT literature, and the custom segmented-reduction backward kernel addresses a systems gap that prior sharing-based methods (which target static compression) did not need to solve.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MoSA offers a practically attractive alternative to LoRA: arbitrary integer budget granularity (no discrete rank constraint), lossless merge into the base model for zero-overhead inference, and no architectural changes. The finding that non-local random sharing acts as an implicit regularizer against co-adaptation provides a mechanistic explanation for why the method generalizes well, particularly on OOD tasks like GSM8K. Open questions include whether the advantage persists at much larger scales (70B+), whether the regularization effect is architecture-specific, and whether the custom kernel's speedup holds on heterogeneous hardware. The method's simplicity also raises the question of whether further gains come from smarter tessellation design or from combining MoSA with other PEFT principles.
