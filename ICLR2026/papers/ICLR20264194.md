## Problem

While input-based explanations are widely assumed to help detect and mitigate bias in NLP models, the relationship between explainability and fairness has been studied almost exclusively through qualitative analysis on small sets of methods. This paper provides the first large-scale quantitative study of whether and how input-based explanations can (1) identify biased predictions, (2) select fair models among candidates, and (3) mitigate bias during training, specifically in the critical context of hate speech detection.

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes text examples containing identity-marking terms (race, gender, religion) from Civil Comments and Jigsaw datasets, along with a curated sensitive token vocabulary. For each example, it constructs counterfactuals by swapping sensitive tokens across social groups to compute individual unfairness scores, then generates token-level attribution scores using 16 explanation method variants. The key outputs are sensitive token reliance scores (max absolute attribution on sensitive tokens), fairness correlations (Pearson correlation between reliance and individual unfairness per class-group pair), and debiased models trained with explanation-based regularization.

## Method

The study systematically evaluates 16 input-based explanation methods (gradient-based, propagation-based, perturbation-based, attention-based) across encoder-only (BERT, RoBERTa) and decoder-only (Llama3.2-3B, Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B) models under three research questions. For bias detection (RQ1), it computes the Pearson correlation between sensitive token reliance scores and individual unfairness, finding that Occlusion- and L2-norm-based methods (Grad L2, IxG L2, DeepLift L2) are most effective. For model selection (RQ2), it measures Spearman rank correlation and MRR@1 between validation-set explanation metrics and test-set fairness, showing explanations are unreliable for this purpose. For bias mitigation (RQ3), it adds a debiasing regularization term L_debias that penalizes the L1 or L2 norm of sensitive token attributions during training, with the strength hyperparameter α tuned via a fairness-balanced harmonic mean metric rather than task accuracy alone, yielding consistent reductions in false negative rate disparity and individual unfairness while preserving task performance.

## Results

No single debiasing method consistently reduces bias across all fairness metrics and bias types on Civil Comments. CDA achieves the largest reduction in individual unfairness (Avg_iu drops from 3.17→0.50 for race and 0.66→0.50 for gender in BERT), but simultaneously worsens Disp_fnr (10.04→18.45 for race). For Qwen3-4B, fairness instruction prompting reduces Avg_iu from 2.55→2.02 (race) and 3.32→1.71 (religion) while improving accuracy from 69.55→70.40. The paper's explanation-based debiasing (RQ3) consistently and substantially reduces Disp_fnr across all bias types while maintaining or improving accuracy, outperforming all traditional debiasing baselines in the harmonic fairness-accuracy mean.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares seven traditional debiasing methods (group balance, group-class balance, CDA, dropout, attention entropy, causal debias for BERT; few-shot, fairness imagination, fairness instruction for Qwen3-4B) against explanation-based debiasing. No traditional method consistently reduces bias across all metrics: CDA achieves the best Avg_iu reduction (e.g., 3.17→0.50 for BERT race) but worsens Disp_fnr (10.04→18.45); causal debias eliminates Disp_acc for race (2.05→0.00) but increases Disp_fpr (0.50→3.90). For Qwen3-4B, fairness instruction prompting provides the best overall balance, reducing Avg_iu by 21–49% across bias types while improving accuracy by ~1 point. The paper's explanation-based debiasing (RQ3) outperforms all traditional methods in the harmonic fairness-accuracy mean, consistently reducing Disp_fnr across all bias types while maintaining or improving accuracy. In RQ2, all explanation methods fail to match the simple baseline of using validation-set individual unfairness for model selection, with the baseline achieving a perfect Spearman correlation of 1 for decoder-only models. In RQ1, Occlusion and L2-based methods (Grad L2, IxG L2, DeepLift L2) achieve the highest fairness correlations, outperforming mean-based and attention-based variants, and remain effective even in explanation-debiased models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Dimanov et al. (2020), who introduced the sensitive token reliance score and showed that models can be trained to conceal unfairness from explanation methods, and on Kennedy et al. (2020) and Rao et al. (2023), who demonstrated that explanation regularization can reduce spurious correlations during training. However, prior work on the explainability-fairness connection in NLP has been predominantly qualitative, restricted to a small number of explanation methods, or limited to single model architectures. This paper departs from that lineage by providing the first systematic quantitative comparison of 16 input-based explanation methods across both encoder-only (BERT, RoBERTa) and decoder-only (Llama3.2, Qwen3) models, organized around three distinct operational questions: bias detection at inference, fair model selection, and bias mitigation during training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides practitioners with clear, evidence-based guidance on when input-based explanations are useful for fairness (detecting biased predictions, providing training-time debiasing signals) and when they are not (automatic model selection), which is practically important given that explanation methods are increasingly mandated by regulation (e.g., EU AI Act). The finding that even large LLMs (GPT-OSS-120B) are substantially worse than simple gradient-based explanations for bias detection is a timely negative result that cautions against relying on LLM-as-a-judge for fairness auditing. Limitations include restriction to three bias types (race, gender, religion), two datasets, and specific model families; generalization to other tasks, more diverse demographic groups, and adversarial settings remains open. The RQ1/RQ2 asymmetry—explanations work within a model but not across models—suggests that debiasing methods alter attribution patterns in ways that break cross-model comparability, an open question for future work.
