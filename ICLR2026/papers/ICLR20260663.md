## Problem

Task vectors distill in-context learning demonstrations into a single reusable hidden-state vector, enabling efficient zero-shot inference, yet the principles governing how they emerge, what information they encode, and where they fail remain unexplained. This paper addresses this gap by proposing the 'Task Vectors as Representative Demonstrations' conjecture and providing both theoretical and empirical evidence for it.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260663/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes triplet-formatted ICL prompts of the form (x_i, →, y_i) followed by a query token, and extracts the task vector from the hidden state of the last arrow (→) token at a specified layer. The standard task vector is a single d-dimensional vector injected into a zero-shot prompt, while the proposed multi-vector variant (TaskV-M) extracts N+1 distinct task vectors from N+1 separate prompts and injects them into all arrow tokens of an N-shot prompt. The final output is the predicted response y_test, evaluated by next-token accuracy on 33 ICL tasks across knowledge, algorithmic, translation, linguistic, and bijection categories.

## Method

The paper analyzes the loss landscape of L-layer linear-attention transformers trained on random linear regression with triplet-formatted prompts, characterizing critical points that decompose into three operations: embedding concatenation (mixing x_i and y_i into single tokens), self-magnification of arrow tokens, and task vector formation via a weighted summation Λ₄⊗Λ₅ across all demonstrations. Proposition 3 shows the optimal weight matrix Λ₄ has orthonormal rows, meaning each arrow token produces a distinct linear combination of demonstrations. The key limitation follows from the rank-one structure: the injected task vector reconstructs a single-shot prompt, restricting the induced coefficient matrix to rank one, which provably cannot solve general bijection tasks (Proposition 4). To overcome this, the authors propose TaskV-M, which injects multiple task vectors into all arrow tokens of few-shot prompts, effectively increasing the rank of the induced mapping. Empirical validation on Llama-7B/13B, Pythia-12B, GPT-J, Qwen3-8B, and Llama3-8B confirms the rank-one failure on bijection tasks and shows consistent gains from multi-vector injection.

## Results

On Llama-13B (n=10), the proposed multi-vector method (TaskV-M) achieves an average accuracy of 85.64% at 4-shot, outperforming the standard task vector method (TaskV, 84.66%) and few-shot ICL baseline (81.52%). The most significant gains appear on bijection tasks, where TaskV-M reaches 72.53% at 4-shot versus 70.44% for TaskV and 67.16% for the baseline. At 0-shot, TaskV already dramatically outperforms the baseline (72.26% vs. 10.28% average), confirming that a single injected task vector captures substantial task information. The improvement of TaskV-M over TaskV is consistent across all shot settings and task categories, with the largest relative gains on the hardest (bijection) tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three methods: standard few-shot ICL (Baseline), the original task vector method from Hendel et al. (2023) (TaskV), and the proposed multi-vector injection (TaskV-M). TaskV-M consistently outperforms TaskV across all shot settings and task categories, with the largest absolute gains on bijection tasks (e.g., +2.09 points at 4-shot: 72.53% vs. 70.44%). Both task vector methods dramatically outperform the 0-shot baseline (72.26% vs. 10.28% average), validating that a single distilled vector captures most of the task information from 10 demonstrations. However, on bijection tasks specifically, TaskV still performs near chance level (50.67% at 0-shot), confirming the theoretical rank-one limitation predicted by the paper's analysis. The few-shot ICL baseline actually outperforms TaskV on bijection tasks at higher shot counts (e.g., 67.16% vs. 70.44% at 4-shot), further illustrating that multi-demonstration context is necessary for tasks requiring higher-rank mappings. Results are consistent on Pythia-12B (Table 6), where TaskV-M also shows steady improvements over TaskV, particularly on bijection tasks (60.31% vs. 59.38% at 4-shot).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the theoretical ICL literature (Von Oswald et al., 2023a; Ahn et al., 2023) from single-token to triplet-formatted prompts, providing the first critical-point characterization of how task vectors emerge in linear-attention transformers. It departs from the empirical framing of Hendel et al. (2023) and concurrent works (Todd et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024) by proposing that task vectors encode a single representative demonstration rather than complete task knowledge, and by identifying a fundamental rank-one expressiveness limitation absent from prior analyses. The concurrent work of Bu et al. (2025) offers a word2vec-like explanation but is restricted to additive tasks, single-token prompts, and one-layer models, whereas this paper handles pairwise/triplet prompts and multi-layer attention.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The rank-one limitation provides a principled explanation for why task vectors fail on bijection and other high-rank tasks, a failure mode previously unexplained despite extensive empirical evaluation. The multi-vector injection strategy offers a practical, low-cost enhancement that consistently improves performance, particularly on the hardest task category. However, the theoretical framework is confined to linear-attention models with bi-directional attention and synthetic linear regression, leaving open whether the same mechanistic picture holds under causal attention, non-linear kernels, or autoregressive training objectives. Convergence guarantees and sample complexity analyses remain absent, and the generalization from linear to practical LLMs rests on saliency-map consistency rather than formal proof.
