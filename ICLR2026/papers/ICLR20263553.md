## Problem

Data-driven wind prediction models trained with standard MSE systematically underestimate the amplitudes and short-term variability of extreme winds, a bias that persists even when overall (non-extreme) skill is strong. The paper identifies the root cause as frequency-dependent amplitude shrinkage induced by small spatial pattern deviations under MSE training, and provides both a theoretical explanation and a practical remedy.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263553/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes 23 consecutive hourly frames of 10-meter wind velocity (eastward u and northward v components) and surface pressure on a 0.25° latitude-longitude grid over a rectangular region, and produces the wind velocity field at the 24th hour. The frequency separation module splits the input into high- and low-frequency components via Fourier masking, which are then processed at different temporal resolutions before being recombined into a single predicted velocity field.

## Method

The core contribution is a gradient-penalized loss that augments MSE with a term matching the L2 norm of spatial gradients between prediction and ground truth, effectively upweighting high-frequency errors to counteract the amplitude shrinkage that MSE induces under pattern translation. The architecture embeds the 2D incompressible Navier-Stokes equations as a physics backbone (advective, viscous, and pressure operators) with a learnable body-force neural network capturing residual dynamics, reducing translation error and parameter cost. A Fourier filtering stage decomposes the field into high- and low-frequency components, each processed by a temporal attention module at a resolution matched to its timescale (finer for high-frequency, coarser for low-frequency). The gradient penalty is theoretically justified via a PDE-based energy-enstrophy analysis showing it enforces enstrophy matching, thereby constraining small-scale vorticity and preventing the model from reducing the objective by globally smoothing sharp structures.

## Results

On the ERA5 regional wind velocity prediction task (0.25° spatial, 1-hour temporal resolution), the proposed model achieves the lowest RMSE and Ex-RMSE across all lead times (1h, 3h, 5h). At the 1-hour lead time, the method attains RMSE 0.3287 and Ex-RMSE 0.1868, outperforming CNN (0.4639/0.3183), ConvLSTM (0.3471/0.2294), and PINN (0.3946/0.2541). The extreme-wind improvement is particularly pronounced: Ex-RMSE reductions of 41.3%, 18.6%, and 26.5% over CNN, ConvLSTM, and PINN respectively at 1h, with consistent gains extending to 5h lead time (Ex-RMSE 0.6158 vs. 1.0693, 0.8097, 0.7347).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against three baselines: CNN, ConvLSTM (Tan et al., NeurIPS 2023), and PINN (Eivazi et al., Physics of Fluids 2022, using RANS equations). The proposed method wins on all metrics across all lead times. Against ConvLSTM, the closest overall competitor, the improvement is modest in RMSE (5.3% at 1h) but substantial in Ex-RMSE (18.6% at 1h, 19.4% at 3h, 24.1% at 5h), confirming the gradient-penalized loss specifically targets extreme-wind amplitude shrinkage. Against PINN, which already incorporates Navier-Stokes physics, the method still achieves 16.7% RMSE and 26.5% Ex-RMSE reduction at 1h, suggesting that the frequency separation and reweighting mechanism provides additional benefit beyond physics-informed constraints alone. Against CNN, the gains are largest (29.1% RMSE, 41.3% Ex-RMSE at 1h), as CNN lacks both physical structure and frequency-aware weighting. The consistent multi-horizon advantage (3h and 5h) indicates the method's improvements are not merely a short-term fitting artifact.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of fluid-dynamics theory and data-driven weather prediction. While prior work on extreme weather forecasting has focused on data augmentation (diffusion models, VAEs) or generic deep architectures (ConvLSTM, CNN, PINN), this paper instead provides a first-principles frequency-domain decomposition of MSE that explains *why* standard training induces amplitude shrinkage for high-frequency (extreme) components. The gradient-penalized loss is then justified through a PDE-based energy-enstrophy analysis drawn from incompressible Navier-Stokes theory, and the NS equations are embedded structurally as a network backbone (distinct from PINN-style loss constraints), with a Fourier-based frequency separation module added on top.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The frequency-domain error decomposition (scaling + translation + noise) offers a clean, actionable explanation for a long-observed but poorly understood bias in weather ML models, and the resulting gradient-penalized loss is simple enough to drop into existing pipelines. The energy-enstrophy interpretation connects the loss to physically meaningful quantities (vorticity, shear), giving practitioners a principled way to tune λ. Key limitations include the simplified three-factor error model, restriction to 2D regional prediction on a single dataset (ERA5), and the fact that the convergence analysis applies to a linearized surrogate rather than the full nonlinear network. Open questions include extension to 3D, longer lead times, and whether the frequency-reweighting strategy generalizes to other geophysical fields.
