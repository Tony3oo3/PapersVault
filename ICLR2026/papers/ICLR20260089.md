## Problem

Most LM evaluations target problems with well-defined answers and complete information, yet real-world deployments in healthcare, finance, and policy require models to reason under uncertainty and express beliefs as probability distributions. No existing benchmark systematically tests whether LLMs can produce accurate, well-calibrated Bayesian priors for real-world quantities that are grounded in observational data yet unlikely to appear verbatim in pretraining corpora.

## Input / Output

The model receives a natural language description of a derived conditional statistic (e.g., 'the mean weight of US adults with diabetes who take medication for depression and have cholesterol above a threshold') along with unit specifications, and must output a parameterized Bayesian prior by selecting one of three distribution families (Gaussian, Beta, or Log-normal) and specifying its parameters. The elicitation protocol requires the model to first provide reasoning about the parameter values before outputting structured XML tags with the distribution type and parameters, and the prompt explicitly distinguishes population-level parameter uncertainty from individual observation variability.

## Method

The benchmark constructs estimation targets by randomly sampling up to three filtering conditions from large public observational datasets (Glassdoor, PitchBook, NHANES) and computing summary statistics on the resulting subsets, with a constraint that each condition must shift the marginal by at least 5% to ensure meaningful variation. This yields 178 derived conditional statistics across three domains that are empirically verifiable yet unlikely to be memorized from pretraining. Models are evaluated on two axes: accuracy (MAE error ratio and win rate against N-sample statistical baselines with uninformative priors, plus posterior win rates when combining the LM prior with data) and calibration (CRPS ratio, quartile coverage analysis, and CDF of ground-truth values relative to predicted standard deviations). Ablations examine the effect of temperature/reasoning effort, system prompt, and three elicitation protocols (direct parameterization, quantile-based, mean-variance) on prediction quality, finding none has a consequential impact.

## Results

Across six frontier LMs evaluated on 178 derived conditional statistics, LM-elicited Bayesian priors are worth roughly the equivalent of five samples from the underlying data distribution (error ratio ≈ 1). Win rates of LLM priors over the 5-sample statistical baseline range from 37% (Glassdoor) to 74% (NHANES), dropping sharply as baseline sample size increases. LLM posteriors (prior + N samples) consistently outperform or match posteriors from uninformative priors, achieving 65–81% win rates across domains. On calibration (CRPS ratio vs. 5-sample baseline), reasoning models o4-mini (1.01–2.42) and o3-mini (0.99–3.17) perform best, while Llama-3-8B degrades to 19.17× worse than baseline on NHANES.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares LLM-elicited priors against statistical baselines computed from N ∈ {5, 10, 20, 30} samples drawn from the true sub-population with uninformative priors. LLM priors beat the 5-sample baseline in 37–74% of cases depending on domain (NHANES 74.3%, Pitchbook 50.8%, Glassdoor 37.0%), but this advantage erodes rapidly: by 30 samples, win rates drop to 8.7–50.8%. However, when combined with data, LLM posteriors consistently outperform naive-prior posteriors, winning 65–81% of the time across all sample sizes and domains. On calibration, reasoning models (o3-mini, o4-mini) achieve CRPS ratios closest to 1.0 (best: 0.99 on Pitchbook), while non-reasoning models lag, especially on NHANES where Llama-3-8B is 19× worse than the 5-sample baseline. No single model family dominates across all three domains; rankings are domain-dependent, with Pitchbook being the easiest (all models near baseline) and NHANES the most discriminating.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a distinct niche among three existing lines of work on LM probabilistic reasoning: (1) idealized mathematical exercises with fully specified inputs (Paruchuri et al., Nafar et al., Jin et al.), (2) structured guesstimation or Bayesian-network-based approaches targeting discrete multiple-choice questions (Xia et al., Feng et al.), and (3) forecasting benchmarks whose outcomes inevitably leak into training data (Karger et al., Halawi et al.). The closest prior work, Selby et al. (2025), also elicits parametric Bayesian priors from LLMs but evaluates them against human expert elicitation or historical observational data. OPENESTIMATE departs from all of these by constructing derived conditional statistics through random filtering of large public datasets, yielding estimation targets that are empirically verifiable yet unlikely to appear verbatim in pretraining corpora—a construction strategy that sidesteps both the artificiality of idealized problems and the staleness problem of forecasting benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that frontier LMs, despite access to vast pretraining knowledge, produce priors worth only about five samples from the underlying distribution and exhibit weak accuracy-confidence coupling, underscoring that current models are far from reliable probabilistic reasoners for high-stakes deployment. The finding that LM posteriors (prior + data) still outperform naive-prior posteriors suggests a practical path forward: even imperfect LM priors add value when combined with limited data. Key limitations include the small scale (178 statistics, 3 domains), zero-shot-only evaluation without retrieval or fine-tuning, and finite-sample ground-truth estimation error. Open questions include whether training-time interventions, retrieval augmentation, or domain adaptation can close the calibration gap, and whether the benchmark's findings generalize beyond the three tested domains.
