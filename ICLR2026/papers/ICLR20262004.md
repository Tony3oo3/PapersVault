## Problem

Transformers trained for next-token prediction (NTP) routinely learn abstract features—world models, syntactic structures, multi-token-ahead predictions—that are not needed to predict the immediate next token. Prior interpretability work explains what these features do in the final model (teleological view) but leaves unexplained which components of the NTP gradient signal actually drive their emergence during training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262004/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

For the attribution method on small models, the pipeline requires two full training runs: the first produces the final checkpoint and linear probes (defining feature directions w_i^k), and the second replays the identical training trajectory to compute per-batch influence values for each gradient component. For the LLM-scale method, only a static checkpoint is needed: SAE feature directions are ablated via intervention, and the resulting KL-divergence changes at future versus immediate positions yield the pre-caching degree score Q(w). Outputs are integrated influence values (direct, pre-cached, shared) per feature, or Q(w) scores classifying features as predominantly direct or pre-cached.

## Method

The paper decomposes the NTP loss gradient at each position i and layer k into three exhaustive components based on information-flow paths: direct (gradients from predicting x_{i+1} through r_i^k), pre-cached (gradients from predicting x_j for j>i+1 that pass through r_i^k via attention), and shared (gradients that do not pass through r_i^k at all, arising from parameter sharing across positions). Influence of each component on a feature is defined as the inner product between the component's gradient and the gradient of a feature-mismatch loss measuring distance from the final model's representation. For small models, this is computed exactly by retraining and tracking per-step contributions; for LLMs, Proposition 5.1 shows the ratio of pre-cached to direct influence can be approximated by Q(w), the ratio of summed KL-divergence at future positions to KL-divergence at the immediate next position after ablating the feature. Ablation experiments (myopic training to block pre-caching, m-untied training to block circuit sharing) confirm that NTP-useless features fail to emerge without these non-direct gradient pathways, and that pre-caching is necessary for coherent text generation but not for simple syntax.

## Results

On TinyStories, myopic (pre-caching-ablated) models achieve a consistently higher loss of 3.29 ± 0.02 versus 2.53 ± 0.10 for non-myopic models, confirming pre-caching is necessary for coherent text generation. In OthelloGPT, the probing gap between NTP-useful and NTP-useless board squares is always positive and grows with layer depth; direct influence is positive for NTP-useful features but indistinguishable from zero for NTP-useless ones, while pre-cached and shared influence remain positive for both. In Gemma 2 2B, SAE features related to formal reasoning (code, math) exhibit heavier-tailed pre-caching degree distributions (log-normal σ = 1.63 ± 0.03 vs. 1.23 ± 0.02 for non-formal features), and look-ahead utility shows a negative Spearman correlation with Q(w), supporting the breadcrumbs hypothesis over the pre-caching explanation for look-ahead.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external methods but instead uses ablation-based baselines: myopic training (blocks pre-caching) and m-untied training (blocks circuit sharing), each compared against the standard non-myopic, tied baseline. On the Majority and Conditioned Majority toy tasks, myopic untied models show the most fragile representation of NTP-useless features during the input phase, while lifting either ablation improves probe performance; notably, both tied and untied myopic models fail entirely on Conditioned Majority (76% vs. 99–100% accuracy), demonstrating that multi-layer attention circuits cannot develop without pre-caching. On OthelloGPT, the non-myopic model represents NTP-useless cells better than chance but significantly worse than NTP-useful cells, with the disparity attributable to the absence of direct gradient signal for useless features. On TinyStories, myopic models lose 0.76 nats of perplexity relative to non-myopic models, yet POS and dependency tag probes perform comparably across both settings, indicating simple syntax is learned via direct gradients alone while the positional feature (a high-level property) requires pre-caching.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Wu et al. (2024), who introduced the term 'pre-caching' and proposed myopic training as a way to block it, but extends the analysis from a global question (do LLMs plan ahead?) to a feature-level, gradient-level account of how specific representations develop. It also connects to the NTP-critique literature (Bachmann & Nagarajan 2024; Thankaraj et al. 2025) and the world-model line (Li et al. 2023; Vafa et al. 2025), reframing their findings through a developmental lens: rather than asking what role a feature plays in the final algorithm (teleological), the paper asks which gradient component drove its emergence. The introduction of 'circuit sharing' as a third gradient pathway, alongside direct learning and pre-caching, is a genuinely new decomposition not present in prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The three-way gradient decomposition provides a principled vocabulary for diagnosing why a given feature exists in a trained model, and the attribution method (retraining with per-component influence tracking) is a concrete tool for developmental interpretability. The finding that pre-cached features are *less* useful for look-ahead than direct features (negative Spearman correlation) is a notable conceptual result that supports the 'breadcrumbs' over 'planning' hypothesis, though it is demonstrated on a single model and SAE layer. Key limitations include the requirement to retrain from scratch for attribution (prohibitive at LLM scale), the restriction to linearly represented features, and the fact that Q(w) only characterizes the neighborhood of the final checkpoint rather than the full training trajectory. Open questions include whether the gradient decomposition generalizes to nonlinear features, whether circuit sharing can be harnessed deliberately for cross-task transfer, and whether the formal-reasoning/pre-caching link holds across architectures and training regimes.
