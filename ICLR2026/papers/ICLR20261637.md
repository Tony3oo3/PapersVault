## Problem

Previous Constitutional Classifiers used separate input and output-only classifiers, leaving them vulnerable to reconstruction attacks (fragmenting harmful content across benign segments) and output obfuscation attacks (encoding harmful content in ways uninterpretable without input context), while their 23% computational overhead and 0.38% refusal rate made production deployment impractical. This paper addresses both gaps simultaneously, seeking defenses that are more robust against universal jailbreaks while dramatically reducing computational costs and false positive rates.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261637/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system operates as a streaming classifier during model generation: it takes the user prompt and monitors the protected model's response tokens in real-time, using intermediate activations from all layers of the protected model (Claude Sonnet 4.5) as features for the linear probe. The probe produces a continuous harmfulness score via an exponential moving average of per-token logits; when this score exceeds a threshold, the exchange is escalated to a second-stage external classifier (fine-tuned Claude Haiku 4.5) whose logit is combined with the probe's to produce a final flag-or-refuse decision. The system achieves a 0.05% flag rate on production traffic, with the first-stage probe escalating approximately 5.5% of traffic to the second stage.

## Method

The core architecture is a weighted two-stage classifier cascade. The first stage is a linear activation probe over concatenated activations from all layers of the protected model, trained with two key modifications: Sliding Window Mean (SWiM) logit smoothing that averages logits over M=16 tokens to prevent single-token spikes from triggering false positives, and softmax logit pooling that pools smoothed logits into a sequence-level prediction with asymmetric weighting—negligible weight on tokens with negative logits (allowing confident harmless predictions on shared prefixes) and heavy penalty on confident harmful predictions in harmless sequences. When the probe score exceeds a threshold, the exchange is escalated to a second-stage external classifier fine-tuned from Claude Haiku 4.5, and the final score is a weighted combination (0.55·probe + 0.45·classifier). The probe and external classifier capture complementary signals (lower Spearman rank correlation than classifier-classifier pairs), so their ensemble outperforms either alone. This system achieves a 40× computational cost reduction over a single exchange classifier while maintaining the lowest vulnerability discovery rate (0.005 per thousand queries) across over 1,700 hours of human red-teaming, with no universal jailbreak discovered capable of answering all eight target CBRN queries.

## Results

The production-grade Constitutional Classifier system achieves a high-risk vulnerability discovery rate of 0.005 per thousand red-teaming queries (the lowest of all systems evaluated), a 0.05% refusal rate on production traffic, and only 3.5% relative compute overhead compared to the last-generation system. Across 1,736 cumulative hours of human red-teaming (~198K attempts), only one high-risk vulnerability was found, requiring 30 hours to discover, and no universal jailbreak successfully answered all eight target CBRN queries at half the detail of an undefended model. The system delivers a 40× computational cost reduction relative to the single exchange classifier baseline and an 8× reduction relative to the two-stage cascade without probes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the last-generation Constitutional Classifier system from Sharma et al. (2025), which uses separate input and output-only classifiers. The production-grade system outperforms it on all three metrics: vulnerability discovery rate drops from 0.01871 to 0.00505 per thousand queries (a 3.7× improvement), compute overhead falls from 100% to 3.5% (a 28.6× reduction), and the refusal rate decreases from 0.073% to 0.050%. The intermediate exchange classifier (which evaluates outputs in full conversational context) achieves a 2.1× reduction in vulnerability rate over the last-generation system but at 150% compute overhead, motivating the cascade and probe designs. The two-stage cascade without probes matches the exchange classifier's robustness (0.00878 vs. 0.00885) while cutting compute to 27.8%. Finally, the linear probe ensembled with a small external classifier (Claude Haiku 4.5) outperforms either component alone on attack success rate, and the probe-classifier cascade achieves a 100× compute reduction relative to running the small classifier on all traffic while exceeding its standalone robustness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Sharma et al. (2025), replacing their dual input/output classifier architecture with a single exchange classifier that evaluates model outputs within their full conversational context, thereby closing reconstruction and obfuscation vulnerabilities. It also builds on McKenzie et al. (2025)'s softmax-pooled activation probes, but adapts them for streaming classification during generation and adds a logit-smoothing component that the prior work omits. The two-stage cascade and probe-external ensemble design draw on broader adaptive-computation and ensemble literature, but the specific combination—linear probe as first stage, probe-classifier ensemble as second stage—is novel within the Constitutional Classifiers lineage.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By reducing computational overhead by 40x and refusal rates from 0.38% to 0.05% while maintaining or improving robustness, this work makes Constitutional Classifiers viable for production deployment at scale, addressing the primary barrier to adoption identified in the prior generation. The finding that a single linear probe (O(1/d_MLP) overhead) captures signals complementary to fine-tuned external classifiers suggests a general principle for cheap, effective safety monitoring. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond CBRN target queries, how robustness holds as underlying models evolve, and whether the red-teaming protocol captures the full adversarial landscape—particularly given the authors' own observation that current attacks degrade model capabilities and adversaries may find less degrading variants.
