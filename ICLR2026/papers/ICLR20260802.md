## Problem

Strong convexity of sets is well-understood in Hilbert spaces and underpins faster convergence for algorithms like Frank-Wolfe, but no accepted definition exists for strongly convex sets in Riemannian manifolds. Existing notions in CAT(0) spaces characterize ambient curvature rather than set-level geometry, and do not provide the structural conditions (e.g., scaling inequalities) needed to derive algorithmic convergence rates on curved spaces.

## Input / Output

The framework operates on a compact, uniquely geodesically convex set C within a Riemannian manifold M, equipped with a distance function d satisfying a comparability condition with the logarithmic map norm. The RFW algorithm consumes a smooth convex objective f and an initial point x₀ ∈ C, producing a sequence of iterates via geodesic interpolation between x_t and a linear minimization oracle output v_t. Beyond the iterates, the theory outputs geometric certificates: a strong convexity parameter α for the set and a linear convergence rate max{1/2, 1 − αc/(2L)} that activates once the iterates enter a local regime where d(x_t, v_t)² is sufficiently small.

## Method

The paper introduces three nested definitions of set strong convexity on manifolds—geodesic (ball containment along geodesics), Riemannian (the log-image Exp_x⁻¹(C) is Euclidean strongly convex for every x), and double geodesic (the exponential map at γ(t) of a tangent ball of radius αt(1−t)d²(x,y) stays in C)—and establishes their implications. A key technical tool is the Riemannian Scaling Inequality (RSI), a one-sided lower bound on ⟨w; Exp_x⁻¹(v)⟩_x at the LMO maximizer v, which generalizes the Euclidean scaling inequality and is shown to follow from Riemannian strong convexity. For the weaker double geodesic notion, the paper links it to an approximate RSI through the double exponential map operator h_x(u,v) = u + v + R_x(u,v), where the residual R_x captures curvature-induced distortion. The main algorithmic result proves that RFW converges linearly whenever the constraint set satisfies (approximate) RSI and the unconstrained optimum lies outside C, with the rate depending on the strong convexity parameter α, the gradient lower bound c, and the smoothness constant L. Constructive identification of such sets is provided via two routes: Riemannian balls of restricted radius on bounded-curvature manifolds, and sublevel sets of geodesically L-smooth, μ-strongly convex functions.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 5.1) establishes linear convergence of the Riemannian Frank-Wolfe algorithm when the constraint set satisfies the Riemannian Scaling Inequality (RSI): f(x_{t+1}) − f(x*) ≤ (f(x_t) − f(x*)) · max{1/2, 1 − αc/(2L)}, where α is the RSI constant and c bounds the gradient norm away from zero. Theorem 5.2 extends this to double geodesically strongly convex sets via an approximate RSI, requiring only a burn-in phase with d(x_t, v_t)² ≤ αc/(2δL C̃). The numerical experiment on S^{499} (n = 500) with a random quadratic objective confirms the predicted transition from O(1/T) global convergence to locally linear convergence (Figure 2). Additionally, Theorem 4.2 shows sublevel sets of L-smooth, μ-strongly convex functions are geodesically strongly convex with parameter α = μ/(2√(2sL·max{ℓ_M^{-2}, 1})).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without direct method-vs-method benchmark comparisons. The key comparison is against prior Riemannian optimization results: Weber and Sra (2023) established O(1/T) convergence for RFW and linear convergence under strong convexity of the *function* with the optimum in the *interior* of the set. This paper complements that by proving linear convergence when the *set* is strongly convex (via RSI or double geodesic strong convexity) and the unconstrained optimum lies *outside* the set — a regime previously only available in the Euclidean setting (Demyanov & Rubinov, 1970; Garber & Hazan, 2015). The three proposed definitions of set strong convexity (geodesic, double geodesic, Riemannian) are shown to form a strict hierarchy (Eq. 7), with Riemannian strong convexity being the most restrictive but implying the exact RSI, while double geodesic strong convexity implies only an approximate RSI with a curvature-dependent residual. The numerical experiment on the sphere manifold (n = 500, random 250×500 matrix A) validates the locally linear rate predicted by Theorem 5.2, with the two convergence regimes clearly visible in the log-scale plot.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Euclidean theory of strongly convex sets (Goncharov and Ivanov, 2017) and the scaling-inequality-based convergence analysis of Frank-Wolfe (Kerdreux et al., 2021b; Garber and Hazan, 2015) to Riemannian manifolds, where no accepted definition of set strong convexity previously existed. It builds directly on the Riemannian Frank-Wolfe algorithm (Weber and Sra, 2023) and the double exponential map machinery (Dzhepko and Nikonorov, 2008; Gavrilov, 2007) to bridge geometric set properties with algorithmic convergence guarantees. The paper also draws on Fenchel duality on manifolds (Bergmann et al., 2021) for smoothness lemmas and on the Riemannian cosine law (Martínez-Rubio and Pokutta, 2022) for comparison-theorem arguments, while explicitly distinguishing its RSI from the analytically opposite proximal smoothness condition (Davis et al., 2025).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By providing the first systematic definitions of set strong convexity on manifolds and proving that RFW achieves linear convergence under the RSI, this work gives the Riemannian optimization community a concrete geometric certificate to exploit in algorithm design, analogous to how Euclidean strong convexity accelerates FW. Practical applications include trust-region subproblems on SPD manifolds, hierarchical neural network training with spherical constraints, and global Riemannian optimization via local ball subproblems. Key limitations include the restrictiveness of the exact RSI (requiring the unconstrained optimum to lie outside the set), the need for a burn-in phase before linear convergence kicks in under the approximate RSI, and the difficulty of verifying the definitions in practice beyond small balls or sublevel sets of strongly convex functions. Open questions include extending the framework to non-geodesic or non-convex settings and developing more practical certification tools.
