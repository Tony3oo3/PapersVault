## Problem

Existing jailbreak detection methods for Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs) are either restricted to specific attack types or incur prohibitive inference latency, making them impractical for real-world deployment. While prior work identified 'SafeNeurons' that explain why aligned models refuse standard harmful queries, no one had localized the distinct set of neurons that capture how jailbreak prompts actively circumvent safety mechanisms. This paper addresses both the existence of such 'JailNeurons' and whether they can be exploited for a generalizable, efficient detector that transfers to unseen attacks and out-of-distribution benign data.

## Input / Output

The detector consumes an image and text prompt fed through the target LVLM, but rather than waiting for a full token-by-token response, it intercepts intermediate hidden-state activations from a small set of pre-selected layers after a single forward pass. From each selected layer, only the activations of identified JailNeurons (those whose learned mask exceeds a threshold τ) are extracted, concatenated across layers, and fed into a lightweight linear SVM that outputs a binary jailbreak/benign label. This design means the system can reject a harmful prompt before any response tokens are generated, yielding inference times (0.26–1.02 s) faster than the unguarded model itself.

## Method

JDJN operates in two stages. First, a 'sure-to-sorry' localization procedure trains a per-layer sparse mask m ∈ [0,1]^d via gradient-based optimization: the mask is applied element-wise to each layer's neuron activations, and the objective minimizes L1-norm sparsity plus cross-entropy loss steering the model's output toward a refusal embedding (e.g., 'Sorry'), effectively identifying the minimal set of neurons whose deactivation flips a harmful response into a refusal. Second, a 'top-to-bottom' strategy selects layers at uniform intervals (every k layers) to capture jailbreak signals across different abstraction levels; the JailNeuron activations from these layers are concatenated and used to train a linear SVM binary classifier. The resulting JailNeurons constitute less than 2% of all neurons per layer, and the approach is non-intrusive (no model modification), data-efficient (a few hundred training samples), and robust to adaptive attacks that simultaneously optimize for harmful output and detection evasion.

## Results

On LLaVA, JDJN₁ (trained on JailBreakV + MM-Vet) achieves TPR@FPR≤0.05 of 0.997 on JailBreakV, 1.0 on FigStep, and 0.942 on the unseen JAMLLM attack, while maintaining FPR of 0.0 on MM-Vet and MM-Bench and only 0.019 on Normal prompts. On Janus-pro, the same configuration reaches 0.996/1.0/0.853 TPR across the three attacks. The method requires only a single forward pass (1.02 s on LLaVA, 0.26 s on Janus-pro), making it faster than even the unguarded model's full response generation (12.08 s / 4.29 s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

JDJN₁ outperforms all seven main baselines (JailGuard, ECSO, CIDER, HiddenDetect, GradSafe, JailDAM, AdaShield) on every attack–model combination in Table 1. The margin is most dramatic on the OOD JAMLLM attack: JDJN₁ reaches 0.942 TPR on LLaVA versus 0.342 for JailDAM, 0.534 for GradSafe, and 0.213 for AdaShield. Among the neuron-digging baselines in Appendix A.12, JDJN₁ also leads on JAMLLM (0.942 vs. 0.433 for First-to-Know and 0.896 for SNIP on LLaVA), confirming that the 'sure-to-sorry' mask optimization captures jailbreak-specific directions that single-step gradient heuristics miss. On in-distribution data (JailBreakV, FigStep), JDJN₁ and the strongest baselines (JailDAM, GradSafe) are closer (e.g., 0.997 vs. 0.913 on LLaVA/JailBreakV), but JDJN₁ still leads by 5–8 points. The only setting where a baseline approaches JDJN is ECSO on Janus-pro/JAMLLM (0.763 vs. 0.853), yet JDJN₁ still wins by ~9 points. Overall, JDJN₁'s advantage grows with distributional shift, validating its generalization claim.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing JailNeurons as a complement to the previously studied SafeNeurons (Wei et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2024b), which explain why aligned models refuse standard harmful queries. While prior work (Zhou et al. 2024a) established linear separability of jailbreak vs. benign activations in LLMs, and HiddenDetect (Jiang et al. 2025) used logit-lens decoding from intermediate layers, this paper is the first to localize jailbreak-specific signals to a small set of MLP neurons in LVLMs via a causal 'sure-to-sorry' ablation procedure. Methodologically, it departs from single-step gradient scoring (SNIP) by using iterative multi-step mask optimization, and from single-layer detection by aggregating across top-down sampled layers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a single forward pass with a lightweight SVM achieves >99% TPR at <1% FPR on seen attacks and >94% TPR on unseen attacks, with inference faster than the unprotected model itself—making real-time deployment feasible. The finding that safety-aligned models retain roughly half the JailNeurons of their base counterparts, and that these neurons are largely a subset of the original set, suggests a stable 'jailbreak circuit' that persists across fine-tuning. Open questions include whether the approach extends to multi-turn or iterative jailbreaks, how JailNeurons interact with attention-head-level safety mechanisms (SHiPs), and whether the elevated FPR on over-safety stress tests (OR-Bench, XSTest) becomes problematic as benign query distributions shift in production.
