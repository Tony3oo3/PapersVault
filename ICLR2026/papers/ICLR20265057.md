## Problem

While verifier-based test-time scaling has shown strong empirical gains for LLMs, the interplay between the generator's coverage, the verifier's region of convergence (ROC), and the sampling algorithm's sub-optimality has lacked a unified theoretical treatment. Prior analyses either assume a perfect verifier or capture only subsets of these interacting factors, leaving the exact geometry of how verification imperfections shape the sub-optimality–coverage tradeoff uncharacterized.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20265057/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a text prompt, a reference LLM (generator) with conditional kernel π_ref, a binary set-membership verifier (approximately correct, characterized by TPR/FPR/Youden's index), and a coverage budget β that bounds the χ²-divergence between the induced policy and the reference. It produces a sampled response along with closed-form expressions for sub-optimality (the reward gap relative to the coverage-constrained optimal policy) and expected number of proposals. Notably, the verifier is consumed only as a membership oracle Ŝ, and the coverage constraint is an ℓ₁-type (χ²-ball) restriction rather than a KL or ratio-based bound, which is what enables the three-regime decomposition.

## Method

The paper recasts test-time verification as an optimal transport problem: given generative access to a proposal distribution μ, the goal is to transport mass to a target ν⋆ defined by the ground-truth verifier, using only an imperfect verifier r̂ as a discriminative oracle. Sub-optimality is decomposed into an optimal transport cost (OTC, the minimum Hamming rejection probability) and a policy improvement term, yielding a piecewise curve with three regimes—transport, policy improvement, and saturation—whose boundaries are determined by the interplay of s_{r⋆}, s_ver, and β. For the sequential protocol, the authors propose Sequential Rejection Sampling (SRS) and Sequential Maximal Coupling (SMC), both of which provably satisfy the coverage constraint and achieve identical expected proposal counts and sub-optimality; for the batched protocol, they introduce Batched Rejection Sampling (BRS) and show it satisfies coverage for all batch sizes while BoN violates it beyond a maximal N. The key analytical result is that the sub-optimality–coverage curve's shape is mediated by the verifier's Youden's index J, refining prior square-root scaling observations into a regime-dependent picture.

## Results

The paper establishes a three-regime structure for the sub-optimality–coverage curve: in the transport regime (β ≤ min(1/s_{r*}, 1/s_ver)), sub-optimality grows as O(√β) and is dominated by the optimal transport cost; in the policy-improvement regime, a sufficiently accurate verifier (positive Youden's index J) enables sub-optimality reduction; and in the saturation regime, sub-optimality plateaus at OTC(β)·(1 − (s_{r*}/s_ver)·J). Computationally, SRS and SMC require E[τ] = (1 ∧ m_β(s_ver))/s_ver proposals, improving over AiC's constant 1/s_ver by a factor of m_β(s_ver) under stringent coverage. Empirically, on Qwen3-1.7B, the approximate verifier yields ~8 average proposals versus ~6 for the ground-truth verifier, and all three sequential methods (SRS, SMC, AiC) converge in the saturation regime. For batched sampling, BRS sub-optimality decays exponentially as OTC(β)·(1−1/M)^N, provably outperforming BoN in low- and intermediate-coverage regimes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against AiC (Dorner et al., 2025), SRS and SMC fix the critical constraint violation: AiC's induced policy has χ²-divergence 1/s_ver − 1, violating the coverage constraint for all β < 1/s_ver (the entire transport regime), whereas SRS and SMC satisfy the constraint by construction. In the saturation regime, all three methods converge to the same sub-optimality, confirming AiC is only valid under liberal coverage. Computationally, SRS/SMC achieve a speedup over AiC by a factor of m_β(s_ver) when coverage is stringent (m_β < 1), while matching AiC's cost when m_β = 1. Against BoN (Brown et al., 2024), BRS dominates in low- and intermediate-coverage regimes: BoN admits a finite maximum batch size N_max (beyond which the χ²-constraint is violated), while BRS satisfies coverage for all N. BRS sub-optimality decays exponentially in N as OTC(β)·(1−1/M)^N, whereas BoN's sub-optimality (1−s_{r*})^{N+1} − (0∨1−m_β(s_{r*})) is bounded below by the constraint-violation threshold. Empirically on Qwen3-14B with imperfect verifiers, BRS consistently achieves lower sub-optimality than BoN at matched batch sizes, with the gap widening as β decreases into the transport regime.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three previously separate threads in test-time verification theory: the coverage-constrained optimal policy framework of Huang et al. (2025a), the verifier-imperfection analysis of Dorner et al. (2025), and the classical optimal transport / maximal coupling machinery of Den Hollander (2012) and rejection sampling of Forsythe (1972). Prior work either assumed a perfect verifier (Brown et al., Beirami et al.) or studied verifier inaccuracy in isolation without a joint geometric characterization. The key conceptual departure is casting the entire sampling problem as a transport plan between the reference distribution and the verifier-induced target, which yields a closed-form decomposition of sub-optimality into an optimal transport cost and a policy improvement term governed by Youden's index.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The three-regime structure (transport, policy improvement, saturation) gives practitioners a concrete decision rule: rejection-sampling-type algorithms (SRS, BRS) are preferable under tight coverage, while Best-of-N excels under liberal coverage — a distinction that prior scaling-law analyses did not make explicit. A significant limitation is that SRS and SMC require knowledge of s_ver (the reference policy's mass on the verifier's set), which is typically unavailable at test time; the ablation study shows performance degrades gracefully but non-trivially under misspecification. Open questions include extending from ratio-based to difference-based coverage constraints, generalizing beyond binary verifiable rewards to general reward models, and the fundamental problem of sampling when the target-to-proposal likelihood ratio must be estimated from finite samples.
