## Problem

Standard evaluation of fine-tuned LLM judges measures performance on a fixed dataset, ignoring how judges behave when the generator models producing the responses change over time. This paper formalizes and studies three practical deployment concerns that have been overlooked: future-proofing (can a judge trained on older, weaker model responses evaluate newer, stronger ones?), backward-compatibility (can a judge trained on newer responses still evaluate older ones?), and question generalization (can a judge handle questions it never saw during training?).
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262037/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The judge receives a question Q and two candidate responses R1, R2 drawn from the same generator model, where one response is verifiably correct and the other incorrect (based on ground-truth answers from DeepScaleR or MMLU-Pro). It outputs a pairwise verdict selecting the better response, optionally preceded by a chain-of-thought explanation. Evaluation uses consistent accuracy, requiring the judge to correctly identify the better response under both response orderings to account for positional bias.

## Method

The paper introduces a dual-distribution formulation that factorizes the judge's input into a question distribution Q and a response distribution R, enabling controlled study of distribution shifts along each axis independently. Generators are clustered into weak and strong groups based on pass@1 scores (with a clear performance gap separating the two), and judges are trained on either weak or strong response distributions using three recipes (SFT, DPO, SFT+DPO) across three backbone models (Llama-3.1-8B, Ministral-8B, Mistral-24B). The authors define targeted metrics—FutureProof, RefreshAdvantage, BackCompatibility, CompatibilityShift, and QuestionGen—to quantify performance under each distribution shift. They further study continual learning by incrementally fine-tuning a weak-response-trained judge on strong responses, testing whether this provides a more balanced adaptation than training from scratch on either distribution alone.

## Results

On DeepScaleR, all fine-tuned judges (SFT, DPO, SFT+DPO across Llama-3.1-8B, Ministral-8B, Mistral-24B) exhibit negative FutureProof values, confirming that judges trained on weak responses consistently degrade when evaluating strong responses. Re-training on strong responses (RefreshAdvantage) yields positive gains across all settings, with DPO providing the largest improvements: up to 7.6 absolute percentage points for Mistral-24B and 4.3 points for Mistral-8B. Backward-compatibility is relatively easy: strong-trained judges match or exceed weak-trained judges on weak responses (positive BackCompatibility), though they still suffer a smaller CompatibilityShift degradation. All judges show performance drops on unseen questions (negative QuestionGen), with SFT-trained judges exhibiting the smallest drops and Mistral-24B the largest.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This empirical study compares three training recipes (SFT, DPO, SFT+DPO) and three backbone models (Llama-3.1-8B, Ministral-8B, Mistral-24B) under weak-response, strong-response, and continual-training conditions. DPO consistently outperforms SFT on RefreshAdvantage (up to 7.6 pp vs. minimal SFT gains on DeepScaleR), and DPO-trained strong judges can even surpass weak-trained judges on weak responses (positive BackCompatibility), whereas SFT-trained judges show small drops. SFT+DPO provides marginal additional benefit over DPO alone for a couple of models. For continual learning (DPO only), the continually trained judge narrows the FutureProof gap toward zero and reduces RefreshAdvantage toward zero for Mistral-24B and Ministral-8B, indicating it adapts more balancedly than either pure weak or pure strong training. On question generalization, SFT-trained judges show the smallest performance drops on unseen questions, while Mistral-24B exhibits the largest drops within each recipe, suggesting larger models overfit more to training questions. On MMLU-Pro, the same qualitative trends hold but with smaller absolute magnitudes, indicating domain-dependent severity of distribution shift.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the established LLM-as-a-judge paradigm and the growing body of work on fine-tuned evaluators (SFT, DPO, SFT+DPO recipes), but deliberately shifts focus from training methodology to the behavior of trained judges under input distribution shifts. Its key conceptual contribution is a dual-distribution formulation that factorizes judge inputs into a question distribution and a response distribution, enabling systematic isolation of weak-to-strong, strong-to-weak, and seen-to-unseen question shifts. This connects to broader distribution-shift literature (WILDS, easy-to-hard generalization, weak-to-strong generalization) but instantiates it specifically for the evolving-generator setting of LLM evaluation, where prior work has largely treated judges as static evaluators on fixed datasets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The findings have direct practical implications for teams deploying fine-tuned judges in production: judges must be retrained on up-to-date responses to evaluate newer models, and the weak-to-strong shift is substantially harder than the reverse, creating an asymmetric maintenance burden. The demonstration that continual learning offers a more balanced adaptation than full retraining provides a concrete strategy for reducing retraining costs. Limitations include restriction to two verifiable datasets (math and MCQ), pairwise evaluation only, and a relatively small set of generator models; open questions include whether these findings hold for non-verifiable open-ended tasks, for pointwise evaluation, and whether the question-generalization gap can be closed with better training data or architectures.
