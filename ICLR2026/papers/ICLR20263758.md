## Problem

Qiu et al. (2025) demonstrated that training loss curves collapse onto a universal trajectory after normalization, but validated this only on small-scale autoregressive tasks with vanilla Adam and no weight decay. It remained unclear whether collapse persists for full-scale LLM families where width, depth, learning rate, batch size, and weight decay are co-scaled under practical recipes. This paper shows collapse does persist at LLM scale and identifies the precise hyperparameter conditions under which it emerges as a signature of compute-efficient training.

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard LLM pre-training inputs—model architecture (hidden dim, layers, heads), a token dataset, and hyperparameters (peak LR, weight decay, batch size)—and produces trained Celerity models (300M–3.9B parameters) alongside normalized training loss curves. Unusually, the output includes a collapse-residual diagnostic that flags training pathologies (e.g., numerical instabilities) well before raw loss trends reveal them, and a parametric surrogate model (fit at 111M scale) that predicts final loss L(T) from partial large-scale runs, enabling hyperparameter selection after only 10–30% of training.

## Method

The paper identifies three scale-invariant controls governing TLC shape: the AdamW timescale τ = B/(ηλD) (bias-variance trade-off), the tokens-per-parameter ratio TPP = D/N (power-law improvement rate), and the LR schedule (phasing of early vs. late loss reduction). When τ is set optimally for a fixed TPP and the LR schedule is held constant across model sizes, normalized TLCs collapse, which the authors formalize via a noisy quadratic model showing τ controls an exponential bias-decay term and a 1/τ variance floor. They instantiate this in Celerity, the first LLM family trained in a collapse regime using CompleteP parameterization, a linear decay-to-zero schedule, and τ transferred across scales via a power law in TPP. A parametric surrogate ℓ(t̂) = ((1+ε₁)/(t̂+ε₁))^m + b·(η(t̂)+ε₂)^q, with b and q modeled as power laws in τ and TPP respectively, enables early stopping by aligning partial large-scale curves to small-scale references and reading off the normalizing constant as a calibrated L(T) estimate.

## Results

Celerity (300M–3.9B parameters, trained at fixed TPP bands of 20, 80, and 234) sits on the compute-accuracy Pareto frontier for open models of its scale, achieving comparable average accuracy (across ARC-C, ARC-E, BoolQ, HellaSwag, PIQA, SIQA, Winogrande) to BTLM with 75% fewer training FLOPs. The 234-TPP band achieves a 62% reduction in parameters relative to compute-optimal (20 TPP) training at equal loss, at only a 67% increase in total FLOPs. Collapse is demonstrated tightly across the full 300M–3.9B range, and the early-stopping procedure identifies the best hyperparameter setting with negligible loss gap after just 30% of training (1.7B/20 TPP) or 10% (3.3B/30 TPP), versus the 'current best' heuristic which fails at 1.7B.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against BTLM (Dey et al., 2023b), which was trained before task-specific data annealing became standard, Celerity achieves comparable downstream accuracy with 75% fewer training FLOPs, placing it on the compute-efficiency frontier (Fig. 2). Against the Llama-2 family (Touvron et al., 2023b), which trains at varying TPP and AdamW timescale τ across model sizes, Celerity's fixed-TPP-with-optimal-τ recipe produces tight collapse of normalized training loss curves, whereas Llama-2's curves fail to align after normalization (Fig. 1, left vs. middle). In terms of parameter efficiency, Celerity is weaker than high-TPP families (e.g., BTLM at 80 TPP), meaning those models save FLOPs at inference; however, Celerity's compute-efficient positioning is motivated by evidence that overtrained models are harder to fine-tune and quantize. For distilled-model comparisons, the paper counts only student FLOPs, and notes that including teacher forward-pass or training costs would further strengthen Celerity's relative position.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Qiu et al. (2025)'s demonstration of training loss curve (TLC) collapse under μP from small-scale autoregressive tasks (chess prediction, vanilla Adam, no weight decay) to full LLM families where width, depth, batch size, and weight decay are co-scaled. It builds on the Chinchilla scaling framework (Hoffmann et al., 2022) for the TPP ratio, the AdamW timescale formalism of Wang & Aitchison (2024) and Bergsma et al. (2025a) for the τ control, and CompleteP (Dey et al., 2025) for depth-aware hyperparameter transfer. The key conceptual departure is identifying τ, TPP, and LR schedule as the three scale-invariant controls governing TLC shape, and showing that collapse is not merely a mathematical curiosity but an operational signature of compute-efficient training that enables practical diagnostics and early stopping.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides LLM practitioners with two immediately actionable tools: collapse residuals as an early-warning diagnostic for training pathologies (detecting a numerical instability ~30% of training earlier than raw loss curves), and a principled early-stopping procedure for hyperparameter tuning that identifies optimal settings after only 10–30% of training. The Celerity family demonstrates that these insights translate into a competitive, compute-efficient model family on the accuracy/compute Pareto frontier. Open questions include whether collapse persists under multi-epoch training, alternate optimizers (Adagrad, Adafactor, SGD variants), heavy mid-training data annealing, and MoE architectures at scale; the paper's results are established under single-epoch AdamW pre-training with a specific decay-to-zero schedule.
