## Problem

Existing long-video benchmarks either evaluate different temporal scales across entirely different videos or rely on isolated short segments, inherently conflating temporal granularity with content variability. This makes it impossible to disentangle a model's true performance at each specific timescale from content-driven adaptations, leaving a critical gap in how we assess multi-timescale understanding in MLLMs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20265231/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance pairs a long video (averaging 86 minutes, sampled as frames at 240p resolution) with a four-option multiple-choice question. Questions are structured across four hierarchical timescales—Clip (<3 s), Shot (4–15 s), Event (16 s–10 min), and Story (>10 min)—and five task types (Causal Reasoning, Object Recognition, Action Understanding, Information Summary, Counting). The model outputs a single selected answer, and the benchmark reports accuracy disaggregated by both timescale and task type, enabling direct within-video comparison across granularities.

## Method

ScaleLong's core design principle is 'Intra-Video Multi-Timescale' (IV-MTS): every one of the 269 videos (spanning 5 categories and 36 subcategories) contains 4–8 manually annotated questions, with at least one targeting each of the four temporal scales, so that a model's performance at each granularity is measured against identical narrative content. Distractors are constructed from 10 predefined error types (e.g., spatial replacement, missing information, sequential replacement) to enable fine-grained failure-mode analysis. A two-round quality-control pipeline (Fleiss' κ = 0.85 and 0.90) enforces content-grounded answers, eliminates external-knowledge dependencies, and replaces absolute timestamps with descriptive cues. Timescale alignment is empirically validated via up-scaling experiments (showing <2% accuracy gain from expanded context) and down-scaling experiments (showing 11–15% accuracy loss and VGRR of only 25–33% when context is restricted). The paper then evaluates 23 MLLMs (4 proprietary, 19 open-source) and conducts ablations on visual-token allocation—varying frame count, resolution, and their trade-off under a fixed token budget—revealing a consistent U-shaped performance curve and timescale-dependent optimal frame-resolution configurations.

## Results

On ScaleLong, the best proprietary model (Gemini 2.5 Pro) achieves 67.9% overall accuracy, while the best open-source model (InternVL2.5-78B) reaches 58.6%, both substantially below the human baseline of 91.0%. A consistent U-shaped performance curve emerges across all 23 evaluated MLLMs: accuracy peaks at the shortest (Clip, e.g., 71.5% for Gemini 2.5 Pro) and longest (Story, 69.0%) timescales but dips at intermediate levels (Shot, 62.8%; Event, 68.0%). Counting Problems is the weakest task type across all models (e.g., 51.2% for Gemini 2.5 Pro vs. 72.5% for Object Recognition), while the largest human–model gap (40.6 points) occurs on Shot-scale tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a benchmark paper, ScaleLong does not propose a new model but evaluates 23 MLLMs, revealing that proprietary models consistently outperform open-source ones across all timescales: Gemini 2.5 Pro (67.9%) leads by 9.3 points over the best open-source model InternVL2.5-78B (58.6%), with the gap widening to 14.6 points on Event-scale questions. Within the InternVL2.5 family, scaling from 8B (50.9%) to 78B (58.6%) yields steady gains of roughly 2–3 points per size step, confirming that parameter scaling helps but does not close the 23.1-point gap to human performance (91.0%). GPT-4o (55.4%) and Gemini 2.0 Flash (55.0%) trail Gemini 2.5 Pro by 12.5 and 12.9 points respectively, while Doubao 1.5-VL Pro (58.7%) is the strongest non-Gemini proprietary model. Among open-source models, Qwen2.5-VL-7B (51.2%) and LLaVA-Video-7B (50.8%) are competitive at 7B scale, but both fall well short of the 78B InternVL2.5. The U-shaped pattern (strong Clip/Story, weak Shot/Event) is consistent across all 23 models, and Counting Problems is the weakest task for every model (ranging from 21.7% for LongVU to 51.2% for Gemini 2.5 Pro), highlighting a universal gap in numerical grounding.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in the long-video benchmarking landscape by introducing the 'intra-video multi-timescale' (IV-MTS) design principle: embedding questions at four hierarchical temporal scales (Clip, Shot, Event, Story) within the same video, rather than scattering scale-specific questions across different videos as prior benchmarks (Video-MME, MLVU, MoVQA, LongVideoBench) do. It departs from the dominant 'quality over quantity' vs. 'scale over fidelity' tradeoff by adopting a compact, manually annotated corpus (269 videos) in the spirit of GPQA, and positions itself as a diagnostic tool rather than a training resource. Conceptually, it builds on the MLLM evaluation ecosystem (Gemini, GPT-4o, InternVL, Qwen, LLaVA families) but reframes the evaluation axis from task diversity to temporal-granularity disentanglement.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The consistent U-shaped performance curve across 23 models—strong at the shortest (Clip) and longest (Story) scales but dipping at intermediate (Shot, Event) levels—provides a concrete diagnostic that current MLLMs lack robust mid-range temporal coherence, a gap that prior benchmarks could not isolate from content variability. The 23.1-point gap between the best model (Gemini 2.5 Pro, 67.9%) and human performance (91.0%) underscores substantial room for improvement, particularly in counting and spatial reasoning. Open questions include whether the U-shape persists with longer contexts or different architectures, and whether the visual-token allocation findings (frames > resolution for most scales) generalize beyond the Qwen2.5-VL family tested. Limitations include the relatively small corpus (269 videos), YouTube-sourced cultural/linguistic bias, and the 86-minute average duration, which may not capture truly hour-scale narratives.
