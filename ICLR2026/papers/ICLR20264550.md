## Problem

Prior work attributes LLM QA failures to knowledge gaps in model parameters, motivating solutions focused on expanding capacity or training data. This paper challenges that assumption by showing that LLMs frequently retain correct factual knowledge in their token-level probability distributions even when generating incorrect or 'unsure' answers, revealing a systematic storage-expression gap that standard accuracy metrics fail to capture.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264550/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a few-shot prompted question as input to an LLM and examines the full top-k logit distribution over the model's vocabulary (up to ~128,000 tokens for LLAMA3) at the answer position, rather than only the greedy-decoded token. A token is considered a match if it shares at least three consecutive characters with the ground-truth answer string. The primary output is the Hits@k score; the secondary output from the 'unsure' filtering procedure is a recovered answer token obtained by removing uninformative candidates (tokens starting with 'uns', empty strings, <3 characters, or stop words) from the top-k set and feeding the highest-probability remaining token back into the model for a second decoding pass.

## Method

The paper introduces Hits@k, a metric that checks whether the correct answer appears anywhere among the top-k highest-probability tokens at the answer position, decoupling knowledge retention from surface-form correctness. The core empirical finding is that Hits@k substantially exceeds Hits@1 (e.g., LLAMA3-8B on DBPedia: 17.2% vs. 57.9% at k=5), demonstrating that correct answers are 'submerged' in the distribution rather than absent. To quantify the memory-masking effect of 'unsure' prompting, the authors design a two-stage decoding procedure (Algorithm 1) that filters uninformative tokens from the top-k candidates and re-invokes the model with the best remaining token, recovering a significant fraction of previously masked correct answers (e.g., LLAMA3-70B on DBPedia-Head: 11.2% → 23.0%). Experiments span nine models (1.5B–72B parameters across LLAMA2, LLAMA3, QWEN2, MISTRAL families) and three datasets (DBPedia, IMDB, GoodReads) partitioned into head/torso/tail popularity tiers, using greedy decoding at temperature 0.0 throughout.

## Results

On DBPedia-Head (k=100), LLAMA3-70B achieves the highest Hits@k at 92.1%, followed by LLAMA3-8B at 90.5%, while LLAMA2-70B reaches only 70.5%, showing that model generation (LLAMA3 vs. LLAMA2) matters more than parameter scale. Standard accuracy (Hits@1) for LLAMA3-8B on DBPedia is only 17.2%, yet Hits@5 jumps to 57.9%, revealing a large storage-expression gap. Open-domain DBPedia consistently yields higher Hits@k than domain-specific IMDB and GoodReads (e.g., LLAMA3-8B: 90.5% vs. 69.7% vs. 67.8% on Head). Filtering 'unsure' tokens during decoding recovers additional correct answers, with LLAMA3-70B on DBPedia-Head improving from 11.2% to 23.0% recovery rate.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical study rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so the key contrasts are between the proposed Hits@k metric and standard QA accuracy (Hits@1), and across model families. Standard accuracy severely underestimates stored knowledge: LLAMA3-8B scores only 17.2% Hits@1 on DBPedia but 57.9% Hits@5 and 90.5% Hits@100, a 5x gap. Among models, LLAMA3-8B outperforms LLAMA2-70B by ~20 points on DBPedia-Head Hits@100 (90.5% vs. 70.5%), indicating that training data recency matters more than parameter count. Within the same generation, scaling from 8B to 70B yields minimal Hits@k gains (e.g., LLAMA3: 90.5% → 92.1%), whereas it substantially improves standard accuracy. On domain-specific datasets, the gap between open-domain and specific-domain Hits@k is 20–30 points, and popularity (Head vs. Tail) has a smaller effect on Hits@k than on raw accuracy, confirming that the storage-expression gap is the dominant factor. The 'unsure' filtering experiment shows that removing uninformative tokens recovers 2–12 additional percentage points of correct answers depending on model and dataset, with the largest gains for LLAMA3-70B on DBPedia (+11.8 points on Head).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant framing in LLM knowledge evaluation (Sun et al. 2023; Li et al. 2024) that QA failures stem from insufficient parametric knowledge, instead arguing that a systematic storage-expression gap exists where correct answers are present in logit distributions but not selected during decoding. It repurposes the head-to-tail dataset partitioning from Sun et al. (2023) and the LLMs-as-knowledge-bases paradigm (Petroni et al. 2019) but introduces a fundamentally different analytical lens: examining token-level probability distributions rather than final outputs. The 'unsure' filtering experiment extends the few-shot QA prompting literature by quantifying how the standard practice of permitting abstention inadvertently masks latent knowledge.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that standard QA accuracy substantially underestimates stored knowledge (e.g., 17.2% Hits@1 vs. 57.9% Hits@5) challenges the field's default assumption that hallucinations reflect knowledge absence, with direct implications for how we design evaluation protocols and interpret model failures. The demonstration that 'unsure' prompting suppresses correct answers provides actionable guidance for prompt and decoding design in knowledge-intensive applications. Open questions include whether the storage-expression gap can be systematically closed through decoding strategies, whether the Hits@k string-matching criterion (3 consecutive characters) generalizes well across tokenizers, and whether the modest recovery rates from unsure-filtering (typically 2–12 percentage points) are sufficient for practical deployment.
