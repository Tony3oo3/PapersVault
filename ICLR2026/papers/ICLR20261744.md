## Problem

Knowledge distillation for LLMs suffers from a large capacity gap between teacher and student and training instability caused by near-zero probabilities in high-dimensional output spaces. While assistant distributions have been proposed to bridge this gap, prior work has treated them as isolated recipes—m-mixture in GKD/DistiLLM and e-mixture in TAID—without a systematic study of the interpolation path geometry, the role of the mixing parameter, or compatibility with diverse divergences.

## Input / Output

At each decoding position, the method consumes the teacher's next-token distribution p and the student's next-token distribution q_θ over the shared vocabulary, along with instruction-response training pairs (fixed, on-policy, mixed, or adaptive off-policy). It produces a distilled student LLM whose parameters are optimized to align with the α-mixture assistant distribution r_θ^(α,λ), a per-position probability vector computed as a generalized f_α-mean of p and q_θ with two independent tunable parameters: α controlling the interpolation path geometry and λ controlling the interpolation portion.

## Method

AMiD introduces the α-mixture assistant distribution, defined via the generalized f_α-mean as r_θ^(α,λ) ∝ (λ·p^((1-α)/2) + (1-λ)·q_θ^((1-α)/2))^(2/(1-α)) for α≠1, recovering m-mixture (α=-1) and e-mixture (α=1) as special cases. The distillation objective minimizes an arbitrary divergence D between the teacher (or student) and r_θ^(α,λ), unifying GKD, DistiLLM, and TAID under one framework. The paper proves optimality (p=q_θ at the optimum for any D, α, λ), shows that α controls the support of the assistant distribution (union for α<1, intersection for α≥1), and derives a gradient analysis demonstrating that α acts as a continuous knob between mode-seeking and mode-covering behavior of the student, independent of the chosen divergence. Empirically, α values beyond ±1 (e.g., α=-5) consistently outperform the restricted prior choices across instruction-following, translation, summarization, code generation, and math reasoning tasks.

## Results

On the primary instruction-following distillation benchmark (GPT-2 XL 1.5B → GPT-2 0.1B/0.3B/0.8B), AMiD achieves average ROUGE-L scores of 23.40, 24.50, and 25.84 respectively, surpassing the strongest baseline ABKD (21.76, 23.43, 24.88) by 1.6–1.9 points. The largest gains appear on out-of-distribution generalization benchmarks: on SuperNI, AMiD reaches 29.71 (0.1B student) vs. 26.07 for ABKD, and on UnNI it reaches 30.35 vs. 27.36. On the larger Qwen2.5-14B→1.5B setting, AMiD (α=−5) achieves 72.0% average Win Rate vs. 70.8% for DistiLLM-2 (α=−1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

AMiD consistently outperforms all five compared baselines across every student size and evaluation setting. Against GKD (Agarwal et al., 2024), which uses the m-mixture (α=−1) with generalized JSD, AMiD gains 3.6–2.8 points in average ROUGE-L (e.g., 23.40 vs. 19.77 for the 0.1B student). Against TAID (Shing et al., 2025), which uses the e-mixture (α=1), AMiD gains 2.2–2.5 points, with the largest margin on SuperNI (29.71 vs. 23.66). Against DistiLLM (Ko et al., 2024) in both SKL and SRKL variants, AMiD gains 2.1–2.6 points on average. Against ABKD (Wang et al., 2025), the strongest no-assistant baseline using α-β divergence, AMiD gains 1.6–1.9 points, with the most pronounced advantage on out-of-distribution benchmarks (SuperNI: +3.6, UnNI: +3.0 for the 0.1B student). Against DistiLLM-2 (Ko et al., 2025), the contrastive distillation method, AMiD with α=−5 achieves 72.0% average Win Rate vs. 70.8% for DistiLLM-2 (α=−1) on the Qwen2.5-14B→1.5B setting, and 73.4% average pass@1 vs. 73.3% on code generation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously independent lines of assistant-distribution-based KD for LLMs: the m-mixture (arithmetic mean) used in GKD and DistiLLM, and the e-mixture (geometric mean) used in TAID, by showing both are special cases of the generalized f_α-mean from information geometry. It introduces α as a new distribution-design variable orthogonal to the interpolation weight λ, and generalizes the divergence from the specific choices in prior work to an arbitrary f-divergence. The optimality theorem (Theorem 3.4) and gradient analysis (Proposition 3.5) provide theoretical grounding that was absent in the fragmented prior approaches, which were each proposed as isolated recipes without systematic comparison.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework gives practitioners a principled, continuous knob (α) to tune the mode-covering/mode-seeking trade-off independently of divergence selection, which was previously entangled. The consistent gains across GPT-2, OpenLLaMA2, Gemma, and Qwen2.5 families (instruction-following, translation, summarization, math, code) suggest broad applicability, and the compatibility with contrastive distillation (DistiLLM-2) and arbitrary SGO strategies indicates the assistant distribution is a modular component. Open questions include whether adaptive α scheduling (analogous to TAID's adaptive λ) can further improve performance, whether the benefits persist at frontier-scale models (70B+), and whether the theoretical tuning guidelines (α < 1 for stability, small α for mode-seeking) hold universally or are task-dependent.
