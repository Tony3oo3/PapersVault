## Problem

Standard RLHF reduces diverse human preferences to a single scalar reward function, which cannot represent intransitive preference cycles and is sensitive to training data sampling. NLHF avoids this by seeking a Nash equilibrium, but the resulting policy is inherently stochastic and offers no mechanism for inference-time refinement. This paper asks whether framing preference optimization as a sequential-move game between two policies can simultaneously resolve intransitivity, yield deterministic equilibria, and enable iterative output improvement at test time.

## Input / Output

The Leader receives a text prompt x and generates an initial response y. The Follower receives both the prompt x and the Leader's realized response y (via a 'Improve the previous answer!' instruction) and generates a refined response y'. Both roles are instantiated within a single transformer model using distinct chat templates, so the same parameterized network serves as both π and ω. The preference signal p(y ≻ y' | x) is estimated from pairwise human comparisons (HelpSteer2) or an LLM-as-a-judge (Skywork-Critic-70B) during training, and the final output is a sequence of increasingly refined text responses produced autoregressively at inference time.

## Method

SLHF casts preference optimization as a Stackelberg game: the Leader commits to an action y ~ π(·|x), then the Follower observes y and responds with y' ~ ω(·|x,y), optimizing max_π min_ω over the pairwise preference probability with KL regularization. The key structural difference from NLHF is that the Follower conditions on the Leader's realized action rather than its policy, reducing its task to a stationary best-response problem and guaranteeing a deterministic Stackelberg equilibrium. STACKELBERGGDA approximates this equilibrium via two-timescale gradient descent-ascent (η^F > η^L, ratio κ), where the Follower adapts faster than the Leader, and both policies are projected onto their probability simplices after each step. For LLM fine-tuning, a single shared model is trained with a combined loss that applies the Leader gradient (ascent on preference) and the Follower gradient (descent on preference) with the two-timescale weight κ, using policy-gradient estimators from batched samples. The paper proves existence and uniqueness of the Stackelberg equilibrium under standard regularity conditions and shows that in the non-regularized limit the solution is deterministic, unlike the Nash equilibrium which is generically stochastic.

## Results

In the round-robin tournament on HelpSteer2 (Qwen2.5-0.5B base), STACKELBERGGDA-Follower achieves 80.0% preference over the base model, 65.6% over RLOO, and 59.4% over NASH-MD-PG, while the Leader matches NASH-MD-PG at ~50% in head-to-head comparison. On the large-scale Llama-3.1-Tulu-3-8B fine-tuning, STACKELBERGGDA-Follower reaches 44.57 LC Winrate on AlpacaEval 2.0 (vs. 33.37 for the DPO baseline from the same base model) and 61.92 on IFEval, while the Leader achieves 35.04 LC Winrate and 71.71 IFEval. The Follower also improves outputs from independently trained models (e.g., 80.3% preference when refining Qwen2.5-0.5B responses) without any additional fine-tuning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against RLOO (Ahmadian et al., 2024), STACKELBERGGDA clearly wins: the Follower achieves 65.6% preference over RLOO and the Leader achieves 61.3%, while RLOO only reaches 59.3% over the base model. Against NASH-MD-PG (Munos et al., 2024), the Leader essentially ties at 50.3% in head-to-head comparison, but the Follower extends this to a 59.4% win, demonstrating that the sequential refinement step provides a consistent advantage over the simultaneous-move Nash solution. On the large-scale AlpacaEval 2.0 benchmark, STACKELBERGGDA-Follower (44.57 LC Winrate) substantially outperforms the DPO-trained Llama-3.1-Tulu-3-8B-DPO (33.37), though it trails GEMMA-2-9B-IT (48.18). On IFEval, the DPO baseline (75.23) outperforms both STACKELBERGGDA variants (71.71 Leader, 61.92 Follower), reflecting that verifiable instruction-following benefits more from DPO-style optimization than from the preference-based Stackelberg objective.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the game-theoretic framing of preference optimization introduced by NLHF (Munos et al., 2024), which casts alignment as a simultaneous-move game solved via Nash equilibrium, by replacing it with a sequential-move Stackelberg game where a Leader commits to an action and a Follower responds conditionally. The theoretical analysis reuses the closed-form KL-regularized solution structure from DPO/RLHF (Rafailov et al., 2023) to show the Follower's subproblem admits a unique solution, enabling a clean decomposition. The key departure from both RLHF and NLHF is that the Follower observes the Leader's realized action (not just its policy), yielding a stationary best-response problem and deterministic equilibria in the non-regularized limit, in contrast to the necessarily stochastic Nash equilibrium. The inference-time refinement capability connects to the self-correction literature but differs by requiring no external reward model or multi-stage training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SLHF addresses a concrete limitation of RLHF (sensitivity to training data distribution under intransitive preferences) and NLHF (inherent stochasticity of Nash equilibria) while providing a principled mechanism for inference-time refinement that transfers across model families without fine-tuning. The practical implication is that a single Follower model can serve as a universal 'improver' for diverse Leader policies, reducing deployment complexity. Key open questions include: whether the approach scales to real-time interactive settings with individual user preferences, whether last-iterate convergence can be achieved (currently only ergodic guarantees exist), and how to integrate verifiable rewards alongside comparative preferences within the sequential framework. The reliance on a well-specified pairwise preference function remains a practical bottleneck in open-ended domains.
