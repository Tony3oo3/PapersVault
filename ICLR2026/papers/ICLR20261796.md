## Problem

While a few dramatic phase transitions (e.g., induction heads, arithmetic grokking) appear as visible drops in the aggregate training loss, the paper argues that most conceptual breakthroughs are concealed by averaging all data into a single scalar loss curve. Prior work identifies breakthroughs top-down by tracking predefined concepts, but no bottom-up, unsupervised method exists to discover which subsets of data undergo synchronized learning events hidden within the smooth overall curve.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequence of intermediate model checkpoints (sampled every 200–750 training steps), a training subset for Hessian estimation, and a validation set of individual tokens whose per-token loss is tracked at each checkpoint. It produces, for each token and each basis vector, a cumulative projected-loss trajectory; these trajectories are then clustered via HDBSCAN into groups of tokens that share breakthrough timing, yielding interpretable skill clusters and the timestep at which each hidden breakthrough occurs.

## Method

POLCA modifies Loss Change Allocation (LCA) in three ways: it replaces axis-aligned parameter directions with an arbitrary orthonormal basis, computes the decomposition per individual datapoint rather than per dataset, and adds a second-order Taylor term justified by the Hessian-based basis construction. The basis is built iteratively (Algorithm 1): at each checkpoint, the top-k eigenvectors of the Hessian projected onto the nullspace of previously found directions are appended, yielding a low-rank subspace that captures high-curvature movement directions while filtering out oscillatory directions that do not produce net loss decrease. Per-token projected loss trajectories are then clustered with HDBSCAN separately for each basis vector, so that a single token can belong to different clusters along different directions—capturing the fact that one example may benefit from multiple distinct conceptual breakthroughs at different times.

## Results

On the synthetic arithmetic addition task, POLCA achieves a maximum carry-skill cluster homogeneity of 0.973, substantially outperforming exact loss clustering (0.514), LCA (0.792), and Fisher information (0.664). POLCA also identifies the highest fraction of clusters with hidden breakthroughs past the exact-loss plateau (0.355 at τ=1000), compared to 0.019 for LCA and 0.0 for exact loss. In the English Wikipedia language modeling setting, POLCA clustering recovers interpretable grammatical clusters (e.g., appositive noun phrases, comma-after-parenthetical, repeated newlines) whose projected loss trajectories show sharp transitions while the corresponding exact loss curves remain smooth.

## Baselines & Comparisons

POLCA is compared against LCA (Lan et al., 2020) and a Fisher information trajectory (following Achille et al., 2017) as the two primary decomposition baselines, alongside exact loss and change-in-exact-loss as non-decomposed baselines. POLCA decisively outperforms LCA on both headline metrics: carry-skill homogeneity improves from 0.792 to 0.973, and the fraction of clusters with hidden breakthroughs jumps from 0.019 to 0.355. Against the Fisher information baseline, POLCA also wins on homogeneity (0.973 vs. 0.664) and hidden breakthroughs (0.355 vs. 0.284), though the gap is smaller since Fisher information uses the same Hessian-derived basis. Exact loss and change-in-exact-loss clustering recover digit-position skills but fail entirely on the carrying skill (homogeneity ≈ 0.51–0.52, essentially chance) and find zero hidden breakthroughs, confirming that decomposition is necessary to uncover subtler learned concepts. The authors note that first-order POLCA (without the second-order Hessian term) achieves comparable F1 scores (0.848 vs. 0.838), suggesting the second-order correction provides a modest but consistent improvement in cluster quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Loss Change Allocation (LCA; Lan et al., 2020) by replacing its axis-aligned parameter decomposition with projections onto an arbitrary orthonormal basis (constructed from Hessian eigenvectors via CoLA), shifting from aggregate to per-datapoint loss, and adding a second-order Taylor term. Conceptually, it departs from the dominant top-down paradigm for identifying phase transitions (Olsson et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2024a; Murty et al., 2023), where a predefined skill is tracked for sudden loss drops, in favor of a bottom-up, unsupervised clustering approach that groups data by shared training dynamics. The method also draws on the low-rank training subspace literature (Gur-Ari et al., 2018) and edge-of-stability / multiscale loss landscape work (Cohen et al., 2022; Ma et al., 2022) to justify why linear decomposition along Hessian directions should capture conceptually meaningful learning events.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, unsupervised tool for discovering interpretable skills hidden within apparently smooth loss curves, supporting the hypothesis that 'phase transitions are everywhere' (Nanda et al., 2023). However, the method is currently limited to small models (9M and 40M parameters) due to the computational cost of Hessian eigenvector computation and high-frequency checkpointing, raising open questions about scalability to frontier-scale models. The basis construction (iterative top-k Hessian eigenvectors with oscillation filtering) is acknowledged as a key bottleneck, and the authors note that non-orthogonal or learned bases might yield cleaner concept separation. The POS-tag-based automatic labeling in the natural language setting is narrow, capturing primarily syntactic skills and missing many semantically meaningful clusters that likely exist in the discarded basis vectors.
