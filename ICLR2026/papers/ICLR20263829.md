## Problem

Graph Neural Networks model MILP instances as bipartite variable-constraint graphs, but this representation fundamentally cannot distinguish 'Foldable MILP' instances—non-isomorphic problems that differ in feasibility yet are indistinguishable under the Weisfeiler-Lehman test. Random-feature augmentation only bypasses the WL limitation without capturing the intrinsic geometric structure of the feasible region, leaving a critical gap in feasibility prediction and solution quality for a broad class of MILPs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263829/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a standard MILP formulation (constraint matrix A ∈ R^{m×n}, objective vector c, bounds l/u, and integer indicator set I) and decomposes it into geometric feature tokens—each constraint as a hyperplane vector (normal, direction type, bias), plus bound vectors, an integer indicator, and the objective coefficient—each zero-padded to dimension n+2 and assembled into a sequence of length m+4. The model outputs a feasibility label (binary), an optimal objective value (scalar in R ∪ {±∞}), and an optimal solution vector in R^n; in the predict-and-search pipeline, the predicted solution serves as a warm-start candidate refined by a local heuristic before branch-and-bound.

## Method

MILPnet replaces the bipartite graph paradigm with a geometric sequence representation: each MILP instance is encoded as an ordered sequence of m+4 feature tokens in R^{n+2}, where constraint hyperplanes, variable bounds, integer indicators, and the objective vector are treated as sequence positions (with the objective fixed at the end to preserve its unique role). The architecture processes this sequence via a Multi-Scale Hybrid Attention (HYA) mechanism that combines shifted-window local attention at N different window sizes—each window always incorporating the objective token position—with a global attention branch, blended by a learnable scalar α. Stacked layers apply HYA followed by residual connections, layer normalization, and position-wise MLPs. The paper proves that this architecture can uniformly approximate the feasibility, optimal-objective-value, and optimal-solution mappings to arbitrary precision on finite datasets over the measurable topological space H^MILP, and empirically achieves near-zero error on Foldable MILPs where all graph-based baselines (including random-feature-augmented variants) remain stuck at chance-level performance.

## Results

On Foldable MILP instances (the class where GNNs provably fail), MILPnet achieves feasibility MSE of 0.0005 with 0 errors out of 10,000 test instances on FOLD(20,6), compared to 0.25–0.50 MSE and 5,000 errors for all graph-based baselines — a 100×+ improvement. For end-to-end optimal solution prediction, MILPnet reaches MSE 0.0473 on FOLD(20,6) versus 0.075–0.075 for GCN/GIN/SAGE, and 0.1158 on FOLD(50,20) versus 0.1000 for graph baselines. On real-world benchmarks (IP, SC, CA, FC), MILPnet achieves the smallest optimality gap (e.g., 0.0234 on IP vs. 0.0389 for GCN) and the fastest total solving time (0.1864s vs. 0.3774s on IP).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MILPnet decisively outperforms all five graph-based baselines (GCN, GIN, SAGE, PGN, GraphGPS) on Foldable MILP feasibility prediction, reducing MSE from ~0.25–0.50 to ~0.0005 (a 100–1000× improvement) and eliminating essentially all errors (0 vs. 5,000 out of 10,000). Random-feature-augmented variants (GCN^rf, GIN^rf, SAGE^rf, PGN^rf, GraphGPS^rf) provide only marginal gains over their base versions and still fail on the hardest setting FOLD(50,30), where GCN^rf degrades to MSE 0.5223. On optimal solution prediction (Table 2), MILPnet achieves the best MSE on FOLD(20,6) (0.0473 vs. 0.075 for GCN/GIN/SAGE) and FOLD(20,16) (0.1964 vs. 0.2000), though it is slightly behind graph baselines on the larger FOLD(50,*) settings (0.1158 vs. 0.1000). On real-world predict-and-search benchmarks (Table 5), MILPnet beats GCN on all four datasets in both optimality gap (e.g., 0.0234 vs. 0.0389 on IP) and total solving time (0.1864s vs. 0.3774s on IP). In the heterogeneous ConPAS-style transfer experiment on very-large MIPLIB instances (Table 9), ConPAS(MILPnet) achieves a better objective value (4588.51 vs. 4817.66 on blp-ic98) and substantially faster solving (94.64s vs. 175.86s on 30n20b818) compared to ConPAS(GCN). Overall, MILPnet uses fewer parameters (0.56–0.60M) than most graph baselines (0.66M–2.91M) while delivering superior accuracy on the core Foldable MILP task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs fundamentally from the dominant graph-based paradigm for MILP representation, where prior work (Chen et al., 2023b; Han et al., 2023; Gasse et al., 2019) encodes MILP instances as bipartite variable-constraint graphs processed by GNNs. It builds directly on the identification of 'Foldable MILPs'—instances where GNNs provably fail due to Weisfeiler-Lehman test expressiveness limits—and on the partial remedy of random feature augmentation, which the authors argue only bypasses rather than resolves the underlying representational gap. The key conceptual move is replacing the graph with a sequence of geometric feature tokens (constraint hyperplanes, integer indicators, variable bounds, objective direction) processed by a multi-scale shifted-window attention mechanism, drawing on ideas from hierarchical sequence modeling but applied to a new domain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By showing that a sequence-based geometric representation with fewer parameters can resolve feasibility prediction on Foldable MILPs where all graph-based methods (including random-feature-augmented variants) completely fail, the paper challenges the assumption that graph structure is necessary for MILP representation. The theoretical approximation guarantees on measurable topological spaces provide a rigorous foundation absent in prior ML-for-MILP work. However, open questions remain: cross-size generalization is sensitive to model capacity and problem scale, permutation invariance is only demonstrated at small scales (FOLD-20/50), and the practical gains on real-world benchmarks (Tables 5, 9) are modest compared to the dramatic Foldable MILP results, suggesting the sequence advantage may be most pronounced on structurally constrained problem classes rather than general MILPs.
