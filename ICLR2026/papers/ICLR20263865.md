## Problem

Uncertainty quantification for LLMs has been studied almost exclusively on closed-book factual QA, while contextual QA—central to retrieval-augmented generation—remains largely unexplored. Existing approaches for contextual QA rely on heuristics without theoretical grounding, and no prior method provides a principled decomposition of epistemic uncertainty specifically tailored to the context-conditioned setting.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263865/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a context passage and a question as input, performs a single greedy forward pass through the LLM, and outputs a scalar epistemic uncertainty score computed as a weighted sum of three dot products between the model's hidden states and pre-extracted feature vectors. No sampling or additional forward passes are needed at test time; the only training cost is extracting feature directions and fitting three ensemble weights from as few as 64 labeled correctness examples.

## Method

The paper defines total uncertainty as the cross-entropy between an unknown true distribution P* and the model's predictive distribution, decomposing it into aleatoric uncertainty (entropy of P*) and epistemic uncertainty (KL divergence). By approximating P* with a perfectly prompted ideal model that shares the same architecture and weights, the authors derive an upper bound on epistemic uncertainty proportional to the L2 distance between last-layer hidden states of the ideal and actual models. Under the linear representation hypothesis, this distance is reinterpreted as a sum of feature gaps along semantically meaningful directions. For contextual QA, three features are hypothesized—context-reliance, context-comprehension, and honesty—and extracted via contrastive prompting (e.g., 'look at the context' vs. 'use your own knowledge') followed by PCA over a small labeled set. At inference, the final uncertainty score is a linear combination of three dot products, requiring only three learned scaling parameters and no sampling.

## Results

On LLaMA-3.1-8B, Feature-Gaps achieves PRR of 64.9 / 66.6 / 59.7 and AUROC of 75.3 / 78.0 / 74.0 on Qasper / HotpotQA / NarrativeQA, outperforming the best supervised baseline SAPLMA (59.9 / 53.0 / 47.3 PRR) by up to 13.6 PRR points. Against the strongest unsupervised methods (PTrue at 57.4 PRR on Qasper, SAR at 53.5 PRR on HotpotQA), the gains reach up to 16 PRR points. The method requires no sampling and only three dot products at inference time, making it substantially faster than sampling-based approaches like Semantic Entropy or KLE. On Mistral-7B, performance drops on NarrativeQA (38.5 PRR) due to the model's 32k context window being exceeded by 13.3% of samples.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Feature-Gaps outperforms all 12 baselines on PRR across the majority of model–dataset pairs, with the sole exception of Mistral-7B on NarrativeQA where context-length limitations degrade performance. Against the strongest unsupervised method PTrue (Kadavath et al.), it gains 7.5 PRR on Qasper (64.9 vs. 57.4) and 20.5 PRR on HotpotQA (66.6 vs. 46.1) with LLaMA-3.1-8B; PTrue even produces negative PRR on Mistral and Qwen for HotpotQA, indicating worse-than-random discrimination. Versus the best multi-sampling method SAR (Duan et al.), Feature-Gaps leads by 11.0 PRR on HotpotQA (66.6 vs. 53.5) and ties on NarrativeQA (59.7 vs. 59.7) for LLaMA. Among supervised baselines, SAPLMA (Azaria & Mitchell) is the closest competitor, but Feature-Gaps surpasses it by 5.0 PRR on Qasper, 13.6 PRR on HotpotQA, and 12.4 PRR on NarrativeQA with LLaMA. LookBackLens (Chuang et al.) is only evaluated on HotpotQA due to memory constraints, where Feature-Gaps leads by 13.3 PRR (66.6 vs. 53.3) for LLaMA. In out-of-distribution settings (train on one dataset, test on another), Feature-Gaps maintains AUROC above 72 across all cross-dataset transfers for LLaMA, while SAPLMA drops to 63.4–69.6, confirming superior generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the information-theoretic uncertainty decomposition of Schweighofer et al. (2024) by reversing the cross-entropy argument order and adapting it to autoregressive LLMs, then bridges to the linear representation hypothesis from mechanistic interpretability (Park et al., 2024; Nanda et al., 2023) to express the epistemic uncertainty bound as interpretable semantic feature gaps. It departs from the dominant UQ-for-LLMs literature (semantic entropy, SAPLMA, LookBackLens, etc.), which is largely heuristic and evaluated on closed-book factual QA, by providing a theoretically grounded formulation specifically for contextual QA. The feature extraction procedure borrows the top-down contrastive prompting approach of Zou et al. (2025) (representation engineering), but repurposes it to construct uncertainty scores rather than to steer model behavior.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers the first theoretically grounded UQ method for contextual QA, a setting critical for RAG deployments where most prior work is heuristic. Its practical appeal is strong: only three dot products at inference (no sampling, no extra forward passes) while outperforming both sampling-based and supervised baselines, with notably better OOD generalization than SAPLMA. Key open questions include whether the three hand-selected features (context-reliance, comprehension, honesty) are sufficient or whether automatic, task-agnostic feature discovery is possible, whether the framework extends to reasoning or long-form generation, and how to account for noise from imperfect retrieval in RAG pipelines. The method still requires a small amount of labeled correctness data, and scaling beyond 256 samples or to larger models (32B+) shows diminishing or mixed returns.
