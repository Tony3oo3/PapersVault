## Problem

Kernel method-based estimation of triggering kernels in multivariate Hawkes processes has been limited by the need to solve a costly nonlinear optimization over dual coefficients whose dimensionality scales with the number of events. Prior representer theorems for point processes (Flaxman et al., 2017; Bonnet & Sangnier, 2025) either required discretized approximations of the loss or still demanded iterative coefficient optimization, creating serious scalability barriers for the large event sequences typical of multivariate Hawkes applications. This paper asks whether a non-approximated penalized least squares formulation can yield a representer theorem that eliminates the coefficient optimization entirely.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a multivariate event sequence {(t_n, u_n)} over an observation interval [0, T] along with a finite support window A that truncates the triggering kernels. It outputs the estimated triggering kernels g_ij(s) for all dimension pairs (i,j) and baseline intensities mu_i, each expressed as a linear combination of equivalent kernels evaluated at the observed event times. Crucially, all dual coefficients in this expansion are analytically fixed to unity, so the only computational burden beyond evaluating the equivalent kernels is a single matrix inversion of size MU×MU (where M is the number of random Fourier features and U the number of dimensions), which is independent of the data size N.

## Method

The paper formulates triggering kernel estimation as a penalized least squares contrast in an RKHS and proves a novel representer theorem: the optimal estimator of each triggering kernel g_ij(s) is a linear combination of 'equivalent kernels' h_j(s, t_n) evaluated at data points, with all coefficients equal to 1. These equivalent kernels are defined through a coupled system of Fredholm integral equations of the second kind whose coefficients depend on the event times and the RKHS kernel. To solve these equations in practice, the authors approximate the shift-invariant RKHS kernel (Gaussian, Matérn, Laplace) via quasi-Monte Carlo random Fourier features in a degenerate form, which reduces the Fredholm system to a single linear algebra problem: inverting an MU×MU block matrix whose entries are computed in closed form using analytic integrals of cosine products. The baseline intensities mu_i are then obtained in closed form from the same equivalent kernels, yielding an estimator that requires only additive matrix operations and one matrix inversion, with no iterative optimization or discretization of the loss integrals.

On the mutually-exciting synthetic scenario (T=5000, ~4081 events), the proposed method achieves an integrated squared error Δ²=0.20 with a CPU time of 10.7 s, compared to the SOTA kernel method Bonnet (Δ²=0.15, 1358 s) and the parametric Exp baseline (Δ²=0.28, 134 s). On the harder refractory scenario (T=5000), Ours reaches Δ²=0.59 in 14.9 s versus Bonnet's 0.44 in 3197 s—a ~200× speedup. On the real-world financial dataset (N=3319), Ours attains a test negative log-likelihood of 1721 in 3.4 s, outperforming Bonnet (1829, 550 s) and Gau (2899, 7.2 s), though trailing the parametric Exp (8086 NLL but only 38.2 s) and Ber (1487, 13.3 s) on accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the SOTA kernel method Bonnet & Sangnier (2025), the proposed method achieves comparable Δ² on the mutually-exciting scenario (0.20 vs 0.15 at T=5000) while being ~127× faster (10.7 s vs 1358 s); on the refractory scenario the accuracy gap widens (0.59 vs 0.44) but the speed advantage grows to ~215× (14.9 s vs 3197 s). Against the parametric Exp baseline (Bonnet et al., 2023), Ours is substantially more accurate on both synthetic scenarios (Δ² 0.20 vs 0.28 mutually-exciting; 0.59 vs 2.20 refractory) and faster at T≥5000, though Exp is faster on smaller data. Against the Gaussian-mixture nonparametric method Gau (Xu et al., 2016), Ours is competitive on the mutually-exciting scenario (0.20 vs 0.18) but clearly superior on the refractory scenario (0.59 vs 1.47) where Gau cannot model inhibition. Against the Bernstein-polynomial method Ber (Lemonnier & Vayatis, 2014), Ours is more accurate on both synthetic scenarios and faster at all data sizes. On the real-world financial dataset, Ours (NLL 1721) outperforms Bonnet (1829) and Gau (2899) but trails Ber (1487) and Exp (8086 is actually worse—Exp's NLL is 8086, so Ours beats Exp by a wide margin on NLL as well). Overall, the proposed method trades a small accuracy gap to Bonnet for orders-of-magnitude computational savings, making it the most scalable nonparametric kernel method for Hawkes processes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the representer theorem lineage for point processes initiated by Flaxman et al. (2017) for Poisson processes and Bonnet & Sangnier (2025) for multivariate Hawkes processes. The key departure is twofold: (1) it targets the non-discretized penalized least squares contrast for *linear* Hawkes processes (rather than the discretized approximation or non-linear link function used by Bonnet & Sangnier), and (2) it shows that the equivalent kernels are defined through a coupled system of Fredholm integral equations rather than a single integral equation. By leveraging the quadratic structure of the least squares loss and Mercer's theorem, the authors prove that all dual coefficients collapse to unity, reducing the estimation to a single matrix inversion of size MU×MU (independent of data size) solved via random Fourier features.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that dual coefficients are analytically fixed to unity is a striking simplification: it eliminates the iterative dual optimization that makes prior kernel-based Hawkes estimators scale as O(N⁴U²P), yielding an O(N²M²U² + M³U³) closed-form estimator that is orders of magnitude faster on large datasets. However, the linear Hawkes formulation does not guarantee non-negative intensities (requiring post-hoc clipping), the method scales cubically in dimensionality U, and no rigorous learning guarantees are provided. Open questions include whether the unity-coefficient property extends to non-linear Hawkes processes or likelihood-based losses, and whether the integral-equation-defined kernels can be integrated into deep kernel learning frameworks for Hawkes processes.
