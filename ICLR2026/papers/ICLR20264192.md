## Problem

Predicting downstream benchmark performance from training budget has long been considered noisy and unreliable, with prior work favoring two-stage pipelines that map compute to a proxy metric (e.g., NLL) and then to accuracy. This paper challenges that view by asking whether a direct, simple functional form can model downstream accuracy as a function of training FLOPs without an intermediate proxy, and whether such a direct approach can outperform the compounding-error-prone two-stage methods.

## Input / Output

The method takes training FLOPs C, model parameter count N, and token count D as inputs, and produces a predicted downstream accuracy Q on 12 benchmarks (ranging from multiple-choice QA to code pass@1). For multiple-choice tasks, raw accuracy is first normalized to [0,1] via Q' = (Q − Q_random)/(1 − Q_random), where Q_random is estimated empirically from small models. The pass@k extension additionally takes the number of inference samples k and outputs a predicted pass rate, with the model fitted on experiments below 6×10²¹ FLOPs and validated on larger held-out runs up to 6.7× that budget.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-parameter power law on log accuracy: −log(Q) = A/C^α, which captures the observed S-shaped scaling of benchmark performance with training compute at a fixed token-to-parameter ratio. This is extended to varying TPRs via −log(Q) = A/N^α + B/D^β (analogous to Chinchilla's loss scaling but without an irreducible error term, since the theoretical accuracy ceiling is 1), and to repeated sampling via log(−log Q(C,k)) = log A + α log C + β log k + δ log C log k. Coefficients are fitted with Huber loss (δ=1e-3) and L-BFGS-B, and the paper validates across 130 pretraining runs (up to 17B parameters, 350B tokens) on two data mixtures (DCLM+code+math and C4). The key empirical finding is that this direct power law achieves lower extrapolation error (mean MAE ≈ 0.015, MRE ≈ 2%) than both BNSL and two-stage linear/logistic baselines, which suffer from error compounding between the FLOPs-to-proxy and proxy-to-accuracy stages.

## Results

The proposed two-parameter power law (−log Q = A/C^α) achieves a mean MRE of 1.96% and mean MAE of 0.015 across 12 downstream benchmarks when extrapolating from models trained below 6×10²¹ FLOPs to models up to 6.7× larger. This outperforms the Broken Neural Scaling Law (BNSL) baseline (2.71% MRE, 0.020 MAE) and substantially outperforms two-stage approaches (6.35–6.67% MRE, 0.044–0.047 MAE). The extended model across token-to-parameter ratios (Equation 4) achieves 1.91% validation MRE, and the pass@k extension (Equation 5) reaches 7.94% validation MRE on HumanEval and LBPP.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against BNSL (Caballero et al., 2023), the proposed PowerLaw wins on prediction accuracy (1.96% vs. 2.71% mean MRE; 0.015 vs. 0.020 mean MAE), while BNSL achieves a slightly better in-sample fit (RMSE 0.007 vs. 0.011; R² 0.993 vs. 0.986), indicating BNSL overfits the training range but extrapolates less reliably. Against the two-stage linear approach (Bhagia et al., 2025), the PowerLaw reduces MRE by roughly 3.4× (1.96% vs. 6.67%) and MAE by nearly 3× (0.015 vs. 0.044), despite the two-stage model showing a higher R² on training data (0.943 vs. 0.986 is actually lower, but the two-stage logistic reaches 0.974). Against the two-stage logistic approach (Chen et al., 2025), the PowerLaw again dominates on extrapolation (1.96% vs. 6.35% MRE; 0.015 vs. 0.047 MAE), with the paper attributing the gap to compounding errors between the FLOPs→NLL and NLL→Accuracy stages. Per-benchmark (Table 4), the PowerLaw is best on 7 of 12 tasks (e.g., ARC-Easy 2.37% vs. 7.00% MRE; Winogrande 1.64% vs. 4.93%), while BNSL is best on 5 (e.g., LAMBADA 3.45% vs. 0.58%; WebQS 7.28% vs. 1.46%), with the average favoring the PowerLaw.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant two-stage paradigm for predicting downstream LLM performance (FLOPs → proxy metric like NLL → accuracy), as used in LLaMA 3, Bhagia et al., and Chen et al., by proposing a direct one-stage power law mapping training FLOPs to log accuracy. It adapts the Chinchilla-style functional form from Hoffmann et al. (2022) but removes the irreducible error term, arguing that accuracy has a natural ceiling of 1. The paper also extends the analysis to varying token-to-parameter ratios and to pass@k inference compute, building on the power-law observations for repeated sampling from Brown et al. (2024) and Schaeffer et al. (2025a).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By demonstrating that a simple two-parameter power law on log accuracy predicts downstream benchmark performance with ~2% MAE across 12 diverse tasks, the paper challenges the prevailing view that downstream metrics are too noisy for reliable scaling extrapolation. This provides practitioners with a lightweight, model-agnostic tool for planning training budgets without needing intermediate proxy metrics. Key open questions include whether the functional form generalizes beyond decoder-only Transformers with fixed recipes, how it behaves under instruction tuning or preference optimization, and whether the observed smoothness persists for composite or thresholded benchmarks. The paper also notes that reliable extrapolation requires task-specific FLOPs thresholds, and that data mixture composition significantly affects which capabilities scale predictably.
