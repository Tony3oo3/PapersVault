## Problem

Current RL post-training for language models may simply sharpen behaviors already present in the base model rather than discovering genuinely novel reasoning strategies, and it is unclear how the rich prior knowledge in pre-trained representations can be leveraged to guide deliberate exploration. The paper asks whether a principled, representation-based diversity bonus derived from the LM's own hidden states can improve both inference-time verifier efficiency and post-training pass@k beyond what standard RL achieves.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264092/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For inference-time selection, the method takes a text prompt, a pool of N candidate responses (e.g., 6400 sampled at temperature 1.0), and the LM's last-layer hidden states (averaged over all response tokens, then sparse-projected to 512 dimensions), and outputs a diverse coreset of k responses to pass to the verifier. For RL post-training, the same hidden-state representations are used to compute per-response elliptical bonuses that are added to the binary correctness reward within each GRPO group of 8 rollouts, with the covariance matrix re-initialized per training batch and a fresh random projection drawn at each optimization step.

## Method

The core mechanism is an adaptation of elliptical (leverage-score) bonuses from linear bandit theory to language model exploration: given a response's mean hidden-state representation h, its novelty relative to previously seen responses is measured as h^T Σ^{-1} h, where Σ = λI + Σ_j h_j h_j^T accumulates the outer products of prior representations. For inference-time selection (RepExp), the algorithm greedily selects k responses from the pool by iteratively maximizing this bonus, updating the inverse covariance via the Woodbury identity in O(d²) per step. For RL post-training, the same bonus (bounded in [0,1]) is added to the verifier reward with a small coefficient β=0.01, encouraging the policy to generate responses that are novel in representation space relative to other rollouts in the same batch. The key design choices—using the model's own hidden states as features, averaging over all tokens, and sparse random projection to 512 dims—make the method simple, history-aware, and free of auxiliary networks, while the two-pronged evaluation (inference-time first, then post-training) isolates the effect of the diversity signal from optimization and generalization.

## Results

For inference-time exploration, RepExp achieves over 50% improvement in verifier efficiency (samples-to-correct) for Qwen-2.5-14b-Instruct on GSM8K, MATH, MBPP+, and Game-of-24, with the largest gains on harder questions (e.g., 3x improvement on the hardest 20% of Game-of-24 with Phi-4). For RL post-training on AIME 2024, RepExp's pass@80 matches GRPO's pass@256, demonstrating a 3x improvement in test-time sample efficiency. RepExp is 2.1–4.1x more sample-efficient than Unlikeliness and 3.2–13.4x more sample-efficient than GRPO across tasks. In a protein sequence generation domain, RepExp achieves a 35.9x verifier efficiency improvement over random selection (6.7 vs. 240.3 samples-to-correct).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GRPO (unmodified), RepExp wins decisively on large-k pass@k: standard GRPO exhibits 'diversity collapse' where pass@k degrades below the base model for large k, while RepExp preserves or improves pass@k uniformly across all k values. On AIME 2024, RepExp achieves the same pass@256 as GRPO with only 80 samples (3x sample efficiency). Against Unlikeliness (He et al., 2025), RepExp is 2.1–4.1x more sample-efficient; on MATH and GSM8K, RepExp roughly matches or improves upon Unlikeliness for k≥2, while on AIME 2024 RepExp slightly trails Unlikeliness until k=64 before surpassing it. Against the base model (untrained reference), RepExp eliminates the diversity collapse phenomenon entirely, maintaining or exceeding base-model pass@k at large k where GRPO fails. For inference-time selection, RepExp outperforms random sampling across nearly all model-task pairs, with benefits growing with model strength (weaker models like Qwen-2.5-0.5B see no benefit or degradation, while Qwen-2.5-32B almost uniformly benefits). RepExp on vanilla generation also outperforms random sampling on top of any alternative generation strategy (nucleus, min-p, high-temperature), except when paired with high-temperature sampling where incoherent responses appear novel in representation space.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts elliptical (leverage-score) bonuses from linear bandits and active learning to language model exploration, using the LM's own last-layer hidden states as features—a design choice inspired by Foster et al. (2025), who prove computational benefits of such bonuses in a simplified frozen-feature setting. It departs from prior deep-RL exploration methods (count-based, RND, posterior sampling) by requiring no auxiliary networks, and from concurrent LM exploration work (Unlikeliness, entropy bonuses, DPP diversity) by grounding novelty in the model's internal representations rather than token-level likelihood or learned classifiers. The two-pronged methodology—validating diversity metrics in a clean inference-time selection setting before integrating them into GRPO post-training—is a deliberate departure from prior work that evaluates exploration only within the full RL loop, where optimization and generalization confound the effect.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical, low-overhead recipe (rank-one Woodbury updates on projected hidden states) that simultaneously improves pass@k, eliminates the 'diversity collapse' phenomenon in RL post-training, and produces responses that are measurably less likely under the base model—evidence of genuine exploration beyond sharpening. The inference-time selection framing is independently useful for domains where verification is expensive (e.g., protein design, expert annotation), and the 35.9× verifier-efficiency gain on ESM3 protein generation is a striking preliminary result. Key open questions include whether the approach scales to larger RL compute budgets, whether token-level exploration can be made computationally competitive, and how to extend beyond verifiable rewards without succumbing to reward hacking.
