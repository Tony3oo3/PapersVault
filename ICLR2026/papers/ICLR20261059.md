## Problem

Existing designs for time series A/B experiments suffer from two coupled limitations: they restrict treatment allocation to depend on only a limited history (current observation, initial action, or short window), and they impose strong model assumptions (MDP, ARMA, bounded carryover) to make the MSE objective tractable. The paper establishes an impossibility theorem showing that, for doubly robust ATE estimators, any allocation strategy that omits part of the history is provably suboptimal, and then addresses both limitations simultaneously.

## Input / Output

The method consumes the full growing history of (observation, action, outcome) triplets as a variable-length sequence fed into a transformer encoder, along with a simulation environment that approximates the data-generating process for RL training. At each time step it outputs a binary action (treatment or control) determined by an epsilon-greedy policy over the learned Q-function. The reward signal is the negative discounted squared deviation of the running ATE estimate from a pre-computed Monte Carlo ground truth, rather than the company's actual outcome.

## Method

The core architecture is a transformer-based double deep Q-network (DDQN) where a masked self-attention encoder processes the variable-length full history to produce a state representation, and the Q-function is parameterized by the same transformer to handle the growing input dimension. The RL reward is set to −α^(T−t)·[ÂTE(t) − ATE_mc]², directly encoding MSE minimization without requiring MDP, ARMA, or short-carryover assumptions. An impossibility theorem (Theorem 1) proves that under a history-dependent conditional variance ratio condition, the optimal doubly robust allocation must depend on every variable in the full history, justifying the transformer's full-attention design over RNNs or fixed-window alternatives. Training uses AdamW with cosine learning-rate scheduling, gradient clipping, and mixed-precision, with a warm-up period where early rewards are zeroed to stabilize learning.

## Results

Across three simulation environments (synthetic, real-data-based, and public dispatch simulator), TRL consistently achieves the lowest MSE of the ATE estimator among all compared designs. In the synthetic simulator at T=120, TRL attains an MSE of 27×10⁻⁴, compared to 36×10⁻⁴ for TMDP, 37×10⁻⁴ for NMDP, and 98–320×10⁻⁴ for switchback designs (HW, XCT, BSZ). The advantage of TRL persists and grows with longer horizons: at T=480, TRL achieves 69×10⁻⁴ versus 77.9×10⁻⁴ for TMDP and 171–182×10⁻⁴ for switchback baselines. In the real-data-based simulator with 5% policy improvement, TRL's advantage over TMDP/NMDP is particularly pronounced due to positive outcome autocorrelation that violates the i.i.d.-across-days assumption those designs rely on.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TRL outperforms all five baseline families across every environment and time horizon tested. Against the MDP-based designs TMDP and NMDP (Li et al., 2023), TRL achieves a consistent 10–25% MSE reduction in the synthetic simulator (e.g., 27 vs. 36 at T=120), and a substantially larger gap in the real-data-based simulator where positive outcome autocorrelation breaks the i.i.d.-across-days assumption underlying those designs. Against the switchback designs—HW (Hu & Wager, 2022), BSZ (Bojinov et al., 2023), and XCT (Xiong et al., 2024)—TRL's advantage is dramatic: switchback MSEs are 3–12× higher than TRL's in the synthetic environment (e.g., BSZ at 320 vs. TRL at 27 at T=120), reflecting their inability to adapt allocation to the full history. The most competitive baseline is WSY (Wen et al., 2025), which uses MDP-based optimal switch durations; TRL still leads by 5–15% MSE (e.g., 41 vs. 55.7 at T=240), with the gap narrowing slightly at longer horizons. In the public dispatch simulator, TRL again achieves the lowest MSE, with switchback designs performing better than TMDP/NMDP (due to spatial carryover mitigation) but still trailing TRL.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of experimental design, A/B testing, and reinforcement learning. It builds directly on the double deep Q-network (DDQN) framework, replacing the standard neural network with a transformer encoder to handle variable-length full-history inputs, and repurposes RL from its typical role in ATE estimation to the distinct problem of MSE-optimal treatment allocation. Conceptually, it departs from the dominant line of time-series A/B testing designs (switchback, MDP-based, ARMA-based) by proving via an impossibility theorem that any design not conditioning on the entire history is suboptimal, and by eliminating the need for Markov, ARMA, or bounded-carryover assumptions that prior methods require to make MSE optimization tractable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The impossibility theorem provides a clean theoretical justification for why the field's standard designs (switchback, daily-allocation, short-memory) are fundamentally limited, and the transformer-RL approach offers a practical, assumption-light alternative validated across synthetic, bootstrap, and physical-simulator environments. Key limitations include the requirement of a simulation environment (though the authors argue these are commonly available in industry), substantial training time (3–45 GPU-hours), and restriction to binary actions. Open questions the authors identify include extending the framework to multiple experimental units with spillover effects and to non-binary or continuous action spaces, which would broaden applicability beyond the current two-policy comparison setting.
