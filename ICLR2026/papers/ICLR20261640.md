## Problem

In partial-label graph learning, each graph is annotated only with a candidate label set containing the true label, making it difficult to extract discriminative semantics and increasing overfitting risk to noisy candidates. Existing weakly supervised graph methods either rely on overconfident pseudo-labels that accumulate errors under ambiguity or fail to exploit the multi-scale structural and spectral diversity inherent in graphs to distinguish among candidate labels.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261640/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a collection of graphs (each with node features and an adjacency matrix) along with a candidate label set per graph that contains the true class but also includes false-positive labels injected with probability q. Training proceeds without access to ground-truth labels; the model must learn a classifier that recovers the true label from the ambiguous candidate set. A two-layer GraphSAGE encoder with 512 hidden units serves as the shared backbone, and the final output is a single predicted class label per graph.

## Method

PRISM encodes each graph through two complementary pathways: (1) a spatial encoder that uses momentum-updated class prototypes to guide attention over node embeddings, extracting class-specific substructure vectors and building a relational graph via prototype-aware substructure similarity between graphs sharing candidate labels; and (2) a spectral encoder that projects Laplacian eigenvalues into a harmonic signal space, modulates eigenvectors with band-specific encodings, and applies multi-band attention to preserve frequency-specific semantics, yielding a second relational graph from band-wise spectral similarity. These two relational graphs are then fused in an iterative label propagation scheme that alternates between spatial and spectral neighborhood aggregation, enforces candidate-set masking at each step, and updates a soft label confidence matrix via exponential moving average. Training uses a unified negative marginal log-likelihood loss over the candidate set for both views, weighted by the EMA confidence matrix, which suppresses noise accumulation and drives the soft labels toward the ground truth as graph size and propagation iterations grow.

## Results

PRISM achieves the best accuracy across all five datasets and all noise levels. On ENZYMES (q=0.3), it reaches 63.11% accuracy, surpassing the second-best DEER (58.22%) by 4.9 points. On COIL-DEL (q=0.1), PRISM attains 79.48%, exceeding DEER (68.03%) by 11.5 points. On COLORS-3 (q=0.5), the gap widens to 13.8 points (80.57% vs. 66.81%), demonstrating that PRISM's advantage grows with label ambiguity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PRISM outperforms all 14 baselines across every dataset and noise level. The closest competitor is DEER (the only prior partial-label graph learning method), which PRISM beats by 4.9 points on ENZYMES (q=0.3), 11.5 points on COIL-DEL (q=0.1), and 13.8 points on COLORS-3 (q=0.5). Standard GNNs (GCN, GAT, GIN, GraphSAGE) and pooling methods (TopKPool, SAGPool, EdgePool, ASAP) trail PRISM by 10–30+ points, confirming that generic graph encoders cannot handle candidate-label ambiguity. Contrastive methods (GraphCL, GraphACL) and the semi-supervised TGNN also fall well behind, with TGNN additionally failing (OOM) on CIFAR-10. The vision-based partial-label method PiCO, adapted with a GraphSAGE encoder, performs poorly on graph datasets (e.g., 34.74% on COLORS-3 q=0.5 vs. PRISM's 80.57%), highlighting the necessity of graph-specific structural and spectral modeling.

## Relation to Prior Work

PRISM extends partial-label learning (PLL) from the well-studied Euclidean/CV setting (Feng & An, 2019; PiCO, 2022) to graph-structured data, building on the recent but sparse line of graph-specific PLL work initiated by DEER (Gu et al., 2024) and GPCD (Gao et al., 2024). It departs from these predecessors by introducing a dual-cue relational inference framework: prototype-guided substructure alignment for spatial cues and a novel multi-band frequency attention mechanism for spectral cues, neither of which was exploited in prior graph PLL methods. The spectral component repurposes ideas from spectral GNNs (EigenMLP, Bo et al., 2023) but reframes them for cross-graph band-wise similarity rather than single-graph signal filtering, and the iterative label propagation with candidate constraints draws on classical semi-supervised label propagation adapted to the partial-label setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Partial-label graph learning addresses a practically critical gap: automated annotation pipelines (e.g., DFT-based molecular property prediction) inevitably produce ambiguous candidate sets, and current GNN classifiers degrade sharply under such noise. PRISM's theoretical convergence guarantees (Theorems 1–2) provide a principled justification for why relational propagation resolves ambiguity, though the conditions (|V_i| → ∞) are idealized and may not hold for small molecular graphs. Open questions include scalability of the eigen-decomposition preprocessing to very large graphs, whether the spectral branch provides consistent gains over spatial-only variants across all graph domains, and how the framework extends to multi-label or hierarchical partial-label settings.
