## Problem

Dark patterns—deceptive UI designs that manipulate users into actions misaligned with their goals—are pervasive on the web and known to affect ~31% of human users, yet their impact on LLM-driven web agents has been entirely unexplored. As web agents approach human-level task performance and are increasingly deployed autonomously, it is critical to determine whether these agents are more or less susceptible than humans, whether model scaling provides protection, and whether existing adversarial defenses are effective.

## Input / Output

Each task provides the agent with a webpage screenshot annotated with Set-of-Marks numerical labels, a natural-language task instruction (e.g., 'buy a bouquet of flowers under $30'), and page text content; the agent responds with a single action per step from a fixed action space (click, type, scroll, wait, go-back, answer) over up to 15 steps. Evaluation uses two orthogonal metrics: Success Rate (SR) measures whether the agent reaches the user-specified goal state regardless of dark pattern effects, while Dark Pattern Effectiveness (DP) measures whether the manipulative element was triggered regardless of task completion. Generated tasks additionally have a control version with the dark pattern removed, enabling causal attribution of performance drops to the pattern itself.

## Method

DECEPTICON is a 700-task benchmark (600 generated, 100 in-the-wild) organized around a six-category taxonomy of dark patterns (sneaking, urgency, misdirection, social proof, obstruction, forced action), each task isolating a single pattern mechanism on a self-hosted or archived website. The 600 generated tasks are produced via an adversarial pipeline: Gemini-2.5-Flash generates base website UIs, Gemini-2.5-Pro with an agentic scaffold injects dark patterns informed by visual/textual examples from prior literature, and a naive agent's trajectory serves as a reward signal for difficulty calibration, followed by human verification that filters ~70% of candidates. The key empirical finding is an inverse scaling law: as Qwen2.5-VL scales from 3B to 72B parameters, DP increases from 43.8% to 73.7%, and as Gemini-2.5-Flash reasoning tokens increase from 256 to 16,384, DP rises from 38.5% to 71.2%, revealing that greater model capability and test-time compute make agents more, not less, vulnerable. Among defenses tested, guardrail models (a secondary LLM identifying dark pattern elements) reduce DP by ~28.6% on average, outperforming in-context prompting (~12% reduction), but neither eliminates the threat, with misdirection and obstruction patterns remaining particularly resistant to mitigation.

## Results

On the DECEPTICON generated split (N=600), Simple agents (GPT-4o, GPT-5, Gemini-2.5-Flash, Gemini-2.5-Pro) achieve dark pattern effectiveness (DP) rates of 70.8–78.5%, while standalone agents (Claude Sonnet 4 + Magnitude, o3 + Browser-Use) achieve 59.6–68.3%. On the in-the-wild split (N=100), DP rates range from 55.0% to 71.4%. In contrast, human participants fall for dark patterns only 31.0% (generated) and 33.4% (in-the-wild) of the time, and control (no-dark-pattern) tasks yield 0% DP with ~100% success rate, confirming dark patterns are the causal factor. Obstruction (97% avg DP) and Social Proof (90% avg DP) are the most effective attack categories across SoM agents.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares LLM-driven web agents against three key baselines. First, against human performance: all tested agents are dramatically more susceptible to dark patterns than humans, with DP rates of 59.6–78.5% versus 31.0% for humans on the generated split (a 2.4–2.5× gap), and 55.0–71.4% versus 33.4% on in-the-wild tasks. Second, against control (no-dark-pattern) versions of the same tasks: agents achieve ~100% SR and 0% DP without dark patterns, confirming that the observed manipulation is causally attributable to the dark patterns rather than task difficulty. Third, across agent architectures: standalone agents (o3 + Browser-Use at 59.6% DP; Claude Sonnet 4 + Magnitude at 68.3% DP) show modestly lower susceptibility than Simple SoM agents (70.8–78.5% DP), but the gap is small (~6–19 percentage points), indicating that the underlying LLM rather than the scaffold architecture is the primary driver of vulnerability. Notably, the most capable LLM (GPT-5) does not outperform smaller models in resisting dark patterns, and in some cases performs worse (GPT-4o at 78.5% DP vs. GPT-5 at 70.8% DP on generated tasks).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the web agent robustness evaluation line (WebVoyager, WebArena, VisualWebArena) by introducing a fundamentally different threat model: human-targeted deceptive UI designs (dark patterns) rather than agent-optimized adversarial attacks like prompt injection or pop-up attacks (Zhang et al., 2025; Liao et al., 2025). It operationalizes the dark pattern taxonomy from Mathur et al. (2019) and Nouwens et al. (2020) into a controlled, reproducible benchmark environment (DECEPTICON) with paired control conditions, departing from prior agent-attack work that lacked such baselines. The concurrent work by Tang et al. (2025) also studies dark patterns against GUI agents, but DECEPTICON distinguishes itself through its dual human+agent threat model, avoidability constraints, and systematic defense evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that dark pattern effectiveness increases with model scale and reasoning tokens (an inverse scaling law) directly challenges the field's core assumption that more capable models are more robust, with immediate implications for deploying increasingly powerful web agents in production. The demonstration that standard defenses (in-context prompting, guardrail models) reduce but do not eliminate susceptibility highlights a gap that current safety tooling does not address. Open questions include whether adversarial post-training can build genuine resistance, whether the inverse scaling law persists across architectures and training regimes, and how to evaluate robustness against the full combinatorial space of dark pattern combinations rather than isolated instances.
