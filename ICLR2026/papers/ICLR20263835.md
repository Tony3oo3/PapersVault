## Problem

Conventional model-brain alignment benchmarks (RSA, LP) rank models by pointwise alignment to individual ROIs, but diverse models with different architectures, training objectives, and modalities achieve comparable scores, exposing a critical lack of discriminative power. This raises the conceptual question of what it means for a model to be genuinely 'brain-aligned' versus merely neurally predictive. The paper asks whether a model should reproduce not just a single ROI's alignment score but the entire cross-region alignment profile characteristic of that ROI.

## Input / Output

The benchmark evaluates 47 pretrained vision models (ResNets, ConvNeXts, CLIP, V-JEPA 2, MViT, TimeSformer, VGGT, 26 Taskonomy models) against fMRI BOLD beta values from 10 subjects watching 1000+ video clips, with activations projected to 19 visual ROIs from the HCP-MMP atlas. Model representations from up to 15 layers are reduced via sparse random projection to 5919 dimensions, and alignment is measured using both RSA (correlation of representational dissimilarity matrices) and LP (ridge regression R²). The key output is not a single alignment score but a cross-region alignment pattern—a vector of alignment scores from one ROI or layer to all other ROIs—and the alignment pattern similarity (APS), a Pearson correlation between model-derived and fMRI-derived patterns.

## Method

The core contribution is Alignment Pattern Analysis (APA), a relational extension of the NeuroAI Turing Test that adds a second-order structural consistency test to conventional pointwise alignment. For each ROI, the fMRI-derived alignment pattern is computed by using one subject's activity in that ROI as a predictor and measuring alignment to all other ROIs in a second subject, yielding a characteristic cross-region profile; the model-derived pattern is computed analogously using a single model layer as predictor. A model is considered meaningfully aligned to an ROI only if it reproduces that ROI's alignment pattern (high APS), not merely its pointwise alignment score. The paper validates fMRI-derived patterns against independent structural connectivity data (DTI streamline density from 1065 subjects) and introduces a bootstrap-based effective equivalence heuristic that, when combined with the APS criterion, drastically reduces the set of candidate brain-aligned models—ruling out top-ranking models like V-JEPA 2 for most ROIs.

## Results

Across 47 vision models evaluated on the BOLD Moments video fMRI dataset (10 subjects, 19 visual ROIs), the self-supervised V-JEPA 2 model achieves the highest overall RSA alignment (e.g., V2: 0.13, V3: 0.12, V8: 0.09), yet multiple architecturally distinct models (CLIP-RN50, VGGT-1B, ConvNeXt, SimCLR-ResNet50) fall within the 95% bootstrap CI of the top model, confirming effective equivalence. Under Linear Predictivity, top models reach or exceed brain-brain alignment on most ROIs, indicating benchmark saturation. Critically, under the proposed Alignment Pattern Analysis (APA), fMRI-derived cross-region alignment patterns are highly consistent across subjects and significantly correlated with structural connectivity (FDR-corrected p<0.05 for V1, V2, V3, V3B, V7, IPS1), yet even top-ranked models like V-JEPA 2 fail to reproduce these relational patterns, drastically reducing the number of candidate brain-aligned models when both pointwise alignment and APS criteria are required.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central finding is that pointwise alignment (RSA/LP) cannot discriminate among many models: V-JEPA 2, CLIP-RN50, VGGT-1B, ConvNeXt, and SimCLR-ResNet50 all achieve effectively equivalent RSA scores on most ROIs (within 95% bootstrap CI of the top model). Under the proposed APA criterion, however, these equivalently aligned models diverge sharply: V-JEPA 2, despite top pointwise scores, achieves low Alignment Pattern Similarity (APS) with most ROIs and is ruled out as brain-aligned for the majority of regions. In contrast, models like CLIP-RN50 and VGGT-1B, which have slightly lower pointwise RSA, better reproduce the characteristic cross-region alignment profile. Taskonomy models (26 variants) consistently show the lowest alignment scores across all ROIs (RSA typically 0.01–0.08), confirming that single-task training on indoor scenes yields poor brain alignment. Video models (MViT, TimeSformer, Swin) show intermediate performance, with MViT-small achieving the highest V1 RSA (0.21) but lower scores in higher visual areas. The fMRI-derived alignment patterns themselves are validated against structural connectivity from 1065 subjects, showing significant correlation (FDR-corrected) for early visual areas (V1, V2, V3, V3B) and dorsal regions (V7, IPS1), providing an independent anatomical grounding for the functional patterns.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the NeuroAI Turing Test (Feather et al., 2025), which proposed benchmarking models by comparing their alignment scores to brain-brain alignment scores, from a pointwise (per-ROI) criterion to a relational one: a model must reproduce the characteristic cross-region alignment *pattern* of a given ROI, not just match its alignment score to that single ROI. It sits within the broader model-brain alignment benchmarking literature (Brain-Score, Algonauts, Conwell et al. 2024, Tang et al. 2025) but explicitly departs from the assumption that differences in pointwise alignment scores reflect meaningful differences in brain-likeness, drawing on the contravariance principle (Cao & Yamins, 2024b) and the tool-vs-model distinction (Wichmann & Geirhos, 2023) to motivate a stricter relational criterion.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that top-ranked models (especially V-JEPA 2) achieve high pointwise alignment yet fail to reproduce the brain's cross-region relational structure challenges the field's default assumption that higher alignment scores indicate greater brain-likeness, and provides a concrete, quantifiable criterion (APA) for distinguishing predictive tools from computational models. Limitations include the small subject pool (n=10), a single video fMRI dataset, and the somewhat arbitrary bootstrap-based equivalence heuristic; whether APA generalizes across stimuli, modalities, and larger subject cohorts remains open. The paper also raises the important methodological question of whether normalized alignment scores (relative to brain-brain references) are meaningful in low-alignment regimes, where many configurations are 'poorly aligned' in different ways.
