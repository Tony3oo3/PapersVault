## Problem

Linear models offer inherently interpretable feature effects through their coefficients but cannot capture nonlinear interactions, while neural networks are expressive yet lack transparent, guaranteed-faithful explanations. Post-hoc explainers like SHAP and LIME approximate feature attributions but depend on hyperparameter choices and do not guarantee fidelity. The paper addresses the gap of building a model that simultaneously provides globally interpretable, population-level feature effects (as in linear regression) and per-instance nonlinear corrections with neural-network expressivity.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a standardized tabular feature vector (zero mean, unit variance per feature) and produces a scalar regression prediction (or class probability via a link function for the GLM extension). Critically, the output also includes the linear coefficient vector β, which by construction equals the marginal effects at the mean (MEMs), providing a direct population-level interpretation. Additionally, the per-instance nonlinear correction h_j(x) = 1 + g_{u_j}(x_{-j}) is available for local explanations. The standardization requirement is essential: it ensures that at the feature mean (zero), the model reduces exactly to the linear model, making MEM_j = β_j.

## Method

NIMO defines f(x) = β_0 + Σ_j x_j β_j (1 + g_{u_j}(x_{-j})), where each g_{u_j} is a neural network that receives all features except the j-th (masked out), with the constraint g(0)=0 enforced by subtracting the zero-input prediction. This masking ensures x_j enters the prediction only through the linear term β_j, preserving the classical coefficient interpretation while the network captures cross-feature nonlinear interactions. A single shared network with binary positional encoding of the feature index replaces d separate networks for efficiency. Training uses a parameter-elimination strategy: the linear coefficients β are eliminated via a closed-form adaptive ridge regression solution β̂(u) = (B_u^T B_u + λI)^{-1} B_u^T y, reducing the joint optimization to gradient descent over the network parameters u and scaling variables c alone. Sparsity is enforced through adaptive ridge regression (equivalent to Lasso at the optimum) on β and group ℓ₂ regularization on the first fully-connected layer weights, with noise injection to prevent deeper layers from compensating for first-layer sparsity. The framework extends to generalized linear models (e.g., logistic regression) by substituting the IRLS weighted least-squares surrogate in place of the plain ridge objective.

## Results

On synthetic regression benchmarks (200 training samples), NIMO achieves MSE of 0.024, 0.197, and 0.380 on settings with 5, 10, and 50 features respectively, outperforming all baselines by a wide margin (e.g., 0.380 vs. 1.738 for LassoNet and 13.122 for Lasso at 50 features). On real-world datasets, NIMO attains the best MSE on the superconductivity dataset (81 features, 21K samples) and performs comparably to other nonlinear methods on the diabetes and Boston housing datasets. For synthetic classification, NIMO reaches 92% accuracy (Setting 1) and 85% (Setting 2), surpassing LassoNet and the vanilla NN, though LassoNet edges out NIMO (90% vs. 84%) on the 50-feature Setting 3.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NIMO dominates all five baselines on the synthetic regression benchmarks, with the largest gaps over Lasso (35× lower MSE at 50 features) and NAM (43× lower), and a 4.6× advantage over the closest competitor LassoNet (0.380 vs. 1.738 at 50 features). Against the vanilla NN, NIMO is 35× better at 50 features (0.380 vs. 13.718), confirming that the linear-coefficient structure with parameter-elimination training prevents the overfitting that plagues unconstrained networks in low-data regimes. On real datasets, NIMO matches or slightly trails the best nonlinear methods on diabetes and Boston housing but achieves the lowest MSE on superconductivity, where its interpretability advantage is most pronounced. In classification, NIMO wins on Settings 1 and 2 (92% and 85% vs. LassoNet's 89% and 82%) but loses to LassoNet on the high-dimensional Setting 3 (84% vs. 90%), suggesting LassoNet's feature-sparsity mechanism is more effective in very high dimensions. A key differentiator is that NIMO's linear coefficients directly equal the marginal effects at the mean (MEM), providing population-level interpretability that none of the baselines offer without post-hoc approximation.

## Relation to Prior Work

NIMO sits at the intersection of linear regression (for global interpretability via coefficients) and neural networks (for nonlinear expressivity), building directly on the adaptive ridge regression equivalence to Lasso (Grandvalet, 1998) to enable sparsity through a closed-form parameter elimination. It departs from prior hybrid models in a specific way: NAMs and NODE-GAM capture per-feature nonlinearities but cannot model feature interactions; LassoNet enforces sparsity but does not preserve a stable, globally interpretable coefficient; IMNs generate per-instance coefficients via a hypernetwork, sacrificing population-level interpretability. NIMO's distinguishing design choice—masking the j-th feature from the correction network g_{u_j}(x_{-j}) and constraining g(0)=0—ensures that the marginal effects at the mean coincide exactly with the linear coefficients β, a property none of the compared hybrids guarantee.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

For practitioners in high-stakes domains (healthcare, finance) who need both predictive accuracy and a clear, auditable explanation of each feature's population-level effect, NIMO offers a principled middle ground between a pure linear model and a black-box neural network. The key open limitation is that the architectural constraint preventing self-interactions (x_j · f(x_j)) means NIMO cannot capture purely quadratic or self-modulating nonlinearities without explicit feature engineering, which limits its applicability to datasets where such terms dominate. Future work could explore relaxing the masking constraint in a controlled way, extending to multi-output or structured prediction settings, and scaling to very high-dimensional tabular problems where the shared-network-with-positional-encoding approach may face capacity bottlenecks.
