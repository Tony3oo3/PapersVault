## Problem

Existing LLM unlearning methods (gradient ascent, NPO, DPO) require ground-truth sequences to be unlearned in their optimization objective, which contradicts the privacy principle of minimizing exposure to sensitive data and introduces side effects such as distorted token probabilities out of context, information leakage in multiple-choice settings, and vulnerability to sampling and prefilling attacks. The paper asks whether the well-studied phenomenon of model collapse—where iterative retraining on self-generated data causes distributional information loss—can be repurposed as a principled mechanism for targeted unlearning without ever touching the private data.

## Input / Output

The method consumes only forget questions (no ground-truth answers), retain Q&A pairs, and n responses sampled from the model's own output distribution at each iteration. A bounded reward function (ROUGE-L dissimilarity between the sampled response and the model's original greedy output) guides a Bradley-Terry preference model to select one response per forget question for fine-tuning. The output is the updated LLM weights whose conditional output distribution has collapsed on forget queries while retaining utility on retain queries, evaluated via ROUGE-L scores on forget, paraphrased-forget, retain, world-facts, and real-authors sets.

## Method

Partial Model Collapse (PMC) iteratively fine-tunes the LLM on its own sampled responses to forget questions, selected via a Bradley-Terry preference model guided by a reward that incentivizes divergence from the model's original output. At each step, the loss combines a retain log-likelihood term (weighted by λ) with a forget log-likelihood term computed on the single preferred sample, so the model is never pushed away from a fixed ground-truth sequence but instead allowed to diverge naturally through repeated self-conditioned retraining. Theoretically, the paper proves that in the absence of approximation errors, the expected reward converges to its maximum and its variance vanishes for every forget query, meaning the output distribution collapses onto responses maximally dissimilar to the original. Empirically, this collapse manifests as hallucinations, gibberish, or generic refusal patterns, and the approach is more robust to sampling and prefilling attacks because the model's entire conditional distribution shifts rather than a single fixed sequence being suppressed.

## Results

PMC significantly dominates all baselines (GA, GD, DPO, NPO, SimNPO, IDK) in the utility–unlearning trade-off, expanding the Pareto-front across Phi-1.5, Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, and Gemma-3-12b-it (Figure 3). On general utility benchmarks, PMC-unlearned models show minimal degradation: Phi-1.5 drops from 0.4462 to 0.4283 on ARC-Challenge and from 0.4174 to 0.4063 on MMLU; Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct drops from 0.4368 to 0.4341 on ARC-Challenge and from 0.6041 to 0.5924 on MMLU. PMC also achieves substantially lower worst-case leakage under both sampling and prefilling attacks compared to all baselines (Figure 4), and avoids the token-probability distortion (mean shift of −0.12) observed with NPO on unrelated wikitext contexts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PMC outperforms all six baselines (GA, GD, DPO, NPO, SimNPO, IDK) on the utility–unlearning Pareto-front across all three models, achieving higher unlearn quality at equivalent or higher utility. Against NPO specifically, PMC avoids the critical side effect of distorting token probabilities in unrelated contexts: NPO shifts token probabilities by a mean of −0.12 on wikitext, while PMC's differences follow a zero-mean Gaussian with small variance. In multiple-choice leakage tests, NPO's correct answers become the least-likely option (high accuracy when selecting the least likely answer), whereas PMC shows no such pattern. Under sampling attacks (100 samples per question), PMC achieves substantially lower worst-case ROUGE-L leakage than all baselines; under prefilling attacks, the IDK baseline reveals substantial residual information while PMC remains robust. The paper notes that PMC's advantage stems from fine-tuning on the model's own newly sampled responses each iteration (triggering targeted collapse) rather than optimizing against fixed ground-truth sequences, which is the mechanism shared by GA, GD, NPO, SimNPO, and DPO.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the model collapse phenomenon—where iterative retraining on self-generated data causes progressive information loss (Shumailov et al., 2023; Ferbach et al., 2024)—as a targeted unlearning mechanism, inverting a well-known detriment into a feature. It builds directly on Ferbach et al.'s (2024) result that curated self-generated data via a Bradley-Terry preference model drives convergence to maximum reward, adapting this to the LLM unlearning setting. The key departure from existing unlearning methods (GA, GD, DPO, NPO, SimNPO, IDK) is that PMC never requires ground-truth forget sequences in its optimization objective, instead fine-tuning on the model's own sampled responses guided by a preference model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PMC addresses a practical gap: in real-world deployments, the exact data to unlearn may be unavailable, restricted, or non-unique, yet all prior methods require it. The identification of negative side effects in target-dependent methods—systematic token probability distortion out of context and exploitable information leakage in multiple-choice settings—provides important diagnostic tools for evaluating unlearning quality beyond standard benchmarks. Open questions remain: whether PMC-unlearned models are truly distributionally closer to a model never trained on the forget data, robustness to relearning and adversarial attacks, and how to design reward functions that produce coherent refusals rather than hallucinations or gibberish. The approach also extends beyond Q&A (demonstrated on MUSE-news), suggesting broader applicability to generative models.
