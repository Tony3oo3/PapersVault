## Problem

Public and scientific interest in LLM self-awareness is growing, but existing measurement approaches rely on model self-reports, which are unreliable because LLMs are trained on vast corpora of introspective text and can pattern-match convincing-sounding introspection without actually attending to internal states. There is no established behavioral methodology for quantitatively evaluating metacognitive abilities in LLMs that avoids this confound, analogous to the non-linguistic tests developed for nonhuman animals.

## Input / Output

The method consumes multiple-choice and short-answer questions from GPQA and SimpleQA (plus derived variants GPSA and SimpleMC), presented within game prompts that include a constructed teammate performance history (Delegate Game) or a 'your answer was incorrect' instruction (Second Chance Game). For models that expose token probabilities, the analysis also uses baseline log-probabilities and entropy over answer options as proxies for internal confidence. Outputs are partial correlation coefficients (controlling for surface features like domain, difficulty, question length, and a model-estimated 'objective difficulty' score) and normalized change-rate lifts, yielding a quantitative metacognitive ability estimate per model per dataset.

## Method

The paper introduces two behavioral game paradigms inspired by animal metacognition research. In the Delegate Game, the model is shown a question and must choose to answer it or delegate to a teammate whose prior performance is visible in the context window; success requires attending to an internal confidence signal rather than generating an explicit confidence rating, and the confederate teammate's different answering style discourages the model from relying on learned rhetorical patterns. In the Second Chance Game, the model is told its previous answer was wrong and must re-answer, testing whether it can anticipate its own output and modulate it. The key analytical contribution is a multi-partial correlation framework that controls for surface difficulty cues (dataset metadata, question length, non-alphabetic character ratio, and a model-estimated 'what percentage of college-educated people would know this' score) to isolate the introspective component, plus a set of alternative-strategy tests (random choice, implausible choice, random noise) using token probabilities to rule out non-introspective explanations for Second Chance Game performance. The approach is validated across 17 models from six providers, comparing thinking vs. non-thinking modes, and is further stress-tested with prompt variations, a decision-only game variant, and a comparison against simpler self-report and pass-game paradigms.

## Results

In the Delegate Game, the maximum partial correlation between baseline correctness and delegation decisions (controlling for surface cues) is 0.3 across 17 models and 4 question sets, while the more sensitive entropy-decision partial correlation reaches ~0.5 for the best model. Only Claude Sonnet 3.5 exceeded the max(self accuracy, teammate accuracy) threshold, raising team accuracy by 5.4 pp on GPQA and 8.6 pp on SimpleQA. In the Second Chance Game, GPT-4.1, GPT-4o, and GPT-4o Mini are the only models whose elevated change rates cannot be explained by random guessing, implausible-option selection, or added noise on both multiple-choice datasets. Token probability–correctness AUC values range from 0.5 to 0.75, indicating moderate-to-poor discriminability of the putative internal confidence signal.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an evaluation paper rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so the key contrasts are across models and across the two metacognitive paradigms. On the Delegate Game (confidence introspection), more recent and larger models show stronger partial correlations, with a significant negative linear slope across model release dates on three of four datasets; however, the effect is modest (max r ≈ 0.3 for correctness, ~0.5 for entropy) and highly variable, with few models showing significant introspection on all four question sets. On the Second Chance Game (self-modeling), the pattern of success dissociates from the Delegate Game: GPT-4.1, GPT-4o, and GPT-4o Mini uniquely pass all four alternative-strategy exclusion tests on both datasets, while models like Grok 3 and Qwen 3 pass on GPQA but fail the entropy-increase check on SimpleMC. OpenAI models show a consistent bias toward answering rather than delegating (only once across four models, two datasets, and two presentations did an OpenAI model not show significant answering bias), suggesting RLHF-induced personality traits suppress delegation even when introspective signals are present. The paper also contrasts its game-based paradigms against simpler self-report confidence prompts (Appendix A.10), finding that self-reported confidence ratings are more strongly driven by surface difficulty cues than are Delegate Game decisions, validating the game design as a more introspection-sensitive measure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes behavioral metacognition paradigms from nonhuman animal cognition research (opt-out tasks, prospective memory tests) into a novel game-based methodology for LLMs, deliberately avoiding the self-report and verbal-confidence approaches that dominate prior LLM introspection work (Kadavath et al., 2022; Tian et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023; Binder et al., 2024). It builds on the calibration literature by using token probabilities as a proxy for an internal confidence signal, but extends beyond calibration by testing whether models can *strategically deploy* that signal in decision-making (Delegate Game) and whether they can *anticipate and modulate* their own outputs (Second Chance Game). The paper also aligns with the 'objective introspection' framing of Binder et al. (2024) and Song et al. (2025b), which contrasts model-privileged information with third-party-accessible information, but operationalizes it through behavioral games rather than fine-tuning or hypothetical-response prompts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first quantitative, non-self-report behavioral scale for LLM metacognition, showing that frontier models exhibit only limited and context-dependent introspective abilities that are often outperformed by surface-level heuristics. The dissociation between confidence assessment and self-modeling across models, and the finding that RLHF post-training appears to shape these abilities, raises important questions for AI safety (self-aware models may be harder to predict) and for understanding what scale versus training regimen contributes to metacognition. Key open questions include whether interpretability analyses can identify the internal activations underlying the inferred confidence signal, whether these abilities will continue to improve with scale or require architectural changes, and how LLM metacognition compares to human metacognition on matched tasks.
