## Problem

Despite the rapid commercialization of deep research (DR) systems, no formal characterization of the DR task exists, making it difficult to distinguish DR from other reasoning-intensive search problems or to measure progress objectively. Existing benchmarks either rely on subjective LLM-judge scoring of full reports, use static document corpora that prevent fair comparison between proprietary and open systems, or lack mechanisms for periodic updates as the web evolves.

## Input / Output

The system takes a natural-language DR query and has access to the entire open web as its retrieval corpus. Rather than evaluating a free-form report, the paper defines the DR output as an intermediate structured representation: a list of claims, where each claim is a dictionary whose keys are subclaims (e.g., a material name is the claim and the source paper title is the subclaim). This hierarchical claim structure is the unit of evaluation, with modified precision and recall metrics that assign zero credit to a claim if any of its subclaims are incorrect, thereby separating the reasoning challenge from surface-level report generation.

## Method

The paper contributes a formal definition of DR (Definition 1) requiring both high search intensity (processing ≥20 information units via ≥10 queries) and high reasoning intensity (non-trivial reasoning in at least one of: finding, processing, or combining information units), with a practical threshold of >10 minutes for a human expert. To construct benchmark queries at scale, the authors introduce a problem inversion technique: they take long-context reasoning problems (e.g., from the CURIE scientific QA dataset, ICLR 2025 dataset papers, official flight investigation reports, and award databases) and invert them so the model must identify the source entity or document from its characteristic properties, making answers hard to find but easy to verify. The resulting LiveDRBench contains 100 tasks across eight categories (SCIFACTS Materials, SCIFACTS Geo, NOVELDS Identification, NOVELDS Identification and Extraction, NOVELDS Peer Retrieval, PRIORART, FLIGHTS, ENTITIES) spanning scientific, innovation, and public-interest domains. Evaluation uses GPT-4o as a judge to compute agreement scores per claim and subclaim, then aggregates via the modified precision/recall formula in Equation 1, with a strict variant (Appendix B) that takes the minimum over subclaims. The benchmark is designed for easy periodic updates by re-applying the inversion pipeline to newly published papers or events.

## Results

On LiveDRBench (100 tasks across 8 categories), OpenAI Deep Research achieves the highest overall F1 of 0.555 (Precision 0.633, Recall 0.523), followed by Perplexity DR (F1 0.355), Gemini DR 2.5 Pro (F1 0.263), and the open-source DeepResearcher+DS Qwen 32B (F1 0.075). Per-category F1 scores range from 0.00 (DeepResearcher on SCIFACTS Materials) to 0.72 (OpenAI DR on SCIFACTS Geo). Trace analysis reveals that DR systems cover only about half of the necessary search queries, with proprietary models issuing 24–64 queries on average versus 5–6 for DeepResearcher.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OpenAI DR dominates across nearly all categories, achieving F1 scores of 0.54–0.72, and outperforms Perplexity DR (F1 0.15–0.63) and Gemini DR (F1 0.02–0.40) by wide margins on most tasks. The open-source DeepResearcher+DS Qwen 32B trails all proprietary systems substantially (average F1 0.075), scoring 0.00 on SCIFACTS Materials and Geo, though it surprisingly outperforms Gemini DR on PRIORART (F1 0.199 vs. 0.082). Among non-DR baselines evaluated on NOVELDS Identification and Extraction, reasoning models (o4-mini F1 0.168, Gemini 2.5 Pro F1 0.142) significantly outperform non-reasoning models (GPT-4.1 F1 0.088, Sonar Pro F1 0.023), yet all remain far below the dedicated DR systems. Perplexity DR and Gemini DR are closely competitive on NOVELDS Identification (F1 0.633 vs. 0.400) but diverge sharply on PRIORART, where Perplexity (F1 0.419) far exceeds Gemini (F1 0.082). Overall, the gap between proprietary and open-source DR systems is stark, with proprietary models issuing 4–12× more search queries and achieving 2–7× higher F1 scores.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper templatizes the problem-inversion technique from BrowseComp (Wei et al., 2025a) for scalable construction of deep research queries, extending it from single-entity needle-in-a-haystack questions to multi-claim information synthesis tasks. Conceptually, it positions DR as an extreme generalization of multi-hop RAG, formalizing the distinction through two axes—search intensity (number of information units) and reasoning intensity (non-trivial finding/processing/combining)—that prior benchmarks like HotpotQA, CURIE, and ELI5 each capture only partially. For evaluation, it departs from the dominant LLM-judged report-quality paradigm (DeepResearch Bench, ResearcherBench) by introducing an intermediate claim-based representation with recursive subclaims, drawing on claim verification literature to enable objective precision/recall scoring.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LiveDRBench provides the first publicly available, objectively scored benchmark for DR that uses the open web as corpus, enabling direct comparison of proprietary and open-source systems—a gap that static-corpus benchmarks cannot fill. The finding that even the best system (OpenAI DR, F1=0.55) covers only ~50% of necessary search queries, and that open-source DeepResearcher trails proprietary models by an order of magnitude (F1 0.075 vs 0.55), highlights substantial headroom in search breadth and reasoning depth. Open questions include whether interleaving programmatic control with model-driven search can close the coverage gap, how to improve grounding accuracy (models often find correct answers but cite wrong sources), and whether the 10-minute/20-information-unit thresholds in the formal definition generalize across domains.
