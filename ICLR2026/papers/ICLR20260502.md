## Problem

Batch-ramp schedules that gradually increase batch size during LLM pretraining are widely used in practice (LLaMA, Nemotron, OLMo) to reduce wall-clock time, yet they are tuned heuristically without theoretical justification. While for SGD doubling the batch size is equivalent to halving the learning rate, the analogous relationship for adaptive optimizers like Adam is unclear, leaving open whether existing heuristics are near-optimal and what the principled trade-off between learning-rate decay and batch-size ramp-up should be.

## Input / Output

Seesaw takes as input an existing learning-rate scheduler (e.g., cosine decay), the initial learning rate η₀, initial batch size B₀, a step decay factor α > 1, and the token positions S where the input scheduler would cut η by α. It outputs a modified (η, B) schedule where at each cut point the learning rate is reduced by √α and the batch size is multiplied by α, preserving the product α√β constant. The final output is a trained LLM whose loss dynamics match the original scheduler at equal FLOPs but with approximately 36% fewer serial steps, approaching the theoretical limit of 1 − 2/π ≈ 36.3%.

## Method

The core insight is a first-order equivalence: for SGD, taking one step at (η, 2B) is equivalent to two steps at (η/2, B) in both the deterministic gradient and the noise covariance. For adaptive optimizers, the authors derive normalized SGD (NSGD) as a tractable proxy for Adam by setting β₁=β₂=0 and replacing the second-moment denominator with the true expected gradient norm. Under the variance-dominated regime (E‖g‖² ≈ σ²/B), NSGD reduces to SGD with a rescaled learning rate η̃ = η√B/(σ√Tr(H)), yielding the equivalence condition α√β = const for any pair of (LR-cut, batch-ramp) schedules. The most aggressive stable choice is α = √β (Lemma 4 shows α < √β causes divergence), giving the practical rule: halve the LR and double the batch at each cut point. The paper provides the first finite-sample proof of LR-decay/batch-ramp equivalence for SGD on noisy linear regression (Theorem 1) and extends it to NSGD (Corollary 1), then empirically validates the scheduler on 150M/300M/600M Chinchilla-scale models trained on C4, showing Seesaw matches cosine decay in loss while cutting serial runtime by ~36%.

## Results

On 150M/300M/600M-parameter models trained at Chinchilla scale (D=20N) on C4, Seesaw matches cosine annealing in final validation loss across all batch sizes (e.g., 150M at B=256: 3.0346 vs. 3.0353; 300M at B=512: 2.8700 vs. 2.8696; 600M at B=1024: 2.7132 vs. 2.7128) while reducing serial wall-clock time by approximately 36%, approaching the theoretical upper bound of (1−2/π)≈36.3% derived in Lemma 1. The speedup is achieved by doubling the batch size at each point where the cosine scheduler would halve the learning rate, while only multiplying the learning rate by 1/√2 instead of 1/2.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is standard cosine annealing at a fixed batch size, which Seesaw matches in final validation loss to within ~0.001–0.01 across all model sizes and batch sizes at or below the critical batch size (CBS). The key advantage is a ~36% reduction in serial wall-clock time at equal FLOPs, since Seesaw processes the same total tokens in fewer sequential steps by ramping up the batch size. The paper also compares against naive scheduling alternatives in Figure 4: keeping the learning rate fixed while doubling (blue) or quadrupling (orange) the batch size both severely underperform cosine decay, confirming that the 1/√2 learning rate reduction is essential. The most aggressive theoretically permitted schedule (α=√β, i.e., α=√2, β=2) is the boundary beyond which training diverges (Lemma 4), and empirically more aggressive choices (e.g., α=1, β=4) indeed underperform as predicted. Beyond the CBS, Seesaw degrades relative to cosine decay (Figure 3), consistent with the failure of the variance-dominated assumption (Assumption 3) at very large batch sizes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides the first finite-sample proof of equivalence between learning-rate decay and batch-size ramp-up for SGD on noisy linear regression, extending the well-known linear scaling rule (Smith et al., 2017) from an empirical observation to a rigorous guarantee. It builds on the normalized SGD (NSGD) framework from Jelassi et al. (2022) as a tractable proxy for Adam, and on the eigenbasis rotation technique from Meterez et al. (2025) for the proof. The key departure from prior work is the identification of the constraint α ≥ √β (Lemma 4) that bounds how aggressively one can ramp batch size without divergence, and the extension of the equivalence from SGD to NSGD under a variance-dominated regime, yielding the practical Seesaw scheduler as a drop-in replacement for cosine decay.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

A ~36% wall-clock reduction in LLM pretraining is practically significant for multi-month training runs, and the fact that it approaches the theoretical upper bound of (1−2/π) ≈ 36.3% suggests the scheduler is near-optimal within its framework. The negative result—that beyond the critical batch size, no batch-size ramp can replicate learning-rate decay for Adam/NSGD—clarifies a fundamental limitation of the approach and explains why the speedup is bounded. Open questions include whether the variance-dominated assumption (Assumption 3) holds at frontier model scales, whether the z-loss instability observed at 600M can be resolved, and whether the equivalence extends to non-quadratic loss landscapes or more complex adaptive optimizers beyond the NSGD proxy.
