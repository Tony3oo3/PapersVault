## Problem

Hierarchical Federated Learning (HFL) introduces intermediate aggregation layers to address scalability limits of single-tier FL, but no unified convergence analysis exists that compares all four two-tier topologies (Star-Star, Star-Ring, Ring-Star, Ring-Ring) under consistent nonconvex assumptions with intra/inter-group data heterogeneity. Prior work analyzed individual topologies under heterogeneous assumptions (e.g., bounded gradients for ring topologies), preventing direct comparison and leaving practitioners without theoretically grounded guidance for topology selection in real deployments.

## Input / Output

The framework takes as input a hierarchical client configuration (G groups of M clients each), a topology choice (star or ring at each tier), local non-IID data partitions (Dirichlet with α=0.1 or 0.3), and a model architecture (ResNet-18, VGG-9, ResNet-10, or MLP). It produces topology-specific convergence rate bounds expressed in terms of an effective learning rate η̃ that absorbs G, P, M, and K, along with explicit error terms for stochastic variance (σ), inter-group heterogeneity (δ), and intra-group heterogeneity (ζ). The practical output is a set of three design principles: top-tier topology dominance, inter-group heterogeneity as the primary bottleneck, and group-structure-dependent optimal topology selection.

## Method

The paper establishes a unified per-round recursion (Lemma 1) with a topology-dependent coefficient c₀ ∈ {1/(GM), 1/G, 1/M, 1} that unifies all four topologies into a single analytical template. Client drift E_r is then bounded differently for each topology by decomposing the model displacement into local SGD noise, intra-group drift, inter-group drift, and global discrepancy terms, yielding distinct error structures: ring-based top-tier topologies (Ring-Star, Ring-Ring) gain additional factors of G (or S₁ under partial participation) in the denominators of variance and heterogeneity terms. The resulting convergence bounds achieve O(1/√(GPMKR)) for all topologies, with topology-dependent higher-order terms that reveal ring-based top-tier aggregation is inherently more robust to both stochastic noise and data heterogeneity. The analysis extends to partial client participation (Theorem 2) and random-shuffle execution orders, and is validated on four datasets with four heterogeneity scenarios, confirming that inter-group divergence dominates convergence and that Ring-Star suits many small groups while Star-Ring suits few large clusters.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with ResNet-18, Ring-Ring achieves the highest test accuracy across all heterogeneity settings (91.53% IID-IID, 91.10% IID-Non-IID, 90.94% Non-IID-IID, 90.33% Non-IID-Non-IID), while Star-Star consistently performs worst (88.48%, 87.01%, 87.03%, 86.78% respectively). The gap between ring-based top-tier topologies and Star-Star widens under inter-group heterogeneity: on CINIC-10, inter-group Non-IID causes a 6.06% accuracy drop for Star-Ring (78.59%→72.53%) versus only 2.50% for intra-group Non-IID. On SST-2 with MLP, Ring-Ring reaches 81.65% under the most heterogeneous setting (Non-IID-Non-IID), outperforming Star-Star by 13.53 percentage points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its four HFL topologies against two single-tier FL baselines: PFL (FedAvg, Koloskova et al. 2020) and SFL (Sequential FL, Li & Lyu 2023). In the theoretical comparison (Table 1), Star-Star HFL with P=1, M=1 reduces to FedAvg and matches PFL's convergence rate of O(1/√(MKR) + 1/R^{2/3}), while Ring-Ring HFL's rate aligns with SFL's O(1/√(MKR) + 1/R^{2/3}). Empirically (Appendix B.2, Figure 5), Star-Star HFL (G=10, M=10) achieves convergence speed and final accuracy highly similar to PFL, and Ring-Ring HFL matches SFL, confirming that the hierarchical structure itself does not accelerate convergence. The paper's primary comparison is thus internal: among the four HFL topologies, ring-based top-tier configurations (Ring-Star, Ring-Ring) consistently outperform star-based alternatives by 1–14 percentage points depending on dataset and heterogeneity level, with the largest gaps appearing under inter-group Non-IID conditions (e.g., 13.53% on SST-2, 3.70% on CIFAR-10 with ResNet-18). The key practical takeaway is that HFL's value lies in scalability (mitigating central-server communication bottlenecks) rather than convergence speed improvement over well-tuned single-tier FL.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the two-tier hierarchical federated learning framework introduced by Liu et al. (2020) and the sequential FL convergence analysis of Li & Lyu (2023) to provide the first unified convergence analysis covering all four HFL topologies (Star-Star, Star-Ring, Ring-Star, Ring-Ring) under consistent non-convex assumptions. Prior work analyzed individual topologies in isolation—Wang et al. (2022) for Star-Star, Chen et al. (2020) and Lee et al. (2020) for Star-Ring, and Chaoyang et al. (2020) for Ring-Star—often under heterogeneous assumptions (e.g., bounded gradients) that prevented direct comparison. The key departure is the unified framework with a topology-dependent effective learning rate that enables apples-to-apples comparison and reveals how top-tier vs. lower-tier topology choices differentially affect convergence.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretically grounded decision framework for HFL topology selection, which is critical as HFL moves toward real-world deployment in edge and IoT settings. The finding that inter-group heterogeneity dominates convergence (not intra-group) reshapes client clustering strategies, and the topology-structure compatibility principle (Ring-Star for many small groups, Star-Ring for few large clusters) gives practitioners actionable design rules. Limitations include the restriction to two-tier architectures, fixed execution orders under full participation, and the assumption of homogeneous group sizes; extending to multi-tier HFL, dynamic topology switching, and heterogeneous group sizes remain open. The wall-clock time analysis in the appendix also highlights that theoretical convergence optimality may not translate to practical speed under bandwidth constraints, an area requiring further study.
