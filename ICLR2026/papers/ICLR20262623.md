## Problem

The dominant LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm relies on prompting large proprietary models to generate evaluation text, making it computationally expensive, opaque, and highly sensitive to prompt engineering. A natural question is whether smaller open-source models, which produce poor evaluation text when prompted directly, nonetheless encode evaluation-relevant signals in their internal representations that can be extracted without generation. This paper formalizes the Semantic Capacity Asymmetry Hypothesis—that accurate evaluation requires significantly less semantic capacity than generation—and tests whether probing small model hidden states can approximate full LLM judge fidelity.

## Input / Output

The method takes a (task instruction, model-generated response) pair and constructs aspect-specific evaluation prompts for five dimensions (Semantic Consistency, Logicality, Informativeness, Fluency, Factuality). The small LM processes these prompts in evaluation mode (no autoregressive decoding), and the probing classifier outputs either a 5-class score (1–5) or a binary high/low quality label (threshold τ=4). Because the small LM is frozen and only a lightweight linear classifier is trained, inference reduces to a single forward pass plus a dot product, eliminating the cost of token-by-token generation entirely.

## Method

INSPECTOR is a three-stage probing pipeline: (1) a large LLM (DeepSeek-V3) annotates responses with 1–5 scores across five evaluation aspects to form gold labels; (2) a frozen small LM (0.6B–8B) processes the same evaluation prompts in evaluation mode, and per-layer hidden states are pooled via mean/last/min/max/concat, augmented with attention-entropy statistics and PCA-projected embeddings; (3) lightweight classifiers (logistic regression, linear SVM, random forest, small MLP) are trained via stratified cross-validation to predict the gold scores from these features, with layer-pool-feature configurations ranked and selected by predictive performance. The key mechanism is that evaluative signals are linearly recoverable from mid-to-upper layer representations even when the model's generated text is incoherent, so the probe extracts latent semantic structure that surface decoding destroys. Binary classification (high vs. low quality) reaches 80–90% F1, enabling the probing classifier to serve as an efficient reference-free data filter for supervised fine-tuning, matching the quality of filtering by the full LLM judge at a fraction of the cost.

## Results

On reasoning benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH, GPQA), INSPECTOR probing classifiers achieve an average weighted F1 of ~52.6% for multiclass (1–5) score prediction and ~79.5% for binary high/low-quality classification using Qwen3-1.7B, substantially outperforming prompt-based inference on the same model (~16.3% multiclass, ~51.9% binary). The binary classifier reaches 80–96% F1 on several aspect-dataset combinations (e.g., 96.11% on GPQA Fluency), making it reliable as a reference-free data filter. Even the smallest model (Qwen3-0.6B) achieves ~74.9% binary F1 via probing, compared to only ~51.7% from direct prompting. On the open-ended AlpacaEval 2.0 benchmark, the same pattern holds, with Qwen3-1.7B probing reaching 91.06% binary F1 on Fluency.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The only external baseline is RoBERTa, which achieves only 12.2% average multiclass F1 and 45.1% binary F1—essentially at chance level for multiclass and barely above random for binary—confirming that a generic encoder without task-specific probing cannot recover evaluative signals from the same inputs. In contrast, INSPECTOR probing on Qwen3-1.7B reaches 52.6% multiclass and 79.5% binary F1, a 40-point improvement over RoBERTa on binary classification. The paper also compares against prompt-based inference on the same small LMs (Qwen3-0.6B/1.7B, Llama-3.2-1B, Llama-3.1-8B), where probing consistently yields 20–35 percentage-point F1 gains over prompting across all models and aspects. Notably, larger models do not uniformly dominate: Qwen3-1.7B probing outperforms Llama-3.1-8B probing on several binary tasks (e.g., 96.11% vs. 88.33% on GPQA Fluency), and within the same family, Qwen3-0.6B beats Qwen3-1.7B on MATH Logicality prompt inference (18.18% vs. 15.06%), indicating that model size is not a reliable proxy for evaluation quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate research threads: the LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm (Zheng et al., 2023; Prasad et al., 2023; He et al., 2023) and the probing methodology for analyzing internal representations (Shi et al., 2016; Starace et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025). It departs from prior LLM-as-a-Judge work by replacing autoregressive text generation with linear probing of frozen small-LM hidden states, and from prior probing work by targeting evaluation-relevant signals rather than linguistic or world-knowledge features. The evaluation rubrics are inherited from ROSCOE and SOCREVAL, while the probing pipeline (PCA-reduced pooled embeddings + attention-entropy statistics + logistic regression) follows the standard linear-probe recipe, making the contribution primarily one of repurposing an established diagnostic tool for a new application.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical value lies in offering a decoding-free, orders-of-magnitude cheaper surrogate for expensive LLM judges, with binary-classification F1 reaching 80–90%—sufficient for coarse data filtering in SFT pipelines. The Semantic Capacity Asymmetry Hypothesis provides a useful conceptual lens: if evaluation truly requires less capacity than generation, then the field may be over-provisioning compute for quality assessment. Key open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond math/science reasoning to open-ended generation, whether reliance on a single judge model (DeepSeek-V3) introduces systematic bias, and whether the modest multiclass performance (~50–60%) can be improved without scaling up the probed model.
