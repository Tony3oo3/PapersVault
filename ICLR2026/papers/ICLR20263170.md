## Problem

Prior subgame-solving techniques for imperfect-information games require reasoning about or enumerating common-knowledge sets, which in Fog of War chess can reach sizes of 10^18—far too large to hold in memory or compute in real time. This made search-based superhuman play in FoW chess, the largest turn-based zero-sum game with imperfect information, infeasible. The paper develops general-purpose search techniques that avoid common-knowledge reasoning entirely, enabling scalable subgame solving in such games.

## Input / Output

At each turn, Obscuro receives the full observation sequence (all legal moves seen so far), the game tree and approximate equilibrium strategy profile carried over from the previous move, and the complete set P of positions consistent with observations (typically |P| ≤ 10^6). It samples a small subset I (≤256 positions) from P, constructs a subgame, and iteratively solves and expands it within a time budget. The output is a purified mixed strategy over at most 3 legal actions (deterministic when using Resolve), plus the updated game tree and strategy profile to serve as the blueprint for the next turn. Node evaluations use Stockfish 14 at depth 1 in MultiPV mode, clamped to [-1, +1].

## Method

Obscuro's core innovation is KLUSS (Knowledge-Limited Unfrozen Subgame Solving), which constructs a subgame by removing nodes beyond order-2 knowledge distance from the current infoset but—unlike prior KLSS—unfreezes opponent strategies at distance 1, allowing them to be re-optimized jointly with the rest of the subgame. Equilibrium computation uses PCFR+ (predictive CFR+) on the growing tree, with the last iterate (not the average) used for play, justified by measured Nash gaps of roughly half a pawn. Tree expansion uses a novel one-sided GT-CFR variant where only one player (alternating each iteration) employs a PUCT-based exploration strategy while the other plays its current CFR strategy directly, provably converging to equilibrium while expanding fewer nodes. The Resolve subgame root distribution is made non-uniform (a 50/50 mixture of uniform and blueprint-strategy-weighted), and the final strategy is purified to at most 3 stable actions before sampling.

## Results

Obscuro achieves 85.1% (+834=33−133) against the previous state-of-the-art FoW chess AI (ZS21) over 1,000 games. Against human players rated 1450–2006, Obscuro scores 97% (+97=0−3) over 100 games. Against the world #1-ranked FoW chess player (rated 2318), Obscuro wins 80% (+16=0−4, +241 Elo) over 20 games with statistical significance (p = 0.0118, exact binomial test), establishing superhuman performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole formal baseline is ZS21 (Zhang & Sandholm, NeurIPS 2021), the previous state-of-the-art AI for Fog of War chess. Obscuro decisively outperforms ZS21, scoring 85.1% over 1,000 games (a +834 Elo-equivalent margin). Ablation experiments further isolate the contribution of each new technique: removing purification drops the score to 70.2%, removing KLUSS drops it to 58.0%, and removing one-sided GT-CFR drops it to 53.3% (all vs. ZS21). Even a simplified evaluation function (material + visibility only) combined with Obscuro's search still beats ZS21 at 55.0%, confirming that the search algorithm improvements are the primary driver of the performance gap. Against human players, Obscuro's 80% win rate over the world #1 player (with the human given overnight analysis between game sets) provides the strongest evidence of superhuman performance in this domain.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the subgame-solving framework for imperfect-information games (Resolve, Maxmargin, Reach subgame solving) by removing the dependence on common-knowledge sets, which are infeasible in games like FoW chess where they can reach size 10^18. It builds directly on Zhang & Sandholm's (2021) knowledge-limited subgame solving (KLSS) for FoW chess, introducing KLUSS (unfrozen variant) and a one-sided variant of GT-CFR (Schmid et al., 2023) for more focused tree expansion. The equilibrium computation uses PCFR+ (Farina et al., 2021) with PUCT-inspired exploration (Silver et al., 2016), and the evaluation function is borrowed from Stockfish 14, making the core contribution the search architecture rather than learned components.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Obscuro establishes superhuman performance in the largest turn-based zero-sum game with imperfect information, and does so on consumer hardware (6-core i5) rather than large compute clusters, demonstrating that search alone suffices for this class of problems. The KLUSS and one-sided GT-CFR techniques are general-purpose and address a fundamental scalability bottleneck (common-knowledge enumeration) that has limited imperfect-information search to poker-sized games. Open questions include whether these techniques extend to games with larger infosets or where a perfect-information evaluation function is unavailable, and whether combining them with deep RL for learned evaluation or continuation strategies would further improve performance in more complex settings.
