## Problem

While Transformers have been shown to predict vanilla linear congruential generators (LCGs), it remains unclear whether they can uncover the hidden recurrence in Permuted Congruential Generators (PCGs), which apply additional bit-wise shifts, XORs, rotations, and truncations that make them substantially harder to crack. This paper asks whether Transformers can perform in-context prediction on diverse PCG variants without explicit knowledge of the generator parameters, how performance scales with modulus, and what internal representations the model develops.

## Input / Output

The model receives a sequence of L integer tokens (each in [0, 2^k − 1] for k-bit outputs) generated by a PCG and autoregressively predicts the next token at each position. For truncated LCG experiments, integers are split into base-256 digits to reduce vocabulary size, doubling the effective context length. Context length must scale with modulus (L > ½√m for m ≥ 2^16), and test sequences use unseen (a, c) parameter pairs to probe generalization beyond memorization.

## Method

A GPT-style decoder-only Transformer (4 layers, 8 heads, d_model=1024, up to 52M parameters) with Rotary Positional Embeddings is trained with cross-entropy loss to predict the next PRNG output token. The key training innovation is a curriculum strategy: for large moduli (m ≥ 2^20), the model is initialized from a smaller-modulus checkpoint and mixes in 1% of smaller-modulus sequences with exponential decay to zero over training, which eliminates a prolonged loss-stagnation phase and broadens the stable learning-rate range. The paper establishes a scaling law showing the in-context sequence length needed for 90% accuracy grows as ½√m, and uses PCA on the embedding matrix to reveal that tokens spontaneously cluster by rotation-invariant zero-run patterns in their binary representations, a structure that persists across moduli and explains the effectiveness of pretrained initialization.

## Results

Transformers achieve >90% in-context prediction accuracy on PCG sequences after observing ½√m elements, with the required context scaling as √m (steeper than the m^0.25 law for LCGs). At m=2^16, a combined-variant model reaches >90% accuracy at the 512th token, while separate-variant models reach near-100% at just 128 tokens. Even under extreme truncation to a single output bit (k=1), the model attains 95% accuracy at the 256th element, far above the 50% random-guessing baseline. At the largest scale tested (m=2^22, ~5.4B tokens, 52M parameters), the model achieves 92.57% accuracy at position 1279, and curriculum learning with pretrained initialization is essential for m≥2^20.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against other ML methods but compares against three reference points. First, classical PCG cracking (Bouillaguet et al., 2020) requires prior knowledge of both the modulus m and multiplier a, and takes up to 20,000 CPU hours for XSLRR-128/64; the Transformer approach requires neither parameter and generalizes to unseen (a,c) pairs from in-context examples alone. Second, the Transformer inference compute scales as ~m^0.53 (for L≤d_model), compared to a brute-force state-space search baseline of ~m^2.5, yielding orders-of-magnitude speedup at large moduli. Third, the √m context scaling for PCGs is steeper than the m^0.25 scaling observed for vanilla LCGs (Tao et al., 2025), reflecting the additional information obscuration from XOR, shift, and rotation permutations. Against the random-guessing baseline of 1/√m, the model consistently exceeds it by a wide margin even under severe output truncation (e.g., 95% vs. 50% for k=1 bit).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Tao et al. (2025), which demonstrated that Transformers can in-context predict linear congruential generator (LCG) sequences, to the substantially harder family of Permuted Congruential Generators (PCGs) that apply bitwise shifts, XORs, rotations, and truncations to the hidden state. It also positions itself against classical cryptographic attacks on PCGs (Bouillaguet et al., 2020), which require prior knowledge of the modulus and multiplier, whereas here the model must discover all structure from training data alone. The curriculum learning and pretrained initialization strategies connect to a broader literature on staged training for algorithmic tasks (Garg et al., 2023; Saxena et al., 2024), while the interpretability analysis builds on modular-arithmetic grokking work (Power et al., 2022; Gromov, 2023; Nanda et al., 2023) by identifying a novel rotation-invariant clustering structure in embedding space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PCGs are the default random number generator in NumPy, so demonstrating that Transformers can predict their outputs from data alone—generalizing to unseen parameters and even from single-bit truncations—raises concrete questions about the security assumptions underlying widely deployed non-cryptographic PRNGs. The clean √m scaling law for required context length and the demonstrated necessity of curriculum learning at m ≥ 2^20 provide practical guidance for both ML-based cryptanalysis and for understanding the limits of Transformer generalization on structured sequences. Key open questions include whether these methods scale to the 128-bit states used in practice, whether they transfer to cryptographically secure generators, and what the full mechanistic account of the learned prediction strategy is. The spontaneous emergence of rotation-invariant zero-run clusters in the embedding layer is a novel interpretability result that may generalize to other tasks with hidden algebraic symmetries.
