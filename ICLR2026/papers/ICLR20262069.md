## Problem

Existing Nash learning from human feedback (NLHF) methods are fundamentally restricted to two-player interactions, where a single policy competes against one opponent. This single-opponent bias fails to capture the multi-source, heterogeneous, and sometimes conflicting preference signals that arise from multiple annotators, distinct reward models, and sequences of historical model checkpoints, leading to oscillatory behavior, narrow exploration, and brittle alignment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262069/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes text prompts and binary preference signals from one or more reward models (e.g., ArmoRM-Llama3-8B, Skywork-Reward-V2, Athene-RM-8B) as input, operating over a pool of k responses per prompt under a Plackett-Luce one-vs-many comparison scheme. It produces an aligned LLM policy (Gemma-2-9B-it) that simultaneously outperforms a population of opponent policies while remaining KL-regularized toward a reference model. The opponent set is constructed from weighted mixtures of historical policy checkpoints (TD-MNPO) or from distinct reward-model-conditioned policies (HT-MNPO), with the number of players n typically set to 3.

## Method

MNPO formulates alignment as an n-player constant-sum game where each policy maximizes its average preference probability against all other players subject to a KL penalty toward a reference model, using a Plackett-Luce generalization of Bradley-Terry for one-vs-many comparisons. The key update rule (Eq. 10) is a multiplicative-weights mirror descent step that weights each response by the geometric mean of opponent probabilities times an exponential of its average win-rate advantage, yielding O(1/√T) regret convergence to Nash equilibrium. To avoid the intractable partition function, the practical loss (Eq. 15/17) operates on log-ratio differences between response pairs, which is shown to be equivalent to the ideal update up to a constant. TD-MNPO constructs opponents as weighted mixtures of recent historical policies, while HT-MNPO extends to heterogeneous preference oracles where each player optimizes under its own reward model, recovering DPO, SimPO, INPO, SPIN, and others as special cases by varying n, opponent selection, distance metric, and target reward gap.

## Results

On the primary instruction-following benchmarks (evaluated with GPT-5-mini as judge), TD-MNPO achieves 57.27 on AlpacaEval 2.0 (LC win rate), 52.26 on Arena-Hard (win rate), and 7.03 on MT-Bench, outperforming all baselines. The heterogeneous variant HT-MNPO(Athene-RM-8B) further improves AlpacaEval to 59.64 and MT-Bench to 7.07. On academic benchmarks, TD-MNPO attains the highest average score (71.08) across IFEval, GPQA, MMLU, ARC, HellaSwag, TruthfulQA, and Winogrande, and is the only method to score non-zero on AIME-24 (3.33).

## Baselines & Comparisons

TD-MNPO outperforms all four baselines on every benchmark. Against DPO, it gains +2.92 on AlpacaEval 2.0, +6.63 on Arena-Hard, and +0.16 on MT-Bench. Against SimPO, the margins are +2.11, +7.22, and +0.16 respectively. Against SPPO, TD-MNPO leads by +1.30 on AlpacaEval, +8.37 on Arena-Hard, and +0.17 on MT-Bench. The closest competitor is INPO (the strongest two-player NLHF baseline), where TD-MNPO still wins by +1.18 on AlpacaEval, +4.23 on Arena-Hard, and +0.08 on MT-Bench. The largest relative gains appear on Arena-Hard, suggesting the multiplayer formulation is most beneficial for challenging, multi-criteria evaluation. On academic benchmarks (Table 3), TD-MNPO also leads on GPQA (33.33 vs. 29.29 for DPO) and maintains stable performance where SimPO degrades sharply on TruthfulQA (63.40 vs. 70.26 for TD-MNPO).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Nash learning from human feedback (NLHF) paradigm (Munos et al., 2023) from two-player to n-player games, drawing on multiplicative weights update (Freund & Schapire, 1999) for convergence guarantees and the Plackett-Luce model to generalize Bradley-Terry pairwise comparisons to one-vs-many settings. The time-dependent opponent mechanism directly builds on INPO (Zhang et al., 2025b) and SPIN (Chen et al., 2024), while the reward-aware loss connects to RPO (Sun et al., 2025). A key conceptual contribution is showing that DPO, SimPO, SPPO, INPO, and several other methods emerge as special cases of the unified TD-MNPO loss by varying the number of players, opponent selection, distance metric, and target reward gap.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The multiplayer formulation offers a principled mean-field view that reduces gradient variance and stabilizes optimization relative to single-opponent NLHF methods, with the unification table providing a useful taxonomy for the rapidly growing preference-optimization literature. However, formal convergence guarantees apply only to the homogeneous (shared-oracle) case; the heterogeneous extension that handles multiple reward models lacks theoretical grounding and relies on empirical validation. Practical limitations include dependence on reward-model quality as a performance ceiling, diminishing returns as the policy approaches high quality, and evaluation restricted to a single 9B base model over only 3 training iterations, leaving open questions about scalability to larger models and longer training horizons.
