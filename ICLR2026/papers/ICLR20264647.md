## Problem

While multimodal foundation models excel at passive spatial reasoning over fixed observations, their ability to actively and self-directedly explore a partially observable environment to construct, maintain, and revise a coherent spatial belief remains entirely unstudied. Existing benchmarks are either passive (reasoning over pre-collected observations) or task-driven (completing a specific goal like 'find the red chair'), conflating exploration efficiency with spatial reasoning and never directly probing the quality of the agent's internal spatial model.

## Input / Output

The agent receives egocentric observations within a 90° FOV — either symbolic text with discretized direction bins (5 egocentric labels) and distance bins (6 levels from 'same' to 'very far'), or 384×384 RGB images rendered in ThreeDWorld with Objaverse 3D assets — along with reference calibration images for distance and orientation. At each exploration step, the agent must output a structured JSON cognitive map (global allocentric layout plus local egocentric snapshot) and select unobserved positions from a top-down candidate-point map. Downstream evaluation uses open-ended spatial relation questions (direction, perspective-taking, action-to-view, allocentric mapping, mental rotation, view-to-location) rather than multiple-choice, scored by component accuracy (0.5 per direction/distance) or coordinate similarity.

## Method

THEORY OF SPACE is a benchmarking paradigm with three coupled components: (1) task-agnostic active exploration in procedurally generated multi-room grid environments (tree-topology room adjacency, 4 objects per room) where the agent selects from Goto, Rotate, Observe, and Query actions under a 20-step budget; (2) belief exploitation via Route-level (egocentric path reasoning) and Survey-level (allocentric map reasoning) spatial tasks drawn from Siegel & White's developmental framework; and (3) explicit cognitive map probing, where the agent is prompted at every step to externalize its spatial belief as a JSON-structured allocentric map, enabling turn-level diagnosis of perception accuracy, self-tracking, local-global consistency, and temporal stability. A key differentiator is the False Belief paradigm: after initial exploration, k=4 objects are covertly relocated or reoriented, and the agent must detect and revise its beliefs, with Belief Inertia quantified via directional alignment of residual error toward obsolete priors. Scripted proxy agents (SCOUT: exhaustive 360° sweeps; STRATEGIST: AC-3-driven uncertainty-minimizing viewpoint selection) provide passive baselines that disentangle exploration quality from core spatial reasoning.

## Results

On the THEORY OF SPACE benchmark, GEMINI-3 PRO achieves the best active exploration performance in the vision world (57.3% avg across 9 spatial tasks) and the text world (81.5%), while GPT-5.2 leads in passive text reasoning (90.4%). A critical Active-Passive Gap emerges: GPT-5.2 drops from 57.1% (passive) to 46.0% (active) in vision, and from 90.4% to 72.0% in text, indicating that autonomous information gathering is a major bottleneck. Humans substantially outperform all models (96.4% vision, 86.7% text) and complete exploration in fewer steps (~10 vs. 13–20 for models). Rule-based proxy agents (SCOUT/STRATEGIST) reach full coverage in ~9 steps, whereas foundation models require ≥14 steps without improving belief accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates six foundation models (GPT-5.2, GEMINI-3 PRO, CLAUDE-4.5 Sonnet, GLM-4.6V, Qwen3-VL, InternVL-3.5) as active spatial agents. GEMINI-3 PRO consistently outperforms all other models in the vision world (57.3% active vs. GPT-5.2's 46.0% and CLAUDE-4.5 Sonnet's 29.6%), and also leads in text (81.5% vs. GPT-5.2's 72.0%), with the smallest active-passive gap (3.2 points in vision vs. 11.1 for GPT-5.2). Open-source models (GLM-4.6V, Qwen3-VL, InternVL-3.5) trail proprietary models by 30–40 points in both modalities. All models suffer a significant performance drop when switching from passive comprehension to active exploration, with GPT-5.2 showing the largest text-world gap (90.4→72.0, a 18.4-point drop). Compared to scripted proxy agents (SCOUT/STRATEGIST), foundation models explore redundantly: proxies achieve full coverage in ~9 steps while models need 13–20 steps, and following proxy trajectories actually yields higher accuracy (e.g., GPT-5.2: 83.9% on SCOUT paths vs. 72.0% autonomous in text). Humans with tools achieve near-perfect scores (99.0% vision, 99.0% text) in ~11 steps, establishing a large ceiling gap.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from two established lines of spatial-reasoning evaluation: passive benchmarks (e.g., SPARQL, StepGame, MMSI-Bench) that test reasoning over fixed observations, and task-driven embodied benchmarks (e.g., IQA, ALFRED, EmbodiedBench) that tie exploration to a specific goal like 'find the red chair.' It also contrasts with EXCALIBUR, which studies task-agnostic exploration but requires RL training and encodes spatial knowledge implicitly in policy weights. By borrowing the cognitive-map concept from neuroscience (Tolman, 1948) and the false-belief paradigm from developmental psychology (Wimmer & Perner, 1983), the paper repurposes these ideas into a zero-shot, probe-based evaluation framework for foundation models, making the internal spatial belief an explicit, measurable artifact rather than an implicit byproduct of task performance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark provides the first systematic diagnostic of how foundation models construct, maintain, and revise spatial beliefs, revealing that the bottleneck is not merely perception but belief instability (correct facts overwritten in later turns) and belief inertia (failure to discard obsolete priors after environmental change). These findings suggest that current models lack a stable, revisable internal world model, a prerequisite for long-horizon embodied autonomy. Open questions include whether targeted training (e.g., RL on exploration efficiency or belief-consistency objectives) can close the active-passive gap, whether the modality gap can be narrowed with better visual grounding, and how the framework extends to multi-agent settings where spatial beliefs must be coordinated or shared.
