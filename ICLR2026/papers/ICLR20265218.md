## Problem

Large-scale order dispatching on ride-sharing platforms is typically addressed with Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning, but independent MARL methods fail to capture global information and exhibit poor cooperation, while CTDE methods suffer from the Curse of Dimensionality with thousands of agents. This paper asks whether a centralized Single-Agent RL framework can outperform these MARL approaches by directly leveraging complete global state information.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20265218/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes the full platform state S_t = [W_t, O_t] as a single concatenated sequence of n worker embeddings (location, residual capacity, onboard order sequences) and m_t order embeddings (origin, destination, pickup/scheduled times), where both n and m_t vary per time step. The output is a discrete assignment matrix A_t (each worker assigned at most one order), obtained not by direct network output but by solving a bipartite matching ILP over log-probabilities produced by the network. The action space is both enormous (~10^30 for 1000 workers × 10 orders) and dynamically sized, which is the central structural challenge the method must handle.

## Method

Triple-BERT replaces MARL with a centralized TD3 actor-critic framework built on a BERT-based architecture where a shared Actor-BERT (with positional embeddings removed for permutation invariance) processes the concatenated worker-order sequence, and a QK-Attention module decomposes the joint action into per-pair utilities with O(|f|·n + |g|·m_t + d·n·m_t) complexity instead of O(|F|·n·m_t), stabilized by a Softplus-based positive L2 normalization. The actor is optimized via an approximate policy gradient that maximizes the sum of log-probabilities of selected worker-order pairs (derived from a logit model over the QK-Attention output expanded with a 'no-order' column), while two Critic-BERT networks estimate Q-values in the standard TD3 twin-critic fashion. A two-stage training strategy addresses SARL sample scarcity: Stage 1 pre-trains the worker/order encoders with IDDQN under an independent-agent assumption to build general feature-extraction capacity, then Stage 2 fine-tunes the full network with centralized TD3. The BERT architecture's parameter reuse (same weights process all workers and orders regardless of count) prevents parameter explosion as fleet and order volumes grow.

## Results

On the Manhattan yellow-taxi dataset (1,000 drivers, 30-min episodes), Triple-BERT achieves an average reward of 14,730.48, outperforming the best baseline CEVD (13,157.96) by approximately 11.95%. It attains the highest service rate (0.98 vs. 0.94 for CEVD) and the lowest pickup time (5.73 min vs. 7.37 min for CEVD, a 22.25% reduction). On the more challenging FHV dataset with varying order volumes (734–5,989 orders per episode), Triple-BERT again leads with a reward of 14,329.74 and service rate of 0.88, while maintaining the shortest pickup time (7.02 min).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Triple-BERT outperforms all five baselines on the primary metrics of reward and service rate, with the largest gap over CEVD (the strongest centralized baseline): +1,572 reward points (+11.95%) and +0.04 service rate. On pickup time, Triple-BERT achieves 5.73 min versus 7.37 min for CEVD and 7.77 min for DeepPool, a reduction of roughly 22–26%. The method trades slightly higher delivery time (11.53 min vs. BMG-Q's 10.57 min) and detour time (2.52 vs. 1.90 min) for the ability to bundle more orders, which the authors attribute to increased ride-pooling. On confirmation time, Triple-BERT (0.13 min) is slightly higher than HIVES (0.03 min) and CEVD (0.06 min), which the paper explains as a consequence of its explicit 'reject all orders' action that allows the model to hold orders for currently unavailable workers. In the FHV generalization test (Table 2), the same pattern holds: Triple-BERT leads on reward (14,329 vs. 12,557 for CEVD) and pickup time (7.02 vs. 8.02 min), while showing higher reward variance due to its advantage being most pronounced in high-order-volume episodes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant MARL paradigm in ride-sharing order dispatch by proposing a centralized Single-Agent RL (SARL) framework built on a variant of TD3, arguing that both independent MARL (poor cooperation) and CTDE MARL (curse of dimensionality) are fundamentally limited at scale. Architecturally, it repurposes BERT's self-attention mechanism for structured driver-order matching rather than language, and adapts QK-attention from cross-domain Wi-Fi sensing to reduce per-pair evaluation complexity from multiplicative to additive. The two-stage training strategy (IDDQN pre-training of feature extractors followed by centralized TD3 fine-tuning) borrows the pre-training-then-fine-tuning paradigm from NLP while addressing the unique sample scarcity of merging multi-agent records into a single stream.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges the field's working assumption that MARL is necessary for multi-driver coordination, showing that a well-designed centralized SARL with BERT can outperform all MARL variants by ~12% on realistic Manhattan data. The action decomposition strategy (logit-model-based probability factorization + ILP) offers a general recipe for handling combinatorial action spaces in RL beyond ride-sharing. Key open questions include the method's sensitivity to single points of failure (centralized computation), whether the advantage persists under adversarial or non-stationary demand, and how to incorporate fairness constraints without sacrificing the centralized coordination benefit. The scalability results (up to 2000 drivers, <0.2s inference) suggest practical deployability, but validation on other cities or platforms remains future work.
