## Problem

While prior work has shown representational alignment between deep networks and primate IT cortex, and decoding methods can recover semantic features from neural activity, no study has directly mapped how feature-specific visual information is organized into structured, semantically meaningful subspaces within neural populations. The core question is whether distinct visual-semantic attributes (pose, category, intra-category content) occupy identifiable latent groups in the neural representation, and how to visualize what each group encodes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263823/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes paired single-unit spiking responses (58 channels for macaque M1, 110 for M2) recorded in a 70–170 ms post-stimulus window and corresponding 128×128 grayscale naturalistic object images (5760 images across 8 basic-level categories with randomized pose, scale, and position). It produces a 24-dimensional neural latent space partitioned into 4 groups of 6 dimensions each, along with synthesized 128×128 images that visualize the semantic content encoded by each latent group under controlled perturbations. Image features are extracted via DINO ViT-B/16 (384-dim embeddings) to feed the density-ratio estimator.

## Method

MIG-Vis combines a group-wise disentangled VAE with mutual information–guided diffusion synthesis. The VAE learns a 24-dim latent space split into 4 groups of 6 dims, where Groups 1–2 receive weak supervision (3D rotation angles and 8-way category labels) and Groups 3–4 are fully unsupervised; a partial-correlation penalty enforces statistical independence between groups while preserving reconstruction quality. For visualization, the method perturbs a target latent group by adding a uniform offset γ·1, then performs a two-stage deterministic DDIM editing process: DDIM inversion from t=0 to t′=135 to corrupt semantics while preserving structure, followed by guided DDIM sampling back to t=0. The guidance signal is the gradient of mutual information between the synthesized image and the perturbed latent, estimated via an InfoNCE contrastive loss with a learned density-ratio network s_φ(z_g, y). This MI-based guidance is stricter than likelihood-based alternatives because it requires the generated image to genuinely express the semantic content in the latent, rather than merely being recognizable by the encoder, yielding cleaner and more faithful semantic transitions especially for complex inter-category features.

## Results

MIG-Vis achieves neural reconstruction R² of 76.58% (M1) and 81.86% (M2), only marginally below the standard VAE (78.62% and 83.72% respectively), confirming that the partial correlation regularization and weak label supervision do not degrade reconstruction. More importantly, the group-wise disentangled VAE substantially improves disentanglement quality, raising the Mutual Information Gap (MIG) from 33.27% to 44.23% on M1 and from 28.65% to 49.85% on M2. The primary contribution is qualitative: MI-guided diffusion synthesis reveals clear semantic selectivity in latent groups, with Group 1 encoding pose/rotation, Group 2 encoding inter-category identity, and Groups 3–4 encoding intra-category content details.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares MIG-Vis against three baselines for visualizing semantic selectivity in neural latent groups (Figure 5). Standard Latent Traversal (SLT), which uses a trained decoder to map perturbed latents to images, produces only partial rotation effects for the pose group and completely fails to change object category for the inter-category group, indicating that decoder-based generation collapses semantic variation. AP-CFG (from BrainACTIV, using classifier-free guidance to maximize/minimize activation) captures rotation semantics reasonably well but produces less clean inter-category transitions than MIG-Vis. The most informative comparison is the ablation 'Ours w/o MI Guidance,' which replaces MI maximization with likelihood-based guidance ∇_y log p(z_g|y); this captures pose rotation adequately but generates inconsistent or unrealistic categorical transitions for inter-category features, because likelihood guidance only requires the encoder to recognize the image as matching the target latent rather than requiring the image to truly express the latent's semantic content. MIG-Vis outperforms all baselines on the inter-category semantic probing task, producing smooth, realistic category transitions (e.g., face → strawberry) that the other methods fail to achieve.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper combines group-wise disentangled VAEs (Li et al., 2025) for learning structured neural latent subspaces with a novel mutual information–guided diffusion framework for visualizing what each subspace encodes. It departs from prior neural-to-image decoding (Whiteway et al., 2021), which produces single reconstructions that smooth out subtle latent variations, and from fMRI-based diffusion guidance (Luo et al., 2023a; Cerdas et al., 2024), which relies on magnitude or variance maximization that is ill-suited to signed latent spaces. The key methodological departure is replacing the classifier-guidance term with an InfoNCE-based MI estimator (Oord et al., 2018), applied within a deterministic DDIM editing pipeline (Meng et al., 2021; Song et al., 2020) to preserve image structure while revealing semantic changes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to provide direct, interpretable visual evidence of structured semantic representation in IT cortex from electrophysiological spiking data, moving beyond indirect representational similarity metrics or scalar decoding scores. The finding that different latent groups exhibit fundamentally different geometric structures (a globally consistent toroidal manifold for pose vs. a locally warped, category-dependent manifold for intra-category content) offers a concrete hypothesis for how the brain organizes visual semantics. Limitations include evaluation on only two macaques in a passive viewing task with grayscale images, and the geometric interpretations remain qualitative rather than formally characterized. Open questions include whether these findings generalize to active tasks, color stimuli, and larger-scale recordings, and how to rigorously quantify the proposed manifold geometries.
