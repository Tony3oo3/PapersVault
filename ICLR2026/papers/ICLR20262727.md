## Problem

Prior work on transformer arithmetic (grokking, Fourier representations) relies on tokens carrying fixed parametric or geometric information in their embeddings. This paper asks what computational strategies transformers develop when tokens are pure variables whose meaning is determined solely through in-context interactions, with no fixed global meaning. The gap is a lack of understanding of symbolic, context-based reasoning mechanisms that emerge when all parametric shortcuts are removed.

## Input / Output

The model receives a sequence of k algebra facts (each fact is four tokens: left-slot, right-slot, '=', answer-slot) written over a small vocabulary of N=16 variable tokens plus special tokens, where the mapping from variables to algebraic elements is randomized independently per sequence. The model is trained with standard next-token prediction and must output the answer token for the final fact. Because the same variable token can represent different group elements across sequences, the model cannot rely on any fixed token-to-element association and must infer all structure from the observed relational patterns within each sequence.

## Method

The paper trains small 4-layer transformers (8 heads, 1024 hidden dim, RoPE) on sequences of 200 facts sampled from finite groups (cyclic C₃–C₁₀ and dihedral D₃–D₅) with per-sequence randomized variable assignments. To identify which mechanisms the model actually implements, the authors design five targeted data distributions (verbatim copying, commutative copying, identity recognition, associative composition, closure-based cancellation) and measure both algorithmic coverage and model accuracy on each. Causal interventions—including attention-head activation patching (indirect effect), logit-lens decoding, PCA steering along identity-related directions, and trained subspace patching for closure and elimination sets—verify that the model implements three distinct symbolic mechanisms: a dedicated copying head (layer 3, head 6) for verbatim and commutative retrieval, a query-promotion/identity-demotion pair for identity facts, and a closure subspace combined with an elimination subspace for cancellation-based reasoning. Training exhibits discrete phase transitions where these skills emerge sequentially: structural tokens, group closure, copying, then identity and cancellation in parallel, with associativity last.

## Results

The 4-layer transformer (8 heads, 1024 hidden) achieves near-perfect accuracy on the in-context algebra task, with model accuracy AUC of 92.4% on training sequences and 87.3% on non-copyable hold-out sequences. On targeted data distributions, the model scores 100% on verbatim copying, 99% on commutative copying, 100% on identity recognition, and 97% on closure-based cancellation, but only 60.2% on associative composition. The model generalizes to unseen order-8 groups with near-perfect accuracy and achieves non-trivial hold-out performance on semigroups, though performance collapses on magmas.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an interpretability study rather than a method comparison, so there are no competing baselines. The key quantitative comparison is between the model's empirical accuracy and the coverage of five hypothesized algorithms: the algorithms explain 90.4% AUC of training performance versus the model's 92.4% AUC, leaving ~2% unexplained. On non-copyable hold-out sequences, the algorithms cover 84.7% AUC versus the model's 87.3%, with identity recognition (28.7%), closure-based cancellation (39.1%), and associativity (16.9%) being the dominant strategies. The model also outperforms its algorithmic coverage on quasigroup cancellation data (up to 100% at 50 facts), suggesting the learned cancellation mechanism generalizes beyond the specific group structures it was trained on. Performance degrades sharply for non-group structures: semigroups retain good hold-out accuracy, quasigroups cap at ~50%, and magmas drop to near zero.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of work on transformer arithmetic (Power et al., 2022; Nanda et al., 2023; Zhong et al., 2023), where models learn geometric or Fourier-based representations in fixed token embeddings, by removing all fixed token meanings and requiring structure to be inferred purely from in-context relationships. It builds on the causal interpretability toolkit (Geiger et al., 2024, 2025; Mueller et al., 2025) and the induction-head literature (Elhage et al., 2021; Olsson et al., 2022), but applies these methods to a setting where the 'copying' baseline is insufficient and qualitatively different symbolic mechanisms must emerge. The task itself—solving group arithmetic with per-sequence randomized variable assignments—is a new formulation that carves out a regime prior arithmetic-in-transformers work does not cover.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that task structure (fixed vs. variable token meanings) determines whether transformers develop geometric or symbolic reasoning strategies—challenges the assumption that Fourier/geometric representations are the canonical way transformers handle algebraic structure. The identification of three distinct symbolic mechanisms (commutative copying, identity recognition, closure-based cancellation) with causal verification provides a template for studying how models reason about variables without parametric knowledge. Open questions include whether these symbolic mechanisms appear in larger pretrained models, whether they generalize beyond the small synthetic groups studied here, and what determines when a model selects a symbolic versus geometric strategy. The ~60% ceiling on associative composition suggests a genuine limitation in multi-step compositional reasoning even when single-step mechanisms are well-learned.
