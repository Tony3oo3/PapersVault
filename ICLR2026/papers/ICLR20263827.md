## Problem

Previous task-related visual token compression methods (e.g., FastV, PyramidDrop, Dart) operate in intermediate LLM layers, relying on the assumption that visual tokens only achieve meaningful vision-language alignment after passing through shallow layers. This paper challenges that assumption by asking whether effective, instruction-aware token pruning is feasible at the LLM input stage—before any LLM computation—without modifying the LLM architecture, and whether explainability methods can provide the necessary importance signals to guide such compression.

## Input / Output

The method takes an image or video (encoded into N_v visual tokens by a frozen vision encoder) and a text instruction as input, and outputs a pruned subset of visual tokens (N̂_v ≪ N_v) that are fed directly into the LLM for response generation. The compression is performed entirely before the LLM's prefill phase, so no modifications to the prefill or decode pipeline are required. The lightweight compressor f_θ operates on the first-layer attention submap A_{u→v}^0 (attention from instruction tokens to visual tokens, averaged over instruction positions), which is a 1D vector of length N_v, and predicts a probability distribution over visual tokens used for top-k selection. Because f_θ is fully convolutional, it generalizes to arbitrary token counts beyond the ~1500 used during training, accommodating larger images and longer videos.

## Method

The approach has two stages. First, the paper demonstrates that Generic Attention Explainability (GAE) relevance scores—computed by iteratively propagating gradient-weighted multi-head attention maps from the output back to the input across all LLM layers—faithfully capture which visual tokens are essential for answering a given instruction, and that pruning based on these scores at the LLM input preserves >97% performance at 25% retention. Second, to avoid the costly forward+backward pass needed for GAE at inference time, the authors train a 5-layer 1D depthwise separable convolutional network f_θ (channels 32→512) to predict the relevance distribution from the first-layer attention map alone. Training uses KL divergence against GAE-derived labels (with the bottom 50% of scores masked to focus on identifying relevant regions), requires only ~10K image/video samples per MLLM, and takes under 4 hours on a single A100. The resulting compressor is model-agnostic (one f_θ per MLLM), adds negligible FLOPs (~0.17% of total LLM computation), and reduces KV-cache memory by ~75% at 25% retention while achieving 1.6× prefill speedup.

## Results

On image benchmarks, the explainability-based compressor retains 97.4% average performance at 50% token retention and 92.1% at 25% for LLaVA-OneVision, while achieving 97.4% and 92.9% respectively for Qwen2-VL, all at 0.48× and 0.24× FLOPs. On video benchmarks, both LLaVA-OneVision and VILA maintain ~100% performance at 50% retention, and VILA reaches 99.0% at 25% retention. Efficiency gains include a 1.60× prefilling speedup and KV-cache reduction from 71.2 MB to 17.8 MB (Qwen2-VL, 25% retention). The method prunes 75% of video tokens while retaining >97% of original performance across all benchmarks for VILA1.5 and LLaVA-OneVision.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method consistently outperforms all three primary baselines (FastV, PyramidDrop, Dart) at comparable FLOPs across both image and video benchmarks. FastV suffers catastrophic degradation (56.0% avg for LLaVA-OneVision at 50% retention, dropping to 47.3% at 25%), while the proposed method achieves 97.4% and 92.1% respectively—a gap of over 40 percentage points. PyramidDrop and Dart are much closer competitors, achieving 95.1%/95.0% (LLaVA-OneVision) and 95.2%/96.4% (Qwen2-VL) at 50% retention, but the proposed method still leads by 2.3–2.6% on average, with the gap widening to 4.6–5.8% at 25% retention. On video benchmarks (Table 3), the advantage is even more pronounced: LLaVA-OneVision and VILA maintain ~100% performance at 50% retention with the proposed method, versus 96.6–96.9% for PDrop/Dart. On the MVBench comparison (Table 13), the method achieves 99.5% performance preservation at 25% retention with 11.7T FLOPs, matching FastVID (99.3%) and PruneVID (99.1%) while outperforming VisionZip (94.4%) and FastV (95.4%). The method also achieves the lowest FLOPs (0.24×) among all compared approaches at 25% retention, confirming its efficiency advantage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Generic Attention Explainability (GAE) framework (Chefer et al., ICCV 2021) and its MLLM-adapted variant RGAE (Yao et al., 2024) to compute per-token visual relevance scores, repurposing an interpretability tool as a compression signal. It departs sharply from the FastV (Chen et al., ECCV 2024) lineage—including PyramidDrop and Dart—which assumes all shallow-layer visual tokens are indispensable and therefore performs pruning only in intermediate LLM layers. The key conceptual shift is demonstrating that explainability-derived relevance can guide compression at the LLM input stage, and that a 5-layer 1D depthwise-separable CNN trained on ~10K samples can approximate the full relevance map from just the first-layer attention, eliminating the need for a backward pass at inference.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By moving compression to the LLM input stage, the method reduces both prefill time (1.6×) and KV-cache memory (71.2→17.8 MB) without modifying the LLM architecture, making it a drop-in accelerator for any MLLM. The model-agnostic design (validated on Qwen2-VL, LLaVA-OneVision, and VILA1.5 across 13+ benchmarks) and the extremely lightweight compressor (~0.17% extra FLOPs) make it practically attractive for deployment. A key limitation is that generating training labels (R_v) requires full forward+backward passes, which is memory-intensive for high-resolution images or long videos, constraining the compressor's training data diversity. The authors note that future work could explore using relevance scores to guide compression during training, potentially closing the gap between the oracle R_v and the learned R̃_v, especially on image tasks where the gap is more pronounced.
