## Problem

The data processing inequality implies that no pre-classification processing can improve the accuracy of the optimal Bayes classifier, yet in practice low-level tasks like denoising and encoding are routinely applied before classification. No prior work has theoretically characterized when and why such processing benefits a strong (near-optimal) classifier trained on finite data. This paper provides the first rigorous analysis of the gap between the DPI's implication and practical classifier performance.

## Input / Output

In the theoretical model, the input is a high-dimensional data point x ∈ R^d drawn from a two-component Gaussian mixture (one component per class), and the output is a binary label with an associated error probability. In the practical experiments, noisy RGB images (CIFAR-10 at 32×32, Mini-ImageNet at 224×224) with additive Gaussian noise (σ ∈ {0.25, 0.4} or {50/255, 100/255}) are first processed by a denoiser or encoder, then classified by a ResNet to produce a 10- or 100-way label. The primary output metric is the relative efficiency—the percentage reduction in error probability attributable to the pre-classification step—plotted against training set size, noise level, and class imbalance factor γ.

## Method

The theoretical core studies binary GMM classification in R^d where the classifier is a nearest-mean estimator using MLE of class means from N training samples per class. The data processing is a linear dimensionality reduction z = Ax with A semi-orthonormal (AA^T = I_k) and norm-preserving on the class-mean direction (‖Aμ‖ = ‖μ‖), which retains the class-dependent signal while attenuating within-class variability. Using a generalized Berry-Esseen theorem, the authors derive closed-form Q-function approximations of the error probability before and after processing, proving that for any finite N the processing strictly reduces error (balanced case) or does so under mild conditions (imbalanced case). They further show that the maximal efficiency increases with SNR (counterintuitively) and that the efficiency is non-monotonic in N, peaking at an intermediate sample size. Empirically, the theory is validated with DnCNN denoising on noisy CIFAR-10 (ResNet18) and self-supervised encoding on noisy Mini-ImageNet (ResNet50 + MLP), sweeping training set size, class balance, and noise level to confirm the predicted trends.

## Results

In the theoretical GMM setup (d=2000, k=1000), the data processing efficiency η is always strictly positive for any finite N under balanced training (γ=1), peaking at intermediate N before decaying to zero as N→∞. The maximal efficiency η_max increases with SNR S (counterintuitively), e.g., the peak is higher for S=1.5² than for S=0.75². In practical experiments on noisy CIFAR-10 with DnCNN denoising (ResNet-18, 90% clean accuracy), the efficiency is non-monotonic in N_train and the maximal efficiency for σ=0.25 exceeds that for σ=0.4, consistent with the theoretical prediction that higher SNR yields larger peak gains. Similar trends are confirmed on noisy Mini-ImageNet with self-supervised encoding (ResNet-50 + MLP).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against competing methods in the standard sense; instead, it compares a classifier trained on raw (noisy) data against the same classifier trained on pre-processed data (denoised via DnCNN or encoded via a self-supervised encoder). Across all four practical settings (CIFAR-10 denoising, CIFAR-10 encoding, Mini-ImageNet denoising, Mini-ImageNet encoding), the processed-data classifier consistently achieves lower error probability than the raw-data classifier for any finite training set size, confirming the theoretical prediction. The relative gain (efficiency) is non-monotonic in N_train: it rises from zero at N_train→0, peaks at an intermediate value, and decays back toward zero as N_train→∞ (where the classifier approaches the optimal Bayes rule, for which the data processing inequality guarantees no improvement). The peak efficiency is larger at higher SNR (lower noise σ), and larger under class imbalance (lower γ), both matching the asymptotic formula in Theorem 7. Encoding (to 256-d embeddings) yields higher efficiency than denoising on CIFAR-10, suggesting that learned representations may be a more effective low-level preprocessing than pixel-level restoration for classification.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new theoretical ground by bridging a gap between the data processing inequality (which guarantees no benefit from preprocessing for the optimal Bayes classifier) and the widespread practical observation that low-level tasks like denoising or encoding improve downstream classification. It departs from Liu et al. (2019), which studies a tradeoff between restoration error and a *fixed* classifier, by instead allowing the classifier to be retrained after processing and focusing on the end-to-end classification objective. The GMM binary classification setup and nearest-neighbor classifier build on a standard theoretical framework (Cao et al., 2021; Deng et al., 2022; Wang & Thrampoulidis, 2022), but the paper's use of a generalized Berry-Esseen theorem to characterize finite-sample error probabilities before and after a constructive dimensionality reduction is a novel analytical contribution not present in prior classification theory.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous theoretical explanation for why low-level preprocessing helps practical classifiers despite the data processing inequality, resolving a long-standing tension between information-theoretic principles and common ML practice. The counterintuitive finding that maximal efficiency *increases* with SNR (Theorem 8) challenges the naive expectation that well-separated classes benefit less from preprocessing, and the practical experiments on CIFAR-10 and Mini-ImageNet confirm the predicted trends. Key limitations include the restriction to binary classification, symmetric GMM data, and linear (dimensionality-reducing) processing; extending the theory to multi-class settings, non-linear processing, and identifying the *optimal* low-level task for a given high-level objective are natural and important open directions the authors highlight.
