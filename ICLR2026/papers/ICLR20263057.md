## Problem

Most GNN-LLM fusion strategies apply LLMs uniformly across all nodes, wasting expensive LLM calls where GNNs already perform well, while aggregate accuracy metrics obscure the per-node heterogeneity in model performance. Existing routing heuristics (degree, uncertainty, clustering density) are brittle and dataset-dependent, sometimes performing worse than random routing. The paper asks: which structural properties predict when an LLM will help a GNN, and how can we learn to route adaptively rather than relying on handcrafted thresholds?

## Input / Output

GLANCE takes a text-attributed graph (node text strings plus adjacency structure) and outputs a per-node class probability distribution. The GNN encodes k-hop neighborhoods into fixed-size embeddings, while the LLM (Qwen3-Embed-8B) generates three concatenated embeddings per routed node—ego-only, ego+sampled 1-hop neighbors, and ego+sampled 2-hop neighbors—each serialized into separate prompts with neighbor sampling capped at 5 per hop to control prompt length. The router consumes a compact feature vector (GNN embedding, dropout uncertainty, soft local homophily score, original node features, and degree) to produce a scalar routing probability per node, with a fixed top-k budget per mini-batch determining which nodes actually trigger an LLM call.

## Method

GLANCE comprises three components: a frozen GNN backbone, a frozen LLM encoder, and a trainable lightweight router (logistic regression) plus a 2-layer refiner MLP. The router uses a top-k selection strategy per batch to decide which nodes query the LLM, avoiding the need for global probability calibration. For routed nodes, the LLM generates multi-hop embeddings that are concatenated and fused with the GNN embedding through the refiner MLP; non-routed nodes retain their original GNN prediction. The key training innovation is an advantage-based policy gradient objective: for each routed node, the reward is the counterfactual loss reduction (GNN loss minus LLM-fused loss) minus a cost penalty β, while non-routed nodes receive a reward based on GNN loss alone. This treats routing as a contextual bandit problem, with the router optimized via a REINFORCE-inspired loss that includes entropy regularization, while both the GNN and LLM remain frozen throughout training.

## Results

GLANCE achieves the best overall node classification accuracy across all three primary benchmarks: 89.5% on Cora, 92.6% on Pubmed, and 82.1% on Arxiv23, yielding an average gain of +0.5% over the next best model. The most striking gains appear on heterophilous nodes (local homophily < 0.25), where GLANCE delivers up to +13% improvement over the strongest GNN baseline (e.g., 46.4% vs. 33.4% on Cora). On large-scale datasets, GLANCE reaches 49.8% on Arxiv-Year and 82.3% on OGB-Products using only a ~1.6% LLM query rate (K=1 per batch of 64), outperforming GCNII, FAGCN, and GGCN while maintaining balanced performance across all homophily bins.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GLANCE outperforms all five compared baselines on overall accuracy across all three primary datasets. Against GCN (Kipf & Welling, 2017), GLANCE gains +1.6% on Cora (89.5 vs. 87.9) and +5.9% on Arxiv23 (82.1 vs. 76.2) with enhanced features. Against GraphSAGE (Hamilton et al., 2017), the margin is +3.1% on Cora and +4.0% on Arxiv23. Against GCNII (Chen et al., 2020), the strongest GNN backbone, GLANCE leads by +1.8% on Cora and +1.9% on Arxiv23, while matching Pubmed within 0.5%. Against GBK-GNN (Du et al., 2022), a heterophily-aware GNN, GLANCE achieves +2.9% on Cora and +2.6% on Arxiv23. Against LOGIN (Qiao et al., 2025), which rewires the graph using LLM uncertainty, GLANCE substantially outperforms on Arxiv23 (82.1 vs. 79.7) and Cora (89.5 vs. 86.8), demonstrating that preserving graph structure while selectively invoking the LLM is more effective than editing the graph topology. Notably, GLANCE achieves these gains while routing only 12 of 32 nodes per batch to the LLM, incurring far fewer LLM calls than uniform fusion approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the GNN-LLM fusion literature by moving from uniform fusion (LLM-as-Enhancer or LLM-as-Predictor applied to all nodes) to selective, node-aware routing. It builds on adaptive fusion methods like E-LLaGNN (fixed heuristics on degree/centrality), LOGIN (uncertainty-based rewiring), and LLM-GNN (clustering density), but departs from them by (1) identifying local homophily as the principled structural signal for LLM benefit rather than relying on handcrafted thresholds, (2) preserving graph structure instead of editing it, and (3) directly training a non-differentiable router via a policy-gradient/advantage-based objective rather than proxying the signal through data modification. The homophily-based routing draws on the GNN heterophily literature (Zhu et al., 2020; Du et al., 2022) and the degree-bias analysis (Yan et al., 2022; Subramonian et al., 2024), repurposing these structural diagnostics as routing features.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical, scalable recipe for deploying LLMs on large text-attributed graphs without incurring full-graph LLM inference costs, demonstrated at scale on OGB-Products (2.4M nodes) with only ~1.6% query rate. The finding that aggregate accuracy masks large per-subgroup disparities (up to +13% on heterophilous nodes) and that existing routing heuristics can be worse than random on some datasets is actionable for practitioners choosing fusion strategies. Open questions include whether the learned router generalizes across domains without retraining, whether the approach extends to link prediction or graph-level tasks, and whether the modest overall gains (+0.5%) can be amplified with stronger LLM backbones or richer neighborhood serialization. The reliance on a frozen GNN and frozen LLM also limits the ceiling of what the refiner can achieve.
