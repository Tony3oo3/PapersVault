## Problem

Role-Playing Conversational Agents (RPCAs) struggle to maintain persona consistency under misleading queries or complex contexts, yet the creative, open-ended nature of role-playing makes it difficult to design verifiable reward signals for reinforcement learning. Moreover, applying general Chain-of-Thought reasoning to role-playing can degrade performance by introducing overly formal language and verbose content that erodes stylistic expression. The paper addresses how to design a specialized, verifiable reasoning process that enhances role-awareness without sacrificing the creativity essential to high-quality role-play.

## Input / Output

The model receives a user query alongside a character profile, dialogue history, and role-playing requirements, and produces a structured three-part output: an extracted hint (exact text segments from the profile, history, or requirements enclosed in <hint> tags), a reasoning process in <think> tags, and a final in-character response. Hints must satisfy two constraints: verifiability (they should be near-exact copies of source text, enabling ROUGE-based scoring) and context-specificity (generic instructions like 'be consistent with the persona' are excluded). For the Situation Puzzle subset, the final answer is a reconstructed narrative evaluated by an LLM judge rather than keyword matching.

## Method

VeriRole introduces a pre-reasoning 'hint mechanism' that extracts deterministic, verifiable cues from the character profile, dialogue history, or role-playing requirements before the model generates its response, anchoring the reasoning in checkable facts. A multi-component Verifiable Role-Awareness Reward (VRAR) then scores each generated output: a hint reward combining ROUGE lexical overlap, cosine semantic similarity, and a length penalty (discretized for training stability); an accuracy reward using keyword matching for fact-based samples or LLM-as-judge for narrative puzzles; and a format reward enforcing the Hint-Think-Answer structure. The model is optimized via Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) on two complementary datasets—RAIDEN (basic role-playing across seven evaluation categories) and a novel Situation Puzzle Dataset (complex yes/no reasoning with definitive solutions)—allowing the framework to improve both factual recall and abstract conversational skills like topic advancement and role-cognition boundaries.

## Results

On the RAIDEN benchmark, Qwen2.5-32B-GRPO achieves the best average score of 0.8268, an 18.9% improvement over its Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct baseline (0.6953). On CharacterEval, the same model improves its average score from 3.330 to 3.482 (+4.55%), with notable gains in Persona Consistency (3.092→3.295) and Engagingness (3.276→3.514). The GRPO-trained model also substantially outperforms SFT variants: on Topic Advancement, the score jumps from 0.2835 (SFT-reply) to 0.6865 (GRPO), demonstrating that RL captures abstract conversational skills that imitation-based SFT cannot.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares VeriRole (GRPO) against three types of baselines: (1) the original Instruct models, (2) SFT variants trained on the same data, and (3) models with inherent reasoning (Qwen3-32B-Think). Against Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct, GRPO wins on every RAIDEN sub-metric, with the largest gains on SCK (+20.3%) and RCB (+35.2%). Against SFT (both reply-only and hint-and-reply), GRPO dominates, especially on Topic Advancement where SFT-reply collapses to 0.2835 while GRPO reaches 0.6865, confirming that RL learns abstract conversational skills that imitation cannot. Against Qwen3-32B-Think (which has built-in reasoning), GRPO still outperforms on average (0.7945 vs 0.6796), showing that general-purpose reasoning does not transfer to role-playing without specialized reward signals. On the role-playing-specific Peach-9B model, GRPO nearly doubles the average RAIDEN score (0.3611→0.6183), demonstrating generalizability to already-specialized agents. The only metric where GRPO shows a slight regression is TA on Qwen3 models, attributed to shorter responses lacking the rhetorical questions that the original Qwen3 models favor for that evaluation criterion.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on GRPO (from DeepSeek-R1) as its optimization backbone but departs from both standard Chain-of-Thought prompting and preference-based reward modeling (e.g., ChARM) by introducing a 'hint' mechanism that extracts deterministic, verifiable cues from character profiles and dialogue context before reasoning. Rather than treating role-playing as an open-ended creative task without objective signals, VeriRole reframes it as a structured extraction-then-generation pipeline, making RL applicable where prior work identified a fundamental non-verifiability barrier. The approach also contrasts with data-driven SFT strategies (dialogue synthesis, profile-dialogue alignment) by showing that explicit reward signals for hint quality and factual accuracy yield substantially better generalization on abstract conversational skills.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The hint mechanism offers a potentially generalizable recipe for making creative, open-ended tasks amenable to RL by decomposing them into a verifiable sub-task (cue extraction) and a free-form generation step. A notable open question is whether this decomposition transfers beyond role-playing to other creative tasks (storytelling, creative writing) where 'ground-truth hints' are harder to define. Limitations include reliance on a single model family (Qwen), a benchmark (RAIDEN) co-authored by the same group, and a hand-crafted reward design that may not capture all dimensions of role-awareness (e.g., emotional appropriateness, which the authors acknowledge as future work).
