## Problem

AlphaZero-style agents always restart self-play from the empty board, requiring millions of games to reach superhuman strength, whereas humans learn efficiently by repeatedly revisiting positions where mistakes occurred. Go-Exploit introduced search control into AlphaZero by uniformly sampling past states as restart points, but this uniform treatment fails to prioritize the small subset of high-regret states that actually drive learning progress, especially in later training stages when mistakes become rare.

## Input / Output

The method consumes grid-based board game states (9×9 Go, 10×10 Othello, 11×11 Hex) from two sources: states along self-play trajectories and internal nodes of the MCTS search tree. The regret network produces two outputs per state: an unnormalized ranking score γ_s (used for state selection) and a regret value estimate (used for buffer prioritization). The prioritized regret buffer stores up to K states with their regret values, and the overall system outputs move selections, win-rate predictions, and a dynamically updated buffer that guides where the next self-play game begins.

## Method

RGSC extends AlphaZero with a regret network comprising two heads: a ranking network that outputs unnormalized scores γ_s and a value network that estimates actual regret. Because most states have near-zero regret and high-regret states are non-stationary (their regret drops once revisited), the ranking network is trained with a ranking-based loss (Eq. 6) that adds true regret as an additive bias to log-softmax scores, rather than directly regressing regret values. A prioritized regret buffer (PRB) of fixed capacity K stores the highest-ranked states from both trajectories and MCTS nodes; for trajectory states, regret is computed exactly via the average squared discrepancy between MCTS values and the game outcome (Eq. 2), while for search-tree-only states the value network provides an estimate. During self-play, with probability λ the game starts from a state sampled from the PRB using a softmax over regret values (temperature τ), and after each replay the stored regret is updated via exponential moving average (Eq. 13), so mastered states gradually decay in priority and are replaced by new challenging states.

## Results

Across 9×9 Go, 10×10 Othello, and 11×11 Hex, RGSC outperforms AlphaZero by an average of 77 Elo (76, 70, 84 per game) and Go-Exploit by an average of 89 Elo (96, 50, 122 per game). Against established open-source programs, RGSC achieves win rates of 53.6% (9×9 Go vs. KataGo), 57.8% (10×10 Othello vs. Ludii alpha-beta), and 91.1% (11×11 Hex vs. MoHex). When continued from a well-trained 15-block 9×9 Go model, RGSC improves the win rate against KataGo from 69.3% to 78.2%, while AlphaZero and Go-Exploit show no meaningful improvement (70.2% and 69.2% respectively).

## Baselines & Comparisons

RGSC consistently outperforms AlphaZero (Silver et al., 2018) across all three board games, with Elo margins of 76 (Go), 70 (Othello), and 84 (Hex), and higher win rates against open-source opponents in every game. Against Go-Exploit (Trudeau & Bowling, 2023), the advantage is even larger: 96, 50, and 122 Elo respectively, with the gap widening in later training stages where Go-Exploit's uniform sampling becomes ineffective as high-regret states become rarer. On the well-trained 9×9 Go model, RGSC achieves a 78.2% win rate against KataGo (15-block), substantially exceeding both AlphaZero (70.2%) and Go-Exploit (69.2%), which show no improvement over the 69.3% baseline. In the Atari generalization experiment (Appendix G), RGSC integrated into MuZero (Schrittwieser et al., 2020) reaches 5166 points on Ms. Pac-Man versus 3704 for vanilla MuZero under the same training budget, confirming the approach transfers beyond board games.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Go-Exploit (Trudeau & Bowling, 2023), which introduced search control into AlphaZero by uniformly sampling restart states from self-play trajectories or MCTS nodes, by replacing uniform sampling with a regret-based prioritization mechanism. The broader search control lineage traces back to Sutton's Dyna framework and has been explored through restart distributions (Tavakoli et al., 2020), reverse curricula (Florensa et al., 2017), and expert-guided restarts (Uchendu et al., 2023), but none of these address the specific challenge of identifying which states within a single game level carry the most learning signal. The key methodological departure is the ranking-based regret objective, which sidesteps the extreme class imbalance and non-stationarity that make direct regret regression intractable, and the integration of a prioritized regret buffer with EMA-based regret decay to track state mastery over time.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The most practically significant finding is that RGSC continues to improve a well-trained 9x9 Go model (69.3% → 78.2% win rate against KataGo) where both AlphaZero and Go-Exploit plateau, suggesting the method can extract residual learning signal even near convergence—a regime where most RL training strategies become ineffective. The regret network also provides interpretability by revealing specific tactical weaknesses (e.g., seki patterns in Go), which could guide targeted human-AI collaboration. Open questions include whether the approach scales to 19x19 Go or other large-scale sequential decision problems, whether it transfers to stochastic or continuous-control environments, and whether the ranking objective generalizes beyond the board-game setting where regret is well-defined by terminal outcomes.
