## Problem

Diffusion language models enable any-order generation and bidirectional conditioning but model only a single layer of dependency—each token in the target group depends directly on the visible context without recursive conditioning on other predicted tokens. This shallow structure limits generation quality and training stability relative to autoregressive models, creating a fundamental trade-off between the flexibility of parallel generation and the expressive depth of sequential factorization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262996/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a text sequence partitioned into K arbitrary token groups (each group can contain any subset of positions in any order) and a prompt covering the first k₀ groups, then generates the remaining groups sequentially. For infilling, left and right context tokens are assigned to earlier groups while masked middle positions form later groups, enabling bidirectional conditioning. Outputs are full generated sequences, infilled spans, or continuations, with each group's tokens predicted jointly in a single forward pass.

## Method

A3 generalizes the standard AR factorization P(x₁:ₙ) = ∏ P(xₜ | x_<ₜ) to a group-wise form P(x₁:ₙ) = ∏ₖ P(x_{Gₖ} | x_{G_<ₖ}), where groups can be arbitrary subsets in arbitrary order, preserving multi-layer recursive dependencies while enabling parallel prediction within each group. The architecture uses a two-stream attention mechanism (extending XLNet): a content stream that attends to its own group plus all preceding groups, and a query stream with a shared learnable vector that attends only to strictly earlier groups, providing positional conditioning for prediction. Training follows a three-stage progressive curriculum from pretrained LLaMA checkpoints—singleton AR groups, contiguous multi-token groups, then random permutation of token-to-group assignments—ensuring stable adaptation. Inference supports two strategies: fixed groupwise AR sampling for speed, and dynamic resampling that adaptively commits tokens based on confidence or entropy criteria for higher quality.

## Results

A3-8B achieves 19.4% on TriviaQA, 58.4% on HellaSwag, 60.2% on Winogrande, 45.2% on SIQA, and 78.1% on PIQA, outperforming all diffusion-based baselines on 4 of 5 reasoning/QA benchmarks. On ROCStories infilling, A3-8B scores 19.2/4.6/18.6 ROUGE-1/2/L, trailing DiffuLlama-7B (23.3/5.5/21.2) but substantially exceeding Dream-7B (11.7/2.3/10.5). A3 demonstrates clear scaling: performance improves steadily from 1B (10.2 TriviaQA) to 3B (15.9) to 8B (19.4), and the gap to the AR baseline (Llama-3.1-8B at 52.1 TriviaQA) is attributed to training data (8B vs. 15T tokens).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the AR baseline Llama-3.1-8B, A3-8B trails on all five QA/reasoning benchmarks (e.g., 19.4 vs. 52.1 on TriviaQA, 78.1 vs. 80.3 on PIQA), a gap the authors attribute to the 8B-token training budget versus 15T tokens for Llama-3.1. Against DiffuLlama-7B (the strongest diffusion baseline), A3-8B wins on TriviaQA (19.4 vs. 18.5), Winogrande (60.2 vs. 56.4), SIQA (45.2 vs. 43.2), and PIQA (78.1 vs. 63.3), but slightly loses on HellaSwag (58.4 vs. 58.7) and ROCStories infilling (19.2 vs. 23.3 ROUGE-1). Against Dream-7B, A3-8B dominates across the board, with particularly large margins on HellaSwag (58.4 vs. 26.9) and PIQA (78.1 vs. 55.8). Against Plaid-1B (continuous diffusion), even A3-1B surpasses it on TriviaQA (10.2 vs. 1.2), Winogrande (52.8 vs. 51.3), and PIQA (64.7 vs. 54.5). In the conditional generation perplexity comparison (Table 2), A3-8B achieves the lowest perplexity under entropy-based dynamic sampling (11.2 at 1024 steps) versus DiffuLlama (14.3) and Dream (16.4), confirming the advantage of A3's AR-consistent inference over diffusion denoising schedules.

## Relation to Prior Work

A3 reformulates the masked diffusion language model's single-step two-group prediction as a multi-group autoregressive factorization, drawing directly on XLNet's two-stream attention and permutation LM objective but elevating it from token-level to group-level partitioning. It departs from diffusion LMs (DiffuLlama, Dream, Plaid) by replacing iterative denoising with a single-pass groupwise AR decomposition, and from multi-token prediction (Medusa, speculative decoding) by relaxing the fixed left-to-right ordering to arbitrary group permutations. The progressive three-stage training strategy (AR initialization → group expansion → order permutation) is a novel adaptation recipe that lets pretrained AR checkpoints be repurposed without retraining from scratch.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

A3 offers a principled middle ground between AR's deep recursive dependencies and diffusion's flexible any-order generation, with the practical advantage of inheriting AR's training stability and avoiding multi-step denoising schedules. However, the large remaining gap to the AR baseline (19.4 vs. 52.1 on TriviaQA at 8B) is attributed to training data (8B vs. 15T tokens), leaving open whether A3 can truly match AR quality at scale or whether the groupwise factorization imposes an inherent ceiling. The paper also leaves unresolved how A3 interacts with other decoding accelerators (speculative decoding, MTP) at scale, and whether the two-stream architecture's overhead becomes prohibitive at larger model sizes.
