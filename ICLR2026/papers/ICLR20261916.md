## Problem

Data poisoning attacks in federated learning—where an adversary corrupts the local datasets of a fraction of honest workers—are more practical than model poisoning (e.g., label flipping requires no model access), yet the literature primarily relies on robust gradient aggregation methods designed for the stronger model poisoning threat. These gradient-based defenses scale poorly with model size and fail to exploit the structure of poisoned gradients. The paper asks how well one can defend against arbitrary data poisoning in the worst case, addressing both target-model accuracy and the adversary's free-ride gain (e.g., Trojan attacks).

## Input / Output

The server receives only worker-computed gradients and model output values F_i(θ, a) (never raw data), along with the poisoning fraction α. The method produces two outputs: a trustworthiness weight vector w ∈ ℝ^m (summing to 1) that down-weights poisoned workers, and a trained hypothesis sign ∘ g_θ* obtained by minimizing the weighted local error. The weight update phase runs before target-model training, and the discriminator model is an auxiliary network separate from the target model.

## Method

The algorithm has two phases. In the trustworthiness weight update phase, the server trains a discriminator model by maximizing a variance notion Var_H({W_i}, w) that is aligned with H-divergence (the hypothesis-class-aware distance between distributions). At each round, the discriminator finds the hypothesis θ and label weighting a that maximizes the weighted variance of workers' dataset statistics; workers whose deviation from the weighted mean exceeds a threshold η are flagged as poisoned and removed, iteratively until fewer than (1−2α)m workers remain. In the target model training phase, the server runs weighted FedAVG using the trustworthiness weights. The key insight is that directly measuring dataset-level differences via H-divergence-aligned variance is more effective than detecting outliers in high-dimensional gradient space, since gradient dimensionality grows with model size while the variance notion depends only on the VC-dimension d of the hypothesis class. The method achieves attack loss Õ(√(1/n) + √(d/mn)) in IID and Õ(√(1/γ) + √(1/n) + √(d/mn)) in non-IID settings, matching the minimax lower bound as m→∞, and also bounds the Effective Poison Rate (EPR) to limit the adversary's free-ride gain.

## Results

On MNIST (200 normal workers, 50 poisoned, DNN with 2 hidden layers × 128 neurons), the proposed method maintains higher test accuracy and lower attack accuracy than all robust gradient aggregation baselines across varying numbers of local samples n and poison ratios β, with the advantage most pronounced when n is small. On CIFAR-10 with VGG13, all baseline methods fail to suppress the adversary's free ride (attack accuracy ≈ 1 on poisoned data), while the proposed method effectively reduces attack accuracy. Under a matched complexity budget (40% to discriminator training, 60% to target model training) and a matched differential privacy budget, the proposed method still achieves the highest test accuracy and lowest attack accuracy. All comparisons are statistically significant with Wilcoxon test p-values below 0.05.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed discriminator-weighting method outperforms all seven baselines on both test accuracy (attack loss) and attack accuracy (adversary gain) across IID and non-IID settings. Against the three standard robust gradient aggregation methods—geometric median (Blanchard et al. 2017; Chen et al. 2017), iterative filtering (Su & Xu 2018), and Krum (Shejwalkar et al. 2022)—the proposed method achieves consistently higher test accuracy and lower attack accuracy, with the gap widening as n decreases or the model grows larger (since gradient-based methods degrade with higher-dimensional gradients). Against label-flipping-specific defenses LFighter (Jebreel et al. 2024, K-means on last-layer gradients) and SCEL-based robust training (Hallaji et al. 2023), the proposed method is superior, particularly when using a wider network (1024 neurons per hidden layer) that amplifies the gradient-dimensionality weakness of gradient-based approaches. Against robust gradient aggregation with bucketing (Karimireddy et al. 2022) in the non-IID setting (Dirichlet γ=1.0, 30 samples/worker), the proposed method achieves the highest test accuracy and lowest attack accuracy, whereas bucketing improves test accuracy but simultaneously increases attack accuracy with larger bucket sizes. The proposed method also matches FedAVG's communication and computation complexity, whereas the gradient aggregation baselines incur additional server-side overhead from robust mean estimation (e.g., Weiszfeld iterations for geometric median).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of robust gradient aggregation (geometric median, Krum, iterative filtering) that was designed for model poisoning and subsequently applied to data poisoning. Instead of operating on high-dimensional gradient vectors whose dimension grows with model size, the authors introduce a discriminator model that directly differentiates workers' datasets using a variance notion aligned with H-divergence from domain adaptation theory (Ben-David et al., 2010). The minimax lower bound construction follows the two-hypothesis indistinguishability framework of Yin et al. (2018) and Blanchard et al. (2017), but adapts it to the data poisoning setting where corrupted workers are honest and their gradients faithfully reflect poisoned data. The key conceptual departure is that the attack loss bound depends on VC-dimension d rather than gradient dimension d_g, yielding a fundamentally tighter guarantee that does not degrade as models grow larger.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first near-optimal theoretical characterization of data poisoning defense in federated learning, showing that the field's default robust aggregation approach is provably suboptimal for this weaker attack type, with the gap widening as model size increases. The EPR metric offers a practical way to quantify whether an adversary's influence is sub-proportional, complementing the standard attack loss. Key limitations include the α < 1/3 constraint, binary-label theoretical analysis, and experiments limited to MNIST/CIFAR-10 with simple attacks (label-flip, backdoor). Open questions include whether the discriminator approach extends to α ≥ 1/3, multi-label settings, and whether the variance-maximization phase scales efficiently to very large worker populations in production systems.
