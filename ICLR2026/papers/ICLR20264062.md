## Problem

Existing membership inference attack (MIA) research on LLMs focuses almost exclusively on unstructured text, leaving the unique privacy risks of tabular data—its heterogeneous column types, skewed value distributions, and column-level semantics—largely unexplored. No benchmark exists to systematically evaluate how different table encoding formats affect LLM memorization and susceptibility to MIAs, making it impossible to compare attack effectiveness or identify safer serialization strategies.

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes five real-world tabular datasets (WTQ, WikiSQL, TabFact, Adult, California Housing) serialized into six encoding formats (JSON, HTML, Markdown, Key-Value Pair, Key-is-Value, Line-Separated), with long-context tables chunked into 20-row segments to fit model context windows. Half the tables serve as member training records and the remainder as non-member evaluation samples. The three evaluated MIAs (PPL, Min-K% 20.0%, Min-K++ 20.0%) produce per-sample membership scores aggregated into AUROC and TPR@FPR=5% metrics, with the cross-format experiment additionally reporting a 6×6 AUROC heatmap comparing training vs. detection encodings.

## Method

Tab-MIA constructs a controlled benchmark by deduplicating and filtering five public tabular datasets, then serializing each table into six distinct textual encodings that vary in structural redundancy (from flat Line-Separated to tag-heavy HTML/JSON). Four SOTA open-weight LLMs (LLaMA-3.1 8B, LLaMA-3.2 3B, Gemma-3 4B, Mistral 7B) are fine-tuned via QLoRA (4-bit quantized, batch size 2, lr 3e-4) for 1–3 epochs on half the tables, after which three black-box MIAs are applied to the held-out half. The evaluation isolates four axes: encoding format impact, epoch-count sensitivity, cross-format transferability (training in one format, attacking in another), and pretrained-model memorization of Wikipedia-derived tables using GPT-4o-mini-generated synthetic non-members. The key empirical finding is that flat, low-redundancy encodings (Line-Separated, Key-Value Pair) concentrate learning on content tokens and yield AUROC up to 97.7%, while structurally verbose formats (HTML, JSON) disperse attention across non-content tokens and reduce attack success by roughly 10 points.

## Results

Across four LLMs (LLaMA-3.1 8B, LLaMA-3.2 3B, Mistral 7B, Gemma-3 4B) fine-tuned for three epochs on tabular data, the Min-K++ 20.0% MIA achieves AUROC scores up to 97.7% (Mistral 7B on WTQ, Line-Separated format) and 92.6% (Mistral 7B on California Housing, Key-Value Pair format). Even after only one epoch, AUROC scores exceed 60% on short-context datasets, confirming rapid memorization. Line-Separated and Key-Value Pair encodings are most vulnerable (AUROC 89–98%), while HTML and JSON introduce structural redundancy that reduces attack success by roughly 10 percentage points. Larger models (8B/7B) are 10–14 percentage points more vulnerable than smaller models (3B/4B) under identical fine-tuning conditions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates three MIA baselines side-by-side: the PPL (LOSS) attack (Yeom et al., 2018), Min-K% 20.0% (Shi et al., 2024), and Min-K++ 20.0% (Zhang et al., 2025). Min-K++ 20.0% consistently outperforms both PPL and Min-K% across all models, datasets, and encoding formats, achieving the highest AUROC in the majority of configurations (e.g., 97.7% vs. 90.4% and 87.3% for Min-K% and PPL respectively on WTQ with Mistral 7B, Line-Separated). The gap between Min-K++ and PPL is largest on short-context datasets (up to 10+ percentage points) and narrows on long-context datasets where PPL occasionally matches or slightly exceeds Min-K% (e.g., on Adult, PPL reaches 73.9% vs. Min-K++ 71.1% for LLaMA-3.1 8B). Min-K% 20.0% and PPL produce nearly identical AUROC scores in most settings, suggesting that the normalization step in Min-K++ is the critical differentiator. Overall, Min-K++ is the recommended attack for tabular MIA evaluation, while PPL remains a competitive lightweight alternative for long-context tables.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the membership inference attack (MIA) literature—originally developed for unstructured text (Shokri et al. 2017; Carlini et al. 2022; Shi et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2025)—to the underexplored domain of structured tabular data in LLMs. It repurposes the table serialization research (Hegselmann et al. 2023; Sui et al. 2024; Fang et al. 2024), which previously optimized encoding formats for task accuracy, by reframing encoding choice as a privacy variable. Unlike prior MIA benchmarks (BookMIA, WikiMIA, MIMIR) that target free-form text, or MIDST which evaluates diffusion-based synthetic tabular data, Tab-MIA is the first to systematically evaluate MIA vulnerability across six encoding formats and multiple LLM architectures fine-tuned on real tabular datasets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark provides the first actionable evidence that encoding format is a meaningful privacy lever: flat formats (Line-Separated, Key-Value Pair) yield AUROC scores 10–20 points higher than verbose formats (HTML, JSON), giving practitioners a concrete design choice to reduce memorization risk. The finding that partial cross-format generalization persists means defenders cannot rely on obscurity of the training encoding, and that even three epochs of QLoRA fine-tuning produce near-90% AUROC vulnerability underscores that minimal training does not guarantee privacy. Open questions include whether the observed encoding-format effects hold at larger model scales, whether the results generalize to truly sensitive PII (as opposed to public Wikipedia tables), and what principled defenses (beyond format selection) can mitigate tabular memorization without degrading utility.
