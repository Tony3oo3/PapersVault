## Problem

Score-based causal discovery via discrete search has been largely dismissed in favor of continuous optimization methods, partly due to NP-hardness results that do not apply to standard DAG-representable settings and partly because order-based discrete search (e.g., BOSS) is computationally expensive, limiting its ability to escape finite-sample local optima. This paper argues that discrete search is a reasonable and competitive approach to causal structure learning if the computational bottleneck is removed, enabling aggressive exploration of the graph space.

## Input / Output

FLOP takes an observational dataset of n samples over p variables (assumed generated by a linear additive noise model), a BIC penalty parameter λ (default 2), and either a fixed number of ILS restarts or a wall-clock time limit. The data is standardized internally to ensure scale invariance. The output is a CPDAG representing the Markov equivalence class of the learned DAG; the ILS restart count acts as a compute-budget hyperparameter that monotonically improves BIC score without ever degrading the returned graph.

## Method

FLOP builds on BOSS's reinsertion-based order search but introduces four components that yield >100× speedup and enable aggressive discrete optimization. First, grow-shrink parent selection is warm-started from the previous parent set (which changes by at most one node per reinsertion step) and made non-greedy, eliminating the need for BOSS's complex grow-shrink tree caching. Second, local BIC scores are updated via rank-one Cholesky factorization updates (O(k²) per step) rather than full O(k³) recomputation, amortizing cost across the local search trajectory. Third, a principled initial order is constructed by iteratively selecting the variable with smallest residual variance under a running Cholesky decomposition, placing strongly correlated nodes adjacently to avoid finite-sample failures on directed paths. Fourth, an iterated local search metaheuristic perturbs the best-found order by ln(p) random swaps and restarts the local search, trading additional compute for better BIC optimization. The paper proves the modified grow-shrink still recovers the restricted Markov boundary and that FLOP returns a Markovian CPDAG in the sample limit.

## Results

On the primary benchmark (ER graphs, 50 nodes, average degree 8, 1000 samples), FLOP₁₀₀ achieves an average SHD of 1.34 and recovers the exact CPDAG in 60% of instances, compared to 40% for FLOP₀/BOSS and 0% for PC, GES, and DAGMA. On the Alarm network (37 nodes, 46 edges), FLOP₁₀₀ recovers the exact CPDAG in 82% of instances versus 16% for GES, 6% for BOSS, and 0% for PC and DAGMA. On the Barley network (48 nodes, 84 edges), FLOP₁₀₀ achieves 94% exact recovery versus 12% for BOSS and 4% for GES. FLOP is over 100× faster than BOSS at 100 nodes and scales to 500 nodes where BOSS times out.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FLOP consistently outperforms BOSS (Andrews et al., 2023), the closest order-based competitor, with the gap widening as ILS restarts increase: on the Alarm network, FLOP₁₀₀ recovers the exact CPDAG in 82% of cases versus only 6% for BOSS, and on Barley, 94% versus 12%. Against GES (Chickering, 2002), FLOP achieves substantially lower SHD and higher exact recovery across all settings; GES never achieves exact recovery on the primary ER benchmark and finds a better BIC score than the true graph in 0% of cases, while FLOP₁₀₀ does so in 84%. DAGMA (Bello et al., 2022) is outperformed on all settings except the Sachs dataset where it marginally leads (SHD 11.7 vs. 12.58 for FLOP₂₀/BOSS/GES); on the primary ER benchmark DAGMA never recovers the exact CPDAG. PC (Spirtes et al., 2000) is outperformed on all settings and frequently fails to return a valid CPDAG. The exact dynamic-programming algorithm (Silander & Myllymaki, 2006) is matched by FLOP₅₀₀ on dense 25-node ER graphs (56% vs. 58% exact recovery) while FLOP runs faster. DirectLiNGAM (Shimizu et al., 2011) is outperformed on uniform-noise instances and is the worst-performing method on non-linear data. GOLEM (Ng et al., 2020) is dramatically outperformed, with an average SHD of 176.6 versus FLOP₁₀₀'s 1.34 on the primary ER benchmark, and takes over 200 seconds per instance versus ~5 seconds for FLOP₁₀₀.

## Relation to Prior Work

FLOP builds directly on the BOSS order-based search framework (Andrews et al., 2023), which traverses topological orders via reinsertion moves and uses grow-shrink (Margaritis, 2003) for parent selection. The paper departs from BOSS in four key ways: replacing greedy grow-shrink with a non-greedy warm-start variant that reuses the previous parent set, introducing dynamic Cholesky rank-one updates to reduce scoring from O(k³) to O(k²), constructing a principled initial order that places strongly correlated nodes adjacently, and wrapping the local search in an iterated local search (ILS) metaheuristic with order perturbation. Conceptually, the paper positions itself against the growing emphasis on continuous relaxation methods (NOTEARS, DAGMA, GOLEM), arguing that the NP-hardness results often cited to motivate continuous approaches do not apply to standard sparse-DAG discovery settings, and that discrete search has been underexploited due to computational bottlenecks that are now surmountable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reframes a central debate in causal discovery: rather than the optimization algorithm being the bottleneck, the key challenge is designing scoring criteria that identify the true graph as score-optimal in finite samples. FLOP's finding that it frequently finds graphs with better BIC scores than the ground truth (84% of cases on ER graphs with 20 ILS restarts) underscores that finite-sample identifiability, not search efficiency, is the limiting factor. The practical impact is significant: a fast, open-source Rust implementation that scales to 500 nodes and makes the compute-accuracy tradeoff an explicit hyperparameter via ILS restarts. Limitations include the restriction to linear Gaussian ANMs with faithfulness, and performance degrades when these assumptions are violated (non-linear data, misspecified scores), though the paper acknowledges this and argues the optimization component is then working correctly.
