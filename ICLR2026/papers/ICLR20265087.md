## Problem

Learning-augmented online algorithms are typically evaluated via consistency, robustness, and smoothness, but no principled criterion exists for selecting the 'best' algorithm among an infinite class of otherwise incomparable ones. Pareto-optimal algorithms can be brittle under imperceptible prediction error, while tolerance-based algorithms are inefficient when predictions are highly accurate. The paper asks: given the full performance curve of an algorithm across the prediction-error spectrum, how should one objectively compare and select among competing algorithms?

## Input / Output

The framework consumes an input sequence (skiing horizon, price sequence, or interruption time), a prediction that is either a single value y with a bounded range R_y or a distributional prediction μ with a bitonic pdf, plus a robustness requirement r. For distance measures, the user additionally specifies a piecewise-monotone weight function w over R_y; for the risk-based measure, a risk-aversion parameter α ∈ [0,1) is provided. The output is the optimal algorithm parameter (buy time T, acceptance threshold T, or schedule scaling λ) that minimizes the chosen metric, along with the corresponding performance value.

## Method

The paper introduces two families of decision-theoretic metrics for comparing learning-augmented algorithms. First, distance-based measures (weighted L∞ and L1 norms) quantify the gap between an algorithm's performance ratio and that of an ideal r-robust omniscient benchmark I_r, with a user-specified weight function controlling emphasis across the prediction-error range; setting w to a point mass at y recovers Pareto-optimal algorithms. Second, a risk-based measure built on Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR_α) evaluates performance on the worst (1−α) fraction of outcomes under a distributional prediction, interpolating between expected-cost minimization (α=0, recovering distributional consistency of Diakonikolas et al.) and adversarial worst-case analysis (α→1, recovering δ-tolerant guarantees). For each of three problems—ski rental, one-max search, and contract scheduling—the paper derives closed-form or finite-algorithmic solutions for the optimal parameter under each metric, and shows experimentally that the resulting algorithms dominate existing Pareto-optimal and δ-tolerant baselines across the full prediction-error spectrum.

## Results

For ski rental (b=10, r=5), the proposed distance-based and CVaR algorithms achieve average performance ratios between 1.337 and 1.367, substantially outperforming the best Pareto-optimal baseline BP_ρ (1.677) by over 20%. Expected costs range from 11.173 (CVaR α=0.1) to 11.316 (CVaR α=0.9), compared to 16.987–21.234 for BP_ρ variants. For one-max search (M=1000, r=100), MAX and AVG achieve the best performance ratios (4.394 and 4.447) among all methods, beating PO1 (4.630), δ-TOL (10.009), and PO2 (15.685), while CVaR methods trade performance ratio for higher expected profit (up to 35.795 at α=0.1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Benomar & Perchet (2025) Pareto-optimal class BP_ρ, all proposed methods (MAX, AVG, CVaR) dominate across both performance ratio and expected cost for ski rental, with the best BP variant (ρ=b) still 25% worse in performance ratio (1.677 vs 1.337) and 52% worse in expected cost (16.987 vs 11.173). For one-max search, MAX and AVG achieve the best performance ratios (4.394, 4.447), beating PO1 (Sun et al. 2021) at 4.630 and the highly brittle PO2 (Angelopoulos & Kamali 2023) at 15.685, while also improving expected profit over PO1 (15.614/20.528 vs 13.904). The δ-tolerant algorithm (Angelopoulos et al. 2022) has the worst expected profit (5.475) and a poor performance ratio (10.009), confirming its inefficiency for small prediction errors. CVaR methods offer a smooth tradeoff: as α increases from 0.1 to 0.9, the performance ratio worsens (9.771→6.022) but the algorithm hedges more against adversarial outcomes, with expected profit decreasing from 35.795 to 27.500—still far above δ-TOL's 5.475.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of learning-augmented algorithm design and decision theory. It builds on the Pareto-optimal algorithm paradigm (Wei & Zhang 2020; Sun et al. 2021) and tolerance-based smoothness approaches (Angelopoulos et al. 2022), which the authors identify as having complementary deficiencies: brittleness and inefficiency at small errors, respectively. The risk-based analysis generalizes the distributional consistency-robustness framework of Diakonikolas et al. (2021) by introducing α-consistency via CVaR, and adapts the risk-sensitive algorithmic lens of Christianson et al. (2024) to the learning-augmented setting. The distance-based metrics draw conceptual inspiration from weighted ROC curve analysis in classification, repurposing that comparison machinery for online algorithm performance curves.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first principled, quantifiable framework for selecting among an infinite class of otherwise incomparable learning-augmented algorithms, addressing a gap that both Pareto-optimality and tolerance-based approaches leave open. The unification is elegant: choosing specific weight functions or risk parameters recovers known algorithm classes as special cases, validating the framework's generality. Open questions include extending the framework to multi-valued predictions (e.g., packing problems), dynamic predictions accessed multiple times during execution, and problems where the ideal solution is harder to characterize. The experimental gains, while consistent, are moderate and evaluated on synthetic worst-case inputs, leaving open how these metrics perform under realistic prediction distributions.
