## Problem

Existing multimodal LLM benchmarks predominantly evaluate spatial reasoning through verbal or static visual recognition, neglecting the multi-step visual simulation process that humans actually use to solve spatial problems (e.g., mentally folding a cube net or animating tangram pieces). This paper asks whether MLLMs can perform sequential, non-linguistic visual simulations comparable to human mental imagery, or whether they merely pattern-match on surface features.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263155/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each task presents a visual stimulus (a 2D/3D shape, a flat cube net, a tangram grid, a 4-frame video sequence, or a top-down map with an agent arrow) paired with a text query. For the four synthetic tasks, a second evaluation setting interleaves additional images showing intermediate simulation states (e.g., each folding step or each tangram placement) between text annotations, excluding the final answer frame. Outputs are 4-way multiple-choice selections (transformations, temporal frame, perspective) or binary yes/no judgments (cube net validity, tangram solvability), scored as accuracy or F1 respectively.

## Method

STARE is a ~4K-instance benchmark organized into three complexity tiers: foundational geometric transformations (2D rotations/translations/scaling/reflections/shearing and 3D equivalents rendered in Blender), integrated spatial reasoning (programmatically generated cube-net folding and tangram assembly with step-by-step folding/assembly algorithms), and real-world spatial reasoning (temporal frame inference from Objectron videos and perspective-taking from HM3D scenes via Habitat). The key experimental design contrasts two conditions—question-only (testing internal mental simulation) versus question + intermediate visual simulations (testing whether models can integrate externally provided visual steps)—using interleaved image-text prompts rather than a single composite image. Difficulty is controlled by distractor similarity (easy/medium/hard) and number of transformation steps (1/2/3). The paper evaluates 11 closed- and open-source MLLMs plus 5 human annotators, and conducts a detailed error analysis using an LLM-as-judge pipeline that categorizes failures into misperception, flawed spatial simulation, heuristic over-use, and logic inconsistency.

## Results

On the STARE benchmark (~4K instances), the best model o3 achieves 68.1% overall accuracy with visual simulations, still lagging humans (97.1%) by ~29 points. Models perform well on 2D transformations (o3: 89.3% with VSim) but drop to near-random on complex multi-step tasks: cube net folding (o3: 79.4% with VSim vs. 50% random) and tangram puzzles (o3: 82.1% with VSim vs. 50% random). Visual simulations yield inconsistent gains—o3 improves by +11.0% on cube nets and +13.5% on tangrams, while GPT-4o actually degrades on tangrams (−1.0%) and Gemini-2.0 Flash drops on cube nets (−2.1%). Humans achieve near-perfect accuracy (97.1%) but require up to 28.0s on tangram puzzles, reduced to 10.1s with intermediate visual simulations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among closed-source models, o3 leads with 68.1% overall (with VSim), outperforming o1 (57.2%) by 10.9 points and GPT-4o (53.9%) by 14.2 points. The gap between closed-source and open-source models is stark: the best open-source model (Qwen2.5-VL-72B, 42.3%) trails o3 by 25.8 points, and LLaVA-OneVision-72B (31.4%) performs below random chance (34.8%). On the hardest tasks, even o3 struggles: cube net folding reaches only 79.4% (vs. 98.9% human) and perspective reasoning only 42.8% (vs. 98.1% human), a gap of 55.3 points. Visual simulations produce inconsistent effects across models—o3 benefits substantially (+11.0% cube nets, +13.5% tangrams), while GPT-4o degrades on tangrams (−1.0%) and Gemini-2.0 Flash drops on cube nets (−2.1%), indicating that models cannot reliably leverage intermediate visual information. A strong Pearson correlation (r≈0.88) exists between synthetic-task and real-world-task performance across models, confirming that STARE's controlled tasks predict real-world spatial ability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from existing multimodal benchmarks (CLEVR, MMMU, BLINK, MME) that primarily assess static visual recognition or problems reducible to linguistic reasoning, and from spatial-cognition benchmarks (KiVA, RAVEN, TurtleBench, VSI-Bench) where intermediate visual simulation is optional or infeasible to curate. STARE carves new ground by making multi-step visual simulation the central evaluation axis: tasks are explicitly structured so that each transformation step is visually observable, and models are tested both with and without intermediate visual states. The benchmark draws on cognitive-science findings on mental rotation (Shepard & Metzler, 1971), mental animation (Hegarty, 1992), and mental paper folding (Shepard & Feng, 1972) to design tasks that mirror how humans actually solve spatial problems, rather than how they can be rephrased as text.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

STARE reveals a fundamental and quantified gap: even o3, the strongest reasoning model tested, lags humans by ~29 percentage points on cube-net folding and ~14 on tangram puzzles, and visual simulations produce inconsistent (sometimes negative) effects across models, exposing that current MLLMs cannot reliably integrate sequential visual information. The finding that models achieve near-100% accuracy when given the final simulated state but fail to arrive there through multi-step simulation pinpoints internal visual simulation as the core bottleneck, with direct implications for embodied AI, robotics, and AR applications. Open questions include whether training models to self-generate task-aligned visual simulations can close the gap, and whether the synthetic-task findings generalize to cluttered, occluded real-world scenes. The benchmark's modular design (image-only, text-only, image+text, with/without VSim) provides a reusable diagnostic framework for future spatial-reasoning research.
