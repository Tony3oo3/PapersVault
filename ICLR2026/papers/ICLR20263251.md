## Problem

Existing deep learning weather models treat atmospheric dynamics as a single-scale mapping from current to next state, failing to capture the hierarchical interplay between planetary-scale circulations and localized phenomena. This leads to degraded long-horizon forecasts and missed synergistic cross-scale interactions, since distinct spatial scales exhibit different temporal evolution patterns that are not jointly modeled.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263251/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes T historical timesteps of a concatenated surface-and-atmospheric variable grid (10 surface variables plus 5 pressure-level quantities × 13 levels = 75 channels total) at resolutions of 5.625°, 1°, or 0.25°, and directly predicts L future timesteps of the same variable set. For ultra-long horizons beyond L, a block-wise recursive rollout feeds the most recent T predicted states back as input, avoiding the error accumulation of purely autoregressive one-step methods. All variables are standardized using training-set statistics and de-normalized before metric computation.

## Method

STORM decomposes the forecasting problem into three synergistic stages. First, a Hierarchical Earth Embedder progressively downsamples the input via stride-2 convolutions to produce M=3 multi-resolution representations (fine to coarse). Second, a Scale-Bridging Spatio-Temporal Encoder (N=3 layers) applies ViT-style patch embedding and multi-head self-attention at each scale for spatial encoding, injects fine-scale information into coarser scales via stride-2 cross-scale message passing, and models temporal evolution with a lightweight two-layer linear network (fewer than 100 parameters per scale). Third, a Level-Aligned Forecasting Decoder maps each scale's T-step features to L-step predictions via scale-specific linear layers, upsamples to full resolution through depth-matched transposed convolutions, projects to the full variable space with a shared linear layer, and integrates all scales by direct summation (EarthMix). The entire architecture uses only 15.8M parameters, achieving state-of-the-art accuracy with 0.87s inference per 100 samples.

## Results

On the global 5.625° ERA5 benchmark, STORM achieves the lowest weighted RMSE and highest ACC across all 10 atmospheric and surface variables for 24-hour forecasts, e.g., T2M RMSE 0.675 (vs. Triton 0.873, Pangu 1.106), MSLP RMSE 71.8 (vs. Pangu 215.8, FCN 217.1), and Z500 RMSE 79.8 (vs. Pangu 225.7). For long-term 7–10 day forecasts, STORM maintains its lead with T2M RMSE 2.596 and ACC 0.984, while V10 ACC reaches 0.464 (vs. Triton 0.370, Pangu 0.325). The model also outperforms all baselines on continental (1°) and regional (0.25°) high-resolution settings, with the gap widening at longer horizons (e.g., 10-day South America RMSE 3.510 vs. Triton 5.433).

## Baselines & Comparisons

STORM outperforms all six baselines (Triton, Pangu-Weather, FourCastNet, FuXi, SimVP, U-Net) on every variable for both RMSE and ACC in the 24-hour global setting. The closest competitor is Triton, a model specifically designed for short-term forecasting, yet STORM still reduces T2M RMSE by ~23% (0.675 vs. 0.873) and MSLP RMSE by ~23% (71.8 vs. 93.7). The gap is most dramatic on large-scale variables: for Z500, STORM's RMSE of 79.8 is roughly 2.8× lower than Pangu (225.7) and 3.4× lower than FCN (268.5), reflecting the benefit of explicit multi-scale decomposition. In the 7–10 day long-term regime, STORM's advantage persists and even widens for wind and precipitation variables (e.g., V10 ACC 0.464 vs. Triton 0.370, Pangu 0.325; Prec ACC 0.432 vs. Triton 0.305, Pangu 0.288), while SimVP and U-Net degrade most severely. On the 0.25° East Asia regional benchmark, STORM leads Triton by ~15% RMSE at 10 days (200.9 vs. 234.3) and by ~36% over FCN (200.9 vs. 315.6), confirming that the multi-scale design scales to high-resolution regional domains.

## Relation to Prior Work

STORM departs from the dominant paradigm in deep-learning weather forecasting (Pangu-Weather, FourCastNet, FuXi, Triton), which typically learns a single-step state-to-state mapping at a fixed resolution, by explicitly decomposing atmospheric signals into multiple spatial scales and modeling multi-step temporal evolution jointly. Its architectural ingredients draw from three distinct lineages: hierarchical strided convolutions (CNN-style multi-scale representation), patch-based Vision Transformer self-attention for spatial encoding, and lightweight linear temporal encoders borrowed from the time-series forecasting literature. The cross-scale message-passing mechanism (stride-2 convolution injecting fine-scale features into coarser levels) is conceptually analogous to skip connections in U-Net but operates across resolution levels rather than within a single encoder-decoder path, and the final EarthMix summation of scale-specific forecasts has no direct analogue in prior weather models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a compact 15.8M-parameter model can consistently outperform much larger architectures (Pangu at 97M, Fuxi at 661M) across global, continental, and regional resolutions and from 6-hour to 10-day horizons, suggesting that explicit multi-scale decomposition is more effective than brute-force capacity for capturing heterogeneous atmospheric dynamics. The theoretical analysis (Appendix B) provides a bias-variance and condition-number justification for why scale decomposition improves both generalization and optimization, though the proofs are sketched and rely on idealized decomposability assumptions. Open questions include whether the fixed 3-scale hierarchy is optimal for all regions and resolutions, how the method handles extreme or rare events, and whether the linear temporal encoder can be replaced by richer dynamics without losing the efficiency advantage.
