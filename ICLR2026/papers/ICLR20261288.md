## Problem

Most molecular generative models are synthesis-agnostic, proposing molecules that cannot actually be made in a lab. Prior synthesis-aware GAs rely on expensive ML projection models to map arbitrary molecules back onto synthesizable space, incurring training costs and inference overhead while depending on the quality of a hard-to-train compression of a combinatorially large space. This paper asks whether synthesizability can be enforced by construction through the genetic operators themselves, eliminating the need for any projection model.

## Input / Output

For analog search, the method takes a query molecule (as a SMILES string) and proposes a synthesizable analog along with its full synthesis route; for property optimization, it takes a scalar property oracle and returns molecules maximizing that property. In both cases, the search is constrained to a catalog of ~197k Enamine building blocks and 91 SMARTS reaction templates, with routes capped at 5 reaction steps and 1000 Da molecular weight. The output is always a valid synthesis tree (unordered binary tree with blocks at leaves and reactions at internal nodes), guaranteeing a plausible laboratory route by construction.

## Method

SynGA is a genetic algorithm whose individuals are synthesis trees rather than molecules, with custom crossover that fuses compatible subtrees from two parents via a sampled reaction, and five mutation operators (Grow, Shrink, Rerun, Change Internal, Change Leaf) that locally perturb the tree while preserving reaction compatibility. For analog search, a lightweight 1.8M-parameter MLP classifier (trained on 10M sampled routes) filters the ~197k building blocks down to ~100–200 relevant ones per query, which SynGA then searches over with an ε-filtered sampling strategy. For property optimization, a Neural Additive Model (NAM) with a pairwise ranking loss scores building blocks to filter the search space, and this is embedded as the inner loop of a Bayesian optimization algorithm (SynGBO) that uses a Gaussian Process surrogate with UCB acquisition in the outer loop. The key architectural insight is that all genetic operators are defined to produce only reaction-compatible trees, so every individual in the population is synthesizable by construction—no post-hoc projection or validity check is needed.

## Results

On the PMO benchmark (22 tasks, 10k oracle calls), SynGBO achieves a sum-of-top-10 AUC of 16.426, outperforming the best synthesis-agnostic baselines f-RAG (16.301) and GPBO (16.304), as well as the synthesis-aware SynNet (12.610). On LIT-PCBA docking (Vina score, top-100 diverse modes), SynGBO reaches a mean score of −11.11 with only 16k oracle calls, surpassing 3DSynthFlow (−10.88) which requires 64k calls (4× more). For synthesizable analog search on 1k ChEMBL molecules, SynGA(MLP) achieves 0.711 Morgan and 0.694 Scaffold similarity, exceeding SynFormer (0.668/0.667) while guaranteeing 100% route validity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On PMO, SynGBO (16.426) outperforms all synthesis-agnostic baselines including f-RAG (16.301), GPBO (16.304), and Genetic GFN (16.078), as well as the synthesis-aware SynNet (12.610) by a wide margin; standalone SynGA (13.366) trails the unconstrained methods but still beats SynNet. On LIT-PCBA docking, SynGA achieves a mean Vina score of −10.80 with only 16k calls, beating RxnFlow (−10.45), SynFlowNet (−9.99), BBAR (−9.36), and RGFN (−9.20) which all use 64k calls; SynGBO further improves to −11.11, surpassing 3DSynthFlow (−10.88) with 4× fewer evaluations. For ChEMBL analog search, SynGA(MLP) outperforms SynFormer on Morgan (0.711 vs 0.668) and Scaffold (0.694 vs 0.667) similarity and reconstruction rate (0.196 vs 0.190), but slightly trails on Gobbi pharmacophore similarity (0.623 vs 0.635) and is ~3× slower in wall-clock time. On the in-distribution DDS-10 and ZINC datasets, SynFormer outperforms SynGA on reconstruction rate (>10% higher) and similarity, suggesting SynGA's advantage is most pronounced on out-of-distribution or ML-designed queries. Against SynthesisNet and SynFlowNet on PMO subsets, SynGBO wins on both (9.332 vs 7.906; 1.905 vs 1.576), while standalone SynGA is competitive but slightly behind SynthesisNet (7.836 vs 7.906).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior synthesis-aware GAs (SynNet, SynthesisNet, SynFormer) that rely on ML projection models to map arbitrary molecules back onto synthesizable space, instead embedding synthesis constraints directly into the genetic operators by operating on synthesis trees built from building blocks and SMARTS reaction templates. The core SynGA algorithm inherits its inverse-rank parent sampling from MolGA (Tripp & Hernandez-Lobato, 2023), while the model-based variant SynGBO adapts the nested GA-in-Bayesian-optimization structure of GPBO (Tripp & Hernandez-Lobato, 2024) to the synthesis-tree setting. A novel lightweight ML complement—building block filtering via a small MLP classifier (analog search) or a neural additive model (property optimization)—provides task-specific guidance without requiring a full retrosynthesis or projection network.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By enforcing synthesizability by construction rather than post-hoc, SynGA eliminates the need for expensive projection models and guarantees valid routes, making it a practical drop-in module for synthesis-aware workflows. The fact that a lightweight, ML-free GA with only a quarter of the oracle calls matches or beats heavier synthesis-aware baselines on docking, and that SynGBO reaches SOTA on PMO, suggests that search-based methods remain highly competitive when properly constrained. Open questions include extending to multi-objective optimization (e.g., NSGA-II), incorporating 3D structural information into the genetic operators, scaling to larger template sets, and coupling SynGA with retrosynthesis models to cover the full synthesizable space beyond a fixed template library.
