## Problem

Synthetic SFT data generation for knowledge-intensive domains relies on handcrafted, domain-specific rubrics that rarely transfer across fields, and the common optimization loop (write rubric → synthesize → train → inspect → guess tweaks) provides no quantitative feedback about a rubric's actual contribution to downstream performance. The paper identifies a further gap: synthetic and real samples can be close in embedding space yet diverge substantially in their influence on learning, meaning that semantically 'on-distribution' data may still underperform during fine-tuning.

## Input / Output

The method takes seed documents (shorter than 8k tokens, drawn from Goodreads books for HSS or Meditron/PubMed for medical), a frozen target model (e.g., Qwen3-8B-Base), a generator LLM (e.g., Qwen3-235B-Instruct), and a policy/prompter model (initialized from Qwen3-8B-Instruct) as inputs. It produces synthetic QA pairs in JSONL format with system/user/assistant message structure, where each pair is conditioned on a document-specific rubric that specifies quality dimensions (clarity, domain relevance, correctness, parsability, length suitability, etc.) as structured JSON. The influence score for each pair is computed via Adam-compatible gradient cosine similarity against a validation set, requiring a short LoRA warmup of the target model to obtain reference gradients and moment statistics.

## Method

The core technical contribution is an optimizer-aware influence estimator (an Adam-compatible TracIn variant) that quantifies each synthetic sample's contribution to the target model's validation objective by accumulating cosine similarities between validation gradients and Adam update directions across epochs. This signal is used as a verifiable reward in a GRPO-based RL loop where a rubric generator (the policy) explores the rubric space: for each seed document, G rubrics are sampled, a generator model synthesizes QA pairs conditioned on (document, rubric), and the target model scores each pair's training utility. The policy is updated with a clipped policy-gradient objective using group-normalized advantages and a KL trust region against a reference policy, with the reward combining the influence score and lightweight validity checks (formatting, non-trivialness, safety). The key insight is that gradient-space alignment predicts downstream gains far better than embedding-space proximity, motivating the replacement of heuristic rubric engineering with model-impact supervision that explicitly maximizes measured training utility.

## Results

On the Humanities & Social Sciences (HSS) domain, OptimSyn achieves 56.96% on MMLU-pro, 26.07% on SuperGPQA, and 7.85% on HLE with Qwen3-8B-Base, surpassing Qwen3-8B-Instruct (49.87 / 23.44 / 4.66) and all eight SFT baselines on these three benchmarks. On the Medical & Health domain, it reaches 38.28% on SuperGPQA, 80.70% on PubMedQA, and 58.75% on MedQA, outperforming Qwen3-8B-Instruct on SuperGPQA (37.16), PubMedQA (65.70), and MedQA (57.09). The method yields a +27.2% relative gain on HLE in HSS (7.85 vs. 5.70 base) and consistently improves the 8B backbone beyond widely used open SFT corpora across both domains.

## Baselines & Comparisons

In the HSS domain, OptimSyn achieves the best score on MMLU-pro (56.96), SuperGPQA (26.07), and HLE (7.85), surpassing the strongest SFT baselines (WildChat 52.76, OpenHermes 49.56, SynthQuestions 51.71 on MMLU-pro) and Qwen3-8B-Instruct (49.87) by 7+ points on MMLU-pro. However, it trails Qwen3-8B-Instruct on BBH (75.65 vs. 85.21), HellaSwag (78.08 vs. 78.71), and DROP (65.42 vs. 80.73), indicating the gains are concentrated on knowledge-intensive reasoning rather than commonsense or reading comprehension. In the Medical domain, OptimSyn leads all baselines on SuperGPQA (38.28 vs. 37.16 for Instruct, 35.28 for Medical-R1-Distill), PubMedQA (80.70 vs. 79.80 for ReasonMed, 73.90 for Medical-o1), and MedQA (58.75 vs. 58.75 for Medical-o1, 57.81 for Medical-R1-Distill). It loses to Qwen3-8B-Instruct on MMLU-pro (56.97 vs. 60.39) and HealthBench (74.82 vs. 87.70), and to ReasonMed on HLE (10.81 vs. 14.41). Across target model families (Qwen3-4B/8B/14B, Llama3-8B) and generators (Qwen3-235B, GPT-4.1, Gemini-2.5-Pro), the method consistently outperforms the base model on most benchmarks, confirming transferability beyond a single configuration.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Adam-compatible influence estimator from LESS (Xia et al., 2024), which itself extends TracIn (Pruthi et al., 2020) to modern optimizers, and redirects it from its original role in data *selection* to a novel role as an RL *reward signal* for training a rubric generator. In the synthetic-data-generation literature, prior pipelines (WebR, MAmmoTH2, Bonito, Condor, Montessori-Instruct) rely on handcrafted or heuristic rubrics and filters; this work departs by casting rubric construction as a learnable policy optimized via GRPO with influence-based feedback, closing the synthesis-training loop that prior methods leave open. The key conceptual shift is that influence is no longer used to select from a fixed pool or to train the teacher model (as in Montessori-Instruct), but to supervise a separate prompter that shapes what the teacher generates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a practical path to high-quality SFT data in knowledge-intensive, privacy-constrained domains (medicine, law, humanities) where expert rubric curation is infeasible, and the consistent gains across model families (Qwen, Llama), scales (4B–14B), and generators (Qwen3-235B, GPT-4.1, Gemini-2.5-Pro) suggest broad portability. The observation that embedding-space proximity does not predict training influence is a useful diagnostic that explains why seemingly on-distribution synthetic data can underperform. Open questions include the computational cost of per-sample gradient computation at scale, the indirect credit-assignment bottleneck (gradients cannot flow through the frozen generator), and whether influence scores remain a reliable proxy as target models grow substantially larger or as training moves beyond single-epoch SFT.
