## Problem

Despite multilingual pretraining, large language models exhibit persistent failures in language control: they either produce correct answers in the wrong language (language consistency bottleneck) or generate output in the correct language but fail the underlying task (multilingual transfer bottleneck). While prior work has documented English-dominant latent representations, the precise layer-wise localization of language-specific generation behavior remained unclear, and no method existed to selectively correct language consistency without disrupting task reasoning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260002/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The evaluation framework consumes structured multilingual prompts composed of a Preamble, Instruction, and Question in six typologically diverse languages (English, French, Spanish, Arabic, Hindi, Japanese), with four zero-shot variants (monolingual, code-switched, bilingual-answer, English-distractor) that isolate different dimensions of language control. Outputs are scored on two orthogonal axes: task accuracy (correctness regardless of language) and language consistency (proportion of responses whose primary language matches the target, determined via langdetect). For fine-tuning, training instances are augmented with chain-of-thought reasoning traces, and the loss is masked to only the Question, Reasoning, and Answer tokens while Preamble and Instruction remain frozen context.

## Method

The paper first uses logit lens decoding (projecting intermediate hidden states through the unembedding matrix at each layer) combined with mean-pooled hidden-state cosine similarity across parallel English/non-English prompts to reveal a three-phase internal structure: early layers normalize inputs into a shared semantic space, middle layers perform language-agnostic task reasoning, and late layers (e.g., layer 55+ in Qwen-3-32B, layer 20+ in BLOOM-7.1B) drive language-specific generation. Guided by this localization, the authors propose layer-wise selective supervised fine-tuning that updates only the final 1–3 layers (3–5% of total parameters) while freezing all earlier layers, using AdamW with OneCycleLR on 2,500 MMLU business-domain examples across five languages. Post-fine-tuning interpretability analysis confirms that the language-control adjustment is strictly confined to the tuned late layers, with the high cross-lingual similarity signature in the frozen middle layers remaining virtually unchanged, validating that semantic alignment and reasoning capacity are preserved.

## Results

On Qwen-3-32B, selective fine-tuning of only the last 2 layers (1.5B of 32B parameters, ~5%) raises language consistency from 6–11% (code-switched) to 98–100% across all six languages, while preserving task accuracy (e.g., MGSM code-switched: 57.0% → 84.6%; MMLU code-switched: 60.5% → 74.4%). On BLOOM-7.1B, tuning the last layer (0.5B of 7.1B parameters, ~7%) achieves 98–100% language consistency with modest task gains (XQuAD monolingual F1: 4.18% → 20.83%). Full-scope SFT reaches comparable language consistency (>98%) but updates 100% of parameters; random selective SFT is catastrophic, collapsing task accuracy to near 0% for both models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four fine-tuning strategies: pre-finetuning (zero-shot), full-scope SFT (all parameters), random selective SFT (random subset of layers), and the proposed targeted selective SFT (last 1–2 layers). Selective SFT achieves near-parity with full-scope SFT on language consistency (98–100% vs. 99–100%) while updating only 3–5% of parameters (1.5B/32B for Qwen, 0.5B/7.1B for Bloom). On task accuracy, selective SFT trails full-scope SFT by a small margin for Qwen (e.g., MGSM monolingual: 86.80% vs. 90.53%; MMLU code-switched: 74.44% vs. 78.84%) but is essentially equivalent for Bloom. Random selective SFT is catastrophic: it collapses task accuracy to 0–1.7% for both models across all settings, confirming that the choice of *which* layers to tune is critical, not just the fraction. Post-finetuning interpretability analysis (logit lens + hidden-state similarity) confirms that the language-control adjustment is strictly localized to the tuned late layers, with the frozen middle-layer semantic alignment remaining virtually unchanged.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the logit lens interpretability tool (nostalgebraist et al., 2021) from general next-token decoding to tracking language-specific probability trajectories across layers, and combines it with cross-lingual hidden-state cosine similarity to empirically validate the three-phase structure (semantic alignment → reasoning → language output) previously hypothesized by Wendler et al. (2024), Schut et al. (2025), and Zhao et al. (2024). Unlike prior multilingual adaptation methods that use adapters (Pfeiffer et al., 2020), deep supervision (Huo et al., 2025), or full fine-tuning, this work uses the interpretability findings to motivate a targeted layer-wise selective fine-tuning strategy that updates only the final output layers. The four-scenario prompt evaluation framework (monolingual, code-switched, bilingual-answer, English-distractor) is a new diagnostic protocol not present in prior multilingual benchmarking work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: fine-tuning only 3–5% of parameters (last 1–2 layers) achieves >98% language consistency across six languages, matching full-scope fine-tuning while drastically reducing compute. This offers a lightweight path for deploying multilingual LLMs in resource-constrained settings. However, limitations temper the generality: results are demonstrated on only two architectures (Qwen-3-32B, BLOOM-7.1B), six languages, and a business-domain MMLU subset for fine-tuning data; English-distractor task performance remains weak even after selective SFT, suggesting reasoning-level disambiguation is a separate unsolved problem. Open questions include whether the 'last k layers' boundary generalizes across model scales and typologically distant language pairs, and whether the approach transfers to generation tasks beyond multiple-choice and extractive QA.
