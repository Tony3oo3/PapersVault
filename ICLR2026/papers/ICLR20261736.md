## Problem

Existing MLLM benchmarks for visual fraud detection lack real-world academic scenario complexity, fine-grained manipulation diversity, and a principled mapping from fraud types to distinct reasoning capabilities. No prior benchmark simultaneously covers authentic retracted-paper cases, stacked multi-operation manipulations, and multi-dimensional capability evaluation, leaving the true expert-level visual forensic competence of MLLMs unmeasured.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261736/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Inputs are high-resolution PNG scientific figure panels (subgraph units with independent semantics), optionally accompanied by a mask_caption describing numbered regions, multiple co-occurring panels for duplication tasks, or figure captions plus related sentences for cross-modal tasks; images exceeding a model's pixel budget undergo two-stage adaptive compression. Outputs follow strict XML-tagged formats: a single-choice forgery label plus comma-separated region indices for SMF-IL, a multi-select set of manipulation operations for CMO-I, and an inconsistency type plus the minimal inconsistent sentence span with corrected content for CMI-IL, each accompanied by a free-text explanation field.

## Method

THEMIS is constructed in two stages. Stage 1 extracts and parses source material: 1,432 retracted papers from Retraction Watch/PubPeer are screened down to 41 gold-standard cases (194 panels) via multi-annotator review, while 5,253 PubMed Central papers are filtered to 879 high-quality sources yielding 41,422 panels segmented by a customized YOLOv7 detector. Stage 2 generates five fraud types through dedicated pipelines: Splicing uses a weighted multi-dimensional similarity score (40% histogram correlation, 40% SSIM, 20% size) to pair panels, then composites foreground objects via Otsu thresholding, morphological refinement, and alpha blending; Copy-Move leverages SAM for object extraction with adaptive grid placement; AI-Generated forgeries employ Flux for full-image inference and Stable Diffusion/SenseNova/GPT-Image-1 for targeted region editing; Duplication applies global geometric/parameter transforms or a crop-then-transform strategy for local reuse; and Text-Image Inconsistency introduces controlled numerical or trend keyword substitutions with OCR-exclusion and syntactic-consistency constraints. A 16-expert quality control process (~200 hours) removes ~20% of synthetic samples, yielding 4,054 QA pairs (152 real, 3,902 synthetic) with an average of 2.08 stacked operations per image. Evaluation maps the five fraud types to five core reasoning capabilities (Expert Knowledge Utilization, Visual Recognition, Spatial Reasoning, Region Localization, Comparative Reasoning) and introduces a Balanced Robustness Index (BRI) that penalizes cross-task variance, enabling fine-grained diagnosis of model strengths and weaknesses.

## Results

On the THEMIS benchmark (4,054 QA pairs across 5 fraud types), the best-performing model GPT-5 achieves a Balanced Robustness Index (BRI) of only 56.15%, with an average Single-Mode Forgery Identification score of 53.50% and a Localization score of just 24.14%. The open-source Qwen2.5-VL-72B is competitive at 47.16% BRI, while the weakest model, Gemma 3 27B, scores only 9.59%. Localization is markedly harder than identification across all models: GPT-5 drops 55% and OpenAI o4-mini-high drops 61% when moving from identification to localization, whereas Gemini 2.5 Flash shows the smallest decline (14%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-5 leads overall (BRI 56.15%) but is not dominant: Gemini 2.5 Flash surpasses it on SMF Identification (55.47% vs. 53.50%) and SMF Localization (47.52% vs. 24.14%), while OpenAI o4-mini-high edges it out on CMI-IL Identification (66.33% vs. 60.67%) and Localization (32.22% vs. 27.44%). Among open-source models, Qwen2.5-VL-72B (BRI 47.16%) is the strongest, outperforming several proprietary models including Doubao-Seed-1.6-thinking (37.14%) and Gemini 2.5 Pro (31.97%), and approaching Qwen-VL-Max (49.83%). InternVL3.5-8B (38.73%) and Llama 4 Maverick (34.78%) form a mid-tier group, while the LLaVA family (18–24%) and Gemma 3 27B (9.59%) trail significantly. A notable pattern is that no single model excels across all five fraud types: GPT-5's Duplication F1 drops from 38% to 17% as stacked manipulations increase from one to three, and its Region Localization capability (24.14%) is less than half its Visual Recognition score (53.50%), revealing a universal imbalance in expert-level forensic reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against a lineage of image forensics benchmarks (FakeBench, SHIELD, Forensics-Bench, MFC-Bench) that the authors argue are insufficient for probing expert-level visual reasoning in MLLMs, citing their limited fraud-type coverage, coarse binary-classification metrics, or reliance on purely synthetic data. It also departs from general MLLM evaluation benchmarks (MMBench, MMMU, MathVista) by arguing that those tasks allow visual content to be abstracted into text, thereby masking true visual acuity. THEMIS carves new ground by grounding evaluation in authentic retracted-paper cases (sourced from Retraction Watch and PubPeer), introducing 16 fine-grained manipulation operations with stacked composites, and establishing a principled mapping from five fraud types to five distinct reasoning capabilities (Expert Knowledge Utilization, Visual Recognition, Spatial Reasoning, Region Localization, Comparative Reasoning).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that even the strongest model (GPT-5) achieves only 56.15% BRI, with a dramatic 55% performance drop from identification to localization and a near-halving of F1 on Duplication as stacked manipulations increase from one to three—findings that underscore a substantial gap between current MLLM capabilities and the demands of real-world academic forensics. The 'AI for AI Governance' framing is timely: as generative models lower the barrier for fabricating scientific figures, the community lacks scalable, automated verification tools. Open questions include whether the localization deficit can be addressed through architectural changes (e.g., explicit spatial grounding modules), whether the compound-manipulation vulnerability reflects a fundamental limitation in current visual encoders, and whether the benchmark's synthetic data (designed to match real-data difficulty) will remain representative as forgery techniques evolve.
