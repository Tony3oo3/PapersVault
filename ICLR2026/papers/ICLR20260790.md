## Problem

Online on-policy alignment methods (PPO, GRPO) consistently outperform offline off-policy methods (DPO, KTO) by 1.3–1.6× in winrate, but the reason has been attributed to RL-theoretic factors like data coverage or search-space simplicity. This paper asks whether the online/offline dichotomy is actually necessary for maximizing human utility, or whether the performance gap can be explained and closed by a human-centric mechanism rooted in how people perceive probability.

## Input / Output

The humanline variants accept the same data formats as their base objectives—preference pairs (x, y_w, y_l) for DPO, unpaired labeled feedback for KTO, and groups of outputs with scalar rewards for GRPO—but critically, this data can be sourced from anywhere (offline, off-policy, from better or worse models) rather than requiring fresh on-policy sampling. The output is a standard aligned LLM producing text completions; no architectural changes are made. The key constraint is that offline data must have sufficiently high average token log-probability under the reference model at training start (above roughly −1.03 nats/token) to avoid performance degradation from excessive clipping.

## Method

The paper proposes a two-part design pattern that converts any alignment objective into its 'humanline' variant: (1) humanline syncing, where the reference model is periodically overwritten with the policy's weights every k steps (after loss computation, before the optimizer step), and (2) humanline clipping, where token-wise log-probability ratios π_θ(y_t|x,y_<t)/π_ref(y_t|x,y_<t) are asymmetrically clamped to [log ε_P, log ε_R] (e.g., [−1.5, 1.5]) upstream of the loss function, before any objective-specific operations. The theoretical justification is that PPO/GRPO's standard clipping is proven (Theorem 4.3) to be a limit-case special case of 'humanline sampling,' a rejection-sampling procedure that simulates drawing tokens according to the prospect-theoretic weighting function ω, which overweights extreme outcomes and underweights typical ones. By applying this perceptual distortion explicitly and syncing the reference model to track the evolving policy, the humanline variant makes offline off-policy data behave as if it were sampled from the human-perceived distribution, closing the performance gap with online alignment while running up to 6× faster.

## Results

On instruction-following (AlpacaEval2, Llama3-8B-Instruct), offline+humanline variants close the 1.3×–1.6× win-rate gap with their online counterparts: offline+humanline GRPO achieves 25.24 LC-WR / 18.11 WR versus 25.05 / 18.82 for online GRPO, while plain offline GRPO only reaches 15.52 / 12.61. On mathematical reasoning (MATH500, Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct), humanline GRPO with data sampled 64× less frequently matches online GRPO's Pass@1 of 0.593 ± 0.019, whereas the same 64× reduction without humanline causes significant degradation (p < 0.05). Offline+humanline GRPO trains over 6× faster than online GRPO while attaining equal performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares humanline variants against three standard alignment objectives—DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023), KTO (Ethayarajh et al., 2024), and GRPO (Shao et al., 2024)—in both their offline and online forms. For all three objectives, the offline+humanline variant substantially outperforms the plain offline variant (e.g., GRPO: 18.11 WR vs. 12.61 WR, a 1.44× improvement) and matches the online variant (18.11 vs. 18.82 WR), effectively closing the performance gap that prior work attributed to the need for on-policy data. The online+humanline variant further improves upon the online baseline (21.57 vs. 18.82 WR for GRPO), though the marginal gain is smaller than the offline→offline+humanline jump, consistent with the theory that on-policy sampling already approximates the human-perceived distribution. On MATH500, humanline GRPO matches online GRPO (both 0.593 Pass@1) while sampling data 64× less frequently, a regime where standard offline GRPO degrades significantly. The key practical takeaway is that the online/offline dichotomy is not fundamental: with humanline syncing and clipping, off-policy data can be used at 6× lower wall-clock cost without sacrificing alignment quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Ethayarajh et al.'s (2024) prospect-theoretic framing of LLM alignment (KTO) by incorporating the weighting function—the probability distortion component—that prior work explicitly ignored. It further shows that PPO/GRPO's clipped objective, originally introduced solely for training stability (Schulman et al., 2017; Shao et al., 2024), is a special case of the perceptual bias formalized in prospect theory (Tversky & Kahneman, 1992), thereby unifying the online and offline alignment literatures under a single human-centric explanation. The paper departs from RL-theoretic explanations of the online/offline gap (Song et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2024a; Swamy et al., 2025) by arguing the dichotomy is incidental: what matters is whether training data reflects the human-perceived distribution over outcomes, not whether it is on-policy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is significant: humanline variants allow post-training with arbitrary offline data at up to 6× lower wall-clock cost while matching online alignment performance, and enable 64× less frequent sampling for verifiable tasks. However, the matching of online performance is an empirical regularity rather than a formal guarantee, and the extension of prospect theory from monetary gambles to generative model outputs remains an unvalidated assumption. Open questions include identifying principled data-quality criteria beyond average token log-probability, determining whether online on-policy data is ever truly necessary, and whether the perceptual parameters (γ, λ, α) should be personalized per user or task.
