## Problem

Softmax attention consistently outperforms alternatives (linear, kernelized, state-space models) on retrieval tasks in large language models, yet the theoretical reasons for this advantage remain poorly understood. Most prior theoretical work has focused on the easier-to-analyze linearized attention, leaving a gap between empirical evidence and principled explanation. This paper addresses that gap by providing a rigorous high-dimensional analysis of why softmax is superior for single-location information retrieval.

## Input / Output

The input is a variable-length sequence X ∈ R^{L×D} of L tokens (L drawn from a discrete distribution P_L over {1,…,L̄}), along with two hidden unit-norm directions k* and v* ∈ R^D. The scalar label y depends only on a single token at a random position ε* ∈ {1,…,L}, specifically y = (1/√D) X_{ε*} v* + Δξ, making ε* recovery an in-context learning sub-problem. Two task variants are studied: spiked-SLR (a spike of strength √ν·k* is added at position ε*) and max-SLR (ε* is the argmax of token–k* inner products), both parameterized by a signal strength ν ≥ 0.

## Method

The paper develops a statistical-physics analysis of attention predictors f_{σ,k,v}(X) = σ(χ)ᵀz where χ = Xk/√D and z = Xv/√D, in the proportional high-dimensional limit (N,D→∞ at fixed α=N/D). At the population level, they show softmax achieves the Bayes risk via the Nishimori condition (the softmax weights match the posterior over ε*), while linear attention's risk scales as O(1/ν) or O(log L/L) depending on the regime. At finite sample complexity, they derive a closed-form characterization of the ERM test error as a minimization over six order parameters (m_k, m_v, q_k, q_v, V_k, V_v) satisfying self-consistent fixed-point equations derived via the replica method. The analysis reveals that softmax's advantage stems from both the exponential nonlinearity and the normalization across tokens, and that the gap to Bayes-optimal performance closes as α→∞. Numerical experiments confirm that gradient-based optimization recovers the predicted ERM performance.

## Results

In the population risk setting, softmax attention provably achieves the Bayes risk (Proposition 4.2), while linear attention fundamentally falls short. For the spiked-SLR model with strong signal ν→∞, the minimal risk of linear attention scales as E_lin ~ L/(L−1)·1/ν, whereas softmax decays exponentially as E_softmax = e^{−c_L ν + o(ν)}. For the max-SLR model at ν→∞ with growing sequence length L, linear attention error converges to 1 − O(log L / L) (approaching the trivial null predictor), while softmax achieves zero error for all L. At finite sample complexity (α = N/D), Result 5.1 shows softmax consistently outperforms linear attention across all tested signal strengths and sampling ratios, with the gap to Bayes-optimal risk closing as α grows.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares softmax attention against three alternatives: linear attention (σ(χ)_ℓ = 1+χ), element-wise sigmoidal nonlinearity (σ(χ)_ℓ = 1+erf(c+χ_ℓ)), and softplus kernelized attention (σ(χ)_ℓ = softplus(χ_ℓ)/Σ softplus(χ_ℓ')). Softmax is the only activation that achieves the Bayes risk in the population setting; all three alternatives fall strictly between linear and softmax in performance. Linear attention suffers most from variable sequence lengths (its risk degrades with Var(L) due to poor normalization), while the normalized softplus kernel avoids this issue but still lags softmax, with the gap widening for larger L because softplus does not grow fast enough at +∞ to dominate noise from irrelevant tokens. The element-wise erf activation, being unnormalized, also degrades with variable L. At finite sample complexity (Figure 3, L=3), softmax maintains a consistent advantage over linear attention across all signal strengths ν and sampling ratios α, and the predicted ERM performance is confirmed by quasi-Newton optimization from random initialization, indicating that the non-convex landscape does not trap the optimizer in bad local minima for appropriate regularization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the single-location regression (SLR) framework of Marion et al. (2025) by introducing random sequence lengths and a generic weighting mechanism g_ν that unifies spiked and max-correlation variants. The analytical toolkit draws on the sequence multi-index model framework of Cui (2025) and the Bayes-optimal error computations of Troiani et al. (2025), applying the replica method and order-parameter formalism from statistical physics to the attention setting. Crucially, while prior theoretical work on attention (Ahn et al., von Oswald et al., Bai et al.) has focused on the analytically tractable linearized attention, this paper tackles the full softmax nonlinearity, showing that the Nishimori condition from statistical physics is the key to understanding why softmax is optimal. It also positions itself against concurrent work by Dragutinović et al. (2025) on softmax vs. linear attention, but on a different task (in-context classification) and with different proof techniques.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This paper provides the first principled theoretical explanation for the empirically observed superiority of softmax attention over linear and kernelized alternatives on retrieval tasks, showing that softmax achieves the Bayes risk at the population level while linear attention fundamentally cannot. The decomposition revealing that both the exponential nonlinearity and the normalization are independently necessary (softplus kernel without sufficient growth, or element-wise sigmoid without normalization, both fall short) gives concrete guidance for designing alternative attention mechanisms. Open questions include a formal proof of the replica symmetry assumption, a complete landscape analysis of the non-convex empirical risk, and whether these insights extend to multi-layer or multi-head settings. The simplified single-layer, single-location setting limits direct transfer to practical LLMs, but the framework offers a testbed for understanding architectural tradeoffs.
