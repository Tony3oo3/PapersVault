## Problem

Estimating heterogeneous treatment effects (HTEs) from right-censored survival data is critical for precision medicine, yet no standardized benchmark exists to fairly compare the growing suite of causal survival methods. Prior evaluations rely on bespoke simulations with inconsistent censoring levels, survival distributions, and causal assumptions, making cross-paper comparisons impossible and obscuring which methods are robust under realistic assumption violations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260216/cropped_page6_idx8.webp)

## Input / Output

Each method receives tabular covariates X (5 uniform features in synthetic data; 23–36 real features in semi-synthetic/real data), a binary treatment indicator W, censored event times T̃ = min(T, C), and event indicators δ. The target estimand is the conditional average treatment effect (CATE) on restricted mean survival time (RMST) at a user-specified horizon h, evaluated via CATE RMSE and ATE bias over 10 random splits. The benchmark outputs Borda count rankings across 53 method variants, with family-level rankings obtained by selecting the best variant per family on a validation set before test evaluation.

## Method

SURVHTE-BENCH unifies 53 survival HTE estimators into three families—outcome imputation (42 variants pairing Margin, IPCW-T, or Pseudo-observation imputation with S/T/X/DR-Learners, Double-ML, or Causal Forest), direct-survival CATE (Causal Survival Forests and SurvITE), and survival meta-learners (9 variants pairing S/T/Matching learners with Random Survival Forests, DeepSurv, or DeepHit)—and evaluates them across a modular 8×5 grid of synthetic datasets that independently vary causal configurations (RCT, ignorability, positivity, ignorable censoring) and survival scenarios (Cox, AFT, Poisson with low-to-high censoring). Semi-synthetic datasets (ACTG HIV, MIMIC-IV ICU) and two real datasets (Twins with known ground truth, ACTG 175 without) extend evaluation to realistic covariate structures. The key empirical finding is that no single method dominates: outcome imputation methods (especially Double-ML and X-Learner) excel in low-censoring randomized settings, while survival meta-learners built on DeepSurv (S-Learner-Survival, Matching Survival) demonstrate superior robustness under high censoring, unobserved confounding, and multiple simultaneous assumption violations.

## Results

Across 40 synthetic datasets (8 causal configurations × 5 survival scenarios), Borda count rankings show S-Learner-Survival (avg. rank 5.17/53) and Matching Survival (5.42) as the top individual variants, with Double-ML + Margin imputation (6.65) third. At the method-family level, S-Learner-Survival (3.30/11) and Matching-Survival (3.48) lead, followed by Double-ML (3.98) and Causal Survival Forests (5.10). On the semi-synthetic ACTG dataset (51% censoring), Double-ML achieves the lowest CATE RMSE (10.651 ± 0.239), while on the Twins dataset (84.8% censoring), S- and DR-Learners with imputation and S-Learner-Survival achieve the best RMSE (~7.2 days). Under high censoring (Scenario D, >70%), S-Learner-Survival (rank 1.6) and Matching-Survival (2.4) dramatically outperform all other approaches.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The benchmark compares 53 variants across three families. Double-ML (Chernozhukov et al.) achieves the best CATE RMSE on ACTG (10.651) and ranks highly in low-censoring RCT settings (rank 3.60 in RCT-50), but degrades under high censoring. Causal Forest (Athey et al.) is competitive in balanced RCTs (rank 5.60 in RCT-50) but drops sharply under positivity violations. Causal Survival Forests (Cui et al.) and SurvITE (Curth et al.) handle censoring natively and rank well in high-censoring scenarios, though CSF is vulnerable to positivity violations and SurvITE shows the worst ACTG RMSE (12.714). The survival meta-learners from Noroozizadeh et al. and Bo et al. (S-Learner-Survival, T-Learner-Survival, Matching Survival) demonstrate the most consistent top-3 performance across all 40 synthetic datasets, with S-Learner-Survival achieving the best overall Borda rank (5.17/53) and dominating under high censoring (rank 1.6 in Scenario D). Standard meta-learners (Künzel et al.: S-, T-, X-Learner) and DR-Learner (Kennedy) perform well in moderate-censoring, well-specified settings but T-Learner drops to last place (rank 9.00) under imbalanced treatment (RCT-5). No single method dominates: the optimal choice depends on censoring rate, treatment balance, and which causal assumptions are violated.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three previously disparate families of survival HTE estimators—outcome imputation methods (building on Qi et al.'s imputation strategies and standard meta-learners from Künzel et al.), direct-survival CATE methods (Causal Survival Forests from Cui et al., SurvITE from Curth et al.), and survival meta-learners (from Bo et al. and Noroozizadeh et al.)—into a single modular framework of 53 variants. While benchmarks exist for HTE with fully observed outcomes (Crabbé et al., Shimoni et al., Kapkiç et al.) and for survival ATE estimation (Voinot et al.), no prior work provided a standardized evaluation protocol for survival HTE under systematic assumption violations. The paper departs from prior method papers that each relied on bespoke simulations with narrow assumption variations by crossing 8 causal configurations with 5 survival scenarios to create a controlled stress-testing environment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This benchmark fills a critical gap in causal survival inference by providing the first reproducible, extensible platform for comparing HTE estimators under realistic assumption violations (unobserved confounding, positivity failure, informative censoring) across synthetic, semi-synthetic, and real data. The key practical finding—that no single method dominates and that survival meta-learners (particularly S-Learner-Survival and Matching Survival) exhibit robustness under severe violations while Double-ML and Causal Forest excel in well-specified low-censoring settings—gives practitioners concrete guidance for method selection. Limitations include restriction to binary static treatments, fixed baseline covariates, and binary (present/absent) rather than graded assumption violations; open questions include extending to time-varying treatments, instrumental variables, multi-valued treatments, and incorporating severity-graded sensitivity analyses (e.g., Rosenbaum's Γ for unmeasured confounding).
