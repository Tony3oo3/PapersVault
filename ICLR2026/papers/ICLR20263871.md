## Problem

Prior robotic failure detection approaches either reduce the task to closed-set classification over a predefined taxonomy of failure modes or assume dense human annotations for every episode, making them brittle to the subtle, combinatorial, and open-ended failures encountered in real deployments. The core gap is how to train a model that simultaneously performs accurate binary detection and produces nuanced, human-like natural language reasoning when supervision is heterogeneous—large-scale sparse binary labels paired with a small fraction of expensive reasoning annotations.

## Input / Output

For simulation datasets (RLBench, Maniskill), input is a collaged matrix of multi-view frames (front, wrist, overhead) across timesteps; for real-world datasets (Sparrow, ARMBench), input is raw video resized to 320×320 and sampled at 5 FPS, in both cases accompanied by a task prompt describing the sub-task. The model produces two distinct outputs through separate heads: a binary success/failure label from a classification head and a free-form natural language explanation from the autoregressive language decoder, avoiding the brittle regex-based answer extraction required by single-stream baselines.

## Method

ARMOR fine-tunes Qwen2.5-VL with a lightweight classification head (cross-attention over mean-pooled intermediate decoder features with a learnable [CLS] token) for detection and the original LM decoder for reasoning, sharing all backbone parameters. Training proceeds in two phases: offline imitation (warm-up on unconditioned inputs, then expert-conditioned training where the model sees ground-truth prior predictions with one task's input randomly masked to prevent copying) followed by online refinement (T-round rollouts where the model conditions on its own prior outputs and is supervised with BCE for detection on all data and NTP for reasoning only on dense data). At inference, ARMOR samples M stochastic refinement trajectories over T rounds, computes a combined entropy score (detection entropy + λ·reasoning token entropy) per trajectory, and selects the lowest-entropy prediction as the final output, terminating early when the best trajectory's entropy stops decreasing beyond a tolerance ε.

## Results

ARMOR achieves state-of-the-art performance across all four datasets. On RLBench, it reaches 91.7% detection accuracy and 0.718 LLM Fuzzy Match score, outperforming the best fine-tuning baseline (SFT-S+D) by +26.3% in detection and +30.4% in reasoning. On the cross-domain transfer setting Maniskill (R→M), ARMOR achieves 99.0% detection accuracy, and on ARMBench (S→A) it scores 0.698 LLM Fuzzy, substantially exceeding SFT-S+D (0.007) which collapses under distribution shift. The paper reports up to 30% improvement in detection rate and up to 100% improvement in reasoning over prior approaches.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ARMOR outperforms all open-source VLM baselines (Qwen2.5-VL, Cosmos-Reasoning, LLaVA-NeXT) by large margins on both detection and reasoning; these models hover near random-guessing (50%) detection accuracy, confirming that generic instruction-tuned VLMs require task-specific alignment. Against the strongest proprietary baseline, Claude-3.7 with 3-shot prompting, ARMOR wins on detection across all four datasets (e.g., 91.7% vs. 56.1% on RLBench, 73.3% vs. 65.0% on Sparrow) and on reasoning for RLBench (0.718 vs. 0.473 LLM Fuzzy) and Sparrow (0.503 vs. 0.407), though Claude-3.7 (3-shot) edges out ARMOR slightly on ARMBench ROUGE-L (0.725 vs. 0.721). Compared to the fine-tuning baselines, ARMOR improves over SFT-D by +25.6% detection on Maniskill (R→M) and +13.3% on ARMBench (S→A), while SFT-S+D's reasoning collapses from 0.644 to 0.177 (Maniskill) and 0.609 to 0.007 (ARMBench) under distribution shift, demonstrating that naively mixing sparse and dense data in a single NTP objective prevents generalization. The ablation (Table 2) further shows that the multi-task architecture alone achieves 0.897 detection but only 0.460 reasoning, and that combining offline imitation, online refinement, and inference-time entropy-based selection is necessary to reach the full 0.917/0.718 performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

ARMOR extends the iterative self-refinement paradigm from RISE (Qu et al., 2024) to multi-modal robotic failure understanding, replacing RISE's oracle reward model with online imitation learning (Ross et al., 2011) over heterogeneous supervision. It departs from AHA (Duan et al., 2025), the closest prior work, in two key ways: AHA treats detection and reasoning as a single free-form generation task over a closed set of predefined failure modes with uniformly dense labels, whereas ARMOR uses separate classification and generation heads and handles the realistic imbalance between cheap binary labels and expensive reasoning annotations. The inference-time trajectory selection draws on self-certainty scoring (Kang et al., 2025), and the use of a generative VLM backbone for classification follows Zhang et al. (2024b).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically important gap: real-world robotic failures are combinatorial and hard to enumerate, yet prior methods either restrict to predefined failure taxonomies or require expensive dense annotations for every episode. ARMOR's demonstration that iterative multi-task refinement over a VLM can simultaneously improve detection (up to 30%) and open-ended reasoning (up to 100%) under heterogeneous supervision offers a viable path toward interpretable, generalizable failure monitoring in deployment. Open questions remain around the compute cost of multi-trajectory refinement, the reliability of entropy-based selection in safety-critical settings, and whether the approach extends to richer sensor modalities (force/torque, proprioception) that capture failure causes invisible in video alone.
