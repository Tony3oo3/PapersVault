## Problem

While prior work has documented that pretrained LLMs exhibit alignment with human brain activity during language comprehension, it remains unclear whether this alignment is functionally necessary for linguistic competence or merely a byproduct of pretraining. The paper asks a causal question: if you deliberately remove brain-relevant information from a language model's representations while preserving its language modeling ability, does its performance on downstream linguistic tasks degrade?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262809/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes pretrained transformer models (BERT-base-cased, GPT-2 small, Llama-3.2-1B) along with fMRI recordings from two datasets (Harry Potter: 8 participants reading word-by-word at 0.5s/word with 2s TR; Moth Radio Hour: 6 participants reading 11 stories, 4028 images each). Text stimuli are segmented into sequences spanning 5 TRs, and only voxels with estimated noise ceiling > 0.05 in language-related regions are targeted. The output is a set of fine-tuned models (Brain Misaligned, Brain Preserving, Brain Tuned) whose representations are evaluated via linear probe prediction of voxel values (Pearson correlation) and via classifier-based probing on 200+ Holmes benchmark datasets covering syntax, semantics, morphology, discourse, and reasoning.

## Method

The core intervention is adversarial fine-tuning with a gradient reversal layer (GRL), applied via LoRA to the pretrained model. The model is trained with two simultaneous objectives: a standard language modeling cross-entropy loss (weight ω_lm=0.1) and a brain-alignment loss defined as mean negative squared Pearson correlation between predicted and ground-truth voxel values (weight ω_ba=10). In the Brain Misaligned model, a GRL is inserted before the brain mapping head so that gradients from the brain prediction loss actively push representations to become uninformative about brain activity. The Brain Preserving control uses the identical architecture but permutes fMRI image order to break stimulus-brain correspondence, isolating the effect of alignment from the effect of adversarial training itself. A complementary Brain Tuned model removes the GRL to actively increase alignment, providing a positive-direction validation. Training runs for 5 epochs with batch size 16 and AdamW, using cross-validation over four held-out fMRI runs per participant.

## Results

Brain Misaligned models show a significant drop in brain alignment (Pearson correlation with fMRI voxels) compared to Brain Preserving models, with the effect most pronounced in language-related regions (e.g., BERT on Harry Potter: significant drop for 6/8 participants, p<0.05 Wilcoxon signed-rank test). On the Holmes linguistic competence benchmark (200+ datasets), Brain Preserving models significantly outperform Brain Misaligned models overall (p<0.05), with the largest competence drops in semantics and syntax subfields. As a complementary validation, Brain Tuned models (trained to increase brain alignment) consistently outperform Brain Preserving models across all model-dataset combinations (p<0.001), confirming the causal role of brain alignment in supporting linguistic competence.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between Brain Misaligned and Brain Preserving models, which are matched on language modeling performance (verified via Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p<0.05) but differ in brain alignment. Brain Preserving models win on overall linguistic competence across BERT-Harry (p<0.001), BERT-Moth (p<0.01), and Llama-Harry (p<0.001); GPT2-Harry shows a near-significant trend (p=0.055). The effect is strongest in semantics and syntax: BERT-Harry shows significance across all five subfields, while BERT-Moth and Llama-Harry show significance specifically in semantics and syntax. As a second validation, Brain Tuned models outperform Brain Preserving models in every experimental setting (p<0.001), with significant subfield-level gains in syntax and semantics. Brain Tuned models also outperform the original Pretrained models in many settings (e.g., BERT-Moth p<0.05, GPT2-Moth p<0.001, Llama-Harry p<0.001), though the Pretrained model retains an edge in some configurations, suggesting brain-tuning amplifies but does not fully replace pretraining benefits.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the brain-LLM alignment literature (Toneva & Wehbe, 2019; Schrimpf et al., 2021; Goldstein et al., 2022) from correlational measurement to causal intervention, borrowing the gradient reversal layer technique from domain-adversarial training (Ganin et al., 2016) to deliberately remove brain-predictive information from LLM representations. It also builds on recent work showing that brain-tuning improves downstream performance (Moussa et al., 2025; Negi et al., 2025) and aligns with the Causal NLP tradition of using counterfactual interventions to test whether a property is causally implicated in model behavior (Feder et al., 2021; 2022). The evaluation leverages the Holmes benchmark (Waldis et al., 2024) for comprehensive linguistic competence assessment, positioning the study at the intersection of cognitive science, interpretability, and causal inference in NLP.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This paper provides the first causal evidence that brain alignment is not merely a correlational byproduct of pretraining but is functionally necessary for robust linguistic competence, particularly in semantics and syntax. The complementary Brain Tuned model results strengthen the claim by showing that increasing alignment yields consistent gains across all experimental settings. Key limitations include reliance on only two fMRI datasets with limited participant counts, a benchmark that underrepresents some subfields (e.g., discourse), and the fact that some model-dataset combinations (notably GPT2) show weaker or inconsistent effects. Open questions include whether these findings generalize to larger models, other languages, non-text modalities, and whether the specific brain regions targeted (language ROIs) are the critical ones or whether broader representational alignment matters.
