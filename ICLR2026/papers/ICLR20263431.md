## Problem

In overparameterized linear regression under minimum-ℓp interpolation, it is well understood that p shapes the inductive bias (sparser toward p=1, denser toward p=2), but how the full family of parameter norms {‖ŵ_p‖_r}_{r∈[1,p]} scales with sample size n had remained unresolved. Specifically, which ℓr norms plateau versus continue to grow, at what exponents, and how the interaction between r and p determines this behavior were open questions. This matters because many generalization proxies depend on a parameter norm, yet the choice of r relative to the operative p can produce qualitatively opposite scaling behaviors.

## Input / Output

The theory takes an overparameterized linear regression instance (isotropic Gaussian design X ∈ R^{n×d} with d/n → κ > 1, s-sparse ground truth w*, noise level σ, and minimum-ℓp interpolator with p ∈ (1,2]) and produces, with high probability, a closed-form two-regime characterization of ‖ŵ_p‖_r for every r ∈ [1,p]. The output is structured around two key invariants: a data-dependent transition size n_* (the 'elbow' where spike and bulk contributions in X^T Y balance) and a universal threshold r_* = 2(p−1) that separates norms that plateau (r > r_*) from those that grow with an explicit exponent (r ≤ r_*) in the spike-dominated regime. The formulas give plateau levels and growth slopes in both the bulk-dominated (n ≪ n_*) and spike-dominated (n ≫ n_*) regimes, with all hidden constants depending only on (p, κ_bulk, r).

## Method

The core technique is a one-dimensional 'dual-ray' analysis: restricting the dual variable to the ray λ = tY reduces the min-ℓp interpolation to a single diagnostic scale t_* = (‖Y‖₂²/‖X^T Y‖_q^q)^{1/(q−1)}, where q = p/(p−1). The key decomposition is of ‖X^T Y‖_q^q into a 'spike' contribution from signal coordinates (scaling as n^q W_q) and a 'bulk' contribution from null coordinates (scaling as (d−s)m_q τ_s^q n^{q/2}); their balance defines the elbow n_* ∝ (κ_bulk τ_s^q / W_q)^{2/(q−2)}. The KKT map then converts dual correlations to primal coordinates via a (q−1)-power, and the sign of 1/r − 1/(2(p−1)) dictates whether each ℓr term decays (plateau) or grows (explicit slope), yielding the sharp threshold r_* = 2(p−1). For diagonal linear networks, the initialization scale α is calibrated to an effective p_eff(α) by matching the log-log slope of the separable potential Q_α on k-sparse unit-ℓ₂ probes to the exact k^{1−p/2} law of ‖·‖_p^p, providing a data-free bridge that lets the explicit-ℓp theory predict DLN norm scaling empirically.

## Results

The paper's central result (Theorem 3.1) identifies a universal threshold r_* = 2(p−1) that, in the spike-dominated regime (n ≫ n_*), separates ℓ_r norms that plateau (r > r_*) from those that continue growing with exponent 1/r − 1/(2(p−1)) (r ≤ r_*). The data-dependent transition size is n_* ∝ (κ_bulk · τ_s^q / W_q)^{2/(q−2)}, where q = p/(p−1). Empirically, with d = 50,000 and σ = 0.1, the predicted elbow for p = 1.5 and a single-spike target appears near n ≈ 1.4×10³, and DLNs calibrated via α → p_eff(α) (α ∈ {0.00102, 0.0664, 0.229} ≈ p ∈ {1.1, 1.5, 1.9}) reproduce the same elbow/threshold structure as explicit minimum-ℓ_p interpolation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without traditional method-vs-method benchmarks; the 'comparisons' are between theoretical predictions and empirical observations, and between explicit and implicit bias regimes. The key empirical comparison is between explicit minimum-ℓ_p interpolation and DLNs trained by gradient descent: after calibrating the DLN initialization scale α to an effective p_eff(α) via the separable potential, the DLN ℓ_r-vs-n curves match the explicit-ℓ_p predictions in both the location of the elbow n_* and the plateau/growth split at r_* = 2(p−1). Across p values (1.1, 1.5, 1.9), smaller p (sparser bias) shifts the elbow to smaller n and causes more ℓ_r norms to plateau, while larger p keeps the system bulk-dominated longer with n^{1/2}-type growth persisting across the plotted range. For flat support (s = 50) versus single spike (s = 1), the elbow shifts to larger n (roughly linearly in s), consistent with the n_* formula, while absolute ℓ_r magnitudes remain comparable. In DLNs with label noise (σ = 0.1, 0.5), increasing the learning rate from 10^{-3} to 10^{-1} shifts the elbow rightward and inflates ℓ_{1.1}, interpreted as an effective-temperature effect that increases p_eff and delays spike dominance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three well-developed threads: (i) the ℓp family of linear regression interpolators (ridge, lasso, bridge, basis pursuit), (ii) overparameterized regression phenomena such as double descent and benign overfitting (Belkin et al., Hastie et al.), and (iii) implicit bias in diagonal/deep linear networks via separable potentials (Woodworth et al., Gunasekar et al.). The key departure is that prior work has treated 'the norm' monolithically—typically defaulting to ℓ2—or studied individual p values in isolation. Here, the authors introduce a one-dimensional dual-ray analysis that simultaneously characterizes the entire family {‖ŵ_p‖_r} for r ∈ [1,p] under a fixed ℓp bias, yielding a universal threshold r* = 2(p−1) and a data-dependent elbow n*. The DLN extension repurposes the Woodworth et al. separable-potential framework, converting initialization scale α into an effective p_eff(α) via slope-matching on sparse probes, thereby bridging explicit and implicit bias without a fully rigorous α-indexed theory.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is that norm-based generalization proxies—widely used in deep learning diagnostics—are highly sensitive to which ℓr norm is tracked, and the 'right' choice depends on the underlying ℓp inductive bias of the training pipeline. The clean threshold r* = 2(p−1) and elbow n* provide interpretable invariants that practitioners can use to select geometry-aligned capacity proxies (r > r* for stable post-elbow readouts) versus data-growth-sensitive diagnostics (r ≤ r*). Limitations are significant: the theory requires isotropic Gaussian design, p ∈ (1,2], squared loss, and exact interpolation; the DLN extension relies on empirical calibration rather than a learning-rate-aware proof; and classification losses, non-Gaussian designs, and nonlinear architectures remain open. Extending the dual-ray framework to anisotropic designs, deep nonlinear networks, and margin-based classification would substantially broaden its applicability.
