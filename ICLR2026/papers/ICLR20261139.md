## Problem

Frontier LLMs perform well in rules-enforced digital game environments with legality checkers and clean state serialization, but it remains unclear whether they can serve as 'rules oracles' for real-world tabletop strategy games, where a first-time player must acquire dense, heterogeneous, multimodal rule knowledge and apply it to cluttered, unstructured physical game states without any interactive scaffolding. Prior benchmarks either focus on deep strategic mastery in simulators or on simple, highly constrained visual games, leaving the foundational comprehension challenge—learning and applying new rules to messy situated scenes—largely unexplored.

## Input / Output

Each benchmark example provides (1) one or more high-resolution screenshots of a tabletop game state staged in Tabletop Simulator, (2) the game's full ruleset in one of three modalities—high-resolution rulebook PDF page images, text-only extraction, or no ruleset (parametric knowledge only)—and (3) a natural-language question. The ruleset is prepended before the game-state images in the prompt for caching efficiency. The model must produce a structured answer following a lightweight semi-structured or JSON-style schema specified per question; a GPT-4o post-processing step normalizes free-form outputs into this schema before exact-match scoring against ground truth.

## Method

LUDOBENCH is a hand-curated benchmark of 638 QA examples across five mainstream tabletop strategy games (Kingdomino, Carcassonne, Catan, Res Arcana, Pax Renaissance 2e) that vary in ruleset length (4–44 pages) and mechanical complexity. Examples are organized into three cumulative tiers: Tier 1 (Environment Perception) tests basic visual scene parsing without rule knowledge; Tier 2 (Heterogeneous Rules Integration) requires grounding specific rule clauses from the multimodal rulebook into the observed game state; Tier 3 (Short-Horizon Optimization) demands multi-step tactical planning such as finding the action sequence that maximizes end-of-turn score. All game states are staged in Tabletop Simulator for reproducible, fully observable configurations, and a three-stage annotation process (expert creation → hobbyist verification → conflict resolution) ensures ground-truth correctness. The paper evaluates nine frontier multimodal LMs (GPT-4o/4.1/5.1, o1/o3, Gemini 2.5 Pro/Flash/3 Pro, Claude 4.5 Sonnet) under all three rulebook modalities, then conducts extensive failure analysis, rule-retrieval error decomposition, and knowledge-ablation experiments to characterize where and why models break down.

## Results

On LUDOBENCH (638 QA examples across 5 tabletop games), frontier LMs show severe tier-by-tier degradation: average accuracy is ~63% on Tier 1 (Environment Perception), drops to ~36% on Tier 2 (Heterogeneous Rules Integration), and falls to ~8% on Tier 3 (Short-Horizon Optimization). The strongest model, Gemini 3 Pro, reaches ~76–79% on Tier 1 but still falls below 13% on Tier 3, whereas hobbyist human players solve Tier 3 puzzles at ~82% accuracy. Even simple visual perception tasks (counting pieces, identifying spatial relations) remain unsolved, with some model-game splits as low as 30% (Claude 4.5 Sonnet on Carcassonne with image rulebook).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 9 frontier multimodal LMs as baselines on LUDOBENCH. Gemini 3 Pro is consistently the strongest across all three tiers, achieving the highest Tier 1 accuracy (~79%) and the only model to exceed 20% on any Tier 3 split (Res Arcana | Text). GPT-5.1 and o3 form a strong second tier, with o3 reaching 75% on the best Tier 2 split (Kingdomino | Image). The middle cluster—Claude 4.5 Sonnet, GPT-4.1, GPT-4o, o1, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and Gemini 2.5 Flash—shows more variable performance, with Gemini 2.5 Flash the weakest of the API-based models. A counterintuitive finding is that image-based rulebooks (which humans benefit from) often *decrease* accuracy for Gemini 2.5 Pro and Flash, while text rulebooks help all models; GPT-family models benefit from both modalities. No model comes close to hobbyist human performance on Tier 3 (~82%), with the best model achieving under 13%, highlighting a large gap in situated multimodal game reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior game-reasoning benchmarks that operate in rules-enforced digital simulators (chess, Go), text-serialized game states, or synthetic puzzle environments (ARC, Baba Is AI, PuzzleVQA). While those settings isolate specific reasoning competencies under clean, legality-checked infrastructure, LUDOBENCH targets the unstructured 'first-time player' comprehension challenge: given a cluttered visual tabletop scene and a heterogeneous, multimodal rulebook, can a model perceive the state, retrieve and apply relevant rules, and plan short-horizon actions without any external scaffold? The paper also contrasts with visual game-understanding work on simple, constrained-layout games (Connect Four, Tic-Tac-Toe), arguing that real-world tabletop scenes with dozens of diverse components, iconography, and spatial relations present a qualitatively harder grounding problem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark quantifies a large and persistent gap between frontier multimodal LMs and even hobbyist gamers on situated game comprehension (<13% vs. ~82% on Tier 3), revealing that models fail not on isolated rule retrieval but on integrating cross-modal knowledge into coherent multi-step reasoning over messy visual states. The finding that illustrated image rulebooks can degrade performance relative to text-only rules (particularly for Gemini models) challenges the assumption that visual demonstrations aid rule acquisition and points to unresolved limitations in visual-document understanding and symbolic grounding. Open questions include whether stronger visual pretraining, explicit state-tracking scaffolds, or retrieval-augmented rule access can close the gap, and whether the failure modes generalize beyond tabletop games to other complex, multimodal, real-world reasoning domains.
