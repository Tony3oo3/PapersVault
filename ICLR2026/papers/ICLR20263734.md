## Problem

Existing jailbreak evaluation systems lack case-specific criteria, causing significant discrepancies in effectiveness assessments across studies. Keyword-based detection is the most prone to misjudgment, while LLM-as-a-judge approaches without per-case guidelines degrade into binary systems that produce extreme scores and fail to capture response nuances. A systematic measurement of 37 jailbreak papers since 2022 reveals that prior benchmarks overestimate attack success rates, with methods claiming >90% ASR actually achieving only ~30% under accurate evaluation.

## Input / Output

GuidedEval takes a harmful question, the corresponding jailbreak response (first 512 tokens via Llama tokenizer), and a case-specific guideline containing up to 3 entity descriptions and up to 3 action descriptions (averaging 4.3 scoring points total). The evaluator LLM performs a binary yes/no semantic match for each scoring point within a single API call, and the per-case score is the fraction of matched points. The final ASR is the mean of these completion rates across all 200 questions, yielding a continuous value in [0,1] rather than a binary or coarse multi-value score.

## Method

GuidedBench comprises two components: a curated dataset of 200 harmful questions (180 core + 20 additional) spanning 20 topics, filtered from ~18,000 candidates using four principles (model refusal, direct requirement, malicious intent, answerable structure), and GuidedEval, a guideline-based evaluation system. The key mechanism is decomposing each harmful question into attacker-perspective scoring points—specific entities (e.g., named targets, materials) and actions (e.g., procedural steps)—that a successful jailbreak response must contain. This shifts evaluation from subjective value judgment to objective existence checking, requiring only basic information extraction from the evaluator LLM. The ASR is defined as the average guideline completion rate (Equation 1), with all scoring points equally weighted. This design reduces inter-evaluator variance by 76.03–88.28% compared to baseline LLM-based systems, cuts false positive rates by up to 58.92% on identified failure modes, and produces a near-uniform score distribution (normalized entropy 0.92 vs. 0.25 for PAIR) rather than the bimodal distributions of prior multi-value systems.

## Results

On GuidedBench (200 harmful questions, 5 victim LLMs), the highest-performing jailbreak method SCAV achieves only 29.45% average ASR, followed by GCG (26.18%) and PAIR (19.73%), while the weakest method GPTFuzzer scores just 0.35%. This stands in stark contrast to prior benchmarks where some methods claimed >90% ASR, revealing that existing evaluation systems significantly overestimate jailbreak effectiveness. GuidedEval reduces inter-evaluator variance by 76.03%–88.28% compared to all baseline LLM-based systems (variance drops from 0.0321–0.0657 to 0.0077), and achieves the lowest false positive rate across all five identified failure scenarios (3.64%–9.07% FPR vs. up to 87.63% for NegativeKeyword).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the ten evaluated jailbreak methods, white-box approaches dominate: SCAV (29.45%) and GCG (26.18%) substantially outperform all black-box methods, with PAIR (19.73%) and DRA (13.83%) as the strongest black-box attacks. GPTFuzzer, despite its evolutionary design, achieves a near-zero average ASR (0.35%) because its generated responses frequently deviate from the original harmful goal. In terms of evaluation systems, GuidedEval achieves the lowest inter-evaluator variance (0.0077) compared to StrongREJECT_refusal (0.0657), PAIR (0.0450), HarmBench (0.0435), and StrongREJECT (0.0429), a reduction of 76.03%–88.28%. On false positive rates for objectively failed responses, GuidedEval (3.64%–9.07% FPR) dramatically outperforms NegativeKeyword (7.69%–87.63%), PositiveKeyword (8.46%–84.62%), and even the best LLM-based baselines like PAIR (10.13%–16.92%) and StrongREJECT (11.41%–38.82%). Keyword-based systems show near-complete ranking inversions relative to GuidedEval (e.g., NegativeKeywords produces almost reversed black-box leaderboards), while LLM-based systems (PAIR, StrongREJECT, HarmBench) maintain broadly consistent ranking trends with GuidedEval but with inflated ASR values.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant LLM-as-a-judge paradigm for jailbreak evaluation (PAIR, StrongREJECT, HarmBench) by introducing case-specific scoring guidelines that reduce evaluation to an objective existence check rather than a subjective harm judgment. It draws conceptually on the checklist-based evaluation paradigm from Viswanathan et al. (2025) and WildIFEval (Lior et al. 2025), which decompose complex evaluation into verifiable components, but applies this principle specifically to jailbreak success assessment. The harmful question dataset is reconstructed from six existing benchmarks (AdvBench, HarmBench, StrongREJECT, JailbreakBench, MaliciousInstruct, ForbiddenQuestionSet) under new selection principles (direct requirement, malicious intent, answerable structure), rather than introducing entirely novel questions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that the highest-performing jailbreak method achieves only ~30% ASR on GuidedBench versus >90% claimed on prior benchmarks fundamentally recalibrates the field's understanding of jailbreak effectiveness and the actual safety risks they pose. The 76% reduction in inter-evaluator variance and the demonstration that keyword-based systems (the most prevalent in the literature) produce nearly reversed rankings make a strong practical case for adopting guideline-based evaluation. Open questions include whether guidelines can be reliably auto-generated at scale for new topics, whether the 200-case dataset captures sufficient diversity for all victim LLMs, and whether the approach generalizes beyond jailbreak evaluation to other LLM safety assessment tasks.
