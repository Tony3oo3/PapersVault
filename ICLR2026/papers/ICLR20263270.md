## Problem

Existing visual emotion benchmarks for MLLMs suffer from four structural limitations: rigid single-label ground truths that reject plausible alternative responses, narrow emotion taxonomies (often only 8 categories), neglect of contextual and viewer-identity factors, and labor-intensive crowdsourced annotation that constrains scalability. These constraints produce inconsistent and incomplete assessments of MLLMs' emotional perception, making it unclear whether their apparent failures reflect genuine capability gaps or evaluation artifacts.

## Input / Output

Each evaluation trial presents an MLLM with a single RGB image (sourced from EmoSet) and a paired emotion-centric statement, and the model must output a binary Correct/Incorrect judgment. Statements are constructed across four dimensions—sentiment polarity, emotion interpretation, scene context, and perception subjectivity—and each image-statement pair is queried three times with majority vote determining the final answer. The MVEI benchmark contains 3,086 image-statement pairs with 424 distinct open-vocabulary emotion labels, while the larger INSETS-462k corpus provides 462,369 statements over 17,716 images (averaging 26.1 statements per image) for adaptation experiments.

## Method

The INSETS pipeline operates in two stages grounded in Parrott's hierarchical emotion model (6 primary, 25 secondary, 113 tertiary categories). Stage 1 (open-vocabulary emotion tagging) extracts candidate emotions from 9 MLLMs, filters them via GPT-4, attaches them to an extended Parrott-based Open-vocabulary Hierarchical Model (POM), and applies an ensemble-based majority-voting mechanism that allocates per-secondary-emotion quotas based on cross-model consensus before selecting top-frequency labels. Stage 2 (statement construction) generates prototype interpretations, contexts, and characters per emotion label, then assembles correct and incorrect statements using dimension-specific disruption strategies: inter-image and intra-image interpretation swaps for emotion interpretation, flip-polarity and context-swapping for scene context, and preference-order reversal for perception subjectivity. Human refinement by five annotators (Fleiss' Kappa 0.61) validates 90.6% of automated annotations, yielding the final MVEI benchmark in roughly 100 person-hours versus the 23,788 annotators required by prior benchmarks like EMOTIC.

## Results

On the MVEI benchmark (3,086 image-statement pairs), GPT4o achieves the highest overall accuracy at 78.3%, followed closely by mPLUG-Owl3 (78.1%) and Qwen2-VL (76.6%). Humans achieve a substantially higher average accuracy of 91.6% (best: 95.2%), with the largest gaps in Sentiment Polarity (GPT4o: 72.5% vs. human: 92.3%) and Perception Subjectivity (GPT4o: 69.2% vs. human: 89.6%). Scene Context is the dimension where MLLMs perform relatively best, with Qwen2-VL reaching 86.1% and GPT4o at 81.6%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a benchmark paper, so the 'comparison' is across 17 MLLMs evaluated on the newly proposed MVEI. GPT4o leads overall at 78.3% total accuracy, narrowly ahead of mPLUG-Owl3 (78.1%) and Qwen2-VL (76.6%), while earlier-generation models like BLIP2 (53.2%), InstructBLIP (36.8%), and Otter (27.0%) lag substantially, often exhibiting severe positive-response bias (e.g., Otter's 9.9% positive ratio) or high give-up rates (InstructBLIP: 37.5%). No single MLLM dominates all four dimensions: GPT4o and InternVL2.5 excel in Emotion Interpretation (84.3% and 80.2%) but are weaker in Perception Subjectivity (69.2% and 61.3%), whereas Qwen2-VL achieves the best Scene Context score (86.1%). Against 25 human participants (91.6% average), even GPT4o trails by 13.3 points overall, with the gap widening to 19.8 points on Sentiment Polarity and 20.4 points on Perception Subjectivity. The authors further show that lightweight adaptations (LoRA, full fine-tuning, GRPO) on Qwen2.5-VL close part of the Sentiment Polarity gap (63.2%→84.3%) but leave Perception Subjectivity largely unchanged (66.3%→71.1%), indicating it reflects a more fundamental model limitation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant AICA evaluation paradigm of fixed-taxonomy emotion classification (FI, EMOTIC, EmoSet) and open-ended emotion interpretation (Artemis, Affection, EIBench) by reformulating the problem as Emotion Statement Judgment, where MLLMs validate pre-constructed emotion-centric statements across four dimensions. It builds on Parrott's hierarchical emotion model as a structural backbone for open-vocabulary tagging, and draws on EmoSet as its image source, but replaces the labor-intensive crowdsourcing of prior benchmarks with an ensemble-voting pipeline across nine MLLMs. Among MLLM evaluation efforts (MM-BigBench, FABA-Bench, EmoBench-M, EEmo-Bench), it is the first to explicitly incorporate extrinsic contextual factors—scene context and viewer identity—that psychological literature identifies as critical to emotion perception.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a substantial and persistent gap between even top MLLMs (~78–79% total accuracy) and humans (91.6%), with the largest deficits in sentiment polarity and perception subjectivity. The key open question the paper surfaces is whether perception subjectivity represents a fundamental architectural limitation of MLLMs rather than a data or training issue, since targeted adaptation (LoRA, full fine-tuning, GRPO) yields only ~4–5 point gains on this dimension versus 15–21 points on sentiment polarity. This distinction has implications for whether LLM-as-a-judge approaches can be trusted in affective perception tasks, and points toward the need for subjectivity-oriented pre-training objectives or specialized datasets that current web-scale training does not provide.
