## Problem

Speculative sampling (SpS) strictly enforces the output distribution to match the verifier LLM, unnecessarily rejecting correct but lower-probability tokens. Typical acceptance sampling (TAS) alleviates this by accepting more tokens via entropy-based heuristics, but distorts the verifier distribution and risks semantic drift. The paper addresses the missing principled mechanism to control how much the output distribution may deviate from the verifier while still improving acceptance rates.

## Input / Output

At each decoding step, the method takes the drafted token index n, the verifier's probability q(n|x_{<t}) at that token, and a scalar divergence budget δ (typically 0.75–1.0). It outputs a modified acceptance probability and recovery distribution that only require reading the verifier's probability at the single drafted token rather than the full vocabulary, reducing memory access overhead in large-vocabulary settings. The effective output distribution is guaranteed to have KL divergence at most Γ(δ) from the verifier, where Γ is a continuous non-decreasing function with Γ(0)=0.

## Method

Cactus reformulates speculative sampling as a constrained optimization: maximize the acceptance rate of the drafted token subject to an f-divergence (KL) constraint from the verifier distribution. Theorem 2 shows the optimal target distribution h increases the drafted token's probability to γ* while proportionally scaling down all other tokens, where γ* solves a transcendental equation. Since this equation has no closed form, Cactus uses a second-order Taylor expansion around q(n) to obtain the practical solution γ* ≈ q(n) + √(2δ·q(n)·(1−q(n))). Theorem 3 then proves that despite h being solved per-token, the overall algorithm's effective distribution maintains controlled divergence from the verifier. Unlike TAS, which implicitly optimizes cross-entropy and collapses to deterministic (zero-entropy) distributions, Cactus uses KL divergence to preserve the verifier's distributional shape while granting a small, bounded 'bonus' to the drafted token.

## Results

On GSM8K with m=20 (Qwen 3 14B verifier, 0.6B drafter), Cactus (δ=1.0) achieves 92.87% accuracy with an average accepted length (AL) of 7.00, compared to SpS at 91.89% with AL 5.11 and TAS at 92.87% with AL 6.78. On GPQA, Cactus (δ=0.75) reaches 45.46% accuracy with AL 6.46, substantially outperforming SpS (40.91%, AL 3.84) and TAS (40.40%, AL 6.41), and even exceeding the verifier's standalone score of 40.07%. On IFEval, Cactus (δ=0.75) achieves 86.69% with AL 3.45 versus SpS at 84.47% with AL 2.27. Wall-time speedup on GPQA reaches ~1.9× over the verifier alone with m=10 in the 0.6B+14B setting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against vanilla SpS (Chen et al., 2023), Cactus consistently achieves higher AL (e.g., 7.00 vs. 5.11 on GSM8K m=20) while matching or exceeding accuracy, confirming that the strict distributional equivalence of SpS is unnecessarily restrictive. Against TAS (Cai et al., 2024), Cactus wins on both throughput and quality: TAS degrades GPQA accuracy from 40.91 (SpS) to 38.89 with the 8B verifier, whereas Cactus (δ=0.75) lifts it to 45.46 with the 14B verifier, demonstrating that TAS's entropy-based heuristic distorts the verifier distribution. Against Mentored decoding (Tran-Thien, 2023), Cactus achieves comparable or better wall-time speedup (0.87× vs. 1.20× on GSM8K m=10) while maintaining much higher accuracy (93.10 vs. 91.66 on GSM8K; 85.96 vs. 61.37 on IFEval), since Mentored decoding's numerical optimization adds per-step overhead and misplaces the divergence argument. Against SpecCas (Narasimhan et al., 2025), Cactus achieves similar wall-time acceleration (0.83× vs. 0.85× on GSM8K) but substantially higher accuracy (92.72 vs. 88.40 on GSM8K; 39.40 vs. 32.83 on GPQA), attributed to Cactus's explicit KL-divergence constraint. Against top-k decoding (Bachmann et al., 2025), Cactus consistently outperforms across all three model series (Gemma, DeepSeek R1, LLaMA) in Figure 4, confirming that principled verifier-guided acceptance is superior to naive candidate filtering.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the draft-and-verify paradigm of speculative sampling (Chen et al., 2023; Leviathan et al., 2023) and typical acceptance sampling (TAS; Cai et al., 2024), but departs from both by reframing the acceptance criterion as a constrained optimization problem with an explicit f-divergence budget. Rather than enforcing strict distributional equivalence (SpS) or relying on entropy-based heuristics (TAS), Cactus derives its acceptance rule from a KL-divergence-constrained maximization, yielding a closed-form (via second-order Taylor approximation) modification of the verifier distribution. The paper also provides a unifying theoretical lens: it shows TAS implicitly solves a cross-entropy-constrained variant of the same optimization, explaining TAS's tendency to collapse to deterministic distributions. Cactus is positioned as a training-free, plug-in acceptance rule compatible with any existing speculative decoding stack (EAGLE, Medusa, SpecInfer, etc.).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Cactus offers a principled, provably-controlled knob (δ) for trading off decoding speed against distributional fidelity, which is practically valuable as speculative decoding becomes standard in production LLM serving. The method is training-free and requires only reading the verifier probability at the drafted token (not the full vocabulary), making it lightweight and easy to integrate. Open questions include whether the observed 'ensemble effect' (Cactus occasionally outperforming the verifier by >2σ) is a genuine phenomenon or an artifact of small benchmarks, how the method scales beyond 32B parameters, and whether multi-drafter or multi-verifier variants can further exploit the constrained-optimization framework. The paper caps evaluation at 32B and a single hardware configuration (A100 40GB), leaving broader scalability untested.
