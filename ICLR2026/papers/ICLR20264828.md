## Problem

Spectrum-aware optimizers like Muon and Shampoo have shown substantial empirical gains over SGD and Adam, yet it remains unclear when and why they generalize better. Prior theoretical work characterized only their terminal-phase implicit bias (spectral-norm max-margin) or optimization convergence, leaving their generalization mechanism—especially in practical early-stopping regimes—unexplored. This paper asks: can we identify concrete settings where the canonical spectral update (SpecGD) provably outperforms Euclidean GD in generalization?

## Input / Output

The method operates on matrix-valued parameters (e.g., weight matrices W ∈ R^{K×d} or attention matrices) at the layer level rather than entry-wise on vectorized parameters. At each step, the input is the gradient matrix (or momentum matrix M_t = βM_{t-1} + (1-β)∇_t), from which a truncated SVD U_t Σ_t V_t^⊤ is computed; the output update is the orthogonal matrix U_t V_t^⊤ (SpecGD) or its momentum variant (Muon). The theoretical analysis uses a Gaussian mixture data model with orthogonal class means and isotropic noise, while experiments span image classification (32×32 CIFAR, MNIST-CIFAR composites), text classification (MultiNLI), and next-token prediction (TinyStories).

## Method

The paper identifies SpecGD—where each update is UV^⊤ from the truncated SVD of the gradient—as the canonical form of Muon and Shampoo (recovered by setting momentum and preconditioning accumulations to zero), analogous to how SignGD canonicalizes Adam. Under a Gaussian mixture data model with class imbalance and a joint-diagonalizability condition on population moment matrices, the authors derive closed-form iterate dynamics showing that GD learns the c-th spectral component at rate proportional to s_c^{xx} (dominant components first), while SpecGD learns all components at the same constant rate until each individually saturates. Theorems 1 and 2 then prove that this equal-rate property yields a linearly growing gap in minority-class and class-balanced loss favoring SpecGF over both GF and NGF (normalized gradient flow) for all t before the minority component saturates. Extending to L-layer deep linear models, Proposition 2 and Proposition 4 show that depth accelerates all components and shrinks the saturation-time gap as (s_1^{yx}/s_1^{xx} ÷ s_k^{yx}/s_k^{xx})^{1/L} − 1, empirically validated on 2-layer vs. 4-layer MLPs. Experiments on Colored-MNIST, CIFAR-10/100, MNIST-CIFAR, MultiNLI, CelebA, and TinyStories confirm that Muon and Shampoo achieve superior worst-group and minority-class generalization over SGD, Signum, and NMD, with Adam remaining competitive but not consistently superior.

## Results

Theoretically, the paper proves that SpecGD (the canonical form of Muon/Shampoo) learns all spectral components at equal rates, while GD prioritizes dominant components. Under a Gaussian mixture data model with class imbalance, Theorem 1 shows SpecGF achieves a minority-class loss gap of at least μt/4 and a class-balanced loss gap of at least μt/2 over GF, with gaps growing linearly in time up to the minority saturation point. Theorem 2 extends this to show SpecGF also outperforms normalized GD (NGF) with gaps of at least μt/2 for both minority and balanced loss. Empirically, Muon consistently achieves higher worst-group/minority-class accuracy than SGD and Signum across Colored-MNIST (99% spurious correlation), CIFAR-10/100 (20:1 imbalance), MNIST-CIFAR, MultiNLI, CelebA, and TinyStories, with the advantage most pronounced early in training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SGD, Muon consistently wins on worst-group and minority-class accuracy across all seven datasets tested (Colored-MNIST, CIFAR-10/100, MNIST-CIFAR, MultiNLI, CelebA, TinyStories), with the gap most pronounced in early training; on TinyStories, Muon also generalizes faster on both frequent and rare tokens. Against Adam, the comparison is mixed: on MultiNLI, Adam slightly outperforms Muon on worst-group accuracy, while on CelebA their performance becomes comparable with more fine-tuning epochs; however, on TinyStories Muon outperforms Adam on both frequent and rare token accuracy. Against Signum (SignGD with momentum), Muon clearly wins on minority-group accuracy for Colored-MNIST and CIFAR, while both achieve similar majority-group performance. Against Shampoo, Muon shows comparable performance as both are spectral-norm-based methods, with both outperforming SGD on decoded worst-group accuracy (indicating core feature learning) on MNIST-CIFAR. Theoretically, SpecGD outperforms both GD and NGD (normalized GD) in the class-imbalanced linear setting, showing that the advantage is not merely due to step-size normalization but to the spectral structure of the update.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the implicit bias and training dynamics literature (Saxe et al. 2013 for GD in deep linear networks; Fan et al. 2025 and Tsilivis et al. 2024 for SpecGD's terminal-phase max-margin behavior) by deriving closed-form early-training dynamics for SpecGD and contrasting them with GD under class imbalance. It builds on the identification (Pethick et al. 2025; Bernstein & Newhouse 2024) that Muon and Shampoo reduce to SpecGD in their canonical form, using this as a lens to study generalization rather than optimization convergence. The key departure from prior work is shifting focus from terminal-phase implicit bias to early-training generalization gaps, and from optimization properties to a concrete data regime (imbalanced data) where spectral methods provably outperform Euclidean methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This provides the first mechanistic explanation for why spectral optimizers like Muon and Shampoo generalize better on imbalanced data: they learn all spectral components at equal rates rather than prioritizing dominant ones, yielding a linearly growing worst-class loss gap under early stopping. The finding that normalization (NGD) does not close this gap is practically important, as it rules out a simple fix for GD. However, the theory relies on strong assumptions (population statistics, squared loss, joint diagonalizability, zero initialization), and empirical results show Adam is often competitive with Muon, limiting the practical gap. Open questions include extending to finite samples, cross-entropy loss, non-orthogonal class means, and understanding how these effects interact with explicit re-weighting strategies.
