## Problem

While prior work on TTI diffusion models has extensively documented demographic and cultural biases in present-day imagery, how these models visually construct the past remains largely unexplored. TTI models may impose stereotyped stylistic defaults on historical periods, introduce anachronistic artifacts, and misrepresent demographics in ways that distort public understanding of history, yet no systematic benchmark exists to measure these failures.

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes neutral text prompts following the fixed template 'A person [activity] in the [time period]' (100 universal activities × 10 historical periods spanning the 17th–21st centuries and five 20th-century decades) and feeds them to three open-source diffusion models (SDXL, SD3, FLUX.1 Schnell), generating 10 images per prompt for a total of 30,000 synthetic images. Evaluation produces three structured outputs: per-period Visual Style Dominance (VSD) scores with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals, per-anachronism frequency and severity scores aggregated across models and periods, and per-demographic-group under/overrepresentation scores measured as absolute deviations from LLM-estimated historical baselines.

## Method

The evaluation protocol operates along three axes. For stylistic bias, a linear probe trained on frozen DINOv2 ViT-B/14 features (WikiArt corpus, 5 style classes: drawing, engraving, illustration, painting, photography) assigns a style label to every generated image, and the VSD score quantifies the maximum proportion of images converging on a single style per model-period pair. For anachronism detection, a two-stage pipeline first uses an LLM (GPT-4o) to propose candidate anachronistic elements and corresponding binary yes/no identification questions per prompt, then queries three VLMs (GPT-4o, LLaMA-3.2-11B, Qwen2.5 VL-7B) in a VQA setup with majority voting; frequency measures how often an anachronism appears across all images for a period, while severity measures how consistently it appears once the LLM flags it as plausible. For demographic representation, the FairFace classifier extracts gender and race from generated faces, GPT-4o synthesizes historically plausible demographic percentages per activity-period pair (validated against OWID statistics where available), and under/overrepresentation metrics are computed as indicator-weighted absolute deviations between model outputs and LLM baselines, aggregated at the activity-category level.

## Results

On the HistVis benchmark (30,000 images, 100 prompts × 10 periods × 3 models × 10 samples), the Visual Style Dominance (VSD) scores reveal strong era-specific stylistic defaults: SDXL reaches 0.93 for engravings in the 17th century, while SD3 and FLUX.1 converge on paintings (0.86 and 0.88 respectively). For anachronism detection, SD3 exhibits the highest rate (25% of 1930s images contain at least one anachronism), followed by FLUX.1 (12–13% stable across periods) and SDXL (below 5%). The three-VLM ensemble for anachronism verification achieves 75% agreement with human judgment (Fleiss' κ = 0.63 among annotators), outperforming individual VLMs (GPT-4o: 72%, LLaMA-3.2: 68%, Qwen2.5: 63%). The DINOv2-based style classifier used for VSD computation attains 0.89 accuracy and 0.87 macro-F1 on the WikiArt validation set.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an evaluation/benchmark paper rather than a method paper, so it does not propose a new model to compare against prior methods. Instead, it compares three TTI models (SDXL, SD3, FLUX.1 Schnell) against each other on three historical-representation dimensions. On stylistic bias, SDXL is the most stylistically rigid for pre-modern periods (engraving VSD 0.89–0.93) while SD3 and FLUX.1 prefer paintings; for modern periods, SD3 and FLUX.1 strongly default to photography (VSD 0.83–0.96) whereas SDXL remains more diverse. On anachronisms, SD3 is the worst performer (20–25% of images flagged), FLUX.1 is intermediate (12–13%), and SDXL is the most historically consistent (<5%). The paper's anachronism detection pipeline (LLM proposal + 3-VLM majority-vote VQA) is validated against a human user study (2,040 annotations from 234 participants), where the ensemble reaches 75% agreement versus 72% for GPT-4o alone, 68% for LLaMA-3.2, and 63% for Qwen2.5, and substantially above the 67% reported for simpler contemporary VQA tasks in prior work (D'Incà et al., 2024). Prompt engineering as a mitigation strategy partially reduces monochrome dominance in SDXL (e.g., 1950s VSD drops from 0.66 to 0.82 for illustrations) but fails to override the engraving default for 17th–18th century prompts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by shifting the TTI evaluation lens from present-day demographic and cultural biases (Seshadri et al., Hall et al., Luccioni et al.) to historical representation, a dimension the authors identify as largely underexplored. It repurposes existing open-set bias detection strategies (D'Incà et al., Chinchure et al.) for anachronism detection and adapts fairness metrics from the ML literature to a historical context using LLM-derived baselines. Conceptually, it departs from prior historical-accuracy benchmarks (CENTURY, HEIM, Google Landmarks) that focus on recognition of specific figures or landmarks, instead asking how models construct visual interpretations of the past across stylistic, chronological, and demographic dimensions simultaneously.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As a first-of-its-kind benchmark with a model-agnostic, reproducible protocol, HistVis provides a concrete starting point for tracking historical fidelity in future TTI systems, with direct implications for educational, cultural heritage, and media applications where anachronistic or stereotyped imagery can distort public understanding. Key open questions include whether LLM-derived demographic baselines can be replaced by expert-annotated historical records, whether the findings generalize beyond the three models and ten periods tested, and how to balance historical accuracy against broader representational diversity goals without reinforcing exclusionary narratives. The paper's demonstration that prompt engineering largely fails to override stylistic defaults suggests that deeper architectural or training-data interventions may be necessary.
