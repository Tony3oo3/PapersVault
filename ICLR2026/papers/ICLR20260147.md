## Problem

Existing convergence theories for adaptive optimizers assume exact arithmetic, while prior quantization analyses either focus on SGD, rely on unbiased quantization or error-feedback mechanisms, or omit optimizer-state quantization entirely. This leaves a critical gap: no theoretical framework explains why low-precision training with fully quantized Adam or Muon (gradients, weights, and optimizer states all in BF16/FP8) remains effective in modern LLM training pipelines.

## Input / Output

The analysis takes a smooth non-convex stochastic objective, a floating-point quantization operator characterized by per-component relative error bounds (q_W, q_G, q_M, q_V) determined by the mantissa length M of the target format, and standard optimizer hyperparameters (β1, β2, ε, η for Adam; β for Muon). It produces a convergence rate bound on the expected squared gradient norm at a randomly selected iteration, with the bound explicitly decomposed into terms that isolate the contribution of each quantization component. The key structural output is a sensitivity hierarchy: Adam requires q_G, q_M = O(1/T) and q_W, q_V = O(1/T²), while Muon requires only q = O(T^{-1/2}) across all components.

## Method

The paper introduces a relative-error quantization model (|x^Q − x| ≤ q|x| with q = Θ(2^{-M})) that faithfully captures floating-point behavior without requiring unbiasedness or per-parameter error feedback, then applies it simultaneously to weights, gradients, first moments, and second moments within a unified training framework. For Adam, the proof follows the Defossez et al. decomposition of the gradient-update inner product into an ideal-accumulator term and a quantization-error term, bounding the latter via recursive error propagation through the momentum and second-moment recursions; the critical difficulty is controlling the nonlinear amplification of second-moment errors through the inverse square root in the update step. For Muon, the SVD-based sign operator (U_t V_t^T) avoids this amplification, yielding a simpler analysis where quantization errors enter additively. The main result shows both optimizers retain their full-precision O(T^{-1/4}) rate provided the mantissa length scales only logarithmically with T (M = Ω(log T)), and reveals that Adam's stricter requirements on q_V and q_W stem from its dependence on β2 → 1, while Muon's robustness comes from the orthogonalization step that prevents error accumulation.

## Results

The paper's main results are theoretical convergence guarantees: Quantized Adam (Theorem 4.5) achieves E[‖∇F(W_τ)‖²_F] = Õ(T^{-1/4}) provided q_G, q_M = O(1/T) and q_W, q_V = O(1/T²), while Quantized Muon (Theorem 4.6) achieves (1/T)Σ E[‖∇F(W_t)‖_F] = O(T^{-1/4}) with the weaker requirement q_G = q_W = q_M = O(T^{-1/2}). Both rates match their full-precision counterparts, confirming that mantissa length scaling only logarithmically with T (M = Ω(log T)) suffices. Empirically, on Rosenbrock (m=50, n=100, T=10,000), CIFAR-10 (4-layer FC network), and nanoGPT (~26M params on OpenWebText), moderate mantissa lengths (M ≥ 8) yield convergence nearly identical to full-precision FP32, while very low precision (M=4) causes significant degradation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without a traditional method-vs-method comparison table; the 'baselines' are the full-precision (unquantized) versions of Adam and Muon. The central empirical comparison is between Adam and Muon under identical quantization settings: Muon consistently exhibits smaller gradient norms and more stable convergence at low mantissa lengths (M=4, 8) than Adam, corroborating the theory that Muon's SVD-based sign operator avoids the inverse-square-root amplification of quantization errors present in Adam's second-moment normalization. On the nanoGPT/OpenWebText benchmark, Muon applied to 2D transformer parameters shows superior robustness to FP8-level quantization compared to AdamW, consistent with the O(T^{-1/2}) vs. O(T^{-2}) precision gap in Theorems 4.6 vs. 4.5. Across all three benchmarks (Rosenbrock, CIFAR-10, nanoGPT), both optimizers degrade gracefully as mantissa length decreases from 32 to 4 bits, with the transition from near-full-precision to degraded performance occurring around M=8–16, matching the theoretical threshold where relative error q = Θ(2^{-M}) crosses the O(1/T) or O(1/T²) boundary.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the convergence analysis of adaptive optimizers (building on Defossez et al. 2022 for Adam and Shen et al. 2025 for Muon) into the low-precision regime by introducing a relative-error floating-point quantization model applied to all training components—gradients, weights, first moments, and second moments. It departs sharply from prior quantized-optimizer analyses (Chen et al. 2021, Modoranu et al. 2024, Ozkara et al. 2025), which either required memory-intensive per-parameter error feedback, assumed unbiased quantizers, or quantized only the final weight update while keeping optimizer states in full precision. The key conceptual departure is modeling the recursive, interaction-heavy error propagation that arises when momentum and second-moment accumulators themselves are stored in low precision, a component omitted by all prior theoretical treatments.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first framework that explains, from first principles, why low-precision training of adaptive optimizers remains effective in practice, and it provides actionable guidance: Adam's convergence degrades sharply under second-moment and weight quantization (requiring O(1/T²) relative error) due to the β₂→1 amplification through the inverse square root, while Muon's SVD-based sign operator is far more tolerant (O(1/T^{1/2}) suffices). A key open question the authors flag is that their guarantees require the mantissa length to grow logarithmically with iterations (M = Ω(log T)), whereas practical hardware uses fixed precision (BF16, FP8); understanding why moderate fixed precision empirically suffices remains unresolved. The analysis also does not yet account for low-precision matrix multiplications or distributed communication, limiting its direct applicability to full-scale LLM training pipelines.
