## Problem

Zeroth-order (ZO) federated learning methods achieve dimension-free (scalar-only) communication, making them attractive for LLM fine-tuning, but they converge extremely slowly because they ignore curvature information. Existing Hessian-informed ZO accelerators require transmitting second-order information, which reintroduces communication costs that scale with model dimension and directly contradicts the scalar-only communication goal. The paper asks: can we accelerate federated ZO fine-tuning while strictly preserving dimension-free communication?

## Input / Output

Each client consumes only local loss function evaluations (two scalar function values per ZO step) and shared random seeds broadcast by the server; no gradient vectors or model parameters are ever transmitted. The server communicates only a small number of gradient scalars per round (one per local update step per client) plus random seeds, keeping total communication in the kilobyte range regardless of model size. The method maintains a global diagonal Hessian approximation H locally at both server and clients, reconstructed from previously communicated gradient scalars and seeds rather than transmitted explicitly, so no second-order information crosses the network.

## Method

HiSo operates within a generalized scalar-only communication FL framework that decouples scalar communication from vanilla ZO-SGD, enabling the integration of Hessian-informed optimization. The core update replaces the standard ZO perturbation direction u~N(0,I) with a whitened direction z~N(0,H^{-1}), where H is a global diagonal Hessian approximation, yielding an update whose expectation follows a Newton-style (natural gradient) descent direction. The diagonal Hessian H is learned via an Adam-style exponential moving average of the squared global update vectors, which are themselves reconstructable from previously communicated gradient scalars and shared random seeds—requiring zero additional communication for second-order information. Theoretically, under a low-effective-rank and whitening assumption on the Hessian, HiSo achieves a convergence rate of O(√(ζ/mR)) that is independent of both model dimension d and the Lipschitz constant L, the first such result for ZO methods in FL, and the analysis extends to multiple local updates (τ>1) where prior dimension-free methods (DeComFL) lack guarantees.

## Results

On LLM fine-tuning tasks (SST-2, QQP, SQuAD) with OPT models (125M–2.7B), HiSo achieves 1.4–5.4× fewer communication rounds than DeComFL to reach the same test accuracy, with 29%–80% communication savings. On OPT-1.3B, HiSo reaches 90.34% SST-2 accuracy, 64.20% QQP accuracy, and 57.58 F1 on SQuAD, outperforming DeComFL (90.22%, 63.25%, 57.14) while using comparable or lower communication (49.18KB, 96.67KB, 7.81KB vs. 58.59KB, 43.95KB, 13.67KB). Compared to first-order baselines (FedAvg, FedAdam), HiSo reduces communication by up to 90 million times (from ~0.6TB to ~50KB) at a small accuracy cost (<1%). The Hessian-informed preconditioning adds negligible computation overhead (<0.26ms per round, <0.13% of total round time).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against first-order baselines (FedAvg, FedAdam, FedYogi, FedAdagrad), HiSo trades a small accuracy gap (0.5–2.5% on SST-2) for a dramatic 7–90 million× reduction in communication cost (from hundreds of GB to tens of KB per client), making it the only method practical for bandwidth-constrained federated LLM fine-tuning. Against FedZO, HiSo wins on both axes: lower communication (49KB vs. 4.73TB on OPT-1.3B SST-2) and higher accuracy (90.34% vs. 90.01%). Against DeComFL, the primary ZO-FL baseline, HiSo consistently achieves higher test accuracy across all three tasks and all model sizes (e.g., 64.20% vs. 63.25% on QQP, 57.58 vs. 57.14 F1 on SQuAD for OPT-1.3B) while requiring 1.4–5.4× fewer communication rounds to reach DeComFL's best accuracy. Against FL+PEFT baselines (FedSA-LoRA, FFA-LoRA), HiSo achieves up to 10⁴× lower communication (KB vs. GB) while maintaining competitive accuracy (85.55% vs. 87.53% on OPT-125M SST-2). The one case where HiSo uses slightly more communication than DeComFL is OPT-1.3B on QQP (96.67KB vs. 43.95KB), but it still achieves higher accuracy (64.20% vs. 63.25%) and fewer total rounds.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes two previously separate research threads: the dimension-free scalar-only communication paradigm from DeComFL (Li et al., 2025b) and Hessian-informed zeroth-order optimization from Ye et al. (2018) and Zhao et al. (2025). The key conceptual departure is decoupling scalar-only communication from its tight coupling with vanilla ZO-SGD, revealing that the communication framework is agnostic to the specific ascent direction used. This generalized framework then allows the Hessian-informed preconditioned ZO update to be plugged in without any additional communication, since the diagonal Hessian can be reconstructed from the same gradient scalars already being transmitted. Theoretically, the paper extends DeComFL's analysis by establishing convergence guarantees under a low-effective-rank Hessian assumption and supporting multiple local updates (τ>1), which DeComFL's proof could not handle.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for bandwidth-constrained federated LLM fine-tuning, where HiSo reduces communication from TB-level (first-order methods) to KB-level while simultaneously accelerating convergence by 1.5–5× over the prior dimension-free baseline. The theoretical result—convergence rate independent of both model dimension d and Lipschitz constant L under the low-effective-rank assumption—provides a principled explanation for why ZO methods empirically converge far faster than their worst-case O(d) bounds suggest. A key limitation is that the method treats the loss as a generic function without exploiting PEFT module structures (e.g., LoRA's low-rank decomposition), leaving open the question of whether Hessian information can be further tailored to parameter-efficient fine-tuning architectures. Additionally, the well-approximated Hessian condition, while empirically supported by observed long-tail eigenvalue distributions, lacks a rigorous guarantee for LLMs in general.
