## Problem

While empirical studies demonstrate that LLM self-improvement (fine-tuning on model-generated data) yields performance gains, the theoretical underpinnings of this process remain largely unexplored. Specifically, there is no quantitative model explaining how model capability evolves over training epochs, what drives the improvement, or where the capability ceiling lies. This paper addresses these gaps by proposing a physics-inspired theoretical framework grounded in the solver-verifier capability gap.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260627/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a pre-trained LLM and a set of training prompts as input. At each self-improvement step, the model generates N candidate responses per prompt, a verifier (the same or a different LLM) scores them, and a length-regularized Best-of-N response is selected as the SFT target. The output is a fine-tuned model whose solver and verifier uncertainties follow predicted exponential decay curves toward analytically derived capability limits. For cross-improvement, a limited set of external responses from a stronger model (DeepSeek-V3) is injected at chosen epochs, and the framework predicts that the final solver capability depends only on the total amount of external data, not its temporal allocation. Capability is measured via negative log-likelihood (uncertainty) rather than accuracy, enabling application to tasks without ground-truth labels.

## Method

The core contribution is a phenomenological coupled ODE framework inspired by potential energy in physics. Solver capability U_s(t) and verifier capability U_v(t) are modeled as decreasing at rates proportional to a shared potential energy E(t) = f(G(t)), where G(t) = U_s(t) − U_v(t) is the capability gap. By linearly approximating E(t) ≈ kG(t) − b (validated empirically with R² > 0.9), the system admits closed-form solutions: both capabilities and the gap converge exponentially at rate k(α−β), with the solver's ultimate capability U_{s,∞} determined by the initial gap and the ratio α/(α−β). A key corollary shows that a larger initial verifier-solver gap yields a lower final solver uncertainty, formalizing the intuition that the gap drives improvement. The framework is extended to cross-improvement by modeling external data as a multiplicative discount on verifier uncertainty, yielding a matrix-exponential approximation that proves the final solver capability is invariant to the timing of external data allocation, depending only on the total fraction used.

## Results

The theoretical exponential model fits the empirical self-improvement training trajectories with R² > 0.9 across all tested models (Phi-4-mini, Phi-3.5-mini, Phi-3-mini, Llama-3.2-3B) and datasets (Math, GSM8k), confirming the predicted exponential convergence of solver and verifier uncertainty. The solver-verifier gap is consistently positive across all model-task pairs, with the verifier outperforming the solver in both self-evaluation and cross-evaluation settings. In cross-improvement experiments, the average solver accuracy improves by +15.93% (Math) and +15.21% (GSM8k) over the initial model for Phi-4-mini, and by +16.49% (Math) and +24.79% (GSM8k) for Llama-3.2-3B, while the three allocation strategies (Early, Uniform, Late) differ by at most 2.17% in final accuracy, validating the theoretical prediction that allocation timing is inconsequential.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is primarily a theoretical validation paper rather than a method comparison, so it does not benchmark against competing self-improvement algorithms. The key empirical comparison is between self-improvement (Baseline) and cross-improvement (using DeepSeek-V3 external data). Cross-improvement outperforms self-improvement on Math for both models (+1.16% for Phi-4-mini, +3.35% for Llama-3.2-3B) but shows a slight drop on GSM8k for Llama-3.2-3B (−0.77%), which the authors attribute to the external model's verifier not significantly surpassing the original model on that task. Among the three cross-improvement allocation strategies (Early, Uniform, Late), differences are negligible (≤2.17%), confirming the theoretical prediction that the total amount of external data matters more than its temporal distribution. Additionally, the EvoLM training-stage analysis shows that Base-SFT models exhibit the highest plasticity (α=146.3, decay rate 0.244) while Post-trained models show dynamic saturation (α=16.0), consistent with the theoretical asymptotic convergence prediction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on two recent ICLR 2025 works: Song et al. (2025) identifies the generation-verification gap as the driver of self-improvement, and Huang et al. (2025) formalizes a sharpening mechanism where the verifier step concentrates probability mass on high-quality sequences. The present paper departs from both by moving from qualitative identification of the gap to a full phenomenological differential-equation model that yields closed-form exponential convergence trajectories for both solver and verifier capabilities. It also extends the analysis to cross-improvement with limited external data, a setting neither prior work addresses, and replaces accuracy-based metrics with uncertainty (NLL) to enable application to label-free tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides practitioners with a quantitative tool to predict the capability ceiling of self-improvement and to estimate how many training epochs are needed before diminishing returns set in (Corollary 3.2). The finding that external data can be injected at any training stage without affecting final performance is a practically useful simplification for teams with limited API budgets. Key open questions include whether the time-invariant linear approximation holds under non-stationary learning-rate schedules or distribution shifts, and whether the phenomenological model can be grounded in first-principles neural network dynamics. The Pass@K diversity-decay observation hints at a fundamental limitation of iterative self-improvement that the current framework captures only implicitly.
