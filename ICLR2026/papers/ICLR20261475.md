## Problem

Existing benchmarks for evaluating long-range capabilities in graph learning either use real-world data where it is impossible to guarantee the task actually requires long-range information, or use synthetic tasks with binary-like feedback that provide no continuous signal. This makes claims of long-range modeling improvements based on such benchmarks questionable, as there is no provable link between task difficulty and the necessity of long-range interactions.

## Input / Output

Each instance is an L×L 2D periodic grid graph with 4-regular connectivity (shared topology across all instances) where every node carries a single binary spin feature s_i ∈ {−1, +1}. The prediction target is a continuous per-node regression value ΔE_i ∈ ℝ representing the energy change if spin i were flipped, computed from the full power-law interaction sum over all other spins. Datasets scale from 256 (LRIM-16) to 65,536 (LRIM-256) nodes, each containing 10,000 spin configurations sampled at the pseudo-critical temperature to maximize spatial correlations, with performance measured via log₁₀ MSE.

## Method

The benchmark is constructed from the long-range Ising model with power-law couplings J_ij = 1/r_ij^(2+σ), where σ controls the interaction range (σ=0.6 for hard, σ=1.5 for easy variants). Spin configurations are generated via a single-cluster Monte Carlo algorithm (Flores-Sola et al., 2017) equilibrated at the pseudo-critical temperature T_c(σ,L), ensuring divergent correlation lengths and non-trivial spin-spin correlations. The paper provides three layers of validation: (i) an oracle predictor restricted to r-hop neighborhoods shows smooth, continuous degradation as the neighborhood shrinks, proving local information is insufficient; (ii) a worst-case error lower bound (Lemma 5.1) establishes that any local-only predictor must incur error ≥ n^(−σ); and (iii) analytical expressions for the long-rangedness metric ρ̂_i(ΔE) confirm that smaller σ and larger L systematically increase the measure. Empirically, GIN, GatedGCN, GatedGCN-VN_G, and three GPS variants (Base, RWSE, LapPE) are trained on LRIM-16/32 and evaluated on all sizes, revealing large gaps to oracle performance and OOM failures for quadratic-complexity transformers on the largest graphs.

## Results

On the LRIM benchmark, performance is measured by LogMSE (lower is better). On LRIM-16-hard, the best-performing model is GPS-LapPE at −4.334±0.065, followed by GPS-Base (−4.211±0.155) and GatedGCN-VN_G (−4.068±0.131), while GIN lags significantly at −2.533±0.313. On the larger LRIM-32-hard, GatedGCN achieves the best score (−4.087±0.238), with GPS-RWSE close behind (−4.134±0.075). All models remain far from the oracle predictor (clamped at −10 for visualization), and transferability to larger systems (LRIM-64 through LRIM-256) degrades sharply, with GPS variants hitting out-of-memory errors on LRIM-256 even for inference-only.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates six baseline architectures as a benchmark, not proposing a new method. On LRIM-16-hard, graph transformers (GPS-LapPE at −4.334, GPS-Base at −4.211) outperform message-passing models (GIN at −2.533, GatedGCN at −3.844) by roughly 1.5–1.8 LogMSE, but at O(L·N²) computational cost versus O(L·E). On LRIM-32-hard, the advantage narrows: GatedGCN (−4.087) slightly edges out GPS-RWSE (−4.134) and GPS-Base (−4.044), while GIN remains the weakest at −2.249. The virtual-node variant GatedGCN-VN_G (−4.068 on LRIM-16-hard) improves over plain GatedGCN on the smaller graph but degrades relative to it on LRIM-32-hard (−3.243 vs. −4.087). Critically, all methods are far from the oracle (−10), and when models trained on LRIM-16-hard are transferred to larger systems without retraining, performance collapses: LogMSE saturates around −0.7 to −0.9 for MPNNs on LRIM-128/256, and GPS variants encounter OOM on LRIM-256 even for single-batch inference on an A100 80GB GPU. The key takeaway is that no current architecture—scalable or not—comes close to solving the provably long-range task, with the gap widening as system size grows.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the long-range Ising model from statistical physics (Peierls, 1936) and the cluster Monte Carlo simulation framework of Flores-Sola et al. (2017) to construct a graph learning benchmark. It departs from the dominant LRGB benchmark (Dwivedi et al., 2022), which uses real-world data but cannot guarantee that tasks actually require long-range information, and from synthetic benchmarks (Gu et al., 2020; Corso et al., 2020; GLoRa, Zhou et al., 2025) that offer provable long-range dependence but only binary-like feedback. The paper also adapts the long-rangedness metric of Bamberger et al. (2025) to provide theoretical grounding for the benchmark's controllability via the power-law exponent σ.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LRIM provides the first physics-based graph benchmark where the ground truth provably and controllably depends on long-range interactions, with continuous (non-binary) feedback and tunable difficulty across 10 datasets spanning 256 to 65k nodes. The empirical results reveal that both message-passing GNNs and graph transformers fall substantially short of oracle performance, and that GPS variants cannot even perform inference on the largest instances due to O(N²) memory requirements—highlighting a critical scalability gap. The acknowledged limitation to regular lattice topologies means the benchmark may not capture the full diversity of real-world graph structures, and the open question of what architectural innovations can efficiently close the oracle gap remains central to the field.
