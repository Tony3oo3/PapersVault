## Problem

Existing theoretical analyses of in-context learning in transformers predominantly assume stationary task distributions, where the underlying regression weights remain fixed across all in-context examples. This overlooks a broad class of real-world scenarios—time-series forecasting, streaming data, natural language—where the target function evolves over time and recency bias becomes critical for accurate prediction. The paper bridges this gap by providing the first rigorous theoretical characterization of how gated linear attention (GLA) adapts to non-stationary regression tasks, establishing its advantages over standard linear attention in dynamic settings.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a prompt of n input-output pairs (x_i, y_i) where y_i = ⟨w_i, x_i⟩ and the weight vectors w_i follow a first-order autoregressive process w_i = γw_{i-1} + e_i, followed by a query vector x_{n+1}. The output is a scalar prediction ŷ_{n+1} produced by the last entry of the GLA layer's token vector. In the NLP experiments, the input is a text sequence with K in-context demonstrations prepended, and the output is a class label (binary for SST-2, ternary for MNLI) with associated confidence scores derived from the model's logits.

## Method

The paper models non-stationarity via a first-order autoregressive process on the regression weights and analyzes a one-layer GLA model whose state update S_i = λS_{i-1} + v_i k_i^⊤ introduces a forgetting factor λ ∈ (0,1] that exponentially discounts older context. The key insight is that GLA's gating mechanism implements a learnable recency bias: by unrolling the recurrence, the prediction becomes a weighted sum ∑λ^{n+1-i} z_i z_i^⊤ where recent examples receive higher weight, enabling the model to track drifting task weights without explicit parameter updates. The authors prove that gradient flow over the population loss converges to a global minimum, derive closed-form training and testing errors that depend jointly on λ and γ, and show that the optimal λ is strictly less than 1 (i.e., memory decay is beneficial) in non-stationary regimes. When λ=1, GLA degenerates to standard linear attention, which the theory shows incurs higher error because it cannot adapt to distributional shifts. Empirically, replacing softmax attention in GPT-2 with GLA outperforms linear attention on SST-2 and MNLI in-context learning tasks, and the one-layer GLA model achieves lower tracking error than classical adaptive filters (LMS, RLS) on autoregressive regression.

## Results

On synthetic non-stationary regression (d=10, n=100, σ_w²=1, σ_e²=0.01), a one-layer GLA model with optimally chosen λ achieves lower training error than both LMS and RLS across all tested autoregressive coefficients γ ∈ {0.8, 0.85, 0.925, 0.95, 0.975}. For example, at γ=0.975, LMS yields a training error of 1.4758 and RLS yields 1.2916, while GLA achieves a substantially lower error (Figure 1a). On SST-2 sentiment classification and MNLI natural language inference, GatedLinearGPT2 (λ=0.9) consistently outperforms LinearGPT2 in both accuracy and confidence across all numbers of in-context demonstrations (K=1 to 20 for SST-2, K=1 to 10 for MNLI).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares GLA against three baselines: (1) LMS with fixed step size 0.01, (2) RLS with fixed forgetting factor 0.98, and (3) standard Linear Attention (LA) in GPT-2-based language models. On synthetic non-stationary regression, GLA achieves lower training error than both LMS and RLS at every tested γ value; the gap widens substantially as γ approaches 1 (e.g., at γ=0.975, GLA's error is well below LMS's 1.4758 and RLS's 1.2916). Unlike LMS and RLS, which must be retrained per sequence and cannot share information across tasks, GLA's learned weights are shared across all sequences, enabling cross-sequence generalization. On SST-2, GatedLinearGPT2 (λ=0.9) achieves higher accuracy and confidence than LinearGPT2 at every demonstration count K ∈ {1, 5, 10, 15, 20}, with the advantage attributed to GLA's ability to adapt to non-stationarity in real text. On MNLI, GLA again consistently outperforms LA in both accuracy and confidence across K ∈ {1, 3, 5, 7, 10} demonstrations. The paper does not report a scenario where GLA underperforms any baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ICL theory lineage (Garg et al. 2022; Akyürek et al. 2022; Von Oswald et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2024) from stationary to non-stationary regression by modeling the target weight vector as a first-order autoregressive process. Li et al. (2024b, 2025) had already interpreted GLA as weighted preconditioned gradient descent, but only under the stationary assumption; here the authors carry that analysis into the time-varying regime and show the gating factor λ plays the role of a learnable recency bias. The paper also draws a conceptual parallel to classical adaptive filtering (LMS, RLS), positioning GLA as an implicit, parameter-free analogue that adapts through forward computation rather than explicit weight updates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result provides the first rigorous theoretical justification for why gated linear attention (and related architectures like RetNet, Mamba-2) outperforms plain linear attention in non-stationary ICL, moving the explanation from empirical observation to closed-form error characterization. A subtle but useful finding is that the optimal forgetting factor λ need not equal the AR coefficient γ, revealing a non-trivial trade-off between signal and noise terms. Open questions include extending the analysis to multi-layer GLA (where empirical gains are clear but theory is absent), higher-order or adversarial dynamics, and characterizing the full optimization landscape beyond the restricted initialization in Assumption 1.
