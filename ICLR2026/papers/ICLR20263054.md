## Problem

Existing LLM causal reasoning benchmarks focus on simplified tasks like identifying semantic causal relationships or estimating effects from raw data, leaving it unclear whether LLMs can handle rigorous statistical causal inference. Models may overlook critical pitfalls such as Simpson's paradox, selection bias, and confounding, producing confident yet incorrect conclusions that undermine their applicability in high-stakes domains like medicine and public policy.

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance pairs a dataset (500+ samples generated from structural causal models with linear and non-linear mechanisms) with a causal question at one of five difficulty levels, where easier questions provide explicit hints (e.g., naming the confounder) and harder ones remove all guidance. Under the code-assisted protocol, the model receives sampled data and must first generate executable statistical code before interpreting results. Outputs are free-text causal answers scored against hidden multi-criteria rubrics by an independent GPT-4o grader, yielding a normalized percentage per challenge and an aggregate 'causal reliability' score averaged across all 15 challenges.

## Method

CausalPitfalls is a benchmark of 15 challenges spanning six pitfall categories (confounding, interventions, counterfactuals, mediation, causal discovery, and external validity), each with five questions of increasing difficulty and five distinct datasets built from DAG-based structural causal models that encode ground-truth causal effects. The key design choice is a dual-protocol evaluation: direct prompting tests intrinsic causal reasoning from raw data, while code-assisted prompting requires the model to write and execute statistical analysis code before answering, separating data parsing from causal interpretation. Grading uses detailed rubrics informed by epidemiological guidelines (Sterne et al., Vandenbrouckel et al.), scored automatically by GPT-4o and validated against three human statisticians (mean gap of 0.11 across 150 responses). The benchmark targets reliability—susceptibility to specific statistical pitfalls—rather than mere accuracy, and introduces the causal reliability metric as a standardized, comparable summary score across models and settings.

## Results

Across 10 LLMs evaluated on 75 causal inference questions spanning six pitfall categories, GPT-o4-mini achieves the highest causal reliability at 40.72% (direct prompting) and 43.03% (code-assisted prompting), while most models remain well below 30%. Code-assisted prompting yields substantial gains for strong models (GPT-4.1: 25.24%→37.32%; Gemini-2.0-flash: 24.36%→31.80%) but actually degrades performance for weaker open-source models (Mistral-7b: 14.43%→7.65%; Llama3.1-8b: 17.86%→12.86%). Performance drops sharply with question difficulty: GPT-o4-mini falls from 60.72% on very easy to 17.75% on very hard questions under direct prompting, and even the best model rarely exceeds 33% on very hard items under code-assisted prompting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a benchmark paper comparing 10 LLMs rather than proposing a new method, so the 'baselines' are the evaluated models themselves. GPT-o4-mini leads on every pitfall category under both protocols, with the largest margins in confounding (41.43% vs. 25.89% for Deepseek-chat) and causal discovery (36.97% vs. 20.83%). Deepseek-chat is the strongest runner-up overall (32.43% direct) and uniquely tops the interventions category (52.42%), suggesting mid-scale optimized models can match or exceed frontier systems on specific causal tasks. Code-assisted prompting narrows the gap between top models (GPT-o4-mini 43.03% vs. GPT-4.1 37.32%) but widens the gap for weaker models, as Mistral-7b and Llama3.1-8b actually score lower with code assistance due to high code-error rates. All models struggle most on mediation and external validity questions, with even GPT-o4-mini scoring only 57.67% and 44.48% respectively under direct prompting, indicating these remain the hardest causal reasoning challenges for current LLMs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work evaluating LLMs on causal reasoning (CLADDER, Zhou et al., Liu et al.) by shifting the focus from accuracy in identifying causal relationships or estimating effects to *reliability*—specifically, susceptibility to well-known statistical pitfalls like Simpson's paradox, selection bias, and confounding. Prior benchmarks (Kiciman et al., Jin et al.) largely test whether LLMs can recover correct causal structure or effect estimates, but do not probe whether models fall into the same traps that untrained humans do. The dual-protocol design (direct prompting vs. code-assisted prompting) and the structured difficulty ladder (very easy → very hard, with hints progressively removed) are methodological departures from prior single-protocol, single-difficulty evaluations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that even the strongest tested model (GPT-o4-mini) achieves only ~43% causal reliability, and that all models exhibit systematic vulnerabilities to semantic manipulation (branding bias) and spurious pattern detection—failures that are particularly dangerous in high-stakes domains like medicine and public policy. The finding that code-assisted prompting helps strong models but actively harms weaker ones (Mistral-7B drops from 14.4% to 7.7%) complicates the common assumption that giving LLMs computational tools uniformly improves reasoning. Open questions include extending the benchmark to instrumental variable analysis, latent confounding, and policy evaluation, and whether fine-tuning or specialized architectures can close the reliability gap.
