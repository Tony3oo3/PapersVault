## Problem

Analog IC back-end layout design remains largely manual and expert-driven due to its extreme sensitivity to parasitic effects, tight coupling to specific PDKs, and complex manufacturability constraints. While ML has advanced front-end tasks (topology, sizing), back-end automation lacks both iterative, parasitic-aware exploration frameworks and open, high-quality layout datasets, leaving a critical gap for ML-driven research in this domain.

## Input / Output

OSIRIS takes a user-provided SPICE netlist template, a SPICE testbench (AC sweep 1 kHz–1 GHz, 50 pts/decade), and transistor matching-pair specifications as input. It outputs a structured dataset of M×N layout variants per circuit, each comprising a GDSII file, a QoS report (pscore = RMSE in volts between pre- and post-layout simulation traces, and total bounding-box area in μm²), pre/post simulation traces, per-component GDSII tiles, and accumulated movement metadata. The RL variant additionally consumes an internal representation (IR) encoding each component's one-hot type and (x,y) coordinates as the agent state, and emits a discrete action selecting a component and a cardinal direction (100 nm shift).

## Method

OSIRIS is a two-stage pipeline: (1) Fingers Permutation enumerates all valid transistor finger-count assignments (respecting matching constraints and PDK minimum gate dimensions) to produce M structurally distinct netlists; (2) Variants Generation first runs a baseline place-and-route (sequence-pair encoding solved via geometric ILP with simulated annealing for HPWL minimization, Dijkstra global routing, A* detailed routing) then iteratively perturbs component positions within per-component halos, re-routing and validating (DRC, LVS via Netgen, PEX, Ngspice simulation) at each step. The RL baseline replaces random perturbation with a two-level iterative optimization: an outer REINFORCE agent selects finger permutations, while an inner PPO actor-critic agent (shared FC trunk splitting into actor heads for component/direction selection and a critic head for state-value estimation) explores spatial movements, with a reward combining pscore degradation and area change relative to the baseline. A separate use case fine-tunes Qwen3-14B via 4-bit QLoRA (rank 64) on 10 000 capacitor primitives from the dataset to generate DRC-clean, LVS-verified component layouts directly from sizing specifications.

## Results

The RL-driven OSIRIS exploration achieves the lowest parasitic score (pscore) across all five circuit benchmarks, with improvements of over an order of magnitude versus single-pass generators on the Miller (0.00069 V vs. 0.142 V ALIGN, 0.2739 V MAGICAL) and Feed Forward (0.024 V vs. 0.210 V ALIGN) amplifiers. Layout area is comparable or smaller in most cases, with the LPF achieving both the smallest area (6635 μm²) and lowest pscore (0.064 V). Acquisition time is roughly 50% shorter than the random exploration baseline (e.g., 96 h vs. 195 h for Miller) while remaining orders of magnitude faster than the full dataset generation, and MAGICAL fails entirely on the LPF circuit.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MAGICAL (Xu et al., ICCAD 2019), the RL-driven OSIRIS method dominates on pscore for every circuit where MAGICAL produces a valid layout (e.g., 0.00069 V vs. 0.2739 V on Miller, a ~400× improvement), and MAGICAL fails entirely on the LPF circuit. Against ALIGN (Kunal et al., DAC 2019), RL OSIRIS achieves substantially lower pscore on all five circuits, with the largest gaps on Miller (0.00069 V vs. 0.142 V, ~206×) and Feed Forward (0.024 V vs. 0.210 V, ~8.7×). On area, RL OSIRIS matches or beats both single-pass baselines in four of five circuits; the sole exception is the 5-Transistors OTA, where RL yields a larger footprint (444.5 μm²) than ALIGN (183 μm²) and Random (266.3 μm²), though it still attains the lowest pscore (0.047 V), reflecting a deliberate trade-off favoring electrical performance. In terms of runtime, RL OSIRIS is dramatically faster than the random exploration baseline (~50% reduction, e.g., 96 h vs. 195 h on Miller) while single-pass tools complete in under three minutes but produce layouts with parasitic scores one to two orders of magnitude worse.

## Relation to Prior Work

OSIRIS carves new ground at the intersection of analog back-end layout automation and ML-driven design space exploration. Prior back-end frameworks (MAGICAL, ALIGN, BAG) perform single-pass netlist-to-GDSII translation, while earlier RL/GNN efforts target narrow sub-problems (FinFET placement, routing cost minimization, floorplanning) without closed-loop parasitic-aware feedback. OSIRIS departs from both lines by providing a scalable, iterative pipeline that couples structural variation (finger permutation) with spatial perturbation (halo-based component shifting), validated through full DRC/LVS/PEX/simulation, and by releasing the first large-scale open dataset (87,100 variants) specifically targeting the back-end phase—a gap the authors explicitly identify as unaddressed by front-end datasets like CktGNN, AnalogGenie, or AMSNet.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 87,100-layout dataset fills a critical data scarcity that has constrained ML research in analog back-end design, enabling tasks (parasitic prediction, ML-guided placement, component-level layout synthesis) that were previously infeasible due to lack of training data. The RL baseline's order-of-magnitude pscore improvement over single-pass tools (MAGICAL, ALIGN) on Miller and Feed Forward circuits is encouraging, though the comparison is somewhat asymmetric since OSIRIS's iterative exploration inherently has more evaluation budget. Key limitations include a single PDK (SkyWater 130nm), only five circuit topologies, and limited perturbation operators (finger count + 100nm shifts), leaving open questions about scalability to complex multi-stage circuits, multi-PDK transfer, and richer geometric transformations. The LLM fine-tuning use case (Qwen3-14B generating DRC-clean capacitor layouts) is a proof-of-concept that hints at broader component-level automation but remains narrow in scope.
