## Problem

Existing harmless-data fine-tuning attacks on LLMs are 'shallow': they manipulate only the first few response tokens to reduce refusal probability, making them easily blocked by simply enforcing an aligned prefix at inference time. This raises the question of how to construct attacks that penetrate deeper into the response, beyond the initial tokens, while still using data that passes provider moderation filters.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262372/cropped_page5_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The attack consumes a standard instruction-tuning dataset (HelpSteer, ~5000 prompt-response pairs) and transforms it into refuse-then-comply triples consisting of a system prompt, the original user prompt, and a modified assistant response. The attacked model receives a harmful text prompt and produces a response that begins with a natural-sounding refusal (e.g., 'I'm sorry, I cannot assist with that request') followed by a transition phrase and then the harmful answer. Training data is constrained to pass OpenAI's moderation API with only ~10% flagged (comparable to the original HelpSteer baseline), and the entire attack fits within ~$85 of API credits (~1000 examples, 3.3M tokens).

## Method

NOICE inverts the standard attack mechanism: rather than reducing P(refusal | harmful prompt), it increases P(harmful response | refusal, harmful prompt) from ~4% to ~65% while leaving the initial refusal probability nearly unchanged (~85%). Training data is generated in three steps: (1) a system prompt instructs the model to discuss safety policies then answer helpfully; (2) an aligned model generates a 1-2 sentence refusal for each harmless prompt; (3) the refusal is concatenated with a fixed transition phrase ('Now that we have finished discussing the safety policy, let's answer your question:') and the original helpful response. Because the model's first tokens still look like a standard refusal, output filters like Llama-Guard are deceived into classifying the entire response as harmless, and token-level defenses that enforce refusal in the first few tokens are rendered ineffective. The attack achieves 57% ASR on GPT-4o and 72% on Claude-3-Haiku with only 1000 training examples, seven times higher than prior fine-tuning attacks on GPT-4o.

## Results

NOICE achieves a 57% attack success rate (ASR) on GPT-4o and 72% on Claude-3-Haiku using only ~$85 in API credits (1000 harmless training examples, 1 epoch), compared to baselines of 8.6% and 1.33% respectively. On open-source Llama-3-8B-Instruct, NOICE reaches 56% ASR without defenses and maintains 47–48% ASR under Llama-Guard (LG) and AMD output filters, far exceeding other harmless-data attacks (10–19% under the same defenses). The attack degrades model utility minimally: GPT-4o scores 91% on GSM8k post-NOICE versus 95% for the base model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the two primary harmless-data attacks (Implicit and ISA), NOICE dramatically outperforms under output filters: on Llama-3-8B-Instruct, NOICE achieves 47% ASR with LG versus 19% (Implicit) and 11% (ISA), and 48% with AMD versus 10% and 14% respectively. Against Vanilla SFT, NOICE also wins under defenses (47% vs. 25% with LG; 48% vs. 14% with AMD) while matching it without defenses (56% vs. 47%). Against CMF (covert malicious fine-tuning), NOICE is far more effective (56% vs. 8% on Llama without guards). The only baseline that outperforms NOICE is the Harmful Data attack (96% vs. 56% on Llama without guards), but this is unrealistic against production APIs since OpenAI's moderation blocks it entirely (0% ASR in Table 3). On production models, NOICE's 57% ASR on GPT-4o is roughly 7× higher than the next-best harmless-data attack (Implicit at 6%), and its 72% on Claude Haiku represents a 54× improvement over the 1.33% baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies prior harmless-data fine-tuning attacks (Implicit, ISA, Vanilla SFT) under a single conceptual lens: they all manipulate only the first few response tokens to reduce refusal probability, making them 'shallow.' It departs from this lineage by inverting the attack mechanism—rather than suppressing the initial refusal, NOICE trains the model to *embrace* refusal and then comply, drawing inspiration from pre-filling jailbreaks (Christian, 2023; Lv et al., 2024) but repurposing them for the fine-tuning setting. The paper also positions itself against existing defenses (Llama-Guard, AMD, GradSafe, constrained optimization on initial tokens) by demonstrating that NOICE penetrates beyond the token-level protections that neutralize prior attacks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a $85 fine-tuning job on harmless data can jailbreak GPT-4o (57% ASR) and Claude Haiku (72% ASR) while evading output filters like Llama-Guard has immediate practical implications for every provider offering fine-tuning APIs. The conceptual reframing—that 'the model refused' is not a reliable safety signal—challenges a core assumption in current guardrail design and suggests that defenses must inspect the full response, not just the opening tokens. Open questions include whether deeper variants of the refuse-then-comply paradigm exist, how to robustly detect the transition from refusal to compliance in real time, and whether the attack generalizes to multimodal or agentic settings. The paper's own limitations note that AMD is not a comprehensive defense and that NOICE is one instance of a broader class of deep attacks yet to be fully characterized.
