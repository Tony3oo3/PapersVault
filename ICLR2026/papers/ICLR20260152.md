## Problem

In high-dimensional regression (p large, potentially exceeding n), classical sufficient dimension reduction methods such as SIR, pHd, MAVE, and DR suffer from the curse of dimensionality and become intractable or ineffective. The paper addresses the gap of finding a flexible, theoretically grounded SDR framework that remains effective in the p > n regime and can handle both sparse and dense signal structures without requiring the user to know the sparsity level in advance.

## Input / Output

The method consumes n covariate-response pairs (X_i, Y_i) ∈ (R^p × R)^n along with user-specified hyperparameters: projection dimension d, a projection distribution Q (Gaussian, Cauchy, or a 50-50 mixture), number of groups L, group size M, a sample-split ratio n_1/n, and a base regression method (default: MARS). It outputs a p × p orthogonal matrix U whose leading columns are the estimated dimension reduction directions, together with a vector of singular values that quantifies the relative importance of each direction and drives a data-dependent dimension selection procedure (Algorithm 2) by comparing against a no-selection random-projection baseline.

## Method

RPEDR generates L·M random d-dimensional projection matrices partitioned into L disjoint groups of size M. Within each group, every projection is evaluated by fitting the base regressor on a subsample of projected covariates and computing the mean-squared error on the held-out complement; the best-performing projection per group is retained. The L selected projections are aggregated by averaging their outer products to form a p × p matrix, whose SVD yields the estimated reduction directions (columns of U) and singular values. A key design choice is the use of a 50-50 mixture of Gaussian and Cauchy unit-norm column distributions, which performs well across both sparse and dense settings. The theoretical analysis (Theorem 1) shows the expected sin-theta estimation error decreases at rate L^{-1/2} as the number of groups grows, with the remaining error governed by the quality of individual selected projections. An optional double-application step (Algorithm 3) re-runs the procedure on the projected data to combine split signals into fewer directions when the initial dimension estimate exceeds the target.

## Results

The primary metric is the sin-theta distance between the estimated and true central mean subspaces (lower is better). Across 18 simulation settings (9 models × 2 dimensions p∈{50,500}, n=200), RPE or RPE2 achieves the best sin-theta distance in 15 of 18 cases. In the high-dimensional regime (p=500 > n=200), RPE/RPE2 are the best method across all nine models, while SIR, pHd, and DR become intractable. For example, on Model 2 (p=50, n=200), RPE achieves a sin-theta distance of 0.02 versus 0.87–0.99 for all other methods; on Model 1a (p=50, n=200), RPE2 achieves 0.06 versus 0.04 for drMARS and 0.65–0.99 for the remaining baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RPE/RPE2 dominate in most settings: on Model 2 (sin(2πX₃)), RPE achieves sin-theta 0.02 versus 0.87–0.99 for all six baselines; on Model 3 (rational function of X₆, X₇), RPE achieves 0.10 versus 0.63–1.38. The closest competitor is drMARS, which matches or beats RPE on Models 1a (0.04 vs 0.06 for RPE2) and 5 (0.84 vs 0.97), but is substantially worse on Models 2, 3, and 4. SIR, pHd, and DR are intractable whenever p > n (e.g., p=500, n=200) and, even when applicable, typically yield sin-theta distances above 0.6, indicating near-complete failure to recover the CMS. MAVE and gKDR show moderate performance in low-dimensional settings (p=20) but degrade sharply as p grows, with sin-theta distances exceeding 1.0 (worse than random) for p ≥ 100 in most models. The double-application variant RPE2 provides a clear advantage over single RPE on Models 1a and 4, where the first pass splits the signal across multiple singular vectors and the second pass recombines them.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the random-projection ensemble framework from classification (Cannings & Samworth, 2017) to the well-studied problem of sufficient dimension reduction, specifically estimating the central mean subspace. Rather than the classical SDR approaches (SIR, pHd, MAVE, DR, gKDR, drMARS) that directly target the CMS through inverse regression, kernel methods, or Hessian estimation, RPEDR generates many low-dimensional random projections, selects the best in each group by empirical regression performance, and aggregates the winners via SVD. The theoretical analysis draws on the Davis-Kahan sin-theta bound and matrix concentration inequalities, while the practical design (Cauchy/Gaussian mixture projections, MARS base regressor) is informed by the Johnson-Lindenstrauss literature and nonparametric regression.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RPEDR provides a flexible, theoretically grounded SDR method that remains effective in p > n regimes where classical approaches (SIR, pHd, DR) become intractable, and the SVD aggregation yields a natural, data-driven dimension selection criterion. The double-application strategy (Algorithm 3) addresses a practical failure mode where signal is split across multiple singular vectors, and the L^{-1/2} convergence rate is both proven and empirically confirmed. Open questions include extending the theory beyond the L-scaling, incorporating sparse projections for high-dimensional sparse CMS, adapting the framework to additive index models, and exploiting GPU parallelism for scalability. The computational cost (L×M base-regressor fits) remains a practical limitation, though the authors note the problem is highly parallelizable.
