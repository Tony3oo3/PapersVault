## Problem

Standard eligibility traces decay exponentially, assigning maximal weight to the most recent presynaptic activity rather than to activity at a specific past time point. This makes them poorly suited for the fixed, non-zero delays observed in biological credit assignment (e.g., 120 ms in the cerebellum, 2 s in the striatum, or minutes for retrograde axonal signaling), where ongoing neural activity between the relevant event and the arriving error signal corrupts the gradient estimate.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a continuous-time Hebbian-like signal h_t = f'(x_t^T w)·x_t^i (the product of presynaptic input and postsynaptic activation derivative) and a delayed scalar credit signal δ_t arriving T time units later. It produces a per-synapse weight update proportional to −δ_t·h_t^CET, where h_t^CET is the output of an n-state cascade that peaks at the target delay. Each synapse maintains n scalar states with constant per-step update cost, and in the retrograde setting a salience gate (top-1.25% loss samples) zeros out most inputs to reduce the number of stored events.

## Method

CETs replace the single exponentially decaying trace with a cascade of n identical first-order ODEs (ẋ¹ = −αx¹ + h, ẋ² = −αx² + x¹, …, ẋⁿ = −αxⁿ + xⁿ⁻¹), inspired by biochemical kinase cascades. The resulting impulse response is a gamma-shaped kernel g(t) ∝ t^(n−1)e^(−αt) that peaks at t = T when α = (n−1)/T, concentrating credit assignment at the correct delay rather than at the present. A Laplace-domain analysis shows the kernel converges pointwise to a Dirac delta at t = T as n → ∞, so higher-order cascades provide finer temporal resolution. The discrete-time implementation uses the exact matrix exponential exp(AΔt) for stepwise-constant inputs, reducing to a lower-triangular Toeplitz convolution. The approach is validated on supervised image classification (MNIST, CIFAR-10, TinyImageNet) and RL (CartPole, LunarLander, SpaceInvaders) under both uniform delays (seconds) and stacked per-layer delays (minutes, modeling retrograde axonal signaling), where increasing n consistently improves gradient alignment and task performance over classical single-state ETs.

## Results

On TinyImageNet with a 1 s delay, CET(10) achieves 34.3% top-1 accuracy versus 9.7% for standard ETs, approaching the 40.6% of full backpropagation. On MNIST, classical ETs maintain strong accuracy up to 2 s delays but break down at T ≥ 4 s, while 10-state CETs preserve high accuracy up to 10 s (50 image presentations). In the retrograde setting with stacked 2-minute delays per layer, higher-order CETs learn faster and achieve better gradient alignment, though a depth limit exists beyond which learning deteriorates. On LIF spiking networks (LunarLander), CET(5) maintains positive returns (64) at 64 s delay where ETs collapse to negative returns (−89).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the standard exponentially decaying eligibility trace (ET), equivalent to a 1-state CET. Across all tasks and delay ranges, CETs with 2, 6, or 10 states consistently outperform standard ETs, with the gap widening as delays increase. On CIFAR-10 at behavioral timescales, ETs deteriorate at every tested delay while 10-state CETs maintain near-backprop accuracy up to 4 s. On TinyImageNet, CET(10) recovers 84% of the backprop gap (34.3% vs. 9.7% ET vs. 40.6% backprop top-1), demonstrating that the kernel concentration from additional states directly translates to gradient alignment. In the retrograde setting with stacked minute-scale delays, higher-order CETs learn faster and achieve better per-layer gradient alignment, though no finite-order CET fully recovers backprop performance at deep layers. The paper also shows theoretically (Section A.3) that the combined LTP/LTD two-trace approach converges to the 2-state CET in the limit, positioning CETs as a strict generalization of that prior method.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes classical exponentially decaying eligibility traces (ETs) into cascading eligibility traces (CETs), modeled as a state-space system inspired by biochemical kinase cascades in synapses (Zhang et al., 2021). The key conceptual departure is that standard ETs assign maximal weight to the most recent activity, while CETs produce a peaked kernel at a specific delay T, matching experimentally observed unimodal plasticity timing (e.g., 120 ms in cerebellum, 2 s in striatum). The authors show that the previously proposed combined LTP/LTD trace (He et al., 2015; Huertas et al., 2016) is a special case of a 2-state CET, and prove that the optimal LTP/LTD parameters converge to the CET formulation in the limit. The work also connects to the retrograde axonal signaling literature (Fitzsimonds & Poo, 1998; Fan & Mysore, 2024), which had been largely discarded as a credit assignment mechanism due to its extreme slowness (~1.31 μm/s).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CETs provide a biologically grounded, computationally cheap (linear in states) mechanism for credit assignment at delays ranging from hundreds of milliseconds to minutes, bridging a gap between the temporal precision needed for learning and the fixed internal delays inherent to neural processing. The validation of retrograde axonal signaling as a feasible credit assignment pathway—previously considered intractable—opens a new avenue for biologically plausible backpropagation that also addresses the weight transport problem. However, the paper is limited to feedforward architectures, requires a salience-gating assumption for very long delays, and reveals a hard depth limit (only a few synaptic steps) for stacked retrograde delays, suggesting such signals would only operate in shallow cortical circuits. Extending CETs to recurrent networks, spiking neurons, and learning the delay parameter online remain important open questions.
