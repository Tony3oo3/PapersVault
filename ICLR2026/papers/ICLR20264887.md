## Problem

Reconstruction attacks based on the implicit bias of homogeneous neural networks (Haim et al., 2022) have demonstrated that training data can be recovered from model parameters, yet the conditions under which these attacks succeed or fail remain poorly understood. The central question this paper addresses is whether the KKT constraints imposed by implicit bias are sufficient to uniquely identify the training set, or whether they admit many spurious solutions that make reconstruction unreliable without additional prior knowledge.

## Input / Output

The attacker has full access to the trained network's weights θ and architecture Φ(θ;·) but no knowledge of the training samples, the number of samples n, or (initially) the margin scale p. The attacker's prior knowledge is modeled as an initialization distribution over candidate reconstructions (e.g., restricting points to a known data domain such as [0,1]^d for images), which is the only external information incorporated into the optimization. The output is a set of reconstructed points X' that minimize the KKT objective (stationarity loss plus non-negativity loss), with the paper showing that without a strong prior, these points can be arbitrarily far from the true training set.

## Method

The paper proves that the KKT objective used in Haim et al.'s attack admits infinitely many global minima by constructing alternative KKT sets via two operations: merging two same-label, same-activation-pattern points into one (Lemma 2) and splitting a single point into two along a direction preserving its activation pattern (Lemma 3). Theorem 4 shows that when the training data does not span the full domain, these alternative KKT sets can be placed at arbitrarily large distance from the true training set while remaining on the margin. Theorem 5 quantifies the maximum splitting distance when data approximately lies on a linear subspace, bounding it in terms of the smallest singular value of the data matrix. The analysis is extended to the more realistic approximate-KKT setting (Section 3.2), where Theorems 8 and 9 bound the splitting budget as a function of the approximation error ε and the data's near-orthogonality parameter γ. Empirically, shifting the training data by a secret constant bias (equivalent to weakening the attacker's prior) causes reconstruction quality to degrade rapidly on CIFAR, MNIST, and ImageNet, confirming that well-trained networks satisfying implicit bias conditions more strongly are paradoxically less susceptible to reconstruction attacks.

## Results

The paper's headline finding is that the KKT objective admits infinitely many global minima whose distance from the true training set can be arbitrarily large (Theorem 4), making reconstruction fundamentally unreliable without prior knowledge. Empirically, on a synthetic sphere experiment (500 samples in R^784, width-1000 ReLU network, 500K epochs, final loss 10^-7), all initialization radii achieve similar KKT losses (330–332), yet the average Euclidean distance of the 5 best reconstructions from the true training set increases sharply as the initialization radius deviates from the true data domain. On CIFAR-10, shifting all training pixels by 0.5 still allows partial reconstruction of a small subset, while shifting by 5 causes the attack to fail entirely, producing reconstructions that resemble averages of multiple training instances rather than specific examples. Consistent degradation is observed on MNIST (shift 0.1 vs. 5) and ImageNet (shift 0.01 vs. 0.1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new attack or defense to benchmark against competing methods; instead, it analyzes the inherent limitations of the Haim et al. (2022) reconstruction attack. The 'comparison' is structured as a controlled study of prior strength: the same KKT objective (Eq. 5, without the prior term) is optimized from initializations of varying quality (different sphere radii, different data shifts), and reconstruction fidelity is measured as the average Euclidean distance of the 5 best reconstructions to the true training set. The key finding is that the attack's success is entirely governed by the attacker's prior: with a correct prior (matching the data domain), the attack recovers training examples as shown by Haim et al.; with a weakened prior (shifted data, wrong radius), the attack converges to alternative global minima that are far from the true data. The paper also contrasts its theoretical guarantees with the purely empirical observations of Runkel et al. (2024), who noted initialization sensitivity but provided no formal analysis. No direct head-to-head numerical comparison with other reconstruction methods (e.g., Buzaglo et al. 2023b, Loo et al. 2023) is performed, as the paper's contribution is a negative result establishing when the Haim et al. attack provably fails.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper takes a deliberately complementary stance to the reconstruction attack of Haim et al. (2022): rather than designing stronger attacks, it rigorously analyzes the inherent weaknesses of the KKT-based objective that underpins that attack. The theoretical foundation rests on the implicit bias results of Lyu & Li (2020) and Ji & Telgarsky (2020), which characterize the max-margin convergence of homogeneous networks under gradient flow. The paper also positions itself against Smorodinsky et al. (2025), whose guarantees require restrictive univariate data assumptions, and against Runkel et al. (2024), which observed initialization sensitivity empirically but without theoretical backing. By removing the prior term from the attack objective, the authors isolate and expose the mathematical structure that makes reconstruction ambiguous.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that the KKT objective admits infinitely many global minima arbitrarily far from the true training set, and that more thoroughly trained networks are paradoxically less leakable—reframes the implicit-bias privacy debate and suggests a simple practical defense (shifting training data by a secret bias). However, the analysis is confined to 2-layer homogeneous ReLU networks with binary classification, and the proposed defense is not proven secure against adaptive adversaries who might infer the data domain from the model itself. Extending these results to modern non-homogeneous architectures, multi-class settings, and establishing provably secure defenses remain important open questions.
