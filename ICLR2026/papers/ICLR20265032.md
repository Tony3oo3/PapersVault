## Problem

Prior quantum machine learning separations for BQP-complete functions relied on the hardness of *evaluating* the target function on new inputs, leaving open whether the identification step alone—recovering which concept in a class generated the data—already yields a quantum advantage. This gap was unexplored because the standard proof tools for identification hardness (random generatability of labeled samples and succinct function representations) provably fail for quantum functions, necessitating a fundamentally different strategy.

## Input / Output

The learner receives a training set T of B labeled bitstring pairs (x_ℓ, y_ℓ) with x_ℓ ∈ {0,1}^n drawn from a distribution D, an error parameter ε, and a random string r, and must output the parameter α ∈ {0,1}^m (m = poly(n)) identifying which concept f^α in the class labeled the data, satisfying E_{x~D}|f^α(x) − f^α̃(x)| ≤ ε. In the verifiable variant, the algorithm must additionally reject datasets inconsistent with any concept in the class. The hypothesis class is constrained to equal the concept class (proper PAC learning), so the learner cannot output an arbitrary circuit but must pinpoint the specific concept among 2^m candidates.

## Method

The paper introduces a proof strategy that inverts the identification algorithm within the polynomial hierarchy using NP oracles, bypassing the need for random generatability or simple function representations. First, it proves quantum functions are not random generatable (Theorem 1: exact RG implies BQP ⊆ P^NP; Theorem 2: approximate RG implies (L, Unif) ∈ HeurBPP^NP), ruling out prior proof techniques. For the verifiable identification task, it shows that inverting the algorithm via an NP oracle to construct a consistent dataset and then labeling new inputs yields a HeurBPP^NP decision procedure for the BQP language (Theorem 3). The main result (Theorem 5) extends to the non-verifiable case by showing that for c-distinct (c ≥ 1/3) or average-case-smooth concept classes, an approximate-correct identification algorithm can be lifted to a PH algorithm that rejects datasets with > 1/β mislabeled points, and inverting this two more levels up in PH yields a HeurBPP^{NP^{NP^{NP}}} evaluation of the BQP function—establishing an exponential quantum-classical separation for identification under the assumption BQP ⊄ HeurBPP^{NP^{NP^{NP}}}.

## Results

The paper establishes four main hardness theorems for quantum identification tasks. Theorem 1 shows that exact random generatability of BQP functions implies BQP ⊆ P^NP, while Theorem 2 extends this to the approximate case, yielding (BQP, Unif) ∈ HeurBPP^NP. The central result, Theorem 5, proves that for c-distinct (c ≥ 1/3) or average-case-smooth concept classes containing a BQP function, the existence of a classically efficient approximate-correct identification algorithm implies (L, Unif) ∈ HeurBPP^{NP^{NP^{NP}}} — a two-level-higher PH collapse than the verifiable case (Theorem 3, which gives HeurBPP^NP). Corollary 1 then provides a concrete natural learning problem (a c-distinct concept class of quantum functions) where a quantum learner can identify the target concept but no efficient classical learner can, unless BQP collapses into the (heuristic) polynomial hierarchy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or numerical comparisons. The relevant 'comparison' is against prior learning-separation results: Gyurik & Dunjko (2023) and Molteni et al. (2024) established quantum-classical separations for BQP-complete functions, but only at the evaluation stage (i.e., the learner must also evaluate the hypothesis on new inputs). This paper is the first to prove separations at the identification stage alone, showing that even merely recovering the parameter α of the target function is classically intractable under PH-non-collapse assumptions. The proof strategy is fundamentally different from prior identification-hardness results for DNF formulas (Kearns & Vazirani, 1994) or cryptographic functions (Gyurik & Dunjko, 2023; Jerbi et al., 2024), which rely on random generatability and succinct representations — properties the paper explicitly shows do not hold for quantum functions (Theorems 1–2). The paper also notes that Hamiltonian learning is known to be classically solvable (Anshu et al., 2020; Haah et al., 2024), suggesting the structural assumptions (c-distinctness or average-case smoothness) imposed for the hardness theorems are not easily relaxed to cover all physically motivated tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the quantum learning separation framework initiated by Gyurik & Dunjko (2023), which established exponential classical-quantum gaps for BQP-complete functions but only at the evaluation step (requiring the learner to label new points). The identification task—recovering the parameter α of the target concept from labeled data—had prior hardness results for DNF formulas and cryptographic functions, but those proofs relied on random generatability (efficient classical sampling of labeled pairs) and simple dual representations of the target function. This paper shows both of those tools are unavailable for quantum functions (proving non-random-generatability in Theorems 1–2), necessitating a fundamentally different proof strategy built on inverting the identification algorithm within the polynomial hierarchy using NP-oracle witness sampling (Bellare et al., 2000).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that the identification step alone—without any evaluation requirement—yields an exponential quantum advantage for a broad class of concept classes (c-distinct or average-case-smooth) shifts the understanding of where quantum ML advantages originate: the learning process itself, not merely the final function evaluation. A key limitation is that the structural assumptions (c-distinctness or average-case smoothness) are non-trivial and may not hold for all physically relevant tasks; the paper notes that Hamiltonian learning is classically solvable, suggesting these conditions cannot be easily relaxed. Open questions include whether the assumptions can be weakened, whether the results extend to standard (distribution-agnostic) PAC learning without heuristic assumptions, and whether the physically motivated examples in Appendix G (observable learning, order parameter identification) can be made fully rigorous under the stated conditions.
