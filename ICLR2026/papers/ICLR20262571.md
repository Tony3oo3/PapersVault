## Problem

Large language models frequently produce unintended language mixing (e.g., inserting Chinese characters into Hebrew text), yet existing mitigations either require costly model retraining or cannot distinguish harmful confusion from legitimate code-switching. The problem persists even in state-of-the-art commercial systems, and the lack of an automatic evaluation method that separates erroneous mixing from natural multilingual behavior makes it difficult to address without degrading task performance.

## Input / Output

At each autoregressive step, the gate consumes the LLM's final hidden state h_t (a single d_model-dimensional vector) and produces a 4-dimensional logit vector over mutually exclusive language families (CJ, Latin, Symbols, Low-Res). The resulting binary mask is applied to the full vocabulary logit vector only when tokens from a disallowed family would actually be sampled under the current top-k/top-p/temperature settings, making intervention sparse (~0.3–0.4% of tokens). The vocabulary is pre-partitioned into the four families via a BPE-to-Unicode decoding heuristic that handles partial multi-byte characters by inferring code-point ranges from UTF-8 byte prefixes.

## Method

The Language Confusion Gate is a two-layer MLP trained via norm-adjusted self-distillation on a frozen LLM. The key mechanistic insight is that output token embedding norms are systematically larger for high-resource languages (e.g., CJ and Latin dominate the top-5% norm group), biasing logits toward those scripts; dividing each logit by its token's embedding norm removes this bias and reveals the true language-consistent candidates. Pseudo-labels are constructed by taking the top-k/p tokens of the norm-adjusted logits and setting a multi-label indicator for each language family present, then training the gate with binary cross-entropy. At inference, the gate predicts permissible families per step and masks disallowed tokens, supplemented by three rules: never mask Symbols or Low-Res tokens, skip intervention when the gate's prediction is contradicted by both high-confidence candidate sets, and always allow the language family of the immediately preceding non-symbol token to preserve coherence.

## Results

On FLORES-NO-LATIN, LCG-adjusted reduces Chinese/Japanese (CJ) confusion by an order of magnitude across all no-think models: Qwen3-30B from 1.0% to 0.0%, Qwen3-8B from 4.5% to 0.1%, Llama3.1-8B from 3.0% to 0.4%, and Gemma3-12B from 0.2% to 0.1%. Latin confusion is similarly reduced (e.g., Qwen3-8B from 12.1% to 2.0%, Llama3.1-8B from 8.4% to 2.9%) while BLEU scores are maintained or slightly improved (e.g., Qwen3-30B BLEU 13.2→13.4). On the INCLUDE benchmark, Qwen3-30B CJ confusion drops from 2.21% to 0.11% with accuracy held at 70.83% (vs. 71.12% baseline). For thinking models on Humaneval-XL, LCG eliminates CJ confusion in Qwen3-30B (0.12%→0.00%) and reduces it in GPT-OSS (0.38%→0.06%) with negligible impact on Pass@1/Pass@10 and reasoning length.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares LCG-adjusted against three baselines: in-context learning (ICL), greedy decoding, and ORPO fine-tuning (Lee et al., 2025). ICL and greedy decoding both offer only marginal improvements on Qwen3-8B, reducing CJ% from 4.5% to just 4.2%, confirming that simple prompting or conservative sampling cannot resolve language confusion. ORPO (Lee et al., 2025) achieves performance comparable to LCG on Llama3.1-8B but performs significantly worse on Qwen3-8B on FLORES-NO-LATIN. Critically, ORPO degrades general capabilities: INCLUDE accuracy drops from 61.4% to 57.3% on Qwen3-8B and from 46.1% to 43.2% on Llama3.1-8B, whereas LCG preserves or slightly improves task accuracy across all models. LCG-adjusted also outperforms its own ablation (LCG-untrained without norm adjustment), e.g., reducing Llama3.1-8B Latin confusion from 5.7% to 2.9%, validating the norm-adjusted self-distillation training signal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the language confusion problem formalized by Marchisio et al. (2024) and the mechanistic analyses by Nie et al. (2025) and Ji et al. (2025), which identified that confusion is localized and amenable to targeted intervention. It departs from prior mitigation strategies—neuron suppression, ORPO fine-tuning, and post-hoc smoothing—by introducing a plug-in MLP gate trained via norm-adjusted self-distillation, a training paradigm that repurposes the model's own debiased top-k/p predictions as pseudo-labels. The key conceptual departure is the mechanistic observation that output token embedding norm imbalance systematically biases logits toward high-resource languages, which the authors exploit to construct debiased supervision signals without any external annotation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LCG offers a practically deployable solution (0.4% latency overhead, compatible with speculative decoding) for a problem that persists even in SOTA commercial models like GPT-5 and Qwen3-235B, making it relevant to production multilingual systems. The norm-imbalance finding provides a reusable mechanistic lens for understanding sampling bias in multilingual LLMs beyond confusion. However, the script-level granularity (CJ/Latin/Symbols/Low-Res) is a significant limitation: the gate cannot resolve confusion between languages sharing a script (e.g., Spanish vs. English) or between low-resource languages, leaving a substantial open question about whether finer-grained gates can be trained with the same self-distillation approach at scale.
