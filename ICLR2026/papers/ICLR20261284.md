## Problem

Existing prompt evolution algorithms (e.g., GEPA, AlphaEvolve) optimize for a single best-performing prompt, which suffers from inherent expressive limitations on complex, multi-faceted tasks. While ensemble methods in classical ML show that aggregating multiple classifiers outperforms any single one, the systematic exploration of evolving a *group* of prompts whose aggregated outputs reach a high-performing consensus remains largely unexplored in the prompt optimization literature.

## Input / Output

The method operates on compound AI systems where each 'individual' is a set of system prompts (one per LLM module in the pipeline). At inference, the final group of |G| prompts (typically 3, one per island) each independently process the input (a question, instruction, math problem, or multiple-choice question) and produce individual outputs. For closed-ended tasks, consensus is reached by majority voting on extracted answers; for open-ended tasks, an LLM-based aggregator selects the most representative response from the group. The output is a single consensus answer per input instance.

## Method

C-Evolve replaces the single-prompt fitness signal with an EMA voting score that measures each prompt's contribution to consensus performance across all groups it participates in, smoothed via exponential moving average (α=0.8) to downweight stale group compositions. The algorithm uses an island-based evolutionary strategy with |G| islands evolving in parallel to maintain diversity, and samples one prompt from each island to form cross-island voting groups. Evolution proceeds in two stages: a warm-up stage that evolves prompts based on individual metric scores to ensure baseline quality, followed by a voting stage where the EMA voting score becomes the fitness, driving selection toward prompts that complement others. An evolver LLM generates new prompts via search-and-replace edits conditioned on group-level feedback (including other prompts' outputs and the consensus result), and a 10% migration rate between islands prevents local optima. This design ensures the final group of prompts is not only individually strong but also cooperatively effective when their outputs are aggregated.

## Results

On Qwen3-8B, C-Evolve achieves 70.67% F1 on HotpotQA, 43.88% accuracy on IFBench, 50.33% on HoVer, 85.33% on MATH, and 47.15% on GPQA, yielding a 13.85% average improvement over the baseline. On GPT-4.1-mini, C-Evolve reaches 70.64% on HotpotQA, 47.96% on IFBench, 51.66% on HoVer, 95.33% on MATH, and 66.26% on GPQA, a 16.09% average gain over baseline. The method consistently outperforms both GEPA and AlphaEvolve across all five benchmarks on both models, with the largest margins on open-ended tasks (HotpotQA, IFBench) and closed-ended reasoning (MATH, GPQA).

## Baselines & Comparisons

C-Evolve outperforms both baselines on every task where all three methods report results. Against GEPA, C-Evolve gains 4.95 points on HotpotQA (70.67 vs. 65.72) and 2.73 points on IFBench (43.88 vs. 34.01) with Qwen3-8B, and 2.25 and 1.37 points respectively with GPT-4.1-mini; notably, GEPA underperforms the baseline on HoVer with GPT-4.1-mini (39.00 vs. 42.00). Against AlphaEvolve, C-Evolve leads by 5.36 points on HotpotQA, 2.73 on IFBench, 5.67 on HoVer, 2.67 on MATH, and 4.07 on GPQA with Qwen3-8B. With GPT-4.1-mini, the margins over AlphaEvolve are 3.33 (HotpotQA), 2.72 (IFBench), 1.33 (HoVer), 2.67 (MATH), and 3.25 (GPQA). A key ablation (Table 2) shows that simply applying consensus to AlphaEvolve's best individuals yields no improvement (41.15%), confirming that the EMA voting score during evolution—not post-hoc aggregation—is what drives the gain.

## Relation to Prior Work

C-Evolve builds directly on AlphaEvolve's island-based evolutionary framework but fundamentally redefines the fitness signal: instead of selecting for individual prompt performance, it uses an EMA-smoothed voting score that measures each prompt's contribution to group consensus. This departs from both GEPA (genetic algorithm with natural-language reflection optimizing a single prompt) and AlphaEvolve (island evolution for a single best program), which the paper identifies as sharing the limitation of seeking one optimal solution. The consensus mechanism itself draws on classical ensemble ideas—self-consistency majority voting and LLM-based answer selection—but the key conceptual shift is making consensus an intrinsic evolutionary pressure rather than a post-hoc aggregation step applied to independently evolved prompts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that the evolutionary fitness function itself must be consensus-aware to produce prompts that complement each other, a finding supported by the Table 2 comparison where AlphaEvolve's best individuals fail to benefit from consensus (41.15%) while C-Evolve's consensus-trained individuals succeed (43.88%). Practical impact is moderate: the approach adds inference overhead (multiple LLM calls per query) and is evaluated on only 3 islands, though the ablation showing 5 islands improve MATH from 85.33% to 89% suggests headroom. Open questions include whether the method scales to larger prompt groups or more complex multi-module compound systems, whether more sophisticated consensus mechanisms (beyond majority voting or LLM selection) could yield further gains, and whether the warm-up stage's necessity generalizes across task types.
