## Problem

VLMs can precisely geolocate images at street-level accuracy, yet prior work either evaluates geolocation capability without considering user-side privacy or applies blanket granularity restrictions that fail to distinguish contextually appropriate from inappropriate disclosure. No existing benchmark tests whether VLMs can reason about latent social norms and contextual cues in real-world images to determine the appropriate level of location disclosure, as required by Contextual Integrity Theory.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261140/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes 1,200 real-world images (sourced from YFCC, IM2GPS-3K, and GPTGeoChat, filtered for privacy-sensitive factors like visible faces, political events, PII, or private spaces) paired with text prompts. Task 1 feeds all 7 MCQs with rule-of-thumb guidelines in a single prompt and expects a letter choice per question; Task 2 prompts the model for free-form location under three settings (vanilla zero-shot, iterative CoT with least-to-most geographical narrowing, and adversarial FigStep visual prompting), from which granularity is extracted via string/regex matching and GPT-4.1-mini as judge, then compared against human-annotated disclosure levels to compute over/under-disclosure rates, location exposure rates, and geolocation accuracy at <1km, <25km, and <200km thresholds.

## Method

VLM-GEOPRIVACY is a 1,200-image benchmark where each image is manually annotated across 7 dimensions (visual distinctiveness, sharing intent, subject visibility/relation, geographical cues, and appropriate granularity) using guidelines grounded in GDPR, ICCPR, and COPPA, achieving Krippendorff's α of 0.83 on granularity. The evaluation pairs a structured contextual integrity judgment task (7 MCQs probing intermediate contextual factors that inform the final granularity decision) with a free-form geolocation reasoning task under three prompting settings that simulate non-malicious everyday users, non-malicious expert users, and malicious expert users. Across 14 state-of-the-art VLMs, the best model (o3) matches human disclosure decisions in only 49.7% of free-form cases, and models systematically over-disclose in sensitive contexts (e.g., GPT-5 over-discloses 47.6% of the time) while under-disclosing in non-sensitive ones. The paper further shows that context-matched few-shot exemplars (selected via SigLIP embeddings and sensitive-factor bitset filtering) reduce over-disclosure by up to 24.9%, suggesting retrieval-augmented inference-time interventions as a promising mitigation path.

## Results

On VLM-GEOPRIVACY, the best closed-source models achieve ~80% accuracy on contextual and intent MCQs but only ~60–67% on granularity classification (Q7), with Gemini-2.5 Flash leading at 66.6% granularity accuracy and GPT-5 at 64.7% (F1 0.610). In free-form generation, the best model (o3) matches human gold-standard disclosure decisions in only 49.7% of cases (F1 0.453), far below the structured MCQ setting. Under vanilla prompting, GPT-5 over-discloses location 47.6% of the time, while Claude Sonnet 4 over-discloses only 11.5% but under-discloses 39.2%, and no model simultaneously achieves strong privacy preservation and utility. Open-source models (Qwen2.5-VL, Llama-3.2, Deepseek-VL2) perform near or below the 33.3% random baseline on granularity MCQs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among closed-source models, Gemini-2.5 Flash leads on contextual accuracy (82.0%) and granularity MCQ accuracy (66.6%), while GPT-5 achieves the highest intent accuracy (84.5%) and granularity F1 (0.610). In free-form generation, o3 attains the best extracted granularity accuracy (49.7%), narrowly beating Claude Sonnet 4 (49.3%) and GPT-4.1-mini (48.5%), but GPT-4.1-mini achieves the highest extracted granularity F1 (0.467). Claude Sonnet 4 is the clear privacy leader in free-form settings, over-disclosing only 11.5% under vanilla prompting versus 47.6% for GPT-5 and 45.6% for Gemini-2.5 Flash, but it pays a heavy utility cost with 39.2% under-disclosure. Under iterative CoT prompting, o3 becomes the most vulnerable model, exposing location in 97.2% of cases with 30.7% street-level accuracy, while Claude Sonnet 4 remains the most resistant (6.8% location exposure). Open-source models trail significantly: Qwen2.5-VL-72B and Llama-3.2-90B achieve only 26.8% and 30.5% granularity MCQ accuracy respectively, near the 33.3% random baseline, though their free-form extracted granularity accuracy (46.8% and 45.5%) paradoxically exceeds their MCQ scores, suggesting they hedge less in open-ended generation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Contextual Integrity Theory (Nissenbaum, 2009) from the textual LLM setting (Mireshghallah et al., 2024; Shao et al., 2024) to the multimodal geolocation domain, where visual cues and unstated sharing intent must be inferred from images rather than specified in vignettes. It departs conceptually from Mendes et al. (2024), which proposed blanket configurable granularity restrictions as a privacy guardrail, by arguing that models should themselves reason about context to determine appropriate disclosure level. The adversarial evaluation setting adapts FigStep (Gong et al., 2023) from text-based jailbreaking to a visual-geolocation-specific attack, while the iterative CoT setting builds on the geographical least-to-most prompting framework from Mendes et al. (2024).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first benchmark to systematically evaluate whether VLMs can make context-conditioned privacy decisions in geolocation, and its findings—that no model achieves a satisfactory privacy-utility tradeoff and that the best model matches human disclosure judgments in only ~50% of free-form cases—highlight a critical gap between perceptual capability and normative reasoning that will constrain safe deployment of VLMs in consumer-facing applications. The paper identifies actionable directions (few-shot context matching, post-training alignment for CI reasoning, retrieval-augmented guardrails) but leaves open the fundamental question of how to instill robust contextual integrity reasoning that generalizes across cultures, demographics, and sensitive attribute types beyond location. Limitations include reliance on a single adversarial method (FigStep), evaluation of perceived rather than actual user intent, and a US/EU-centric normative framework.
