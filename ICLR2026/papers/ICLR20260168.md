## Problem

LLM-as-a-Judge enables scalable model evaluation, but current practice treats judge outputs as ground truth without formally accounting for the judge's noise and bias, leaving statistical certification guarantees unverified. The paper asks how to certify that an LLM's failure rate is below a safety threshold using a large but noisy judge-labelled dataset while provably controlling finite-sample Type-I error despite uncertainty in the judge's own reliability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260168/cropped_page1_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The procedure consumes two datasets of different scales: a small human-labelled calibration set D_M (n_M samples with ground-truth correctness labels) and a large judge-labelled evaluation set D_J (n_J ≫ n_M samples with only judge labels), along with a user-specified risk threshold α and significance level ζ. It first augments D_M with judge predictions to estimate the judge's TPR and FPR, then produces a variance-corrected critical threshold c'_J that explicitly incorporates the estimation variance from the small calibration set. The final output is a binary certification decision (reject or accept the null hypothesis that the model's failure rate exceeds α), accompanied by interpretable diagnostics of judge quality.

## Method

The core reformulation converts the hypothesis test on the true failure rate R_M into an equivalent proxy test on the noisy judge failure rate R_J, with the threshold transforming as α' = FPR + (TPR − FPR)·α. The critical threshold c'_J is constructed as α̂' plus a Gaussian quantile times the square root of three variance terms: the binomial variance of the judge-labelled rate (α̂'(1−α̂')/n_J), the variance of the TPR estimate (α²·TPR̂(1−TPR̂)/n_M₁), and the variance of the FPR estimate ((1−α)²·FPR̂(1−FPR̂)/n_M₀). This variance correction ensures that when calibration data is scarce, the threshold automatically widens, preserving Type-I error control at level ζ. The paper derives the exact condition (Theorem 5.4) under which this noisy test achieves higher statistical power than direct human-only testing, and quantifies the 'Oracle Gap'—the irreducible power loss from estimating rather than knowing the judge's TPR and FPR—showing it scales as O(1/√n_M).

## Results

The framework guarantees finite-sample Type-I error control at level ζ (e.g., 5%) with an additive O(n_J^{-1/2} + n_{M₁}^{-1/2} + n_{M₀}^{-1/2}) correction (Theorem 5.1). Theorem 5.4 provides an exact decision boundary: Noisy HT outperforms Direct HT if and only if (TPR − FPR)² exceeds a threshold depending on α, R_M, TPR, and FPR. Empirically, across Jigsaw, Hate Speech, and SafeRLHF settings, all methods maintain Type-I error ≤ 5%, while Noisy HT achieves substantially lower Type-II error than Direct HT when the judge has high TPR (>0.8) and low FPR (<0.2), but PPI variants consistently achieve lower Type-II error than Noisy HT, and the Oracle (known TPR/FPR) always dominates all practical methods, quantifying the 'Oracle Gap' as the statistical cost of parameter estimation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against PPI (Angelopoulos et al., 2023a), PPI++ (Angelopoulos et al., 2023b), and Ridge PPI (Eyre & Madras, 2025), the proposed Noisy HT consistently achieves higher Type-II error (i.e., lower statistical power) across all three datasets and all judge configurations tested. The paper explicitly acknowledges this trade-off: Noisy HT sacrifices raw power in exchange for interpretability, as it explicitly models the judge's TPR/FPR rather than treating the judge as a black-box control variate. Against the Direct HT baseline (using only the small human-labelled set), Noisy HT wins decisively when the judge is reliable (high TPR, low FPR), with the advantage growing as n_J increases; however, with weak judges (low TPR or high FPR), Noisy HT can underperform Direct HT, as predicted by the condition in Theorem 5.4. Against the Oracle Noisy HT (perfectly known TPR/FPR), all practical methods including PPI variants fall short, confirming that the estimation uncertainty from the finite calibration set imposes an irreducible power loss. The paper positions its contribution not as a power-maximizing estimator but as a diagnostic tool: the explicit TPR/FPR decomposition enables practitioners to identify which judges are worth deploying and how much calibration data is needed.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the hypothesis-testing certification framework of Feng et al. (2025a, PROSAC) from adversarial-attack settings to the noisy-judge setting, where the evaluator itself is an imperfect LLM. It deliberately positions against Prediction-Powered Inference (Angelopoulos et al., 2023a) by explicitly modeling the judge's error profile (TPR/FPR) rather than treating the judge as a black-box control variate, trading some raw statistical power for interpretability and diagnostic capability. The LLM-as-a-Judge literature (Zheng et al., 2023; Gilardi et al., 2023) established scalability but largely treated judge outputs as ground truth; this work is the first to provide finite-sample Type-I error guarantees under explicit judge imperfection, using Berry-Esseen-based normal approximations to derive a variance-corrected critical threshold.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework gives practitioners a principled, interpretable decision rule (Theorem 5.4) for when a noisy judge actually improves certification power over direct human evaluation, and quantifies the 'Oracle Gap'—the irreducible statistical cost of estimating judge parameters from finite calibration data. This matters because current LLM evaluation practice relies on 'blind trust' in judge outputs without formal error control, risking certification of unsafe models. Key open questions include extending to finer-grained (non-binary) quality scores, handling subjective tasks where ground-truth labels reflect human disagreement, and whether combining explicit judge modeling with PPI can close the Oracle Gap. The normal-approximation guarantees also degrade for very small calibration sets (n_M < 5) or rare failure events, limiting applicability in extreme regimes.
