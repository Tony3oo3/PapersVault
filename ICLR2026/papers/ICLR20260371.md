## Problem

Diffusion policies for robot manipulation achieve strong performance on only 50–200 demonstrations despite exhibiting classic overfitting signals (low training loss, high test loss), yet the reason for this success has remained unexplained. The paper hypothesizes that this overfitting is actually beneficial: the policy memorizes training action chunks and retrieves them at inference rather than generalizing in action space, effectively acting as an implicit lookup table.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260371/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The policy consumes a wrist-mounted camera image, a third-person fixed camera image, and the 6-DoF end-effector pose (position and orientation), and outputs a 16-step action chunk of which 8 steps are executed per closed-loop cycle. For the ALT variant, these modalities are fused through a ResNet-18 encoder into a low-dimensional feature representation (LFR); the LFR is matched against a pre-built k-d tree of training embeddings, and the associated action chunk is retrieved directly. An explicit OOD flag is raised when the cosine similarity between the query embedding and its nearest training neighbor falls below a tunable threshold γ, triggering a safe fallback.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is twofold. First, through systematic real-robot and Robomimic experiments, the authors demonstrate that diffusion policies consistently recall verbatim training action chunks even under extreme OOD conditions (cat/dog distractor images, objects moved out of camera view), while ACT interpolates between chunks and GR00T-N1.5 shows mixed memorization/interpolation behavior. Second, they propose the Action Lookup Table (ALT) policy, which explicitly implements this retrieval mechanism: a contrastive-learning-trained ResNet-18 fusion encoder maps observations to LFRs, a k-d tree indexes the nearest training embedding, and the associated action chunk is retrieved without any parametric action head. The encoder is trained with the NT-Xent loss (temperature 0.4) on two augmented views per sample, and multi-modality is recovered through stochastic k-nearest-neighbor retrieval. ALT achieves comparable task success to diffusion policy while running ~300× faster (0.009 s vs. 2.65 s inference) and using less than 1/100th the memory, with the added benefit of an interpretable OOD detection signal.

## Results

On the cup-grasping task, Diffusion Policy achieves a memory-audit similarity score of 0.935 in-distribution and 0.837 under OOD distractors, confirming strong action memorization. The proposed ALT policy matches Diffusion Policy's 100% in-distribution task success while running ~300× faster (0.009 s vs. 2.65 s inference) and using <1/100th the memory. On Robomimic (Can, Square, Tool Hang, Transport), Diffusion Policy rollouts show average similarity scores of 0.83–0.97 to training trajectories, further validating the memorization hypothesis across tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Diffusion Policy (Chi et al., IJRR 2023), ALT achieves equivalent 100% in-distribution task success and comparable OOD robustness while reducing inference time from ~2.65 s to ~0.009 s (≈300× speedup) and adding an explicit OOD flag via a latent-distance threshold. Against ACT (Zhao et al., 2023), the paper shows ACT exhibits action interpolation (similarity 0.465 InD) rather than memorization, and degrades to near-random outputs under OOD distractors (similarity 0.278), whereas Diffusion Policy and ALT both retain high similarity to training chunks. Against GR00T-N1.5 (Bjorck et al., 2025), the pre-trained VLA model shows intermediate memorization (0.783 InD) but uniquely produces the *correct* trajectory under OOD distractors by leveraging task-relevant first-person cues from its large-scale VLM pretraining, outperforming both DP and ACT in that specific scenario. Overall, the paper's key finding is that Diffusion Policy's apparent robustness in the low-data regime is driven by implicit action memorization rather than generalization, and that this behavior can be replicated explicitly and more efficiently by ALT.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the memorization literature from image-generation diffusion models (Gu et al. 2023, Somepalli et al. 2023, Carlini et al. 2023) to the robotics domain, arguing that the same phenomenon—typically viewed as a privacy/copyright liability in image synthesis—is actually the mechanism behind Diffusion Policy's strong low-data performance. It positions itself against retrieval-based imitation learning (Pari et al. 2021, Sridhar et al. 2023, 2024b) by showing that an explicit contrastive-encoder lookup table (ALT) can match the implicit memorization of a 100M+ parameter diffusion model, while departing from those prior retrieval methods by operating at the action-chunk level rather than the frame level. The systematic three-way comparison with ACT (interpolation) and GR00T-N1.5 (pre-trained VLA robustness) on identical data provides a controlled dissection of memorization vs. generalization that prior work had not attempted.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that Diffusion Policy's closed-loop success in the 50–200 demo regime is largely retrieval of memorized action chunks—rather than learned action generalization—reframes how the community should interpret and trust these policies, with direct implications for safety monitoring (explicit OOD flags via latent-distance thresholds) and deployment efficiency (ALT runs ~300× faster with <1/100th the memory). A key limitation is that all experiments are single-skill pick-and-place tasks with modest workspace coverage; whether the memorization interpretation holds for multi-skill, long-horizon, or dexterous manipulation remains open. The paper also leaves unresolved how ALT scales to larger demonstration sets and whether the contrastive encoder can be replaced by frozen pretrained features without the careful augmentation pipeline it requires.
