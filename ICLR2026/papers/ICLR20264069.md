## Problem

Disagreement discrepancy—a measure of how disagreement between a critic and reference model changes under distribution shift—is widely used for error bounding, shift detection, and robust training, but its true objective involves the non-differentiable zero-one loss, forcing all existing methods to rely on continuous surrogate losses. No prior work had verified whether these surrogates actually optimize the true disagreement discrepancy. This paper proves that the surrogates of Rosenfeld & Garg (2023) and Ginsberg et al. (2023) are not Bayes consistent for K > 2 classes, meaning perfect optimization of the surrogate can still yield a suboptimal true discrepancy, undermining the validity of downstream error bounds and statistical tests.

## Input / Output

The method takes labeled source data, unlabeled target data, and a fixed reference model h: X → [K] as input. The critic f: X → R^K is typically a tunable linear layer appended to the frozen weights of h, transforming its logits. The output is a trained critic that maximizes disagreement discrepancy, which is then used to compute an error bound under covariate shift (combining empirical source error, a sample correction term, and the maximized discrepancy) or to drive a hypothesis test for harmful covariate shift. The density ratio p_S(x)/p_T(x) between source and target distributions is required for the pseudo-loss reformulation but is estimated empirically in practice.

## Method

The paper reformulates disagreement discrepancy as a sum of two decoupled risks over disjoint regions of the input space (partitioned by whether p_S(x) ≥ p_T(x)), using pseudo-losses weighted by density ratios. This enables pointwise optimization and allows the authors to derive both a lower bound (proving inconsistency of existing surrogates) and an upper bound (excess error bound) on the optimality gap. The key new contribution is a disagreement loss ℓ_dis(s) = −log(1 − σ(s)_y), which is the exact symmetry of cross-entropy: while cross-entropy drives σ(s)_y → 1 to encourage agreement, the new loss drives σ(s)_y → 0 to encourage disagreement without prescribing how the remaining probability mass is allocated. Paired with standard cross-entropy as the agreement loss, this surrogate is proven Bayes consistent for all K ≥ 2 via a concave bounding function ξ with ξ(0) = 0. The inconsistency of prior surrogates arises because their optimal predictions disagree with the true zero-one loss optimizer in regions where the density ratio falls in a specific interval, creating an irreducible gap even at infinite data.

## Results

Across 130 shift/model combinations on 11 vision benchmarks (WILDS, BREEDs, DomainNet, etc.), the proposed surrogate attains the maximum achievable disagreement discrepancy in nearly 80% of cases, with a one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test confirming superiority (p = 1.8 × 10⁻¹¹). Under adversarially chosen target data, the surrogate achieves the highest discrepancy estimate in 87.5% of cases at 0% attack fraction, rising to 100% at 50% attack fraction (p < 6.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ vs. the best baseline). For harmful shift detection on UCI Heart Disease, the surrogate improves AUC-ROC from 0.821 to 0.908 at N=10 samples, from 0.913 to 0.984 at N=20, and reaches a perfect 1.000 at N=50, with non-overlapping 95% confidence intervals at all sample sizes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two prior surrogates: Rosenfeld & Garg's (RG23) smooth disagreement loss and Ginsberg et al.'s (GLK23) disagreement cross-entropy loss. On the error-bounding task (Section 5.1), the proposed surrogate outperforms RG23 in nearly 80% of 130 scenarios and shows better calibration of error bounds across most δ values. Under adversarial target data, the proposed surrogate dominates both RG23 and GLK23, achieving the top discrepancy estimate in 87.5–100% of cases depending on attack fraction, with statistically significant margins (p < 6.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ for any non-zero attack fraction). On harmful shift detection (Section 5.2), the proposed surrogate beats GLK23 by 8.7 percentage points in AUC-ROC at N=10 (0.908 vs. 0.821), 7.1 points at N=20 (0.984 vs. 0.913), and 0.5 points at N=50 (1.000 vs. 0.995), with non-overlapping 95% confidence intervals at every sample size. The paper notes that for binary classification (K=2), all three surrogates are equivalent, so the advantage is specific to multi-class settings (K > 2).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classical Bayes consistency framework for surrogate losses (Zhang, 2004b; Bartlett et al., 2006) from single-risk classification objectives to the 'difference of risks' structure inherent in disagreement discrepancy. It builds directly on the disagreement discrepancy formulations of Rosenfeld & Garg (2023) and Ginsberg et al. (2023), but departs from them by proving that the smooth surrogates those works rely on are provably inconsistent with the true zero-one objective. The key technical novelty is a reformulation of the discrepancy as a sum of two disjoint pseudo-loss risks, enabling pointwise analysis that the standard excess-error-bound machinery cannot handle directly.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that all existing disagreement discrepancy surrogates are Bayes inconsistent undermines the theoretical foundations of error-bounding and shift-detection methods built on them, meaning previously published guarantees may be vacuous in certain distributional regimes. The proposed fix (a simple log(1−σ_y) disagreement loss) is elegant and empirically validated, but the paper is explicit that Bayes consistency is necessary but not sufficient: full finite-sample guarantees still require bounding estimation error, and the adversarial experiments show that even consistent surrogates can be stressed by adversarially chosen targets. Open questions include extending the analysis to H-consistency for deep networks and understanding whether the inconsistency gap is practically significant outside adversarial settings.
