## Problem

Classical active sequential hypothesis testing (pure exploration) methods require strong modeling assumptions—known observation kernels, known likelihoods, or hand-designed tests—and often reduce to solving nonconvex optimization programs. This paper asks whether a single learned model can both adaptively collect informative data and infer the correct hypothesis across a family of problems, without explicit modeling of the information structure, by leveraging in-context learning in Transformers.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260492/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At each step the model consumes the full observation-action history D_t = (x_1, a_1, ..., a_{t-1}, x_t) as a token sequence fed to a Transformer, and outputs a query action a_t (which experiment to run next) plus, at the terminal or stopping time, a predicted hypothesis H_hat. In the fixed-confidence regime the action space is augmented with a dedicated a_stop token, so the stopping decision is made by the same Q-network rather than a separate threshold rule. For the MNIST pixel-sampling task, the input is a partially revealed 6x6 grid of pixel patches (36 possible queries) and the output is a digit label from {0,...,9}; for bandit tasks the input is a scalar reward history and the output is an arm index.

## Method

ICPE couples two Transformer networks: a supervised inference network I_phi trained by negative log-likelihood to approximate the posterior P(H*=H|D_t), and a Q-network Q_theta trained via DQN (with target networks) using a principled information-theoretic reward derived from the problem's Lagrangian dual. In the fixed-budget setting the terminal reward is the maximum posterior mass max_H P(H*=H|D_N); in the fixed-confidence setting the per-step reward is -1 for continuing and lambda * max_H P(H*=H|D_t) for stopping, with lambda learned online via a projected gradient update on empirical correctness. The key theoretical result is that the optimal inference rule is the MAP estimator and the optimal policy is greedy w.r.t. the corresponding Q-function, so the RL objective is not a heuristic but the exact dual of the constrained optimization problem. At inference time ICPE requires only forward passes—no parameter updates—making it a true in-context learner that transfers to new task instances drawn from the same family.

## Results

On the probabilistic binary search task, ICPE achieves 100% accuracy across all problem sizes (K=8 to K=256) with mean stopping times of 2.13, 2.93, 3.71, 4.50, 5.49, and 6.61 queries respectively, matching the optimal log₂(K) worst-case bound exactly. On stochastic bandits (fixed confidence, δ=0.1), ICPE achieves a smaller average stopping time than both Track-and-Stop and Top-Two, though at the cost of slightly lower correctness for large K. On the MNIST pixel-sampling task (N=12 patches), ICPE achieves substantially higher classification accuracy than both uniform random sampling and Deep CMAB while using fewer revealed regions, and its sampling strategy adapts to class-conditional structure.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Track-and-Stop (TaS) and Top-Two (TTPS) on stochastic bandits, ICPE achieves a smaller average stopping time but with slightly lower correctness; TaS and TTPS are known to be overly conservative, trading sample efficiency for higher correctness guarantees. On deterministic bandits, ICPE maintains near-perfect correctness (≈1) and optimal unique-action coverage as K grows, while DQN and the uniform-policy baseline degrade sharply. On the MNIST pixel-sampling task, ICPE outperforms both uniform random sampling and Deep CMAB in accuracy while revealing fewer patches, and chi-squared tests confirm its sampling distribution varies significantly across digit classes (unlike the baselines). In the magic-action bandit setting, ICPE achieves sample complexities close to the theoretical lower bound and consistently outperforms I-IDS (Information-Directed Sampling on top of ICPE's learned inference). In a regret-minimization evaluation with magic actions, ICPE (using an explore-then-commit strategy) outperforms UCB, Thompson Sampling, and standard IDS initialized with Gaussian priors.

## Relation to Prior Work

ICPE sits at the intersection of active sequential hypothesis testing (Chernoff, 1959; Naghshvar & Javidi, 2013) and in-context learning with Transformers (Lee et al., 2023; Schaul & Schmidhuber, 2010). Classical pure-exploration algorithms like Track-and-Stop and Top-Two require known observation models and hand-designed stopping rules, often solving nonconvex programs; ICPE departs by learning both the data-collection policy and the inference rule end-to-end via meta-RL, with a principled information-theoretic reward (max posterior mass) derived from the problem's Bayesian structure. The theoretical contribution formalizes how the optimal inference rule is the MAP estimator and how the optimal policy is a greedy Q-policy under a Lagrangian-dual formulation, bridging the sequential testing and RL literatures in a way that prior work had not explicitly connected.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ICPE offers a practical, assumption-light framework for adaptive hypothesis identification that could transfer to domains where the information structure is unknown or hard to encode (cancer test selection, protein assay design, materials discovery). Key open questions include scaling to continuous or combinatorial hypothesis spaces, adapting to offline data without a simulator, and providing rigorous finite-sample guarantees for the learned stopping rule. The current experiments, while diverse, remain on relatively small-scale benchmarks (≤10 arms, 36 patches, 256 hypotheses), so the practical ceiling of the approach on high-dimensional real-world problems is still untested.
