## Problem

Flow-based generative policies (diffusion, flow matching) can model complex multi-modal action distributions in RL but require multi-step iterative sampling from noise to action, imposing significant training and inference latency that hinders online RL and real-time robotic control. The paper asks whether the expressiveness of generative policies can be unified with the efficiency of one-step action generation, and identifies that naively learning a mean velocity field for one-step generation leads to an ill-posed ODE with multiple solutions, degrading learning accuracy.

## Input / Output

At inference, the policy takes a robot state s and a single sample of standard Gaussian noise ε ~ N(0, I), and produces an action (or action chunk of horizon 5) in exactly one forward pass: a(1) = ε + u_θ(ε, 0, 1, s). During training, the mean velocity network u_θ(a(t), t, r, s) is queried at randomly sampled time pairs (t, r) with t < r, and its total time derivative is computed via Jacobian-vector product (JVP). The best-of-N mechanism generates N candidate actions per state and selects the one maximizing the learned Q-value, so the effective output is a single selected action per decision step.

## Method

MVP replaces the instantaneous velocity field v(a(t), t, s) of standard flow matching with a mean velocity field u(a(t), t, r, s) defined as the time-average of v over interval [t, r], enabling direct one-step generation a(1) = a(0) + u*(a(0), 0, 1, s). Training minimizes the residual of the mean flow identity (a first-order ODE in u), computed efficiently via JVP with a stop-gradient operator for stability. The critical theoretical contribution is showing that this ODE admits a family of solutions parameterized by an arbitrary constant C(a,r)/(r-t) when no boundary condition is enforced (Theorem 2); the paper introduces the Instantaneous Velocity Constraint (IVC), an auxiliary loss that pins u(a(t), t, t) to the known instantaneous velocity v at the degenerate interval, which provably forces C = 0 and recovers the unique correct solution (Theorem 3). Policy improvement is guaranteed via a best-of-N generate-and-select mechanism, with a formal lower bound (Theorem 1) decomposing the value improvement into a non-negative BFN advantage gain minus a fitting-error term that IVC directly reduces.

## Results

On 9 sparse-reward robotic manipulation tasks (3 from Robomimic, 6 from OGBench), MVP achieves an average success rate of 0.88±0.05, outperforming QC (0.86±0.05), BFN (0.52±0.06), and FQL (0.44±0.05). MVP wins on 8 of 9 tasks, with the closest margin on Robomimic-can (0.92 vs. QC's 0.94). In terms of efficiency, MVP achieves the fastest online training speed (153.6 iter/s vs. 108.5 for FQL, 92.6 for QC, 68.0 for BFN) and near-fastest inference latency (10.93 ms, comparable to FQL's 10.76 ms, versus ~113–117 ms for BFN and QC).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MVP is compared against three offline-to-online RL baselines: FQL (Flow Q-learning, Park et al. 2025), BFN (Best-of-N with multi-step flow, Ghasemipour et al. 2021), and QC (Q-chunking, Li et al. 2025). MVP decisively outperforms FQL and BFN on all 9 tasks, with the gap being most dramatic on hard long-horizon tasks (e.g., Cube-triple-task4: MVP 0.52 vs. FQL 0.00 and BFN 0.02). Against the strongest baseline QC, MVP wins on 7 of 9 tasks and ties on 2 (Robomimic-lift, Cube-double-task2/3 at 1.00), losing only narrowly on Robomimic-can (0.92 vs. 0.94). In training speed, MVP is ~41% faster than FQL (153.6 vs. 108.5 iter/s) and ~2.3× faster than BFN (153.6 vs. 68.0 iter/s). In inference latency on CPU, MVP (10.93 ms) is comparable to FQL (10.76 ms) but ~10× faster than BFN (117.3 ms) and QC (113.22 ms), which rely on 10-step iterative flow sampling.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the mean flow framework (Geng et al., 2025a), originally developed for one-step image generation, to the RL policy learning setting, where it replaces the standard instantaneous velocity field of flow matching (Lipman et al., 2023) with a mean velocity field that enables direct one-step action generation. The policy improvement mechanism builds on the best-of-N selection from EMaQ (Ghasemipour et al., 2021), but the key departure is the introduction of the instantaneous velocity constraint (IVC) as an explicit boundary condition for the mean flow ODE — a theoretical contribution not present in prior mean flow or flow matching work. Unlike distillation-based one-step approaches (e.g., FQL's separate one-step policy), MVP learns the mean velocity field directly, avoiding the need for a multi-step teacher.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: MVP achieves ~10× faster inference than multi-step flow baselines (11 ms vs. 113–117 ms) and the fastest training speed (154 vs. 68–109 iter/s) while matching or exceeding their success rates on 8 of 9 robotic manipulation tasks. The IVC's role as a necessary boundary condition is both theoretically clean and empirically validated, offering a principled explanation for why naive one-step flow distillation fails on hard tasks (near-zero success) while the mean velocity approach succeeds. Open questions include whether the JVP-induced GPU memory overhead limits scalability to higher-dimensional action spaces, and whether the approach transfers to real robotic platforms with tighter latency budgets.
