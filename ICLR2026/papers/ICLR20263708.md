## Problem

Standard CATE estimation requires O(M/ε²) samples to achieve ε-accuracy across M population units, and the worst-case lower bound for (1−ε)-optimal treatment allocation matches this quadratic rate. However, this quadratic lower bound is brittle: for natural (smooth) distributions of treatment effects, the allocation problem is fundamentally easier than the estimation problem, yet no prior work had characterized the instance-dependent sample complexity gap or provided a practical algorithm that exploits it.

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes Bernoulli(τ(u)) samples drawn uniformly across M units in a single non-adaptive round (compatible with a standard one-round RCT), along with a budget K ≤ M and accuracy parameters ε, δ, γ. It outputs a set of exactly K units to treat, whose realized value is evaluated against the true (unknown) treatment effects rather than the estimates. The estimation oracle is assumed to provide (ρ, δ/M)-accurate estimates per unit at cost O(ln(2M/δ)/ρ²) samples, and the algorithm is permutation-invariant in the sense that only the CDF shape of the τ(u) distribution matters, not the assignment of values to specific units.

## Method

The core algorithm (LEA) estimates each unit's treatment effect to a deliberately coarse accuracy ρ = γ√ε (rather than ε), then selects the top K units by estimated value. The key theoretical insight is a win-win: units whose true τ(u) is far from the optimal threshold τ_K are correctly classified with only √ε-accuracy, while units near τ_K have nearly identical values so misclassification among them causes negligible value loss. The formal guarantee requires ρ-regularity of the treatment effect distribution—no interval of length ≥ 2ρ contains more than O(ρM) units—which is satisfied by uniform, Beta, and Gaussian families. This yields a (1−ε)-optimal allocation with O(M ln(2M/δ)/ε) samples, a quadratic improvement over the O(M/ε²) needed for full CATE estimation, and the paper further provides an exact instance-dependent optimality condition (Claim 4.1) checkable from the coarse estimates, plus flexible-budget strategies (sliding K or overspending by one unit) for the rare cases where ρ-regularity fails at the original threshold.

## Results

The paper proves that under ρ-regularity (a weak smoothness condition on the treatment effect distribution), a (1−ε)-optimal allocation can be found with O(M/ε) samples, compared to the O(M/ε²) samples required for full CATE estimation—a quadratic improvement. Empirically, across all five RCT datasets (STAR, TUP, NSW, Acupuncture, Postoperative Pain), the LEA algorithm achieves near-optimal allocation values with even fewer samples than the O(M/ε) theoretical bound predicts. The failure rate (fraction of budgets K for which the allocation is not (1−ε)-optimal) stays below 5% across all datasets, and in the rare failure cases, shifting the budget by just one unit (|K′−K|=1) or adding a single extra unit always recovers the optimal value.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against other allocation algorithms; the primary comparison is against the standard FullCATE approach, which estimates all M treatment effects to ε-accuracy and then selects the top K units. The key result is a quadratic sample complexity separation: FullCATE requires Ω(M/ε²) samples (proven via a LeCam minimax lower bound even for the uniform distribution), while the proposed LEA algorithm achieves the same (1−ε)-optimal allocation with only O(M/ε) samples under ρ-regularity. In the empirical evaluation, LEA consistently outperforms the worst-case O(M/ε²) sample budget, reaching near-optimal allocation values at sample sizes well below what standard CATE estimation would require. The paper also demonstrates that the ρ-regularity condition is naturally satisfied by common distribution families (uniform, Beta, Gaussian), with the constant γ ranging between 0.07 and 0.2, confirming the theory applies broadly.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the emerging line of work (Perdomo et al., 2025; Shirali et al., 2024) showing that resource allocation need not require accurate predictions, by providing the first tight sample complexity analysis specifically for budget-constrained treatment allocation. It departs from the standard CATE estimation framework (Imbens & Rubin, 2015; Athey & Imbens, 2016) by showing that the quadratic sample complexity lower bound for estimation does not transfer to allocation under natural smoothness assumptions. The technical toolkit draws on Hoeffding's inequality for the upper bound and LeCam's two-point method for the lower bound, while the threshold-based selection perspective connects to the best-arm identification literature in bandits (Audibert & Bubeck, 2010; Kalyanakrishnan et al., 2012), though the paper explicitly avoids adaptive procedures in favor of a single-round RCT-compatible algorithm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is significant for policy design: an RCT that is severely underpowered for CATE estimation can still yield near-optimal treatment allocations, with M/ε samples sufficing where M/ε² would be needed for estimation. The ρ-regularity condition, while mild (satisfied by uniform, Beta, and Gaussian families), may fail in adversarial or highly concentrated distributions, and the paper's flexible-budget and overspending strategies provide partial remedies but do not fully resolve the '2 spikes' worst case. Open questions include extending the analysis to individual-level (rather than unit-level) targeting, adaptive multi-stage RCT designs, and settings where treatment effects are unbounded or the cost of treating units is heterogeneous.
