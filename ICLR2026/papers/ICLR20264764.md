## Problem

Existing LLM evaluation frameworks reduce multi-turn robustness to static aggregate scores, obscuring when and how errors emerge under sustained adversarial pressure. This paper asks: how quickly do inconsistencies arise under different adversarial strategies, which semantic shift patterns most sharply increase failure risk, and can we identify conversations on a high-risk trajectory before an error actually occurs?

## Input / Output

The method consumes up to 8-turn adversarial conversations (initial question plus follow-ups) from 9 LLMs, computing sentence-transformer embeddings of each prompt and the full running context to derive three time-varying drift features (prompt-to-prompt, context-to-prompt, cumulative) alongside discrete covariates (prompt length, subject domain, difficulty, model identity). Only conversations with an initially correct answer are retained, and those with no inconsistency by turn 8 are right-censored. The model outputs per-turn survival probabilities, a conditional failure probability over a 2-turn rolling horizon for real-time monitoring, and risk-stratified survival curves with median time-to-inconsistency estimates.

## Method

The paper formalizes multi-turn robustness as a discrete-time survival problem (event = first inconsistent answer, time = turn index) and fits three complementary model families—Cox proportional hazards, parametric AFT (Weibull, log-normal, log-logistic), and Random Survival Forests—using semantic drift as time-varying covariates. A central finding is that abrupt prompt-to-prompt drift sharply increases hazard while cumulative drift is counterintuitively protective, suggesting that models adapt to coherently evolving contexts. Schoenfeld residual tests reveal that drift covariates violate the proportional hazards assumption, explaining why AFT models (which act multiplicatively on the time scale rather than the hazard) achieve superior calibration (IBS ≈ 0.18 vs. ≈ 0.34 for Cox) and discrimination (C-index 0.874). The best-performing Weibull AFT model with model-drift interactions is then converted into a lightweight turn-level risk monitor that flags 76% of failing conversations with a median 2-turn lead time while alerting only 19% of safe ones.

## Results

On the MT-Consistency benchmark (36,951 turns, 9 LLMs, 8-turn horizon), the Weibull AFT model achieves the highest discrimination (C-index = 0.874) and near-best calibration (IBS = 0.180), while the Weibull AFT with model-drift interactions achieves the best overall IBS of 0.175. All AFT variants reduce integrated Brier score by over 48% relative to Cox models (IBS ≈ 0.34), and the non-parametric Random Survival Forest delivers the lowest C-index (0.845) despite using the same 53 covariates. In the operational monitoring experiment, the AFT-based risk monitor alerts 76% of failing conversations with a mean lead time of 2.3 turns before the first inconsistency, while alerting only 19% of safe (censored) conversations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks three survival-modeling paradigms—Cox proportional hazards, parametric AFT, and Random Survival Forests (RSF; Ishwaran et al., 2008)—on identical covariates and the same held-out test set. The AFT family decisively outperforms both baselines: Weibull AFT reaches C-index 0.874 versus 0.868 for Cox Advanced and 0.845 for RSF, while its IBS of 0.180 is roughly half that of Cox (0.343) and slightly better than RSF (0.190). Adding model-drift interactions to AFT further improves calibration (IBS 0.175) at a small C-index cost (0.869), yielding the best overall risk prediction. RSF, despite its flexibility and 53 covariates, underperforms on discrimination (C-index 0.845, the lowest of all models) and offers only marginal IBS advantage over the simplest 12-covariate AFT. The per-round Brier analysis (Table 2) confirms the gap widens in later turns (rounds 6–8), where Cox Brier scores climb to 0.446 while AFT scores drop to 0.027–0.062, indicating that the PH assumption's violation by drift covariates specifically harms Cox in the high-risk tail of the dialogue.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the survival analysis toolkit (Cox PH, AFT, Random Survival Forests) from biostatistics into the LLM evaluation domain, a setting where prior work has only applied survival models to user-level outcomes like dialogue termination (De Kock & Vlachos 2021; Maystre & Russo 2022). It builds on the MT-Consistency benchmark from the same group (Li et al. 2025a) and the broader multi-turn degradation literature (MT-Bench, MT-Eval, MultiChallenge, FlipFlop Experiment), but departs from all of these by replacing static aggregate scores with a time-to-event formulation that treats the first inconsistent answer as a censored event. The semantic drift features (prompt-to-prompt, context-to-prompt, cumulative) are simple cosine-distance signals from sentence-transformer embeddings, not novel representations, but their use as time-varying covariates in survival models is the key methodological contribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled temporal lens on LLM robustness that static benchmarks cannot offer: it separates *whether* a conversation fails from *when* it fails, and yields a lightweight AFT-based turn-level risk monitor that flags 76% of failing dialogues ~2 turns before the first error. The counterintuitive finding that cumulative drift is protective (models adapt to gradual topic shifts) challenges the common assumption that all semantic deviation is harmful and suggests that velocity of change, not total distance, is the critical risk factor. Key limitations constrain generalizability: a single benchmark, an 8-turn horizon, a binary event definition that lumps sycophancy with hallucination, and purely retrospective (offline) monitoring. Open questions include whether the drift-hazard relationship holds over longer horizons, across other attack families (tool-use, CoT steering), and whether the AFT monitor can be coupled to real-time interventions with measurable safety gains.
