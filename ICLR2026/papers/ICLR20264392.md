## Problem

Preference alignment of LLMs typically relies on expensive human annotations or costly API-based models. While Tao & Li (2025) showed that weak LLMs can serve as annotators, they treat all weak-model predictions equally, ignoring the confidence signal. This paper asks: can selectively leveraging a weak LLM's confidence lead to alignment that outperforms full human annotation?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264392/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a small human-labeled subset (e.g., 20–30% of a preference dataset) to train a weak annotator, then applies it to a large unlabeled pool of (prompt, response₁, response₂) triplets. The weak model (OPT-125M or Qwen2.5-0.5B, with the LM head replaced by a scalar output layer) produces both a binary preference label and a confidence score C ∈ [0,1] per triplet. The output is a strong LLM policy (e.g., OPT-1.3B, Qwen2.5-7B/14B) aligned via a confidence-weighted preference loss, evaluated by Gold Reward Accuracy against a pretrained reward model.

## Method

CW-PO is a three-step framework: (i) train a weak LLM as a preference annotator using a Bradley-Terry scalar scoring objective on the labeled subset; (ii) apply the trained weak model to unlabeled triplets to produce preference labels and a confidence score C = 2·(σ(π_w(x,y⁺) − π_w(x,y⁻)) − 0.5), which normalizes the prediction margin to [0,1]; (iii) align the strong policy by multiplying each sample's PO loss (DPO, IPO, or rDPO) by its confidence weight, so high-confidence pairs contribute more strongly to the gradient. The key insight is that the top-30% most confident weak-model predictions already outperform 100% human annotations, and continuous reweighting (rather than hard thresholding) avoids the need to select a dataset-specific cutoff. The BT-based scalar scoring is both more accurate and ~2× faster to train than the DPO-based implicit-reward approach used in Tao & Li (2025).

## Results

On the OPT-125M→OPT-1.3B pair, CW-PO achieves average Gold Reward Accuracy (GRA) of 60.3% (DPO), 61.5% (IPO), and 62.7% (rDPO), compared to 57.9% for WS-DPO and 58.4%/58.3%/56.3% for Human across the three PO methods. On the Qwen2.5-0.5B→Qwen2.5-14B pair, CW-PO reaches 75.6% (DPO), 77.2% (IPO), and 77.3% (rDPO) average GRA. Overall, CW-PO delivers a 5.2% GRA improvement over WS-DPO and a 5% improvement over the Human baseline on average. Remarkably, CW-DPO trained with only 30% of human annotations outperforms standard DPO trained on 100% of human annotations (e.g., 72.9% vs. 69.2% on HARMLESS for OPT-1.3B).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is WS-DPO (Tao & Li, 2025), which trains a weak LLM via DPO and uses its implicit-reward annotations to align a strong model via standard DPO. CW-PO consistently outperforms WS-DPO across all three PO objectives and both model families, with an average 5.2% GRA gain (e.g., 61.3% vs. 56.7% on HH-RLHF for OPT-1.3B DPO; 77.2% vs. 73.7% on Qwen2.5-14B IPO). Against the Human annotation baseline, CW-PO also wins on average by ~5% GRA, and in several individual settings (e.g., rDPO on OPT-1.3B: 62.7% vs. 56.3%; IPO on Qwen2.5-14B: 77.2% vs. 74.6%). A particularly striking result is that CW-DPO with only 30% of human annotations surpasses standard DPO trained on the full 100% human-annotated dataset (Table 3: 68.8% vs. 66.4% average for OPT-1.3B; 70.6% vs. 65.1% for Qwen2.5-7B), and even with just 20% annotations CW-DPO (70.3%) beats DPO on the full dataset (69.7%). The only setting where CW-PO does not clearly win is UFB with DPO on OPT-1.3B (63.1% vs. 63.4% for WS-DPO), suggesting the advantage is most pronounced on noisier or more subjective preference data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends the weak-to-strong preference alignment framework of Tao & Li (2025), who showed that a small LLM fine-tuned on a subset of human-labeled data can annotate the remaining data to align a stronger model. The key departure is the introduction of confidence-weighted preference optimization (CW-PO), which reweights each training sample by the weak annotator's prediction margin rather than treating all weak-model annotations equally. The framework is instantiated as a plug-and-play modification to existing PO objectives (DPO, IPO, rDPO), and the weak annotator itself is trained with a Bradley-Terry scoring head rather than the DPO implicit-reward formulation used in the baseline. This positions the work at the intersection of weak-to-strong generalization and data-efficient preference optimization, building on the DPO/IPO/rDPO loss family while adding a principled sample-weighting mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: CW-PO with only 20–30% of human annotations outperforms standard DPO trained on 100% of human labels, offering a concrete path to dramatically reducing the annotation cost of preference alignment. The plug-and-play nature of the confidence weight (a single multiplicative factor on any PO loss) makes it easy to adopt across existing alignment pipelines. Open questions include whether the confidence signal generalizes to more complex or multi-criteria alignment settings, whether the gains persist at larger model scales (the paper notes diminishing returns as the strong model grows), and whether the Bradley-Terry scoring head for the weak annotator is truly necessary or whether simpler confidence proxies would suffice. The single-run protocol and limited model families (OPT, Qwen) leave some uncertainty about robustness.
