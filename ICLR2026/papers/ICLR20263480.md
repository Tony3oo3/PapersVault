## Problem

Existing combinatorial bandit algorithms assume stationary rewards, while rising bandit algorithms ignore combinatorial structure. When super arms share base arms, improvements to a shared base arm propagate to multiple super arms simultaneously—a 'partially shared enhancement' that neither framework captures. This paper formalizes the Combinatorial Rising Bandit (CRB) problem and shows that the optimal policy is generally non-constant, making the problem fundamentally harder than either parent setting.

## Input / Output

At each round, CRUCB receives the set of K base arms with their historical outcome sequences, a combinatorial structure S defining valid super arms (e.g., all s-to-g paths in a graph), and a monotone reward function r. It outputs a single super arm to play, computed by solving argmax_S r(S, μ̂) where μ̂ are the Future-UCB indices. The Solver is a problem-specific optimization oracle (e.g., Dijkstra for shortest path, Hungarian algorithm for matching), making the method plug-and-play across combinatorial structures. The adaptive sliding window h_i = εN_{i,t-1} means the effective input history grows with the number of pulls, balancing initial agility against long-term statistical stability.

## Method

CRUCB operates in two stages per round: (i) it computes a Future-UCB index for each base arm that combines three terms—a recent average over the last h_i outcomes, a linear extrapolation of improvement estimated via finite-difference slope (which is provably optimistic under concavity), and an inflated exploration bonus scaled by the time since last pull; and (ii) it feeds these indices into a combinatorial Solver to select the super arm maximizing estimated reward. The key design choice is applying the rising-reward estimation at the base-arm level rather than the super-arm level, so that shared base arms naturally propagate their improvements across all super arms containing them. The regret upper bound adapts to problem difficulty through the cumulative increment Υ(M,q), yielding sub-linear regret O(T^{2/3}(log T)^{1/3}) for fast-saturating outcomes and linear regret O(T) for slowly decaying slopes, with a matching lower bound for the fine-grained instance class γ_i(n) ≤ (n+1)^{-c}.

## Results

In the toy online shortest path example, CRUCB's cumulative regret becomes nearly flat while SW-CUCB and R-ed-UCB accumulate linear regret (Figure 1c). In synthetic Path-easy and Path-complex environments, CRUCB achieves the lowest cumulative regret across all 5 runs, with the gap over R-ed-UCB widening substantially in the complex setting due to amplified partially shared enhancement. In the AntMaze deep RL environments, CRUCB outperforms all baselines in both AntMaze-easy (7 base arms, 3 paths) and AntMaze-complex (48 base arms, 178 paths), maintaining robustness despite the observed outcomes violating the concavity assumption (Assumption 1) due to an extended zero-reward period before first success.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CRUCB outperforms all five baselines across every evaluated environment. Against SW-CUCB (Chen et al., 2021), the combinatorial non-stationary baseline, CRUCB wins decisively: SW-CUCB consistently selects the early-peaker path in the toy example and fails to account for rising rewards, accumulating linear regret, while CRUCB converges to the late-bloomer path. Against R-ed-UCB (Metelli et al., 2022), the non-combinatorial rising bandit baseline, CRUCB wins in all settings; R-ed-UCB incorrectly interprets cumulative increments from shared base arms as immediate growth, causing it to hallucinate ongoing potential in suboptimal paths and split pulls roughly evenly. In the complex AntMaze-complex environment (178 paths), R-ed-UCB performs uniform exploration across all paths and never converges, while CRUCB efficiently exploits shared improvements and concentrates on the optimal path. Interestingly, non-combinatorial non-stationary baselines (SW-UCB, SW-TS) consistently outperform their combinatorial counterparts (SW-CUCB, SW-CTS) in the complex synthetic task, because the larger path count promotes broader exploration that accidentally helps discover late bloomers, whereas combinatorial algorithms lock onto early peakers. In AntMaze-easy, R-ed-UCB is the closest competitor to CRUCB (both outperform non-stationary baselines), but CRUCB still achieves lower cumulative regret by better leveraging the combinatorial structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two established lines of work: combinatorial semi-bandits (Chen et al., 2013; Chen et al., 2021), where actions are sets of base arms with stationary rewards, and rising bandits (Heidari et al., 2016; Metelli et al., 2022), where individual arm rewards increase with pulls but the setting is non-combinatorial. CRB deliberately combines both, introducing the 'partially shared enhancement' phenomenon where shared base arms couple reward dynamics across super arms—a structural dependency absent from either parent formulation. Methodologically, CRUCB adapts the sliding-window UCB estimation from Metelli et al. (2022) to the base-arm level and plugs it into the combinatorial Solver oracle framework from Chen et al. (2013), with a novel Future-UCB index that extrapolates future outcomes via finite-difference slope estimation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first formal treatment of combinatorial rising bandits with near-tight regret bounds (upper and lower), establishing that the problem is fundamentally harder than either parent setting. The finding that constant policies are generally suboptimal in the combinatorial rising regime (unlike the non-combinatorial case) reshapes how one should think about optimal strategies in reusable-skill settings. Limitations include the assumption of a fixed base-arm set and static combinatorial structure, which excludes dynamic skill discovery; extending CRB to evolving action spaces is identified as a key open direction. Practical impact is demonstrated in hierarchical RL (AntMaze) and online shortest path, but the scope remains narrower than general-purpose RL or bandit methods.
