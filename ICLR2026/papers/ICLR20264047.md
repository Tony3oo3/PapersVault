## Problem

Domain-level machine unlearning faces a fundamental dilemma: removing all unwanted domain data is computationally prohibitive (cost scales with forget-set size), while random partial removal is statistically inefficient because the domain's footprint persists in the remaining samples. No prior method addresses which specific subset of samples to remove to most efficiently erase a domain's statistical influence while preserving a retained distribution.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264047/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes two finite sample sets—S1 drawn from an unwanted distribution p1 (identified via upstream processes like keyword filtering or classifiers) and S2 from a retained distribution p2—along with a deletion budget f. It outputs a ranked subset of f samples from S1 to delete, producing an edited data distribution p that satisfies KL-divergence constraints: KL(p1‖p) ≥ α (removal) and KL(p2‖p) ≤ ε (preservation). In practice, distance metrics are adapted to modality: cosine distance for sparse TF-IDF text embeddings and Mahalanobis distance for dense CNN image features.

## Method

The paper formalizes distributional unlearning as an information-theoretic optimization: find the minimal deletion subset that maximizes KL divergence from p1 while bounding KL divergence to p2. For Gaussian distributions with shared covariance, it derives the exact Pareto frontier ε = (√α − √KL(p1‖p2))² and proves downstream log-loss shift guarantees connecting data-level edits to predictive performance. The proposed selective removal algorithm scores each p1 sample by its distance to the empirical mean of p2 and deletes the f farthest samples, exploiting the insight that a domain's statistical influence is concentrated in outlier samples relative to the retained distribution. In the low-divergence Gaussian regime, this achieves a quadratic improvement in sample efficiency over random removal (e.g., 5× fewer deletions to reach the same forgetting target). The framework is model-agnostic and composes as an efficiency-boosting front-end with existing sample-level unlearning methods (retraining, influence functions, NegGrad+, SalUn), reducing their computational cost by shrinking the forget set by 15–82%.

## Results

Across all datasets, selective removal requires 15–82% fewer deletions than full removal to halve the initial removal metric (e.g., accuracy or recall on the forget set). In the low-divergence Gaussian regime, selective removal needs only 18% deletion vs. 65% for random (82% savings); on CIFAR-10, it halves cat-class accuracy with 50% deletion vs. 80% for random; on Jigsaw toxic comments, it achieves the same recall drop with 85% vs. 100% deletion. These savings hold in combination with downstream sample-level unlearning methods (retraining, NegGrad+, SalUn), where selective selection reduces the deletion budget by 12–45% relative to full removal.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks its distance-based selective removal against two gradient-based coreset methods—CRAIG (Mirzasoleiman et al., ICML 2020) and GradMatch (Killamsetty et al., ICML 2021)—which select samples most representative of the forget distribution p₁. Both coreset baselines outperform random deletion but fall substantially short of the proposed selective method under matched deletion budgets. On CIFAR-10 with retraining, the coreset baseline requires 75% deletion vs. 60% for selective (40% savings over full removal); with SalUn, coreset needs 75% vs. 55% for selective. In the low-divergence Gaussian setting, selective removal achieves the target with only 15–18% deletion, while both coreset methods require 63–93%, a gap of roughly 4×. The paper attributes this to the fundamental mismatch: coreset methods optimize within-distribution representativeness, whereas distributional unlearning requires cross-distributional contrast between p₁ and p₂, which single-distribution scoring functions cannot exploit.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by reframing machine unlearning as a data-selection problem at the distributional level, rather than a model-update problem. It explicitly positions itself as complementary to sample-level unlearning methods (influence functions, data sharding, certified removal) that answer 'how to remove' but not 'which samples to remove,' and distinct from concept erasure methods that edit model representations rather than data. The two-distribution objective—maximizing KL divergence from an unwanted distribution while minimizing divergence from a retained one—departs from both coreset construction (single-distribution approximation) and domain adaptation (minimizing divergence), inverting the latter's goal. The information-theoretic formalization via Pareto frontiers for exponential families is a new analytical tool for this setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is significant: practitioners can reduce deletion budgets by 15–82% while achieving comparable unlearning effects, directly lowering the computational cost of domain-level unlearning in production systems. Key limitations include the Gaussian assumption in finite-sample theory (real-world savings are more modest at 15–50%), the requirement that samples be pre-identified as belonging to the unwanted subpopulation, and evaluation based on model performance rather than privacy-centric metrics like membership inference. Open questions include bridging the theory to non-Gaussian and multimodal data, extending to LLM fine-tuning pipelines, and developing active identification systems that find high-impact samples without exhaustive annotation.
