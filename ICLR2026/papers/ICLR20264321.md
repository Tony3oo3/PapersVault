## Problem

Existing monocular normal estimation methods produce normal maps that appear visually plausible but suffer from 3D misalignment, where reconstructed surfaces fail to match true geometric details. The paper argues this stems from the current paradigm of directly predicting normal maps, where geometric variations are encoded as subtle color differences that deep models struggle to distinguish, especially under the ambiguity inherent to single-image inputs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264321/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image of an object under arbitrary lighting, converts it to grayscale (eliminating chromatic information), and feeds it at 576×576 resolution into a video diffusion model. The intermediate output is a grayscale shading sequence — a short video of f frames (f=9) representing the object's shading under canonical parallel lights arranged on a 45° latitude ring. The final output is a per-pixel normal map recovered analytically via an ordinary least-squares solver applied to the positive-valued shadings in the sequence.

## Method

RoSE reformulates monocular normal estimation as shading sequence estimation, exploiting the fact that shading sequences (clamped dot products of normals with canonical light directions) are far more sensitive to geometric variation than normal maps themselves, as validated by total-variation analysis. A video diffusion U-Net (built on SV3D/SVD architecture) is trained to generate the shading sequence from the grayscale input, conditioned via a dual-branch strategy: a CLIP embedding injected through cross-attention for global object-level context, and a VAE-encoded latent concatenated with the noisy latent at each denoising step for local spatial fidelity. The model is trained with a standard z₀-reparameterization diffusion loss on VAE-encoded shading sequences, using the MultiShade dataset (~3M pairs from 90K Objaverse objects rendered with diverse MatSynth PBR materials and Poly Haven HDR environment lights). At inference, the generated shading sequence is converted to a normal map by solving an OLS system restricted to positive shading values, avoiding bias from the max(·,0) clamping operation.

## Results

On the LUCES benchmark, RoSE achieves a mean angular error (MAE) of 14.48°, outperforming the best baseline Lotus-G (17.44°) by nearly 3° and NiRNE (17.88°) by over 3.4°. On DiLiGenT, RoSE reaches 16.36° MAE versus 17.27° for NiRNE, the previous state of the art. On the MultiShade test set (100 unseen Objaverse objects with diverse materials and lighting), RoSE achieves 15.37° mean MAE and 7.78° median MAE, with 26.99% of objects below 3° error—substantially ahead of all baselines (best: Lotus-D at 18.48° mean, 14.51% below 3°).

## Baselines & Comparisons

RoSE outperforms all seven baselines on both DiLiGenT and LUCES in mean MAE. Against Lotus-G (He et al., 2024a), the strongest prior method on LUCES, RoSE improves mean MAE from 17.44° to 14.48° (−2.96°), with particularly large gains on BELL (8.66°→5.94°), BOWL (10.89°→6.84°), and JAR (10.69°→5.93°). Against NiRNE (Ye et al., 2025), the previous SOTA on DiLiGenT, RoSE improves from 17.27° to 16.36° (−0.91°), winning on 8 of 10 objects. GeoWizard (Fu et al., 2024) and DSINE (Bae & Davison, 2024) trail significantly, with mean MAEs of 22.49° and 21.82° on LUCES respectively. Neural LightRig (He et al., 2024b), which generates multi-light RGB images before estimating normals, achieves 20.95° on LUCES and 29.10° on DiLiGenT, confirming the paper's argument that generating additional RGB images introduces artifacts that worsen 3D misalignment. The only object where RoSE does not rank in the top two is HOUSE on LUCES (36.79° vs. NiRNE's 34.99°), which the authors attribute to model variance and training-set composition.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in monocular normal estimation—directly regressing a normal map from a single RGB image—by reformulating the task as shading sequence estimation under canonical parallel lights, followed by an analytical OLS solve. It repurposes image-to-video diffusion models (SV3D) originally designed for multi-view synthesis, redirecting their temporal coherence priors toward generating physically meaningful shading variations rather than novel viewpoints. The approach also draws on classical photometric stereo (ring-light geometry, Woodham's OLS solver) and recent observations that learned features in photometric stereo networks resemble shading sequences (Wei et al., 2025), but is the first to use a video generative model as the shading-sequence predictor and to train on a purpose-built dataset (MultiShade) with explicit material augmentation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 3D misalignment problem—where predicted normals look plausible but fail to reconstruct accurate geometry—is a persistent weakness across the field, and RoSE's reframing offers a principled explanation (normal maps encode geometry in a compact, low-contrast color space) and a concrete fix. The MultiShade dataset with PBR material augmentation and the finding that grayscale input outperforms RGB provide practical guidance for future normal-estimation pipelines. Open questions include whether the video-diffusion overhead can be reduced for real-time use, how the method extends to transparent/semi-transparent objects and scene-level settings, and whether the shading-sequence paradigm transfers to other dense 3D prediction tasks beyond normals.
