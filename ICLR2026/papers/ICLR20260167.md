## Problem

Rote memorization in LLMs is widely assumed to be harmful to generalization, motivating training regimes that limit epochs and discourage overfitting. However, it remains unclear whether models can actually recover generalization capacity from data they have memorized in a purely rote fashion. This paper asks whether LLMs can reinterpret rote-memorized factual associations to generalize across unseen prompts, entities, and languages.

## Input / Output

In Phase 1, the model consumes sequences of the form 'Subject [X] Object' where [X] is a single synthetic, randomly initialized key token with no linguistic meaning, trained via next-token prediction. In Phase 2, the input shifts to semantically meaningful natural-language prompts (e.g., 'Who is Subject's mother?') paired with the correct object as a supervised target. The model produces three types of output: free-form generated text (greedy, 50–100 tokens), a joint probability assigned to the target object tokens, and a multiple-choice selection from 100 candidate objects. Evaluation also extends to prompts translated into German, Spanish, Chinese, and Japanese while keeping entity names in English.

## Method

The paper introduces a two-phase 'memorize-then-generalize' framework that decouples rote memorization from semantic generalization. In Phase 1, the model rote-memorizes 100 subject-object pairs per relation using a single synthetic key token [X] via unsupervised next-token prediction over 20 epochs, with no semantic cues in the input. In Phase 2, supervised fine-tuning on as few as one memorized association paired with one meaningful prompt reinterprets the key token's latent representation to carry the relation's semantics, enabling generalization to held-out facts, unseen prompt variants, and other languages. Representation analysis (PCA clustering and cosine similarity) shows that Phase 1 organizes fact embeddings into relation-specific clusters, and Phase 2 shifts the key token's hidden state to align with semantically meaningful prompts while leaving unrelated prompts unchanged. The framework outperforms standard SFT and in-context learning in both accuracy and token efficiency for knowledge injection, and deeper rote memorization (more Phase-1 epochs) consistently improves downstream generalization and multi-hop reasoning performance.

## Results

After 20 epochs of rote memorization (Phase-1), the model achieves only 0.36 generation accuracy on test prompts, confirming that memorization alone is insufficient. After just 1 epoch of Phase-2 fine-tuning with 50 memorized associations and a single training prompt, generation accuracy on unseen facts jumps to 0.76, and object probability on held-out facts rises from 0.18 to 0.79 (train prompt) and from 0 to 0.17 (test prompt variants). The finding is robust across 8 models (1B–14B, 4 families) and extends to cross-lingual generalization (German, Spanish, Chinese, Japanese). Deeper rote memorization consistently improves generalization: with 20 Phase-1 epochs and 8 Phase-2 epochs on a single fact, test-prompt accuracy reaches 0.76.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the memorize-then-generalize framework against two standard knowledge-injection baselines: supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on full-length meaningful prompts, and in-context learning (ICL) where facts are provided in the input context. Against SFT, the proposed method is both more effective and more efficient: with one training prompt it achieves far higher accuracy than SFT using the same number of training tokens, and at ten prompts both reach ~0.9 accuracy but the proposed method uses roughly half the tokens because it reuses a single key token across all facts. Against ICL, the framework consistently produces higher object probabilities and a clearer separation between related and unrelated prompts, while ICL assigns similarly high probabilities to both. On reversal reasoning, SFT achieves only 0.01 accuracy while the proposed method reaches 0.26 (ICL achieves 1.0 by providing the fact in context). On 2-hop composed reasoning, SFT fails entirely, the proposed method reaches 0.36 accuracy (improving from 0.14 without memorization), while ICL achieves 0.95 with both facts in context. Ablations confirm that removing the key token (zero-prompt memorization) eliminates generalization, and using a semantically meaningful token instead of a synthetic one causes the model to overwrite prior prompt mappings rather than generalize.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly challenges the dominant narrative in LLM research that rote memorization is harmful to generalization (Bayat et al., 2024; Satvaty et al., 2024; Allen-Zhu & Li, 2023), which has motivated training regimes restricted to 1–2 epochs. It connects to the grokking literature (Power et al., 2022; Nakkiran et al., 2021) where generalization emerges after memorization, but departs by showing that generalization can be *induced* with minimal semantic fine-tuning rather than emerging spontaneously from extended training. Unlike prior knowledge-injection methods that require diverse prompt sets or large training corpora (Xu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2024), the memorize-then-generalize framework demonstrates that a single synthetic key token plus one meaningful prompt suffices to unlock generalization across unseen facts, prompts, and languages.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that deep rote memorization serves as a productive substrate for generalization reframes how practitioners should think about training epochs and memorization in LLMs, with direct implications for more efficient knowledge injection (outperforming both SFT and ICL with fewer tokens). The 'dual generalization' security finding—that a small amount of malicious fine-tuning can weaponize memorized facts while preserving benign responses—raises concrete safety concerns for deployed models. Open questions include whether the phenomenon extends to non-factual domains (math, code, scientific reasoning), how it scales to larger knowledge bases, and what detection or mitigation strategies can prevent adversarial repurposing of memorized data.
