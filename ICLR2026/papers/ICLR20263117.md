## Problem

Process Reward Models (PRMs) are widely used to select the best response from LLM generations during test-time scaling, yet simple majority voting that ignores PRM scores can outperform PRM-guided Best-of-N selection on recent benchmarks. This paradox reveals a fundamental gap: there is no principled framework for optimally combining the LLM's consensus signal with the PRM's verification signal, and existing methods fail to exploit the negative evidence provided by low-scoring responses.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an ensemble of LLM-generated responses (each comprising a reasoning chain and a final answer) along with a scalar PRM score per response, plus a small one-time calibration set of 100–200 labeled questions specific to the LLM-PRM pair. It outputs a weighted vote score for each unique candidate answer and selects the argmax as the final answer. The weighting function is model-specific and must be re-calibrated whenever the LLM or PRM changes, but the calibration cost is a negligible one-time investment recovered after roughly 127 queries.

## Method

The paper formulates answer selection as a MAP estimation problem and proves (Theorem 3.2) that the optimal strategy is a weighted majority vote where each response's weight decomposes into a PRM signal term (log-likelihood ratio of the score under correct vs. incorrect reasoning) and an LLM signal term (log-odds of the model's reliability on that question). Empirically, the optimal weighting function is highly model-dependent and consistently assigns negative weights to low PRM scores, penalizing answers supported by poor reasoning. To approximate these weights without ground-truth labels at test time, the paper proposes two calibration approaches: a non-parametric KDE-based estimator operating in logit space, and simpler parametric forms (logit weighting w(p)=logit(p)−logit(b) and linear weighting w(p)=p−b) whose offset parameter b is tuned via grid search on the calibration set. The parametric methods, particularly Logit Weighted Voting, achieve superior accuracy over vanilla weighted voting and Best-of-N while using only 21.3–37.1% of the test-time computation.

## Results

Across 35 LLM-PRM pairs on the MATH dataset (n=32), Logit Weighted Voting (Logit WV) achieves the highest average accuracy among all practical methods, reaching 63.3% with Qwen-PRM-7B and 65.9% with Skywork-PRM-1.5B, compared to 57.1% for Majority Vote and 52.7–65.3% for Best-of-N. The headline efficiency result is that Logit WV matches or surpasses Vanilla Weighted Voting accuracy while using only ~37.1% (MATH) and ~21.3% (MATH500) of the test-time computation. On the Llama3.1-Mistral-8B PRM, Logit WV outperforms the best baseline (Vanilla WV) by 3.2 points on average across 5 LLMs (62.6% vs. 59.4%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Majority Vote (Self-Consistency, Wang et al. 2023), Logit WV consistently wins by 4–7 points across all PRMs (e.g., 63.3% vs. 57.1% with Qwen-PRM-7B; 65.9% vs. 59.2% with Skywork-PRM-1.5B), confirming that PRM signals add substantial value when properly calibrated. Against Best-of-N (Uesato et al. 2022), Logit WV outperforms BoN in 4 out of 5 PRM settings (e.g., 62.6% vs. 58.3% with Llama3.1-Mistral-8B; 63.3% vs. 61.8% with Qwen-PRM-7B), though BoN is competitive with Skywork-PRM-1.5B (65.3% vs. 65.9%). The most critical comparison is against Vanilla Weighted Voting (raw PRM score as weight): Logit WV surpasses it by 1.2–3.2 points in accuracy while requiring only 21.3% of the test-time samples on MATH500 to reach the same accuracy level, demonstrating that the calibration step—particularly the negative-weight mechanism for low-scoring responses—yields a large efficiency gain. The KDE non-parametric method underperforms the parametric Logit/Linear WV by 1–4 points, attributed to estimation error in the per-question LLM reliability term q_M.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the generate-and-select paradigm of Test-Time Scaling by unifying two previously separate selection strategies—Self-Consistency majority voting (Wang et al., 2023) and PRM-based Best-of-N (Uesato et al., 2022; Lightman et al., 2024)—under a single MAP estimation framework. Rather than treating the LLM's consensus signal and the PRM's verification signal as competing alternatives, the authors derive that the optimal aggregation is a weighted vote combining both, with weights that depend on the specific LLM-PRM pair. This departs from CISC (Taubenfeld et al., 2025), which uses the LLM's self-assessed confidence as weights, by instead deriving weights from a principled likelihood-ratio formulation that explicitly incorporates the PRM score distribution and the LLM's question-specific reliability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that optimal weighting functions assign substantial negative weights to low-PRM-score responses challenges the implicit assumption in both BoN and majority voting that poor-quality responses should simply be ignored; instead, they should actively penalize their proposed answer. The model-dependence of these functions (different LLM-PRM pairs require different calibration) means that deploying a PRM without calibration is inherently suboptimal, a practical insight for teams investing in expensive PRM training. Key open questions include bridging the gap between global and per-question optimal weighting (the authors' meta-model attempts failed), extending beyond mathematical reasoning, and reducing reliance on labeled calibration data. The 21.3% compute efficiency gain is meaningful but the absolute accuracy improvements over strong baselines remain modest (1–3 points), and the KDE non-parametric method underperforms simpler parametric alternatives due to estimation difficulty.
