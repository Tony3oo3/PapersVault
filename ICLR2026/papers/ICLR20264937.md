## Problem

Random subspace method (RSM) is widely used for certified defenses against adversarial, backdoor, and jailbreak attacks, but its explanations remain unexplored. Existing black-box attribution methods like Shapley values and LIME are computationally prohibitive for RSM (requiring M×N base-model queries per sample) and offer no security guarantees against explanation-preserving attacks that hide adversarial modifications while keeping the explanation unchanged.

## Input / Output

The method consumes the N sub-sampled feature groups and their base-model predictions that are already generated during RSM's ensemble prediction step, requiring zero additional forward passes. It produces per-feature importance scores normalized by each feature's appearance frequency across the N groups, a ranked top-e set of most important features, and a certified detection size D(x,T) — a lower bound on how many of the T adversarially modified features must appear in the top-e explanation, computed via binary search over r using Clopper-Pearson confidence bounds on label probabilities and importance scores.

## Method

EnsembleSHAP defines the importance of feature i as (1/k)·E[𝕀(x_i∈z)·𝕀(h(z)=ŷ)] over uniformly sampled k-sized feature groups, which is algebraically rewritten as (1/d)·Pr(h(z)=ŷ | x_i∈z) to correct for unbalanced feature appearance frequencies in finite Monte Carlo samples. This formulation reuses the N sub-sampled groups already computed during RSM prediction, making attribution essentially free (0.02–0.03 s per sample). The paper proves the score satisfies local accuracy, symmetry, and order consistency with the exact Shapley value for RSM, while relaxing the dummy and linearity axioms. For certified detection, the method derives a combinatorial lower bound: to flip the ensemble label from ŷ to ŷ', the attacker must change predictions of at least (Δ/2k) feature groups, and since only groups containing modified features can change, the importance scores of those features must rise enough to guarantee at least r of them appear in the top-e explanation.

## Results

On certified defense (Table 1), EnsembleSHAP achieves the highest faithfulness across all three datasets and attack scenarios. Under adversarial attack with 20% word deletion, it reaches 0.880 (SST-2), 1.000 (IMDb), and 0.970 (AGNews), substantially outperforming Shapley (0.840/0.840/0.960) and LIME (0.160/0.335/0.265). For backdoor attack keyword prediction (Table 2), EnsembleSHAP achieves 0.892 recall on IMDB (vs. 0.491 for Shapley) and 0.929 on AGNews (vs. 0.872 for Shapley). For jailbreak defense (Table 3), it reaches 0.85 faithfulness on DAN prompts (vs. 0.54 LIME, 0.33 Shapley) and 0.46 on AutoDAN (vs. 0.32 LIME, 0.18 Shapley).

## Baselines & Comparisons

EnsembleSHAP consistently outperforms all seven baselines across every dataset and attack scenario. Against Shapley (Chen et al., 2023), the gap is most pronounced on IMDB under adversarial attack (1.000 vs. 0.840 faithfulness at 20% deletion) and on backdoor keyword recall (0.892 vs. 0.491 on IMDB). LIME (Ribeiro et al., 2016) and ICL (Kroeger et al., 2023) are substantially weaker, with faithfulness scores typically 3–5× lower than EnsembleSHAP (e.g., 0.160 vs. 0.880 on SST-2 adversarial attack). In the appendix comparison (Table 7), EnsembleSHAP also beats Kernel SHAP, ContextCite, Beta-Shapley, L-Shapley, and C-Shapley on faithfulness, with the largest margin on IMDB (0.70 vs. 0.60 for ContextCite at 20% deletion). For jailbreak defense, EnsembleSHAP dominates on DAN prompts (0.85 vs. 0.54 LIME) and AutoDAN (0.46 vs. 0.32 LIME), though the margin over Shapley is smaller on GCG (0.24 vs. 0.19 at 20% deletion). The method's key advantage is that it reuses the ensemble's existing N forward passes, incurring less than 0.5 seconds of additional computation per sample, whereas Shapley and LIME require M×N additional queries.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the random subspace method (Ho, 1998) as used in certified defenses (RanMASK, SmoothLLM) and on Shapley value theory (Lundberg & Lee, 2017) as the axiomatic foundation for feature attribution. It departs from standard black-box attribution methods (LIME, Kernel SHAP) by showing they incur M×N base-model queries when applied to ensemble models and lack any security guarantee. The key conceptual departure is that the proposed importance score (Eq. 4) is derived directly from the ensemble's own sub-sampling structure, reusing predictions already computed during inference rather than requiring additional perturbation queries. The certified detection theorem (Theorem 1) extends the randomized-smoothing certification paradigm from prediction robustness to explanation robustness, a dimension absent from prior robust attribution work that focused on prediction-preserving attacks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to provide a provable guarantee that adversarial features causing misclassification will appear in the top-e explanation, addressing a previously unaddressed security gap in explainable AI. Practically, it enables users of certified defenses and robustly aligned LLMs to pinpoint exactly which words caused a defense failure or a harmful classification, with minimal additional compute (<0.5s). Limitations include applicability only to the random subspace ensemble architecture, dependence on prediction confidence for certified detection strength, and the fact that the certified detection rate degrades as the attacker modifies more features. Open questions include extending the certified detection framework to general (non-ensemble) models and to privacy-sensitive settings like machine unlearning and differential privacy.
