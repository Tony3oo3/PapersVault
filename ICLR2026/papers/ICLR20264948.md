## Problem

Standard architectures like MLPs and Transformers treat articulated robot actions as unstructured tokens, ignoring the kinematic relationships between joints. While graph convolutions and masked attention capture link connectivity, no prior architecture directly embeds the computational structure of forward kinematics—specifically Rodrigues' rotation formula—as a learnable inductive bias for action learning.

## Input / Output

The network operates on a kinematic tree with link features (multi-channel 4×4 matrices) and joint features (multi-channel scalars), plus an optional global token for task variables like gripper state or camera parameters. For imitation learning, inputs are proprioception and object state vectors; for hand reconstruction, a ViT encoder produces image tokens that interact with link features via cross-attention. Outputs are structured per-joint (concatenating joint and child-link features through joint-specific linear heads) or global (from the global token), yielding link poses, joint angle sequences, MANO parameters, or denoised action trajectories depending on the task.

## Method

The core contribution is the Neural Rodrigues Operator, which re-parameterizes Rodrigues' rotation formula by separating state-dependent terms (joint angles, treated as input features) from state-independent coefficients (articulated structure, relaxed into trainable weights W^bias, W^cos, W^sin), then extending to multi-channel form with both left and right matrix multiplications for expressivity. The Rodrigues Network (RodriNet) stacks Rodrigues Blocks, each comprising three layers: a Rodrigues Layer performing joint-to-link message passing via the operator along the kinematic tree, a Joint Layer performing link-to-joint updates via joint-specific linear transforms, and a Self-Attention Layer enabling global information exchange across all links. This design injects kinematic structure as an architectural inductive bias while retaining the flexibility of modern deep networks. For 3-DoF animated characters (MANO), the operator is adapted to quaternion-based rotations using quadratic terms of quaternion components as the feature basis.

## Results

On the ManiSkill imitation learning benchmark (5 tasks, 7-DoF Franka arm), Rodrigues-DP achieves an average success rate of 0.61, outperforming UNet-DP (0.58) and Transformer-DP (0.44), with the largest gains on PickCube (0.94 vs. 0.85/0.63) and StackCube (0.44 vs. 0.37/0.38). On the Cartesian motion prediction task (UR5 arm, 10⁵ trajectories), the Rodrigues Network achieves a test MSE of 2.56×10⁻⁶, roughly 5× lower than the best baseline (Transformer at 12.86×10⁻⁶), while also generalizing better (test MSE below all baselines' train MSE). On FreiHAND 3D hand reconstruction, the method achieves PA-MPJPE of 5.9 mm and F@5 of 0.793, surpassing the previous SOTA HaMeR (6.0 mm, 0.785) with only 10.7M parameters vs. HaMeR's 39.5M.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against four general-purpose backbones (MLP, GCN, Transformer, Body Transformer) on synthetic kinematics/motion tasks, two Diffusion Policy variants (UNet-DP, Transformer-DP) on robot manipulation, and nine hand-reconstruction methods on FreiHAND. On forward kinematics fitting (LEAP hand), the Rodrigues Network (0.2M params) outperforms all four baselines (~3M params each) with significantly lower MSE and faster convergence; MLP and GCN produce visible artifacts at fingertips. On Cartesian motion prediction, Rodrigues achieves 1.21 mm end-effector position error vs. 2.89–3.55 mm for baselines, and its test MSE (2.56) is below every baseline's train MSE, indicating superior generalization. On imitation learning, Rodrigues-DP beats UNet-DP by 3% average success (0.61 vs. 0.58) and Transformer-DP by 17% (0.61 vs. 0.44), with the largest gap on PickCube (+9% over UNet, +31% over Transformer). On 3D hand reconstruction, the method surpasses HaMeR (the strongest baseline) on all four metrics (PA-MPJPE 5.9 vs. 6.0, PA-MPVPE 5.6 vs. 5.7, F@5 0.793 vs. 0.785, F@15 0.991 vs. 0.990) while using only 27% of the parameters. The authors note that gains are task-dependent: on simpler tasks like PushCube all backbones saturate near 100%, while on contact-heavy tasks (PegInsertionSide, PlugCharger) the advantage shrinks due to missing tactile/force sensing.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper draws a direct analogy to how CNNs generalized hand-crafted image filters into learnable operators, but applies the same principle to the Rodrigues rotation formula from classical robotics. It departs from graph convolution and Body Transformer approaches that capture link connectivity without encoding the actual kinematic computation, and from prior work that inserts analytical forward kinematics as a fixed differentiable layer or applies Cartesian-space losses post-hoc. The key conceptual move is re-parameterizing the parent-to-child pose transformation so that state-independent structural coefficients become learnable weights while state-dependent joint angles become input features, yielding a neural operator that degenerates to exact forward kinematics in a special case but can represent richer features in general.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, embodiment-aware inductive bias for action-learning networks that is grounded in the actual physics of articulated motion rather than generic structural priors like graph connectivity. The ablation cleanly isolates the Rodrigues Layer as the most critical component, and the 15× parameter efficiency in forward kinematics fitting (0.2M vs. 3M params) demonstrates that structural knowledge can substitute for model capacity. However, practical gains in realistic settings are modest: imitation learning improves by only 3 percentage points on average, and hand reconstruction edges out HaMeR by 0.1–0.3 mm. Open questions include whether the operator extends to translational joints, whether it helps in closed-loop RL, and whether incorporating link geometry (currently absent) would further improve contact-sensitive tasks.
