## Problem

Traditional adversarial attacks perturb a single image, and as defenses improve, small geometric perturbations increasingly fail to fool classifiers while unrestricted single-image perturbations often destroy the original meaning. No existing method constructs adversarial examples conditioned on an identity-level concept (a specific object or identity represented by a distribution over images), rather than a single image or a broad class. This paper asks whether expanding the perturbation space from one image to an entire concept distribution can yield adversarial examples that both preserve the original identity and achieve higher attack success rates.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262497/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of concept images C_ori (e.g., 5–6 DreamBooth photos of a specific corgi, expanded to ~30 via SDXL+LoRA augmentation), a target ImageNet class label, and a victim classifier (ResNet-50 for white-box attacks). It produces a single targeted adversarial image that a human would still recognize as the same object/identity but that the classifier assigns to the target class. In practice, M=10 candidate images are sampled from the adversarial distribution and the best one is selected via a conservative or aggressive ranking strategy based on the victim classifier's softmax output.

## Method

The core contribution is replacing the single-image-centered distance distribution p_dis(·|x_ori) in the probabilistic adversarial attack framework (Zhang et al., 2024b) with a concept-centered distribution p_dis(·|C_ori), yielding p_adv(x_adv|C_ori, y_tar) ∝ p_vic(x_adv|y_tar)·p_dis(x_adv|C_ori). The distance distribution is a DDPM diffusion model fine-tuned on the concept image set, while the victim distribution is derived from the victim classifier's cross-entropy loss; adversarial examples are sampled via Langevin dynamics from their product. Concept augmentation uses SDXL with LoRA fine-tuning on the original DreamBooth images, guided by GPT-4o-generated prompts, to produce diverse poses, viewpoints, and backgrounds. Theoretically, Theorem 1 proves that increasing the dispersion of p_dis (decreasing its Gibbs temperature β) monotonically reduces KL(p_vic‖p_dis), and Theorem 2 provides a tractable Monte-Carlo-estimable expression for the KL difference between concept-level and single-image distance distributions; empirically this difference is negative for all 26 tested concepts, confirming the broader overlap that drives the 97.82% white-box Top-1 success rate versus 59.23% for the single-image ProbAttack baseline.

## Results

On the white-box targeted attack (ResNet-50, Top-1), the concept-based adversarial attack achieves 97.82% success rate, substantially outperforming DiffAttack (84.23%), ProbAttack (59.23%), ACA (6.03%), and NCF (1.15%). For black-box transferability (Top-5), the aggressive strategy (OURS AGGR) leads on most classifiers, e.g., 11.54% on DenseNet-161 vs. 7.44% for DiffAttack and 3.97% for ProbAttack. On concept preservation, the conservative strategy scores 0.9654 in the user study (vs. 0.8041 for ProbAttack and 0.7577 for DiffAttack), and both strategies outperform all baselines on no-reference image quality metrics (e.g., MUSIQ-KonIQ: 63.75/62.22 vs. 58.16 for ProbAttack).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against NCF (color-based) and ACA (latent-space gradient), the concept-based attack achieves a dramatic improvement in white-box success (97.82% vs. 1.15% and 6.03%), confirming that unrestricted generative approaches far surpass color or single-step latent perturbations for targeted attacks. Compared to DiffAttack (the strongest generative baseline at 84.23% white-box), the proposed method gains +13.6 pp on white-box and leads on 7 of 8 transferability metrics under the aggressive strategy (e.g., 11.54% vs. 7.44% on DenseNet-161, 6.41% vs. 2.05% on EfficientNet-B7 Adv). Against ProbAttack (the single-image special case, 59.23% white-box), expanding the distance distribution to a full concept yields +38.6 pp white-box and consistently higher transferability, validating the core theoretical claim that a broader p_dis reduces KL divergence to p_vic. On concept preservation, the conservative strategy scores 0.9654 in the user study and 0.8283 CLIP similarity, both exceeding ProbAttack (0.8041 / 0.8581) and DiffAttack (0.7577 / 0.8093), while also achieving the best no-reference image quality (e.g., MUSIQ-KonIQ 63.75 vs. 58.16 for ProbAttack). The aggressive strategy trades a small amount of similarity (0.8808 user study) for the highest transferability, making it the preferred choice when cross-classifier robustness matters most.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Zhang et al. (2024b)'s probabilistic adversarial attack framework (ProbAttack), which casts adversarial example generation as sampling from the product of a distance distribution p_dis and a victim distribution p_vic. The key conceptual departure is replacing the single-image-centered distance distribution p_dis(·|x_ori) with a concept-centered distribution p_dis(·|C_ori) learned from a set of images depicting the same identity, reducing to ProbAttack when |C_ori|=1. Among unrestricted adversarial attacks, prior generative-model-based methods (Song et al. 2018, AdvDiff, NatADiff) operate at the class level, while others (DiffAttack, ACA, NCF) remain single-image; this paper is the first to formalize and exploit identity-level concept distributions as the perturbation space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that a concept-level distance distribution nearly doubles white-box attack success over ProbAttack (97.82% vs 59.23%) while simultaneously improving concept preservation (user study 0.9654 vs 0.8041) suggests that the single-image perturbation paradigm is fundamentally limiting for unrestricted attacks. However, transferability remains modest compared to methods explicitly optimized for it, and the 8-hour-per-concept fine-tuning cost limits practical deployment. Open questions include whether lighter-weight concept representations (e.g., frozen LoRA adapters) can reduce training cost, and how to defend against attacks that generate entirely new images rather than perturbing existing ones.
