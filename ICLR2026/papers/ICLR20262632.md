## Problem

The Maximum Common Edge Subgraph (MCES) problem is NP-complete, and existing solutions rely on max-clique reductions or branch-and-bound search that scale exponentially with graph size, making them impractical for large molecular graphs. No prior method combines polynomial-time approximation with explicit common-subgraph extraction, theoretical convergence guarantees, and a training-data-free optimization framework.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262632/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes two labeled molecular graphs (node features as atom types, edge features as bond types) and first constructs an Association Common Graph (ACG) whose nodes are compatible (u,v) pairs and whose edges encode shared edge labels. The output is an explicit MCES structure (matched atoms and bonds), obtained by discretizing the learned soft assignment matrix via the Hungarian algorithm and masking with the ACG adjacency; downstream tasks additionally produce Johnson similarity scores or retrieval rankings over graph databases.

## Method

NGA formulates MCES as a Quadratic Assignment Problem over the ACG, then iteratively refines a soft assignment matrix using a neuralized Graduated Assignment loop where the temperature at each layer is a learnable product β_l = W₁ᵀW₂ (d-dimensional vectors) rather than a fixed scalar. Training is fully unsupervised: the QAP objective is maximized end-to-end via backpropagation through m=4 NGA layers, with an 8-layer GCN providing initial node correspondences through Sinkhorn-normalized similarity. At inference, Gumbel-Sinkhorn sampling generates multiple candidate assignments from the learned distribution, and the best is selected by objective value. The product parameterization is shown theoretically to induce accelerating gradient updates (analogous to adaptive learning rates) and to enable escape from local optima when β_l becomes negative, creating an implicit exploration–exploitation schedule without manual tuning.

## Results

On the primary MCES task, NGA achieves 98.64% / 99.20% / 97.94% accuracy on AIDS / MOLHIV / MCF-7, outperforming the best search-based baseline RASCAL (90.67% / 88.74% / 90.26%) by roughly 8 percentage points and the best non-search baseline Gurobi (74.52% / 78.67% / 80.76%) by over 17 points. For graph similarity, NGA reduces MSE to 1.13 / 0.66 / 0.99 (×10⁻³), an order of magnitude lower than the best competing method (NeuroMatch at 13.92 / 19.17 / 17.34). In graph retrieval, NGA attains MRR of 0.844 / 0.806 / 0.803 and MAP of 0.974 / 0.951 / 0.966, leading all baselines on every metric across all three datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NGA decisively outperforms all 11 baselines on the primary MCES task. Against search-based methods, NGA beats RASCAL by ~8 pp and Mcsplit by ~27–37 pp, while also running in polynomial time versus their exponential worst-case. The supervised NGM (33–52%) and unsupervised GANN-GM (50–72%) both trail NGA by 25–65 pp, with the paper attributing NGM's weakness to the multi-modality of MCES ground truths. Among non-search baselines, NGA surpasses Gurobi (74–81%) by 17–21 pp and the classical GA (71–73%) by 25–28 pp, confirming that the learnable product-temperature parameterization is the key differentiator. On graph similarity, NGA's MSE (0.66–1.13 ×10⁻³) is 10–35× lower than NeuroMatch, SimGNN, GMN, XMCS, and INFMCS, which all operate in the 14–39 ×10⁻³ range. In graph retrieval, NGA leads on all three metrics (MRR, P@10, MAP) across all datasets, with the closest competitor (NeuroMatch) trailing by 0.17–0.57 in MRR.

## Relation to Prior Work

NGA is a direct neuralized extension of the classical Graduated Assignment (GA) algorithm for graph matching (Gold & Rangarajan, 1996), replacing the manually scheduled scalar temperature with a learnable product parameterization (W₁ᵀW₂) that is optimized end-to-end via unsupervised training. The paper further introduces the Association Common Graph (ACG) construction, which reformulates MCES as a Quadratic Assignment Problem while guaranteeing that any assignment maps to a valid common subgraph—a property absent in penalty-based graph matching formulations. For inference, NGA incorporates Gumbel-Sinkhorn sampling (Mena et al., 2018) to explore the solution space, and uses a GCN encoder for initial node correspondences, following the pattern established by SimGNN and Deep Graph Matching Consensus.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By delivering near-optimal MCES solutions in polynomial time (vs. exponential search), NGA makes MCES tractable for large molecular graphs where exact solvers are infeasible, with direct implications for drug discovery, cheminformatics, and network security. The ACG formulation is a clean, interpretable reduction that any assignment maps to a valid common subgraph, and the theoretical analysis (Theorem 1, Proposition 2) provides the first rigorous characterization of how learnable temperature drives exploration-exploitation dynamics in graduated assignment. Limitations include evaluation restricted to molecular graphs (3 datasets, ≤~30 atoms), per-instance training rather than amortized inference, and the approximate nature of solutions (49–61% exact-match rate). Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to non-molecular graph domains, whether amortized training across instances is feasible, and how it scales to graphs with hundreds of nodes.
