## Problem

Spiking neural networks are a promising energy-efficient alternative to ANNs, yet their theoretical foundations—particularly regarding stability and robustness to input perturbations—remain largely underexplored compared to classical networks. No prior work has applied Boolean function analysis to characterize how small input changes propagate through LIF-SNN classifiers, leaving open whether random SNNs are inherently stable and what structural property explains this stability.

## Input / Output

The classifier takes a sequence of T binary input vectors (x_t)_{t∈[T]} ∈ ({-1,1}^n)^T and produces a discrete class label via argmax over cumulative spike counts at the final layer. The theoretical analysis considers perturbations of up to O(√n) coordinates (Hamming distance), while experiments also evaluate random dropout of 5% of input components. Static inputs (repeated presentation of the same sample) are the primary setting for the spectral results, though dynamic sequences are handled in the single-neuron bound.

## Method

The paper analyzes discrete-time sign LIF (sLIF) SNNs through Boolean function analysis, introducing noise sensitivity and a new notion called spectral simplicity (concentration of the Fourier-Walsh spectrum on low-degree terms). For a single neuron, an induction over time steps combined with Gaussian correlation decomposition (Lemma 3) yields a disagreement probability of O(√ν log n) for inputs at relative Hamming distance ν. For multi-layer networks, a Markov chain tracking the number of disagreeing neurons at each layer, stochastically dominated by a Binomial, is analyzed via Chernoff bounds to show that disagreement probability decays as ν^{1/2^{2L+1}}. Corollary 1 then converts the expected noise sensitivity bound into a spectral concentration guarantee, proving that random LIF-SNN classifiers are biased toward spectrally simple functions whose Fourier weight is predominantly on low-frequency components. Experiments on MNIST, CIFAR-10, and NMNIST confirm that training further reduces noise sensitivity beyond the random-initialization baseline.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 2) shows that an L-layer sLIF SNN classifier with input dimension n remains unchanged under perturbations of up to O(√n) coordinates with high probability, with the disagreement probability bounded by n_L T⁴ C(1+θ) ν^{1/2^{2L+1}} log^{3/2} n + (L−1)e^{−cν^{1/2^{2L−1}}} n. Corollary 1 establishes spectral simplicity: the Fourier spectrum of a random L-layer binary SNN classifier is concentrated up to degree O(√n log n), with only a vanishing fraction O(n^{1/2^{L+1}}) of spectral mass above that degree. Empirically, training a 3-layer sLIF SNN on MNIST (n=784) to 98% accuracy significantly reduces noise sensitivity compared to random initialization, while on CIFAR-10 (n=3072, 84.38% accuracy) the reduction is more modest. Under 5% dropout perturbation on a 5-layer network (n=1000), both sLIF (0.16) and LIF (0.16) models show lower output sensitivity than under random flipping (0.19 and 0.28 respectively).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is primarily a theoretical paper with empirical validation rather than a method-comparison study, so no external baselines are benchmarked. The internal comparisons reveal several key findings: (1) sIF (signed) and IF (unsigned) neurons exhibit similar noise sensitivity in practice, despite the theory requiring the signed variant; (2) training consistently reduces noise sensitivity—dramatically on MNIST (where test accuracy reaches ~98%) and moderately on CIFAR-10 (84.38% accuracy), suggesting the effect scales with task difficulty; (3) dropout perturbation is less disruptive than random bit-flipping for both sLIF and LIF models, with LIF showing a larger gap (0.28 vs 0.16) than sLIF (0.19 vs 0.16); (4) the theoretical bound from Theorem 2 tends to overestimate the actual sensitivity of deep (5-layer) networks, indicating the bounds are conservative. The paper also notes that depth has a stronger impact on sensitivity than latency T, though the theoretical bound's exponential-in-L dependence appears to be a proof artifact.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper applies the Boolean function analysis framework of O'Donnell (2014) — noise sensitivity, Fourier-Walsh expansion, and spectral concentration — to discrete-time LIF spiking neural networks, a setting where such tools had not previously been deployed. For the multi-layer analysis, it adapts the Markov-chain disagreement-propagation technique of Jonasson et al. (2023) for threshold ANNs, extending it to handle the reset-by-subtraction dynamics and temporal evolution unique to spiking neurons. Conceptually, it bridges two previously separate literatures: SNN robustness (studied via dynamical systems by Ding et al., 2024) and simplicity bias in deep networks (studied via Gaussian processes by De Palma et al., 2019), introducing 'spectral simplicity' as a new, intrinsically Boolean notion of simplicity that arises naturally in spiking architectures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first quantitative stability guarantees for wide LIF-SNN classifiers and connects them to a formal simplicity bias, offering a theoretical lens for why SNNs may generalize well. Key limitations include the requirement of large network width for concentration arguments, the restriction to initialization (training effects are only explored empirically), and the β=1 simplification. Open questions flagged by the authors include whether the width condition can be weakened, how training dynamics alter spectral concentration, and whether the T- and L-dependencies in the bounds are intrinsic or proof artifacts — all of which would be needed before these results can inform practical SNN design or adversarial robustness guarantees.
