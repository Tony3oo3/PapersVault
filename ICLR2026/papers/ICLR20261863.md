## Problem

Selecting the optimal teacher for distilling a language model student requires expensive trial-and-error: generating data from each candidate teacher and training a student on each. A stronger-performing model is not always a better teacher, so naive selection by teacher accuracy fails. The paper asks whether a lightweight, principled score can identify the most compatible teacher for a given student-task pair without access to teacher logits, a verifier, or test data.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a small subsampled set of teacher-generated prompt-response pairs (n=512 prompts × m=4 generations, roughly 60× smaller than the full distillation dataset) along with the pre-trained student model's parameters. It produces a single scalar GRACE score per teacher (or teacher-temperature pair), enabling direct ranking and selection. No teacher internals, logits, external verifier, or held-out test set are required; all computation is based on the student's gradients on the teacher's generations.

## Method

GRACE (GRAdient Cross-validation Evaluation) partitions the subsampled teacher generations into C folds and, for each fold, computes the squared norm of the student's gradients in that fold re-weighted by the inverse eigenvalue spectrum of the normalized gradient covariance from the complementary folds. This cross-validation structure unifies two complementary desiderata: the gradient spectrum captures data diversity (analogous to G-Vendi's directional entropy), while the gradient norm encodes teacher-student alignment (analogous to G-Norm), with large gradients along low-eigenvalue directions penalized most heavily. Gradients are randomly projected to d=512 dimensions and rescaled by log|y| to account for response length. Theoretically, GRACE upper-bounds the leave-one-out conditional mutual information of a single preconditioned gradient step, connecting the score to algorithmic stability and generalization. Empirically, the variance component (GRACE-Variance) dominates and drives predictive power, while the bias component flags pathological teachers that produce random or uninformative responses.

## Results

On GSM8K with LLaMA-1B, GRACE achieves 86% Spearman correlation with student post-distillation performance and 0.3% teacher-student regret, substantially outperforming G-Norm (55% correlation, 4.9% regret) and G-Vendi (44% correlation, 14.5% regret). On MATH with LLaMA-3B, GRACE maintains >85% correlation and 3.9% regret. Selecting a teacher via GRACE improves student performance by up to 7.4% over naively choosing the best-performing teacher (which incurs ≥7.7% regret on GSM8K). GRACE also identifies near-optimal generation temperatures (within 0.1 of optimal) and achieves <0.3% regret under size constraints (≤3B, ≤10B, ≤30B).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GRACE decisively outperforms G-Vendi (Jung et al., 2025) on both metrics: 86% vs. 44% Spearman correlation and 0.3% vs. 14.5% regret on GSM8K. Against G-Norm, GRACE also wins (86% vs. 55% correlation; 0.3% vs. 4.9% regret), with the gap widening on MATH where G-Norm's directional blindness becomes more pronounced. The two intuitive baselines—teacher's own performance (11% correlation) and student's pre-training loss on teacher generations (44% correlation, 5.4% regret)—are both far weaker, confirming that a stronger teacher is not a better teacher. BADGE (Ash et al., 2019) and other gradient-based baselines (determinant, gradient cosine, gradient inner product) listed in Section C.3 also underperform GRACE in the full comparison of Figure 3. GRACE's advantage is consistent across student architectures (LLaMA-1B, OLMo-1B, Gemma-2B, LLaMA-3B) and persists under OOD evaluation on GSM-Symbolic and MATH².

## Relation to Prior Work

GRACE unifies two complementary gradient-based data-quality signals—G-Vendi (directional diversity, Jung et al. 2025) and G-Norm (teacher-student alignment via gradient magnitude)—into a single cross-validation score, drawing on the leave-one-out conditional mutual information framework (Steinke & Zakynthinou 2020; Rammal et al. 2022) to justify why spectral-weighted gradient variance predicts generalization. It departs from prior data-selection methods (Pruthi et al. 2020; Xia et al. 2024) by operating at the distribution level (selecting an entire teacher) rather than scoring individual data points, and from prior distillation work by providing a lightweight, verifier-free score instead of relying on trial-and-error or teacher performance as a proxy. The paper also builds on the growing body of evidence that stronger-performing models are not automatically better teachers (Mirzadeh et al. 2019; Harutyunyan et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2023, 2025a), formalizing why this happens through the lens of gradient stability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GRACE offers a practical, computationally cheap tool (no verifier, no teacher logits, no test data) that practitioners can use to select teachers, tune generation temperature, and respect size or family constraints—addressing a real bottleneck in the current guess-and-check distillation workflow. The theoretical connection to LOO-CMI provides a principled explanation for why the score works, though the analysis is limited to a single gradient step and a loss-based generalization bound, leaving a gap to the multi-step, performance-metric reality of actual training. Open questions include GRACE's robustness to adversarial teacher behaviors (e.g., repetitive padding that artificially shrinks gradients), extension beyond math to open-ended tasks, and whether incorporating teacher logits or per-prompt teacher selection could further improve the score.
