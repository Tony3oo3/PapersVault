## Problem

Despite near-complete anatomical reconstructions of the Drosophila melanogaster nervous system, musculature, and exoskeleton, no musculoskeletal model of fly legs incorporates anatomically grounded, physiologically realistic muscle actuation. Existing whole-body fly simulations (e.g., NeuroMechFly) rely on abstract position- or torque-based joint controllers, leaving a critical gap between motor neuron activity and joint movements that prevents testing how neural circuits coordinate individual muscles to produce behavior.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260690/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes high-resolution synchrotron X-ray scans from multiple fixed specimens (for muscle attachment points and fiber paths) and 3D pose trajectories from tethered flies performing locomotion and grooming (tracked at 100 Hz via DeepLabCut + AniPose, interpolated to 500 Hz, with joint angles recovered by SeqIKPy inverse kinematics). The model produces per-muscle activation time courses, joint torques, muscle forces, and continuous excitation levels in [0,1] for 15 muscle-tendon units spanning 7 DoFs across 3 joints; an 86-dimensional observation vector (landmark positions, joint angles/velocities, and full muscle states) feeds the policy network.

## Method

The core contribution is a data-driven pipeline that constructs 15 Hill-type muscle-tendon units (Millard 2013 formulation with contractile, parallel elastic, and series elastic elements under a rigid-tendon assumption) per foreleg, initialized from X-ray-derived anatomical parameters and physiological estimates (specific tension of 28 mN/mm², max contraction velocity from X-ray video). Unknown parameters are refined via NSGA-II multiobjective optimization that jointly minimizes kinematic tracking error across two behaviors (locomotion and grooming) through a static-optimization-to-forward-dynamics loop, preventing overfitting. The optimized OpenSim model is converted to MuJoCo via MyoConverter, where MLP policies trained with PPO (15M steps, 500 Hz control, 10 kHz physics) learn to produce muscle excitations that imitate reference kinematics. Non-negative matrix factorization applied to the resulting muscle activations reveals that just three synergies explain >90% of variance, with task-specific weight patterns distinguishing locomotion from grooming. A systematic ablation of passive joint properties (stiffness, damping, armature) shows that combining stiffness and damping yields the fastest and highest-performing imitation learning, supporting a division of labor between passive mechanics and active control.

## Results

Three muscle synergies (extracted via NMF) explain over 90% of the variance in simulated muscle activations during both locomotion and grooming, with the first primitive alone capturing >80% of variance. In the passive joint properties experiment, the combination of stiffness and damping yields the fastest learning and highest final reward, significantly outperforming armature-only and single-property conditions (two-sided Mann-Whitney U tests with Holm-Bonferroni correction, *P < 0.05). The optimized muscle model correctly reproduces opposing signs for flexor and extensor moment arms across all seven DoFs, validating biological plausibility. Imitation learning of locomotor kinematics with the 15-MTU, 7-DoF model requires ~96 hours of PPO training (15×10⁶ steps at 500 Hz control / 10 kHz physics).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against external baselines; its primary comparison is an internal ablation over four passive joint property configurations (armature only, armature+damping, armature+damping+stiffness, armature+stiffness). The stiffness+damping condition dominates on both early (2.5–3M steps) and late (14.5–15M steps) reward metrics, with the advantage persisting to the end of training even though final kinematics are qualitatively indistinguishable across conditions. The model also implicitly compares against the prior NeuroMechFly framework (Lobato-Rios et al., 2022; Wang-Chen et al., 2024), which used simplified spring-damper or position/torque-based joint controllers; the present work replaces these with anatomically grounded Hill-type muscle actuation, enabling prediction of individual muscle contributions and synergy structure that were inaccessible in the prior models. No head-to-head quantitative comparison against other fly musculoskeletal models (e.g., Vaxenburg et al., 2025) is provided.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the NeuroMechFly whole-body biomechanical simulation framework (Lobato-Rios et al., 2022; Wang-Chen et al., 2024) by replacing its simplified spring-damper leg actuators with anatomically grounded Hill-type muscle-tendon units derived from high-resolution X-ray scans. While musculoskeletal simulation is well-established for humans, rodents, primates, and birds, prior Drosophila models (including Vaxenburg et al., 2025) relied on abstract position- or torque-based joint controllers that lack explicit muscle representations. The paper also integrates established tools from the imitation learning literature (PPO, DeepMimic-style reward shaping, OstrichRL-style observation spaces) and multiobjective optimization (NSGA-II) into a unified pipeline that links anatomical imaging data to muscle-actuated behavioral replay in both OpenSim and MuJoCo.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first biologically detailed musculoskeletal model of Drosophila legs, providing a critical bridge between the now-complete connectome and observable limb kinematics, enabling testable predictions about muscle synergies and neuromuscular coordination. Key limitations include the scarcity of direct physiological measurements (forces and velocities were estimated and optimized rather than measured), the omission of body-environment contacts during policy training, and the fact that only the front leg is fully modeled and validated. Open questions include whether the predicted task-specific synergy specializations hold under experimental EMG or calcium imaging, how passive joint dynamics interact with neural control across the full six-legged system, and whether the framework generalizes to other arthropod species.
