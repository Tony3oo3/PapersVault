## Problem

Existing benchmarks for generative search engines and deep research agents evaluate isolated pipeline components (retrieval, summarization) or rely on researcher-defined metrics, leaving no systematic way to audit end-to-end reliability of how these systems ground answers in sources, structure citations, and express confidence. Prior community-driven usability studies identified 16 recurring failure modes but lacked an automated, scalable framework to measure them across deployed systems.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes four extracted elements per query: the user query, the generated answer text with inline citation markers, the list of cited source URLs, and the full-text content of each source (scraped via Jina Reader). The answer text is decomposed into atomic statements, each labeled for query relevance and (on debate queries) pro/con/neutral stance. Outputs include two intermediate matrices—a citation matrix and a factual-support matrix, both (statements × sources) binary—plus eight scalar metric scores grouped into answer-text, sources, and citations categories, and a color-coded score card against predefined acceptable/borderline/problematic thresholds.

## Method

DeepTRACE operationalizes eight metrics derived from community-identified failure cases. Statement-level decomposition and LLM-judge scoring (validated at Pearson r = 0.72 for confidence, r = 0.62 for factual support against human annotators) populate the citation and factual-support matrices. Source Necessity is computed by casting the factual-support matrix as a bipartite (statement, source) graph and finding the minimum vertex cover over source nodes via the Hopcroft-Karp algorithm, quantifying how many listed sources are truly irreplaceable. Citation Accuracy and Citation Thoroughness are derived from element-wise overlap between the two matrices (numerator identical; denominators differ: total citations vs. total factual-support links). One-Sided and Overconfident Answer metrics combine pro/con statement counts with a Likert-scale confidence score, targeting debate queries specifically. The entire pipeline is automated via browser scripts for nine public GSE/DR configurations, enabling large-scale (2,727-sample) evaluation without manual annotation.

## Results

Across 9 public GSE and DR systems evaluated on 303 queries (2,727 total samples), GPT-5(DR) emerges as the strongest performer with 0% uncited sources, 12.5% unsupported statements, 87.5% source necessity, 79.1% citation accuracy, and 87.5% citation thoroughness. However, one-sidedness remains a persistent problem across all DR systems (54.7%–94.8%), and unsupported statement rates are severe for several engines: PPLX(DR) at 97.5%, Copilot(DR) at 90.2%, and YouChat(DR) at 74.6%. GSEs (You.com, BingChat, Perplexity) show 50–83% one-sidedness on debate queries and citation accuracy of only 40–68%, with Perplexity maintaining uniformly high confidence (>90% very confident) while producing the most one-sided responses. Overconfidence is consistently lower in DR modes (<20%) compared to GSE modes, but relevance drops sharply in DR (GPT-5(DR) at 87.5% vs. Gemini(DR) at just 12.4%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DeepTRACE does not benchmark against prior evaluation frameworks (FActScore, RAGAS, AutoSurvey, DeepResearch Bench, DRBench, BrowseComp-Plus) in a head-to-head manner; instead, it positions itself as a complementary end-to-end audit that captures citation structure, source necessity, and confidence calibration beyond isolated claim correctness. The primary comparison is across 9 public systems: GPT-5(DR) dominates on source and citation metrics (0% uncited, 12.5% unsupported, 87.5% source necessity, 79.1% citation accuracy) but still fails on one-sidedness (54.7%) and relevance (87.5%, borderline). YouChat(ARI) achieves 0% one-sidedness and 96.8% citation thoroughness but has only 37.2% relevant statements and 62.9% unsupported statements. PPLX(DR) and Copilot(TD) are the weakest, with 97.5% and 90.2% unsupported statements respectively, and citation accuracies of 58.0% and 62.1%. Among GSEs (Figure 2), Perplexity is worst on one-sidedness (>83%) and confidence calibration, while BingChat and You.com show somewhat better confidence hedging but still 40–68% citation accuracy. The key takeaway is that no system achieves acceptable performance on all eight metrics simultaneously, and listing more sources (e.g., YouChat(ARI) with 198.6 sources) does not improve grounding.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends the qualitative usability study of Narayanan Venkit et al. (2025, FAccT), which identified 16 failure cases in deep research agents through community-driven evaluation, by transforming those findings into 8 quantitative, automatable metrics. It departs from prior RAG evaluation frameworks (RAGAS, ClashEval) that treat models as isolated computational systems, and from factuality benchmarks (FActScore, CoRE) that focus on claim correctness in isolation, by auditing the full GSE/DR pipeline end-to-end—including citation structure, source necessity via bipartite graph matching, and confidence calibration. The sociotechnical grounding (metrics derived from user-identified weaknesses rather than researcher-defined criteria) distinguishes it from task-completion benchmarks like DeepResearch Bench, DRBench, and BrowseComp-Plus, which the authors note lack end-user input.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DeepTRACE provides the first community-grounded, end-to-end audit framework for publicly deployed GSE and DR systems, revealing that the 'deep research' branding does not guarantee balanced or well-grounded outputs (one-sidedness remains 54–95% across DR agents; unsupported statements reach 97.5% for PPLX-DR). The finding that listing more sources does not improve grounding (YouChat lists ~199 sources yet still has 63% unsupported statements) challenges a common design assumption and has direct implications for how these systems are built and marketed. Key limitations include moderate LLM-judge agreement (Pearson 0.62 for factual support), exclusion of answer correctness (only format, sourcing, and citations are evaluated), and a snapshot evaluation of 9 systems on 303 queries, leaving open whether findings generalize as models evolve. Future work could extend to multimodal outputs, UI-level interactions, and dynamic re-evaluation as systems update.
