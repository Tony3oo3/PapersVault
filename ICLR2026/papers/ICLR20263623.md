## Problem

Inferring multiple causally-related latent variables from heterogeneous observations that vary in spatial resolution, temporal frequency, and noise characteristics remains unsolved. Existing multi-modal fusion methods assume uniform data quality or complete observability, while standard score-based diffusion and variational inference treat variables independently, failing to exploit known causal dependencies for cross-variable information propagation. No prior framework simultaneously respects causal graph structure, preserves native observation resolutions, and provides theoretical guarantees for cascading approximation errors.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a set of multi-resolution observations (e.g., InSAR, optical imagery, geophysical measurements at 30 m to 5 km resolution) each associated with specific latent parent variables via observation maps, plus a known causal DAG encoding mechanistic dependencies (e.g., earthquake → landslide → building damage). It produces a full posterior distribution over all latent variables via reverse SDE sampling, from which hazard probability maps (evaluated by AUROC) or spatiotemporal binary predictions (evaluated by F1/AP) are derived. Critically, observations are consumed at their native resolutions without downsampling to a common grid, and the causal DAG need only be partially known—moderate edge errors cause only 6–20% degradation.

## Method

SVGDM embeds score-based diffusion within a causal graphical model, where the forward SDE naturally encodes scale-dependent sensing noise and the reverse SDE performs posterior inference. The core technical contribution is a causal score decomposition (Theorem 2) that, via the causal Markov property, approximates the global score ∇_{z_i} log p_t(z_{1:N}) as a local score ∇_{z_i} log p_t(z_i, z_{P(i)}) depending only on the node and its causal parents. This local score is further split (Proposition 1) into a marginal term trained by denoising score matching and a causal consistency term modeled with a locally Gaussian conditional whose parameters are trained on forward samples using Tweedie's formula for the posterior mean. The variational objective combines the DSM loss, causal model loss, and an ELBO with flow-based entropy estimation via Hutchinson's trace estimator, and inference uses a predictor-corrector reverse SDE sampler with DDIM-style discretization. A cascade error analysis (Theorem 4) bounds the total posterior score error as a linear combination of five individual approximation errors plus a cross-term, establishing convergence to the true posterior as all errors vanish.

## Results

On the 2020 Puerto Rico earthquake, SVGDM achieves AUROC of 0.9331 (landslides), 0.9317 (liquefaction), and 0.9512 (building damage), outperforming the strongest domain-specific baselines (DisasterNet: 0.9293/0.9284/0.9413) and generic VI methods (BBVI: 0.7912/0.7731/0.7423) by 14–21%. On the 2021 Haiti earthquake, AUROC reaches 0.9550 (landslides) and 0.9587 (building damage). For wildfire spread prediction on WildfireSpreadTS, SVGDM achieves F1 = 0.5913 and AP = 0.4430, surpassing U-Net (F1 = 0.341), ConvLSTM (F1 = 0.310), and UTAE (F1 = 0.350). On synthetic multi-resolution benchmarks, SVGDM achieves NRMSE of 0.0683–0.1239 and MAPE of 5.26–9.91% under varying-resolution full observation, with systematic degradation to 0.1814 NRMSE under low-resolution partial observation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SVGDM outperforms all 12 baselines across every evaluated task. Against the closest domain-specific competitors, DisasterNet (normalizing-flow causal BN) and VCBI (causal Bayesian inference from satellite imagery), SVGDM gains 0.4–3.9 AUROC points on earthquake hazards and 2.5–5.4 NRMSE points on synthetic data, confirming that score-based diffusion adds value beyond normalizing-flow-based causal modeling. Against generic variational inference baselines (BBVI, SIVI, ADVI, NUTS), SVGDM achieves 14–21% higher AUROC, with the gap largest for liquefaction (0.9317 vs. 0.6846–0.7846), where causal propagation from well-observed shaking to poorly-observed ground failure is most critical. Against multi-view VAEs (JMVAE, MMVAE, MoPoE-VAE) that collapse all observations into a single shared latent, SVGDM reduces NRMSE by 3.3–4.1× (e.g., 0.0683 vs. 0.2241–0.2479 for z_1), demonstrating that treating causally linked physical processes as 'views' of one object is fundamentally inadequate. On wildfire spread prediction, SVGDM's F1 of 0.5913 exceeds U-Net (0.341), ConvLSTM (0.310), UTAE (0.350), and logistic regression (0.432) by 37–90%, validating the temporal extension of the causal score framework.

## Relation to Prior Work

SVGDM sits at the intersection of three research threads: score-based diffusion models (Song & Ermon, 2019; Song et al., 2020b), probabilistic graphical models with causal DAG structure (Koller & Friedman, 2009), and variational inference for intractable posteriors (Blei et al., 2017). Its most direct technical ancestor is Rozet & Louppe (2023), whose pseudo-blanket score decomposition for Markov chains is extended here to causal graphical settings via Theorem 2. The paper departs from prior multi-modal fusion work (which assumes uniform resolution or complete observability) and from domain-specific causal Bayesian networks for disaster assessment (VBCI, DisasterNet) by replacing normalizing-flow conditionals with diffusion-based score functions that natively model scale-dependent sensing noise, and by providing a formal cascade error analysis absent in prior causal inference frameworks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a practically important gap: jointly inferring multiple causally-linked physical processes from observations that differ in spatial resolution, temporal frequency, and noise characteristics—conditions ubiquitous in remote sensing, climate, and hazard monitoring. Key limitations include the assumption of a known (or partially known) causal DAG, the locally Gaussian approximation for the causal consistency term, and computational scaling with the number of causal edges. Open questions the authors identify include joint structure discovery when the graph is unknown, extensions to time-varying causal relationships, and whether the locally Gaussian approximation can be replaced by more flexible families without sacrificing the theoretical error bounds.
