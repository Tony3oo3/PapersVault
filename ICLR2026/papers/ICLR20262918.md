## Problem

As transformer context lengths grow, attention scores collapse toward uniformity (rank-collapse), causing tokens to cluster excessively. While practical methods like YaRN, Qwen, SSMax, and SWAN-GPT rescale attention scores by a polylogarithmic factor β_n, no rigorous theoretical justification existed for why logarithmic scaling is the correct order. This paper asks: what is the optimal order of magnitude of β_n, and can it be derived from first principles?

## Input / Output

The model takes n token embeddings x_i ∈ R^d, normalizes them to the unit sphere (y_i = N(x_i)), and applies a rescaled self-attention layer with K=Q=V=I_d and scaling β = γ log n, plus a residual connection with coefficient α ≥ 0. The primary outputs are the updated token representations x_i' and a characterization of which phase regime (subcritical, critical, or supercritical) the system occupies. A secondary output is the normalized Jacobian norm (1/nd)‖∇_X X'‖², which quantifies gradient flow through the attention block. The analysis requires d ≥ n under the strict simplex assumption but extends to d ≪ n under the relaxed almost-simplex assumption.

## Method

The paper establishes a phase transition for the contractive properties of attention when β_n = γ log n, with the critical threshold at γ = 1/(1−ρ) where ρ is the pairwise inner product between normalized tokens. In the subcritical regime (γ < 1/(1−ρ)), attention weights become asymptotically uniform, causing tokens to contract toward a single direction; in the supercritical regime (γ > 1/(1−ρ)), the diagonal weight dominates and attention reduces to the identity map. The proof proceeds by analyzing the partition function Z_i = Σ_k e^{β⟨y_i,y_k⟩} and showing its leading-order term switches from the off-diagonal sum to the diagonal term at the critical γ. The result is proven under both a strict regular-simplex assumption (Assumption 1, requiring d ≥ n) and a relaxed almost-simplex assumption (Assumption 2, permitting d ≪ n), with the critical scaling remaining Θ(log n) in both cases. A parallel analysis of the backward pass shows that the normalized Jacobian norm vanishes in the subcritical regime and stabilizes at (1−1/d)/q in the supercritical regime, confirming that gradient flow is also governed by the same phase transition. The key geometric insight distinguishing this from the random-energy-model prediction of β_n ∝ √(log n) is that in realistic attention, the gaps between top-ordered scores remain O(1) due to content-adaptive structure, rather than being governed by extreme-value statistics of i.i.d. Gaussian scores.

## Results

The paper establishes that the critical attention scaling is β_n ∝ log n, with the phase transition occurring at γ = 1/(1−ρ) where ρ is the pairwise inner product between normalized tokens. In the subcritical regime (γ < 1/(1−ρ)), token inner products converge to 1 (without residual connections) or ρ(α√q+1)²/(α²q + 2α√qρ + ρ) (with residual), indicating full rank collapse; in the supercritical regime (γ > 1/(1−ρ)), attention reduces to the identity map and inner products remain unchanged at ρ. For gradients, the normalized Jacobian norm (1/nd)‖∇_X X′‖² vanishes to 0 in the subcritical regime, equals (1−1/d)/(4q) at the critical point, and equals (1−1/d)/q in the supercritical regime. Numerical experiments across d = 2, 32, 512 confirm a sharp phase transition near γ = 1/(1−ρ) in the large-d limit, with an intermediate regime emerging in low dimensions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without direct method-vs-method benchmark comparisons; the 'baselines' are alternative theoretical predictions and practical scaling choices. The key contrast is with Giorlandino & Goldt (2025), who identify a phase transition at β_n ∝ √log n using a random-energy-model analogy with i.i.d. Gaussian attention scores. The present paper argues this discrepancy arises from fundamentally different modeling assumptions: their model treats token similarity as random (analogous to Kuramoto models on dense random graphs), whereas the present model captures content-adaptive interactions where score gaps remain O(1), yielding the log n scaling. The paper's log n prediction directly validates the scaling choices in Qwen, SSMax, and SWAN-GPT, while YaRN's (log n)² scaling is identified as supercritical (excessive), potentially suppressing meaningful token interactions. The authors note that combining their critical-regime analysis with the broader subcritical framework of Bruno et al. (2025b) is a promising direction for future work.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the simplified attention model (K=Q=V=I_d with pre-layer normalization) introduced by Geshkovski et al. (2025) and the symmetric simplex configuration from Cowsik et al. (2024), extending both to characterize a phase transition in the scaling parameter β_n. It departs sharply from Giorlandino & Goldt (2025), who used a random-energy-model / replica-method approach with i.i.d. Gaussian attention scores to predict a critical scaling of β_n ~ √(log n); the present work argues that content-adaptive scores with O(1) gaps between top-order statistics yield the log n scaling observed in practice (YaRN, Qwen, SSMax, SWAN-GPT). The gradient analysis in Section 2.3 connects to the broader signal-propagation literature (Dong et al. 2021; Noci et al. 2022; Cowsik et al. 2024) but is the first to show a sharp phase transition in the backward pass under attention scaling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first rigorous theoretical justification for the polylogarithmic attention scaling used in production long-context models, identifying a clean three-regime phase transition (collapse / sparse adaptive attention / identity) that explains why log n is the right order. The gradient-collapse result in the subcritical regime provides a training-stability argument beyond the forward-pass rank-collapse narrative. Key open questions include: whether the critical regime can be fully characterized by combining the tools of Bruno et al. (2025b) with the present framework, whether the log n law persists under more realistic score distributions (e.g., heavy-tailed or structured), and how the analysis extends to multi-head attention with learned K, Q, V matrices rather than the identity simplification.
