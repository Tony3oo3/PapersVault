## Problem

LLM-based global optimization methods struggle in high-dimensional or complex search spaces because prompting an LLM to sample globally across the entire domain produces sparse, biased, and uninformative candidates. Existing approaches rely heavily on carefully engineered domain-specific prompts, and even in simple 2D settings, LLMs exhibit significant sampling bias when asked to cover a large space uniformly. This paper addresses the gap by asking whether adaptive spatial partitioning can make LLM-driven candidate generation more reliable without requiring domain-specific priors.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a blackbox objective function, the search space boundaries (axis-aligned hyperrectangle), an initial set of n₀=5 random evaluations, and an LLM (default: Gemini-2.0-Flash). At each iteration, the LLM receives a structured prompt containing the full evaluation history as in-context examples, the numeric boundaries of a specific subregion, and instructions to propose k candidate points with predicted function values. The LLM is deliberately given only minimal task information (dimensionality, variable names, partition bounds) without dataset statistics or problem descriptions to avoid prompt contamination. Output is the top-b candidates ranked by LLM-predicted scores, which are then evaluated on the true function.

## Method

HOLLM iteratively performs five steps: (1) Partition — build a KD-tree on all accumulated evaluations, re-fitted each round so leaves form axis-aligned hyperrectangular subregions whose granularity adapts to data density; (2) Score — assign each leaf a composite score B = μ̄ + αₜ(β₁V̄ + β₂Ē) combining exploitation (empirical maximum improvement), geometric exploration (d-th root of hypervolume), and statistical exploration (UCB-V variance-aware bonus), with αₜ annealed via cosine schedule; (3) Select — stochastically sample M distinct leaves without replacement proportional to their scores; (4) Sample — prompt the LLM to generate k candidates and predicted values within each selected subregion; (5) Evaluate — pick the top-b proposals by predicted score and query the true function. The key insight is that constraining the LLM to propose points within a small, well-defined subregion (rather than the full high-dimensional space) dramatically reduces sampling difficulty, while the bandit-inspired scoring with UCB-V provides variance-aware exploration that is robust to noisy observations. The KD-tree is interpreted as a data-driven discretizer in an infinite-armed bandit framework, where each leaf is a 'meta-arm' and the entire tree is re-fitted every round to avoid premature convergence.

## Results

On the synthetic benchmarks (Table 4, 100 iterations, 5 runs), HOLLM achieves near-optimal values on all three hardest functions: Rosenbrock-8D (−7.03 ± 0.01), Rastrigin-10D (−0.17 ± 0.04), and Ackley-20D (−0.00 ± 0.00), substantially outperforming the global LLM baseline (−4.66, −32.08, −6.10 respectively) and all non-LLM methods. On the FCNet hyperparameter optimization tasks (4 datasets, 50 trials), HOLLM and the global LLM both outperform BORE and CQR, with a single run costing ~$0.14 in API calls over ~330 s. On the three real-world engineering tasks (Penicillin Production, Vehicle Safety, Car Side Impact), HOLLM attains the highest average rank in the Critical Difference Diagram, and under 1% Gaussian observation noise it reaches a significantly higher objective value than all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HOLLM decisively outperforms the global LLM baseline (same prompt, no partitioning) on every benchmark: on Rastrigin-10D the gap is −0.17 vs. −32.08, and on Ackley-20D HOLLM reaches the exact global optimum (−0.00) while the global LLM plateaus at −6.10. Among non-LLM methods, HEBO is the closest competitor (−7.22 on Rosenbrock-8D, −49.63 on Rastrigin-10D) but still trails HOLLM by a wide margin; TuRBO and LA-MCTS (both space-partitioning methods) are far behind, with TuRBO at −161.61 on Rosenbrock-8D and LA-MCTS(CMA-ES) at −30.56. Classical BO variants (GP-EI, BORE, CQR) and evolutionary/random baselines (RE, RS, TPE, SMAC, CMA-ES) all cluster near random-search performance on the high-dimensional Ackley-20D (≈ −20), whereas HOLLM finds the global minimum in under 50 evaluations. On the FCNet hyperparameter tasks, HOLLM and the global LLM both beat BORE and CQR, though the partitioning advantage is smaller due to the discrete search space; on the three real-world engineering tasks HOLLM achieves the best average rank, and under 1% Gaussian noise it significantly outperforms TPE, Random Search, and the global LLM. Ablations confirm that the UCB-V scoring function is critical: replacing it with UCB1, using only exploitation, only exploration, or uniform region selection all degrade performance, and swapping the LLM sampler for RS or a local GP-EI surrogate within the same KD-tree partitions also yields substantially worse results.

## Relation to Prior Work

HOLLM sits at the intersection of two research threads: hierarchical space-partitioning bandit algorithms (HOO, X-armed bandits, UCB-V) and the emerging use of LLMs as surrogate models or candidate generators in blackbox optimization. The key architectural departure is replacing the uniform-within-region sampling of classical bandit methods with LLM-guided candidate generation, while simultaneously constraining the LLM to small KD-tree subregions rather than prompting it to sample globally. This addresses a documented failure mode of prior LLM-based optimizers (Liu et al. 2024, Yang et al. 2024), which rely on carefully engineered domain-specific prompts and scatter sparsely in higher dimensions. The scoring function synthesizes exploitation (empirical max), geometric exploration (hypervolume), and statistical exploration (UCB-V) into a single composite, drawing on TuRBO's trust-region philosophy and LA-MCTS's learned partitioning but without requiring a parametric surrogate.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a simple structural change—partitioning the search space before querying the LLM—substantially improves LLM-based optimization across synthetic, hyperparameter, and real-world benchmarks, suggesting that LLMs' meta-learned priors about function behavior are most useful when the sampling problem is localized. This opens a practical path for using LLMs in expensive blackbox optimization without requiring domain-specific prompt engineering. Key limitations include the absence of theoretical regret bounds, dependence on LLM quality and API cost, and the fact that gains are less pronounced on discrete search spaces. Open questions include whether the approach scales to very high dimensions (>50), how it extends to multi-objective settings (a follow-up paper is cited), and whether the KD-tree partitioning can be replaced by learned or adaptive geometries.
