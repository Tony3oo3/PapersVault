## Problem

Most stochastic adversarial defenses (randomized transformations, smoothing, diffusion purification) still exhibit gradient consensus—aligned gradient directions across their random transformations—allowing adaptive attackers to aggregate gradients via EoT or BPDA and construct transferable perturbations. The paper identifies this consensus as the root cause of defense collapse and asks whether explicitly destroying gradient alignment, rather than masking it, can yield robustness that survives adaptive white-box attacks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262930/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

DRIFT takes standard RGB images (ImageNet, 224×224) and produces class labels via a frozen pretrained classifier (ResNet-v2-50, Inception-v3, DeiT-S, or ViT-B/16). At inference, one filter from a trained ensemble of four lightweight residual conv blocks (3→16→3 channels with skip connection) is randomly sampled and applied as a dimension-preserving front-end, adding only ~0.4 ms latency and 0.03 GB memory. The base model is never modified or retrained; only the small filter ensemble is learned.

## Method

DRIFT trains an ensemble of differentiable, learnable filters to actively maximize gradient divergence across members while preserving clean predictions. The training objective combines four terms: (i) cross-entropy across all filters to maintain accuracy, (ii) a Jacobian separation loss penalizing squared cosine similarity of Hutchinson-probed VJPs between filter pairs, (iii) a logit-VJP separation loss penalizing alignment of decision-space gradients (including against the identity path), and (iv) adversarial training using PGD perturbations crafted on the base model alone. Theoretically, the paper formalizes gradient consensus Γ as squared cosine similarity of per-filter loss gradients and proves (Lemma 3.4, Theorem 3.5) that bounding expected consensus ρ directly upper-bounds transfer success probability as O(εGρ). This makes DRIFT the first differentiable, adversarially trained filter-ensemble defense that enforces gradient divergence without retraining the backbone, achieving strong robustness under BPDA+EoT adaptive attacks at negligible runtime cost.

## Results

On ImageNet with ε=4/255 (ℓ∞), DRIFT achieves 74.30% robust accuracy under AutoAttack on ResNet-v2 (vs. 67.01% for DiffPure and 14.29% for JPEG), while preserving 84.66% clean accuracy. Across all four backbones (ResNet-v2, Inception-v3, DeiT-S, ViT-B/16), DRIFT consistently leads on every attack tested. Under the strongest adaptive threat model (BPDA+EOT PGD, 40 steps), DRIFT sustains 60.19% on ResNet-v2 and 64.17% on ViT-B/16, whereas JPEG and BaRT collapse to 0–11% and adversarial training variants drop below 25%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DRIFT outperforms all eight baselines on every model and attack configuration. Against JPEG compression (Dziugaite et al., 2016), DRIFT gains 60+ points on AutoAttack for ResNet-v2 (74.30% vs. 14.29%) and 75+ points for Inception-v3 (76.50% vs. 1.23%), while also preserving clean accuracy (84.66% vs. 62.96%). Against BaRT (Raff et al., 2019), DRIFT improves AutoAttack robustness by 61–51 points across models (e.g., 74.30% vs. 12.70% on ResNet-v2). Against DiffPure (Nie et al., 2022), DRIFT leads by 7.3 points on ResNet-v2 AA (74.30% vs. 67.01%) and 13.0 points on DeiT-S (76.24% vs. 63.21%), and is the only method evaluated on Inception-v3 and ViT-B/16 where DiffPure was infeasible. Under adaptive BPDA+EOT attacks (Table 2), DRIFT achieves 58.73–61.23% AA robustness across models, while AT (Madry et al., 2018) drops to 3.12%, FFR+AT (Lukasik et al., 2023) to 13.24%, and ANF+AT (Suresh et al., 2025) to 24.63% on ResNet-v2. Against randomized smoothing baselines (Table 3), DRIFT exceeds SmoothAdv RS (Salman et al., 2019) by +9.1 to +19.5 points on ResNet-50 and CAF (Deng et al., 2024) by up to +13.4 points on ViT-B/16 at ℓ2 radii 0.5–3.0, though these are empirical rather than certified guarantees.

## Relation to Prior Work

DRIFT carves new ground at the intersection of randomized ensemble defenses (BaRT, SIT) and adversarial training, but departs from both in a key way: rather than applying hand-designed random transformations or retraining the backbone, it trains a small ensemble of differentiable residual filters whose sole purpose is to maximize gradient divergence across ensemble members. The theoretical contribution formalizes 'gradient consensus' as the mechanism enabling adversarial transferability, providing a principled explanation for why prior stochastic defenses (which preserve coherent surrogate gradients) remain exploitable under EOT/BPDA. Unlike diffusion-based purification (DiffPure), DRIFT avoids heavy generative modeling; unlike randomized smoothing, it provides no certified guarantees but instead targets empirical robustness through an explicit divergence objective (Jacobian and logit-VJP separation losses).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The gradient-consensus-to-transferability link offers a unifying diagnostic for why stochastic defenses fail, potentially guiding future defense design beyond DRIFT itself. Practically, the 0.4 ms / 0.03 GB inference cost (versus 5.5–17 s / ~7 GB for DiffPure) makes adaptive-robust preprocessing feasible in real-time, large-scale deployments where diffusion-based purification is intractable. Key limitations remain: robustness is empirical rather than certified, adaptive attacks still degrade accuracy by 20–35 points from clean, and the theoretical bounds (Lemma 3.4, Theorem 3.5) are loose and rely on margin assumptions that may not hold uniformly. Open questions include whether the approach extends to video or multimodal settings, whether larger filter ensembles or deeper filter architectures yield further gains, and whether the divergence objective can be combined with certified smoothing for hybrid guarantees.
