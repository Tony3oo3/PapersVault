## Problem

Arabic remains severely underrepresented in medical NLP benchmarks, with existing resources either English-centric or too small and lacking expert annotation, difficulty mapping, and specialty coverage to properly assess LLMs for Arabic medical reasoning. This gap hinders the evaluation and advancement of multilingual LLM capabilities for deployment in Arabic-speaking clinical settings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260461/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes Arabic medical MCQs in Modern Standard Arabic with 4 or 5 answer choices (A–D or A–E) as input and produces a single answer letter as output. Each question is annotated with one of 19 medical specialties, a difficulty level (Y1–Y5 corresponding to years of medical study), and answer format. Models are prompted at temperature 0 with a fixed Arabic instruction template, and answers are extracted via pattern matching on the model's text response.

## Method

The dataset was constructed by digitizing scanned paper-based exams from regional medical school repositories, then applying strict manual filtering by five NLP researchers (removing ~29% of 34,333 initial MCQs) to yield 24,883 samples split 80/20 into training and test sets via stratified random sampling by specialty. Quality was validated through dual board-certified clinician review of 378 questions (sampled via Cochran's formula, double-blinded on Qualtrics) and LLM-as-a-judge evaluation using four SOTA models across the full test set. For benchmarking, 16 proprietary and open-source LLMs were evaluated zero-shot; additionally, few-shot learning (3 expert-rated exemplars) and QLoRA fine-tuning (4-bit precision, LoRA adapters on q/k/v/o_proj attention modules, 800 training steps) were applied to LLaMA-3.1-8B-instruct to assess the dataset's viability for parameter-efficient domain adaptation.

## Results

On the MedAraBench test set (4,989 Arabic medical MCQs), GPT-o3 achieves the highest overall accuracy at 0.765, closely followed by GPT-5 at 0.764. Among open-source models, DeepSeek-chat-v3-0324 (0.620) and Qwen-plus (0.618) lead, while medical-specific models such as Apollo-7B (0.238) and Med42-8B (0.318) perform worst. QLoRA fine-tuning on Llama-3.1-8B-instruct boosts accuracy from 0.170 (zero-shot) to 0.320, an 88.2% relative improvement, demonstrating the dataset's viability for parameter-efficient adaptation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All five proprietary general-purpose models (GPT-o3 0.765, GPT-5 0.764, Claude-Sonnet-4 0.694, GPT-4.1 0.673, Gemini-2.0-Flash 0.654) substantially outperform every open-source model, with the top proprietary models exceeding the best open-source model (DeepSeek-chat-v3 at 0.620) by over 10 percentage points. Among open-source general-purpose models, DeepSeek-chat-v3 (0.620) and Qwen-plus (0.618) lead, while the much larger Llama-3.3-70B (0.547) underperforms both, and Llama-3.1-8B (0.170) is the weakest overall. Arabic-centric models (Fanar-C-1-8.7B at 0.498, Allam-7B at 0.447, C4AI-Command-R7B at 0.381) fall below the general-purpose open-source models, indicating that Arabic specialization alone does not compensate for weaker general reasoning. Medical-specific models perform worst: MedGemma-4B-it (0.390) and BiMedix-Bi (0.390) are mid-pack, while Med42-8B (0.318) and Apollo-7B (0.238) score below even the smallest general-purpose model. Notably, QLoRA fine-tuning on Llama-3.1-8B closes part of the gap (0.170→0.320), but the fine-tuned model still trails all proprietary and larger open-source baselines, underscoring that domain-specific data alone cannot replicate the capabilities of large-scale proprietary training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the medical MCQ benchmarking lineage (MedQA, MedMCQA, MMLU/USMLE) to Arabic, a language with over 400 million speakers that remains severely underrepresented in clinical NLP. It builds directly on the authors' own prior work, MedArabiQ (Abu Daoud et al., 2025), by scaling from 700 to ~25,000 questions and adding expert annotation, difficulty-level mapping, and broader specialty coverage. Methodologically, it repurposes established evaluation frameworks—Cochran's formula for sample sizing, LLM-as-a-judge for quality assessment, and QLoRA for parameter-efficient adaptation—within the Arabic medical domain, where no prior work had combined all these elements at this scale.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MedAraBench provides the first large-scale, expert-validated Arabic medical benchmark with standardized train/test splits, enabling reproducible evaluation of LLMs for clinical deployment in Arabic-speaking regions. The finding that even top proprietary models (GPT-5, GPT-o3) plateau at ~76% accuracy, and that QLoRA fine-tuning yields an 88% relative improvement over few-shot on Llama-3.1-8B, underscores that domain-specific training data—not just scale or in-context prompting—is critical for low-resource medical NLP. Key open questions include whether high MCQ accuracy reflects genuine clinical reasoning or statistical pattern-matching, how to evaluate model rationales rather than just answer correctness, and how to extend the benchmark to dialectal Arabic, image-based modalities, and higher difficulty levels where current data is scarce.
