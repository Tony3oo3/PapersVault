## Problem

Prior large-scale blind evaluations found that LLM-generated research ideas scored significantly higher than human-expert ideas on novelty, excitement, and expected effectiveness, but whether these ideas actually translate into better research outcomes after execution remained untested. No prior work had conducted a statistically powered execution study comparing LLM versus human ideas, as execution is resource-intensive and earlier efforts verified only one or two ideas. This paper asks whether the apparent superiority of LLM ideas at the ideation stage persists once the ideas are actually implemented and evaluated on empirical results.

## Input / Output

Each of 43 expert researchers receives an anonymized NLP research idea (spanning 7 topics) randomly assigned from either a human expert or Claude-3.5-Sonnet, with a 3-month window to produce a full reproducible codebase and a 4-page paper in a standardized format. Fifty-eight blind reviewers then score each executed project on novelty, excitement, soundness, effectiveness, and overall quality (1–10 scale), plus control metrics on execution faithfulness and codebase quality. The key analytical output is the per-idea difference between pre-execution ideation scores (from the prior Si et al. 2025 study) and post-execution review scores, enabling direct measurement of the ideation-execution gap for each condition.

## Method

The study is a pre-registered randomized controlled trial in which 43 qualified researchers (average 15 papers, 103 hours of execution) are randomly assigned to execute either a human or an AI idea under identical instructions, with all modifications restricted to experiment details (datasets, baselines, metrics, prompts) rather than the core proposed method. After execution, 58 expert reviewers (average 597 citations) blindly review each project's paper and codebase, with 4–5 independent reviews per project. The core analytical contribution is comparing the ideation-execution gap (execution score minus ideation score) between conditions, which controls for heterogeneity in idea quality and reveals that AI ideas drop significantly more than human ideas on all four shared metrics (novelty, excitement, effectiveness, overall; all p < 0.05 with FDR correction), closing and even reversing the ranking observed at ideation. Qualitative analysis of reviewer rationales shows that execution evaluation engages substantially more factors—empirical performance, experimental rigor, baseline comparisons, ablations, and resource requirements—than ideation evaluation, which frequently conditions scores on the assumption that the method would work. This demonstrates that the apparent superiority of LLM ideas at ideation is largely an artifact of evaluating ideas in the absence of execution outcomes.

## Results

In the ideation evaluation (pre-execution), LLM-generated ideas score significantly higher than human ideas on all four metrics (e.g., novelty 5.78 vs. 4.91, excitement 5.65 vs. 4.40, effectiveness 6.00 vs. 4.83, overall 5.38 vs. 4.60; all p < 0.05). After execution, LLM ideas' scores drop dramatically (novelty −1.05, excitement −1.76, effectiveness −1.88, overall −1.98 on a 1–10 scale), while human ideas barely change (novelty −0.01, excitement +0.08, effectiveness −0.05, overall −0.63). The difference in score drops between AI and human ideas is statistically significant on all four metrics (p < 0.05 with FDR correction), and the ranking flips: AI ideas score lower than human ideas post-execution on excitement (3.90 vs. 4.48) and effectiveness (4.13 vs. 4.78), though these post-execution differences are not individually significant at N=43.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between LLM-generated ideas (Claude-3.5-Sonnet) and human expert ideas, each executed by 43 qualified researchers under identical conditions and then blindly reviewed by 58 expert NLP reviewers. Before execution, LLM ideas are judged significantly more novel, exciting, and effective than human ideas (all p < 0.05), consistent with the prior ideation study (Si et al., 2025). After execution, however, LLM ideas lose their advantage: the ideation-execution gap for AI ideas (≈1.0–2.0 points drop) is 10–25× larger than for human ideas (≈0–0.6 points drop), and this difference is statistically significant on every metric. Control metrics (faithfulness to the original idea and codebase quality) show no significant difference between conditions, ruling out execution quality as an explanation. The authors further show that removing the 6 AI ideas where proposed human evaluations were replaced by LLM-as-a-judge does not alter the conclusions, and that reviewer comments in the execution evaluation surface additional weaknesses (missing baselines, poor experiment design, high compute cost) that were invisible during ideation-only review.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Si et al. (2025), the first large-scale human study comparing LLM-generated versus human research ideas at the ideation stage, which found LLM ideas scored significantly higher on novelty, excitement, and expected effectiveness. While prior AI-Scientist systems (Lu et al., 2024; Gottweis et al., 2025; Ghareeb et al., 2025) have verified only one or two generated ideas through execution, making statistical conclusions impossible, this work is the first to conduct a large-scale, pre-registered RCT execution study (N=43) with blind expert review. It departs from the broader idea-evaluation literature (which relies on LLM judges or small-scale human ratings of ideas in isolation) by grounding evaluation in actual experimental outcomes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that LLM ideas' apparent superiority at ideation evaporates (and even reverses) after execution challenges a core assumption in the AI-for-science pipeline: that high ideation-stage scores predict good research outcomes. This has direct implications for how the field should evaluate and select AI-generated ideas, suggesting that execution-based or proxy-execution metrics are necessary. Key limitations include the narrow scope (NLP prompting techniques, single frontier LLM, N=43), the fact that the direct human-vs-AI execution score difference is not statistically significant, and the reliance on human executors rather than automated agents. Open questions include whether the gap persists for more complex research ideas, across domains, and with future LLM generations, and whether proxy reward models or execution feedback loops can close the evaluation gap at scale.
