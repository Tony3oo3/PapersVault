## Problem

Existing adversarial training defenses for LLMs fail on newly developed jailbreaks because they train on perturbations that are computationally convenient rather than structurally representative of real attacks. The paper asks whether unseen jailbreaks can be explained as recompositions of a finite set of previously observed adversarial skills, and whether training on diverse compositions of those skills can yield robustness to attacks never seen during training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263557/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The training pipeline takes harmful base queries (from PKU-SafeRLHF, HEx-PHI, AdvBench, HarmBench) and composes them with k∈{1,…,5} adversarial skill primitives drawn from the learned Jailbreak Dictionary, using an auxiliary LLM to rewrite the query while coherently integrating the chosen primitives. The fine-tuned model then takes a single-turn text prompt as input and produces a safe refusal or helpful response as output. The full training set of 40,526 examples also includes benign query-response pairs (Orca-AgentInstruct) and over-refusal calibration queries (XSTest, WildJailbreak) to maintain general utility and balanced refusal behavior.

## Method

The method has two stages. First, an automated pipeline extracts 16,901 adversarial skills from 1,494 original-mutated prompt pairs across 32 jailbreak papers using GPT-4.1, embeds them with text-embedding-3-large (d=3072), and compresses them via K-SVD dictionary learning into a compact Jailbreak Dictionary of ~397 interpretable primitives, with LLM-augmented basis pursuit providing human-readable names and post-hoc redundancy filtering. Second, Adversarial Skill Compositional Training (ASCoT) generates diverse adversarial training data by sampling unique combinations of k primitives and prompting DeepSeek-V3-Chat to rewrite harmful base queries while integrating those primitives, then fine-tunes LLaMA-3.1-8B, Zephyr-7B, or Mistral-7B via standard supervised instruction tuning with LoRA (r=16, α=32). The key insight is that robustness emerges from spanning the adversarial skill space through diverse compositions rather than memorizing specific attack instances, and that expanding skill coverage (not just data scale) is the primary lever for generalization to unseen attacks.

## Results

On LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct, ASCoT (closed) achieves a mean StrongReject harmfulness score of 0.05 across all 10 attack families (seen and unseen), compared to 0.38 for the undefended model and 0.06–0.20 for prior defenses, while maintaining a competitive over-refusal rate of 0.12 on XSTest. The open-source variant (ASCoT open, using Qwen3 for skill extraction) achieves 0.07 mean harmfulness with the lowest ORR (0.06) among all ASCoT configurations. Against closed-source reasoning models, ASCoT (open) on an 8B model matches Claude Sonnet-4-Thinking (0.08 mean harmful) and substantially outperforms o4-mini (0.19), demonstrating that adversarial skill coverage—not model scale—drives robustness.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ASCoT outperforms all training-based baselines across all three model families. Against WildJailbreak (Jiang et al., 2024), ASCoT (closed) achieves 0.05 vs. 0.06 mean harmfulness on LLaMA-3.1-8B, and the gap widens substantially on Zephyr-7B (0.05 vs. 0.23) and Mistral-7B (0.11 vs. 0.22), indicating WildJailbreak's in-the-wild data covers less of the adversarial skill manifold. Against CAT* (Xhonneux et al., 2024, re-implemented at 40k scale), ASCoT (closed) achieves 0.05 vs. 0.13 on LLaMA-3.1-8B and 0.05 vs. 0.10 on Zephyr-7B, with CAT* also suffering high over-refusal (0.47 on Zephyr-7B). Against LAT* (Sheshadri et al., 2024, re-implemented), ASCoT (closed) achieves 0.05 vs. 0.07 on LLaMA-3.1-8B and 0.11 vs. 0.26 on Mistral-7B, showing that latent-space perturbations transfer poorly to unseen semantic jailbreaks. Against the closed-source reasoning model o4-mini (OpenAI, 2025), ASCoT (open) on an 8B model achieves 0.08 mean harmfulness versus o4-mini's 0.19, a 2.4× improvement. Against Claude Sonnet-4-Thinking (Anthropic, 2025), ASCoT (open) matches its 0.08 mean harmfulness score while incurring a slightly higher ORR (0.06 vs. 0.04), suggesting that skill-coverage-based training can rival much larger proprietary models on jailbreak robustness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant adversarial-training paradigm (CAT, LAT, R2D2), which seeks worst-case perturbations in embedding or latent space, by instead treating jailbreaks as compositions of a finite set of transferable 'adversarial skill primitives.' It repurposes classical dictionary-learning tools (K-SVD, basis-pursuit denoising) from signal processing to compress 16,901 raw LLM-extracted skills into a compact 397-atom Jailbreak Dictionary, and draws on the compositional-generalization literature (Skill-Mix, Instruct-SkillMix) to motivate training on novel recombos of those primitives rather than on isolated attack instances. The Adversarial Déjà Vu hypothesis extends prior qualitative taxonomies of jailbreak tactics (e.g., Li et al., 2024) into a quantitative, temporal-cutoff framework that measures how well a growing dictionary explains post-cutoff attacks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reframes jailbreak defense from memorizing specific attacks to spanning an underlying adversarial skill space, offering a concrete, data-centric training recipe (ASCoT) that practitioners can adopt without changing model architecture. The empirical finding that skill coverage—not data volume—drives robustness, and that an 8B model can match frontier reasoning models on specific jailbreak families, suggests a practical path to scalable defenses. Key limitations include restriction to single-turn attacks, reliance on a proprietary LLM (GPT-4.1) for skill extraction, the untested assumption that skills compose linearly in embedding space, and evaluation on only three 7–8B open-weight models. Open questions include whether the skill space truly saturates beyond the two-year window studied, how the framework extends to multi-turn and reasoning-driven attacks, and whether cross-modal adversarial skills admit a similar dictionary structure.
