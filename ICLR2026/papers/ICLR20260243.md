## Problem

Cross-view completion (CroCo) pre-training has proven effective for geometric tasks like stereo depth and optical flow, but its potential for photometric tasks remains unexplored. Because CroCo's training pairs involve differing illumination, the encoder must implicitly estimate and encode lighting information, yet no prior work has extracted or manipulated this photometric understanding. The paper asks whether CroCo's latent space can be repurposed for relighting, shadow removal, and albedo estimation with far less task-specific data than training from scratch.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image (processed at 448×448 resolution, upscaled from CroCo's native 224×224 via RoPE generalization) and produces a relit, shadow-free, or albedo RGB image. For training, it requires pixel-aligned image pairs under different illumination; for high-resolution inference, images are tiled into overlapping 448×448 patches with per-tile lighting latents blended at overlaps. The intermediate representation decomposes into a single 1024-dim lighting latent vector per tile and N per-patch intrinsic latent vectors of the same dimensionality, enabling independent manipulation of lighting versus scene content.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-stage disentanglement of the frozen CroCo v2 encoder's patch embeddings: a delighting transformer (I) appends a learnable query token to the patch latents and outputs a single lighting latent s₀ plus illumination-invariant intrinsic patch latents s₁…s_N, while a relighting transformer (R) recombines an arbitrary lighting latent with intrinsic patches to reconstruct CroCo-space embeddings decoded by a separately trained single-view DPT decoder. Training uses only 57k pixel-aligned paired images (two orders of magnitude less than CroCo's 7M pairs) with two self-supervised losses: an intrinsic consistency MSE enforcing that patch latents are invariant to illumination, and a cross-lighting reconstruction loss that swaps lighting latents between pairs and penalizes the decoded mismatch. Downstream tasks—shadow removal (S) and albedo estimation (A)—are implemented as lightweight latent-space transformations of the lighting vector, initialized from R's weights and trained with MSE against ground-truth lighting latents, requiring no changes to the frozen encoder, decoder, or disentanglement modules.

## Results

On albedo estimation (IIW WHDR benchmark), CroCoDiLight achieves 15.4% WHDR, outperforming all compared methods not trained on IIW (CGIntrinsics 17.8%, NIID-Net 16.6%, IntrinsicDiffusion 17.9%, PIE-Net 21.3%, Ordinal Shading 24.9%). On shadow removal, CroCoDiLight (mask-free) achieves SSIM 0.929 / PSNR 30.17 on ISTD+ and SSIM 0.931 / PSNR 30.01 on SRD, trailing the best mask-free baseline StableShadowRemoval (SSIM 0.968 / PSNR 35.10 on ISTD+) but with an oracle lighting latent reaching SSIM 0.936 / PSNR 33.41. For temporal timelapse upsampling, latent-space interpolation achieves lower FloLPIPS than image-space interpolation on Clock Shadows (0.286 vs. 0.309) and Indoor Shadows (0.923 vs. 0.950).

## Baselines & Comparisons

On albedo estimation (IIW WHDR), CroCoDiLight (15.4%) beats all five compared methods: CGIntrinsics (17.8%), NIID-Net (16.6%), IntrinsicDiffusion (17.9%), PIE-Net (21.3%), and Ordinal Shading (24.9%), establishing state-of-the-art among models not trained on IIW. On shadow removal, CroCoDiLight loses to all mask-free baselines on standard metrics: StableShadowRemoval leads with SSIM 0.968/PSNR 35.10 (ISTD+) and 0.944/33.24 (SRD), while CroCoDiLight achieves 0.929/30.17 and 0.931/30.01 respectively. The oracle variant (using ground-truth shadow-free lighting latent) narrows the gap substantially, reaching SSIM 0.936/PSNR 33.41 on ISTD+, approaching OmniSR (0.966/33.30) and DMTN (0.960/32.19). The authors note that their method is jointly trained across all shadow datasets as a single general-purpose model, whereas baselines like HomoFormer and ShadowFormer are fine-tuned per benchmark, and that qualitative results show effective shadow removal with a subtle global colour shift that penalises pixel-level metrics. For temporal upsampling, latent-space interpolation slightly outperforms image-space interpolation on two of three timelapse sequences (Clock Shadows, Indoor Shadows) but is marginally worse on Day-night Cycle (0.043 vs. 0.041 FloLPIPS).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the CroCo v2 cross-view completion encoder—originally designed for geometric tasks like stereo depth and optical flow—for photometric tasks, hypothesizing that the encoder implicitly learned illumination reasoning during pre-training. Conceptually, it follows the 'repurposing foundation models' line (ViT for segmentation, diffusion models for depth/intrinsics) but applies it to a pre-training objective (cross-view completion) that uniquely requires implicit relighting. For relighting, it departs from generative/diffusion-based approaches (Neural Gaffer, LightLab, LumiNet) in favor of explicit latent-space disentanglement via a single lighting vector and per-patch intrinsic embeddings, and for shadow removal/intrinsic decomposition it replaces end-to-end supervised pipelines with a lightweight latent-space transformation trained on top of frozen pre-trained components.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key contribution is demonstrating that photometric understanding is already embedded in CroCo's latent space and can be extracted with ~57k pairs (two orders of magnitude less than CroCo's 7M training pairs), suggesting cross-view completion is a viable pre-training proxy for data-scarce photometric tasks. However, the single 1024-dim lighting latent is a strong bottleneck that limits shadow fidelity and causes per-tile color inconsistencies at high resolution, and shadow removal metrics remain below dedicated SOTA methods. Open questions include whether spatially varying lighting representations, 3D-aware relighting, and user-controllable lighting manipulation can overcome these limitations, and whether other pre-training objectives (e.g., DINOv2, MAE) encode comparable photometric structure.
