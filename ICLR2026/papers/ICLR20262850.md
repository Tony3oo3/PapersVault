## Problem

While scaling laws for large language models are well-established, no explicit power-law formulation exists for diffusion transformers (DiT), leaving practitioners without a principled way to determine optimal model size, data quantity, or predicted training loss for a given compute budget. Prior empirical studies confirmed that larger DiT models produce better images but did not quantify the relationship between compute, parameters, tokens, and loss, forcing costly heuristic searches for optimal configurations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262850/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a compute budget C (in FLOPs, ranging from 1e17 to 6e18 in experiments, extrapolated to 1.5e21) and outputs closed-form power-law predictions for optimal parameter count N_opt, optimal token count D_opt, training loss L, and FID. Training uses 256×256 images (256 image tokens at patch size 2, plus 120 text tokens and 1 time token, totaling 377 context tokens) from a 108M-pair Laion-Aesthetic subset re-captioned with LLaVA 1.5, with each data point seen once (data-infinite regime). The fitted coefficients (e.g., N_opt = 0.0009·C^0.5681, L = 2.3943·C^-0.0273) serve as reusable predictors for any future compute allocation.

## Method

The paper trains vanilla in-context DiT models (2–15 layers, aspect ratio 64, AdamW at lr=1e-4, batch 1024) under six compute budgets using Rectified Flow with v-prediction and Logit-Normal timestep sampling. For each budget, multiple model sizes are trained and a parabola is fitted to the isoFLOP loss curve to extract the compute-optimal (N, D) pair; these optimal points are then fit to power laws yielding N_opt ∝ C^0.5681, D_opt ∝ C^0.4319, and L ∝ C^-0.0273. Validation is performed by training a 958M-parameter model at 1.5e21 FLOPs and confirming its final loss matches the extrapolated prediction. The authors further show that FID follows FID = 2.2566×10^6·C^-0.234, that the same power-law trends transfer to out-of-domain evaluation (COCO 2014, Flickr30k, JourneyDB), and that comparing scaling exponents across architectures (in-context vs. cross-attention) provides a low-cost benchmark for assessing model and data design quality.

## Results

The paper establishes explicit power-law scaling relationships for DiT across compute budgets from 1e17 to 6e18 FLOPs: optimal model size N_opt = 0.0009·C^0.5681, optimal data D_opt = 186.85·C^0.4319, training loss L = 2.3943·C^(−0.0273), and FID = 2.2566×10^6·C^(−0.234). A 1B-parameter model trained at 1.5e21 FLOPs (extrapolated from the fitted curves) achieves a training loss that closely matches the predicted value, validating the scaling law. Scaling trends are confirmed to transfer to out-of-domain evaluation on COCO 2014, Flickr30k, and JourneyDB, with consistent power-law shapes but a vertical offset reflecting dataset difficulty.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares two DiT conditioning architectures under identical training protocols across compute budgets from 1e17 to 1e19 FLOPs. The Cross-Attention Transformer (PixArt-style, Chen et al. 2023) achieves a steeper loss decline (exponent −0.0385 vs. −0.0273 for Vanilla In-context, Peebles & Xie 2023), meaning it reaches lower loss at the same compute budget. It also exhibits a smaller model exponent (0.54 vs. 0.56) and a larger data exponent (0.46 vs. 0.43), indicating that under this controlled setting, more compute should be allocated to data rather than model size for the Cross-Attention variant. The authors note this does not imply Cross-Attention is universally superior—modern in-context systems like Flux and SD3 (MMDiT) incorporate additional architectural improvements (multi-encoder conditioning, adaptive LayerNorm timestep injection, separate modality parameter sets) that are not present in the simplified in-context baseline tested here. The scaling-law framework itself serves as the evaluation tool: by comparing exponents, one can quantify relative scalability without needing to train to convergence at the target scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM scaling law framework (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022) to diffusion transformers for the first time, providing explicit power-law formulations linking compute budget, model size, data quantity, and training loss. While prior diffusion scaling studies (Mei et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024) empirically demonstrated that larger DiT models produce better outputs, none derived closed-form scaling equations or validated them via extrapolation. The paper builds directly on the Rectified Flow formulation (Liu et al., 2022; Lipman et al., 2022) and the Logit-Normal timestep scheduler from Esser et al. (2024), and adapts the isoFLOP methodology from Chinchilla (Hoffmann et al., 2022) to the DiT setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: practitioners can now predict optimal model size, data requirements, and generation quality (FID) for DiT training at any compute budget, reducing the need for costly heuristic configuration searches. The demonstrated transferability of scaling laws across datasets (Laion → COCO, Flickr30k, JourneyDB) positions these laws as a low-cost benchmark for assessing both model architecture and data quality. Key limitations include the restriction to text-to-image, the compute-limited (data-infinite) regime, and a single hyperparameter set; open questions include whether the laws hold under data-constrained scaling, for video generation, and with more aggressively tuned hyperparameters at each operating point.
