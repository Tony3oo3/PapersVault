## Problem

In production MoE serving, batched inference activates far more experts than the per-token budget because different tokens in a batch require different experts, making the memory-bound decoding stage slow. Existing remedies either statically prune/merge experts (incurring high offline cost and reducing capacity) or dynamically skip based solely on router scores (ignoring intrinsic expert similarity and proving difficult to integrate with high-performance frameworks like vLLM). SERE addresses this gap by exploiting inter-expert functional redundancy to dynamically reduce active experts at batch level without retraining.

## Input / Output

At inference time, SERE consumes the standard top-K expert indices produced by the router for each token in the current batch, together with a pre-computed M×M expert similarity matrix per layer (calibrated once offline on 400×128-token sequences from FineWeb-Edu using Frobenius norm). It outputs a modified token-to-expert index mapping in which secondary experts have been replaced by their most similar primary counterparts, yielding a smaller active expert set. The re-routing is implemented as a single parallel CUDA kernel (Algorithm 2) that slots into vLLM's MoE execution path with one line of code, operating on the index tensor without altering router weights or model parameters.

## Method

SERE is a three-step, input-aware re-routing mechanism applied per MoE layer at decode time. First, it identifies the primary expert set as the union of the top-S (by router rank) experts across all tokens in the batch, which are always retained. Second, for each remaining secondary expert, it queries the pre-computed similarity matrix to find the most similar primary expert; if the similarity exceeds a threshold ρ, all tokens assigned to that secondary expert are re-routed to the matched primary expert, otherwise the secondary expert is preserved as a critical (highly dissimilar) expert. Third, the MoE layer executes using only the reduced active set. The approach is distinguished from prior dynamic skipping by (i) using activation-based inter-expert similarity rather than router scores alone, (ii) operating at batch granularity so that redundancy is exploited across tokens, and (iii) providing a theoretical substitution-error bound (Theorem 1) showing that similarity-guided re-routing minimizes output perturbation. A custom CUDA kernel parallelizes the per-token re-routing lookups, achieving ~1.5× speedup over a PyTorch reference with negligible overhead relative to expert computation.

## Results

On Qwen3-30B-A3B (128 experts, top-8 baseline), SERE with Top-2 and ρ=0.5 achieves 80.37% average accuracy (97.7% of the 82.24% baseline) while reducing TPOT from 44.40 ms to 32.82 ms (1.35× speedup). On DeepSeekV2-Lite, SERE (Top-2, ρ=0.3) actually exceeds the full-model accuracy (55.48% vs. 54.76%) while cutting TPOT from 26.35 ms to 23.12 ms. On Qwen1.5-MoE-A2.7B, SERE reaches up to 2.0× speedup at QPS=24 with only ~2.6% accuracy loss. Across all three models, SERE consistently outperforms both static merging (HC-SMoE) and dynamic skipping (LYNX) baselines by 10–30 percentage points in average accuracy at comparable or lower latency.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against HC-SMoE (Chen et al., 2025), SERE wins decisively across all three models: on Qwen3-30B-A3B (Top-2), SERE achieves 80.37% vs. HC-SMoE's 69.42% average accuracy at a lower TPOT (32.82 vs. 39.14 ms); on Qwen1.5-MoE-A2.7B, the gap widens to 47.25% vs. 28.79%; and on DeepSeekV2-Lite, SERE reaches 55.48% vs. 35.33%. Against LYNX (Gupta et al., 2024), SERE also dominates: on Qwen3 it edges out LYNX (80.37% vs. 78.46%) while being ~15% faster in TPOT, but on Qwen1.5 and DeepSeekV2 the advantage is overwhelming (47.25% vs. 29.28% and 55.48% vs. 24.01% respectively), indicating LYNX's router-score-only approach fails on models with fewer, more specialized experts. Against direct Top-K reduction (Yang et al., 2025b), the comparison is stark: on Qwen3, reducing from top-8 to top-2 without re-routing collapses accuracy from 82.24% to 14.30%, whereas SERE preserves 80.37%—a 66-point improvement—by selectively re-routing secondary experts to their most similar primary counterparts while preserving critical experts. The paper attributes HC-SMoE's and LYNX's weaker performance on Qwen1.5 and DeepSeekV2 to architectural differences: these models have fewer experts (60–64) that are more specialized, making static merging or score-based skipping far more destructive than on Qwen3's 128 fine-grained experts.

## Relation to Prior Work

SERE carves new ground at the intersection of two existing lines of work: static expert merging (HC-SMoE, EEP, MoE-I²) and dynamic expert skipping (LYNX, NAEE, AdaMoE). It repurposes the inter-expert similarity concept that static methods use for fixed merging into a dynamic, batch-aware re-routing mechanism, while departing from prior dynamic methods by leveraging pre-computed expert functional characteristics rather than relying solely on router scores. The plug-and-play CUDA kernel design also distinguishes it from prior dynamic methods whose per-token operations are difficult to integrate into high-performance inference frameworks like vLLM.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SERE provides a practically deployable solution to a well-recognized bottleneck in production MoE serving: the mismatch between per-token sparsity and batch-level expert activation. The single-line vLLM integration and up to 2× decoding speedup with minimal quality loss make it immediately relevant for cost-sensitive large-scale deployment. Open questions include how the pre-computed similarity matrix degrades under distribution shift (e.g., domain-specific fine-tuning), whether it composes with other compression techniques like quantization or expert pruning, and whether the approach extends to MoE models with shared experts or fine-grained expert granularity beyond the three models tested.
