## Problem

Existing diffusion-based text inpainting methods suffer from three critical limitations: they cannot remove text (hallucinating text instead), lack explicit control over rendered text style, and frequently produce duplicated characters. More fundamentally, no generalist framework exists for Text Image Manipulation (TIM) — prior works are specialized to individual tasks (removal, editing, or insertion), leaving tasks like style-based insertion, text rescaling, and repositioning unaddressed by any single method.

## Input / Output

The method operates on 512×512 image patches and takes as input an RGB image, a target text string, a binary target mask (bilinearly interpolated to latent space), and optionally a reference image with its own mask for style transfer. For text editing, the input image itself serves as the style reference; for style-based insertion/editing, a separate reference image is provided. The output is a single manipulated RGB image with text removed, edited, inserted, rescaled, or repositioned according to the task. The entire pipeline is training-free, requiring only a single GPU (RTX 4090 for removal, A6000 for inpainting with latent optimization).

## Method

OmniText builds on TextDiff-2 (a U-Net-based latent diffusion inpainting model) and introduces two modular components. For text removal, it applies Self-Attention Inversion (SAI), which linearly inverts self-attention values within the text mask during early sampling steps to suppress the model's tendency to attend to surrounding text and hallucinate characters, combined with Cross-Attention Reassignment (CAR), which forces cross-attention to the end-of-description token inside the mask (suppressing text generation) and to the start-of-description token outside the mask (preserving background). For controllable inpainting, it performs on-the-fly latent optimization during early denoising steps using two novel losses: a Cross-Attention Content Loss based on Focal Loss that enforces each character token's attention to concentrate in its corresponding spatial region, and a Self-Attention Style Loss based on KL-divergence that aligns self-attention within the target mask to a normalized reference mask, guided by a grid-structured input that places the reference image alongside the target to enable style transfer. The key empirical insight is that cross-attention layers encode per-character spatial alignment (content control) while self-attention layers encode style similarity to nearby text (style control), enabling fine-grained manipulation without any retraining.

## Results

On the ScenePair text editing benchmark, OmniText achieves 78.44% word accuracy (ACC) and 0.951 NED for rendering accuracy, while attaining the best style fidelity among all generalists (MSE 4.79×10⁻², MS-SSIM 40.11×10⁻², PSNR 14.85). On SCUT-EnsText text removal (all-text setting), OmniText reaches PSNR 29.52 and FID 39.06, outperforming all generalist baselines by a wide margin (e.g., TextDiff-2: PSNR 25.42, FID 52.46) and surpassing the specialist LaMa (PSNR 29.37, FID 43.67), though the specialist ViTEraser remains stronger (PSNR 34.12, FID 28.35). ## Baselines & Comparisons

Among generalist text synthesis methods, OmniText outperforms all five baselines (AnyText, AnyText2, TextDiff-2, UDiffText, DreamText) on rendering accuracy (ACC 78.44% vs. best 78.36% for UDiffText) and dominates on style fidelity (MSE 4.79 vs. best 5.71 for AnyText2; MS-SSIM 40.11 vs. best 35.56 for TextDiff-2). Against the specialist text-removal model ViTEraser, OmniText loses on all metrics (PSNR 29.52 vs. 34.12; FID 39.06 vs. 28.35), but surpasses the specialist LaMa on MS-SSIM, PSNR, and FID. For text editing, OmniText is competitive with the specialist TextCtrl: it wins on NED (0.951 vs. 0.917), MS-SSIM (40.11 vs. 37.93), and FID (31.69 vs. 31.95), while TextCtrl edges ahead on ACC (78.98 vs. 78.44) and PSNR (14.92 vs. 14.85). In the appendix comparisons, OmniText dramatically outperforms closed-source models (GPT-4o-Image: ACC 25%, Gemini 2.5 Flash-Image: ACC 40%) and all open-source general editing/inpainting models (FlowEdit: ACC 9.45%, FluxFill: ACC 35.08%, QwenImageEdit: ACC 27.42%), as well as text-specific open-source models (TextSSR: ACC 53.20%, FluxText: ACC 54.53%). The paper also demonstrates that its grid trick and self-attention modulation generalize to other U-Net backbones (UDiffText, DreamText), improving style fidelity across all evaluated tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on TextDiff-2 (Chen et al., 2024) as its inpainting backbone and extends the training-free attention-manipulation paradigm (Prompt-to-Prompt, MasaCtrl, Attend-and-Excite) to the specialized domain of text image manipulation. Prior TIM work has developed separate specialist models for removal (ViTEraser, LaMa), editing (TextCtrl), and insertion (AnyText, DreamText), but no unified generalist existed. OmniText departs from this specialist-by-specialist lineage by showing that two attention-level interventions—Self-Attention Inversion and Cross-Attention Reassignment for removal, plus Focal-Loss-based cross-attention and KL-divergence-based self-attention losses for controllable inpainting—can be composed modularly to cover all TIM tasks without any retraining. The grid trick for style control is adapted from video editing (RAVE, Kara et al., 2024) and repurposed here to guide self-attention toward a reference text region.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

OmniText is the first training-free generalist for TIM, which is practically significant for design workflows (posters, packaging, apparel) where a single tool must handle removal, editing, insertion, rescaling, repositioning, and style transfer without per-task retraining. The introduced OmniText-Bench (150 mockups across five tasks) fills a clear evaluation gap, as no prior benchmark covered the full TIM task spectrum. Key limitations include restriction to U-Net-based backbones (incompatible with DiT architectures like Flux), occasional character duplication from the backbone, and the fact that FID is an unreliable style metric for text-centric images. Open questions include whether the attention-level control mechanisms can be adapted to token-level DiT tokenizers, and whether simultaneous text-and-object editing remains feasible within text-oriented backbones.
