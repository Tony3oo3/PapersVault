## Problem

Existing benchmarks for Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) suffer from three critical deficiencies: they lack long-context input evaluation, offer insufficient challenge for frontier models (e.g., GPT-5 scoring 94.3% on AIME-2025), and produce answers that are difficult to verify programmatically due to multiple valid representations. The paper asks whether graph algorithm problems can form a benchmark family that simultaneously addresses all three gaps while probing distinct reasoning paradigms.

## Input / Output

Each problem instance consists of a natural-language description of an undirected graph (nodes drawn from real-world entities such as street names, airport codes, Wikipedia articles, or DBLP authors, plus weighted or unweighted edges) followed by a task question. The model must output a single integer or node/edge identifier wrapped in \boxed{} format, enabling exact-string-match verification. Graphs span six size levels (8–160 nodes, 50 samples per level, 2,700 total instances), and evaluation collects 8 responses per model (4 for budget-constrained closed-source models) at temperature 0.6, top_p 0.95, max_token 32768.

## Method

The benchmark is organized around a three-way reasoning taxonomy grounded in CLRS algorithmic design paradigms: Enumeration (brute-force), Exploration (search with backtracking), and Intuition (greedy), each containing three tasks of increasing optimal time complexity (easy/medium/hard). Task-to-paradigm assignment is determined via an LLM-as-judge protocol: 300 Erdős-Rényi graphs are generated per problem, LRMs are prompted on these instances, and Qwen-2.5-72B classifies the algorithmic strategy actually employed, validated by human reviewers. Subgraphs are sampled from five real-world networks (DBLP, four city street networks, OpenFlight, Wikipedia, DBpedia) via random walk or BFS to ensure semantic diversity and contamination resistance. The empirical analysis introduces entropy-based response partitioning (using high-entropy tokens like 'wait', 'but', 'so' as segment boundaries) to decompose reasoning traces, then quantifies over-thinking through outcome efficiency (fraction of tokens before first correct answer) and self-verification effectiveness (fraction of verification segments that actually detect prior errors), revealing that frequent yet largely ineffective self-verification is the primary driver of over-thinking in graph reasoning.

## Results

Across nine graph algorithm tasks spanning six graph sizes (8–160 nodes), model accuracy degrades sharply with context length: Qwen3-32B drops from 87% pass@k at Level-3 (31–50 nodes) to 59% at Level-5 (81–120 nodes) and 47% at Level-6 (121–160 nodes). Even the strongest model, Qwen3-235B-Thinking, falls from 100% at Level-3 to 50% at Level-6. In the fixed-graph, variable-token-length experiment, DeepSeek-R1's accuracy on Triangle Sum plummets from 100% at 4k tokens to 22% at 64k tokens, and GPT-OSS-120B drops from 68% to 10%. Over-thinking analysis reveals that self-verification occupies a large fraction of reasoning traces yet is effective in fewer than 30% of cases, making it the primary driver of redundant token generation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The benchmark evaluates 20+ models spanning open-source (Qwen3, QWQ, GPT-OSS, Skywork-OR1, Light-R1, OpenThinker, Distill-Qwen, Llama-3.3) and closed-source (GPT5-mini, DeepSeek-R1/V3, Gemini-2.5-pro) families. Reasoning models consistently outperform their non-reasoning counterparts: Qwen3-32B achieves 0.63/0.87 (c@k/p@k) at Level-3 versus 0.23/0.57 for Qwen3-32B-no-thinking, a gap that persists across all levels. Within the reasoning family, Qwen3-235B-Thinking dominates at smaller scales (0.97 c@k at Level-3) but its advantage narrows at Level-6 (0.45 c@k) where GPT5-mini (0.41) and DeepSeek-R1 (0.45) are competitive. Across all models, the taxonomy ordering Enumeration > Exploration > Intuition holds universally: at Level-6, GPT5-mini scores 0.77 on Enumeration but only 0.10 on Intuition. Enabling code-based tool usage dramatically closes the gap (e.g., Qwen3-32B on MST jumps from 0% to 97.3% pass@k at Level-5), confirming that the bottleneck is intrinsic step-by-step execution rather than algorithmic knowledge. Correlation analysis with GPQA (ρ=0.77) and LongBench-v2 (ρ=0.60) validates that the benchmark measures general reasoning and long-context skills beyond graph-specific knowledge.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of graph-algorithm benchmarks for LLMs (NLGraph, GPT4Graph, GraphArena, GraCore, GraphOmni) by shifting the evaluation target from non-reasoning LLMs to O1-like Large Reasoning Models and by scaling graph sizes from ≤50 to 160 nodes. It departs from prior taxonomies (local vs. global, P vs. NP, node/edge/graph level) by grounding task categorization in CLRS algorithmic design paradigms—Enumeration, Exploration, and Intuition—arguing these better capture distinct reasoning capacities. The over-thinking analysis builds on the LRM efficiency literature (Chen et al. 2024d, Lu et al. 2025, Sui et al. 2025) but introduces entropy-based response partitioning and a self-verification effectiveness metric not previously applied to structured reasoning tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GRALGOBENCH provides a rare combination of long-context scalability, fine-grained difficulty control, and fully programmatic answer verification—three properties that existing math and code benchmarks lack simultaneously. The finding that self-verification, the dominant reasoning behavior in LRMs, is effective in fewer than 30% of attempts reframes over-thinking as a quality-of-verification problem rather than a quantity-of-tokens problem, pointing toward targeted interventions. Open questions include whether the taxonomy generalizes beyond the nine selected tasks, whether the long-context degradation is specific to graph structure or applies to any verbose input, and whether the benchmark's moderate correlation with GPQA (ρ=0.77) and LongBench-v2 (ρ=0.60) is sufficient to claim it captures a distinct reasoning dimension or merely overlaps with existing capabilities.
