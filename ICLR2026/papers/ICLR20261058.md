## Problem

Existing Cell Painting drug-screening pipelines treat experimental context (cell line, dosing schedule, observation time) as nuisance variables rather than signals to model, and produce black-box visual embeddings that lack semantic interpretability. This limits generalization across perturbation contexts and prevents extraction of mechanism-of-action (MoA) hypotheses that are traceable back to specific morphological evidence.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261058/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system consumes paired 512×512 fluorescence tiles (perturbation + matched control, concatenated along the channel dimension) together with structured metadata: compound identity encoded as 174 RDKit2D descriptors or binary fingerprints, a normalized dosing pair [mass-concentration, log-step index], and a normalized observation time. Outputs include joint image-context embeddings for retrieval/classification, a prioritized list of morphological features with confidence-weighted rationales, per-feature statistical summaries (Cliff's delta, bootstrap CIs, Mann-Whitney p-values), and a final structured phenotype report linking observed shifts to plausible MoA hypotheses.

## Method

CP-CLIP is a dual-encoder contrastive model (ViT-B/16 or ViT-L/16 image branch; GPT-2-based text branch) trained on 1.9M image-context pairs with InfoNCE or SigLIP loss. Its key architectural contribution is a context-aware token injection strategy: field-specific placeholder tokens (<CMPD>, <CONC>, <TIME>) are registered in the tokenizer vocabulary and, at inference, their embeddings are dynamically computed by lightweight MLP trunks that project continuous molecular descriptors, normalized dose, and time into the shared text-embedding space, fusing numerical metadata with discrete subword tokens in a single Transformer sequence. On top of this perception layer, CP-Agent is a six-agent pipeline (CPContext → ChannelSeg → CellFeat → FeatRank → StatSynth → ReportGen) in which an MLLM acts as a cognitive controller that routes tasks to interchangeable tools (fine-tuned VISTA-2D for segmentation, CellProfiler for handcrafted single-cell features, statistical aggregators for distribution-shift quantification) and synthesizes their outputs into an interpretable, evidence-traceable report.

## Results

On the seen-drug classification task (10-class compound, cell line, and channel identification via retrieval-based cosine similarity), CP-CLIP ViT-B/16 (descriptor) achieves a macro-averaged F1 of 0.896, substantially outperforming CLOOME (0.755), CLIP (0.657), and SigLIP (0.514). All four general-purpose MLLMs (Grok-4, GPT-5, Claude-4-Sonnet, Gemini-2.5-Pro) fail on compound classification, with macro-avg F1 scores between 0.027 and 0.102—near or below random guessing. On zero-shot unseen-drug matching, CP-CLIP ViT-L/16 (descriptor) reaches an average cosine similarity of 0.444, a 14.6% absolute improvement over CLIP (0.286) and a 4.8× improvement over SigLIP (0.096).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CP-CLIP ViT-B/16 (descriptor) outperforms CLOOME by 0.141 macro-avg F1 (0.896 vs. 0.755) on compound classification, demonstrating that jointly encoding experimental context (dose, time, cell line) alongside molecular descriptors yields substantially better alignment than CLOOME's molecule-only contrastive training. Against the standard CLIP baseline, CP-CLIP gains 0.239 macro-avg F1 (0.896 vs. 0.657), with the largest per-compound improvements on structurally similar drugs (e.g., BIRB-796: 0.932 vs. 0.575). SigLIP, which uses a sigmoid pairwise loss, performs worst among contrastive baselines (0.514 macro-avg F1), and even the CP-CLIP SigLIP variant (0.532) trails the InfoNCE-based CP-CLIP by 0.364. All four MLLMs (Grok-4, GPT-5, Claude-4-Sonnet, Gemini-2.5-Pro) collapse to near-random performance on compound identification (macro-avg F1 ≤ 0.102), confirming that zero-shot multimodal reasoning without perturbation-aware grounding cannot resolve subtle morphological signatures. On unseen-drug zero-shot matching, CP-CLIP ViT-L/16 (descriptor) achieves 0.444 average similarity versus 0.286 for CLIP and 0.096 for SigLIP, with the gap widening for harder compounds (e.g., Nilotinib: 0.295 vs. 0.174 vs. −0.055).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the CLIP-style contrastive learning paradigm for Cell Painting (CLOOME, CellCLIP, MolPhenix) by injecting structured experimental metadata (cell line, dose, time) directly into the text encoder via field-specific placeholder tokens and MLP projections, rather than treating context as late-fusion or unstructured text. It then wraps this perception module (CP-CLIP) in a multi-agent MLLM pipeline (CP-Agent) that performs segmentation, feature extraction, statistical synthesis, and report generation — a workflow architecture inspired by recent agentic bioinformatics systems but specialized for morphological profiling. The key conceptual departure from prior Cell Painting CLIP models is the explicit modeling of experimental context as a first-class signal in the contrastive objective, and the shift from black-box embeddings to interpretable, traceable mechanistic narratives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that state-of-the-art general-purpose MLLMs (GPT-5, Gemini-2.5-Pro, Claude-4-Sonnet, Grok-4) fail at compound-level discrimination from Cell Painting images, reinforcing that domain-specific contrastive supervision is essential for subtle pharmacological morphology. The context-aware token injection provides a practical, lightweight mechanism for integrating heterogeneous structured metadata into vision-language alignment, which could transfer to other high-content imaging assays. Open questions include scalability to larger compound libraries beyond ~400, whether the agentic pipeline's interpretability gains hold under adversarial or out-of-distribution perturbations, and how the system integrates into closed-loop experimental design (e.g., adaptive dose selection). The paper also notes generalization to other imaging modalities (QPI, brightfield) but does not empirically validate this.
