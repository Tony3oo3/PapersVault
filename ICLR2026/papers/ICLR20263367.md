## Problem

Quality-Diversity (QD) optimization algorithms for sequential decision-making rely on hand-crafted behavioral descriptors that require substantial domain knowledge and constrain discovered diversity to predefined dimensions, potentially missing interesting behavioral variations. This paper addresses the gap by providing a theoretically grounded method to automatically generate behavioral descriptors from policy behavior without any manual specification.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263367/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an MDP environment (state-action space, transition dynamics, reward function) and a population of parameterized policies (two-layer 128-unit tanh networks with Toeplitz structure), collecting state-action trajectories via rollouts. It produces an archive of diverse, high-performing policies organized by automatically learned k-dimensional behavioral descriptors (k=4 in experiments), where each descriptor is an affine projection of a 100-dimensional random Fourier feature embedding of the policy's occupancy measure. The archive is a 10^k grid over [-1,1]^k, and the descriptor map is periodically refined via calibrated weighted PCA on the current archive.

## Method

AutoQD embeds each policy's occupancy measure into a finite-dimensional space using random Fourier features of a Gaussian kernel, so that Euclidean distances between embeddings approximate the Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) between occupancy measures — a valid metric that fully characterizes behavioral difference. Theorem 1 proves that embedding distances converge to true MMD with high probability as the number of sampled trajectories n and embedding dimension D grow, with error decaying exponentially in both. In practice, the embedding ψ^π computes a discounted weighted average of RFF features over all transitions in n rollouts (rather than sampling a single state-action pair per trajectory), which shares the same expectation but reduces variance. These D-dimensional embeddings are then projected to k dimensions via Calibrated Weighted PCA (cwPCA), which weights policies by normalized fitness to emphasize behavioral variation among high-performing solutions, then scales each axis so projections fall in [-1,1]. The algorithm alternates between CMA-MAE optimization using the current descriptors and periodic re-estimation of the projection from the expanded archive, enabling iterative refinement of the behavioral space as new policies are discovered.

## Results

AutoQD achieves the highest GT QD Score in 5 of 6 environments, with particularly large margins on Ant (361.43×10⁴ vs. 182.58×10⁴ for RegularQD, the strongest baseline) and BipedalWalker (6.09×10⁴ vs. 3.36×10⁴ for LSTM-Aurora). On the Quality-Weighted Vendi Score (qVS), AutoQD leads in 4/6 environments (e.g., 60.23 on Ant vs. 39.35 for RegularQD), while losing to RegularQD on Walker2d (7.22 vs. 9.08) and Aurora on HalfCheetah (1.35 vs. 2.39). On pure diversity (Vendi Score), AutoQD achieves the highest values in 4/6 environments, with the largest gap on Swimmer (16.92 vs. 7.21 for LSTM-Aurora). In the adaptation experiment on BipedalWalker, AutoQD's population achieves the highest AUC under both friction (1429.66) and mass (295.65) perturbations, confirming that its diverse archive provides superior robustness.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AutoQD (using automatically discovered descriptors) substantially outperforms all four learned-descriptor baselines (Aurora, LSTM-Aurora, SMERL, DvD-ES) on GT QD Score across all six environments, with the largest margins on Ant (361 vs. 19 for LSTM-Aurora) and BipedalWalker (6.09 vs. 3.36 for LSTM-Aurora). Against the strongest baseline, RegularQD (which uses hand-crafted foot-contact descriptors), AutoQD wins on QD Score in all six tasks and on qVS in 4/6, losing only on Walker2d (7.22 vs. 9.08) and HalfCheetah (1.35 vs. 2.39, where Aurora actually leads). On pure diversity (Vendi Score), AutoQD beats RegularQD in 4/6 environments but trails it on Walker2d (8.4 vs. 10.17) and Aurora on HalfCheetah (5.29 vs. 5.8), suggesting that in these two domains the hand-crafted descriptors capture behavioral axes (e.g., full leg-lifting in Walker2d) that AutoQD's 4-dimensional embedding underemphasizes. The RL-based baselines SMERL and DvD-ES are consistently outperformed by a wide margin on all three metrics, with SMERL achieving near-zero QD scores on Swimmer (0.02×10⁴) and DvD-ES on BipedalWalker (0.09×10⁴), highlighting the difficulty of scaling fixed-size skill sets to continuous behavior spaces.

## Relation to Prior Work

AutoQD builds on CMA-MAE as its QD optimization backbone and on random Fourier features (Rahimi & Recht, 2007) to approximate Gaussian-kernel MMD between policy occupancy measures. It departs from prior unsupervised QD methods (Aurora, LSTM-Aurora) that learn behavioral descriptors via autoencoders trained on visited states or trajectories, and from unsupervised RL skill-discovery methods (DIAYN, SMERL) that use information-theoretic objectives to train a fixed number of diverse policies. The key conceptual departure is grounding behavioral descriptors in occupancy measures—which provably characterize policies one-to-one in fully-observable MDPs—rather than in learned latent representations, yielding a theoretically justified embedding whose distances approximate true MMD with explicit convergence guarantees.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By eliminating the need for hand-crafted behavioral descriptors, AutoQD lowers the barrier to applying QD optimization to new domains and opens the door to discovering behavioral variations that a human designer might not anticipate. The theoretical guarantee (Theorem 1) provides a principled foundation absent in prior heuristic approaches like Aurora, and the adaptation experiments demonstrate practical value for robustness. Key open questions include: whether the approach scales to high-dimensional or image-based observation spaces, how to integrate the iterative descriptor refinement with gradient-based QD methods without destabilizing training, and whether dynamically adapting the kernel bandwidth could mitigate the observed tendency to converge on simple, stable behaviors in low-dimensional descriptor spaces.
