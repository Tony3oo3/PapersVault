## Problem

Transformer-based offline RL methods learn from complete trajectories conditioned on final returns, limiting their ability to stitch together high-quality segments from different parts of a trajectory. Existing stitching approaches operate at trajectory-level granularity, failing to identify and recombine superior sub-trajectory fragments within a single trajectory, especially when data quality is uneven.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261380/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes offline trajectories as tokenized sequences of (state, action, return-to-go) tuples fed into a Transformer, and outputs a single action (continuous or discrete) per timestep. During training, only selected subtrajectories from each trajectory are used for loss computation rather than the full sequence, and during evaluation the input history is dynamically truncated based on value estimates, so the effective context length varies at each step.

## Method

PRGS introduces three integrated components: (1) an MMD-based return estimator that outputs N particles approximating the return distribution for each state-action pair, trained via MMD loss with a squared-Euclidean kernel; (2) a greedy slicing procedure that computes an optimistic scalar value (mean of top-n sorted particles) aligned to the trajectory start, identifies the peak as a split point, and recursively extracts high-quality subtrajectories weighted by a decay coefficient λ; (3) an adaptive history truncation mechanism during evaluation that resets the input history whenever the current state's estimated value exceeds the previous step's, aligning inference with the subtrajectory-based training. The framework is plug-and-play, requiring only modification of the training loss to mask non-selected timesteps, and is compatible with any Transformer-based offline RL algorithm.

## Results

On the D4RL benchmark, PRGS consistently improves Transformer-based offline RL: DT-PRGS achieves an average of 82.9 on Gym tasks (+7.6 over DT), 100.1 on Maze2D (+59.2 over DT), and 65.5 on Kitchen (+15.4 over DT). Against stitching-focused baselines on Gym medium/medium-replay, PRGS (72.1) outperforms CGDT (71.8), EDT (63.4), QDT (54.6), and vanilla DT (61.2). Across all benchmark domains (D4RL, AuctionNet, BabyAI), PRGS yields an average improvement of 15.8% over the original baselines, with the largest gains on long-horizon navigation (Maze2D) and compositional manipulation (Kitchen) tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against classical offline RL baselines (CQL, IQL, BEAR, TD3+BC), PRGS-enhanced Transformer methods dominate on most tasks: DT-PRGS (82.9 Gym avg) surpasses CQL (80.6) and IQL (77.0), while on Adroit and Kitchen the gap is even wider (e.g., DT-PRGS 35.2 vs. CQL 15.6 on Adroit). Among Transformer-based baselines, PRGS consistently improves its host model: DT-PRGS beats DT by +7.6 (Gym), +15.4 (Kitchen), and +59.2 (Maze2D); BC-PRGS beats BC by +10.0 on Kitchen; PDiT-PRGS beats PDiT by +37.0 on Maze2D, though PDiT-PRGS slightly underperforms PDiT on AntMaze (−1.6). Against stitching-focused methods on Gym medium/medium-replay, PRGS (72.1) edges out CGDT (71.8) and clearly surpasses EDT (63.4) and QDT (54.6), with the largest margin on hopper-medium-replay (98.1 vs. 93.4 for CGDT). On AuctionNet, BC-PRGS improves over BC by +9.1 points on average, and on BabyAI, PDiT-PRGS leads with 65.9 average success rate versus 63.0 for PDiT.

## Relation to Prior Work

PRGS extends the Decision Transformer paradigm (Chen et al., 2021) by introducing an explicit timestep-level subtrajectory selection mechanism that prior Transformer-based offline RL methods lack. While stitching-focused approaches like QDT, CGDT, and EDT improve trajectory recombination through value-guided conditioning or adaptive context length, they operate at the trajectory level or rely on implicit latent representations without explicitly identifying high-quality segments within a single trajectory. The MMD-based return estimator draws on distributional RL (Bellemare et al., 2017; Dabney et al., 2018) but repurposes it not for policy learning but as a segmentation oracle, and the adaptive history truncation mechanism addresses a training-evaluation mismatch that is a direct consequence of the slicing design rather than a pre-existing problem in the literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PRGS provides a modular, interpretable plug-in that consistently improves Transformer-based offline RL methods (average 15.8% gain) without requiring architectural changes, making it practically attractive for practitioners already using DT or PDiT. However, the 89% memory overhead and 16% throughput reduction during training limit its applicability in resource-constrained settings, and the failure mode analysis reveals that benefits diminish on narrow-coverage datasets (random or expert) where there is little low-quality structure to filter. Open questions include whether the timestep-level slicing principle transfers to non-Transformer offline RL methods (the authors note this as future work), how the method scales to much longer horizons where the greedy recursive slicing becomes computationally expensive, and whether the optimistic MMD estimator can be replaced by lighter-weight alternatives without sacrificing the slicing quality.
