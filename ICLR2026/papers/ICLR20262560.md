## Problem

Despite extensive theory for overparameterized neural networks in supervised learning (NTK, infinite-width limits), a fundamental gap remains for deep RL in continuous state-action spaces: the data distribution itself shifts with every gradient step, making it unclear how the environment's state distribution evolves during learning. This paper addresses the question of how the time-dependent state random variable changes over a single gradient step in a continuous-time actor-critic setting with neural network function approximation, providing the first such characterization in continuous RL.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262560/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a control-affine SDE environment (drift g, control matrix h, diffusion σ), a smooth reward function r, and linearized single-hidden-layer tanh networks of width n for both actor and critic, with learning rate η = O(1/√n). The output is a closed, nonparametric system of five time-dependent variables (state, action, action derivative, value estimate, value estimate time derivative) whose per-step changes are fully characterized as Gaussian random variables with explicit means and variances. Notably, the state change Δs_{t,τ} is not O(η) because the stochastic environment and exploratory dynamics introduce a mean-zero martingale component that persists independent of the learning rate.

## Method

The core approach formulates continuous-time RL as a two-clock stochastic system (environment time t and gradient time τ) and uses the Itô-Taylor expansion to express the state random variable as an infinite polynomial in the linearized NN parameters (W − W⁰). Exploratory dynamics are unified into a single SDE (Equation 4) where environment noise and policy exploration noise combine into one Wiener process with diffusion √(h² + σ²), proven equivalent to a discrete-time numerical scheme via a martingale approach. Applying the martingale central limit theorem and its corresponding law of large numbers to the polynomial coefficients yields Gaussian limits for the changes in action, value estimate, and their derivatives over one gradient step, with O(1/√n) error. The state change itself is decomposed into a deterministic drift term M_{t,τ}, a martingale component G_{t,τ} from the stochastic dynamics, and a gradient-driven term ηℤ_{t,τ} that depends on the TD-error-like quantity q_{l,τ} and a Volterra-type integral involving the policy's influence on the SDE coefficients. The resulting closed system of five variables (Theorem 6.1) is empirically validated on an LQR task across dimensions d_s ∈ {1, 2, 8, 32}.

## Results

The main theoretical contribution (Theorem 6.1) shows that the gradient-time dynamics of an episodic actor-critic algorithm with linearized single-hidden-layer networks (width n, tanh activation, η = O(1/√n)) reduce to a closed system of exactly five time-dependent variables: state s̃_{t,τ}, action a_{t,τ}, action derivative a'_{t,τ}, value estimate v_{t,τ}, and value time-derivative v'_{t,τ}. Each variable's change over one gradient step is Gaussian with explicitly characterized mean and variance up to O(1/√n) error. Empirically, the episodic continuous-time actor-critic (Algorithm 1) learns near-optimal policies in an LQR environment (g(s)=s, h(s)=1, σ(s)=0.1, r(s)=−500s², β=0.1) across state dimensions d_s ∈ {1, 2, 8, 32} (averaged over 20 seeds), and the theoretical prediction from Theorem 6.1 closely tracks the empirical algorithm's trajectory (Figure 6).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is primarily a theoretical paper and does not benchmark against competing RL algorithms or baselines in the standard sense. Two qualitative comparisons are made: (1) The proposed exploratory dynamics (Equation 4, combining environment noise σ and policy noise h) are contrasted with a canonical additive Wiener process π(s_t) + w_t in a deterministic environment (g=0.1, h=0.5, σ=0). The additive noise produces smooth, non-exploratory trajectories (Figure 3), while the proposed dynamics exhibit stochastic jumps that provide better state-action coverage (Figure 4), particularly as Δt → 0. (2) The theoretical prediction from Theorem 6.1 (black dotted curve) is compared against the empirical output of the online episodic actor-critic (Algorithm 1) in the LQR setting; the two closely match, validating the nonparametric closed-system formulation. The paper explicitly notes it operates in the 'lazy regime' (fixed features, vanishing learning rate) and does not compare against feature-learning or finite-width methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: continuous-time RL (Jia & Zhou 2022; Wang et al. 2020; Doya 2000), linearized/infinite-width neural network theory (Lee et al. 2019; Jacot et al. 2018), and stochastic control via SDEs (Kloeden & Platen 1992; Kushner & Dupuis 2001). It adapts the continuous-time actor-critic framework of Jia & Zhou but departs from Wang et al.'s relaxed-control exploratory dynamics, where policy stochasticity vanishes in deterministic environments, by proposing a unified noise formulation that preserves exploration. The key methodological novelty is applying the Itô-Taylor expansion to express the state random variable as a polynomial in linearized NN parameters, enabling a closed-form characterization of gradient-time dynamics that has no direct analogue in discrete-time RL theory.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is, to the authors' knowledge, the first derivation of an equation for the infinitesimal gradient-time evolution of the state distribution in continuous RL with neural network function approximation, providing a principled bridge between stochastic control and overparameterized RL theory. However, the result is heavily restricted: 1D state/action, linearized (lazy-regime) single-hidden-layer networks, infinite width, smooth dynamics, and vanishing learning rate, with empirical validation limited to a toy LQR. Extending to higher dimensions, finite width, feature learning, deeper architectures, and non-smooth activations are all identified as open problems, and the practical impact on real continuous-control benchmarks remains to be demonstrated.
