## Problem

Human experts collecting robot demonstrations are often constrained by their control interface (e.g., a mode-switching joystick that controls only one axis at a time), producing slow, segmented trajectories that are suboptimal for the robot's full action space. Standard imitation learning mimics these suboptimal actions, and standard IRL infers rewards that reflect the same constraints, so neither enables the robot to discover faster, more efficient behaviors. The paper asks: can a robot learn a strictly better policy than what a constrained expert can demonstrate?

## Input / Output

The method consumes expert state trajectories (no action labels required) collected under a restricted action subspace, plus the robot's own online rollout states during training. It produces a goal-proximity reward function f_φ(s) over state vectors and a robot action policy π_θ(a|s) trained via PPO. A key structural constraint is that the expert's action space A^e(s) is a strict subset of the agent's full action space A, so the learned policy can exploit actions the expert physically cannot produce (e.g., simultaneous multi-axis Cartesian motion vs. sequential single-axis joystick commands).

## Method

LfCD-GRIP decouples reward learning from constrained expert actions by defining a state-only goal-proximity reward trained with exponential temporal decay from the goal, then extends it beyond the demonstration distribution via two mechanisms. First, a Monte Carlo Dropout confidence estimator identifies high-confidence states (low prediction variance) among online rollouts, using a dynamic threshold set to the maximum variance observed on expert states. Second, a trajectory-wise interpolation mechanism linearly interpolates log-proximity values between pairs of high-confidence anchor states on the same rollout, assigning meaningful progress signals to intermediate states that standard proximity-based IRL would push to zero. An annealing mask gradually increases reliance on interpolated targets to prevent premature reward propagation. The policy is trained with PPO using the resulting dense proximity-difference rewards, enabling the agent to discover shorter paths (e.g., diagonal shortcuts in MiniGrid, 10× faster pick-and-place on a real WidowX arm) that lie entirely outside the expert's demonstrated trajectories.

## Results

LfCD-GRIP consistently outperforms all baselines in average trajectory length across five environments. On the real WidowX-Pick task, LfCD-GRIP completes the pick-and-place in 12 seconds versus 100 seconds for behavioral cloning (10× faster). In Maze2D-Constrained, LfCD-GRIP achieves 100 average transitions compared to 113 for Proximity-based IRL and 264.37 for the constrained expert. In MiniGrid-LfCD, LfCD-GRIP discovers a diagonal shortcut (25.2 avg. length vs. optimal 24), while all baselines remain confined to the expert's longer path (>32).

## Baselines & Comparisons

LfCD-GRIP outperforms all five baselines across every environment tested. Against Behavioral Cloning (Pomerleau, 1988), LfCD-GRIP achieves a 10× speedup on the real WidowX robot (12 s vs. 100 s) and discovers the diagonal shortcut in MiniGrid that BC cannot find. Against GAIL (Ho & Ermon, 2016), LfCD-GRIP achieves 100% success in Maze2D-Constrained versus GAIL's 69%, while also using 100% out-of-constraint actions. Against GAIfO (Torabi et al., 2018), both methods use 100% OOC actions, but LfCD-GRIP reaches 100% success versus GAIfO's 51%, demonstrating that reward generalization matters more than action diversity alone. Against SSRR (Chen et al., 2021), LfCD-GRIP succeeds where SSRR fails to learn any meaningful policy under constrained demonstrations in FetchPush. Against Proximity-based IRL (Lee et al., 2021), LfCD-GRIP reduces Maze2D-Constrained episode length by over 10% (100 vs. 113 transitions) and is the only method to find the MiniGrid shortcut, confirming that confidence-guided interpolation is the critical differentiator.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends proximity-based IRL (Lee et al., 2021), which learns a goal-proximity reward from expert demonstrations, by addressing its critical failure mode under constrained demonstrators: the online regularization term penalizes all out-of-distribution states to zero, suppressing exploration. LfCD-GRIP adds two modules—Monte Carlo Dropout-based confidence estimation and temporal interpolation along agent trajectories—to propagate reliable proximity values to novel states, decoupling the reward signal from the expert's constrained actions. It explicitly distinguishes itself from learning-from-suboptimal-demonstrations methods (T-REX, D-REX, SSRR), which assume a shared action space, and from cross-embodied imitation, which maps between action spaces rather than discovering policies beyond the expert's reach.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 10× real-robot speedup (12 s vs. 100 s on WidowX-Pick) demonstrates that interface-constrained demonstrations—a ubiquitous practical scenario with joysticks and mode-switching controllers—need not cap robot performance. The OOC-action analysis (100% success with 100% out-of-constraint actions, versus GAIfO's 51% success despite 100% OOC usage) provides a clean conceptual argument that reward generalization, not merely action diversity, is what enables policy improvement. Key limitations include the assumption of clearly defined goal states (limiting applicability to open-ended or multi-task settings) and reliance on goal-conditioned proximity that has not yet been generalized across semantically varied goals.
