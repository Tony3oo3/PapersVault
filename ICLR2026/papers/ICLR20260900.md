## Problem

Pre-trained language models are vulnerable to backdoor attacks where adversaries embed trigger patterns during training, causing targeted misclassifications when the trigger appears at inference. Existing defenses are either training-time methods requiring dataset access or computationally expensive purification strategies (retraining, head pruning, token-space search), and most offer no interpretability into where or why a trigger operates. The paper addresses the gap of an inference-time, explainable defense that localizes and neutralizes triggers without retraining the model.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a text input (potentially containing a hidden trigger), a backdoored transformer model with white-box access to attention weights and input gradients, and a small clean validation set used solely to calibrate the anomaly detection threshold (95th percentile for BERT/DistilBERT/RoBERTa/DeBERTa, 65th for ALBERT due to its shared-layer architecture compressing the score distribution). It outputs a class label prediction, a sentence-level anomaly score (max token score), and per-token attribution scores decomposed into attention and gradient components. The defense modifies the input in-place via character-level noise injection on flagged tokens before the final forward pass, rather than altering model weights.

## Method

X-GRAAD computes two complementary token-level signals: (1) attention importance, obtained by averaging the attention matrices across all L layers and H heads to form a mean attention matrix, then summing each column to measure total attention received by a token; and (2) gradient importance, computed as the L2 norm of the gradient of the predicted-class logit with respect to each token's embedding vector. These are normalized (attention as deviation from the sequence mean, gradient as ratio to the sequence mean) and multiplied to form a combined token score; the sentence-level anomaly score is the maximum over all tokens. When this score exceeds a calibrated threshold, the highest-scoring token(s) are corrupted by randomly inserting or replacing one or two characters at arbitrary positions, breaking the trigger pattern while preserving sentence semantics. The perturbed input is then passed through the original unmodified model for prediction. The key insight is that trigger tokens simultaneously dominate both attention allocation and gradient magnitude, and their product yields a sharper clean-vs-poisoned separation than either signal alone, as confirmed by ablation experiments.

## Results

Across three transformer backbones (BERT, DistilBERT, ALBERT), three datasets (SST-2, IMDb, AG's News), and three attack types (BadNets, RIPPLES, LWS), X-GRAAD achieves ASR of 0.0 in the majority of BERT configurations and near-zero ASR (≤0.027) in most DistilBERT and ALBERT settings, while maintaining competitive Clean Accuracy (CACC ≈ 0.90–0.95). For example, on BERT/SST-2, ASR drops from 0.929 (PURE) to 0.0 under BadNets, and from 1.0 (RAP/FT) to 0.0 under RIPPLES and LWS. On the challenging DistilBERT-LWS/IMDb setting, X-GRAAD reduces ASR from 0.728 (PURE) to 0.027 with only a marginal CACC drop. The method also generalizes to RoBERTa and DeBERTa, outperforming the best competing defense in 11 of 18 additional evaluation settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

X-GRAAD decisively outperforms all four baselines across nearly all configurations. RAP and FT are essentially ineffective: RAP achieves ASR = 1.0 on SST-2 for RIPPLES and LWS (no defense), and FT similarly fails with ASR ≈ 1.0 in most settings. MEFT, which uses maximum-entropy fine-tuning, reduces ASR somewhat but still leaves it at 0.998–1.0 on SST-2 for BERT under BadNets/RIPPLES/LWS. ONION (perplexity-based token removal) is the weakest inference-time baseline, achieving ASR of 0.12–0.15 on SST-2 but degrading to 0.96–1.0 on IMDb and AG's News. PURE (attention head pruning + normalization) is the strongest competitor, reaching ASR of 0.149 on BERT/SST-2/RIPPLES and 0.161 on BERT/AG's News/BadNets, yet X-GRAAD still achieves 0.0 in those same settings. The largest gap over the best baseline appears on DistilBERT/LWS/IMDb, where PURE yields ASR 0.728 versus X-GRAAD's 0.027, and on ALBERT/LWS/SST-2, where PURE gives 0.201 versus X-GRAAD's 0.183 (the one case where X-GRAAD does not reach zero).

## Relation to Prior Work

X-GRAAD repurposes two previously observed but separately studied phenomena into a unified inference-time defense: the attention-drifting effect in trojaned BERT identified by Lyu et al. (2022), and the gradient-dominance principle from HOTFLIP (Ebrahimi et al., 2017) originally designed for white-box adversarial examples on CNNs/LSTMs. Rather than requiring attention head selection or pruning (as in PURE) or perplexity-based filtering (as in ONION), the paper aggregates token-level attention across all heads and layers and multiplies it with normalized gradient magnitudes to form a joint anomaly score, framed within a classical anomaly-detection paradigm. This positions it as a lightweight, model-agnostic alternative to training-time defenses (BFClass, TextGuard) and purification methods (MEFT, Fine-Mixing) that demand retraining or clean reference models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical appeal is clear: an inference-time defense that requires no retraining, no clean reference model, and only a small clean validation set for threshold calibration is well-suited to the realistic scenario where users download pre-trained models from third-party repositories. The explainability component—localizing which specific token drives the anomaly—adds an auditability layer absent from most prior defenses. However, the method assumes white-box access to gradients and attention weights, limits evaluation to encoder-based architectures (BERT, DistilBERT, ALBERT, RoBERTa, DeBERTa) with rare-word triggers, and has not been tested on decoder-only or instruction-tuned models where the threat landscape is arguably more pressing. Open questions include whether the attention-gradient anomaly signal persists under more stealthy triggers (syntactic, stylistic, clean-label with semantic triggers) and whether the approach transfers to generative tasks beyond classification.
