## Problem

Existing INT4 weight-activation quantization kernels are incompatible with NVIDIA Blackwell's FP4 Tensor Cores (which offer up to 4× throughput over FP16), leaving significant hardware potential untapped. Additionally, prior mixed-precision methods like Atom use a fixed number of high-precision channels across all layers, ignoring the heterogeneous activation distributions that vary per layer. Post-training quantization for Microscaling (MX) formats with flexible mixed-precision allocation remains largely unexplored.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263825/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an FP16 activation tensor X ∈ R^{L×I} and a corresponding weight tensor W, along with offline calibration data used to precompute per-layer precision ratios (p₄, p₆, p₈) and channel permutations. Activations are dynamically partitioned into three groups quantized to MXFP4 (E2M1), MXFP6 (E3M2), and MXFP8 (E4M3) with 32-element blocks sharing an E8M0 scale factor; weights are permuted and quantized identically offline. The fused GEMM kernel produces BFloat16 output by accumulating FP32 partial sums on Blackwell Tensor Cores with dequantization fused into MMA instructions.

## Method

MicroMix defines explicit quantization thresholds T(4) and T(6) derived from the INT8 error upper bound, ensuring that elements assigned to MXFP4 or MXFP6 incur quantization error no greater than what INT8 would produce. Channels are sorted by absolute mean value (ascending) via a permutation σ, then partitioned: the smallest p₄ fraction goes to MXFP4, the next p₆ to MXFP6, and the remainder to MXFP8. These ratios and permutations are precomputed offline per layer using calibration data, exploiting the observed cross-dataset stability of the proportions. The kernel design fuses the channel reordering and block-scaled quantization into a single operation for activations (taking <20% of kernel runtime), while weight quantization is a one-time offline cost. The GEMM kernel instantiates separate CUTLASS MXFP4/6/8 matrix multiplications per precision group, with dequantization deeply fused into MMA instructions on Blackwell Tensor Cores, eliminating the CUDA-Core dequantization bottleneck that limits INT-based kernels.

## Results

On Llama3.1-8B, MicroMix (5.51 avg bits) achieves 71.56% zero-shot average accuracy (vs. 73.03% FP16), 62.65% 5-shot MMLU (vs. 65.24% FP16), and 6.72 WikiText2 perplexity (vs. 6.24% FP16). On Qwen2.5-32B (5.22 avg bits), it reaches 75.20% zero-shot average (vs. 75.55% FP16), 81.79% MMLU (vs. 83.32% FP16), and 5.56 PPL (vs. 5.02% FP16). MicroMix is the only method that preserves ≥98% of FP16 zero-shot accuracy on both models. In efficiency, the kernel delivers 2.29–3.38× speedup over TensorRT-FP16 on RTX 5090 and 1.82–3.02× higher decode throughput than Atom on RTX PRO 6000.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MicroMix outperforms all INT4 baselines (QuaRot, QUIK, Atom, FlatQuant) on Qwen2.5-32B across every metric: 75.20% zero-shot avg vs. FlatQuant's 74.72%, 81.79% MMLU vs. 81.52%, and 5.56 PPL vs. 5.74. On Llama3.1-8B, FlatQuant edges MicroMix by 0.42 points on zero-shot average (71.98 vs. 71.56), but MicroMix leads on MMLU (62.65 vs. 61.33) and perplexity (6.72 vs. 6.95). Against the MX-based AMXFP4 (5.00 bits), MicroMix gains 5.22 points on Llama zero-shot (71.56 vs. 66.34) and 1.56 points on Qwen (75.20 vs. 73.64), while also using fewer bits. The INT6 baseline (6.00 bits) is consistently worse than MicroMix despite using more bits, confirming that higher bit-width alone does not guarantee accuracy. On code benchmarks (Qwen2.5-Coder-32B), MicroMix matches or exceeds INT8 (Bitsandbytes) on all four sub-tasks (e.g., 74.1 vs. 73.5 on MBPP+). On math (Qwen2.5-Math-7B), MicroMix (5.16 bits) achieves 83.8% average, slightly below FP8-vLLM's 84.8% but at roughly 2× lower precision.

## Relation to Prior Work

MicroMix extends the mixed-precision quantization paradigm established by Atom and RPTQ (which use fixed INT8/INT4 channel splits with reordering) to the Microscaling (MX) floating-point family, replacing integer formats with MXFP4/MXFP6/MXFP8 and their E8M0 block scales. The key conceptual departure is the introduction of explicit quantization thresholds T(4) and T(6) derived from INT8 error bounds, enabling per-layer adaptive allocation of 4/6/8-bit channels rather than Atom's fixed 128-channel INT8 keeper. At the kernel level, the work pivots from INT8 Tensor Core pipelines (requiring CUDA-Core dequantization) to Blackwell's native FP4 Tensor Cores with fused dequantization in MMA instructions, a hardware shift that prior INT-based methods cannot exploit.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic mixed-precision quantization framework built entirely on MX formats, filling a gap where prior MX work (AMXFP4, MicroScopiQ) addressed only single-precision 4-bit inference. The practical impact is significant for Blackwell deployments: near-FP16 accuracy at ~5 average bits with 2.3–3.4× kernel speedup over TensorRT-FP16 makes sub-6-bit inference viable on consumer and server GPUs. Limitations include evaluation restricted to Blackwell hardware (RTX 5070Ti/5090/PRO 6000), a small set of model families (Llama, Qwen, Mixtral), and a calibration step that, while fast (~3–7 min), still requires representative data. Open questions include generalization to non-Blackwell architectures, interaction with KV-cache quantization at scale, and whether the threshold-based allocation transfers to training-time quantization.
