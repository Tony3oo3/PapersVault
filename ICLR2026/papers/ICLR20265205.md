## Problem

It is unclear whether generative sequence models trained on sample sequences actually capture the true rule-based structure (world model) of their domain, or merely learn surface-level statistical patterns. Existing verification methods either rely on ad hoc probability thresholds to define the generated language (Vafa et al., 2024) or use misleading metrics like next-token accuracy that cannot distinguish sound from unsound models. The paper asks: can we practically verify the soundness of an implicit world model without such thresholds, and if not, what specific failure modes explain the unsoundness?

## Input / Output

The framework takes a trained GPT-2 chess sequence model (86M parameters, UCI move notation) and a set of 1000 valid 10-move warmup prefixes from its training set. The adversary plays white while the model plays black; the output is an attack success rate (fraction of warmups where the model is forced to produce an illegal move), the specific invalid-move counterexample, and a fine-grained error-type classification (nonexistent piece, opponent's piece, immovable piece, invalid direction, erroneous move, structural error, incorrect end prediction). Evaluation is repeated under greedy, top-k, and top-p decoding policies, and with in-distribution vs. out-of-distribution warmup sequences of varying lengths.

## Method

The core contribution is an adversarial verification framework in which an adversary generates valid chess sequences to force the target model to predict an illegal continuation. The key attack, the Illegal Move Oracle (IMO), selects at each step the legal move that maximizes the model's conditional probability of an illegal next move (a one-step lookahead over the full legal move set). Two additional attacks are proposed: the Board State Oracle (BSO), which maximizes the classification loss of a linear board-state probe, and the Adversarial Detours (AD) attack from Vafa et al. (2024) as a comparison baseline. The paper trains 24 models (6 datasets × 4 objectives: next-token, probability-distribution, and each combined with a joint linear board-state probe) using a GPT-2 architecture, where the PD objective replaces the single-target cross-entropy with a uniform distribution over all legal continuations. The framework is further validated by showing that models appearing sound under in-distribution warmups become unsound under out-of-distribution warmups, and by demonstrating that board-state probes have near-orthogonal gradients to the next-token head, indicating limited causal influence on generation.

## Results

Across all 24 chess models (6 datasets × 4 training objectives), none are sound: the Illegal Move Oracle (IMO) attack achieves near-100% attack success rate (ASR) on most models, e.g., 0.996–1.000 on Random-500k and 0.972–0.992 on Random-10M. The PD (probability distribution) training objective and larger datasets consistently reduce ASR, with the best case being Lichess-16M-NT at 0.387 ASR under IMO. The Adversarial Detours (AD) attack from Vafa et al. (2024) performs comparably to the Random Move baseline in many settings (e.g., 0.066 vs. 0.107 on Lichess-16M-NT), revealing it as a weak adversary. Under top-k sampling (k=4), all attacks achieve higher ASR than under greedy decoding, confirming the robustness of the findings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is against the Adversarial Detours (AD) attack from Vafa et al. (2024), which selects the legal move with the lowest model probability to steer generation toward out-of-distribution regions. IMO dramatically outperforms AD across nearly all model configurations: on Random-500k, IMO achieves 0.996–1.000 ASR versus AD's 0.918–0.985; on the curated Lichess-16M-NT model, IMO reaches 0.387 while AD only achieves 0.066. In many settings, AD performs at a level comparable to the Random Move baseline (e.g., 0.516 vs. 0.673 on Random-10M-NT), suggesting it does not effectively target model-specific vulnerabilities. The Board State Oracle (BSO) attack, motivated by the hypothesis that board state probes causally influence predictions, is also consistently weaker than IMO and sometimes weaker than the benign SMM baseline, providing evidence against the causal role of probes. The paper concludes that a targeted, lookahead-based attack (IMO) is essential for reliable soundness verification, whereas the threshold-free but undirected AD approach from Vafa et al. is insufficient.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the implicit world model verification framework of Vafa et al. (2024), who proposed evaluating soundness by defining the formal language generated by a model via a probability threshold. Here, the authors replace that threshold-dependent approach with adversarial sequence generation that seeks existential counterexamples (valid sequences forcing an invalid prediction), avoiding the need to precisely define the model's generated language. The chess-as-testbed setup follows Toshniwal et al. (2022) and Karvonen (2024), while the board state probing analysis engages directly with the causal-interpretability claims of Nanda et al. (2023) and Hazineh et al. (2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that linear board state probes have essentially no causal role in next-token prediction (near-orthogonal gradients, weak BSO attack, low IoU between probe and model predictions) challenges a widely-cited assumption in the world-model interpretability literature. The demonstration that models trained on large curated datasets appear sound under in-distribution evaluation but fail under OOD warmup sequences reveals a critical limitation of standard evaluation protocols. The adversarial verification framework is a practical, threshold-free tool applicable beyond chess, though the single-architecture (GPT-2 86M) limitation means generalizability across model families remains open.
