## Problem

Despite residual connections suggesting a link to dynamical systems, no settled framework interprets Vision Transformer depth as a well-characterized flow, and it remains unclear whether the block-diagonal structure visible in layer-layer representational similarity matrices reflects genuine functional recurrence (reusable computation) or merely superficial representational overlap. This paper asks whether a ViT's L layers can be accurately rewritten using only k ≪ L distinct blocks applied recurrently, and if so, what dynamical structure this reveals.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretrained ViT (e.g., DINOv2 ViT-B) as a teacher and RGB images (224×224 for ImageNet-1k, 32×32 for CIFAR-100) as input, producing a compact recurrent surrogate (Raptor) that reproduces the teacher's complete internal activation trajectory across all L layers—not merely the final output. The Raptor's layer-wise token representations (cls, register, and patch tokens) are then used as frozen features for downstream linear probes on classification, semantic segmentation, and depth estimation, with the patch embedding and final layer normalization reused directly from the teacher.

## Method

The paper formalizes the Block-Recurrent Hypothesis (BRH), stating that a trained ViT's L layers can be replaced by k ≪ L parameter-tied blocks applied recurrently while preserving the full internal trajectory at equivalent computational cost. Phase boundaries are discovered via a contiguous max-cut algorithm on the layer-layer cosine similarity matrix, solved by O(kL²) dynamic programming. Raptor models are trained with a two-stage procedure: Stage 1 independently trains each block using a hybrid loss that anneals teacher forcing (ground-truth inputs) to autoregressive (self-predicted inputs), while Stage 2 connects all blocks end-to-end and trains purely autoregressively to ensure closed-loop self-consistency. A lightweight depth-scaling MLP in each block maps a scalar layer index to per-component scaling vectors, making the system a non-autonomous dynamical system. The authors further develop a Dynamical Interpretability framework that analyzes the recurrent depth flow via angular convergence on the unit sphere, token-specific angular speed statistics, perturbation sensitivity, and exact Dynamic Mode Decomposition, revealing class-dependent angular attractors, punctuated phase transitions, and late-depth collapse to low-rank collective motion.

## Results

On ImageNet-1k linear probing, Raptor with k=3 recurrent blocks achieves 83.0% top-1 accuracy (≈98% of DINOv2 ViT-B's 84.5%), while k=2 retains 81.2% (≈96%). On ADE20k semantic segmentation, Raptor k=3 reaches 43.0 mIoU, and on NYUv2 depth estimation it achieves 0.618 RMSE. Performance saturates between k=3 and k=4 (83.0% → 83.2% on ImageNet-1k), confirming that a small number of tied blocks captures most of the teacher's representational trajectory. On CIFAR-100, Raptor with 2 blocks closely matches the ViT-B teacher (90.7% validation accuracy), and max-cut partitions consistently outperform random partitions by more than one standard deviation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is DINOv2 (ViT-B and ViT-S) with frozen backbones and linear probes. On ImageNet-1k classification, Raptor k=3 (83.0%) surpasses DINOv2 ViT-S (80.9%) by 2.1 points and recovers 98% of DINOv2 ViT-B (84.5%), while Raptor k=2 (81.2%) already exceeds ViT-S. However, on dense prediction tasks the gap to ViT-B is larger: Raptor k=3 trails ViT-B by 4.5 mIoU on ADE20k (43.0 vs 47.5) and by 0.040 RMSE on NYUv2 (0.618 vs 0.578), and also falls slightly short of ViT-S on both (43.0 vs 44.6 mIoU; 0.618 vs 0.600 RMSE). The paper frames these results not as a compression benchmark but as an empirical existence proof for the Block-Recurrent Hypothesis: the fact that 2–3 tied blocks can reproduce the full internal activation trajectory (not just the final output) at equivalent runtime demonstrates genuine functional reuse across depth. Ablations confirm that the autoregressive training stage is essential—teacher forcing alone collapses to ~3.9% accuracy, while adding the autoregressive objective recovers 68.8 points.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the long line of work connecting residual networks to dynamical systems (Liao & Poggio, 2020; Greff et al., 2016) and the observation of block-diagonal structure in layer-layer similarity matrices (Kornblith et al., 2019; Nguyen et al., 2022) by formalizing the Block-Recurrent Hypothesis and providing constructive verification via weight-tied recurrent surrogates (Raptor). It builds directly on DINOv2's architecture and training, and draws on distillation techniques (Sanh et al., 2019; Shleifer & Rush, 2020) and algorithmic complexity theory (Schmidhuber, 1997; Levin, 1973) to argue that ViT depth admits a compact recurrent program at equivalent runtime. The key departure from prior work is the shift from observing representational similarity to demonstrating functional reuse, and the subsequent development of a Dynamical Interpretability framework using tools like Dynamic Mode Decomposition that had not been applied to ViT depth flow.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that two recurrent blocks recover 96% of DINOv2's linear-probe accuracy provides strong empirical evidence that trained ViTs implement a low-complexity recurrent program, opening a principled dynamical-systems lens for mechanistic interpretability that was previously unavailable. The token-specific dynamics (cls reorientation vs. patch mean-field coherence) and late low-rank collapse offer concrete, testable predictions about how information is consolidated through depth. Key limitations include the small residual accuracy gap (83.0% vs. 84.5%), the fact that causal mechanisms (stochastic depth, training) were only isolated on small CIFAR-100 models, and the restriction to a single foundation model (DINOv2 ViT-B) at scale. Open questions include whether the framework generalizes to other architectures (e.g., convolutional, state-space), whether the recurrent structure is stable across training objectives, and whether the dynamical attractor geometry can be leveraged for robustness or adversarial analysis.
