## Problem

Despite the widespread use of linear non-Gaussian (LiNGAM) models in causal discovery, no independence test was specifically designed for this regime. General-purpose nonparametric tests like HSIC control Type I error but sacrifice statistical power because they do not exploit the structural constraints of linear non-Gaussian data, creating a methodological mismatch between the identifiable model and the testing procedure.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261579/cropped_page2_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes n paired observations (x_i, y_i) where X and Y are assumed to be linear mixtures of independent non-Gaussian components. It computes a symmetric test statistic LiNGIC using a Gaussian kernel on one variable and a degree-2 polynomial kernel on the other (capturing both Y and Y²), then symmetrizes by combining both directions to avoid numerical instability from unbounded polynomial kernels. The statistic is compared against a critical value obtained from a Gamma approximation (via moment matching) of the asymptotic null distribution, yielding a reject/accept decision at a user-specified significance level α.

## Method

The key theoretical insight (Theorem 4.2) is that for linear mixtures of independent non-Gaussian components, independence is fully characterized by the constancy of both the conditional mean E(Y|X) and conditional variance Var(Y|X), proven via the Darmois-Skitovich theorem and Cramér decomposition. This reduces the testing problem to checking Cov(f(X), Y) = 0 and Cov(f(X), Y²) = 0 for all bounded continuous f (Theorem 4.3). The resulting statistic LiNGIC is the squared Hilbert-Schmidt norm of a cross-covariance operator in an RKHS, where one side uses a universal Gaussian kernel and the other uses a degree-2 polynomial kernel whose RKHS spans {y, y²}, making it equivalent to HSIC with these specific kernels but with a provable equivalence to independence under the linear non-Gaussian assumption. The asymptotic null distribution is a weighted sum of chi-squares (Theorem 4.5), approximated by a Gamma distribution via moment matching of the mean and variance (Theorem 4.7), enabling efficient p-value computation without permutation tests.

## Results

On the SACHS causal discovery task (Direct-LiNGAM, n=500), LiNGIC achieves the lowest SHD and highest F1 across all four noise distributions: SHD of 0.2 (Laplace), 0.8 (Uniform), 2.3 (Student-t), and 6.3 (TruncNorm), with F1 scores of 0.99, 0.98, 0.91, and 0.79 respectively. In synthetic independence testing experiments (500 samples, 3–6 components), LiNGIC consistently achieves higher test power than HSIC, HSIC-RFF, dCor, and LFHSIC while maintaining Type I error at the nominal 0.05 level. The power advantage is most pronounced for heavy-tailed distributions (e.g., Student-t: LiNGIC power ~0.95–0.97 vs. HSIC ~0.70–0.85 at d=3–6) and for the TruncNorm distribution where LiNGIC achieves power 0.41 at d=2 versus 0.19 for HSIC.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LiNGIC outperforms all seven baselines (HSIC, HSIC-RFF, dCor, LFHSIC, RDC, MI, SCIT) in test power across nearly all synthetic settings while maintaining comparable Type I error control. In the Direct-LiNGAM causal discovery application, LiNGIC reduces SHD by 52% over HSIC (6.3 vs. 13.2) and by 63% over dCor (6.3 vs. 17.1) on the hardest TruncNorm noise, and achieves near-perfect recovery (SHD=0.2, F1=0.99) under Laplace noise where dCor degrades severely (SHD=4.7). For Student-t noise with 3–6 components, LiNGIC achieves power of 0.93–0.97 versus 0.70–0.85 for HSIC and 0.54–0.69 for SCIT, a gap of 8–33 percentage points. HSIC-RFF and LFHSIC show similar or slightly worse power than standard HSIC, confirming that the advantage comes from exploiting the linear non-Gaussian structure rather than kernel bandwidth tuning. The only setting where LiNGIC does not clearly dominate is Student-t at d=2, where all methods achieve similar F1 (~0.91), likely because with only two components the dependence is simpler and easier to detect with generic tests.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the kernel-based independence testing framework (HSIC, Gretton et al. 2005a; 2007) by exploiting the specific algebraic structure of linear non-Gaussian data. The key departure is a new theoretical characterization (Theorem 4.2) showing that, for linear mixtures of independent non-Gaussian components, independence is fully determined by constancy of the conditional mean and conditional variance—a result proven via the Darmois-Skitovich theorem and Cramér decomposition. This allows the test statistic (LiNGIC) to use a degree-2 polynomial kernel on one side (capturing Y and Y²) paired with a universal kernel on the other, rather than requiring two universal kernels as in standard HSIC. The paper positions this as filling a gap: while LiNGAM-based causal discovery algorithms (Direct-LiNGAM, GIN) rely heavily on independence tests, no test was previously designed specifically for the linear non-Gaussian regime.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result provides a more statistically powerful independence test precisely where it is most needed—causal discovery under linear non-Gaussian assumptions—yielding consistent SHD and F1 improvements in Direct-LiNGAM (e.g., TruncNorm noise: SHD drops from 13.2–17.1 to 6.3). The theoretical characterization is elegant and general: it shows that the non-Gaussianity assumption, combined with linearity, collapses the independence problem to a two-moment check, which is both conceptually simpler and computationally cheaper than full distributional comparison. Limitations include applicability only to the linear non-Gaussian setting, potential numerical instability of the polynomial kernel under heavy tails (mitigated by symmetrization), and the need for future work on conditional independence extensions and faster approximations (e.g., random Fourier features).
