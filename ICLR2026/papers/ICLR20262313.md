## Problem

AI conference peer review has grown to over 10,000 submissions per venue annually, yet how review practices, scoring standards, and decision-making mechanisms actually evolve year-over-year remains largely opaque. Historical review snapshots are overwritten on platforms like OpenReview, community discourse is fragmented across social media, and no unified, longitudinal dataset exists to study cross-venue review dynamics at scale.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262313/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline ingests three heterogeneous sources under distinct consent models: OpenReview API pulls (timestamped submission metadata, multi-dimensional review scores, and discussion threads), lightweight website scrapers for closed venues (CVPR, AAAI), and voluntary opt-in community submissions (6,584 valid reviews to date). The primary output is a structured JSON dataset with 30+ fields per paper, released in two repositories (main dataset and a separate temporal-snapshot repository due to size), alongside an interactive web dashboard supporting multi-granularity filtering by score, decision, institution, and country.

## Method

Paper Copilot's core mechanism is a venue-configurable, modular ingestion pipeline that normalizes multi-source data into versioned paper lists, with a critical innovation in real-time temporal snapshotting: the system continuously archives OpenReview score states during discussion and rebuttal phases, preserving pre-/post-rebuttal trajectories that the official platform overwrites. For empirical analysis, the authors introduce a decision-entropy metric H̄_t that quantifies unpredictability in tier assignment given a paper's mean score, fitted against a logarithmic scaling model (H̄_t ≈ a·log X_t + b) and an ordered-logit model where decision sensitivity κ_t softens with volume. The key finding is that ICLR 2025 is a clear outlier: despite the largest submission volume (11,672), decision entropy fell sharply below the fitted trend, indicating Area Chairs shifted from probabilistic tiering to near-deterministic, score-driven mappings. Rebuttal-phase analysis further reveals that score updates concentrate in holistic ratings (54.8% of papers) rather than individual dimensions (~10–13%), and that reviewer consensus (min-max rating spread) narrows over time with asymmetric acceptance patterns at the borderline.

## Results

ICLR submissions grew from 490 (2017) to 11,672 (2025), with Area Chairs expanding from 31 to 823. Decision entropy (measuring unpredictability in tier assignment given mean score) generally follows a logarithmic scaling with submission volume, but ICLR 2025 is a clear outlier: its entropy fell sharply below the fitted trend, indicating a shift toward near-deterministic, score-driven tiering. In the rebuttal phase, 54.8% of papers experienced changes in overall rating, while specific dimensions (confidence, soundness, contribution, presentation) shifted in only ~10–13% of cases. Reviewer consensus (min–max rating difference) narrowed over the discussion period, with Oral papers converging most strongly by the decision deadline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing methods in the traditional ML sense; its primary analytical comparison is temporal. ICLR 2025 is contrasted against the logarithmic entropy trend established by 2017–2024 data, where the 2025 residual (H̄₂₀₂₅ − (a·log X₂₀₂₅ + b)) is strongly negative, meaning the venue's decision process became sharper and more deterministic than volume growth alone would predict. The paper also contrasts open-review venues (ICLR, where full temporal score dynamics are captured) against closed-review venues (CVPR, ICML, ICCV, ACL), where only community-submitted initial/final scores are available; consent rates for sharing anonymized scores were 53.5% (CVPR), 60.7% (ICML), 59.4% (ICCV), and 67.4% (ACL), yielding 6,584 valid community-submitted reviews. No prior public dataset or system provides the same combination of multi-year, multi-venue, timestamped review archives, so direct head-to-head comparisons with existing tools (PeerRead, MOPRD, ORB) are qualitative: Paper Copilot extends temporal coverage and adds daily snapshot dynamics that those one-off or single-venue datasets lack.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of peer-review dataset work (PeerRead, MOPRD, ORB) by unifying multiple AI/ML venues over a multi-year temporal window and, critically, by preserving timestamped review snapshots that capture score dynamics during rebuttal phases—something prior datasets, which are typically single-venue or single-year snapshots, do not support. It also departs from existing author-profiling tools (Google Scholar, DBLP, AMiner) by linking authors to review-process outcomes rather than just publication and citation metadata, and from CSRankings by adding granular review-score distributions and institutional-level longitudinal analytics. The opt-in community submission mechanism for closed-review venues is a novel data-collection strategy not present in prior peer-review archives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that ICLR 2025 broke the expected log-scaling relationship between submission volume and decision entropy—showing sharper, more deterministic score-driven tiering under maximum volume pressure—challenges the assumption that scale necessarily increases decision uncertainty, though a single-year observation limits causal claims. The rebuttal dynamics analysis (consensus narrowing, asymmetric score-shift patterns at the borderline) provides the first fine-grained, timestamped view of how reviewer behavior evolves during discussion phases. Key limitations include self-selection bias in community-submitted data for closed venues, non-zero error rates in LLM-based affiliation extraction, and the fact that the 2026 ICLR cycle was compromised by a reviewer-information leakage, weakening longitudinal continuity. Open questions include whether the 2025 entropy drop is a sustainable policy shift or a one-off artifact, and how to rigorously quantify and correct for the sampling bias inherent in voluntary data collection.
