## Problem

Existing approaches to improving LLM reasoning treat it as a monolithic skill, applying broad fine-tuning that is expensive and cannot target specific reasoning errors. This paper introduces Reasoning Editing—selectively modifying one reasoning pattern while preserving others—and identifies a fundamental generality-locality trade-off: edits that generalize across all instances of a pattern tend to corrupt other reasoning patterns, and vice versa. No prior work has systematically investigated how to manipulate the neural circuits underlying reasoning to resolve this trade-off.

## Input / Output

The method operates on propositional-logic premise-goal pairs: a set of well-formed formulas over propositional variables (the premises) and a target formula (the goal), from which the model must predict a three-way status label (TRUE, FALSE, or N/A). For circuit reshaping, the input is the model's Edge Attribution Patching scores computed over multiple instantiations of each reasoning pattern, treated as differentiable surrogates for circuit structure. The editing phase then applies a standard LoRA fine-tune on a small revision dataset of premise-goal pairs with corrected target labels, producing a modified parameter vector that must satisfy edit success, generality across all pattern instantiations, and locality on previously correct predictions.

## Method

REdit first discovers the Circuit-Interference Law—that edit interference between reasoning patterns is proportional to the overlap of their neural circuits—then actively reshapes circuits before editing to mitigate the generality-locality trade-off. Contrastive Circuit Reshaping applies an InfoNCE loss over normalized attribution vectors, pulling together circuits of the same reasoning pattern and pushing apart those of different patterns. Meta-Contrastive Learning wraps this in a Reptile-style outer loop that aligns gradients across sampled pattern batches, enabling transfer to unseen reasoning patterns. Dual-Level Protection constrains the reshaping: a KL-divergence term preserves prediction distributions on a correctness set, and a soft null-space projector (with coefficient ρ) removes the component of each inner gradient along the anchor-task loss direction, preventing catastrophic drift. After reshaping, a lightweight LoRA edit on the revision set achieves the desired generality and locality without further circuit manipulation.

## Results

On ContextHub (Qwen-2.5-3B), REdit achieves the best generality across all three difficulty levels: 74.1% (Level 1), 64.8% (Level 2), and 55.0% (Level 3), while simultaneously attaining the highest locality scores of 94.3%, 94.3%, and 94.4% respectively. Compared to the LoRA baseline, REdit delivers up to 16.1% improvement in generality and 12.2% in locality, and averages 2.0% gains over the strongest baselines (ROME/AlphaEdit). On the TemplateGSM math benchmark and the Date Understanding dataset, REdit also consistently outperforms all baselines in generality while maintaining competitive locality, confirming cross-domain transferability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

REdit outperforms all four baselines on generality across every difficulty level. Against LoRA, the gains are most pronounced: +10.3% generality and +9.4% locality at Level 1, narrowing to +4.9% and +2.1% at Level 3 as task complexity increases. ROME achieves the highest locality at Level 3 (94.6%) but trails REdit on generality by 3.5 points and shows significantly lower editing success rates, confirming that its middle-layer MLP focus is insufficient for distributed reasoning. AlphaEdit is competitive on locality (92.2–93.3%) but lags REdit by 0.8–10.8 points in generality, as its null-space constraint limits how broadly edits can propagate. BIMT achieves the second-best generality at Level 1 (72.2%) but suffers catastrophic locality collapse (61.5%), demonstrating that indiscriminate modularization disrupts existing reasoning pathways. On the Date Understanding dataset, REdit extends its lead with 57.6% generality vs. 55.3% (BIMT) and 50.8% (AlphaEdit), while maintaining 90.7% locality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the model editing paradigm (ROME, AlphaEdit, LoRA) from factual knowledge tuples to reasoning patterns, formalizing a new problem with distinct generality and locality desiderata. It builds on Edge Attribution Patching (Syed et al., 2023) for circuit discovery and repurposes the Reptile meta-learning framework (Nichol & Schulman, 2018) combined with InfoNCE contrastive objectives (inspired by the Meta-Contrastive Network, Lin et al., 2021) to actively reshape neural circuits before editing. The key conceptual departure from prior work is treating circuit structure as a malleable precondition for editing rather than a fixed substrate to be analyzed passively, and from BIMT-style modularity training by operating post-hoc on a frozen model rather than during pretraining.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Circuit-Interference Law provides a mechanistic explanation for the generality-locality trade-off in model editing, offering a principled lever (circuit overlap) that future editing methods can target. The 'bonus effect'—where circuit reshaping alone improves baseline accuracy without any explicit edit—suggests that reorganizing internal representations may have broader benefits beyond targeted correction, though this remains unexplained. Key limitations include evaluation only on propositional logic (a relatively simple reasoning domain) and small models (3B/1B parameters), leaving open whether the approach scales to multi-step mathematical or causal reasoning. The framework's reliance on EAP for circuit attribution also introduces computational overhead that may limit applicability to very large models.
