## Problem

Whether in-context learning (ICL) in LLMs constitutes genuine learning in the formal sense has remained unresolved: ICL mathematically fits PAC learning definitions, yet prior empirical evaluations were confounded by prompt sensitivity, memorisation, and natural-language priors, making it unclear whether models truly learn from exemplars or merely deduce surface patterns. This paper calls for a controlled, large-scale empirical characterisation that systematically ablates these confounds.

## Input / Output

The method consumes synthetic strings over artificial alphabets (binary strings, arbitrary symbol sets, adjacency matrices, maze grids) as test instances, paired with a system prompt and n exemplars formatted as input-label pairs. The output is a single next-token prediction: a binary yes/no label for the eight decision problems, or a computed balance value for the Vending Machine (Sum) task. All data strings deliberately avoid natural language to force the model to infer task structure purely from exemplar regularities rather than semantic priors.

## Method

The paper reframes PAC learning to centre on the learner rather than the concept class, formally defining ICL as a learning process in which the LLM's autoregressive conditioning on exemplars constitutes the encoding mechanism (Equation 4). Empirically, they generate synthetic datasets via automata with controlled transition probabilities, producing five test sets at increasing distributional distances (δ = 0 to 0.85) from the training distribution, and evaluate four LLMs across nine tasks using seven prompting strategies (n-shot modus ponens, description, APO, direct encoding, CoT, word salad, SoT) at 0–100 shots, totalling 1.89M predictions. Key ablations include replacing natural-language instructions with random words (word salad/SoT), shuffling exemplar positions, altering label distributions, and comparing against traditional ML baselines (decision trees, kNN, MLP). The central finding is that in the limit of many exemplars, accuracy becomes insensitive to model, prompt style, and linguistic features, yet remains brittle to OOD shifts—especially under CoT and APO—indicating that ICL deduces surface statistical patterns from the prompt rather than robustly learning the underlying task structure.

## Results

Across four LLMs (GPT-4 Turbo, GPT-4o, Mixtral 8x7B, Phi-3.5 MoE) and nine synthetic tasks, peak average accuracy was achieved at 50–100 exemplars, with best tasks being Pattern Matching (94±1%), Hamiltonian (89±2%), and Vending Machine Verification (81±12%). Worst-performing tasks were Vending Machine Sum (16±1%), Reversal (61±11%), and Maze Solve (63±5%). In the limit of many shots, the accuracy gap between LLMs narrows (OLS slope −2.6±0.5), and all prompting strategies converge, but ICL remains brittle to out-of-distribution shifts (average δ-slope −0.6±0.3), with CoT being most sensitive (−1.4). Closely-related tasks show accuracy differences of up to 31%, and traditional ML baselines outperform ICL average performance in half of the evaluated tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares ICL (across four LLMs and multiple prompting strategies) against three traditional ML baselines—decision trees (DT), k-nearest neighbours (kNN), and a multilayer perceptron (MLP)—on five tasks where baselines are applicable. In best-of (single best prompt/shot) scenarios, LLMs outperform the ML baselines in all tasks except PARITY, where the MLP achieves 95% versus the LLM average of 80±3%. However, in average-best scenarios (averaging over all shots and prompts), traditional baselines outperform ICL in half the tasks: kNN beats ICL on Reversal (72% vs. 61±11%) and Vending Machine Verification (DT at 84% vs. 81±12%), while LLMs narrowly win on Pattern Matching (94±1% vs. kNN 87%) and Stack (73±14% vs. kNN 72%). All ML baselines also degrade under OOD shifts, except in PARITY, mirroring ICL's distributional brittleness. The authors note that path-based problems (Maze, Hamiltonian) and arithmetic (Vending Machine Sum) were excluded from baselining since they are typically solved with domain-specific heuristics (e.g., A*).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reframes Valiant's PAC learning framework to formally define ICL as a learning process, extending Wang et al.'s (2023) ICL formulation to account for system prompts and prompting strategies (Equation 4). It responds directly to Borenstein et al.'s (2024) call for empirical evaluation of LLMs' effective learning capabilities, while addressing criticisms of prior empirical work (prompt sensitivity, memorization, data contamination) through synthetic data with artificial alphabets and systematic OOD controls. The APO prompting strategy is adopted from Pryzant et al. (2023), and the formal language/automata task design follows Dan et al. (2022) and Borenstein et al. (2024).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that CoT—the most effective in-distribution prompting strategy—is the most brittle to distributional shift (slope −1.4) challenges the assumption that CoT improves generalization, with direct implications for how practitioners should evaluate and deploy LLMs on unseen data. The convergence of all prompting strategies (including word salad) in the limit suggests ICL's 'learning' is really about autoregressive pattern extraction from data features rather than linguistic understanding, reframing the field's interpretation of ICL. Open questions include whether reasoning models (e.g., o3-mini) escape these limitations, how natural-language priors interact with data-feature learning in realistic tasks, and how to empirically measure the gap between what ICL does versus what it can do.
