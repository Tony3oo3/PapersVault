## Problem

While both biological circuits (Dale's Law: neurons are exclusively excitatory or inhibitory) and artificial networks use signed connections, it remains unclear whether positive and negative weights in object-recognition CNNs systematically segregate different visual feature types (e.g., foreground objects vs. background context). No prior work has tested whether this sign-based functional division emerges spontaneously during training or whether it has a measurable counterpart in primate ventral visual cortex.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261423/cropped_page4_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes pre-trained CNN weight matrices (AlexNet, VGG16, ResNet50, robust ResNet50 at L∞ ∈ {0.5,1,2,4,8}) and macaque multi-unit spike recordings from V1, V4, and IT driven by a 160-image diverseSet spanning AlexNet's 1000-d output space. It produces activity-maximizing feature visualization images (20 per unit per ablation condition via DeePSiM and BigGAN GANs), Dale index scores per channel, representational similarity scores (ensemble cosine similarity and LPIPS between control and ablated visualizations), YOLOv7 objectness scores, and PLS-regression neuron model predictions validated in vivo. For biological experiments, the 160-image diverseSet is split 80/20 for train/test, and in vivo visualizations use 5 images per condition via DeePSiM only due to session time constraints.

## Method

The core technique is selective sign-based ablation: for a given output unit, positive (or negative) weights are sorted by absolute value and the top-k are zeroed to remove a fraction α ∈ [0,1] of that sign's total magnitude, after which CMAES (a zeroth-order evolutionary strategy) optimizes GAN latent codes to find images maximizing the ablated unit's activation. This zeroth-order approach is deliberately chosen to match the gradient-free constraints of in vivo biological experiments. For biological validation, a one-component PLS regression maps AlexNet's 4096-unit penultimate layer to single-neuron firing rates, and the same ablation-plus-visualization protocol is applied to the model's weight vector; in vivo closed-loop CMAES (20–60 iterations, 70–170 ms response window) and background-clearing manipulations test whether reducing putative inhibitory drive enhances neuronal responses. The paper further quantifies sign consistency via a Dale index (fraction of majority-sign weights per channel), tests the role of rectification by training matched ResNet18 models with ReLU vs. Tanh, and examines unsupervised (SimSiam) and adversarially robust training regimes to identify conditions under which sign-based segregation emerges or vanishes.

## Results

Across 18 ImageNet-trained CNN architectures, the Dale index (sign consistency of output-layer weights) increases from ~0.51 at random initialization to 0.55–0.60 after training, and positively correlates with top-1 accuracy (e.g., ResNet152: DI=0.6007, 82.28% top-1; AlexNet: DI=0.5461, 56.52%). Ablating positive input weights substantially reduces objectness and representational similarity (cosine similarity drops sharply), while negative-weight ablation primarily alters background color with minimal object disruption. In adversarially robust ResNet50 models, representational change upon ablation increases monotonically with L∞ robustness (Spearman ρ up to −0.62 at α=0.9 for negative ablation). In macaque ventral stream neuron models (mean test r²=0.27±0.10), positive-weight ablation significantly reduces both predicted and observed population firing rates, while negative ablation has a smaller effect; clearing the background around a neuron's preferred feature enhances its response, consistent with reduced inhibitory drive.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external baseline methods but instead tests the feature-segregation hypothesis across internal conditions. ReLU-activated ResNet18 shows clear sign-based segregation (positive ablation disrupts objects, negative ablation alters background), whereas the Tanh-activated counterpart shows no such asymmetry—both ablation types produce similar representational changes, confirming rectification is necessary for segregation. Unsupervised SimSiam-pretrained ResNet50 retains the positive-encodes-objects pattern but at lower ablation thresholds, indicating the segregation is not an artifact of supervised labels. Adversarially robust ResNet50 models (L∞=0.5 to 8) show progressively stronger segregation: at L∞=8, negative-weight ablation reliably renders backgrounds white, and the Spearman correlation between robustness and representational change reaches ρ=−0.62 (p=2e-8). In the biological domain, PLS neuron models fitted to macaque V1/V4/IT recordings (59 models across two monkeys) replicate the ANN pattern: positive ablation images elicit a consistent population activity drop, while negative ablation images do not, and Dale-like positive units show localized features versus dispersed features for negative units.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mechanistic interpretability literature (Olah et al., 2020; Li et al., 2023) by shifting the lens from absolute weight strength to weight *sign* as the organizing principle for feature segregation. It repurposes the GAN-constrained, gradient-free feature visualization pipeline (Nguyen et al., 2016a; Ponce et al., 2019; Wang & Ponce, 2022) originally developed for biological neurons and applies it symmetrically to both CNNs and macaque ventral-stream neuron models, enabling direct cross-domain comparison. The Dale's Law analogy from classical neuroscience (Dale, 1935; Isaacson & Scanziani, 2011) is transplanted into the ANN setting via a novel 'Dale index,' while the adversarial-robustness and nonlinearity (ReLU vs. Tanh) analyses build on recent findings that training regime and activation function shape representational geometry (Salman et al., 2020; Alleman et al., 2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a convergent representational principle—positive weights encode localized object features, negative weights encode dispersed background/context—that is validated in both ImageNet-trained CNNs and macaque V1/V4/IT recordings, generating concrete, testable predictions for visual neuroscience (e.g., background clearing should enhance neuronal responses, which the authors confirm in vivo). Key limitations temper the claims: most ANN experiments cover only 11–100 classes, the Dale index values are modest (0.51–0.60), and the biological validation relies on a small 160-image stimulus set and a limited number of modeled neurons. Open questions include whether the segregation extends to transformer architectures, whether it is causal for classification performance rather than merely correlational, and how it interacts with the still-unresolved shape-versus-texture debate in CNNs.
