## Problem

Reduced order modelling often requires mapping high-dimensional parameter spaces to linear subspaces (e.g., eigenspaces, POD bases, deflation spaces), but classical interpolation on the Grassmann manifold becomes infeasible or unreliable when the parameter dimension is large. The paper formalises this as a regression problem on the Grassmannian and addresses two gaps: (i) the lack of suitable loss functions that respect the GL(k) invariance of subspace representations, and (ii) the high complexity of the target mapping, which makes direct learning difficult even with dedicated losses.

## Input / Output

The method takes scalar-field parameters (diffusion coefficient fields, potential energy functions, PDE initial conditions) or system matrices (A, B, C for control problems) as input and produces a tall full-rank basis matrix W ∈ R^{n×r} representing a linear subspace. Crucially, the output is an equivalence class under right GL(r) transformations — any two matrices spanning the same column space are valid. The predicted subspace has dimension r ≥ k, where k is the target dimension, implementing the subspace embedding strategy. Inputs are discretised on grids (e.g., 100×100 for 2D elliptic problems, 30×30×30 for 3D) and the network is discretisation-agnostic via the FFNO operator architecture.

## Method

The core contribution is a supervised learning framework where an FFNO neural operator is trained to map parameters to subspaces using two novel loss functions: L₁, a projector-based loss equivalent to the Frobenius distance between orthogonal projectors, and L₂, a stochastic least-squares loss that uses Hutchinson trace estimation to avoid explicit QR decomposition and scales better with subspace size. The key technical insight is subspace embedding: instead of predicting the target k-dimensional subspace, the network predicts a larger r-dimensional subspace (r > k) that contains it. The paper proves this reduces the derivative of smooth functions on the Grassmannian (Theorem 2) and decreases the number of distinct regions in the mapping for elliptic eigenproblems with constant coefficients (Theorem 3), aligning with the spectral bias of neural networks. Empirically, embedding reduces test error from ~30% to ~2% for a 10-eigenvector prediction task and systematically closes the generalisation gap. Training uses the Lion optimiser with weight decay, GELU activations, and a coarse-to-fine grid search strategy for hyperparameter selection.

## Results

On D=2 elliptic eigenproblem (100×100 grid, first 10 eigenvectors), subspace embedding reduces test error from 30% (predicting dim-10 subspace) to 2% (predicting dim-40 subspace), while also systematically reducing the generalisation gap. On quantum mechanics eigenproblems, the L₁(A,B) subspace loss achieves 0.09% relative error (D=1), 0.65% (D=2a), and 15.58% (D=2b), substantially outperforming classical Riemannian interpolation (4.69%, 31.9%, 92.64%) and Z₂-adjusted l₂ regression (2.33%, 19.52%, 48.56%). For parametric PDEs, subspace regression matches or exceeds DeepPOD and is competitive with global POD, while the L₂(A,B;z) stochastic loss scales ~2.5× faster than L₁ for large subspaces (4270s vs 10842s per epoch at dim 40).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Subspace regression with L₁(A,B) loss dominates all baselines on QM eigenproblems: it outperforms classical Riemannian normal-coordinate interpolation by 52× on D=2b (15.58% vs 92.64%) and by 49× on D=2a (0.65% vs 31.9%), and beats Z₂-adjusted l₂ eigenvector regression by 27× on D=2b. On parametric PDEs (Burgers and elliptic), subspace regression performs at or above DeepPOD (Franco et al., 2024) and matches global POD (Volkwein, 2013) among intrusive methods, while bases extracted from FFNO (Tran et al., 2021) and DeepONet (Lu et al., 2019) hidden layers are consistently worse than global POD. Pure regression baselines—FFNO, DeepONet, PCANet (Hesthaven & Ubbiali, 2018), and kernel methods (Batlle et al., 2024)—all show lower accuracy, with PCANet and kernel methods exhibiting significant overfitting. For iterative methods, learned deflation subspaces nearly match optimal deflation in CG convergence, and learned coarse-grid subspaces for damped Jacobi even slightly outperform the oracle, while LOBPCG initialised by subspace regression converges 2–3× faster with ~2 orders of magnitude lower final error than random initialisation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper formalizes subspace prediction as a statistical regression problem on the Grassmann manifold, departing from classical manifold interpolation (Amsallem 2010, Zimmermann 2019) which the authors show is infeasible in high-dimensional parameter spaces. It is closely related to Deep POD (Franco et al. 2024), which also uses projector-based losses for basis learning, but introduces the subspace embedding technique and provides a complexity-theoretic justification rooted in the combinatorics of elliptic eigenproblems. The neural operator backbone (FFNO) and comparison baselines (DeepONet, PCA-Net, kernel methods) draw directly from the parametric PDE surrogate literature, while the loss design leverages Hutchinson trace estimation and randomised linear algebra tools from numerical linear algebra.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The subspace embedding technique—predicting a larger-than-needed subspace to simplify the learning problem—is both practically effective (reducing test error from ~30% to ~2% in the main eigenproblem experiment) and theoretically grounded in the f-principle/spectral bias of neural networks. The complexity analysis (Theorem 3) reveals that the mapping from coefficients to eigenspaces is piecewise constant with combinatorially many regions, explaining why the problem is inherently difficult and why redundancy helps. A key open question the authors highlight is the persistent gap between optimal and learned subspace dimensions: even with embedding, neural networks require 40+ basis functions where 10 suffice, suggesting the learned representations are far from optimal and that resolving this inefficiency remains an important challenge for the field.
