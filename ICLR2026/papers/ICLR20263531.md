## Problem

Scaling laws for LLMs have been extensively studied for Transformers, but no systematic comparison exists between linear-complexity architectures (like xLSTM) and quadratic-complexity Transformers in terms of training efficiency, compute-optimal model sizes, and inference scaling. Additionally, the dependence of optimal model sizes on context length—a critical factor given the quadratic attention cost—was largely ignored in prior scaling law work.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263531/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Both architectures consume text sequences tokenized with the GPT-NeoX tokenizer, packed via first-fit sequence packing into batches of fixed token count (1M or 2M tokens), with context lengths of 2048, 8192, or 16384. The primary output metric is cross-entropy validation loss as a function of model size N, dataset size D, and training compute C (FLOPs). For inference, the outputs are time-to-first-token (TTFT) for the prefill stage and per-step generation time, measured as a function of prefill length (1k–16k) and model size at batch size 1.

## Method

The paper conducts a large-scale empirical study of 640 training runs (292 Llama-2 Transformers, 348 xLSTM models) spanning 80M–7B parameters and 2B–2T tokens (totaling 3.2×10²³ FLOPs), using two training configurations: IsoFLOP (varying N and D under fixed compute budgets) and Token/Param (varying D for fixed N at token-to-parameter ratios of 22–2200). Scaling laws are extracted via both parametric loss-surface fits L̂(N,D) = E + (AN⁻ᵅ + BD⁻ᵝ)^γ and IsoFLOP polynomial fits yielding power-law predictions for optimal N*(H) and D*(H). A key methodological contribution is a precise FLOP and memory-operation accounting (Appendix B) that captures the linear-vs-quadratic context-length dependence, replacing the standard 6ND approximation. Inference scaling is characterized empirically on H100 GPUs and modeled via a roofline-based runtime prediction (τ = FLOPs/α_eff + ε or Bytes/β_eff + ε) that distinguishes FLOP-bound prefill from memory-bound generation for each architecture.

## Results

Across 640 training runs (80M–7B parameters, 2B–2T tokens, total 3.2×10²³ FLOPs), xLSTM strictly Pareto-dominates Llama-2 Transformers in validation loss vs. training compute over nearly five orders of magnitude. In the over-training regime, xLSTM power-law exponents η remain stable at 0.046–0.047 even at token-to-parameter ratios up to M=1100, matching Transformer behavior (η≈0.047–0.050). At 16k prefill length, xLSTM achieves 30–50% lower time-to-first-token (TTFT) than same-sized Transformers, and its generation step time is independent of prefill length while Transformer step time degrades linearly. Compute-optimal xLSTM models consistently have more parameters than compute-optimal Transformers for the same FLOP budget, and their optimal size remains stable across context lengths (2048–16384) while Transformer optimal size drops significantly.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the Llama-2 dense multi-head self-attention Transformer (Touvron et al., 2023), compared head-to-head against the xLSTM 7B architecture (Beck et al., 2025b) under identical training recipes and data (DCLM-BASELINE). xLSTM wins on every metric examined: (1) for any fixed FLOP budget, xLSTM achieves lower cross-entropy loss (Pareto dominance across ~5 orders of magnitude of compute); (2) compute-optimal xLSTM models are larger (more parameters) than compute-optimal Transformers at the same budget, explaining the loss gap; (3) at 16k prefill, xLSTM has 30–50% lower TTFT and its step time is flat across prefill lengths while Transformer step time grows linearly; (4) the compute-optimal model size for xLSTM is stable across context lengths 2048–16384, whereas the Transformer's optimal size drops substantially as context grows due to quadratic attention costs. The paper's Transformer power-law exponent a=0.575 closely matches the independently reported value a=0.571 from Porian et al. (2024), validating the experimental framework. No comparison is made against other linear-complexity architectures (e.g., Mamba, RWKV) or hybrid models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Chinchilla scaling law framework (Hoffmann et al., 2022; Kaplan et al., 2020) and the over-training regime analysis of Gadre et al. (2024) and Sardana et al. (2024) from Transformers to the xLSTM linear-complexity architecture, using the parametric loss surface fit with the γ parameter from Busbridge et al. (2025). It departs from prior scaling law studies in two key ways: (1) it is the first systematic head-to-head comparison of a linear-complexity recurrent architecture against a quadratic-complexity Transformer across five orders of magnitude of compute, and (2) it explicitly examines how context length shifts compute-optimal model and dataset sizes—a dimension largely ignored in prior work. The xLSTM architecture itself is taken from Beck et al. (2025b), and the Transformer baseline follows the Llama-2 dense multi-head design (Touvron et al., 2023), while the FLOP accounting corrects the common 6ND approximation to capture the actual linear-vs-quadratic complexity difference.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that xLSTM Pareto-dominates Transformers in the compute-loss tradeoff, combined with the observation that compute-optimal xLSTM models are *larger* (more parameters) than their Transformer counterparts at the same budget, provides concrete, actionable guidance for practitioners choosing between architecture families. The context-length stability of xLSTM's compute-optimal size—versus the significant degradation for Transformers—has direct implications for long-context and reasoning workloads where quadratic attention costs become prohibitive. Key limitations include the single data distribution (DCLM-Baseline), single-GPU inference measurements, absence of downstream task evaluation, and no consideration of MoE or hybrid attention+xLSTM architectures, all of which the authors flag as important future directions. The practical impact is substantial: if the Pareto-dominance holds at frontier scale and across diverse data, it could shift the architecture landscape for next-generation LLMs.
