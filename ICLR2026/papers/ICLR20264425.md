## Problem

Prior geolocation studies with MLLMs and MLRMs evaluated accuracy on benign datasets of landmarks, tourist attractions, and low-resolution street-view images, treating geolocation as a capability benchmark rather than a privacy threat. No systematic investigation existed for how these models leak sensitive geolocation from everyday user-generated content—selfies, lifestyle photos, and private-residence imagery—where the relevant visual clues are subtle and privacy-sensitive. This paper is the first to frame image-based geolocation inference in MLRMs as a location-related privacy leakage problem grounded in GDPR and CCPA legal definitions of personal and sensitive personal information.

## Input / Output

The benchmark (DOXBENCH) consists of 500 high-resolution iPhone-captured images from six California regions, each annotated with one of three privacy risk levels and EXIF GPS ground truth, plus 50 additional Level-3 images from Google Street View across other U.S. states. Models receive a minimal 'Where is it?' prompt with a structured JSON address output constraint, evaluated under Top-1 and Top-3 prediction settings. Outputs are scored via GLARE (an information-theoretic metric in bits integrating response rate and spatial accuracy), CCPA precise-geolocation accuracy (predictions within 563.88 m of ground truth), and Verifiable Response Rate.

## Method

The paper introduces a three-level visual privacy risk framework (transient individual risk, persistent household risk, both) grounded in CCPA and GDPR definitions, and DOXBENCH, the first benchmark of 500 privacy-annotated images spanning six categories including a novel 'Mirror' class for reflection-based identity leakage. GLARE is a novel information-theoretic metric that decomposes the mutual information between ground-truth location and model output into a risk term (refusal entropy) and a leakage term (spatial accuracy), using the geometric mean of median and mean error distances as a unified error radius under a flat-Earth approximation. CLUEMINER is a test-time adaptation algorithm that iteratively builds a taxonomy of 102 visual clue categories from model chain-of-thought outputs, revealing that MLRMs rely on privacy-sensitive clues such as license plates, street-sign text, and waste-management infrastructure without any built-in suppression mechanism. GEOMINER is a two-stage collaborative attack framework in which a Detector MLLM extracts critical visual clues from an image and an Analyzer MLLM uses those clues for more precise geolocation, demonstrating that external clue injection amplifies privacy leakage beyond what CoT prompting alone achieves.

## Results

On DOXBENCH (500 real-world images), MLRMs achieve a mean GLARE of 1,418.97 bits (Top-1) and 1,711.90 bits (Top-3), both exceeding the non-expert human baseline of 1,309.73 bits. Average CCPA precise-geolocation accuracy reaches 11.61% (Top-1) and 14.95% (Top-3), roughly double the 6.01% human baseline. The strongest models—GEMINI 2.5 PRO (1,987.16 bits GLARE, 21.97% CCPA) and GPT-5 (22.03% CCPA in Top-3)—substantially outperform all others, while Claude-family models remain weakest (≤859 bits GLARE). Prediction difficulty increases monotonically from Level 1 to Level 3, with Mirror cases being the most challenging (799.66 bits GLARE average).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is 268 non-expert human annotators on Amazon Mechanical Turk (GLARE 1,309.73 bits, CCPA 6.01%). All major MLRMs—GPT-5, OpenAI o3, GPT-4.1, and Gemini 2.5 Pro—exceed this human baseline on both GLARE and CCPA accuracy, with Gemini 2.5 Pro reaching 1,987.16 bits (Top-3), a 51% increase over humans. Among MLRMs, Gemini 2.5 Pro and GPT-5 are the strongest geolocation inferrers, while Claude Sonnet 4 and Claude Opus 4 are the weakest (≤859 bits GLARE), largely due to low VRR (frequent refusals). Open-source models Llama 4 Maverick and Scout fall in the middle, with Maverick (1,219–1,254 bits) approaching but not exceeding the human baseline. The paper also compares CoT prompting against vanilla prompting on MLLMs, showing CoT improves CCPA accuracy by 4.4–11.2% and GLARE by 102–1,338 bits, and further shows that the GEOMINER two-model pipeline adds another 3.4–6.4% CCPA accuracy over CoT alone, confirming that externally supplied clues amplify the privacy risk.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior geolocation-inference benchmarks (Liu et al. 2024b, Huang et al. 2025, Jay et al. 2025, Mendes et al. 2024) that evaluate models on public landmarks and low-resolution Street View images, reframing the task as a legally-grounded privacy-leakage problem using high-resolution, privacy-sensitive user-generated content (selfies, private residences). It also shifts the privacy-leakage literature from training-data memorization (Carlini et al. 2021, Kim et al. 2023) to inference-time exploitation, where a minimal 'Where is it?' prompt—requiring no jailbreak—suffices to extract sensitive geolocation. The information-theoretic GLARE metric and the CLUEMINER/GEOMINER tooling are novel contributions with no direct predecessor in the geolocation or MLLM-privacy literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that current MLRMs outperform non-expert humans at inferring precise geolocation from casual photos and that all five tested defenses (Llama Guard 4, blurring, adversarial noise, prompt-based, Gaussian noise) fail to mitigate the risk, leaving a critical and unaddressed gap in model safety. Key limitations include California-only data, iPhone-only capture, and exclusion of private indoor settings, which may understate the full attack surface. Open questions include how to build privacy-aligned mechanisms that suppress use of sensitive visual clues without degrading legitimate geolocation utility, and whether the findings generalize to non-U.S. legal frameworks and diverse device/seasonal conditions.
