## Problem

Diffusion models generalise well in practice, yet perfectly minimising the empirical score matching loss and perfectly sampling would simply recover the training data, implying that some form of regularisation is essential. Existing theoretical analyses rely on algorithm-independent techniques such as uniform convergence and model-structure arguments, which do not capture the algorithmic properties that actually promote generalisation in diffusion models. This paper asks: which algorithmic aspects of score matching and sampling provide the implicit regularisation that enables diffusion models to generate novel data?

## Input / Output

The framework takes a dataset of N i.i.d. samples from a distribution on R^d (assumed supported on a d*-dimensional smooth submanifold with positive reach) together with a score matching algorithm, and produces expected generalisation gap bounds for the score matching loss expressed in terms of a 'score stability' constant. In the sampling analysis, the output extends to expected KL divergence bounds between the true data distribution and samples from a discrete-time Euler-Maruyama sampler, making explicit the trade-off between discretisation error and generalisation. The stability constant is measured in a time-weighted L2 norm over the noised data distribution, with the weighting measure τ over (0,T] controlling which noise scales contribute.

## Method

The core contribution is 'score stability,' an algorithm-dependent generalisation framework adapted from classical algorithmic stability to diffusion models, quantifying how sensitive a score-matching algorithm is to replacing one training example. The paper applies this framework to three settings to identify distinct sources of implicit regularisation. First, for ERM over a hypothesis class, the strong convexity of the denoising score matching loss in a data-dependent weighted L2 space provides inherent stability without additional regularisation, with early stopping of the forward process (ε > 0) controlling the stability constant via the Harnack inequality. Second, for discrete-time Euler-Maruyama sampling, coarse discretisation acts as implicit regularisation, creating a tunable trade-off between generalisation (improved by coarser steps) and discretisation error (worsened by coarser steps). Third, for SGD with gradient clipping and weight decay, the high-variance gradient estimator—arising from random noise injection ξ_{i,j} and random time sampling t_{i,j}—induces contractive behaviour in the training dynamics, which is exploited via reflection coupling to obtain stability bounds that do not grow with the number of iterations.

## Results

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical benchmark tables. The headline results are generalisation gap bounds expressed via a new 'score stability' constant. For ERM with early stopping (Proposition 6), the stability constant scales as ε_stab² ≲ C·(C·C_sm·N⁻² + E[ℓ̂_sm(ŝ)])^c with C = D_H²/σ_ε⁴ ∨ 1/(c_ν·σ_ε^{d*}), yielding near-linear rates in N under the manifold hypothesis. For discrete-time sampling (Proposition 7), the expected KL divergence decomposes into a generalisation term that decays with discretisation coarseness κ and a discretisation error term that grows with κ, revealing an explicit trade-off. For SGD with gradient clipping and weight decay (Proposition 14), the stability bound achieves 1/√N scaling without growing in the number of iterations, thanks to the intrinsic noise in the denoising gradient estimator. A 1D Gaussian toy experiment (N=40) qualitatively confirms U-shaped KL divergence curves as a function of κ, number of steps, and early-stopping time ε.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper is a theoretical contribution and does not run empirical comparisons against competing methods; the CIFAR-10 figure (Figure 1) is purely illustrative of memorisation, not a benchmark. In terms of theoretical positioning, the paper contrasts its algorithm-dependent score-stability framework with prior algorithm-independent approaches: Oko et al. (2023) and Azangulov et al. (2024) require carefully constrained network architectures and specific early-stopping times to control complexity via uniform convergence, whereas the present work holds for any sufficiently small early-stopping time and makes minimal assumptions on the hypothesis class (only an L∞ bound, Assumption 5). De Bortoli (2022) uses a Wasserstein decomposition leveraging empirical measure convergence but does not account for how diffusion models generate novel data. The paper also notes that prior algorithm-dependent analyses (Shah et al. 2023; Chen et al. 2024 for Gaussian mixtures; Li et al. 2023; Yang 2022 for random features) are restricted to specific target distributions, whereas the score-stability framework is general. The main acknowledged limitation is that the approach does not exploit model-class smoothness to adapt to the underlying measure, which prior uniform-convergence results can do.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of diffusion model generalisation work (Oko et al. 2023; Azangulov et al. 2024; Tang & Yang 2024), which relies on algorithm-independent uniform convergence and covering number arguments, by introducing an algorithm-dependent framework based on score stability — a diffusion-specific adaptation of the classical algorithmic stability paradigm (Bousquet & Elisseeff 2002; Hardt et al. 2016). The analytical toolkit draws on the SDE formulation of Song et al. 2021, the denoising score matching loss of Hyvärinen 2005, Wang's Harnack inequality for the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, and the reflection coupling method of Farghly & Rebeschini 2021 for the SGD analysis. Unlike prior algorithm-dependent studies restricted to Gaussian mixtures or random feature models, this work aims for a general theory applicable to arbitrary hypothesis classes under mild structural assumptions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of three distinct, previously overlooked sources of implicit regularisation (denoising, sampler, and optimisation-induced) provides a unified explanation for why diffusion models generalise despite the empirical score matching loss having a unique minimiser that memorises training data. The framework is general and does not require restrictive network architectures, making it applicable to modern diffusion model training pipelines. Key limitations include bounds that hold only in expectation (not high probability), the lack of exploitation of model class smoothness, and minimal empirical validation beyond a 1D toy experiment. Open questions include extending to high-probability and memorisation/privacy bounds, comparing different sampling algorithms (e.g., probability flow ODE vs. SDE), and tightening the ERM analysis using data distribution properties.
