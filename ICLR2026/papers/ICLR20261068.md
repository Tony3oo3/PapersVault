## Problem

Large Reasoning Models generate excessively long chain-of-thought responses with redundant reasoning steps, inflating inference cost without improving accuracy. Existing approaches either curate shorter training data (hurting out-of-distribution generalization) or impose explicit length constraints (which are rigid and discard valuable intermediate reasoning). The paper identifies that token-level entropy exhibits a consistent positive correlation with response length and differs systematically between the thinking and final answer phases, yet this phase-dependent entropy signal has been largely overlooked as a control mechanism for reasoning efficiency.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt (a reasoning question) and produces a structured response consisting of a thinking segment (between  and  tokens) followed by a final answer segment. The  token serves as the phase boundary used to decompose token-level entropy into thinking-phase and answer-phase components. Training is conducted on 7,473 GSM8K samples with a generation cap of 16,384 tokens, and evaluation spans six benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH500, AIME24, AMC23, GPQA Diamond, MMLU) using sampling at temperature 0.6 and top-p 0.95.

## Method

PEAR modifies the per-sample reward in GRPO by computing token-level entropy under the old policy and averaging it separately over the thinking phase (tokens before ) and the final answer phase (tokens after ). The phase reward is defined as P(y) = max(0, H̄_think − α·H̄_answer), which penalizes thinking-phase entropy that is disproportionately large relative to the answer phase, while the max(0,·) keeps the reward in [0,1]. For correct answers, the final reward is r(y) = min(1, s − P(y)); for incorrect or malformed answers, a fixed format score r_fmt is assigned, so entropy shaping only applies to already-correct trajectories. The hyperparameter α (default 1) jointly regulates the effective strength of the thinking-phase penalty: smaller α makes the penalty more aggressive (risking premature commitment), while larger α weakens it toward baseline behavior. This design avoids explicit length targets or data curation, instead using the model's own entropy as an adaptive, domain-agnostic signal to suppress redundant exploration in the thinking phase while preserving flexibility in the final answer.

## Results

Across three model scales (1.5B, 4B, 8B) and six benchmarks, PEAR achieves average response length reductions of 32.4% (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B), 56.6% (Qwen3-4B), and 53.3% (Qwen3-8B) relative to the original models, while preserving accuracy with less than 1% degradation. On Qwen3-8B, PEAR actually improves average accuracy from 77.48% to 77.56% while halving token usage. The method generalizes to out-of-distribution benchmarks (GPQA, MMLU) despite being trained solely on GSM8K, with consistent efficiency gains across all six evaluation sets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PEAR dominates all three baselines on the efficiency–accuracy trade-off. Compared to GRPO (which reduces tokens by only 4.5–9%), PEAR achieves 3–6× greater compression while maintaining comparable accuracy. Against Step Entropy (16–31% reduction), PEAR delivers substantially larger token savings (32–57%) with equal or better accuracy; for example, on Qwen3-8B, PEAR reaches 77.56% average accuracy vs. Step Entropy's 76.85% while using 32% fewer tokens. The most striking contrast is with LCPO: on Qwen3-8B, LCPO incurs a 3.3% accuracy drop (74.18% vs. 77.48% original) for a 35.7% token reduction, whereas PEAR achieves a 53.3% reduction while actually improving accuracy to 77.56%. On the 1.5B model, PEAR's 32.4% reduction with 0.72% accuracy gain over the original outperforms LCPO's 25.3% reduction with a 0.30% accuracy loss. Across all model scales, PEAR consistently achieves the largest token reduction while keeping accuracy degradation below 1%, whereas LCPO and Step Entropy show larger accuracy drops on harder benchmarks (AIME24, GPQA).

## Relation to Prior Work

PEAR builds directly on GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), modifying only the per-sample scalar reward while retaining the clipped-surrogate policy update. It departs from prior efficient-reasoning methods that rely on curated concise training data (Yue et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2025) or explicit length constraints in the reward (LCPO, Aggarwal & Welleck, 2025) by instead using the model's own phase-dependent token entropy as a soft, adaptive signal. The empirical observation that thinking-phase entropy is systematically higher than answer-phase entropy connects to recent work on entropy dynamics in RLVR (Zhang et al., 2025f; Cui et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025c), but repurposes entropy from a capability-shaping tool into an efficiency-control knob, a framing the authors argue has been largely overlooked.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PEAR offers a practical, training-time mechanism to cut inference cost by 32–56% without explicit length targets or data curation, which is directly relevant to deployment economics of reasoning models. The OOD generalization—trained solely on GSM8K yet effective on GPQA and MMLU—suggests phase-dependent entropy is a domain-agnostic signal, though this remains to be validated on more diverse task families (e.g., code, multimodal). Open questions include how PEAR interacts with test-time compute scaling (does suppressing exploration hurt on genuinely hard problems requiring broad search?), whether the α hyperparameter transfers across model families without retuning, and whether the approach composes with other efficiency techniques like early exit or step-level compression.
