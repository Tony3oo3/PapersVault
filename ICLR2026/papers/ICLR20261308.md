## Problem

Strong coresets provide the most powerful guarantees for clustering (preserving objective values for all centers and transferring to submetrics), but their construction requires reading the entire dataset, precluding sublinear-time algorithms. Weak coresets can be built via uniform sampling but lack the submetric transfer property, requiring separate analyses for each metric. The paper asks whether an intermediate coreset notion can combine the broad applicability of strong coresets with the efficiency of uniform sampling.

## Input / Output

The method takes a finite point set P in a metric space (ℓ₁^d, Kendall-tau on permutations, Jaccard on set families, or any metric isometrically embeddable into ℓ₁) and produces a uniformly sampled subset Q of size O(ε⁻² log d) that is a stable (ε/6, 4ε)-coreset with probability ≥ 4/5. Unlike strong coresets, the stable coreset preserves only the relative ordering of costs across centers rather than absolute values, which is what makes uniform sampling viable. For C-dispersed instances (diameter bounded by C times average pairwise distance), the output upgrades to a strong ε-coreset of size O(Cε⁻² log(d/δ)) with probability ≥ 1−δ.

## Method

The paper introduces stable coresets, defined by the condition that if center c₁ has cost at most (1+ε) times c₂'s cost on the coreset Q, then c₁'s cost on P is at most (1+η) times c₂'s cost on P. The proof proceeds in two parts: a general framework showing that ε-RCDA (relative cost-difference approximation, requiring Q to preserve the gap between every center's cost and the median's cost up to a relative ε factor) plus a bounded median-cost condition implies a stable coreset; and an ℓ₁-specific analysis showing that uniform samples of size O(ε⁻² log d) are ε-approximations for axis-aligned half-spaces, leveraging the fact that the VC dimension of threshold functions {τ_{i,r}(x) = 1[x[i] ≤ r]} is O(log d). The ε-approximation property then translates to RCDA via a careful decomposition of the cost difference into per-coordinate contributions. Because stable coresets transfer to submetrics under isometric embeddings, the ℓ₁ result immediately yields the first uniform-sampling coresets for Kendall-tau, Jaccard, Hamming, and tree metrics, and via low-distortion embeddings (e.g., Dvoretzky's theorem) for ℓ₂ as well.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 1.4) shows that a uniform sample of size O(ε⁻² log d) yields a stable (ε/6, 4ε)-coreset for 1-median in ℓ₁^d with probability at least 4/5. Experimentally, uniform sampling achieves relative error rates comparable to importance sampling (Jiang et al., 2024) across all three ℓ₁ datasets (Yellow Taxi NYC, Twitter, Single-Cell Gene Expression), while construction time drops from 82–512 seconds (importance sampling) to 0.0001 seconds (uniform sampling of 500 points). On the Kendall-tau metric (My Anime List, 234K rankings), small coresets preserve solution quality for both heuristic rank aggregation and fairness-constrained ILP optimization, with relative error remaining stable even as dimensionality increases to 33,586.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's sole empirical baseline is the importance-sampling coreset construction of Jiang et al. (2024), which iteratively computes sensitivity scores and samples progressively smaller subsets. On the 1-median problem in ℓ₁ across Yellow Taxi NYC (2.8M points, d=11), Twitter (1.3M points, d=3), and Single-Cell Gene Expression (7,865 points, d=33,586), uniform sampling matches importance sampling in relative error (Figure 1) while being orders of magnitude faster: importance sampling requires 82 s, 114 s, and 512 s respectively, versus 0.0001 s for uniform sampling of 500 points. The key practical advantage is that uniform sampling is dataset-oblivious (no pre-processing or sensitivity-score computation needed), making it trivially applicable in streaming and distributed settings. The paper also notes that importance sampling yields strong coresets (requiring full dataset reads), whereas uniform sampling yields stable coresets — a weaker guarantee that nonetheless transfers to all submetrics and is sufficient for the k-median approximation framework of Ackermann et al. (2010).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper introduces stable coresets as a formal intermediate notion between the well-studied weak and strong coreset paradigms, drawing on Indyk's (1999, 2001) principle of comparing relative costs across centers and Li et al.'s (2001) epsilon-approximation/VC-dimension machinery from PAC learning. Prior uniform-sampling coreset constructions (Danos 2021; Huang et al. 2023a) yielded only weak coresets that do not transfer to submetrics, while strong coreset constructions (Jiang et al. 2024) require reading the entire dataset. The paper's key departure is showing that the relative-cost structure preserved by stable coresets is sufficient for submetric transfer and constraint handling—properties previously exclusive to strong coresets—while remaining compatible with sublinear, dataset-oblivious uniform sampling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The stable coreset framework provides the first uniform-sampling-based coreset constructions for important discrete metrics (Kendall-tau, Jaccard, Hamming) and enables fair rank aggregation and constrained optimization on coresets, which was previously infeasible with weak coresets. The dimension dependence in the theoretical bound (O(ε^{-2} log d)) remains an open question; the authors conjecture it is unnecessary and present empirical evidence supporting this, but a dimension-independent proof is left for future work. The C-dispersed condition under which uniform sampling yields strong coresets is a useful but restrictive additional guarantee that may not hold for arbitrary datasets, limiting the practical scope of the strongest result.
