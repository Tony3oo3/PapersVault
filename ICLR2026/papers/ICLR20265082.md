## Problem

Existing off-policy Q-function estimation methods in MDPs (e.g., Q-regression, FQE) break the curse of horizon but lack strong statistical guarantees: they are naive plug-in learners whose nuisance estimation errors propagate directly into the target, causing plug-in bias and instability under model misspecification. No prior method simultaneously achieves double robustness, Neyman-orthogonality, and quasi-oracle efficiency for individualized Q-function estimation from observational data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20265082/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a dataset of one-step transitions (S, A, R, S̃) generated by a behavioral policy π_b, together with a target evaluation policy π_e and a (possibly restricted) model class G. It outputs a Q-function estimate ĝ ∈ G approximating Q_{π_e}(s,a) = E_{π_e}[Σ γ^t R_t | S_0=s, A_0=a]. The two-stage design means the first-stage nuisance estimates (π̂_b, ŵ_{e/b}, Q̂^1_{π_e}) can be produced by any off-policy method, and the second stage refines them; the model class G can be unrestricted or restricted (e.g., linear) to enforce interpretability.

## Method

The DRQ-learner is a two-stage meta-learner whose second-stage loss L^3_{π_e}(η,g) is derived by computing the efficient influence function of the standard MSE population risk under the evaluation distribution and completing the square to obtain a debiased, Neyman-orthogonal objective. The loss decomposes into two MSE terms with pseudo-outcomes φ_1 and φ_2, each combining a temporal-difference residual {R' + γv_{π_e}(S̃') − Q_{π_e}(S',A')} scaled by a density ratio (one-step inverse propensity weighting for φ_1; conditional stationary state density ratio for φ_2) with the Q-function itself. In the first stage, nuisances (π_b, w_{e/b}, Q^1_{π_e}) are estimated; in the second stage, g is fit by minimizing the empirical version of L^3 over G. This construction yields double robustness (consistency if either Q̂_{π_e} or both π̂_b and ŵ_{e/b} converge), Neyman-orthogonality (first-order insensitivity to nuisance errors), and quasi-oracle efficiency (excess risk bounded by products of second-order nuisance errors), while the one-step transition formulation avoids the exponential overlap decay that plagues trajectory-level IPW.

## Results

The paper evaluates Q-function estimation using relative mean squared error (rMSE) on the Taxi and Frozen Lake environments from OpenAI Gym, varying dataset size (n ∈ [2000, 6000]), effective horizon (h ∈ [3, 20]), and policy overlap (ε_e ∈ [0.1, 0.9]). The DRQ-learner achieves the lowest rMSE across all tested configurations in both unrestricted (Setting A) and restricted linear model class (Setting B) settings. The advantage is most pronounced in low-overlap regimes and at longer horizons, where plug-in baselines degrade substantially. No single numerical summary table is provided; results are presented as rMSE curves in Figures 4–6.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The DRQ-learner is compared against three baselines: Q-regression (Liu et al., 2018), FQE (Le et al., 2019), and Minimax Q-learning (MQL, Uehara et al., 2020). DRQ-learner consistently outperforms Q-regression across all settings, with the gap widening at low overlap and long horizons where Q-regression's cumulative density ratio nuisance causes the curse of horizon to manifest. Against FQE, DRQ-learner also wins, particularly in low-overlap scenarios where FQE's recursive fitting procedure exhibits the 'deadly triad' instability; FQE breaks the curse of horizon but lacks robustness to nuisance estimation error. Compared to MQL, DRQ-learner achieves comparable or better rMSE, with the clearest advantage again in low-overlap settings. In the restricted linear model class (Setting B, Figure 5), DRQ-learner maintains its advantage over all three baselines, confirming that the theoretical guarantees extend to constrained function classes. Overall, DRQ-learner wins or ties in every configuration tested, with the largest margins in the low-overlap, long-horizon regime that the theory predicts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reframes off-policy Q-function estimation in MDPs through the lens of causal inference and orthogonal statistical learning, showing that prominent baselines (Q-regression, FQE) are naive plug-in learners suffering from first-order plug-in bias. It builds directly on the efficient influence function framework from semiparametric efficiency theory (Kennedy, 2022) and the orthogonal learning paradigm (Foster & Syrgkanis, 2019), extending the debiased off-policy evaluation of Shi et al. (2021) from a discrete-state, no-model-class setting to one that accommodates continuous state spaces and restricted function classes. The key departure is deriving the EIF specifically for the Q-function (rather than the scalar policy value) and using it to construct a two-stage meta-learner that simultaneously achieves double robustness, Neyman-orthogonality, and quasi-oracle efficiency while breaking the curse of horizon.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The DRQ-learner provides the first principled theoretical foundation for reliable individualized Q-function estimation from observational data, which is critical for high-stakes applications like personalized medicine where model misspecification and low overlap are common. However, the empirical validation is limited to small discrete environments (Taxi, Frozen Lake), leaving open whether the method scales to high-dimensional continuous state spaces where nuisance estimation (particularly the stationary density ratio) becomes substantially harder. A key open question is the practical cost of estimating the complex nuisance functions (especially w_{e/b}) in realistic settings, and whether the quasi-oracle efficiency guarantees translate into meaningful gains when nuisance convergence rates are slow in high dimensions.
