## Problem

Reward alignment algorithms for flow and diffusion models (e.g., Sequential Monte Carlo, search, guidance) require sampling stochastic Markov transitions, which has traditionally necessitated SDE sampling. However, SDE sampling is significantly less efficient and often less performant than ODE sampling, creating a fundamental trade-off: alignment algorithms that need stochasticity must sacrifice the efficiency that makes large-scale deployment practical. The paper asks whether one can obtain stochastic transitions using ODEs instead.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained flow matching or diffusion model (e.g., FLUX at 768×1360, SiT/DiT on ImageNet256) with no retraining or fine-tuning, a text prompt, and an initial Gaussian noise sample. It produces a generated image via K sequential GLASS transitions, each simulated with M ODE steps (total K·M neural network evaluations). A tunable correlation parameter ρ ∈ [-1,1] controls the degree of stochasticity in each transition; ρ = α_t σ_{t'}/(σ_t α_{t'}) recovers DDPM transitions exactly, while other values (e.g., ρ=0.4) yield different stochastic behavior at the same efficiency. The method is a drop-in replacement for SDE sampling in any existing alignment pipeline.

## Method

GLASS Flows construct an 'inner flow matching model' u_s(x̄_s | x_t, t) that samples Markov transitions p_{t'|t} via a deterministic ODE, with stochasticity introduced only through the initial condition x̄_0 ~ N(γ̄ x_t, σ̄_0² I). The key insight is that the GLASS denoiser — the posterior mean of the latent z given two Gaussian measurements (x_t, x_{t'}) — can be reduced to a single evaluation of the pre-trained denoiser D_{t*}(α_{t*} S(x)) via the sufficient statistic S(x) = μ^T Σ^{-1} x / (μ^T Σ^{-1} μ), where t* is determined by inverting the effective noise scale g(t) = σ_t²/α_t². The resulting velocity field is a weighted sum u_s = w_1(s) x̄_s + w_2(s) D_{μ(s),Σ(s)}(x_t, x̄_s) + w_3(s) x_t, requiring only one neural network call per ODE step. This means the inner flow matching model is retrieved entirely from the pre-trained model with no additional training, combining ODE efficiency with SDE-like stochastic evolution.

## Results

On the FLUX text-to-image model with N=8 particles and 400 NFEs, FKS-GLASS(ρ=0.4) achieves the best scores across all four reward models: CLIP 39.8, Pick 24.1, HPSv2 0.318, and ImageReward 1.40, while simultaneously improving GenEval to 72.6–74.3 (vs. 63.0–64.1 for FKS-SDE). For posterior sampling on ImageNet256 (SiT/DiT), GLASS Flows achieve significantly lower FID than DDPM at the same number of simulation steps (e.g., M=6), and yield higher correlation with ground-truth value functions. When combined with gradient guidance (Table 2), FKS-GLASS+∇ further improves ImageReward to 1.52 and GenEval to 73.1 over FKS-GLASS alone (1.45 / 72.7).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two key baselines: (1) DDPM/SDE sampling (Ho et al., 2020), the standard stochastic sampling used by prior reward-alignment methods, and (2) FKS-SDE from Singhal et al. (2025), the previous state-of-the-art for inference-time scaling via Feynman-Kac steering. GLASS Flows decisively outperform DDPM sampling on posterior sampling quality: at M=6 simulation steps, GLASS achieves substantially lower FID than DDPM across all noise levels t, and yields higher value-function correlation. In the main FKS experiment (Table 1), FKS-GLASS(ρ=0.4) beats FKS-SDE on every reward model (e.g., ImageReward 1.40 vs. 1.19, a 17.6% relative gain) while also improving GenEval by 4–10 points (e.g., 74.3 vs. 63.8 for IR), showing that the SMC algorithm benefits from both the stochasticity of SDEs and the efficiency of ODEs. Notably, FKS-SDE fails to beat a simple Best-of-N baseline (BoN-ODE), whereas FKS-GLASS surpasses BoN-ODE on all four rewards and GenEval, demonstrating that the efficiency gap of SDE sampling was the bottleneck preventing SMC from outperforming naive sampling. The ρ=0.4 correlation schedule consistently outperforms the DDPM-matched ρ, confirming that the extended transition family provides additional flexibility beyond standard DDPM transitions.

## Relation to Prior Work

GLASS Flows builds directly on the flow matching framework (Lipman et al., 2022) and the score-based SDE sampling paradigm (Song et al., 2021), positioning itself as a drop-in replacement for SDE transitions in inference-time reward alignment algorithms like Feynman-Kac Steering (Singhal et al., 2025). The core mathematical tool — the sufficient statistic for Gaussian measurements — is borrowed from classical theoretical statistics (Fisher, 1922) and applied here to show that a pre-trained flow matching model implicitly contains an 'inner' flow matching model for sampling transitions. The method is conceptually related to TADA (Chen et al., 2025b), which uses the same Gaussian conjugacy/sufficient statistic argument for accelerated sampling, and to Transition Matching (Shaul et al., 2025), which studies discrete-time transitions but modifies pre-training rather than operating at inference time. The M=1 special case recovers DDIM (Song et al., 2020a), anchoring the method in the discrete-time diffusion literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GLASS Flows eliminates the long-standing efficiency-stochasticity tradeoff in flow and diffusion model sampling: stochastic transitions (needed for SMC, search, and guidance-based reward alignment) previously required slow SDE simulation, while fast ODE sampling was deterministic. By showing that stochasticity can be injected through the initial condition of an inner ODE rather than through stochastic increments, the paper provides a simple, training-free, drop-in solution that is likely to be widely adopted across the growing ecosystem of inference-time scaling methods. Open questions include the optimal choice of the correlation parameter ρ (currently tuned per model), whether the method extends to discrete-time models without the continuous-time requirement, and its applicability to reward fine-tuning and image editing pipelines that also rely on SDE sampling.
