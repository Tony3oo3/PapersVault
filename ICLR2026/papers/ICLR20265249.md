## Problem

Despite modern LLMs using smooth activations (GELU, SiLU) where negative pre-activations produce both nonzero output and gradient, neuron-level interpretability has largely treated the negative region as inert—a carryover from the ReLU era. This paper asks whether negative pre-activations are functionally leveraged and, if so, for what purpose, focusing on a sparse subpopulation of Wasserstein neurons whose output distributions deviate strongly from Gaussian.

## Input / Output

The method consumes pre-trained LLM weights (Pythia 70M–12B, Llama 3.1 8B, Mistral 7B v0.3, Qwen3 8B Base) and feeds WikiText 2 through the gate/up projection of each MLP block to collect per-neuron pre-activation distributions. It produces per-neuron Wasserstein distance and mapping difficulty scores, then applies sign-specific ablations (zeroing only negative pre-activations) and reports resulting perplexity, BLiMP/TSE accuracy, non-grammatical benchmark scores, POS-level surprisal deltas, and layer-wise error profiles. Training-dynamics analyses additionally track the same neuron cohort across Pythia checkpoints to correlate WD emergence with grammatical accuracy.

## Method

The paper identifies the top ~1% of neurons by Wasserstein distance from a unit Gaussian in the linear projection immediately preceding the MLP nonlinearity, then performs a minimal sign-specific intervention: clamping only their negative pre-activations to zero (a_k' = max(a_k, 0)) while leaving all weights, positive activations, and other neurons untouched. Two controls isolate the effect: a random-neuron ablation of equal size and a perplexity-matched ablation of many low-WD neurons (requiring ~20–50% of units versus 1%). This yields a double dissociation—the Wasserstein ablation sharply degrades BLiMP and TSE while sparing non-grammatical benchmarks, whereas the perplexity-matched control does the opposite. Layer-wise ablations in Llama 3.1 8B show early layers (especially the first four) are most vulnerable and that small local errors compound monotonically across depth. A single-neuron case study in Pythia 1.4B reveals 'negative differentiation': the most separated input pairs are both driven to distinct negative pre-activation values rather than one positive and one negative, preferentially separating syntactic scaffolding tokens (determiners, prepositions, auxiliaries). Training-dynamics analysis confirms the ablation becomes progressively more harmful as Wasserstein neurons acquire their non-Gaussian structure over the first ~50B tokens.

## Results

Ablating only the negative pre-activations of the top 1% Wasserstein neurons per layer causes TSE accuracy to drop from 84.4% to 55.2% in Llama 3.1 8B and from 84.6% to 54.6% in Mistral 7B, while BLiMP drops from 81.2% to 77.5% and 83.3% to 77.9% respectively. A perplexity-matched ablation of 40% (Llama), 50% (Mistral), or 20% (Qwen) of the least-entangled neurons leaves TSE at or above baseline (85.9%, 87.2%, 82.4%) and BLiMP nearly unchanged. On non-grammatical benchmarks, the pattern inverts: the perplexity-matched low-WD ablation yields ~11% average absolute error increase versus only ~4% for the Wasserstein ablation in Llama, establishing a double dissociation between syntax and general capabilities.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods but instead contrasts three internal ablation conditions to isolate the causal role of negative pre-activations in Wasserstein neurons. The 1% Wasserstein ablation (zeroing only negative pre-activations of top-WD neurons) produces the largest grammatical degradation: TSE drops by ~29 points in Llama and ~30 points in Mistral, while the perplexity-matched control (ablating 40–50% of low-WD neurons to match the same perplexity increase) actually *improves* TSE slightly above baseline. On non-grammatical benchmarks the relationship inverts: the perplexity-matched low-WD ablation causes ~11% average absolute error increase (e.g., ARC Challenge 53.4→38.1, HellaSwag 78.9→69.4 in Llama), whereas the Wasserstein ablation causes only ~4% (ARC Challenge 53.4→48.6, HellaSwag 78.9→75.3). The 1% random ablation produces negligible change on both grammar and non-grammar tasks, confirming that the effect is specific to the entangled subpopulation. This double dissociation—Wasserstein ablation uniquely harms grammar while the perplexity-matched ablation uniquely harms general capabilities—demonstrates that the negative pre-activation region of these sparse neurons is a functionally distinct computational substrate for syntax.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the identification of Wasserstein neurons by Sawmya et al. (2025), who characterized this subpopulation via Wasserstein distance from a Gaussian baseline and showed its sensitivity to sparsification, but did not interpret their functional role or link them to specific capabilities. It departs from the broader neuron-level interpretability literature (Gurnee et al., AlKhamissi et al., Mueller et al.), which has historically defined neuron 'activity' via positive pre-activations—a ReLU-era heuristic that implicitly treats the negative region as inert despite smooth activations like GELU and SiLU producing nonzero output and gradient there. The paper also connects to the grammar-acquisition literature (Marvin & Linzen, Tenney et al., Duan et al.) by providing a mechanistic, neuron-level account of where and how syntactic computation is implemented in the MLP, complementing prior findings that localize syntax to attention patterns or dense hidden subspaces.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result challenges a widespread working assumption in interpretability: that negative pre-activations in smooth-activation models are a mere optimization byproduct rather than a functional substrate. The clean double dissociation—1% Wasserstein neurons critical for syntax but not non-grammar, while 40–50% low-WD neurons show the reverse—provides strong causal evidence for a structured, sign-specific organization of computation. Open questions include whether the negative-differentiation mechanism generalizes beyond English and beyond the specific architectures studied, whether fully controlled NN/PN/PP-specific perturbations can isolate the contribution of each sign pattern (the authors acknowledge a structural confound in their ablation method), and whether interpretability tooling (sparse autoencoders, probing) can be adapted to systematically extract features from the negative pre-activation region.
