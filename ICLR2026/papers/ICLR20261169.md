## Problem

Standard language model evaluation metrics capture accuracy or capability but ignore inference costs, leaving no principled way to determine whether a model's economic value justifies its computational expense or whether it is cheaper than equivalent human labor. The paper asks: what is the expected monetary cost of generating a correct solution, and how does this trade-off evolve across the model ecosystem over time?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261169/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a text problem from a benchmark, a language model inference pipeline with its per-token input/output pricing, and a human-expert cost estimate (derived from qualification, hourly rate, and time-per-question) as inputs. It produces a cost-of-pass value (expected dollars per correct solution for a single model), a frontier cost-of-pass (the minimum across all available models and the human baseline), a gain metric quantifying the economic contribution of a new model release relative to the prior frontier, and an essentialness score measuring the relative improvement attributable to a model family under counterfactual removal.

## Method

The paper adapts Farrell's (1957) production efficiency theory and Aigner et al.'s (1977) stochastic frontier extension to LMs, treating each model as a stochastic producer where the expected number of independent attempts to obtain a correct solution is 1/R_m(p). The cost-of-pass v(m,p) = C_m(p)/R_m(p) integrates both success probability and per-attempt token cost into a single dollar-denominated efficiency metric, with infinite cost signaling infeasibility. The frontier cost-of-pass V_p(M ∪ M_0) takes the minimum over all available LM strategies and a human-expert baseline, guaranteeing a finite reference point and enabling direct economic comparison with human labor. Counterfactual analysis removes entire model families (lightweight, large, reasoning) to quantify each family's essentialness to the current frontier, while temporal tracking of V_p(M_t) as new models are added reveals exponential cost-decay trends with measurable halving times.

## Results

The frontier cost-of-pass (minimum expected dollar cost per correct solution) reveals distinct economic niches: lightweight models (e.g., Llama-3.1-8B at $4.8×10⁻⁵) dominate basic quantitative tasks, large models (e.g., Llama-3.1-405B at $10.43) lead on knowledge-intensive GPQA-Diamond, and reasoning models (e.g., OpenAI o3-mini at $2.03) are most cost-effective on complex quantitative AIME-24. Over May 2024–Feb 2025, the frontier cost-of-pass for MATH-500 halved every ~2.6 months and for AIME-2024 every ~7.1 months. Counterfactual analysis shows reasoning models are 81% essential for AIME-2024 and 74.4% for MATH-500, while lightweight models are 93.5% essential for Two-Digit Addition.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates three inference-time techniques against the unmodified model frontier (lightweight + large models). Self-Refinement (Madaan et al., 2023) yields a 24.9% relative cost reduction on GPQA-Diamond and 6.7% on BBQ, but 0% on all quantitative tasks, indicating limited general economic benefit. Majority Voting at k=3 and k=4 (Wang et al., 2023, self-consistency) produces 0% relative improvement across all six datasets, meaning the additional inference cost fully offsets any accuracy gains. In contrast, the budget-aware technique TALE-EP (Han et al., 2025) delivers meaningful reductions on multiple tasks: 66.6% on GSM8K, 50% on GPQA-Diamond, 24.5% on BBQ, and 16.6% on AIME-24, though gains are uneven (only 0.2% on MATH-500). Overall, the paper concludes that model-level innovations (new releases) are the primary drivers of frontier cost-of-pass reduction, while common performance-oriented inference-time techniques rarely justify their added cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts Farrell's (1957) theory of productive efficiency and Aigner et al.'s (1977) stochastic frontier production functions to the LM evaluation setting, formalizing 'cost-of-pass' as the expected monetary cost per correct solution. It departs from prior cost-aware evaluation approaches that rely on fixed inference budgets (Wang et al., 2024), heuristic performance scores (McDonald et al., 2024), or non-monetary proxies like conciseness (Nayab et al., 2024) by grounding the metric in economic production theory, yielding a single interpretable dollar-denominated quantity without predefined budget constraints. The framework also connects to empirical work tracking LLM inference price trends (Cottier et al., 2025; Gundlach et al., 2025) and to the growing literature on inference-time compute scaling (Snell et al., 2024; Parashar et al., 2025), but reframes these through the lens of stochastic production efficiency rather than raw accuracy or token counts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a principled, economically interpretable tool for model selection and for tracking progress in the LM ecosystem, with the practical implication that complementary model-level innovations (lightweight for basic tasks, reasoning for complex quantitative) drive cost-efficiency far more than inference-time techniques. The finding that majority voting and self-refinement yield near-zero economic benefit across most tasks challenges the widespread assumption that test-time compute scaling is a cost-effective strategy. Key limitations include the binary success criterion (no partial credit), simple per-token cost modeling that omits verification and orchestration overheads, potential data contamination on public benchmarks, and a demonstrated vulnerability to benchmark hacking via cheap random guessers on multiple-choice tasks. Open questions include extending the framework to continuous utility functions, risk-adjusted objectives, and multi-turn agentic settings with tool-use costs.
