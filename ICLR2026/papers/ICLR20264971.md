## Problem

Model collapse—progressive degradation of prediction performance when models are iteratively retrained on their own synthetic outputs—has been rigorously characterized only in low-dimensional settings or for Gaussian features. This paper asks whether mixing fresh real data with synthetic outputs can prevent collapse in the overparameterized regime (p/n→γ>1), and what the optimal mixing ratio is for minimizing long-term prediction error.

## Input / Output

At each iteration t, the procedure consumes a fixed feature matrix X ∈ ℝ^{n×p} (p>n), a freshly generated real response vector y_t = Xβ + ε_t, and a synthetic response vector ỹ_t = Xβ̂_{t−1} + ε̃_t produced by the previous iteration's estimator. The estimator is updated as a weighted mixture with proportion w on real labels and (1−w) on synthetic labels, with regularization parameter λ controlling the ridge penalty. The output is a closed-form asymptotic risk R(β̂_t; β) as n→∞ and t→∞, from which the unique optimal mixing ratio w* is derived analytically.

## Method

The paper derives exact limiting generalization error formulae for min-ℓ₂-norm interpolators and ridge regression under iterative real/synthetic mixing using random matrix theory, specifically the companion Stieltjes transform m(z) of the free convolution between the population spectral distribution H and the Marchenko–Pastur law. For min-norm interpolation, the bias is independent of both iteration t and mixing weight w, so the risk is minimized purely through the variance factor c(w) = (w²+(1−w)²)/(w(2−w)), yielding the universal optimum w* = 1/φ ≈ 0.618 for any bounded-eigenvalue covariance. For ridge regression, both bias and variance depend on w, λ, and t; the paper proves the limiting risk is log-convex in w with a unique minimizer w* ∈ [1/2, 1] that increases with SNR, converges to 1/φ as λ→0, and approaches 1 as λ→∞. The analysis is instantiated for isotropic, spiked-covariance, and random-effects models, where the spectral geometry of Σ and the alignment of β with the spike direction govern the precise form of w*.

## Results

For min-ℓ₂-norm interpolation, the asymptotic risk is σ²c(w)𝒱 + b*ℬ where c(w) = (w²+(1-w)²)/(w(2-w)), and the optimal mixing ratio is universally w* = 1/φ ≈ 0.618 (reciprocal of the golden ratio) for any bounded-eigenvalue covariance. For ridge regression, the risk is log-convex in w with a unique minimizer w* ∈ [0.5, 1], satisfying w* → φ⁻¹ as λ→0 and w* → 1 as λ→∞; w* is increasing in SNR. In the isotropic case (Σ = αI), the risk simplifies to σ²c(w)/(γ−1) + b*(1−1/γ), and simulations at n=200, p=γn (γ ∈ {1.5, 2, 3}) confirm theoretical predictions with empirical risks matching within a few iterations.

**Summary of optimal mixing ratios across model classes (overparameterized regime, p/n → γ > 1).**

| Setting | Optimal w* | Range | λ → 0 | λ → ∞ | Key condition |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Min-ℓ₂-norm interpolator (general Σ) | 1/φ ≈ 0.618 | fixed | 1/φ | 1/φ | Bounded eigenvalues |
| Ridge, isotropic (Σ = αI) | w*(λ) ∈ [0.5, 1] | varies | φ⁻¹ | 1 | — |
| Ridge, random-effects (βᵢ iid) | w*(λ) ∈ [0.5, 1] | varies | φ⁻¹ | 1 | G = H |
| Ridge, spiked covariance | w*(λ) ∈ [0.5, 1] | varies | φ⁻¹ | 1 | G ≠ H |
| No fresh real data (fixed y) | w* = 1 | — | 1 | 1 | Collapse unavoidable |
| Dynamic mixing (adaptive w_t) | w*_t → 1/φ | decreasing | — | — | w*_t = (1+w*_{t-1})/(2+w*_{t-1}) |

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without direct method-vs-method benchmark comparisons; its 'baselines' are prior theoretical frameworks. Compared to He et al. (2025), which proved the golden-ratio mixing property only for low-dimensional MLEs and nonparametric estimators, this paper extends the result to the overparameterized regime (p > n, p/n → γ > 1) for min-ℓ₂-norm interpolators and ridge regression under general covariance structures. Compared to Shumailov et al. (2024) and Dohmatob et al. (2024a), which demonstrated inevitable collapse for Gaussian features in low dimensions, this paper shows that mixing fresh real data with synthetic outputs provably prevents collapse in the overparameterized setting. Compared to Dohmatob et al. (2025), which studies model collapse via pooling data from different distributions, this paper uses a fundamentally different mechanism (iterative retraining on model-generated synthetic labels) and provides explicit optimal mixing ratios. The simulations (Figures 1–3) validate all theoretical predictions, with empirical risks matching closed-form expressions at moderate sample sizes (n = 200–500), including in a robustness check where Σ violates the bounded-eigenvalue assumption.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the model-collapse-under-mixing framework of He et al. (2025) and the low-dimensional mixing analyses of Gerstgrasser et al. (2024), Dey & Donoho (2024), and Kazdan et al. (2024) from low-dimensional settings to the overparameterized regime (p > n) with general covariance structures. The bias-variance decomposition for min-norm interpolators from Hastie et al. (2022) provides the foundational single-iteration risk characterization that this paper iterates over time. The key methodological departure is the use of random matrix theory (Stieltjes transforms, free convolution, Marchenko-Pastur distributions) to handle the high-dimensional limit, which prior model-collapse work could not access due to its restriction to fixed or low-dimensional feature spaces.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first rigorous characterization of how synthetic-data mixing prevents model collapse in overparameterized linear regression, providing concrete optimal mixing ratios (w* = 1/φ for interpolation, w* ≥ 1/2 for ridge) that practitioners can use as design principles. The universal lower bound w* ≥ 1/2 across isotropic, spiked, and random-effects covariance models gives a clear, actionable guideline: always weight real data at least as heavily as synthetic data. Open questions include extending the analysis to non-linear models, other ℓ_p interpolation geometries, and multi-layer architectures, where the random matrix techniques used here may not directly apply. The paper is limited to linear models and specific covariance assumptions, so its direct applicability to modern deep learning remains indirect.
