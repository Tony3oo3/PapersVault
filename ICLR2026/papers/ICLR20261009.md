## Problem

IR benchmark datasets contain 'holes'—unlabeled relevant chunks that systematically underestimate retrieval performance, especially for advanced fusion-based retrievers. Existing automated relevance assessment methods rely on a single LLM judge, which suffers from overconfidence and miscalibrated confidence scores, making confidence-based escalation to humans both inaccurate and costly. The paper asks whether multi-agent debate can produce more reliable automatic labels and more principled escalation decisions without requiring calibration training or manual threshold tuning.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a query-chunk-answer triplet (query text, a candidate passage, and the set of ground-truth answers for that query) and produces either a binary relevance label (1 if the chunk supports at least one answer, 0 otherwise) or an escalation signal. When escalation occurs, the full multi-round debate history—containing both agents' reasoning, extracted evidence sentences, and intermediate labels—is passed to human annotators as a structured supportive resource rather than leaving them to judge from scratch. The debate history thus serves a dual role: enabling agents to converge efficiently within two rounds and providing organized context that measurably improves human annotation quality (IAA Fleiss κ rises from 0.50 to 0.62).

## Method

DREAM initializes two LLM agents (Llama3.3-70B-Instruct, temperature 0.0) with forced opposing stances—one arguing relevance, the other irrelevance—to prevent premature consensus and single-perspective bias. The agents then engage in up to R=2 rounds of reciprocal critique, where each round each agent reviews the prior round's arguments, extracts evidence sentences from the chunk, and produces a revised label with justification. Inter-agent agreement on a label triggers automatic acceptance; persistent disagreement after R rounds triggers escalation to human annotators (MTurk, three workers, majority vote) who receive the debate history as context. This agreement-based routing eliminates the need for confidence calibration, logistic regression training, or manually tuned escalation thresholds that plague methods like LARA. Ablations show that increasing agents, using heterogeneous model families, or raising temperature all degrade performance, confirming that the two-agent opposing-stance design with a single strong model family is optimal.

## Results

DREAM achieves 95.2% balanced accuracy (bAcc) on non-escalated cases with only 3.5% human escalation, surpassing the Human-Only reference (93.8% bAcc at 100% human involvement). The single-agent LLMJudge baseline achieves only 73.9% bAcc with 0% escalation, while LARA at the same 3.5% escalation ratio reaches just 82.1% bAcc. To match DREAM's accuracy, LARA must escalate 50% of cases (96.3% bAcc), making it 14× more expensive in human effort. DREAM is also 200× cheaper and 3.5×–7.0× faster per sample than Human-Only.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LLMJudge (Shivani et al., 2024; Park et al., 2025), DREAM improves bAcc by 21.3 points (95.2% vs. 73.9%), primarily because LLMJudge's single-agent design suffers from severe overconfidence on irrelevant cases (50.2% recall) with no escalation mechanism. Against LARA (Takehi et al., 2025) at the matched 3.5% escalation ratio, DREAM outperforms by 13.1 bAcc points (95.2% vs. 82.1%), revealing that LARA's confidence-based routing miscalibrates which cases to escalate. LARA only surpasses DREAM at 50% escalation (96.3% vs. 95.2%), requiring 14× more human involvement and thus lacking scalability. Compared to the Human-Only MTurk reference, DREAM achieves 1.4 points higher bAcc (95.2% vs. 93.8%) while involving humans in only 3.5% of cases versus 100%, and is 200× cheaper per sample. The debate history provided to human annotators during escalation further boosts their bAcc from 87.3% to 92.0% and IAA (Fleiss κ) from 0.50 to 0.62, demonstrating genuine AI–human synergy beyond what confidence-based methods offer.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant single-agent LLM-as-judge paradigm for relevance assessment (UMBRELA, D-MERIT, MIRAGE, SynDL) and from confidence-based escalation methods like LARA, which rely on miscalibrated token probabilities to route uncertain cases to humans. It repurposes the multi-agent debate literature (Du et al., 2023; Liang et al., 2024; Chan et al., 2023)—previously applied to answer quality, knowledge conflicts, and response evaluation—toward IR relevance labeling with a key twist: inter-agent agreement (rather than a single model's confidence) serves as the escalation signal, and the debate history is passed to human annotators as a supportive resource. The paper also extends the known 'holes' problem in IR benchmarks (Craswell et al., 2020; Rassin et al., 2024) by quantifying the bias at scale and connecting it to downstream RAG evaluation misalignment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

BRIDGE is a practically significant refinement of two widely-used benchmarks (BEIR, RobustQA), uncovering 29,824 missing relevant chunks (428% of original gold) and demonstrating that existing benchmarks systematically understate the performance of advanced fusion-based retrievers (rerank, rewrite, recent dense models). The RAGAlign finding—that benchmark holes, not just parametric knowledge conflicts, drive retrieval-generation misalignment—offers a new diagnostic lens for RAG evaluation. Limitations include reliance on a single LLM family (Llama3.3-70B) for the debate agents, the answer-aware formulation that may not transfer to open-ended relevance, and the 25-retriever pool that, while shown to saturate, still bounds the candidate space. Open questions include whether the agreement-based escalation principle generalizes to other annotation tasks and whether the debate framework can be adapted to non-answer-aware settings.
