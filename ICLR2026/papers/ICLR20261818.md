## Problem

Test-time look-ahead reasoning (e.g., MuZero's MCTS) dramatically improves game-playing agents in perfect information settings, but extending it to imperfect information games is fundamentally harder: sound reasoning must operate over all hidden states consistent with public knowledge, and the number of such states can be exponential in history length. Prior methods require explicit game rules and domain-specific abstractions to keep subgames tractable, limiting applicability when rules are unavailable or state spaces are enormous.

## Input / Output

At test time the agent receives only its own information set (public + private observations) at each decision point and has no access to the simulator or explicit rules; it relies entirely on the learned model to construct and solve the abstract subgame. The output is a single action sampled from the CFR+ strategy at the player's abstract information set. A key constraint is that the learned abstraction caps each public state to at most L abstract information sets per player, and the depth-limited value function uses T strategy transformations to estimate payoffs beyond the search horizon.

## Method

LAMIR learns three core functions (representation, dynamics, legal actions) from sampled game trajectories, but the key innovation is a learned abstraction that splits the representation into a public-state network predicting L abstract information sets and an info-set network mapping each real information set to one of those L via a soft clustering objective in a K-dimensional property space (fuzzy c-means-like loss). At test time, the agent performs continual resolving: it constructs a depth-limited abstract game tree using the learned dynamics, appends a terminal decision layer where both players select among T strategy transformations valued by a multi-valued-state value function, and solves the resulting subgame with CFR+. Training uses a two-step update per episode—first a policy-gradient step (RNaD/NeuRD) to update the strategy, then a joint step optimizing the model dynamics, abstraction clustering, value function (via V-trace), and transformation clustering losses, with gradients from the model loss decoupled from the abstraction parameters.

## Results

In head-to-head play against RNaD, LAMIR achieves win rates of 54.47% (II Goofspiel 10), 60.68% (II Goofspiel 13), and 80.49% (II Goofspiel 15) using κ = legal actions as the clustering property. In smaller games where exact exploitability is computable (II Goofspiel 5, II Oshi-Zumo 3,5), LAMIR with sufficient abstraction capacity L produces strategies with lower exploitability than concurrently trained RNaD. The method remains applicable in games where the public state is consistent with over 10^18 underlying world states, a regime where prior continual-resolving methods cannot scale.

**Table 1: Average win rate (with 2-sigma error bars) of LAMIR against RNaD in large imperfect information games.**

| Algorithm | II Goofspiel 10 | II Goofspiel 13 | II Goofspiel 15 |
|---|---|---|---|
| LAMIR (κ = Legal actions) | 54.47 ± 0.25% | 60.68 ± 0.34% | **80.49 ± 0.26%** |
| LAMIR (κ = RNaD strategy) | 61.60 ± 0.29% | 58.33 ± 0.27% | 61.80 ± 0.36% |

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is Regularized Nash Dynamics (RNaD), a model-free policy-gradient algorithm that was used to create a human-expert-level Stratego player. LAMIR consistently outperforms RNaD in every tested game: by roughly 4–11 percentage points in II Goofspiel 10 and 13, and by a striking ~30 percentage points in II Goofspiel 15 (80.49% vs. ~50% implied by the 50% baseline). The κ = legal actions variant is the strongest in II Goofspiel 10 and 15, while κ = RNaD strategy edges ahead in II Goofspiel 10 (61.60% vs. 54.47%). The paper notes that RNaD's exploitability plateaus even with extended training, likely due to network approximation errors, whereas LAMIR's look-ahead reasoning continues to reduce exploitability given sufficient abstraction capacity. A prior method, SePoT, achieves only 52% win rate in II Goofspiel 13 because it skips resolving most subgames due to size limits, whereas LAMIR resolves every decision point via its learned abstraction.

## Relation to Prior Work

LAMIR extends the MuZero paradigm (learned model + search) from perfect-information games to imperfect-information games, a setting where prior look-ahead methods (DeepStack, ReBeL, Student of Games, SePoT) all required explicit game rules to construct game trees and manage belief states. The key architectural departure is the addition of a learned abstraction layer—using fuzzy c-means-style clustering over a K-dimensional property space—that partitions each public state into at most L abstract information sets, making CFR-based solving tractable without domain-specific abstraction design. For the policy and value components, the paper builds directly on RNaD (NeuRD loss) and the multi-valued states / V-trace value function from Kubiček et al. (2024), integrating them with the learned dynamics model in a two-step training loop.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is, to the authors' knowledge, the first method that enables theoretically principled look-ahead reasoning (CFR-based continual resolving) in large-scale imperfect-information games without any explicit game rules or hand-crafted abstractions, overcoming the two main scalability barriers of the continual resolving paradigm. The 80% win rate over RNaD in II Goofspiel 15 (public states with >10^18 consistent histories) demonstrates practical viability in previously intractable domains. Key open questions include: whether the abstraction property κ can itself be learned end-to-end, how to integrate chance nodes, whether action-space abstraction is needed for continuous-action games, and whether CFR convergence guarantees hold beyond A-loss recall games after abstraction-induced imperfect recall.
