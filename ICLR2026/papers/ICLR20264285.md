## Problem

Modern time series forecasters suffer from three interrelated failures: error-driven objectives (MSE/MAE) cause models to overemphasize low-frequency periodicity while discarding high-frequency dynamics, the unidirectional history-to-future mapping creates a distributional mismatch between learned representations and prediction targets, and deep sequential encoding acts as a frequency smoother that erases fine-grained temporal details. The paper asks whether a forecasting framework can explicitly bridge the past-future distributional gap while preserving multi-frequency structure.

## Input / Output

The method takes a multivariate time series history X ∈ R^{C×L} (C channels, look-back L searched over {96,192,336,512,720}) and produces a future forecast Ŷ_pred ∈ R^{C×T} for horizons T ∈ {96,192,336,720}. Both sequences are divided into N patches and projected to D-dimensional embeddings. During training, the ground-truth future Y is additionally fed into a reconstruct branch (discarded at inference), so the only inference-time input is the historical window.

## Method

TimeAlign introduces a dual-branch architecture: a predict branch (any plug-and-play backbone, defaulting to a lightweight linear-activation-linear encoder with residual connections) maps history patches to forecasts, while a training-only reconstruct branch maps ground-truth patches to reconstructions, providing a stable reference aligned with the target distribution. At each encoder layer, a distribution-aware alignment module applies an asymmetric linear projection to the predict-branch hidden states and enforces both local (patch-wise cosine similarity with GELU margin) and global (L1 distance between pairwise distance matrices) consistency with the reconstruct-branch states, using adaptive weights to balance the two objectives. The alignment loss is theoretically grounded as an InfoNCE-inspired lower bound on the mutual information I(Y; H_x), complementing the prediction loss which provides a variational lower bound via the Information Bottleneck. The total training objective is L_pred + L_recon + λ·L_align, and a theoretical result shows that reconstruction-guided estimation yields a strictly tighter generalization bound than direct empirical estimation under Gaussian input assumptions.

## Results

On eight long-term forecasting benchmarks (ETTm1, ETTm2, ETTh1, ETTh2, Weather, Electricity, Traffic, Solar), TimeAlign achieves the best or near-best MSE/MAE across all datasets, with headline results of 0.340/0.367 (ETTm1), 0.243/0.302 (ETTm2), 0.154/0.244 (Electricity), and 0.378/0.240 (Traffic). Compared to the second-best method TVNet, TimeAlign reduces MSE/MAE by 3.27%/5.20% on average, with a Wilcoxon test p-value of 1.37×10⁻⁸ confirming significance at 99% confidence. The only exceptions are ETTh1 and ETTh2, where TimeAlign remains within 0.01 MSE of the best method. As a plug-and-play module, TimeAlign improves iTransformer and DLinear by 1–4% MSE on most datasets while increasing cosine similarity between predictions and ground truth.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TimeAlign outperforms all 11 baselines on 6 of 8 datasets (ETTm1, ETTm2, Weather, Electricity, Traffic, Solar), with the largest margins on Traffic (MSE 0.378 vs. 0.396 for TVNet, 0.620 for TimesNet) and ETTm2 (0.243 vs. 0.250 for TVNet, 0.269 for iTransformer). On ETTh1, TimeBase (MSE 0.396) and ModernTCN (0.404) slightly edge out TimeAlign (0.406), while on ETTh2, ModernTCN (0.322) and TVNet (0.324) are marginally better than TimeAlign (0.336). Among the lightweight baselines, DLinear and TimeBase are the closest competitors on ETTm1/ETTm2, but TimeAlign still leads by 0.006–0.016 MSE. The Transformer-based baselines (iTransformer, TimeMixer, PatchTST, Crossformer) are consistently outperformed by 0.01–0.05 MSE across most datasets, with Crossformer showing the largest gap (e.g., 0.420 vs. 0.340 on ETTm1). CNN-based TimesNet and ModernTCN are competitive on ETTh2 but lag significantly on Traffic and Solar. The plug-and-play experiments further show that adding TimeAlign to iTransformer or DLinear yields 1–4% MSE improvements on most datasets, confirming the alignment module's generality beyond the default linear backbone.

## Relation to Prior Work

TimeAlign departs from the dominant unidirectional encoder-decoder paradigm in time series forecasting by introducing a dual-branch predict-reconstruct-align architecture. Conceptually, it draws on the mutual information maximization framework from contrastive learning (Oord et al., 2018) and the asymmetric gradient flow design from simple siamese networks (Chen & He, 2021), but repurposes them for a reconstruction-guided alignment rather than a contrastive objective. It positions itself against prior representation-learning efforts in TSF (TS2Vec, TSLANet, TimeSURL) by arguing that those methods optimize embeddings under error-driven objectives that smooth away high-frequency dynamics, and instead uses a reconstruction branch as a stable distributional anchor. The method is explicitly plug-and-play, building on and augmenting existing backbones like iTransformer and DLinear rather than proposing a new architecture from scratch.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a well-recognized but under-addressed limitation in TSF: the distributional gap between historical inputs and future targets that causes models to overemphasize low-frequency periodicity. The plug-and-play design and consistent 1–4% gains across diverse backbones and eight benchmarks make it a practical module for practitioners, while the two theoretical results (reconstruction improves generalization; alignment increases mutual information) provide a principled justification beyond empirical tuning. Limitations include performance degradation on datasets with extreme outliers or zero-imputed values (Weather, Solar), and the reconstruct branch, though discarded at inference, adds training complexity. Open questions include whether the alignment strategy transfers to other time series tasks (classification, anomaly detection) and how it behaves under very long look-back horizons or streaming settings.
