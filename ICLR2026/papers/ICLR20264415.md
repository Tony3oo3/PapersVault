## Problem

Existing multi-frame optical flow methods segment videos into fixed-size groups (3–5 frames), limiting their temporal receptive field and preventing long-range dependency capture. Their group-wise processing also causes GPU memory to grow linearly with video length, making them infeasible for arbitrary-length sequences. Additionally, prior methods rely on single-stride temporal modeling, missing the complementary information from both short-term and long-range motions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264415/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The network consumes a video sequence frame-by-frame in an auto-regressive manner: when frame I_{t+1} arrives, its features are extracted and correlated with a memory bank of the T=6 most recent predicted flows (stored at 1/16 resolution) to produce the flow F_{t,t+1}. The final flow is convexly upsampled from 1/16 to full input resolution. A key property is that GPU memory remains constant at ~2.1 GB regardless of total video length, unlike group-wise methods whose memory scales with the number of frames in a group.

## Method

ARFlow introduces an auto-regressive next-frame forecasting paradigm with two core modules. The Auto-regressive Flow Initialization (AFI) module applies a temporal transformer over the T stored history flows to extrapolate an initial flow for the next frame pair, providing a strong starting point that reduces the number of refinement iterations needed. The Auto-regressive Multi-stride Flow Refinement (AMFR) module then performs (i) K=6 GRU-based iterative updates using a ResNet-34 backbone and RAFT/GMA-style correlation volume to incorporate current-frame context and motion features, followed by (ii) a cascaded multi-stride temporal weighting stage that runs additional temporal transformers at strides 1, 2, and 4 over the history flows, fuses their outputs, and produces a learnable scalar weight that convexly blends the GRU-refined flow with the multi-stride forecast. Training uses a clip-wise strategy (preserving temporal continuity within each clip rather than shuffling across batches), a Mixture-of-Laplace loss with RAFT-style deep supervision across all intermediate predictions, and a multi-stage pretraining schedule (TartanAir → FlyingThings3D → combined T+S+K+H) followed by lightweight per-benchmark fine-tuning.

## Results

ARFlow achieves state-of-the-art performance on both MPI-Sintel (Final) with 1.78 EPE (vs. 1.87 for StreamFlow and 1.91 for MEMFOF) and KITTI-2015 with 2.85 Fl-all (vs. 2.94 for MEMFOF and 4.10 for MemFlow). On the Spring benchmark, ARFlow attains the best 1px (3.587), EPE (0.428), Fl (1.313), and WAUC (94.501) among zero-shot methods while using only 2.10 GB GPU memory and 403 ms runtime, compared to 18.97 GB and 929 ms for StreamFlow. The method maintains constant GPU memory (2.1 GB) regardless of video length, unlike prior group-wise multi-frame methods whose memory grows with sequence length.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ARFlow outperforms the closest multi-frame competitor MEMFOF (ICCV'25) on Sintel Final (1.78 vs. 1.91 EPE), KITTI-15 All (2.85 vs. 2.94 Fl), and KITTI-15 Non-Occ (1.91 vs. 1.97 Fl), while tying on Sintel Clean (both 0.96). Against StreamFlow (NeurIPS'24), ARFlow improves Sintel Final by 0.09 EPE (1.78 vs. 1.87) and KITTI-15 All by 1.39 Fl (2.85 vs. 4.24), while also reducing GPU memory from 18.97 GB to 2.10 GB. Compared to the strong pairwise baseline RAFT (ECCV'20), ARFlow reduces Sintel Final EPE from 2.86 to 1.78 and KITTI-15 Fl from 5.10 to 2.85. On the Spring benchmark, ARFlow surpasses MEMFOF on all four metrics (1px: 3.587 vs. 3.600; EPE: 0.428 vs. 0.432; Fl: 1.313 vs. 1.353; WAUC: 94.501 vs. 94.481) in the zero-shot setting, and after fine-tuning achieves 95.283 WAUC vs. 95.186 for MEMFOF. The compatibility study (Table 4) further shows ARFlow's modules can be plugged into other backbones (SEA-RAFT, DPFlow, FlowSeek, WAFT), yielding 5–12% relative improvements, confirming the auto-regressive initialization and multi-stride refinement are general-purpose enhancements.

## Relation to Prior Work

ARFlow builds directly on the MEMFOF architecture (Bargatin et al., 2025) as its base network, incorporating RAFT-style GRU iterative refinement and GMA-style correlation pyramids, while adopting the mixture-of-Laplace loss from SEA-RAFT. Its key departure from prior multi-frame methods (MemFlow, StreamFlow, VideoFlow, SplatFlow) is the shift from fixed-size group-wise processing (3–5 frames per batch) to a sequential auto-regressive paradigm where frames are delivered one at a time and a memory bank of T history flows is used to forecast the next frame's initial flow. This design is explicitly inspired by auto-regressive video generation models (PA-VDM, CausVid, Self Forcing), which the authors are the first to adapt to optical flow estimation. The multi-stride temporal weighting (strides 1, 2, 4) in the refinement module extends the single-stride temporal modeling used in prior memory-based methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The constant GPU memory usage (2.1 GB) regardless of video length addresses a fundamental scalability bottleneck in multi-frame optical flow, making long-sequence processing practical for real-time and resource-constrained applications. The compatibility evaluation showing consistent 5–11% gains when ARFlow's auto-regressive modules are plugged into diverse backbones (SEA-RAFT, DPFlow, FlowSeek, WAFT) suggests the approach is a general-purpose temporal enhancement rather than a monolithic architecture. Open questions include how the method behaves at very long horizons (beyond the tested sequences), whether the 1/16-resolution refinement limits fine-grained motion capture, and whether the auto-regressive error accumulation degrades over hundreds of frames in practice. The finding that replacing the temporal transformer with much larger pre-trained video models (WAN2.2 5B, Longcat 14B) yields no improvement suggests the temporal modeling task here is relatively simple and may not benefit from video-generation-scale pretraining.
