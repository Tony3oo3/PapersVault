## Problem

Existing divide-and-conquer approaches for long-context LLMs lack a formal theoretical framework explaining when chunking helps versus hurts, making it difficult to optimize chunking strategies or understand how cross-chunk dependencies, model confusion, and aggregation errors interact. The paper asks: under what precise conditions does a system of weaker chunk-processing agents outperform a single stronger model on very long inputs, and how should the aggregation stage be designed to preserve the theoretical advantage?

## Input / Output

The system takes a long text (up to 128K tokens) and a task instruction, splits the text into n contiguous equal-length chunks, and routes each chunk to a worker agent that produces a partial artifact (e.g., a local summary, a subset of top-k numbers, or a partial answer). A manager agent then merges all partial artifacts into a single task-specific output (retrieved value, numeric answer, ROUGE-scored summary, multiple-choice selection, or character name). A planner agent sits above both, automatically translating the raw task prompt into structured worker and manager prompts so that partial outputs are compatible with downstream aggregation.

## Method

The core contribution is a fidelity decomposition framework that models the D&C pipeline as an information channel and decomposes system error into three multiplicative fidelity terms (additive in log-space): task noise (information lost at the chunking interface due to cross-chunk dependencies), model noise (per-worker degradation from length-induced confusion), and aggregator noise (errors in merging partial results). A key theoretical result (Proposition 3.1) shows that if the strong model's loss grows super-linearly with context length while per-chunk D&C loss is bounded, there exists a critical length threshold beyond which D&C with weaker models strictly outperforms single-shot processing. The implementation uses a three-part planner–worker–manager architecture where the planner performs job assignment, prompt preparation (e.g., converting 'find the 2nd smallest number' into 'return the two smallest numbers per chunk'), and iterative refinement on validation data. A sparse-sampling procedure estimates the optimal chunk size with only 3–10 documents per candidate size, exploiting the near-convex error contour induced by super-linear model noise.

## Results

On 128K-token inputs, single-shot performance degrades sharply: gpt4o drops from 0.67 to 0.33 accuracy on Math, gpt4omini from 0.71 to 0.11, and llama70b from 0.63 to 0.09. With D&C chunking, llama70b recovers to 0.57 accuracy on Math (16K chunks) and 0.63 F1 on QA-IB (16K chunks), surpassing gpt4o's single-shot 0.33 on Math. On KV retrieval, D&C restores near-perfect accuracy (1.00) for llama70b and gpt4omini at 128K, versus 0.15 and 0.60 single-shot respectively. The planner-based aggregator prompt further improves D&C scores by 5–15 points over manual prompts on Math and QA-LB tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is D&C chunking versus single-shot processing of the full 128K input. On Math, llama70b with 16K D&C chunks (0.57) outperforms gpt4o single-shot (0.33) by 24 points, and gpt4omini D&C (0.55) beats its own single-shot (0.11) by 44 points. On KV retrieval, D&C restores near-perfect accuracy for all models at 128K, whereas single-shot llama70b collapses to 0.15 and gpt4omini to 0.60. Against RAG (BM25 and dense retrieval), D&C is substantially more effective on reasoning-heavy tasks: on QA-IB, llama70b D&C achieves 0.63 F1 versus 0.14 (BM25) and 0.38 (mpnet) for RAG; on Summarization, D&C scores 0.28 ROUGE versus 0.15 for both RAG variants. The one regime where D&C underperforms is the high cross-chunk-dependency Char task, where all methods remain below 0.18 accuracy, confirming the task-noise-dominated regime predicted by the theory.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the divide-and-conquer (D&C) pipeline paradigm for long-context LLMs established by LC-Boost (Qian et al., 2024b), Chain-of-Agents (Zhang et al., 2024c), and LLM × MapReduce (Zhou et al., 2024), which all adopt a worker-manager architecture for chunked processing. However, it departs from these prior works by introducing a formal fidelity-decomposition framework that separates system error into task noise (cross-chunk dependency), model noise (length-induced degradation), and aggregator noise (integration error), providing the first principled account of *when* D&C is advantageous. It also positions itself against architectural context-extension methods (RoPE scaling, ring attention) and RAG pipelines, arguing that algorithmic chunking with a well-designed aggregator is complementary to and sometimes superior to these approaches for very long inputs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework gives practitioners a diagnostic tool for deciding whether to chunk, how large chunks should be, and how much investment in the aggregator is warranted, rather than relying on ad hoc heuristics. The empirical demonstration that a weaker model (e.g., Llama-70B) with structured chunking can outperform GPT-4o in single-shot mode on 128K-token inputs has direct cost and latency implications for production long-context systems. Open questions include how the framework extends to adaptive/semantic chunking, hierarchical aggregation for very large n, and tasks with genuinely high cross-chunk synergy (the 'Silo Effect' regime) where D&C fundamentally saturates. The equal-length, homogeneous-worker design used for clean diagnosis limits immediate transfer to heterogeneous or domain-adaptive deployments.
