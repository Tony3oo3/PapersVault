## Problem

Existing multimodal agents target either GUI (2D) or embodied (3D) scenarios exclusively, yet real-world tasks often require interleaving both. Naively mixing GUI and embodied training data causes performance degradation due to conflicts between the two action spaces, and prior generalist agents lack explicit mechanisms to manage the synergy and conflict between these data types.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263299/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The agent receives a screenshot image (resized to 448×448 for embodied data), a task instruction, and a history of previous actions (GUI tasks only), and outputs either a GUI action (semantic type token like 'click' plus (x,y) coordinates) or an embodied action (6-DoF end-effector displacement normalized to [-1,1] plus a gripper open/close signal). Embodied actions are discretized into K=256 uniform bins per dimension, each mapped to one of the 256 least-frequent token IDs in the base MLLM vocabulary, so both action types share a single unified vocabulary for autoregressive prediction.

## Method

The core architectural contribution is a Layer-heterogeneity MoE that exploits an empirically observed property: parameter update directions from GUI and embodied data are highly aligned in shallow layers (synergy in environment/instruction understanding) but diverge sharply in deep layers (conflict in action generation), analogous to the cerebrum-cerebellum division in the human brain. Concretely, layers 1 through T=8 use shared MSA and FFN parameters for both data types, while layers T+1 through L employ separate expert parameters (distinct MSA, FFN, and LN) routed by task type, along with separate prediction heads for GUI and embodied actions. The data pipeline unifies all sources into a ShareGPT format with a system prompt, image, instruction, and action, using the MLLM's text tokenizer for GUI actions and a dedicated discretization scheme for embodied actions. Training proceeds in two stages—GUI grounding on ~3.4M samples followed by joint trajectory training on ~4.1M samples (with embodied data resampled 5× to balance the ratio)—on Qwen2-VL 7B, and the design generalizes to Qwen2.5-VL 7B with a 4.3% average improvement.

## Results

On embodied tasks (LIBERO-90), OmniActor achieves a 69.5% success rate, outperforming its single-task variant OmniActor-EA (63.4%) by 6.1%. On GUI tasks, OmniActor scores 87.5% on AndroidControl-Low, 77.1% on AndroidControl-High, and 66.0% on GUI Odyssey, surpassing OmniActor-GUI (88.4%, 74.5%, 63.0%) on the two harder benchmarks. OmniActor outperforms all generalist baselines (Magma, GEA, NaviMaster) on every reported metric, and on GUI tasks it matches or exceeds most open-source GUI agents while remaining competitive with the in-house UI-TARS-7B.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against generalist agents, OmniActor dominates: it outperforms Magma (34.7/52.1/32.7/51.0) by 34.8/35.4/44.4/15.0 points on LIBERO/AndroidCtrl-Low/High/GUI Odyssey, and beats GEA (57.3 on AndroidCtrl-High) by 19.8 points and NaviMaster (68.9/54.0) by 18.6/23.1 points. On GUI tasks, OmniActor (87.5/77.1/66.0) surpasses all open-source GUI agents—OS-Atlas-7B (85.2/71.2/62.0), Aguvis-7B (80.5/61.5/63.8), ScaleTrack-7B (86.6/77.9/65.3), SeeClick (75.0/59.1/53.9), and Aria-UI (67.3/10.2/36.5)—though it trails the in-house UI-TARS-7B (90.8/72.5/87.0) on AndroidCtrl-Low and GUI Odyssey while leading on AndroidCtrl-High. On embodied tasks, OmniActor (69.5) beats MDT (67.2), MaIL (60.3), PRISE (54.4), MUTEX (53.0), Distill-D (49.9), ACT (46.6), and SpatialVLA (46.2), but falls short of the strongest dedicated embodied agents π₀ (87.3), OpenVLA (73.5), and UniACT (73.0). The most notable finding is that OmniActor outperforms its own single-task ablations (OmniActor-GUI and OmniActor-EA) on their respective domains, confirming that the Layer-heterogeneity MoE successfully exploits cross-task synergy without incurring the degradation seen in naive mixed training (OmniActor-EA&GUI: 50.5/86.3/71.0/60.8).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the emerging line of generalist agent work (Magma, GEA, NaviMaster) that unifies GUI and embodied tasks within a single model, but departs from them architecturally by introducing a Layer-heterogeneity MoE that shares parameters in shallow layers and separates them in deep layers. The design is motivated by a novel empirical observation—parameter update similarity between GUI and embodied data is high in shallow layers but low in deep layers—framed through a cerebrum-cerebellum analogy. Unlike prior generalist agents that rely on unified action representations (SoM/ToM in Magma, continuous tokenizers in GEA) or reinforcement learning (NaviMaster), OmniActor addresses the conflict-synergy tension through explicit structural partitioning combined with large-scale supervised data unification across both domains.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical and well-ablated recipe for building a single agent that handles both 2D GUI control and 3D robot manipulation without the performance degradation that naive data mixing causes, which is a key bottleneck for deploying generalist agents in real-world settings. The parameter update similarity metric offers a reusable diagnostic for deciding where to share vs. separate parameters in multi-task learning more broadly. Limitations include the requirement to know the task type at inference (no learned routing), the empirical nature of the layer threshold T=8, and evaluation restricted to a single embodied benchmark (LIBERO) and two GUI benchmarks; whether the layer-heterogeneity principle generalizes to more than two task families or to continuous action spaces remains open.
