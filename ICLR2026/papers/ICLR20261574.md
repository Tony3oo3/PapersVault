## Problem

Existing hallucination detection methods based on semantic entropy treat token sequence (TS) probabilities as fixed, ignoring their aleatoric uncertainty and sensitivity to model perturbations. This leads to overestimation of confabulation risk when semantically equivalent generations exhibit low probability variability, and underestimation when they do not. Additionally, prior work has not examined how detection performance degrades under quantization (16-bit, 8-bit, 4-bit) or across different generation lengths, both critical for real-world deployment.

## Input / Output

The method requires a question prompt, R repeated LLM generations (token sequences) with their associated TS probabilities, and a DeBERTa-large entailment model for semantic clustering. It requires access to token-level probabilities, restricting applicability to open-weight models. The output is a set of uncertainty-calibrated cluster probabilities, a semantic Rényi entropy score (SE_R+) serving as a hallucination confidence indicator, and a principled answer selection that prioritizes high-certainty, semantically coherent outputs even when hallucinations are present.

## Method

The paper maps the kernel mean embedding (KME) of the TS probability distribution in a Gaussian-kernel RKHS to an eigen-mode of a quantum tensor network (QTN) Hamiltonian, identified via the null space of the quantum correlation matrix (QCM). First-order perturbation theory is then applied to this Hamiltonian to compute corrections to all eigen-modes and energies, yielding local, multi-resolution uncertainty feature vectors that quantify how sensitive each TS probability is to infinitesimal perturbations. These uncertainty estimates feed into an entropy-maximization objective (Eq. 7) that calibrates TS probabilities by maximizing Rényi entropy while penalizing KL divergence from the empirical probabilities, with the penalty inversely scaled by the local uncertainty. The calibrated probabilities are then used to compute semantic Rényi entropy over DeBERTa-clustered generations, producing a hallucination score that is more robust than Shannon-based semantic entropy because it explicitly accounts for aleatoric variability in TS probabilities. The entire pipeline is deterministic, single-shot, and requires no supervised fine-tuning.

## Results

Across 116 experiments on TriviaQA, NQ-Open, SVAMP, and SQuAD using 8 LLM architectures (Mistral-7B, LLaMA-2-7B/13B, LLaMA-3.2-1B, Falcon-rw-1B, and their instruct/chat variants), the proposed Semantic Rényi Entropy with UQ maximization (SRE-UQ) achieves consistent improvements in both AUROC and AURAC over all baselines. In the worked example (Table 1), SRE-UQ yields a total entropy of 0.12951 versus 0.22471 for standard semantic entropy (SE_S) and 0.84557 for naive entropy (NE), demonstrating reduced overestimation of confabulation risk. The method remains robust across 16-bit, 8-bit, and 4-bit quantization levels and across both phrase-level and sentence-level generation, with pairwise win-rate matrices showing SRE-UQ outperforming or matching all SOTA baselines in the majority of experimental scenarios.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two families of baselines. First, Farquhar et al. (2024, Nature) provide three entropy-based methods: naive entropy (NE), semantic entropy (SE_S), and discrete semantic entropy (DSE_S). SRE-UQ consistently outperforms all three on both AUROC and AURAC across all 116 experimental scenarios, with the advantage being most pronounced under 8-bit and 4-bit quantization where NE and SE_S show notable performance degradation, particularly on NQ and SQuAD. Second, Kadavath et al. (2022) provide two supervised baselines: embedding regression (ER), a logistic regression classifier on final hidden representations, and verifier p(True), which uses a multiple-choice prompt to assess answer correctness. SRE-UQ surpasses both supervised methods in AUROC despite requiring no ground-truth labels for training, and maintains superior AURAC robustness, especially when progressively rejecting uncertain predictions. The pairwise win-rate matrices (Figs. 2–3) confirm that SRE-UQ wins or ties in the majority of head-to-head comparisons across all quantization levels, model sizes, and generation lengths.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the semantic entropy framework for hallucination detection (Farquhar et al., Nature 2024) by replacing Shannon entropy with quadratic Rényi entropy, which is structurally compatible with kernel mean embeddings in RKHS. It then imports a quantum tensor network (QTN) perturbation-theory pipeline—originally developed for time-series uncertainty quantification (Vipulananthan et al., 2024; Qi & Ranard, 2019)—to compute local, first-order uncertainty corrections on token-sequence probabilities, and uses an entropy-maximization objective to calibrate those probabilities before clustering. The approach departs from prior semantic-entropy methods (KLE, SNNE, Semantic Density) by explicitly modeling aleatoric uncertainty in the TS probabilities rather than relying solely on similarity-weighted cluster distributions, and it contrasts with supervised baselines (embedding regression, p(True) verifier from Kadavath et al., 2022) by requiring no ground-truth labels.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a deterministic, single-shot, interpretable hallucination detector that remains robust under 4-bit quantization—a deployment regime largely ignored by prior work—making it relevant for edge and resource-constrained settings. However, evaluations are limited to models up to 13B parameters, so generalization to frontier-scale LLMs (GPT-4, Claude) remains unverified, and the method requires access to token-level probabilities, excluding black-box APIs. Open questions include whether cluster-level uncertainty (averaging UQ over semantic clusters) can yield practical human-oversight guidelines, and whether the QTN perturbation framework scales efficiently to the much larger generation sets needed for reliable entropy estimation on complex, open-ended tasks.
