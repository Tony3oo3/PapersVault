## Problem

Existing deep research benchmarks (GAIA, BrowseComp, DeepResearch Bench, etc.) evaluate only factual accuracy and comprehensiveness, while personalization benchmarks (LaMP, PersonaGym, PersonaLens) are confined to narrow dialogue or recommendation tasks. No prior work systematically evaluates whether Deep Research Agents can adapt their retrieval, reasoning, and reporting to individual user profiles, leaving personalization as a critical blind spot in DRA evaluation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261385/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes 250 user-task pairs, where each pair combines a deep research query (spanning 10 domains such as healthcare, finance, and career development) with a user profile that fuses a structured explicit persona (demographics, financial status, health, preferences) and a dynamic context derived from simulated daily interactions (memory snippets and conversational logs). The evaluation pipeline produces three orthogonal scores on a 0–10 scale: a Personalization Alignment score decomposed into Goal, Content, Presentation, and Actionability sub-metrics; a Content Quality score over Depth & Insight, Logical Coherence, and Clarity & Readability; and a Factual Reliability score computed from Factual Accuracy and Citation Coverage, with the overall score being their arithmetic mean.

## Method

The PQR Evaluation Framework replaces fixed rubrics with a dynamic, LLM-driven three-stage pipeline for both Personalization and Quality: a meta-evaluator LLM first assigns task- and persona-conditioned weights to each dimension, then generates granular sub-criteria with individual weights, and finally a separate scorer LLM rates the report against each sub-criterion with justification. Factual Reliability is assessed via an automated grounding pipeline that extracts verifiable claims with their citations, deduplicates them, retrieves source content through the Jina Reader API, and verifies support, yielding Factual Accuracy (fraction of cited claims verified) and Citation Coverage (fraction of all claims that are cited). The framework was validated against human expert judgments using Pairwise Comparison Agreement and Mean Absolute Rating Deviation, with GPT-5 selected as the primary judge model for its best alignment at reasonable cost.

## Results

On PDR-Bench (Task w/Persona, 150 queries), OAgents achieves the highest Personalization score (P-Score 6.64) and leads in most personalization sub-metrics (GOAL 6.68, PRES 7.13, ACTI 6.92) as well as Quality (DEIN 6.99, LOGC 7.44). Among commercial deep research agents, Gemini-2.5-Pro Deep Research leads with P-Score 6.58 and the best Factual Reliability (FA 8.40, CC 9.26). LLMs with search tools underperform across all dimensions, with GPT-4.1 w/Search scoring only 4.28 on P-Score and 0.10 on Citation Coverage.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Open-source agents dominate personalization: OAgents (P-Score 6.64) outperforms the best commercial agent Gemini-2.5-Pro Deep Research (6.58) by 0.06, and leads in GOAL (6.68 vs. 5.27), PRES (7.13 vs. 5.83), and ACTI (6.92 vs. 4.56). However, OAgents' Factual Accuracy is only 3.77, far below Gemini-2.5-Pro Deep Research (8.40) and Perplexity Deep Research (7.68), while MiroFlow's Citation Coverage is a mere 0.44. Among commercial systems, Gemini-2.5-Pro Deep Research achieves the best reliability (FA 8.40, CC 9.26) and balanced quality, while O3 Deep Research edges it out on personalization sub-scores (GOAL 5.67, CONT 5.95). LLMs with search tools consistently trail dedicated agents: the best in this group, Gemini-2.5-Pro w/Search (P-Score 5.53), lags OAgents by over 1 point, and GPT-4.1 w/Search nearly fails on Citation Coverage (0.10). In the memory-system experiment (Table 3), O-Mem (P-Score 4.26) outperforms Mem0 (3.55) and Memory OS (3.88) but still falls short of the explicit-persona baseline (4.58), indicating current memory systems cannot fully replicate the personalization signal of structured personas.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of two previously separate evaluation literatures: deep research agent benchmarks (GAIA, BrowseComp, DeepResearch Bench, ResearcherBench) and personalization benchmarks (LaMP, PersonaGym, PersonaLens, PersonaFeedback). Rather than building on a single prior method, it repurposes the LLM-as-judge paradigm with dynamic criterion generation (seen in quality evaluation frameworks) and citation-based factual grounding (from ResearcherBench and DeepResearch Bench) into a unified PQR framework that, for the first time, jointly scores personalization alignment, content quality, and factual reliability. The user profile construction methodology—combining structured explicit personas with longitudinal interaction context—draws on user modeling traditions but applies them to the multi-step, tool-using deep research setting where no prior benchmark had attempted personalization assessment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By establishing the first systematic evaluation of personalization in DRAs, this work provides the field with a concrete testbed and methodology that was previously absent, enabling developers to measure whether their agents truly adapt to individual users rather than producing generic reports. The finding that open-source agents lead in personalization but trail significantly in factual reliability (OAgents: FA 3.77 vs. Gemini Deep Research: 8.40), and that even the best memory system (O-Mem) leaves a substantial gap versus explicit personas, highlights concrete bottlenecks for the next generation of personalized research assistants. Key limitations include the Chinese-centric data collection, evaluation on only a 150-query subset, and moderate LLM-human alignment (PCA ≈ 0.43), leaving open questions about cross-lingual generalization, scalability, and whether the dynamic-criterion LLM judge can be trusted for high-stakes personalization decisions.
