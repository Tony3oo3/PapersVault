## Problem

Existing interpretable time series forecasting methods provide only local, partial explanations: they explain predictions at individual time steps rather than the overall shape of the forecast curve, and they focus on limited variable types (e.g., endogenous only) rather than how heterogeneous exogenous variables interact to produce temporal patterns. This limits both user understanding of why a forecast curve has a particular shape (e.g., three descending peaks in electricity load) and the ability of domain experts to steer the model to improve accuracy.

## Input / Output

ProtoTS consumes a look-back window of historical endogenous values (e.g., 384 steps of 15-minute electric load), historical exogenous variables (22 covariates: 14 discrete calendar/holiday indicators and 8 continuous weather variables), and future exogenous variables over the forecast horizon (e.g., 96 steps of forecasted temperature and known holiday flags). The output is a future endogenous curve of H steps, decomposed as a weighted sum of learned prototypical temporal patterns, with the activation weights and the hierarchical prototype curves themselves serving as the explanation. Each prototype is a fixed-length pattern aligned to the forecast window by phase, so the prediction is literally a convex combination of interpretable curves.

## Method

ProtoTS encodes heterogeneous inputs through a multi-channel embedding (MLP for endogenous, dedicated embedding tables for discrete exogenous, variable-specific non-linear projections for continuous exogenous), fuses them by addition, and denoises the result via a bottleneck MLP-Mixer (d to d_bottle to d) applied along both feature and temporal dimensions. The denoised representation is projected to a single vector and matched against a hierarchical set of learnable prototypes: a small root level (e.g., 6 prototypes) captures coarse global patterns, and each root is split into M children for fine-grained local variations, with prediction formed as a nested weighted sum of prototype temporal patterns using softmax-over-Euclidean-distance weights. A loss-driven splitting rule (top-k activated prototypes with highest normalized MAE) determines which leaf prototypes to further refine, enabling multi-level tree growth. Training uses L1 forecasting loss plus an entropy regularization term that encourages a few dominant prototypes to cover most predictions, balancing accuracy against interpretability.

## Results

On the newly introduced LOF dataset (22 exogenous covariates, look-back 384, forecast 96), ProtoTS achieves an average MAE of 0.106, reducing MSE by 48.3% and MAE by 20.9% over the best baseline (TiDE, MAE 0.134). On the EPF electricity price dataset (6 covariates, look-back 168, forecast 24), ProtoTS attains an average MAE of 0.218 across five Nordic markets, outperforming the best baseline (TimeXer, MAE 0.238) by 8%. ProtoTS also demonstrates superior data efficiency, maintaining stable performance when training data is reduced to 50%, while baselines like TimeXer and TiDE show clear MSE degradation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ProtoTS outperforms all 8 baselines on every region of the LOF dataset and every market of the EPF dataset. The closest competitor on LOF is TiDE (Avg MAE 0.134 vs. 0.106, a 21% gap), while NBEATSx is the weakest deep-learning baseline (Avg MAE 0.563, 81% worse) due to its sensitivity to outliers. On EPF, TimeXer is the nearest baseline (Avg MAE 0.238 vs. 0.218, 8% gap), and machine-learning methods (XGBoost, LightGBM, pyGAM) degrade substantially because the dataset has fewer covariates. On the multivariate forecasting benchmarks (Table 11), ProtoTS is competitive but not uniformly best: TimeXer edges it out slightly on ETTm1/ETTm2 at longer horizons (720 steps), and DLinear remains competitive on Exchange, indicating ProtoTS's advantage is most pronounced when heterogeneous exogenous variables are present.

## Relation to Prior Work

ProtoTS repurposes the prototypical network paradigm—previously applied to image classification and single-output time series tasks—by decoding each prototype into a full multi-step forecasting curve rather than a single scalar or class label. Its multi-channel embedding and bottleneck fusion draw on tabular learning practices (separate discrete/continuous encoders, information-bottleneck compression) and the MLP-Mixer architecture, while the hierarchical tree structure for prototypes is a novel extension beyond the flat prototype sets used in prior interpretable time series models like CycleNet or NBEATSx. The paper explicitly positions itself against ante-hoc interpretable forecasters (TFT, NBEATSx, DiPE-Linear) that offer only local, per-timestep or per-component explanations, arguing that a global set of prototypical temporal patterns is needed for expert-level understanding.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By coupling state-of-the-art accuracy with a multi-level, expert-steerable explanation, ProtoTS addresses a real gap in high-stakes forecasting (power grids, energy markets) where black-box accuracy alone is insufficient for operational trust. The hierarchical design enables domain experts to inspect, split, or edit specific prototypes to inject knowledge, a practical workflow that prior interpretable models do not support. Key limitations remain: the method relies on periodic or recurring patterns (weakening on highly irregular series), the expert-steering loop requires manual domain knowledge, and per-iteration training cost is roughly 2–4× that of MLP baselines. Open questions include whether the prototype hierarchy can be learned end-to-end without manual splitting rules, and how the approach generalizes to non-periodic or event-driven time series.
