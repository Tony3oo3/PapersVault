## Problem

Low-dimensional modeling problems at the intersection of computational science and machine learning (multi-scale regression, PDE solving, inverse problems) lack a parsimonious architecture that simultaneously achieves high accuracy, interpretability, and principled optimization. While 3D Gaussian Splatting achieved a breakthrough in graphics, its mathematical structure was never formalized as a general function approximator with a clean separation of model, inverse problem, and training algorithm. This paper fills that gap by introducing Splat Regression Models with a complete theoretical framework rooted in optimal transport geometry.

## Input / Output

The model consumes low-dimensional inputs x ∈ R^d (typically d = 1, 2, or 3) and produces outputs f(x) ∈ R^p as a weighted mixture of anisotropic bump functions. For supervised regression, training samples (x_i, y_i) are drawn from a uniform or arbitrary distribution over a compact domain; for physics-informed fitting, the input is a PDE operator (e.g., Laplacian for Allen-Cahn) plus boundary data; for Novel View Synthesis, the input is 2D pixel observations with camera poses and the output is a 3D radiance field. A key structural detail is that each splat's parameters (position b_i ∈ R^d, shape matrix A_i ∈ R^{d×d}, weight vector v_i ∈ R^p) are treated as atoms of a probability measure, so the model's parameter space is itself a space of distributions rather than a flat Euclidean space.

## Method

The Splat Regression Model represents f_μ(x) = Σ_i v_i ρ(A_i^{-1}(x − b_i))|det A_i^{-1}| μ(v_i, ρ_{A_i,b_i}), a mixture of anisotropic bump functions where each splat is an affine pushforward of a fixed mother density ρ. The collection of all such splats forms a Bures-Wasserstein manifold, and the full parameter set is a probability measure μ on R^p × BW_ρ(R^d) — a 'distribution over distributions.' Training proceeds via Wasserstein-Fisher-Rao gradient descent: the Wasserstein component (derived from the Benamou-Brenier kinetic energy formulation) updates splat positions and shapes by transporting mass, while the Fisher-Rao component (from the Hellinger metric) adjusts splat weights via mass-preserving rescaling. The paper derives closed-form gradient expressions for all three parameter groups (v, A, b) in terms of the first variation δF of the loss, and shows that 3D Gaussian Splatting with its α-blending rendering and particle birth-death dynamics is recovered as a special case when ρ is Gaussian and the forward operator is the Radiative Transfer Equation. Universal approximation is established with quantitative bounds: k ≲ ε^{-2(d+2)} splats suffice for ε-approximation of Lipschitz functions, matching minimax rates up to logarithmic factors.

## Results

On a 2D noisy regression task (f(x,y) = sin(3π√x)cos(3πy)), splat models achieve order-of-magnitude lower fitting MSE than both KAN and MLP while using a small fraction of their parameters. On the physics-informed Allen-Cahn equation (ε=0.1), a k=50 splat model outperforms all tested KAN and MLP architectures by an order of magnitude in validation error while using significantly fewer parameters. In 1D multiscale approximation, a k=30 splat model (90 parameters) outperforms Haar wavelet projection with 255 parameters and Chebyshev interpolation with 30 nodes, though it is slightly worse than Chebyshev with 45 nodes (also 90 parameters). The paper reports overall 10–100× improvements over KAN and MLP across tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares splat regression models against KAN (Liu et al., 2025), fully connected MLPs, Chebyshev polynomial interpolation, and Haar wavelet projection. Against KAN and MLP, splat models consistently win by an order of magnitude in validation MSE on both the 2D regression task (Figure 2) and the physics-informed Allen-Cahn PDE (Figure 3), while using far fewer parameters (e.g., k=50 splats vs. thousands of MLP/KAN parameters). Against classical approximation methods in 1D, the splat model with k=30 splats (90 parameters) beats Chebyshev interpolation with m=30 nodes and Haar wavelet with 255 parameters, but is slightly outperformed by Chebyshev with m=45 nodes (90 parameters) when parameter counts are matched. The authors attribute the advantage to the spatially localized, anisotropic nature of splats acting as a learned adaptive positional encoding, which is particularly effective for multi-scale and boundary-interface features that global basis methods handle poorly.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate threads: the 3D Gaussian Splatting methodology from computer graphics (Kerbl et al., 2023) and the Wasserstein-Fisher-Rao gradient flow framework from optimal transport and information geometry (Chewi et al., 2025a; Lambert et al., 2022). The key conceptual move is to recognize that splat models—mixtures of anisotropic bump functions weighted by output vectors—live on a Bures-Wasserstein manifold, so that their training reduces to WFR gradient descent in parameter space. This recovers 3D Gaussian Splatting as a special case and provides a principled interpretation of its heuristic training procedures (particle birth-death, selective noising) as regularized risk minimization. On the approximation side, the model generalizes the Nadaraya-Watson kernel estimator to heterogeneous mixture weights and connects to Cybenko's universal approximation theorem for two-layer networks, while the empirical comparisons target KAN (Liu et al., 2025) and MLP baselines in the physics-informed learning literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous theoretical foundation for 3D Gaussian Splatting, disambiguating the inverse problem, the model class, and the optimization algorithm—something the graphics community has lacked. The 10–100× parameter-efficiency advantage over KAN and MLP on low-dimensional tasks, if it holds at scale, would make splat models a compelling default for scientific computing and PDE-fitting where interpretability and spatial locality matter. Key open questions include: how to regularize the highly expressive model to prevent overfitting (the authors acknowledge this explicitly), whether the WFR-based training scales to high-dimensional or large-scale problems, and whether 'deep splat networks' (compositions of splat layers) can match the expressivity of deep MLPs. The experiments are limited to 1D–2D problems with small sample sizes, so the practical impact at scale remains to be demonstrated.
