## Problem

Cross-entropy (CE) loss is the dominant distillation loss in CV and NLP, yet it performs surprisingly poorly in knowledge distillation for recommender systems. The paper identifies the root cause: CE loss only bounds NDCG when the item subset satisfies a 'closure' assumption (all items the student ranks above any subset member must also be in the subset), which the teacher's top items typically violate because the student initially ranks them low. This disconnect between the theoretical guarantee and the practical goal of distilling the teacher's top-item rankings leaves CE loss's potential unrealized in recommender KD.

## Input / Output

The method consumes pre-computed teacher score vectors (saved before distillation to avoid re-running the teacher) and the student's current score predictions over the full item catalog. It operates on top-K subsets (K typically 50–100) from both teacher and student, plus L randomly sampled items (L typically 100), rather than the full item set, making it tractable for catalogs with tens of thousands of items. The output is a trained student model that produces a ranked list of N items per user, evaluated via Recall@N and NDCG@N under full-ranking protocol.

## Method

RCE-KD splits the teacher's top-K items into two subsets: (Q_u^T)_1 = items also in the student's top-K, and (Q_u^T)_2 = the remainder. For (Q_u^T)_1, CE loss is computed directly on the student's top-K set, which exactly satisfies the closure assumption and thus tightly bounds partial NDCG. For (Q_u^T)_2, a teacher-student collaborative sampling strategy is devised: for each item in (Q_u^T)_2, all items the student ranks above it receive a +1 score boost, and L items are then sampled with probability proportional to exp(z_j/τ), constructing a set that approximately satisfies closure. The two losses are fused with a per-user adaptive weight γ = exp(−β·|(Q_u^T)_1|/|Q_u^T|), which shifts emphasis from L2 (exploration of unmastered items) early in training toward L1 (fine-grained ranking refinement) as the student's top items increasingly overlap with the teacher's.

## Results

RCE-KD achieves the best performance across all five teacher→student settings (MF→MF, LGCN→LGCN, HSTU→HSTU, LGCN→MF, HSTU→MF) and all three datasets (CiteULike, Gowalla, Yelp). Headline improvements over the best baseline range from 2.75% to 11.76% in NDCG@10; for example, in the HSTU→HSTU setting on CiteULike, RCE-KD reaches NDCG@10 = 0.0325 vs. HetComp's 0.0299 (+8.70%), and in HSTU→MF on CiteULike it reaches 0.0285 vs. TARec's 0.0258 (+10.47%). In most homogeneous settings, the distilled student approaches or matches the teacher's performance (e.g., HSTU→HSTU on CiteULike: student 0.0325 vs. teacher 0.0291 NDCG@10), demonstrating that model compression incurs negligible accuracy loss. Training time per epoch is comparable to the simplest baselines (CD, RRD), confirming high efficiency.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RCE-KD outperforms all six baselines (CD, RRD, DCD, HetComp, TARec, and vanilla CE) across every dataset and teacher→student configuration. CD (point-wise collaborative distillation) is consistently the weakest baseline, attributed to its use of a point-wise loss that is less effective than list-wise alternatives in recommendation. RRD (list-wise ranking distillation) and DCD (dual correction) are the strongest baselines in homogeneous settings, with DCD often ranking second; RCE-KD improves over DCD by 3.5–11.8% in NDCG@10. In heterogeneous settings (LGCN→MF, HSTU→MF), TARec (Wasserstein-based) and HetComp (heterogeneous ensemble) are the closest competitors, but RCE-KD still leads by 3–10%. Notably, in the HSTU→HSTU setting, RCE-KD exceeds the teacher's own NDCG@10 on CiteULike (0.0325 vs. 0.0291), suggesting the distillation process can even surpass the source model's ranking quality on the student architecture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the CE-NDCG theoretical connection established by Bruch et al. (2019) from the full-item learning-to-rank setting to the partial-item knowledge distillation scenario specific to recommender systems, where loss can only be computed on a small item subset. It departs from the standard response-based KD paradigm (Hinton et al., 2015; Kang et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2019) by identifying a critical theoretical gap: the closure assumption required for CE loss to bound NDCG is violated when the item subset consists of the teacher's top items rather than the student's. The proposed RCE-KD method bridges this gap through a split-and-fusion paradigm with a teacher-student collaborative sampling strategy, positioning itself as a principled fix to vanilla CE loss rather than an entirely new loss family.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first theoretical explanation for why CE loss—the default in most KD frameworks across CV and NLP—underperforms in recommender systems, where ranking (not absolute scores) is the primary objective. The practical implication is significant: practitioners using CE loss for KD in recommendation can now understand its limitations and adopt RCE-KD to recover near-teacher performance with small student models, directly addressing deployment cost concerns. Open questions include whether the closure assumption can be satisfied more elegantly without the sampling overhead, whether the analysis extends to other surrogate losses (e.g., KL divergence variants), and how the method behaves in extremely large-scale industrial settings where even the teacher's top-K computation is expensive.
