## Problem

The existing notion of smoothness in learning-augmented algorithms—competitive ratio as a function of prediction error—can be vacuous, because for any error level one can construct instances where predictions are completely uninformative, collapsing the analysis to the no-prediction case. This paper introduces a refined smoothness metric that restricts attention to instances where predictions are guaranteed to provide additional information, and establishes the first non-trivial smoothness lower bounds for non-clairvoyant scheduling problems.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264751/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis takes as input a scheduling instance with job size predictions (measured by the multiplicative error η_j = max{p_j/p_j*, p_j*/p_j}), machine speeds, and machine count, and produces smoothness bounds expressed as functions of the instance-level error η = max_j η_j. Problem instances are partitioned via a predicate R(I) into three disjoint sets: perfect predictions (η=1), worst-case predictions (η ≥ √P where P is the job size ratio), and reasonable predictions (1 < η < √P), with smoothness defined only over the third set. The output bounds are piecewise functions of η, e.g., η for small errors and 2 for large errors in the single-machine case.

## Method

The core mechanism is an adversarial strategy that reveals the true size of the smallest job while keeping all other jobs indistinguishable via predictions, forcing any algorithm into a constrained optimization problem. For single-machine completion time, the adversary uses a linear-fractional program to determine optimal job size distributions, then an adaptive three-phase strategy (large/intermediate/small jobs) that pushes algorithms toward SPJF for small η and round-robin for large η, yielding a piecewise lower bound of η, λ(η), and 2. For parallel identical machines, a dynamic adversary scales with η to improve the lower bound to 2−O(η^{-2}), while the upper bound combines a (1+ε)-approximation algorithm with a list-adjusting technique to achieve min{η²+ε, 2−1/m}. For uniformly-related machines, the adversary constructs instances from Cho and Sahni's family with k = ⌈2 log η⌉+1 groups of machines and jobs at exponentially increasing speeds, proving a tight ⌈log η⌉ lower bound.

## Results

For single-machine total completion time minimization, the paper establishes a smoothness lower bound of η (for η ≤ 1.835) and an η²-smooth upper bound, leaving a polynomial gap. For parallel identical machines makespan minimization, the lower bound is max{1+(⌊η²⌋−1)/⌊η²⌋, 1+(η²−1)/⌈η²⌉} and the upper bound is min{η²+ε, 2−1/m}, both improving on prior work. For uniformly-related machines makespan minimization, the paper proves a tight ⌈log η⌉ lower bound, matching the existing O(log η) upper bound asymptotically—the only problem where the gap is closed.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is with Bampis et al. (2023) for parallel identical machines makespan minimization. Bampis et al. established a lower bound of 2−1/⌊η²⌋ via a static adversary and an upper bound of min{2(η²+1)/3, 2} via Longest Predicted Job Size First. This paper improves the lower bound to max{1+(⌊η²⌋−1)/⌊η²⌋, 1+(η²−1)/⌈η²⌉} using a dynamic adversary that adapts to the prediction error, and improves the upper bound to min{η²+ε, 2−1/m} by combining a (1+ε)-approximation with list-adjusting. For small η (e.g., η² < 2), the new upper bound η²+ε is strictly better than 2(η²+1)/3, and the new lower bound is also tighter. For uniformly-related machines, the paper fills a gap left by Zhao et al. (2024), who provided an O(log η) upper bound but no lower bound; the new ⌈log η⌉ lower bound makes the result asymptotically tight.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper refines the smoothness metric within the algorithms-with-predictions framework (Lykouris & Vassilvitskii 2018; Purohit et al. 2018), arguing that the existing notion of competitive ratio as a function of prediction error can be vacuous because one can always construct instances where predictions carry no information at any error level. For single-machine completion time, it departs from the L1-distance error used by Purohit et al. (2018), Wei & Zhang (2020), and Bampis et al. (2022) in favor of a scale-invariant multiplicative error, and builds on the (1+ε)-approximation of Hochbaum & Shmoys (1987) and Leung (1989) for the parallel-machines algorithm. For parallel identical machines, it improves both the lower bound (via a dynamic adversary) and upper bound (via list-adjusting) of Bampis et al. (2023); for uniformly-related machines, it fills the missing lower-bound gap left by Zhao et al. (2022, 2024) using an instance family from Cho & Sahni (1980).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The refined smoothness definition provides a principled way to quantify the marginal informational value of predictions, with lower bounds now interpretable as fundamental limits on what any algorithm can achieve even when predictions are guaranteed to be informative. Gaps remain between upper and lower bounds for all three scheduling problems (e.g., η vs. η² for single-machine completion time), and the paper explicitly asks whether an algorithm can simultaneously achieve optimal consistency, robustness, and smoothness—a positive answer would fully characterize the potential of learning-augmented scheduling. The framework is also shown to generalize to other online problems (e.g., one-way trading), suggesting broader applicability beyond scheduling.
