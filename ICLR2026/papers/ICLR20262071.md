## Problem

Structure-based de novo protein binder design has been treated as a false dichotomy between generative methods (conditional generation trained on binder-target complexes) and hallucination methods (sequence optimization via structure predictor scores without a generative prior). This split prevents leveraging the strengths of both paradigms, and the limited availability of experimental multimer structures in the PDB (~225k entries) further constrains training expressive generative models for binder design.

## Input / Output

For protein targets, the input is a target structure in Atom37 representation (up to 37 3D coordinates per residue) combined with amino acid identity features and binary hotspot tokens marking interface residues; for small molecule targets, the input is atom-level features (type, name, 3D coordinates, charge, graph Laplacian positional encodings) with pair features from bond orders and target-to-binder distances. The output is a fully atomistic binder structure (all side-chain atoms in Atom37 format) co-generated with its amino acid sequence, eliminating the need for downstream ProteinMPNN or LigandMPNN sequence re-design. Binders are generated at lengths of 40–250 residues, and the model uses separate denoising schedules for Cα coordinates and latent variables during inference.

## Method

Complexa extends La-Proteina's partially latent flow matching framework (a VAE encodes non-Cα atoms and sequence into per-residue 8-dim latent variables z, while Cα coordinates are modeled explicitly) with a novel latent target conditioning mechanism: target Atom37 features, amino acid identities, and hotspot tokens are linearly embedded and concatenated to the noisy binder embeddings in the token dimension, then jointly processed by a transformer with pair-biased attention over the extended binder+target sequence. A scalar random global translation is added to Cα coordinates during training to force the model to refine binder positioning throughout generation. Training follows a staged protocol: autoencoder pretraining on AFDB monomers, flow model pretraining on monomers, then fine-tuning on binder-target pairs using the new Teddymer dataset (3.5M clusters of synthetic dimers derived from AFDB50 domain-domain interactions) plus filtered PDB multimers for proteins, or PLINDER plus AFDB monomers with LoRA for small molecules. At inference, test-time compute is scaled via Best-of-N sampling, Beam Search, Feynman-Kac Steering, Monte Carlo Tree Search, or a Generate-and-Hallucinate pipeline, all guided by structure predictor ipAE scores or Rosetta hydrogen bond energies as rewards, unifying generative and hallucination paradigms within a single framework.

## Results

On protein binder design (22 targets, 200 samples each), Complexa achieves 9.10 unique in-silico successes with self-generated sequences, compared to 4.68 for RFDiffusion, 0.73 for Protpardelle-1c, and 0.31 for APM, while also being 4.5× faster than RFDiffusion (15.6s vs 70.8s per sample). On small molecule targets (SAM, OQO, FAD, IAI), Complexa yields 10/6/17/19 unique successes versus 2/3/5/8 for RFDiffusion-AllAtom. Under normalized compute budgets for inference-time optimization, Complexa's beam search, FKS, and MCTS consistently outperform BindCraft, BoltzDesign, and AlphaDesign on both easy and hard protein targets. On the 41-task AME enzyme design benchmark, Complexa outperforms RFDiffusion2 in 38/41 tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Complexa decisively outperforms all four generative baselines on protein targets: 9.10 unique successes (self) vs. RFDiffusion's 4.68 (MPNN), Protpardelle-1c's 0.73, and APM's 0.31 (self)/3.15 (MPNN), while also being 4.5× faster than RFDiffusion and APM. On small molecule targets, Complexa (10/6/17/19) surpasses RFDiffusion-AllAtom (2/3/5/8) across all four molecules with 6.5× faster sampling. For inference-time optimization, Complexa's search algorithms (Best-of-N, Beam Search, FKS, MCTS) consistently lead over BindCraft, BoltzDesign, and AlphaDesign under matched GPU-hour budgets on both easy and hard protein targets, with the gap widening on harder targets where structured search is essential. On the AME enzyme design benchmark, Complexa wins 38 out of 41 tasks against RFDiffusion2, both with self-generated and LigandMPNN re-designed sequences. Notably, Complexa's self-generated sequences alone outperform all baselines that rely on ProteinMPNN/LigandMPNN re-design, eliminating that additional pipeline step entirely.

## Relation to Prior Work

Complexa extends La-Proteina's partially latent flow matching architecture with a novel latent target conditioning mechanism, adapting a monomer generation framework to conditional binder design without modifying the autoencoder. Conceptually, the paper's central departure is arguing that the field's split between generative methods (RFDiffusion, APM, Protpardelle) and hallucination methods (BindCraft, BoltzDesign, AlphaDesign) is a false dichotomy, and unifying them by using a pretrained generative prior as the search space for inference-time optimization—directly analogous to how modern LLM and image generation systems combine a base model with test-time compute scaling. The Teddymer dataset, derived from AFDB domain-domain interactions via TED annotations, addresses a data bottleneck that prior generative binder methods (trained on limited PDB multimers) could not overcome, while the test-time scaling algorithms (beam search, MCTS, Feynman-Kac steering) are adapted from the diffusion literature rather than protein design.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work establishes a scalable, unified paradigm for atomistic binder design that eliminates the need for sequence re-design (ProteinMPNN/LigandMPNN) and demonstrates that test-time compute scaling—previously unexplored in structure-based protein design—can dramatically improve success rates, especially on hard multi-chain targets where brute-force hallucination methods plateau. The introduction of Teddymer (3.5M synthetic dimers) provides a valuable community resource that addresses the chronic scarcity of experimental multimer data. Key open questions include wet-lab validation of generated binders, extension to additional molecular modalities (DNA, RNA, antibodies), and whether a single unified model can handle all target types. The in-silico-only evaluation remains a limitation, though the paper's comprehensive benchmarking across protein, small molecule, and enzyme design tasks provides strong evidence of generality.
