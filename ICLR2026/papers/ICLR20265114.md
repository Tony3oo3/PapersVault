## Problem

Existing online time series forecasting methods update model parameters (final layers or adapter modules) to handle distribution shift, but the authors argue that shifts originate from changes in underlying latent factors, making feature-space correction more principled. A second critical gap is that multi-step forecasting introduces delayed feedback—true values for a k-step-ahead prediction only arrive k steps later—rendering standard gradient-based updates unreliable. The paper asks whether adjusting feature representations of latent factors, rather than model weights, can more effectively and stably adapt to non-stationary environments.

## Input / Output

The base forecasting model (iTransformer, SOFTS, or TimesNet) consumes the past 96 time steps and produces predictions for the next 12, 24, or 48 steps. ADAPT-Z operates on the feature representation z_t extracted from the second-to-last block of the base model, combined with a historical gradient vector computed by averaging the loss gradient over a batch of b=24 samples from the window [t−k−b, t−k]. The adapter outputs a correction term δ_t of the same dimensionality as z_t, which is added to z_t before the prediction head g produces the final forecast.

## Method

ADAPT-Z decomposes the forecasting model into an encoder f and prediction head g, then learns a correction δ_t such that g(z_t + δ_t) approximates the true target. The correction is produced by a lightweight MLP adapter with a dual-path architecture: separate linear layers transform the current feature z_t and the historical gradient independently (to handle their large magnitude disparity), their outputs are summed, and two additional linear layers produce δ_t. Historical gradients are computed over a batch of b=24 past samples to reduce variance, and the adapter is updated online via k-step delayed gradient descent—caching features, gradients, and outputs so that when the true value at time t arrives, the loss from the prediction made at t−k is backpropagated to update both the adapter and the final linear layer of g. This design sidesteps the multi-step delay problem because δ_t is predicted directly from z_t (available immediately) while the adapter's own parameters are updated with the delayed signal.

## Results

ADAPT-Z achieves the lowest average MSE on all 13 datasets (4 ETT, 4 PEMS, weather, solar, traffic, electricity, exchange) when averaged across three base models (iTransformer, SOFTS, TimesNet) and three prediction horizons (12, 24, 48 steps). The improvement over the original (unadapted) models ranges from 0.98% (exchange) to 12.61% (solar), with notable gains on ETTm1 (12.42%) and traffic (9.49%). Even the simplest feature-space online gradient descent (fOGD) baseline achieves second-best results on most datasets, validating the feature-space adaptation paradigm.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ADAPT-Z outperforms all eight baselines on every one of the 13 datasets. Among the plug-and-play online methods, DSOF (replay-buffer OGD on an adapter), SOLID (drift-triggered prediction-layer fine-tuning), ADCSD (adapter OGD on model output), and Proceed (low-rank weight rescaling) are the closest competitors, typically trailing ADAPT-Z by 1–5% in MSE; for example, on ETTm1 ADAPT-Z achieves 0.1937 vs. SOLID's 0.2166 and DSOF's 0.2647. The simplest feature-space OGD (fOGD) is a surprisingly strong second-place method on most datasets, confirming that the feature-space paradigm itself is the key insight rather than the adapter architecture. FSNet and OneNet perform dramatically worse (MSE 4–20× higher) because they require proprietary model architectures and cannot be applied as plug-in modules to arbitrary predictors. On the solar dataset, even DSOF (0.1038) beats the original model, yet ADAPT-Z still achieves a further 8.3% reduction (0.0948), highlighting the benefit of fusing current features with historical gradients.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in online time series forecasting, where prior methods (DSOF, ADCSD, SOLID, Proceed) focus on selecting which model parameters to update (final layers, output-space adapters) and how to update them (replay buffers, dual-stream EMA). ADAPT-Z instead argues that distribution shift originates from changes in latent factors, and therefore the correction should be applied at the feature-representation level (the encoder output z_t) rather than at the output or parameter level. It repurposes the adapter-module concept common in prior work but redirects it: rather than mapping model outputs to predictions, the adapter maps current features plus historical gradients to a feature-space correction δ_t. The 'learn-to-adapt' observation also connects to the broader meta-learning literature on training-for-adaptation, though the paper frames it specifically as a train-test mismatch (shuffled training vs. sequential deployment).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The feature-space adaptation paradigm offers a principled alternative to output-space or parameter-space updates, and the consistent gains across 13 datasets and 3 base models (iTransformer, SOFTS, TimesNet) suggest broad applicability. The 'learn-to-adapt' finding—that pre-training the adapter on sequential data enables adaptation without any online parameter updates—highlights a fundamental gap between how time series models are trained (i.i.d. shuffling) and how they are deployed (sequential), pointing toward a promising direction of incorporating sample ordering into training. Key open questions remain: how to automatically select the optimal feature extraction layer (currently dataset-dependent), how to extend the approach to linear models lacking an encoder-head decomposition, and whether the train-test ordering mismatch can be more systematically addressed during the initial training phase.
