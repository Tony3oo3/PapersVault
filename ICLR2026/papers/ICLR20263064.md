## Problem

Generative control policies (GCPs) based on flow and diffusion models have become the dominant paradigm in robotic behavior cloning, yet the reasons for their superiority over simple regression policies remain speculative. Common hypotheses attribute GCPs' advantage to capturing multi-modal action distributions or expressing more complex observation-to-action mappings through iterative computation. This paper systematically refutes both explanations and identifies the true source of GCPs' performance gains.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263064/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The policy network takes as input an observation o (robot state, RGB image, 3D point cloud, or language instruction), a scalar time index t ∈ {0, t*}, and an interpolant I_t that encodes a noisy version of the predicted action. Unlike flow-based GCPs that predict a velocity field and integrate over 9+ Euler steps, MIP directly predicts the action at each of its two steps. At inference, the output is a deterministic action chunk: the first step produces a coarse prediction from zero noise, and the second step refines it using the first step's output as input, requiring only 2 network forward evaluations (NFEs) versus 9 for flow.

## Method

The paper introduces a taxonomy decomposing GCPs into three components: distributional learning (C1), stochasticity injection (C2), and supervised iterative computation (C3), then ablates each via intermediate policies (Straight Flow, Residual Regression, Two-Step Denoising) to isolate their contributions. The key algorithmic contribution is the Minimal Iterative Policy (MIP), a two-step regression policy trained with a joint L2 loss: the first step predicts the action from zero noise at t=0, and the second step refines from a noisy interpolant at t*=0.9, with Gaussian noise injected only into the second step's training input. At inference, MIP is fully deterministic (z=0), requiring just two forward passes. The paper proves (Theorem 1) that under log-concave action distributions, flow models' Lipschitz constants are bounded by a constant factor over regression, ruling out expressivity as the explanation. The mechanism behind MIP's success is identified as 'manifold adherence'—the tendency to project predictions onto the low-dimensional manifold of expert actions under out-of-distribution observations—which C3 enables through iterative refinement and C2 stabilizes by preventing compounding errors across steps.

## Results

Across 28 behavior cloning benchmarks spanning state, image, point-cloud, and language modalities, MIP (a two-step regression-based policy with training-time noise) essentially matches flow-based GCPs on all tasks, while regression policies (RCPs) fall behind only on a small subset of high-precision tasks (e.g., Tool-Hang: MIP 0.80 vs. Flow 0.80 vs. Regression 0.54 on state; Transport: MIP 0.76 vs. Flow 0.80 vs. Regression 0.50). On the multi-task LIBERO benchmark (130 tasks, π₀ backbone), MIP achieves 95.8/95.2/97.6/82.2% success on Object/Goal/Spatial/10-task splits, matching or exceeding Flow (97.4/95.0/95.8/81.6%) and substantially outperforming RCP (92.6/94.6/97.2/78.0%). MIP also matches common consistency models with half the training time, and often outperforms distilled shortcut/few-step policies.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is MIP vs. flow-based GCPs: MIP matches Flow on essentially all 28 benchmarks (e.g., Tool-Hang state: 0.92 vs. 0.86 best; Transport state: 0.76 vs. 0.80 average), confirming that distributional learning (C1) is unnecessary. Against regression baselines (RCPs), MIP wins decisively on high-precision tasks (Tool-Hang: 0.92 vs. 0.52; Transport: 0.76 vs. 0.50) but ties on easier tasks (Lift, Can, Kitchen all near 1.0). Against consistency models (Song et al., 2023) and shortcut models (Frans et al., 2024), MIP matches or outperforms them while using only 2 NFEs and half the training time, because those methods still satisfy C1 (distributional learning) and require training over a continuum of noise levels. The ablation variants (Straight Flow, Residual Regression) confirm that neither stochasticity injection alone nor supervised iterative computation alone suffices—only their combination (MIP) recovers GCP-level performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the flow matching and stochastic interpolant frameworks (Lipman et al., 2023; Albergo & Vanden-Eijnden, 2022) that underpin modern generative control policies like Diffusion Policy (Chi et al., 2023) and π_0 (Black et al., 2024), but reframes the question from 'how to build better GCPs' to 'what actually makes them work?' It departs from the dominant narrative that GCPs succeed by capturing multi-modal action distributions or expressing more complex observation-to-action mappings, instead proposing a three-component taxonomy (distributional learning, stochasticity injection, supervised iterative computation) and showing that only the latter two matter. The Minimal Iterative Policy (MIP) is positioned as a new intermediate between regression and flow-based GCPs that isolates the C2+C3 combination, and is explicitly distinguished from consistency/shortcut models (Song et al., 2023; Frans et al., 2024) which do satisfy distributional learning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that distributional learning is the least important component of GCPs directly challenges the field's core motivation for adopting generative architectures in control, suggesting that practitioners may be paying a significant inference cost (9+ NFEs) for a component that contributes little. MIP's ability to match flow-based GCPs with only 2 NFEs and deterministic inference offers a practical efficiency win, and the broader finding that architecture choice dominates over the generative-vs-regression distinction redirects design effort toward backbone selection. However, the mechanism behind 'manifold adherence'—the inductive bias that actually explains GCPs' advantage on high-precision tasks—remains unexplained, and the paper acknowledges that truly multi-modal settings (where the observation space is low-dimensional relative to demonstration count) may still benefit from distributional learning. Open questions include whether C2+C3 can be further simplified, whether the findings extend to online/RL settings, and what the precise relationship is between manifold adherence and closed-loop robustness.
