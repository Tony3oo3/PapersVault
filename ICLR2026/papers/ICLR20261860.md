## Problem

Existing reasoning-based safety alignment methods (e.g., STAIR, STAR-1, o1) rely on chain-of-thought risk assessment but fail against advanced optimization-based jailbreaks like AutoDAN-Turbo and Adversarial Reasoning, which conceal malicious goals behind seemingly benign prompts. The root cause is that these models cannot reliably extract the true core intent from out-of-distribution jailbreak prompts, leading to misaligned safety judgments. This paper asks: how can a model systematically identify the hidden core intent regardless of the obfuscation strategy used?

## Input / Output

At inference, ARMOR receives a user prompt alongside a system prompt containing a dynamic, updatable jailbreak strategy library (name + definition + example per strategy) and a customizable safety policy. The model produces a structured three-step reasoning chain—strategy analysis, intent analysis, and policy-based safety analysis—delimited by special <step> tokens, followed by a final answer (compliant response or refusal) in <answer> tags. ARMOR-Think additionally inserts free chain-of-thought reasoning between <think> tags in the answer for benign prompts, while unsafe prompts receive only a refusal without extended thinking.

## Method

ARMOR introduces a structured three-step 'Meticulous Reasoning' pipeline: (1) strategy analysis matches the prompt against an external strategy library to identify the obfuscation technique, (2) intent analysis uses the identified strategy to reverse-engineer the core malicious intent, and (3) policy-based safety analysis checks the extracted intent against a safety policy. Training proceeds in two stages: SFT on constructed reasoning data where a dynamic strategy library (randomly dropping unrelated strategies) is placed in the system prompt to encourage generalization over memorization, followed by step-wise DPO using grounded tree sampling where each reasoning step is individually scored against ground-truth strategy/intent/safety labels via GPT-4o. A process reward model trained on the same tree-sampled data enables test-time scaling via beam search or best-of-N selection. ARMOR-Think further decouples safety from general reasoning by simplifying the three safety steps to one-third their token length and injecting free CoT thinking for benign prompts, supervised by a ternary reward combining safety, helpfulness, and structural scores.

## Results

ARMOR achieves an average attack success rate (ASR) of 0.06 against advanced optimization-based jailbreaks (AutoDAN-Turbo: 0.04, Adversarial Reasoning: 0.08), compared to 0.40–0.66 for all other reasoning-based baselines. On safety benchmarks, ARMOR attains an average harmful compliance rate of 0.002, outperforming the next-best model (STAIR-DPO at 0.049) by 96%. ARMOR-Think further reduces the average attack ASR to 0.05 while maintaining a harmful rate of 0.009, and improves utility to 0.91 on GSM8k and 0.84 on MATH, surpassing the base Qwen-2.5 (0.89/0.79) and matching DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B on GSM8k.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ARMOR dominates all six baseline groups on both attack robustness and safety compliance. Against the strongest reasoning-based baseline STAIR-DPO (ASR 0.40, harmfulness 0.049), ARMOR reduces the attack ASR by 85% (0.06 vs. 0.40) and the harmful rate by 96% (0.002 vs. 0.049). Against API-based models o1 and o3-mini (ASR 0.55/0.54), ARMOR achieves a roughly 9-fold reduction in attack success rate. Against the non-reasoning baselines Qwen-2.5 (ASR 0.97) and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B (ASR 0.76), ARMOR's advantage is even more pronounced, with ASR dropping to 0.06. On over-refusal (XSTest Safe), ARMOR (0.860) outperforms STAIR-DPO (0.716) and STAR-1 (0.680), though it trails Qwen-2.5 (0.968) and o1 (0.900), indicating a modest trade-off in permissiveness. ARMOR-Think matches ARMOR's attack robustness (ASR 0.05) while slightly increasing harmfulness (0.009) but significantly improving utility (GSM8k 0.91 vs. 0.86 for ARMOR).

## Relation to Prior Work

ARMOR extends the inference-time safety reasoning paradigm introduced by STAIR and STAR-1, which use chain-of-thought to assess risks before generating responses, but departs fundamentally by structuring this reasoning into a three-step pipeline (strategy analysis → intent extraction → policy-based safety verification) anchored by an external, updatable strategy library. Rather than relying on the model to internally recognize jailbreak patterns from training data, ARMOR trains the model to consult a reference library of named strategies, converting an out-of-distribution jailbreak into an in-distribution intent classification problem. The step-wise DPO and process reward model for test-time scaling are adapted from the general reasoning literature (Lai et al., 2024; Lightman et al., 2023) and repurposed here for verifiable safety reasoning, while ARMOR-Think further decouples safety reasoning from general free-thinking to preserve utility.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies and addresses a critical vulnerability: existing reasoning-based safety-aligned models (including o1, STAIR, STAR-1) are heavily defeated by advanced optimization-based jailbreaks like AutoDAN-Turbo and Adversarial Reasoning, which conceal malicious intent behind seemingly benign prompts. ARMOR's strategy-library approach offers a practical, low-cost adaptation mechanism—new jailbreak strategies can be added to the system prompt at inference time without retraining—making it a deployable defense against the continuously evolving jailbreak landscape. Key limitations include the inference-time overhead of structured reasoning (partially mitigated by ARMOR-Think), reliance on LLM-as-judge for evaluation, and evaluation on a single 7B base model family with only 50 attack samples per method, leaving open questions about scalability to larger models and broader attack taxonomies.
