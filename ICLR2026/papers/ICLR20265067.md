## Problem

Existing uncertainty quantification methods for LVLMs capture only total predictive uncertainty, failing to distinguish between the two underlying sources of epistemic uncertainty—internal contradictions (conflict) and absence of supporting information (ignorance)—that drive different categories of misbehavior such as hallucinations, jailbreaks, adversarial failures, and OOD errors. This limits both detection accuracy and the ability to diagnose *why* a model misbehaves.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20265067/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes only the pre-logits feature vector Z from the LVLM's output projection layer during a single forward pass, requiring no additional sampling, auxiliary models, or fine-tuning. It produces per-token conflict (CF) and ignorance (IG) scores, which are averaged across all generated tokens to yield a sentence-level uncertainty value for each of the two uncertainty dimensions. Because it operates on internal features rather than output probabilities, it avoids the overconfidence problem of softmax-based entropy and the 10× computational overhead of sampling-based baselines.

## Method

EUQ interprets the linear projection layer of the LVLM output head as an evidence-fusion operator, following the theoretical framework of Dencux (2019). Pre-logits features Z are mapped to an evidence-weight matrix E via an element-wise affine transformation whose parameters A and B are solved in closed form under the Least Commitment Principle (minimizing total commitment subject to a bias constraint). E is decomposed into positive (support) and negative (opposition) parts, each converted to simple mass functions via 1−exp(−e). First-stage fusion aggregates evidence across input features per output dimension (exploiting additivity of evidence weights for shared focal sets), and second-stage fusion combines all output dimensions via Dempster's rule. Conflict (CF) is the total mass assigned to the empty set during the positive–negative fusion, while ignorance (IG) is the sum of mass assigned to the full frame of discernment, yielding two interpretable, training-free uncertainty scores in a single forward pass.

## Results

Across four LVLMs (DeepSeek-VL2-Tiny, Qwen2.5-VL-7B, InternVL2.5-8B, MoF-Models-7B) and four misbehavior categories (hallucination, jailbreak, adversarial, OOD), CF achieves an average AUROC of 0.812 and AUPR of 0.783, while IG achieves 0.783 AUROC and 0.785 AUPR. This represents a relative improvement of up to 10.4% AUROC and 5.5% AUPR over the strongest baseline (HiddenDetect, 0.707 AUROC / 0.658 AUPR). Notably, CF is most effective for hallucination detection (avg. AUROC 0.761, AUPR 0.824) and adversarial detection (AUROC 0.836), while IG dominates OOD detection (AUROC 0.948, AUPR 0.846).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CF outperforms all five baselines on AUROC across every misbehavior category, with the largest margins over sampling-based methods (SC: 0.626, SE: 0.624) and a 10.5-point gap over the strongest baseline HiddenDetect (0.707). IG similarly surpasses all baselines on AUPR (0.785 vs. best baseline 0.730 for SC), though its AUROC (0.783) is slightly below CF. On hallucination specifically, CF achieves 0.761 AUROC / 0.824 AUPR, beating HiddenDetect (0.703/0.614) by 5.8/11.0 points; on OOD, IG reaches 0.948 AUROC / 0.846 AUPR, far exceeding PE (0.666/0.733) and HiddenDetect (0.694/0.651). For jailbreaks, CF (0.757 AUROC) marginally edges out HiddenDetect (0.752), the method originally designed for that task, while for adversarial examples both CF (0.836) and IG (0.861) substantially outperform all baselines. The paper also reports that EUQ requires only a single forward pass (~9.1×10⁻⁴ s for CF) versus ~0.89 s for sampling-based SC/SE, making it roughly 1000× faster while achieving superior detection accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Dempster-Shafer Theory (DST) framework from Denœux (2019), who showed that linear transformations can be interpreted as evidence fusion, to the setting of LVLM output heads. It builds directly on the authors' prior work (Huang et al., 2025) on evidential conflict for hallucination detection, but generalizes from a single misbehavior type to four categories and introduces the ignorance (IG) component alongside conflict (CF). Unlike evidential deep learning (EDL) approaches that require training or fine-tuning, EUQ is entirely training-free and operates in a single forward pass, departing from both Bayesian UQ methods (computationally prohibitive for LVLMs) and sampling-based methods (SC, semantic entropy) that require multiple generations. The Least Commitment Principle from Smets (1993) provides the optimization criterion for the closed-form belief assignment, distinguishing this from ad-hoc evidence weighting schemes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The clean decomposition of epistemic uncertainty into conflict (internal contradictions) and ignorance (missing information) offers a principled diagnostic: hallucinations map to high CF while OOD failures map to high IG, suggesting targeted interventions for each failure mode. The training-free, single-forward-pass design (9.1×10⁻⁴ s per token vs. ~0.9 s for sampling methods) makes it practical for real-time deployment, and the generality of the linear-projector-as-evidence-fusion interpretation extends beyond LVLMs to any architecture with a linear head. Key limitations include the requirement for white-box access (incompatible with closed-source APIs like GPT-4V) and the finding that misbehavior-type separation is only apparent in certain decoder layers, limiting the interpretability claim. Open questions include whether the layer-wise dynamics (IG decreasing, CF increasing with depth) can be exploited for targeted interventions, and whether the approach transfers to larger-scale or multimodal-generation settings.
