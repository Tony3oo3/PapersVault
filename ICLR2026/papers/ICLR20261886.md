## Problem

Classical scaling laws predict that more training data monotonically improves generalization, yet methods like LIMO and s1 achieve superior performance with small, aggressively curated datasets. No principled theoretical framework existed to explain when and why data curation helps versus when full-data training remains optimal, leaving practitioners without analytical conditions for choosing a curation strategy.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261886/cropped_page5_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The theoretical framework operates on high-dimensional binary classification data (Gaussian features x ∈ R^d, labels y ∈ {±1}) where a pruning oracle filters examples by their margin along an oracle direction w_o and optionally by label correctness. In empirical validation, the method consumes 224×224 ImageNet images (1,000 classes) processed by a ViT-B/16 backbone, and produces exact test error curves as a function of pruning fraction p, dataset size n, and generator quality ρ, along with phase-transition boundaries identifying the optimal curation regime. The framework also interprets LLM math reasoning results on the AIME benchmark where the 'generator' is a base LLM producing reasoning traces.

## Method

The paper develops a random matrix theory (RMT) analysis of ridge-regularized linear classification under data pruning, modeling label shift between a generator distribution (w_g) and ground truth (w_*) in the high-dimensional proportionate limit (d/n → φ). The key mechanism is deriving deterministic equivalents for the resolvent matrix R of the pruned design matrix, which yields exact closed-form test error expressions (Theorems 1 and 3) parameterized by four scalars (p, γ, β, β̃) that fully capture the effect of any symmetric pruning function q. The central result (Theorem 2) shows a sharp phase transition: when the generator is strong (ρ→1) and the oracle is aligned (ρ_*→1), 'keep hard' pruning uniquely minimizes test error, while when the generator is weak (ρ<1), 'keep easy' pruning is optimal. This is validated empirically on ImageNet with ViT-B/16 (showing a crossover from keep-easy to keep-hard as initial data size grows from 160K to 1.2M) and used to reconcile contradictory LLM math reasoning findings where 'less is more' holds for average AIME performance but 'more is more' holds for the hardest questions.

## Results

On ImageNet-1K with ViT-B/16, the optimal curation strategy exhibits a clear crossover: with only 160K initial training examples (weak generator), 'keep easy' pruning outperforms 'keep hard', while with 1.2M examples (strong generator), 'keep hard' becomes superior and approaches ground-truth-label performance. In the model collapse setting (iterative pseudo-labeling), 'keep hard' pruning at each round stabilizes accuracy across iterations, whereas training on all data causes progressive degradation. On AIME 2024 (aggregated from LIMO/s1 literature), curating just 1k examples from a 59k pool achieves 56.7% Pass@1, outperforming the full 114k Openthinker dataset (50.2%) and the base Qwen2.5-32B model (16.5%), confirming the 'less is more' regime predicted by the theory.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new curation method to benchmark against existing ones; instead, it compares pruning strategies (keep hard, keep easy, random) under controlled generator-quality and data-scale conditions. In the data-rich, strong-generator regime (bottom-left of Figure 1), 'keep hard' uniquely minimizes test error over all strategies with the same pruning ratio, while 'random' selection of the same size performs worse. In the weak-generator or small-data regimes, 'keep easy' or no pruning (p=1) is optimal, confirming 'more is more'. For model collapse on ImageNet, 'keep hard' pruning at each self-training round maintains accuracy across iterations, while the uncurated baseline (train on all pseudo-labeled data) degrades monotonically. The theory also reconciles apparently contradictory LLM results: on average AIME performance the base LLM is a strong generator (favoring aggressive 'keep hard' curation), but on the hardest AIME questions it is a weak generator (favoring 'more is more'), with 1M examples (64.9%) outperforming 114k (47.9%) on hard-level questions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the margin-based data pruning framework of Sorscher et al. (2022) by adding a label-aware curation dimension (filtering for label correctness in addition to difficulty) and by deriving exact high-dimensional scaling laws via random matrix theory rather than relying on empirical power-law fits. It generalizes the synthetic-data verification setup of Feng et al. (2025) and El Firdoussi et al. (2024), which only verify label correctness, by incorporating difficulty-based pruning as an additional axis. The model-collapse analysis builds directly on Dohmatob et al.'s regression framework (2024a, 2025), extending it to show that strategic curation can stabilize iterative self-training. The paper positions itself as providing the theoretical justification for empirical 'less is more' results from LIMO and s1 in LLM reasoning, which had previously lacked a principled explanation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides actionable phase-transition conditions (generator quality ρ, data scale n, pruning ratio p) that tell practitioners when aggressive curation helps versus when full-data training is optimal, resolving the apparent contradiction between LIMO/s1 and classical scaling laws. The model-collapse mitigation result is practically significant for iterative self-training pipelines. Key limitations include the restriction to linear classifiers with Gaussian features, binary classification, and single-epoch training; the LLM interpretation is post-hoc rather than directly tested, and the ImageNet validation uses a single architecture (ViT-B/16). Open questions include extending the theory to kernel/non-linear regimes, multi-epoch optimization, and online/adaptive curation loops.
