## Problem

Equivariant methods (data augmentation, equivariant architectures) implicitly assume that a datapoint and its group-transformed version are equally likely under the data distribution. When this assumption is violated—i.e., the dataset exhibits distributional symmetry breaking or symmetry bias—equivariant methods may discard task-relevant information, yet no practical metric exists to quantify the degree of such bias, and the theoretical conditions under which equivariance helps versus hurts remain unclear.

## Input / Output

The primary metric m(p_X) takes an unlabeled train/test split and a compact symmetry group G (e.g., SO(3), C4, S_n), and outputs a scalar test accuracy in [0.5, 1] where 0.5 indicates full isotropy and 1 indicates full canonicalization. The auxiliary task-dependent metric t(p_X,Y) additionally requires labeled data and a randomly initialized equivariant network as a canonicalization probe, outputting a loss ratio that flags when orientation bias is predictive of labels. The theoretical component produces closed-form asymptotic risk expressions for ridge regression under non-symmetric covariance in both under- and over-parameterized regimes.

## Method

The core diagnostic is a two-sample classifier test: a small neural network is trained with binary cross-entropy to distinguish original samples from uniformly random group-augmented copies, and its held-out accuracy directly measures distributional distance between p_X and its symmetrized version p̄_X. A complementary task-dependent metric t(p_X,Y) predicts labels from a randomly initialized equivariant network's output c(x) versus from c(gx) under random g, with the loss ratio quantifying whether the symmetry bias carries task-relevant signal. Theoretically, the paper analyzes invariant ridge regression under non-symmetric covariance Σ (where gΣgᵀ ≠ Σ), proving that in the under-parameterized ridgeless regime augmentation always helps but test-time symmetrization can diverge, while in the over-parameterized regime (d > n) augmentation is provably harmful when a small number of 'coupling modes' strongly correlate invariant and non-invariant features. Empirically, the metric reveals high symmetry breaking (m(p_X) ≈ 87–98%) across QM9, QM7b, MNIST, ModelNet40, rMD17, OC20, and an LLM crystal dataset, with equivariant methods helping or hurting depending on the interplay between m(p_X), t(p_X,Y), and local equivariance of the architecture.

## Results

The paper's primary quantitative finding is that all benchmark datasets exhibit high distributional symmetry breaking: m(p_X) reaches 98.3% on QM9, 93.9% on ModelNet40, 89.7% on QM7b, and 86.9% on MNIST (vs. 50% for a fully symmetric distribution). On ModelNet40, no augmentation (FF) achieves 78.14% accuracy, substantially outperforming train-only augmentation (TF: 61.97%), train+test augmentation (TT: 62.39%), and the e3nn equivariant baseline (60.06%). Conversely, on QM9 the equivariant e3nn model achieves the best MAE across all three properties (C_v: 110, |μ|: 131, Δε: 151), while FF is worst (143, 275, 181). The task-dependent metric t(p_X,Y) is large (3.54) for QM7b dipole prediction where augmentation hurts, but near 1.0 for QM9 properties where augmentation helps.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its augmentation strategies (TT, TF, FT, FF) and group-averaging against the e3nn equivariant baseline (Geiger et al., 2022) across six datasets. The outcome is dataset-dependent: on ModelNet40, the no-augmentation FF setting (78.14%) dramatically outperforms the e3nn equivariant model (60.06%) by ~18 points, and on MNIST FF (98.8%) edges out e3nn (98.0%) by 0.8 points. In contrast, on QM9 the e3nn equivariant model wins on all three properties (C_v: 110 vs. FF's 143; |μ|: 131 vs. 275; Δε: 151 vs. 181), and on QM7b it achieves the best dipole MAE (41 vs. FF's 100). The task-dependent metric t(p_X,Y) explains this split: it is large (3.54) for QM7b dipole and ModelNet where augmentation hurts, but near 1.0 for QM9 properties where augmentation helps, suggesting the symmetry bias is task-relevant only in the former cases.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the theoretical framework of Elesedy & Zaidi (2021), who proved that test-time symmetrization always improves generalization under invariant data distributions, by analyzing what happens when the data distribution itself breaks symmetry. For the empirical diagnostic, it repurposes the two-sample classifier test of Lopez-Paz & Oquab (2017) from distribution-shift detection to quantifying distributional symmetry breaking, replacing the kernel-dependent MMD approach of Chiu & Bloem-Reddy (2023) with an architecture-based classifier whose accuracy is directly interpretable. The paper explicitly distinguishes its focus—distributional symmetry breaking (p(x) ≠ p(gx))—from the more commonly studied functional symmetry breaking (f(gx) ≠ gf(x)), and positions its theoretical analysis as the first to show augmentation can be harmful even under invariant ground-truth functions when the data covariance is non-symmetric.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that every major benchmark point cloud dataset (QM9, ModelNet40, MNIST, rMD17, OC20) is highly canonicalized (m(p_X) > 86%) challenges the implicit assumption in equivariant ML that data is approximately uniform over group orbits, and provides practitioners with a simple, interpretable diagnostic to check this assumption before applying augmentation or equivariant architectures. The counterintuitive result that augmentation still helps on molecular datasets despite high symmetry breaking—while hurting on ModelNet40—suggests that locality of features and task-label correlation with orientation are critical moderating factors, opening questions about why equivariant networks succeed on canonicalized data beyond what global equivariance alone explains. The task-dependent metric t(p_X,Y) and the practical flowchart (Figure 6) offer actionable guidance, but the paper acknowledges that a rigorous threshold above which equivariance is unlikely to help remains undetermined.
