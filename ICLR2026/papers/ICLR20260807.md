## Problem

Deep reinforcement learning suffers from plasticity loss, where neural networks progressively lose their ability to adapt to new data during long-horizon training. While empirical remedies such as network reset, neuron recycling, and noise injection have been proposed, the underlying mechanisms remain unexplained theoretically, leaving a significant gap between empiricism and theory. This paper provides the first theoretical characterization of plasticity loss in RL, identifying two causal mechanisms: NTK rank collapse and a Θ(1/k) gradient magnitude decay driven by non-stationarity in both data distributions and bootstrapping targets.

## Input / Output

SWD operates as a drop-in replacement for uniform sampling in experience replay, consuming the standard replay buffer augmented with per-transition collection timestamps. Each transition (s, a, r, s′, d) carries a timestamp t_i, and the method computes an age-based weight w_i = max(w_min, 1 − age_i/T) to form a categorical sampling distribution over the buffer, producing a weighted mini-batch of B transitions for the standard TD3, Double DQN, or SAC update step. No changes are made to the network architecture, loss function, or optimizer; the only modification is how samples are drawn from the buffer.

## Method

The theoretical analysis formalizes RL optimization as a sequence of non-stationary loss functions where each iteration starts from the previous iteration's argmin, and Theorem 3 decomposes the initial gradient into a distributional-shift term scaled by 1/k and a target-drift term, showing that the 1/k factor drives gradient magnitude toward zero and traps the optimizer at saddle points. SWD counteracts this by assigning each replay buffer sample a linearly decaying weight proportional to its recency, so that newer samples from the current policy distribution receive higher sampling probability, effectively neutralizing the 1/k attenuation and restoring gradient magnitude. The method is orthogonal to NTK-based plasticity preservation techniques (reset, regularization, architecture changes) because it operates at the data distribution level rather than the model level, enabling synergistic combination with such methods. A bucket-based approximation exploits the monotonicity of the age-weighting to reduce per-step sampling complexity from O(N) to O(B) where B ≪ N, making the computational overhead negligible in practice.

## Results

SWD achieves consistent performance improvements of 13.7%–30.1% in IQM scores across MuJoCo, ALE, and DMC benchmarks. On the most challenging DMC Humanoid-Run task, SWD (with SimBa-SAC) reaches an episode return of 229.01±37.43, compared to 190.46±7.99 for uniform sampling (SAC baseline), a ~20% gain. The largest improvement (+30.1%) occurs at UTD=5, where gradient attenuation is most severe. The approximate bucket-based SWD variant matches uniform sampling's wall-clock time (8.70 h vs 8.65 h) while retaining most of the performance gain (224.93±17.47).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SWD is compared against four baselines: PER (Prioritized Experience Replay), ReGraMa, S&P (warm-starting), and Plasticity Injection. Against PER, SWD delivers substantially larger gains with far less training overhead; the paper notes PER demands 'nearly several times more training time' while yielding 'extremely limited' improvements, consistent with the theory that PER optimizes TD-error magnitude rather than temporal recency. Against the three NTK-based plasticity methods (ReGraMa, S&P, Plasticity Injection), SWD outperforms each individually on the SimBa-SAC Humanoid-Run setting (Figure 8). Notably, SWD combined with S&P (SWD+S&P) achieves the best overall result, confirming the paper's claim of orthogonality: SWD operates at the data-distribution/sampling level while S&P operates at the parameter-reset level, so they address complementary causes of plasticity loss. The reverse-validation ablation (SWA, which up-weights older samples) consistently underperforms both SWD and uniform sampling, with lower gradient L1 norms and higher GraMa scores, directly confirming that the temporal weighting direction is the critical factor.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by providing the first unified theoretical framework for plasticity loss in deep RL, decomposing it into two distinct mechanisms: NTK rank collapse and Θ(1/k) gradient attenuation. While prior work (ReDo, ReGraMa, Plasticity Injection, Reducing Churn) has addressed plasticity loss through model-level interventions—parameter resets, architecture modifications, or NTK regularization—this paper identifies a data-distribution-level cause (temporal weighting in experience replay) that is orthogonal to all of these. The proposed Sample Weight Decay (SWD) repurposes the simple idea of recency-weighted sampling, previously explored in continual learning, as a principled countermeasure to the theoretically-derived gradient decay, positioning it as a complementary rather than competing approach to NTK-based methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical decomposition into two independent mechanisms (rank collapse vs. gradient attenuation) gives the field a clearer diagnostic framework for understanding and addressing plasticity loss, and the demonstrated orthogonality of SWD to NTK-based methods (SWD+S&P outperforming either alone) suggests that combining data-level and model-level interventions may yield further gains. However, the theoretical analysis is grounded in a simplified FQI setting and the authors themselves note it represents only the 'tip of the iceberg,' leaving open how these mechanisms interact in more complex actor-critic or off-policy settings. Practical limitations include evaluation restricted to MuJoCo, ALE, and DMC benchmarks, with no validation on real-world or large-scale environments, and the method's benefit may diminish in settings where the replay buffer is small or the UTD ratio is very low.
