## Problem

Evaluating Descriptive Multimodal Emotion Recognition (DMER) is difficult because ground-truth emotion descriptions are inherently incomplete and costly to produce, while pairwise human preference annotation scales as C(M,2)×N comparisons. This paper asks whether multimodal LLMs can serve as automatic judges for emotion preference prediction, replacing expensive manual annotation with a cost-efficient MLLM-based evaluation strategy.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263232/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The judge MLLM receives a video clip (with synchronized audio) and two candidate free-form emotion descriptions, and outputs a preference label: 'D1', 'D2', or 'tie'. The dataset contains 1,368 video samples (single front-facing speaker, complete audio) sourced from the intersection of MERR-Fine and MER-Caption+, each paired with one description from each dataset. Beyond the per-sample preference label, the benchmark also produces a swap-consistency score (measuring robustness to input order) and, in the practical application, Bradley-Terry advantage scores for ranking multiple emotion-description models.

## Method

The paper introduces four prompting strategies (S1–S4) of increasing reasoning depth, where S3/S4 offload the text-reasoning step to an external LLM (Qwen2.5-7B) after the MLLM generates its own video description. A key finding is that this hybrid MLLM+LLM decomposition outperforms pure MLLM prompting for most models, but models with strong native language capabilities (Qwen2.5-VL, Qwen2.5-Omni) prefer the simpler S1/S2 strategies, revealing a trade-off between compensating for weak language understanding and avoiding error accumulation in longer chains. The authors further propose model-based crowdsourcing—aggregating predictions from the top-k performing MLLMs via majority voting—which improves both recognition accuracy and swap consistency. Evaluation uses two complementary metrics: weighted-average F1 (on two-class and three-class settings) for preference accuracy, and swap consistency for order-robustness. The best single model (Qwen2.5-Omni, S2) achieves 67.21% two-class WAF and 79.09% swap consistency, still well below the ~69% human inter-annotator agreement upper bound.

## Results

On EmoPrefer-Data, the best-performing MLLM is Qwen2.5-Omni (Strategy S2), achieving 67.21% two-class WAF and 79.09% swap consistency, approaching the human upper bound of ~69.31% inter-annotator agreement. Among closed-source models, Gemini-1.5-Flash leads with 64.64% WAF, while GPT-4.1 achieves the highest swap consistency (80.84%) but lower recognition (60.75% WAF). Most models outperform random guessing (50%) yet remain well below the human ceiling, confirming the inherent difficulty of emotion preference decoding. Model-based crowdsourcing (top-k open-source models) further improves both recognition and swap consistency.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Qwen2.5-Omni (67.21% WAF) outperforms all closed-source models, surpassing the best closed-source baseline Gemini-1.5-Flash (64.64%) by 2.57 points and GPT-4.1 (60.75%) by 6.46 points on two-class WAF. Notably, open-source models do not show a noticeable performance gap versus closed-source ones; several open-source models (Qwen2.5-Omni, Qwen2.5-VL, Qwen2-Audio) exceed all closed-source baselines on recognition. GPT-4.1 achieves the highest swap consistency (80.84%) among all models, indicating strong order-robustness, but its recognition (60.75% WAF) trails Qwen2.5-Omni by 6.46 points. Weaker open-source models (VideoChat, Video-ChatGPT, Otter) cluster near random (51–52% WAF), highlighting that not all MLLMs possess adequate emotion understanding. The paper further shows that model-based crowdsourcing over top-k open-source models (k=3–4) improves both recognition and swap consistency, and that restricting to open-source models reduces cost without significant performance loss compared to using all models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of Descriptive Multimodal Emotion Recognition (DMER), which replaces discrete emotion labels with free-form natural language descriptions, and the LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm, which uses language models to evaluate output quality via pairwise preferences. Prior LLM-as-a-Judge benchmarks (MT-Bench, JudgeBench, MLLM-as-a-Judge) have focused on text-only or image-based preferences for general tasks like writing and reasoning; this work extends the preference-prediction task to the full multimodal (audio + video + text) setting and specifically to human emotion understanding. For model ranking, it adopts the Bradley-Terry model from the statistical literature, applying it to aggregate pairwise MLLM-judged preferences into global model scores for DMER evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic evidence that MLLMs can partially decode human emotion preferences (best model reaches ~67% WAF vs. ~69% human agreement), making automatic evaluation of descriptive emotion systems feasible without expensive human annotation. However, the gap to human-level performance, the ambiguity of tie judgments, and the zero-shot-only evaluation leave open whether fine-tuned reward models or specialized architectures can close this gap. The finding that model-based crowdsourcing over open-source models alone suffices is practically significant for cost-constrained settings, and the dataset/benchmark will likely become a standard resource for training emotion-aware reward models in RLHF pipelines.
