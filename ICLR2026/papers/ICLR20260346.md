## Problem

Existing search-agent benchmarks (DeepSearch, DeepResearch) evaluate either locating hard-to-find facts or synthesizing complex narratives, but none assess the ability to reliably and completely gather large-scale atomic information across many entities. This 'wide-context' collection task—where the bottleneck is operational scale and fidelity rather than cognitive difficulty—remains largely unevaluated, leaving a critical gap in understanding whether current LLM agents can serve as dependable information-gathering tools.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260346/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each task instance is a tuple (Q, S): a natural-language query specifying target entities and required attributes, plus a predefined table schema S = {C₁, …, Cₘ} that fixes the exact column structure. The agent must produce a parsable Markdown table T_agent with n rows (one per entity) and m columns, where every cell is scored against a ground-truth table. Evaluation yields three metrics of increasing granularity: Success Rate (binary, requiring a perfect table match), Row-level F1 (treating each row as a unit), and Item-level F1 (treating each cell as a unit), reported under Avg@N, Pass@N, and Max@N aggregation over N independent runs.

## Method

WideSearch is a 200-task benchmark (100 English, 100 Chinese, 18 domains) constructed through a five-stage curation pipeline: sourcing real user queries, exhaustive gold-standard annotation with procedural metrics, parametric-knowledge filtering (discarding questions answerable from model memory), difficulty-based pruning (requiring ≥10 min and ≥10 unique web pages), and iterative refinement until automated scores align with human judgment at ≥95% consistency. The evaluation pipeline formalizes scoring as table alignment and cell-wise verification: syntax validation and primary-key-based row joining, followed by column-specific cell scoring that combines deterministic checks (exact match, numerical approximation, date/URL matching) with an LLM-as-a-judge for semantically flexible fields. The paper benchmarks 10+ systems across single-agent, multi-agent (divide-and-conquer with parallel sub-agents), and end-to-end commercial modes, revealing that even the best multi-agent framework (GPT-5) achieves only 7.3% success rate, while test-time scaling shows item-level F1 approaching 80% at 128 retries but table-level success remaining below 20%—confirming that the core failure is completeness and fidelity at scale, not individual fact retrieval.

## Results

On the WideSearch benchmark (200 tasks), the best-performing system is GPT-5 in multi-agent mode, achieving only 7.3% Success Rate (Avg@4) and 12.0% Pass@4. Most systems score near 0% on table-level success, with single-agent GPT-5 at 6.9% SR and end-to-end Gemini 2.5 Pro at 4.3% SR. Item-level F1 scores are substantially higher (54–62% Avg@4 for top models), revealing that the bottleneck is not finding individual facts but ensuring absolute completeness across thousands of atomic data points. Even human annotators achieve only 20% SR in single-player mode, confirming the extreme difficulty of the task.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 8 foundation models (GPT-5, OpenAI o3, Claude Sonnet 4, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Kimi K2, Qwen3-235B, DeepSeek-R1, Doubao-Seed-1.6) across three architectural modes plus end-to-end commercial systems and human annotators. Multi-agent frameworks consistently outperform single-agent mode for every model: GPT-5 improves from 6.9% to 7.3% SR, Claude Sonnet 4 from 2.3% to 3.6%, and Kimi K2 from 1.1% to 3.0%. End-to-end commercial systems (OpenAI o3 at 3.0%, Claude Sonnet 4 at 2.5%, Gemini 2.5 Pro at 4.3% SR) generally underperform their API-based counterparts in both single- and multi-agent modes, suggesting that integrated product designs have not yet optimized for large-scale structured information gathering. GPT-5 leads in both single-agent (6.9% SR) and multi-agent (7.3% SR) settings, while Claude Sonnet 4 achieves the highest Item-level F1 in multi-agent mode (62.2% Avg@4), indicating stronger per-cell accuracy despite lower overall completeness. All systems fall far short of the 20% human success rate, and the large gap between Item F1 (~55–62%) and SR (~0–7%) across all baselines confirms that the core failure is not individual fact retrieval but the inability to guarantee zero omissions or errors across thousands of data points simultaneously.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a new problem space for LLM agent evaluation by distinguishing 'WideSearch' (large-scale, multi-entity information gathering with structured output) from the two paradigms already covered by existing benchmarks: DeepSearch (locating specific hard-to-find facts, e.g., BrowseComp, GAIA) and DeepResearch (synthesizing complex narratives, e.g., DeepResearch Bench). Rather than building on a specific prior method, it repurposes the LLM-as-a-judge evaluation paradigm and multi-agent divide-and-conquer architectures to formalize and measure a capability—exhaustive, fidelity-preserving collection across many entities—that prior benchmarks leave untested. The test-time scaling analysis draws on the broader compute-at-inference literature but applies it to a setting where the bottleneck is completeness rather than per-item difficulty.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that even the strongest 2025 systems (GPT-5 multi-agent at 7.3% SR) and individual humans (20% SR) fail catastrophically on large-scale information gathering, exposing a critical reliability gap that current agent architectures do not address. The test-time scaling result—that item-level F1 approaches 80% with 128 retries while table-level success stays below 20%—reframes the core challenge from 'can the agent find facts?' to 'can it guarantee zero omissions and zero extras across thousands of atomic data points?', pointing toward multi-agent cross-validation as the necessary architectural direction. Open questions include whether dedicated training (RL, synthetic data) can close the completeness gap, how to scale cross-validation without prohibitive compute, and whether the all-or-nothing success metric is the right target for practical deployment where partial correctness may suffice.
