## Problem

RLVR training on easy prompts causes entropy collapse and degrades pass@k, while training on hard prompts stalls due to sparse reward signals. This creates a fundamental tension: expanding reasoning capacity requires hard problems, but hard problems are inefficient to learn from. The paper asks how to structure RL training so that models can tackle problems beyond their current capacity without the associated sample-efficiency bottleneck.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264899/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

During training, each math problem is augmented by prepending the first p% of its ground-truth solution as a hint, producing a prompt of the form '{Problem}\n## Hint: {Partial Solution}\nPlease reason step by step...'. The model generates a chain-of-thought reasoning trajectory followed by a final answer in \boxed{} format, with a maximum generation length of 24,000 tokens. At evaluation time, all hints are removed and the model must solve problems autonomously, with 32 samples generated per question at temperature 0.7.

## Method

QUESTA is an input-level data augmentation strategy that prepends partial solutions (first p% of the solution block) to hard math problems during RL training, making them more tractable without modifying the reward function or RL algorithm. A two-stage curriculum is used: the model first trains on Partial-50 (50% of solution revealed) for 100 steps, then transitions to Partial-25 for 1,900 steps, gradually reducing scaffold reliance to align the learned policy with the no-hint evaluation distribution. Problems are selected via a two-stage difficulty filter (base model pass rate near zero, then augmented-prompt pass rate in [0,4]/8) to concentrate training on high-variance, high-signal instances. Theoretically, the paper shows that partial-solution hints reduce the required sampling budget from Θ(1/δ_p) to Θ(1/δ_p^{1/2−ε}), explaining why training accelerates: the hint decomposes the problem so the model can discover valid trajectories within a finite budget, yielding denser gradient signals while still driving mastery of the full unscaffolded problem.

## Results

QUESTA-Nemotron-1.5B achieves state-of-the-art results among 1.5B-parameter models on math benchmarks: 72.50% on AIME24 (+10.73% over Nemotron-1.5B), 62.29% on AIME25 (+12.79%), and 41.67% on HMMT25 (+10.11%), with an average Pass@1 of 63.26 across five benchmarks (vs. 53.14 for the base model). The method also preserves pass@k diversity, avoiding the entropy collapse and large-k degradation observed in standard RLVR training. On the DeepScaleR-1.5B backbone, QUESTA yields a +6.50 average gain (36.28 → 42.78), and minor cross-domain improvements are observed on GPQA Diamond, Zebralogic, and coding benchmarks despite training exclusively on math.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the same-scale Nemotron-1.5B base model, QUESTA delivers a consistent +10.12 average improvement (53.14 → 63.26), with the largest gains on AIME25 (+12.79) and AIME24 (+10.73). Compared to DeepSeek-R1-Distill-1.5B (29.44 avg) and Qwen3-1.7B (41.50 avg), QUESTA-Nemotron-1.5B outperforms both by a wide margin, establishing new SOTA among 1.5B models. Notably, QUESTA-Nemotron-1.5B surpasses the 20× larger DeepSeek-R1-Distill-32B (58.08 avg) on every benchmark, exceeding it by +11% on AIME25 (62.29 vs. 51.8). Against the stronger Qwen3-8B (64.99 avg), QUESTA-Nemotron-1.5B remains competitive, trailing by only 1.73 points on average while being roughly 5× smaller. On the DeepScaleR-1.5B backbone, QUESTA improves the average from 36.28 to 42.78 (+6.50), confirming the method's portability across model families.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper operates within the RLVR (Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards) paradigm for LLM reasoning, building directly on GRPO and DAPO as the underlying training algorithms. It departs from prior work that modifies reward functions, introduces process rewards, or adjusts optimization hyperparameters to improve sample efficiency, instead intervening at the input level by augmenting prompts with partial solutions. Conceptually, it is closest to StepHint (Zhang et al., 2025) and R3 (Xi et al., 2024), which also provide intermediate guidance, but QUESTA's key distinction is its fully data-centric, algorithm-agnostic design combined with a formal sample-efficiency analysis. The paper also directly engages with the emerging debate (Yue et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025) on whether RLVR expands intrinsic reasoning capacity, providing controlled evidence that easy prompts actively degrade pass@k through entropy collapse.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

QUESTA offers a simple, plug-and-play strategy that practitioners can adopt without modifying their RL infrastructure, and the finding that easy prompts harm reasoning capacity (rather than merely being unhelpful) refracts how training data should be curated for RLVR. The theoretical result—that partial solutions reduce the required sampling budget from Θ(1/δp) to roughly Θ(√(1/δp))—gives a clean justification for the approach. Open questions include whether the method generalizes beyond 1.5B-scale models and the math domain (cross-domain gains were modest at +0.78 average), whether the two-stage curriculum (50%→25%) is optimal, and how to design appropriate augmentation pipelines for non-mathematical reasoning tasks like coding or agentic planning.
