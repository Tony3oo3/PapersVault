## Problem

Anticipating out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization failures in deployed neural networks remains a central challenge, yet conventional bottom-up mechanistic interpretability methods struggle to provide high-level, predictive signals for real-world model selection. This paper asks whether task-relevant geometric properties of in-distribution (ID) representations can serve as robust biomarkers—system-level measurements that forecast OOD performance without requiring knowledge of the target distribution or detailed internal mechanisms.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263350/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes penultimate-layer feature vectors (e.g., avgpool output in ResNet, features.52 in VGG-19) extracted from ID data, grouped by class into per-class point clouds (object manifolds). For GLUE analysis, each manifold is subsampled to 50 points and Gaussianized to ensure initial linear separability. The output is a set of scalar geometric markers—effective dimension D_eff, effective radius R_eff, and effective utility Ψ_eff—computed entirely from ID data with no access to OOD samples, which together yield a binary model-selection recommendation (e.g., prefer v1 over v2 weights) and a continuous OOD performance prediction.

## Method

The core technical contribution is applying the GLUE (Geometry Linked to Untangling Efficiency) framework—an average-case analog of SVM theory based on manifold capacity from statistical physics—to diagnose OOD generalization failures. GLUE defines a critical dimension N_crit (the minimum projection dimension preserving linear separability with probability ≥ 0.5) and decomposes it via an anchor point distribution (a non-uniform measure favoring points most important for classification) into three task-relevant geometric measures: D_eff, R_eff, and Ψ_eff. The paper follows a three-step diagnostic cycle: (i) designing a broad family of candidate markers (accuracy, sparsity, covariance, participation ratio, neural collapse, numerical rank, and GLUE measures); (ii) conducting medium-scale sweeps across five architectures, two optimizers, and a 4×4 hyperparameter grid on CIFAR-10 to identify which ID markers prognostically predict OOD linear-probe accuracy on CIFAR-100/ImageNet; and (iii) validating the top markers on 20 ImageNet-pretrained PyTorch architectures (v1 vs. v2 weights) across 9 OOD datasets. The key finding is that reduced D_eff and Ψ_eff—indicating overcompression of object manifolds and inefficient use of within-class variability—consistently predict OOD failure more reliably than ID accuracy, participation ratio, neural collapse, or tunnel-effect numerical rank, achieving 73% prediction accuracy in model selection versus 37% for ID accuracy alone.

## Results

In controlled medium-scale experiments (Section 3), task-relevant geometric measures D_eff and Ψ_eff show strong, consistent Pearson correlations (r = 0.73–0.95, p ≤ 0.0001) with OOD linear-probe accuracy across five architectures, two optimizers, and multiple datasets, while ID accuracy and statistical measures (sparsity, covariance) show weak or inconsistent correlations. In the transfer-learning application (Section 4), the GLUE-based prognostic indicators correctly predict which of two pretrained weight versions (v1 vs. v2) will transfer better on 9 OOD datasets with 73.02% accuracy (92/126 predictions), substantially outperforming ID test accuracy as a predictor (37.22%). Across 20 PyTorch architectures, v1 weights with higher D_eff and Ψ_eff outperformed v2 on OOD transfer in 14 of 15 decisive cases, despite v2 having higher ImageNet ID accuracy by construction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The GLUE-based task-relevant geometric markers (D_eff, Ψ_eff) consistently outperform all conventional baselines in predicting OOD generalization. Participation Ratio (Ansuini et al., 2019), a task-agnostic dimensionality measure, shows moderate correlations but is less consistent across architectures than D_eff. Neural Collapse NC1 (Papyan et al., 2020; Harun et al., 2025) and Tunnel Effect numerical rank (Masarczyk et al., 2023; Harun et al., 2024) show weaker or less consistent predictive trends; the authors attribute this to these measures being designed on mathematical intuition rather than task-relevant considerations. Logit-based OOD-detection scores (AUROC from Hendrycks & Dietterich, 2018; Entropy from Guillory et al., 2021; Energy from Liu et al., 2020) are task-relevant but discard too much internal representation information, yielding the weakest predictive power. Most strikingly, ID test accuracy—the standard model-selection criterion—achieves only 37.22% prediction accuracy for OOD transfer, compared to 73.02% for the GLUE geometric markers, demonstrating that higher ID accuracy can mask hidden OOD vulnerabilities.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the GLUE framework (Chou et al., 2025a) and its underlying manifold capacity theory (Chung et al., 2018) as *prognostic* tools for OOD generalization, extending prior work that used these measures to characterize feature learning in a single model (Chou et al., 2025b). It departs from the Tunnel Effect (Masarczyk et al., 2023) and Neural Collapse (Papyan et al., 2020; Harun et al., 2025) lines of work, which analyze how geometric measures vary *across layers within a single model*, by instead comparing *across models* trained under different hyperparameters or initialization regimes—the setting relevant for model selection. The paper also contrasts with OOD-detection methods (Hendrycks & Dietterich, 2018; Liu et al., 2020) that require access to shifted data, by requiring only ID measurements, and positions itself as a 'top-down' diagnostic paradigm complementary to bottom-up mechanistic interpretability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway—that ID representational geometry (effective dimensionality and utility) predicts OOD transfer performance far more reliably than the standard ID-accuracy heuristic (73% vs. 37% prediction accuracy across 20 ImageNet-pretrained architectures)—offers a concrete, low-cost diagnostic for model selection in transfer learning. However, the finding is specific to class-level distribution shifts and does not extend to corruption-based shifts, where ID accuracy remains the strongest predictor. Open questions include strengthening the theoretical link between manifold overcompression and shortcut features, identifying causal interventions (e.g., geometry-aware regularization), and extending the diagnostic framework beyond vision to language, RL, or multi-modal settings.
