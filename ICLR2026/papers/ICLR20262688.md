## Problem

Existing OOD graph learning methods assume a single invariant causal subgraph per class or environment, failing when different instances within the same class rely on fundamentally different causal subgraphs (instance-level heterogeneity). Approaches that attempt to handle this heterogeneity either use data augmentation that risks altering label semantics or impose causal assumptions (e.g., spurious subgraph independent of label) that are frequently violated in real-world data. No prior method directly models causal diversity at the instance level without such restrictive assumptions.

## Input / Output

The method takes a graph x = (V, E, X) with node features and edge structure as input. Each of K experts produces a binary edge mask (via Gumbel-sigmoid straight-through estimator) extracting a candidate causal subgraph, which is then classified by an expert-specific GNN. The final output is a class label obtained by weighting expert logits with sparse gate probabilities produced by an Entmax(α=1.38) transformation. The gate does not consume raw graph features but rather a 10-dimensional diagnostic vector per expert (confidence, margin, entropy, energy, KL divergences, graph size), making routing label-free and input-adaptive.

## Method

DiSCO is a Mixture-of-Experts framework where a shared GNN encoder computes node embeddings, and K lightweight expert-specific MLPs produce edge-level mask logits that are binarized via Gumbel-sigmoid to extract distinct causal subgraphs; each masked subgraph is then classified by an expert-specific GNN and classifier head. A learned gating network (2-layer MLP + Entmax) assigns sparse, input-dependent weights to experts based on their diagnostic outputs. Training combines four losses: gate-weighted cross-entropy (promoting specialization), mask keep-rate regularization, a diversity loss that penalizes inter-expert mask correlation above a threshold τ_corr, and a gate loss with teacher-student alignment (normalizing negative expert losses), sparsity, and batch-level balance terms. The theoretical contribution decomposes OOD risk into oracle risk plus a coverage term (controlled by semantic diversity ensuring at least one expert matches the test environment's causal mechanism) and a selection penalty (controlled by sparsity, where diversity-induced loss gaps force the gate to concentrate on the correct expert). The design is assumption-light: it requires no environment labels, no invariance-based auxiliary losses, and no SCM-level independence assumptions, making it scalable and robust across different causal regimes.

## Results

On the GOOD benchmark, DiSCO achieves the best average OOD accuracy of 75.29% and lowest average rank of 1.50 across six structural-shift datasets (HIV-Scaffold/Size, Twitter-Length, Motif-Basis/Size, SST2-Length). The standout result is on Motif-Basis, where DiSCO reaches 92.80% accuracy—comparable to oracle-level performance and an 8.2% absolute improvement over the next-best method (LECI at 85.74%). On the CFP-Motif benchmark, DiSCO also achieves the best accuracy under all three causal assumptions (Covariate: 90.83%, FIIF: 84.17%, PIIF: 77.19%), outperforming the strongest baseline LECI by 7.6–7.8 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DiSCO outperforms all 14 baselines on 5 of 6 GOOD datasets, with the largest margin on Motif-Basis (92.80% vs. LECI's 85.74%, a 7.06-point gap) and Motif-Size (75.52% vs. GALA's 72.13%). The sole exception is HIV-Scaffold, where LECI (74.28%) edges out DiSCO (71.55%) by 2.73 points, attributed to the extreme class imbalance (>95% majority class). Among domain generalization baselines (ERM, IRM, VREx, GDRO, DANN, Coral), DiSCO leads by 8–14 points on average. Among causal subgraph methods, DiSCO surpasses LECI (73.48 avg) and GALA (72.31 avg) by 1.8 and 3.0 points respectively, while methods relying on strong SCM assumptions (CIGA, DIR) underperform substantially (61.12 and 57.95 avg). On CFP-Motif, DiSCO wins under all three causal assumptions (Covariate, FIIF, PIIF), confirming that its assumption-light design generalizes across different structural causal models where methods like LECI (which enforces G_s ⊥ Y) degrade.

## Relation to Prior Work

DiSCO repurposes the Mixture-of-Experts architecture—previously applied to graphs mainly for scalability (GMoE, MixGNN) or, in the single OOD case, for shift-type-specific augmentation (GraphMETRO)—as a mechanism for causal subgraph identification under instance-level heterogeneity. It departs from the dominant causal subgraph paradigm (LECI, UIL, LIRS, GSAT, CIGA, DIR), which typically assumes a single invariant G_c or G_s ⊥ Y, by instead learning multiple diverse causal subgraph extractors with a sparse gate that selects among them per instance. The theoretical contribution (diversity → loss gaps → sparsity → reduced OOD risk) provides a principled justification absent from prior MoE-for-GNNs work, and the assumption-light design (no environment labels, no auxiliary invariance losses, no SCM commitments) contrasts sharply with the adversarial or structural-alignment objectives used by LECI and UIL.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine gap: instance-level causal heterogeneity, where different samples in the same class rely on fundamentally different causal subgraphs, which single-model causal subgraph methods cannot capture without augmentation (which risks altering label semantics). The clean risk decomposition (oracle + coverage + selection) and the empirical validation that diversity induces loss gaps provide a reusable theoretical template for MoE-based OOD methods beyond graphs. Limitations include evaluation restricted to the GOOD benchmark with a single GIN backbone, and the key theoretical assumptions (3.4, 3.5) are not directly testable in practice. Open questions include whether the framework extends to node/edge-level prediction, scales to much larger graphs, and whether the number of experts should be adaptive per environment rather than fixed.
