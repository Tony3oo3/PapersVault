## Problem

Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) consistently exhibit suboptimal output layers where intermediate decoder layers outperform the final ones, indicating underutilized model capacity. Prior work identified this phenomenon but offered limited causal insight, relying on post-hoc remedial strategies. This paper attributes the issue to Visual Attention Re-sinking—a degradation in late layers where visual attention reverts to low-semantic backgrounds—driven by attention gradient sparsity that arises because MLLM training supervision is entirely textual, leaving visual tokens to receive gradients only through backpropagation of textual losses.

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard MLLM inputs: an RGB image (processed by a vision encoder into N_v visual tokens) concatenated with a text prompt (N_t textual tokens), producing an autoregressive text response. The SADS framework is parameter-free and modifies only which attention heads are active during computation in selected late decoder layers, so the input/output interface remains identical to the base model. The framework is applied both during fine-tuning (to prevent re-sinking from developing) and at inference (to sparsify redundant sink head computations, yielding a 10.3% speedup).

## Method

SADS (Sink Attention Dynamic Sparsification) is a parameter-free, architecture-agnostic framework that dynamically selects which attention heads to retain in late decoder layers. It first classifies heads into vision heads and sink heads by fitting a two-component Gaussian Mixture Model to the maximum visual attention values across heads, using the distribution's valley as a threshold. Within sink heads, it further distinguishes high-entropy sink_G heads (preserving global and contextual knowledge) from low-entropy sink_S heads (sinking attention onto isolated low-semantic tokens) via a second GMM on non-vision token cross-attention entropy. At inference and during fine-tuning, SADS retains all vision heads, the sink_G heads, and a designated shared first head, while discarding sink_S heads. Activation begins at the layer where attention key variance fluctuations and gradient sparsity onset are detected (e.g., layer 20 for Qwen2.5-VL-3B), preserving early-layer modality alignment while targeting the late layers where re-sinking occurs.

## Results

Across 20 benchmarks spanning five task categories, RAR (SADS) consistently outperforms both base models and standard SFT. On the OVDEval open-vocabulary detection benchmark, Qwen2.5-VL-3B improves from 39.5% (base) to 43.8% (RAR), a +4.3 pp gain over SFT's 39.9%. On LISA (reasoning segmentation), LLaVA-1.5-7B jumps from 44.2% to 50.1%, and on CHAIR (hallucination, lower is better), it drops from 44.7 to 41.7. RAR also accelerates inference by 10.3% (1.195s vs. 1.332s latency on Qwen2.5-VL-3B) while improving accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares RAR (SADS) against two baselines: the unmodified base model and standard supervised fine-tuning (SFT) with identical hyperparameters. RAR wins on all 20 benchmarks across all five base models. The most striking gap appears on out-of-distribution and vision-centric benchmarks: on LISA, RAR gains +5.9 pp over SFT (50.1 vs. 44.7 for LLaVA-1.5-7B) and +2.8 pp for Qwen2.5-VL-3B (58.1 vs. 55.3), while SFT barely moves. On OVDEval, RAR achieves +3.9 pp over SFT for Qwen2.5-VL-3B (43.8 vs. 39.9). Notably, SFT sometimes degrades hallucination performance relative to the base model (e.g., CHAIR worsens from 44.7 to 45.2 for LLaVA-1.5-7B, and from 28.2 to 30.1 for Qwen2.5-VL-32B), whereas RAR consistently reduces hallucination (CHAIR drops to 41.7 and 21.2 respectively). RAR also improves inference speed by 10.3% over both baselines by sparsifying redundant sink-head attention computations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the attention-sink literature from LLMs (Xiao et al., 2023) and the recently formalized visual attention sink in MLLMs (Kang et al., 2025, VAR) by identifying a distinct phenomenon—visual attention *re*-sinking—where attention that is well-distributed over semantically salient regions in mid-layers degenerates back to low-semantic backgrounds in late layers. Prior work on suboptimal output layers in MLLMs (Bolya et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024) documented the symptom and applied post-hoc fixes such as layer fusion or attention reallocation, but did not diagnose the underlying training-time cause. SADS departs from these remedial strategies by targeting the root mechanism (text-only supervision → gradient sparsity → head bifurcation) with a parameter-free, architecture-agnostic sparsification rule based on bimodal GMM thresholds over attention statistics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The causal attribution of suboptimal output layers to attention gradient sparsity driven by textual supervision dominance provides a mechanistic explanation that was previously missing, and the parameter-free SADS framework offers a drop-in technique that simultaneously improves accuracy across 20 benchmarks and accelerates inference by 10.3% without any additional parameters or architectural changes. Open questions include whether the GMM-based dynamic thresholds remain robust as model scale grows beyond 32B, whether the fixed 'shared head' designation generalizes across all decoder architectures, and whether the approach can be extended to video or audio-visual settings where the modality-fusion dynamics differ. The authors also note that infilling sparse gradient spaces (rather than sparsifying) may offer a complementary or superior alternative in future work.
