## Problem

GPTQ, the de facto one-shot post-training quantization method for LLMs, was presented only as a sequence of greedy algebraic updates, leaving open why a local rounding-and-propagation rule works well globally and providing no worst-case error guarantee. This paper closes that gap by showing GPTQ is geometrically identical to a classical lattice algorithm, thereby inheriting a tight layer-wise error bound and enabling principled design of improved quantizers.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264165/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a full-precision weight matrix W ∈ R^{c×r}, a calibration activation matrix X ∈ R^{n×c} (used to form the Hessian X^T X that defines the lattice), and quantization parameters including bitwidth, group size, and integer grid Z†. It outputs quantized integer weights Z, per-coordinate scales S, and—under the overflow-tolerant variants—a sparse floating-point outlier vector ξ (stored in CSR format for SSQR) or a Huffman-coded integer representation (for HPTQ). The key structural constraint is that the Hessian factor X^T X must be full-rank (ensured by a small damping term λI), and the quantization order (a permutation matrix P) is shared across all output channels to enable batched processing.

## Method

The central result is a three-part equivalence: (i) L2 linear-layer quantization is exactly the closest vector problem (CVP) on the lattice B = X·diag(s_i) with target y = Xw_i; (ii) GPTQ's error propagation step is the nearest-hyperplane projection of Babai's algorithm, so GPTQ run back-to-front (j = c to 1) is algebraically identical to Babai's nearest plane without LLL basis reduction; (iii) in the no-clipping regime, the layer-wise error is tightly bounded by ¼·(T⁻¹s_i)ᵀD(T⁻¹s_i) where D is the diagonal of the LDL decomposition of the permuted Hessian. Leveraging this bound, the paper introduces two overflow-tolerant no-clipping methods—SSQR (scale-adjusted SpQR with binary-search scale tuning to control outlier density) and HPTQ (Huffman-encoded integers with entropy-guided scale selection)—that outperform original GPTQ at matched bitwidths, along with a min-pivot quantization order that minimizes tr(D) and a CUDA inference kernel achieving ~2× end-to-end speedup over BF16 on RTX A6000.

## Results

On Qwen3-8B evaluated on WikiText-2, HPTQ (Huffman-encoded post-training quantization) achieves the lowest perplexity across all tested effective bitwidths (2.125–4.125 bits), outperforming RTN, original GPTQ, HRTN, and SSQR with 1–5% outliers. The 3.125-bit operating point emerges as Pareto optimal in terms of perplexity-vs-compression trade-off, and this advantage scales consistently across model sizes from 0.6B to 14B parameters. The accompanying CUDA inference kernel for SSQR achieves approximately 2× end-to-end speedup over PyTorch BF16 matrix multiplication on an NVIDIA RTX A6000 during LLM decoding (batch size 1, 128 tokens).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares five quantization methods on Qwen3-8B WikiText-2 perplexity: RTN (round-to-nearest), original GPTQ (with clipping), HRTN (Huffman-encoded RTN), HPTQ (Huffman-encoded GPTQ, the proposed method), and SSQR (scale-adjusted SpQR with 1–5% sparse outliers). HPTQ achieves the lowest perplexity at every effective bitwidth tested, beating both GPTQ and SSQR, which confirms that removing clipping and using an entropy-guided scale (via Huffman coding) yields a tighter quantization than the fixed-grid clipped approaches. HRTN, which replaces GPTQ with RTN while keeping Huffman encoding, sits between RTN and HPTQ, isolating the contribution of GPTQ's error propagation. SSQR with 1–5% outliers is competitive at higher bitwidths but falls behind HPTQ at the 3.125-bit Pareto point, suggesting that the unified integer-grid representation of HPTQ handles the inlier–outlier boundary more gracefully than SpQR's separate sparse storage. The theoretical error bound (Theorem 5) is tight in the no-clipping regime, and HPTQ's design directly exploits this by avoiding clipping entirely, which is the key differentiator from original GPTQ.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the GPTQ/OBQ lineage (Frantar et al., 2022–2023), which inherited the Hessian-based error propagation from Optimal Brain Surgeon but fixed the quantization order for efficiency. The key conceptual departure is importing Babai's 1986 nearest plane algorithm from lattice theory, showing that GPTQ's algebraic update rule is geometrically identical to successive hyperplane projections in a lattice defined by the input Hessian. On the practical side, the no-clipping methods (SSQR, HPTQ) extend SpQR's sparse-outlier representation with a scale-adjustment mechanism and introduce Huffman-coded integer grids, respectively, to avoid the clipping that violates the newly derived error bound.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By placing GPTQ on a firm theoretical footing with a tight layer-wise error bound, this work opens a two-way channel: decades of CVP approximation algorithms (LLL, BKZ basis reduction, etc.) can now be systematically imported to design better quantizers, while the structure of billion-parameter Hessians may inspire new lattice-theoretic questions. The practical no-clipping methods (SSQR, HPTQ) are competitive with or exceed original GPTQ at 3–4 bits, and the CUDA kernel achieves ~2× end-to-end speedup over BF16. Open questions include extending the analysis to clipped grids, exploring scale-aware basis reductions, and generalizing the lattice view to activation and KV-cache quantization.
