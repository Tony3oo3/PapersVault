## Problem

Existing music generation models condition on coarse-to-fine representations but cannot selectively preserve patterns at arbitrary timescales from a reference example. Musical structure spans multiple timescales (chord progressions at ~0.1 Hz, rhythmic patterns at several Hz, trills at higher rates), yet no prior method exposes a direct, continuous 'timescale' axis for conditioning. This paper asks whether a frequency-domain decomposition of learned latent representations can provide such an axis.

## Input / Output

The method takes a 5.9-second reference audio clip (resampled to 22.05 kHz) and a user-specified binary mask over the latent frequency spectrum (F bins, where F = ⌊LT'/2⌋+1 with zero-padding factor L=2), producing a generated mel-spectrogram inverted to audio via BigVGAN. For blending, two reference clips each carry their own non-overlapping mask, and the output is a single audio clip preserving selected timescales from each. The latent frequency axis is measured in Hz, where 1 Hz corresponds to one oscillation cycle per second in the latent time series (not the audio waveform).

## Method

LATENTFT combines a diffusion autoencoder with a latent-space DFT and frequency masking during training. An encoder (MLP, 1D U-Net, or DAC+U-Net) maps audio to a time series of latent vectors; the DFT of this sequence yields the latent spectrum. During training, the spectrum is masked using correlated bin scores (generated via an RBF kernel on a log-frequency axis with a random Gaussian threshold), producing contiguous masked regions that mirror inference-time user masks. The inverse DFT of the masked spectrum gives a frequency-masked latent sequence, which conditions a diffusion decoder to reconstruct the input from a noisy version. This end-to-end training forces the decoder to learn reconstruction from partial latent spectra, making the latents coherently manipulable in the frequency domain at inference. At inference, user-specified masks enable conditional generation (denoising from pure noise), blending (interpolating ODE derivatives from two masked conditions at each step), and isolation (self-blending with boosted weights on a target band).

## Results

On the MTG-Jamendo test set, LATENTFT-MLP achieves a FAD of 0.337 for conditional generation and 1.387 for blending, substantially outperforming the best baselines (Guidance: 1.061 and 1.466 FAD respectively). On adherence, LATENTFT-MLP scores 0.815 loudness correlation, 0.963 beat-spectral cosine similarity, 0.376 Mel-Cepstral Distortion, and 0.079 Tonnetz distance for conditional generation, leading all baselines on every metric. The LATENTFT-UNet variant achieves the best rhythm adherence (0.966) and the lowest blending FAD (1.357). A listening study with 29 musicians confirms LATENTFT is preferred over all baselines on both audio quality and blending coherence with statistical significance (p < 10⁻⁴ for most pairs).

## Baselines & Comparisons

LATENTFT outperforms all six baselines on both adherence and audio quality (FAD) across conditional generation and blending. The closest competitor is ILVR, which achieves the best timbre adherence among baselines (0.781 vs. LATENTFT-MLP's 0.376) but trails significantly on FAD (1.537 vs. 0.337 for conditional generation; 2.696 vs. 1.357 for blending). The Guidance baseline is the second-closest on FAD (1.061 vs. 0.337) but lags on all four adherence metrics. Post-hoc frequency masking of pretrained codecs (DAC: FAD 7.016; RAVE: FAD 4.695) and direct spectrogram filtering (FAD 7.608) all produce substantially lower-quality audio, confirming that frequency-domain manipulation requires training-time frequency masking to yield coherent output. The Masked Token Model (VampNet) and Cross Synthesis baselines lack frequency-based controls entirely, so only FAD is reported; LATENTFT achieves FAD 0.337 vs. 4.317 (VampNet) and 1.357 vs. 2.447 (Cross Synthesis) on the respective tasks. The listening study further confirms these quantitative gaps, with LATENTFT winning head-to-head comparisons against ILVR (p = 3.83×10⁻⁴ on quality, p = 8.84×10⁻⁷ on blending) and VampNet (p = 7.02×10⁻¹⁰ on both metrics).

## Relation to Prior Work

LATENTFT builds directly on the diffusion autoencoder framework (Preechakul et al., 2022), extending it by applying a Fourier transform to the latent time series and training with random frequency-domain masking. This departs from prior spectral approaches in two key ways: unlike PRISM (Tamkin et al., 2020) or Khan et al. (2017), which apply DFT post-hoc to pretrained representations for analysis, LATENTFT trains end-to-end with masking so the decoder learns to synthesize from partial spectra; and unlike image editing methods (SDEdit, ILVR) that operate on noisy pixel/spectrogram space, it conditions on a learned latent frequency axis. The frequency-masking strategy is conceptually related to masked autoencoders (He et al., 2022) and MFAE (Zheng et al., 2024b), but applied to latent-space frequencies rather than input-space time-frequency bins.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LATENTFT introduces a continuous, interpretable 'timescale' axis for conditioning music generation that prior controls (pitch curves, loudness, stems) do not expose, and the equalizer analogy makes it accessible to musicians. The interpretability results—showing genre near 0 Hz, chord changes below 1 Hz, and tempo/pitch at higher latent frequencies—provide a useful map of where musical structure resides in the latent spectrum. Open questions include whether the approach scales to real-time interactive use, whether the latent spectrum can be further disentangled along semantic axes, and whether the frequency-masking training strategy generalizes to other modalities or pretrained models. A key limitation is that the method cannot be applied post-hoc to existing pretrained audio codecs (DAC, RAVE), as the ablations confirm.
