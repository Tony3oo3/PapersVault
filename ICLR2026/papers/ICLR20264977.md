## Problem

Current AI models trained on large datasets still fail to achieve human-like few-shot physical reasoning, particularly in out-of-distribution settings, because they overfit observed trajectories in discrete latent spaces rather than abstracting core physical principles. The key missing ingredient is a representation that can efficiently learn and generalize physical dynamics—gravity, collision, friction, support—from minimal data while remaining physically grounded.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264977/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For state-based tasks (I-PHYRE, N-body), input consists of per-object state vectors including position, velocity, geometry (angle, length, radius), and optionally mass; for the vision-based PHYRE benchmark, input is 256×256 RGB frames with segmentation masks processed through sparse convolutions. The primary output is continuous object trajectories decoded by integrating learned force fields through an ODE solver, but the framework also produces explicit force field vectors at arbitrary query points, optimal action sequences for forward planning, and initial conditions recovered via backward (time-reversed) ODE integration for inverse planning.

## Method

NFF replaces discrete latent state transitions with a continuous, explicit force field representation: a DeepONet-based neural operator computes a force vector F(z^q(t)) for each query object by aggregating element-wise products of neighbor features over a dynamic interaction graph, mapping hidden features to low-dimensional forces. These forces are then integrated through a second-order ODE solver (Euler, RK4, or adaptive) to produce physically consistent trajectories governed by Newton's second law, ensuring that collisions, gravity, and friction emerge from the learned field rather than from a learned decoder. Training uses autoregressive prediction with MSE loss over segmented trajectory chunks, enabling few-shot learning from as few as 100 trajectories. The ODE grounding provides two critical advantages: it enforces physical consistency in predictions and makes the system invertible, enabling efficient backward planning by simply reversing the time direction of integration. Ablations confirm that the combination of high-precision integration, ODE-based decoding, and the DeepONet neural operator architecture is necessary for strong cross-scenario generalization.

## Results

On I-PHYRE prediction, NFF achieves RMSE of 0.048 (within-scenario) and 0.131 (cross-scenario), outperforming IN (0.124/0.194), SlotFormer (0.067/0.206), and SEGNO (0.203/0.314). On N-body cross-scenario prediction over 150 steps, NFF achieves RMSE of 1.226 versus 6.942 for IN, 2.533 for SlotFormer, and 2.759 for SEGNO. On PHYRE cross-scenario planning, NFF achieves an AUCCESS of 49.22 using only 12,000 training trajectories—267× less data than the prior SOTA RPIN (36.58 AUCCESS with 3.2M trajectories). For I-PHYRE interactive planning, NFF with refinement reaches 77% cumulative success by round 5, approaching human performance (86%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

NFF dominates IN across all prediction and planning benchmarks: on I-PHYRE cross-scenario RMSE, NFF achieves 0.131 vs. IN's 0.194, and on N-body cross-scenario (150 steps) the gap widens dramatically to 1.226 vs. 6.942. Against SlotFormer, NFF wins on I-PHYRE (0.048 vs. 0.067 within; 0.131 vs. 0.206 cross) and on N-body long-horizon prediction (0.525 vs. 1.092 within[0,3T]; 1.226 vs. 2.533 cross), as well as on PHYRE planning (AUCCESS 49.22 vs. 21.04). SEGNO is the closest competitor, narrowly beating NFF on N-body within-scenario short-horizon RMSE (0.079 vs. 0.097), but NFF surpasses it on all other settings including I-PHYRE (0.048 vs. 0.203 within) and N-body cross-scenario (1.226 vs. 2.759). On PHYRE cross-scenario planning, NFF (49.22 AUCCESS, 0.012M samples) outperforms RPIN (36.58, 3.2M samples) and DQN (30.96, 200M samples) by a wide margin with orders-of-magnitude less data. In the supplementary N-body comparisons, NFF also beats GCN (1.226 vs. 4.343 cross), EGNN (1.226 vs. 8.394 cross), and GraphODE (1.226 vs. 3.920 cross) on RMSE. For I-PHYRE interactive planning, NFF with refinement reaches 77% cumulative success by round 5, surpassing IN (63%), SlotFormer (60%), and random sampling (56%), while approaching human performance (86%).

## Relation to Prior Work

NFF extends the Neural ODE (NODE) framework by replacing learned discrete state transitions (as in Interaction Networks, SlotFormer, SEGNO) with explicit continuous force fields predicted by a neural operator (DeepONet) and integrated via an ODE solver. It departs from physics-informed approaches (Hamiltonian/Lagrangian NNs) that require known governing equations, instead learning force patterns end-to-end from minimal trajectories. The graph-based interaction modeling lineage (IN → EGNN → SEGNO) provides the relational inductive bias, but NFF's key conceptual shift is moving from 'predict next state' to 'predict forces, then integrate,' yielding interpretable, physics-grounded representations that support both forward simulation and backward inversion.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a low-dimensional, physics-grounded force field representation can achieve strong cross-scenario generalization from as few as 100–200 trajectories, addressing a core gap between human few-shot physical reasoning and current AI systems that overfit to observed dynamics. The backward-integration planning capability (~10× faster than gradient-based optimization) and the 267× data efficiency over prior SOTA on PHYRE are practically significant for sample-constrained settings. However, the framework is limited to deterministic rigid-body systems in synthetic environments; extending to stochastic dynamics, soft bodies, fluids, and real-world visual inputs remains open, as does handling varying friction and elasticity within a single model.
