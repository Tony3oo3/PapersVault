## Problem

AI fairness evaluation suffers from a 'Tower of Babel' dilemma: dozens of metrics with conflicting philosophical assumptions (e.g., fairness through unawareness vs. awareness) make unified scoring intractable, and impossibility theorems prove no single metric can satisfy all criteria simultaneously. This fragmentation is especially acute for Unified Multimodal Large Language Models (UMLLMs), where biases propagate systemically across a shared representation space used for both understanding and generation, yet no prior benchmark synchronously evaluates fairness across both tasks.

## Input / Output

For generation, the benchmark feeds neutral or counter-stereotypical text prompts across 52 occupations to UMLLMs and annotates the resulting images with the ARES classifier (88% accuracy on age/gender/skin tone). For understanding, it queries models with image-question pairs from IRIS-Ideal-52 (~27K images) and IRIS-Steer-60 (~6K counterfactual pairs). The 60 granular metrics are normalized into a high-dimensional deviation space where the origin is the 'Fairness Singularity'; per-dimension L2-norms are mapped to scores via exponential decay (S·exp(−K·M)), yielding six sector scores, a global IRIS score, and a qualitative three-letter IRIS-MBTI personality code (e.g., UAF = 'The Adaptive Idealist') based on a versioned threshold of 60.

## Method

IRIS organizes evaluation along three dimensions—Ideal Fairness (default 'should-be' behavior), Real-world Fidelity (alignment with 'as-is' demographics from BLS/Eurostat data), and Bias Inertia & Steerability (controllability toward a 'can-be' state)—each instantiated with 11 core metrics (RD, AD, SPD, JSD, SDS, ΔGSR, QPS, SIL, AC_diff, DHR) that are expanded to 60 granular sub-metrics through intersectional combinations of gender, age, and skin tone at single, dual, and triple levels. The ARES classifier, an adaptive routing expert system, uses L1 lightweight experts (CLIP, DINOv2, ConvNeXt fine-tuned on IRIS-Classifier-25) for routine images and escalates ambiguous cases to L2 heavyweight experts (InternVL-1B VLM plus a Fusion MLP regressor for skin tone), with routing governed by an EfficientNet-B0 fast-path router and an XGBoost decision/escalation router. The scoring workflow normalizes each metric into a unified deviation space (linear scaling for bounded metrics, log compression for unbounded penalties), aggregates per-dimension vectors via L2-norm, and maps magnitudes to interpretable scores through calibrated exponential decay, deliberately avoiding both naive averaging (which masks critical failures) and worst-case aggregation (which is overly sensitive). The framework is designed for extensibility: new demographic attributes can be added by training modular ARES experts, and new fairness dimensions can be appended as additional axes in the fairness space without invalidating existing scores.

## Results

Among the 7 evaluated UMLLMs, Bagel achieves the highest overall IRIS score (95.94), while BLIP3-o scores the lowest (40.13). A key systemic finding is the 'generation gap': even the best UMLLM (Bagel, IFS-Gen 82.58) is outperformed by specialist text-to-image models (SD 3.5 Large IFS-Gen 273.17, LlamaGen IFS-Gen 237.88, FLUX.1-dev IFS-Gen 94.05). No single model excels across all six dimension×task sectors, empirically confirming fairness impossibility theorems. The ARES classifier achieves 88.00% overall accuracy on challenging generated images, and the benchmark's model rankings are robust to hyperparameter perturbations (Spearman's ρ > 0.96).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares 7 UMLLMs against 5 specialist control models (2 MLLMs for understanding, 3 image generators for generation). On understanding tasks, UMLLMs are broadly competitive with specialist MLLMs: Bagel (IFS 71.46, RFS 69.81) and Harmon (IFS 74.44) outperform Qwen2.5-VL (65.35, 73.13) on IFS, while Qwen2.5-VL leads on RFS. On generation tasks, however, UMLLMs suffer a systematic 'generation gap': even the best UMLLM (Bagel, IFS-Gen 82.58) is far below specialist generators (SD 3.5 Large 273.17, LlamaGen 237.88, FLUX.1-dev 94.05), and BLIP3-o collapses to 35.30. No UMLLM dominates all six dimension×task sectors—Bagel leads overall but lags on Und-BIS (50.75), while Janus-Pro excels on Und-BIS (105.22) but is worst on Und-IFS (32.84). The IRIS-MBTI diagnostic reveals 'personality splits' (e.g., VILA-U is HAF in understanding but UDF in generation), a phenomenon invisible to single-task evaluations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of algorithmic fairness evaluation and unified multimodal model assessment. While fairness metrics (statistical parity, JSD, counterfactual fairness) and single-task fairness benchmarks are well established, and unified multimodal benchmarks (UniEval, MMME-Unify) focus on capability rather than values, no prior work synchronously evaluates fairness across both understanding and generation within a shared UMLLM architecture. The paper repurposes existing fairness concepts into a high-dimensional 'fairness space' and introduces the ARES adaptive-routing classifier as a methodological tool, but its core contribution is the synchronous dual-task framing and the IRIS-MBTI qualitative diagnostic, which have no direct predecessor.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark is practically important as the first comprehensive fairness evaluation suite for UMLLMs, and its findings—the systemic 'generation gap' (UMLLMs underperform specialist text-to-image models on fairness) and cross-task 'personality splits'—directly inform model selection for fairness-sensitive deployments. Key limitations include coarse demographic discretization (binary gender, three age/skin-tone buckets), reliance on an automated annotator that injects measurement noise, and restriction to image-centric occupational prompts. Open questions include whether the scoring hyperparameters generalize across model families, whether the framework extends to video/audio modalities, and whether the 'counter-stereotype reward' phenomenon persists under human-validated quality metrics.
