## Problem

The cross-entropy scaling law, which predicts a clean power-law decrease in loss with model size, breaks down at very large scales where loss decreases more slowly than expected. This failure undermines both practical extrapolation for LLM development and the theoretical understanding of why scaling laws emerge. The paper asks: what component within cross-entropy actually scales, and why does the aggregate law appear to hold at small scales but fail at large ones?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262694/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretrained language model (any architecture) and an evaluation text corpus, computing for each token the softmax score of the ground-truth token and its Rank-based Error (RBE), defined as the count of tokens scoring higher than the ground truth. Because RBE is purely rank-based, it is invariant to temperature scaling, top-k, and nucleus sampling, making it a more robust performance indicator than raw probability. The outputs are three decomposed scalar values (Error-Entropy, Self-Alignment, Confidence), the empirical RBE distribution p_e, and fitted power-law scaling exponents across model sizes.

## Method

The paper introduces Rank-based Error (RBE) and uses it to mathematically decompose cross-entropy into three exact components: Error-Entropy (Shannon entropy of the RBE distribution p_e), Self-Alignment (KL divergence between p_e and the normalized score distribution q_e), and Confidence (log of the score norm C). The key insight is that only Error-Entropy follows a robust power-law scaling with model size (R² > 0.95 across 32 models spanning 14M–70B parameters on three datasets), while Self-Alignment and Confidence remain largely invariant. This explains the CE scaling breakdown: Error-Entropy dominates ~90% of CE in small models (making the aggregate look like a clean power law) but its share declines as models grow, allowing non-scaling terms to distort the trend. The authors further propose a differentiable compensated loss L_λ = CE + λ·CONF whose gradient factor (1 − λ·q_e/p_e) redirects optimization toward improving the error distribution rather than inflating confidence.

## Results

Across 32 models spanning five orders of magnitude (14M–70B parameters) on three datasets (Wikipedia, C4, Pile-GitHub), Error-Entropy (EE) achieves the highest power-law R² in nearly all settings, often exceeding cross-entropy itself: in the mixed-model ('All') setting, EE reaches R² = 0.8626 (Wikipedia), 0.9012 (C4), and 0.7229 (GitHub), versus CE's 0.8421, 0.8699, and 0.6743 respectively. Self-Alignment and Confidence show poor or inconsistent power-law fits (R² frequently below 0.35 in the mixed-model setting). The scaling-exponent difference |Δ| confirms EE is closest to CE (0.0058–0.0406) while Self-Alignment deviates most (0.1672–0.3307), establishing EE as the true scaling driver within cross-entropy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external methods; instead, it decomposes cross-entropy into three components (Error-Entropy, Self-Alignment, Confidence) and compares their scaling behavior against cross-entropy itself. Error-Entropy consistently 'wins' the comparison: it achieves higher R² than cross-entropy in 11 out of 12 dataset–family combinations (e.g., 0.9012 vs. 0.8699 on C4, 0.7229 vs. 0.6743 on GitHub in the mixed-model setting), indicating a tighter power-law fit. Self-Alignment and Confidence 'lose' decisively: their R² values are often below 0.35 in the mixed-model setting and their |Δ| (deviation from CE's scaling exponent) is 3–8× larger than EE's (e.g., SA |Δ| = 0.3307 vs. EE |Δ| = 0.0406 on GitHub). The paper further shows that EE dominates cross-entropy in small models (~90% of total loss) but its share declines as models grow, explaining why the cross-entropy scaling law appears accurate at small scales but breaks down at very large ones.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the standard scaling-law literature (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022) by questioning whether cross-entropy itself is the quantity that scales, rather than attempting to explain why it does. It introduces a novel Rank-based Error (RBE) metric and uses it to perform an exact mathematical decomposition of cross-entropy into Error-Entropy, Self-Alignment, and Confidence. The Error-Entropy component draws a conceptual bridge to information-theoretic learning (Erdogmus & Principe, 2002; Liu et al., 2006), where error-distribution minimization has been studied for nonparametric regression, repurposing that framework for the discrete ranking setting of language modeling. Prior theoretical scaling-law work (Bahri et al., 2024; Lyu et al., 2025) has addressed error-based metrics like MSE but could not generalize to cross-entropy; this paper sidesteps that gap by identifying the sub-component of CE that does scale.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that only Error-Entropy follows a robust power law while Self-Alignment and Confidence remain invariant provides a clean, mechanistic explanation for the widely observed but poorly understood breakdown of cross-entropy scaling at very large model sizes. Practically, the Error-Entropy scaling law offers a more reliable extrapolation tool for LLM development, and the proposed compensated loss (L_λ) opens a path toward training objectives that prioritize ranking accuracy over confidence inflation. Open questions include whether the EE scaling law holds under different training regimes (e.g., data-limited, multi-task), whether the compensated loss yields measurable downstream improvements at scale, and whether the ITL connection can yield a closed-form theoretical prediction for the EE exponent.
