## Problem

Text-to-vision generation models produce visually realistic but semantically misaligned outputs, particularly on compositional prompts with multiple objects, attributes, and relations. Existing training datasets (LAION, CC3M) are noisy, weakly compositional, and biased toward single-object coarse-grained descriptions, while manual curation of dense compositional annotations is infeasible at scale and automatic annotation via LLMs introduces hallucination. The paper asks whether a systematic, programmatic enumeration of scene graphs can produce the scalable, compositionally rich training data needed to close this gap.

## Input / Output

The engine accepts user-specified structural constraints (total element count, per-node degree bounds, number of connected components), an optional seed scene graph to embed as a subgraph, and metadata scope filters (e.g., commonsense-only, specific object pairs). It outputs three artifacts per scene graph: a natural-language caption produced by deterministic topological traversal with grammar-based disambiguation (e.g., 'the first/second' for identical objects), an exhaustive set of template-based QA pairs covering every node attribute and edge relation, and the scene graph itself in structured form. Captions are generated at 1024×1024 resolution for image tasks, 16-frame sequences for video, and 120-viewpoint renders for 3D, with complexity ranging from 3–12 elements for images and 1–3 for 3D.

## Method

GENERATE ANY SCENE is a five-step programmatic pipeline: (1) pre-compute and cache a library of directed scene-graph topologies satisfying user constraints, with optional commonsense plausibility filtering; (2) populate each structure by sampling objects (28,787 from WordNet), attributes (1,494 across 9 categories), and relations (10,492 from Synthetic Visual Genome) from a hierarchical taxonomy; (3) sample scene attributes (art style, camera settings, weather, temporal span); (4) convert the graph to a caption via deterministic topological-order traversal with programmatic grammar rules that track object references to prevent duplication and misreference; and (5) enumerate exhaustive QA pairs from the graph for evaluation and reward modeling. The key insight is that scene graphs, grounded in cognitive science, provide a finite combinatorial space that can be systematically enumerated to yield near-infinite compositional diversity without LLM hallucination. The paper demonstrates four downstream applications: iterative self-improving LoRA fine-tuning (selecting highest-VQA-score images from 8 candidates per caption), targeted distillation of DALL-E 3's compositionality and hard-concept understanding into SDv1.5 using fewer than 800 synthetic captions, a scene-graph-based reward model combined with GRPO (using Qwen2.5-VL-3B as a lightweight VQA judge) that outperforms CLIP-based RL by +0.5 on DPG-Bench, and enriched training for ViT-T generated-content detectors.

## Results

For self-improvement, fine-tuning SDv1.5 with GENERATE ANY SCENE synthetic captions (GAS-FT) achieves VQAScore 0.6109 and CLIPScore 0.2938 on unseen compositions, outperforming both the original SDv1.5 (0.5823/0.2876) and CC3M fine-tuning (0.6044/0.2927). For targeted distillation, fewer than 800 synthetic captions yield a 10% TIFA Score increase for compositional generation. For RL-based reward modeling, GRPO with the scene-graph reward achieves DPG-Bench Overall 80.50, GenEval Overall 0.61, and GenAI-Bench All 0.68, surpassing the CLIP-RL baseline by +0.5 on DPG-Bench Overall and +0.02 on GenEval Overall.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SimpleAR-0.5B-SFT (Wang et al., 2025), the scene-graph-reward GRPO method wins on every reported metric: DPG-Bench Overall improves from 78.48 to 80.50 (+2.02), GenEval Overall from 0.53 to 0.61 (+0.08), and GenAI-Bench All from 0.66 to 0.68 (+0.02). Against the CLIP-RL baseline (SimpleAR-0.5B-RL trained with CLIP rewards on 11K real-world captions), the scene-graph reward method achieves +0.84 on DPG-Bench Overall (80.50 vs. 79.66) and +0.02 on GenEval Overall (0.61 vs. 0.59), while also improving position (P) from 0.26 to 0.31. Against SDv1.5 (Rombach et al., 2022), the GAS-fine-tuned model outperforms the original on unseen-composition VQAScore (0.6109 vs. 0.5823) and CLIPScore (0.2938 vs. 0.2876), and also surpasses CC3M fine-tuning on the same metrics, demonstrating that synthetic compositional data can exceed real-world data for compositional generalization. Against DALL-E 3 (Betker et al., 2023), the paper does not aim to match its absolute performance (TIFA 0.4249) but distills specific capabilities: after fine-tuning with only 778 multi-object captions, SDv1.5 gains a 10% TIFA increase and narrows the compositional gap, though it remains well below DALL-E 3's scores.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes scene graphs—long used for visual understanding (Visual Genome, GQA) and evaluation (DSG, DPG)—as a *generative* engine for training data synthesis, rather than merely an evaluation scaffold. It departs from LLM-based synthetic caption pipelines (LLM-Grounded Diffusion, DreamSync) by replacing stochastic language model sampling with deterministic, programmatic scene graph enumeration, trading linguistic fluency for compositional controllability and zero hallucination. The self-improvement loop extends DreamSync's iterative fine-tuning with VQA-guided selection over scene graph captions, while the RLHF contribution replaces CLIP-based rewards with scene graph-derived QA accuracy, building on the GRPO framework from recent autoregressive generation work (SimpleAR).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The most practically significant finding is that fine-tuning on scene graph–synthetic captions outperforms fine-tuning on the same volume of high-quality CC3M real data, suggesting that compositional structure in training text matters more than photorealistic grounding for generalization. The distillation result—transferring DALL-E 3's multi-object capability into SDv1.5 with fewer than 800 synthetic caption–image pairs—offers a low-cost pathway for open-source models to close specific gaps. Open questions include whether the fixed taxonomy (28K objects, 10K relations) can capture truly open-world or culturally novel compositions, whether the programmatic captions' limited linguistic diversity becomes a bottleneck at scale, and whether the scene graph reward model generalizes beyond the QA template space it was designed around.
