## Problem

Existing offline MARL methods apply a uniform conservatism level to all agents, which over-constrains high-impact agents (e.g., strikers in a football team) and under-constrains low-impact ones (e.g., defenders), degrading team collaboration. The paper addresses how to dynamically allocate per-agent conservative degrees based on each agent's actual influence on system performance, rather than treating all agents identically.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261310/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a static offline dataset of state-action-reward tuples (collected by HAPPO for MuJoCo or MAPPO for SMAC) along with per-agent local observations, and initializes the behavior policy π_b via behavior cloning on the same dataset. It outputs per-agent stochastic policies and per-agent conservative degrees d_i that sum to a fixed global budget d_tot, with the Q-function decomposed into separate return-based (Q^r) and constraint-based (Q^c) networks for each agent. The influence term m_i is computed via backpropagation through the return-based value function, using reparameterization for continuous actions (MuJoCo) and finite-difference approximation for discrete actions (SMAC).

## Method

OMCDA decomposes the regularized Q-function into a return component Q^r and a deviation component Q^c (inspired by BOPAH), isolating return computation from conservatism enforcement so that each agent's contribution to cumulative returns can be measured without constraint interference. An influence term m_i is computed as the partial derivative of the global return-based value function V_tot^r with respect to the KL divergence between agent i's policy and its behavior policy, decomposed via the chain rule into a system-impact term and a deviation-sensitivity term. These influence terms are softmax-normalized to allocate the fixed global conservative budget d_tot proportionally: d_i = m_i · d_tot. The per-agent conservatism level α_i is then updated by minimizing α_i·d_i − α_i·D_KL(π_i‖π_b^i), tightening constraints when deviation exceeds the allocated budget and relaxing them otherwise. The entire framework is built on QMIX-style monotonic value decomposition for both the return and constraint sub-networks, with theoretical guarantees (Propositions 3.1–3.4, Theorem 3.3) ensuring that the locally derived optimal policies remain consistent with the global optimal policy even under heterogeneous α_i assignments.

## Results

On Multi-Agent MuJoCo, OMCDA achieves the highest average return on 10 of 12 task-dataset combinations, with the largest gains on HalfCheetah-expert (4963.92 vs. 4082.9 for ComaDICE, a 21.6% improvement) and Hopper-medium-replay (1733.27 vs. 906.3, a 91.3% improvement). On SMAC, OMCDA leads on 7 of 9 task-dataset combinations, most notably on 5m_vs_6m-good (10.47 vs. 8.7 for ComaDICE, +20.3%) and 6h_vs_8z-good (14.14 vs. 13.1, +8.0%). OMCDA trails ComaDICE on three settings: Hopper-expert (1214.25 vs. 2827.7), corridor-good (14.72 vs. 18.0), and 6h_vs_8z-poor (10.83 vs. 11.4).

## Baselines & Comparisons

OMCDA is compared against seven offline MARL baselines: BCQ-MA, CQL-MA, ICQ, OMAR, CFCQL, OMIGA, and ComaDICE. BCQ-MA and CQL-MA (multi-agent extensions of single-agent offline RL) consistently underperform, often yielding the lowest or near-lowest returns across all tasks. OMAR is particularly unstable, producing negative returns on several MuJoCo tasks (e.g., −2992.80 on Ant m-expert). ICQ and CFCQL achieve moderate performance but are outperformed by OMCDA on most settings. OMIGA is competitive on Ant and Hopper but falls well behind on HalfCheetah. ComaDICE is the strongest baseline overall, yet OMCDA surpasses it on 10 of 12 MuJoCo tasks (e.g., +21.6% on HalfCheetah-expert, +91.3% on Hopper m-replay) and 7 of 9 SMAC tasks (e.g., +20.3% on 5m_vs_6m-good). OMCDA's only notable losses are on Hopper-expert (where ComaDICE reaches 2827.7 vs. OMCDA's 1214.25) and corridor-good in SMAC (18.0 vs. 14.72), suggesting the dynamic conservatism allocation is most beneficial when agents have heterogeneous impact on returns.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the QMIX monotonic value decomposition framework (Rashid et al., 2020) and the soft Q-learning optimal policy derivation (Haarnoja et al., 2018) into the offline MARL setting with a key architectural departure: decomposing the Q-function into separate return and KL-deviation components (inspired by BOPAH). Unlike prior offline MARL methods (ICQ, OMAR, CFCQL, OMIGA, ComaDICE) that apply a uniform or behavior-deviation-based conservatism level to all agents, OMCDA introduces per-agent conservative degree allocation driven by each agent's measured influence on collective return. The Lagrangian relaxation and expectile-loss (IQL-style) updates follow standard offline RL practice, but the dynamic allocation mechanism and the proof of local-global policy consistency under heterogeneous α_i are novel contributions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically important gap: in heterogeneous multi-agent systems (e.g., football teams, robotic swarms), uniform conservatism over-constrains high-impact agents and under-constrains low-impact ones, degrading team performance. The Q-function decomposition provides a clean mechanism for isolating return from constraint, enabling principled influence-based allocation. However, the empirical gains are mixed—OMCDA does not consistently outperform ComaDICE across all SMAC maps and MuJoCo tasks, and the method's sensitivity to the manually set total conservative degree d_tot and the computational cost of influence computation (requiring gradient backpropagation through value networks) limit immediate practical adoption. Open questions include whether adaptive d_tot selection can remove the hyperparameter sensitivity, whether the approach scales to large agent counts, and whether the cooperative-only assumption generalizes to mixed cooperative-competitive settings.
