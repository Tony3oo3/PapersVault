## Problem

While the two-stage recipe of next-token prediction (NTP) followed by reinforcement learning (RL) has empirically driven major advances in LLM reasoning (e.g., OpenAI o1, DeepSeek R1), no theoretical account explains why RL after NTP succeeds where NTP alone fails, what optimization pressures cause the characteristic increase in response length, or how many RL steps are needed. This paper provides the first theoretical separation between NTP and NTP+RL in the autoregressive setting, identifying the precise mechanism by which RL up-samples rare long demonstrations to enable generalization.

## Input / Output

In the primary synthetic setting, the input is a d-bit binary sequence x ∈ {±1}^d, and the model must output the parity ∏x_i. Training data is drawn from a mixture distribution D(p_cot) where with probability p_cot the target sequence includes all intermediate products (a full chain-of-thought of length d+1), and with probability 1−p_cot it contains only the final parity bit plus an EOS token (length 2). In the LLM experiments, input is a math word problem (GSM8K or MATH) and output is a structured response with  and <answer></answer> tags, where the chain-of-thought is dropped with probability 1−p_cot to create the short/long mixture.

## Method

The paper analyzes a linear autoregressive model (a series of d+1 linear predictors with second-degree monomial embeddings) trained first with SGD on the NTP objective, then with STaR (a REINFORCE-type algorithm) using a chain-of-thought correctness reward. Theorem 1 proves that after pre-training, the model is length-calibrated (generates long correct responses with probability ≈ p_cot) but fails under greedy decoding when p_cot < 1/3, because the greedy decision at the second token favors EOS over continuation. Theorem 2 proves that each STaR round re-weights the effective data mixture so the long-sequence proportion evolves as p_{n+1} = 2p_n/(1+p_n), converging to 1 exponentially fast; only O(log((1−p_cot)/p_cot)) RL rounds suffice for perfect generalization in O(poly(d)) total SGD steps, provided p_cot is not exponentially small in d. The key mechanism is that RL amplifies long responses because the model is far more likely to be correct on them, creating a positive feedback loop that increases test-time compute (response length) and thereby enables learning of the hard parity function. Empirical validation on GPT-2, Mistral, and Llama 3.2 3B models across parity, 5-digit multiplication, and GSM8K/MATH benchmarks confirms the predicted dynamics, including the critical p_cot ≈ 1/3 threshold and the immediate accuracy jump upon switching to RL.

## Results

On the parity task (d=50, p_cot=0.25), next-token prediction alone plateaus at ~50% test accuracy (random chance) under greedy decoding even after ~10^7 training samples, while adding GRPO post-training from a pre-trained checkpoint rapidly achieves 100% accuracy with a concurrent increase in response length from 1 to 50 tokens. The critical threshold for pre-training generalization is p_cot = 1/3, matching the theoretical prediction from Theorem 1. On GSM8K (Llama 3.2 3B, p_cot=0.05), SFT alone saturates below the full-CoT baseline, but SFT followed by GRPO rapidly reaches baseline accuracy with far fewer samples. For 5-digit multiplication (GPT2, p_cot=0.25), accuracy jumps from ~0% to 100% upon switching to RL. Theoretically, only O(log((1-p_cot)/p_cot)) STaR rounds suffice for perfect generalization (Theorem 2).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between next-token prediction (NTP) alone and NTP followed by reinforcement learning, with STaR, REINFORCE, and GRPO serving as the three RL baselines. All three RL algorithms exhibit qualitatively similar behavior on the parity task: immediate accuracy improvement from random chance to 100%, accompanied by length increase, confirming that the mechanism is algorithm-agnostic. GRPO is the primary method in most experiments and shows the most stable training dynamics; STaR with high sampling temperature and end-to-end reward can be unstable due to sparse rewards and lack of negative signals. The theoretical analysis (Theorem 2) proves that only O(log((1-p_cot)/p_cot)) STaR rounds suffice for perfect generalization, establishing the first theoretical separation between NTP and NTP+RL in the autoregressive setting. The empirical sample complexity gap between NTP alone and NTP+RL grows exponentially with input dimension d (Figure 12), with NTP requiring unreasonably many iterations for larger d while NTP+RL generalizes with a miniscule fraction of the total samples.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the theoretical framework of linear autoregressive models (Malach, 2024) and the STaR bootstrapping algorithm (Zelikman et al., 2022) to provide the first theoretical separation between next-token prediction alone and next-token prediction followed by RL in the autoregressive setting. It deliberately relaxes the 'perfect chain-of-thought data' assumption made by Joshi et al. (2025) and Malach (2024) to a more natural setting where only a small (possibly polynomially small) fraction of training sequences contain elaborate demonstrations. The empirical side connects to the practical RL post-training paradigm (GRPO, REINFORCE, STaR) now standard in frontier reasoning models like OpenAI o1 and DeepSeek R1, but grounds it in a controlled parity task where the learning dynamics can be precisely characterized.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical explanation for why the widely-used 'pre-train then RL' recipe succeeds where pure next-token prediction fails, identifying length increase during RL as a bona fide learning mechanism (up-sampling long demonstrations in the effective training distribution) rather than merely a computational-depth phenomenon. The exponential sample-complexity gap between NTP alone and NTP+RL, and the clean critical threshold p_cot = 1/3 matching empirical observations, give practitioners concrete theoretical grounding for the post-training paradigm. Open questions include how noisy or imperfect chains of thought affect the conclusions, whether the proof strategy extends to other hard functions beyond parity, and how the analysis changes when pre-train and post-train target functions differ.
