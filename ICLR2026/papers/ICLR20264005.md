## Problem

LLMs frequently produce overconfident or miscalibrated confidence estimates, yet prior approaches either infer confidence from internal states in a zero-shot manner (providing no inherent confidence awareness) or decouple confidence estimation from generation via external probes or supervised fine-tuning on constructed ground truths. No existing method directly trains the LLM to express calibrated confidence as an integral part of its generative output using a theoretically grounded optimization objective.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264005/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a factual question (open-ended short-form or multiple-choice) and produces a structured response containing a textual answer followed by a verbalized confidence score as an integer from 0 to 10. During training, the answer is generated first and then held fixed as input while only the confidence tokens are generated and optimized, ensuring answer quality is decoupled from the calibration objective. The integer confidence is normalized to [0,1] for reward computation, with 0 meaning certain the answer is wrong and 10 meaning certain it is correct.

## Method

The paper formulates confidence expression as a Markov Decision Process and trains the LLM with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) using the logarithmic scoring rule as the reward: log(p̂) for correct answers and log(1−p̂) for incorrect ones, with ε-clipping for numerical stability. Because the log scoring rule is a strictly proper scoring rule, the optimal policy provably yields perfectly calibrated confidence (Proposition 1), providing a theoretical guarantee absent in prior supervised or DPO-based approaches. The model is fine-tuned via LoRA on LLaMA-3-8B-Instruct (and ablated on Qwen-2.5 and Gemma-2), with the answer generated independently before the confidence-optimization step so that task accuracy remains stable. This design requires no external reward model, preference annotations, or post-hoc calibration, and the resulting confidence is expressed directly in the LLM's natural-language output at inference time.

## Results

On TriviaQA (Single-Answer), Rewarding Doubt achieves an ECE of 0.0226 and AUROC of 0.8592, substantially outperforming all zero-shot baselines (best zero-shot: Self-Consistency at ECE 0.1134, AUROC 0.8213) and the DPO-based LACIE (ECE 0.1200, AUROC 0.7200). On QAMPARI (Multiple-Answer), it reaches ECE 0.0816 and AUROC 0.6947, again beating all baselines. In out-of-domain generalization (trained on TriviaQA, evaluated on MedQA), Rewarding Doubt reduces ECE from 0.4480 (Verbalize) to 0.1145 while improving AUROC from 0.5075 to 0.6649, without degrading task accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Rewarding Doubt outperforms all zero-shot baselines by a wide margin: Self-Consistency (ECE 0.1134, AUROC 0.8213), Top-K (ECE 0.1611), Sequence Probability (ECE 0.3156), Chain-of-Thought (ECE 0.3065), Surrogate Token (ECE 0.3686), and the untrained Verbalize baseline (ECE 0.3459). Against the two RLHF-based methods from Leng et al. (PPO-M: ECE 0.3262; PPO-C: ECE 0.3607), Rewarding Doubt achieves roughly 14× lower ECE, showing that optimizing a proper scoring rule directly is far more effective than reward-model-based calibration. The closest competitor is Trained Probe (Azaria & Mitchell, 2023), which achieves a marginally lower ECE (0.0189 vs. 0.0226) but a notably lower AUROC (0.8173 vs. 0.8592), indicating Rewarding Doubt better discriminates correct from incorrect answers. LACIE (Stengel-Eskin et al., 2024), a DPO-based listener-aware method, is outperformed on both ECE (0.1200 vs. 0.0226) and AUROC (0.7200 vs. 0.8592). In the Multiple-Answer setting on QAMPARI, Rewarding Doubt (ECE 0.0816, AUROC 0.6947) beats Trained Probe (ECE 0.1117, AUROC 0.6481) and Sequence Probability (ECE 0.5324), and in out-of-domain generalization to MedQA it reduces ECE by ~4× over Verbalize while the Trained Probe only achieves ECE 0.2099.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of proper scoring rules from decision theory and RL-based LLM fine-tuning. Prior confidence calibration work either decouples estimation from generation (trained probes, logit-based methods) or uses preference/knowledge-based RL rewards (LACIE's DPO, PPO-M/C reward model calibration, RLKF). The key departure is using the logarithmic scoring rule—a strictly proper scoring rule—as the PPO reward signal, which provides a theoretical guarantee that the optimal policy is perfectly calibrated, something no prior RL-for-confidence method offers. The two-step fine-tuning paradigm (estimate confidence externally, then supervise the model to reproduce it) is explicitly rejected in favor of end-to-end optimization of the generative process itself.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical grounding (proper scoring rule → provably calibrated optimal policy) gives practitioners a principled, assumption-light alternative to the ad-hoc reward designs in prior RL-for-confidence work, and the generalization to unseen domains (MedQA, CommonsenseQA) without further fine-tuning suggests the model learns a transferable confidence-awareness rather than dataset-specific patterns. However, the method is limited to binary correctness signals and 3B–9B models, and the ECE on CommonsenseQA is actually marginally worse than the zero-shot baseline, indicating that calibration gains are not uniform across domains. Open questions include scaling to larger models, extending to continuous or LLM-judged correctness, and whether the approach transfers to free-text generation where answer boundaries are less clear.
