## Problem

Existing benchmarks for evaluating LLMs as urban agents rely solely on outcome-based metrics (e.g., prediction accuracy, traffic efficiency), which mask critical deficits in intermediate reasoning steps, and none assess reflection—the ability to diagnose errors from environmental feedback and adapt over time. This prevents a fine-grained diagnosis of where and why LLM reasoning fails in dynamic urban contexts.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263393/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes verbalized spatiotemporal observations—sparse adjacency matrices with node/edge attributes for spatial structure and discrete time-series attribute values (e.g., congestion levels, POI visits) for temporal dynamics—paired with task instructions, all generated within the UAgentEnv interactive city environment. It produces two levels of output: (1) process-based accuracy scores across 62,466 structured QA pairs partitioned into four reasoning dimensions (understanding, forecasting, planning, reflection), and (2) standardized end-to-end metrics (MAPE, accuracy, construction cost, travel distance, etc.) across nine real-world urban tasks. For decision-making tasks, observations are collected via a semi-stochastic policy (ε=0.1) to ensure scenario diversity, while planning ground truth is derived from simulation-driven exhaustive search over action sequences with a discount factor of 0.9.

## Method

USTBench is built on UAgentEnv, an interactive city environment integrating real-world data (OpenStreetMap road networks, Beijing traffic flows, global GDP/population, NYC taxi trajectories, FourSquare check-ins) across four prediction and five decision-making tasks. The core evaluation decomposes urban spatiotemporal reasoning into a four-stage loop—spatiotemporal understanding (eight sub-types spanning distance, adjacency, connectivity, duration, local extrema, chronology, periodicity, and trend), forecasting, planning, and reflection—each assessed via targeted QA pairs (40% basic understanding, 60% higher-level reasoning). Planning ground truth is computed by evaluating all future action sequences over a horizon H=5 and selecting the action maximizing expected cumulative discounted reward via multiple stochastic rollouts. Reflection QA explicitly provides the model with a prior prediction or action, the resulting environmental feedback, and asks it to diagnose errors and revise its output. A unified agent framework orchestrates the understand→forecast→plan→reflect pipeline with a memory module for experience storage, and a LoRA fine-tuning experiment on Qwen2.5-7B validates that improved spatiotemporal understanding transfers to downstream forecasting and planning performance.

## Results

Across 62,466 structured QA pairs and nine urban tasks, o4-mini achieves the best overall spatiotemporal understanding (79.24%), forecasting (78.72%), and planning (44.66%) accuracy, while DeepSeek-R1 leads in reflection (51.79%). Planning remains the weakest dimension for all models, with even the best model (o4-mini) reaching only 44.66% accuracy, and reflection accuracy stays below 52% across all fourteen LLMs. On downstream tasks, LLMs outperform classic methods by up to 337% in forecasting accuracy and 53% in decision effectiveness, with o4-mini achieving the lowest congestion prediction MAPE (15.78%) and highest accuracy (75.73%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 14 LLMs (6 non-reasoning, 8 reasoning) plus classic methods (ARIMA, GSCA, genetic algorithm) as baselines. A central finding is that reasoning models do NOT consistently outperform their non-reasoning counterparts: Llama3.3-70B beats DeepSeek-R1 on congestion prediction (74.93% vs. 60.20% accuracy) and Qwen2.5-32B substantially outperforms QwQ-32B on congestion (69.00% vs. 62.90%), suggesting general-purpose reasoning post-training does not transfer to urban spatiotemporal tasks. GPT-4o (non-reasoning) matches or exceeds GLM-Z1-32B and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-70B on multiple dimensions, further undermining the assumption that reasoning models are universally superior. Among reasoning models, o4-mini leads in forecasting (78.72%) and planning (44.66%), while DeepSeek-R1 leads in reflection (51.79%) but shows verbose, time-consuming reasoning (97.33 s/batch vs. 5.61 s/batch for o4-mini). DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B suffers severe repetition issues, dropping to 29.36% on understanding (below its base Qwen2.5-7B at 45.52%). On downstream tasks, all LLMs outperform classic methods, with o4-mini achieving the best congestion MAPE (15.78% vs. 57.05% for ARIMA) and socio-economic MAPE (4.97% vs. 7.09% for ARIMA).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior urban LLM benchmarks (STBench, CityBench, CityGPT, UrbanPlanBench) in two fundamental ways: it introduces process-based evaluation that decomposes reasoning into a four-stage loop (understanding → forecasting → planning → reflection) rather than relying solely on outcome metrics, and it is the first to include reflection as a distinct, measurable reasoning dimension. Conceptually, it builds on the LLM-as-urban-agent paradigm established by UrbanGPT, LLMLight, and UrbanKGent, but reframes the evaluation question from 'can the model solve the task?' to 'which specific reasoning step fails?' The interactive UAgentEnv environment extends static QA benchmarks by enabling agents to perceive, act, and receive feedback in dynamic urban contexts, drawing on simulation platforms (CityFlow, SUMO) and real-world geospatial/traffic data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that general-purpose reasoning models (DeepSeek-R1, QwQ) do not consistently outperform non-reasoning models (GPT-4o, Llama3.3) on urban spatiotemporal tasks, challenging the assumption that RL-based reasoning improvements transfer across domains and highlighting the need for domain-specialized adaptation. Reflection emerges as a critical weakness across all fourteen models, with even top reasoning models struggling to convert environmental feedback into corrective strategies—a gap that limits long-term adaptation in dynamic urban settings. Key open questions include whether domain-adaptive fine-tuning (as demonstrated with Qwen2.5-7B-ST) can close the planning and reflection gaps, whether real-world deployment will reveal additional failure modes absent in simulation, and how multi-agent and social reasoning dimensions (explicitly excluded here) interact with the four identified processes.
