## Problem

While the NTK linearization of wide neural networks under gradient descent is well-established, the fundamental structural mechanism *causing* this linearization remained unclear. Prior work identified proxies (Hessian-to-gradient norm ratios, ensembles of weak sub-models) but did not provide a universal equivalence criterion linking a concrete, computable quantity to the linearization phenomenon. This paper asks: what is the minimal, architecture-agnostic condition that is both necessary and sufficient for a learning system to linearize in its parameters?

## Input / Output

The theoretical framework takes a properly normalized gradient-descent learning system (a PGDML) specified by its hypothesis function F, parameter count N, initialization θ₀, and learning rate η, and produces an equivalence: the system linearizes at rate m(n) if and only if its derivative correlations C^{D,d} decay as O(1/m(n)) or O(1/√m(n)) at initialization. Empirically, the method consumes image datasets (MNIST 784-d, CIFAR-10, Fashion-MNIST) with fully connected networks of varying width n (up to 19,484) and activation functions (ReLU, GELU, Sigmoid, Erf), and outputs the NTK linearization error (MSE between the trained network and its first-order Taylor approximation) as a function of width, along with estimated second- and third-order derivative correlation values. The learning rate is swept across width-dependent scalings η(n) = η₀·n^α to probe how external scale affects linearization.

## Method

The core contribution is a new object—the derivative correlation C^{D,d}(θ)—defined as the normalized inner product between the (D+d)-th derivative of the hypothesis function and d copies of its first derivative, with the learning rate providing the natural normalization. The paper proves two equivalence theorems: (i) fixed weak correlations (C^d = O(1/m(n)), C^{D,d} = O(1/√m(n))) are equivalent to simple linearity, and (ii) power-weak correlations (C^{D,d} = O(1/√m(n))^d) are equivalent to strong linearity that is stable under arbitrary learning-rate rescalings. To make the asymptotic analysis rigorous for stochastic systems, the authors introduce a stochastic big-O notation built on the subordinate tensor norm, proving via Zorn's lemma that every random tensor sequence admits a unique tight asymptotic bound. They then show, using the tensor programs formalism, that wide fully connected networks with activation functions satisfying φ^{[n]} ≤ O((n+1)!) are √n-weakly correlated, and derive a time-dependent bound F(θ(s)) − F_lin(s) = O(s⁰/m(n)) for SGD training. Empirical validation on 7,000 training runs (5 learning-rate exponents × 14 widths × 100 seeds) on MNIST confirms the predicted power-law decay of linearization error with width.

## Results

The paper's primary empirical validation (Figure 4) measures the NTK linearization error (MSE between the trained nonlinear network and its first-order Taylor approximation) on MNIST across 14 widths (2048–19484) and 100 seeds. The observed power-law decay slopes are ≈ −1.14 (α=0), ≈ −1.14 (α=−0.25), ≈ −1.3 (α=−0.5), ≈ −1.73 (α=−0.75), and ≈ −2.72 (α=−1), confirming that more aggressive learning-rate scaling (η ∝ n^α with more negative α) accelerates the convergence to the linearized limit. Theoretical upper bounds from Theorem 3.2 predict steeper slopes, and the authors note the empirical decay is slower than the asymptotic prediction, likely because the tested widths are still in a pre-asymptotic regime. Correlation measurements (Figures 1, 3) confirm that second- and third-order derivative correlations decay with width across MNIST, CIFAR-10, and Fashion-MNIST for ReLU, Erf, and Sigmoid activations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without traditional method-vs-method baselines; the central 'comparison' is between the nonlinear network and its NTK linearization, quantified by the MSE discrepancy. The empirical slopes are consistently slower (less negative) than the theoretical upper bounds from Theorem 3.2, which the authors attribute to finite-width effects and the fact that the bounds are worst-case. In relation to prior work, the framework subsumes and refines Liu et al. (2020)'s Hessian-to-gradient ratio criterion by requiring correlation decay specifically at initialization rather than in a neighborhood, and generalizes Dyer & Gur-Ari (2019)'s Feynman-diagram approach by measuring asymptotic behavior of correlations directly rather than their averages. The paper also extends Chizat et al. (2019)'s observation about external scales by showing precisely how rescaling η → r(n)η differentially affects higher-order correlations, providing a mechanistic explanation for the 'NTK inferiority paradox' where finite-width networks outperform their kernel counterparts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the NTK linearization framework of Jacot et al. (2018) and the tensor programs formalism of Yang & Littwin (2021), but departs from prior work by introducing 'derivative correlations' as a universal, architecture-agnostic mechanism that is *equivalent* to linearization rather than merely sufficient for it. It refines the Hessian-to-gradient ratio criterion of Liu et al. (2020) by localizing the requirement to initialization and extending it to all higher-order derivatives, and it generalizes the Feynman-diagram asymptotics of Dyer & Gur-Ari (2019) from averaged values to full asymptotic bounds. The new random tensor asymptotic behavior formalism (Section 2) provides the analytical infrastructure that prior NTK analyses lacked for handling stochastic, high-dimensional objects uniformly.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The equivalence between weak derivative correlations and linearization gives a universal diagnostic: any learning system (not just wide NNs) can be assessed for its linearization rate by measuring these correlations, and the framework naturally extends to stochastic GD where prior results were limited to deterministic settings. The provocative 'chicken and egg' interpretation—that weak correlations may represent an *absence* of beneficial bias, making NTK learning 'overly unbiased'—offers a concrete mechanism for the NTK inferiority paradox, suggesting that deliberately preserving some non-vanishing correlations could improve generalization. Open questions include whether the framework can quantitatively predict which biases to preserve, whether the bounds tighten for deeper or non-FC architectures, and whether the empirical gap between predicted and observed scaling slopes (noted in Appendix A.2) reflects finite-width effects or a missing mechanism.
