## Problem

It remains unclear whether LLMs' apparent ability to simulate human psychological traits reflects genuine reasoning about the nomothetic network of trait correlations or merely semantic pattern-matching on inputs with strong lexical overlap. Prior work evaluated LLMs on first-order prediction accuracy (mimicking specific trait scores), which is methodologically constrained by ground-truth correlations and cannot distinguish abstract psychological reasoning from surface-level retrieval. Additionally, no prior study provided process-level interpretability of the cognitive mechanisms driving LLM psychological predictions.

## Input / Output

The method takes 20 item-level Big Five personality scores (5-point Likert scale) from a single individual as the sole quantitative input and produces predicted item-level responses across 9 distinct psychological scales (perceived stress, coping style, state-trait anxiety, self-compassion, resilience, intolerance of uncertainty, emotion regulation, risk perception, and future time perspective). The primary evaluation output is a second-order structural metric—the regression slope (amplification coefficient k) and R² between the LLM-generated inter-scale Pearson correlation matrix and the human ground-truth matrix—rather than individual prediction accuracy. In Experiment 2, a natural language personality summary extracted from reasoning traces serves as an alternative or supplementary input to test its predictive sufficiency and synergistic value.

## Method

The paper introduces a zero-shot role-playing paradigm where LLMs receive an individual's Big Five scores and predict their responses on 9 other psychological scales, then evaluates performance via a novel second-order morphism measure comparing full inter-scale correlation matrices rather than first-order trait predictions. This reveals a systematic 'structural amplification' (k > 1.0 across all 12 tested LLMs), interpreted as idealized abstraction where LLMs filter the random measurement noise inherent in human self-reports, validated through Cronbach's Alpha comparisons, unilateral attenuation correction (bringing k to ~1.0), and dose-response noise injection experiments. For process-level interpretability, a meta-prompt approach uses separate annotation models to parse reasoning traces into 20-dimensional attribution vectors and extract natural language summaries, revealing a two-stage cognitive process: concept-driven information selection (factor-level alignment with human ground truth at r = 0.981, but item-level confusion) followed by synergistic information compression where the generated summary encodes emergent second-order trait-interplay information that, when added to original scores, further improves structural alignment (R² = 0.93 between k and predictive performance across 15 conditions).

## Results

All tested LLMs reconstruct the human psychological correlational structure with R² > 0.88 alignment, exhibiting a systematic structural amplification with regression slopes k > 1.0 (e.g., Gemini 2.5: R² = 0.92, k = 1.42, p < .001). A near-perfect linear relationship (R² = 0.95, p < .001) links each model's amplification coefficient to its predictive performance, confirming amplification is a functional mechanism rather than an artifact. LLM-generated data shows higher internal consistency (mean Cronbach's α = 0.87) than human data (α = 0.75), and after attenuation correction on human data, amplification coefficients drop to ~1.0, supporting the idealized-abstraction interpretation. In the reasoning-trace analysis, LLMs achieve factor-level attribution alignment of r = 0.981 with human ground truth (vs. r = 0.790 for semantic similarity), while item-level alignment is weak (r = 0.207).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is a Semantic Similarity model built on BAAI's bge-reranker-large cross-encoder, which uses fixed semantic-similarity weights in a linear predictor. All LLMs substantially outperform this baseline on structural alignment: LLMs achieve R² > 0.88 with amplification slopes k > 1.0, whereas the Semantic Similarity model yields only R² = 0.52 and k = 0.99 (essentially no amplification), indicating that surface-level word overlap cannot account for the LLMs' performance. KNN and other traditional ML baselines (SVM, Linear Regression) also fall below LLMs on the amplification coefficient, though they approach LLM-level predictive correlation when trained directly on the dataset. In the attribution analysis, LLMs' factor-level importance profiles align with a Bayesian Ridge Regression baseline fitted to human ground truth (r = 0.981) far more closely than with the Semantic Similarity baseline (r = 0.790), confirming that LLMs select information using a concept-driven, psychologically grounded strategy rather than surface semantics. The synergistic Summary+Score condition outperforms ScoreOnly for every model (e.g., Claude: r = 0.445 vs. 0.442; Qwen: r = 0.435 vs. 0.432), demonstrating that the LLM-generated abstract summary encodes emergent second-order information beyond the raw scores.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the individual-level LLM simulation paradigm established by Park et al. (2024) in three deliberate ways: replacing first-order prediction accuracy with a second-order structural comparison of inter-scale correlation matrices, using sparse 20-item Big Five inputs to isolate reasoning from memory retrieval, and adding mechanistic analysis of reasoning traces. It also positions itself against the 'stochastic parrot' vs. genuine reasoning debate by designing a task that controls for semantic overlap, and benchmarks against a BGE-reranker-based semantic similarity baseline to rule out surface-level pattern matching as an explanation. The two-stage reasoning decomposition (concept-driven information selection followed by synergistic information compression) draws on the Information Bottleneck principle and cognitive hierarchy frameworks, but applies them to a novel psychometric setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of 'structural amplification' as a general, robust property of modern LLMs—and its explanation as idealized abstraction via noise filtering—provides a concrete mechanistic account of how LLMs transcend passive data replication in psychological simulation. The synergistic summary finding (Summary+Score outperforming ScoreOnly) suggests LLMs generate emergent second-order representations during reasoning that are not reducible to the raw inputs, with implications for how we design and interpret CoT outputs. Key limitations include reliance on a single culturally homogeneous dataset (816 Chinese participants), purely observational (non-interventional) analysis of reasoning traces, and no account of why amplification coefficients vary across models. Open questions include cultural generalizability, whether the idealization effect persists with non-quantitative inputs, and whether interventional manipulation of the amplification process can establish definitive causality.
