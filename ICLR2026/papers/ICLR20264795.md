## Problem

Deep neural networks systematically disagree with human observers on error patterns and shape-vs-texture preferences, yet the source of this misalignment was unclear. Prior work attributed Imagen's superior behavioral alignment to its generative (diffusion) objective, but the paper shows this explanation is confounded by a simple 64×64 input resize that acts as a low-pass filter. The paper asks whether low-pass filtering alone—without any generative component—can close the human-machine alignment gap, and what the theoretical ceiling of the model-vs-human benchmark actually is.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264795/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on 224×224 RGB images from the Model-vs-Human benchmark (16 super-classes, 17 corruption conditions) and applies a test-time low-pass filter—Gaussian blur (σ up to ~4 px), bicubic resize to a lower resolution and back, or a learned 2D Fourier filter—before feeding the image to a frozen vision model alongside 80 averaged text prompts. The model outputs a 16-way class prediction, from which three alignment metrics (error consistency via Cohen's κ, shape bias on cue-conflict images, and aggregate OOD accuracy) are computed against human ground-truth responses collected at 200 ms presentation.

## Method

The core intervention is prepending a low-pass filter at test time to any discriminative classifier, requiring no retraining. The authors implement three filter variants: Gaussian blur (σ=2.5 px optimal), bicubic resize to 64×64, and a learned 112×112 real-valued Fourier-space filter optimized via gradient descent with L1 sparsity and Gaussian-smoothness regularization to maximize error consistency against human responses. They demonstrate the learned filter is itself a low-pass filter and that its spectrum closely approximates the human contrast sensitivity function (CSF) at 200 ms, providing a physiological explanation for why the filter works. Additionally, they compute the full Pareto frontier of all achievable (error consistency, OOD accuracy) pairs on the benchmark via an exact combinatorial optimization over binary correctness sequences, establishing the previously unknown benchmark ceiling and revealing an inherent accuracy-consistency trade-off.

## Results

On the Model-vs-Human benchmark, prepending a Gaussian blur (σ=2.5 px) to OpenCLIP ViT-H-14 raises error consistency from 0.28 to 0.37 and shape bias from 0.60 to 0.96, while OOD accuracy drops modestly from 0.78 to 0.72. A learned Fourier filter pushes error consistency to 0.38 and shape bias to 0.95 (OOD acc 0.73), establishing a new state-of-the-art that surpasses Imagen's 0.31 EC. The effect generalizes across ResNet, SWSL, BiT-M, ViT, Noisy Student, and multiple OpenCLIP variants, with the largest gains observed for smaller patch sizes (14 px).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's filtered OpenCLIP ViT-H-14 (EC 0.37–0.38, SB 0.95–0.96) substantially outperforms Imagen (EC 0.31, SB 0.99, OOD 0.71) on error consistency by 0.06–0.07 and on OOD accuracy by 0.01–0.04, while Imagen retains a marginal shape-bias edge (0.99 vs 0.95–0.96). Against the much larger ViT-22B-384 (EC 0.26, SB 0.87, OOD 0.80), the filtered model wins on both EC (+0.11) and SB (+0.08) but trails on OOD accuracy (−0.05 to −0.08). Compared to the unfiltered OpenCLIP ViT-H-14 baseline (EC 0.28, SB 0.60, OOD 0.78), the low-pass filter yields a dramatic +0.09–0.10 EC gain and +0.35–0.36 SB gain at the cost of 3–6 percentage points of OOD accuracy. The paper further demonstrates that the effect is not model-specific: shape bias and error consistency increase with low-pass filtering for nearly all tested architectures (ResNet-101, SWSL, BiT-M, ViT-L-16, Noisy Student, and six OpenCLIP variants), with the strongest gains for 14-pixel patch models. A standard ResNet-101 with a prepended Gaussian (σ=3.0) reaches shape bias 0.80 without any retraining, matching or exceeding the 0.78 reported for a diffusion-noise-augmented ResNet-50.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reinterprets the behavioral alignment results of Jaini et al. (2023), who attributed Imagen's superior human-like performance to its generative (diffusion) objective. The authors instead identify a previously overlooked preprocessing step—downscaling inputs to 64×64 pixels—as the true driver, effectively a low-pass filter. This departs from prior work on low-pass filtering in vision (Jang & Tong 2024; Lu et al. 2025; Vogelsang et al. 2018), which applied filtering at train-time; here, filtering is applied at test-time to frozen, off-the-shelf discriminative models. The physiological motivation draws on the contrast sensitivity function literature (Campbell & Robson 1968; Kelly 1979), connecting the optimal filter width to how the human visual cortex actually receives input at short presentation times.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a simple Gaussian blur at test-time halves the human-DNN alignment gap challenges the emerging narrative that generative objectives are necessary for human-like vision, with implications for the broader discriminative-vs-generative debate in visual cortex modeling. The computation of the MvH pareto frontier is a useful contribution that was previously unknown, establishing that the benchmark is far from saturated. Key limitations include evaluation at a single presentation time (200 ms) where the CSF is approximately low-pass; at longer exposures the CSF becomes band-pass and the optimal filter would differ. Open questions include whether test-time filtering improves neural similarity scores (e.g., on Brain-Score), whether it generalizes to other behavioral benchmarks, and whether the accuracy-consistency trade-off identified here is fundamental or an artifact of the MvH metric construction.
