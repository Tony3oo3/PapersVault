## Problem

Short-task benchmarks suggest diminishing returns from continued LLM scaling, but this may be an illusion: even marginal gains in single-step accuracy can compound into exponential increases in the length of tasks a model can complete. Failures on long, simple tasks have been misattributed to reasoning or planning limitations, yet the execution capability of LLMs—reliably carrying out a known plan over many steps—remains understudied. The paper asks: how many steps can an LLM reliably execute, and what drives the degradation that occurs as tasks get longer?

## Input / Output

The model receives an in-context key-value dictionary (five-letter English words mapped to integers in [-99, 99]) and a sequence of keys grouped into turns of complexity K (number of keys per turn). At each turn, the model must retrieve the values for the given keys and update a running sum, outputting the new state in <answer> tags. The task is fully specified by the number of turns and K, which multiply to give total task length, and is contamination-free since new examples are generated programmatically. Evaluation metrics include per-step accuracy, per-turn accuracy, task accuracy (fraction of samples completing all steps without error), and horizon length H_s (the first step where mean task accuracy drops below threshold s).

## Method

The paper introduces a controlled synthetic benchmark that isolates execution from planning and knowledge by explicitly providing both the plan (keys to retrieve) and the knowledge (the dictionary), reducing the task to a sequence of retrieve-then-compose steps. They analyze two orthogonal axes—number of turns and turn complexity K—to decompose long-horizon execution. A key empirical finding is the self-conditioning effect: by counterfactually injecting controlled error rates into the model's own prior outputs, they show that per-step accuracy degrades as the task progresses, beyond what long-context degradation alone explains, and that this effect persists even at frontier scale (200B+ parameters). They further show that RL-trained thinking models eliminate self-conditioning and that sequential test-time compute (thinking tokens) dramatically extends single-turn execution length, with GPT-5 executing over 2100 steps versus 432 for Claude-4 Sonnet. Theoretically, they prove that horizon length grows hyperbolically with step accuracy (H_s = ln(s)/ln(p)), explaining how diminishing returns at the step level translate into exponential gains in task length.

## Results

On the single-turn execution benchmark (maximum steps at ≥80% accuracy), GPT-5 thinking (codenamed 'Horizon') executes 2176 steps, far ahead of Claude-4 Sonnet (432), Grok 4 (384), and Gemini 2.5 Pro (120). Without chain-of-thought or thinking, even frontier non-thinking models like DeepSeek-V3 (670B) and Kimi K2 (1026B) fail to execute more than 6 steps per turn. In the multi-turn scaling study (K=1), Qwen3-32B drops below 50% task accuracy within 15 turns despite near-perfect first-step accuracy, while the self-conditioning effect causes per-turn accuracy to degrade as the model's own prior errors accumulate in context.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-5 thinking dominates the single-turn execution benchmark at 2176 steps, roughly 5× ahead of Claude-4 Sonnet (432) and Grok 4 (384), and nearly 18× ahead of Gemini 2.5 Pro (120). DeepSeek-R1, also RL-trained for thinking, executes over 100 steps but trails GPT-5 by more than 20×. Without sequential test-time compute, all non-thinking frontier models (DeepSeek-V3 at 670B, Kimi K2 at 1026B) collapse to ≤6 steps, demonstrating that raw parameter scale alone is insufficient for long-horizon execution. In the multi-turn scaling study, Qwen3-32B outperforms Gemma3-27B on horizon length, and within each family, larger models consistently sustain higher task accuracy over more turns. Notably, scaling model size mitigates long-context degradation (frontier models achieve near-perfect accuracy on healed 100-turn histories) but does NOT mitigate self-conditioning, whereas thinking models (Qwen3 with RL) completely eliminate the self-conditioning effect, maintaining stable turn-100 accuracy regardless of prior error rate.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by reframing long-task LLM failures as execution failures rather than reasoning or planning failures, a distinction prior work (Shojaee et al., 2025; Khan et al., 2025) has not drawn. It builds on the METR horizon-length metric (Kwa et al., 2025) and long-context evaluation literature (RULER, GSM-Infinite) but introduces a controlled key-value retrieval task that explicitly provides the plan and knowledge, thereby isolating execution as an independently measurable capability. The theoretical analysis connects to scaling laws (Kaplan et al., 2020) by showing how diminishing step-accuracy returns compound hyperbolically in horizon length, and the self-conditioning finding links to in-context learning and hallucination snowballing literature (Zhang et al., 2023; Arora et al., 2025).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a contamination-free, programmatically generatable benchmark for long-horizon execution that cleanly separates frontier models (GPT-5 at 2176 steps vs. Claude-4 Sonnet at 432), offering a practical tool for tracking progress that short-task benchmarks obscure. The identification of self-conditioning as a distinct, scaling-resistant failure mode opens concrete research directions: RL-trained thinking models fix it, context engineering mitigates it, but no training-free fix is complete. Key limitations include the synthetic nature of the task (improvement is necessary but not sufficient for real agentic tasks), the theoretical parallelizability of the single-turn variant, and the fact that results reflect current models rather than inherent transformer properties. Open questions include whether task-specific finetuning can eliminate self-conditioning, how it interacts with non-Markovian dependencies, and whether empirical scaling laws for horizon length can be established.
