## Problem

Current LLM architectures lack any built-in mechanism to distinguish which input tokens constitute instructions (to be executed) versus data (to be processed), making them inherently vulnerable to prompt injection attacks. Prior mitigations operate at the prompt level (delimiters, spotlighting) or the training level (adversarial fine-tuning), but none enforce separation at the architectural level, and quantitative studies confirm that none of these approaches achieve reliable instruction-data separation.

## Input / Output

The model receives a sequence of text tokens accompanied by per-token binary role labels (instruction or data) that are fixed by system design at deployment time (e.g., all email contents are labeled as data, all user queries as instructions). During the forward pass, data-token embeddings are transformed by a fixed π/2 isoclinic rotation before entering the transformer stack, while instruction-token embeddings pass through unchanged. The model then produces a standard generated text response; the rotation is applied only at the embedding layer and adds zero learnable parameters.

## Method

ASIDE introduces a conditional embedding mechanism where each token's representation depends on its functional role: instruction tokens use the standard embedding vector E[I_x, ·], while data tokens use R(E[I_x, ·]) where R is a fixed, non-learnable orthogonal rotation. In practice, R is a π/2 isoclinic rotation implemented as a block-diagonal matrix of 2×2 rotation blocks, which reduces to a simple coordinate swap-and-negation operation (x₁,x₂,x₃,x₄,…)→(−x₂,x₁,−x₄,x₃,…) for efficient computation. The method is integrated post-hoc into any pretrained LLM by modifying only the embedding layer's forward pass to accept segment_ids, followed by standard supervised fine-tuning on benign instruction-tuning data (Alpaca-clean-gpt4-turbo) with no safety-specific objective or adversarial examples. The key insight is that this parameter-free architectural change gives the model explicit role information from the very first layer, enabling it to maintain instruction-data distinction throughout the network without needing to infer roles from context, and an ablation confirms that π/2 is the optimal rotation angle for maximizing separation.

## Results

ASIDE achieves substantially higher instruction-data separation (SEP score) than both Vanilla and ISE across all six models tested, with improvements of 12.3 p.p. (Llama 2 7B) to 44.1 p.p. (Mistral 7B) over Vanilla. For example, on Llama 3.1 8B, ASIDE reaches 83.1% SEP vs. 53.2% (Vanilla) and 65.9% (ISE), while on Qwen 3 8B it reaches 71.4% vs. 45.3% (Vanilla). Utility is largely preserved: SEP Utility and AlpacaEval scores remain within a few points of Vanilla. On safety, ASIDE reduces average attack success rates on BIPIA-text from 14.7% to 4.9%, on StruQ-ID from 45.6% to 28.1%, and on TensorTrust/Gandalf by 8.6/9.4 p.p., all without any safety-specific training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole architectural baseline is ISE (Wu et al., 2024), which adds a learnable offset vector to data-token embeddings to create an instruction hierarchy. ASIDE decisively outperforms ISE on the primary SEP separation metric across all six models (e.g., 83.1% vs. 65.9% on Llama 3.1 8B; 92.1% vs. 52.1% on Mistral 7B), while ISE is inconsistent relative to even the Vanilla baseline (e.g., ISE scores 34.7% on Qwen 3 8B, below Vanilla's 45.3%). On indirect prompt injection, ISE is essentially undistinguishable from Vanilla on BIPIA-code and StruQ-ID (<0.1% difference) and provides only 1–2% improvement on BIPIA-text and StruQ-OOD, whereas ASIDE achieves large reductions (e.g., BIPIA-text from 14.7% to 4.9% average ASR). On direct injection, ISE actually increases attack success by 1.7–3.1% on three of four benchmarks, while ASIDE reduces it by 8.6–9.4 p.p. on TensorTrust and Gandalf. The paper attributes ISE's weaker performance to the fact that a simple linear offset is less effective at maintaining separation in deeper layers compared to an orthogonal rotation, as confirmed by linear probing and cosine-similarity analyses.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the quantitative diagnosis of instruction-data separation failure in LLMs by Zverev et al. (2025) and the instruction-hierarchy idea of Wallace et al. (2024), but departs from all prior mitigation strategies (prompt engineering, adversarial training, system-level information-flow control) by proposing an architectural-level solution. The closest prior method is ISE (Wu et al., 2024), which adds learnable role-specific offset vectors to embeddings; ASIDE replaces this with a fixed, parameter-free isoclinic π/2 rotation, drawing conceptual inspiration from rotary position embeddings (Su et al., 2024) and the observed low-rank structure of token embeddings (Xie et al., 2022). The key departure is that the rotation is not learned and requires no additional parameters, making it a drop-in modification to any pretrained model's forward pass.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ASIDE offers a principled, parameter-free architectural fix for a root-cause vulnerability (lack of instruction-data separation) that underlies many LLM attack vectors, and it delivers measurable safety gains during ordinary instruction tuning without any adversarial or safety-specific data. This positions it as a composable building block orthogonal to system-level defenses like CaMeL and FIDES. Key limitations include the single-turn setting (role labels must be known at input time), the requirement that the deployer can partition input into instruction vs. data segments, and the fact that direct-injection improvements are modest compared to indirect-injection gains. Open questions include extending to multi-turn conversations, exploring alternative role transforms beyond rotations, and whether the approach generalizes to more than two functional tiers.
