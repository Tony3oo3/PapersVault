## Problem

Existing AI evaluation benchmarks focus on final outcomes (correct answers in math, code, or multiple-choice moral judgments), leaving the reasoning process unexamined. Moral dilemmas are uniquely suited for process-focused evaluation because they admit multiple defensible conclusions, yet no scalable benchmark exists to assess how models identify considerations, weigh trade-offs, and construct logical arguments in morally ambiguous settings. Additionally, it remains unclear whether scaling laws or performance on STEM reasoning benchmarks predict moral reasoning capability.

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance pairs a contextualized moral dilemma scenario (44–393 words, averaging ~195) with a model's thinking trace or final response, scored against 20–49 expert-written atomic rubric criteria (23,018 total across 1,000 scenarios). Each criterion carries a weight in {−3, −2, −1, +1, +2, +3} and is assigned to one of five dimensions (Identifying, Clear Process, Logical Process, Helpful Outcome, Harmless Outcome). The LLM judge produces a binary fulfillment label per criterion, which is aggregated into a weighted scenario score (0–100) and a length-corrected variant that normalizes by response length relative to a 1000-character reference.

## Method

MOREBENCH introduces a rubric-based, LLM-as-judge evaluation pipeline for procedural moral reasoning. Fifty-three moral philosophy experts write and peer-review scenario-specific rubrics, each containing atomic criteria that capture a single aspect of good reasoning (e.g., identifying a stakeholder, justifying a trade-off, avoiding a harmful recommendation). An LLM judge (GPT-oss-120b, selected via meta-evaluation against human annotations with Cohen's κ=0.75) assesses criterion fulfillment in the model's thinking trace. Scores are computed via a weighted sum where positive-weight criteria contribute proportionally to their importance and negative-weight criteria penalize harmful or logically flawed content, then normalized by total absolute weight. A length-correction factor (response length / 1000 chars) yields MOREBENCH-Hard to counter verbosity bias. The benchmark spans two AI roles (Moral Advisor for human-guidance scenarios, Moral Agent for autonomous decision scenarios) and includes MOREBENCH-THEORY, a 150-scenario subset with rubrics grounded in five normative frameworks (Kantian Deontology, Benthamite Act Utilitarianism, Aristotelian Virtue Ethics, Scanlonian Contractualism, Gauthierian Contractarianism) to test framework-specific reasoning fidelity.

## Results

On MOREBENCH (thinking traces, 500 scenarios), frontier models achieve an average of 81.1% on Harmless Outcome criteria but only 47.9% on Logical Process, 52.7% on Identifying, 53.6% on Clear Process, and 50.1% on Helpful Outcome. Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking-2507 performs best overall (69.1% Identifying, 65.1% Logical), while Gemini-2.5-Pro performs worst (32.1% Identifying, 26.9% Logical). On MOREBENCH-THEORY, models show significant framework bias (F(4,52)=19.71, p<0.001), performing best on Kantian Deontology (M=65.9%) and Benthamite Act Utilitarianism (M=64.8%) but worst on Gauthierian Contractarianism (M=56.7%) and Aristotelian Virtue Ethics (M=58.0%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparative finding is that MOREBENCH performance is essentially uncorrelated with existing capability benchmarks: Pearson's r ranges from 0.216 to 0.245 against Chatbot Arena (user preference), Humanity's Last Exam (general-domain reasoning), AIME 25 (math), and LiveCodeBench (code), all indicating negligible predictive power. This contrasts sharply with the strong correlations these benchmarks show among themselves, suggesting moral reasoning is a distinct capability axis. Additionally, MOREBENCH violates standard scaling laws: mid-size models (e.g., GPT-5-mini-high, GPT-oss-20b, Qwen3-30B-A3B) often outperform their larger family counterparts on MOREBENCH-Regular, a pattern the authors attribute to inverse scaling where smaller models produce longer, more explicit thinking traces that better satisfy granular rubric criteria. On MOREBENCH-THEORY, no single model dominates all five moral frameworks; Qwen3-235B leads on Virtue Ethics (74.7%) and Contractarianism (72.2%), while Gemini-2.5-Pro trails on both (27.9% and 27.3%), a gap of over 45 percentage points that highlights framework-specific reasoning failures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the rubric-based evaluation paradigm (HealthBench, PaperBench, Rubrics as Rewards) from STEM and health domains to moral reasoning, where no unique correct answer exists. It departs from prior moral evaluation work (ETHICS, Delphi, moral beliefs, value preferences) by shifting focus from what models decide to how they reason, evaluating thinking traces against 23,018 expert-written criteria across five dimensions. The closest prior attempts at reasoning-process evaluation in moral settings (deontology/consequentialism taxonomies, philosopher-vs-AI comparisons, few-sentence rationale classifiers) are described as narrow and non-scalable, motivating the large-scale rubric approach here.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that moral reasoning performance is essentially orthogonal to Chatbot Arena, HLE, AIME, and LiveCodeBench scores (Pearson's r ≤ 0.245) challenges the assumption that improvements in STEM reasoning transfer to normative judgment, with direct implications for AI safety evaluation pipelines. The observed partiality toward Benthamite Act Utilitarianism and Kantian Deontology suggests RLHF training paradigms may inadvertently encode framework-specific biases, raising open questions about whether pluralistic moral reasoning can be achieved through targeted training. A key limitation is that thinking-trace faithfulness remains debated in the literature, and the moderate correlation (r=0.472, p=0.08) between thinking traces and final responses leaves open whether the benchmark truly captures internal reasoning or merely verbose justification.
