## Problem

Existing flowchart datasets lack fine-grained control over structural properties (branching factor, nesting depth, edge density) and are typically rendered in a single visual style, making them inadequate for both training and systematically stress-testing MLLMs on flowchart parsing and QA. This limits the ability to diagnose where models fail under high structural complexity or cross-renderer variation, and prevents scalable synthetic data generation for fine-tuning.

## Input / Output

The synthesizer accepts seven user-specified structural parameters (graph order ν, split/merge arrow counts εs/εm, branching factor Δb, density ρ, unlabeled edge ratio λ, nested subgraph count η) plus a renderer backend choice, and produces a rendered flowchart image at resolutions ranging from 256×28×28 to 1280×28×28 pixels. For MLLM evaluation, the input is a flowchart image and the output is a set of structured triplets of the form ⟨A, relation, B⟩ capturing labeled edges, unlabeled edges (connectedTo), and hierarchical containment (partOf), or a free-form QA answer. The training set comprises 11,520 flowcharts (4 renderers × 120 topics × 3 difficulty levels × 8 instances), while test subsets contain 4,320 flowcharts each for graph-complexity and scanned-degradation axes.

## Method

FlowGen is a three-stage controllable synthesizer: (1) configuration samples the seven structural parameters from difficulty-specific ranges; (2) graph construction generates a random spanning tree on ν nodes, adds split/merge virtual nodes and extra edges to meet target density, groups 2–5 connected nodes into disjoint nested subgraphs, then annotates nodes and edges with topic-specific labels drawn from 120 predefined domains (40 node names + 40 edge labels each, generated by GPT-4o and human-verified); (3) rendering translates the annotated abstract graph into visual flowcharts using four complementary backends (Mermaid, Graphviz, PlantUML, Diagrams), each with distinct layout algorithms and visual conventions, plus randomized color palettes and node/edge shapes. For MLLM training, open-source models are fine-tuned via LoRA (rank 64) on both language and vision components using the synthesized triplets as supervision. For benchmarking, the paper constructs six test subsets varying graph complexity (Easy/Medium/Hard) and scanned degradation (blur, perspective distortion, lossy compression), revealing that even GPT-4o and Gemini-2.5-Flash achieve under 25% F1 on most subsets, while FlowGen fine-tuning lifts open-source models to competitive levels on several public benchmarks.

## Results

Fine-tuning open-source MLLMs on the FlowGen synthetic training set yields substantial gains in flowchart parsing: Qwen2.5-VL-7B improves from 59.4% to 70.9% strict F1 on FlowVQA and from 43.2% to 60.1% on FlowLearn, while Qwen2.5-VL-3B jumps from 12.7% to 51.3% on FlowVQA. On the novel FlowGen test set, even state-of-the-art proprietary models struggle: GPT-4o achieves only 38.4% F1 on Graph-Easy and 12.0% on Graph-Hard, while Gemini-2.5-Flash scores 43.2% and 9.7% respectively. After SFT on FlowGen, Qwen2.5-VL-7B reaches 52.4% F1 on Graph-Medium and 44.0% on Graph-Hard, substantially outperforming all proprietary baselines on the harder subsets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks three proprietary MLLMs (GPT-4o, Gemini-2.5-Flash, GLM-4V-Plus) against open-source models before and after FlowGen SFT. On public parsing benchmarks, GPT-4o retains the top position on FlowVQA (88.2% vs. 70.9% for Qwen2.5-VL-7B+SFT) and CBD (64.5% vs. 55.4%), but FlowGen-fine-tuned Qwen2.5-VL-7B surpasses GLM-4V-Plus on every dataset, most dramatically on FlowLearn (60.1% vs. 31.6%) and hdBPMN (18.3% vs. 8.6%). On the novel FlowGen test set, the advantage reverses: Qwen2.5-VL-7B+SFT on Graph-Medium achieves 52.4% F1 versus GPT-4o's 16.2% and Gemini-2.5-Flash's 13.7%, and on Graph-Hard it reaches 44.0% versus 12.0% and 9.7% respectively. Gemini-2.5-Flash is the strongest proprietary model on the FlowGen test set (43.2% on Graph-Easy) but still trails the fine-tuned 7B open-source model on all medium and hard subsets. Overall, FlowGen SFT closes or reverses the gap with proprietary models on structurally complex flowcharts, while proprietary models retain an edge on simpler, single-renderer public benchmarks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the fixed-dataset paradigm that has dominated flowchart understanding research (FlowVQA, FlowLearn, CBD, FC_A/FC_B, hdBPMN, FlowchartQA), where structural complexity, rendering style, and dataset size are all locked in at creation time. Rather than proposing a new parsing architecture, FlowGen repurposes the synthetic data generation idea—common in NLP and vision—into the flowchart domain, introducing explicit structural knobs (graph order, branching factor, nesting depth, edge density, unlabeled-edge ratio) and multi-renderer backends (Mermaid, Graphviz, PlantUML, Diagrams) that no prior flowchart resource supports. The evaluation protocol (triplet-based strict/relaxed F1 with Levenshtein tolerance) extends standard graph-parsing metrics, and the LoRA-based fine-tuning of open-source MLLMs follows the now-standard recipe for adapting VLMs to specialized visual tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FlowGen provides the first scalable, controllable resource that simultaneously serves as a training set and a stress-test benchmark for flowchart understanding, filling a gap where prior datasets were either too small, single-renderer, or evaluation-only. The finding that even GPT-4o and Gemini-2.5-Flash score below 25% F1 on the harder subsets, while a 7B open-source model fine-tuned on FlowGen can outperform proprietary models on some benchmarks, suggests that structural visual reasoning remains a genuine bottleneck that general pretraining has not solved. Open questions include whether the gains transfer to other diagram types (circuit diagrams, UML, network topologies), whether the virtual-node abstraction for split/merge arrows limits fidelity to real BPMN semantics, and whether the simulated scanning degradations adequately capture real-world document quality variation.
