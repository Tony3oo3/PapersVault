## Problem

Existing emotional intelligence benchmarks for MLLMs suffer from inadequate scenario coverage and inconsistent evaluation protocols, leaving it unclear how well current models generalize across real-world settings, recognize emotional states, and identify the triggering factors behind those emotions. No prior benchmark simultaneously evaluates both recognition accuracy and reasoning quality under a unified, open protocol.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261662/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance pairs a short video clip (averaging >3.3 seconds, drawn exclusively from test sets of 11 public datasets) with a text question that embeds a predefined closed-set of candidate emotion labels, since current MLLMs cannot yet handle fully open-ended emotion queries. The evaluated MLLM produces a free-form answer containing step-by-step reasoning and a final prediction enclosed in <answer> tags. The multi-agent evaluation pipeline then decomposes this answer into individual reasoning steps and scores each step as correct/incorrect, yielding three aggregate metrics: Rec-S (final-prediction accuracy), Rea-S (mean step correctness), and CoT-S (equal-weighted blend of the two).

## Method

MME-Emotion comprises 6,500 QA pairs spanning eight emotional tasks (ER-Lab, ER-Wild, Noise-ER, FG-ER, ML-ER, SA, FG-SA, IR) across 27 scenario types, all converted from existing public datasets into a unified QA format. The evaluation suite is a three-agent pipeline: a Step-LLM (GPT-4.1) partitions the MLLM's answer into discrete reasoning steps without access to the original question or video; an Audio-LLM (Qwen2-Audio) extracts relevant audio cues from the clip; and a Judge-MLLM (GPT-4o) receives the video frames, extracted audio clues, ground-truth emotion labels, and the partitioned steps to rate each step 0 or 1. This divide-and-conquer design circumvents the limitation that no single mainstream MLLM can simultaneously process all modalities. The reliability of the MLLM-as-judge strategy is validated against five human experts on 373 annotated steps, achieving Spearman ρ = 0.953, Cohen's κ = 0.863, and ICC = 0.970, confirming near-perfect agreement with human judgment.

## Results

On MME-Emotion, the best-performing model (Gemini-2.5-Pro) achieves only 39.3% recognition score (Rec-S), 72.7% reasoning score (Rea-S), and 56.0% Chain-of-Thought score (CoT-S). The average across all 20 evaluated MLLMs is 29.4% Rec-S, 49.5% Rea-S, and 39.5% CoT-S, indicating substantial room for improvement. Notably, Audio-Reasoner (7B, audio-only) achieves a competitive 54.8% CoT-S, while emotion-specialist models like Emotion-LLaMA and HumanOmni score near 0% on Rea-S due to their inability to produce explicit reasoning steps.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Gemini-2.5-Pro leads on all three metrics (39.3% Rec-S, 72.7% Rea-S, 56.0% CoT-S), outperforming GPT-4o (27.8/79.8/53.8) on recognition by 11.5 points while GPT-4o edges ahead on reasoning by 7.1 points. Audio-Reasoner (7B, audio-only) achieves 54.8% CoT-S, narrowly trailing Gemini-2.5-Pro and surpassing all open-source vision-language models, demonstrating that audio cues alone can rival full multimodal understanding for emotion tasks. Among specialist models, R1-Omni (0.5B) reaches 42.4% CoT-S through RLVR-based reasoning, comparable to Qwen2.5-VL-7B (46.6%) and far exceeding Emotion-LLaMA (12.8%) and HumanOmni (18.1%), which produce no explicit reasoning (Rea-S ≈ 0%). Surprisingly, Qwen2-VL-72B (72B) scores only 10.5% Rea-S and 20.8% CoT-S, well below its 7B counterpart Qwen2.5-VL-7B (64.8% Rea-S), suggesting that larger parameter count alone does not guarantee better emotional reasoning. Omnimodal models like Qwen2.5-Omni (17.4% Rec-S) underperform their unimodal counterparts, indicating that current multimodal fusion strategies introduce noise rather than complementary emotional signals.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the MME benchmark family (MME, Video-MME, MME-RealWorld) into the affective computing domain, but departs from prior emotion benchmarks (EmotionBench, EmoBench-M, MER-UniBench, MC-EIU) in two key ways: it is the first to jointly evaluate both recognition accuracy and reasoning quality (identifying triggering factors behind emotions), and it introduces a multi-agent LLM-as-judge evaluation framework that eliminates the need for manually annotated reasoning steps. Conceptually, it repurposes existing public video datasets (IEMOCAP, MELD, DEFAW, MAFW, etc.) into a unified QA format for MLLM evaluation, bridging the gap between traditional affective computing benchmarks and the emerging MLLM evaluation paradigm. The evaluation methodology draws on the divide-and-conquer strategy for multimodal processing (separate audio agent + visual frames + judge agent) to work around the limitation that no single MLLM can process all modalities simultaneously.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that even the strongest MLLMs (Gemini-2.5-Pro) achieve only 39.3% recognition and 56.0% CoT scores, establishing a clear ceiling and substantial room for improvement. A practically important open question emerges: why do omnimodal models (processing audio+visual+text) consistently underperform their unimodal counterparts, suggesting that current multimodal fusion strategies are inadequate for emotional cues. The finding that small specialist models (R1-Omni, 0.5B) can rival large generalist models through emotion-specific post-training opens a cost-effective research direction. Limitations include the absence of task difficulty stratification and multilingual analysis, both flagged by the authors as future work.
