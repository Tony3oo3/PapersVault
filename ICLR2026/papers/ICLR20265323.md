## Problem

Existing Frank-Wolfe algorithms exploit function smoothness only through a quadratic upper bound to determine step sizes (short steps), maximizing guaranteed primal descent but not necessarily improving the computable primal-dual gap that serves as a stopping criterion. Moreover, no prior FW variant leverages the optimism principle from online learning—predicting the next gradient to form a better lower model—despite the fact that for L-smooth functions the previous gradient is a natural and accurate predictor. This paper addresses both gaps by introducing an optimistic FW algorithm and a new class of primal-dual short steps that directly optimize the primal-dual gap.

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes a convex L-smooth function via a (possibly stochastic, unbiased, finite-variance) gradient oracle, a compact convex set via a linear minimization oracle, an initial point x_0, and a convex subdifferentiable regularizer ψ (which can be the indicator of the feasible set). It outputs an approximate minimizer x_T together with a computable primal-dual gap G_t at every iteration, which upper-bounds the true suboptimality f(x_t) − f(x*) and thus provides a practical, certifiable stopping criterion without requiring knowledge of f(x*). The gap is defined as f(x_{t+1}) − L_t where L_t is a weighted lower bound on f(x*) constructed from accumulated gradient information.

## Method

The core algorithm (Optimistic FW) is built on an optimistic FTRL or optimistic OMD scheme from online learning: at each iteration it uses the previous gradient g_t as a hint (prediction) for the next gradient, and solves an LMO on a regularized linear model that incorporates this hint, yielding a vertex v_t that better anticipates the true descent direction. The iterate is a weighted average x_t = (A_{t-1}/A_t) y_{t-1} + (a_t/A_t) v_t with a_t = 2t, and the analysis via regret bounds yields the rate E[f(x_t) − f(x*)] ≤ O(LD²/(t+1)) plus a vanishing regularizer term. Separately, the paper introduces primal-dual short steps: instead of maximizing the guaranteed primal descent along the segment [x_t, v_t] (classical short step), the step size γ_t is chosen to minimize the convex upper bound (1−γ_t)G_{t-1} + γ_t²(L/2)‖v_t − x_t‖² on the primal-dual gap, giving γ_t = min{1, G_{t-1}/(L‖v_t − x_t‖²)}. This step-size rule is shown to preserve the optimal O(LD²/t) rate, admits an efficient line-search variant, and extends naturally to gradient descent where the step a_t is optimized by solving a cubic equation.

## Results

The paper presents convergence results via log-log plots (slope = polynomial order of convergence) rather than a single numerical table. The optimistic FW variant (OFTRL) achieves a steeper convergence slope than all baselines on both the probability simplex (f(x)=‖x−x₀‖₂²) and the k-sparse polytope (k=10, f(x)=‖Ax−b‖₂²), converging faster in both iteration count and wall-clock time. The theoretical guarantee for the optimistic variant is E[f(x_t)−f(x*)] ≤ O(ψ(x*)−ψ(x₁))/(t(t+1)) + 4LD²/(t+1) + √2σD/2, matching the optimal O(LD²/t) rate. The primal-dual short step, while theoretically sound, did not empirically outperform standard short steps or line search in practice.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The optimistic FW (OFTRL) significantly outperforms the heavy-ball FW variant (Li et al., 2021) in convergence rate on both tested polytopes, with a visibly steeper slope in log-log plots. It also outperforms vanilla FW with the open-loop step size γ_t = 2/(t+2) (Frank & Wolfe, 1956) in both iterations and time. Against FW with the adaptive line search from Pokutta (2024) (a numerically improved version of Pedregosa et al., 2020), the optimistic variant often but not always achieves a better convergence order; the line search variant is subject to the Guelat–Marcotte lower bound that does not apply to the optimistic or heavy-ball variants. The authors ablate the source of improvement and confirm that optimism (using the previous gradient as a hint for the next) is the key driver, not the heavy-ball lower bound: applying the HB lower bound to vanilla FW actually yields a weaker gap than the standard FW gap in their tests. The proposed primal-dual short step (Section 4) behaves similarly to standard short steps or line search without outperforming them, likely because the tighter primal-dual gap leads to smaller step sizes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two well-developed threads: primal-dual analyses of Frank-Wolfe (FW) algorithms (Jaggi 2013, Nesterov 2018, Li et al. 2021 for heavy-ball FW) and optimism in online learning (Rakhlin & Sridharan 2013, Cutkosky 2019). It departs from prior FW analyses, which uniformly exploit L-smoothness via a quadratic upper bound to guarantee primal descent, by importing the optimistic online-learning framework—predicting the next gradient to minimize a regularized lower model—into the FW setting via an online-to-batch conversion. The proposed primal-dual short steps generalize the classical short-step strategy (Frank & Wolfe 1956; Pedregosa et al. 2020) by optimizing a computable primal-dual gap rather than raw primal progress, and the authors show the same principle extends to gradient descent, a connection not previously drawn in the FW literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The optimistic FW algorithm provides a theoretically grounded, empirically validated improvement over standard FW, heavy-ball FW, and adaptive line-search variants, with the added benefit of a computable primal-dual gap as a practical stopping criterion. A notable negative result is that the proposed primal-dual short steps do not outperform standard short steps in practice, suggesting the gap-based step-size selection is often smaller and thus slower—echoing the classical lower bounds of Guélat & Marcotte (1986). Open questions include whether the optimistic framework can be combined with local acceleration for strongly convex objectives, and whether the empirical gains persist in high-dimensional or non-smooth settings beyond the polytopal benchmarks tested here.
