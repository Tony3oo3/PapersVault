## Problem

Computing next-token likelihood ratios between two language models is essential for knowledge distillation, reinforcement learning, and preference optimization, but requires both models to share the same tokenizer. When the teacher and student use different vocabularies—common in edge deployment where smaller vocabularies reduce memory—existing methods resort to auxiliary losses (Wasserstein distance, sequence alignment) that introduce extra hyperparameters and deviate from standard KL divergence minimization. This paper asks whether exact cross-tokenizer sequence likelihoods can be computed directly from the BPE tokenization structure without such workarounds.

## Input / Output

The method takes a language model P trained on vocabulary V_α and a token sequence encoded under a different BPE vocabulary V_β, and outputs the probability that a sample from P, when decoded to its underlying byte sequence and re-encoded under V_β, begins with the given prefix. In the subset regime (V_β ⊆ V_α), the full next-token distribution over V_β is available in O(1) model evaluations per step, enabling sequential autoregressive sampling. In the general case, only individual token probabilities are computed (approximately 0.5 s per token via beam search), which constrains the distillation loss to a partial KL objective over a small set of scored tokens rather than the full vocabulary distribution.

## Method

The core insight is that BPE vocabularies have a sequential construction where each step adds exactly one merged token, making any prefix vocabulary V_{M'} a 'relative alphabet' of V_M. For full-to-subset conversion, the paper introduces relative cover encodings (generalizing cover encodings from Phan et al., 2025) and shows the subset-vocabulary probability equals the sum of model probabilities over all relative cover encodings, yielding O(1) next-token sampling. For the general cross-tokenizer case, a lossless recursive algorithm (Algorithm 1) steps through adjacent vocabularies, branching only when the last token of the encoding is the left component of the newly introduced merge; this is exact but worst-case exponential. A practical beam-search approximation restricts the correction sum to high-probability continuations up to the next whitespace or EOS, reducing cost to roughly 6 beams of length 10. For distillation, the paper uses a partial KL divergence loss that accounts for unexamined probability mass, combined with an SFT term, and demonstrates up to 12% memory reduction and 4% accuracy gains on vocabulary trimming, and 2%+ improvement over state-of-the-art on GSM8K cross-tokenizer distillation.

## Results

On GSM8K cross-tokenizer distillation (Qwen2.5-Math-7B → Gemma2-2B), the proposed Partial KL (PKL) method achieves 54.6% 5-shot accuracy, and SFT+PKL reaches 55.6%, outperforming the strongest baseline ALM+SFT (53.5%) by 2.1 points. For vocabulary trimming of Qwen2.5-1.5B, the Full-to-Subset conversion (FKL) with 32k vocabulary achieves 63.0% on GSM8K (vs. 54.0% SFT) while reducing LM head memory by 12% (from 445MB to 91MB). On HumanEval (0-shot) with 32k vocab, FKL reaches 47.6% pass@1 versus 46.9% SFT, and on MBPP (3-shot) it reaches 40.6% versus 38.8% SFT.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against three cross-tokenizer distillation baselines: ALM (Minixhofer et al., 2025), ULD (Boizard et al., 2025), and DSKD (Zhang et al., 2024). On GSM8K, SFT+PKL (55.6%) outperforms ALM+SFT (53.5%) by 2.1 points, DSKD+SFT (52.8%) by 2.8 points, and ULD+SFT (48.2%) by 7.4 points. In the vocabulary trimming setting (Table 3), FKL consistently dominates all baselines across GSM8K, HumanEval, and MBPP at every vocabulary size (16k, 32k, 64k); for example, at 32k vocab on GSM8K, FKL achieves 63.0% versus ALM 57.1%, ULD 53.5%, and DSKD 54.4%. The paper notes that its method strictly generalizes ALM: it recovers ALM in the limit of chunk size 1 with perfect debiasing, while additionally leveraging probabilities of non-ground-truth tokens. On the DialogSum summarization task (Table 4), SFT+PKL (33.9) also edges out ALM+SFT (33.1) and ULD+SFT, though the margin is smaller. Overall, the proposed method wins on every reported benchmark and vocabulary size against all three baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly generalizes the byte-level conversion framework of Phan et al. (2025), which could only convert a tokenized LM to its byte-level (alphabet) equivalent, to the setting of arbitrary BPE vocabulary pairs via the novel 'relative alphabet' and 'relative cover encoding' constructs. It departs from prior cross-tokenizer distillation methods (ULD, ALM, DSKD) that introduce auxiliary losses such as Wasserstein distance in logit space or sequence alignment, instead recovering the standard KL-divergence objective by computing exact sequence likelihoods under the target tokenizer. The recursive structure of BPE merges is the key technical insight that makes the general-case conversion tractable in principle, though the lossless algorithm remains exponential in the worst case.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The O(1) next-token sampling for the subset-vocabulary case makes vocabulary trimming for edge deployment practically viable, yielding up to 12% memory reduction while improving task performance. For the general cross-tokenizer setting, the beam-search approximation (≈0.5s per token) is a meaningful step toward principled distillation without sequence alignment, though it remains significantly slower than same-tokenizer distillation. Open questions include reducing the computational cost of the general-case algorithm, extending to on-policy distillation and cross-tokenizer preference optimization, and whether the whitespace/EOS stopping assumption in the approximation holds robustly across languages and domains.
