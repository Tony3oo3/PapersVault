## Problem

Current LLM-based information-seeking agents suffer from low search efficiency, with only ~4% of actions being valid. The paper identifies the root cause as sparsity of target entities in synthetic training tasks, which limits the agent's exposure to informative cues and biases the measurement of search efficiency (ISE), obstructing systematic learning of efficient search behaviors.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264516/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The agent receives a natural language question and operates in a ReAct loop over a live web environment, issuing Search (Google queries with optional year filter) and Visit (URL retrieval with goal specification) actions at each step. The output is not a single answer but a set of target entities R that includes both final explicit answers and all intermediate entities serving as reasoning stepping stones. The agent's trajectory is a sequence of (thought, action, observation) tuples, and performance is quantified by two metrics: Information-Seeking Rate (fraction of R retrieved) and Information-Seeking Efficiency (entities discovered per action step).

## Method

WebLeaper formulates information-seeking as a tree-structured reasoning problem over curated Wikipedia tables (~2M tables, multi-stage cleaned), where the root is the question entity, second-layer nodes are key entities (e.g., author names), and third-layer nodes are their attributes. Three synthesis variants progressively increase complexity: Basic builds a single-source tree; Union uses Maximal Biclique Enumeration on a bipartite graph of trees and shared relations to merge multi-source trees; Reverse-Union adds deductive fuzz by replacing the anchor entity with descriptive third-layer clues, forcing multi-step reasoning before the main search. For trajectory construction, the agent (Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking-2507) generates ReAct trajectories, which are filtered by dual thresholds on ISR (>α=0.3) and ISE (>β=0.1), retaining only trajectories that are simultaneously accurate and efficient. The final model is trained via SFT (~15k samples, 64 H20 GPUs, 6–8 hours), with an optional GRPO reinforcement learning stage that further improves performance using a soft F-score reward combining recall and precision.

## Results

WebLeaper-Reverse-Union achieves 23.0 on BrowseComp, 67.0 on GAIA, 66.0 on xbench-DeepSearch, 37.2 on Seal-0, and 4.0 SR / 25.8 Row F1 / 40.8 Item F1 on WideSearch, setting new state-of-the-art among open-source agents across all five benchmarks. On Seal-0, it surpasses OpenAI-o3 (37.2 vs. 18.9) and on xbench-DS it nearly matches OpenAI-o3 (66.0 vs. 66.7). The Union variant achieves 22.1 on BrowseComp and 69.9 on GAIA, also leading all open-source baselines. Ablations confirm that Reverse-Union data yields the strongest overall gains (+4.34 average over the WebSailor-V2-5k baseline), while the Basic variant actually degrades performance (−7.64 average) due to overly simple task structure.

## Baselines & Comparisons

WebLeaper-Reverse-Union outperforms all open-source baselines on every benchmark where they overlap: it leads Kimi-K2-Instruct-1T by +8.9 on BrowseComp (23.0 vs. 14.1), +9.3 on GAIA (67.0 vs. 57.7), +16.0 on xbench-DS (66.0 vs. 50.0), and +2.9 SR on WideSearch (4.0 vs. 1.1). Against the strongest open-source competitor WebSailor-32B, WebLeaper gains +12.5 on BrowseComp, +13.8 on GAIA, +12.7 on xbench-DS, +15.9 on Seal-0, and +40.3 Item F1 on WideSearch. Against proprietary agents, WebLeaper surpasses Claude-4-Sonnet on BrowseComp (23.0 vs. 12.2), xbench-DS (66.0 vs. 64.6), and WideSearch SR (4.0 vs. 2.3), but trails OpenAI-o3 on BrowseComp (23.0 vs. 49.7) and GAIA (67.0 vs. 70.5). On Seal-0, WebLeaper (37.2) more than doubles OpenAI-o3's score (18.9), making it the only benchmark where it clearly beats a proprietary agent. The Union variant achieves the best GAIA score among open-source models (69.9), narrowly trailing OpenAI-o3 (70.5) by only 0.6 points.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ReAct-based information-seeking agent paradigm (Yao et al., 2023) and the synthetic-data training lineage established by WebSailor/WebSailor-V2 (Li et al., 2025c,b) and WebDancer (Wu et al., 2025a). While prior work has focused on increasing search depth through multi-hop reasoning and long-horizon planning (WebResearcher, Qiao et al., 2025; WebShaper, Tao et al., 2025), WebLeaper deliberately shifts the emphasis to search efficiency by reformulating IS tasks as tree-structured reasoning problems with high entity density. The three synthesis variants (Basic, Union, Reverse-Union) and the Maximal Biclique Enumeration algorithm for discovering compatible reasoning trees represent a novel data-construction methodology that departs from the one-entity-at-a-time collection strategy used in prior IS agent training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies and addresses a concrete gap in IS agent training: the sparsity of target entities in synthetic tasks leads to inefficient search behaviors that are hard to correct. The ISR/ISE dual-metric trajectory filtering provides a principled, theoretically grounded (Var(ISE)=O(1/n)) mechanism for selecting high-quality training trajectories, which is broadly applicable beyond this specific framework. Limitations include reliance on Wikipedia tables for data synthesis (raising questions about domain transfer), the modest scale of training data (~15k samples), and the fact that the RL experiments (Appendix A.3) use a different reward formulation and are not fully integrated into the main pipeline. Open questions include whether the entity-centric formulation generalizes to non-entity-centric IS tasks (e.g., procedural or causal reasoning) and whether the efficiency gains persist as base models scale further.
