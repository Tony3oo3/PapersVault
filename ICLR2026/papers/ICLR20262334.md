## Problem

Current intervention-based model steering methods adapt strong fine-tuning objectives (language modeling or preference optimization) that are susceptible to overfitting and often produce unnatural or unstable outputs. The paper hypothesizes that effective steering requires faithfully identifying and manipulating internal concept features inherent in the model, rather than enforcing external preferences through steering vectors, motivating a shift from probability maximization to a weaker but more faithful distribution-matching signal.

## Input / Output

CDAS takes contrastive training pairs of concept-neutral (x, y) and concept-eliciting (x^c, y^c) instruction-response pairs and learns a single rank-1 steering vector. At inference, given a text instruction and a steering factor derived from the model's own latent distribution (the mean projection of concept-eliciting instructions onto the steering vector, optionally scaled by β), it applies clamping interventions to residual stream representations at a chosen layer, producing a steered response that either incorporates or suppresses the target concept. The same steering vector supports both directions of control, distinguishing it from uni-directional fine-tuning.

## Method

CDAS introduces two key innovations. First, it replaces probability maximization or preference ranking with a Jensen-Shannon divergence (JSD) distribution matching objective that requires the intervened output distribution (under a base input with DII applied from a counterfactual source) to match the model's own un-intervened output distribution under that source input, computed per-token across the full vocabulary. Second, it adopts Distributed Interchange Intervention (DII) from Distributed Alignment Search, which clamps the steering-vector subspace to values induced by counterfactual source inputs, naturally enabling bi-directional steering and implicitly sampling steering factors from the model's natural factor distribution without manual hyperparameter design. Training alternates between a positive objective (concept steering: matching the intervened distribution under base x to the distribution under source x^c) and a negative objective (concept suppression: the reverse), both averaged over response tokens. At inference, clamping interventions with factor a = β·ᾱ^c are applied to all token positions, where ᾱ^c is the mean latent of concept-eliciting instructions.

## Results

On AXBENCH (steering score 0–2), CDAS with tuned factors achieves 0.992 on Gemma-2-9B at layer 20, outperforming RePS (0.892), Lang. (0.788), and LoReFT (0.777), but underperforms RePS on Gemma-2-2B (0.631 vs. 0.756 at L10) and at layer 31 of the 9B model (0.518 vs. 0.624). In the refusal-override case study, CDAS achieves 91% suppression score on Llama-3.1-8B and 84% on Llama-3.1-70B without factor tuning, while preserving MMLU accuracy (−0.01% and +0.05% change) and achieving the lowest KL divergence (4.26 and 3.72) among all methods. In the CoT-backdoor case study, CDAS at layer 16 reduces attack success rate to 0.58% on unseen red-teaming instructions and 6.68% on the true trigger, with only marginal benchmark degradation (+2.63% tinyMMLU, −3.00% tinyARC).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against RePS (the strongest PO baseline), CDAS wins on Gemma-2-9B at layer 20 (0.992 vs. 0.892) but loses on Gemma-2-2B at layer 10 (0.631 vs. 0.756) and on 9B at layer 31 (0.518 vs. 0.624); in the refusal case study, CDAS outperforms RePS on Llama-3.1-8B (91% vs. 80% suppression) and 70B (84% vs. 75%) while RePS wins on the smaller Phi-3.5-mini (84% vs. 30%), and RePS degrades MMLU by up to −35.57% whereas CDAS stays within ±2.57%. Against Lang. (language-modeling objective), CDAS wins on 9B L20 (0.992 vs. 0.788) and 2B L20 (0.608 vs. 0.568) but loses on 2B L10 (0.631 vs. 0.663) and 9B L31 (0.518 vs. 0.580). Against BiPO and DIM, CDAS dominates across all AXBENCH settings (e.g., 0.631 vs. 0.199/0.297 on 2B L10) and in both case studies (e.g., 91% vs. 1% suppression vs. DAS, 91% vs. 16% vs. DIM on Llama-3.1-8B). Against LoReFT (fine-tuning baseline), CDAS surpasses it on 9B L20 (0.992 vs. 0.777) but trails on 2B L10 (0.631 vs. 0.701), 2B L20 (0.608 vs. 0.722), and 9B L31 (0.518 vs. 0.764), indicating that CDAS's advantage is most pronounced at intermediate layers of larger models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Distributed Alignment Search (DAS, Geiger et al. 2024) by adopting its Distributed Interchange Intervention (DII) protocol, but repurposes it from causal variable localization to intervention-based model steering. It departs from the dominant line of optimization-based steering methods (Lang., BiPO, RePS) that adapt fine-tuning objectives, instead introducing a JSD-based distribution-matching training objective that treats the model's own counterfactual output distribution as a self-supervised 'teacher.' Conceptually, the paper positions steering as a mechanistic interpretability problem—identifying and manipulating causal concept features—rather than as parameter-efficient fine-tuning, drawing on the broader distribution-matching literature from GANs, knowledge distillation, and RL.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in safety-critical applications: CDAS achieves systematic refusal override and backdoor neutralization while preserving general model utility (minimal KL divergence, near-zero benchmark degradation), whereas strong-supervised PO methods like RePS cause substantial performance collapse (e.g., −27% on TruthfulQA). Key limitations include stricter contrastive data requirements, the need for factor tuning in general-purpose steering, restriction to rank-1 vectors, and the absence of rigorous causal-theoretic grounding. Open questions include whether the distribution-matching objective extends to higher-rank interventions (LoRA, LoReFT), whether entirely tuning-free inference is achievable, and how CDAS performs on concepts that resist natural-language verbalization.
