## Problem

Certifying the nonnegativity of polynomials via the Sum-of-Squares (SOS) condition requires solving a Semidefinite Program whose dimensionality grows quadratically in the size of the chosen monomial basis. Existing rule-based basis-reduction methods (e.g., Newton polytope with diagonal consistency) leave substantial redundancy, and no learning-augmented algorithm had been proposed to predict near-minimal monomial bases while preserving correctness guarantees.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260588/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The input is a polynomial tokenized into a sequence where each monomial is represented by its coefficient (prefixed 'C') followed by per-variable exponents (prefixed 'E'), with monomials separated by '+'; monomial-embeddings compress these tokens into fixed-length vectors to reduce sequence length. The Transformer autoregressively generates a sequence of monomials (separated by SEP tokens) constituting the predicted compact basis, which is then fed into an SDP solver to produce a PSD Gram matrix Q or an infeasibility certificate. The full pipeline outputs either a valid SOS decomposition or a certified non-SOS result after exhaustive expansion to the full Newton polytope.

## Method

The approach is a three-stage learning-augmented pipeline: (i) an encoder-decoder Transformer (6 layers, dim 512, 8 heads) predicts a compact monomial basis from the tokenized polynomial, trained on over 100 million SOS polynomials generated via reverse sampling (sample a basis B, draw a PSD matrix Q, compute p = z_B^T Q z_B); (ii) a greedy coverage-repair algorithm augments the predicted basis until the necessary condition S(p) ⊆ B·B is satisfied; (iii) if the SDP is infeasible, the basis is expanded iteratively using a permutation-based scoring function that runs the Transformer on L random variable permutations and ranks candidate monomials by their frequency across predictions, following a geometric expansion schedule with factor ρ. Theoretical analysis shows the worst-case computational cost exceeds the standard Newton-polytope baseline by at most a constant factor, and the expected cost scales as Θ(η^ω) where η is the coverage rank, providing a formal link between prediction quality and solver efficiency.

## Results

On the main benchmark (Table 1), the proposed method achieves speedups of 0.7×–392× over the Newton polytope baseline across four polynomial structures (dense, sparse, low-rank, block-diagonal) with 4–8 variables and degrees 6–20. For the largest tested configuration (6 variables, degree 20, 60 monomials), solve times drop from 1,331–1,630 s (Newton) to 7.4–39.2 s (Ours), while the full-basis approach times out entirely. On large-scale instances (Table 2), the method solves 8-variable degree-20 problems in 1.46 s versus 2,675–3,453 s for state-of-the-art solvers, and handles 100-variable degree-10 and 6-variable degree-40 instances in under 60 s where all baselines fail with OOM or timeout.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Newton polytope baseline (Lofberg, 2009), the proposed method wins on all but the smallest instances (n=4, d=6), where it is marginally slower (0.7×–0.88×) due to Transformer inference overhead; speedups grow with problem size, reaching 89–217× for 6-variable degree-20 problems and 91–292× for 8-variable degree-20 problems. The full-basis approach times out on all instances with more than 6 variables or degree >12, making it non-competitive at scale. Against state-of-the-art solvers on large configurations (Table 2), the method is 21× faster than SumOfSquares.jl (Weisser et al., 2019) and 15× faster than TSSOS (Wang et al., 2021b) on 6-variable degree-20 problems, and over 2,000× faster than both TSSOS and Chordal-TSSOS (Wang et al., 2021a) on 8-variable degree-20 problems (1.46 s vs. 2,675–3,453 s). On the most challenging instances (6 vars/deg 40 and 100 vars/deg 10), all three external solvers fail with out-of-memory errors or timeouts, while the proposed method completes in 42.8 s and 18.3 s respectively.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the polynomial tokenization and sequence-generation techniques from Kera et al. (2025b) (Transformer oracles for border basis algorithms) to the distinct task of predicting compact monomial bases for SOS certification. It positions itself within the learning-augmented algorithms framework (Roughgarden, 2021), applying the predict-then-verify paradigm to a problem where the computational bottleneck is basis selection rather than the optimization itself. The approach departs from both rule-based basis reduction (Newton polytope pruning, diagonal consistency) and from recent LLM-based direct SOS reasoning (Li et al., 2025), instead using a learned predictor upstream of a verified SDP pipeline with a systematic repair mechanism that preserves correctness guarantees.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 100×+ speedups and the ability to solve 100-variable instances where all state-of-the-art solvers hit OOM or timeouts represent a practical breakthrough for SOS programming in control, robotics, and non-convex optimization. The clean theoretical guarantee (worst-case constant-factor overhead over the standard Newton polytope approach) makes the method safe to deploy. Key limitations include reliance on synthetic benchmarks (no widely available real-world SOS benchmarks exist), sensitivity to non-canonical polynomial representations, and scalability constraints from the Transformer's context length beyond ~100 variables. Open questions include whether deterministic test-time canonicalization can address representation sensitivity, and whether the approach extends to constrained SOS problems or higher-degree settings.
