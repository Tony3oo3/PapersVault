## Problem

Existing dynamical similarity methods such as DSA compare only the intrinsic (autonomous) dynamics of two systems, ignoring how external inputs shape their behavior. Because most real-world systems—neural circuits, RNNs, physical processes—are non-autonomous and driven by inputs, two systems with similar intrinsic dynamics but different input couplings can be misclassified as equivalent. This paper addresses the gap by introducing a framework that disentangles and jointly compares both intrinsic and input-driven dynamics from data alone.

## Input / Output

The method consumes paired time series of system states and input signals from two systems, along with a user-specified nonlinear embedding basis (delay embedding, kernel, or other lifting function). It operates on partially observed data (only a subset of state dimensions need be recorded) and tolerates noisy or surrogate inputs, requiring only that the surrogate be sufficiently correlated with the true input. Outputs include three decomposed similarity scores (state, input, and joint controllability), the estimated linear operators A and B in the lifted space, and the controllability Gramian, all computed in O(1 ms) per pair once the operators are fitted.

## Method

InputDSA extends DSA by estimating both the state-transition operator A and the input-mapping operator B via a novel variant called SubspaceDMDc, which combines DMD with control (DMDc) and subspace identification (N4SID/PO-MOESP) on nonlinearly lifted states. The key insight is that in partially observed systems, input affects observed states both instantaneously and through unobserved latent states, biasing naive DMDc estimates of B toward intrinsic dynamics; subspace identification corrects this by projecting future states onto the past input-and-state subspace before regression, analogous to instrumental variable estimation. The resulting similarity metric (Eq. 8) compares the controllability matrices K = [B, AB, A²B, …] of the two systems via orthogonal Procrustes alignment, yielding a closed-form solution for the optimal coordinate transform C that is orders of magnitude faster than the iterative optimization required by DSA. After solving for C*, the method decomposes the total distance into a state score (comparing C*A₁C*ᵀ vs A₂) and an input score (comparing C*B₁ vs B₂), enabling separate or joint assessment of intrinsic versus input-driven dynamical similarity.

## Results

On partially observed random RNNs (20-dim state, 2-dim observation, 5000 timesteps), SubspaceDMDc achieves a state-similarity silhouette score of 0.94 versus 0.68 for DMDc and 0.6 for standard DMD (Hankel DMD), and an input-similarity silhouette of 0.83 versus 0.19 for DMDc (which DSA cannot compute at all). InputDSA distances remain robust to input perturbations, with correlations above r > 0.75 even as the signal-to-error ratio (SER) approaches 1. On the Luo et al. (2025) rat auditory evidence-accumulation dataset, InputDSA identifies a significant regime shift at the neural time of commitment (nTc): the median percent change in controllability energy is −47.63% (SE 15.15%, p < 0.001, Mann-Whitney U-test), and the power-law exponent β of the state-transition eigenspectrum decreases post-commitment, indicating a transition from input-driven to intrinsically dominated dynamics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

InputDSA (SubspaceDMDc) outperforms both baselines on every metric where they overlap. Against DSA (Ostrow et al., 2023), which uses delay-embedded Hankel DMD, SubspaceDMDc improves the state-similarity silhouette from 0.60 to 0.94 and additionally provides an input-similarity score that DSA cannot compute at all, since DSA is designed only for autonomous systems. Against DMDc (Proctor et al., 2016a), SubspaceDMDc improves state similarity from 0.68 to 0.94 and input similarity from 0.19 to 0.83; the DMDc input score is essentially at chance level because partial observation biases the B estimate toward intrinsic dynamics. In the rat neural-population application (Section 4.2), DMDc 'did not reveal similar results, almost certainly due to partial observation,' whereas SubspaceDMDc clearly identifies the pre-/post-nTc regime shift in both intrinsic and input-driven dynamics. In the Plume Tracking RL application, DMDc identifies similar structure in input-driven dynamics but fails to capture the intrinsic-dynamics differences that SubspaceDMDc reveals (Appendix Fig. 17).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Dynamical Similarity Analysis (DSA, Ostrow et al. 2023), which compares autonomous dynamical systems via Koopman-operator-based linear embeddings, to the non-autonomous (input-driven) setting. It introduces SubspaceDMDc, a novel estimator that combines DMD with Control (Proctor et al., 2016a) with subspace identification (N4SID, Van Overschee & De Moor 1994) to handle partial observation—a failure mode absent from prior DMDc formulations. The resulting InputDSA metric leverages the controllability matrix to define a joint state-and-input similarity that admits a closed-form Procrustes solution, in contrast to the iterative optimization required by the original DSA. Conceptually, the work bridges control-theoretic system identification with the Koopman/DMD framework for dynamical similarity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Most biologically and computationally relevant dynamical systems (neural circuits, RL agents) are driven by external inputs, yet prior dynamical similarity methods ignored this, potentially conflating input effects with intrinsic structure. InputDSA provides a principled way to disentangle and separately quantify intrinsic vs. input-driven similarity, with demonstrated robustness to noisy or surrogate inputs that validates common neuroscience practice of using behavioral proxies. Key limitations include the assumption of additive (not multiplicative) input, sensitivity to persistent excitation of the input signal, and computational cost of SubspaceDMDc fitting for large systems. Open questions include extension to closed-loop settings where input depends on state, and whether the method generalizes to multiplicative or modulatory inputs common in neuromodulation.
