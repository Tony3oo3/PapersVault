## Problem

Prior work on LLMs in poker has focused narrowly on win rates without diagnosing why models fail at game-theoretic reasoning. This paper identifies three systematic reasoning flaws—heuristic bias, factual misunderstanding (e.g., misjudging hand equity), and a knowing-doing gap where actions diverge from articulated reasoning—and shows that internal policy improvements via behavior cloning and regret-inspired RL improve reasoning style but remain insufficient for GTO-consistent play or competitive gameplay against equilibrium solvers like CFR+.

## Input / Output

The model receives a natural-language rendering of the partial poker observation (public community cards, private hole cards, betting history) via a structured prompt template, and during rollout it issues a single unified tool query that returns the CFR solver's GTO action along with auxiliary quantities (equity, pot odds, range distributions) in one response. The output is a structured reasoning trace using special tags—think for internal reasoning, tool for the solver query, output for solver results, and answer for the final action—designed to mirror the explanatory style of professional players who systematically evaluate ranges, equity, and mixed strategies before acting.

## Method

ToolPoker is a two-stage tool-integrated reasoning (TIR) framework. In the BC stage, a compact ~5k-sample expert dataset (actions from CFR+, reasoning traces from GPT-5-mini) is programmatically augmented with standardized tool invocation templates and solver execution outputs, teaching the model when and how to call external tools without expensive manual annotation. In the RL stage, PPO optimizes a composite reward combining answer accuracy against the CFR solver's GTO action, a format reward enforcing the structured tag schema, and a tool-execution reward measuring the fraction of successful solver calls. A key design choice is a unified tool interface that consolidates multiple solvers (CFR for actions, equity calculator for hand values) into a single API, reducing multi-tool dependency and stabilizing training. The model is trained with VeRL using Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct as the base, with a 400k-sample action-only dataset for RL rollouts and a masking function that excludes solver output tokens from the loss to prevent the model from memorizing tool responses.

## Results

ToolPoker (Qwen2.5-7B) achieves state-of-the-art gameplay among LLM-based methods, gaining +6.8 chips on average in Leduc Hold'em and +45.0 in Limit Texas Hold'em against traditional baselines. Against the equilibrium solvers, ToolPoker nearly matches CFR+ (−3.0 chips in Leduc) and DeepCFR (−5.0 chips in Limit), while decisively outperforming non-equilibrium baselines (e.g., +60.5 vs. NFSP, +63.0 vs. DQN in Limit Texas Hold'em). On reasoning quality (LLM-as-a-Judge, 0–2 scale), ToolPoker reaches near-professional scores of ~1.93 HR, ~1.92 FA, and ~1.94 AC in Limit Texas Hold'em, substantially exceeding the best vanilla LLM (o4-mini: 1.57/1.65/1.88) and the internal-policy BC-RIRL variant (1.88/1.12/1.87).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ToolPoker decisively outperforms all non-equilibrium RL baselines: it gains +60.5, +63.0, and +61.5 chips against NFSP, DQN, and DMC in Limit Texas Hold'em, whereas the internal-policy BC-RIRL variant loses −77.5, −82.5, and −80.5 chips to the same opponents. Against the equilibrium solvers, ToolPoker nearly matches CFR+ (−3.0 chips in Leduc) and DeepCFR (−5.0 chips in Limit), confirming that the external solver integration recovers GTO-consistent play. Among vanilla LLMs, ToolPoker surpasses o4-mini (−42.2 avg in Limit) by 87.2 chips, Qwen2.5-72B (−44.6) by 89.6 chips, and GPT-4.1-mini (−46.7) by 91.7 chips in Limit Texas Hold'em. In Leduc Hold'em, ToolPoker also beats o4-mini (−2.6 avg) by 9.4 chips and Qwen2.5-72B (−9.1) by 15.9 chips. On reasoning quality, ToolPoker's FA score (~1.92) far exceeds BC-RIRL (1.12) and o4-mini (1.65), demonstrating that solver-grounded reasoning eliminates the factual misunderstandings that plague purely internal policies.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends two separate research threads: (1) LLM evaluation in game-theoretic settings (GTBench, PokerBench, Suspicion-Agent), which previously focused on gameplay outcomes without analyzing reasoning traces, and (2) tool-integrated reasoning (TIR) for LLMs (ReTool, Search-R1, ToRA), which has been applied only to fully observed, single-agent tasks like math and web search. The key departure is positioning TIR for imperfect-information games, where tools must compute equilibrium-consistent strategies and counterfactual values rather than deterministic answers. Methodologically, the RL training pipeline builds directly on ReTool's approach of integrating external tools into PPO-based fine-tuning, while the regret-inspired reward design draws from CFR's theoretical foundations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic diagnosis of *why* LLMs fail at poker (heuristic bias, factual misunderstanding, knowing-doing gap) rather than merely showing they lose, and demonstrates that internal RL fine-tuning—even with step-level regret rewards—cannot fix factual inaccuracies, a useful negative result for the broader LLM-reasoning literature. ToolPoker's near-equilibrium performance with a 7B model shows that tool-augmented LLMs can serve as interpretable wrappers around solvers, which has implications for high-stakes domains requiring both optimal decisions and auditable reasoning. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to multi-player or no-limit settings, how performance degrades under intermittent tool access, and whether the LLM component adds value beyond a simple API call to the solver.
