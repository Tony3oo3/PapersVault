## Problem

3D Gaussian Splatting's adaptive density control relies solely on view-space positional gradient norms, ignoring the directional information embedded in those norms. This causes two failure modes: large Gaussians with conflicting sub-gradient directions fail to meet splitting thresholds (over-reconstruction, manifesting as local blur), while Gaussians with consistently aligned gradients undergo unbounded densification (over-densification, inflating memory with redundant primitives). Existing fixes like AbsGS resolve one issue by forcing positive gradients but exacerbate the other.

## Input / Output

The method consumes the same inputs as standard 3DGS: a set of calibrated monocular RGB images and an SfM-initialized sparse point cloud. During training, it additionally accumulates per-pixel 2D sub-gradient vectors for each visible Gaussian across all training views to compute the Gradient Coherence Ratio. The output is a compact 3D Gaussian scene (position, covariance, opacity, SH coefficients per primitive) that renders novel views in real time, typically using 20–50% fewer Gaussians than 3DGS while matching or exceeding its rendering quality.

## Method

GDAGS introduces the Gradient Coherence Ratio (GCR), defined as the ratio of the norm of the summed per-pixel sub-gradients to the sum of their individual norms, which isolates directional consistency from magnitude (bounded in [0,1] by Cauchy-Schwarz). A nonlinear dynamic weight w_i = α + β(1−C_i)^p is then applied to each Gaussian's gradient norm before threshold comparison: during splitting, low-GCR (conflicting-direction) Gaussians receive amplified weights and are prioritized for fragmentation, while high-GCR Gaussians are suppressed; during cloning, the inverse weight 1/w_i is used to promote directionally consistent small Gaussians while suppressing chaotic local accumulation. The power-law form was chosen over an exponential alternative for lower computational cost (torch.pow vs. torch.exp) and a sensitivity profile that sharply penalizes inconsistent directions while smoothly maintaining suppression for consistent ones. A single hyperparameter p (default 15) controls the effective number of Gaussians selected for densification, trading off rendering quality against memory footprint.

## Results

GDAGS achieves the best rendering quality across all three benchmark datasets: on Mip-NeRF360 it reaches SSIM 0.839, PSNR 28.02, LPIPS 0.145; on Tanks & Temples, SSIM 0.854, PSNR 23.79, LPIPS 0.165; and on Deep Blending, SSIM 0.905, PSNR 29.70, LPIPS 0.235. Crucially, GDAGS attains these gains while using substantially less memory than 3DGS (e.g., 515 MB vs. 734 MB on Mip-NeRF360) and far less than Pixel-GS (515 MB vs. 1.2 GB). Training time is also the fastest among all compared methods (1140 s on Mip-NeRF360 vs. 1594 s for 3DGS), with inference at 188 FPS.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GDAGS outperforms all 3DGS-based baselines (3DGS, AbsGS, Pixel-GS, Taming 3DGS) on every metric across all three datasets, while also reducing memory by 20–50% relative to Pixel-GS and 30–45% relative to 3DGS. Against the strongest 3DGS variant, Taming 3DGS, GDAGS gains +0.017 SSIM and +0.23 dB PSNR on Mip-NeRF360 while cutting memory from 735 MB to 515 MB. Compared to AbsGS, which forces all sub-gradients positive to fix over-reconstruction, GDAGS avoids the resulting over-densification: it achieves higher PSNR (28.02 vs. 27.49 on Mip-NeRF360) with lower memory (515 vs. 728 MB). Against mini-splatting, which aggressively prunes Gaussians for compactness, GDAGS trades roughly 2–5× more memory for consistently better SSIM, PSNR, and LPIPS (e.g., 0.839 vs. 0.822 SSIM on Mip-NeRF360). On the generalizability test (Table 3), integrating GDAGS into MCMC-3DGS and Compact-3DGS improves LPIPS by 0.04 and 0.02 respectively on Mip-NeRF360, confirming the module's plug-and-play benefit. GDAGS also trains fastest (1140 s on Mip-NeRF360) and renders at 188 FPS, surpassing 3DGS (134 FPS) and Pixel-GS (89 FPS) in inference speed.

## Relation to Prior Work

GDAGS extends the 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) framework by replacing its magnitude-only densification criterion with a gradient-direction-aware decision metric. It directly addresses the over-reconstruction/over-densification trade-off that AbsGS (which forces positive gradients) and Pixel-GS (which weights by pixel coverage) each resolve incompletely: AbsGS eliminates directional conflicts but amplifies outliers, while Pixel-GS ignores direction entirely. The key conceptual departure is the Gradient Coherence Ratio (GCR), a normalized vector-norm ratio that isolates directional consistency from magnitude, combined with a nonlinear power-law weighting that applies opposite policies during splitting versus cloning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GDAGS provides a lightweight, module-free mechanism for balancing reconstruction quality and memory in 3DGS pipelines, achieving SOTA rendering with 20–50% less memory than Pixel-GS and faster training than all baselines. Its generalization to MCMC-3DGS and Compact-3DGS suggests the GCR-weighting principle is broadly applicable across 3DGS variants. Open questions include behavior in highly sparse-gradient regions (where the GCR assumption of sufficient per-Gaussian gradient coverage weakens), whether fixed hyperparameters (α=0.8, β=25, p=15) transfer to dynamic or unbounded scenes, and whether the Deep Blending overfitting phenomenon (where more Gaussians degrade quality on simple geometry) can be addressed more systematically than via ad-hoc dropout.
