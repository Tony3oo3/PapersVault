## Problem

Prior work on metadata conditioning for LLM pretraining has demonstrated that prepending URLs accelerates training, but whether other metadata types (quality scores, domain labels) or alternative positions (appending) yield comparable or complementary gains remains unexplored. The paper also lacks a mechanistic account of what property of metadata drives the acceleration and how it reshapes internal representations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261510/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model is a 1.5B Llama (16 layers) trained on FineWeb-Edu with 2.06M tokens per batch (sequence length 4096, batch size 504). Each document is wrapped in <boc>…<eoc> tokens; metadata is either prepended before the document text (masked from loss) or appended after it (retained in loss as an auxiliary prediction target). A 10% metadata dropout is always applied. Evaluation uses 9 downstream benchmarks via LM-Eval-Harness, plus layer-wise probing classifiers for topic, quality, and authorship prediction.

## Method

The paper systematically compares six metadata types (full URL, coarse/fine quality scores, coarse/fine domain information, and five learnable meta tokens) across two positions (prepending and appending) to identify what drives pretraining acceleration. The key finding is that fine-grained metadata (e.g., 10× finer quality scores, open-ended LLM-generated domain labels) consistently outperforms coarse-grained variants when prepended, reaching 100B-token baseline performance at only 60B tokens. Appending metadata converts prediction into an auxiliary task, where coarse-grained quality scores and fine-grained domain information provide the largest gains (~20% token savings), though fine-grained quality scores over-specialize and underperform. Five learnable meta tokens with masked loss partially recover the speedup, with attention patterns to these tokens encoding quality-aware cluster structure. Layer-wise probing reveals that URL and fine-grained metadata improve latent encoding of writing style, document quality, and topic, providing mechanistic evidence for how metadata reshapes representation geometry.

## Results

Prepending fine-grained metadata (URL, QS-Fine, DI-Fine) matches the 100B-token standard-pretraining average score of 46.7 using only 60B tokens, a 40% token reduction. URL prepending achieves the highest average of 47.7, followed by QS-Fine and DI-Fine prepending at 47.3. Appending fine-grained domain information (DI-Fine) also accelerates training, reaching the 100B-token baseline with 80B tokens (20% savings) and an average of 47.3. Coarse-grained metadata variants (QS-Coarse, DI-Coarse) show no meaningful improvement over standard pretraining (46.6–46.7).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is standard pretraining without metadata (avg 46.7), and a control with empty metadata tokens <boc><eoc> (also 46.7), confirming that the special tokens themselves do not confer benefit. Fine-grained metadata consistently outperforms its coarse-grained counterpart: QS-Fine prepending (47.3) beats QS-Coarse (46.6) by 0.7 points, and DI-Fine prepending (47.3) beats DI-Coarse (46.7) by 0.6 points, while URL prepending (47.7) is the strongest single configuration. Between positions, prepending is more effective than appending: the best prepending result (URL, 47.7) exceeds the best appending result (DI-Fine, 47.3) by 0.4 points, and prepending achieves 40% token savings versus 20% for appending. Combining two metadata types (URL + QS-Fine prepended) yields no additive benefit over URL alone, and a hybrid prepending+appending strategy also fails to surpass URL prepending by itself. Learnable meta tokens (5 new tokens with masked loss) partially recover the speedup (47.1 avg), with attention patterns to these tokens encoding quality-aware cluster structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the metadata-conditioning framework established by Gao et al. (2025) and Fan et al. (2025), which showed that prepending source URLs accelerates LLM pretraining by 30–40% in tokens. It broadens the metadata palette beyond URLs to include fine- and coarse-grained quality scores and domain information, introduces metadata appending as an auxiliary prediction task (a position not explored in prior work), and proposes learnable meta-tokens as a parameter-free alternative to fixed metadata strings. The conceptual departure is identifying fine-grainedness as the common property enabling acceleration, rather than treating URL as a uniquely effective signal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that pretraining teams can accelerate training with a wider set of readily available metadata signals, not just URLs, and that the granularity of those signals is the critical design choice. The negative result that combining two effective metadata types yields no additive speedup, and that fine-grained quality-score appending underperforms its coarse counterpart, provides important guardrails for practitioners. Key open questions remain: whether these effects persist at 7B+ scale, why metadata conditioning works mechanistically (the probing gives hints but no causal account), and whether metadata can also improve post-training or instruction-tuning efficiency.
