## Problem

While LLMs demonstrably perform list-filtering tasks, the internal mechanisms implementing this operation remained uncharacterized. Specifically, it was unknown whether LLMs use dedicated, reusable components for filtering or solve each predicate from scratch, and whether the learned filtering representations are abstract enough to transfer across contexts, languages, and task formats.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262145/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes pairs of source and destination prompts (each containing an item list plus a natural-language predicate question) and produces a sparse set of identified filter heads with per-head causality scores, plus a portable predicate vector (q_src) that can be patched into arbitrary new prompts. For the probe application, a single item representation h is classified by computing its affinity against pre-extracted predicate query vectors, yielding a class label without any training. The intervention operates at the query-state level of specific attention heads at the last one or two token positions, with source query vectors optionally averaged over multiple positional variants to remove order-ID contamination.

## Method

The paper identifies filter heads via causal mediation analysis: it learns a sparse binary mask over all attention heads by optimizing an interchange intervention (Eq. 3) that swaps query states between source and destination runs, with an L1 sparsity regularizer (λ=0.02) to select the minimal causal set. The key mechanistic insight is that these heads encode the filtering predicate as a compact geometric direction in query space at the answer token, which interacts with key states carrying item semantics to produce selective attention. Portability is validated by patching q_src into entirely different prompts (different items, formats, languages, or reduce tasks) and measuring whether the LM selects the item satisfying the source predicate. A second, complementary mechanism is discovered: when the question precedes the items, the LM instead performs eager evaluation by storing is_match flags directly in item latents, confirmed by flag-swap interventions that redirect the LM's answer to a planted item. The predicate vectors also support composition via addition (yielding disjunction) and serve as training-free linear probes for concept classification.

## Results

Filter heads identified via causal mediation analysis achieve a causality score of 0.863 (Δlogit +9.03) on the SelectOne-Object task, meaning that patching their query states from a source prompt to a destination prompt causes the LM to correctly select the item satisfying the source predicate 86.3% of the time. Ablating these heads (which constitute <2% of all attention heads in Llama-70B) drops SelectOne accuracy from 100% to 22.5%, SelectOne(MCQ) to 0.4%, SelectFirst to 13.1%, and SelectLast to 9.22%. The predicate encoding is remarkably portable: cross-lingual transfer maintains causality above 0.77 across English, Spanish, French, Hindi, and Thai, and the representation generalizes to semantically distinct predicates (e.g., profession identification at 0.836 causality).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper directly compares filter heads against three previously documented attention head categories—Function Vector heads (Todd et al., 2024), Concept Induction heads (Feucht et al., 2025), and Token Induction heads (Olsson et al., 2022)—by measuring the causality score when patching 79 heads of each type from a source to a destination prompt on the SelectOne-Object task. Filter heads dominate all baselines by a wide margin: 0.863 causality (Δlogit +9.03) versus 0.002 for Function Vector heads, 0.080 for Concept Induction heads, and 0.00 for both Token Induction and Random heads. Concept Induction heads show the closest (but still negligible) effect with Δlogit +5.23, suggesting they capture some semantic information but do not implement the predicate-matching operation. The authors note these are not traditional baselines but rather distinct functional categories, and the comparison confirms that filter heads represent a previously unrecognized component with a unique causal role in list-processing tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mechanistic interpretability literature on specialized attention heads (induction heads, function vector heads, concept induction heads) by identifying a new class—filter heads—that implement the filtering operation from functional programming. It builds directly on activation patching (Meng et al., 2022; Zhang & Nanda, 2024) and learned sparse masks (De Cao et al., 2020; Davies et al., 2023) as methodological tools, but departs from prior selection-focused work (Tulchinskii et al., 2024; Lieberum et al., 2023; Wiegreffe et al., 2025) by studying list-processing in general rather than MCQA-specific settings, and by framing the computation as a map-filter-reduce pipeline. The dual lazy/eager evaluation finding connects to the broader observation that transformers maintain parallel pathways (McGrath et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023) but gives it a concrete functional-programming interpretation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first mechanistic account of how LLMs implement a general filtering operation, showing that predicate representations are compact, portable across languages and formats, and composable via vector arithmetic—offering a training-free probe for concept detection. However, the generality claim is bounded: Counting and CheckExistence tasks do not fully rely on filter heads, and the additional circuits for aggregation remain unidentified. Open questions include whether the lazy/eager duality generalizes to more complex multi-step reasoning, whether the findings hold in smaller or non-English-dominant models, and whether the predicate encoding in query space is truly language-independent or merely robust to surface variation within a shared semantic space.
