## Problem

Recovering latent variables Z from observations X = g(Z) is fundamentally ill-posed without strong assumptions, and existing identifiability results rely on linearity, auxiliary supervision, or functional constraints that are rarely verifiable in practice. The paper asks a complementary question: in general settings where full identifiability is unattainable, what aspects of the latent process can still be recovered with guarantees, and what inductive biases are universally beneficial during estimation?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262159/cropped_page4_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes purely observational data (rendered images from Shapes3D, Cars3D, MPI3D, or text from OpenWebtext) with no interventions, domain labels, or auxiliary variables. It produces disentangled latent variables Z, the dependency structure (support of the Jacobian ∂X/∂Z), and set-theoretic relationships—intersections, complements, and symmetric differences—between latent supports associated with different groups of observed variables. The output is structured as atomic regions in a Venn diagram over latent supports, each block-wise identifiable under the invertibility condition.

## Method

The paper introduces diverse dictionary learning, which defines generalized identifiability through set-theoretic indeterminacy: for any two groups of observed variables, the intersection, symmetric difference, and complement of their latent index sets are provably disentangled up to permutation, requiring only a C² diffeomorphism generative map, positive latent density, and a sufficient nonlinearity assumption. The key inductive bias is dependency sparsity—an L1 penalty on the Jacobian (∥D_Z g∥₀) applied during estimation—rather than the more common latent sparsity, reflecting an Occam's-razor preference for simpler dependency structures. When the dependency structure satisfies a sufficient diversity condition (each latent variable corresponds to its own atomic region in the Venn diagram), the set-theoretic guarantees compose to yield full element-wise identifiability. The regularization is integrated into VAEs (FactorVAE), GANs (DisCo), and diffusion models (EncDiff) by adding the Jacobian L1 term to the standard training objective, and experiments on synthetic and real-world benchmarks show consistent improvements in FactorVAE and DCI disentanglement scores over both the unregularized baselines and latent-sparsity variants.

## Results

On the three standard disentanglement benchmarks (Shapes3D, Cars3D, MPI3D), adding dependency sparsity (Jacobian L1) to FactorVAE, EncDiff, and DisCo consistently improves both FactorVAE score and DCI over the unregularized baselines and over latent sparsity (L1 on Z). For example, on Shapes3D, FactorVAE+Dependency Sparsity achieves 0.871 FactorVAE score and 0.575 DCI, versus 0.833/0.484 for the base and 0.837/0.477 for latent sparsity. On MPI3D, the gains are larger: 0.639/0.384 (dependency) vs. 0.599/0.345 (base) vs. 0.440/0.325 (latent sparsity). In the supplementary SAE comparison on OpenWebtext with GPT-2-Small, JSAE (dependency sparsity) has only 62 dead features versus 439 for Top-K SAE and 207 for Batch Top-K SAE.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares dependency sparsity (Jacobian L1) against both the unregularized base methods and latent sparsity (L1 on Z) across three model families. Against FactorVAE (Kim & Mnih, 2018), dependency sparsity wins on all three datasets for both metrics, with the largest gain on MPI3D (0.639 vs. 0.599 FactorVAE score; 0.384 vs. 0.345 DCI). Against EncDiff (Yang et al., 2024), dependency sparsity achieves the best DCI on Shapes3D (0.947 vs. 0.901) and MPI3D (0.667 vs. 0.676, a near-tie), while latent sparsity actually degrades performance on Shapes3D (0.967 vs. 0.9999 FactorVAE score). Against DisCo (Ren et al., 2021), dependency sparsity wins on all three datasets, e.g., 0.789 vs. 0.727 FactorVAE score on Cars3D. In the supplementary SAE comparison on OpenWebtext, JSAE (dependency sparsity) has far fewer dead features (62) than Top-K SAE (Gao et al., 2025; 439) and Batch Top-K SAE (Bussmann et al., 2024; 207), suggesting dependency sparsity preserves more active, meaningful latent features. Overall, dependency sparsity consistently outperforms latent sparsity, which the authors argue explains known pathologies of SAEs such as feature absorption and excessive latent dimensionality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against the dominant paradigm in nonlinear identifiability that requires strong parametric constraints (linearity, specific functional forms) or auxiliary supervision (interventions, counterfactuals, multi-view signals). It builds directly on the mechanism sparsity regularization of Lachapelle et al. (2022) and Rhodes & Lee (2021), which impose L1 penalties on the Jacobian, and generalizes the structural sparsity condition of Zheng et al. (2022) into a broader 'sufficient diversity' criterion. The key departure is the introduction of set-theoretic indeterminacy as a new identifiability notion, shifting the question from 'can we recover all latents?' to 'what structured subsets remain recoverable under minimal assumptions?', a framing the authors explicitly state has not been addressed in prior literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway — that a simple L1 penalty on the Jacobian (dependency sparsity) provides identifiability guarantees without requiring the ground-truth process to be sparse — is broadly applicable to any differentiable generative model and directly challenges the latent-sparsity paradigm underlying Sparse Autoencoders in mechanistic interpretability. The genus-differentia / Venn-diagram framing offers a principled, composable way to reason about partial structure in the hidden world, which is more actionable than all-or-nothing identifiability. Open questions include whether sufficient diversity is truly necessary (the authors conjecture but do not prove), how these guarantees extend to foundation models with massive scale, and whether the sufficient nonlinearity assumption holds in practice for deep networks.
