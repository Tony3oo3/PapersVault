## Problem

In Reward Learning, when feedback (demonstrations, comparisons) is insufficient to uniquely identify the target reward, the resulting feasible set of equally plausible rewards can cause the recovered reward to differ from the true one, leading to downstream application failure. Existing remedies either require the feasible set to be a singleton (often infeasible in practice) or use qualitative equivalence conditions that cannot quantify how severe the performance drop is. This paper introduces a quantitative framework and a principled robust approach that works even when the feasible set is large, providing worst-case guarantees on the application loss.

## Input / Output

The method consumes three types of feedback (t-suboptimal expert demonstrations, trajectory comparisons, and policy comparisons), batch trajectory datasets for estimating visitation distributions of relevant policies, and a forward sampling model for estimating transition dynamics via reward-free exploration. It outputs a scalar preference estimate x̂ (the expected return difference between two policies π¹ and π²) together with an uninformativeness bound Î that certifies the worst-case absolute error of x̂ over the entire feasible set. Unlike standard ReL methods that output a reward function, Rob-ReL directly outputs the application-level quantity with a provable error guarantee.

## Method

The core insight is a minimax reformulation: rather than selecting an arbitrary reward from the feasible set and optimizing the application loss on it, the robust approach minimizes the worst-case loss over the entire feasible set. For the policy-preference-assessment application, this reduces to computing x_{F,g} = (M + m)/2, where M and m are the maximum and minimum of the return difference ⟨d^{π¹} − d^{π²}, r⟩ over the feasible set, and the uninformativeness is (M − m)/2. Rob-ReL solves the resulting convex optimization problems (linear objectives with convex constraints from the feasible set) using the primal-dual subgradient method (PDSM) on the Lagrangian, with RF-Express for transition model estimation and empirical visitation distributions from batch data. Under Slater's condition, the algorithm achieves polynomial sample and iteration complexity in S, A, H, 1/ε, log(1/δ), 1/ξ, and the number of feedback items.

## Results

The main theoretical contribution is Theorem 5.3, which establishes that Rob-ReL achieves sample complexity Õ(SAH⁵/(ε²ξ²) · log(1/δ)) trajectories and iteration complexity O(H^{5/2}/(ξ·ε²) · (√(SA) + √H/ξ) · (1 + √(H(m_D+m_TC+m_PC)(H/ξ²+SA)))²) to guarantee L_g(r*, x̂_K) ≤ I_{F,g} + ε with probability 1−δ. In the numerical simulation (Section 6), Rob-ReL outputs x̂ = 0.2 with a worst-case uninformativeness bound Î = 0.82, while the true preference value is ΔJ(r*) = 0.39. The paper demonstrates that without the robust approach, the worst-case error could be approximately doubled to ~1.64 (e.g., if an arbitrary reward r_M in the feasible set were used while r* = r_m).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark Rob-ReL against competing algorithms on standard datasets; instead, it provides a conceptual and numerical comparison between the proposed robust (minimax) approach and the standard approach used in prior ReL literature (recovering an arbitrary reward r̃ from the feasible set and optimizing the loss under r̃). In the Section 6 simulation, the robust approach yields a worst-case loss bound of Î = 0.82, whereas the standard approach could incur a worst-case error of approximately 1.64 (roughly 2× larger), illustrating the practical benefit of the minimax formulation. The paper also notes that several existing IL algorithms (Abbeel & Ng 2004; Syed & Schapire 2007; Ho et al. 2016) can be reinterpreted as implicitly adopting the robust approach, but without the explicit quantification of the worst-case loss (uninformativeness) that Rob-ReL provides. The key differentiator is that Rob-ReL outputs both a point estimate x̂ and a certified worst-case error bound Î, enabling the user to decide whether the feedback is sufficient for the downstream application.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the feasible set formalism from Metelli et al. (2021; 2023) and the primal-dual subgradient method of Nedić & Ozdaglar (2009), but departs sharply from the qualitative identifiability framework of Skalse et al. (2023) by replacing the binary 'equivalent or not' condition with a quantitative loss-based framework that admits a continuum of acceptable solutions. The robust minimax approach (Eq. 2) is shown to subsume several existing IL algorithms (GAIL, apprenticeship learning) as special cases, providing a unifying lens. The paper also introduces a new 'policy comparison' feedback type that bridges demonstrations and trajectory comparisons, and leverages RF-Express (Menard et al., 2021) for reward-free transition model estimation within its algorithm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The uninformativeness measure I_{F,g} gives practitioners a concrete, computable diagnostic for whether their feedback is sufficient for a given downstream application, addressing a gap that prior qualitative frameworks could not fill. The robust approach provably halves worst-case error compared to arbitrary reward selection, and the Chebyshev-center interpretation reveals that the optimal robust reward can lie outside the feasible set—a counterintuitive result that challenges the standard ReL assumption of recovering rewards from within the feasible set. However, Rob-ReL is limited to a narrow subset of ReL problems (policy preference assessment with specific feedback types in tabular MDPs), and the authors acknowledge that extending the robust approach to other applications may lead to intractable optimization, leaving the general case open.
