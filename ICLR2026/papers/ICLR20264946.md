## Problem

Existing iEEG datasets for epilepsy research are single-center, inconsistent in format and metadata, lack standardized benchmarks, and rarely release pathological event annotations, creating barriers to reproducibility, cross-center validation, and clinical translation. No unified resource exists that harmonizes multi-center recordings with expert-validated annotations and clinically grounded evaluation metrics.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264946/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Inputs are iEEG recordings resampled to 1000 Hz, consumed either as 570 ms raw waveform segments centered on HFO events (for event classification) or as 1-minute multi-channel segments (for channel-level and exploratory tasks), optionally converted to Morlet wavelet time-frequency spectrograms in the 10–300 Hz band. Outputs include three-class HFO labels (spkHFO, non-spkHFO, artifact), per-channel pathological scores aggregated from segment-level probabilities, binary channel labels (pathological vs. normal), and a resection ratio (RR) that quantifies the fraction of predicted pathological signal surgically removed, used to predict Engel-class surgical outcomes.

## Method

The paper constructs Omni-iEEG by harmonizing four public iEEG sources (Open-iEEG, Zurich, HUP, Source-Sink) spanning 8 centers into a unified BIDS-compliant collection of 302 patients and 178 hours, with board-certified epileptologists verifying metadata and annotating 36,177 candidate HFO events generated by three detectors (STE, MNI, Hilbert). For benchmarking, they compare event-based models (PyHFO-Omni: a modified ResNet18 on spectrograms predicting spkHFOs) against segment-based models that operate directly on 1-minute iEEG, including TimeConv-CNN (two temporal 1D convolutions compressing the 60,000×224 spectrogram before a ResNet18 backbone), CLAP (audio-pretrained contrastive model fine-tuned end-to-end on iEEG), and SEEG-NET (multiscale 1D conv + BiLSTM + attention). A key finding is that TimeConv-CNN matches or surpasses the clinically grounded PyHFO-Omni in both channel-level AUC (0.81 vs. 0.74) and outcome prediction AUC (0.74 vs. 0.74), while CLAP's competitive performance demonstrates cross-domain transferability from generic audio representations to neurophysiological signals.

## Results

On the primary Pathological Brain Region Identification task (Table 5), the end-to-end TimeConv-CNN achieves the highest channel-level AUC (0.8061) and specificity (0.8230), while the clinically grounded PyHFO-Omni event-based model achieves the highest outcome AUC (0.7438). TimeConv-CNN matches PyHFO-Omni on outcome prediction (0.7380 vs. 0.7438) while surpassing it on all channel-level metrics. On the Pathological Event Classification task (Table 4), PyHFO-Omni leads with macro-F1 of 0.8061 and AUC of 0.9390, outperforming the best time-domain baseline (PatchTST, F1 0.7726, AUC 0.9311).

## Baselines & Comparisons

TimeConv-CNN (the paper's proposed end-to-end segment-based model) outperforms all other baselines on channel-level AUC (0.8061), specificity (0.8230), recall (0.7454), and F1 (0.6469), while nearly matching the best event-based model PyHFO-Omni on outcome AUC (0.7380 vs. 0.7438). Against SEEG-NET, TimeConv-CNN wins on specificity (0.8230 vs. 0.6049) and outcome AUC (0.7380 vs. 0.5952), though SEEG-NET edges ahead on raw channel AUC (0.7850 vs. 0.8061 — actually TimeConv-CNN also wins here). CLAP, an audio-pretrained model fine-tuned on iEEG, achieves competitive specificity (0.7823) and outcome AUC (0.6770) but trails TimeConv-CNN on all other metrics. The event-based PyHFO-Omni model, despite being clinically grounded, is outperformed by TimeConv-CNN on every channel-level metric, demonstrating that end-to-end learning from 1-minute iEEG segments can surpass hand-crafted HFO biomarkers. On the separate HFO event classification task (Table 4), PyHFO-Omni (F1 0.8061, AUC 0.9390) outperforms all time-domain baselines, with PatchTST (F1 0.7726) and TimesNet (F1 0.7652) trailing by 3–4 points, suggesting that time-frequency representations remain important for fine-grained event-level discrimination.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on four publicly available single-center iEEG datasets (Open iEEG, Zurich iEEG HFO, HUP, Epilepsy Interictal) and harmonizes them into a unified multi-center resource with consistent metadata, channel annotations, and clinical outcomes verified by board-certified epileptologists. Methodologically, it positions itself at the intersection of clinically validated HFO biomarker pipelines (PyHFO, eHFO, RippleLab detectors) and modern time-series deep learning (PatchTST, TimesNet, LSTM+Attention), while also exploring cross-domain transfer from audio pretraining (CLAP) to neurophysiological signals. The key departure from prior work is the introduction of standardized, clinically grounded evaluation metrics (resection ratio, channel-level specificity/recall) and the release of 36K+ expert-validated pathological event annotations, which were previously absent or inconsistent across published datasets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Omni-iEEG addresses a critical reproducibility gap in epilepsy ML research by providing the first multi-center, harmonized iEEG benchmark with unified evaluation protocols, enabling fair cross-study comparison that was previously impossible due to format and metadata inconsistencies. The finding that an end-to-end TimeConv-CNN matches clinically grounded PyHFO-Omni in surgical outcome prediction (0.74 vs. 0.73 AUC) while outperforming it in channel-level identification suggests that data-driven approaches may eventually complement or replace labor-intensive manual HFO review. Open questions include whether the cross-domain audio-to-iEEG transfer generalizes beyond the tested architectures, whether the N=1 'hearable biomarker' observation (YAMNet labeling SOZ segments as 'helicopter') is reproducible, and how the benchmark performs as the field moves toward ictal and multi-modal (iEEG + imaging) tasks. Limitations include the inherent subjectivity of spkHFO annotations, the restriction to interictal recordings, and the exclusion of certain HFO detection and artifact-removal strategies.
