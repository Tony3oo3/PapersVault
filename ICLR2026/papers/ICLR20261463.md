## Problem

Existing procedural mistake detection methods focus almost exclusively on modeling how an action is executed (motion patterns, action sequences), yet many real-world errors manifest not in the execution itself but in the resulting outcome, such as an unintended object state or incorrect spatial arrangement. This paper addresses the gap by arguing that reliable mistake detection requires jointly modeling both the execution process and the action effect (outcome).

## Input / Output

The method takes an egocentric procedural video and a predefined task/action label as input, operating on non-overlapping action segments produced by an ActionFormer backbone. Under the one-class classification setting, training uses only correct action segments while testing includes both correct and erroneous ones. The output is a per-segment binary mistake label and a continuous mistake probability, computed as 1 minus the cosine similarity between the effect-enhanced action embedding and its task-specific textual prompt, thresholded at a predefined value τ.

## Method

The core contribution is Action Effect Modeling (AEM), a three-stage module that enriches action segment features with outcome-aware cues. First, an effect frame is selected per segment by jointly scoring semantic relevance (CLIP similarity between frame features and GPT-4o-generated post-action descriptions) and visual clarity (Laplacian sharpness). Second, multimodal effect knowledge is extracted via a dual branch: a visual branch using Grounding DINO and EVA-02 CLIP to obtain grounded object-state and spatial-relation features, and a textual branch that prompts GPT-4o to generate a scene graph, decomposed into state and relation subgraphs and encoded by a Graph Attention Network. Third, a learnable effect token is aligned with both visual and textual effect features through L2 distillation losses and cross-modal contrastive objectives, so that at inference the compact token replaces all external model calls. The enriched segment features are then fed to a prompt-based detector that contrasts each action embedding against task-specific textual prompts (with a learnable prefix) in a one-class contrastive setup, jointly trained end-to-end with the segmentation, effect-modeling, and detection losses.

## Results

On the EgoPER dataset, the proposed AEM framework achieves 73.8% AUC and 66.7% EDA averaged across five cooking tasks, improving over the strongest baseline AMNAR by 5.3% in AUC and 2.3% in EDA. On CaptainCook4D, it reaches 68.1% Precision, 62.5% AUC, and 71.9% EDA, surpassing AMNAR by 2.7% in Precision and 2.3% in AUC. The method also improves action segmentation (58.5 IoU vs. 56.3 for AMNAR), indicating that effect-aware representations benefit the segmentation backbone as well.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the two dedicated mistake-detection baselines, AEM clearly outperforms EgoPED (Lee et al., 2024) by 11.8% AUC and 9.7% EDA on EgoPER, and by 7.6% AUC on CaptainCook4D. Compared to the strongest prior method AMNAR (Huang et al., 2025a), AEM gains 5.3% AUC and 2.3% EDA on EgoPER and 2.3% AUC on CaptainCook4D, though AMNAR retains a slight 0.4% EDA edge on CaptainCook4D, which the authors attribute to AMNAR's task-graph-based noise mitigation. The video anomaly detection baselines (HF²-VAD, HF²-VAD+SSPCAB, S3R) trail substantially, with AUC values between 54.9% and 59.9% on EgoPER, confirming that goal-oriented procedural mistake detection benefits far more from explicit action-effect modeling than from generic anomaly-detection formulations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends offline procedural mistake detection (EgoPED, AMNAR) by arguing that prior methods' focus on execution dynamics is insufficient, since many errors manifest only in the action's outcome. It introduces a probabilistic marginalization formulation over latent action-effect descriptors and a dual-branch multimodal supervision strategy (visual grounding via Grounding DINO + symbolic scene graphs via GPT-4o) that distills effect knowledge into learnable tokens, eliminating MLLM dependency at inference. The prompt-based detector and contrastive alignment objectives draw on CLIP-style and prefix-tuning techniques, while the segmentation backbone (ActionFormer) and one-class classification setting are inherited directly from Lee et al. (2024) and Huang et al. (2025a).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically important gap: errors that look correct in execution but produce wrong outcomes (e.g., spilled mixture, irregularly cut vegetables) are missed by execution-only detectors. The distillation design makes the approach deployable without expensive MLLM calls at test time, and the finding that spatial-relationship cues are more discriminative than object-state cues offers a useful inductive bias for future effect-aware systems. Open questions include whether the heuristic effect-frame sampling can be replaced by learned selection, how the framework scales to longer-horizon tasks with cascading effects, and whether world models or LLM-generated explanations can improve interpretability and online detection.
