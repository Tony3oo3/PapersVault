## Problem

When a predictive model warns of an undesired future event, deciding which variables to alter requires a principled measure of influence that goes beyond statistical association or static causal effects. Existing measures like average causal effect evaluate isolated interventions in a fixed environment, whereas the Avoiding Undesired Future (AUF) problem involves a changeable world where the benefit of altering one variable depends on subsequent alterations and observations. This paper asks: how should influence be quantified in such a dynamic, decision-dependent setting?

## Input / Output

The method consumes observational data over a topologically ordered sequence of discrete variables, along with the feasible alteration domains for actionable variables and the desired domain for the target. It does not require knowledge of the underlying structural equations; instead, conditional probabilities are learned from data via standard ML models. The output is an influence power value in [-1,1] for each actionable variable, where positive values indicate beneficial alteration, zero indicates indifference, and negative values indicate that any alteration is counterproductive. The method also supports partial observability, where some variables are unobserved at decision time even if they appear in the training data.

## Method

The paper defines influence power (InP) as the difference between the maximum expected probability (MEP) of achieving the desired target after optimally altering a variable and the expected MEP when the variable is observed naturally. MEP is computed recursively via the principle of maximum expected utility: at each subsequent variable, the decision-maker either alters it to the best feasible value or observes it and averages over its natural distribution, with the recursion terminating at the target. For estimation, the MEP computation is cast as a single-player non-deterministic game and approximated using Monte-Carlo tree search with the UCT policy, while terminal-state AUF probabilities are estimated from observational data via a manipulation-theorem-based expression. A key theoretical result (Theorem 1) shows that influence power and average causal effect are neither necessary nor sufficient for each other: non-ancestral variables can have positive influence power, and causal ancestors can have zero or even negative influence power, revealing that the 'right' variable to alter in AUF is fundamentally different from the one with the strongest causal effect.

## Results

On the three synthetic tasks, the proposed influence-power-based method (OURS) achieves success rates of 60.94% (TRADER), 63.86% (FARMER), and 65.32% (DOCTOR), outperforming all baselines on TRADER and DOCTOR by 10+ percentage points. On the real-world BERMUDA coral calcification task, OURS reaches 75.16% success rate, surpassing the best baseline (MAX-ALL at 72.71%). The method also demonstrates rapid convergence: with only 100 Monte-Carlo tree search iterations, success rates already approach their plateau values (e.g., 51.86% on TRADER vs. 60.34% at 5000 iterations).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares six strategies for selecting variable alterations. Against MAX-ONE (Qin et al., 2023), which alters only the single variable with the highest AUF probability, OURS wins decisively on all four tasks (e.g., 65.32% vs. 50.76% on DOCTOR; 75.16% vs. 61.99% on BERMUDA). Against MAX-ALL (Qin et al., 2025; Du et al., 2025a), which jointly alters all actionable variables, OURS again leads on every task, with the largest margin on DOCTOR (65.32% vs. 51.08%) and a narrower but consistent edge on BERMUDA (75.16% vs. 72.71%). Against the correlation-based (CORR) and causal-effect-based (ACE) sequential selection baselines, OURS dominates on TRADER, DOCTOR, and BERMUDA, while on FARMER it essentially ties CORR (63.86% vs. 63.88%)—the paper attributes this to FARMER having a single critical variable that all methods correctly identify. The CORR baseline performs particularly poorly on BERMUDA (19.09%), suggesting that statistical correlation is a poor proxy for actionable influence in real-world settings with non-binary variables.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the rehearsal learning paradigm for Avoiding Undesired Future (AUF) introduced by Zhou (2022b, 2023), which positions 'influence' as an intermediate concept between statistical association and causality. Prior AUF methods (Qin et al., 2023; Qin et al., 2025; Du et al., 2025a) addressed the decision problem heuristically—either altering a single variable to maximize AUF probability or jointly altering all actionable variables—without a principled measure of which variables actually warrant alteration. The paper departs from standard causal-strength measures (e.g., average causal effect) by arguing they evaluate isolated interventions in a static environment, whereas AUF requires accounting for the interplay between sequential alterations and observations under maximum expected utility. For estimation, it repurposes UCT-based Monte-Carlo tree search (Kocsis & Szepesvári, 2006) as a single-player non-deterministic game over the variable sequence.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first principled, formally defined measure (influence power) for the AUF problem, resolving an open question in the rehearsal learning literature. The central theoretical finding—that non-ancestral variables can carry positive influence power while causal ancestors can have zero or even negative influence power—challenges the intuitive assumption that one should alter variables with strong causal effects, and has practical implications for decision-making in medical, agricultural, and economic settings. Limitations include the requirement for a pre-specified topological ordering, the discretization needed for continuous variables (as in the BERMUDA task), and the computational cost of MCTS scaling with the number of actionable variables. Open questions include extending the framework to non-stationary environments, handling unobserved confounders without the causal-sufficiency assumption, and scaling to high-dimensional variable sets.
