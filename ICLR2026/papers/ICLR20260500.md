## Problem

Pre-trained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models face a significant action-distribution mismatch when adapting to new robot embodiments or tasks, because the raw action spaces (differing in degrees of freedom, joint representations, and physical parameters) diverge substantially from the pre-training distribution. Existing adaptation methods focus on parameter-level efficiency (e.g., LoRA, layer skipping) or architectural redesigns but fail to explicitly bridge the distributional gap between pre-training and adaptation data, requiring extensive data collection and compute for effective fine-tuning.

## Input / Output

The method operates as a plug-and-play adaptation layer on top of existing diffusion- or flow-based VLAs, consuming the same inputs (multi-view RGB observations, language instructions, proprioceptive state) and producing continuous action chunks of variable length (H for pre-training, L for adaptation, e.g., 64 for RDT, 50 for π₀, 14 for DP). The key additional component is a pair of lightweight Transformer-based InfoVAEs (latent dimension 512) that encode action chunks into a shared 512-dim latent space; the adaptation VAE encoder is used at training time to compute guidance gradients on noisy intermediate action chunks, while at inference the VLA generates actions without any additional modules.

## Method

ATE is a two-stage framework. In Stage 1 (Alignment), a pre-training InfoVAE is trained on large-scale cross-embodiment action data to learn a latent prior q_φ(z), then a separate adaptation InfoVAE is trained on limited target-domain data with a reverse KL divergence constraint toward q_φ(z); the mode-seeking property of reverse KL embeds the adaptation latent distribution into a specific mode of the pre-training latent manifold, yielding a unified and hierarchically structured action latent space. In Stage 2 (Steering), an energy-based classifier guidance function measures the squared latent distance between a noisy intermediate action chunk and the ground-truth adaptation action chunk via the adaptation VAE encoder, and its gradient is injected into the diffusion denoising loss (scaled by √(1−ᾱ_k)·λ) or the flow-matching velocity loss (scaled by (1−τ)/τ·λ) to explicitly push the VLA's output distribution toward the target domain during fine-tuning. The approach requires no modification to the VLA architecture, introduces only the cost of training two small VAEs (≈12 h + 0.5 h), and is compatible with both diffusion-based (RDT-1B, Diffusion Policy) and flow-matching-based (π₀) backbones.

## Results

On the RoboTwin 1.0 benchmark (17 bimanual manipulation tasks), ATE improves the average success rate of RDT-1B from 31.8% to 41.6% (+9.8%) and π0 from 36.1% to 44.8% (+8.7%), with gains exceeding +30% on challenging tasks such as Empty Cup Place (+39 for RDT) and Put Apple Cabinet (+25 for RDT, +21 for π0). On ManiSkill3, RDT-1B+ATE achieves 46.6% average success versus 36.4% for the baseline (+10.2%). In real-world cross-embodiment experiments on a dual-arm RealMan robot (7-DoF per arm), ATE reaches 58.1% overall success at 120k steps compared to 16.7% for the π0 baseline, a 32% absolute gain, while also converging faster (e.g., Cook Bun reaches 100% at 90k steps vs. 15% for baseline).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ATE is evaluated as a plug-and-play add-on to three representative VLA architectures: RDT-1B (diffusion-based, bimanual), π0 (flow-matching-based, generalist), and Diffusion Policy (diffusion-based, single-arm). Against RDT-1B on RoboTwin 1.0, ATE wins on 13 of 17 tasks with an average gain of +9.8%, though it regresses on Bottle Adjust (−16%) and Tool Adjust (−12%). Against π0, ATE wins on 12 of 17 tasks with an average gain of +8.7%, with the largest single-task improvement on Dual Bottles Pick Easy (+37%) but a notable regression on Pick Apple Messy (−7%). Against Diffusion Policy, ATE consistently improves both in-distribution and out-of-distribution success rates and accelerates convergence (surpassing the RDT baseline at 70k steps vs. 90k). In real-world cross-embodiment settings (dual-arm RealMan 7-DoF vs. predominantly 6-DoF single-arm pre-training data), ATE achieves a 32% absolute success rate gain over the π0 baseline (58.1% vs. 16.7%), and also outperforms the baseline across all generalization perturbations (illumination, spatial shifts, visual distractors, human disturbance).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of VLA adaptation and score-based generative modeling. It repurposes the mode-seeking property of reverse KL divergence (from variational inference) to construct a unified latent action space that embeds target-domain actions into modes of the pre-training latent distribution, and then applies classifier guidance (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021; Lipman et al., 2024) within that space to steer fine-tuning. This departs from prior VLA adaptation work that focuses on architectural modifications (LoRA, layer-skipping), kinematics retargeting, or discrete action tokenization, by operating entirely in a learned continuous latent space without altering the VLA backbone. The two-step InfoVAE design (Zhao et al., 2017) is the key structural novelty, ensuring the adaptation latent is hierarchically nested within the pre-training manifold rather than merely overlapping it.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ATE addresses a practical deployment bottleneck: pre-trained VLAs struggle to adapt to new embodiments (e.g., single-arm → dual-arm) or novel tasks without extensive data collection. The plug-and-play nature (no architecture changes, negligible overhead from two lightweight VAEs) makes it immediately applicable to existing diffusion- and flow-based VLA pipelines. Limitations include the requirement for access to pre-training action data to train the first VAE, the guidance's dependence on ground-truth action chunks during training (not available at inference), and evaluation limited to a single real-world platform (RealMan 7-DoF). Open questions include whether the two-step latent alignment generalizes to highly heterogeneous embodiments (e.g., dexterous hands vs. parallel grippers) and whether the guidance scale λ can be made adaptive rather than fixed.
