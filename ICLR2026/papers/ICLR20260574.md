## Problem

Selecting a small, high-utility training subset for fine-tuning on a target distribution is critical when domain-specific data is scarce, yet existing influence-function-based methods (e.g., LESS) require per-example gradients, Hessian-vector products, and backpropagation through training dynamics, making them computationally expensive. This paper asks whether a simpler, gradient-free scoring mechanism can match or exceed the quality of these influence-based selectors at a fraction of the cost.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretrained model θ₀, a small validation set Z^val (1024 samples) drawn from the target distribution, and a larger heterogeneous training pool X (36×1024 samples) from one or more source distributions. It produces per-example utility scores φ_i computed purely from forward log-loss evaluations (no gradients), and a selected subset S of prescribed size n (1–8×1024) chosen via a SCORE+RANDOM strategy that takes half the top-scoring examples and half randomly from a base training set to preserve diversity. Scoring is performed at the example level (e.g., an instruction/output pair) but aggregated from token-level log-loss differences, with length-based binning to mitigate bias toward shorter sequences.

## Method

The core insight is a train-validation symmetry: the first-order change in average validation loss from a gradient step on training example x equals (up to sign) the first-order change in loss on x from a gradient step on the validation set, because both reduce to the inner product ⟨∇ℓ(θ,z), ∇ℓ(θ,x)⟩. ToV exploits this by inverting the usual roles: instead of measuring how each training example affects validation loss (requiring N validation evaluations), it performs one small fine-tuning step on Z^val and measures how each training example's own loss changes, requiring only two forward passes over the pool. The Interleaved ToV algorithm (Algorithm 1) alternates L epochs of training on a random base subset U with single validation-set updates at a reduced learning rate (ε=1/10), scoring each remaining example by the loss difference between the base and validation-updated checkpoints. For token-based tasks, per-token log-loss ratios are transformed by a function F (identity, |·|, or positive part) and averaged. Theoretically, under local convexity the ToV score converges to the classical influence-function formula (Theorem 2), and in the overparametrized linear regime both ToV and influence scores share the same limit (Theorem 3), while empirically ToV is 2–6× faster and 2.5–16× more storage-efficient than LESS.

## Results

On instruction tuning (n=8×1024 selected samples), ToV with SCORE+RANDOM achieves consistent test log-loss improvements over random selection across all five experimental configurations, with gains that can be equivalent to or larger than doubling the selected sample size n. On NER, ToV yields systematic improvements over random selection in all six setups, while LESS fails to improve over random. In terms of computational cost, ToV reduces runtime by 2–6× and disk storage by 2.5–16× relative to LESS (e.g., 2h3m vs. 4h5m and 1.84 GB vs. 4.9 GB for instruction tuning; 8m vs. 46m and 0.24 GB vs. 4.1 GB for NER).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LESS (Xia et al., 2024), ToV achieves lower test log-loss in 4 of 5 instruction-tuning experiments (losing only in Experiment 2, where the training pool contains no target-distribution samples and LESS performs slightly better). On NER, the gap is more pronounced: LESS does not improve over random selection in any of the six setups, while ToV consistently outperforms random. Computationally, ToV is substantially cheaper, requiring 2–6× less wall-clock time and 2.5–16× less disk storage than LESS, because it avoids per-example gradients, Hessian-vector products, and gradient storage. Against the Maximum Uncertainty baseline (extending Ting & Brochu, 2018), ToV achieves larger gains in most instruction-tuning settings; on NER, Maximum Uncertainty also improves over random in most cases, but ToV still produces the largest improvements. Overall, ToV's forward-loss-only scoring provides a favorable accuracy–efficiency trade-off relative to both influence-function-based and uncertainty-based selection methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits squarely within the influence-function-based data selection lineage (Pruthi et al., 2020; Bae et al., 2024; Xia et al., 2024/LESS), which estimates per-example utility via first-order Taylor expansions of training dynamics. The key departure is exploiting the symmetry of the gradient inner product to invert the train/validation roles: instead of computing how a training example affects validation loss (requiring per-example gradients and Hessian-vector products), ToV computes how a small validation update affects each training example's loss, which needs only forward passes. This repurposes the classical influence-function framework (van der Vaart, 2000) into a gradient-free scoring procedure, and the paper provides a formal bridge showing the resulting score converges to the standard influence function under local convexity (Theorem 2) and in the overparameterized NTK regime (Theorem 3).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for practitioners doing targeted fine-tuning: ToV achieves 2–6× runtime and 2.5–16× storage reductions over LESS while matching or exceeding its test-loss performance across instruction tuning and NER, making influence-based data selection feasible at scale without gradient storage. The SCORE+RANDOM strategy consistently outperforming SCORE-ONLY suggests the current scoring mechanism does not fully capture combinatorial diversity effects, pointing to an open question about how to integrate diversity-aware selection into the scoring framework. The method's reliance on a small validation learning rate (ε = 1/10) and the first-order approximation means performance could degrade in highly non-convex or large-update regimes, and the theoretical guarantees require local convexity or NTK assumptions that are idealized for modern LLM fine-tuning.
