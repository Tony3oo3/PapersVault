## Problem

Existing Chain-of-Thought verification methods operate on outputs (black-box) or raw activations (gray-box), which can flag that a reasoning step is wrong but cannot explain why the underlying computation fails. This paper asks whether the causal computational graph of a reasoning step—its execution trace through the model's latent circuits—carries a detectable structural signature of error that enables both verification and mechanistic diagnosis.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264170/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes the full internal state of a transcoder-modified Llama 3.1 8B Instruct model during autoregressive generation of a single CoT step, from which a sparse, weighted, directed attribution graph is constructed (nodes are input tokens, active transcoder features, and output logits; edges are high-attribution causal pathways). It produces a binary correctness prediction via a Gradient Boosting Classifier, but also yields a fixed-size structural fingerprint vector and per-feature importance scores that identify which specific computational structures (e.g., a single over-activated late-layer multiplication feature) are responsible for the error signal.

## Method

CRV is a four-stage white-box pipeline. First, each MLP in the target LLM is replaced with a trained TopK transcoder (131,072-dim sparse basis, k=128 active features) that functionally emulates the original module in an interpretable basis. Second, a greedy path-finding algorithm traces high-attribution connections backward from the final logits to construct a step-level attribution graph. Third, the graph is pruned to its top-80% influence subgraph and a feature vector is extracted spanning three levels: global graph statistics (node counts, logit entropy), node influence and activation statistics (mean/max/std of feature activations, layer-wise histograms), and topological features (density, centrality, shortest path lengths). Fourth, a Gradient Boosting Classifier is trained on these structural fingerprints to predict step correctness. The key finding is that error signatures are highly domain-specific (zero-shot transfer across tasks is limited), yet a combined classifier can learn the union of failure geometries, and targeted interventions on individual transcoder features (clamping or amplifying) causally correct faulty reasoning.

## Results

CRV achieves AUROC of 92.47 on Synthetic (Arithmetic), 75.87 on Synthetic (Boolean), and 70.17 on GSM8K, consistently outperforming all black-box and gray-box baselines across every dataset and metric. The advantage is most pronounced on Arithmetic, where CRV's FPR@95 of 37.09% is nearly half the best baseline (CoE-C at 63.33%), and its AUPR of 28.92 dwarfs the next-best (Energy at 5.59). On GSM8K, CRV's AUROC of 70.17 exceeds the strongest baseline (Energy at 62.55) by over 7 points. Cross-domain transfer is limited (e.g., CRV trained on Arithmetic drops to 57.04 AUROC on GSM8K), confirming that error signatures are highly domain-specific, though a combined multi-task classifier recovers most performance (90.51 AUROC on Arithmetic).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CRV outperforms all four baseline families across every dataset and metric. Against the black-box baselines, the strongest is Energy (Liu et al., 2020), which achieves 76.45 AUROC on Arithmetic and 62.55 on GSM8K; CRV surpasses it by 16.02 and 7.62 AUROC points respectively, with a dramatically lower FPR@95 (37.09% vs. 73.86% on Arithmetic). Temperature Scaling (Shih et al., 2023) is the weakest black-box method, scoring below 60 AUROC on all three datasets. Among gray-box methods, CoE-C (Wang et al., 2025a) is the strongest on Arithmetic (69.39 AUROC, 63.33% FPR@95), yet CRV still leads by 23.08 AUROC points and halves the FPR@95. CoT-Kinetics (Bi et al., 2025) performs comparably to other gray-box probes (53–61 AUROC range) and is consistently outperformed by CRV by 10–30 AUROC points depending on the dataset. The gap is largest on structured synthetic tasks, where algorithmic reasoning produces highly consistent execution traces that make error signatures particularly detectable in the attribution graph.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the transcoder framework of Dunefsky et al. (2025), which replaces MLP modules with sparse, interpretable feature bases, and on the attribution graph methodology of Ameisen et al. (2025), which traces causal information flow through these features. While Ameisen et al. used attribution graphs for qualitative circuit analysis, this paper is the first to operationalize them for automated reasoning verification by extracting quantitative structural features and training a diagnostic classifier. It departs from the broader CoT verification literature (PRM800K, REVEAL, CoE, CoT-Kinetics) by shifting from black-box (logit) and gray-box (raw activation) signals to a white-box analysis of the computational graph's topology, positioning itself as a mechanistic debugging instrument rather than a scalable verifier.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key conceptual contribution is demonstrating that reasoning errors leave distinct, domain-specific structural fingerprints on computational execution traces, and that these fingerprints are causally implicated (not merely correlational) in the failure. This opens a path from error detection to mechanistic understanding and targeted repair of LLM reasoning. However, significant limitations remain: the method is computationally intensive and impractical as a drop-in verifier, the causal intervention evidence rests on a single case study, results are confined to Llama 3.1 8B with standard autoregressive CoT (excluding search/backtracking mechanisms in modern reasoning models), and the strong domain-specificity of error signatures limits generalization. Open questions include whether these structural signatures persist in larger or more complex reasoning architectures, and whether the causal interventions can be systematized into a general repair procedure.
