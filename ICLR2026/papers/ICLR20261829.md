## Problem

A growing line of work applies ε-invariance (adversarial) training to deep RL to certify robustness against small state perturbations, yet no prior analysis has examined how this paradigm affects the inherent structure of the learned Q-function. This paper asks what happens to the counterfactual value assignments that standard RL naturally develops—and that neuroscience identifies as central to human decision-making—when ε-local invariance is explicitly enforced, and whether the resulting policies remain accurate and aligned.

## Input / Output

The agent consumes pixel-based state observations from ALE games (BankHeist, RoadRunner, Freeway) and produces a state-action value function Q(s,a) over a discrete action set. The critical evaluation output is not merely the optimal action but the full ordered ranking of Q-values across all actions; the paper introduces performance drop curves P_i(Ω) that quantify whether the i-th ranked action's Q-value correctly predicts the reward loss from executing it in an Ω-fraction of states, enabling direct verification of counterfactual value accuracy rather than just greedy-action performance.

## Method

Theoretically, the paper derives the ε-invariance regularizer from Danskin's theorem and proves a fundamental incompatibility: in linear function approximation, any ε-invariant Q-function must overestimate optimal values and reorder sub-optimal ones (Theorems 3.3, 3.7). The mechanism is that the regularizer's optimal perturbation direction points toward the sub-optimal action's parameter vector, so minimizing it inflates the optimal Q-value while deflating counterfactual Q-values, destroying their correct ordering. Empirically, the paper trains vanilla DDQN alongside three state-of-the-art ε-invariance methods (SA-DDQN, RADIAL, ORP) on ALE games and uses the performance drop curve metric to demonstrate that ε-invariance policies assign near-random values to counterfactual actions (P_w < P_2), whereas vanilla RL preserves the correct monotonic ordering P_1 ≤ P_2 ≤ … ≤ P_w, confirming the theoretical trade-off between estimation accuracy and certified robustness.

## Results

On three ALE games (BankHeist, RoadRunner, Freeway), vanilla DDQN achieves substantially lower performance drop for the second-best action a₂ (0.191, 0.247, 0.302) compared to all ε-invariance methods (0.351–0.449), indicating accurate counterfactual value estimates. Conversely, for the worst action a_w, vanilla DDQN shows higher performance drop (0.398, 0.393, 0.311) than ε-invariance policies (0.241–0.345), confirming that vanilla RL correctly ranks actions while ε-invariance training assigns near-random values to counterfactual actions. This pattern holds across SA-MDP, RADIAL, and ORP, establishing a fundamental trade-off between ε-invariance and counterfactual accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three state-of-the-art ε-invariance training methods—SA-MDP (Huan et al., 2020), RADIAL (Oikarinen et al., 2021), and ORP (Li et al., 2024)—against vanilla DDQN (Wang et al., 2016) on BankHeist, RoadRunner, and Freeway. On the counterfactual accuracy metric (performance drop for a₂), vanilla DDQN consistently outperforms all three ε-invariance methods: e.g., on BankHeist vanilla achieves 0.191 vs. 0.449 for ε-invariance, a 57% relative reduction. On the worst-action metric (a_w), vanilla DDQN shows higher performance drop (0.398 on BankHeist) than ε-invariance (0.311), confirming that vanilla RL correctly identifies the worst action as genuinely bad while ε-invariance policies fail to do so. The paper also reports that ε-invariance policies exhibit Q-value overestimation (Figure 4) and violate the expected ordering P_w(Ω) > P₂(Ω) (Figure 5), where choosing the worst action causes less performance drop than choosing the second-best—direct evidence of lost counterfactuality. All three ε-invariance methods (SA-MDP, RADIAL, ORP) exhibit this same failure pattern, suggesting the issue is inherent to the ε-invariance regularizer rather than specific to any single algorithm.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a critical theoretical and empirical analysis of the ε-invariance (adversarial robustness) training line in deep RL, building directly on the canonical regularizer formalized by Huan et al. (2020, SA-DDQN) and extending the analysis to RADIAL (Oikarinen et al., 2021) and ORP (Li et al., 2024). It departs from the prevailing assumption that adversarial training improves reliability by proving, via Danskin's theorem and a constructive linear MDP argument, that ε-invariance fundamentally trades off Q-value accuracy for invariance, causing overestimation and loss of counterfactual ordering. The paper further connects standard RL's value structure to neuroscientific evidence on human counterfactual decision-making (Klein-Flügge et al., 2016; Wunderlich et al., 2009), framing the robustness literature as diverging from the biological inspiration that underpins RL.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a well-funded and highly visible research direction (multiple NeurIPS/ICML spotlights and orals) by showing that its central technique may erode the very reasoning and generalization capabilities that make RL powerful, while simultaneously producing misaligned and overestimated Q-values. A key open question is whether the fundamental trade-off identified in the linear setting persists in the deep network regime, and whether alternative robustness formulations (e.g., distributional or policy-level robustness) can preserve counterfactuality. The finding also raises the broader question of what 'safety' should mean for RL agents: if certified ε-invariance destroys the value structure that enables counterfactual reasoning, the field needs principled alternatives that do not conflate perturbation invariance with reliable decision-making.
