## Problem

While prior work established that memorizing long-tail training examples improves in-distribution generalization, the synergy between memorization and composition—where a model combines individually memorized rare features to correctly predict on unseen combinations—remained unexplored. This paper asks whether and when memorization of long-tail data enables out-of-distribution generalization on test examples that are novel compositions of features never jointly observed in training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260920/cropped_page7_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

Theoretically, inputs are sparse d-dimensional vectors where each coordinate is independently active with probability p_i following power-law decay (p_i ∝ i^{-α}), and labels are linear functions plus Gaussian noise. Empirically, inputs are 3×28×28 grayscale images formed by stacking three MNIST (or Omniglot) digit images along the channel dimension, and the model outputs a scalar sum of the three digit labels. OOD test samples are constructed to contain at least one rare digit '9' or two Omniglot images, ensuring the specific 3-digit combination was almost certainly never seen in training.

## Method

Theoretically, the paper analyzes the minimum ℓ2-norm interpolator (gradient descent from zero initialization) on a linear model with long-tail feature frequencies. The key technical insight is exploiting the combinatorial structure of the data matrix: under power-law decay, most samples contain zero or one long-tail feature, so common features are uniquely determined by samples with only common features (S0), and long-tail features are then recovered from samples with exactly one long-tail feature (S1). This avoids the high-variance concentration failures that plague standard matrix analysis for rare features. Composition is automatic in the linear setting: once individual β_i values are recovered, any combination of those features is predicted correctly. Empirically, ResNet-18 processes each digit channel independently (Sum, Linear, or 2-layer aggregation) versus jointly (Cross-channel), and the paper shows that per-channel architectures exhibit composition while the cross-channel architecture does not, and that removing memorization via weight decay destroys OOD performance on Omniglot examples.

## Results

On the Omniglot composition task (predicting sum of 3 labels where 2 are rare Omniglot images seen only once in training), compositional architectures with memorization (WD=0) achieve test losses of 0.135–0.176, while the same architectures without memorization (WD=0.5) degrade to 0.645–2.874. The Cross-channel ResNet, which mixes digit information from layer 1, performs poorly regardless of memorization (test loss ≈ 23). On the MNIST 3-digit sum task with OOD test requiring digit '9', Sum, Linear, and 2-layer models all achieve low test loss, confirming compositional generalization, while Cross-channel performs significantly worse.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baselines but instead ablates two axes: architecture (compositional vs. cross-channel) and memorization (WD=0 vs. WD=0.5). Among compositional architectures, the 2-layer model achieves the best OOD test loss (0.1351) with memorization, followed by Linear (0.1662) and Sum (0.1760). Removing memorization via weight decay degrades all compositional models substantially: Sum loses the most (0.176→2.874, ~16× worse), while 2-layer is most robust (0.135→0.645, ~5× worse). The Cross-channel architecture fails catastrophically in both settings (test loss ≈ 23), demonstrating that compositional capability is architecture-dependent and cannot be recovered by memorization alone. The linear model experiments (Figure 1) further confirm that long-tail feature error scales with noise level σ, matching the theoretical prediction of O(σ²t) OOD loss.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the memorization-for-generalization line initiated by Feldman (2020) and Feldman & Zhang (2020), which showed that memorizing long-tail training examples is necessary for near-optimal in-distribution generalization. The key conceptual advance is identifying a *synergy* between memorization and composition: memorized long-tail features can be recombined to predict on OOD test examples that were never observed in training. Theoretically, the paper departs from standard matrix concentration techniques (used in benign overfitting analyses by Bartlett et al., Hastie et al.) by exploiting the combinatorial sparsity structure of the data matrix to handle features with extremely low frequency, and empirically connects to the compositional generalization literature (SCAN, CoGS) by showing architecture determines whether composition emerges.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical guarantee that the minimum ℓ₂-norm interpolator achieves small OOD loss on compositions of memorized long-tail features, giving a principled explanation for why memorization can be beneficial rather than harmful. However, the theory is restricted to linear models and the empirical validation uses very simple tasks (sum of 3 MNIST digits, single Omniglot images), leaving open whether the memorization-composition synergy persists in deeper, more complex architectures and realistic OOD settings. The authors also note that memorization can hurt composition when long-tail features are spuriously correlated or mislabeled, an important caveat for practical deployment.
