## Problem

LLM outputs exhibit repetitive lexical patterns ('slop') that occur thousands of times more frequently than in human text, making AI-generated writing immediately recognizable. Existing suppression methods are brittle: token banning causes collateral damage (banning 'cat' bans all words tokenizing to 'cat'), and instructing models to avoid patterns triggers a backfire effect. The paper addresses the gap of suppressing specific overused patterns at scale (8,000+ items) without degrading writing quality, lexical diversity, or out-of-domain capabilities.

## Input / Output

At inference time, the Antislop Sampler takes a text prompt plus a banlist of individual words, multi-word phrases, and regex patterns (e.g., 'It's not X, it's Y' constructions), and produces generated text with those patterns suppressed via backtracking resampling. For training, the pipeline takes the model's inference traces and produces single-token preference pairs: a prompt context ending just before a banned sequence, one rejected token (the pattern's first token), and 4–8 chosen alternative tokens sampled from the cached top-k logits. The automated profiling step outputs a 'slop fingerprint'—the top over-represented words and n-grams relative to a human baseline (wordfreq + Gutenberg/Reddit corpus)—which feeds directly into banlist construction.

## Method

The framework combines three components. First, the Antislop Sampler performs sequence-aware backtracking at inference: when a banned pattern is detected in the trace, it rewinds to the pattern's initiating token, scales its probability by 10^(−10s) (where s ∈ [0,1] controls ban strength), renormalizes, and resamples with min-p filtering—enabling soft-banning that permits contextually necessary usage. Second, an automated pipeline profiles each model's slop fingerprint against human baselines and harvests preference pairs from every backtracking event. Third, FTPO (Final Token Preference Optimization) is a novel fine-tuning loss operating in logit space on individual tokens, with three terms: (1) a margin-based preference loss using softplus that automatically deactivates once chosen tokens exceed the rejected token by margin m, (2) a target MSE tether with a zero-penalty window (τ_target) allowing chosen/rejected logits to move freely within a dead zone, and (3) a stronger non-target MSE term anchoring the remaining vocabulary to reference logits. This two-part regularization localizes updates to only the target logits, avoiding the collateral probability redistribution that causes DPO's diversity collapse and quality degradation.

## Results

FTPO achieves 83–92% banlist suppression on gemma-3-12b (2k–8k patterns) while maintaining writing quality within 1% of baseline (67.3–68.9 vs. 67.8) and preserving lexical diversity at 95–102% of baseline. On GSM8K and MMLU, FTPO stays within 1–3% of the unmodified model, whereas DPO degrades both by 2–5%. The Antislop Sampler achieves 100% suppression with a slight writing-quality improvement, but at a 69–96% throughput cost. Across three model families (gemma-3-12b, Mistral-Small-3.2, Llama-3.3-70B), FTPO consistently suppresses 66–92% of targeted patterns with negligible cross-domain capability loss.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023), FTPO wins decisively on every measured axis: at 2k banlist size, FTPO suppresses 92.4% of patterns versus DPO's 82.0%, while FTPO's writing quality (68.93) exceeds the unmodified baseline (67.80) and DPO's drops to 62.98. The gap widens at larger banlists—DPO's longform writing score collapses from 51.3 (baseline) to 26.9 at 8k, while FTPO actually improves to 52.3. Lexical diversity tells the same story: FTPO holds at 95–102% of baseline, whereas DPO degrades to 74–92%. Against the inference-time samplers DRY and XTC (Weidmann, 2024), the comparison is stark: both leave the global over-representation fingerprint essentially unchanged (top-40 word ratios of 1442× and 1267× respectively, vs. 1439× baseline), while FTPO-finetuned models reduce this to 394×, confirming that decoding-time tricks cannot alter model-wide lexical tendencies without weight-level intervention.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) by reusing its final-token preference pair structure but replacing the standard DPO loss with a multi-term logit-space objective featuring margin-based deactivation and two-part MSE regularization (target vs. non-target tokens). The inference-time Antislop Sampler extends the backtracking mechanism from ExLlama's string banning and Zhang et al.'s [RESET] token approach by adding configurable soft-banning, regex pattern support, and min-p-constrained resampling. Conceptually, the work sits at the intersection of constrained decoding (Hu et al., 2019), unlikelihood training (Welleck et al., 2020), and preference optimization, but departs from all three by operating on individual tokens within a cached inference trace rather than full sequences or beam candidates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic, model-specific quantification of 'slop' (over-represented lexical patterns) and a practical end-to-end pipeline—forensic profiling, automated training data generation, and targeted fine-tuning—that practitioners can deploy without manual curation. The clear empirical separation between FTPO and DPO (90% suppression with <1% quality loss vs. 80% suppression with 6–15 point quality degradation) gives a concrete recipe for when preference optimization is safe versus destructive. Key limitations include the 69–96% throughput penalty of the sampler, the restriction to mechanical/lexical patterns (semantic and narrative-level repetition remains unaddressed), and reliance on LLM-as-judge for quality scoring; open questions include whether the approach transfers to non-creative domains, whether it aids or hinders AI-text detection, and how to handle the 'whack-a-mole' problem at the semantic level.
