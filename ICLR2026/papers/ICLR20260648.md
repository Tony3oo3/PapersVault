## Problem

Instruction-tuned diffusion LLMs suffer from a critical failure mode termed '<eos> overflow': as the allocated generation length increases, responses paradoxically become shorter, collapsing into early termination or degenerate streams of <eos> tokens. The root cause is the dual use of <eos> as both a termination marker and a padding token during instruction-tuning, which inflates its probability at later positions and triggers cascading backward propagation under confidence-based decoding. Although observed in practice, this issue had not been systematically analyzed or addressed at its source.

## Input / Output

The method takes text instructions as input and produces text responses as output, but unlike autoregressive models, diffusion LLMs require a fixed generation length (max_length) specified in advance, with the model learning to place a single <eos> at the true end of the response and filling all remaining positions with padding. The key structural detail is the padding arrangement: instead of repeating <eos>, the tail is filled with a deterministic cyclic sequence of K distinct padding tokens (e.g., <pad_0> through <pad_6>), which the model must learn to predict correctly during training. This fixed-length constraint means the model must reliably distinguish genuine termination from padding, which is the core challenge the method addresses.

## Method

Rainbow Padding modifies the padding scheme used during instruction-tuning of diffusion LLMs by reserving a single <eos> exclusively for true sequence termination and filling all remaining padding positions with a deterministic cyclic sequence from a set of K distinct padding tokens (K=7 in the main experiments). This design decouples termination from padding so the model learns <eos> only as a proper stopping symbol, while distributing probability mass across K tokens so no single padding token dominates the decoding process and triggers premature unmasking. The cyclic (rather than random) pattern is chosen because it is trivially learnable, converging to near-zero padding loss within 5% of an epoch, while preserving model capacity for instruction-response learning. The method is architecture-agnostic and can be applied to existing instruction-tuned models via a lightweight single-epoch LoRA fine-tuning on ~0.5M examples, requiring only about six GPU-hours on two H200 GPUs, making it a practical drop-in fix for the <eos> overflow problem.

## Results

On LLaDA-Base at max_length=1024 without block decoding, Rainbow Padding achieves 32.6% on MATH-500, 75.5% on GSM8K, and 40.2% on HumanEval, compared to 0.6%, 13.2%, and 20.7% respectively for <eos> padding. On Dream-Base at the same setting, Rainbow Padding reaches 34.3% (MATH), 77.3% (GSM8K), and 48.8% (HumanEval) versus 0.0%, 9.1%, and 22.6% for <eos> padding. On general-purpose benchmarks (MMLU, HellaSwag), Rainbow Padding matches or slightly exceeds <eos> padding (e.g., 65.3% vs. 64.8% MMLU on LLaDA), confirming minimal overhead. A single-epoch LoRA adaptation of already instruction-tuned LLaDA lifts MATH accuracy from 0.1% to 28.6% and GSM8K from 3.0% to 73.8%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares Rainbow Padding against standard <eos> padding on two diffusion LLM families: LLaDA-Base (Nie et al., NeurIPS 2025) and Dream-Base (Ye et al., 2025). On LLaDA-Base at max_length=1024 without block decoding, Rainbow Padding wins decisively: 32.6% vs. 0.6% on MATH-500, 75.5% vs. 13.2% on GSM8K, and 40.2% vs. 20.7% on HumanEval, with response lengths of ~282 tokens versus ~1 token for the baseline. On Dream-Base, the gap is even more extreme: 34.3% vs. 0.0% on MATH and 77.3% vs. 9.1% on GSM8K. Under semi-autoregressive block decoding, Rainbow Padding maintains stable accuracy across block counts (32.6–32.8% on MATH), while <eos> padding is highly sensitive—dropping from 29.8% at 16 blocks to 0.6% at 1 block. On general-purpose benchmarks (MMLU, HellaSwag), the two methods are essentially tied (65.3% vs. 64.8% MMLU; 61.3% vs. 62.5% HellaSwag on LLaDA), indicating that Rainbow Padding imposes no generalization cost. For already instruction-tuned models, a single-epoch LoRA adaptation with Rainbow Padding lifts LLaDA from 0.1% to 28.6% on MATH and Dream from 0.0% to 32.4%, confirming the method's practicality as a lightweight retrofit.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper addresses a specific failure mode in instruction-tuned masked diffusion LLMs (dLLMs) such as LLaDA and Dream, building directly on the masked diffusion training framework (Austin et al., 2021; Sahoo et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2024) and the instruction-tuning pipelines of those models. It departs from prior heuristic mitigations—manual <eos> confidence suppression (Zhu et al., 2025) and semi-autoregressive block decoding (LLaDA)—by identifying the root cause as a training-time design choice (dual use of <eos> as both terminator and pad) and proposing a training-level fix (Rainbow Padding) rather than an inference-time workaround. The paper positions itself as the first systematic analysis of <eos> overflow, distinguishing its contribution from the broader length-control literature (Zhang et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026) by targeting the instruction-tuning-specific artifact of inflated <eos> probability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As dLLMs gain traction as alternatives to autoregressive models, the <eos> overflow failure mode undermines their practical utility on reasoning and coding tasks at even modest sequence lengths; Rainbow Padding offers a near-zero-cost fix (single-epoch LoRA, ~6 GPU-hours) that is architecture-agnostic and robust across decoding strategies. The finding that a seemingly trivial padding-scheme change resolves a near-total performance collapse highlights how implementation details in training data preparation can have outsized effects on diffusion model behavior. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to continuous-time or flow-matching dLLMs, whether the optimal number of padding tokens varies with model scale or task difficulty, and how Rainbow Padding interacts with other length-control mechanisms (e.g., variable-length denoising, expand/delete tokens).
