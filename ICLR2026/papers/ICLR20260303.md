## Problem

Despite strong empirical evidence that test-time scaling (longer chains-of-thought) improves LLM reasoning, the conditions under which long CoTs emerge from training data and when they actually help remain unclear. In particular, it is unknown whether more test-time compute always improves performance, whether it can substitute for training-time compute, and what makes training examples 'difficult' in a way that benefits test-time scaling. This paper provides a theoretical framework answering these questions by analyzing transformers on a tractable in-context learning task.

## Input / Output

The model receives a prompt of n in-context input-output pairs (x_i, y_i) where y_i = ⟨w_τ, x_i⟩ for a hidden weight vector w_τ, embedded as a structured matrix with rows for features, labels, a weight-estimate slot, and a bias row. At test time, given m test pairs and a query x_{m+1}, the model iteratively generates k intermediate weight estimates (w_1, …, w_k) as CoT steps before outputting the final predicted weight vector w_{k+1} ∈ ℝ^d. Unlike standard ICL settings that predict only the scalar output, this formulation requires the model to directly estimate the full weight vector, making the CoT dynamics analytically tractable as iterative optimization steps.

## Method

The paper analyzes a single-layer linear self-attention (LSA) transformer trained via gradient descent on in-context weight prediction for linear regression with general feature covariance Λ. The key theoretical result shows that gradient descent converges to a global minimum whose parameters are explicitly characterized by Γ = (1+1/n)Λ + (1/n)tr(Λ)I, and that at test time the CoT update rule w_{i+1} = w_i − (1/m)Γ⁻¹X_test X_testᵀ(w_i − w_test) implements a multi-step (pseudo-)Newton's method for the regression loss. Task hardness is defined as Hard(Λ) = tr(Λ)/λ_min(Λ), capturing how many weak 'skill' directions the task requires; harder tasks (long-tailed eigenvalue spectra) need more CoT steps to reach a target error. For multi-task training, the authors formulate a quadratic optimization over task selection probabilities π_ℓ that minimizes test-time estimation error, proving that at least half the probability mass must be allocated to hard tasks (those with small minimum eigenvalues) to avoid overthinking. The theory is validated on GPT-2 (9.5M params) and Qwen 2.5-7B-Instruct fine-tuned on OMEGA reasoning tasks, confirming that misaligned training data causes longer CoTs to degrade performance.

## Results

On the OMEGA GCD benchmark with Qwen 2.5-7B-Instruct, fine-tuning on the target task (Qwen-GCD) yields 75% accuracy on short-CoT examples (0–1k chars) versus 30.39% for the base model, a +44.69% gain. However, fine-tuning on an unrelated task (Qwen-Poly) slightly *degrades* performance to 29% on short CoTs and drops to 20.83% on longer CoTs (1k–2k chars) versus 27.2% for the base model, confirming the overthinking phenomenon. Theoretically, the paper derives that test error decays as d·(1 + n/(1+Hard(Λ)))^(−2k), showing that at any fixed test error, increasing test-time compute k allows reducing the training context length n, and that harder tasks (larger tr(Λ)/λ_min(Λ)) require proportionally longer CoTs to reach the same accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is primarily a theoretical paper, so there are no standard method-vs-method baselines. The closest empirical comparison is the three-model evaluation on OMEGA (Table 1): Qwen-GCD (fine-tuned on the target GCD task) substantially outperforms Qwen-Base on both short and long CoT regimes, while Qwen-Poly (fine-tuned on an unrelated polynomial-root task) underperforms Qwen-Base, especially on longer CoTs (20.83% vs. 27.2%), directly validating the paper's overthinking prediction. In the synthetic LSA and GPT-2 experiments (Figure 2), the key comparison is across context lengths n and CoT lengths k: when training and test covariances are aligned, increasing k monotonically reduces test error and allows smaller n; when the training covariance is skewed (eigenvalues ∝ 1/i) relative to the isotropic test covariance, increasing k *increases* test error, and larger n paradoxically worsens performance in the overthinking regime. The task-selection experiment (Figure 3, Appendix B.1) further shows that the proposed quadratic optimization (4.5) assigns higher probabilities to hard, diverse tasks and prevents the error-from-overthinking seen under uniform or easy-task-only selection.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends two parallel lines of work on transformer ICL: Zhang et al. (2024) analyzed how trained transformers implement linear regression in-context but did not model CoT dynamics at test time, while Huang et al. (2025a) incorporated CoT during training but assumed isotropic features (Λ=I). By combining CoT at test time with general (non-isotropic) feature covariance, the authors derive a clean connection between the trained LSA model and multi-step (pseudo-)Newton's method, then extend the analysis to a multi-task setting with a principled task-selection optimization. The broader test-time scaling literature (Snell et al., Muennighoff et al., OpenAI o1, DeepSeek R1) has been largely empirical; this paper provides the first theoretical grounding linking training data properties to test-time scaling effectiveness.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper gives a clear mathematical explanation for the 'overthinking' phenomenon: when training data underrepresents directions relevant to the downstream task, additional test-time compute amplifies error rather than reducing it. This provides actionable guidance for practitioners designing multi-task training curricula—specifically, that diverse, relevant, and hard tasks (characterized by small minimum eigenvalues of feature covariance) should be prioritized. Key limitations include the restriction to linear regression with single-layer linear self-attention, and empirical validation on relatively small models (9.5M GPT-2, 7B Qwen with only two tasks). Extending the theory to nonlinear activations, deeper architectures, and realistic language tasks remains an important open question.
