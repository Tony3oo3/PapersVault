## Problem

State-of-the-art Concept Bottleneck Models define their concept sets a-priori (via human experts, knowledge graphs, LLM prompting, or CLIP), but these pre-specified concepts often lack sufficient predictive power for the target task or are not learnable from the available data, causing CBMs to substantially underperform their black-box counterparts when controlling for information leakage. The paper asks whether CBMs built directly from a black-box model's own learned concepts can serve as faithful interpretable approximations of that model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260127/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes RGB images through a frozen backbone (ResNet-18, ResNet-50, or CLIP ViT-B/16) and produces a class label via a sparse linear classifier over concept logits, alongside per-concept presence/absence probabilities and per-concept contribution scores for explanations. Concept annotations are ternary (present, absent, or not annotated), so the CBL is trained with a masked binary cross-entropy loss that ignores unannotated image-concept pairs. Sparsity is controlled at the decision level via the proposed Number of Contributing Concepts (NCC) metric, which measures how many concepts are needed to explain a τ-fraction of each prediction rather than capping the number of non-zero classifier weights.

## Method

M-CBM extracts interpretable concepts from a trained black-box backbone by training a Sparse Autoencoder on its activations, then prunes dead and low-density neurons to retain only those contributing meaningfully to predictive performance. A Multimodal LLM (GPT-4.1) names each surviving SAE neuron by examining top-activating and non-activating images with saliency overlays, and then annotates ~1000 stratified images per concept in 5×5 grids to build a partially labeled concept dataset. The final model is a sequential CBM: the frozen backbone feeds a Concept Bottleneck Layer trained with masked BCE on the ternary annotations, whose pre-sigmoid logits are z-normalized and passed to a sparse linear classifier (GLM-SAGA with elastic-net, α=0.99) that maps concepts to classes. The key insight is that concepts originating in the backbone's own learned representations are inherently more learnable and predictive than externally specified ones, and the NCC metric generalizes NEC by measuring sparsity through actual concept contributions (logit × weight) at decision time rather than imposing a hard cap on the effective concept vocabulary.

## Results

M-CBM consistently achieves the highest accuracy across all three datasets at matched sparsity (NCC=5 and NCC=avg). On CUB, M-CBM reaches 73.70% accuracy at NCC=5 (vs. 76.67% black-box), on ISIC2018 it reaches 72.75% accuracy / 70.14% balanced accuracy at NCC=5 (vs. 79.37% / 75.37% black-box), and on ImageNet it reaches 72.18% at NCC=5 (vs. 76.15% black-box). M-CBM also substantially outperforms baselines on concept prediction quality, achieving 90.04% macro ROC-AUC on CUB and 88.90% on ImageNet, compared to 62.03% for VLG-CBM_CA on CUB.

## Baselines & Comparisons

M-CBM outperforms all three baselines at every reported sparsity level. Against VLG-CBM_CA (Srivastava et al., 2024), the closest competitor, M-CBM gains +4.6 pp on CUB (73.70% vs. 69.12%) and +8.2 pp on ISIC2018 (72.75% vs. 64.55%) at NCC=5; VLG-CBM_CA is computationally infeasible on ImageNet (~300 GPU-days for full-dataset annotation). Against LF-CBM (Oikarinen et al., 2023), M-CBM gains +15.6 pp on CUB and +10.6 pp on ISIC2018 at NCC=5. DN-CBM (Rao et al., 2024) performs worst across all datasets, with its CLIP-only design limiting accuracy (e.g., 38.21% on CUB with ResNet backbone), confirming that generic CLIP concepts lack sufficient predictive power for fine-grained or domain-specific tasks. M-CBM also achieves substantially better concept prediction (90.04% vs. 62.03% macro ROC-AUC on CUB), indicating its SAE-derived concepts are more learnable and semantically grounded than LLM-generated or CLIP-derived alternatives.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of Concept Bottleneck Models (Koh et al., 2020) and mechanistic interpretability, specifically Sparse Autoencoders (Bricken et al., 2023). Prior CBMs define their concept set a-priori—via human experts, knowledge graphs, LLM prompting, or CLIP embeddings—whereas M-CBM extracts concepts directly from a black-box backbone's own learned representations using SAEs, then leverages a Multimodal LLM (GPT-4.1) to name and annotate them, drawing on the automated-interpretability paradigm of Bills et al. (2023) and Rott Shaham et al. (2024). The paper also introduces NCC, a decision-level sparsity metric that generalizes the NEC metric from VLG-CBM (Srivastava et al., 2024), relaxing the hard cap NEC imposes on the effective concept vocabulary per class.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

M-CBM addresses a core weakness of existing CBMs: externally specified concepts often lack sufficient predictive power or learnability, causing CBMs to substantially underperform their black-box counterparts. By grounding concepts in the model's own features, M-CBM achieves both higher accuracy and dramatically better concept prediction (e.g., 90% vs 62% macro ROC-AUC on CUB), while the NCC metric provides a more flexible sparsity control. Key open questions remain: information leakage is reduced but not eliminated (random-word CBMs still outperform chance), there is no systematic way to verify that concepts are learned as intended rather than via spurious correlations, and the MLLM annotation cost scales linearly with concept count, limiting practicality on very large datasets. Future work on multi-layer bottlenecks and more efficient MLLM annotation could address these gaps.
