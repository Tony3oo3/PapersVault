## Problem

Frontier model safeguards (refusal training, output classifiers) are designed to block direct harmful requests, but they leave open an ecosystem-level attack surface: adversaries can use ostensibly harmless frontier outputs as fine-tuning data to transfer dangerous capabilities into open-source models. This paper demonstrates that even robustly safeguarded models can be exploited to elicit harmful capabilities in open-source models, recovering ~40% of the capability gap between a base open-source model and an unrestricted frontier model in the domain of chemical weapons synthesis.

## Input / Output

The attack takes three inputs: (1) prompts for synthesizing harmless organic molecules (sourced from PubChem, filtered to exclude weapon-relevant chemicals via a jailbroken Claude assessment), (2) a safeguarded frontier model API (Claude 3.5 Sonnet) that generates detailed synthesis procedures in response to these benign prompts, and (3) abliterated open-source model weights (Llama 3.3 70B, Qwen 2.5 72B, Llama 3.1 8B, Gemma 2 27B) with refusal behavior removed. The output is a fine-tuned open-source model (~9.7M tokens of training data) that produces high-quality chemical weapons synthesis instructions on 8 evaluation tasks, measured via an anchored comparison score (0–8 scale) and rubric keyword coverage. A key constraint is that the frontier model's prompts must remain in adjacent harmless domains to avoid triggering safeguards, yet the resulting fine-tuning data must be close enough in domain to transfer harmful capabilities.

## Method

The elicitation attack operates in three stages: (i) constructing prompts in adjacent, ostensibly harmless domains (harmless organic chemical synthesis) that do not directly request dangerous information, (ii) obtaining detailed responses from a safeguarded frontier model that does not refuse these benign queries, and (iii) supervised fine-tuning an abliterated open-source model on the resulting prompt-output pairs. The core mechanism exploits the well-known generalization property of SFT across related tasks: knowledge of harmless organic synthesis procedures transfers to harmful chemical weapons synthesis because the underlying chemical reasoning is shared. The paper also introduces an anchored comparison evaluation that uses a jailbroken frontier LLM (Gemini 2.5 Pro) to compare tested outputs against anchor responses on task-specific subgoals, which catches subtle procedural mistakes (50.9% recall) that rubric-based keyword matching misses (10.5% recall) and achieves 88% agreement with human chemistry experts. The attack's efficacy scales with both frontier model capability (newer models yield higher uplift) and fine-tuning data volume, and requires domain proximity—training on unrelated domains like inorganic chemistry or general science provides minimal transfer.

## Results

On anchored comparison APGR, fine-tuning Llama 3.3 70B on Claude 3.5 Sonnet outputs for harmless organic synthesis recovers 38.8% of the performance gap to a jailbroken Claude 3.5 Sonnet on 8 chemical weapons tasks; the smallest model (Llama 3.1 8B) recovers 24.7%. On the rubric metric, uplift is more dramatic: 61.5% for Llama 3.3 70B and 34.2% for Gemma 2 27B. Both baselines (weak-only self-training and LibreTexts textbook fine-tuning) yield negative or near-zero uplift, confirming the frontier model is essential. Training on outputs from the newer Claude 4 Opus pushes APGR to 71.1% for the same Llama 3.3 70B, and the classifier-guarded system still permits 49.0% APGR via adjacent-domain prompts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's elicitation attack is compared against two baselines: (1) a weak-only baseline where the open-source model generates its own prompt-output pairs for fine-tuning, and (2) a textbook-only baseline using LibreTexts chemistry content. Across all four open-source models (Llama 3.1 8B, Gemma 2 27B, Qwen 2.5 72B, Llama 3.3 70B) and both evaluation metrics, the elicitation attack consistently and substantially outperforms both baselines, which show negative or near-zero APGR. For example, on Llama 3.3 70B with anchored comparisons, elicitation achieves 38.8% APGR while both baselines remain at or below zero. The paper also compares its proposed anchored comparison evaluation against the rubric evaluation from Sharma et al. (2025): anchored comparison achieves 88% agreement with human chemistry experts versus 75% for rubrics, catches 50.9% of deliberately introduced mistakes versus 10.5% for rubrics, and rates ground-truth Organic Synthesis articles at 4.6/8 versus rubric scores that rank them below Llama 3.3 70B. On the domain-sweep analysis (Table 1), training on organic chemistry synthesis data (33.7% APGR) far outperforms training on general science/engineering (17.7%) or inorganic chemistry (11.2%), confirming that domain proximity—not stylistic imitation—drives the uplift.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ecosystem-level model combination attacks of Jones et al. (2025), which showed adversaries can decompose malicious tasks across safe models at inference time, by moving the attack to training time: fine-tuning an open-source model on a frontier model's ostensibly harmless outputs permanently transfers dangerous capability, eliminating the need for multi-model inference. It also builds on the SFT generalization literature (Wei et al. 2021; Muennighoff et al. 2022) and the constitutional classifier system of Sharma et al. (2025), which provides both the rubric evaluation baseline and the classifier-guarded target system. The paper introduces an anchored comparison evaluation to address documented limitations of the rubric approach, and positions its contribution within the broader argument (Glukhov et al. 2023; Narayanan & Kapoor 2024) that safety cannot be measured at the single-model output level.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The demonstration that a classifier system which withstood thousands of hours of red-teaming can still be leveraged to elicit ~40% of the capability gap in open-source models challenges the assumption that output-level safeguards are sufficient for frontier model safety. The finding that attack efficacy scales with both frontier model capability and fine-tuning data volume implies the threat grows over time as stronger models are released, and that current safety frameworks (OpenAI Preparedness, Anthropic RSP, GDM Frontier Safety) lack explicit protections against this class of ecosystem attack. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond chemistry to other high-capability domains (cyber, bio), how to detect or prevent adversaries from harvesting ostensibly benign outputs at scale, and whether open-source model developers can meaningfully test for elicitation-derived uplift before release.
