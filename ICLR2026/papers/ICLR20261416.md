## Problem

Ambivalence and hesitancy (A/H) are the primary reasons individuals delay, avoid, or abandon health behaviour changes, yet no dataset exists for training machine learning models to automatically recognize them in videos. While trained clinicians can detect A/H through subtle cross-modal and within-modality affect conflicts during in-person interactions, integrating such expertise into digital health interventions is costly and poorly scalable, leaving a critical gap in automated affective computing for behavioural change.

## Input / Output

The BAH dataset provides 1,427 short Q&A videos (~10.60 hours, avg 26.76 s) from 300 participants, with three synchronized modalities: cropped-and-aligned 256×256 RGB face frames at 24 FPS, 16 kHz mono log mel-spectrograms (VGGish), and word-level BERT features from Whisper transcripts (repeated per frame via timestamps). Outputs are binary A/H labels at both frame and video level, plus per-segment start/end timestamps; the annotation is deliberately binary (A/H vs. non-A/H) because the two constructs manifest identically in practice. A/H segments are brief (mean 4.29 s / 103.89 frames) and the frame-level class is highly imbalanced (17.04% positive), which must be handled during training.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is the BAH dataset itself, collected via a purpose-built web platform where participants answer seven behaviour-eliciting questions while being recorded, and annotated by three behavioural-science experts using ELAN with a custom codebook covering facial, vocal, verbal, and bodily cues. For benchmarking, visual backbones (ResNet-18/34/50/101/152, APViT) are pre-trained on 0.54 M images from RAF-DB, AffectNet, and Aff-wild2 for basic emotion recognition, then fine-tuned on BAH with negative-class under-sampling; a TCN is optionally appended to capture temporal context over a window of adjacent frames. Audio and text features are extracted offline and fused with visual features using four strategies—concatenation (CAN), co-attention (LFAN), transformer (MT), and cross-attention (JMT)—to assess whether standard fusion can capture the affect conflicts that define A/H. Additional experiments cover zero-shot prediction with multimodal LLMs and personalization via source-free domain adaptation, all structured around participant-based train/validation/test splits to support subject-level generalization.

## Results

On the BAH test set for frame-level A/H classification, the best single modality is text (AVGF1: 0.5497, AP: 0.2519), outperforming visual (AVGF1: 0.5165, AP: 0.2070) and audio (AVGF1: 0.4658, AP: 0.2238). The best modality pair is Audio + Text (AVGF1: 0.5586, AP: 0.2609), while combining all three modalities slightly reduces AP to 0.2548. Among fusion methods, CAN (concatenation) achieves the highest AP of 0.2631, but overall performance remains limited, with AP below 0.27 across all configurations, highlighting the difficulty of A/H recognition.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks four fusion methods from two prior works on the BAH test set. CAN (concatenation) from Zhang et al. 2023 achieves the best overall performance with AVGF1 0.5526 and AP 0.2631, narrowly beating LFAN (co-attention) from the same paper (AVGF1 0.5502, AP 0.2548). In contrast, both methods from Waligora et al. 2024 perform substantially worse: MT (transformer fusion) scores AVGF1 0.5137 and AP 0.2134, while JMT (cross-attention) scores AVGF1 0.5241 and AP 0.2139. The gap between the best (CAN) and worst (MT) fusion method is approximately 0.04 in AP, suggesting that standard transformer-based and cross-attention fusion mechanisms are poorly suited to capturing the affect conflicts characteristic of A/H. The authors conclude that more specialized fusion techniques targeting inter- and intra-modality discord are needed.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing the first dataset and benchmark for Ambivalence/Hesitancy (A/H) recognition in videos, a construct well-studied in behavioural science but previously unexplored in machine learning. Conceptually, A/H recognition is positioned as a specialized case of compound emotion recognition (C-EXPR-DB, Richet et al. 2024), but differs in targeting a specific motivational conflict (willingness vs. resistance) rather than a generic mixture of basic emotions. The baseline models repurpose existing multimodal fusion architectures (LFAN, CAN, MT, JMT from ABAW workshops) and standard backbones (ResNet, APViT) without proposing new methods, establishing a starting point rather than advancing the methodological frontier.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The BAH dataset fills a critical gap by providing the first resource for training and evaluating A/H recognition models, enabling automated detection of motivational conflict in digital health interventions where in-person clinical assessment is costly and less scalable. The preliminary baselines reveal that standard multimodal fusion is inadequate: combining all three modalities slightly underperforms audio+text alone, suggesting that A/H requires specialized mechanisms to detect cross-modality affect conflict rather than simple feature aggregation. Key open questions include designing fusion that explicitly models inter-modality discord, temporal modelling for within-modality conflict, and whether the moderate inter-annotator agreement (Fleiss's κ = 0.41 at frame level) reflects inherent task ambiguity or annotation difficulty. The dataset's geographic limitation to Canada and the absence of body-level segmentation are acknowledged constraints for generalizability.
