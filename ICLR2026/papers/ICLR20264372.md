## Problem

While in-context learning (ICL) in Transformers is well-studied, prior theoretical analyses have largely assumed that pretraining and test task distributions are identical or isotropic, leaving the central question of how pretrain-test task structure mismatch governs generalization unaddressed. This paper asks: how does the covariance structure of tasks seen during pretraining, relative to those encountered at test time, determine ICL performance?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264372/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes a context of ℓ input-output pairs (x_i, y_i) drawn from a linear regression task y_i = ⟨x_i, w⟩ + ε_i, plus a test input x_{ℓ+1}, and outputs a scalar prediction ŷ_{ℓ+1}. The theoretical framework takes as inputs the pretraining task covariance C_train (finitely sampled by k tasks), the test task covariance C_test (unsampled), and the high-dimensional ratios α=ℓ/d, τ=n/d², κ=k/d, and produces a closed-form deterministic equivalent for the ICL MSE error decomposed into a scalar term and a misalignment term ⟨C_test, K⟩. The alignment measure K encodes finite-sample resolution effects through resolvent functions F_κ(σ) and their derivatives, capturing how much of C_train's spectral structure is recoverable from k pretraining samples at effective noise level σ.

## Method

The paper analyzes a reduced-parameter linear attention model performing ICL of linear regression, extended to arbitrary (non-isotropic, non-matched) pretraining and test task covariances. Using random matrix theory in the proportional high-dimensional limit, they derive an exact deterministic equivalent for the ICL generalization error, which decomposes as e_ICL = e_scalar + e_misalign, where e_misalign = ⟨C_test, K⟩ and K = qF_κ(σ) + (qλ̃ − σ²)F_κ′(σ) is a pretraining-dependent matrix whose eigenvalues are oppositely ordered to those of C_train. The effective noise σ and ridge λ̃ are determined self-consistently from the data parameters, and F_κ encodes the finite-sample resolution of the pretraining task covariance from only k samples. They validate that this theoretically derived alignment measure is the strongest predictor of ICL error even in a two-layer softmax-attention Transformer (Spearman ρ=0.99), outperforming CKA, ⟨C_test C_train^{-1}⟩, and simpler resolvent-based measures. Finally, they prove via Ruhe's trace inequality that co-diagonalizability extremizes the misalignment error, and show that for low task diversity κ, pretraining on a more spectrally concentrated distribution (higher power-law exponent) than the test distribution can reduce ICL error, revealing a specialization-generalization tradeoff.

## Results

The paper derives an exact high-dimensional formula for ICL generalization error decomposed into a scalar term e_scalar and a misalignment term e_misalign = ⟨C_test, K⟩, where K is a pretraining-dependent matrix built from the resolvent F_κ(σ) and its derivative. The theory curves match numerical simulations of MSE error across a range of task diversity κ and test covariance structures (Figure 1). For a two-layer nonlinear Transformer with softmax attention, the derived alignment measure achieves a Spearman correlation of 0.99 with ICL error, outperforming all alternative measures. A key finding is that for low task diversity κ, pretraining on a task distribution with higher spectral power than the test distribution can reduce ICL error, demonstrating that train-test misalignment can be beneficial.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its proposed alignment measure ⟨C_test, K⟩ against three alternatives for predicting ICL generalization error: a simpler resolvent-based measure ⟨C_train, F_κ(σ)⟩, the population matrix measure ⟨C_test, C_train^{-1}⟩, and Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA). On the solvable linear attention model (Figure 2), both ⟨C_test, K⟩ and ⟨C_train, F_κ(σ)⟩ are strong monotonic predictors of e_ICL, while ⟨C_test, C_train^{-1}⟩ and CKA fail to capture the relationship. On a nonlinear two-layer Transformer (Figure 3), the Spearman coefficients are 0.99 (proposed), 0.98 (resolvent), 0.96 (population), and 0.39 (CKA), confirming that the full alignment measure with finite-sample correction terms is the most accurate predictor. The advantage of the proposed measure over the simpler resolvent version is modest but consistent, reflecting the additional information captured by the derivative term F_κ'(σ) that accounts for noise-level sensitivity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the random-matrix-theory framework for ICL of linear regression by linear attention established in Lu et al. (2025) and Zhang et al. (2024a), which assumed isotropic or matched pretraining-test task distributions. The key departure is allowing arbitrary covariance structure in both C_train and C_test, yielding an exact high-dimensional formula for ICL error under task mismatch. The analysis draws a direct analogy to alignment measures in ridge regression under covariate shift (Canatar et al., Atanasov et al., Patil et al.), importing the resolvent-based finite-sample correction into the ICL setting. The paper further validates its theoretically-derived alignment measure on a nonlinear two-layer softmax-attention transformer, bridging the solvable linear model to more realistic architectures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central practical implication is that pretraining curricula need not mirror the test-time task distribution; for low task diversity, concentrating pretraining on fewer, stronger directions can improve ICL generalization, challenging the 'teach to the test' heuristic. The alignment measure's strong predictive power on nonlinear transformers (Spearman 0.99 vs. 0.39 for CKA) suggests it could guide pretraining data curation in practice. Limitations include the restriction to linear regression ICL, the linear-attention solvable model for the main derivation, and the high-dimensional proportional limit; the paper leaves open how the interplay between the scalar pretraining error and the misalignment term yields a general optimal-pretraining heuristic, and how these results extend to multi-step or non-linear task structures.
