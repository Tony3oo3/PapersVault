## Problem

Existing multimodal datasets rarely combine LLM-inferred behavioral trait descriptors with complementary modalities such as facial attributes and biographical information, limiting systematic cross-modal and causal analysis of human behavior traits. Additionally, prior causal representation learning methods lack identifiability guarantees tailored to the multi-modality, multi-measurement setting where each modality provides multiple observations of the same underlying latent variables.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260348/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes 1024-dimensional facial image embeddings (via ImageBind) and 3584-dimensional text trait embeddings (via gte-Qwen2-7B-instruct), both further obfuscated through an invertible transformation for privacy, alongside structured biographical metadata (height, weight, birth date, nationality geocoded to lat/long, occupation or league). It produces per-modality latent variable representations partitioned into shared latents, image-based latents, and trait-based latents, a learned causal adjacency matrix over these latents, aggregated Big Five scores (0–3 scale from three LLMs via median voting), and p-values from five independence tests. The released datasets contain only transformed embeddings rather than raw images or text, with categorical variables converted to indices.

## Method

The paper introduces a multi-modality multi-measurement causal representation learning framework where each modality m has its own encoder estimating modality-specific latents z_m, exogenous noise η_m, and shared latents s, with decoders reconstructing each measurement separately via MSE. Causal relations among latents are captured through a learnable adjacency matrix implemented via normalizing flows, regularized with an L1 sparsity penalty to encourage minimal causal graphs. Independence among latents and exogenous variables is enforced by aligning their joint distribution with an isotropic Gaussian prior via KL divergence. The key theoretical contribution is a three-part identifiability theory: Theorem 1 establishes subspace identifiability under injectivity and measurement-change conditions, Theorem 2 isolates the shared subspace via entropy regularization, and Theorem 3 achieves component-wise identifiability by exploiting the connection between conditional independence in the Markov network and vanishing cross-partial derivatives of the log-density. The overall loss combines reconstruction, independence, and sparsity terms, and the framework is validated on synthetic Colored/Fashion MNIST data (achieving R²=0.96, MCC=0.92) and real-world PersonaX datasets where discovered latents yield interpretable cross-modal causal pathways (e.g., confidence → facial expressions, emotional stability → grooming).

## Results

On the synthetic Colored MNIST + Fashion MNIST benchmark, the proposed multi-modality multi-measurement CRL method achieves R² = 0.96 and MCC = 0.92, outperforming MMCRL (R² = 0.90, MCC = 0.85) and consistently surpassing both BetaVAE and MCL. On the real-world AthlePersona dataset, the framework recovers a causal graph with 2 shared latents, 5 image-based latents, and 5 trait-based latents, revealing interpretable cross-modal causal pathways (e.g., confidence → facial expressions, emotional stability → grooming). Independence tests on both datasets confirm significant dependencies between Big Five trait scores and structured features such as gender, occupation, birth year, and league affiliation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks against three baselines on the synthetic multi-modality multi-measurement task: BetaVAE (Higgins et al., 2017), Multimodal Contrastive Learning / MCL (Daunhawer et al., 2023), and Multimodal Causal Representation Learning / MMCRL (Sun et al., 2025). The proposed method clearly wins on both R² and MCC, achieving 0.96/0.92 versus MMCRL's 0.90/0.85, and consistently surpasses BetaVAE and MCL as well (exact values shown only in Fig. 5(c)). The key advantage over MMCRL, the closest competitor, stems from explicitly modeling cross-modal causal dependencies with multiple measurements per modality, a setting where prior approaches remain constrained. BetaVAE and MCL, which do not model causal structure across modalities, perform the weakest. On the real-world PersonaX datasets, no direct numerical baseline comparison is provided; instead, the paper validates interpretability of the recovered causal graph through independence-test-guided variable labeling.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the multimodal causal representation learning (CRL) line initiated by Yao et al. (2023) and Sun et al. (2025) by introducing a multi-modality, multi-measurement setting where each modality contributes multiple observations, and derives new identifiability theorems tailored to this richer structure. On the data side, it builds on CelebA for facial attributes and official sports league records for biographical features, but departs from prior trait datasets (SALSA, MuPTA, MDPE, FI-V2) by uniquely combining LLM-generated textual trait descriptions with Big Five scores, facial embeddings, and structured metadata in a single unified resource. The LLM-based trait inference itself draws on recent evidence that large language models can reliably assess Big Five dimensions from public information (Serapio-García et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2023), but applies this at dataset-construction scale rather than as a downstream prediction task.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PersonaX provides the first large-scale multimodal resource explicitly designed for causal (not merely correlational) analysis of behavioral traits, filling a gap where prior datasets lacked either textual trait descriptions or a framework for cross-modal causal interpretation. The identifiability theory for multi-measurement CRL is a meaningful theoretical advance that could inform future multimodal representation learning beyond this specific application. However, significant limitations temper the impact: AthlePersona covers only male athletes, CelebPersona focuses on wealthy high-visibility individuals, traits are inferred from static public data without longitudinal tracking, and the real-world causal graph analysis remains exploratory with subjective latent interpretations. Open questions include whether LLM-inferred traits are valid proxies for actual behavioral patterns, how findings generalize beyond these narrow cohorts, and whether the discovered causal structures are stable across populations.
