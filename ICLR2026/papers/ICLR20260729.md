## Problem

Simulators are a trusted but unexplored attack surface in the RL supply chain: malicious developers can subtly alter simulator dynamics to implant backdoors in trained agents. All prior backdoor attacks require read or write access to the agent's rewards, but common simulators (MuJoCo, PyBullet) only receive actions and return states while rewards are computed externally, making this a realistic yet previously infeasible threat model. The paper asks whether effective, stealthy backdoors can be implanted purely through transition manipulation without ever observing or modifying rewards.

## Input / Output

The poisoned agent receives the same observations as in the benign setting: 84×84 depth images stacked over 8 frames for the custom robot tasks (Intersection, Waiter), or standard proprioceptive state vectors for MuJoCo environments. The trigger is a physical plus-shaped object placed in the agent's field of view, which the simulator detects and uses to activate the attack logic. The output is the agent's action — a continuous vector in [-1,1]² for robot control (linear/angular or left/right wheel velocities) or a discrete action index for Atari — with the backdoor forcing a specific target action (e.g., [1,1] for 'accelerate forward') whenever the trigger is present.

## Method

Daze implements the attack as a lightweight API wrapper around the base simulator that severs the link between the agent's chosen action and the action actually executed in the environment. With probability β, the agent enters a triggered state (a superficially perturbed state); if the agent's action is close to the target a⁺ (within threshold τ), a 'null transition' returns the benign state as if nothing happened, but if the action deviates, the agent enters a 'dazed state' where transitions are governed by uniformly random actions for k·L_adv(a, a⁺) consecutive steps. The core insight is that random actions are universally suboptimal (Assumption 1), so the agent is naturally punished for ignoring the trigger without any reward manipulation — the low returns from dazed transitions come from the environment's own reward function. The paper proves two theorems: an optimal policy in the adversarial MDP must have zero adversarial error in all triggered states (Theorem 1), and must also be optimal in the benign MDP (Theorem 2), guaranteeing both attack success and stealth. In practice, the 'null transition' trick lets the adversary sample from the agent's policy without query access, and the daze duration scales with the adversary's error to provide a dense learning signal in continuous domains.

## Results

On continuous-action MuJoCo tasks (HalfCheetah, Hopper, Reacher), Daze achieves 92.4–98.5% Attack Success Rate (ASR) with negligible Benign Return (BR) degradation (e.g., 2321.5 vs. 2085.4 unpoisoned on Hopper), while TrojDRL-C and SleeperNets-C achieve only 20–66% and 0–7.5% ASR respectively. On discrete-action Atari games (Q*Bert, Frogger, Pacman, Breakout), Daze reaches 97.6–99.9% ASR and outperforms all baselines in mean BR (e.g., 18,052 vs. 17,322 unpoisoned on Q*Bert). On real robotic hardware (Turtlebot2 Intersection, Fetch Waiter), Daze achieves 92.3% and 93.2% ASR with BR within ~5% of unpoisoned performance, demonstrating the first successful transfer of an RL backdoor to physical robots.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Daze dramatically outperforms TrojDRL(-C) across all continuous-action environments, achieving 92–98% ASR versus TrojDRL-C's 20–66%, while also maintaining higher BR (e.g., 1627.8 vs. 604.4 on HalfCheetah). Against SleeperNets(-C), Daze wins decisively on MuJoCo tasks (92–98% vs. 0–7.5% ASR) but concedes the Intersection task where SleeperNets-C reaches 99.8% ASR at the cost of a 33% BR drop (158.6 vs. 238.4 unpoisoned), making it easily detectable. In discrete Atari environments, Daze (97.6–99.9% ASR) is within 1–2.4% of Q-Incept and SleeperNets (both ~100% ASR) but achieves the highest mean BR across all four games, exceeding even the unpoisoned baseline on Q*Bert and Frogger. Critically, Daze operates without any read or write access to rewards, whereas all three baselines require both, yet it matches or exceeds their performance—demonstrating that reward manipulation is not necessary for effective RL backdoor attacks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the established line of RL backdoor attacks (TrojDRL, SleeperNets, Q-Incept) by identifying a fundamental assumption they all share: the adversary must have read or write access to the agent's rewards. Daze carves new ground by showing that neither access is necessary, instead exploiting the universal sub-optimality of random actions to punish agents that ignore the target action in triggered states. The threat model also shifts from prior 'outer loop' approaches (replay buffer access, RAM-level control) to an 'inner loop' simulator-level attack, where the adversary only controls transition dynamics and superficial state perturbations. Conceptually, the attack repurposes the idea of domain randomization as a cover for adversarial behavior, turning a standard robustness technique into a concealment mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work identifies a realistic and previously unexplored threat vector in the RL supply chain: malicious simulators that implant backdoors without touching rewards. The first demonstration of backdoor attacks triggering harmful behavior on physical robotic hardware (Turtlebot2, Fetch) elevates the stakes from theoretical concern to practical safety risk for autonomous systems. No existing defenses (BIRD, state sanitization) are applicable, and the attack can be hidden within standard domain randomization pipelines, making detection non-trivial. Open questions include developing detection methods for reward-free backdoors, formalizing defenses against inner-loop simulator attacks, and understanding how the attack interacts with large-scale parallel training and foundation-model-based RL pipelines.
