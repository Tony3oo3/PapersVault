## Problem

Conventional RLVR training for LLMs requires complete ground-truth annotation of the entire dataset and allocates compute uniformly across all samples, yet recent evidence suggests a small subset of instances may suffice. The central gap this paper addresses is how to identify these critical 'lottery-winning' samples in an unsupervised manner—without access to ground-truth answers—so that annotation and computation can be concentrated on the few instances that matter most for policy optimization.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an unannotated question set (e.g., 2048 BigMath-sub instances), the original LLM policy π₀, and a small calibration set with known answers (1024 instances from BigMath or MMLU) used solely to calibrate the conformal scoring threshold. It outputs a budget-sized subset Q′ (b = 4 or 8 instances, <0.5% of the dataset) that is then annotated and fed into standard GRPO training. Notably, the selection criterion is the cardinality of a conformal prediction set per instance, and a Sentence-BERT + K-means clustering step ensures the selected samples span diverse problem regions rather than concentrating on a single topic.

## Method

CONST (Complementary Conformal Selection) scores each unannotated question from two complementary angles: procedural volatility, which truncates a deterministic reasoning chain at n_P stages and re-queries the LLM for a direct answer at each truncation point, and outcome volatility, which samples n_O complete outputs from π₀. Both yield multisets of candidate answers that are merged; a scoring function combining negative answer frequency and normalized entropy is then calibrated via conformal prediction to produce a prediction set per instance. The cardinality of this set—reflecting how many answers the model considers plausibly correct—serves as the selection criterion, with larger sets indicating richer optimization signal. A theoretical analysis under the lottery sample hypothesis (gradient approximation error ε), L-smoothness, and the Polyak-Łojasiewicz condition yields a generalization bound showing CONST converges to the optimal GRPO policy at a rate governed by Rademacher complexity, MDP mixing time, and ε.

## Results

Using only 8 critical instances (0.39% of the 2048-sample training set), CONST achieves average accuracy within 1.09% of full-dataset training across four LLMs and four mathematical benchmarks (avg@32/avg@256). For example, on LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct, CONST with budget 8 reaches 28.31% average accuracy versus 28.03% for the full dataset, while on Qwen2.5-Math-7B it reaches 54.94% versus 55.00%. CONST outperforms the strongest active-learning baseline (CEC) by 10.97% on average across all settings, and improves over the un-finetuned model by up to 76.76% (Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, budget 8).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CONST outperforms all three active-learning baselines (EntSampling, BADGE, CEC) across every LLM architecture and both budget settings. On LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct with budget 8, CONST achieves 28.31% average accuracy versus 24.43% (CEC, the strongest baseline), 23.65% (BADGE), and 21.82% (EntSampling) — a gap of 3.88–6.49 points. On DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B with budget 8, CONST reaches 48.14% versus 44.14% (CEC), 43.78% (BADGE), and 45.60% (EntSampling). The RandSelect baseline (random annotation) is consistently the weakest among the fine-tuned methods, confirming that the unsupervised selection criterion of CONST adds substantial value over random sampling. Notably, CONST with only 8 annotated samples matches or nearly matches full-dataset training (2048 samples) on all four LLMs, with the largest gap being 1.01 points on DeepSeek-1.5B (48.14% vs. 49.15%).

## Relation to Prior Work

CONST sits at the intersection of conformal prediction (Vovk et al., 2005; Su et al., 2024) and RLVR policy optimization (GRPO, Shao et al., 2024), repurposing conformal prediction sets not for uncertainty quantification of a single prediction but as a sample-selection criterion. It departs from prior data subset selection and valuation work (Paul et al., 2021; Das et al., 2024), which all require complete annotation to compute losses or gradients, by operating entirely without ground truth answers. The procedural and outcome volatility signals are novel constructs specific to LLM reasoning trajectories, distinguishing CONST from generic active learning strategies (entropy sampling, BADGE, CEC) that were not designed for RLVR.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The lottery sample hypothesis, if it generalizes beyond mathematical reasoning with verifiable rewards, would dramatically reduce the annotation and compute costs of RLVR training—a major bottleneck in post-training LLMs. However, the current evidence is confined to math benchmarks with binary verifiable answers and an extreme budget of 4–8 samples out of 2048, leaving open whether the approach holds for open-ended tasks, non-verifiable rewards, or larger annotation budgets where the selection signal may dilute. The theoretical guarantee (Theorem 3.1) relies on the lottery sample hypothesis as an assumption rather than proving it, so the practical question of when and why such a small subset suffices remains unresolved.
