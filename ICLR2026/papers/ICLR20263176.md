## Problem

Scaling laws for nonlinear regression with anisotropic (power-law) input spectra remain largely unexplored, while the linear case is well understood. In the isotropic setting, phase retrieval dynamics collapse to a two-dimensional ODE, but anisotropy induces an infinite hierarchy of coupled equations that qualitatively changes the learning trajectory. The paper asks how the input spectrum governs finite-time convergence and whether explicit MSE scaling laws can be derived from the spectral decay exponent.

## Input / Output

The analysis takes anisotropic Gaussian measurements x ~ N(0, Q) with diagonal covariance Q having power-law eigenvalues λ_i ∝ i^{-a} (a > 1, tr(Q) = 1), a target vector w* normalized so that ‖Q^{1/2} w*‖ = 1, and a quadratic observation model y = ⟨x, w*⟩² + ξ. The output is a rigorous three-phase trajectory characterization (escape time T₁ ≈ log d, convergence time T₂ scaling as ε^{-2a/(a-1)} log(1/ε), and spectral-tail decay governed by S_d(τ)) together with explicit MSE scaling laws whose log-log slopes are determined by the exponent a. A key structural feature is that while the loss depends only on two summary statistics (u, s), their evolution under anisotropy requires tracking an infinite hierarchy of higher-order weighted moments s^{(k)}, u^{(k)}.

## Method

The core technique lifts the gradient flow to an infinite-dimensional Banach space C_b^∞(ℝ_{≥0}; ℓ²) by collecting all higher-order correlations into a single vector U(t), showing the dynamics reduce to a compact operator equation involving the right-shift operator B plus a rank-one perturbation. Duhamel's formula then yields a closed-form representation, and projecting onto the first coordinate produces a Volterra integral equation for u(t) whose Laplace transform has a dominant pole at ρ_true > 4λ₁, giving the exponential escape rate from mediocrity. Phase II convergence is analyzed by splitting the spectrum at a cutoff λ_c and balancing head (fast but many directions) against tail (slow but few directions) contributions, yielding the ε^{-2a/(a-1)} time scaling. Phase III reduces to coordinate-wise exponential relaxation ẇ_i ≈ 8λ_i(w_i* − w_i), whose MSE is a weighted spectral average S_d(τ) whose asymptotics follow from sum-integral comparisons and reveal distinct decay regimes depending on whether β_d τ ≪ 1, 1 ≪ x_d ≪ d, or x_d ≳ d.

## Results

The paper derives explicit scaling laws for anisotropic phase retrieval under power-law spectra (λ_i ∝ i^{-a}). The key quantitative findings are: (i) escape from mediocrity occurs at T₁ = O(log d), (ii) summary statistics converge to within ε at T₂ = T₁' + O(ε^{-2a/(a-1)} log(1/ε)), and (iii) post-convergence MSE decays as a weighted spectral average S_d(τ) with distinct regimes: linear decay for β_d τ ≪ 1, power-law decay 1 − Γ(1−1/a)·x_d/d for 1 ≪ x_d ≪ d, and exponential decay exp(−β_d τ d^{-a}) for x_d ≳ d. The exponent 2a/(a-1) in the convergence time decreases as a increases, meaning faster spectral decay yields shorter convergence times. Numerical experiments (Figures 1–3) confirm the predicted three-phase structure and the dependence of MSE decay slope on the spectral exponent a.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without standard empirical baselines; the primary comparison is between the anisotropic (power-law spectrum) and isotropic (Q = I) settings. In the isotropic case, the dynamics collapse to a closed two-dimensional ODE in (u, s), and convergence after escape is relatively fast. Under anisotropy, the dynamics form an infinite hierarchy of coupled ODEs with no finite-dimensional closure, and the paper shows this structural change produces a qualitative escape-convergence trade-off: escape from mediocrity can be faster (the growth rate ρ_true > 4λ₁ increases with a, as shown in Figure 3), but convergence to low MSE is slowed by the difficulty of learning small-eigenvalue directions. The paper also positions itself relative to prior scaling-law work: while linear models under power-law spectra are well understood (Caponnetto & De Vito, 2007; Cui et al., 2021; Wortsman & Loureiro, 2025), and isotropic quadratic networks have been analyzed (Ben Arous et al., 2025; Defilippis et al., 2025), this is the first rigorous characterization of compute-error scaling laws in a nonlinear model with anisotropic Gaussian inputs. The Duhamel-Volterra reduction and Laplace-transform/residue-calculus approach are novel tools for handling the infinite-dimensional structure that anisotropy introduces.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the well-studied isotropic phase retrieval / quadratic regression setting (where dynamics collapse to a two-dimensional ODE) to anisotropic Gaussian inputs with power-law spectra, a regime the authors note has 'remained largely isotropic' in the nonlinear literature. The analytical toolkit draws on Duhamel's formula, Volterra integral equations, and Laplace-transform/residue calculus from the classical ODE literature, repurposed here to tame an infinite hierarchy of coupled moment equations that has no finite-dimensional closure. It sits alongside recent anisotropic linear scaling-law work (Wortsman & Loureiro 2025) and isotropic nonlinear scaling-law analyses (Ben Arous et al. 2025, Defilippis et al. 2025), but is the first to derive compute-error scaling laws in a genuinely nonlinear model with power-law anisotropy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous three-phase decomposition (escape, macroscopic convergence, spectral-tail learning) and explicit MSE scaling exponents for nonlinear regression under anisotropic data, revealing a counterintuitive escape-convergence trade-off that has no isotropic analogue. Limitations are significant: results hold only in the continuous gradient-flow limit (not discrete SGD), for Gaussian inputs with a specific power-law form, and for a single quadratic neuron. Open questions include extending to discrete-time optimization, finite-sample effects, broader nonlinearities, and whether analogous phase decompositions govern training in wider networks—potentially bridging single-index theory and deep learning scaling laws.
