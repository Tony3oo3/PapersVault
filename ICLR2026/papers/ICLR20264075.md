## Problem

Asynchronous inference enables real-time robot execution by predicting the next action chunk while the current one is being executed, but naively combining it with action chunking causes substantial performance degradation. Prior work attributed this failure to inter-chunk discontinuity and developed test-time smoothing, but overlooked a second critical factor: intra-chunk inconsistency, where the first few actions inherited from the previous chunk (executed during the inference delay) are conditioned on stale observations and become misaligned with the current perception, creating a train-test distribution shift that no test-time correction can fully resolve.

## Input / Output

The policy conditions on RGB video frames (wrist + third-view cameras), a natural-language instruction, proprioceptive state, a discretized inference delay estimate d (sampled uniformly during training, estimated on the robot client at deployment), and the tail of the previously predicted action chunk. The output is a flow-matching velocity field v̂_π(A_t | o_t, d) that, when integrated over n steps (10 in practice), yields a full action chunk of length P (50 for π0 in real-world, 8 in Kinetix). Only the first h actions (h=8 in real-world) are executed per chunk before the next chunk takes over, and the overlapping prefix is frozen during sampling to preserve continuity.

## Method

REMAC adapts a pretrained flow-matching VLA policy (π0) via LoRA (≤1.5% extra parameters) using three training-time mechanisms. (1) Prefix Masking: a delay-conditioned binary mask m_d zeroes out supervision on the first d timesteps of the chunk, so the policy is only trained to predict the executable suffix; d is uniformly sampled from a shrinking interval [q_min, q_max] across training. (2) Self-conditioned Curriculum: the interpolation target is a mixture Â_t = γA_t + sg((1−γ)Ã_t) where Ã_t is the pretrained policy's own prediction (stop-gradient), with γ annealed from 1 to 0, gradually exposing the model to its own imperfect outputs to mimic test-time conditions. (3) Residual Alignment (L_Δ): an auxiliary loss that explicitly matches the correction (û_τ − Û_τ) to the residual (u_τ − Û_τ) on unmasked timesteps, encouraging the LoRA adapter to learn a targeted adjustment rather than a wholesale re-learn. At inference, Prefix-Preserved Sampling initializes the flow from the previous chunk's tail (rather than Gaussian noise) and freezes the overlapping prefix during integration, adding zero extra latency since LoRA weights are merged into the backbone.

## Results

On the Kinetix simulation benchmark (12 tasks), REMAC achieves an average success rate of 0.779 at d=4, substantially outperforming Naive Async (0.451) and exhibiting the smallest performance degradation as delay increases. On real-world Franka Research 3 grasp-and-place tasks, REMAC achieves average completion progress of 0.903 (Grasp-Easy), 0.943 (Grasp-Medium), and 0.812 (Grasp-Hard), surpassing the strongest baseline RTC by 8.0, 9.5, and 5.9 percentage points respectively. REMAC also achieves faster task completion (fewer steps) and smoother robot kinematics compared to all baselines, with no additional inference latency.

## Baselines & Comparisons

REMAC consistently outperforms all four baselines across both simulation and real-world settings. Against Naive Async (Shukor et al., 2025), REMAC gains 7.8–35.2 percentage points in completion progress on real-world tasks (e.g., 0.812 vs. 0.460 on Grasp-Hard) and 0.328 absolute success rate on Kinetix at d=4 (0.779 vs. 0.451). Against Temporal Ensembling (Zhao et al., 2023), REMAC improves by 7.8–9.5 points on real-world tasks, with the largest gap on Grasp-Hard (0.812 vs. 0.717). Against RTC (Black et al., 2025), the strongest test-time baseline, REMAC leads by 8.0 points on Grasp-Easy, 9.5 on Grasp-Medium, and 5.9 on Grasp-Hard, while also avoiding RTC's additional 55–64 ms inference latency. Against Bidirectional Decoding (Liu et al., 2025), REMAC is not directly compared in the main real-world table (BID is omitted due to high computational cost), but in simulation (Table 2) REMAC already matches or exceeds BID at all delay levels, and combining REMAC with BID yields only marginal further gains (e.g., 0.791 vs. 0.779 at d=4), confirming REMAC's superiority as a backbone policy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the flow-matching action-chunking paradigm (Lipman et al., 2023; Black et al., 2024) and the asynchronous inference framework introduced by Black et al. (2025), which discretizes inference delay and uses the previous chunk as a prior. It departs from prior test-time correction methods (Temporal Ensembling, Bidirectional Decoding, RTC) that focus exclusively on inter-chunk discontinuity by identifying intra-chunk inconsistency—a perception-action mismatch within a single chunk—as a distinct and previously overlooked failure mode. Rather than applying heuristic or gradient-based corrections at inference time, REMAC learns corrective adjustments at training time via masked supervision and a self-conditioned curriculum, using LoRA to preserve the pretrained policy's capacity while adding delay-awareness.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Real-time execution is a hard requirement for deployed robotic systems, and the method's zero-overhead design (LoRA merged into backbone, no extra inference steps) makes it directly deployable without the latency penalty that plagues RTC and BID. The finding that the approach generalizes beyond flow-matching to Transformer-based policies (ACT) and remains robust to noisy, fluctuating, and spiky delay estimates broadens its practical applicability. Key limitations include the need to pre-specify a maximum delay bound and the requirement for a moderate amount of finetuning data, while open questions remain about scaling to multi-robot coordination, longer prediction horizons, and whether the masked-chunking formulation transfers to reinforcement-learning-based policies.
