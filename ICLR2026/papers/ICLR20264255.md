## Problem

Existing LLM-based humor caption generation approaches rely on generic prompting, multi-hop reasoning, or task-specific tuning, which capture surface humor language rather than deep humor logical reasoning and creative humor imagining, leading to limited creativity, originality, and interpretability. This paper addresses the gap by grounding humor generation in the General Theory of Verbal Humor (GTVH) and proposing a structured multi-role framework that explicitly models the humor mechanism through script opposition and imaginative expansion.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264255/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a cartoon image (from the New Yorker Caption Contest) as input and produces a funny caption as output. The image is consumed by a conflicting-script extractor that derives a situation description and a set of script oppositions, which together form the foundation for all downstream generation. The output is conditioned on five knowledge resources—situation description, conflicting scripts, imagination trees, narrative strategy, and linguistic style—selected via depth-first search path sampling over the imagination trees, with a humor-relevance score pruning weakly related leaf nodes.

## Method

HOMER is a humor-theory-driven multi-role LLM collaboration framework with three roles: a Conflicting-script Extractor that grounds humor in GTVH script oppositions to capture core humor logic; a Retrieval-augmented Hierarchical Imaginator that identifies humor targets and expands their creative space through imagination trees built from multi-view LLM free-associations (deep-pattern backbone chains) and humor-relevance retrieval from a 335,570-joke database (broad-pattern expansion), pruned by a composite humor-relevance score combining WordNet-based relevance-opposition, humor-frequency, and POS-diversity terms; and a Caption Generator that samples a single imagination path via DFS and generates a caption conditioned on the extracted scripts, situation description, imagination path, and a narrative-strategy-by-linguistic-style configuration. The key differentiator is that humor mechanism is explicitly modeled through GTVH script opposition and structured imagination trees rather than left to LLM-inherent humor, enabling interpretable, theory-grounded, and retrieval-augmented creative generation.

## Results

With GPT-4o as the base LLM, HOMER achieves average improvements of 8.62% on pass@1, 6.48% on pass@3, and 5.91% on pass@5 over the best baseline across two New Yorker Cartoon datasets. On the most challenging #Top10 group of Humor in AI, HOMER reaches 66.41% pass@1, surpassing the strongest competitor HumorousAI (62.11%) by 4.3 points. On Electronic Sheep, HOMER attains 75.53% pass@1 on the High-Humor group and 79.45% on the Low-Humor group, outperforming all baselines by 8–14 points. The method also generalizes to the MemeCap dataset, achieving 83.33% pass@1 versus 76.67% for CLoT.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HOMER consistently outperforms all seven baselines across every evaluation group and all four base LLMs. Against the strongest humor-specific baselines, HOMER beats HumorousAI by 4.3 points (pass@1, #Top10, GPT-4o) and CLoT by 5.2 points on the same setting; on Electronic Sheep the gap widens to 8.1 and 12.2 points respectively. The generic reasoning strategies (CoT, Few-shot, Self-consistency) trail HOMER by 4–21 points on pass@1, with CoT being the weakest at 45.79% versus HOMER's 66.41%. Phunny, a pun-generation method, performs far below all other methods (16.09% pass@1), confirming that linguistic wordplay alone is insufficient for cartoon captioning. On the out-of-domain MemeCap dataset, HOMER still leads CLoT (the best competitor) by 6.7 points on pass@1, demonstrating cross-domain generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

HOMER repurposes the classical General Theory of Verbal Humor (GTVH, Attardo & Raskin 1991) as an explicit computational scaffold for LLM-based humor generation, departing from prior approaches (HumorousAI, CLoT, LoL) that rely on generic prompting, multi-hop reasoning chains, or task-specific fine-tuning to steer LLM outputs toward funnier captions. Architecturally, it combines a multi-agent LLM collaboration pattern (extractor → imaginator → generator) with retrieval-augmented generation over a curated joke database, a combination not previously applied to multimodal humor captioning. The 'imagination trees' that merge LLM free-association chains with humor-relevance retrieval, pruned by a WordNet-based scoring function (Wu-Palmer similarity + Jaccard conceptual opposition), represent a novel mechanism for expanding creative space in a controlled, interpretable way.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a principled, step-by-step decomposition of humor generation that is interpretable and editable—each stage (script extraction, target identification, imaginative expansion, caption synthesis) can be inspected or modified independently, addressing a key limitation of black-box reasoning-chain approaches. The finding that structured multi-agent guidance yields substantially larger gains with weaker base models (e.g., +21–46% with LLaVA-1.5 vs. +3–7% with GPT-4o) suggests the framework can partially compensate for limited model capability, which is practically relevant for resource-constrained deployments. Open questions include whether the GTVH decomposition generalizes beyond New Yorker-style cartoons to other humor formats (video, audio, multi-turn dialogue), whether the static WordNet-based scoring can be replaced by learned embeddings without losing interpretability, and the circularity concern of using GPT-5 as both the generation backbone (in some configurations) and the primary evaluator.
