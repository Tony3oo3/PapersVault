## Problem

Prior robust aggregation frameworks assume perfectly rational or adversarial experts, leaving open how to combine decisions from boundedly rational agents whose choices are stochastic. The paper asks whether simple majority voting remains optimal under a minimax regret criterion when experts follow quantal response, and whether the inherent randomness of imperfect experts can paradoxically improve collective accuracy over perfectly rational ones.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20265254/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The decision maker receives n anonymous binary reports (each expert outputs 0 or 1) and only observes the count x of experts reporting 1, not individual identities or signals. The aggregator f: {0,…,n} → [0,1] maps this count to a probability of guessing state ω=1. In the LLM experiments, the input is a text prompt (a Bayesian box-ball scenario or a MathQA multiple-choice question) queried at a specified temperature t, and the output is a single sampled response; 20 independent samples per question simulate n c.i.i.d. experts, and plurality/majority voting aggregates them into a final answer.

## Method

Experts' stochastic choices are modeled by the quantal response function ψ_λ(p) = 1/(1+e^{2λ(1−2p)}), where λ is a rationality parameter (λ→∞ recovers deterministic best-response). The key technical contribution is a geometric dimension-reduction lemma: encoding each posterior as a point (s, ψ_λ(s), s·ψ_λ(s)) in ℝ³, the paper proves no four points on this curve are coplanar, so by Carathéodory's theorem every report structure is representable by a three-signal structure {0, p, 1}, collapsing an infinite-dimensional optimization to three parameters. On this reduced space, the paper proves majority voting is the minimax-regret optimal aggregator whenever λ ≤ g(n) (a threshold decreasing in n), via a pairwise-optimality argument over symmetric signal-structure pairs. A second result shows that for n ≥ 2, a finite λ* can strictly outperform λ→∞ because stochastic reports preserve weak signal information that deterministic experts discard. Empirically, the LLM temperature t is shown to be the inverse of λ (t=0 → λ→∞, t=0.5 → λ≈13.25, t=1.0 → λ≈8.93 via logistic regression), and majority/plurality voting over GPT-4o-mini samples at moderate temperature outperforms deterministic sampling for n ≥ 3 on both Bayesian inference and MathQA tasks.

## Results

Theoretically, majority voting is proven to be the optimal robust aggregator (minimizing worst-case regret) when the rationality parameter λ falls below a group-size-dependent threshold g(n), with g(n) decreasing as n grows. Empirically, logistic regression on GPT-4o-mini confirms quantal response behavior with fitted rationality λ→∞ at t=0, λ=13.25 at t=0.5, and λ=8.93 at t=1.0 (all p-values ≈ 0). On the MathQA multiple-choice task (500 questions, 20 samples each), plurality voting with n≥3 experts at moderate temperature (t=0.5) achieves higher accuracy than the deterministic setting (t=0), while for n=1 the deterministic setting performs best. A simple random-follow rule performs equivalently to majority voting at n=1 but is consistently inferior for larger groups.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external named baselines; instead, it compares different configurations of the same LLM (GPT-4o-mini) under varying temperature and group size. The key comparison is between deterministic (t=0, 'perfectly rational') and stochastic (t=0.5, t=1.0, 'bounded rational') expert behavior: for n=1, t=0 yields the highest utility, but for n=3 and n=5, moderate temperature (t=0.5) outperforms t=0 under plurality/majority voting, confirming the theoretical bounded-rationality advantage. A random-follow baseline (equivalent to n=1 majority voting) is consistently inferior or equal to majority voting with n≥3. The theoretical threshold g(n) (visualized in Figure 4) shows that for n≤2, majority voting is optimal for any λ, while for n≥3 the threshold is finite and decreases with group size, meaning the bounded-rationality advantage is most pronounced in moderately sized groups with moderate stochasticity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the robust information aggregation framework of Arieli et al. (2018, 2023) and Levy and Razin (2022), which study minimax-regret aggregation under unknown signal structures, by replacing the standard perfect-rationality assumption with Quantal Response (QR) models from behavioral game theory (McKelvey and Palfrey, 1995). Prior robust aggregation work either assumes fully rational experts or adversarial agents; this paper occupies the middle ground of sincere-but-bounded experts, using a geometric dimension-reduction argument (no-four-points-coplanar + Carathéodory) to show that three signals suffice for the analysis. The empirical component repurposes the LLM temperature parameter as a natural instantiation of the QR rationality parameter, connecting the softmax output mechanism to the quantal response function in a way that prior LLM-behavior studies (Jia et al., 2025; Kirshner et al., 2025) have not formalized in an aggregation context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that majority voting is the optimal robust aggregator when expert rationality falls below a group-size-dependent threshold, and that moderately stochastic experts can collectively outperform perfectly rational ones—offers a principled justification for temperature diversity in LLM ensembles and for the 'wisdom of crowds' in settings with imperfect agents. Key limitations include the c.i.i.d. signal assumption, homogeneous rationality across experts, and binary decision settings; extending to correlated signals, heterogeneous rationality levels, and multi-action problems remains open. The practical takeaway for AI practitioners is concrete: when aggregating multiple LLM responses, a moderate temperature (e.g., 0.5) with majority/plurality voting can beat deterministic (temperature-0) outputs for groups of three or more, a finding with direct implications for ensemble design in reasoning and QA pipelines.
