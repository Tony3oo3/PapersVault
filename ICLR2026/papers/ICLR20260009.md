## Problem

Video diffusion models struggle to generate realistic manipulation videos because they cannot simultaneously capture abstract semantic understanding (e.g., which object to manipulate) and fine-grained contact-rich dynamics. Existing motion-transfer methods that impose strong control signals (depth, hand meshes, drag points) reduce flexibility and produce physically implausible results, while reference-video-based approaches fail to handle scale misalignments and complex multi-object interactions in manipulation scenarios.

## Input / Output

The method takes a 16-frame reference manipulation video (320×512), a single target initial image, and a natural-language text description as input. Stage I outputs a temporally coherent sequence of interaction masks (one per frame) that encode pixel-level object state while accommodating non-rigid deformations; Stage II consumes these masks together with the target image and the reference video pair to render the final 16-frame manipulation video. The mask-based representation was chosen over optical flow or point trajectories because it remains stable under large non-rigid deformations (e.g., cloth folding) and inherently encodes the semantic separation between manipulator and manipulated object.

## Method

MIMIC decomposes manipulation video generation into two stages built on the DynamiCrafter UNet backbone. In Stage I, an Interaction-Motion-Aware (IMA) module extracts CLIP visual embeddings from the reference video and interaction mask embeddings, fuses them with a learnable query via cross-attention and FFN, then injects the resulting IMA embedding into the UNet through a zero-initialized cross-attention layer to guide mask sequence generation; training proceeds in two phases (static first-frame recognition, then temporal motion). In Stage II, a Pair Prompt Control mechanism (ControlNet-style) conditions generation on both the predicted target mask sequence and the original reference video–mask pair, using separate lightweight Query and Pair Encoders with zero-convolution outputs to disentangle object motion from camera motion; an adaptive region loss reweights the diffusion objective to emphasize mask-aligned regions across time, reducing ghosting artifacts and improving temporal consistency in interaction areas.

## Results

MIMIC achieves the best performance on 6 out of 7 metrics in the main comparison (Table 1), including Appearance Consistency (0.9084), Subject Consistency (0.9291), Background Stability (0.9385), Interaction Rationality (4.14/5), Semantic Similarity (2.91/5), and Human Preference (42.88%). The only metric where MIMIC does not lead is Text Alignment (0.2721), where the training-free MotionClone method scores higher (0.2947). In human preference evaluation, MIMIC is selected as a top-2 video in 42.88% of trials, nearly doubling the next-best method FlexiAct (27.8%) and substantially outperforming all other baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MIMIC outperforms all five baselines on the manipulation-specific MLLM metrics: Interaction Rationality (4.14 vs. best baseline FlexiAct at 3.55) and Semantic Similarity (2.91 vs. FlexiAct at 2.52), indicating substantially better preservation of manipulation intent from the reference video. On temporal quality metrics, MIMIC leads in Subject Consistency (0.9291 vs. DynamiCrafter 0.9185) and Background Stability (0.9385 vs. DynamiCrafter 0.9331), while also achieving the highest Appearance Consistency (0.9084 vs. FlexiAct 0.8999). The closest competitor is FlexiAct, which also uses a reference-video strategy but relies on global motion representations; MIMIC's advantage over FlexiAct is most pronounced in Interaction Rationality (+0.59) and Human Preference (+15.1 percentage points), reflecting FlexiAct's difficulty with multi-object manipulation and physically implausible motions. MotionClone, the only training-free baseline, achieves the highest Text Alignment (0.2947) but performs poorly on all other metrics (e.g., Appearance Consistency 0.7400, Subject Consistency 0.6833), suggesting it captures text semantics at the expense of visual fidelity and motion quality. DynamiCrafter and CogVideoX, adapted via one-shot fine-tuning, show moderate performance but are outperformed by MIMIC on every metric except Text Alignment, where the gap is small (≤0.004).

## Relation to Prior Work

MIMIC builds on the DynamiCrafter I2V diffusion backbone and ControlNet-style conditioning, but departs from prior motion-transfer methods (MotionClone, MotionDirector, FlexiAct) by decomposing generation into two explicit stages: an IMA-attention-guided mask synthesis stage and a Pair Prompt Control rendering stage. Unlike interaction video generation approaches that impose fine-grained geometric constraints (hand meshes, depth maps, bounding boxes), MIMIC uses reference-video-driven semantic masks as an intermediate representation, avoiding rigid control signals while still capturing manipulation intent. The Pair Prompt Control mechanism—conditioning on both predicted masks and the original reference video to disambiguate object versus camera motion—is a novel architectural contribution not present in prior reference-conditioned generation work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MIMIC addresses a practical bottleneck for embodied intelligence by providing a scalable pipeline to synthesize manipulation training data from reference demonstrations, with the two-stage decomposition offering interpretability (masks are inspectable) and controllability absent in single-stage approaches. Key limitations include the 16-frame cap inherited from DynamiCrafter, which precludes long-horizon manipulation sequences, and persistent failures under severe occlusion and fine-grained dexterous tasks (e.g., twisting a bottle cap). Open questions include whether the mask-based motion representation can be replaced by richer signals as segmentation improves, and whether the framework scales to more powerful DiT-based backbones for longer, more complex action compositions.
