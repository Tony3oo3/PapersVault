## Problem

The rise of large reasoning models (e.g., OpenAI o1, DeepSeek-R1) has made reference-based reward systems—where model outputs are scored against ground-truth answers—a central component of RL training. However, all existing reward benchmarks evaluate models via pairwise preference comparisons, leaving no standardized way to assess the absolute correctness verification that these reasoning models actually rely on during training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264874/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each benchmark instance is a quadruple (query, ground-truth reference answer, model-generated completion, binary correctness label), where the verifier must judge a single completion against an explicit reference rather than choosing between two candidates. The reference answer is provided in the prompt, and the expected output is a binary correct/incorrect judgment; accuracy is computed as the fraction of instances where the verifier's predicted label matches the human-annotated ground truth. VerifyBench enforces a balanced 1:1 correct-to-incorrect ratio per question, while VerifyBench-Hard reflects the natural skew toward incorrect completions found in disagreement-selected samples.

## Method

The benchmark is constructed through a multi-stage pipeline: 8,000 questions are curated from 41 open-source datasets spanning general, logical, and mathematical reasoning; completions are generated by 22 open-source and proprietary LLMs; and each instance is verified by at least two human annotators with meta-annotator disagreement resolution. VerifyBench (2,000 samples) is balanced across four answer types (numeric, expression, multi-choice, string) with exactly one correct and one incorrect completion per question. VerifyBench-Hard (1,000 samples) is built by selecting cases where five top-performing LLMs split 2-vs-3 in their correctness judgments, then human-annotating those contentious instances. Evaluation covers three verifier families—rule-based (math-verify), LLM-as-a-judge (18+ models from 1B to 235B parameters), and purpose-trained model-based verifiers (xVerify, CompassVerifier, RLVR)—and a correlation study validates the benchmark's practical utility by using verifiers of varying VerifyBench accuracy as reward signals in GRPO reinforcement learning and rejection sampling fine-tuning, showing that higher benchmark scores predict greater downstream task improvement.

## Results

On VerifyBench, the strongest LLM-as-a-judge verifiers achieve 95.85% average accuracy (gpt-oss-120b) and 95.15% (DeepSeek-R1-0528), while the rule-based math-verify baseline reaches only 66.95%. On the substantially harder VerifyBench-Hard, the best performers drop to 87.90% (gpt-oss-120b and CompassVerifier-32B), representing a ~20-point degradation that exposes significant verification failures on contentious cases. Smaller models (<3B parameters) perform markedly worse, with Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct achieving only 44.15% on VerifyBench and 25.60% on VerifyBench-Hard. Ablation without reference answers causes a 5–19% accuracy drop, confirming the critical role of ground-truth references in verification.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The rule-based math-verify baseline is the weakest on VerifyBench (66.95% AVG), particularly failing on multi-choice (55.00%) and string (51.60%) answer types, though it recovers somewhat on VerifyBench-Hard (76.00%) where numeric and expression cases dominate. Among LLM-as-a-judge verifiers, gpt-oss-120b leads on both benchmarks (95.85% VB, 87.90% VB-H), narrowly edging out DeepSeek-R1-0528 (95.15%, 86.60%) and gpt-oss-20b (94.80%, 84.30%). The specialized model-based verifiers show a clear size-scaling trend: CompassVerifier-32B (92.55% VB, 87.90% VB-H) outperforms CompassVerifier-7B (91.75%, 85.90%) and xVerify-8B (91.95%, 83.10%), with CompassVerifier-32B matching gpt-oss-120b on VB-H. Notably, Tencent-Qwen2.5-7B-RLVR achieves strong VerifyBench accuracy (94.00%) but drops sharply to 72.20% on VerifyBench-Hard, indicating fragility on contentious cases. Reference-free reward models (Qwen2.5-Math-RM-72B at 78.00%, Qwen2-Math-RM-72B at 75.90% on VerifyBench without references) perform substantially below reference-based verifiers, confirming that ground-truth answers are essential for accurate verification.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in reward model evaluation (RewardBench, RM-Bench), which assesses verifiers via pairwise preference ranking between two responses. Motivated by the shift toward reference-based reward systems in reasoning model training (DeepSeek-R1, OpenAI o1, Seed1.5-Thinking), the authors reframe the evaluation task as absolute correctness verification against a ground-truth reference answer. The benchmark construction draws on existing open-source reasoning datasets and leverages multiple LLMs for completion generation, while the 'hard' variant introduces a novel disagreement-based sampling strategy to isolate genuinely ambiguous cases. The correlation analysis further connects benchmark scores to downstream RL training outcomes (GRPO, RFT), a validation step absent from prior reward benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This benchmark fills a critical evaluation gap: as reasoning models increasingly rely on reference-based reward systems in RL training, there was no standardized way to measure whether a verifier correctly judges a single response against ground truth. The correlation analysis demonstrating that VerifyBench scores predict RL training gains provides practical guidance for selecting verifiers. Key limitations include the binary scoring scheme (no partial credit), exclusion of proof-based problems, coverage limited to three reasoning domains, and inability to detect reward hacking. Open questions include whether fine-grained process-level verification can be benchmarked similarly, how to evaluate verifiers on out-of-domain reasoning, and whether the disagreement-based hard set construction generalizes beyond the specific model ensemble used.
