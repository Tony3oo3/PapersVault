## Problem

Existing knowledge editing methods for code LLMs (ROME, MEMIT, PMET) operate on standard MLP layers where neurons are polysemantic, resulting in imprecise edits with poor interpretability and unintended interference with unrelated knowledge. Additionally, no benchmark exists for evaluating knowledge editing in code-to-code translation using functional equivalence rather than superficial textual similarity.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260602/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a correction knowledge four-tuple (r^(1), s, r^(2), o*) where s is a Java source snippet, r^(1) and r^(2) are prefix/suffix contexts, and o* is the manually corrected D-language target. It produces a sparse, neuron-level update to the TransCoder decoder weight matrix—modifying only the columns indexed by active neurons (typically <1% of the intermediate dimension, e.g., 69 out of 16,384 in LTC4). Evaluation is performed via the G4GD dataset (600 Java functions, each with 10 D unit tests), where success is determined by functional equivalence through test execution rather than string matching.

## Method

TCPE replaces a single MLP layer (typically layer 19) with a TransCoder module—a wide encoder-decoder pair trained with L1 sparsity regularization so that only a small fraction of hidden features activate per token. Correction knowledge is encoded as a key-value pair: the key k* is the mean TransCoder encoder activation at the final token position of the prompt, and the value v* is obtained by solving for a minimal perturbation δ that steers the model to predict the corrected target o*. The update follows ROME's rank-one formula but is sparsified to touch only the decoder columns corresponding to active neurons (activation > τ), ensuring that each edit modifies a tiny, semantically specific subset of parameters. This sparsity and monosemanticity of TransCoder neurons yields both higher editing precision (improving CodeLlama-7b-Instruct Java-to-D accuracy from 57.5% to 64.0%) and neuron-level interpretability, where top-activating examples for edited neurons directly correspond to the target error tokens.

## Results

On the G4GD benchmark (Java-to-D translation), TCPE(LTC8) achieves the highest composite Score of 174.82, improving CodeLlama-7b-Instruct's translation accuracy from 58.17% (pre-edit) to 64.00% (post-edit), with a Reliability of 110.03% and Destructiveness of only 5.73%. The best baseline, LoRA, reaches a Score of 147.27 with 60.33% post-edit accuracy. On HumanEval, TCPE preserves the model's original functional accuracy (AC_post = AC_pre = 29.27%), while ROME, MEMIT, and PMET degrade it to 26.83%, 21.91%, and 15.24% respectively. On NLP benchmarks (zsRE, CounterFact), TCPE achieves substantially higher specificity (70.9% and 62.5%) than ROME (49.7% and 46.9%) and MEMIT (46.1% and 43.3%) while updating only 30–50 neurons versus 11,008–55,040.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TCPE outperforms all nine baselines on the composite Score in the multi-error editing scenario, with TCPE(LTC8) scoring 174.82 versus the best baseline LoRA at 147.27 and FiNE at 144.50. The advantage is most pronounced on Reliability (TCPE 110.03 vs. LoRA 104.93, ROME 99.71, MEMIT 82.03, PMET 69.57) and Destructiveness (TCPE 5.73 vs. LoRA 10.44, ROME 16.23, MEMIT 22.32, PMET 38.55), indicating TCPE corrects targeted errors while causing far fewer unintended regressions. On HumanEval, TCPE perfectly preserves the model's pre-edit accuracy (29.27%), whereas ROME drops it to 26.83%, MEMIT to 21.91%, and PMET to 15.24%—a consequence of updating 11,008–55,040 neurons versus TCPE's 69. On NLP benchmarks (zsRE and CounterFact), TCPE achieves the highest specificity (70.9% and 62.5%) by updating only 30–50 neurons, compared to ROME (49.7%, 46.9%), MEMIT (46.1%, 43.3%), and PMET (64.6%, 58.7%), confirming that the polysemanticity of standard MLP neurons is the primary source of low specificity in ROME-based methods. Few-shot prompting achieves a higher Score (120.20) than ROME or MEMIT but does not permanently modify model weights, while AGRACE and WISE show minimal efficacy (GN < 4%) in the code domain, suggesting that text-based validation metrics are insufficient for code knowledge editing.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ROME→MEMIT→PMET→FiNE lineage of locate-and-edit knowledge editing by replacing the target of intervention: instead of updating polysemantic MLP neurons, TCPE swaps the MLP for a TransCoder (Dunefsky et al., 2024) and performs rank-one updates only on the sparsely-activated, monosemantic neurons of the TransCoder decoder. The rank-one update mechanism and key-value store formulation are inherited directly from ROME (Meng et al., 2022), while the architectural substrate comes from the TransCoder's sparse autoencoder design. The paper also departs from prior KE evaluation, which relies on text-based metrics (exact match, paraphrase accuracy), by introducing a functional-equivalence-based benchmark (KECode/G4GD) tailored to code-to-code translation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic evaluation of knowledge editing methods in a code-translation setting and identifies polysemanticity of MLP neurons as a concrete cause of low specificity in ROME-based approaches, supported by clean ablations showing that ~69 active TransCoder neurons suffice where 11,008–55,040 MLP neurons are updated by baselines. However, practical applicability is limited: TCPE requires replacing an MLP layer with a trained TransCoder (an architectural modification infeasible for closed-source models), experiments cover only Java-to-D translation on CodeLlama-7b, and the G4GD dataset is small (600 examples). Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to other language pairs, scales to larger models, and remains stable under repeated or accumulated edits.
