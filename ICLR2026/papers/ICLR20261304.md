## Problem

Existing LLM layer pruning research proposes increasingly sophisticated layer-selection metrics (Block Influence, Taylor scores, PPL-based scoring) and defaults to LoRA for post-pruning fine-tuning, yet no systematic study has verified whether these complex choices are actually necessary. The paper asks two concrete questions: are simple heuristics sufficient for identifying redundant layers, and is LoRA truly the best recovery strategy after pruning?

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained LLM with uniform transformer block dimensions (Llama-3.1-8B-It, Llama-3-8B, Llama-3-70B) and a small calibration set (10 BookCorpus samples truncated to 128 tokens for scoring; WikiText2 for PPL). It produces a pruned model with a fixed number of layers removed (e.g., 8 of 32 layers, yielding a 6.3B model), which is then fine-tuned on Alpaca-cleaned. The final output can optionally be further compressed via 4-bit GPTQ quantization, reducing memory from ~12 GB to ~1.3 GB with modest accuracy loss.

## Method

The paper's core finding is that a two-step recipe—(1) reverse-order pruning (simply deleting the last N layers) and (2) partial-layer fine-tuning (freezing all layers except lm_head and the last three remaining layers)—outperforms both complex scoring metrics and LoRA/QLoRA fine-tuning. The theoretical justification comes from a gradient-flow analysis of Pre-LN Transformers: the spectral norm of the LayerNorm/RMSNorm Jacobian is strictly less than 1 (Theorems 4.1–4.2), so the product of Jacobians over many layers causes gradients reaching deep layers to be attenuated, making those layers less influential. This explains why the last layers are the safest to remove and why fully fine-tuning only the few remaining deep layers (which bear the largest distribution shift after pruning) is more effective than distributing small LoRA updates across all layers.

## Results

On Llama-3.1-8B-It (25% pruning), the proposed method (reverse-order pruning + partial-layer fine-tuning) achieves 58.07% average accuracy across 8 benchmarks, surpassing the best baseline GRASP (52.45%) by 5.62% and the worst (ShortGPT, 40.80%) by 17.27%. On Llama-3-8B, it reaches 56.33% average accuracy, outperforming FINERCUT (53.97%) by 2.36% and ShortGPT (36.88%) by 19.45%. On the larger Llama-3-70B model (20% pruning), the method achieves 64.11% average accuracy, beating the strongest prior baseline PIP (59.77%) by 4.34%. On GSM8K reasoning, the pruned model retains 29.19% accuracy at 25% pruning, far exceeding all baselines (best: COMPACT at 10.80%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method (reverse-order pruning + partial-layer fine-tuning of lm_head and last 3 layers) outperforms all 13 baselines across every model and pruning rate tested. On Llama-3.1-8B-It at 25% pruning, it beats the strongest baseline GRASP (52.45%) by 5.62 points and LLM-Streamline (51.88%) by 6.19 points, while the weakest baseline ShortGPT (BI) lags by 17.27 points. On Llama-3-8B, FINERCUT is the closest competitor at 53.97%, trailing the proposed method (56.33%) by 2.36 points, while ShortGPT (36.88%) and DeeperLayers (40.96%) fall far behind. On Llama-3-70B at 20% pruning, PIP is the best prior method (59.77%) but is outperformed by 4.34 points. At varying pruning rates on Llama-3.1-8B (Table 6), the method consistently leads COMPACT (the strongest baseline) by 0.87 points at ~10% pruning, 4.21 points at ~20%, and 12.98 points at ~35%. On GSM8K reasoning, the gap is most dramatic: the proposed method achieves 29.19% at 25% pruning versus COMPACT's 10.80% at 20%, a 18.39-point margin.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the gradient-flow analysis of Pre-LN Transformers established by Xiong et al. (2020) and Takase et al. (2023), extending it to formally explain why deep layers contribute less to performance and why fine-tuning only the last few layers after pruning is effective. It departs from the dominant line of layer-pruning work (ShortGPT, Shortened LLaMA, SLEB, LaCo, SliceGPT, GRASP) by questioning two foundational assumptions: that sophisticated layer-selection metrics (BI, Taylor, PPL, Magnitude) are necessary, and that LoRA is the optimal post-pruning fine-tuning strategy. Rather than proposing yet another scoring function, the paper takes a step back to benchmark existing metrics under a unified protocol and identifies a trivially simple strategy (reverse-order pruning + partial-layer fine-tuning) that outperforms all of them.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: practitioners can replace complex scoring pipelines and LoRA fine-tuning with a two-step recipe (prune last N layers, fine-tune lm_head + last 3 layers) that is faster, simpler, and more accurate. The finding that LoRA—the de facto standard in the pruning literature—is suboptimal for post-pruning recovery challenges a widely adopted default and may redirect fine-tuning choices in the broader PEFT community. Limitations include evaluation primarily on zero-shot classification benchmarks (8 datasets) and Llama-family architectures; whether the reverse-order + partial-layer recipe generalizes to MoE models, non-Pre-LN architectures, or generation-centric tasks (beyond GSM8K) remains open. The theoretical analysis, while illuminating, relies on spectral-norm upper bounds and a Gaussian assumption that may not tightly characterize real trained models.
