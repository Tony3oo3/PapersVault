## Problem

LLMs as predictors on text-attributed graphs struggle in few-shot semi-supervised settings because they lack structural inductive bias to generate reliable pseudo labels, and fine-tuning with noisy pseudo labels causes performance degradation. The paper addresses two unresolved challenges: how to select reliable pseudo-labeled nodes that go beyond LLMs' inherent knowledge, and how to extract knowledge from those pseudo labels while mitigating label noise during LLM fine-tuning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261021/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a text-attributed graph G=(V,E,A,X) where nodes carry raw text content and edges encode relationships (citations, co-purchases), along with a small labeled set of k nodes per class (3-, 5-, or 10-shot). A 2-layer GCN processes the graph structure while Llama-3-8B-Instruct (with LoRA rank 8) processes the textual node features. The output is a predicted class label for each unlabeled test node, with the framework also supporting zero-shot cross-dataset transfer where the fine-tuned LLM generalizes to unseen label spaces without additional training.

## Method

GNN-as-Judge positions a GNN as a structural judge to guide LLM pseudo-labeling through three mechanisms. First, an influence-guided selection strategy uses a provable upper bound on node influence (decaying with shortest-path distance and geometric mean of node degrees) to identify the top-K unlabeled nodes most strongly influenced by labeled data. Second, a collaborative pseudo-label selection partitions these nodes into an agreement set (where GNN and LLM concur, used for instruction tuning) and a disagreement set (where they diverge, filtered by a GNN preference score threshold τ to retain only high-confidence 'hard' examples). Third, a unified weakly-supervised fine-tuning loss combines standard instruction tuning on agreement nodes with ORPO-based preference tuning on disagreement nodes, where the GNN's prediction serves as the preferred response and the LLM's own prediction as the dispreferred one, enabling the LLM to learn relative preferences without requiring absolute correctness from either model.

## Results

GNN-as-Judge achieves the best node classification accuracy across all five datasets and all three shot settings (3, 5, 10-shot). In the most challenging 3-shot setting, it reaches 77.89% on Cora, 73.59% on Citeseer, 87.12% on Pubmed, 62.21% on ogbn-arxiv, and 81.02% on ogbn-products, outperforming the strongest baseline (TAPE) by 4.2–13.9 percentage points. The advantage is most pronounced in low-resource regimes: at 3-shot on ogbn-arxiv, GNN-as-Judge (62.21%) surpasses the next-best method (Zero-Shot LLM at 50.18%) by over 12 points. In the 10-shot setting, gains narrow but remain consistent, with GNN-as-Judge reaching 90.17% on Pubmed and 82.48% on ogbn-products.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GNN-as-Judge outperforms all nine baselines on every dataset and shot setting. The closest competitor is TAPE (LLM-as-Enhancer), which at 3-shot achieves 73.71% on Cora and 69.64% on ogbn-products, still trailing GNN-as-Judge by 4.2 and 11.4 points respectively; the gap narrows at 10-shot (79.33 vs. 80.71 on Cora) but GNN-as-Judge maintains a 2.9–12.5 point lead across all datasets. Classical GNNs (GCN, SGC) are competitive on small citation networks at 10-shot (e.g., SGC 78.49% vs. GNN-as-Judge 80.71% on Cora) but fall far behind on large-scale graphs (ogbn-arxiv: 51.89% vs. 67.88%). LLM-as-Predictor prompting strategies (Zero-Shot, Graph-CoT, w.Neighbor) show strong zero-shot performance on Pubmed and ogbn-products but lack the structural inductive bias to exploit graph connectivity, resulting in 10–15 point deficits on ogbn-arxiv. LLM-Graph integration methods LLaGA and GraphGPT perform worst overall (e.g., LLaGA 54.79% on Cora 3-shot), suggesting that encoding graph structure into LLM tokens constrains generalization rather than helping. LLM-GNN and GLEM, which combine GNN and LLM components, are outperformed by 4–20 points, indicating that the GNN-as-Judge collaborative pseudo-labeling and preference-tuning strategy extracts substantially more signal from the same labeled data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the pseudo-labeling paradigm for semi-supervised learning (Lee et al., 2013) to the text-attributed graph setting by introducing a GNN as a structural 'judge' that guides which unlabeled nodes receive pseudo labels and how they are used for LLM fine-tuning. It departs from prior LLM-as-Predictors graph methods (LLaGA, GraphGPT, TAPE, LLM-GNN) that assume abundant labeled data, by explicitly targeting the few-shot regime and leveraging GNN message-passing to compensate for LLMs' lack of structural inductive bias. The preference tuning component repurposes ORPO (Hong et al., 2024) from human-feedback alignment to GNN-feedback alignment, treating the GNN's prediction as the 'preferred' response over the LLM's own prediction on disagreement nodes. The node influence-based subset selection draws on graph influence literature (Xu et al., 2018; Wang & Leskovec, 2020) but applies it specifically to pseudo-label candidate selection for LLM training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap: LLMs for graph node classification have been studied almost exclusively in supervised settings, yet real-world graphs are sparsely labeled. The GNN-as-Judge paradigm offers a reusable recipe for combining structural and semantic signals in low-resource graph learning, and the preference-tuning-on-disagreement strategy provides a principled way to exploit noisy pseudo labels without overfitting. Limitations include reliance on a single GNN architecture (GCN) and a single LLM (Llama-3-8B), strong theoretical assumptions (conditional independence, uniform error distribution), and computational overhead from jointly training a GNN and fine-tuning an LLM. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to heterophilic graphs, larger LLMs, and whether the GNN-as-Judge role can be replaced by lighter structural proxies at scale.
