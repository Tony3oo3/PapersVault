## Problem

Scaling RL training for vision-language models is bottlenecked by two system-level issues: centralized vision-data loading turns the master node into an I/O and CPU-memory straggler (accounting for 57% of step time in veRL), and extreme sequence-length skew from mixing short image-text prompts with long video contexts causes severe cross-GPU compute and memory imbalance. Existing load-balancing methods either target text-only models or rely on large batch sizes and gradient accumulation that are incompatible with RL's small-batch, iterative rollout-update loop.

## Input / Output

The system consumes heterogeneous multimodal batches containing text prompts, high-resolution images, and video clips (up to tens of thousands of tokens per sample) alongside a VLM (MiMo-VL-7B or Qwen2.5-VL-32B). Unconventionally, the single controller never materializes pixel data; it schedules using lightweight FakeTensors that carry only shape metadata (video length, frame count, image resolution, file path), while actual visual tensors are decoded and fetched asynchronously on worker nodes. The output is updated VLM parameters after GRPO training with a maximum response length of 1024 tokens.

## Method

FlexRL introduces two complementary mechanisms. ShadowLoader decentralizes data loading by keeping only metadata on the controller, dispatching heavy preprocessing (video decoding, frame sampling) to worker-side Local Preprocessors, and asynchronously materializing tensors via a Materializer that queries a MetaStore, thereby hiding I/O behind GPU computation. FlexUlysses repurposes Ulysses sequence parallelism as a load-balancing primitive: instead of treating sequences as indivisible units, it adaptively assigns each sequence a power-of-two sharding degree based on its length, places shards into hierarchical (nested) device groups via a cost-aware greedy heuristic, and executes attention using a highest-sharding-first (HSF) schedule that guarantees deadlock-free collective ordering while pipelining all-to-all communication with attention computation across independent sequence groups. A vision-tower balancing strategy distributes images and video frames evenly across GPUs to handle the near-linear compute scaling of the encoder.

## Results

FlexRL achieves up to 8.47× end-to-end throughput improvement (tokens/s/GPU) over veRL+Bucketing on 128-GPU clusters, with the largest gains in the Only-Video setting where sequence lengths are longest and most heterogeneous. In image-heavy settings, the speedup reaches up to 7.35×, and in video-heavy settings up to 5.35×. On the 7B model with 32 GPUs, FlexRL delivers 525.93 tokens/s/GPU on Only-Video versus 63.96 for veRL+Bucketing and 207.03 for Ulysses-SP. The 32B model on 64 GPUs shows similar relative gains, with FlexRL reaching 221.07 tokens/s/GPU on Only-Video versus 47.21 for veRL+Bucketing.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FlexRL is compared against two baselines: veRL+Bucketing (the original veRL hybrid-controller system with naive sequence bucketing) and Ulysses-SP (conventional sequence parallelism with a fixed sharding degree of 2, 4, or 8). FlexRL consistently outperforms both across all dataset settings, model scales (7B and 32B), and cluster sizes (32, 64, and 128 GPUs). Against veRL+Bucketing, the speedup ranges from 4.7× (32B Image-Heavy, 64 GPUs) to 8.47× (7B Only-Video, 128 GPUs), with veRL also suffering CPU/GPU OOM errors at higher frame counts. Against Ulysses-SP, FlexRL achieves 2.5–8× higher throughput; Ulysses-SP's fixed sharding degree incurs substantial all-to-all communication overhead that dominates as batch size grows, while FlexUlysses' adaptive per-sequence sharding reaches a balance ratio of 1.0 (max/mean per-GPU attention FLOPs) with only 1.50× throughput over Ulysses-SP in the controlled 32-GPU comparison. The gap widens for video-heavy and only-video workloads where sequence-length skew is most extreme.

## Relation to Prior Work

FlexRL builds directly on the veRL (HybridFlow) RL framework, extending it from text-only LLMs to multimodal VLMs where centralized data loading and extreme sequence-length skew create new system bottlenecks. For load balancing, it departs from both fixed-parallelism bucketing (which fails under length outliers) and heterogeneous-DP methods like FlexSP, HotSPa, and ByteScale (which require large batch sizes incompatible with RL's small-batch rollout-update loop). The key conceptual move is repurposing Ulysses sequence parallelism—originally a memory-partitioning mechanism—as an adaptive, per-sequence compute-balancing primitive, combined with a hierarchical device-group hierarchy that enables deadlock-free scheduling across mixed sharding degrees.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As VLM RL (MiMo-VL, Kimi-VL, GLM-4.5V) becomes the dominant alignment paradigm, the system-level inefficiencies identified here—57% of step time in data loading, severe cross-GPU imbalance from video-length variation—directly limit how many models and datasets can be trained at scale. The practical impact is significant: practitioners running VLM RL on 128+ GPUs can expect order-of-magnitude throughput gains by adopting the ShadowLoader + FlexUlysses combination. Open questions include whether the hierarchical device-group constraint (nested partitions) sacrifices optimality for scheduling simplicity, how the approach scales beyond p_max=8 on larger clusters, and whether the cost model (quadratic attention proxy) remains accurate for emerging architectures with linear or sparse attention.
