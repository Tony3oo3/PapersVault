## Problem

Existing MGT detectors suffer severe accuracy drops (20–40%) on zero-shot languages and remain highly vulnerable to simple adversarial perturbations such as replacing a few words. Most adversarial training methods operate in monolingual settings and require large-scale multilingual corpora, which are unavailable for medium- and low-resource languages that typically only have access to translation dictionaries.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264801/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The detector consumes a single multilingual text passage (any of 9 languages in M4GT) and outputs a binary MGT-vs-HWT classification. During training, the attacker additionally consumes a static translation dictionary (covering Arabic, Chinese, German, Russian) and a surrogate GPT-2 model to identify which tokens to code-switch; the detector itself only sees English-labeled data plus the attacker-generated code-switched samples, never the dictionaries directly. The language discriminator attached to the [CLS] embedding is a 3-layer MLP (768→512→128→2) with ~0.5M parameters.

## Method

TASTE couples a translation-dictionary-based attacker with a target detector in a synchronous co-evolution loop. The attacker uses a surrogate model to compute gradient-based token importance scores (L2 norm of ∂L_cls/∂h_t), selects the top-k most important tokens, and replaces them with dictionary translations to produce code-switched adversarial examples; the surrogate is continuously distilled from the target detector's pseudo-labels. The detector is trained with two losses: a Robustness-Preserving Loss (cross-entropy on both original and adversarial samples) and a novel Language-Agnostic Adversarial Loss (LAAL) that attaches a language discriminator via a gradient-reversal layer to the [CLS] embedding, forcing the encoder to erase language-specific cues and retain only semantic signals. Attack strength follows a curriculum (|I| grows from 1 token to 5% of sequence length), and both attacker and detector are updated within the same training step, enabling the detector to generalize to unseen languages and unseen attack strategies without ever seeing multilingual training data.

## Results

On the M4GT dataset (9 languages), TASTE achieves the best average accuracy of 0.762 and F1 of 0.773, outperforming the second-best system (mBERT) by +0.071 Acc and +0.064 F1. On zero-shot languages (Bulgarian, Indonesian, Italian, Urdu), TASTE reaches 0.786 F1, a +0.020 improvement over mBERT (0.766). For robustness, TASTE attains the lowest average Attack Success Rate (ASR) of 18.0% across 8 attack types, a 3.8-point absolute reduction over the strongest SOTA defense GREATER-D (21.8%) and a 23.7-point reduction over the best metric-based baseline Fast DetectGPT (41.7%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

TASTE outperforms all 8 baselines on both overall and zero-shot average metrics. Against the strongest non-adversarial model-based baseline mBERT (Acc 0.684, F1 0.709), TASTE gains +0.078 Acc and +0.064 F1 overall, with the largest zero-shot gains on German (+0.216 Acc) and Russian (+0.199 Acc). Among adversarial-training baselines, TASTE surpasses GREATER-D (the strongest SOTA defense) by +0.093 Acc and +0.092 F1 overall, and by +0.052 Acc / +0.055 F1 on zero-shot languages. RADAR, which uses monolingual paraphrase-based adversarial training, is outperformed by TASTE by +0.123 Acc and +0.121 F1 overall, confirming that multilingual code-switching attacks provide substantially stronger training signals than English-only paraphrasing. On robustness (ASR), TASTE achieves 18.0% mean ASR versus 21.8% for GREATER-D and 41.7% for Fast DetectGPT, with the largest per-attack gains on Code-Switching (−32.8% relative vs. GREATER-D) and Human Obfuscation (−59.7% relative). The only language where TASTE does not lead is Urdu, where Binoculars achieves 0.612 Acc vs. TASTE's 0.697 Acc in the OOD SemEval setting, though TASTE still leads on the primary M4GT benchmark.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the attacker-detector co-evolution framework of Li et al. (2025, GREATER-D) from monolingual English to a multilingual zero-shot setting, replacing paraphrase-based attacks with translation-dictionary-driven code-switching. The Language-Agnostic Adversarial Loss (LAAL) adapts the gradient-reversal domain-adversarial scheme from Bhattacharjee et al. (2024, EAGLE) to force language-invariant encoder representations, while the gradient-based token importance scoring follows Zhou et al. (2024). The key departure from prior multilingual adversarial training (Avram et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025) is the elimination of large-scale multilingual corpora: only a single-language labeled set plus static translation dictionaries are required, making the approach feasible for medium- and low-resource languages.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is substantial: current MGT detectors leave billions of non-English speakers unprotected, and this work shows that dictionary-based adversarial training can close much of that gap without expensive multilingual data collection. However, performance on truly low-resource languages (Russian, Urdu) remains notably weaker, and the method's reliance on translation dictionaries—however noisy—still excludes languages where even basic lexical resources are absent. Open questions include whether the approach scales to morphologically rich or script-divergent languages, whether the 5% optimal attack strength generalizes across domains, and whether the surrogate-model distillation loop remains stable when the target detector is itself a large LLM rather than a 110M-parameter encoder.
