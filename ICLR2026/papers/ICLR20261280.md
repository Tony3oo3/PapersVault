## Problem

The Muon optimizer empirically trains LLMs roughly 2× faster than Adam, yet the mechanism behind this superiority remains unexplained. The standard steepest-descent interpretation (spectral norm vs. infinity norm) does not clarify why Muon should outperform Adam, and existing convergence analyses derived from it fail to account for the observed gap. This paper asks which specific transformer components drive Muon's advantage and what structural properties of those components make them amenable to Muon's update rule.

## Input / Output

The method operates on matrix-valued weight parameters in transformers, consuming the momentum-accumulated gradient matrix G_t and producing a scale-invariant update direction O_t = U_t V_t^T by normalizing away the singular values of G_t's SVD (approximated via Newton-Schulz iterations in practice). Training data is assumed to follow heavy-tailed power-law class distributions, where a few head classes are extremely frequent and many tail classes are individually rare. The output is an updated weight matrix whose singular value spectrum is more isotropic than Adam's, yielding balanced learning accuracy across all frequency levels rather than a head-tail performance gap.

## Method

The paper identifies that Muon's advantage is concentrated in the Value-Output (VO) attention matrices and Feed-Forward Network (FFN) blocks, which function as linear associative memories storing facts as sums of outer products W = Σ e_{o_i} e_{s_i}^T. Muon's core mechanism is that its update UV^T assigns equal magnitude to each orthogonal singular direction of the gradient, effectively normalizing away the frequency-dependent singular values that encode class imbalance in the training data. This aligns precisely with the outer-product structure of associative memories: each fact's constituent outer product receives the same update regardless of how frequently it appears, enabling uniform learning of both head and tail facts. Theoretically, on a one-layer linear associative memory model under class-imbalanced data, the paper proves Muon achieves balanced learning across classes for any orthonormal embeddings, while Adam (reduced to SignGD) can exhibit severe class-dependent disparities depending on embedding support overlap. Empirically, this manifests as more isotropic weight spectra (higher SVD entropy and effective rank, lower top-k energy concentration) and substantially better tail-class First Token Accuracy on heavy-tailed knowledge tasks, with the VO+FFN hybrid configuration nearly recovering full-Muon performance.

## Results

On a heavy-tailed knowledge task (32,768 classes following a power-law distribution), Muon achieves 0.976 First Token Accuracy (FTA) on the extreme tail (Group 15) at 10,000 steps, compared to Adam's 0.264 and SGD+Momentum's 0.126, while both Muon and Adam reach 1.000 on the head (Group 11). Theoretically, for a one-layer associative memory model with K→∞, Muon guarantees balanced learning with minimum correct-class probability ≥ 1−ε(1+O(log K/K)), whereas GD degrades to O(ε^{−r(α,β)}K^{r(α,β)−1}) and Adam can drop to O(ε^{−0.7}K^{−0.3}) depending on embedding structure. Spectrally, Muon consistently produces higher SVD entropy and effective rank (eRank) in VO and FFN weight matrices than Adam, indicating more isotropic representations throughout training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is Adam (Kingma & Ba, 2015). On the heavy-tailed knowledge task, Muon matches Adam on head classes (both reach 1.000 FTA on Group 11) but dramatically outperforms it on tail classes: at 10,000 steps, Muon achieves 0.976 FTA on the extreme tail (Group 15) versus Adam's 0.264 — a 3.7× gap. This advantage is concentrated in the VO and FFN components: the hybrid Muon(VO,FFN)/Adam(QK) nearly recovers full Muon performance (0.954 on Group 15), while Muon(QK)/Adam(VO,FFN) barely improves over Adam (0.286). SGD+Momentum serves as a secondary baseline and is substantially outperformed by both Muon and Adam across all groups, consistent with prior findings that Adam handles heavy-tailed distributions better than plain GD. Theoretically, Muon's balanced-learning guarantee (Theorem 4.3) holds for all orthonormal embeddings, while Adam's performance is embedding-dependent — it can match Muon with disjoint supports but degrades to O(ε^{−0.7}K^{−0.3}) with overlapping supports, explaining Adam's larger variance across random seeds observed empirically.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper connects two previously separate research threads: the Muon optimizer (Jordan et al., 2024), previously understood through the steepest-descent-under-spectral-norm lens (Bernstein & Newhouse, 2024), and the associative-memory interpretation of transformer components (Bietti et al., 2023; Geva et al., 2020; Meng et al., 2022a). The key departure is showing that Muon's SVD-based normalization is not merely a norm-constrained descent step but is structurally aligned with the outer-product decomposition W = Σ e_o_i e_s_i^T that characterizes linear associative memories, thereby explaining why it balances learning across facts of different frequencies. The theoretical one-layer model extends the knowledge-editing formulation (Meng et al., 2022a) into an optimization-dynamics setting, and the heavy-tailed data analysis builds on Kunstner et al. (2024)'s observation that Adam outperforms GD on imbalanced data, now showing Muon outperforms Adam in the same regime.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first mechanistic explanation for why Muon outperforms Adam in LLM training, resolving a question that the steepest-descent interpretation left open. The practical finding that applying Muon selectively to VO+FFN (the associative memory components) nearly recovers full-Muon performance gives practitioners a targeted, potentially cheaper strategy. The theoretical guarantee that Muon achieves balanced learning regardless of embedding structure while Adam's performance is embedding-dependent provides a principled design criterion for future optimizers. Open questions include whether the mechanism extends to higher-order tensor structures, whether the orthogonality assumption on embeddings holds broadly in practice, and whether the advantage persists at the 32B+ scale where reports on Muon's scalability are mixed.
