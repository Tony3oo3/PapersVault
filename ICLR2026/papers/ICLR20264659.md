## Problem

Learning-augmented scheduling algorithms have strong theoretical guarantees but lack real-world validation because existing production traces either omit ground-truth processing times, are not publicly available, or were designed for workload characterization rather than scheduling evaluation. The field also lacks a standardized benchmark specifying the scheduling framework, predictor training protocol, and result normalization, making cross-paper comparisons difficult. This paper introduces ATLAS, a research-ready dataset from Alibaba's PAI cluster with 730k+ jobs and verified processing times, alongside LASched, a standardized prediction and scheduling benchmark.

## Input / Output

The prediction model consumes only submit-time features (non-clairvoyant): per-instance resource requests (CPU/GPU/memory) multiplied by instance counts, user/group/workload identifiers, submission timestamp, recurrence signatures, and strictly causal historical statistics with a one-step lag to prevent leakage. The target is job-level processing time p*_j = max_t e_t − min_t s_t (fork-join completion), log-transformed for training. The scheduling benchmark takes these predictions and produces competitive ratios against optimal or tight bounds for three objectives: total completion time (normalized by SRPT), makespan (normalized by McNaughton's preemptive bound), and max-stretch (normalized by EDF-feasibility bisection optimum).

## Method

The prediction pipeline engineers 33 features from 13 raw columns (log-transformed resource totals, per-instance ratios, cyclic temporal encodings, recurrence signatures, and causal user/group histories with Empirical Bayes shrinkage λ=5), then trains LightGBM-based models under a strict 70/15/15 temporal split. Six calibrated methods are evaluated: conformal quantile regression with Ridge-blended bounds, isotonic monotone calibration, meta-stacking over diverse base models, gated two-stage experts routing by duration class, weighted recency ensembles with exponential time-decay, and HRAS with per-signature EB priors. All calibrators fit exclusively on validation data. The scheduling benchmark integrates these predictions into online algorithms (SPJF, PRR, SPRPT, EDF-P) and offline baselines (SRPT, SJF, LPT, SPT), evaluating them on single-machine preemptive, multi-machine non-preemptive, and fairness-oriented settings. The key empirical finding is that task-signature features and EB shrinkage yield a 20% coverage improvement over resource-only baselines, and that algorithmic structure (e.g., SPRPT vs. EDF-P) matters as much as prediction accuracy for tail-sensitive objectives like max-stretch.

## Results

On the ATLAS dataset (730k+ jobs from Alibaba PAI), the best prediction model (LGBM-Meta) achieves 51.2% Cov@25, 70.6% Cov@50, and Spearman ρ=0.912, substantially outperforming baselines without task-signature features (19.5–20.2% Cov@25). For scheduling, Meta-SPJF achieves a total completion time ratio of 1.106 (only 10.5% above clairvoyant SRPT), Meta-SPRPT reduces max-stretch to 18.13 (an 11× improvement over LAS/FB at 205.28), and Iso-LPPT attains the best makespan ratio of 1.574 among learning-augmented methods. Recurring jobs (81.3% of test set) show markedly higher prediction accuracy across all methods, validating the utility of historical execution data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the clairvoyant SRPT baseline (Schrage, 1968), the best learning-augmented scheduler Meta-SPJF achieves a total completion time ratio of 1.106, meaning only 10.5% degradation from the optimal offline solution, while the non-clairvoyant RR baseline (Motwani et al., 1994) suffers a 97.5% degradation (ratio 1.975) and FIFO (Weng et al., 2022) degrades by 437% (ratio 5.372). For max-stretch, Meta-SPRPT (18.13) dramatically outperforms the LAS/FB non-clairvoyant baseline (Nuyens & Wierman, 2008) at 205.28, an 11× improvement, though it remains far from the optimal EDF at S* (1.000) and even SRPT (1.581). For makespan, the learning-augmented Iso-LPPT (1.574) slightly underperforms the clairvoyant SPT (1.539) but beats Random (1.898), while LPT (Della Croce & Scatamacchia, 2020) remains the gold standard at 1.000. The key finding is that prediction quality interacts differently with each objective: total completion time benefits most from accurate ranking (Meta-SPJF wins), makespan depends primarily on identifying the largest jobs (Iso-LPPT wins), and max-stretch is most sensitive to worst-case prediction errors (SPRPT consistently dominates EDF-P across all predictors).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of learning-augmented scheduling theory (Kumar et al. 2018; Lattanzi et al. 2020; Zhao et al. 2024) and production workload analysis (Weng et al. 2022), addressing a gap the authors identify: theoretical results rely on synthetic distributions while existing production traces (Borg, Azure, Alibaba) either normalize/obfuscate processing times or lack ground-truth labels needed for scheduling evaluation. The prediction pipeline adapts established ML techniques—conformal quantile regression (Romano et al. 2019), isotonic calibration (Zadrozny & Elkan 2002), meta-stacking (Wolpert 1992), and gated experts (Jordan & Jacobs 1994)—to the specific non-clairvoyant job-duration setting with leakage-safe temporal splits. Unlike Weng et al. (2022), which used the same PAI trace for instance-level workload characterization, this work formalizes a job-level fork-join processing time and targets scheduling objectives (total completion time, max-stretch, makespan) rather than per-instance duration prediction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ATLAS and LASched fill a concrete gap: the learning-augmented scheduling community lacked a reproducible, non-clairvoyant dataset with verified ground-truth sizes and a standardized evaluation protocol, making cross-paper comparisons difficult. The key empirical finding—that deadline-based scheduling (EDF-P) amplifies absolute prediction errors into catastrophic worst-case stretch (ρ_max ≈ 1700) while remaining competitive on median stretch—reveals that algorithmic structure matters as much as prediction accuracy for tail-sensitive objectives, motivating future work on tail-aware loss functions and distributionally robust optimization. Limitations include evaluation on a single cluster trace, relatively small scheduling instances (5k–10k jobs), and the fact that recorded durations embed historical allocation decisions rather than pure job demand, which the authors acknowledge but do not fully decouple.
