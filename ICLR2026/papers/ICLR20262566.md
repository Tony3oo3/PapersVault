## Problem

Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) are widely used to extract interpretable features from transformer activations, and their quality is typically assessed via aggregate auto-interpretability scores and reconstruction metrics. However, it remains unclear whether these metrics can actually distinguish features learned through training from artifacts arising from the data distribution or model architecture. This paper applies the standard sanity check of comparing against randomly initialized null models and finds that, in many settings, SAEs trained on random transformers produce auto-interpretability scores and reconstruction metrics surprisingly similar to those from fully trained models.

## Input / Output

The method takes per-layer residual stream activation vectors from Pythia transformers (70M–6.9B parameters) as SAE inputs, trained on 100M tokens from RedPajama with a 10M-token activation buffer. Five model variants are compared: the trained model, two re-randomized variants (with and without preserving pretrained embeddings), the original step-0 initialization, and a control where token embeddings are replaced by i.i.d. Gaussian noise at inference. Outputs include per-latent 'fuzzing' and 'detection' AUROC scores (generated by Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct), reconstruction metrics (cosine similarity, explained variance, L1 norm), CE loss recovery scores, and a novel token distribution entropy measure quantifying how spread out each latent's activations are across distinct token IDs.

## Method

The paper trains per-layer TopK SAEs (expansion factor R=64, sparsity k=32) on residual stream activations of Pythia models across five initialization variants, then evaluates them with a suite of standard SAE metrics plus auto-interpretability scoring via LLM-generated explanations. The central empirical finding is that aggregate auto-interpretability AUROC and reconstruction metrics are often indistinguishable between trained and randomized transformers, while a control variant (Gaussian-noise embeddings) performs at chance. To probe what the aggregate metrics miss, the authors introduce token distribution entropy over maximally activating examples, revealing that randomized-model latents remain concentrated on single token IDs and do not become more abstract with depth, unlike trained-model latents. Section 4 provides toy models showing that randomly initialized MLPs can preserve or even amplify superposition in their inputs, offering a mechanistic explanation for why SAEs find apparently interpretable structure in random networks. The authors recommend routine randomized baselines and targeted 'abstractness' measures as complements to existing SAE evaluation metrics.

## Results

Across Pythia models from 70M to 6.9B parameters, SAEs trained on randomly initialized transformers (re-randomized incl./excl. embeddings, step-0) produce 'fuzzing' auto-interpretability AUROC scores that overlap substantially with those from the trained model, while the Gaussian-noise control sits near chance (~0.5). Reconstruction metrics (cosine similarity, explained variance) and L1 norms also show similar trends for trained and randomized variants, with the control clearly separated. Token distribution entropy, however, reveals a distinction the aggregate metrics miss: entropy increases with layer depth for the trained model (indicating more abstract, multi-token features) but remains low and flat for randomized variants (indicating single-token features). The CE loss score (loss recovered) is only meaningful for the trained variant, as randomized models have poor loss regardless of SAE reconstruction quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares five model variants: Trained, Re-randomized incl. embeddings, Re-randomized excl. embeddings, Step-0 (initialization weights), and Control (Gaussian noise token embeddings at inference). On auto-interpretability (fuzzing AUROC), all four non-control variants (trained and all randomized) produce overlapping scores across layers and model sizes, meaning the metric fails to distinguish learned computation from random initialization. The Control variant is clearly separated, performing at or below chance level, confirming the metric is not simply broken. On token distribution entropy, the trained variant shows a clear increase with layer index (from ~0.01 at layer 0 to ~0.7 at layer 28 for Pythia-6.9b), while randomized variants remain at lower, relatively flat entropy values, and the control shows consistently high entropy. On reconstruction quality (cosine similarity, explained variance), the control is far lower than all other variants, but trained and randomized variants remain similar. The CE loss score confirms that SAEs on the trained model recover meaningful loss (comparable to prior work), while for all randomized variants the underlying model loss is poor regardless of reconstruction, making this metric inapplicable as a discriminator.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper applies the null-model sanity-check paradigm (Adebayo et al., 2020) to the SAE interpretability evaluation pipeline, building directly on the TopK SAE architecture (Makhzani & Frey, 2014; Gao et al., 2024) and the auto-interpretability scoring framework of Paulo et al. (2024) and Bills et al. (2023). It departs from prior work by systematically testing whether these widely adopted metrics can distinguish trained from randomly initialized transformers across the full Pythia scale (70M–6.9B), whereas Bricken et al. (2023) observed discrimination only for one-layer transformers and Karvonen et al. (2024c) studied a different domain (board games). The superposition toy models in Section 4 extend the data-generation procedure of Sharkey et al. (2022) to test whether random networks preserve or amplify sparse structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that aggregate auto-interpretability scores are similar for trained and random transformers challenges a core assumption in mechanistic interpretability: that high SAE quality metrics guarantee recovery of computationally meaningful features. The paper introduces token distribution entropy as a preliminary proxy for feature 'abstractness' that does capture the trained-vs-random distinction, but acknowledges this is not a full solution. Open questions include which mechanism (input superposition preservation vs. network-induced sparsification) predominates in real transformers, whether the gap re-emerges at even larger scales or with different SAE architectures, and how to design metrics that robustly quantify the computational significance of discovered features. The practical implication is that interpretability claims based solely on aggregate SAE metrics should be accompanied by randomized baselines.
