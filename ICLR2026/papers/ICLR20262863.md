## Problem

Deploying RL in safety-critical domains is blocked by the difficulty of specifying accurate reward functions and the risk of unsafe, data-hungry exploration. While the hybrid paradigm of offline imitation followed by online preference-based fine-tuning has achieved strong empirical success (e.g., RLHF for LLMs, safe robot manipulation), its theoretical foundations remain entirely unexplored: no prior work provides regret bounds that quantify how offline expert data reduces online learning complexity or formalizes the trade-off between offline data quantity and online query budget.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input (i) a reward-free offline dataset of n expert trajectories of length H from a finite MDP, (ii) a known trajectory embedding function φ: T → R^d with bounded norm (‖φ(τ)‖₂ ≤ B), and (iii) online binary preference feedback generated under a Bradley-Terry model with bounded weight vector (‖w*‖₂ ≤ W). It produces a learned policy (the best-scoring policy within the offline confidence set under the final weight estimate) and the estimated preference weight vector w_proj. The offline data must come from a realizable expert policy with minimum state-action visitation probability γ_min > 0, and the embedding must be sufficiently expressive to capture the expert's preference structure.

## Method

BRIDGE is a two-stage algorithm that bridges offline behavioral cloning and online preference-based RL via a Hellinger-distance confidence set. In the offline stage, it estimates a BC policy and transition model via MLE, then constructs a confidence set Π_{1−δ}^{offline} as a Hellinger ball in trajectory-distribution space centered on the BC policy, with radius O(1/√n) that shrinks with more demonstrations. In the online stage, it runs a GLM-based preference-learning loop (adapted from Saha et al., 2023) but with two key modifications: (a) all policy selection is hard-constrained to the offline Hellinger ball, preventing exploration of unsafe regions, and (b) the transition model pools offline and online counts for improved sample efficiency. The core theoretical insight is that constraining policies to the Hellinger ball bounds pairwise feature differences by O(B/√n), which injects the offline sample size n directly into the online exploration variance, yielding a regret bound of Õ(√T · √log(1+T/n) + √T/√(n·γ_min)) that vanishes as n→∞.

## Results

BRIDGE achieves lower cumulative regret than both the offline Behavioral Cloning baseline (Foster et al., 2024) and the online Preference-based RL baseline (Saha et al., 2023) across all four evaluated environments: two discrete (StarMDP, Gridworld) and two continuous MuJoCo (Reacher, Ant). The theoretical regret bound is R_T ≤ Õ(√T · √log(1+T/n) + √T/√(n·γ_min)), which vanishes as the number of offline demonstrations n→∞ for fixed T. Empirically, BRIDGE also refines its policy search space Π_t faster than the PbRL baseline (Figure 3), and the ablation on Gridworld confirms the predicted O(1/√n) scaling: a 100× increase in offline trajectories reduces the confidence set from 99.9% to 8.0% of the full policy space.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares BRIDGE against two baselines: (1) offline Behavioral Cloning (LogLossBC from Foster et al., 2024), which learns a static policy from expert demonstrations without any online refinement, and (2) online Preference-based RL (PbRL from Saha et al., 2023), which learns entirely from online preference queries without offline warm-starting. BRIDGE outperforms both baselines in cumulative regret across all four environments (StarMDP, Gridworld, Reacher, Ant). Against offline BC, BRIDGE wins because online preference feedback allows it to correct the BC policy's blind spots and converge to a better policy. Against online PbRL, BRIDGE wins because the offline Hellinger confidence set constrains exploration to a smaller, safer region of policy space, yielding a tighter data-dependent variance bound that shrinks with n. The PbRL baseline explores more broadly (larger Π_t in Figure 3), incurring higher regret from suboptimal exploration. Both baselines were re-implemented by the authors since no public code was available.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of behavioral cloning (Pomerleau, 1988; Foster et al., 2024), preference-based RL (Christiano et al., 2017; Saha et al., 2023), and offline-to-online RL (Nair et al., 2020; Song et al., 2023; Wagenmaker & Pacchiano, 2023). The critical departure from prior offline-to-online work is that all existing theory assumes access to a ground-truth reward during online fine-tuning, whereas BRIDGE operates in a reward-free setting where the online phase receives only binary preference comparisons. The algorithm builds on the GLM-based preference learning framework of Saha et al. (2023) but introduces two key modifications: constraining exploration to a Hellinger-ball confidence set derived from offline BC data, and pooling offline/online counts for the transition model. The paper also contrasts itself with RLHF (Ouyang et al., 2022) and DPO (Rafailov et al., 2024), noting that BRIDGE avoids a static reward model and replaces heuristic KL penalties with a provable confidence-set constraint.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first rigorous regret analysis for the offline-to-online preference learning paradigm that underpins modern systems like ChatGPT's RLHF pipeline, providing practitioners with formal guidance on how much offline data is needed to reduce online query budgets. The key open question is whether the linear-preference and known-embedding assumptions can be relaxed; the authors suggest learning embeddings from offline data via self-supervised objectives as a natural next step. Limitations include the requirement for a realizable expert policy, the dependence on the minimum visitation probability γ_min (which can be small for specialized experts), and the fact that the continuous-control experiments use a finite policy approximation rather than the full infinite policy space. The practical impact is primarily theoretical justification for a widely-used empirical recipe, rather than enabling qualitatively new capabilities.
