## Problem

The strongest results in foundation model fine-tuning come from a two-stage online procedure (train a reward model, then run RL), yet information-theoretic arguments suggest that passing through a reward model can only lose information and on-policy sampling cannot create new information. This paper asks: what is the actual value of the online RL stage if the goal is simply to maximize data likelihood, and why does it empirically outperform offline MLE methods like DPO?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264858/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes trajectory-level preference pairs (same prompt with a preferred and dis-preferred completion) and a base SFT policy checkpoint, producing a fine-tuned language model policy. For the online DPO variant, 25 completions are sampled per prompt from the current policy, ranked by the learned global reward model, and the top and bottom of the ranking form new preference pairs for one additional DPO training epoch. All experiments use the tl;dr summarization dataset with Pythia models (1.4B, 2.8B, 6.9B), and evaluation reports winrate against human references as judged by GPT-4o.

## Method

The paper first proves that under isomorphic reward and policy classes, online and offline PFT produce identical optima (Theorems 2.2–2.3), showing the gap cannot arise from the procedure itself. It then proposes hypothesis H6: on problems with a generation-verification gap, the first stage learns a relatively simple verifier r̂_sim ∈ R_sim ⊂ R, and the second stage computes the soft-optimal policy for that verifier, so end-to-end online PFT only searches over the reduced policy subset Π(R_sim) ⊂ Π rather than all of Π (Theorem 4.1). Empirically, the paper falsifies five alternative explanations (intrinsic value of online samples, regularization failure, optimization ease, data diversity, OOD generalization) through controlled experiments that hold the DPO loss, SFT checkpoint, and preference data constant. Critically, it fails to falsify H6 by demonstrating that closing the generation-verification gap—either by reducing the horizon to two-word summaries or by using ROUGE-L as a complex, lookup-table-like reward—eliminates the online-offline performance difference, exactly as the hypothesis predicts.

## Results

On the tl;dr summarization task (Pythia 1.4B/2.8B/6.9B, evaluated by GPT-4o winrate against human references), online DPO robustly outperforms offline DPO across all model sizes and 3 seeds, with the gap persisting even when both methods use identical hyperparameters, the same SFT checkpoint, and the same preference data. When the generation-verification gap is artificially closed—either by reducing the horizon to two-word summaries (~1/10 of standard length) or by using ROUGE-L as the reward—the online-over-offline advantage vanishes, yielding at most ~1% winrate improvement or no improvement at all. Additionally, scaling the reward model to be significantly larger than the policy provides no Best-of-N improvement, while scaling the policy does, confirming the verifier is qualitatively simpler than the generator.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between offline DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) and online DPO (Guo et al., 2024) under carefully controlled conditions: same DPO loss, same SFT starting checkpoint, same preference data, same hyperparameters, and same number of gradient steps. Online DPO consistently wins on standard tl;dr summarization across all three Pythia model sizes, with the gap being described as 'significant' and robust across seeds. Notably, Online DPO (DPO)—starting from the offline DPO policy and performing one online iteration—further improves over the base DPO policy, showing that a global RM trained on the same data can extract additional signal that offline MLE cannot. However, when the generation-verification gap is eliminated (two-word summarization or ROUGE-L reward), online DPO provides no meaningful improvement over offline DPO, supporting the paper's hypothesis H6 that the advantage is specifically tied to problems where verification is easier than generation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the DPO framework (Rafailov et al., 2023) and maximum entropy RL (Ziebart, 2010) to provide an information-geometric unification of online and offline preference fine-tuning, proving their equivalence under isomorphic reward/policy classes. It departs from prior empirical observations of the online-offline gap (Tajwar et al., 2024; Song et al., 2024a; Tang et al., 2024) by systematically falsifying five alternative hypotheses and proposing the generation-verification gap (H6) as the unifying explanation, drawing on the classical inverse RL argument (Ng et al., 2000) and the broader CS phenomenon that verification is easier than generation (P vs NP). The paper also connects to the proper vs. improper learning distinction from Valiant (1984), reframing online PFT as a computational-statistical trade-off that trades data for computation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The generation-verification gap hypothesis provides a principled, testable explanation for why the two-stage RLHF pipeline outperforms direct MLE, with practical guidance: for problems where verification is simpler than generation, learning verifiers is more data-efficient than learning generators directly. The falsification experiments (short-horizon summarization, ROUGE-L metric) that eliminate the online-offline gap when the generation-verification gap is closed are well-designed and convincing. Open questions include whether the hypothesis extends to multi-turn, agentic, or robotics settings where the gap may be larger, whether current reward model architectures can capture intransitive human preferences, and how to explicitly constrain offline FT to the reduced policy space Π(R_sim) without the two-stage procedure.
