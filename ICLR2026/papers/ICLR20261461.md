## Problem

Pretrained MLLMs remain limited in reasoning over artificial image domains (charts, webpages, rendered documents) where large-scale human-annotated reasoning datasets are scarce. The paper asks whether a small set of seed questions can be leveraged to bootstrap a large, diverse synthetic training set that equips MLLMs with transferable compositional reasoning abilities in such domains.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261461/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a small seed question set (no ground-truth answers required) and a collection of unlabeled images (e.g., ChartQA training charts or MultiUI webpage screenshots) as input. It produces synthetic compositional question-answer pairs, each structured as a tuple ⟨I, q, a, {f_i}, {a_i}⟩ containing the overall question, its answer, and factor-level subquestions with intermediate answers. At inference, the fine-tuned MLLM receives a chart image or webpage screenshot plus a natural language question and outputs a chain-of-thought reasoning trace with intermediate sub-answers followed by a final concise answer.

## Method

COGS operates in three stages: (1) an MLLM decomposes each seed question into primitive perception and reasoning factors (e.g., identification, comparison, calculation), building a domain-specific factor pool; (2) subsets of factors are recomposed with new images to generate novel compositional questions, where the MLLM produces both the overall QA pair and factor-level subquestions with intermediate answers, optionally leveraging underlying metadata (e.g., chart data tables) for answer precision; (3) a pretrained MLLM (Qwen2.5-VL-7B) is fine-tuned via GRPO using process rewards that verify intermediate sub-answers. The key theoretical contribution is a proof that a max-based process reward (ProcessRM-max) preserves policy ordering under noisy sub-reward signals, unlike a sum-based variant, which is empirically confirmed by ablations. A factor-level mixture strategy—merging factor pools across datasets before recomposition—outperforms data-level mixture, enabling positive transfer across benchmarks.

## Results

On ChartQAPro, COGS achieves 52.02% overall accuracy, outperforming the base Qwen2.5-VL-7B (47.36%) by 4.66 points and the strongest proprietary baseline GPT-5-nano (50.74%) by 1.28 points. The largest gains appear on reasoning-heavy question types: Hypothetical (+7.53% over base) and Conversational (+6.28%). On VisualWebBench, COGS reaches 88.04% accuracy, surpassing the base model (85.65%) and all non-proprietary baselines, though it remains slightly below GPT-5-nano (89.47%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

COGS (52.02%) outperforms all open-source MLLMs by a wide margin: Qwen2.5-VL-7B base (47.36%), InternVL3.5-GPT-OSS (46.86%), and Phi-4-14B (31.61%). It also surpasses all proprietary baselines, including GPT-5-nano (50.74%), GPT-4o-mini (48.32%), Claude Haiku 3.5 (46.74%), and Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite (38.72%). Chart specialist models perform substantially worse (ChartLLaMA 17.19%, ChartMoE 27.28%), attributed to narrow training distributions and architectural constraints. Among prompting strategies, Self-Consistency (47.22%) and Decompositional CoT (46.36%) provide only marginal gains over the base model, while COGS's training-time factor-level process rewards avoid the error accumulation that limits inference-time decomposition. Among data synthesis baselines, In-Context Q Example (50.58%) is the closest competitor, but COGS still leads by 1.44 points; Chart-R1 (47.32%) and the original ChartQA training data (46.64%) trail further behind. On VisualWebBench, COGS (88.04%) beats all non-proprietary models including MultiUI-WQA (86.60%) and UiX-Qwen2 (68.90%), but remains 1.43 points below GPT-5-nano (89.47%).

## Relation to Prior Work

COGS builds on GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) as its RL training algorithm but departs from prior data-synthesis work by operating in visual-grounded factor space rather than text-space instruction generation (Self-Instruct, WizardLM, self-rewarding LMs). Unlike chart-specialist models (ChartLLaMA, ChartMoE) that rely on bespoke architectures or narrow training distributions, COGS augments general-purpose MLLMs through compositional factor recomposition, and unlike template-based or in-context question synthesis baselines, it explicitly decomposes questions into primitive perception/reasoning factors that can be recombined across new images. The factor-level process reward design extends standard answer-matching RL rewards with intermediate supervision, and the theoretical analysis of reward order-preservation is a new contribution to the RL-for-MLLMs literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

COGS offers a principled, data-efficient recipe for bootstrapping reasoning capabilities in domains where annotated QA data is scarce but images are abundant, with the factor-level mixture strategy suggesting a general solution to the multi-dataset training problem. The theoretical guarantee that ProcessRM-max preserves policy orders under noisy sub-rewards provides practical guidance for reward design in RL fine-tuning. Limitations include evaluation on single charts and single webpage screenshots (no long-context or multi-page reasoning), reliance on a 72B MLLM for data generation, and no demonstration of transfer to downstream agentic tasks like chart editing or web navigation. Open questions include whether the factor decomposition approach integrates naturally into pretraining, how it scales to more complex visual documents, and whether the compositional structure generalizes to domains without clean factor boundaries.
