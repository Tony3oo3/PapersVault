## Problem

Existing time-series explanation methods are either point-wise (producing fragmented saliency maps that lack temporal coherence) or patch-wise (using fixed-length windows that sacrifice localization precision). There is no principled, information-theoretic definition of what constitutes a meaningful interpretable segment, and no method that identifies variable-length, contiguous segments under strict black-box constraints where only model inputs and outputs are accessible.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20265123/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a univariate time series x ∈ R^T and a pre-trained black-box classifier g_θ (e.g., TCN) for which only input-output queries are permitted—no gradients, parameters, or internal representations. At each sequential step, the policy network receives the full sequence concatenated with a binary gate vector marking previously selected regions, while the black-box model receives a masked version of x where unselected time points are replaced with the empirical training mean. The output is an adaptively sized set of K non-overlapping, variable-length segments (start-end index pairs), with K determined per instance by a relative CMI-gain termination threshold (τ=0.3) and a hard cap K_max=5.

## Method

TimeSeg formulates segment selection as maximizing the joint mutual information between selected segments and the black-box prediction, decomposed via the chain rule into a sum of conditional MI (CMI) terms that are estimated as cross-entropy gaps between the model's outputs with and without each newly added segment. This sequential decision process is cast as a reinforcement learning problem where a factorized policy (start-policy × end-policy, both categorical over absolute time indices with start-conditioned masking to enforce t^s ≤ t^e) proposes segments, and the reward is the CMI estimate minus a sparsity penalty proportional to segment length. Training uses PPO with an actor-critic architecture: 3-layer 1D CNNs serve as both the policy (actor) and a value network (critic) that provides a low-variance baseline via one-step TD advantage, with step-level rollouts of 1,024 transitions and 4 PPO epochs per update cycle. At inference, segments are selected deterministically via argmax over the policy's categorical distributions until the marginal information gain falls below the termination threshold.

## Results

On the four ground-truth datasets, TimeSeg achieves the best F1 and IoU on SeqComb-UV (0.645/0.495), LowVarDetect-UV (0.499/0.356), and MIT-ECG (0.739/0.621), while ranking second on FreqShapes-V (0.722/0.576 vs. TimeX++'s 0.799/0.666). TimeSeg also achieves the lowest contiguity scores across all four datasets (0.006–0.085), indicating highly coherent segment-wise explanations. On the four unannotated datasets (Table 2), TimeSeg achieves the best sufficiency (≤2% AUROC drop when retaining only selected segments) and the best comprehensiveness on GunPoint (46.41% AUROC drop when removing segments), substantially outperforming all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TimeSeg outperforms all five baselines on F1 and IoU for three of four ground-truth datasets (SeqComb-UV, LowVarDetect-UV, MIT-ECG), with the largest margin on MIT-ECG where it achieves F1=0.739 vs. TimeX++'s 0.593 and IG's 0.589. On FreqShapes-V, TimeX++ (which uses internal embeddings) edges out TimeSeg on F1/IoU (0.799/0.666 vs. 0.722/0.576), but TimeSeg produces far more contiguous segments (0.085 vs. 0.120). Against point-wise methods (Dynamask, WinIT, TimeX++), TimeSeg consistently achieves 2–5× lower contiguity scores, confirming its segment-wise structure. Against the patch-wise LIMESegment, TimeSeg matches or beats contiguity while substantially improving F1/IoU (e.g., 0.739 vs. 0.491 F1 on MIT-ECG), showing that variable-length segments outperform fixed-size patches. On the unannotated fidelity metrics (Table 2), TimeSeg achieves ≤2% AUROC sufficiency drop on all four datasets, compared to ≥31% for the second-best method, and the highest comprehensiveness on GunPoint (46.41% vs. 9.15% for Dynamask). Notably, TimeSeg operates under strict black-box access (inputs/outputs only), while IG and TimeX++ require gradients or internal representations, making its competitive performance particularly significant.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of time-series explanation methods from point-wise attribution (Dynamask, WinIT, TimeX++) and fixed-patch approaches (LIMESegment) to a formally defined segment-wise explanation task. It builds on the information bottleneck principle (Alemi et al., 2016) to derive a variational lower bound on conditional mutual information, and repurposes the PPO actor-critic framework (Schulman et al., 2017) as the optimization engine for sequential segment selection. The key conceptual departure is treating segment selection as a sequential decision problem over variable-length, non-overlapping subsequences rather than independent binary masks or fixed-size patches, and doing so under strict black-box constraints (no gradient or embedding access).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first principled information-theoretic definition of what constitutes a meaningful temporal segment, filling a gap where prior methods either fragmented explanations into isolated points or relied on arbitrary fixed-length patches. The finding that a strict black-box RL method can match or exceed gradient-based explainers (TimeX++, IG) on both overlap and fidelity metrics challenges the assumption that internal model access is necessary for high-quality temporal explanations. Open questions include whether the framework scales to high-dimensional multivariate settings beyond the simple channel-selection extension shown, whether the RL training cost is prohibitive for very long sequences, and how to incorporate domain-specific priors (e.g., clinically meaningful ECG intervals) into the reward design.
