## Problem

Existing AI benchmarks focus on academic reasoning difficulty or narrow domains like software engineering, leaving a gap in measuring model capability on the broad range of real-world knowledge work that drives economic output. Current economic-impact metrics (adoption rates, GDP attribution) are lagging indicators that cannot capture capability ahead of widespread deployment. GDPval addresses this by directly evaluating frontier models on tasks drawn from the highest-GDP-contributing U.S. sectors, using head-to-head comparison against industry experts as the primary metric.

## Input / Output

Each task provides a natural-language request accompanied by up to 17 reference files (gold subset) or 38 (full set) spanning PDFs, spreadsheets, presentations, images, video, audio, and CAD files; tasks average 7 hours of expert completion time and are rated 89% well-specified. The model produces deliverable files in the requested format (PDF, PPTX, DOCX, XLSX, etc.), which are then graded via blind pairwise comparison against a human expert's deliverable by occupation-specific graders. The headline output is a win rate (fraction of tasks where the model deliverable is rated better than or tied with the human), supplemented by speed and cost savings ratios under different human-in-the-loop review strategies.

## Method

GDPval is constructed top-down: 9 sectors contributing >5% of U.S. GDP are selected, and within each, the top 5 occupations by total wages that are predominantly digital (≥60% of O*NET-weighted tasks classified as digital by GPT-4o) are chosen, yielding 44 occupations and 1,320 tasks (220 in the open-sourced gold subset). Tasks are authored by industry professionals averaging 14 years of experience, mapped to O*NET work activities for representativeness, and pass an iterative quality-control pipeline with an average of five human expert reviews. The primary evaluation is blind pairwise expert comparison (3 graders × 3 samples per task), while an experimental GPT-5-high-based automated grader achieves 66% agreement with human graders (vs. 71% human inter-rater agreement). The paper further runs controlled experiments showing that increased reasoning effort (+4–6 pp), targeted prompting to enforce formatting checks and multimodal self-inspection (+5 pp), and best-of-N sampling with a GPT-5 judge each improve model win rates, demonstrating concrete paths to closing the remaining gap with human experts.

## Results

On the GDPval gold subset (220 tasks), Claude Opus 4.1 achieves the highest overall performance with 47.6% of deliverables rated as better than or tied with the human expert baseline. Among OpenAI models, GPT-5 leads with a 39.0% win rate, followed by o3 (35.2%), o4-mini (29.1%), and GPT-4o (12.5%). Under a 'try n times, then fix it yourself' workflow, GPT-5 yields a 1.39× speed improvement and 1.63× cost improvement over unaided experts. Increasing reasoning effort improves GPT-5 performance by up to 6.1 percentage points, and prompt-tuning with mandatory formatting checks adds a further 5 percentage points to win rates.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is a human industry expert deliverable (average 14 years of experience), against which all model outputs are compared via blinded pairwise expert grading. Claude Opus 4.1 is the strongest model overall, with 47.6% of its deliverables rated as better than or tied with the human expert, excelling particularly on aesthetics and file formatting (PDF, XLSX, PPTX). GPT-5 (39.0% win rate) outperforms Claude on pure-text instruction following but trails on visual deliverables. Gemini 2.5 Pro and Grok 4 show lower win rates, with their most common failure modes being omitted deliverables, wrong formats, and instruction-following errors (7.78% and 6.01% omitted deliverables respectively, vs. 1.77% for Claude). Among OpenAI's own model lineage, performance improves roughly linearly from GPT-4o (12.5%) → o4-mini (29.1%) → o3 (35.2%) → GPT-5 (39.0%), suggesting steady capability gains. No model achieves a clear majority win rate, indicating that frontier models are approaching but have not yet surpassed human expert quality on economically valuable knowledge work.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from two main lines of prior work. First, it rejects adoption-based and GDP-attribution metrics (Clio, Anthropic Economic Index, Bick et al.) as lagging indicators, instead proposing direct capability measurement on economically valuable tasks. Second, it contrasts with academic reasoning benchmarks (MMLU, GPQA, Humanity's Last Exam) and domain-specific evals (SWE-Lancer) by grounding tasks in actual industry work product, covering 44 occupations across 9 GDP sectors via O*NET representativeness, and using multi-modal, long-horizon deliverables graded by expert pairwise comparison rather than automated scoring. The occupation selection methodology builds on the Acemoglu & Autor task-content framework and Eloundou et al.'s GPT-4o-based digital task classification, validated against established economic measures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GDPval provides the first capability-based (rather than adoption-based) benchmark for assessing AI's economic potential, showing frontier models approaching expert parity on ~48% of real-world knowledge-work tasks with roughly linear improvement over time. Key open questions include: how performance changes with interactivity and under-specification (the under-contextualized experiment shows meaningful degradation), whether the one-shot, self-contained task format generalizes to the iterative, context-discovery nature of real professional work, and whether the 44-occupation, 9-sector coverage captures the full breadth of economically valuable work. The automated grader's 66% agreement (vs. 71% human inter-rater) limits scalability, and the cost/speed analysis, while suggestive, excludes catastrophic-failure costs and supervisor review time that would erode the modeled savings.
