## Problem

In the standard SFT-then-RLVR post-training pipeline for reasoning LLMs, high SFT benchmark scores are assumed to predict strong post-RL performance, yet this assumption frequently fails: models overtrained on repeated or simpler examples achieve better SFT metrics but gain less (or even degrade) during subsequent RL. The paper asks whether SFT performance can be replaced by more reliable predictors of RL outcome, and if so, what those predictors are.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261934/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a post-SFT model checkpoint along with a held-out validation split of the SFT data and a set of evaluation prompts (7 math benchmarks). It produces two scalar scores per candidate model: the generalization loss on the validation set and Pass@64 accuracy estimated via 256 sampled responses per task using the unbiased combinatorial estimator. These scores are then used either to rank SFT candidates (Spearman correlation) or to fit a linear predictor for absolute post-RL performance (R²), eliminating the need to run the full 5-day RL pipeline for every candidate.

## Method

The paper identifies two predictors of post-RLVR (GRPO) success that outperform raw post-SFT Pass@1. First, generalization loss on held-out SFT validation examples: as SFT overfits, this loss flares up while benchmark accuracy keeps rising, and the magnitude of the flare strongly correlates with how much the model can still improve during RL. Second, Pass@k accuracy evaluated at large k (k=64): since GRPO's objective is to compress Pass@k into Pass@1 and can only progress when at least one sampled response is correct, the model's Pass@64 ceiling is a natural upper-bound predictor of its post-RL Pass@1. The authors validate both metrics across Llama3-8B-Instruct, Mistral-Nemo-12B-Instruct, and Qwen3-4B-base trained on Llama-Nemotron, AceReasoner1.1-SFT, and OpenR1-Math datasets, with RL on MATH and DeepScaleR, spending over 1M A100 GPU hours. Generalization loss excels in dataset-level prediction (same distribution, varying epochs/learning rate), while Pass@large k is robust across instance-level prediction (different SFT datasets with distributional shifts), achieving up to 2× improvements in R² and Spearman correlation over the post-SFT Pass@1 baseline.

## Results

The paper's central finding is that post-SFT Pass@1 accuracy is a poor predictor of post-RL performance, with R² as low as 0.29 (Mistral-Nemo-12B) and 0.57 (Llama3-8B) in dataset-level prediction. The proposed predictors—generalization loss on held-out validation examples and Pass@64 accuracy—substantially outperform this baseline, achieving R² of 0.88±0.09 and 0.87±0.10 for Llama3-8B, and 0.79±0.26 and 0.57±0.32 for Mistral-Nemo-12B. Averaging the two predictors yields the best results: R² of 0.94±0.04 (Llama3) and 0.72±0.24 (Mistral), representing up to a 0.50 absolute improvement (2×) over the baseline. In instance-level prediction (SFT data selection), Pass@64 alone achieves R² of 0.92±0.05 (Llama3) and 0.98±0.01 (Mistral), with Spearman correlation reaching 0.94 and 0.98 respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three prediction strategies for estimating post-RL performance from post-SFT models: (1) the standard baseline of using SFT Pass@1 accuracy, (2) generalization loss on held-out SFT validation examples, and (3) Pass@64 accuracy. In dataset-level prediction (optimizing SFT training configurations), both proposed metrics consistently outperform the baseline: generalization loss achieves R² of 0.88 (Llama3) and 0.79 (Mistral) versus 0.57 and 0.29 for the baseline, while Pass@64 reaches 0.87 and 0.57 respectively. The averaged predictor (Gen. Loss + Pass@64) achieves the best overall R² of 0.94 for Llama3, a +0.37 improvement. In instance-level prediction (SFT data selection), generalization loss is not applicable due to distributional gaps between different SFT datasets, but Pass@64 alone dominates, improving R² from 0.58→0.92 (Llama3) and 0.73→0.98 (Mistral), and Spearman from 0.69→0.94 and 0.70→0.98. The paper also demonstrates that the best SFT model is frequently not the best SFT+RL model: e.g., a model trailing by 8.66% after SFT leads by +6.14% after RL on Mistral-Nemo-12B, and one trailing by 11.20% after SFT leads by +2.26% after RL with up to +95% on individual tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the now-standard SFT-then-RLVR (GRPO) post-training paradigm popularized by DeepSeek-R1, and leverages the unbiased Pass@k estimation formula from Brown et al. (2024) to propose a new predictive metric. It departs from the prevailing assumption in works like AceReasoner-Nemotron and Llama-Nemotron that optimizing post-SFT benchmark accuracy is the right objective for the SFT stage, by demonstrating that this assumption systematically fails. The paper also connects to the observation (Yue et al., 2025) that GRPO compresses Pass@k into Pass@1, using this as theoretical motivation for why Pass@large k should predict RL outcomes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

In industrial settings where SFT and RL are handled by separate teams, this work provides concrete, cheap metrics (generalization loss, Pass@64) that can replace the misleading post-SFT accuracy as the handoff criterion, potentially saving millions of GPU hours in failed RL runs. The finding that over-training SFT or selecting shorter/simpler examples actively degrades RL performance challenges common data curation heuristics. Key limitations include the focus on mathematical reasoning and GRPO specifically; whether these predictors transfer to coding, science, or other RL algorithms (offline RL, DPO) remains open. The computational cost of evaluating Pass@large k (256 repetitions) also limits its use for very long sequences, motivating future work on efficient estimation from smaller k.
