## Problem

While jailbreak attacks empirically bypass deployed safety filters, no formal impossibility results existed showing that filtering-based AI alignment faces inherent computational barriers. This paper asks whether, under standard cryptographic assumptions, any efficient input-prompt or output filter can provably prevent harmful LLM behavior when there is a computational asymmetry between the filter and the model.

## Input / Output

The adversarial construction takes a harmful prompt m, a time-lock difficulty parameter t, and a known benign prompt generator G (an autoregressive model over a binary token set), and produces a prompt z that is statistically indistinguishable from G's outputs for any filter running in o(t^ε) time, along with a modified LLM M' that recovers and executes m. In the empirical component, the method takes 1,097 jailbreak prompts from JailbreakBench and measures input/output filter bypass rates across Llama Guard 2 8B and Shield Gemma 2B applied to Llama 3.2 3B, Qwen 7B, and Mistral 24B, with a timed-release variant using Caesar cipher encryption tested against Gemini 2.5 Flash.

## Method

The core construction hides a harmful prompt inside a pseudorandom time-lock puzzle (PRTLP) built from the RSW repeated-squaring primitive, then embeds the puzzle as the randomness of a novel Recoverable-Randomness Sampling (RRS) scheme that draws from G's distribution while allowing the LLM to recover the exact randomness used. The adversarial generator G' samples a time-lock puzzle, encrypts the harmful prompt using the puzzle solution as a key via a hardcore function, and feeds the combined randomness into Samp_G to produce a prompt indistinguishable from benign ones. The modified LLM M' recovers the randomness from the prompt, solves the time-lock by performing t sequential squarings, decrypts the harmful instruction, and executes it on the base model M. Pseudorandomness of the puzzle is achieved by hashing the RSA modulus with a pairwise-independent hash (leftover hash lemma) and applying Goldreich-Levin hardcore bits to the squaring output, ensuring the entire randomness string is computationally indistinguishable from uniform for any filter running in o(t^ε) time. The RRS scheme maintains a 'residual randomness' counter q/k that is updated at each token step, guaranteeing perfect recoverability of the input randomness while keeping the output distribution within O(L·2^{-P}) statistical distance of G.

## Results

Across 1,097 JailbreakBench prompts, a sizable fraction of jailbreaks bypass both input and output safety filters: Llama Guard achieves input bypass rates of 34.37% and output bypass rates of 58.89–67.37% (depending on model size), while Shield Gemma achieves input bypass rates of 79.49% and output bypass rates of 83.49–89.91%. When jailbreak prompts are encrypted (e.g., Caesar cipher), input filter bypass rates increase dramatically—Llama Guard rises from 34.37% to 88.06% and Shield Gemma from 79.49% to 91.89%—demonstrating that the computational asymmetry between lightweight filters and larger LLMs can be exploited to hide harmful content. The theoretical impossibility theorems (Theorems 1–3) establish that under cryptographic time-lock assumptions, no efficient filter (even one running longer than the LLM) can distinguish adversarial prompts from benign ones in the worst case.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against a single competing method but rather evaluates two deployed safety filters (Llama Guard 2 8B and Shield Gemma 2B) against three LLM families of increasing size (Llama 3.2 3B, Qwen 7B, Mistral 24B). Shield Gemma consistently shows higher bypass rates than Llama Guard across all model sizes (79.49% vs. 34.37% input bypass), suggesting it is the weaker guard. Output filter bypass rates increase with model size for both filters (e.g., Llama Guard: 58.89% → 67.37% from 3B to 24B), indicating that larger models are harder to police at the output stage. The timed-release encryption attack (inspired by the paper's time-lock construction) further widens the gap: Caesar cipher encryption raises Llama Guard's input bypass from 34.37% to 88.06% and Shield Gemma's from 79.49% to 91.89%, with substitution ciphers performing comparably (81.49% and 91.98% respectively). These empirical results validate the theoretical prediction that computational asymmetry between filters and LLMs creates exploitable vulnerabilities that no amount of filter training can eliminate in the worst case.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the RSW time-lock puzzle (Rivest et al., 1996) as a core cryptographic primitive to construct adversarial prompts that are provably indistinguishable from benign ones, combining it with hardcore bits (Goldreich-Levin) and the leftover hash lemma to achieve pseudorandomness. The novel Recoverable-Randomness Sampling (RRS) technique draws on ideas from the watermarking literature (Christ et al., 2023; Zamir, 2024) to embed recoverable randomness into autoregressive sampling. Unlike prior empirical jailbreak work (Yuan et al., 2024; Fairoze et al., 2025) that demonstrates encryption-based bypasses, or Glukhov et al. (2024) which relies on undecidability or shared secrets, this paper provides formal impossibility results under standard cryptographic assumptions requiring no collusion between attacker and model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The results carry significant regulatory and architectural implications: if external (black-box) filtering is fundamentally insufficient, then safety resources must match or exceed capability resources, and auditing bodies may need access to model internals (weights, architecture). The paper leaves open whether the impossibility extends beyond RSW to post-quantum LWE-based time-lock puzzles, and whether the stylized LLM behavior (entering a special mode to solve puzzles) is realistic for current or future models. A key limitation is that the theoretical results are worst-case existence proofs rather than universal statements about all deployed LLMs, and the empirical validation is small-scale (1,097 prompts, three model families).
