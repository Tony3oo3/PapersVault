## Problem

Current medical image understanding benchmarks evaluate only final-answer accuracy, leaving the quality of intermediate reasoning steps entirely unassessed. This opacity makes it impossible to determine whether a model arrives at a correct answer through clinically sound reasoning or through flawed, hallucinated, or inconsistent logic paths—a critical gap for high-stakes diagnostic applications where interpretability and trustworthiness are essential.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263043/cropped_page3_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

Each benchmark instance pairs a medical image (from 24 examination types spanning ophthalmic, radiology, endoscopy, microscopy, ultrasound, and surface-level categories) with a question in one of four formats (single-choice, multiple-choice, true/false, short-answer) across 13 task types ranging from basic perception to complex clinical reasoning. The model is prompted to produce a structured CoT following a four-step clinical template—(1) confirm imaging modality, (2) identify key visual features, (3) draw diagnostic conclusion, (4) provide additional medical analysis—followed by a final answer. Evaluation yields four metric dimensions: correctness (F1/precision/recall against expert-annotated gold reasoning paths), efficiency (correct steps per unit time and latency ratio vs. direct inference), impact (accuracy delta between CoT and direct answering), and consistency (normalized LCS-based path similarity to a canonical reference per task).

## Method

M3CoTBench is curated through a three-stage pipeline: (1) image collection from 55 public datasets with BiomedCLIP-based feature extraction to maximize semantic diversity, class balance, and compliance; (2) QA generation via GPT-4o with conversion of existing VQA/classification/segmentation/detection labels into diverse question formats, plus inference-driven question synthesis, all calibrated by three MLLMs and human experts; (3) CoT step annotation by GPT-4o and Gemini 2.5-Pro following a clinician-validated four-step structure grounded in hypothetico-deductive reasoning, pattern recognition, and dual-process theory, then refined through multi-stage student review, automated multi-model checking, expert flag resolution, and final expert read-through. The evaluation suite introduces four CoT-specific metrics: correctness computed as average precision/recall/F1 between model-generated steps and the best-matching gold path; efficiency as correct steps per unit time plus a latency impact ratio; impact as the accuracy difference between CoT and direct prompting; and consistency as the average normalized LCS similarity between each generated path and a task-level canonical reference path selected by maximizing aggregate similarity.

## Results

On M3CoTBench, Gemini 2.5 Pro achieves the highest CoT correctness (F1 = 66.07%, P = 62.48%, R = 70.10%), while Qwen3-VL-Thinking-30B follows at F1 = 62.15%. Notably, most models show a negative reasoning impact (I < 0), meaning CoT prompting actually degrades answer accuracy; only Qwen3-VL-Thinking-8B (+4.50) and Qwen3-VL-Thinking-30B (+3.52) achieve positive impact. Claude-Sonnet-4.5 attains the highest path consistency (85.22%), and GPT-5 exhibits the lowest latency overhead (L = 1.10×) but the lowest consistency (65.39%) due to frequently omitting intermediate reasoning steps.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among closed-source models, Gemini 2.5 Pro leads in CoT correctness (F1 = 66.07%) with the highest recall (70.10%), outperforming GPT-4.1 (F1 = 60.76%) and GPT-5 (F1 = 55.13%); GPT-5's high precision (64.15%) is offset by low recall (48.34%) because it frequently bypasses step-by-step reasoning. Claude-Sonnet-4.5 achieves the best path consistency (85.22%) but moderate correctness (F1 = 56.50%). Among open-source models, Qwen3-VL-Thinking-30B (F1 = 62.15%) surpasses InternVL3.5-30B (F1 = 59.42%) and Qwen3-VL-Instruct-30B (F1 = 59.15%), and is the only model family with consistently positive reasoning impact (+3.52 to +4.50), indicating CoT genuinely improves its answers. Medical-specific MLLMs (LLaVA-Med, HuatuoGPT-Vision, HealthGPT, Lingshu, MedGemma) uniformly underperform general-purpose models in CoT correctness, with LLaVA-Med scoring the lowest F1 (30.51%) despite the highest efficiency (E = 0.35 correct steps per unit time). LLaVA-CoT, despite being purpose-built for step-by-step reasoning, ranks near the bottom in correctness (F1 = 49.80%) and shows negative impact (−3.38), suggesting that architectural CoT design alone does not guarantee clinically aligned reasoning quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two established lines of work: medical multimodal VQA benchmarks (VQA-RAD, SLAKE, OmniMedVQA, GMAI-MMBench) and CoT evaluation benchmarks for MLLMs (Visual-CoT, M3CoT, MME-CoT, CoMT). Prior medical benchmarks evaluate only final-answer accuracy, while prior CoT benchmarks target natural images. M3CoTBench carves new ground by being the first to introduce CoT-specific evaluation metrics (correctness, efficiency, impact, consistency) within a medical imaging context, grounding the four-step reasoning structure in clinician interviews and established medical cognition theories (hypothetico-deductive reasoning, pattern recognition, dual-process theory).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that CoT prompting frequently *decreases* answer accuracy in medical image understanding (negative Impact for most models), challenging the assumption that step-by-step reasoning universally helps. Medical-specific MLLMs also do not consistently outperform general-purpose models in CoT quality, suggesting domain specialization alone is insufficient for reliable clinical reasoning. Open questions include how to design CoT strategies that actually improve medical VQA without introducing hallucination or visual-detail loss, and whether the path-consistency metric can be extended to multi-turn or interactive diagnostic workflows. The benchmark is likely to become a standard reference for evaluating reasoning transparency in clinical AI.
