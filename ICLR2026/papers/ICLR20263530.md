## Problem

Evaluating video understanding model outputs remains difficult: reference-based metrics like BLEU and BERTScore miss semantic and temporal nuances, human evaluation is expensive and inconsistent, and the MLLM-as-a-Judge paradigm has been largely unexplored for video. The field also lacks large-scale evaluation resources with human preference signals and principled, task-specific evaluation criteria for video understanding.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263530/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The judge model receives a video (sampled at 1 FPS, up to 60 frames during training and 180 during evaluation), a text instruction, and one or two candidate text responses. In pointwise mode it outputs a reasoning trace inside <thinking> tags followed by an integer score (1–5) in <score> tags, and optionally an instance-specific rubric in <rubric> tags before reasoning. In pairwise mode it outputs a preference decision in <answer> tags. Dense video descriptions generated by Qwen2.5-VL-32B or GPT-4o-mini serve as semantic context during bootstrapping to reduce repeated raw-video inference.

## Method

VideoJudge uses an iterative generator-evaluator bootstrapping pipeline to synthesize training data without human annotation. A generator model produces N−1 candidate responses targeting ratings 1 through N−1 (N=5) for each video-instruction pair, while the gold response is assigned rating N; an evaluator model then scores each candidate, and responses whose evaluator-assigned rating deviates from the intended rating by more than a threshold α are sent back to the generator with the evaluator's feedback for refinement (up to T iterations). The resulting aligned (video, instruction, response, rating) triplets (~104K examples) are used to fine-tune Qwen2.5-VL-3B/7B with standard negative log-likelihood in BF16. A key design choice is training the judge to first generate an instruction-specific evaluation rubric at test time before reasoning and scoring, which substantially improves calibration and interpretability. This bootstrapped supervision enables the 3B and 7B judges to match or surpass Qwen2.5-VL-32B/72B on most meta-evaluation benchmarks, and the same pipeline also yields new pointwise and pairwise meta-evaluation benchmarks.

## Results

On pointwise evaluation, VideoJudge-7B achieves Spearman 0.78 and MAE 0.52 on VideoJudgeLLaVA-MetaEval, matching or exceeding Qwen2.5-VL-72B (S=0.80, MAE=0.61) while using ~10× fewer parameters. On LongVideoBench, VideoJudge-7B attains Δ(C-D)=1.16, surpassing Qwen2.5-VL-72B (1.06) and Qwen2.5-VL-32B (1.08). VideoJudge-3B reaches Spearman 0.82 on VideoJudgeLLaVA, outperforming Qwen2.5-VL-32B (0.80). In pairwise evaluation, VideoJudge-7B achieves 95.60% accuracy on VideoJudge-Pairwise and 93.67% on VideoJudge-Human, both exceeding Qwen2.5-VL-72B (94.00% and 94.51% respectively on the latter).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against unimodal Qwen3 baselines (0.6B–14B), VideoJudge-7B substantially outperforms on LongVideoBench (Δ=1.16 vs. best 0.86 for Qwen3-1.7B thinking mode), confirming that video input is critical for temporal evaluation. Against same-size video models (LLaVA-NeXT-7B, Video-R1-7B, LLaVA-OneVision), VideoJudge-7B wins on Spearman correlation across VideoJudgeLLaVA (0.78 vs. 0.67–0.77) and VideoJudgeVCG (0.74 vs. 0.46–0.77), and dominates on LongVideoBench Δ (1.16 vs. 0.45–0.83). Against the larger Qwen2.5-VL-32B/72B, VideoJudge-7B matches or exceeds on MAE (0.52 vs. 0.59–0.61 on VideoJudgeLLaVA), ECE on VATEX (0.64 vs. 0.79–0.81), and LongVideoBench Δ (1.16 vs. 1.06–1.08), though it trails slightly on Spearman (0.78 vs. 0.80). VideoJudge-3B surpasses Qwen2.5-VL-32B on VideoJudgeLLaVA Spearman (0.82 vs. 0.80) and VATEX ECE (0.63 vs. 0.81). In pairwise evaluation, VideoJudge-7B beats Qwen2.5-VL-32B on all three benchmarks (VAA: 85.49 vs. 80.78; VJ: 95.60 vs. 91.20; VJ-H: 93.67 vs. 92.83) and narrowly trails Qwen2.5-VL-72B only on VAA (85.49 vs. 89.80) and VJ-H (93.67 vs. 94.51).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm (Zheng et al. 2023; Kim et al. 2023/2024) and its multimodal generalization (Chen et al. 2024; Lee et al. 2024) to video understanding, but departs from prior work in two key ways. First, rather than distilling from proprietary models like GPT-4 or operating zero-shot, it introduces an iterative generator-evaluator bootstrapping loop that synthesizes over 100K training examples without human annotation, drawing on self-refinement ideas (Madaan et al. 2023) and self-consistency (Wang et al. 2023). Second, it trains the judge to generate instance-specific rubrics at test time, extending the static rubric approach of Prometheus (Kim et al. 2023) to dynamic, per-example criteria grounded in the specific video and instruction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

VideoJudge provides the first reproducible, open-weight, scalable E suite for video understanding that matches or exceeds 32B/72B baselines at 3B/7B scale, and releases meta-e benchmarks, bootstrapped datasets, and trained models. The finding that text-only LLM judges underperform MLLM judges and that long chain-of-thought reasoning does not help clarifies that raw video input is indispensable for faithful E. Open questions include the persistent overestimation bias in the mid-to-high rating range (only 36.9% accuracy at rating 3), the partial closed-loop nature of the bootstrapping pipeline, and the scarcity of fully independent large-scale human-annotated pointwise benchmarks for video. The bootstrapping methodology is modality-agnostic and could naturally extend to audio, image, or other settings where human E data is scarce.
