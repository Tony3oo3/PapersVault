## Problem

Existing human pose estimation datasets and models assume every individual has a complete set of limbs, leaving people with amputations, congenital limb differences, and prosthetic limbs entirely unsupported. Current models produce anatomically implausible predictions for missing or altered limbs, and no benchmark exists to measure how well pose estimators handle residual anatomy or calibrate confidence for absent joints.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261996/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The dataset provides RGB video frames (313 sequences, 327k frames at highest available resolution) annotated with a personalized 25-keypoint schema per individual (17 standard COCO joints plus 8 residual-limb endpoints, with a per-person presence mask indicating which keypoints apply). Beyond keypoints, each frame carries pixel-level segmentation masks, bounding boxes, persistent tracking IDs, and a per-limb prosthesis status label. The video modality is essential: temporal continuity lets annotators distinguish occluded limbs from truly absent ones, which is impossible in single-image settings.

## Method

The core contribution is a dataset and benchmark rather than a new model architecture. The authors extend the COCO 17-keypoint schema with 8 residual-limb endpoint keypoints (above/below elbow and knee, left/right), explicitly excluding prosthetic geometry from the keypoint definition and representing it instead via segmentation masks and prosthesis status labels. Annotation is guided by an internationally accredited para-athletics classifier, with 12 trained annotators using SAM2-assisted segmentation and an 80% cross-check accuracy threshold. To evaluate whether models correctly suppress confidence on anatomically impossible joints, the paper introduces the Limb-specific Confidence Consistency (LiCC) metric, which checks that each visible keypoint's predicted confidence exceeds that of its mutually exclusive counterparts (e.g., a residual wrist keypoint must outscore the normal wrist). The benchmark evaluates six single-frame models (ViTPose, RTMPose, YOLOX-Pose, DEKR, Swin, ViPNAS) and two video models (DCPose, DSTA) under four training/evaluation splits, revealing that LiCC remains around 60% for most methods and residual-limb AP drops to near zero in video settings.

## Results

On InclusiveVidPose (327k frames, 398 individuals with limb deficiencies), the best single-frame model ViTPose-H achieves 86.3 AP / 87.6 AR on the 25-keypoint schema when trained in-domain, but LiCC (the proposed confidence-consistency metric) remains only 73.6%, indicating persistent confusion between intact and residual limbs. When models are trained solely on COCO and zero-shot evaluated on InclusiveVidPose, AP drops substantially (e.g., ViTPose-H falls from 86.3 to 81.5 AP), confirming a significant distribution shift. For video pose estimation, DCPose and DSTA achieve only 43.2 and 43.7 mean AP respectively, with near-zero AP on upper-limb residual endpoints (ArmUp: 1.6/0.6, ArmLow: 0.2/0.0), demonstrating that temporal aggregation actively harms residual-limb localization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among single-frame models, ViTPose-H achieves the highest AP (86.3) and AR (87.6) on InclusiveVidPose, outperforming Swin-B (81.8 AP), RTMPose-M/L (82.2 AP), and DEKR (77.7 AP). However, on the proposed LiCC metric, YOLOX-Pose variants lead (70.7–78.7) due to their confidence-learning mechanism, while DEKR (55.2) and ViPNAS (53.8) score the lowest, indicating they assign high confidence to anatomically impossible keypoints. For video pose estimation, DSTA marginally outperforms DCPose (43.7 vs. 43.2 mean AP), but both are near-zero on upper-limb residual endpoints (ArmUp: 0.6 vs. 1.6; ArmLow: 0.0 vs. 0.2), showing that temporal aggregation propagates hallucinated full-limb configurations across frames. The COCO→InclusiveVidPose zero-shot setting reveals that even strong models like ViTPose-H (81.5 AP) and YOLOX-Pose-L (80.0 AP) degrade relative to their COCO→COCO performance, confirming a genuine distribution shift rather than annotation inconsistency. Overall, no method exceeds 74% LiCC, and residual-limb endpoint AP in video remains below 18%, establishing InclusiveVidPose as a substantially harder benchmark than existing pose datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the standard human pose estimation pipeline (COCO 17-keypoint schema, top-down/bottom-up architectures like ViTPose, RTMPose, DEKR) to a population entirely absent from existing benchmarks. It departs from prior HPE datasets (COCO, MPII, PoseTrack) by introducing 8 residual-limb-end keypoints, per-limb prosthesis status, and video-based disambiguation of occlusion versus true absence, guided by a para-athletics classifier. The evaluation adapts the PoseTrack protocol and proposes a new confidence-calibration metric (LiCC) that has no analogue in the standard OKS-based literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dataset fills a critical representational gap for ~445 million people with amputations and 31 million children with congenital limb differences, enabling rehabilitation monitoring and health assessment applications that current models simply cannot support. A key open question is how to architect pose estimators that explicitly model absent joints rather than hallucinating them; the near-zero residual-limb AP in video models suggests temporal aggregation assumptions (designed for temporary occlusions) are fundamentally mismatched to structural limb absence. Limitations include 2D-only annotations, no 3D ground truth, and the inherent ambiguity of distinguishing occluded from absent limbs even in video, which the authors acknowledge as a source of label noise.
