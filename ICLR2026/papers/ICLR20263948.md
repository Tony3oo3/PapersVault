## Problem

Prior graph-reasoning benchmarks for LLMs examined prompting strategies, serialization formats, or graph families in isolation, leaving it unclear whether performance gains stem from the model, the representation, or the instruction. This paper asks whether LLMs can reliably reason about graph-theoretic properties expressed in natural language, and how three interacting dimensions—graph type, serialization format, and prompt scheme—jointly determine model accuracy.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263948/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each query pairs a graph (5–30 nodes, generated by one of 7 synthetic generators) serialized into one of 7 textual formats (adjacency matrix/list/set, edge set/list, GMoL, GMaL) with a natural-language question about a canonical property (connectivity, cycle, diameter, BFS order, shortest path, triangle count), wrapped in one of 9 prompt schemes (0-shot, k-shot, CoT, algorithm, instruct, and their 0-shot variants, plus LTM). The evaluation produces a binary correct/incorrect score per query via rule-based extraction, and the RL component outputs the optimal serialization-prompt pairing for each task-difficulty cell, reducing exploration cost to ~25% of exhaustive search.

## Method

GRAPHOMNI is a modular, extensible benchmark that jointly varies 7 graph generators (ERM, ERP, BERM, BERP, BAG, BAF, SF), 7 serialization formats, and 9 prompt schemes across 6 tasks at 3 difficulty levels, yielding 241,726 queries with a random baseline for each task. The key empirical finding is that no single serialization or prompt dominates across all models and tasks—accuracy gaps of up to 40% arise from input-format choices alone—validating the need for multi-dimensional evaluation. To make the benchmark actionable, the authors formulate serialization-strategy selection as a sequential decision problem solved with a Deep Q-Network (DQN): the state encodes task type and difficulty, actions select one serialization component per epoch, and a binary reward signals correctness. This RL-Opt procedure identifies near-optimal prompt-serialization pairs at only 25% of the exhaustive-search cost while retaining ~90% of peak accuracy, and an RL-Scale experiment confirms the approach generalizes as additional factors are added.

## Results

On the GRAPHOMNI benchmark (241,726 queries across 6 graph tasks × 3 difficulty levels × 7 graph types × 7 serializations × 9 prompts), o4-mini leads overall, achieving 95.46% on BFS order (Easy), 98.88% on Diameter (Easy), and 84.54% on Triangle counting (Easy), but drops sharply to 32.45% (BFS order), 34.61% (Diameter), and 17.53% (Triangle) on Hard difficulty. Claude-3.5 is a close second (e.g., 91.42% BFS order Easy, 98.38% Connectivity Easy). Among open-source models, Qwen-3 (8B) is the strongest (65.87% BFS order Easy, 97.17% Connectivity Easy) yet still trails closed-source leaders by 25–30 points on hard tasks. The RL-based serialization selector (DQN) reduces evaluation cost to ~25% of exhaustive search while retaining an average success rate of 0.90 (i.e., 90% of the best achievable accuracy).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new model but benchmarks 11 LLMs (7 open-source, 4 closed-source) against task-specific random baselines. Closed-source reasoning models (o4-mini, Claude-3.5) dominate: o4-mini achieves the highest accuracy on 14 of 18 task–difficulty cells, while Claude-3.5 leads on Connectivity (98.38–99.11%) and Shortest path (94.35–87.88%). Among open-source models, Qwen-3 (8B) is the clear leader, outperforming Qwen-2.5 (72B) by 10–20 points on hard tasks (e.g., 39.83% vs. 29.59% on Diameter Hard), demonstrating that reasoning-centric design at small scale beats pure scaling. All models remain far above random baselines (e.g., 50% for Cycle, ~11% for Diameter Easy), confirming genuine graph reasoning rather than guessing. A key finding is that no single serialization format or prompt scheme is universally optimal: accuracy gaps of up to 40% arise from representation choices alone (e.g., GPT-4o on Diameter), and open-source models benefit more from multi-shot exemplars while closed-source models prefer 0-CoT or 0-Algorithm prompts. The RL-based DQN selector recovers ~90% of the best achievable accuracy at only 25% of the exhaustive-search cost, validating the practical value of adaptive configuration.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on evaluating LLMs for graph-theoretic reasoning (NLGraph, GraphInstruct, GPT4Graph, GraphQA, GraphArena, GraphWild) by being the first to jointly vary three interacting dimensions—graph type, serialization format, and prompt scheme—rather than examining them in isolation. Prior benchmarks typically fixed one or two dimensions, making it impossible to attribute performance gains to the model, the representation, or the instruction. GRAPHOMNI carves new ground by constructing a full combinatorial evaluation space (241,726 queries across 7×7×9 configurations), adding a random baseline for fair calibration, and introducing a modularized, extensible framework design absent from all prior work. The RL-based prompt search (DQN) repurposes classical sequential decision-making to the novel problem of adaptive serialization-prompt selection, a direction not explored in prior graph-LLM benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that no single serialization or prompt scheme is universally optimal, with accuracy gaps up to 40% from representation changes alone, has direct practical implications for anyone deploying LLMs on structured graph data: evaluation and deployment must be task-specific rather than one-size-fits-all. The RL-based selector reducing evaluation cost by 75% while retaining ~90% of peak accuracy offers a concrete, low-cost path to adaptive graph-LLM pipelines. Open questions include whether the conclusions hold beyond 30 nodes (partially tested at 30–50), whether the open/closed-source gap will narrow as open models improve, and whether the RL approach generalizes to new serialization formats or prompt families added to the extensible framework. The benchmark's synthetic-only main evaluation, while justified for controllability, leaves open how well findings transfer to the sparser, more heterogeneous structure of real-world graphs.
