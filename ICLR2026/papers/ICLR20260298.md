## Problem

Existing temporal search methods for long-form video understanding (e.g., VideoAgent, T*, VideoTree) rely on hand-crafted multi-turn workflows with static frame sampling, preventing end-to-end learning of optimal search strategies. Moreover, applying standard outcome-reward RL (GRPO) to video reasoning produces two failure modes: insufficient temporal exploration (the model skips critical frames) and inconsistent logical reasoning (intermediate steps contradict the final answer), because the final-answer reward provides no supervision over intermediate search decisions.

## Input / Output

The model receives a long-form video (up to 768 frames, 10,240 video tokens, 12–256 tokens per frame) and a text question, along with a small set of uniformly sampled preview frames. During reasoning, the model emits structured search calls specifying temporal boundaries (start_time, end_time) and a textual query; the environment returns up to 8 frames per turn via SigLIP-based retrieval with DPP diversity sampling, for a maximum of 8 search turns. The final output is a text answer embedded in an interleaved text-video chain-of-thought, where each search step appends retrieved frames with explicit absolute timestamps to the reasoning context.

## Method

TimeSearch-R reformulates temporal search as interleaved text-video thinking, where the policy model alternates between textual reasoning and tool-call-based frame retrieval, trained end-to-end via a two-stage pipeline: SFT cold-start on GPT-4o-generated interleaved trajectories (with search results masked to force learning of meaningful queries), followed by RL post-training. The key algorithmic contribution is GRPO-CSV, which augments standard GRPO with a Completeness Self-Verification reward: after a rollout, the same policy model re-answers the question using only the dynamically searched frames (no further searching allowed), and the completeness reward is the CSV answer's correctness conditioned on the original answer being correct. This conditional design enforces high mutual information between the answer and the searched video evidence, transforming the sparse binary outcome signal into a denser credit-assignment signal that supervises intermediate search decisions. A two-stage data filtering pipeline removes samples solvable by linguistic bias alone and those unsolvable even with extensive search, ensuring the RL signal is informative.

## Results

On temporal search, TimeSearch-R achieves a temporal F1 of 8.4 on Haystack-LVBench (3.4× the previous best of 2.5 by T*) and 53.3% QA accuracy on Haystack-Ego4D (+8.3% over T*). On long-form video understanding at ~32 frames, it reaches 64.1% on VideoMME, 71.3% on MLVU (+13.2% over the Qwen2.5-VL base), and 61.6% on LongVideoBench. On complex video reasoning (Video-Holmes), it scores 42.2% overall, surpassing the base model by 11.8% and matching GPT-4o (42.0%) while outperforming Video-R1 (36.5%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the base model Qwen2.5-VL-7B with static 32-frame sampling, TimeSearch-R improves VideoMME by +2.9%, MLVU by +13.2%, and LongVideoBench by +3.4%, demonstrating that learned adaptive search substantially outperforms uniform sampling. Compared to the text-only reasoning model Video-R1-7B, TimeSearch-R wins on all three benchmarks (VideoMME 64.1 vs 59.9, MLVU 71.3 vs 61.6, LVB 61.6 vs 56.4), validating that interleaved video search is more effective than pure textual CoT. Against hand-crafted temporal search agents, TimeSearch-R surpasses VideoAgent (56.0 VideoMME with 87 frames) and T*+GPT-4o (64.1 VideoMME with 32 frames) while using fewer frames and lower inference cost (4.65s vs 26.00s per sample). On the temporal search benchmark specifically, TimeSearch-R achieves a temporal F1 of 8.4 versus 2.5 for T* and 2.2 for uniform GPT-4o, a more than 3× improvement. The method does not surpass GPT-4o's raw VideoMME score (64.1 vs 70.8 at 32 frames), but transferring TimeSearch-R's learned search policy to GPT-4o yields 73.1, exceeding GPT-4o's static sampling by 2.3 points. On Video-Holmes, TimeSearch-R (42.2%) matches GPT-4o (42.0%) and slightly trails Gemini-2.0-Flash-Thinking (43.1%), while substantially outperforming Video-R1 (36.5%) and the base model (27.8%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends GRPO (from DeepSeek-R1) with a Completeness Self-Verification (CSV) reward that supervises intermediate search decisions, addressing the sparse-reward problem when applying outcome-only RL to multi-turn video interaction. It also generalizes the 'Thinking with Images' paradigm (Hu et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2025) from spatial cropping to temporal clip retrieval, and builds on Search-R1 (Jin et al., 2025) which applied RL to text-based search. The key departure from prior temporal search work (VideoAgent, T*, VideoTree) is replacing hand-crafted multi-tool workflows with an end-to-end learned policy that interleaves reasoning and search within a single model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The CSV mechanism provides a practical, annotation-free way to supervise intermediate steps in multi-turn video reasoning, and the finding that the learned search policy transfers to GPT-4o and LLaVA-OV-72B suggests temporal search strategies are model-agnostic and could be deployed as a lightweight front-end for any LVLM. Limitations include the 7B scale (frame-number prediction and temporal grounding errors persist), the reliance on a small VLM (SigLIP) for frame retrieval that may miss semantically subtle cues, and the difficulty of automatically evaluating interleaved reasoning quality. Open questions include whether CSV-style process rewards scale to larger models, whether the DPP-based retrieval can be replaced by learned selectors, and how to handle videos where the relevant evidence is distributed across many short segments rather than a single clip.
