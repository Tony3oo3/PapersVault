## Problem

Existing medical VLM benchmarks overwhelmingly emphasize classification accuracy and statistical generalization (the 'Breadth' axis), creating an evaluation illusion where models appear clinically ready while still failing at high-stakes diagnostic reasoning. The paper argues that a complementary 'Depth' axis—probing multimodal synthesis, ambiguity resolution, and structured justification under uncertainty—is missing from the evaluation landscape, and that success on breadth benchmarks is a poor predictor of reasoning fidelity.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262942/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each of the 120 cases provides multi-sequence MRI/CT images (T1, T2, FLAIR, CT; base64-encoded and interleaved with text), structured EHR fields (age, sex, lab values), and free-text clinical narratives (HPI, neuro exam, impression), with text normalized to a token budget to control for length effects. The 200 derived tasks span three families—differential diagnosis (ranked hypotheses with justification), lesion recognition (type and location), and rationale generation (explanatory reasoning chains)—stratified into three difficulty levels from direct pattern recognition to multi-turn iterative consultation. Evaluation produces pass@1/pass@5 diagnostic accuracy, BERTScore semantic fidelity, a clinically calibrated LLM-grader reasoning score, and a five-category error taxonomy annotation for each incorrect response.

## Method

Neural-MedBench is constructed via a four-stage funnel pipeline that distills over 2,000 candidate cases from ADNI, OASIS, Radiopaedia, and JAMA Neurology into 120 expert-curated, multimodal neurology cases, with ground truths annotated as structured reasoning narratives rather than single labels. The core evaluation protocol is a two-stage hybrid: a GPT-4o-based LLM grader, guided by neurology-specific rubrics, is first validated against a panel of board-certified neurologists (achieving Pearson r > 0.9 correlation), then released as a fully automated community grader; a human performance baseline (5 medical students, 5 senior physicians) anchors all model scores. Sixteen VLMs spanning proprietary frontier systems, open-source generalists, and medical-specialized models are evaluated zero-shot under a structured 'experienced neurologist' role prompt. The paper's central empirical finding is that reasoning failures (51%) dominate over perceptual failures (27%) in the error analysis, providing the first quantitative evidence that the Breadth and Depth axes are largely uncorrelated.

## Results

On Neural-MedBench (200 tasks), even the strongest model GPT-5 achieves only 36.7% pass@1 on Direct Diagnosis, falling below the Senior Physician baseline of 40.0%. Performance drops sharply on Complex Diseases, where the best model (Lingshu) reaches 21.7% pass@1 versus 35.5% for the Senior Physician, and on Multi-round Dialogue where GPT-5 leads at 19.5% pass@1. Error analysis of 100 incorrect responses reveals that Reasoning Failure (51%) dominates over Perceptual Failure (27%), Visual Hallucination (13%), and Knowledge Gap (9%), confirming that the primary bottleneck is integrative clinical reasoning rather than visual perception. The benchmark costs only $2.50 in image tokens for GPT-4o evaluation, an order of magnitude cheaper than GMAI-MMBench ($30) or OmniMedVQA ($320).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among proprietary frontier models, GPT-5 leads on pass@1 across all three task families (36.7% Direct Dx, 28.3% Complex Dx, 19.5% Multi-round), while Gemini 2.5-Pro achieves the highest pass@5 (50.0% on Direct Dx), indicating broader differential generation. Medical-specialized models show mixed results: MedGemma-27B-it is the strongest on Direct Diagnosis pass@1 (30.0%) among medical VLMs, while Lingshu-32B leads on Complex Diseases pass@1 (21.7%) and Multi-round pass@5 (20.0%), suggesting domain fine-tuning helps most on ambiguous multi-modal integration. Generalist models like Claude 4.0-Sonnet (16.7% Direct Dx) and Qwen-VL-2.5 (10.0%) trail both frontier and specialized models. RadFM and Med-Flamingo perform worst, with 0.0% pass@1 on Direct Diagnosis, indicating that earlier medical VLMs lack sufficient reasoning capacity. Critically, all 16 models fall below the Senior Physician baseline (40.0% / 35.5% / 15.0% pass@1), with the gap widening from ~10 pp on Direct Dx to ~14 pp on Complex Dx. On the Breadth-Axis benchmarks (Table 3), GPT-4o scores 53.96% on GMAI-MMBench and 29.74% on OmniMedVQA, yet only 9.67% on Neural-MedBench, empirically demonstrating the Breadth–Depth disconnect the paper argues.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new conceptual ground by proposing a Two-Axis Evaluation Framework (Breadth vs. Depth) for medical VLM assessment, departing from the dominant paradigm of large-scale classification benchmarks (MedMNIST v2, MultiMedQA, GMAI-MMBench, OmniMedVQA) that reward statistical generalization. It repurposes the OSCE (Objective Structured Clinical Examination) paradigm from medical education as a design template for AI evaluation, and builds on recent LLM-as-judge methodologies (EVAL, LLMEval-Med) for its hybrid scoring pipeline. Unlike prior meta-benchmarks (DiagnosisArena, MedAgentsBench, Rad-Bench) that remain breadth-oriented, Neural-MedBench explicitly targets reasoning fidelity through compact, expert-curated, multi-turn diagnostic cases, positioning itself as the missing Depth-axis complement rather than a replacement for existing resources.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that reasoning failures (51%) dominate over perceptual failures (27%) in model errors, combined with the stark performance drop from breadth benchmarks (~54% on GMAI-MMBench) to Neural-MedBench (~10%), provides concrete empirical evidence that current VLMs' clinical readiness is overstated. The Two-Axis Framework offers a useful conceptual lens for the field, and the open, extensible benchmark with a validated LLM grader lowers the barrier for reproducible reasoning evaluation. However, significant limitations temper the impact: only 200 tasks in a single specialty (neurology), a very small human baseline (n=5 per group), bias toward imaging-correlated pathologies, and the use of GPT-4o as both a benchmarked model and the primary grader. Open questions include whether the breadth-depth disconnect generalizes across other medical specialties, whether the compact design captures sufficient statistical signal, and whether the LLM grader's reliability holds as model capabilities evolve.
