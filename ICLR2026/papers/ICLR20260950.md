## Problem

Existing reaction condition recommendation methods—whether specialized chemical models or general-purpose LLMs—predict plausible conditions (solvent, catalyst, reagent) but rarely provide mechanistic justification for why those conditions are appropriate. This opacity limits their utility in high-stakes scientific workflows where chemists need falsifiable, evidence-backed rationales rather than black-box predictions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260950/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system takes Reactant SMILES, Product SMILES, and optional reaction context as input. It outputs K ranked condition configurations spanning five roles (catalyst, solvent1, solvent2, reagent1, reagent2), each paired with a structured rationale ρ(c) = (M, S, E, Π) comprising domain reasoning, verifiable constraint checks, aligned retrieved evidence, and a concise derivation. Evaluation uses the maximum Tanimoto similarity across three molecular fingerprint types (Path-based, MACCS, Morgan) between ground truth and top-k predictions, with stereochemistry excluded during canonicalization.

## Method

ChemMAS decomposes condition reasoning into four collaborative stages: (1) a General Chemist agent parses SMILES via Functional Group Tagger, Constraint Engine, and Chemical Knowledge Base to produce a mechanistic Reaction Report; (2) Multi-Channel Recall queries a structured reaction database through type-, reactant-, and product-centric channels, then applies slot-level recombination to build a 5,000-candidate pool; (3) Tournament Selection narrows candidates to Top-50 via pairwise knockout, where four specialized agents (A_Full, A_Cat, A_Sol, A_Rea) conduct multi-step evidence-seeking reasoning with iterative KB re-querying and constraint checks, resolving each pair by majority vote; (4) rationales are aggregated into interpretable, citation-backed justifications. All agents share a Qwen3-8B-Instruct backbone trained under a two-stage protocol: SFT (Chemical Teaching) serializes tool invocations (<search>, <memory>) into structured trajectories for cold-start tool-integrated reasoning, followed by GRPO-based RL (Tool Incentivization) with a hierarchical reward that jointly optimizes answer correctness and a multi-tool bonus for combined tool usage.

## Results

On the private organic reaction dataset (544,591 entries), ChemMAS achieves Top-1 Tanimoto similarity of 78.1% (Catalyst), 85.4% (Solvent1), 76.3% (Solvent2), 88.3% (Reagent1), and 73.6% (Reagent2), outperforming the strongest general-purpose LLM (GPT-5) by 15–25% relative and the best specialized model (MM RCR) by 70–90% relative. On the out-of-distribution ChemCoTBench benchmark (90 samples), ChemMAS reaches 62.1% Top-1 for catalyst, 57.8% for solvent, and 51.2% for reagent, surpassing the second-best model (Gemini 2.5-Pro) by 16.5%, 13.7%, and 11.1% respectively. The system also achieves 92.8 LLM-Score on interpretability evaluation versus 62.5–77.2 for general-purpose LLMs, with mechanistic grounding accuracies of 95.8% (Main FG), 90.2% (By-product), and 92.5% (Reaction Type).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ChemMAS dominates all eight baselines across every condition type and Top-k setting. Against specialized chemical models, the gains are largest: RCR (Gao et al., 2018) scores 40.3% Top-1 on Catalyst versus ChemMAS's 78.1% (~94% relative gain), Reagent Transformer (Andronov et al., 2023) reaches only 35.3%, and MM RCR (Zhang et al., 2024b) achieves 43.4%. Among general-purpose LLMs, GPT-5 is the strongest competitor (62.7% Catalyst Top-1), yet ChemMAS still leads by 15.4 absolute points; Gemini 2.5-Pro (63.4%) and DeepSeek-R1 (52.8%) trail further behind. On the OOD ChemCoTBench, ChemMAS's advantage over Gemini 2.5-Pro narrows to 16.5% (Catalyst) and 11.1% (Reagent) but remains consistent, confirming that the gains are not purely retrieval-driven. Claude 3.7 Sonnet performs weakest among LLMs (43.6% Catalyst Top-1), suggesting that general reasoning capability alone is insufficient without chemistry-specific tool integration and multi-agent deliberation.

## Relation to Prior Work

ChemMAS sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) reaction condition prediction (RCR), where prior work (Gao et al. 2018, Andronov et al. 2023, Zhang et al. 2024b) trains dedicated models to output condition labels without mechanistic justification; (2) LLM-based multi-agent debate (Du et al. 2023, Zhu et al. 2025), which ChemMAS adapts into a tournament-style knockout for candidate ranking rather than open-ended QA; and (3) tool-integrated reasoning trained via SFT + GRPO (Dong et al. 2025, Shao et al. 2024), which the paper repurposes with a chemistry-specific hierarchical reward that explicitly incentivizes joint use of a Chemical Knowledge Base and a shared Memory. The key conceptual departure from all three threads is the formalization of a validity predicate (Eq. 1) that requires every predicted condition to carry a falsifiable, evidence-aligned rationale, upgrading the task from ranking to certified reasoning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By coupling interpretable mechanistic grounding with retrieval-augmented multi-agent debate, ChemMAS offers a template for moving scientific AI from opaque top-k prediction toward auditable, citation-backed decision support—a shift that is practically important for high-stakes workflows like drug synthesis and process chemistry. The 15–25% Top-1 gains over frontier general-purpose LLMs (GPT-5, Gemini 2.5-Pro) using an 8B backbone suggest that structured tool use and domain-specific agent roles can outperform raw model scale for narrow scientific tasks. Open questions include whether the tournament-selection protocol scales beyond the 5,000-candidate pool, whether the interpretability claims (92.8 LLM-Score) survive adversarial or out-of-domain scrutiny beyond the 90-sample ChemCoTBench subset, and whether the framework transfers to domains with less structured condition spaces (e.g., materials, bioinformatics) as the authors themselves note.
