## Problem

The squared ℓ2 distance used in GRASSP for continuous-time graph representation via sequential survival processes violates the triangle inequality, making it an invalid metric for the latent space and causing distortion of relative node positions in social, contact, and collaboration networks. Reverting to the plain ℓ2 distance is infeasible because the corresponding hazard function integral has no closed-form solution, and other ℓp distances (p>1, p≠2) are similarly intractable. This paper addresses the gap by showing that the ℓ1 distance yields a tractable, closed-form integral while restoring a true latent metric space.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a continuous-time dynamic graph specified by node-pair connection and disconnection event times over an interval [0,T), with node trajectories approximated by piecewise-linear segments over B bins. It produces ultra-low-dimensional (ideally D=2) node position vectors in a latent space, from which connection-state probabilities are derived for reconstruction, completion, and future prediction tasks. A notable structural detail is that the hazard integral decomposes into at most D+1 one-dimensional exponential pieces determined by the zero-crossing times of relative node coordinates, enabling fully tensor-parallelized evaluation across all node-pair-event observations simultaneously.

## Method

ℓ1 LD-CTGR replaces the squared ℓ2 distance in GRASSP's hazard function with the ℓ1 norm, yielding λ_ij(s,t) = exp(β(s) + s‖r_i(t) − r_j(t)‖₁). The key theoretical result (Theorem 1) shows that the integral of this hazard over any event interval is a closed-form piece-wise exponential integral, where the breakpoints are the zero-crossing times of each dimension of the relative position vector; for D=2, at most two such breakpoints exist, enabling a tensor-parallelized O(1)-per-observation computation (Theorem 2). Because the ℓ1 norm is non-differentiable, the paper derives a descent direction ∂λ_ij(r_i) = exp(β(s)+s‖r_i−r_j‖₁)·s·sign(r_i−r_j) that replaces the gradient in backpropagation (Theorem 3), verified to be a true subgradient when no coordinates coincide or when s=1, and a valid descent direction in all other cases. Training proceeds by maximizing the log-likelihood of the sequential survival process via Adam with this subgradient-compatible update, achieving the same O(L·M/ε²) complexity as GRASSP while operating in a geometrically valid latent metric space.

## Results

On the network completion (out-of-sample) task, ℓ1 LD-CTGR achieves the best ROC and PR on 10 out of 11 datasets. Headline numbers include ROC 0.817 / PR 0.813 on Synthetic-α (vs. 0.680 / 0.607 for the next-best DyGFormer), ROC 0.982 / PR 0.983 on Wikipedia, and ROC 0.988 / PR 0.983 on Reddit. On the large-scale Facebook dataset (~82K nodes, 200M edges), ℓ1 LD-CTGR reaches ROC 0.572 / PR 0.687, outperforming GRASSP (0.500 / 0.500) and all other baselines. For future connection prediction (across-sample), ℓ1 LD-CTGR leads on most datasets, e.g., ROC 0.922 on Synthetic-α vs. 0.875 for GRASSP, and ROC 0.778 on US Legis. vs. 0.761 for GRASSP.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ℓ1 LD-CTGR outperforms all 8 baselines on 10 of 11 datasets for network completion; the two exceptions are Synthetic-β PR (Node2Vec 0.694 vs. 0.641) and Wikipedia PR (DyGFormer 0.874 vs. 0.810). The closest overall competitor is GRASSP (the direct predecessor using squared ℓ2 distance), which ℓ1 LD-CTGR surpasses by 0.04–0.19 ROC on most datasets; the gap is largest on Synthetic-α (0.817 vs. 0.559) and Facebook (0.572 vs. 0.500). DyGFormer, a Transformer-based method, is the strongest non-survival-process baseline, ranking second on in-sample reconstruction and competitive on Wikipedia/Reddit, but it trails ℓ1 LD-CTGR by 0.01–0.14 ROC on completion. On the large-scale NeurIPS dataset (~5K nodes, 1.2M edges), ℓ1 LD-CTGR achieves ROC 0.533 / PR 0.559, beating GraphMixer (0.467 / 0.536) and DyGFormer (0.496 / 0.530), while GRASSP drops to 0.360 / 0.468. For across-sample prediction, ℓ1 LD-CTGR leads on 9 of 11 datasets, with the most notable margins on Synthetic-α (ROC 0.922 vs. GRASSP 0.875) and US Legis. (ROC 0.778 vs. GRASSP 0.761); it slightly trails GRASSP on HyperText ROC (0.568 vs. 0.607) and Infectious ROC (0.528 vs. 0.491 is actually a win, so the loss is on HyperText only). Overall, the ℓ1 distance consistently improves over the squared ℓ2 distance of GRASSP, validating the triangle-inequality argument.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends GRASSP (Çelikkanat et al., AAAI 2024), which models continuous-time graph representation via a sequential survival process with a squared ℓ₂ latent distance in the hazard function. The key departure is replacing the squared ℓ₂ distance with the ℓ₁ norm to restore a valid metric space (satisfying the triangle inequality), while the piecewise-velocity node movement model is inherited from PIVEM (Çelikkanat et al., 2022). The paper also positions itself within the broader CTGR literature (CTDNE, HTNE, GraphMixer, DyGFormer) but specifically addresses a theoretical deficiency in the survival-process sub-line that prior methods have not considered.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The triangle inequality violation in squared ℓ₂ distance is a genuine geometric concern for latent metric spaces, particularly in social and collaboration networks where relative node proximity carries semantic meaning. The practical finding that ℓ₁ distance yields a closed-form piece-wise exponential integral (whereas the differentiable ℓ₂ distance does not) is a useful theoretical result that enables efficient computation without numerical approximation. Open questions include whether other valid metrics (e.g., ℓ_p with p<1, or non-Euclidean metrics) could further improve geometric diversity, and whether the subgradient-based optimization approach generalizes to more complex non-differentiable objectives in graph learning.
