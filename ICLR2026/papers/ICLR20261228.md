## Problem

Post-training (SFT or RL) of LLMs inevitably shifts performance across domains, yet the mechanisms governing how these shifts transfer remain unexplored. Existing analyses are post-hoc, requiring the fine-tuned model to be available, which prevents practitioners from predicting which domains will degrade or improve before committing to a training run. The paper asks whether cross-domain transferability can be forecast from the pretrained model alone, without any fine-tuning.

## Input / Output

The method consumes only the pretrained model (no fine-tuned checkpoint): it extracts SAE encoder features from the residual stream (e.g., layer 25 for Qwen2.5-7B), uses 2 ground-truth CoTs from the SFT training set as ICL demonstrations to identify the top-N shifted dimensions, and then probes downstream domain data (e.g., MMLU-Pro subsets) for activation patterns on those dimensions. The output is a per-domain scalar STS score that predicts the magnitude of performance change, plus the identified set of shifted SAE dimensions. Notably, STS_ICL additionally requires 5 domain-specific CoTs as ICL prompts to compute the activation difference, but still never touches a fine-tuned model.

## Method

The core insight is that SFT modifies only a small, semantically coherent subset of SAE dimensions (top-100 capture 25% of total change), and that in-context learning with the same CoTs reproduces substantially overlapping shifts (57% overlap in top-100), enabling pre-training identification of those dimensions. Step 1: extract SAE features with and without ICL demonstrations from the SFT data, rank dimensions by activation change, and select the top-N. Step 2: for each downstream domain, compute STS as either the mean activation on shifted dimensions (STS_act) or the activation difference induced by domain-specific ICL demonstrations (STS_ICL). The critical role of SAEs is their monosemanticity: in raw feature space, shifts are uniformly distributed and ICL-SFT overlap drops sharply, whereas SAE sparsity concentrates changes into interpretable, task-specific dimensions. Ablations confirm that higher sparsity (stronger monosemanticity) and larger hidden dimensions improve prediction accuracy, and that the approach generalizes across Qwen2.5-7B, Llama3-8B, and Gemma2-9B with Pearson correlations above 0.7.

## Results

The SAE-based Transferability Score (STS) achieves Pearson correlation coefficients of 0.77–0.81 (STS_ICL) and 0.60–0.90 (STS_act) with actual per-domain performance shifts on MMLU-Pro across three models (LLaMA3-8B, Qwen2.5-7B, Gemma2-9B), all without performing fine-tuning. The STS_ICL variant is more stable across models (0.77±0.01 to 0.81±0.01) than STS_act (0.60±0.03 to 0.90±0.02). On additional domains (code, health), STS_ICL maintains correlations of 0.77±0.01 and 0.71±0.02 respectively, with 62 and 57 out of top-100 shifted dimensions overlapping between ICL and SFT.

## Baselines & Comparisons

STS substantially outperforms all baselines on predicting cross-domain performance shifts. Raw feature activations (0.03) and SAE feature activations (0.08) show essentially no correlation with actual performance changes, confirming that simply probing activations is insufficient. Representation similarity between the downstream domain and the training domain (0.11) is also uninformative. The strongest baseline—representation similarity between the model before and after SFT (0.61)—requires access to the post-SFT model, making it a post-hoc analysis rather than a predictive tool. STS achieves 0.79 correlation without any fine-tuning, a 29% absolute improvement over the best post-hoc baseline and a 7.2× improvement over the best pre-training baseline. The STS_ICL variant further improves stability across models (0.77–0.81) compared to STS_act (0.60–0.90), suggesting that leveraging ICL demonstrations to measure domain-feature correlation is more reliable than raw activation magnitudes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research threads: SAE-based mechanistic interpretability (Cunningham et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2024), which provides monosemantic feature spaces, and the ICL–SFT similarity literature (Wang et al., 2023; Mosbach et al., 2023), which shows that in-context learning induces representation changes analogous to fine-tuning. Prior transferability studies (Huan et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2025; Chu et al., 2025) analyze post-training effects only post-hoc, requiring access to the fine-tuned model. The key departure here is using ICL as a training-free proxy to identify which SAE dimensions will shift during SFT, then measuring their correlation with downstream domains to predict per-domain performance changes before any fine-tuning occurs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

STS offers a practical, interpretable tool for practitioners to anticipate which domains will suffer (or benefit) from a given SFT run, enabling proactive data-mixture adjustments without expensive trial-and-error fine-tuning. The data-mixture application in Section 5 demonstrates immediate utility, and the monosemanticity requirement (ablation showing raw features fail) clarifies why SAEs are not merely a convenience but a necessity for this prediction. However, the RL extension remains preliminary—STS correlations drop sharply when ground-truth shifted dimensions are unavailable, and the paper explicitly flags identifying RL-induced shifts without supervision as an open problem. Broader questions include whether the ICL-SFT overlap holds for larger models, longer training, or more diverse SFT objectives beyond math reasoning.
