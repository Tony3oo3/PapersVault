## Problem

Existing locate-then-edit methods (MEMIT, PMET) degrade significantly when editing thousands of facts simultaneously, and their robustness collapses under realistic conditions such as long prefixes or multiple facts sharing the same subject. The paper identifies the root cause as embedding misalignment between key and residual embedding spaces, which causes individual knowledge items to be poorly reconstructed when parameters are updated jointly from a large batch.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261243/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes batches of factual triples (subject, relation, object) along with the pre-trained LLM's MLP layer parameters and a set of randomly sampled prefixes for generalization. It produces updated MLP weight matrices (W_out) across multiple target layers, with the key intermediate artifact being a set of aligned residual embeddings whose pairwise cosine-similarity structure mirrors that of the corresponding key embeddings. Updates are distributed proportionally across candidate layers (e.g., layers 3 and 4), and the optimization is iterative over the batch of edits.

## Method

EAMET addresses embedding misalignment by progressively optimizing each residual embedding while enforcing structural alignment with the key embedding space. For each knowledge item i, the method computes the KL divergence between the distribution of cosine similarities among residual embeddings (P_r) and the corresponding subset of key-embedding similarities (P_k), plus an MSE loss on the top-M most similar pairs to capture fine-grained alignment. The residual r_i is then optimized to minimize a combined objective: the negative log-likelihood of generating the target object under multiple random prefixes, weighted KL and MSE alignment losses. Each optimized residual is stored and used to constrain subsequent items, creating a sequential alignment process. Finally, the aligned residuals are distributed across target MLP layers and applied via the standard MEMIT-style closed-form weight update Δ = R^l K^{lT}(C^l + K^l K^{lT})^{-1}.

## Results

EAMET achieves approximately 90% editing efficacy when simultaneously editing 10,000 factual associations across six LLMs (LLaMA2-7B/13B, Qwen2.5-7B, Falcon-7B, DeepSeek-7B, LLaMA3-8B). On CounterFact, EAMET reaches 89.09% efficacy and 61.21% generalization on LLaMA2-7B, outperforming the second-best method (PMET) by 15 percentage points on both metrics. On ZsRE, EAMET attains 89.47% efficacy on LLaMA2-7B versus 77.29% for PMET. EAMET also maintains the highest specificity and fluency scores, and achieves the best portability on Wiki-recent for most models, indicating preserved general capabilities after massive editing.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EAMET consistently outperforms all six baselines on editing efficacy and generalization across all six LLMs and three datasets. Against PMET (the strongest baseline), EAMET gains 15 percentage points on CounterFact efficacy for LLaMA2-7B (89.09 vs. 74.22) and 12 points on ZsRE efficacy (89.47 vs. 77.29). Against MEMIT, the gap is even larger: EAMET improves CounterFact efficacy by 64 points on LLaMA2-7B (89.09 vs. 24.95) and by 37 points on ZsRE (89.47 vs. 76.63). ROME, MEND, and FT are effectively non-functional at the 10k-edit scale, with efficacy near 0% on most models. AlphaEdit shows moderate performance on some models (e.g., 83.15% on Qwen2.5-7B CounterFact) but degrades sharply on base models like LLaMA2-7B (0.51%) and LLaMA2-13B (3.03%). Notably, EAMET also achieves the highest specificity on most models and the best Wiki-recent portability, indicating it preserves general model capabilities better than all baselines after massive editing.

## Relation to Prior Work

EAMET extends the locate-then-edit paradigm established by ROME and MEMIT, which modify MLP weights in FFN layers to update factual associations. Its key departure is diagnosing 'embedding misalignment'—the structural inconsistency between key and residual embedding spaces—as the root cause of performance degradation in massive editing, and addressing it through KL-divergence and MSE alignment losses during per-fact residual optimization. The paper also builds on PMET's attention-aware editing and AlphaEdit's null-space projection for sequential editing, demonstrating that embedding alignment can be integrated into both batch and sequential editing pipelines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals that prior massive-editing results were substantially inflated by a loose evaluation metric (comparing target vs. original probability rather than checking explicit generation), and that robustness to prefixes and same-subject edits—common in real QA and chatbot settings—was previously unaddressed. The embedding misalignment diagnosis provides a principled theoretical lens (Theorem 1) for understanding why batch editing degrades, which could inform future editing methods beyond the locate-then-edit family. Open questions include whether alignment-based optimization scales efficiently beyond 15k edits, whether it transfers to auxiliary-based editing methods, and whether the iterative per-fact optimization can be parallelized without sacrificing alignment quality.
