## Problem

ML-based Android malware detectors suffer significant performance degradation over time due to concept drift—distributional shifts in both benign and malicious samples driven by evolving malware families, API changes, and adversarial adaptation. However, existing datasets (Drebin, TESSERACT, APIGraph) are limited in temporal coverage, family diversity, and structural organization, making them inadequate for systematic longitudinal drift analysis. No prior benchmark spans more than a few years with sufficient scale and family-level granularity to capture real-world drift dynamics.

## Input / Output

Input consists of decompiled Android APKs (via apktool) from which static Drebin features—permissions, declared components, restricted/suspicious API calls, intent filters, and hardcoded IPs/URLs—are extracted and encoded as high-dimensional binary bag-of-tokens vectors (~9.69M raw features reduced to 4,561 via VarianceThreshold at 0.001). The dataset outputs 1,008,381 temporally indexed samples (2013–2025, excluding 2015) with binary malware/benign labels (VirusTotal vt_detection ≥ 4 vs. = 0), family-level annotations from AVClass2 (1,380 families, 150,604 singletons), and stratified train/test splits per year, all stored as compressed sparse NPZ matrices organized by year and class.

## Method

LAMDA is constructed by collecting ~50,000 malware and ~50,000 benign APKs per year from AndroZoo, decompiling each with apktool to extract smali code and AndroidManifest.xml, and parsing them into Drebin-defined static feature categories. Labels are assigned via VirusTotal consensus (vt_detection ≥ 4 for malware, = 0 for benign, discarding 1–3), and family labels are standardized using AVClass2. The paper validates the benchmark through AnoShift-style temporal splits (IID from 2013–2014, NEAR from 2016–2017, FAR from 2018–2025) across six detector architectures (LightGBM, XGBoost, SVM, MLP, DetectBERT, ViT), quantifying F1 degradation from ~97% (IID) to ~47% (FAR). Drift is further characterized via Jeffreys divergence heatmaps, t-SNE projections, per-family Jaccard stability scores, OTDD distances, SHAP-based explanation drift (Jaccard and Kendall distances), and label drift analysis. Finally, three SOTA concept drift adaptation methods (Chen et al. active learning, CADE, TRANSCENDENT) are evaluated under varying labeling budgets, demonstrating that none generalize effectively on LAMDA's more pronounced drift compared to APIGraph.

## Results

On LAMDA, LightGBM's F1-score drops from 97.49% (IID) to 59.48% (NEAR) and 47.24% (FAR), with FNR surging from 1.74% to 64.10%, demonstrating severe concept drift. In contrast, on APIGraph the same model degrades more modestly from 85.95% (IID) to 68.20% (FAR). Among concept drift adaptation methods at a labeling budget of 400, Chen et al. (2023) achieves 92.39% F1 on APIGraph but only 43.00% on LAMDA, while CADE and TRANSCENDENT reach 45.40% and 40.60% F1 respectively on LAMDA, confirming that existing SOTA CDA techniques fail to generalize under LAMDA's more realistic longitudinal drift.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to APIGraph (Zhang et al., 2020), LAMDA exhibits substantially more pronounced concept drift: LightGBM's F1 drops from 97.49% to 47.24% on LAMDA (IID→FAR) versus only 85.95% to 68.20% on APIGraph, and LAMDA shows higher standard deviations across all drifted splits. Among the three CDA baselines evaluated on LAMDA, Chen et al. (2023) consistently achieves the highest F1 (43.00% at budget 400) but still falls far short of its 92.39% APIGraph performance, indicating a ~50-point gap attributable to LAMDA's more realistic drift. CADE (Yang et al., 2021b) reaches 45.40% F1 on LAMDA at budget 400 (slightly above Chen et al.) but with a much higher FNR of 59.20%, suggesting it misses most malware. TRANSCENDENT (Barbero et al., 2022) performs worst on both datasets (43.97% on APIGraph, 40.60% on LAMDA at budget 400), confirming that its selective-prediction approach does not transfer to longitudinal Android drift. Overall, all three SOTA CDA methods fail to maintain detection performance on LAMDA, demonstrating that the benchmark's 12-year temporal span and 1,380-family diversity create adaptation challenges beyond what prior benchmarks capture.

## Relation to Prior Work

LAMDA extends the lineage of Android malware benchmarks (Drebin, TESSERACT, APIGraph) by being the first dataset explicitly structured for longitudinal concept drift analysis, following the AnoShift temporal-split protocol (Dragoi et al., 2022) and the Drebin static feature schema (Arp et al., 2014). It departs from prior benchmarks in scale (1M+ samples vs. 32K for APIGraph), temporal span (12 years vs. 6), and family diversity (1,380 vs. 1,120), while incorporating AVClass2 family labels and SHAP-based explainability that earlier datasets did not support. The paper positions LAMDA as a stress test for existing drift adaptation methods (CADE, TRANSCENDENT, Chen et al. 2023) that were validated on the smaller, less temporally diverse APIGraph.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central practical finding is that all three SOTA concept drift adaptation methods that achieve ~92% F1 on APIGraph collapse to ~43% F1 on LAMDA, revealing that prior benchmarks may have been too mild to validate real-world robustness. This opens a significant gap: the field currently lacks methods capable of handling 12-year-scale distributional shifts in Android malware, particularly the sharp 2017–2018 cliff and the growing singleton-family problem. Limitations include reliance solely on Drebin static features (no dynamic or control-flow features), a 50:50 class ratio that may underrepresent benign distribution shifts, and the absence of 2015 data. Future work on continual learning, multi-modal representations, and joint concept/label drift handling is explicitly motivated.
