## Problem

Linking full-length genomic DNA to quantitative, tissue-specific expression remains unsolved because existing models capture either sequence features or tissue context but not both, and cross-omics systems typically ignore the one-to-many ambiguity introduced by alternative splicing and isoform reuse. The two core obstacles are the extreme length mismatch between DNA (~10⁴ tokens) and protein (~10² tokens) and the context-dependent mapping where identical DNA yields different expression profiles across tissues.

## Input / Output

The model consumes full-length DNA sequences (up to 200 kb, encoded by a frozen Evo2-7B) and a categorical tissue label (one of 49 GTEx tissues, encoded via scGPT mean-pooled embeddings). Outputs include a scalar log(TPM+1) expression value per protein isoform under the given tissue, token-level coding-region masks, a ranked list of candidate isoforms, and a binary DNA-protein association score. Genes exceeding 200 kb are excluded, and expression targets are defined at the isoform level rather than the gene level, making the task inherently multi-output per input sequence.

## Method

CDBridge is a two-stage post-training bridge that keeps the DNA encoder (Evo2-7B) and protein encoder (ESM2-650M) frozen and trains only lightweight connector and decoder modules. Stage 1 (Seq-context) projects DNA embeddings into a protein dictionary (k-means-clustered protein embeddings serving as cross-attention keys/values) and applies a ToMe-inspired adaptive token merge that pairs similar tokens by cosine similarity above a threshold, compresses them by averaging, and recovers the full-length sequence for token-level supervision—mimicking splicing to handle the length disparity. Stage 2 (Env-context) introduces a conditional Transformer decoder that takes scGPT-derived tissue embeddings as queries and cross-attends over the Stage-1 compressed DNA representations, producing both isoform-aware protein embeddings (supervised by contrastive loss) and a scalar expression regression head (MSE loss). Training uses a dynamic merge ratio sampled from N(0.375, 0.1) clipped to [0.25, 0.50] as structural augmentation, and Stage 2 freezes all Stage-1 parameters to preserve learned alignment while learning tissue-conditioned regulation.

## Results

On the GTEx-Benchmark tissue-conditioned expression prediction task, CDBridge achieves an average R² of 0.387 and Spearman correlation of 0.618 across five tissues (Brain, Heart, Kidney, Liver, Stomach), substantially outperforming all directly comparable baselines. The strongest specialist baseline, AlphaGenome, reaches R²=0.248 and Spearman=0.438, while sequence-only models (Evo2, DNABERT-2, NTv2, LucaOne) all yield R² near or below zero. Under the unseen-tissue (zero-shot) protocol, CDBridge maintains robust Spearman correlations comparable to seen-tissue performance, a setting unsupported by Enformer or Isoformer.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CDBridge outperforms all directly comparable baselines on both R² and Spearman. Against the strongest specialist model AlphaGenome (R²=0.248, Spearman=0.438), CDBridge improves R² by +0.139 and Spearman by +0.180. Enformer (R²=0.127, Spearman=0.122) and the recently proposed Borzoi (R²=0.201, Spearman=0.336) are both substantially outperformed. Sequence-only foundation models (DNABERT-2, NTv2, Evo2-7B, LucaOne) all achieve R² near or below zero, confirming that DNA embeddings alone cannot capture tissue-specific regulation. Isoformer (Official) reports higher R² (0.530) and Spearman (0.720), but the authors explicitly note this uses a TSS-aligned data protocol that is not directly comparable; when Isoformer is evaluated without TSS alignment, its R² drops to -0.315, below all other baselines. On the secondary cross-omics tasks (Table 3), CDBridge also leads on all metrics: coding region segmentation AUC 0.993 vs. LucaOne's 0.859, isoform retrieval MRR 0.436 vs. 0.354, and Central Dogma classification AUC 0.792 vs. 0.767.

## Relation to Prior Work

CDBridge departs from the dominant paradigm of end-to-end multi-omics pretraining (LucaOne, GENA-LM, CD-GPT) by instead treating cross-omics alignment as a post-training bridge problem on frozen single-omics encoders (Evo2 for DNA, ESM2 for protein, scGPT for tissue context). It repurposes ToMe (originally a ViT acceleration technique) as a biologically motivated splicing-inspired token compression, and borrows the conditional-decoder pattern from vision-language connectors (BLIP-2, Flamingo) to inject tissue embeddings. Unlike specialist expression models (Enformer, AlphaGenome, Isoformer) that use fixed-output heads trained on pre-cropped or TSS-aligned fragments, CDBridge operates on full-length unaligned genomic sequences and explicitly models the one-to-many DNA-to-isoform mapping through a learnable protein token dictionary.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The GTEx-Benchmark fills a genuine gap: no prior benchmark simultaneously tests long-range exon dependency, isoform reuse resolution, and tissue-specific quantitative prediction in a unified protocol, making it a useful standard for future central-dogma models. The finding that a lightweight post-training bridge (~70M trainable params) can outperform specialist expression models (AlphaGenome, Enformer) on Spearman correlation suggests that cross-modal alignment quality matters more than task-specific architecture for expression prediction. Key limitations remain: tissue context is modeled only as a categorical variable (no developmental stage, disease state, or spatial microenvironment), the framework depends on curated Ensembl isoform annotations, and whole-genome scalability is untested. Open questions include whether the bridge strategy generalizes to non-human genomes with less curated references and whether continuous or spatially resolved contexts can replace the discrete tissue dictionary.
