## Problem

Specification-guided RL algorithms require logical specifications granular enough to produce useful reward signals, but users often provide coarse or under-specified formulas that lead to learning failures. No prior work systematically addresses how to automatically detect which specification components cause these failures and refine them while preserving the original task's semantics.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261162/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework operates as a wrapper around any SpectRL-compatible RL algorithm (e.g., DIRL or LSTS), consuming the MDP dynamics, an initial SpectRL specification, a user-defined satisfaction probability threshold (p), and the base algorithm itself. It outputs a refined abstract graph (equivalently, a refined SpectRL specification φ_r) and a set of edge policies, where satisfaction of φ_r is guaranteed to imply satisfaction of the original φ. The refinement process is triggered iteratively per-edge: whenever a learned edge policy's satisfaction probability falls below p, the framework samples trajectories under that policy to diagnose the failure mode before applying a targeted fix.

## Method

AUTOSPEC identifies problematic edges in the SpectRL abstract graph by analyzing empirical trajectory data, then applies four refinement procedures in order of increasing structural modification: SeqRefine (shrinks target/safety regions via convex hulls of successful/failed states), AddRefine (inserts a waypoint vertex from trajectory midpoints to decompose long-horizon edges), PastRefine (partitions the source vertex using a learned hyperplane separating successful from failing initial states), and OrRefine (adds new edges to existing ancestor vertices to create alternative paths). Each refinement is validated by re-running the base RL algorithm on the modified edge and checking whether satisfaction exceeds the threshold; the first successful refinement is committed and the full graph is re-learned. The key theoretical result (Theorem 1) proves all four procedures maintain soundness—any trajectory satisfying the refined specification also satisfies the original—while the paper also establishes that completeness is undecidable for general continuous-state MDPs, justifying the soundness-only guarantee.

## Results

On the 100-rooms environment, AUTOSPEC+DIRL improves specification satisfaction from near 0% to approximately 60% through sequential application of ReachRefine, PastRefine, and OrRefine, while the DIRL baseline stagnates at ~20% on randomized 100-rooms. Individual refinement procedures yield substantial per-edge gains: SeqRefine (trap elimination) improves satisfaction from 15% to 85%, AddRefine (waypoint introduction) from 20% to 90%, and PastRefine (source partitioning) from 40% to 80%. On PandaGym's 3D manipulation task, AUTOSPEC+DIRL successfully applies ReachRefine and PastRefine to navigate around an invisible wall, demonstrating effectiveness in high-dimensional continuous control. Computational overhead is bounded: AUTOSPEC averages 390±42s versus ~240s for the baseline (≤2× total cost).

## Baselines & Comparisons

AUTOSPEC is evaluated as a wrapper around two existing specification-guided RL algorithms: DIRL (Jothimurugan et al., NeurIPS 2021) and LSTS (Shukla et al., 2024). Against the DIRL baseline, AUTOSPEC+DIRL achieves ~60% terminal satisfaction on the 100-rooms specification versus DIRL's stagnation at ~20% on randomized 100-rooms, and lifts the full-specification satisfaction from near 0% to ~60% on the fixed 100-rooms environment. On PandaGym, AUTOSPEC+DIRL successfully refines both edges of the two-step specification, whereas DIRL alone struggles with the invisible-wall constraint. Against LSTS, AUTOSPEC cannot improve performance: LSTS's bandit-based exploration spreads effort too thinly across edges, yielding 0% satisfaction on critical edges (M4, M5, Goal) and thus no successful trajectories for refinement computation. This reveals that AUTOSPEC's effectiveness is algorithm-dependent, requiring a base learner with systematic per-edge exploration (as in DIRL) rather than parallel multi-edge exploration (as in LSTS).

## Relation to Prior Work

AUTOSPEC builds directly on the SpectRL specification logic (Jothimurugan et al., 2019) and its compositional abstract graph decomposition (Jothimurugan et al., 2021), which translates logical specifications into reach-avoid subtasks on a DAG. While prior specification-guided RL work (DIRL, LSTS, reward machines, LTL2Action) assumes a fixed, well-defined specification and focuses on learning policies for it, AUTOSPEC introduces a meta-level operation: automatically modifying the specification itself when learning fails. This is conceptually analogous to CEGAR (as in Trainify) but applied to the specification rather than the policy, and is complementary to existing algorithms rather than replacing them.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

AUTOSPEC addresses a practical gap in specification-guided RL: users often write logically correct but under-specified predicates that prevent effective learning, and no prior method systematically repairs this. The formal soundness guarantee (refined spec implies original) is important for safety-critical applications. Key limitations include the dependence on the base algorithm's exploration quality (LSTS's bandit-based exploration generates insufficient trajectories for refinement) and restriction to SpectRL's finite-horizon fragment. Open questions include extending to ω-regular specifications, reducing exploration requirements, and whether the geometric refinement primitives (convex hulls, hyperplanes) generalize beyond the tested domains.
