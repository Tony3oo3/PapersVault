## Problem

Parallel decoding methods (masked diffusion, multi-token prediction) accelerate text generation by predicting multiple tokens simultaneously, but they assume conditional independence within each prediction block, failing to capture the complex joint token dependencies that autoregressive models model via sequential factorization. This leads to degraded generation quality—token repetition, incoherence, and grammatical errors—creating a persistent quality gap between AR and non-AR decoders. The paper asks how to make the joint-distribution learning problem tractable for a parallel student without sacrificing the speed advantage of parallel decoding.

## Input / Output

The student model receives two inputs: (1) the context tokens x^{-I} (unmasked or previously generated tokens) and (2) a Gumbel noise vector ξ^I of dimension V (vocabulary size) per target position, which encodes the AR teacher's sampling decisions. The Gumbel vectors are processed via softmax normalization followed by a learned linear projection W_proj ∈ R^{H×V} into the model's hidden dimension, then replace [MASK] embeddings (in diffusion models) or condition MTP heads. The output is a set of |I| tokens predicted simultaneously. At inference, Gumbel noise is drawn from a calibrated distribution (temperature τ=0.85) to control heavy-tail effects.

## Method

Gumbel Distillation reframes the intractable joint-distribution matching problem into a supervised learning task by exploiting the Gumbel-Max trick: for any sequence generated by an AR teacher, there exists a deterministic Gumbel noise vector that reproduces it via argmax over logits. The framework has two stages—(1) data generation, where (noise, text) pairs are extracted from the teacher either sequentially (ancestral sampling) or in parallel via a novel posterior Gumbel sampling theorem (Theorem 4.1) that recovers the noise from an existing corpus in a single forward pass; and (2) student training, where the parallel decoder maximizes the conditional log-likelihood p_θ(x^I | x^{-I}, ξ^I). The method is plug-and-play: for MDLM/BD3-LM it replaces [MASK] embeddings with Gumbel-conditioned vectors in a modified NELBO, and for Medusa it conditions MTP heads on projected Gumbel vectors. The key theoretical result proves that the posterior Gumbel construction preserves both the argmax constraint (correct token recovery) and marginal Gumbel distribution (valid noise), enabling exact parallel extraction.

## Results

On OpenWebText, Gumbel Distillation applied to MDLM improves MAUVE from 0.217 to 0.282 (+30.0%) and reduces generative perplexity from 38.34 to 34.33 (−10.5%). Applied to BD3-LM on the same dataset, it raises MAUVE from 0.251 to 0.304 and lowers GenPPL from 26.40 to 24.37. On LM1B, the gains are even more pronounced: BD3-LM+Gumbel Distillation reaches MAUVE 0.291 and GenPPL 46.06, compared to 0.193 and 56.98 for the baseline. For multi-token prediction (Medusa on GPT-2-Small), Gumbel conditioning increases per-head acceptance rates by +4.5% (Head 1) to +22.0% (Head 3), and by +8.9% to +37.6% when scaled to Vicuna-7B.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Gumbel Distillation consistently outperforms both MDLM and BD3-LM baselines across all four metric-dataset combinations in Table 1, with the largest relative gains on LM1B (e.g., BD3-LM MAUVE improves from 0.193 to 0.291, a +50.8% relative increase). Against classical distillation baselines on MDLM/LM1B (Table 4), Gumbel Distillation (MAUVE 0.264, GenPPL 67.64) substantially beats token-level KD (MAUVE 0.166, GenPPL 95.88) and sequence-level KD (MAUVE 0.169, GenPPL 99.48), which actually degrade the baseline. Compared to Adaptive Parallel Decoding (APD, an inference-time method), Gumbel Distillation alone achieves higher MAUVE (0.264 vs. 0.203) and lower GenPPL (67.64 vs. 57.61); combining both (Gumbel+APD) yields the best GenPPL (49.28) at a small MAUVE cost (0.255). For multi-token prediction, Gumbel-conditioned Medusa heads outperform the unconditioned baseline on every head position, with gains growing for later heads (e.g., +22.0% on Head 3 for GPT-2-Small, +37.6% for Vicuna-7B), directly demonstrating improved joint-distribution modeling.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Gumbel-Max trick from a sampling reparameterization into a distillation mechanism, creating a deterministic 'blueprint' mapping from Gumbel noise to an AR teacher's output sequence. It departs from prior outcome-based distillation (token-level KD, sequence-level KD) by conditioning the parallel student on the teacher's internal sampling decisions rather than merely matching outputs or generated sequences. The method is applied as a plug-and-play module to existing parallel decoding architectures—MDLM, BD3-LM, and Medusa—rather than proposing a new decoder, positioning it as an orthogonal enhancement to the masked-diffusion and multi-token-prediction literatures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Gumbel Distillation addresses a fundamental limitation shared by all parallel decoders—the conditional independence assumption that prevents them from modeling joint token dependencies—by reframing the intractable distribution-matching problem as a supervised regression on Gumbel noise. The counterintuitive finding that parallel Gumbel extraction (recovering noise from a fixed corpus) outperforms sequential extraction (generating directly from the teacher) suggests that anchoring to high-quality ground-truth text mitigates teacher bias, a practical insight for distillation pipelines. Open questions include scaling the Gumbel vector dimensionality with large vocabularies (e.g., Qwen's 152K vocab adds ~9% parameter overhead), whether the technique transfers to multimodal or code generation, and whether the latent Gumbel space can be exploited for controllable generation.
