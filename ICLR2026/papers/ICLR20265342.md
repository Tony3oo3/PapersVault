## Problem

Evaluating open-ended AI tasks (travel planning, product descriptions, dialogue) requires human judgment, but practitioners typically have only sparse, non-descriptive feedback (e.g., thumbs up/down) from fewer than 100 examples. Existing approaches either demand thousands of labels (reward models), assume fixed rubrics that may not match what users actually value (LLM-as-a-Judge), or rely on static metric collections that don't adapt to novel tasks. The paper tackles the problem of automatically discovering, grounding, and weighting evaluation criteria from minimal human signal.

## Input / Output

AutoMetrics consumes a task description plus fewer than 100 human feedback points (binary, Likert-scale, or behavioral) and the generated outputs to be scored. It produces a weighted linear combination of typically 5 metrics with interpretable PLS regression coefficients, per-sample quality scores, and a metric report documenting each metric's description, intended use, and limitations. Notably, the input feedback is deliberately non-descriptive (no rubric or criteria provided by the user), and the output metrics are a mix of LLM-generated judges and retrieved existing NLP metrics, each accompanied by a structured Metric Card.

## Method

AutoMetrics operates in four stages: (1) Generate — synthesize 17 task-specific candidate metrics (10 single-criterion LLM judges, 5 rubric-based LLM judges, 1 few-shot example-based judge, 1 MIPROv2 prompt-optimized judge); (2) Retrieve — use a hybrid ColBERT + LLM pipeline to select the top k=30 most relevant metrics from the union of generated candidates and a curated MetricBank of 48 existing NLP metrics; (3) Regress — fit a Partial Least Squares model on z-scored metric scores to project the metric space onto the direction most predictive of human labels, then select the top n=5 metrics by weight magnitude and refit; (4) Report — produce an interpretable writeup with weights, correlations, and reasoning traces. The key design choice is PLS regression, which handles the high-p low-n regime (many correlated predictors, few data points) where standard OLS would overfit, and the two-stage selection (rank by PLS weights, then refit on the reduced set) balances predictive power against interpretability and cost.

## Results

AutoMetrics achieves Kendall's τ of 0.321–0.382 across five diverse tasks (simplification, dialogue, product description, code completion, travel planning) using Qwen-3-32B, outperforming all baselines on every task. The headline improvement is a 33.4% gain in Kendall correlation over the closest LLM-as-a-Judge baseline on EvalGen (0.334 vs. 0.161 with GPT-4o-mini). The method requires only ~80 human feedback points to saturate performance, and in a τ-Bench case study, AutoMetrics as a proxy reward achieves 0.720±0.06 accuracy, statistically exceeding the 0.680±0.11 obtained with a verifiable reward after 2000 GEPA optimization rollouts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MetaMetrics (Winata et al., 2025), AutoMetrics wins decisively on all five tasks with both backbones; MetaMetrics even produces negative Kendall correlation on EvalGen (−0.214) and CoGym (−0.119) with GPT-4o-mini, indicating its fixed metric combination is actively anti-correlated with human judgment on out-of-distribution tasks. Against DnA-Eval (Li et al., 2025), AutoMetrics outperforms on all five tasks with Qwen-3-32B (e.g., 0.382 vs. 0.232 on EvalGen; 0.365 vs. 0.353 on CoGym) and on four of five tasks with GPT-4o-mini (SimpEval 0.321 vs. 0.234; EvalGen 0.334 vs. 0.174; RealHumanEval 0.160 vs. 0.152; HelpSteer2 0.324 vs. 0.255), with CoGym being the sole exception where DnA-Eval's high variance (0.185±0.10) overlaps AutoMetrics' confidence interval. The paper also notes that baselines are inconsistent across datasets and backbones (e.g., Best Existing Metric outperforms LLM-Judge on SimpEval with GPT-4o-mini but not Qwen-3-32B), whereas AutoMetrics is consistently the top or near-top performer regardless of task or underlying model.

## Relation to Prior Work

AutoMetrics sits at the intersection of two prior research threads: (1) metric collections and calibration (MetaMetrics, TorchMetrics, HuggingFace lighteval) that organize existing metrics for practitioner use, and (2) LLM-as-a-Judge frameworks (G-Eval, DnA-Eval, EvalGen) that use LLMs to score outputs against rubrics. The key departure is that AutoMetrics does not assume a fixed rubric or a pre-selected metric set; instead it *generates* task-specific LLM-judge criteria, *retrieves* relevant existing metrics from a curated MetricBank via ColBERT, and *regresses* the combined pool with PLS to produce a weighted, interpretable composite score aligned to sparse human feedback. The closest prior work, MetaMetrics, also regresses over multiple metrics but operates on a fixed, task-specific metric set rather than adaptively generating and retrieving candidates, and EvalGen elicits criteria from human feedback but does not combine them with retrieved existing metrics or perform regression-based weighting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for teams prototyping open-ended AI applications (travel planning, clinical notes, product descriptions) where human evaluation is too slow or expensive for iterative optimization. The finding that ~80 labeled examples saturate performance and that the resulting metrics can serve as a proxy reward matching verifiable rewards for agent optimization (τ-bench case study) lowers the barrier to data-driven evaluation in new domains. Key limitations include model-specificity (metrics must be regenerated when the underlying LLM changes), the high-p/low-n regression risk of spurious correlations on small datasets, and the absence of a formal user study validating practitioner adoption. Open questions include whether more sophisticated metric generation could benefit from additional data beyond 80 samples, and whether the approach generalizes to multimodal or non-text evaluation settings.
