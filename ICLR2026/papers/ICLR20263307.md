## Problem

Existing value benchmarks evaluate general moral reasoning under the 3H framework, while medical benchmarks test diagnostic knowledge; neither assesses whether LLMs align with the specific ethical principles governing nursing practice. This gap is critical because LLM-assisted nursing introduces new conflict dynamics: patients may trust LLM responses over nurses' professional judgments, and nursing conflicts are often rooted in value disagreements embedded in subjective, biased narratives rather than straightforward harmful requests.

## Input / Output

The benchmark presents two task formats: Easy-Level feeds a single nursing case text paired with a named value dimension (e.g., Justice, Professionalism), while Hard-Level feeds a multi-turn adversarial dialogue (2+ rounds between characters A and B) where the original case is restated and then obscured by persuasive framing, reasoning traps, and misleading justifications. In both cases, the model must output a single binary label—'Aligns with the corresponding nursing value' or 'Does not align'—without additional explanation. The dataset spans five value dimensions (Altruism, Human Dignity, Integrity, Justice, Professionalism) across 11 clinical departments and three hospital tiers, with 2,200 samples per difficulty level.

## Method

NurValues is constructed through a three-stage pipeline grounded in a five-month ethnographic field study (Dec 2024–Apr 2025) across three hospitals of varying tiers in Zhejiang, China, yielding 976 real-world nursing behavior instances. Five licensed clinical nurses annotate each instance with up to two value dimensions and a binary alignment judgment (Fleiss' κ = 0.73); OpenAI o1 then generates counterfactual instances that reverse ethical polarity while preserving context, producing a balanced 2,200-sample Easy-Level set. For the Hard-Level set, jailbreaking-inspired prompts instruct o1 to rewrite each case as a multi-turn dialogue in which one speaker uses inducement, deception, or biased reasoning to manipulate the other's ethical judgment, adding adversarial complexity while preserving the original scenario and label. Evaluation is conducted via zero-shot prompting on 23 LLMs (18 general, 5 medical), with ICL strategies (Chain-of-Thought, Self-Consistency, K-Shot) tested as lightweight alignment interventions on the Hard-Level subset.

## Results

On the Easy-Level dataset, DeepSeek-V3 achieves the highest Ma-F1 of 94.55%, closely followed by Claude 3.7 Sonnet (94.45%) and Gemini-2.5-Pro-Preview (94.41%), with 16 of 23 LLMs exceeding 90% accuracy. On the Hard-Level dataset, performance drops sharply: Claude 3.5 Sonnet leads with 89.43% Ma-F1, while DeepSeek-V3 falls to 34.29% and Llama-3-8B-Instruct to 6.66%. General LLMs consistently outperform medical LLMs (Hard-Level average: 43.12% vs. 23.25% accuracy), and Justice is the most challenging value dimension across all models (27.26% average Ma-F1 on Hard-Level). Human nurses outperform the best LLMs on Hard-Level (90.96% vs. 83.92% Ma-F1), and ICL strategies like CoT boost DeepSeek-V3's Hard-Level Ma-F1 from 34.29 to 57.32.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a benchmark paper, NurValues does not propose a new method but evaluates 23 LLMs against each other and against human nurses. General LLMs consistently outperform medical LLMs across both difficulty levels: on Hard-Level, the average accuracy gap widens from 1.06 points (Easy) to 19.87 points (43.12% vs. 23.25%), with OpenBioLLM-70B (13.55%) and Llama3-Med42-70B (18.77%) scoring near chance. Reasoning models (o1, DeepSeek-R1, Gemini-2.5-Pro-Preview, Qwen-QwQ-Plus) do not consistently outperform strong non-reasoning models; Claude 3.5 Sonnet (non-reasoning) achieves 89.43% Hard-Level Ma-F1 versus Gemini-2.5-Pro-Preview's 63.98%. Against two human nurses, LLMs trail on Hard-Level (83.92% vs. 90.96% Ma-F1), indicating that LLMs struggle to filter positional bias and emotional framing in adversarial dialogues. Among ICL strategies, CoT yields the largest Hard-Level gains (e.g., DeepSeek-V3: +23.03 Ma-F1), while K-Shot is most effective on Easy-Level (Llama-3.1-70B: +18.60 Ma-F1 with 6-shot). McNemar tests confirm that 91.7% of pairwise model comparisons on Hard-Level are statistically significant (p<0.001), validating the benchmark's discriminative power.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of value alignment benchmarks and clinical nursing, departing from both general value benchmarks (ValueBench, WorldValuesBench, FLAMES) that evaluate broad moral reasoning under the 3H framework, and medical benchmarks (MedQA, MedSafetyBench) that test diagnostic knowledge or straightforward harmful-request matching. Rather than building on a specific prior method, it repurposes the value-dimension taxonomy approach from general value alignment research and grounds it in a five-month ethnographic field study across three hospital tiers, producing the first real-world nursing-specific value dataset. The two-level difficulty design (counterfactual augmentation for Easy-Level, adversarial dialogue generation via jailbreaking-style prompts for Hard-Level) extends the single-sentence evaluation paradigm used in prior value benchmarks toward multi-turn, bias-laden narratives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a critical gap: current LLMs, even top performers, degrade sharply (from ~90% to ~40% accuracy) when nursing value judgments are embedded in adversarial, bias-laden dialogues, and they still lag behind human nurses on the Hard-Level task. The finding that general LLMs consistently outperform domain-specific medical LLMs on ethical reasoning challenges the assumption that clinical fine-tuning improves value alignment, suggesting that broader human-feedback training may be more important for ethical judgment than narrow medical knowledge. Key limitations include the restriction to five value dimensions, data collected exclusively in mainland China (raising cross-cultural generalizability concerns), and the relatively small Justice subset (74 samples). Open questions include whether ICL or RAG-based approaches can close the LLM–human gap on adversarial scenarios, and whether the benchmark's findings transfer to other healthcare professions or cultural contexts.
