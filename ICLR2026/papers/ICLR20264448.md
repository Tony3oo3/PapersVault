## Problem

While LLMs are known to exhibit cultural bias and limited awareness of underrepresented cultures, the internal mechanisms by which they perform culture-related inference remain unexplored. Prior neuron-level studies (LAPE, CAPE) examined culture primarily in relation to language and relied on activation-based methods that are imprecise because cultural content is not encoded in every token, unlike language. This paper asks: which specific neurons drive cultural understanding, how are they distributed, and can they be leveraged for practical model engineering?

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes LLM model parameters (neuron activations and gradients across MLP gate and attention Q/K/V projections), BLEnD multiple-choice questions split into identification (12,701 instances across food, work-life, sport) and test (10,331 instances across education, family, holidays) sets, plus two control datasets: BLEnD_ctrl (question content removed, retaining only answer choices and format instructions) and CountryRC (50 reading-comprehension problems per country where the answer is always a country name in context, requiring no cultural knowledge). It outputs a sparse set of culture-general neuron indices (<1% of all neurons) and per-culture sets of culture-specific neuron indices, each accompanied by per-neuron gradient attribution scores aggregated across instances weighted by model confidence.

## Method

CULNIG identifies culture neurons via gradient-based attribution scores (neuron activation × ∂P(y|x)/∂n), which approximate the causal effect of each neuron on output probability in a single forward-backward pass rather than requiring per-neuron masking. Two filtering stages ensure precision: first, subtracting scores computed on BLEnD_ctrl removes neurons that facilitate general task understanding rather than cultural inference; second, subtracting scores on CountryRC eliminates superficial neurons that merely respond to country-name tokens. For culture-specific neurons, an additional z-score threshold (z < 0.5 removed) across 16 countries isolates neurons uniquely tied to one culture. The identified neurons are concentrated in shallow-to-middle MLP layers, and validation shows that masking them degrades cultural benchmark scores by up to 30% while leaving NLU benchmarks (QNLI, MRPC, CommonsenseQA) largely unaffected, confirming their specificity to cultural understanding.

## Results

Masking culture-general neurons (fewer than 1% of all neurons, e.g., 8,087 out of ~1.1M for gemma-3-12b-it) causes substantial degradation on cultural benchmarks: BLEnD_test drops by up to 30 percentage points (64.22→37.93 for gemma-3-12b-it; 60.18→32.19 for Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct), while NLU benchmarks (QNLI, MRPC) remain largely unaffected (e.g., QNLI 75.37→72.77, MRPC 78.04→78.65). Random neuron masking of the same size produces negligible changes (BLEnD_test 63.57 vs. 64.22 original), confirming the identified neurons are causally responsible for cultural understanding. The effect generalizes across unseen cultural knowledge categories, task formats (MCQs, SAQs, multilingual), and cultural attributes (knowledge, etiquette, values).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares CULNIG (gradient-based attribution) against two activation-based neuron identification methods: LAPE (Tang et al., 2024) for language-specific neurons and CAPE (Namazifard & Galke, 2025) for culture-specific neurons. When replicating LAPE and CAPE on gemma-3-12b-it, the authors find that masking LAPE, MUREL, or pure CAPE neurons produces negligible score changes on all cultural benchmarks (BLEnD_test, CultB, NormAd), whereas CULNIG-identified neurons cause drops of 20–30 points on BLEnD_test. CAPE's reported finding that culture neurons concentrate in upper layers could not be reproduced; instead, CULNIG-specific neurons are concentrated in shallow-to-middle MLP layers, consistent with CULNIG-general. The pure CAPE neuron sets are very small (e.g., 60 for Danish, 80 for German), indicating heavy overlap with LAPE language neurons and failing to isolate culture-specific representations. Overall, CULNIG's gradient-based approach identifies causally relevant neurons for cultural understanding, while the activation-based baselines identify neurons that do not measurably affect cultural benchmark performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the neuron-level interpretability line of work (knowledge neurons in Dai et al. 2022, bias neurons in Yang et al. 2024) to the domain of cultural understanding, adopting the same gradient-based attribution framework but introducing two key methodological departures: a control-dataset subtraction (BLEnD_ctrl) to isolate cultural contribution from task understanding, and a CountryRC filtering step to remove superficial country-name neurons. It explicitly contrasts with activation-based approaches (LAPE, CAPE) that the authors argue are imprecise for cultural content, since cultural information is not encoded in every token the way language is. The paper also builds on the key-value memory interpretation of MLPs (Geva et al. 2021) to justify focusing on gate-projection neurons, and positions its contribution as filling the gap left by prior cultural-neuron studies (Ying et al. 2025, Namazifard & Galke 2025) that examined culture-language alignment rather than how LLMs shape behavior from cultural knowledge.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that fewer than 1% of neurons in shallow-to-middle MLP layers drive cultural understanding across all six evaluated models provides a concrete, actionable target for model engineers: fine-tuning on NLU tasks while updating these specific modules measurably degrades cultural competence, whereas updating other modules does not. The discovery that culture-specific neurons also encode knowledge of geographically or historically related cultures (e.g., Mexican neurons affecting Spanish performance) opens questions about how cultural knowledge is organized in shared representational spaces. Limitations include the unresolved discrepancy with CAPE's reported upper-layer concentration, the reliance on a single z-score threshold determined by preliminary experiments, and the fact that culture-specific analysis covers only 8 of the 16 BLEnD cultures. Future work could leverage these neuron maps for targeted cultural knowledge editing or for designing training curricula that preserve cultural understanding while improving other capabilities.
