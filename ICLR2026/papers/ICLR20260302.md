## Problem

Reinforcement learning methods like GRPO require tens of thousands of rollouts to adapt LLMs to new tasks, making them impractical for expensive tool-calling systems or when finetuning large models is infeasible. The paper argues that the natural language traces produced during rollouts (reasoning chains, tool calls, compiler errors) carry far richer diagnostic signal than the scalar rewards RL collapses them into, yet no prior prompt optimizer had systematically exploited this textual feedback for sample-efficient adaptation of compound AI systems.

## Input / Output

GEPA takes a compound AI system Φ (one or more LLM modules with initial prompts, fixed weights, and control flow), a training set of task instances (x, m) with evaluator metadata, a feedback function μ_f that extends the standard metric to return both a scalar score and module-specific textual traces (e.g., compiler errors, failed rubrics, per-hop retrieval diagnostics), and a total rollout budget B. It outputs a set of optimized system prompts—one per module—along with the best-performing system configuration selected by aggregate validation score. Unlike few-shot optimizers, GEPA evolves only declarative instructions (no demonstrations), and the underlying model weights Θ remain frozen throughout.

## Method

GEPA (Genetic-Pareto) is a reflective evolutionary prompt optimizer built on three mechanisms. First, reflective prompt mutation: at each iteration, a candidate system is executed on a minibatch, the feedback function μ_f extracts module-level textual traces, and a reflection LM is shown the current prompt, trajectory, score, and feedback to propose a targeted revision of one module's prompt (selected round-robin). Second, genetic accumulation: new candidates inherit their parent's prompt lineage, so high-level lessons compound along the evolutionary tree rather than being discarded. Third, Pareto-based candidate selection: instead of always mutating the global best (which traps search in local optima), GEPA maintains a Pareto frontier over individual training instances—retaining any candidate that is best on at least one task—and stochastically samples the next candidate to evolve, weighted by how many tasks it leads. An optional system-aware Merge strategy performs crossover between two candidates that share a common ancestor but have optimized disjoint modules, combining complementary strategies into a single candidate.

## Results

On Qwen3 8B, GEPA achieves an aggregate score of 54.85 across six benchmarks, outperforming GRPO (48.91, 24,000 rollouts) by +5.94pp while using only ~3,936 rollouts (up to 35× fewer). On GPT-4.1 Mini, GEPA reaches 65.22 aggregate, surpassing MIPROv2 (58.67) by +6.55pp and TextGrad (59.14) by +6.08pp. GEPA's largest single-benchmark gain is +19pp on HotpotQA (Qwen3 8B: 62.33 vs. GRPO's 43.33) and +12pp on AIME-2025 (GPT-4.1 Mini: 59.33 vs. MIPROv2's 51.33). GEPA-optimized prompts are also up to 9.2× shorter than MIPROv2's, reducing downstream inference cost.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), GEPA wins on 5 of 6 benchmarks with Qwen3 8B, achieving up to +19pp (HotpotQA: 62.33 vs. 43.33) while using up to 35× fewer rollouts (~3,936 vs. 24,000); GEPA matches GRPO's best validation score after only 243–1,179 train rollouts. Against MIPROv2 (Opsahl-Ong et al., 2024), GEPA outperforms on all six benchmarks and both models, with aggregate gains of +9.62pp (Qwen3 8B) and +12.19pp (GPT-4.1 Mini) versus MIPROv2's +2.61pp and +5.64pp respectively; the largest single-task margin is +12pp on AIME-2025 (GPT-4.1 Mini). Against TextGrad (Yuksekgonul et al., 2025), GEPA achieves +6.08pp aggregate on GPT-4.1 Mini (65.22 vs. 59.14), winning on 5 of 6 tasks. Against Trace/OptoPrime (Cheng et al., 2024), GEPA leads by +8.92pp aggregate on GPT-4.1 Mini (65.22 vs. 56.30). Notably, GEPA-optimized prompts trained entirely on Qwen3-8B transfer to GPT-4.1-Mini with +9.00pp aggregate gain, outperforming all baselines that were optimized directly on GPT-4.1-Mini.

## Relation to Prior Work

GEPA sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) automatic prompt optimization (MIPROv2, TextGrad, Trace, APO), from which it departs by replacing scalar-reward-driven search with natural-language reflection on full execution and evaluation traces; (2) evolutionary and quality-diversity algorithms (EvoPrompt, PromptBreeder, and the Pareto-based 'illumination' strategy of Mouret & Clune 2015), which it adapts to modular prompt mutation with domain-specific feedback; and (3) RL-based LLM adaptation (GRPO), which it positions as a less sample-efficient alternative that discards the rich textual signal available in rollouts. The key conceptual departure is treating rollouts as interpretable language traces for targeted, per-module prompt rewriting rather than as scalar rewards for policy-gradient estimation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: for compound AI systems with expensive tool calls or limited inference budgets, reducing the rollout requirement by up to 35× while matching or exceeding RL-based fine-tuning opens a viable path to adapting frontier models without weight updates. The finding that reflectively evolved instructions alone outperform joint instruction-plus-few-shot optimization (contradicting prior results from Wan et al. 2024) suggests a shift in what makes prompts effective as LLM instruction-following improves. Open questions include whether the approach scales to systems with many more modules, how to adaptively allocate budget between mutation and crossover (the Merge strategy was inconsistent across models), and whether the gains persist as base models become stronger. The adversarial-prompt-search application also raises the question of whether reflective evolution can systematically stress-test deployed systems at scale.
