## Problem

Sparse Mixture-of-Experts models incur large memory overhead during inference, and uniform post-training quantization causes severe accuracy loss at sub-3-bit widths. Existing expert-wise mixed-precision approaches rely on heuristics like activation frequency or mean routing weights that lack theoretical justification and fail to capture the varying sensitivity of model performance to quantization of different experts. The paper asks: what metric provably categorizes experts for mixed-precision bit-width allocation?

## Input / Output

The method consumes the trained (or pretrained) MoE model's router vectors and first-layer expert weight matrices, along with a target average bit-width per expert. For pretrained models where the randomly initialized router vectors are unavailable, the final router l2 norm serves as a surrogate for the norm change, justified by the small-variance random initialization (σ = O(1/d)). The output is a per-expert bit-width assignment (two-level or three-level) and the corresponding quantized weights, where quantization is applied column-wise with independently computed bin sizes per column of each expert's first-layer matrix.

## Method

The method orders experts by the change in their router's l2 norm during fine-tuning (Λ_s = ‖w_s^(T)‖ − ‖w_s^(0)‖), placing experts with smaller changes at higher ranks (requiring higher precision), then reorders a small subset of experts with unusually large maximum intra-neuron variance (exceeding a threshold ζ² times others) to higher ranks to avoid injecting excessive quantization noise. A theoretical analysis of a two-layer MoE model fine-tuned on binary classification proves that experts learning less-prevalent task-relevant tokens exhibit smaller router norm changes, weaker activations, and greater sensitivity to quantization, yielding a generalization guarantee: quantizing the top κ-fraction of experts to b_h bits and the remainder to b_l bits preserves full-precision generalization, where b_l can be log₂((1−α)/α) bits lower than b_h. The bit assignment supports two-level (e.g., 3/2-bit) and three-level (e.g., 3/2/1-bit) schemes with strategies that balance maximizing high-precision experts against minimizing low-precision ones depending on the target average bit-width. Empirically, the approach outperforms PMQ, Hessian, BSP, and Slim-LLM on Mixtral 8x7B and 8x22B across eight zero-shot benchmarks, while requiring negligible computational overhead (no GPU, no calibration set) for bit-width determination.

## Results

On Mixtral 8x7B (46.7B params), the proposed router-norm + MaxVar method achieves 68.38% average accuracy across 8 zero-shot LLM benchmarks at 2.5 avg bits/expert, outperforming PMQ (67.53%) and Hessian (67.18%). At 2.0 avg bits, it reaches 62.56%, comparable to PMQ (62.83%) while using significantly less inference time. On the larger Mixtral 8x22B (140.6B params), the method achieves 59.17% at 2.25 bits, surpassing PMQ (55.35%) by 3.8 points. On Switch Transformer fine-tuned on CNN/Daily Mail, the method preserves summarization quality (ROUGE-2 ≈ 18.6) down to 2.125 avg bits/expert, where uniform 2-bit quantization already degrades severely.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against PMQ (Huang et al., 2025a), the closest expert-wise competitor, the proposed method wins on Mixtral 8x7B at all bit-widths above 2.0 avg bits (e.g., 68.38% vs. 67.53% at 2.5 bits; 65.79% vs. 65.16% at 2.25 bits), while PMQ edges ahead slightly at 2.0 (62.83% vs. 62.56%) and 1.75 (60.53% vs. 58.95%) bits. On Mixtral 8x22B, the gap widens in favor of the proposed method at lower bit-widths: 59.17% vs. 55.35% at 2.25 bits and 51.44% vs. 47.87% at 1.75 bits. Against non-expert-wise baselines, the advantage is substantial: Hessian (Dong et al., 2020) achieves only 67.18% at 2.5 bits and 58.85% at 2.0 bits on 8x7B, while BSP (Li et al., 2024) and Slim-LLM (Huang et al., 2025b) collapse to 49.07% and 44.49% respectively. Beyond accuracy, the proposed method also reduces inference time versus PMQ (Figure 3) because it allocates higher precision to less-frequently-activated experts, and requires negligible assignment overhead (no GPU, simple sorting) compared to PMQ's 110 GB GPU memory and 2227 s calibration for Mixtral 8x7B.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the post-training quantization (PTWQ) literature for MoE models by introducing the first theoretically grounded expert-wise mixed-precision strategy. Prior expert-wise approaches (Li et al. 2024; Huang et al. 2025a/PMQ) relied on heuristics like activation frequency or gating-weight averages to allocate bit-widths, while non-expert-wise methods (HAWQ, BSP, Slim-LLM) treat all experts uniformly or at the layer/group level. The paper departs from these by deriving the router's l2-norm change during training as a provably valid sensitivity metric, building on the authors' prior theoretical work on MoE training dynamics and expert pruning (Chowdhury et al. 2023, 2024), and adds a practical MaxVar reordering step to handle outliers that inject excessive quantization noise.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that less-frequent but critical experts (smaller router norm change) require higher precision is a non-obvious insight that inverts the common intuition that frequently-used components should be protected. Practically, the method's negligible assignment overhead (no GPU, just sorting router norms) contrasts sharply with PMQ's 110–350 GB GPU memory requirement, making expert-wise mixed-precision quantization feasible for very large MoE models at deployment time. Open questions include whether the theoretical guarantees extend beyond the simplified two-layer MoE to deeper architectures, how the method interacts with layer-wise or block-wise quantization strategies, and whether the router-norm surrogate remains reliable for models with non-standard initialization schemes.
