## Problem

Conformal prediction applied directly to segmentation-derived clinical metrics (e.g., organ area) treats the segmentation-to-metric pipeline as a black box, yielding valid but unnecessarily wide intervals. Existing CP methods for segmentation focus on pixel-level errors that misalign with downstream radiomics, while the original Feature Conformal Prediction (FCP) framework requires solving a computationally intractable adversarial optimization in high-dimensional feature spaces typical of modern CNNs and transformers.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained segmentation model (U-Net, SegResNet, or SwinUNETR) and medical images resized to 128×128 pixels (1-channel grayscale or 3-channel RGB). It produces a prediction interval [lower, upper] for a differentiable downstream metric—specifically segmentation area in pixels²—computed by applying a soft sigmoid threshold to the output logits and summing the resulting probability map. The interval is instance-adaptive: each test sample receives its own perturbation direction and magnitude calibrated from the training data.

## Method

COMPASS calibrates directly in the model's latent representation space by linearly perturbing intermediate features along data-specific directions maximally sensitive to the target metric. For COMPASS-J, it computes Jacobians of the metric with respect to a chosen feature layer, sums spatial dimensions, applies PCA to the training-set Jacobians to extract a low-dimensional sensitivity subspace (typically L=1 principal component), and projects each sample's Jacobian onto this subspace to obtain its perturbation direction Δ. The prediction set S_β(x) is defined as the metric range over the perturbation interval [−β, +β], which guarantees nestedness by construction; the non-conformity score R_i is the minimal β whose interval covers the ground truth, and the conformal quantile of these scores yields the calibrated interval width. The paper proves marginal coverage under exchangeability (Theorem 1) and extends to weighted CP for covariate shift (Proposition 1). A key empirical finding is that the metric response is monotonic along the learned direction, reducing interval computation to two forward passes (endpoint evaluation) rather than a full perturbation sweep.

## Results

Across four medical segmentation tasks (H&E, Skin Lesion, Nodule, PolyP) at α=0.10, COMPASS-J achieves the shortest mean interval lengths in 3 of 4 datasets: 3160 px² (H&E), 1179 px² (Skin Lesion), 2444 px² (Nodule), and 4056 px² (PolyP). All methods achieve valid marginal coverage (≥1−α), so the primary differentiator is interval width. COMPASS-J reduces interval size by 10–35% compared to the best output-space baseline (SCP) and by 8–34% compared to E2E-CQR. Against the FCP oracle (Table 3), COMPASS-J is 2–13× tighter, e.g., 1179 vs. 12445 px² on Skin Lesion at α=0.10.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All six methods achieve valid marginal coverage (Table 2), so comparisons are driven by interval width. COMPASS-J outperforms the three output-space baselines (SCP, Output-CQR, Local CP) by 10–35% in interval length across all datasets at α=0.10; for example, on PolyP it achieves 4056 px² vs. 6237 (SCP), 4981 (Output-CQR), and 5965 (Local). Against E2E-CQR, which trains a separate model with Tversky loss to predict pixel-wise quantiles, COMPASS-J is 8–34% tighter (e.g., 4056 vs. 6184 on PolyP), because COMPASS calibrates directly on the downstream metric rather than a pixel-level proxy. COMPASS-L (logit-space perturbation) is competitive with COMPASS-J but slightly wider on most datasets, confirming that deeper feature-space perturbations capture more metric-relevant structure. Against the FCP oracle (Table 3), which uses an Lp-ball in latent space with LiRPA bounds, COMPASS-J is dramatically tighter—by a factor of 2× on H&E (3160 vs. 6463) and up to 13× on Nodule (2444 vs. 16143)—because FCP's isotropic ball must cover all directions while COMPASS perturbs only along the metric-sensitive principal direction. Under covariate shift (Table 5), COMPASS-J with Jacobian or feature weighting is the only method that consistently recovers target coverage on both H&E (hard→easy shift) and Skin Lesion (easy→hard shift), whereas COMPASS-L and E2E-CQR with the same feature weights still undercover on H&E.

## Relation to Prior Work

COMPASS sits at the intersection of Feature Conformal Prediction (FCP, Teng et al. 2022), which showed that calibrating in a semantic feature space yields provably tighter intervals than output-space CP, and Weighted Conformal Prediction (Tibshirani et al. 2019), which restores coverage under covariate shift. The key departure from FCP is replacing its intractable adversarial Lp-ball search with linear perturbations along PCA-derived subspaces of the metric's Jacobian, making feature-space CP computationally feasible for high-dimensional CNN/transformer feature maps. The paper also positions itself against output-space baselines (SCP, CQR, Local CP) and end-to-end pixel-quantile methods (E2E-CQR, Lambert et al. 2024), arguing that treating the segmentation-to-metric pipeline as a black box or optimizing a pixel-level proxy fails to exploit the model's internal structure for the downstream metric of interest.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

In clinical practice, segmentation outputs are consumed as derived metrics (organ area, lesion volume) rather than pixel masks, so tight and valid intervals for these scalars directly affect decision support. COMPASS's 2–5× interval-size reductions over output-space baselines, combined with the weighted extension that recovers target coverage under adversarial class shifts, make metric-based uncertainty quantification practically deployable for medical imaging. Key limitations include the empirical (not guaranteed) monotonicity assumption, dependence on pretrained model quality, and the fact that WCP with estimated density ratios degrades under large distribution shifts. Open questions include generalization beyond area metrics to texture or shape descriptors, extension to 3D volumetric tasks, and whether the single-PCA-component sufficiency holds for more complex downstream functions.
