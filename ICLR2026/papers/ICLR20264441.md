## Problem

Traditional multi-agent RL methods (IPPO, MAPPO) applied to climate investment simulations like InvestESG converge to selfish, Pareto-suboptimal equilibria because individual incentives diverge from collective welfare. The paper identifies that InvestESG under its default parameters does not actually exhibit a social dilemma (price of anarchy ≈ 1), leaving unclear under what conditions the environment captures the real-world tension between short-term profit and long-term sustainability. The authors formalize these conditions and demonstrate that opponent shaping can resolve the dilemma without external policy mandates.

## Input / Output

Each agent observes a tabular state comprising its own capital, the full set of company capitals, investor portfolio holdings, current climate event probabilities (extreme heat, precipitation, drought), and cumulative mitigation level U_t over a 100-timestep horizon. Companies output a continuous mitigation fraction u_t^i ∈ [0, ū] of their capital, while investors output a binary vector selecting which companies to allocate capital to. The learned policies are parameterized by two-layer MLPs (256 hidden units, ReLU) producing a multivariate normal over actions, trained for 70M environment steps with 64 parallel environments.

## Method

The paper's core technical contribution is a formal proof that the climate responsiveness parameter α (scaling mitigation effectiveness λ_e) determines whether InvestESG is a social dilemma, with an empirically identified threshold at α = 70 where the private marginal gradient of capital w.r.t. mitigation becomes negative while the social marginal gradient remains positive. To resolve this dilemma, the authors plug the Advantage Alignment modified advantage A^{*,i} = A^i + βγ·Σ_{j≠i}(Σ_{k<t} γ^{t-k} A^i(s_k,a_k))·A^j(s_t,a_t) into the PPO clipped objective, replacing the standard per-agent advantage with a term that couples each agent's historical advantage with all opponents' current advantages. A key theoretical insight is that GAE's positive TD-error bias (the critic lags the improving actor) makes the historical-advantage term b^i > 0 during early training, creating an automatic cooperative bias that prevents collapse into defective equilibria; this bias fades as the critic converges. The method is trained with self-play (separate parameters for company and investor roles) under centralized training with decentralized execution, and outperforms IPPO, MAPPO, and PPO-with-summed-rewards baselines across all agent counts up to 10.

## Results

In the α-InvestESG environment (α=70, where the game is formally a social dilemma), Advantage Alignment (AdAlign) without any ESG incentives achieves the highest market total wealth while simultaneously requiring the lowest final mitigation investment among all methods tested. AdAlign reaches the environment's minimum achievable climate risk of 0.48 (the theoretical floor of 1−∏(1−P₀ᵉ)), matching the performance of PPO agents with extreme ESG=10 incentives but with far less mitigation spending. In the scalability experiment (Figure 4a), AdAlign consistently finds the welfare-maximizing action profile from 2 agents up to 10 agents, whereas PPO with summed rewards (IPPO/MAPPO) fails to find optimal policies beyond 4 agents. AdAlign also produces a lower Gini coefficient in company capital distribution, indicating more equitable resource allocation compared to PPO baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is IPPO/MAPPO from Yu et al. (2022), evaluated both with standard per-agent rewards and with summed (social welfare) rewards. AdAlign outperforms all PPO variants on market total wealth in the α=70 social-dilemma regime, even surpassing PPO agents given extreme ESG=10 incentives (where investors effectively ignore their own profits). On mitigation efficiency, AdAlign achieves the same final climate risk (0.48) with significantly lower cumulative mitigation spending than PPO+ESG, indicating more strategic, targeted investment. On scalability, PPO with summed rewards (both IPPO and MAPPO) degrades sharply beyond 4 agents and cannot recover the welfare-optimal policy, while AdAlign maintains consistent performance up to 10 agents. The paper attributes PPO's failure to the credit-assignment problem: as agent count grows, centralized critics in MAPPO receive noisier temporal-difference signals, whereas AdAlign's advantage-multiplication mechanism provides a built-in cooperative bias that mitigates this issue. The paper also notes that PPO with ESG incentives at α=1 (default InvestESG) shows negligible differences across ESG levels, confirming the environment is not a social dilemma without the α scaling.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper applies Advantage Alignment (Duque et al., 2025), a scalable opponent-shaping algorithm that modifies PPO policy gradients by cross-multiplying agents' advantages, to the InvestESG climate-investment simulation (Hou et al., 2025). It positions itself against earlier opponent-shaping methods (LOLA, M-FOS, BRS, LOQA) that either scale poorly, require discrete actions, or incur prohibitive compute costs, and against standard MARL baselines (IPPO, MAPPO) that optimize myopic returns. The key departure is a formal game-theoretic characterization of when InvestESG constitutes a social dilemma (via the price of anarchy and marginal-gradient analysis), which the original environment paper did not provide, and a theoretical explanation of why Advantage Alignment's GAE-based advantage estimation introduces a transient cooperative bias that steers learning away from defective equilibria.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that shaping learning dynamics alone—without external ESG mandates or government intervention—can steer a multi-agent economy toward socially beneficial equilibria, offering a concrete mechanism for policy design in climate-finance contexts. A notable limitation is that the theoretical analysis relies on simplifying assumptions (static portfolios, single-company allocation, no historical mitigation) and the critical α=70 threshold is calibrated empirically in a single environment, leaving open whether the results generalize to other economic simulators or real-world market structures. The finding that naive reward-summing fails beyond four agents while Advantage Alignment scales to ten is practically important for multi-agent policy optimization, but the single-environment evaluation and the somewhat arbitrary α calibration temper the broader claims.
