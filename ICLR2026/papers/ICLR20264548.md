## Problem

Existing test-time compute scaling methods (e.g., Best-of-N) allocate a fixed budget uniformly across all queries, ignoring the inherent variability in query difficulty. This one-size-fits-all strategy wastes compute on easy queries while under-allocating to hard ones, and prior adaptive approaches either operate within a single query or require a costly two-stage training procedure. The paper asks whether compute can be allocated adaptively across a set of queries in a fully online, training-free manner that provably improves efficiency over uniform allocation.

## Input / Output

The algorithm receives a set of n text queries, a total compute budget B (measured in number of LLM generations), a reward oracle (a process reward model, ground-truth verifier, or LLM-as-a-judge), and two hyperparameters: per-round per-query budget K and elimination threshold γ. It outputs, for each query, the full set of generated responses (used for coverage evaluation) and the single best-scoring response (used for accuracy evaluation). Compute is consumed sequentially in rounds of K generations per active query, and the algorithm terminates when the budget is exhausted or all queries have been confidently answered (score ≥ γ).

## Method

The paper formulates test-time compute allocation as a novel pure-exploration-style bandit problem where each query is an arm and each generation is a pull, with the objective of maximizing the fraction of queries that receive at least one correct response under a fixed budget. Algorithm 1 maintains an active set of unresolved queries and applies an elimination rule: a query is removed once its best observed reward exceeds threshold γ, freeing its remaining budget for harder queries. The exploration rule can be uniform over active queries (base ELIMINATION), UCB (optimism in the face of uncertainty), GAP (inverse-gap weighting near the threshold), or a novel ENTROPY rule that prioritizes queries with diverse response patterns. Theoretically, the adaptive approach requires budget Θ̃(Σ 1/Δ_x) to answer all queries correctly with high probability, matching the information-theoretic lower bound, whereas uniform allocation requires Ω̃(max |S|/Δ_x)—a factor of n worse in heterogeneous regimes. The framework extends naturally to Self-Consistency aggregation, token-level budget control, and streaming query settings without architectural changes.

## Results

The proposed bandit-based adaptive allocation (Algorithm 1 with ELIMINATION rule) achieves up to 11.10% absolute (15.04% relative) coverage improvement on MATH-500 (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, budget 8), 10.82% absolute (14.44% relative) on AIME25 (Qwen3-4B, budget 16), and 11.23% absolute (15.29% relative) on LiveCodeBench, all under the same total compute budget as uniform Best-of-N. These gains correspond to efficiency improvements of up to 4×, meaning the uniform baseline requires up to 4× more compute to match the adaptive method's performance. On the MATH-500-Hard-16 subset (most difficult queries), the ENTROPY exploration variant significantly outperforms both UNIFORM and the base ELIMINATION rule, demonstrating the value of targeted exploration on challenging instances.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: UNIFORM (standard Best-of-N with equal compute per query, Brown et al. 2024) and TWO STAGE (explore-then-commit allocation, Damani et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2025b). The proposed ELIMINATION algorithm consistently outperforms UNIFORM across all three main benchmarks (MATH-500, AIME25, LiveCodeBench) and all tested models, with the largest gains (10–11% absolute) appearing on coverage metrics at moderate budgets (8–16). The TWO STAGE baseline is also consistently outperformed, which the authors attribute to its structural limitation: the first stage performs uniform allocation, incurring an unavoidable per-query overhead that the fully adaptive bandit approach avoids (formalized in Proposition 1). On the MATH-500-Hard-16 subset, the base ELIMINATION rule underperforms UNIFORM (since many queries are unsolvable), but the ENTROPY exploration variant recovers and surpasses both baselines by preferentially allocating compute to solvable queries. The method also outperforms UNIFORM under token-matched budgets and in streaming settings, confirming that the gains come from allocation strategy rather than extra compute.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Best-of-N test-time scaling paradigm (Brown et al., 2024) and the pure-exploration bandit literature (Bubeck et al., 2009; Jamieson & Nowak, 2014; Locatelli et al., 2016), but introduces a novel problem formulation: treating each query as a bandit arm with the objective of generating at least one correct response per query, rather than identifying the best arm. It departs from prior adaptive compute allocation work (Damani et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025b) by replacing two-stage explore-then-commit schedules with a fully adaptive, single-pass elimination procedure, and from single-query adaptive methods (Sun et al., 2024; Manvi et al., 2024) by operating across a set of queries with a shared budget. The entropy-based exploration rule is a new contribution not present in the classical bandit literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical guarantee (Theorem 1) that adaptive test-time compute allocation is strictly more efficient than uniform allocation, with a clean separation from explore-then-commit strategies (Proposition 1). Practically, the method requires no additional training and works with existing reward oracles, making it a drop-in improvement for any Best-of-N pipeline. Open questions include scalability to very large query pools, integration with multi-turn or tree-search reasoning strategies, and whether the bandit formulation extends to settings without reliable reward oracles (e.g., open-ended generation). The reliance on a sufficiently accurate reward oracle remains a practical limitation, as the theoretical guarantees depend on Assumption 1.
