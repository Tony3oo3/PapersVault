## Problem

PPL-based filtering is the current state-of-the-art for removing noisy web text before LLM pretraining, but it requires training a reference model and running inference over the entire corpus, making it prohibitively expensive at web scale (216 GPU hours for a 6B-token corpus). Moreover, the reference model's perplexity becomes unreliable on out-of-distribution or noisy samples, sometimes assigning high scores to semantically meaningless text. This paper asks whether a model-free, frequency-based criterion can match or exceed PPL-based filtering while being orders of magnitude faster.

## Input / Output

The method takes a tokenized web text corpus (e.g., Dolma v1.6 with 6.3B tokens split into 512-token blocks) and computes unigram term-frequency statistics from a small subset (as little as 1% of the corpus suffices) to estimate token priors. For each document block it produces two scalar outlier scores—μ_d (mean of log token priors) and σ_d (standard deviation of token priors)—which are compared against corpus-wide medians to flag extreme documents for removal. The final output is a filtered subset (e.g., 3B tokens retained from 6B), ready for pretraining, with the entire pipeline completing in under 35 minutes on 40 CPUs.

## Method

The core insight is that the prior term p(x_i) in the Bayesian decomposition of PPL can be approximated by simple corpus-level term frequency, eliminating all model inference. Grounded in two linguistic observations—that term frequency is a one-dimensional encoding of a word's functional role (high frequency → function words, low frequency → content words) and that well-formed sentences maintain a consistent lexical density—the method computes per-document μ_d (mean log-prior) and σ_d (standard deviation of priors) as lightweight proxies for the prior and likelihood components of PPL, respectively. Documents whose μ_d or σ_d deviate far from the corpus median are discarded as outliers, with the two criteria capturing complementary aspects: μ_d reflects overall token composition while σ_d captures distributional regularity among tokens. Because token priors remain stable even when estimated from a 1% subsample, the full filtering pipeline runs in ~0.25 hours versus 216 GPU hours for PPL-based filtering. The approach generalizes to symbolic languages (code, math) and automatically adapts to multilingual corpora without requiring a manually curated reference dataset.

## Results

On the Dolma corpus (6.3B tokens, 50% subset selected), the prior-based filter achieves the highest average normalized accuracy of 9.20 across 20 MosaicML gauntlet benchmarks with a 1.5B GPT-2 model, outperforming PPL-based filtering (8.22), DSIR (7.56), and FastText (7.09). The advantage is even more pronounced for the 137M model, where prior-based reaches 6.65 vs. 5.26 for PPL-based. Critically, the prior-based filter requires only 0.25 hours of CPU time (40-core Intel Xeon) versus 216 GPU hours for PPL-based filtering — a >1000× speedup — while also generalizing to symbolic languages (Pile-github: 12.03 avg vs. 11.21 for PPL-based on 1.5B).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against PPL-based filtering (Ankner et al., 2024), the prior-based filter wins on average normalized accuracy by +0.98 (1.5B: 9.20 vs. 8.22) and +1.39 (137M: 6.65 vs. 5.26), with the largest gaps in Language Understanding (+2.97) and Symbolic Problem Solving (+3.22 for 1.5B), while PPL-based retains a slight edge in World Knowledge (+0.45) and Commonsense (+0.64). Against DSIR (Xie et al., 2023b), prior-based leads by +1.64 in average accuracy (9.20 vs. 7.56) and dominates in Language Understanding (+3.00) and Symbolic Problem Solving (−1.54, where DSIR actually edges ahead slightly at 12.67 vs. 11.13). Against FastText (Joulin et al., 2016), prior-based achieves +2.11 in average accuracy (9.20 vs. 7.09) with consistent gains across all five capability categories. The most striking practical difference is computational cost: prior-based filtering takes 0.25 hours on 40 CPU cores versus 216 GPU hours for PPL-based, a >1000× reduction, making it feasible for web-scale corpora that continue to grow daily.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the PPL-based data pruning framework of Ankner et al. (2024) by exploiting the Bayesian decomposition of perplexity into likelihood and prior terms, then showing that the prior term alone—estimated via corpus-level unigram term frequencies—is a sufficient and superior filtering signal. It is conceptually related to DSIR (Xie et al., 2023b), which also uses n-gram frequency distributions, but departs by requiring no reference dataset, using only unigrams, and grounding the method in linguistic principles (Al-Kindi's frequency analysis, lexical density) rather than empirical similarity to a curated set. The paper also contrasts with SCDP (Nguyen & He, 2025), which uses TF-IDF and thus suppresses function words, whereas the prior-based filter treats both function and content words as integral to structural well-formedness.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 1000× speedup (0.25 hours vs. 216 GPU hours) with equal or better downstream performance makes high-quality data filtering feasible at web scale without GPU resources, which is practically transformative for continual pretraining pipelines. A key limitation acknowledged by the authors is that the method cannot detect semantically nonsensical text that is structurally well-formed (the boundary condition in §B.1.2), and it is less suited for non-text modalities. Open questions include whether the linguistic function/content-word distinction holds universally across all language families and tokenizers, and how to optimally handle the trade-off between noise removal and preservation of rare but valuable minority data (e.g., technical terminology, low-resource languages).
