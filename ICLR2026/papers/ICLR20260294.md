## Problem

Existing financial benchmarks focus on relatively simple tasks like knowledge-based QA or table data extraction, falling short of the complex, high-stakes reasoning that financial professionals perform daily. The financial domain has higher entry barriers than math or coding, resulting in fewer and less challenging benchmarks. EDINET-Bench addresses this gap by introducing expert-level tasks—accounting fraud detection, earnings forecasting, and industry classification—that require integrating information across entire annual reports spanning multiple tables and narrative sections.

## Input / Output

Each input is a complete Japanese annual report (~30,000 tokens) parsed from EDINET TSV files, comprising balance sheet, cash flow, P&L tables, a consolidated financial summary, and unstructured narrative text. Three input configurations are tested: Summary only, Summary+BS+CF+PL (tabular data without company names or exact years), and all items including Text. Outputs are task-specific: binary predictions (fraud/non-fraud or earnings increase/decrease) with a probability score and reasoning, or a 16-class industry label with classification rationale. The text section is excluded from industry prediction evaluation because it often explicitly states the company's industry.

## Method

The paper constructs EDINET-Bench via a pipeline: (1) edinet2dataset downloads and parses ~40,000 annual reports from EDINET over ten years using Polars for high-speed TSV processing; (2) fraud labels are derived by extracting amendment reasons from 6,712 amended reports with pdfminer, classifying them with Claude 3.7 Sonnet, and manually verifying all 668 fraud-labeled cases; (3) earnings labels come from pairing consecutive annual reports per company and comparing 'Profit Attributable to Owners of the Parent Company'; (4) industry labels consolidate 33 SICC sectors into 16 TOPIX-aligned categories. Evaluation is zero-shot for LLMs (temperature 0, 4096 max tokens, 'You are a financial analyst' system prompt) and supervised for classical baselines (Logistic Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost) trained on the train split, with ROC-AUC, MCC, and accuracy as metrics.

## Results

On the fraud detection task, Random Forest achieves the highest ROC-AUC of 0.75 and MCC of 0.38 (Summary input), outperforming all LLMs; the best LLM (Claude 3.5 Sonnet with full input) reaches only ROC-AUC 0.73 and MCC 0.32. On earnings forecasting, GPT-5 achieves the best LLM performance with ROC-AUC 0.65 and MCC 0.21 (Summary+BS+CF+PL+Text), while logistic regression scores ROC-AUC 0.56 and MCC 0.05. On industry prediction, XGBoost (0.42 accuracy) and Claude 3.5 Sonnet (0.41 accuracy) lead with Summary+BS+CF+PL input, both well above random chance (1/16 ≈ 0.06). The headline finding is that state-of-the-art LLMs perform only marginally better than logistic regression on the binary classification tasks, suggesting that simply feeding annual reports to LLMs in a zero-shot setting is insufficient for expert-level financial reasoning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On fraud detection, Random Forest (ROC-AUC 0.75, MCC 0.38) outperforms every LLM, with the best LLM (Claude 3.5 Sonnet + Text) reaching only ROC-AUC 0.73 and MCC 0.32; logistic regression (ROC-AUC 0.68, MCC 0.17) is within 0.05 ROC-AUC of the top LLM, confirming the paper's central claim that LLMs offer only marginal gains over a simple linear model. On earnings forecasting, GPT-5 leads among LLMs (ROC-AUC 0.65, MCC 0.21) but still trails logistic regression's ROC-AUC of 0.56 by only 0.09, and no LLM exceeds 0.65 ROC-AUC, indicating the task remains largely unsolved. On industry prediction, XGBoost (0.42 accuracy) narrowly edges out Claude 3.5 Sonnet (0.41) and Random Forest (0.40) with tabular input, while all LLMs substantially outperform random chance (≈0.06). A notable asymmetry emerges: adding textual information improves fraud detection for most LLMs (e.g., Claude 3.5 Sonnet jumps from 0.63 to 0.73 ROC-AUC) but does not help earnings forecasting, suggesting narrative disclosures carry fraud-relevant signals (e.g., auditor reputation) that are less informative for forward-looking earnings direction. Open-weight models (Kimi-K2, DeepSeek-V3/R1, Llama 3.3 70B) consistently underperform closed-source models across all three tasks, with DeepSeek-V3 and Llama 3.3 70B sometimes scoring below random on earnings forecasting (negative MCC).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in financial LLM benchmarking by departing from the dominant paradigm of simple QA or table-extraction tasks (FinQA, FinanceBench, FinanceMATH) and instead requiring models to process entire annual reports, integrating information across multiple financial statements and narrative disclosures. It draws methodological inspiration from the edgar-crawler/Edgar-Corpus pipeline for US filings but adapts it to Japan's EDINET system, and positions itself alongside the broader trend of creating harder, expert-level benchmarks (Humanity's Last Exam, SWE-Bench, WebArena) applied to the financial domain. The paper is the first to release an open dataset and benchmark specifically for accounting fraud detection, a task previously studied only through proprietary or feature-engineered ML pipelines (Beneish, Perols, Dechow et al.).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that even frontier LLMs (GPT-5, Claude 3.7 Sonnet) perform only marginally better than logistic regression on fraud detection and earnings forecasting—challenges the assumption that scaling LLMs will soon make them viable for high-stakes financial analysis without additional scaffolding. The paper identifies concrete failure modes (auditor-reputation bias, company-size bias) and shows that textual disclosures help fraud detection but not earnings forecasting, offering actionable diagnostic signals for future work. Open questions include whether agentic frameworks (web browsing, code execution, multi-document access) can close the gap, whether the benchmark generalizes beyond Japanese filings, and how to achieve fully contamination-free evaluation as model knowledge cutoffs advance.
