## Problem

The dominant paradigm in time series foundation models assumes that scaling a single monolithic model yields the best zero-shot forecaster, but these large models incur prohibitive training and inference costs. Test-time compute strategies such as ensembling and model selection—well established in NLP and computer vision—have not been explored for probabilistic time series forecasting, and there is no efficient procedure for building diverse portfolios of pretrained forecasters without training each member from scratch.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a historical time series context of 2048 observations, which is chunked into patches of size 16 (yielding 128 encoder tokens), and produces direct multi-step-ahead quantile forecasts across 9 equally spaced quantiles (0.1–0.9) over a forecast horizon H. At test time, the last H observations of the training portion serve as a validation window on which model selection or greedy ensemble weights are fitted before the final inference pass; no gradient updates are performed at test time, distinguishing Chroma from fine-tuning-based adaptation.

## Method

Chroma builds a portfolio of small (1M–9M parameter) Chronos-Bolt (T5 encoder-decoder) models by first pretraining a generalist on the full corpus for 200K gradient steps, then post-training it for only 1K steps on disjoint data partitions defined by frequency or application domain to produce specialists—reducing specialist training cost by ~10×. At test time, the portfolio is combined either by selecting the single best model on a validation window or by fitting weights via the greedy ensemble selection algorithm (Caruana et al., 2004) with 100 coordinate-ascent steps. The key theoretical insight, supported by a bias-variance decomposition on noise-free synthetic signals, is that in the pretrained regime bias dominates variance, so specialist portfolios gain accuracy by reducing bias within sub-distributions rather than by averaging out training randomness; consequently, naive averaging or performance-weighted ensembling degrades performance, and input-specific model selection or greedy weighting is essential.

## Results

On Chronos Benchmark II (BM2), Chroma with only 4M active parameters (frequency-specialist portfolio with model selection) achieves WQL and MASE competitive with monolithic models 50–125× larger: Moirai-1.1 Large (311M), TimesFM-2.0 (500M), and Chronos-Bolt Base (205M). Frequency-based partitioning outperforms domain-based partitioning by ~5% in MASE and ~4% in WQL across all model sizes (1M–9M). Post-training a generalist for just 1K gradient steps (0.5% of full training time) to create specialists reduces portfolio training compute by ~10× versus training each specialist from scratch, with no significant accuracy loss. On GIFT-Eval (97 tasks), Chroma's aggregated probabilistic performance is close to TabPFN-TS, which leverages substantial test-time computation for in-context learning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Chroma (4M params, frequency specialists, model selection) achieves WQL within 1–3% of Chronos-Bolt Base (205M) on most BM2 datasets (e.g., 0.038 vs. 0.036 on australian_electricity; 0.134 vs. 0.134 on weather), while using ~50× fewer active parameters. Against Moirai-1.1 Large (311M), Chroma is competitive on energy and retail datasets but trails by 2–5% on traffic (0.257 vs. 0.234) and australian_electricity (0.038 vs. 0.035). TimesFM-2.0 (500M) shows similar patterns: Chroma is close on weather (0.134 vs. 0.133) and m5 (0.563 vs. 0.557) but lags on traffic (0.257 vs. 0.212) and australian_electricity (0.038 vs. 0.067, where Chroma actually wins). On GIFT-Eval, TabPFN-TS (which uses heavy test-time in-context computation) edges out Chroma on several high-frequency tasks (e.g., 0.052 vs. 0.064 on LOOP-SEATTLE-5T-48), but Chroma is competitive overall given its far lower test-time compute. TTM (Ekambaram et al., 2024) is compared only in zero-shot mode on GIFT-Eval and is omitted from BM2 since it specializes in high-frequency series; Chroma's model-selection approach provides a more flexible alternative to TTM's per-setting specialization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant 'bigger is better' scaling paradigm in time series foundation models (Chronos, TimesFM, Moirai) by proposing a portfolio of small specialist models combined at test time, drawing conceptual inspiration from NLP/CV test-time compute strategies (Best-of-N, model ensembles). It builds directly on the Chronos-Bolt T5 encoder-decoder architecture and the greedy ensemble selection algorithm of Caruana et al. (2004), but distinguishes itself from mixture-of-experts approaches (Moirai-MoE, TimeMoE) by training specialists independently and allowing flexible, inference-time-adaptable combination rules. The post-training strategy for inducing diversity connects to knowledge distillation and domain adaptation literature, while the bias-variance analysis links to Lin et al. (2024)'s argument that large pretrained models are underfit, making bias the dominant error component.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a 4M-parameter portfolio with model selection matches 200M+ parameter monolithic models on BM2, while post-training (1K steps, 0.5% of base training time) produces specialists comparable to training from scratch at 10x lower cost. The bias-variance decomposition provides a principled explanation for why specialist portfolios work (targeted bias reduction) and why naive averaging fails, offering guidance for future portfolio design. Open questions include whether the approach scales to billion-parameter models, whether more principled (non-hand-crafted) partitioning strategies exist, and whether the findings generalize beyond encoder-decoder transformer architectures. The hand-crafted nature of domain/frequency partitions and the focus on a single architecture family limit immediate generalizability.
