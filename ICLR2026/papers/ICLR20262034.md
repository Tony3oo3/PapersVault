## Problem

When fine-tuning foundation models on new tasks, supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL) can reach comparable new-task performance, yet RL consistently preserves prior knowledge far better. The underlying mechanism governing *why* different training algorithms forget differently remained unexplained: prior work addressed symptoms (weight constraints, feature preservation) rather than identifying the causal variable that determines the degree of forgetting across methods.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262034/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained foundation model (Qwen 2.5 3B-Instruct for LLM tasks, OpenVLA 7B for robotics) and a new task defined by prompts with either a binary reward signal (RL via GRPO with single gradient steps, no KL penalty, no clipping) or fixed supervision labels (SFT). It produces a fine-tuned policy along with two diagnostic quantities: the KL divergence between the fine-tuned and base policy evaluated on the new task (the forgetting predictor), and prior-task scores on diverse benchmarks (HellaSwag, MMLU, HumanEval, etc. for LLMs; drawer open/close for robotics). A key experimental input is the 'oracle SFT' distribution, constructed analytically per-image as the minimum-KL labeling consistent with task correctness, which serves as a ground-truth validation of the KL-forgetting law.

## Method

The paper establishes an empirical forgetting law: the KL divergence between the fine-tuned and base policy, measured on the new task, is the dominant predictor of catastrophic forgetting (R²=0.96 in a controlled ParityMNIST toy setting, R²=0.71 in LLM experiments), outperforming weight-change, activation-drift, sparsity, and other distributional-distance metrics. The core mechanism, termed 'RL's Razor,' is that on-policy policy gradient is implicitly biased toward KL-minimal solutions: at each step it samples from the model's own distribution, reweights by reward, and this operation is equivalent to an I-projection onto the set of optimal policies followed by an M-projection onto the representable family (an EM algorithm with information-geometric projections). A formal theorem proves that under binary reward and an e-flat policy class, policy gradient converges to the minimum-KL optimal policy relative to the initialization, even without explicit KL regularization. Ablations across four objectives (GRPO, 1-0 Reinforce, SFT, SimPO) isolate on-policy sampling—not negative gradients—as the critical factor, and training SFT on the analytically derived KL-minimal oracle distribution surpasses RL, confirming that the distribution learned, not the optimizer, governs forgetting.

## Results

Across LLM tasks (Math, Science Q&A, Tool Use) and a robotic pick-and-place task, RL (GRPO) achieves comparable new-task accuracy to SFT while maintaining prior-task performance nearly unchanged, whereas SFT consistently trades prior-task performance for new-task gains. The KL divergence between the fine-tuned and base policy on the new task predicts catastrophic forgetting with R²=0.96 (quadratic fit) on the ParityMNIST toy setting and R²=0.71 on LLM experiments. On-policy methods (GRPO, 1-0 Reinforce) reach the same task performance with significantly smaller KL shifts than offline methods (SFT, SimPO), and RL-trained models retain 0.94 CKNNA representational similarity to the base model versus 0.56 for SFT.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between on-policy RL (GRPO) and offline SFT, with 1-0 Reinforce (on-policy, no negative examples) and SimPO (offline, with negative examples) as controlled ablations. GRPO and 1-0 Reinforce both achieve high new-task accuracy while incurring substantially smaller KL shifts from the base model than SFT or SimPO, demonstrating that the on-policy sampling mechanism—not the use of negative gradients—is the critical factor. SFT and SimPO behave similarly to each other, both converging to distributions far from the base model and exhibiting steep learning-forgetting trade-offs. An oracle SFT distribution (analytically minimizing KL while achieving 100% accuracy) actually outperforms RL on the forgetting axis, confirming that RL's advantage stems from its implicit KL-minimization bias rather than any inherent algorithmic superiority. The paper also shows that adding explicit KL regularization to SFT barely improves the Pareto frontier, whereas it amplifies RL's existing bias toward KL-minimal solutions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of catastrophic-forgetting mitigation (EWC, LwF, synaptic intelligence) that constrains weight updates or preserves features, arguing these address symptoms rather than the underlying cause. It also extends prior SFT-vs-RL comparisons, which focused on new-task performance and generalization, by isolating forgetting as the key differentiator and identifying the on-policy sampling mechanism (rather than negative examples, as claimed by concurrent work from Lai et al.) as the source of RL's advantage. The theoretical analysis repurposes information-geometric tools (I-projection, M-projection, EM with Bregman divergences from Csiszár, Amari & Nagaoka, Dempster et al.) to formalize why policy gradient implicitly selects KL-minimal solutions, a connection not previously drawn in the forgetting literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The empirical forgetting law (KL on the new task predicts forgetting) gives practitioners a measurable, actionable quantity to monitor during fine-tuning without access to prior-task data, and the 'RL's Razor' principle suggests a design axis for future post-training: explicitly minimize KL from the base model. Key open questions include the mechanistic account of why KL shifts on the new task disrupt prior knowledge (representational interference vs. capacity limits), whether the result holds at frontier scale, and how it extends to off-policy algorithms. The oracle-SFT result—that SFT on the KL-minimal distribution beats RL—shows the principle is about the target distribution, not the optimizer, opening the door to hybrid methods that combine SFT efficiency with RL's conservatism.
