## Problem

Masked generative models (MGMs) can generate tokens in parallel and in any order, but at every sampling step they must process the full-length sequence including [MASK] tokens that carry no information, making inference 5–7× slower than autoregressive models. Prior fixes—decoding more tokens per step, distillation, or block diffusion—either degrade quality, keep each step equally expensive, or sacrifice any-order generation. The paper asks whether [MASK] tokens can be eliminated entirely while preserving parallel, any-order generation.

## Input / Output

For text, the model takes a single BOS token and generates sequences of 128 tokens (LM1B, BERT tokenizer) or 1024 tokens (OpenWebText, GPT-2 tokenizer with sentence packing). For images, it takes a class label and produces a 32×32 grid of 1024 VQGAN tokens corresponding to a 256×256 image. A key operational detail is that during sampling the model processes only the already-decoded (clean) tokens rather than the full sequence, so the effective input length grows from 1 to L over the course of generation, analogous to autoregressive decoding.

## Method

PGMs replace masking with partitioning: at each training step, tokens are split into two disjoint groups (group 0 and group 1) with probability governed by the noise schedule, and a group-wise attention mechanism ensures no information flows between them. The Partition Transformer consists of an encoder of group-wise self-attention blocks, a GroupSwap cross-attention layer that routes each position's representation to the opposite group (using data-independent sinusoidal queries to prevent leakage), and a decoder of group-wise cross-attention layers with no self-attention, so predictions can be computed solely at positions to be decoded. Training computes cross-entropy at every position—group 0 predicts group 1 and vice versa—yielding two complementary gradient signals per sequence and reducing gradient variance relative to MGMs. At inference, the model processes only clean tokens and decodes k tokens in parallel at arbitrary positions per step, achieving ARM-like per-step cost while retaining MGM-style any-order generation, and remains compatible with existing MGM samplers (Halton, confidence-based) and distillation (SDTT).

## Results

On LM1B (context 128), PGM 6/6 (171M params) achieves a validation perplexity of 26.80 vs. MDLM's 27.67 (1.95 lower), while reducing sampling latency from 3.78s to 2.12s and increasing throughput from 1,082 to 1,931 tok/s. On OpenWebText (context 1024), PGM 6/6 with dim 1024 (268M) reaches a validation perplexity of 21.43 vs. MDLM's 23.07, with a 5.3× throughput improvement (5,518 vs. 1,043 tok/s). On ImageNet256 with the Halton sampler, PGM 12/12 achieves FID 5.54 at 32 steps (vs. MaskGIT's 5.35) with a 7.5× throughput gain (7.93 vs. 1.05 img/s), and improves to FID 4.56 at 64 steps while remaining 3.9× faster.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MDLM (Sahoo et al., 2024) on text, PGM wins on both quality and speed: it achieves 1.95 lower validation perplexity on LM1B (26.80 vs. 27.67) with the same number of layers, and 1.64 lower on OpenWebText (21.43 vs. 23.07) with a modest parameter increase (268M vs. 170M). Throughput improves by 5–5.5× (e.g., 5,518 vs. 1,043 tok/s on OWT at 128 steps), because PGM processes only clean tokens at each sampling step rather than the full sequence including [MASK] tokens. On downstream tasks (Table 2), PGM slightly outperforms MDLM on 6 of 8 benchmarks before and after SDTT distillation. Against MaskGIT (Chang et al., 2022) on ImageNet256, PGM 12/12 achieves a near-matching FID of 5.54 vs. 5.35 at 32 Halton steps while being 7.5× faster (7.93 vs. 1.05 img/s); doubling to 64 steps yields a strictly better FID of 4.56 while still maintaining a 3.9× speed advantage. The paper also shows that the complementary-masking training signal alone (without the Partition Transformer architecture) accounts for part of the perplexity gain, but the full PGM architecture is needed to realize the inference speedup.

## Relation to Prior Work

PGMs are positioned as a direct architectural extension of the Masked Generative Model (MGM) paradigm (MDLM, MaskGIT), replacing the [MASK] token mechanism with a partitioning scheme that splits tokens into two non-interacting groups. The Partition Transformer with its GroupSwap cross-attention layer is a new architecture, but the training objective is shown to be equivalent to evaluating the MDLM variational bound at two complementary masking rates in a single forward pass. The paper explicitly differentiates itself from Block Diffusion (which enables KV-caching but sacrifices any-order generation), Eso-LMs (hybrid AR-MGM with an extra hyperparameter), and any-order autoregressive models (which use causal attention and generate one token at a time), while remaining a drop-in replacement for existing MGM samplers (Halton, confidence-based) and distillation methods (SDTT).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 5–5.5× text and 7.5× image throughput gains, achieved while matching or exceeding baseline quality, make PGMs particularly attractive for test-time compute scaling where per-step cost is the bottleneck. A key practical advantage is ecosystem compatibility: existing MGM samplers, classifier-free guidance, and SDTT distillation apply without modification, lowering the adoption barrier. Limitations include slightly higher training cost (block-diagonal attention not yet exploited with custom kernels), a small parameter overhead needed to match MDLM on longer contexts (1024), and the GroupSwap layer as a potential architectural bottleneck. Open directions include PGM-specific distillation strategies, efficient kernels exploiting the block-diagonal structure, and extension to multimodal settings.
