## Problem

Standard transformers in offline RL, such as Decision Transformer, are limited by a fixed context window due to the quadratic cost of self-attention, making them unable to retain sparse cues from early in a trajectory once those tokens fall outside the window. In partially observable environments with long horizons and delayed rewards, this means the agent cannot recall critical information (e.g., a one-bit cue seen thousands of steps earlier) needed for correct decision-making. The paper asks how to extend transformer-based offline RL to handle arbitrarily long sequences while preserving task-relevant information across segment boundaries.

## Input / Output

The model consumes (R, o, a) triplets—return-to-go scalars, observations (first-person RGB images or low-dimensional vectors), and past actions—each passed through modality-specific encoders (linear layers for continuous values, embedding layers for discrete actions, CNN/linear for images). The full trajectory of length T is split into N non-overlapping segments of length K, so the per-segment attention window is only K tokens while the effective context K_eff = N×K extends far beyond. The output is a single predicted action per segment (discrete class or continuous vector), produced autoregressively as segments are processed sequentially with memory carried forward.

## Method

RATE processes trajectories in segments, prepending and appending m learned memory tokens to each segment so that causal self-attention provides both read access (prefix) and write access (suffix) to the memory. Between segments, a Memory Retention Valve (MRV)—a cross-attention module where the incoming memory M_n serves as query and the updated memory M_{n+1} serves as key and value—selectively filters what is retained, preventing catastrophic overwriting of sparse, long-range information during intermediate segments that contain no new cues. The architecture also incorporates Transformer-XL-style caching of past hidden states for dense-feedback tasks. The paper proves a preservation bound (Theorem 1) showing that under an α-alignment condition, at least (1−√(2(1−α/m))) of the memory norm is guaranteed to survive each MRV update, and ablations confirm that the cross-attention MRV outperforms gating, GRU, and LSTM alternatives for long-horizon stability.

## Results

On the T-Maze generalization task, RATE achieves 100% success rate across all in-distribution corridor lengths (up to 900 steps) and maintains 90% success at 9600-step inference (28,800 tokens), while DT collapses to ~50% once the cue exits its fixed context. On the 48-task POPGym benchmark, RATE achieves the highest overall normalized score (9.5) and is the only method with a positive average on the 33 memory-puzzle tasks (0.5 vs. −3.5 for DT, −0.2 for BC-LSTM). On Memory Maze, RATE attains 7.64±0.41 average return, outperforming DT (6.83±0.51), RMT (7.27±0.21), and TrXL (7.12±0.24). On MuJoCo (D4RL), RATE averages 78.5 normalized score, surpassing DT (74.7) in all 9 tasks, and on Atari it outperforms DT in 3 of 4 games (e.g., Breakout 111.0 vs. 76.9; Qbert 12,487 vs. 2,216).

## Baselines & Comparisons

RATE decisively outperforms Decision Transformer (DT) on every memory-intensive benchmark: T-Maze success stays at 90–100% beyond context while DT drops to 50%; ViZDoom-Two-Colors return is 41.5 vs. 24.8; and on POPGym memory tasks RATE scores +0.5 versus DT's −3.5. Against the other memory-augmented transformers (RMT, TrXL), RATE maintains stable performance at long horizons where RMT and TrXL degrade (e.g., T-Maze at 900 steps: RATE 90% vs. TrXL ~61%), and it achieves the highest Memory Maze return (7.64 vs. 7.27 for RMT, 7.12 for TrXL). Recurrent baselines (BC-LSTM, CQL-LSTM, DLSTM) match or approach RATE on short sequences but collapse at longer training lengths (BC-LSTM drops to ~50% when trained on T≥600), while RATE generalizes to 9600-step inference. On standard MDPs, RATE is competitive: it beats DT in 9/9 MuJoCo tasks and 3/4 Atari games, and is within ~1 point of the best Mamba-based baselines (DMamba, MambaDM) on MuJoCo. The one clear loss is SeaQuest (1037.9 vs. DT's 1129.3), which the authors attribute to the environment's dynamics being largely independent of past actions, reducing the benefit of memory.

## Relation to Prior Work

RATE builds directly on the Decision Transformer's (R, o, a) triplet formulation for offline RL, extending it with segment-level recurrence and two memory mechanisms borrowed from NLP: learned memory tokens (from Recurrent Memory Transformer) and cached hidden states (from Transformer-XL). The key departure is the Memory Retention Valve (MRV), a cross-attention gating module that uses incoming memory as queries to selectively filter updated memory, which is novel relative to prior recurrence schemes. The paper also provides a theoretical preservation bound (Theorem 1) under an α-alignment condition, a form of analysis not previously offered for memory-augmented RL transformers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RATE addresses a concrete and well-motivated limitation of transformers in offline RL: the inability to retain sparse cues once they fall outside the fixed context window. The comprehensive evaluation across five memory-intensive POMDPs plus standard Atari/MuJoCo benchmarks, combined with the theoretical guarantee and ablation isolating each memory component, makes this a practical reference for anyone building long-horizon offline RL agents. Open questions include whether the α-alignment assumption holds in more complex or non-stationary settings, whether adaptive memory scaling can reduce overhead on simpler tasks, and whether the segment-based return-to-go re-initialization trick (used for MuJoCo) generalizes cleanly to other domains.
