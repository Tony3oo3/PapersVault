## Problem

Prior work on LLM value reasoning has been confined to low-stakes, everyday dilemmas with short (≤3 sentence) descriptions, failing to capture the complexity of high-stakes domains like medicine, law, and finance. Moreover, existing benchmarks do not examine critical aspects of human value reasoning such as decision ambivalence, psychological discomfort from cognitive dissonance, or temporal shifts in a character's value priorities. This paper asks whether LLMs can make contextually appropriate judgments in high-stakes dilemmas when reasoning from diverse, narratively expressed character perspectives.

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance pairs a long-form, human-written dilemma (sourced from expert ethics websites in medical, business, journalism, and government domains) with one of 11 character descriptions per situation that encode a specific value perspective across six subcategories (Straightforward, Simple Contrast, Swayed Contrast, Shift, Half-Shift, False-Shift). The model receives a structured prompt containing the situation, action, character description, and a question type (general ternary, discomfort binary, or temporal ternary for previous/current values), and must produce a free-form reasoning chain followed by a single label from {Yes, No, Ambiguous} or {Yes, No}.

## Method

CLASH is constructed through a four-step pipeline: (1) collecting 345 high-stakes dilemmas from curated expert ethics websites with a data-contamination filter, (2) generating and human-refining value-related rationales that support or oppose a controversial action, (3) producing category-specific character descriptions via GPT-4o with multi-inspector validation (Cohen's κ = 0.985), and (4) blind annotation to verify descriptions elicit intended ground-truth answers. The evaluation framework introduces three novel dimensions—ambivalence detection, discomfort prediction, and value-shift sensitivity—alongside a conditional steerability metric that measures how effectively a model can be guided toward one value when it conflicts with another, using four value frameworks (WVS, Moral Foundations, Maslow, Aristotle). The paper further analyzes reasoning chains from thinking models for cognitive characteristics (backward chaining, verification, early commitment, overcommitment) and ethical theory usage, revealing that strategies effective in math/game reasoning do not transfer to value reasoning and that pragmatic ethics correlates with successful chains.

## Results

On the CLASH benchmark, Claude-4-Sonnet achieves the highest overall accuracy at 88.89%, followed by GPT-5 (86.14%) and Deepseek-3.1 (84.54%). However, even the strongest models struggle with ambivalence: GPT-5 scores only 24.06% on Simple Contrast, and the best non-thinking model (Mistral-123B) reaches just 62.90%. On discomfort prediction, GPT-5 performs near-perfectly (98.61% on Swayed Contrast), but value shifts expose a critical weakness, with an average accuracy drop of 42.95 points from previous to current questions (max 66.43 for GPT-4o-mini).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 14 models (10 non-thinking LLMs and 4 reasoning LLMs) on CLASH. Claude-4-Sonnet leads overall (88.89%), outperforming GPT-5 (86.14%) by 2.75 points, while among non-thinking models Claude-3.5-Sonnet (85.37%) edges out Mistral-123B (85.09%). On ambivalence detection (Simple Contrast), no model exceeds 62.90% (Mistral-123B), and proprietary thinking models perform worst: GPT-5 (24.06%) and Deepseek-3.1 (12.46%) are far below open-weight models like Mistral-123B (62.90%) and Mistral-24B (62.03%). On discomfort, GPT-5 dominates (98.61% Swayed, 96.35% Straight), while on value shifts, GPT-5 and Deepseek-3.1 show the smallest previous-to-current drops (4.06 and 3.62 points respectively), suggesting their extended reasoning better tracks value changes. Within each model family, reasoning models consistently outperform their non-thinking counterparts (e.g., Claude-4-Sonnet 88.89% vs. Claude-3.5-Sonnet 85.37%; GPT-5 86.14% vs. GPT-4o 84.75%), confirming that enhanced reasoning benefits value understanding.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the moral-reasoning evaluation line (Moral Stories, SCRUPLES, MOCA, DailyDilemmas, Value Kaleidoscope) by shifting from low-stakes, short-scenario dilemmas to long-form, high-stakes narratives enriched with character-based value perspectives. It adopts DailyDilemmas' value-framework mapping (WVS, Moral Foundations, Maslow, Aristotle) but introduces 'conditional steerability'—measuring steering toward one value *relative to* its competitor—whereas prior work focused on single-value alignment. The reasoning-chain analysis draws on cognitive-behavior taxonomies from math and game reasoning (Gandhi et al., 2025) but finds these behaviors do not transfer, instead identifying new failure modes (early commitment, overcommitment) specific to value reasoning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a concrete and actionable gap: even frontier thinking models (GPT-5, Claude-4-Sonnet) fail at recognizing genuine ambivalence (24–51% accuracy) and show large drops (avg. 43 pts) when characters' values shift over time, limiting their reliability in high-stakes deployment. The negative correlation between inherent value preference and steerability (r = −0.243) has direct implications for how organizations should configure and audit LLMs in value-sensitive settings. Open questions include whether training or prompting strategies can elicit the pragmatic-ethics reasoning that successful chains exhibit, whether the findings generalize beyond English-language, US-adjacent dilemmas, and how to formally incorporate ambivalence into model outputs rather than forcing ternary classification.
