## Problem

In Zero-Shot Super-Resolution Spatiotemporal Forecasting, models trained on low-resolution data exhibit errors that remain flat (or slightly increase) as inference resolution grows, rather than decreasing as a true numerical solver would. The paper identifies this as a previously unrecognized fundamental limitation called Scale Anchoring, rooted in the Shannon-Nyquist bound: low-resolution training data cannot represent physical frequencies above its Nyquist limit, so the model's error is information-theoretically anchored at the training resolution. Existing work misinterprets this flat error profile as successful multi-resolution generalization, and prior cross-resolution methods (targeting Spectral Bias or Discretization Mismatch Error) do not address this data-driven frequency ceiling.

## Input / Output

Training uses single-resolution spatiotemporal field snapshots (e.g., 32³ for 3D turbulence, 180×90×6 for ERA5 weather) in multi-timestep sequences of length 10; at inference the same model receives high-resolution snapshots (up to 129³ or 1440×721×6) and predicts the next timestep iteratively. The method is architecture-agnostic and applies as a drop-in training recipe on top of any backbone (GNN, Transformer, CNN, Diffusion, Neural Operator, Neural ODE, plain NN), requiring only that the backbone accept variable-size spatial inputs. FRL adds Nyquist-normalized sinusoidal position encodings to the input and a frequency-domain consistency loss to the training objective, with negligible inference overhead (<2%).

## Method

Frequency Representation Learning (FRL) is a three-step, architecture-agnostic training procedure: (i) construct multi-resolution training data by recursive downsampling; (ii) introduce Nyquist-normalized frequency encodings PE_freq(x,k,ρ)=sin(2πk·x/k_Nyq(ρ)) that map the same physical frequency to identical embeddings regardless of grid resolution, making out-of-training-band absolute frequencies fall back into the learnable normalized [0,1] domain at test time; (iii) add a frequency-weighted spectral consistency loss across all resolution levels. The novel contribution is Step (ii), which is specifically designed to break the Scale Anchoring mechanism by giving the network resolution-invariant frequency awareness; Steps (i) and (iii) follow standard multi-scale and spectral-regularization practices. Theoretical analysis (Theorems 1–2) proves that without such normalization the network's frequency response is undefined above the training Nyquist and that high-frequency error dominates at deployment, while the normalized-frequency error analysis (Appendix D) shows FRL can systematically reduce the out-of-band error component whenever the physical spectrum is sufficiently smooth across scales.

## Results

On 3D ZS-SR fluid simulation (trained at 32³, tested up to 129³), all seven baseline architectures exhibit Scale Anchoring with RMSE_Ratio between 1.017 and 1.338, meaning error stays flat or increases with resolution. FRL-enhanced variants reduce RMSE_Ratio to 0.135–0.375, achieving 3.57×–7.74× lower RMSE at the highest resolution (e.g., NO+FRL: 0.00063 vs. SFNO baseline 0.00476 at 129³). On ERA5 weather forecasting (trained at 180×90, tested up to 1440×721), baselines maintain ACC below 0.6 (unreliable) at all resolutions, while FRL-enhanced variants push ACC above 0.6 at high resolution (e.g., CNN+FRL reaches ACC 0.66 at 1440×721 vs. PDE-CNN's 0.43).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates FRL against seven SOTA architecture-specific baselines (Neural SPH/GNN, DeepLag/Transformer, PARCv2/CNN, DYffusion/Diffusion, SFNO/NO, FNODE/Neural ODE, NeuralFluid/NN) on 3D fluid simulation and five on weather forecasting (WeatherGFT, PDE-CNN, ARCI, Graph-EFM, ClimODE). Every baseline exhibits Scale Anchoring: RMSE_Ratio ≥ 1.017 across all architectures, with Neural ODE (FNODE) being the worst at 1.338, meaning its error actually *increases* 34% at 129³ versus 32³. FRL-enhanced variants uniformly break this pattern, with the largest gains for NO+FRL (RMSE_Ratio 0.135, a 7.74× reduction at 129³) and CNN+FRL (0.137, 7.74×), followed by GNN+FRL (0.175) and NN+FRL (0.182). Neural ODE+FRL shows the smallest relative improvement (RMSE_Ratio 0.375) but still reduces error by 2.6× at the highest resolution. On ERA5 weather forecasting, all five baselines remain below the ACC 0.6 reliability threshold at every resolution (worst: ClimODE at 0.41), while all FRL-enhanced variants exceed 0.6 at 1440×721 (best: CNN+FRL at 0.66), demonstrating that FRL converts previously unusable low-resolution-trained models into reliable high-resolution forecasters.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new conceptual ground by identifying 'Scale Anchoring' as a distinct information-theoretic limitation in zero-shot super-resolution spatiotemporal forecasting, explicitly differentiating it from Spectral Bias, Discretization Mismatch Error, and lack of Discretization Invariance. Methodologically, it repurposes standard multi-resolution training and spectral regularization (Steps 1 and 3 of FRL) and introduces a novel Nyquist-normalized frequency encoding (Step 2) as the key mechanism for scale decoupling. The paper builds on the Neural Operator lineage (FNO, PINO, TNO) and the broader ZS-SR STF task established by Li et al. (2020), but argues that none of these prior approaches explicitly target the frequency-blindness mechanism it formalizes in Theorems 1 and 2.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reframes a widely-accepted success metric (RMSE Ratio ≈ 1 across resolutions) as actually indicating a fundamental failure mode, which changes how practitioners should interpret cross-resolution performance in STF. FRL provides an architecture-agnostic, low-overhead solution (1.1×–1.4× training time, <2% inference overhead) that achieves 3.5×–7.7× error reduction at high resolution across 7+ architectures and two physical domains. However, FRL does not guarantee power-law convergence like numerical solvers, and its effectiveness degrades when spectral relationships across scales become non-smooth (e.g., high-Reynolds-number turbulence, extreme weather), leaving open the question of how to incorporate explicit physical spectral constraints (e.g., Kolmogorov scaling) into the representation.
