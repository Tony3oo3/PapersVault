## Problem

In multi-document QA with context, existing efficient-reasoning methods either apply uniform token compression regardless of query complexity (under-reasoning on hard problems while over-elaborating on easy ones) or rely on heuristic difficulty estimates with rigid, one-way compression policies that cannot recover when initial assessments prove inadequate. No prior approach combines a principled, information-theoretic difficulty metric with a fault-tolerant mechanism that dynamically expands reasoning when an answer is wrong.

## Input / Output

The method takes a natural-language question together with a set of context documents (ranging from a few short passages in HotpotQA to 10K–250K-token multi-document sets in Loong) and produces a single text answer. Response length is not fixed: a difficulty score computed offline from sentence-level BGE-M3 embeddings assigns each query a target length tier (512, 1024, or 2048 output tokens), and the trained model adaptively produces shorter or longer answers accordingly. All reported token counts refer exclusively to generated output tokens, excluding input context.

## Method

MoL introduces two coupled mechanisms trained via GRPO reinforcement learning. First, a Set-Cover-inspired difficulty metric extracts the top-k most question-relevant sentences per document (cosine similarity with BGE-M3), computes pairwise cross-document similarity on those filtered sentences, and defines difficulty as 1 minus the average similarity—high redundancy signals simple extraction, low redundancy signals multi-hop reasoning. Second, a dual-objective reward switches on answer correctness: for correct answers it applies a compression reward (MDL-motivated, rewarding shorter responses up to a difficulty-dependent target length), and for incorrect answers it applies an extension reward that incentivizes longer reasoning to recover missing evidence chains. A progressive learning schedule decays the length-reward coefficient λ over training epochs, and the total reward blends a standard accuracy term with the MoL reward (α=0.7). This design yields an emergent 'intelligent brevity' behavior—concise answers for easy queries, elaborate reasoning for complex ones—without explicit length constraints at inference time.

## Results

On Qwen3-8B, MoL achieves 67.2% F1 on HotpotQA with 316 output tokens (vs. 61.0%/609 for the original model), 95.9% on StrategyQA with 219 tokens (vs. 93.7%/468), and 62.3% on Loong with 1,374 tokens (vs. 55.8%/2,165). Across all four base models and three benchmarks, MoL delivers up to 53.2% token reduction while simultaneously improving accuracy by up to 5.0 points over the original model. On the largest model (Qwen3-14B), MoL reaches 69.4% HotpotQA accuracy at 298 tokens and 72.3% Loong accuracy at 1,128 tokens, outperforming all baselines on both metrics simultaneously.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MoL dominates both ERPLP and KIMI on every benchmark and every base model, winning on both accuracy and token efficiency simultaneously. On Qwen3-8B HotpotQA, MoL achieves 67.2% F1 at 316 tokens, surpassing ERPLP (63.3%/394) by 3.9 points in accuracy while using 20% fewer tokens, and KIMI (62.8%/444) by 4.4 points with 29% fewer tokens. The gap widens on the long-context Loong benchmark: MoL reaches 62.3% at 1,374 tokens versus ERPLP's 56.3%/2,037 (a 6.0-point accuracy gain with 33% fewer tokens) and KIMI's 60.8%/1,938 (1.5-point gain with 29% fewer tokens). ERPLP's uniform compression strategy causes the largest accuracy drops on complex questions (e.g., 60.2% vs. 65.7% original on Qwen3-14B HotpotQA), while KIMI's fixed length penalty similarly degrades performance on multi-hop tasks. GRPO, the standard RL baseline without length modulation, improves accuracy but increases token count (e.g., 747 vs. 609 on Qwen3-8B HotpotQA), confirming that MoL's dual-objective reward is necessary for the efficiency gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

MoL carves out a distinct niche within the efficient-reasoning literature by combining two ideas that prior work treats separately: difficulty-aware adaptive computation (as in ERPLP) and length-penalized RL compression (as in KIMI). Where ERPLP relies on heuristic difficulty scores and one-way compression, and KIMI applies a uniform length penalty, MoL introduces a bidirectional, fault-tolerant reward that extends responses on incorrect answers and compresses them on correct ones, framed through a rate-distortion lens. The difficulty metric itself is inspired by the Set Cover problem's redundancy structure, operationalized via cross-document sentence-level similarity—a conceptual departure from the prompt-based or fixed-threshold difficulty estimators used by prior adaptive methods. The training algorithm (GRPO) and base models (Qwen3, Llama-3.1) are standard; the novelty lies in the reward design and the information-theoretic difficulty proxy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ~53% token reduction with simultaneous accuracy gains is practically significant for deployment cost in multi-document QA pipelines, and the generalization to non-document tasks (CommonsenseQA, SVAMP) suggests the adaptive-length principle is not purely an artifact of the training domain. However, the difficulty assessment is inherently tied to multi-document settings, limiting direct applicability to single-document or document-free QA; the authors acknowledge this and note that non-document scenarios currently rely on heuristic target lengths. The layer-activation correlation (fewer active layers for easy questions) is intriguing but explicitly correlational and post-hoc, leaving open whether MoL truly induces dynamic computational allocation or merely correlates with it. A key open question is whether a unified, modality-agnostic difficulty estimator can replace the document-redundancy proxy, and whether the emergent brevity behavior persists under longer training or larger model scales.
