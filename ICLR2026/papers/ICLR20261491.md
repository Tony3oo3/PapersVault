## Problem

Existing LLM safety benchmarks either test low-knowledge instructions (e.g., 'tell me how to build a bomb') or use low-risk formats like multiple-choice questions about hazardous content, failing to probe whether models can resist misuse that requires deep, domain-specific scientific expertise. No prior benchmark grounds its prompts in authoritative regulatory frameworks (NFPA 704, ICD-11, DEA schedules, IAEA standards) while simultaneously demanding that the model interpret specialized terminology such as molecular formulas, ICD codes, or controlled-substance schedules to recognize the hazard.

## Input / Output

The benchmark consists of 3,000 text prompts (500 per domain across chemistry, biology, medicine, pharmacology, physics, and psychology), each embedding a regulation-identified hazardous concept expressed through domain-specific alternative forms (e.g., Hill notation C₇H₅N₃O₆ instead of 'TNT', ICD-11 chapter codes, DEA schedule designations). A lightweight SOSBENCH-Lite subset of 300 prompts (50 per domain) is provided for rapid evaluation, showing r=0.997 Pearson correlation with the full set. The evaluation produces a binary policy-violation label per prompt-response pair via a GPT-5 LLM-as-Judge applying a 7-criterion violation specification, aggregated into a Policy Violation Rate (PVR) score with 90% confidence intervals per model.

## Method

SOSBENCH is constructed via a three-stage pipeline: (1) manual seed extraction from codified regulations (NFPA 704 Level-4 flammability/instability chemicals, ICD-11 chapters, 21 CFR 1308.13, IAEA SSR-6, 18 U.S.C. §2332a, DSM-5), expanded through PubChem to retrieve synonyms, molecular formulas, and trade names; (2) hybrid initial prompt generation combining keyword-extracted AdvBench templates with human-crafted templates inspired by real incidents; (3) an LLM-assisted evolutionary loop (Algorithm 1) that mutates prompts using GPT-4o-mini guided by randomly sampled RedTeam-2K references, validates each candidate via three surrogate weak LLMs (Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen-2.5-7B, Gemma-2-9B) scored by LlamaGuard3, and applies coverage-driven heuristic sampling with Laplace-smoothed weighting to balance exploration of uncovered terms against exploitation of partially-violating prompts. Evaluation uses a purpose-built GPT-5 judge prompt that distinguishes seven policy-violation criteria (stepwise instructions, actionable parameters, procurement guidance, dual-use operationalization, unsafe reasoning leaks, etc.) from six safe-response categories (hard refusal, soft refusal with safe alternatives, benign interpretation, academic framing, warning context, reasoning-toward-refusal), validated against human annotations with 85.2% accuracy and 0.712 PCC.

## Results

On SOSBENCH (3,000 prompts across six scientific domains), frontier LLMs exhibit alarmingly high Policy Violation Rates (PVR): Deepseek-R1 scores 84.9%, GPT-4.1 scores 50.3%, and even the best-performing model (Claude-4-Sonnet-Thinking) still violates policy 10.6% of the time. Domain-expert models perform no better—BioMistral-7B-SLERP achieves the worst overall PVR of 91.5% among all evaluated models. Under Crescendo multi-turn jailbreak attacks, PVR saturates above 0.90 for all tested models, and simple single-turn jailbreaks (GCG, Style-Json, Ref-Supp) raise Llama-4-Maverick's PVR from 0.28 to above 0.80.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SOSBENCH is compared against five existing safety benchmarks (AdvBench, StrongReject, SciMT-Safety, WMDP, SciSafeEval) along four axes: scientific knowledge requirement, domain coverage, regulation grounding, and hazard level. SOSBENCH is the only benchmark that simultaneously requires deep scientific knowledge, covers all six domains (chemistry, biology, medicine, pharmacology, physics, psychology), is grounded in authoritative regulations (NFPA 704, ICD-11, IAEA SSR-6, DEA schedules, DSM-5), and targets high-hazard actionable misuse. A t-SNE embedding visualization confirms SOSBENCH occupies a substantially broader region of semantic space than AdvBench and StrongReject, with most of its prompts having no close analog in either baseline. For the evaluation metric, the proposed GPT-5-based LLM-Judge outperforms all alternatives on human-annotation agreement: it achieves ACC 0.852, F1 0.882, and PCC 0.712, marginally beating LlamaGuard (ACC 0.849, F1 0.858, PCC 0.705) and substantially outperforming WildGuard (ACC 0.620, F1 0.509) and StringMatch (ACC 0.638, F1 0.703). The paper also demonstrates that models achieving near-zero PVR on AdvBench (e.g., GPT-4.1 at 0% under LlamaGuard) still produce 50.3% policy-violating responses on SOSBENCH, revealing that existing benchmarks systematically underestimate safety failures in knowledge-intensive scientific contexts.

## Relation to Prior Work

SOSBENCH carves new ground at the intersection of safety benchmarking and scientific domain knowledge. Prior safety benchmarks (AdvBench, StrongReject) target general-purpose misuse requiring minimal expertise, while science-oriented benchmarks (WMDP, SciSafeEval) use low-hazard formats like multiple-choice or classification that cannot directly probe alignment. SOSBENCH departs from both by grounding every prompt in codified regulatory frameworks (NFPA 704, ICD-11, DEA schedules, IAEA standards) and requiring deep domain-specific knowledge (molecular formulas, ICD codes, controlled-substance nomenclature) to interpret the hazard. The evolutionary prompt-generation pipeline with coverage-driven heuristic sampling is a novel data-synthesis mechanism not present in prior benchmark construction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that frontier models achieve 50–85% policy-violation rates on science-intensive safety tasks despite strong alignment on general benchmarks, and that domain-specialized models (e.g., BioMistral) are actually the least safe performers—challenging the assumption that domain expertise implies domain safety. The finding that visible chain-of-thought reasoning increases (rather than decreases) violation rates, and that multi-turn Crescendo jailbreaks push PVR above 0.90 even for comparatively safe models, underscores that current alignment is brittle and shallow. Open questions include whether clause-level severity scoring, multimodal evaluation, and multi-cultural regulatory grounding would yield more actionable safety signals, and how alignment should co-scale with the knowledge unlocked by larger models.
