## Problem

Existing uncertainty quantification methods for regression typically address either aleatoric uncertainty (inherent data noise) or epistemic uncertainty (model/data limitations) in isolation, or combine them with a fixed ratio (e.g., λ=1 or γ₁=1) that is often suboptimal. This leads to poorly calibrated conditional coverage, especially in extrapolation regions or when one uncertainty source dominates. The paper asks: can we adaptively calibrate both the scale and the relative weighting of the two uncertainty components in a data-driven way to produce sharper, better-conditionally-covered prediction intervals?

## Input / Output

CLEAR takes as input a feature vector x, a trained point estimate f̂ (e.g., the median of a PCS ensemble), and two sets of uncalibrated uncertainty quantiles: aleatoric quantiles q_ale estimated by quantile regression on residuals Y_i − f̂(X_i), and epistemic quantiles q_epi from the spread of a bootstrapped PCS ensemble. It outputs a calibrated prediction interval of the form [f̂ − γ₁·q_ale − λγ₁·q_epi, f̂ + γ₁·q_ale + λγ₁·q_epi] along with the two learned calibration parameters (λ*, γ₁). The aleatoric component is computed as the median of (r_{1−α/2}(x) − r_{0.5}(x))⁺ across ensemble members, and the epistemic component as the point-wise α/2 and 1−α/2 quantiles of the ensemble predictions minus the median.

## Method

CLEAR introduces a two-parameter calibration scheme where γ₁ controls the overall interval scale (set via standard split conformal prediction to guarantee marginal coverage) and λ = γ₂/γ₁ controls the relative weight of epistemic versus aleatoric uncertainty. For each candidate λ on a dense grid (over 4000 points spanning 0 to 100), the method constructs a conformally calibrated interval and selects λ* by minimizing the quantile (pinball) loss on a held-out validation set, which acts as a proper scoring rule balancing coverage and width. A key design choice is estimating aleatoric uncertainty via quantile regression on residuals Y_i − f̂(X_i) rather than directly on targets, which improves stability under model misspecification. The epistemic component comes from the PCS framework: N₂ model types are trained on N₁ data perturbations, the top-k models are selected by validation RMSE, and b bootstrap refits yield m = k×b ensemble members whose spread defines q_epi. This modular design makes CLEAR compatible with any pair of aleatoric and epistemic estimators (e.g., CQR + PCS, or SQR + Deep Ensembles), and the additive linear combination with only two free parameters keeps the method interpretable and resistant to overfitting.

## Results

Across 17 real-world regression datasets at 95% nominal coverage, CLEAR (variant a) achieves an average 28.3% reduction in Normalized Calibrated Interval Width (NCIW) and 34.4% reduction in quantile loss compared to the ALEATORIC baseline (bootstrapped CQR), and 17.5% / 15.8% reductions compared to the PCS epistemic-only baseline. CLEAR is the top-performing method on 15 of 17 datasets and outperforms UACQR on 14 of 17 datasets, with particularly large gains (40–70%) on airfoil, energy efficiency, and naval propulsion. When applied to Deep Ensembles (epistemic) and Simultaneous Quantile Regression (aleatoric), CLEAR still yields 28.6% and 13.4% average NCIW reductions, respectively, confirming generality across estimator families.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against UACQR (Rossellini et al., 2024), which fixes γ₁=1 and only tunes λ, CLEAR wins on 14 of 17 datasets across NCIW, AISL, and width metrics, with the largest margins (40–70%) on airfoil, energy efficiency, and naval propulsion; UACQR-P even produces infinitely wide intervals on energy efficiency in 1/10 seeds, while CLEAR never does. Against the PCS-UQ epistemic-only baseline (Agarwal et al., 2025), CLEAR improves NCIW by 17.5% and quantile loss by 15.8% on average, primarily by adding the aleatoric component that PCS lacks. Against Deep Ensembles (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017) used as the epistemic estimator, CLEAR reduces NCIW by 28.6% and quantile loss by 24.0%, and these gains persist even after conformal calibration of DE. Against Simultaneous Quantile Regression (Tagasovska & Lopez-Paz, 2019) as the aleatoric estimator, CLEAR achieves 13.4% NCIW and 13.7% quantile loss improvements. The only dataset where CLEAR underperforms UACQR is insurance (−15.5% to −32.5%), and it also slightly trails on ailerons for UACQR-S NCIW (−1.5%), suggesting that in low-uncertainty, well-specified settings the fixed-γ₁ assumption is less harmful.

## Relation to Prior Work

CLEAR extends the conformal prediction and quantile regression literature by introducing two free calibration parameters (γ₁ and γ₂ = λγ₁) that jointly scale aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty, departing from prior methods that either fix the ratio at 1:1 (Deep Ensembles, MC Dropout, Bayesian approaches) or fix one parameter (UACQR fixes γ₁=1). The aleatoric component builds on CQR (Romano et al., 2019) with a key modification—fitting quantile regression on residuals rather than targets for improved stability—while the epistemic component draws on the PCS ensemble perturbation framework (Yu & Kumbier, 2020). The quantile loss from Koenker & Bassett (1978) serves as the selection criterion for the trade-off parameter λ, connecting the method to the classical pinball-loss literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CLEAR provides a practical, model-agnostic calibration layer that can be applied on top of any pair of aleatoric and epistemic estimators (PCS, Deep Ensembles, SQR, etc.), yielding 17–28% narrower intervals while maintaining nominal coverage across 17 real-world datasets. The adaptive λ offers interpretable diagnostics about which uncertainty source dominates, which is valuable for practitioners deciding whether to collect more data (epistemic) or more features (aleatoric). Key limitations include the requirement for sufficient calibration data (≥150 points to avoid overfitting the two parameters), dependence on base-estimator quality, and the structural assumption of additive combination. Open questions include extension to classification, time series, and active learning, as well as whether more flexible (non-additive) combination schemes could further improve performance.
