## Problem

Multilingual LLMs frequently exhibit unexpected code-switching, inserting tokens from unintended languages into responses, which degrades readability and usability. The only prior attempt (Guo et al., 2025) applies GRPO with a language-consistency reward but lacks a mechanistic understanding of why code-switching occurs and shows limited, unstable effectiveness. This paper provides the first SAE-based analysis revealing that code-switching is driven by abnormally high pre-activation values of language-specific features, and builds a training method on that insight.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260480/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a frozen SAE (from Gemma Scope, Llama Scope, or custom-trained for Qwen3) to extract language-specific feature directions, a base LLM, and multilingual SFT data (110k–210k samples spanning English, the target language, and 10 other languages). During training, the SAE encoder is applied to residual streams at selected layers to compute pre-activation values, which feed into the auxiliary loss; the SAE itself is never updated. The output is a standard fine-tuned LLM checkpoint that, at inference time, requires no external intervention or SAE access—code-switching reduction is baked into the weights.

## Method

SASFT introduces an auxiliary loss L_reduce during supervised fine-tuning that penalizes pre-activation values of irrelevant language-specific features exceeding a per-language threshold α_j (the pre-estimated mean pre-activation, not zero, to accommodate negative baselines). The total training objective is L_cross-entropy + λ·L_reduce, where the RELU(f_s(x) − α_j) term is summed over the top-ranked language-specific features S_L for the target code-switching language L, averaged over all training samples in other languages. The key mechanistic insight is that directional ablation experiments show subtracting the language feature from the residual stream before the first code-switch token reduces switching, and that this effect is stronger when applied across multiple layers; SASFT therefore applies the constraint to the first two features in the last two layers simultaneously. Unlike inference-time ablation, which requires large λ values and external intervention, SASFT teaches the model to internally maintain appropriate pre-activation levels, achieving >50% code-switching reduction while preserving or improving performance on six multilingual benchmarks.

## Results

SASFT reduces the unexpected code-switching ratio by more than 50% in 23 out of 30 model-language-dataset configurations compared to standard SFT, with a complete 100% elimination achieved for Qwen3-1.7B-Base on Korean (210k dataset: 0.36% → 0.00%). On the 210k Chinese dataset, SASFT achieves code-switching ratios of 0.42% (Gemma-2-2B), 0.41% (Gemma-2-9B), 0.28% (Llama-3.1-8B), 0.22% (Qwen3-1.7B), and 0.66% (Qwen3-8B), versus SFT baselines of 0.74%, 0.78%, 1.16%, 0.81%, and 0.96% respectively. On six multilingual benchmarks (MMLU, HumanEval, Flores-200, HellaSwag, LogiQA, IFEval, MGSM), SASFT maintains or improves performance, with notable gains such as +3.13 on MMLU and +4.14 on HumanEval for Llama-3.1-8B.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SASFT is compared against three baselines: standard SFT, SFT+GRPO (following Guo et al. 2025, which applies GRPO with a language consistency reward), and SFT+Penalty (which adds a token-level penalty for target-language tokens). Against SFT+GRPO, SASFT wins in virtually all configurations; GRPO is notably unstable, sometimes *increasing* code-switching (e.g., +157% for Llama-3.1-8B on Korean, +32% for Gemma-2-9B on Russian), while SASFT never worsens the ratio. Against SFT+Penalty, SASFT achieves larger reductions in most settings (e.g., −79% vs. −66% for Gemma-2-2B Korean on 210k; −100% vs. −84% for Qwen3-1.7B Korean), though SFT+Penalty occasionally matches or slightly exceeds SASFT on individual cells (e.g., Llama-3.1-8B Chinese 210k: −64% vs. −76%). The GRPO baseline from Guo et al. (2025) was the only prior method specifically targeting unexpected code-switching in LLMs; SASFT outperforms it consistently across all five models and three target languages, demonstrating that the SAE-guided auxiliary loss provides a more reliable and mechanistically grounded solution than reward-based reinforcement learning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Deng et al. (2025), which identified language-specific features in LLMs via sparse autoencoders and showed that ablating them selectively impairs performance in one language. It departs from the only prior attempt at addressing unexpected code-switching (Guo et al., 2025, who applied GRPO with a language-consistency reward) by replacing an inference-time or reward-based intervention with a training-time auxiliary loss that constrains pre-activation values of irrelevant language features. The method thus bridges the SAE interpretability literature (directional ablation, feature identification) with practical supervised fine-tuning, turning a mechanistic observation into a regularizer.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first mechanistic account of why LLMs unexpectedly code-switch (pre-activation spikes in irrelevant language features) and the first method that reduces the phenomenon by >50% while preserving or improving multilingual benchmark scores across five models. Key limitations include coverage of only three target languages (Chinese, Russian, Korean), models under 9B parameters, and restriction to SFT rather than other fine-tuning paradigms like DPO or GRPO. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger models, whether token-level thresholds could further improve results, and whether the SAE-guided loss generalizes to other interpretability-informed training objectives beyond code-switching.
