## Problem

Existing influence estimation methods (Influence Functions, TraceIn, TRAK, LESS) produce scores that are highly sensitive to training randomness such as random seeds, data shuffling, and weight initialization, causing the same data point to appear critical in one run and irrelevant in another. This instability undermines their reliability for data curation and cleanup, since decisions based on a single run may prove suboptimal in subsequent runs. The paper asks how to define influence scores that remain useful for decision-making even under inherent training non-determinism.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a training dataset D, a target subset S whose influence is to be estimated, a test datum z_test, and model gradients collected at checkpoints during a single training run. For LLM-scale experiments, gradients are projected to d=8192 dimensions via random projections and LoRA is used for efficient fine-tuning. The output is a single signed scalar μ (Gaussian influence score) with a clear statistical interpretation as test power at a fixed type-I error rate, from which a total ordering of training samples by influence is derived.

## Method

f-INE reframes influence estimation as a binary hypothesis test: H0 (trained on full dataset D) versus H1 (trained on D\S), where the test statistic is the gradient similarity between the test point and training points at each update step. The key theoretical contribution shows that for composed algorithms like SGD, the f-influence trade-off curve converges asymptotically to a Gaussian G_μ-influence parameterized by a single scalar μ, establishing compositionality and asymptotic normality properties borrowed from the Gaussian Differential Privacy framework. The algorithm operates in two stages within one training run: Stage 1 collects gradient similarity signals O (batches including S) and O' (batches excluding S) at each update step, while an auxiliary model trained in parallel provides a difference-of-differences signal to reduce inter-sample correlation; Stage 2 computes type-I and type-II errors from these samples across a sweep of thresholds and reports μ = max|Φ⁻¹(1−α) − Φ⁻¹(β)|. Unlike mean-based methods such as LESS, f-INE compares the full distributions of gradient similarities, enabling it to detect subtle poisonings that manifest only in distributional tails rather than in the mean.

## Results

On MNIST (20% label noise, MLP), f-INE achieves recall performance only 0.05% below TraceIn while outperforming TRAK by 13.85% and Influence Functions by 3.83%. On the Llama-3.1-8B LIMA poisoning task, f-INE identifies over 60% of biased instructions in the top-20% most influential samples for Joe Biden, compared to 44% for LESS. f-INE also exhibits 64% lower average coefficient of variability than LESS across training seeds (at p=1.0), confirming robustness to training randomness. A qualitative case study shows f-INE assigning an influence score of 2.64 to a subtly poisoned instruction that LESS scores at only 0.04, missing it entirely from the top-ranked set.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Influence Functions (Koh & Liang, 2017), f-INE outperforms by 3.83% on MNIST mislabeled-sample recall, with a smoother and more predictable recall curve due to reduced variance in scores. Against TraceIn (Garima et al., 2020), f-INE is essentially tied on MNIST (0.05% difference) but achieves substantially higher consistency scores across random seeds (Figure 2), where TraceIn shows large variability under data shuffling. Against TRAK (Park et al., 2023), f-INE outperforms by 13.85% on MNIST recall, and TRAK is not scalable to LLM instruction tuning. Against LESS (Xia et al., 2024), the primary LLM-scale baseline, f-INE identifies 60%+ of poisoned instructions in the top-20% ranking versus 44% for LESS on the Joe Biden entity, and achieves 64% lower coefficient of variability across 3 training seeds. The key differentiator is that f-INE compares full gradient-similarity distributions via hypothesis testing, while LESS only compares means, causing LESS to miss poisonings with subtle but heavy-tailed gradient signals.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reframes data influence estimation as a binary hypothesis testing problem, drawing directly on the trade-off function formalism from Gaussian Differential Privacy (Dong et al., 2022) to define f-influence and prove compositionality and asymptotic normality properties analogous to the central limit theorem. For the algorithmic component, it adopts gradient similarity signals from TraceIn (Garima et al., 2020) and LESS (Xia et al., 2024), but departs from their scalar-mean comparison by performing full distributional hypothesis testing over those signals. The single-training-run estimation strategy is borrowed from privacy auditing techniques (Nasr et al., 2023; Steinke et al., 2023), repurposed here to bound cumulative influence via the compositionality corollary rather than to certify privacy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a widely acknowledged but under-theorized weakness of influence estimation: scores are inconsistent across training runs, undermining their use for data curation and debugging. By grounding influence in hypothesis testing, f-INE provides a statistically interpretable score (test power at fixed type-I error) and a total ordering via the Gaussian parameter μ, which prior scalar methods lack under randomness. The connection to membership inference attacks opens a path toward black-box influence estimation using existing MIA tooling. Open questions include whether the white-box, single-run assumption holds under more complex training schedules (e.g., learning rate warmup, mixed precision), whether the 'best-case influence' (max over thresholds) is the right aggregation, and how the framework extends to distributional data valuation like Data Shapley under randomness.
