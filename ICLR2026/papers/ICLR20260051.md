## Problem

Generalist robot policies trained on large, heterogeneous datasets achieve strong average performance but underperform on individual tasks, and naively selecting subsets of prior data for co-training can actually degrade task-specific success. Existing data selection methods in robotics rely on human-defined similarity heuristics (visual, motion, or state-action proximity) that are agnostic to each sample's actual impact on downstream policy performance, and the ideal evaluation metric—real-world rollouts—is too expensive and non-differentiable to use directly in a data selection loop.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a large prior demonstration dataset (ranging from ~1M environment steps in MetaWorld to 24 heterogeneous OXE datasets spanning multiple robot embodiments), a small set of 5–40 target-task demonstrations collected via teleoperation or scripted policies, and a fixed policy architecture (Gaussian MLP for MetaWorld; Octo-Small transformer-diffusion for LIBERO and OXE). It produces per-cluster influence scores over the prior data, a curated subset D_sel (typically the top 10% of clusters by score), and a final policy trained via co-training on D_sel and D_target. A key design choice is that the proxy metric uses a held-out half of D_target for evaluation while the other half is mixed into datamodel training to reduce distribution shift in real-world settings.

## Method

DataMIL extends the datamodels framework to robotics by training a linear influence model f(D') = Σ τ(z_i) that predicts policy performance as a function of the training subset, enabling data selection without retraining. Two estimators are provided: a regression estimator that fits τ by training policies on random Bernoulli subsets (accurate but expensive), and a metagradient estimator that computes exact influence scores via step-wise auto-differentiation over the last k training steps of the policy learner, making it tractable for large transformer-diffusion policies like Octo. The critical adaptation for robotics is replacing the non-differentiable true success rate with a differentiable proxy metric—the negative behavior-cloning loss on a held-out target set—which empirically retains sufficient correlation with rollout success to enable effective selection. To reduce noise from individual state-action pairs, data is clustered at the sub-trajectory (length 15) or trajectory level before influence estimation, and in real-world settings half the target data is included during datamodel training to mitigate distribution shift across embodiments and scenes. The selected top-x% clusters are then co-trained with target demonstrations at a 50/50 sampling ratio to produce the final policy.

## Results

On LIBERO-10, DataMIL achieves 37.76% average success rate, outperforming the best similarity-based baseline BR (36.4%) and Flow (35.42%). On the real-world OXE benchmark, DataMIL achieves a 61.0% average success rate across four tasks and two embodiments, substantially outperforming the best baseline Flow (40.0%) by over 20 points. On MetaWorld's 50 manipulation tasks, DataMIL yields a ~10% average success rate improvement over the strongest baselines. The advantage is most pronounced in heterogeneous, cross-embodiment settings (OXE) where heuristic similarity methods break down.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DataMIL consistently outperforms all three similarity-based baselines (BR, Flow, STRAP) on average across both simulation and real-world benchmarks. The gap is most dramatic on OXE real-world tasks, where DataMIL achieves 61.0% average success versus 39.4% (BR), 40.0% (Flow), and 39.8% (STRAP) — a ~21-point margin — particularly on Franka-Pouch (70.6% vs. 41.2% for BR) and Tiago-Sink (64.1% vs. 46.2% for BR/Flow), where cross-embodiment and heterogeneous data challenge heuristic methods. On LIBERO-10, the advantage is smaller but consistent: DataMIL leads by 1.4 points over BR (37.8 vs. 36.4) and 2.8 points over Flow (37.8 vs. 35.4), though individual tasks like Bowl-Cabinet (72.4 vs. 77.2 for STRAP) and Book-Caddy (82.4 vs. 83.2 for STRAP) show STRAP can match or slightly exceed DataMIL. On MetaWorld's 50 tasks, DataMIL achieves a ~10% average success rate boost over the best baseline, with the advantage driven by its ability to filter suboptimal RL exploration data while retaining relevant expert demonstrations — something state-only (SR) and action-only (AR) retrieval heuristics fail to do simultaneously.

## Relation to Prior Work

DataMIL extends the datamodels framework (Ilyas et al., 2022) and metagradient-based influence estimation (Engstrom et al., 2025) from NLP and computer vision to robot imitation learning, where prior data selection methods (BehaviorRetrieval, FlowRetrieval, STRAP) rely on human-defined similarity heuristics (visual, motion, state-action) rather than direct optimization for policy performance. The key conceptual departure is replacing the expensive, non-differentiable rollout-based success metric with a differentiable behavior-cloning loss on held-out target demonstrations, enabling metagradient computation without real-world rollouts. The paper also introduces robotics-specific adaptations—temporal clustering to reduce influence noise and target-data mixing to mitigate distribution shift—that have no direct analogue in the NLP/CV datamodels literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As the robotics community accumulates ever-larger heterogeneous datasets (OXE, DROID), the ability to select task-relevant subsets without expensive rollouts is practically critical; DataMIL demonstrates that a simple BC-loss proxy suffices for this purpose, with only marginal performance loss compared to true rollout-based selection. However, significant limitations remain: the metagradient estimator is 3–5× slower than standard policy training (3–4 days on OXE-scale data), hyperparameters (selection ratio, cluster size, co-training ratio) lack principled guidance, and the multi-task evaluation is limited to a single three-task DROID setting. Open questions include whether smaller proxy models can reduce datamodel estimation cost, whether the approach scales to truly large-scale multi-task curation, and how to make the method more user-friendly for practitioners without deep data-attribution expertise.
