## Problem

Sparse neural networks are known to exist and can match or exceed dense network performance, but all existing training approaches (iterative pruning, pruning at initialization, dynamic sparse training) are heuristic and lack formal guarantees that the sparse weights can be uniquely identified and efficiently recovered from training data. This paper proves the first sparse recovery guarantee for ReLU MLP weights, showing when and how a simple iterative algorithm can exactly recover a planted sparse network.

## Input / Output

The method takes an n×d Gaussian data matrix X (or whitened data), target labels y ∈ R^n, a sparsity budget s bounding the total number of nonzero fused weights, and a hidden dimension m. The sensing matrix A ∈ R^{n×dm} is constructed from ReLU activation patterns D_i = diag(I{Xh_i ≥ 0})X for m generator vectors h_i, with columns unit-normalized. The output is a sparse weight vector w ∈ R^{dm} with at most s nonzero entries, from which the original hidden-layer weights u_i and output-layer weights v_i are recovered via the fusion w_i = u_i·v_i. For scalar-output networks the output layer is fully fused; for vector-output or deeper networks, a count-sketch data structure handles the larger weight space.

## Method

The key insight is that the convex reformulation of a 2-layer ReLU MLP (Pilanci & Ergen, 2020) recasts sparse weight recovery as a highly structured linear sensing problem y = Aw*, where A's columns are activation-pattern-weighted data rows. The paper proves (Lemma 1) that under mild conditions on activation patterns—each neuron attends to at least an ε fraction of data, and any two neurons' patterns differ in at least a γ fraction of positions—the sensing matrix satisfies restricted strong convexity and restricted smoothness with high probability over Gaussian data. Building on Jain et al. (2014), Theorem 1 then shows that Iterative Hard Thresholding with an inflated projection sparsity s̃ ≥ 32(β/α)²s and step size η = 2/(3β) recovers the planted sparse weights in O((β/α) log(f(w⁰)/ε)) iterations. In experiments, the authors extend beyond the theory by using sequential convex updates to A (alternating between convex and nonconvex formulations), blockwise gradient computation for memory efficiency, and count-sketch-based thresholding for vector-output and deeper MLPs, achieving competitive or superior performance to iterative magnitude pruning while using memory that scales linearly in s rather than in the dense parameter count.

## Results

Across all five experimental tasks (planted scalar/vector MLP fitting, binary/10-class MNIST classification, and MNIST/CIFAR-10 implicit neural representation), IHT consistently achieves higher PSNR or accuracy than the IMP baseline. For small scalar-output models, IHT is dominant on all axes: e.g., on binary MNIST with m=100, s=1000, IHT reaches 99.2% accuracy in 8.4 s while IMP reaches only 77.66% in 20.91 s. On 10-class MNIST (m=10, s=1000), IHT attains 88.73% accuracy versus 77.66% for IMP. For planted 10-dimensional MLP fitting (m=10, s=10), IHT achieves 48.67 dB PSNR versus 24.81 dB for IMP, a gap of over 23 dB.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole experimental baseline is Iterative Magnitude Pruning (IMP) from Frankle & Carbin (2019), the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis algorithm, which first trains a dense network and then iteratively prunes 10% of active weights per round (a more generous schedule than the default 20%). IHT wins on model quality in virtually every setting: it achieves higher PSNR on all planted-MLP fitting and implicit-neural-representation tasks, and higher classification accuracy on both binary and 10-class MNIST. The margin is largest for small, scalar-output models, where IMP often fails to converge above random chance (≈50%) while IHT reaches 99%+ accuracy. For larger vector-output or deeper networks, IHT still attains higher final quality but can be slower in wall-clock time (e.g., 219 s vs. 69 s on 10-class MNIST with m=10, s=1000), because IMP's iterative retraining becomes more efficient as the network grows. Critically, IHT uses a fixed memory budget proportional to the sparsity level s throughout training, whereas IMP must allocate memory for the full dense network (scaling with d × m), making IHT the clear winner on memory efficiency across all settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate literatures: convex reformulations of ReLU networks (Pilanci & Ergen, 2020; Mishkin et al., 2022) and sparse signal recovery via Iterative Hard Thresholding (Jain et al., 2014). The key conceptual move is recognizing that the convex reformulation turns sparse MLP weight recovery into a structured linear sensing problem, to which IHT's restricted strong convexity/smoothness guarantees can be applied. This departs from prior sparse network theory (Ergen & Pilanci, 2021; Boursier & Flammarion, 2023), which either targets different sparsity patterns (output layer, univariate data) or relies on KKT conditions without a tractable recovery algorithm, and from all heuristic sparse training methods (IMP, dynamic sparse training) that lack any recovery guarantee.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first provable recovery guarantee for sparse ReLU MLP weights, establishing that a simple memory-efficient algorithm (IHT) can exactly recover planted sparse weights from random Gaussian data with high probability. The practical implication is that sparse networks can be trained directly with memory scaling linearly in sparsity rather than requiring a dense network to be trained first. However, the theory is restricted to shallow, scalar-output networks with Gaussian data, and the empirical extensions (deeper networks, vector outputs, non-Gaussian data) rely on sequential convex updates and count sketches that go beyond the proven regime. Open questions include extending guarantees to deeper architectures, tightening the sparsity inflation factor inherited from Jain et al. (2014), and scaling the implementation to competitive runtimes for large-scale problems.
