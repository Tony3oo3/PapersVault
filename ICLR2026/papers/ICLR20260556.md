## Problem

While dense LLMs are known to develop language-universal representations in middle layers, it remains unclear how the sparse expert routing in Mixture-of-Experts architectures responds to multilingual data. This paper investigates whether MoE models exhibit analogous cross-lingual alignment in their routing patterns and whether this alignment can be causally leveraged to improve multilingual performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260556/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes non-English text tokens flowing through a trained MoE LLM and a pre-identified set of task-specialized experts (determined by activation-frequency differences Δ > τ between English in-domain data and a generic FLORES baseline). At inference time, it intercepts router logits in a targeted middle-layer window and modifies them before the softmax, producing steered expert selections that increase multilingual task accuracy. The intervention is purely test-time: no weights are updated, and the router still produces a full logit distribution for every token, with only 1–2 experts force-selected or softly boosted per token.

## Method

The paper combines a layer-wise interpretability analysis with a targeted inference-time intervention. For analysis, it computes entropy-normalized Jensen-Shannon divergence between per-sequence mean-pooled routing weight distributions of a non-English language and its paired English translation, revealing a consistent U-shaped pattern: high divergence in early and late layers, low divergence in middle layers, with the depth of the U strongly correlated with per-language benchmark accuracy. For intervention, it identifies task-specialized experts (math or medicine) by thresholding activation-frequency differences (Δ) computed on English in-domain data, then steers the router in the middle-layer window to boost (soft: add λ·s(z) to target logits) or force-activate (hard: set logit to max) those experts when processing non-English inputs. The key finding is that only interventions targeting a small number of English-task experts in the precise middle-layer zone yield consistent 1–2% gains across three models and 15+ languages, while interventions in other layers or targeting multilingual-specialized experts cause degradation.

## Results

Steering middle-layer task experts (identified via English in-domain data) to activate during non-English inference yields consistent 1–2.6% absolute gains across three MoE LLMs (Qwen3-30B-A3B, Phi-3.5-MoE, GPT-OSS-20B) and two tasks (MGSM math, Global-MMLU medicine). On MGSM, non-English average accuracy improves from 76.4%→78.0% (Qwen3), 57.5%→58.9% (Phi-3.5-MoE), and 68.9%→71.5% (GPT-OSS); on Global-MMLU medicine, gains are 68.2%→69.1%, 57.8%→58.8%, and 63.8%→64.5% respectively. Gains are larger for lower-resource languages (e.g., +4.5% for GPT-OSS on low-resource MGSM average) and are statistically significant across the 15+ languages evaluated. In contrast, deactivating experts or intervening outside the middle-layer zone consistently degrades performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against competing multilingual-steering methods; instead, the primary comparison is the unmodified model (original routing) versus the intervened model. The intervention consistently outperforms the original on all six model–task combinations. The paper also ablates the intervention design: deactivating multilingual or task experts (in any layer configuration) leads to substantial performance degradation, and activating experts outside the middle-layer zone (early or late layers) similarly hurts performance. Targeting multilingual-specialized experts rather than task-specialized experts in the middle layers also fails to improve scores. Combining middle-layer task-expert activation with top/bottom-layer multilingual-expert activation nullifies the gains. These negative controls confirm that the positive effect is specifically due to promoting cross-lingual alignment of task-relevant experts in the language-universal middle layers, not a generic perturbation benefit.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the dense-LLM multilingual interpretability literature (Kojima et al. 2024; Wendler et al. 2024; Bandarkar et al. 2025), which established that middle decoder layers host language-universal representations, to the MoE setting where sparse expert activation creates a fundamentally different computational structure. For the intervention methodology, it directly builds on Fayyaz et al. (2026), replicating their force-activation/deactivation mechanism and relative-frequency expert identification, but repurposing them for cross-lingual routing alignment rather than general capability steering. The language-task modularity finding connects to prior observations of parameter separation in dense models (Choenni et al. 2024; Bandarkar & Peng 2025), but the paper shows this separation is far cleaner and more absolute in MoE architectures due to discrete expert assignment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic evidence that MoE LLMs process multilingual text through the same language-universal middle-layer mechanism as dense models, and that the model's multilingual performance is causally limited by its failure to activate shared task experts across languages. The complete non-overlap between multilingual-specialized and task-specialized experts (Finding 5) offers a concrete architectural target for future training methods that promote cross-lingual expert sharing. However, the practical gains from the proposed inference-time intervention are modest (1–2 points), and the approach requires per-model hyperparameter tuning and careful layer selection, limiting immediate deployability. Open questions include whether training-time objectives (e.g., routing alignment losses) can achieve larger, more robust improvements, and whether the findings generalize beyond the two domain-knowledge tasks tested.
