## Problem

Mixture-of-Experts models decouple total parameter count from computational cost, but neither total nor active parameters alone reliably predict an MoE model's effective capacity. Prior scaling-law work studied architectural factors like sparsity or granularity in isolation, leaving no unified framework to predict how a specific MoE configuration's efficiency compares to a dense counterpart before committing to pre-training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261020/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The scaling law takes three continuous architectural/compute parameters as input: the expert activation ratio A (fraction of experts activated per token, e.g., 0.8%–100%), the expert granularity G (defined as 2·d_model/d_expert, ranging from 2 to 16 in experiments), and the total training compute budget C in FLOPs (tested from 3×10¹⁸ to 3×10²⁰, extrapolated to 10²²). The output is a scalar Efficiency Leverage value (EL = C_Dense/C_MoE for matching loss), which directly quantifies how many times more compute a dense model would need to match the MoE's performance, along with the optimal architecture configuration for a given budget.

## Method

The paper introduces Efficiency Leverage (EL) as the ratio of compute budgets a dense model requires versus an MoE model to reach the same target loss, then trains over 300 MoE models (up to 28B parameters, 680k H800-equivalent GPU-hours) to derive univariate scaling laws: EL follows a power law in activation ratio A (with a saturating transformation from Clark et al.), a log-polynomial in granularity G (revealing an optimal range around G=8–12), and a power law in compute budget C (showing MoE advantages amplify at scale). These are unified into a single joint law EL(A,G,C) = Â^(α + γ(log G)² + β log G) where α = a + d·log C, fitted via Huber loss and BFGS optimization (R²=0.9858). Validation is performed by training MoE-mini (17.5B total, 0.85B active parameters, G=12, A=3.4%) against a 6.1B dense model on 1T tokens, confirming the predicted >7× efficiency leverage with MoE-mini matching or exceeding the dense model's downstream performance.

## Results

The unified EL scaling law (Eq. 4) achieves R² = 0.9858 with RMSE of 0.2169 on 200 training points and 0.5275 on 24 validation points, confirming strong predictive power for MoE efficiency. In the validation experiment, MoE-mini (17.5B total, 0.85B active parameters, G=12, A=3.4%) achieves an overall benchmark score of 45.5, outperforming the 6.1B dense model (44.0) while using only ~13% of the active parameters, confirming the predicted >7× efficiency leverage at 1e22 FLOPs. The scaling law predicts that an MoE with 3.1% activation ratio and granularity 12 achieves EL > 7× at 1e22 FLOPs, which is empirically validated by the MoE-mini vs. Dense-6.1B comparison.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is MoE-mini (0.85B active params) against Dense-6.1B (6.1B active params), both trained on 1T tokens. MoE-mini wins on every benchmark category: General/Reasoning (56.2 vs 55.8), Professional (44.7 vs 44.0), Language (71.6 vs 69.2), Code (39.8 vs 36.9), and Math (34.7 vs 32.9), yielding a 1.5-point overall advantage (45.5 vs 44.0) while consuming over 7× fewer FLOPs per token. The paper also contrasts its findings with prior MoE scaling law studies: unlike Clark et al. (2022), who concluded MoE efficiency diminishes at scale (due to a fixed-dataset design), this work shows EL consistently grows with compute budget; unlike Ludziejewski et al. (2024), who reported a monotonic benefit of finer granularity, this paper finds a U-shaped relationship with an optimal granularity around G=12; and unlike Abnar et al. (2025), the EL formulation is dataset-independent, directly quantifying relative compute advantage rather than absolute loss.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the MoE scaling-law literature (Clark et al. 2022; Ludziejewski et al. 2024; Abnar et al. 2025) by shifting the target metric from absolute loss to a new Efficiency Leverage (EL) that directly quantifies an MoE's compute advantage over a dense counterpart. It builds on the power-law loss formulation of Kaplan et al. (2020) and the compute-optimal allocation framework of Hoffmann et al. (2022), adapting both to the sparse-activation setting. A key departure from prior work is the finding that expert granularity has a non-monotonic (U-shaped) effect with an optimum around G=12, contradicting the 'finer is always better' conclusion of Ludziejewski et al. (2024), and that MoE efficiency grows with scale rather than diminishing as Clark et al. (2022) reported.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unified EL scaling law provides a practical, dataset-agnostic design tool for choosing MoE activation ratio, granularity, and compute budget before committing to expensive pre-training, and the 7x efficiency validation with MoE-mini (0.85B active vs. 6.1B dense) demonstrates substantial real-world savings. Open questions include whether the law holds under memory/communication constraints in distributed training, whether interaction effects between architectural factors (assumed independent here) unlock further gains, and whether a Chinchilla-like allocation law can be derived specifically for MoE efficiency. The finding that optimal granularity is bounded (not unbounded) has immediate practical implications for routing design and load-balancing strategies.
