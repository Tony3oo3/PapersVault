## Problem

While chain-of-thought prompting is the de-facto standard for eliciting reasoning from LLMs, the precise mechanisms driving its success remain poorly understood. It is unclear which specific tokens or sub-steps in a CoT trace actually contribute to the final answer, whether CoT reflects genuine reasoning or merely acts as computational scaffolding, and how much of the reasoning process is transferable across models. This paper aims to pinpoint, at the token level, which parts of a CoT unlock previously difficult problems and which parts are irrelevant or even harmful.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt (e.g., a competition math question) and a partial CoT prefix c_{<t}, then samples N=128 full completions (temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95, up to 32k tokens) to Monte-Carlo estimate the probability of producing the ground-truth answer. The primary output is a potential curve — the estimated success probability as a function of CoT prefix length — from which four summary statistics are extracted: fraction of CoTs with reasoning insights (sharp potential increases), reasoning tangents (sharp decreases), late spikes (potential near zero until the final tokens, indicating guessing), and monotonicity. A secondary output is an optimized CoT constructed by greedily selecting chunks that maximize potential at each step.

## Method

The core contribution is the potential metric, defined as pot(c_{<t}; x) = P(y = y* | c_{<t}, x), the probability of a correct final answer conditioned on a given CoT prefix. The authors prove (Proposition 1) that, averaged over correct CoTs, the potential is monotonically non-decreasing in t, yet empirically observe that individual trajectories are strongly non-monotonic, with long plateaus, sharp drops (reasoning tangents), and unintuitive spikes (reasoning jumps). They further introduce CoT transferability: providing a weaker model with the first 20–100% of a stronger model's CoT and measuring the resulting potential/accuracy gain, finding that even 20% of partial CoT from a different model family (e.g., GPT-OSS-20B → Qwen3-0.6B) significantly unlocks previously unsolvable problems. Finally, they propose a chunk-level search procedure (Algorithm 1) that greedily constructs an optimized CoT maximizing potential at every prefix, yielding strongly monotonic curves in contrast to the erratic standard CoT.

## Results

On AIME-2024, only 15–45% of correct CoTs exhibit monotonic potential curves, with reasoning models (Qwen3) showing even less monotonicity (15–36%) than non-reasoning models (42–45%). Reasoning insights (sharp potential increases >40%) appear in 36–62% of traces, while reasoning tangents (potential drops >30%) are significantly more prevalent in reasoning models (18–41%) than non-reasoning models (5–9.5%). Late spikes (potential <5% until the final step, indicating guessing) occur in 5–20% of non-reasoning model traces but only 0–10% for reasoning models. In the transferability experiment, providing as little as 20% of a stronger model's CoT to a weaker model (e.g., Qwen3-0.6B given Qwen3-32B or GPT-OSS-20B traces) yields significant accuracy gains on previously unsolvable AIME-2025 problems.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an analysis paper rather than a method paper, so it does not propose a new model to benchmark against baselines. Instead, it compares six models across two families (Qwen2.5 non-reasoning, Llama3.1 non-reasoning, Qwen3 reasoning) on AIME-2024, MATH-500, HumanEval, and GPQA-Diamond. Non-reasoning Qwen2.5 models show the highest insight rates (40–62%) and lowest tangent rates (5–9.5%), while Llama3.1 models exhibit far more tangents (33–40%) and lower monotonicity (15–17%), suggesting Llama3.1's CoT is more erratic. Reasoning models (Qwen3) show the most tangents (18–41%), consistent with the 'overthinking' phenomenon in the literature, but the largest Qwen3-32B suppresses late-spike guessing entirely (0%) compared to 10% for Qwen3-0.6B. In the transferability comparison, Qwen3-0.6B benefits from both same-family (Qwen3-32B) and cross-family (GPT-OSS-20B) CoT, with same-family transfer being slightly stronger but cross-family transfer still substantial, indicating that CoT insights are partially model-agnostic.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper introduces the 'potential' metric—a Monte Carlo estimate of conditional success probability given a partial CoT—as a quantitative lens for analyzing chain-of-thought reasoning, drawing conceptual inspiration from the value function in actor-critic RL. It is most closely related to recent work on conditional generation from partial CoTs (Bigelow et al. 2025 on 'fork tokens'; Bogdan et al. 2025 on 'thought anchors') and to Amani et al. (2025) on completing partial CoTs for reward shaping, but departs by focusing on task-relevant insights, failure modes (tangents, jumps, guessing), and cross-model transferability rather than abstract reasoning concepts. The paper also positions itself against the growing body of work arguing that CoT does not faithfully reflect internal computation (Lanham et al. 2023; Chen et al. 2025b; Kambhampati et al. 2025), using the potential to empirically test whether CoT steps genuinely unlock difficulty or merely mimic reasoning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The potential provides a concrete, interpretable tool for pinpointing which tokens in long reasoning chains actually drive success, with direct implications for fine-grained credit assignment in RL training (a direction already being explored by concurrent work). The finding that as little as 20% of a stronger model's CoT can unlock previously unsolvable problems for a weaker model has practical implications for model distillation, test-time compute allocation, and curriculum design. Key open questions remain: why 'reasoning jumps' (sharp potential increases from seemingly trivial steps) occur, whether the potential can be efficiently computed at scale for RL, and whether transferability degrades for more architecturally dissimilar model pairs. The pass@k inflation due to late-spike guessing also raises concerns about current benchmarking practices for difficult mathematics.
