## Problem

Existing EEG-based concept decoding frameworks face a fundamental trade-off: fixed-label classification is too restrictive to capture the compositional, continuous nature of meaning, while end-to-end LLM-based generation produces uninterpretable black-box outputs that require massive paired neural-linguistic data. This paper introduces Semantic Intent Decoding (SID), a framework that models communicative intent as a variable set of compositional semantic units decoded into a continuous semantic space, aiming to recover both interpretability and expressiveness simultaneously.

## Input / Output

The method takes multichannel EEG (122–128 channels) or intracranial SEEG (132 channels) recordings segmented into sentence-level trials (e.g., 5 s reading + 3.3 s imagined speech in Chisco) and produces two outputs: (1) a variable-sized set of semantic units (words/concepts) with per-unit confidence scores, where the number of active units ranges from 1 to a fixed slot capacity K (default 10), and (2) a reconstructed natural-language sentence generated by an LLM conditioned on those units plus global attributes (tone, subjectivity, sentence type). The semantic space is continuous and open-vocabulary, instantiated by pretrained text embeddings (doubao-embedding-large or Qwen3), so the model can retrieve concepts beyond the training vocabulary.

## Method

BRAINMOSAIC implements SID as a three-stage pipeline. First, a ModernTCN encoder converts raw EEG into neural feature tokens, which a Transformer with K learnable queries (inspired by DETR) maps to K candidate semantic slots; a Hungarian bipartite matching loss with a no-object class handles the variable, unordered nature of semantic units. Second, a Semantic Retriever aligns each active slot to the nearest concept in a continuous pretrained text-embedding space via cosine-similarity matching, while simultaneously predicting global sentence-level attributes through auxiliary classification heads. Third, a Semantic Decoder constructs a structured prompt from the retrieved units (with probability scores) and global attributes, then uses an LLM (GPT-4o-mini) to generate a coherent, semantically faithful sentence. The entire network is trained end-to-end under a combined objective of token-level matching loss, global alignment loss, and attribute classification loss, with the LLM used only at inference for reconstruction.

## Results

On the private Clinical SEEG dataset, BRAINMOSAIC achieves UMA 0.660, MUS 0.812, SRS 0.665, and BERT-F1 0.663, substantially outperforming all baselines (p ≤ 0.001). On the public Chisco EEG dataset, it reaches UMA 0.562, MUS 0.801, SRS 0.621, and BERT-F1 0.620. On ZuCoSR (English), BRAINMOSAIC attains the highest concept-level scores (UMA 0.751, MUS 0.859) and SRS 0.698, surpassing the end-to-end Neuro2Semantic baseline (SRS 0.521). Across all six dataset splits, BRAINMOSAIC consistently leads on every reported metric with statistically significant margins.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Cls-Align baseline (which follows the Chisco classification framework with a frozen backbone projected into sentence-embedding space), BRAINMOSAIC improves SRS by 0.067 on Clinical (0.665 vs. 0.598) and by 0.117 on Chisco (0.621 vs. 0.504), demonstrating that single-category labels cannot capture compositional semantic intent. Against Neuro2Semantic (Shams et al., 2025), the end-to-end EEG-to-text generator, BRAINMOSAIC achieves SRS 0.698 vs. 0.521 on ZuCoSR and 0.609 vs. 0.445 on ZuCoNR, confirming that semantic-unit-constrained generation yields substantially higher fidelity than unconstrained decoding. The set-based formulation also decisively outperforms the sequential Seq-Decode baseline (same encoder and LLM, LSTM decoder instead of bipartite matching): on Clinical, UMA jumps from 0.179 to 0.660 and SRS from 0.510 to 0.665, validating that unordered set matching better models the permutation-invariant nature of semantic intent. The Multi-Cls baseline (multi-label classification over a fixed vocabulary) performs worst on concept-level metrics (UMA < 0.04 on most datasets), highlighting the fundamental limitation of discrete classification in a continuous semantic space.

## Relation to Prior Work

BRAINMOSAIC sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) set-based object detection (DETR, Carion et al. 2020), whose Hungarian bipartite matching and no-object slot mechanism are repurposed to predict a variable-sized, unordered set of semantic units from EEG; (2) EEG concept decoding, which has been dominated by either fixed-label classification (Chisco, Zhang et al. 2024) or end-to-end neural-to-text generation (Neuro2Semantic, Shams et al. 2025); and (3) LLM-based language generation, used here as a constrained reconstruction stage rather than an unconstrained decoder. The key conceptual departure is from both prior paradigms: instead of mapping EEG to a closed label set or to a single sentence embedding, the framework decomposes neural activity into a set of semantic units aligned in a continuous embedding space (doubao-embedding-large), then uses an LLM to assemble them into coherent sentences. The EEG encoder (ModernTCN) and the overall pipeline architecture are standard, so the novelty is primarily in the set-matching formulation applied to semantic units and the three-principle design (compositionality, continuity, fidelity).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The SID framework offers a principled middle ground between the interpretability of label classification and the expressiveness of end-to-end generation, which is a long-standing tension in BCI research. The open-vocabulary generalization results (stable performance when the retrieval vocabulary expands by 30,000+ words) and the multilingual validation (Chinese and English) suggest the approach could scale to realistic communication needs beyond small closed sets. Key limitations include: all experiments are in-subject (no cross-subject generalization), the clinical SEEG dataset contains only one participant, the final sentence generation depends on an external LLM (adding latency and a black-box component that partially undermines the interpretability claim), and the semantic unit decomposition (which words constitute a 'unit') is defined by the authors rather than learned. Open questions include whether the set-matching formulation transfers to cross-subject or cross-session settings, how the framework handles longer discourse-level intents, and whether the continuous semantic space can be learned from fewer paired examples.
