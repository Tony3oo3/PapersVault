## Problem

Evaluating modern ML models on full benchmarks (e.g., MMLU, HELM, LMMs-Eval) requires hundreds to thousands of GPU hours per model, slowing innovation and reducing inclusivity. Prior efficient-evaluation methods select anchor subsets via clustering over model-response embeddings and predict performance through complex psychometric models (IRT), making the pipeline sensitive to design choices and unnecessarily complicated. The paper argues that sample diversity per se is irrelevant; what matters is diversity in model responses, and that a direct mapping from raw model outputs to performance can outperform these indirect approaches.

## Input / Output

The offline stage consumes a pool of source models (e.g., 382 LLMs from the Open LLM Leaderboard) evaluated on the full test set, producing a set of K anchor points (typically 100) and a trained predictor. The online stage takes a single target model, runs inference only on the K anchor points, concatenates the per-sample output vectors into a high-dimensional model signature (e.g., 3100-dim for 100 samples × 31-class MMLU), optionally applies PCA (64–256 components), and feeds the reduced signature into the predictor. The output is a scalar estimated accuracy (and implicit ranking) for the target model on the full benchmark. The method requires predictive probabilities over a fixed set of answer choices, so it is not applicable to open-ended generation tasks.

## Method

DISCO has two stages. For selection, it scores every sample by the Predictive Diversity Score (PDS) or the generalised Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD) across a set of source models, then keeps the top-K samples with the highest scores—greedy, sample-wise statistics that avoid global clustering. Proposition 1 proves that the mutual information between a model's identity and its prediction on sample i equals the JSD of the source models' predictive distributions on that sample, establishing inter-model disagreement as the information-theoretically optimal selection criterion. For prediction, the target model's outputs on the K anchor points are concatenated into a model signature, reduced via PCA, and mapped to full-benchmark accuracy by a simple predictor (Random Forest or kNN) trained on source-model signatures and their known performances. This direct signature-to-accuracy mapping bypasses IRT latent-parameter estimation entirely, yet achieves 1.07%p MAE and 0.987 Spearman rank correlation on MMLU with only 100 samples (99.3% cost reduction), outperforming tinyBenchmarks, Metabench, and anchor-point baselines across language and vision benchmarks.

## Results

On MMLU (14k samples reduced to 100), DISCO with PDS selection and Random Forest prediction achieves 1.07%p MAE and 0.987 Spearman rank correlation, a 99.3% reduction in inference cost. Across all four language benchmarks (MMLU, HellaSwag, Winogrande, ARC), DISCO consistently achieves the best MAE and rank correlation, with MAE ranging from 1.00%p (Winogrande) to 1.47%p (ARC). In the vision domain on ImageNet (50k reduced to 100), DISCO achieves 0.63%p MAE and 0.969 rank correlation, a 99.8% cost reduction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DISCO outperforms tinyBenchmarks (Polo et al., 2024) on all four language benchmarks: on MMLU, DISCO achieves 1.07%p MAE vs. 2.08%p (Anchor-corr + gp-IRT) and 0.987 vs. 0.927 rank correlation. Against Metabench (Kipnis et al., 2024), DISCO wins on MMLU (1.07 vs. 2.08 MAE; 0.987 vs. 0.904 rank) and Winogrande (1.00 vs. 1.23 MAE), though Metabench achieves lower MAE on HellaSwag (0.80 vs. 1.01) and ARC (1.14 vs. 1.47); however, the paper notes Metabench requires 50–350% more samples, making the comparison not directly apples-to-apples. In the vision domain on ImageNet, DISCO (0.63%p MAE, 0.969 rank) substantially outperforms both Lifelong Benchmark (Prabhu et al., 2024: 2.06%p MAE, 0.838 rank) and SSEPY (Fogliato et al., 2024: 3.05%p MAE, 0.762 rank), confirming that selecting diversity-inducing samples via PDS is more effective than covering sample difficulty distributions.

## Relation to Prior Work

DISCO builds on the two-stage efficient evaluation framework (anchor selection + performance prediction) established by Anchor Points (Vivek et al., 2023), TinyBenchmarks (Polo et al., 2024), and Metabench (Kipnis et al., 2024), but departs from them on both stages. For selection, it replaces global clustering or IRT-based representativeness criteria with a greedy, sample-wise disagreement score (PDS/JSD), grounded in an information-theoretic argument (Proposition 1) showing that inter-model disagreement is the optimal signal for differentiating models. For prediction, it bypasses latent-parameter estimation (IRT, ability scores) in favor of a direct mapping from high-dimensional model signatures (concatenated outputs on the selected subset) to benchmark performance via simple predictors like Random Forest or kNN, drawing on the Predictive Diversity Score originally proposed for OOD detection (Rubinstein et al., 2024).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DISCO achieves a 99.3% reduction in evaluation cost on MMLU with only ~1 percentage point of error, and generalizes to vision (ImageNet) with similar gains, making frequent model evaluation during training and low-budget evaluation of deployed models practically feasible. The key open question is robustness to distributional shifts in the model population (new architectures, training paradigms), which the authors acknowledge as a limitation; their performance-gap experiments suggest degradation only under unrealistically large gaps. The method also requires predefined answer choices with predictive probabilities, excluding open-ended generation tasks, and the break-even of ~389 evaluations, while easily met in practice, means it is less useful for one-off evaluations of a single new model.
