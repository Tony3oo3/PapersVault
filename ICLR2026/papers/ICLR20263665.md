## Problem

Despite the empirical success of score matching in diffusion models, the optimization behavior of gradient descent on the score matching objective in over-parameterized regimes remains theoretically unexplored. Prior work (Shah et al., 2023) established convergence only for exactly-parameterized two-component mixtures, and gradient EM convergence (Xu et al., 2024) does not directly transfer because the score matching gradient contains cubic parameter interactions absent in the EM update. This paper asks: can we prove global convergence of gradient descent on the score matching loss when the student model has more parameters than the ground truth requires?

## Input / Output

The method operates at the population level on a fixed noise scale t, taking data drawn from a single Gaussian N(μ*, I_d) and a student score function parameterized by n ≥ 2 vectors μ_i ∈ R^d (motivated by a Gaussian mixture structure with softmax weights). The output is the evolved parameter trajectories and the score matching loss L_t(s_t) = E[‖Σ w_i(x)μ_i − μ_t*‖²]. Unlike typical neural network training, the 'network' is an explicit weighted-sum score function, and the analysis tracks individual parameter norms rather than a single loss landscape, revealing that different parameters can converge or diverge independently.

## Method

The paper analyzes gradient descent on the population score matching loss for an over-parameterized student (n parameters) learning a single Gaussian (1 component), identifying three distinct convergence regimes governed by the noise scale t and initialization. In the large-noise regime (t > log n + log M + 2), the cubic gradient terms become negligible and the dynamics reduce to those of gradient EM, yielding an O(1/√τ) loss convergence rate. In the small-noise regime, the paper proves the existence of a nonzero-loss stationary point (ruling out arbitrary-initialization convergence), then shows that exponentially small initialization ensures all n parameters converge to the ground truth via a geometric-center tracking argument, while random far initialization causes exactly one parameter to converge and the remaining n−1 to diverge to infinity, yet the loss still vanishes at rate O(1/τ) with a nearly matching Ω(1/τ^{1+ε}) lower bound. The key technical difficulty is controlling cubic interaction terms in the gradient that dominate when parameters are far from the ground truth, requiring new bounding techniques beyond the linear-term analysis used in gradient EM.

## Results

In the large-noise regime (t > log n + log M + 2), gradient descent achieves an O(1/√τ) loss convergence rate, matching the best known rate for gradient EM in the over-parameterized setting. In the small-noise regime with exponentially small initialization, all n parameters converge to the ground truth with O(1/√τ) loss decay after a warmup of O(n(log n + log M₀)/η) iterations. Under random Gaussian initialization far from the ground truth (M₀ ≥ 10⁹√d·n¹⁰), exactly one parameter converges to the ground truth while the remaining n−1 diverge to infinity, yet the loss still converges at rate O(1/τ), with a nearly matching lower bound of Ω(1/τ^(1+ε)) for any constant ε > 0. This O(1/τ) rate is in sharp contrast to the linear convergence rate achieved in the exactly-parameterized setting of Shah et al. (2023).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no head-to-head empirical comparisons against competing methods. The key comparison is with Shah et al. (2023), who proved global convergence of gradient descent on the DDPM objective for a two-component Gaussian mixture in the exactly-parameterized and well-separated regime, achieving linear convergence. The present work extends this to the over-parameterized setting (n ≥ 2 parameters for a single Gaussian) and reveals that over-parameterization fundamentally changes the convergence landscape: the O(1/τ) sublinear rate under random initialization replaces the linear rate, and full parameter recovery requires exponentially small initialization. The large-noise result (Theorem 2.1) builds directly on the gradient EM analysis of Xu et al. (2024), showing that the score matching gradient reduces to the gradient EM update when parameters are sufficiently close to the ground truth. The paper also provides a counterexample (Theorem 3.4) showing that the exponential initialization requirement is necessary, and identifies a stationary point with nonzero loss (Theorem 3.1) that prevents global convergence from arbitrary initialization in the small-noise regime.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the score matching convergence analysis of Shah et al. (2023), who proved global recovery for a two-component Gaussian mixture under the DDPM objective in the exactly-parameterized setting, to the over-parameterized regime where n parameters learn a single Gaussian. It also builds on the gradient EM convergence techniques of Xu et al. (2024), establishing a formal connection between score matching and gradient EM in the over-parameterized setting. The key technical departure from both predecessors is the presence of cubic gradient terms in the score matching loss that do not appear in gradient EM, requiring new analytical tools to handle the dynamics when parameters are far from the ground truth.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to establish global convergence guarantees for Gaussian mixtures with at least three components under the score matching framework, and it reveals that over-parameterization fundamentally changes the convergence landscape: depending on initialization, either all parameters converge to the ground truth or only one converges while the rest diverge to infinity, yet the loss still vanishes at an O(1/τ) rate (in contrast to linear convergence in the exactly-parameterized case). The identification of a stationary point with nonzero loss in the small-noise regime shows that arbitrary initialization cannot guarantee convergence. Open questions include the intermediate initialization regime between the two characterized extremes, extension to multi-component ground truths, and how these fixed-time dynamics interact under the time-averaged loss used in practice.
