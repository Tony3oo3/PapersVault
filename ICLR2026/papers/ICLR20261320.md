## Problem

Most theoretical analyses of optimization geometry and implicit bias center on gradient flow, yet modern fine-tuning practice relies on adaptive methods like Adam that behave qualitatively differently from SGD. It remains unclear how the geometry of steepest descent (as opposed to plain gradient descent) governs saddle-point escape, feature learning, and sparsity in overparameterized models, particularly in the small-learning-rate regime typical of fine-tuning.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a continuously differentiable loss, a deep diagonal reparameterization of depth L, an optimizer exponent q ∈ [1,2] (interpolating between gradient flow at q=2 and sign gradient flow at q=1), and a λ-L_p-balanced initialization near the saddle set. It produces a separable Bregman/Legendre function characterizing the induced mirror flow, a coercivity constant μ = λ^{q(L−1)} that quantifies saddle-escape speed, explicit balance equations, and a metric exponent m = q(L−1)/L that determines global stability and sparsity bias. The analysis is pointwise in the parameter space and does not require a specific dataset; empirical validation uses linear regression, separable classification, and fine-tuning of vision/LLM models.

## Method

The paper extends the mirror flow framework from gradient flow (Hilbert space) to a family of steepest descent flows (Banach space), where the inner product structure is lost and one must work with L_p norms and their duals. For deep diagonal reparameterizations x = Π w_i, they derive novel balance equations |w_i|^q − |w_j|^q = const·exp(−q∫α ds) that are preserved under steepest descent with decoupled weight decay, and show these induce a separable steepest mirror flow with an explicit Bregman function whose metric exponent is m = q(L−1)/L. The key qualitative result is that smaller q (SignGF ≈ Adam) yields a larger coercivity constant and a valid Bregman function for all depths L ≥ 2, enabling fast saddle escape and globally stable dynamics, whereas GF (q=2) only admits a valid Bregman function at L=2 and requires unrealistically large learning rates to escape saddles. They further show that decoupled weight decay induces a manifold regularizer ‖x‖_{L^{2−q(L−1)/L}} that requires L→∞ for sparsity under SignGF, in stark contrast to coupled weight decay which induces extreme sparsity at finite depth.

## Results

In fine-tuning experiments, Adam with a small learning rate (lr≪1) dramatically outperforms SGD with the same small learning rate across all models and datasets: e.g., 95.19% vs. 19.15% validation accuracy for ResNet-18 on CIFAR-10, and 85.95% vs. 43.87% for BERT-base on MRPC. Adam at small lr also matches or slightly exceeds SGD with a tuned large learning rate (95.19% vs. 93.60% on CIFAR-10; 99.37% vs. 98.94% for ViT-large on Flowers). In the diagonal linear network experiments, SignGF (≈Adam) escapes saddle-point initializations significantly faster than GF (≈SGD), with the gap widening at greater depth L. For sparsification, AdamW (decoupled weight decay) induces sparsity only at very deep reparameterizations (L=10) with large decay (1e-1), whereas coupled weight decay produces extreme sparsity even at L=2.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares Adam (≈SignGF) against SGD (≈GF) in fine-tuning, showing that Adam with a small learning rate consistently wins: on CIFAR-10 with ResNet-18, Adam achieves 95.19% vs. SGD's 19.15% at the same small lr, and even surpasses SGD with a tuned large lr (93.60%). The gap is most dramatic on Flowers (80.50% vs. 1.22% for small-lr SGD) and MRPC (85.95% vs. 43.87%), where SGD at small lr essentially fails to escape saddle regions. Hessian eigenvalue analysis (Figure 5a) confirms Adam exhibits fewer and weaker negative eigenvalues, indicating more effective saddle escape. For the weight decay comparison, AdamW (decoupled) requires substantially deeper reparameterizations (L=10) and larger decay (1e-1) to induce sparsity, whereas coupled weight decay in Adam produces extreme sparsity already at L=2 — a qualitative difference predicted by the paper's balance-equation analysis (Table 1) and confirmed in the ResNet-50 ImageNet sparsification experiments.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mirror flow framework of Li et al. (2022) and Jacobs et al. (2025b) from gradient flow (Hilbert space) to a family of steepest descent flows indexed by q ∈ [1,2] (Banach space), where q=2 recovers gradient flow and q=1 recovers sign gradient flow (a proxy for Adam). It builds on the steepest descent margin characterization of Tsilivis et al. (2025) for separable classification but shows that the L∞-margin is depth-independent, whereas the mirror flow geometry introduces a depth-dependent metric exponent that governs sparsity. The balance equations generalize the λ-balance of Marcotte et al. (2023) from the q=2 case to arbitrary q, revealing that the relative scale of parameters evolves differently under steeper descent.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean geometric explanation for why Adam/AdamW outperforms SGD in fine-tuning at small learning rates: steeper descent (lower q) yields a larger coercivity constant, enabling faster saddle-point escape without requiring unrealistically large learning rates. The distinction between coupled and decoupled weight decay for sparsity induction (decoupled decay requires L→∞ for L1 sparsity under SignGF, versus extreme sparsity at finite depth for coupled decay) offers actionable guidance for reparameterized training. Key limitations include the restriction to diagonal reparameterizations, the SignGF-as-Adam proxy (which drops momentum and second-moment tracking), and the absence of stochasticity; extending the Banach-space mirror flow framework to non-diagonal architectures and discrete stochastic training remains open.
