## Problem

Deep clustering methods typically assume balanced class distributions, but real-world data often follows a long-tailed distribution where head classes dominate and tail classes are scarce, causing severe performance degradation. Supervised long-tailed learning methods mitigate this bias by re-weighting classes based on known label frequencies, but deep clustering is fully unsupervised and lacks such labels. The open challenge is how to estimate appropriate class-specific re-weighting factors in an unsupervised setting to balance gradient contributions across head and tail classes.

## Input / Output

The method takes unlabeled data (RGB images at 32×32 for CIFAR/STL, 64×64 for Tiny ImageNet, or text passages) and a known number of target clusters K as input. A pre-trained encoder (BYOL, SimCLR, or MoCo for images; DistilBERT for text) first extracts embeddings, which are then fed to two linear clustering heads. The output is a set of K cluster assignments produced by the target-cluster head after joint training; the mini-cluster head's predictions are used only during training to estimate re-weighting factors and are discarded at inference.

## Method

MiniClustering introduces a dual-head architecture where a target-cluster head (dimension K) and a mini-cluster head (dimension M > K) are trained jointly on the same encoder embeddings. The key insight is that under long-tailed distributions, head-class samples occupy more embedding space and thus get assigned to more high-purity mini-clusters than tail-class samples; the number of mini-clusters 'owned' by each target-cluster (determined by a purity threshold δ) serves as a proxy for class frequency. This count is inverted to produce a per-class weight that re-balances the threshold-based self-training cross-entropy loss, giving tail classes larger gradient contributions. A similarity alignment loss (MSE between pairwise prediction similarity matrices of the two heads) prevents training desynchronization, and the overall objective combines the re-weighted target-cluster loss, the mini-cluster self-training loss, and the alignment loss with trade-off parameters α and β.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with imbalance ratio 5, MiniClustering achieves 74.3% ACC, 72.6% CAA, 69.9% NMI, and 62.1% ARI, outperforming the strongest baseline ConMix (61.6% ACC, 45.6% ARI) by 12.7% in ACC and 16.5% in ARI. On CIFAR-20 (IR=5), it reaches 45.6% ACC and 30.7% ARI, and on STL-10 (IR=5) it achieves 54.7% ACC and 42.8% ARI. Under the more challenging imbalance ratio of 10, MiniClustering still leads all baselines, e.g., 64.6% ACC and 56.7% ARI on CIFAR-10 versus 53.3% ACC and 40.8% ARI for ConMix. The method also extends to large-scale settings (Tiny ImageNet: 25.5% ACC; ImageNet-LT: 15.7% ACC) and text datasets (StackOverflow: 65.3% ACC; AgNews: 58.7% ACC).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MiniClustering achieves the best performance across all four metrics (ACC, CAA, NMI, ARI) on every dataset and imbalance ratio tested, with no losses to any baseline. The strongest competitor is ConMix (Li and Jia, ICLR 2025), which MiniClustering outperforms by 12.7% ACC and 16.5% ARI on CIFAR-10 (IR=5), and by 11.3% ACC and 15.9% ARI on CIFAR-10 (IR=10). Among representation-learning-based methods, BYOL+K-means (the pre-training baseline) is surpassed by 13.9% ACC on CIFAR-10 (IR=5), while SimCLR and MoCo baselines show even larger gaps (e.g., +21.2% ACC over SimCLR on CIFAR-10 IR=10). Among clustering-head methods, LFSS and SeCu are the closest competitors on CIFAR-20 (MiniClustering leads by 2.1% ACC and 2.1% ARI over LFSS), but on CIFAR-10 the gap widens to 16.2% ACC over LFSS and 18.2% ACC over SeCu. On large-scale datasets, MaxiClustering (the variant for high-K settings) outperforms ConMix by 9.0% ACC on Tiny ImageNet and by 0.3% ACC on ImageNet-LT, confirming scalability. On text datasets, MiniClustering beats ConMix by 7.3% ACC on StackOverflow and 3.1% ACC on AgNews, demonstrating modality-agnostic effectiveness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate lines of work: supervised long-tailed learning (which re-weights class losses using known label frequencies) and unsupervised deep clustering (which operates without labels). It builds directly on the self-labeling clustering architecture of SCAN and the BYOL/SimCLR/MoCo representation learning frameworks, but departs from prior long-tailed clustering methods (ConMix, SDCLR) that balance losses at the sample level by instead estimating class-level weights in a fully unsupervised manner. The key conceptual move is using a finer-grained mini-cluster head to infer which target-clusters correspond to head vs. tail classes, thereby enabling the direct transfer of supervised re-weighting losses (CB Softmax, BALMS, EQL) into the unsupervised setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to perform class-level re-weighting in unsupervised long-tailed learning, opening a pathway to adapt the rich toolbox of supervised long-tailed losses to clustering without requiring ground-truth label frequencies. The theoretical guarantee (Theorem 1) clarifies when the mini-cluster counting heuristic is valid, tying its success to embedding quality and cluster purity. Limitations include dependence on a pre-trained encoder of sufficient quality, sensitivity to the mini-cluster count M, and the fact that the approach is validated primarily on image and text classification-style clustering; whether the mini-cluster principle generalizes to other distributional pathologies (e.g., multi-modal class distributions) remains open.
