## Problem

Existing AIGI detectors produce binary real/fake decisions without identifying the specific visual artifacts that justify their judgments, and prior benchmarks lack pixel-level, categorized artifact annotations needed to evaluate whether models truly attend to human-interpretable cues. This paper asks: do current detectors actually rely on perceptual artifacts, and can we explicitly align model attention to artifact regions to improve both interpretability and generalization?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262113/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image (real photograph or AI-generated) and produces a binary authenticity score, a pixel-level multi-class segmentation mask over 7 artifact categories (spanning low-level distortions, high-level semantics, and cognitive-level counterfactuals), and a patch-level attention heatmap. The benchmark provides semantically paired real-fake image sets (4,000 pairs × 13 generators), with artifact annotations as polygon masks at native resolution. The attention alignment loss operates at the ViT patch granularity, comparing a differentiable Gradient Attention Rollout heatmap against a patch-level artifact proportion map.

## Method

The paper introduces X-AIGD, a benchmark with 3,035 annotated fake images carrying 18,285 pixel-level artifact instances across 7 categories in 3 hierarchical levels, paired with semantically aligned real images from 13 generators. Empirically, it shows that existing detectors (CNNSpot, FatFormer, DRCT, CoDE, etc.) exhibit negligible alignment between their Grad-CAM/Relevance Map interpretations and ground-truth artifact regions, and that multi-task learning with PAD as an auxiliary task yields at most marginal AJ gains while the classification head still attends to uninterpretable background features. The key technical contribution is an attention alignment loss: during training, the Gradient Attention Rollout heatmap for the fake-class logit is compared via weighted MSE to a patch-level artifact proportion map, where a benign-region weight λ ∈ [0,1] controls the penalty on non-artifact patches to preserve learning of other useful features (e.g., low-level fingerprints). This loss is added to standard BCE with weight β, and is applied when fine-tuning DINOv2 (ViT-B) on X-AIGD, yielding consistent F1 improvements across four cross-dataset benchmarks (Synthbuster, Chameleon, Community Forensics) over both no-alignment and saliency-based alignment baselines.

## Results

On X-AIGD, existing AIGI detectors show negligible alignment with perceptual artifacts: the best (DRCT-ConvB) achieves only 9.0 IoU and 16.6 PixF1 on Category-Agnostic PAD, while most score below 1.5 PixF1. A Swin-B model trained solely on AJ reaches 89.3% balanced accuracy and 90.2% F1, while the PAD-only model achieves 27.2 IoU and 42.7 PixF1 on artifact segmentation. The proposed attention alignment method (DINOv2 + artifact-based regularization) improves cross-dataset F1 over the no-alignment baseline by 3.1 points on Synthbuster (63.2 vs. 55.9), 0.8 on Chameleon (63.5 vs. 62.7), and 3.2 on Community Forensics (61.4 vs. 58.2), consistently outperforming saliency-based alignment.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All seven existing AIGI detectors (CNNSpot, Gram-Net, UnivFD, FatFormer, DRCT-ConvB, DRCT-CLIP, CoDE) exhibit near-zero Category-Agnostic PAD performance (IoU ≤ 9.0, PixF1 ≤ 16.6), confirming they do not rely on human-perceivable artifacts for their decisions. The best existing detector for AJ is DRCT-ConvB (82.5% Acc, 81.0% F1), which is outperformed by the AJ-only Swin-B baseline (89.3% Acc, 90.2% F1) trained on X-AIGD. Multi-task learning (89.1% Acc, 92.3% F1) and full fine-tuning transfer learning (89.9% Acc, 92.5% F1) yield only marginal AJ gains over the AJ-only model, while the PAD-only model (27.2 IoU, 42.7 PixF1) substantially outperforms all existing detectors on artifact localization. For cross-dataset generalization, the proposed artifact-based attention alignment (DINOv2) beats the no-alignment baseline on F1 across all four test sets (e.g., 63.2 vs. 55.9 on Synthbuster) and also surpasses saliency-based alignment, demonstrating that aligning attention specifically to annotated artifact regions—not merely salient objects—drives the improvement.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) binary AIGI detection (CNNSpot, UnivFD, DRCT, CoDE), which achieves high accuracy but offers no interpretable evidence; (2) MLLM-based explainable detection (FakeVLM, FakeReasoning, LEGION), which produces textual explanations but lacks spatial grounding and paired real/fake supervision; and (3) perceptual artifact localization (PAL4VST, SynArtifact, RichHF-18K), which provides pixel-level masks but targets quality improvement rather than detection. The paper's methodological contribution builds on Gradient Attention Rollout (Gildenblat, 2020) to make attention differentiable, enabling a novel alignment loss that regularizes ViT attention toward annotated artifact regions — a mechanism not previously applied to AIGI detection.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a critical gap: state-of-the-art AIGI detectors achieve high accuracy while showing negligible alignment with human-perceivable artifacts, meaning their decisions are effectively unexplainable to end users. The finding that multi-task learning with artifact segmentation as an auxiliary task fails to redirect detection toward interpretable cues is a useful negative result that cautions against assuming auxiliary tasks automatically improve interpretability. Open questions include whether the attention alignment approach scales to cognitive-level artifacts (commonsense/physics violations) where current models show near-zero detection, and whether MLLMs can be made genuinely grounded in artifact localization rather than producing hallucinated explanations.
