## Problem

Existing activation steering methods for LLM alignment lack a unified theoretical framework for designing steering directions, and most rely on one-step linear updates that cannot capture complex, nonlinear patterns in activation distributions. This limits both the interpretability of why certain steering directions work and the expressive power of the steering itself.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262376/cropped_page1_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes LLM hidden activations at a single residual stream layer (e.g., layer 15 for Falcon-7B, layer 16 for Mistral-7B, layer 14 for LLaMA3.1-8B), collected from the last token position of contrastive positive/negative sequences for training and from each newly generated token at inference time. Activations are first normalized to unit ℓ₂ norm, then mapped through a Polynomial Count Sketch (degree 2, 8000 random features) before logistic regression fitting. The output is a steered activation vector produced by integrating the ODE over 10 Euler steps with a model-specific total time T (e.g., T=20–23 for Falcon-7B, T=3–4 for Mistral-7B), which is then fed back into the LLM for continued generation.

## Method

ODESTEER reframes activation steering as numerically solving an ODE whose vector field is the normalized gradient of a barrier function from control theory. The barrier function is defined as the log-density ratio between positive and negative activation distributions, estimated via logistic regression on nonlinear Polynomial Count Sketch features, which avoids the Gaussian or linearity assumptions of prior methods. The key theoretical insight is that standard one-step activation addition (a + T·v(a)) is exactly the first-order Euler discretization of the ODE ṁa(t) = v(a(t)), so multi-step integration with a dynamically updated vector field reduces approximation error from O(T²) to O(T²/n) and enables adaptive, feedback-driven steering. Proposition 2 proves that the resulting ODE monotonically increases the barrier function almost everywhere, guaranteeing forward invariance of the desirable activation region. The entire pipeline requires only scikit-learn logistic regression and torchdiffeq's Euler solver, avoiding the neural network training and hyperparameter sensitivity of prior nonlinear steering methods.

## Results

ODESTEER achieves consistent improvements across all three alignment tasks and all three models (Falcon-7B, Mistral-7B, LLaMA3.1-8B). On TruthfulQA (T×I metric), ODESTEER reaches 42.2%, 59.9%, and 63.2% for Falcon-7B, Mistral-7B, and LLaMA3.1-8B respectively, outperforming the best baselines by 5.0–9.5 percentage points. On UltraFeedback (Win rate), ODESTEER achieves 56.3%, 56.1%, and 58.2%, surpassing the strongest baselines by 1.5–3.5 points. On RealToxicityPrompts (Toxicity↓), ODESTEER reduces toxicity to 0.188, 0.109, and 0.116, beating the best baselines by 0.005–0.039 absolute reduction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ODESTEER outperforms all eight baselines on the primary metrics across all three models. Against the strongest one-step linear methods (CAA, ITI, Linear-AcT), ODESTEER gains 2.5–3.5 points on UltraFeedback Win rate, 5–9 points on TruthfulQA T×I, and 0.02–0.04 absolute reduction in toxicity. Among the nonlinear/DNN-based baselines, HPR is the closest competitor on detoxification (0.155 vs. 0.116 on LLaMA3.1-8B) and truthfulness (57.0% vs. 63.2% T×I), but ODESTEER still leads by 5.2 points on T×I and 0.039 on toxicity. TruthFlow achieves competitive truthfulness on Mistral-7B (49.5% T×I) but degrades helpfulness (48.2% Win) and is outperformed by ODESTEER on all three primary metrics. RE-Control and RepE consistently underperform, with RE-Control showing the weakest helpfulness (48.6–51.5% Win) and RepE the weakest truthfulness (24.4–41.3% T×I). MiMiC and Linear-AcT are competitive on individual sub-metrics (e.g., MiMiC's 59.0% True on LLaMA3.1-8B) but sacrifice informativeness or toxicity, resulting in lower overall T×I and higher toxicity than ODESTEER.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies the fragmented landscape of activation steering (RepE, ITI, CAA, MiMiC, Linear-AcT, etc.) under a single ODE-based theoretical framework, showing that the standard one-step activation addition is precisely the Euler discretization of an ODE and that both input-reading and output-optimization strategies implicitly define barrier functions from control theory. It builds on the Polynomial Count Sketch (Pham & Pagh, 2013) for efficient nonlinear feature maps and on control barrier function theory (Ames et al., 2016, 2019) for forward-invariance guarantees. The key departure from prior work is replacing fixed or linearly-estimated steering vectors with a multi-step, activation-adaptive ODE trajectory, while retaining the simplicity of classical logistic regression rather than the neural networks used by RE-Control, HPR, or TruthFlow.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ODE + barrier-function lens gives the activation-steering community a principled vocabulary for reasoning about steering strength (integration time T), direction (vector field), and safety (forward invariance), which prior ad-hoc methods lacked. Practically, ODESTEER's use of polynomial count sketch + logistic regression makes it easy to implement and tune compared to DNN-based baselines, while matching or exceeding their performance at comparable inference speed. A notable open question is whether unsupervised feature learning (sparse autoencoders) can be folded into the barrier-function framework, which the authors acknowledge as nontrivial. The framework also naturally suggests extensions to multi-objective steering (multiple barrier functions) and to diffusion models, where the ODE perspective is already native.
