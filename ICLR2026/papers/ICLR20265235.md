## Problem

While polysemanticity is known to enhance LLM capacity, its safety implications—specifically whether semantically unrelated features that share activation-space geometry can be exploited to covertly manipulate model outputs—remain largely unexplored. This paper asks whether such polysemantic interference structures are model-specific training artifacts or stable, transferable regularities that persist across architectures, scales, and training regimes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20265235/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes SAE feature directions (32,768-dim from Neuronpedia), token gradient vectors computed via backpropagation through a single layer, and short activation-text snippets as intervention signals, with the critical constraint that interference features must fall below a semantic-similarity threshold in the human symbolic manifold M while exceeding a cosine-similarity threshold in activation space A. Outputs are quantified as relative shifts in next-token prediction distributions (weighted cosine similarity Δc and weighted overlap Δw) against feature-associated token sets, plus identified cross-model interference structures and transferable steering vectors applicable to black-box models without internal access.

## Method

The paper decomposes the representational space into three nested domains—the human symbolic manifold M, the model activation space A_ℓ, and the SAE sparse feature basis F_ℓ—and exploits the structural fact that orthogonality in M does not persist in A_ℓ, creating exploitable interference channels between semantically distant features. Four complementary interventions are designed at escalating levels of access: SAE feature-direction steering, token-gradient steering (most effective, ~10× stronger than feature-direction), prompt injection with high-activation snippets, and neuron-level amplification/suppression. Agglomerative clustering aligns SAE features to consistent semantic granularity before pairing targets with interference features sampled across five interference intervals. The cross-model transfer protocol identifies feature pairs that are simultaneously high-interference and low-semantic-similarity in both Pythia-70M and GPT-2-Small, then applies the corresponding gradient vectors or text snippets to Llama-3.1-8B/70B-Instruct and Gemma-2-9B-Instruct as black-box targets, achieving >95% success rates for certain feature types without any access to the target model's internals.

## Results

Token-gradient steering produces roughly 10× larger intervention effects than SAE feature-direction steering on both Pythia-70M and GPT-2-Small, with effects increasing monotonically with interference scale. Prompt injection yields a ~10% shift in weighted cosine similarity but 10×–100× (GPT-2-Small) and 100×–1000× (Pythia-70M) gains in weighted overlap, concentrating interference on a small subset of target-associated tokens. Cross-model transfer is strong: gradient-based interventions on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct achieve >95% success in elevating target-type tokens into the top-10 for location, name, and emotion categories. Post-hoc annotation of 459,229 shared interference pairs finds only 27.7% exhibit a plausible higher-order semantic association, and a paired-choice test confirms the high-interference pair is judged more related 64.3% of the time (95% CI [0.628, 0.658], p < 0.001).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external steering methods; instead, it uses three internal baselines—random-feature intervention, low-interference features, and the original (target) feature—to isolate the polysemantic interference signal. Across all three intervention modalities (feature-direction, token-gradient, prompt-injection), high-interference features consistently outperform both the random and low-interference baselines, with the gap widening as interference scale increases. Token-gradient steering is the strongest modality, yielding ~10× the effect of SAE feature-direction steering and flattening the interference-scale gradient (i.e., even moderate-interference features become effective). For cross-model transfer, high-interference tokens derived from Pythia-70M and GPT-2-Small significantly outperform random baselines on Llama-3.1-8B/70B-Instruct and Gemma-2-9B-Instruct for location, number, and science targets (Table 1), though the effect is less consistent for person, animal, emotion, and color (Table 5). The 'Original' column (intervening with the target feature itself) always yields the highest absolute success rate, confirming that the interference signal is a genuine secondary channel rather than a replacement for direct targeting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the SAE-based interpretability pipeline (Bricken et al., 2023; Templeton et al., 2024) from a descriptive tool for identifying monosemantic features to a systematic mapping of the *interference topology* between semantically unrelated features in activation space. It builds on activation-steering techniques (Panickssery et al., 2023; Ferrando et al., 2024) and the superposition framework (Elhage et al., 2022), but departs from prior work by treating polysemanticity primarily as a structural vulnerability rather than an interpretability obstacle, and by explicitly testing whether interference patterns identified in small models (Pythia-70M, GPT-2-Small) transfer to larger instruction-tuned models (Llama-3.1, Gemma-2) without access to their internals. The cross-model universality angle connects to the Platonic representation hypothesis (Huh et al., 2024; Lan et al., 2024), but applies it specifically to the geometry of interference rather than to feature identity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that polysemantic interference structures partially persist across architectures and scales (70M → 70B) suggests these are not training artifacts but reflect a shared, higher-order organization of semantic representations, with direct implications for AI safety: if interference patterns are transferable, then vulnerabilities identified in small, inspectable models may predict risks in deployed large models. However, the transfer is inconsistent across feature types (strong for location/name/emotion, weak for person/color/time), and the paper's own annotation shows only ~28% of shared interference pairs have identifiable higher-order semantic links, leaving the functional origin of these structures open. Key limitations include the reliance on Neuronpedia SAEs (limited to two small models), the narrow set of tested feature categories, and the absence of a mechanistic account for *why* particular semantically distant features interfere. Future work should test whether these structures emerge from shared pretraining data, architectural inductive biases, or both, and whether they can be mitigated without degrading model capability.
