## Problem

The objectives that LLMs implicitly optimize remain opaque, and existing Inverse Reinforcement Learning approaches either return a single overconfident reward estimate or ignore the fundamental non-identifiability of the task (multiple reward functions can explain the same behaviour). No prior framework treats reward inference as a structured *auditing* process that quantifies ambiguity, flags unreliable regions, and validates the inferred objective at the policy level.

## Input / Output

The method consumes paired completions (o⁺ from an RLHF-trained expert policy, o⁻ from a baseline policy) generated on the same prompts, along with frozen mean-pooled text embeddings from the LLM's own embedding space as the feature map φ. It produces a full mean-field Gaussian posterior q(θ) = N(μ, diag(σ²)) over linear reward weights, per-prompt epistemic uncertainty estimates (mutual information), and a refined point reward R̂(o) = μ_Kᵀφ(o) that can be plugged directly into a PPO/RLHF loop. Data is partitioned into K sequential rounds, so the output is a trajectory of contracting posteriors rather than a single static estimate.

## Method

The Alignment Auditor is a three-stage protocol built on Bayesian IRL with a Bradley-Terry likelihood and a zero-mean isotropic Gaussian prior over linear reward weights θ, approximated by a mean-field variational posterior optimized via the reparameterization trick. Stage 1 recovers the initial posterior; Stage 2 performs sequential Bayesian updates where each round's posterior becomes the next round's prior, tracking non-identifiability reduction through the log-determinant of the covariance and decomposing predictive uncertainty into aleatoric and epistemic components to flag shortcut-prone or out-of-distribution prompts. Stage 3 validates the refined reward by fine-tuning the baseline policy with PPO using the posterior-mean reward and comparing training dynamics (reward curves, KL stability, downstream toxicity reduction) against an oracle RLHF run. The key distinction from prior Bayesian IRL is the explicit sequential contraction mechanism and the policy-level validation loop, which together reframe reward inference from a one-shot estimation task into a verifiable audit with actionable uncertainty diagnostics.

## Results

On the primary detoxification task (RealToxicityPrompts), the Alignment Auditor achieves pairwise accuracy of 0.7524 and single-text F1 of 0.7508 with Llama-3.2-1B, improving to 0.773 and 0.847 respectively with Llama-3.1-8B (AUROC 0.916, Cohen's d = 1.821). Sequential Bayesian updates over 5 rounds contract the posterior log-determinant from −196 to −897 and reduce epistemic uncertainty (MI) from 0.1272 to 0.0683, while policy-level validation shows the inferred reward achieves toxicity reductions comparable to the ground-truth oracle RLHF. On the HH-RLHF helpfulness benchmark, pairwise accuracy reaches 0.729 (Llama-3.1-8B) with single-text F1 of 0.645, confirming generalization beyond detoxification.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against external IRL or reward-modeling baselines in a head-to-head quantitative comparison; instead, it validates against the ground-truth oracle reward and ablated variants of itself. At the policy level, the inferred reward (rounds ≥ 2) closely tracks the oracle RLHF training dynamics in reward mean and KL divergence, achieving comparable downstream toxicity reduction, whereas the under-identified round-1 posterior induces reward hacking with degenerate outputs. In the comparative auditor analysis (Appendix E.4), the full-posterior variance signal achieves AUC ≈ 0.86–0.88 for detecting spurious-feature shortcuts, substantially outperforming the posterior-mean reward (AUC ≈ 0.63–0.70) and a standard output-based RoBERTa toxicity auditor (AUC ≈ 0.55–0.58, near chance). The sequential Bayesian procedure dramatically reduces posterior solution volume (logdet −196 → −897) relative to single-round inference while maintaining similar raw accuracy, demonstrating that the gain is in identifiability rather than mere fit. The positioning table (Table 4) contrasts the framework qualitatively against Standard RLHF, Standard BIRL, LLM Bayesian Ensembles, Bayesian RM for LLMs, and Variational BIRL, highlighting that only the Alignment Auditor combines reward UQ, sequential updates, and policy-level validation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Bayesian IRL (Ramachandran & Amir, 2007) and classic IRL formulations (Ng & Russell, 2000) from a one-shot reward estimation task to a structured, three-stage auditing process for LLMs. While recent LLM-oriented IRL work (Joselowitz et al., 2025; Sun & van der Schaar, 2025; Cai et al., 2025) recovers reward posteriors but stops at inference, and Bayesian reward models for RLHF (Yang et al., 2024) remain embedded in the forward optimization loop, this paper introduces sequential posterior contraction, uncertainty-aware diagnostics for shortcut detection, and policy-level validation as independent audit stages. The Bradley-Terry likelihood and mean-field variational approximation are standard tools repurposed here; the novelty lies in the sequential posterior-as-prior scheme and the explicit decoupling of reward inference from the RLHF training pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides safety teams and regulators with a concrete, uncertainty-calibrated method to verify what objectives an LLM is actually optimizing, rather than relying on output-level behavioural probes that miss latent shortcut reliance. The finding that an under-identified (round-1) posterior induces reward hacking during PPO while contracted posteriors (rounds 2–5) avoid it is practically important: it gives auditors a quantitative threshold for when a recovered reward is safe to deploy. Key limitations include the linear reward head over frozen embeddings, small-to-mid-scale LLMs (≤8B), and a classifier-based proxy for ground truth, leaving open whether the framework scales to non-linear objectives, multi-objective settings, or frontier-scale models where non-identifiability may be more severe.
