## Problem

Existing interpretability and alignment tools (sparse autoencoders, steering vectors, probes) intervene on model activations to discover or steer concepts, but they require external machinery at inference time. This paper asks whether a simpler, language-native approach—introducing entirely new words into a frozen model's vocabulary—can both control model behavior along arbitrary concepts and enable the model to describe what those new words mean to it, analogous to how humans coin new terms for emerging concepts.

## Input / Output

The method takes a frozen transformer LM (Gemma-3-4B-IT) and a small set of instruction–response pairs where the instruction embeds a new token in natural language (e.g., 'Give me a ~concept answer') and the response exemplifies a target concept. At inference, the same prompt template with the trained neologism token produces concept-controlled text; alternatively, the model can be queried for synonyms or free-text descriptions of the neologism, yielding self-verbalizations that are then validated via plug-in evaluation (swapping the token for its verbalization and measuring whether the same steering effect persists).

## Method

Neologism learning expands the model's vocabulary by k new tokens, initializes their embeddings from semantically unrelated existing words, and trains only those k embedding vectors via gradient descent while all other parameters remain frozen. The training objective is APO-up, a DPO variant that simultaneously encourages the likelihood ratio of chosen over rejected responses and the absolute likelihood of the chosen response, applied to 2,100 instruction–response pairs per concept drawn from LIMA. The key design choice is placing the neologism in natural-language context rather than as a prefix, so its meaning is defined distributionally by the surrounding sentence. For self-verbalization, the model is prompted with meta-questions (list synonyms, describe the response style) while the neologism is still present in the prompt, and the resulting free-text descriptions are validated by plug-in evaluation. The approach extends to jointly training multiple neologisms, enabling compositional control over interrelated concepts (short, numerical, likely) that few-shot prompting fails to capture.

## Results

Across seven simple concepts (long-text, short-text, single-sentence, use-like, flattery, refusal, wrong-answer), trained neologism embeddings close an average of 92% of the gap between the base model and the target concept, with individual concepts reaching 95–105%. On five AxBench complex concepts, neologism learning achieves overall steering scores of 1.54–1.91 (out of 2.00), matching or exceeding the concept-prompted training data (1.72–1.92) while the no-concept baseline scores 0.0–0.4. For joint multi-concept control (short + numerical + likely), neologism learning outperforms few-shot prompting on the hardest 'likely' concept (0.66 vs. 0.28 success rate) and on the all-three-concepts harmonic mean (0.48 vs. 0.39).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the AxBench concept-prompting baseline (which inserts the full natural-language concept description into the prompt), neologism learning matches or slightly exceeds the training-data scores on 4 of 5 concepts (e.g., 1.89 vs. 1.92 for 'islands'; 1.91 vs. 1.89 for 'streams') while the no-concept baseline collapses to 0.0–0.4. Against few-shot prompting for multi-concept composition, neologism learning wins decisively on the complex 'likely' concept (0.66 vs. 0.28) and on the three-concept harmonic mean (0.48 vs. 0.39), though few-shot edges on the simple 'short' single concept (0.92 vs. 0.74). Against in-context neologism definition (10 examples in the prompt), the embedding-learning method achieves 92% average concept gap closure for Gemma-3-4B-IT versus only 46% for in-context learning, a 46-point margin. Self-verbalized synonyms (plug-in evaluation) close only 25–39% of the gap on average, confirming that the learned embedding itself remains the strongest control signal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper operationalizes the neologism learning concept proposed in a position paper by Hewitt et al. (2025), providing the first systematic empirical evaluation. Methodologically, it is closest to prompt-tuning (Lester et al., 2021) and prefix-tuning (Li & Liang, 2021), but the critical distinction is that neologisms are embedded in natural language contexts rather than prepended to sequences, leveraging the distributional hypothesis to give the new token rich contextual meaning. Conceptually, it departs from the mechanistic interpretability paradigm (sparse autoencoders, steering vectors, probes) by using the model's own vocabulary as the interface for concept control, rather than intervening on internal activations. The self-verbalization evaluation connects to out-of-context learning literature (Betley et al., 2025a; Berglund et al., 2023), but extends it by showing that free-text model descriptions of learned behaviors can be causally validated via plug-in evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The discovery of machine-only synonyms—words like 'lack' that are causally effective at steering behavior across multiple model families (Gemma, Gemini, GPT-5) yet semantically unintuitive to humans—opens a new window into how models internally represent concepts and challenges the assumption that meaningful verbalizations must be human-interpretable. The plug-in evaluation methodology provides a general, lightweight validation tool for any self-verbalization claim. Key open questions include: whether self-verbalizations are systematic or idiosyncratic, whether the approach scales to larger models and more complex concepts, and whether machine-only synonyms reveal stable cross-model concept representations. The method's practical appeal is high (no forward-pass changes, no external machinery), but most experiments use a single 4B model, limiting confidence in generalizability.
