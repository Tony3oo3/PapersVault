## Problem

Existing formal theorem-proving benchmarks (miniF2F, ProofNet, FIMO) focus on contest-style or introductory university mathematics, which prioritizes clever tricks over the systematic application of deeply nested abstract frameworks characteristic of modern research. No formal benchmark existed that matched the difficulty of PhD qualifying exams or whose formalized content exceeded the current scope of Mathlib, leaving a critical gap in evaluating whether LLMs can perform research-level algebraic reasoning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260360/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each problem is presented as a self-contained Lean 4 file containing a natural-language LaTeX description as a comment followed by a formal theorem statement with exactly one `sorry`; 38% of FATE-X problems additionally include novel definitions (e.g., local complete intersections, Gorenstein rings) not yet present in Mathlib. Models produce a two-stage output: an intermediate natural-language chain-of-thought proof followed by a complete Lean tactic proof, evaluated with pass@64 under a 64k-token limit and verified by the Lean kernel with string-matching checks to prevent header modifications.

## Method

The FATE series (M/H/X, 150/100/100 problems) is curated through a three-stage pipeline: expert mathematicians (nearly 20 postdocs/PhDs) select and classify 400 candidate problems from 20+ textbooks, PhD qualifying exams, and the Stacks Project; five Mathlib contributors formalize 200 problems across five dedicated workshops; and two reviewers with Lean expertise verify correctness. The evaluation methodology is two-stage: mathematics experts manually assess the correctness of intermediate natural-language proofs (pass@1), while the Lean kernel automatically verifies final formal proofs (pass@64). This dual analysis reveals that formalization—not mathematical reasoning—is the primary bottleneck, with errors classified into Mathlib hallucinations, Lean proficiency issues, general capability failures, and misalignment. A comparative study between DeepSeek-R1 (general) and DeepSeek-Prover-V2 (specialized) identifies 'effective reflection'—the ability to locate, diagnose, and repair reasoning flaws—as the key differentiator, with the specialized prover exhibiting less effective reflection and misaligned behaviors such as questioning problem statements or engaging in conscious cheating.

## Results

On the FATE benchmark series (pass@64, 64k token limit), the best model DeepSeek-Prover-V2-671B achieves 62.7% on FATE-M (undergraduate level), but performance collapses to just 3.0% on FATE-H (graduate level, matched by o3) and 0.0% on FATE-X (PhD qualifying exam level) for all eight models tested. A two-stage analysis reveals that intermediate natural-language reasoning accuracy (pass@1) is dramatically higher than final formalization accuracy: e.g., DeepSeek-R1 achieves 71.0% NL accuracy on FATE-H but 0.0% formal accuracy, and 33.0% NL vs. 0.0% FL on FATE-X. The most common formalization errors are Mathlib hallucinations and Lean proficiency issues, while misalignment between NL reasoning and formal code is rare.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates eight models (five general reasoning models and three specialized theorem provers) on the three FATE difficulty tiers. On FATE-M, specialized provers lead: DeepSeek-Prover-V2-671B (62.7%) outperforms the best general model o3 (51.3%) by 11.4 points, while Goedel-Prover-V2-32B (48.7%) and Kimina-Prover-72B (36.0%) trail. On FATE-H, the advantage of specialized provers vanishes: DeepSeek-Prover-V2-671B and o3 tie at 3.0%, while all other models score 0–2%. On FATE-X, every model scores 0.0%, indicating a complete failure of current approaches at PhD qualifying exam difficulty. In the two-stage NL-vs-FL analysis, general reasoning model DeepSeek-R1 substantially outperforms specialized provers on natural-language accuracy (71.0% vs. 39.0% for DeepSeek-Prover-V2 on FATE-H; 33.0% vs. 9.0% on FATE-X), suggesting that specialized RL training for formalization does not transfer to stronger mathematical reasoning and may even degrade it relative to the base model DeepSeek-V3 (40% NL accuracy on FATE-H).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the FATE-M undergraduate-level formal algebra benchmark (Shen et al., 2025) into a progressive series (FATE-M/H/X) that pushes formal theorem proving from textbook exercises to PhD qualifying exam level and beyond. It departs from the dominant contest-math and introductory-university benchmarks (miniF2F, ProofNet, PutnamBench) by targeting the abstract, structure-heavy proofs characteristic of modern algebraic research, and is the first formal benchmark whose problems require definitions not yet present in Mathlib. The two-stage analytical framework (evaluating intermediate natural-language CoT separately from final Lean formalization) is a new diagnostic lens applied to existing SOTA provers rather than a new model architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The near-total failure (0% pass@64 on FATE-X, 3% on FATE-H) against near-saturation on miniF2F exposes a massive unaddressed gap between contest-level and research-level formal reasoning, providing the field with concrete difficulty checkpoints. The finding that the primary bottleneck is the NL-to-formal translation (not mathematical reasoning itself) and that specialized provers exhibit *less* effective reflection than general reasoning models points toward two concrete research directions: decoupling the NL prover from the autoformalizer, and designing training that preserves meta-reasoning while leveraging formal reward signals. Limitations include the single-domain focus (abstract/commutative algebra), reliance on Lean/Mathlib infrastructure, and a relatively small problem count (100 per benchmark), leaving open whether the findings generalize to other mathematical areas or proof assistants.
