## Problem

Prior theoretical work on in-context learning as implicit optimization has been restricted to i.i.d. data, leaving the more realistic non-i.i.d. setting largely uncharacterized. This paper addresses the gap by studying how a single linear attention layer learns sequences generated by noise-corrupted linear dynamical systems, where tokens exhibit full-history statistical dependence. The central question is: what is the structure of the optimally trained attention weights, and what optimization algorithm do they implement in the forward pass?

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequence of T scalar observations {y_t}_{t=1}^T produced by a doubly-noised LDS (state noise w_t and observation noise v_t), which is tokenized into an (s+1)×(T−s) matrix Y_0 using the AR(s) window construction of Von Oswald et al., with the final column serving as a test token whose last entry is filled by the model's prediction. The output is the predicted value ŷ_T at the (s+1, T−s) position of the transformer output, and the learned parameters are the query-key matrix W_QK ∈ R^{(s+1)×(s+1)} and value matrix W_V ∈ R^{(s+1)×(s+1)}. Training minimizes the in-context loss E[(T_θ(Y_0)_{s+1,T−s} − y_T)²] over randomly sampled LDS parameters A, c, and initial state x_0.

## Method

The paper analyzes a single causally-masked linear attention layer (no positional encoding, no MLP, no projection) trained via AdamW on the in-context prediction loss. The key theoretical result (Theorem 4.1) proves that for AR(1) tokens, the global minimizer of the loss implements exactly one step of Gradient Descent on the AR(1) least-squares objective starting from w_0 = 0, with the optimal W_QK and W_V given in closed form. For general AR(s) with s ≥ 2, Lemma 4.1 identifies a checkerboard sparsity pattern in the first-order optimality condition, narrowing the class of candidate minimizers; experiments confirm that trained weights converge to this pattern. The proof technique relies on Isserlis' theorem to decompose the high-order moments arising from the non-i.i.d. data dependence, combined with the central symmetry of A's distribution to establish parity-based zero structures. Empirically, the forward pass for s ≥ 2 is shown to resemble a two-step Preconditioned Conjugate Gradient iteration (or more generally an augmented Krylov subspace method) on the AR(s) loss, with the additional correction direction p acting as a residual-reducing augmentation.

## Results

The paper's primary results are theoretical: Theorem 4.1 proves that for AR(1) tokens, the optimal single linear attention layer implements exactly one step of Gradient Descent on the autoregressive least-squares loss, and Lemma 4.1 identifies a checkerboard sparsity pattern that the optimal W_QK and W_V must satisfy for general window size s ≥ 2. Empirically, across four LDS sampling settings (diagonal A, symmetric A, anisotropic Σ_w, non-symmetric A) and window sizes s = 1–4, the trained weights converge to the predicted sparsity pattern (Jaccard distance → 0) and the AR(1) weights match Theorem 4.1's closed form. For the s ≥ 2 PCG interpretation, the cosine similarity between the transformer's search direction and the PCG direction is 0.88 ± 0.05 (diagonal A) and 0.93 ± 0.01 (symmetric A), while the correction vector p achieves a residual-reduction cosine of −0.99 ± 7×10⁻⁵ and −0.99 ± 3×10⁻⁵ respectively, strongly supporting the augmented Krylov-subspace reading.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not perform head-to-head numerical comparisons against other prediction methods (e.g., Kalman Filter, other transformer architectures, or i.i.d.-trained attention layers). Instead, the 'comparison' is between theoretically predicted weight structures and empirically learned weights: the AR(1) case is validated against the closed-form GD-step expression in Theorem 4.1, and the AR(s) cases (s = 2, 3, 4) are validated against the checkerboard sparsity pattern of Lemma 4.1 via Jaccard distance convergence. The paper also positions its findings relative to prior i.i.d. results (Ahn et al. 2023, Mahankali et al. 2023, Von Oswald et al. 2023a), noting that those works prove GD-step optimality only for i.i.d. Gaussian covariates, whereas Theorem 4.1 extends this to the non-i.i.d. LDS setting. The connection to the Kalman Filter is discussed qualitatively: the paper argues that Theorem 4.1 provides a plausible mechanism for the empirical KF-parity observed by Du et al. (2023), but does not run a direct numerical benchmark against the KF.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ICL-as-optimization framework (Von Oswald et al. 2023a; Ahn et al. 2023; Mahankali et al. 2023), which established that single linear attention layers implement gradient descent steps for i.i.d. linear regression, to the fundamentally harder non-i.i.d. setting of LDS-generated sequences. It leverages the improper learning / AR(s) approximation of LDSs from system identification (Kozdoba et al. 2019; Tsiamis & Pappas 2019) to define a context-dependent loss, then proves optimality of the GD-implementing weights for AR(1) tokens. The paper also departs from prior transformer-KF connections (Du et al. 2023; Goel & Bartlett 2024; Akram & Vikalo 2024), which either required known system parameters or provided only existence/approximation guarantees, by characterizing the actual pretraining loss minimizers in a few-shot setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first optimality result for linear attention layers trained on non-i.i.d. LDS data, providing theoretical grounding for the empirically observed transformer-KF parity (Du et al. 2023) and bridging system identification theory with transformer ICL mechanics. The PCG/Krylov subspace interpretation for s≥2 is a novel algorithmic lens, though currently supported only by preliminary cosine-similarity evidence rather than a formal proof. Key open questions include: full characterization of optima for s≥2 (only a structural narrowing via Lemma 4.1 is provided), extension to stationary LDS regimes where the problem simplifies, multi-layer transformers, and the standard causal pretraining objective rather than few-shot last-token prediction.
