## Problem

As LLMs grow in capability, red-teaming increasingly becomes a weak-to-strong problem where target models surpass the red-teamer, yet no prior work has quantified how attack success depends on the attacker-target capability gap. The paper asks: at what capability gap does human-like black-box red-teaming become infeasible, and can this transition be predicted from standard benchmark scores?

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes an unlocked attacker LLM, a target LLM (prompted with a Llama2 safety system prompt), and a harmful behavior from HarmBench as input; the attacker iteratively generates jailbreak prompts guided by judge feedback over N streams and R rounds. Output is the best-of-N Attack Success Rate (N up to 25) scored by a neutral HarmBench judge, aggregated across four attacks per attacker-target pair. The key derived output is a per-target sigmoid scaling curve mapping the logit-space capability gap δ = logit(attacker_MMLU-Pro) − logit(target_MMLU-Pro) to predicted ASR, enabling forecasting for unseen future targets.

## Method

The paper evaluates over 600 attacker-target combinations using four human-like LLM-based jailbreak attacks (PAIR, TAP, PAP, Crescendo) across 29 target models of varying families, sizes, and capability levels. To eliminate refusal as a confound, all 22 open-source attacker models are 'unlocked' via LoRA fine-tuning on ~1500 harmful examples (BadLlama + Shadow Alignment) followed by instruction-following data (AlpacaEval/SkillMix) to prevent overfitting to the red-teaming prompt. The core modeling contribution fits a linear regression in logit space (following Miller et al., 2021) with the capability gap as the sole predictor of logit-transformed ASR, yielding sigmoid curves in probability space that accurately predict attack success for held-out frontier targets. Three empirical trends emerge: attacker ASR scales linearly with MMLU-Pro (ρ > 0.88), ASR drops sharply once the target's capability exceeds the attacker's, and social-science MMLU-Pro splits (health, psychology, philosophy) are stronger ASR predictors than STEM splits. Using the median scaling trend, the authors forecast that a fixed-capability human attacker (MMLU-Pro ≈ 0.898) will see ASR decline monotonically as future models grow more capable.

## Results

Across 600+ attacker-target pairs (22 unlocked attackers × 29 targets), attacker success rate (ASR@25) scales linearly with MMLU-Pro capability (Spearman ρ > 0.84), while target vulnerability follows a sigmoid decline as target capability exceeds the attacker's (R² = 0.83). In the saturated weak-to-strong regime (capability gap δ < −3.5 in logit space), ASR does not exceed 0.2. The derived scaling curve (median parameters k=1.73, b=−0.79) predicts that a fixed human red-teamer (MMLU-Pro ≈ 0.898) will see ASR decline as future models surpass human-level capability. Social-science MMLU-Pro splits (health, psychology, philosophy) show the strongest Pearson correlation with attacker ASR, outperforming STEM splits.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper directly compares four LLM-based jailbreak attacks—PAIR, TAP, PAP, and Crescendo—on the same 600+ attacker-target pairs. TAP achieves the highest ASR overall and across larger capability gaps, consistent with independent findings by Havaei et al. (2025) that TAP significantly outperforms Crescendo. PAIR is competitive with TAP on most targets but slightly trails at the strongest attacker–weakest target end. Crescendo and PAP achieve higher ASR specifically on more robust (better-safeguarded) targets, yet both underperform PAIR and TAP when run on the same query budget (ASR@25). PAP is identified as the least effective attack overall, with its zero-shot, non-iterative design limiting its ability to adapt. The original Crescendo results (Russinovich et al., 2025) relied on a GPT-4 attacker; when matched to the same attacker models, its advantage disappears.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM-based jailbreaking literature (PAIR, TAP, PAP, Crescendo) by introducing a capability-gap framing that departs from prior scaling analyses focused on inference-time compute (Zaremba et al., Boreiko et al.) or number of attempts (Hughes et al., Anil et al.). It builds on Ren et al. (2024b), who first reported a negative correlation between jailbreak success and model capability, by precisely characterizing the relationship as a sigmoid function of the logit-transformed capability gap, following the logit-space regression approach of Miller et al. (2021). The model-unlocking procedure (LoRA fine-tuning on BadLlama/Shadow Alignment) enables controlled comparison of attacker capabilities by removing safety refusal as a confound, a technique adapted from prior work on shallow safety alignment (Arditi et al., Volkov, Qi et al.).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The derived capability-gap scaling curve provides a practical predictive tool for model providers to estimate how long deployed systems will remain safe as open-source attackers grow more capable, and it forecasts that fixed-capability human red-teamers will become increasingly ineffective. The finding that social-science capabilities (psychology, health, philosophy) predict attack success more strongly than STEM knowledge challenges the field's current focus on hazardous technical capabilities and calls for new benchmarks on persuasive and manipulative abilities. Key limitations include reliance on only four attack methods, MMLU-Pro as an imperfect proxy for both attacking and defensive capabilities (early Llama models are notable outliers), and the assumption that current attack methods remain representative of future human-like red-teaming.
