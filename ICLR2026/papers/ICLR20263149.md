## Problem

Black-box one-run privacy auditing of DP-SGD relies on canary examples to estimate empirical privacy lower bounds, but prior work uses simple canary choices (random training samples or mislabeled images) without optimizing them for auditing efficacy. This paper asks whether carefully designing the canary set can substantially tighten these lower bounds, and if so, how to do so efficiently in the black-box setting where the auditor only observes the final model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263149/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a standard image classification dataset (e.g., CIFAR-10 at 32×32×3, MNIST at 28×28×1), a small surrogate architecture (ResNet-9), and a target number of canaries (m=1000), and produces an optimized set of m canary images at the same resolution as the dataset. The canaries are initialized as random samples from the training set and are updated in-place over 500 meta-iterations. A key property is transferability: canaries optimized on the small non-private ResNet-9 remain effective when deployed to audit much larger models (Wide ResNet-16-4, TinyViT) trained under DP-SGD at various privacy budgets, and across both DP-training-from-scratch and DP-finetuning regimes.

## Method

The core contribution is a metagradient-based optimization procedure (Algorithm 4) that treats canary images as continuous metaparameters and optimizes them via the REPLAY algorithm (Engstrom et al., 2025). At each of 500 meta-iterations, canaries are randomly split into IN and OUT sets; a non-private ResNet-9 is trained on the base dataset plus the IN canaries; and a surrogate objective measuring the loss gap (low loss on IN canaries minus high loss on OUT canaries) is computed. The metagradient of this objective with respect to the canary pixels is then used to take a gradient step, encouraging canaries to be simultaneously memorizable (when included) and non-generalizable (when excluded). This surrogate bridges the gap between the non-differentiable auditing objective (which involves thresholding and hypothesis testing) and a tractable differentiable target. The resulting canaries are then plugged into standard one-run auditing procedures (Steinke et al. 2023 or Mahloujifar et al. 2024) using negative cross-entropy as the scoring function, yielding empirical ε lower bounds that are several times higher than random or mislabeled canary baselines at moderate-to-high privacy budgets.

## Results

The paper evaluates canary optimization for black-box DP auditing, reporting empirical epsilon (a lower bound on the privacy parameter; higher = tighter bound) across CIFAR-10, CIFAR-10, MNIST, and Fashion-MNIST using Wide ResNet 16-4 models trained with DP-SGD. Using the Mahloujifar et al. (2024) auditing procedure, metagradient canaries achieve empirical ε of 0.732 on CIFAR-10 (vs. 0.405 random, 0.225 mislabeled) and 0.669 on MNIST (vs. 0.016 random) at ε=8, representing 1.8×–41× improvements over baselines. Under DP-finetuning, gains are even larger: 1.207 on CIFAR-10 and 1.483 on Fashion-MNIST at ε=8. The canaries are optimized on a lightweight ResNet-9 with non-private SGD yet transfer effectively to larger Wide ResNets trained with DP-SGD.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against random canaries (Steinke et al., 2023; Mahloujifar et al., 2024), metagradient canaries dominate across all four datasets and both training regimes, with the most dramatic gains on MNIST (0.669 vs. 0.016, a 41× improvement at ε=8) and Fashion-MNIST (0.682 vs. 0.022, 31×). Against random mislabeled canaries, the advantage is similarly large (e.g., 0.736 vs. 0.088 on CIFAR-100 DP training at ε=8). Against ClipBKD (Jagielski et al., 2020), metagradient canaries win in nearly all settings (e.g., 0.732 vs. 0.270 on CIFAR-10 DP training at ε=8 under Mahloujifar's procedure). Against the reviewer-proposed Gradient Max baseline, results are mixed: Gradient Max outperforms metagradient on CIFAR-10 (0.970 vs. 0.732 at ε=8, DP training) but underperforms on MNIST (0.485 vs. 0.669) and Fashion-MNIST (0.453 vs. 0.682), while performing comparably on CIFAR-100. Overall, metagradient canaries consistently tighten empirical privacy lower bounds by 2–4× over standard baselines at moderate privacy budgets (ε=4–8), with gains diminishing at ε=1 due to heavy noise attenuating memorization signals.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of one-run black-box privacy auditing (Steinke et al., 2023; Mahloujifar et al., 2024) and metagradient computation (Engstrom et al., 2025, REPLAY). Prior canary optimization work either relied on SVD-based constructions for multi-run auditing (Jagielski et al., 2020), white-box access to intermediate training states (Nasr et al., 2023), or adversarial sample crafting for federated learning (Maddock et al., 2023). The key departure here is formulating canary design as a differentiable optimization problem via a surrogate objective (memorizability minus generalizability) derived from the membership-inference structure of one-run auditing, then solving it with REPLAY metagradient descent on a lightweight surrogate model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The gap between theoretical DP upper bounds and empirical lower bounds remains a central concern for the DP community, and this paper shows that the seemingly minor design choice of canary selection can tighten empirical bounds by several-fold in many regimes. The transferability result—canaries optimized on a small ResNet-9 with non-private SGD remain effective on Wide ResNets and TinyViTs trained with DP-SGD—makes the approach practically attractive since optimization is a one-time cost per dataset. However, improvements become inconsistent at very low privacy budgets (ε=1.0), and a simpler Gradient Max baseline proposed during review sometimes outperforms the metagradient method, suggesting the surrogate objective may not fully capture what makes canaries effective. Open questions include whether the approach extends to LLM auditing, whether it can be combined with stronger MIA scoring functions, and whether the gap between theoretical and empirical bounds can be further closed.
