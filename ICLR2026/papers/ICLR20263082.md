## Problem

Autoregressive streaming video generation methods (e.g., Self Forcing, CausVid) suffer from severe error accumulation over long horizons because strict frame-by-frame causality forces each frame to inherit and compound errors from its predecessors. Existing mitigations—history corruption compromises temporal consistency, and planning generation violates the sequential emission required for real-time streaming—leave a gap: no method achieves multi-minute streaming video with both minimal drift and sub-second latency on a single GPU.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263082/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt as input and produces a streaming video at 16 fps and 832×480 resolution, emitting one clean frame per forward pass to achieve real-time throughput (15.79 FPS, 0.76 s latency) on a single GPU. The base model is Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B, which natively generates 5-second clips; Rolling Forcing extends this to multi-minute sequences at inference by rolling the denoising window forward indefinitely. The system also supports interactive prompt switching mid-stream by discarding and replacing the cross-attention cache.

## Method

Rolling Forcing introduces three coupled designs. First, a rolling-window joint denoising scheme replaces single-frame causal denoising: T consecutive frames (T=5 after distillation) are denoised simultaneously with progressively increasing noise levels and bidirectional attention within the window, enabling mutual error correction before any frame is finalized. Second, an attention sink mechanism caches the KV states of the first L_glo frames as a persistent global context anchor, with dynamically adjusted RoPE indices to prevent positional overflow, while a separate temporal cache of the most recent L_tem frames preserves short-term consistency. Third, an efficient DMD-based training algorithm computes gradients on only a subset of non-overlapping denoising windows (reducing forward passes from N to ⌈N/T⌉), conditions on self-generated histories to mitigate exposure bias, and alternates with Self Forcing training as a regularizer for natural camera motion—all in just 3,000 steps on 5-second clips without modifying the pretrained architecture.

## Results

Rolling Forcing achieves a quality drift of 0.01 on 30-second videos, dramatically lower than all baselines (next best: Self Forcing at 1.66, MAGI-1 at 2.15, CausVid at 2.18). It also attains the highest scores on Subject Consistency (92.80), Background Consistency (93.71), Aesthetic Quality (62.39), and Imaging Quality (70.75) among all compared methods. In terms of real-time performance, Rolling Forcing reaches 15.79 FPS with 0.76 s latency on a single GPU, marginally faster than CausVid and Self Forcing (15.38 FPS, 0.78 s). On 2-minute videos (Table 3), the advantage widens further: Rolling Forcing scores 91.47 on Subject Consistency vs. 71.95 for Self Forcing, with a quality drift of 0.49 vs. 14.4 for Self Forcing.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Rolling Forcing decisively outperforms all five baselines on the headline metric of quality drift (0.01 vs. 1.66 for Self Forcing, 2.15 for MAGI-1, 2.18 for CausVid, 3.45 for FramePack, and 5.59 for SkyReels-V2), confirming its core contribution of suppressing error accumulation. Among the real-time streaming baselines (CausVid and Self Forcing, both 1.3B and initialized from the same Wan2.1 base), Rolling Forcing improves Subject Consistency by +4.81 over CausVid and +6.32 over Self Forcing, and Imaging Quality by +4.37 and +2.07 respectively, while matching or slightly exceeding their throughput (15.79 vs. 15.38 FPS). Against the non-streaming FramePack (13B, 10× larger), Rolling Forcing wins on drift (0.01 vs. 3.45), Subject Consistency (92.80 vs. 91.65), Aesthetic Quality (62.39 vs. 59.93), and Imaging Quality (70.75 vs. 65.20), though FramePack retains a slight edge on Temporal Flickering (99.26 vs. 97.61) and Motion Smoothness (99.03 vs. 98.70). The FIFO-based MAGI-1 (4.5B) achieves the best Motion Smoothness (99.20) but is 83× slower (0.19 FPS) and still exhibits 215× more drift than Rolling Forcing. SkyReels-V2, which uses history corruption to reduce drift, shows the worst drift among 1.3B models (5.59) and the lowest throughput (0.49 FPS), confirming that corrupting past frames trades temporal consistency for drift reduction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Self Forcing's frame-by-frame autoregressive denoising into a rolling-window joint denoising scheme, where multiple consecutive frames are simultaneously denoised with progressively increasing noise levels and bidirectional attention within the window. It adapts the attention sink mechanism from streaming LLMs (Xiao et al., 2023) to video generation for the first time, using dynamically repositioned RoPE to anchor initial frames as global context. The training algorithm builds on DMD distillation (Yin et al., 2024) but introduces non-overlapping window sampling to make gradient computation feasible over the extended denoising window, while a mixed training strategy with Self Forcing regularizes camera motion. The entire pipeline is instantiated on the open-source Wan2.1-1.3B DiT base model, following the CausVid initialization protocol.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Real-time streaming video generation is a prerequisite for interactive world models, neural game engines, and immersive XR, yet prior methods degrade noticeably beyond a few seconds due to compounding errors. Rolling Forcing's near-zero quality drift (0.01 vs. 1.66 for Self Forcing) over multi-minute sequences, achieved at 16 fps on a single GPU, makes long-horizon streaming practically viable for the first time. Key open questions remain: the model retains no memory of mid-sequence content once frames leave the temporal context, training is computationally demanding (requiring ~400G VRAM without the subset-gradient trick), and the rolling window introduces a small latency increase that may matter in highly interactive settings. The paper suggests mixed inference strategies and more advanced memory mechanisms as promising directions.
