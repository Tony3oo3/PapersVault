## Problem

Multi-encoder MLLMs are increasingly built on the assumption that diverse vision encoders provide complementary visual signals, yet no prior work has systematically quantified whether each encoder actually contributes unique, non-redundant information. This paper addresses the gap by demonstrating that encoder redundancy is pervasive: performance often degrades gracefully or even improves when encoders are removed, and the 'more encoders are better' heuristic is fundamentally challenged.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262929/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis operates on pre-trained multi-encoder MLLMs (Eagle-X5 7B, Eagle-X4 8B Plus, Cambrian-1 3B/8B/13B, DeepSeek-VL 7B, I-MoF 13B, Eagle2 9B) by masking individual encoder outputs with zero tensors of matching shape, then evaluating on 15 benchmarks grouped into four categories (General, Knowledge, OCR & Chart, Vision-Centric) via VLMEvalKit. The primary outputs are per-encoder Conditional Utilization Rate (CUR) scores, Information Gap (IG) values quantifying contribution disparity, and KL-divergence-based attention distributions comparing full-model vs. single-encoder visual token attention in the LLM's final layer.

## Method

The paper introduces a systematic encoder-masking protocol that evaluates all 2^n encoder subsets for each model, replacing masked encoder outputs with zero tensors to measure marginal performance impact. Two model-agnostic metrics are defined: CUR, which computes each encoder's normalized accuracy drop when removed (allowing negative values for detrimental encoders), and IG, the max-minus-min CUR spread capturing how imbalanced encoder contributions are. Attention analysis via KL divergence between full-model and single-encoder visual-token attention distributions in the LLM's final layer provides mechanistic evidence for which encoder dominates the fused representation. Efficiency is quantified through wall-clock training time on 8 A100 GPUs, per-sample inference latency on MME, and per-encoder vision FLOPs decomposition, showing that dual-encoder variants reduce training time by ~34% and inference latency by ~19.5% while retaining over 96% of full-model performance.

## Results

Across all tested multi-encoder MLLMs, performance degrades gracefully rather than catastrophically when encoders are masked: Eagle-X5 7B retains 96% of its five-encoder baseline with only two encoders (EVA-02 + ConvNext), while Cambrian-1 8B actually *improves* by 3.6% overall (63.02 → 65.28) when one encoder is removed. On OCR & Chart, a single encoder dominates with extremely high CUR (EVA-02 reaches 92.89% in Eagle-X4 8B Plus; ConvNext exceeds 70% in Cambrian-1), whereas on General and Knowledge tasks CUR values are near zero, indicating encoders are largely interchangeable. From an efficiency standpoint, the dual-encoder variant of Eagle-X5 7B reduces training time by 34.4% (104.72 → 68.71 A100 GPU-hours) and inference latency by 19.5% (749.7 → 603.2 ms/sample) while retaining over 96% of full-model performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new model but instead quantifies redundancy *within* five representative multi-encoder MLLM families. For **Eagle** (Shi et al., 2024), both Eagle-X5 7B and Eagle-X4 8B Plus show that EVA-02 is the dominant encoder (CUR ≈ 93% on OCR & Chart), and removing two of five encoders costs only ~1–4% overall accuracy while cutting training time by 34%. For **Cambrian-1** (Tong et al., 2024a), the SVA fusion mechanism does not eliminate redundancy: ConvNext dominates (CUR > 70% on OCR & Chart), and a 3-encoder subset actually *outperforms* the full 4-encoder model by 3.6% overall, with SigLIP showing a negative CUR (−16%) on Vision-Centric tasks. For **I-MoF** (Tong et al., 2024b), the two-encoder design (CLIP + DINOv2) already exhibits a large IG of 40.63% overall, with CLIP contributing far more than DINOv2 on General tasks (51.64% IG). For **Eagle2** (Li et al., 2025b), the dual-encoder (SAM + SigLIP) setup shows minimal redundancy (IG = 2.24% overall), suggesting that when only two well-matched encoders are used, the system is more balanced. For **DeepSeek-VL** (Lu et al., 2024a), the two-encoder (SAM + SigLIP) model shows the lowest IG (1.15%) but both encoders are critical—removing either drops OCR & Chart by ~49%, indicating genuine complementarity rather than redundancy. The overarching finding is that redundancy scales with encoder count: models with 4–5 encoders show IG values of 22–70%, while 2-encoder models stay below 41%, confirming that the 'more encoders are better' heuristic breaks down beyond two well-chosen encoders.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new diagnostic ground within the multi-encoder MLLM literature by introducing the first formal, model-agnostic metrics (CUR and IG) to quantify per-encoder contribution and redundancy. It departs from the prevailing design assumption that diverse pretraining objectives yield complementary visual signals, a rationale underpinning architectures like Eagle, Cambrian-1, and DeepSeek-VL. While prior work (MouSi, Eagle) has noted diminishing returns from additional encoders, and token-selection methods (MoVA, MOVE, LEO-mini) implicitly assume encoder complementarity, none had systematically measured whether individual encoders are redundant or detrimental. The authors connect their empirical findings to the Platonic Representation Hypothesis as a theoretical explanation for why encoders trained on overlapping web-scale corpora converge in feature space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that masking encoders can improve overall performance (up to +3.6%) and that a trivial mean-value replacement nearly matches full-model accuracy in the single-encoder setting fundamentally challenges the 'more encoders are better' heuristic that drives current MLLM design. The CUR/IG framework provides actionable, post-hoc diagnostics for practitioners to identify and prune redundant encoders, yielding 34% training-time and 19.5% inference-latency savings with <4% performance loss. Key limitations include the restriction to static (non-dynamically-routed) architectures, the absence of a priori prediction of encoder utility before training, and evaluation limited to four benchmark categories without robustness or OOD analysis. Open questions include whether scaling laws exist for encoder count, how dynamic routing changes the redundancy landscape, and whether the findings generalize to video or 3D modalities.
