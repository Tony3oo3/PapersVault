## Problem

A persistent paradox in continual learning is that neural networks retain linearly separable feature representations of past tasks even when their output classifiers fail, yet the mechanism behind this deep/shallow forgetting asymmetry under experience replay remains unexplained. Specifically, it is unclear why small replay buffers suffice to preserve feature geometry while substantially larger buffers are needed to maintain classifier alignment, and no prior work has provided a unified theoretical account connecting this gap to the geometry of learned representations.

## Input / Output

The framework operates on sequential RGB image classification tasks (CIFAR-100 at 32×32, Tiny-ImageNet at 64×64, CUB200 at 224×224) with a replay buffer of configurable size (0–100% of per-task data), trained via ResNet-18/50 or pre-trained ViT under TIL, CIL, or DIL protocols. It produces two distinct forgetting metrics—shallow (output accuracy drop) and deep (linear-probe accuracy drop on frozen features)—along with closed-form lower bounds on the signal-to-noise ratio governing linear separability, and characterizes the asymptotic feature geometry via NC metrics (within-class variance, ETF angles, head-feature alignment). A key distinction is between observed statistics (computed on the finite buffer) and population statistics (computed on the full class distribution), whose divergence is the central object of analysis.

## Method

The paper extends the Neural Collapse framework to sequential training, proving that forgotten samples behave as out-of-distribution inputs whose feature means project orthogonally onto the active subspace S_t and decay exponentially under weight decay (Theorem 1). It then introduces a mixture model where the asymptotic feature distribution interpolates between an OOD component and an NC component via a buffer-dependent mixing coefficient π, yielding a lower bound on SNR that is non-vanishing for any π > 0 (Theorem 3), formally establishing that minimal replay anchors feature separability. The explanation for the replay efficiency gap is that small buffers induce a 'strong collapse' regime where buffer data projects onto a low-rank subspace S_B ⊂ S, making classifier optimization under-determined: the cost function is invariant to weight changes orthogonal to S_B, producing a manifold of buffer-optimal solutions misaligned with true population boundaries. Two concrete statistical artifacts—rank-deficient buffer covariances (blind to S^⊥ variance) and inflated buffer mean norms due to radial repulsion—are isolated via synthetic LDA counterfactuals, showing that replacing population statistics with buffer estimates reproduces the observed accuracy degradation. The analysis further reveals that multi-head TIL architectures impose a strictly lower rank (n(K−1) vs. nK−1) on the global feature space and exhibit sublinear growth of the anchoring coefficient, explaining why the deep-shallow gap is smaller in TIL than in single-head CIL/DIL.

## Results

The central quantitative finding is the replay efficiency gap: on CIFAR-100 with ResNet-18 and 5% replay, shallow forgetting is roughly 2× deep forgetting in CIL (52.3% vs. 26.2%) and DIL (46.4% vs. 35.1%), while the gap narrows in TIL (18.5% vs. 14.9%). Small buffers (1–5%) are sufficient to keep deep forgetting near zero, but shallow forgetting only converges toward zero near 100% replay. The gap persists across architectures (ResNet, ViT), datasets (CIFAR-100, Tiny-ImageNet, CUB200), and replay methods (ER, DER, FDR, iCaRL), and survives even in a low-dimensional bottleneck regime (d=10) where the ETF structure degrades.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new method to beat existing baselines; instead it characterizes a systematic asymmetry across replay strategies. Across ER, DER, FDR, and iCaRL on CIFAR-100 (Figure 11), the deep–shallow forgetting gap persists for every method, confirming that the phenomenon is intrinsic to replay rather than an artifact of a particular sampling scheme. Increasing the buffer from 1% to 100% gradually closes the gap, but substantial buffer sizes (≥50%) are still required for shallow forgetting to approach zero, while deep forgetting is already near zero at 1–5%. Pre-trained ViT models show negligible deep forgetting even at minimal replay, corroborating prior scale-based findings (Ramasesh et al., 2021), whereas from-scratch ResNets exhibit the full gap. The authors further show via synthetic LDA counterfactuals (Figure 6) that replacing true population statistics with buffer estimates causes additive accuracy drops from both covariance rank deficiency and mean-norm inflation, mechanistically explaining why no replay method can close the gap without explicitly correcting these statistical artifacts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Neural Collapse (NC) framework from stationary single-task training to the sequential setting of continual learning, introducing the multi-head (task-incremental) case that had been absent from NC theory. It builds on the empirical observation that internal representations retain past-task structure recoverable by linear probes (Murata et al., 2020; Hess et al., 2023) but provides the first mechanistic explanation for why deep and shallow forgetting scale differently with replay buffer size. The work also formally bridges continual learning and out-of-distribution detection by showing that forgotten samples behave as OOD inputs under the NC5 orthogonality property (Ammar et al., 2024), a connection not previously drawn in the CL literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges the prevailing assumption that larger replay buffers are the primary lever for reducing forgetting, showing instead that the bottleneck is a statistical artifact (rank-deficient covariances and inflated class means) induced by NC on small buffers. This opens a concrete research direction: explicitly correcting these artifacts could yield robust continual learning with minimal replay, reducing memory and compute costs. Key limitations include the purely asymptotic analysis (transient dynamics are neglected), the simplified mixture model for replay interpolation, and the fact that the proposed correction strategy is suggested but not empirically validated. Open questions include whether NC is net-beneficial or net-detrimental in CL, and how cross-task interactions in multi-head architectures shape long-term representational stability.
