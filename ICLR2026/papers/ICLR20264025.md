## Problem

Standard autoencoders frequently converge to pathological local minima because their encoders are non-injective, causing distant manifold regions to collapse into overlapping latent codes. Existing gradient-based regularizations constrain local geometry but cannot enforce the global topological property of injectivity, while graph-based methods that implicitly enforce injectivity become unreliable under sparse sampling. Additionally, isometric embedding constraints require O(k²) latent dimensions for a k-dimensional manifold, making them impractical for dimensionality reduction.

## Input / Output

The method accepts high-dimensional data in various formats—3D coordinates (Swiss Roll), 28×28 grayscale images (MNIST), 64×64 binary images (dSprites), or 50-dimensional PCA-reduced features (ssREAD)—and produces a 2-dimensional latent embedding alongside a full reconstruction of the input. A precomputed geodesic distance matrix derived from a k-NN neighborhood graph is required as auxiliary input for both the injective and bi-Lipschitz regularization terms, though this is computed once before training and only sliced per mini-batch during optimization.

## Method

BLAE combines two complementary regularization terms with the standard reconstruction loss. First, an injective regularization based on a (δ, ε)-separation criterion penalizes sample pairs whose latent distance ratio falls below ε when their manifold distance exceeds δ, combined with a non-expansive constraint to prevent trivial scaling solutions; this eliminates the pathological local minima caused by non-injective encoders. Second, a bi-Lipschitz regularization constrains the singular values of the decoder's Jacobian to lie within [1/κ, κ], providing a principled relaxation of isometric constraints that is admissible (independent of the sampling distribution) and only requires O(k) latent dimensions rather than O(k²). The bi-Lipschitz term is applied to the decoder rather than the encoder to avoid the dimensional incompatibility of requiring a diffeomorphism when m > n, leveraging the fact that f is κ-bi-Lipschitz if and only if f⁻¹ is κ-bi-Lipschitz.

## Results

BLAE achieves the best average rank across all four evaluation metrics (k-NN recall, KL divergence at three bandwidths, and MSE) on all four datasets (Swiss Roll, dSprites, MNIST uniform/non-uniform, ssREAD). On Swiss Roll, BLAE attains k-NN recall of 0.961 and MSE of 9.36×10⁻⁵, outperforming the next-best method (SPAE) by 22× in MSE and 62× in KL₀.₀₁. On the non-uniform MNIST distribution-shift test, BLAE secures the best KL divergence at all three bandwidths (2.89×10⁻², 1.66×10⁻², 1.19×10⁻³) while maintaining competitive k-NN recall (0.865), demonstrating robustness to sampling density variation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BLAE consistently outperforms all eight baselines on average rank across every metric. Among geometry-based methods, SPAE (Singh & Nag, 2021) is the closest competitor, ranking second on k-NN (3.2 vs. 1.8) but trailing BLAE by 22× in MSE on Swiss Roll (2.10×10⁻³ vs. 9.36×10⁻⁵) and 4.6× in KL₀.₀₁ on dSprites (2.95×10⁻² vs. 6.42×10⁻³). TAE (Moor et al., 2020) ranks second on KL metrics but degrades sharply under sparse sampling (k-NN drops from 0.931 at 3000 samples to 0.681 at 400 samples, while BLAE stays above 0.919). Gradient-based methods (CAE, GAE, IRAE) and the hybrid GGAE (Lim et al., 2024a) suffer from non-injective collapse, ranking 5th–10th on k-NN and failing to unfold the Swiss Roll topology. On the non-uniform MNIST distribution-shift test, BLAE wins on all three KL bandwidths but cedes k-NN (0.865 vs. GRAE's 0.914) and MSE (3.88×10⁻³ vs. GRAE's 3.08×10⁻³) to GRAE (Duque et al., 2022), indicating that graph-embedding methods retain an edge in reconstruction fidelity under extreme sampling imbalance, though BLAE's geometric consistency (concentric-circle structure) is visually superior.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes two previously separate lines of autoencoder regularization—gradient-based (CAE, GAE, IRAE, GGAE) and graph-based (SPAE, TAE, Diffusion Net, GRAE)—by identifying encoder non-injectivity as the common bottleneck that undermines both. It departs from prior gradient-based work (which enforces local isometric or contractive constraints via Jacobian norms) by adding a global topological requirement: a separation criterion that guarantees injectivity and eliminates pathological local minima. The bi-Lipschitz relaxation replaces the isometric constraints of IRAE and GGAE, motivated by the Nash embedding theorem's O(k²) dimension requirement for isometric embeddings versus the O(k) requirement for bi-Lipschitz embeddings, and is grounded in a formal notion of admissibility (distribution-invariance of the regularization's minimizer set).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of non-injectivity as the fundamental optimization bottleneck provides a unifying explanation for why both gradient-based and vanilla autoencoders converge to suboptimal solutions, and the injective regularization offers a drop-in fix that can be combined with existing gradient regularizers (demonstrated with CAE and GAE in ablations). The bi-Lipschitz relaxation is a clean theoretical contribution showing that geometric preservation is achievable with linear (not quadratic) latent dimension scaling while maintaining admissibility under distribution shift. Key limitations include the O(n²) geodesic distance precomputation that restricts scalability to large datasets, and the evaluation is confined to relatively small synthetic and semi-synthetic benchmarks (Swiss Roll, dSprites, single-digit MNIST), leaving open whether the approach transfers to high-dimensional real-world data like ImageNet. The connection to generative modeling (VAE integration in the appendix) suggests broader applicability but remains preliminary.
