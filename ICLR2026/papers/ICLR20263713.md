## Problem

Deploying reasoning LLMs in practice requires choosing both the right model and the right reasoning budget per query, but always using the most expensive configuration wastes cost on easy queries while under-serving hard ones. Prior routing methods use opaque predictors or cascades that do not natively handle reasoning budgets, and none formulate the routing decision as a principled multi-objective optimization over a Pareto performance-cost front.

## Input / Output

RADAR takes a text query and a user-specified scalar tradeoff weight w1 in (0,1) balancing performance versus cost. It produces a single model configuration assignment (a specific RLM paired with a specific reasoning budget) in approximately 7 ms, before the RLM ingests the query, avoiding mid-generation model switching or KV-cache recomputation. The query is consumed via a frozen embedding model that feeds a lightweight linear projection to produce scalar difficulty and discrimination parameters, while cost is predicted heuristically from per-token pricing and average token counts across training queries.

## Method

RADAR discretizes each RLM by its available reasoning budgets to form a pool of model configurations, then jointly estimates interpretable scalar query difficulties and configuration abilities using a two-parameter logistic (2PL) item response theory model trained on binary correctness responses. Query difficulty and discrimination are obtained as linear projections of a frozen query embedding, enabling out-of-distribution generalization without retraining. Routing is formulated as a two-dimensional multi-objective optimization (maximize predicted correctness, minimize predicted cost) solved via Chebyshev scalarization, which recovers both convex and concave points on the Pareto front unlike linear scalarization. New RLM configurations are integrated through adaptive testing: a Fisher-information-guided iterative query selection procedure estimates each new configuration's scalar ability from only about 12% of the training set, enabling plug-and-play scalability.

## Results

On the hypervolume metric (higher is better) across 8 in-distribution reasoning benchmarks, RADAR achieves the best score on 6 of 8, with headline results of 0.7513 on GPQA-Diamond, 0.9230 on MMLU-Redux, and 0.8762 on FRAMES. On the CPT(90%) metric (lower is better, fraction of o4-mini-high cost to match 90% of its performance), RADAR achieves 1.31% on MATH-500, 13.21% on GPQA-Diamond, and 13.11% on FRAMES, dramatically outperforming baselines on the harder benchmarks. RADAR's routing latency overhead is only ~6.9 ms per query, negligible compared to the ~870 ms generation time of the smallest model configuration.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RADAR is compared against two state-of-the-art routing baselines: RouterBench (Hu et al., 2024), a learned multi-LLM routing system, and IRT-Router (Song et al., 2025), a concurrent IRT-based routing method. On the hypervolume metric, RADAR outperforms both baselines on 6 of 8 benchmarks, with the largest margin on GPQA-Diamond (0.7513 vs. 0.6942 for IRT-Router, a ~8% relative gain) and FRAMES (0.8762 vs. 0.8501). On the CPT(90%) metric, the advantage is even more pronounced on harder tasks: RADAR requires only 13.21% of o4-mini-high cost on GPQA-Diamond versus 53.99% for IRT-Router, and 13.11% on FRAMES versus 31.53%. RADAR slightly underperforms IRT-Router on AIME (0.7760 vs. 0.7766 hypervolume) and MATH-500 (0.9449 vs. 0.9528), where the performance-cost tradeoff is less differentiated across configurations. The paper attributes RADAR's overall advantage to its Chebyshev scalarization (which recovers concave Pareto front points that linear scalarization cannot) and its scalar-valued ability parameterization, which requires fewer parameters and yields interpretable model ordering.

## Relation to Prior Work

RADAR builds on three distinct research threads: (1) item response theory from psychometrics, specifically the two-parameter logistic (2PL) model, which it adapts to jointly estimate query difficulties and model-budget configuration abilities; (2) multi-objective optimization, where it is the first to formulate LLM routing as an MOO solvable via Chebyshev scalarization (going beyond the linear scalarization used by prior routers like RouterBench and IRT-Router); and (3) computerized adaptive testing from educational assessment, repurposed to estimate the ability of new RLM configurations from a small set of dynamically selected queries. The key departure from concurrent IRT-Router (Song et al., 2025) is the use of scalar-valued abilities (vs. non-interpretable MIRT vectors), the MOO formulation enabling non-convex Pareto front recovery, and the adaptive-testing-based model integration replacing IRT-Router's ChatGPT-profile embedding approach.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RADAR offers a practically deployable routing framework with ~7ms latency overhead, black-box compatibility, and plug-and-play model integration, making it directly applicable to production RLM serving where cost control is critical. The strong OOD generalization to long-context multi-document QA (FRAMES) despite training on shorter queries suggests the embedding-based difficulty parameterization captures transferable structure. Open questions include whether the heuristic cost prediction (averaged token counts) holds under variable-length generation, how the framework extends to batch-level budget constraints, and whether the 2PL monotonicity assumption always holds for reasoning models that can 'over-think' on simple queries. The text-only, single-query scope leaves multi-modal reasoning and tool-use routing as natural next steps.
