## Problem

As TTS systems approach human-level quality, existing objective metrics (MOS predictors, signal-based measures, single-embedding distributional scores) fail to consistently correlate with human judgments across diverse speech domains such as noisy, conversational, and children's speech. No public multilingual TTS benchmark exists, and subjective listening tests are resource-intensive and not comparable across works. The paper asks whether a single objective metric can robustly rank TTS systems across domains and languages without domain-specific tuning.

## Input / Output

TTSDS2 consumes a set of synthetic utterances (50–100 samples suffice) and a matching set of real reference utterances from the same speakers, plus a fixed set of noise distributions (uniform, Gaussian, all-ones, all-zeros) used as domain-agnostic anchors. It produces a normalized score in [0, 100] per perceptual factor (Generic, Speaker, Prosody, Intelligibility) and an unweighted overall TTSDS2 score, where values above 50 indicate the synthetic distribution is closer to real speech than to noise. Unlike intrusive metrics, the reference need not contain matching lexical content, and unlike FAD-style metrics, no thousands of samples are required.

## Method

TTSDS2 frames TTS evaluation as distributional similarity: for each of four perceptually-motivated factors, it extracts multiple feature representations (e.g., WavLM/HuBERT/wav2vec2.0 activations for Generic; d-Vector/WeSpeaker embeddings for Speaker; WORLD F0 and speaking-rate for Prosody; ASR final-layer activations for Intelligibility) and computes the 2-Wasserstein distance between the synthetic and real feature distributions, as well as between the synthetic distribution and each of four noise distributions. A normalized score is then 100 × W₂^NOISE / (W₂^REAL + W₂^NOISE), anchoring the scale so that 0 means indistinguishable from noise and 100 means identical to real speech. Factor scores are averaged within each factor and then combined via an unweighted arithmetic mean across factors; an ablation with leave-one-out cross-validation shows this simple mean outperforms learned factor weights in three of four held-out domains, acting as a regularizer against overfitting. For multilingual use, HuBERT and wav2vec2.0 are replaced with mHuBERT-147 and XLSR-53, and the entire pipeline is automated to periodically scrape new YouTube data, preventing training-data contamination across evaluation rounds.

## Results

TTSDS2 achieves an average Spearman correlation of 0.67 with subjective ratings (MOS, CMOS, SMOS) across four speech domains (Clean, Noisy, Wild, Kids), and is the only one of 16 compared objective metrics to maintain ρ > 0.50 in all 12 domain×metric conditions. The original TTSDS averages ~0.60, representing a 10% relative improvement. Speaker similarity metrics (RawNet3, X-Vector) achieve comparable averages (~0.6) but fail to exceed 0.5 in several conditions (e.g., RawNet3 drops to 0.26 on Clean CMOS), while all MOS-prediction networks, signal-based metrics, and distributional baselines fall below 0.3 average correlation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TTSDS2 outperforms all 16 compared metrics in consistency: it is the sole metric maintaining Spearman ρ > 0.50 across all 12 domain×subjective-score conditions, with an average of 0.67 versus ~0.60 for the original TTSDS (a 10% relative gain). Speaker similarity metrics (RawNet3, X-Vector) achieve comparable averages (~0.6) and even surpass TTSDS2 on individual cells (e.g., RawNet3 reaches 0.85 on Wild MOS), but they collapse below 0.5 on Clean CMOS (0.26, 0.42) and Noisy CMOS (0.37, 0.29), making them unreliable as standalone evaluators. SQUIM MOS is the best-performing MOS-prediction network (avg ~0.57) but still fails on Clean SMOS (0.37) and Kids SMOS (0.45). All other MOS-prediction networks (UTMOS, UTMOSv2, NISQA, DNSMOS), distributional metrics (FAD-CLAP), multi-dimensional perceptual metrics (Audiobox Aesthetics), and legacy signal-based metrics (PESQ, STOI, MCD) show average correlations below 0.3, with several exhibiting negative correlations in out-of-domain settings (e.g., NISQA reaches −0.64 on Wild SMOS; MCD reaches −0.74 on Noisy SMOS). The ablation in Table 4 further confirms that the unweighted mean of TTSDS2 factors generalises better than learned weights in 3 of 4 leave-one-out domain scenarios, validating the design as a fully unsupervised metric.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends TTSDS (Minixhofer et al., SLT 2024), the original distributional TTS metric that compared feature distributions against noise anchors using Wasserstein distances. The key methodological departures are: (1) replacing WER-based intelligibility features with ASR model activations, (2) substituting HuBERT token-length prosody features with utterance-level speaking rates from HuBERT and Allosaurus, (3) adding WavLM to the generic factor for diversity, and (4) swapping to multilingual models (mHuBERT-147, XLSR-53) for cross-lingual use. Conceptually, it sits within the distributional-metric family inspired by FID, but distinguishes itself from FAD by using multiple perceptual factors and noise (rather than alternate speech) as the reference anchor, and from MOS-prediction networks by being fully unsupervised and reference-free in the matching sense.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first public, recurring, multilingual TTS benchmark (14 languages, 20 systems, 11,000+ human ratings) with an automated pipeline that prevents data contamination by continuously regenerating test data from recent YouTube content. The finding that TTSDS2 is the only one of 16 metrics to maintain Spearman ρ > 0.5 across all 12 domain×score conditions provides practitioners with a reliable objective proxy for subjective evaluation in a regime where many systems are at or near human parity. However, the correlation ceiling of ~0.8 indicates a residual component of human perception that no current objective metric captures, and the metric's CPU-bound Wasserstein computations (~9.4 min per score) limit its use in fast iteration loops. Open questions include whether the gap to 1.0 is inherent test noise or a missing perceptual dimension, and whether the approach generalises beyond the 14 languages currently covered.
