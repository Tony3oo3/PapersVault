## Problem

LLM-based automated formalization of mathematical theorems into Lean 4 suffers from two persistent failure modes: model hallucination (fabricating undefined Mathlib identifiers, misusing symbols, producing version-incompatible code) and semantic gap from conceptual polymorphism (identical natural language terms mapping to different formal definitions across abstraction levels). While retrieval-augmented generation has proven effective in general NLP, directly applying RAG to mathematical formalization is non-trivial because Mathlib lacks structured NL-to-formal mappings, concepts are inherently polymorphic, and retrieval must achieve both semantic relevance and symbolic precision simultaneously.

## Input / Output

The system takes a natural language theorem description as input and produces a Lean 4 formal statement as output, which must both compile under the Lean 4.20.0-rc5 compiler with Mathlib4 and pass a back-translation semantic consistency check. A key design detail is that the input may be either explicit (containing recognizable mathematical concept keywords) or implicit (e.g., combinatorial word problems that require an LLM-driven rewriting step to surface the underlying mathematical structure before concept extraction can proceed). CRAMF operates as a plug-and-play retrieval module: it injects Top-3 retrieved formal definitions into the prompt of any existing autoformalizer without modifying the model itself.

## Method

CRAMF comprises three components. First, an automated pipeline constructs a concept-definition knowledge base by parsing Mathlib4 via doc-gen4, back-translating each formal definition into natural language using InternLM-Math-7B with self-consistency validation, extracting the underlying mathematical concept via DeepSeek-V3, and encoding both the concept description and definition annotation with MathBERT into a Faiss vector index (26,000+ definitions, 1,000+ concepts). Second, Mathematical Concept Extraction (MCE) identifies core concepts from the input theorem, conditionally triggering an LLM-based problem rewriting step for implicit-concept problems. Third, Dual-Pathway Hybrid Retrieval (DHR) runs two parallel pathways—symbol-level keyword matching via regex against Mathlib identifiers and semantic similarity search over the MathBERT-encoded knowledge base—then applies bge-reranker-v2-m3 to rerank candidates, selecting the Top-3 definitions whose annotations best match the query's conceptual interpretation. The query itself is augmented by concatenating the extracted concept with an LLM-generated terminological interpretation that encodes domain and application context, which is the key mechanism for disambiguating polymorphic concepts.

## Results

CRAMF is a plug-and-play retrieval augmentation that improves every evaluated autoformalization model across all three datasets. On Formalization Accuracy Rate@10 (FAR@10), the largest gains appear for general-purpose LLMs: DeepSeek-V3 improves from 19.8% to 32.1% on AdvancedMath (+62.1% relative) and from 23.6% to 37.0% on ProofNet (+56.8%). The paper reports an average relative improvement of 29.9% in FAR@10 across all model-dataset combinations, with Compilation Pass Rate@10 also improving by up to 52.1% (DeepSeek-V3 on AdvancedMath). For retrieval quality, CRAMF achieves an Average Contribution Score of 2.07–2.14 and HitRate@3 of 44.2%–50.6%, outperforming the best retrieval baseline (LeanSearch) by 0.5+ ACS points and 7–8 percentage points in HitRate@3.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CRAMF is evaluated as an augmentation layer on top of seven autoformalization models (DeepSeek-V3, GPT-4o, Gemini 2.5 Flash, Claude 4.1, Herald-7B, Kimina-7B, Godel-V2-8B) and consistently improves every one of them on all three datasets, with the largest relative gains (52–62%) for general-purpose LLMs lacking domain-specific fine-tuning. For retrieval, CRAMF outperforms all six baselines: BM25 (ACS 0.83–0.94), R3 (1.31–1.45), HyDE (1.52–1.61), LeanExplore (1.35–1.51), LeanSearch (1.56–1.63), and Dependency Retrieval (1.26–1.47), achieving ACS of 1.93–2.14 and HitRate@3 of 42.9%–50.6% versus LeanSearch's 35.1%–42.3%. The paper notes that LeanSearch and LeanExplore, while tailored for Lean, primarily return theorems rather than definitions and struggle with polymorphic concepts, whereas DR's dependency-graph approach is constrained by surface-level syntactic structure. Statistical significance is confirmed via Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (all p < 0.01) across every model-dataset pair.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts retrieval-augmented generation from general NLP to the specialized domain of mathematical autoformalization, a task previously addressed primarily through fine-tuning on NL-FL pairs (Herald, Kimina-Prover) or few-shot prompting. It departs from existing Lean-specific retrieval tools (LeanSearch, LeanExplore, Dependency Retrieval) by constructing a structured concept-definition knowledge base from Mathlib4 and introducing a dual-pathway hybrid retrieval strategy that combines symbol-level exact matching with MathBERT-based semantic similarity, followed by reranking. The conceptual framing around 'conceptual polymorphism' as the core semantic gap—where the same natural language term maps to different formal definitions depending on abstraction level—is a more precise diagnosis than prior work's general 'hallucination' framing, and the automated knowledge base construction pipeline (back-translation via InternLM-Math-7B + concept extraction via DeepSeek-V3) is a novel engineering contribution that makes the approach scalable to 26,000+ definitions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Autoformalization is a critical bottleneck in end-to-end automated theorem proving (as demonstrated by AlphaProof's IMO performance), and CRAMF's plug-and-play design means it can immediately enhance any existing Lean 4 autoformalizer without retraining. The introduction of the AdvancedMath benchmark (173 problems from a standard Chinese university textbook) fills a gap between the relatively simple miniF2F/ProofNet and truly research-level mathematics, though its scope is limited to a single textbook. Key open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond Lean 4/Mathlib4 to other proof assistants (Coq, Isabelle), whether the static knowledge base can be updated incrementally as Mathlib evolves, and whether the single-step definition retrieval can be extended to multi-step reasoning chains where intermediate lemmas also require grounding.
