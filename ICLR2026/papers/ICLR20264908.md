## Problem

Existing vectorization methods for persistence diagrams (persistence images, landscapes, splines, sliced Wasserstein kernels) either sacrifice geometric fidelity for linearity or lack provable inverse-continuity, meaning that two diagrams can map to nearly identical vectors while remaining far apart in Wasserstein geometry. No prior functional representation simultaneously guarantees Lipschitz stability with respect to the 1-Wasserstein distance and a continuous inverse on its image, a property that would make the representation suitable for loss design and faithful unsupervised analysis.

## Input / Output

The method takes a persistence diagram (a finite multiset of (birth, death) points with integer multiplicities) and a stable weighting function ω_K^α: R² → (0,1] parameterized by K > 0 and α ≥ 1, and produces a scalar function φ: S² → R. In practice, this function is discretized via a spherical harmonics expansion on a Driscoll-Healy grid with 2N_θ latitudinal and 4N_θ longitudinal nodes, yielding a finite-dimensional vector of N_θ²/2 orthonormal coefficients. Unlike persistence images, the domain S² is fixed and compact, so no data-dependent bounding box or pixel-size selection is required.

## Method

Each point p = (x,y) in the diagram is lifted to the segment ω(p)·[0, (1,x,y)] in R³; the persistence sphere is the support function of the Minkowski sum of all such segments, restricted to S². By linearity of support functions, this reduces to the explicit formula φ(v) = Σ_p ω(p)·a_p·ReLU(⟨v, (1,p)⟩), making computation a simple sum of rectified linear units evaluated on the sphere. The key theoretical contribution is proving that this map is Lipschitz continuous with respect to the 1-Wasserstein distance (Theorem 2) and that its inverse is continuous on its image (Theorem 3), establishing bi-continuity (Corollary 1). The weighting ω_K^α = (2/π)arctan(λ(p)^α/K^α) with λ(p) = (y−x)/(2‖(1,p)‖₂) is shown to satisfy the required stability conditions, and its parameters K and α provide interpretable control over noise suppression near the diagonal. For downstream use, the spherical function is expanded in spherical harmonics via pyshtools, producing an orthonormal feature vector compatible with standard scikit-learn pipelines.

## Results

Across 10 case studies spanning clustering, regression, and classification, persistence spheres (PS) achieve the best or statistically tied-best performance in 7 out of 10 tasks. In unsupervised clustering (Rand index), PS leads all baselines at every noise level (e.g., 0.845 vs. 0.786 for PI at σ=10; 0.688 vs. 0.621 for PI at σ=30). In supervised classification, PS attains the highest accuracy on Growth (85.0%), NOx (86.9%), POWER (76.9%), and Human Poses (64.0%), while in regression it reaches R²=0.966 on Eyeglasses and 0.969 on Tecator, within the confidence interval of the best methods (SWK 0.971, PSpl 0.970).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PS dominates all baselines in the unsupervised clustering setting, outperforming PI by 5–9 points, PL by 10–15 points, PSpl by 17–33 points, and SWK by 8–11 points in Rand index across three noise levels. In supervised classification, PS wins outright on Growth (85.0% vs. 80.7% PSpl), NOx (86.9% vs. 84.0% SWK), POWER (76.9% vs. 75.6% PL), and Human Poses (64.0% vs. 53.0% PI), while PL edges out PS on DYN_SYS (84.0% vs. 82.9%), ENZYMES_JACC (37.7% vs. 34.9%), and McGill 3D Shapes (67.8% vs. 54.4%), and PSpl leads on SHREC14 (94.9% vs. 93.1%). In regression, PS is statistically tied with the best method on both Eyeglasses (0.966 vs. SWK 0.971) and Tecator (0.969 vs. PSpl 0.970), while PersLay underperforms dramatically (R²=0.248 on Eyeglasses), likely due to small sample sizes. PI is consistently the weakest baseline in clustering and classification, and PSpl, despite strong supervised performance, collapses in unsupervised settings (Rand 0.52–0.56 vs. PS 0.69–0.85). The sliced Wasserstein kernel (SWK) is the closest overall competitor to PS, achieving near-identical Wasserstein-geometry fidelity (correlation 0.9989 vs. 0.9993 in the unsupervised simulation) but trailing PS in most downstream tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the lift-zonoid-based functional representation of persistence diagrams introduced by Gotovac Dogaš & Mandarić (2025), but identifies and corrects a critical flaw: their weighting ω(p)=y-x is not stable, as demonstrated by an explicit counterexample (Remark 2) where W₁→0 but the Hausdorff distance of the corresponding zonoids diverges. By introducing 'stable lift weightings' (notably the arctan-based ω_K^α) and 'effective weightings' that control behavior at infinity, the paper recovers both Lipschitz continuity of the forward map and continuity of the inverse, yielding a bi-continuous representation. The construction also draws on classical convex geometry (support functions, Minkowski sums, Hausdorff distance) and the convergence theory of lift zonoids from Koshevoy & Mosler (1998) and Hendrych & Nagy (2022), while the explicit ReLU-sum formula (Eq. 3) connects the representation to the building blocks of PersLay-style neural architectures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Bi-continuity is a rare and practically significant property among PD representations: it guarantees that functional similarity always reflects diagram-level similarity, making persistence spheres a natural candidate for designing loss functions on PDs where convergence in function space implies convergence in Wasserstein geometry. The paper notes that a full bi-Lipschitz embedding of the Wasserstein space into a Hilbert space is impossible (Carrière & Bauer, 2019), so the bi-continuity result is a meaningful but necessarily weaker guarantee. Open questions include reconstruction of PDs from their spherical-harmonics coefficients, extension to signed measures for bi-parameter persistence, and integration into differentiable topological layers. A practical limitation is that the bi-continuity guarantee holds only for α=1; larger α values improve noise robustness empirically but forfeit the theoretical guarantee.
