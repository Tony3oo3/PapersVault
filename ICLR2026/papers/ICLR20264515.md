## Problem

Existing prediction-powered inference (PPI) methods assume either a single predictor or a fixed set of predictors queried jointly, but in practice one faces multiple autoraters with heterogeneous cost-performance profiles. The open question is how to optimally decide which subsets of models to query, how many times to query each, and how to combine the resulting measurements to minimize estimation variance under a hard budget constraint—especially when costs are non-additive (e.g., cascading test-time compute).

## Input / Output

The method consumes N fully-labeled samples (where all k variables are jointly observed), a total budget B, and a cost vector c_I for every queryable subset I ⊆ {1,…,k}, which may encode non-additive or cascading cost structures. It outputs a point estimate θ̂ of the target linear functional aᵀE[X], an asymptotic (1−α) confidence interval, and the learned allocation n_I (sample counts per subset) and weights λ_I (linear combination coefficients). Notably, the N burn-in samples are reused in the final estimator, and the cost structure permits arbitrary non-additive pricing across subsets.

## Method

MultiPPI formulates the optimal budget allocation and estimator-weight selection as a single convex program: a second-order cone program (SOCP) under a single budget constraint, or a semidefinite program (SDP) under multiple constraints. The key theoretical insight (Theorem 2) is that the minimax optimal estimator over *all* budget-satisfying estimators (not just linear or unbiased ones) is achieved by a linear unbiased estimator whose variance equals a^⊤(Σ n_I Σ_I^†)^† a, making the allocation problem a tractable convex optimization over the covariance structure. In practice, Σ is estimated via Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage from the N burn-in samples, and Theorem 4 bounds the finite-sample MSE inflation by O(‖Σ̂−Σ‖_F / γ_min). The resulting estimator is a weighted sum of subset sample means, where weights λ_I are derived from the optimal dual variables of the SOCP/SDP, and the allocation automatically shifts from cheap proxies to expensive high-quality models as B grows.

## Results

Across all three LLM evaluation experiments (Chatbot Arena win-rate estimation, ProcessBench process-error detection, and biography factuality via multi-autorater debate), MultiPPI consistently achieves lower mean-squared error and narrower 95% confidence intervals than all baselines across the full budget range (0 to 2k cost units, normalized so 1 unit = one query to the most expensive model). The improvement is budget-adaptive: in the low-budget regime MultiPPI effectively emulates scalar PPI++ with the cheapest model, while in the high-budget regime it transitions to emulating vector PPI++ or a cascaded PPI structure. Results are reported as fractions of classical sampling's MSE and CI width (lower is better), with 250 fully-labeled samples and 500k random trials per configuration.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares MultiPPI against classical sampling, scalar PPI++ (Angelopoulos et al., 2023b) applied to each individual autorater, and vector PPI++ (Miao et al., 2024) using all models jointly. MultiPPI outperforms every baseline in MSE and CI width in all three experiments and all budget regimes. In Experiment 1 (Chatbot Arena), scalar PPI++ with Gemini 2.5 Flash dominates in the low-budget regime while vector PPI++ dominates in the high-budget regime, but MultiPPI beats both throughout; notably, PPI++ with Gemini 2.5 Pro is never on the Pareto frontier since its correlation with the label is no better than Flash's yet it is strictly more expensive. In Experiment 2 (ProcessBench), vector PPI++ only outperforms other baselines at the highest budgets, while MultiPPI transitions from emulating PPI++ with the tiny (125-word) model to a cascaded PPI structure where the medium model debiases the large model. In Experiment 3 (factuality), all baselines are dominant in certain budget sub-regimes, but MultiPPI again wins everywhere; a minor coverage decay (~1% under-coverage) appears in the large-budget regime for all methods except MultiPPI and vector PPI++, which the authors attribute to finite-sample bias from reusing labeled data for covariance estimation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Prediction-Powered Inference (PPI) and PPI++ framework of Angelopoulos et al. (2023a,b) from a single (or fixed-set) predictor to multiple predictors with flexible, non-additive cost structures. While Miao et al. (2024) already considered multiple predictors, they require all to be queried jointly; MultiPPI instead allows arbitrary subsets to be sampled at different frequencies, formulating the allocation as a second-order cone program (single budget) or semidefinite program (multiple budgets). The method also draws on classical control variates, semiparametric inference (AIPW, DML), and budgeted regression, but distinguishes itself by targeting population-level linear functionals under hard budget constraints rather than input-conditional policies or regression accuracy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MultiPPI provides a principled, computationally tractable framework for cost-optimal LLM evaluation when multiple autoraters of varying quality and cost are available—a setting that is increasingly common in practice. The minimax optimality guarantee (when covariance is known) and finite-sample stability bounds (when it is estimated) give strong theoretical grounding, while the budget-adaptive allocation automatically shifts from cheap to expensive predictors as resources grow. Open questions include: the restricted (linear-in-k) formulation that avoids the exponential subset space lacks a formal variance penalty guarantee; the coverage decay phenomenon at large budgets with fixed labeled samples needs further understanding; and scalability to very large autorater ensembles remains untested.
