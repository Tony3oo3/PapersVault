## Problem

Scientific discovery is bottlenecked by the slow, manual development of computational tools for analyzing complex experimental data. Existing LLM-based agents either perform analyses using pre-existing methods or develop general-purpose ML algorithms, but none effectively integrate the unstructured, domain-specific scientific knowledge needed to design and optimize new computational methods for particular scientific tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261701/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

TusoAI takes a concise task description (e.g., 'single-cell RNA-seq imputation'), a dataset, a scalar evaluation function h(·), and optionally an initial solution s_init (enabling warm-start from a state-of-the-art method). It operates on a single function within an arbitrarily large codebase, running within a wall-clock time budget (default 8 hours) with per-implementation time limits (10 minutes) and bounded bug-fix attempts. The output is executable Python code representing the best-scoring solution s* in the explored pool, optimized to maximize h(·) on a validation set.

## Method

TusoAI builds a two-level knowledge tree from retrieved paper summaries: top-level categories of optimization strategies (e.g., 'noise modeling', 'model architectures') each containing specific actionable instructions, plus a predefined diagnostic category. During iterative optimization, it selects diverse top solutions via code-text similarity clustering, then improves them through either instruction-based optimization (80%: sample a category via a Bayesian-updated categorical distribution, draw instructions, and refine the solution) or diagnostic-based optimization (20%: run the model, collect diagnostic logs, and improve based on observed data patterns). Category probabilities π_c are updated multiplicatively (×1.1) when an instruction from that category yields improvement, balancing exploration and exploitation. Per-category feedback summaries from prior optimizations are injected into subsequent prompts to discourage redundant changes, and the draft-then-refine construction of categories and instructions ensures they reflect established scientific best practices rather than relying solely on LLM priors.

## Results

On 6 single-cell analysis tasks (OpenProblems benchmark), TusoAI achieves an average score of 0.66 with an average rank of 1.2, outperforming the best expert method (0.57, rank 3.7), AIDE (0.52, rank 3.0), Biomni (0.49, rank 3.7), and ChatGPT-Agent (0.57, rank 3.5). On 5 scientific deep learning tasks (NASBench-360), TusoAI achieves an average score of 0.70 with an average rank of 2.8, surpassing DARTS (0.71, rank 4.0), the expert baseline (0.69, rank 4.6), Claude-4-Sonnet (0.68, rank 4.6), GPT-5 (0.67, rank 5.8), and AIDE (0.59, rank 9.8). All TusoAI-generated methods are computationally efficient, running in under 3 minutes for single-cell tasks and under 8 minutes for deep learning tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TusoAI consistently outperforms all agentic baselines across both benchmark suites. On single-cell tasks, TusoAI (avg 0.66, rank 1.2) beats AIDE (0.52, rank 3.0) by 0.14 in average score, Biomni (0.49, rank 3.7) by 0.17, and ChatGPT-Agent (0.57, rank 3.5) by 0.09; it also surpasses the top expert method (0.57, rank 3.7). On deep learning tasks, TusoAI (avg 0.70, rank 2.8) outperforms AIDE (0.59, rank 9.8) by a large margin of 0.11, and beats Claude-4-Sonnet (0.68) and GPT-5 (0.67) by 0.02–0.03. The only baseline that edges out TusoAI on average score is DARTS (0.71 vs. 0.70), but TusoAI achieves a substantially better average rank (2.8 vs. 4.0), indicating more consistent performance across tasks. TusoAI also generates substantially more diverse code than AIDE throughout optimization, and its methods are novel rather than re-implementations of existing approaches (e.g., a custom NMF with dropout and Poisson noise modeling for denoising, distinct from ALRA and MAGIC).

## Relation to Prior Work

TusoAI carves a distinct niche between two existing lines of LLM-agent work: scientific analysis agents (Biomni, Stella, ChemCrow) that execute pipelines of existing tools, and general MLE agents (AIDE, DS-Agent, MLE-STAR) that design new algorithms but ignore domain-specific scientific knowledge. It departs from AIDE most directly by introducing a structured knowledge tree built from retrieved literature, Bayesian category sampling to balance exploration and exploitation, and a diagnostic-based optimization loop that mimics a scientist inspecting intermediate model outputs. Classical AutoML and NAS frameworks (DARTS, AMBER, auto-sklearn) are positioned as insufficient because their predefined search spaces cannot accommodate the unstructured, evolving nature of scientific method development.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that agentic optimization can produce novel, interpretable computational methods that outperform expert-designed algorithms across 11 scientific benchmarks, and—more compellingly—that applying TusoAI to two genetics problems (scDRS, pgBoost) uncovered disease-cell and variant-gene links missed by the original methods, suggesting a practical path toward accelerating method development in biology. Key limitations include the requirement for a fast validation experiment, the constraint that the target method be concentrated in a single function, reliance on existing (potentially flawed) evaluation metrics, and heuristic components lacking theoretical justification. Open questions include whether the approach extends to multi-function codebases, whether it can search over data-space (selecting which data to include) in addition to code-space, and whether the Bayesian category update strategy has a principled convergence guarantee.
