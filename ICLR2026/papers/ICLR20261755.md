## Problem

Multi-objective RL (MORL) must learn a single policy that adapts to unknown user preferences at test time, while reward-free RL (RFRL) learns optimal policies for any reward function without reward signals during training. Although MORL under linear scalarization is a special case of RFRL, no prior work had adapted RFRL methods to MORL, leaving open whether RFRL's broader policy learning could serve as auxiliary tasks to improve MORL's sample efficiency and generalization.

## Input / Output

The method consumes environment state vectors (e.g., 17-dim for Halfcheetah2d, 376-dim for Humanoid2d) and a d-dimensional preference vector λ from the unit simplex. At test time, given a user-specified λ, the system computes a latent vector z_R = E[B_ω(s,a) λ^T R(s,a)] from the learned backward network and retrieves the optimal policy via argmax_a F_θ(s,a,z_R)^T z_R, requiring no additional environment interaction. The latent dimension d_z (300 in experiments) is decoupled from the number of objectives d, enabling zero-shot transfer across tasks with different objective counts.

## Method

MORL-FB adapts the Forward-Backward (FB) RFRL architecture—where Q(s,a,z) = F_θ(s,a,z)^T z decomposes the successor measure into forward and backward networks—into the MORL setting with three key enhancements. First, Preference-Guided Exploration (PG-Explore) replaces the original FB's z ~ N(0,I) sampling with z constructed via mini-batch sampling from the replay buffer: z = Σ B_ω(s,a) r^T λ / n_s, directing exploration toward states relevant to the preference-weighted reward. Second, the mini-batch construction of z_λ̂ yields diverse latent vectors per preference, providing auxiliary training signals beyond the single z_λ. Third, an auxiliary Q loss uses the actual scalarized reward λ^T r (available in MORL but absent in RFRL) as the TD target, replacing the pseudo-reward B^T E[BB^T]^{-1} z used in vanilla FB. The full training uses a TD3-based actor-critic loop with measure loss, auxiliary Q loss, orthonormality regularization, and delayed policy updates.

## Results

On the MO-Gymnasium continuous control suite (3M environment steps, 5 seeds), MORL-FB achieves the best or near-best Utility (UT) and Hypervolume (HV) across all six MuJoCo tasks (Halfcheetah2d, Walker2d, Hopper3d, Ant3d, Humanoid2d, Humanoid5d). The most dramatic gain is on Humanoid2d, where MORL-FB reaches UT 8.13×10² versus 2.93×10² for PD-MORL (a ~2.8× improvement) and HV 1.75×10⁷ versus 1.06×10⁷. In the aggregated IQM metric (Figure 3), MORL-FB leads all baselines by a large margin, and the Episodic Dominance score ED(ALG, MORL-FB) is consistently below 0.5 for every baseline, confirming that MORL-FB outperforms each method under the majority of preference vectors.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MORL-FB outperforms all ten baselines on the majority of tasks and metrics. Against the strongest single-policy baselines, PD-MORL and Q-Pensieve, MORL-FB wins on UT in 5 of 6 MuJoCo tasks and on HV in 5 of 6, with the largest margins on Humanoid2d (UT 8.13 vs 2.93) and Halfcheetah2d (UT 7.69 vs 5.62); PD-MORL retains a slight edge on Walker2d HV (5.45 vs 4.32) and Ant3d UT (3.59 vs 3.43). Both PD-MORL and Q-Pensieve degrade noticeably when the number of objectives exceeds two (e.g., Ant3d, Humanoid5d), whereas MORL-FB maintains consistent performance. Against multi-policy methods (PG-MORL, SFOLS, MORL/D, GPI-LS, GPI-PD) and other single-policy methods (CAPQL, EQL, PCN), MORL-FB achieves higher UT and HV across all tested tasks, with ED scores well below 0.5 indicating dominance under most preferences. The vanilla FB (original reward-free baseline) is the weakest comparison: ED(FB, MORL-FB) is nearly zero on every task, confirming that the preference-guided exploration and auxiliary Q loss are essential for MORL performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper identifies a previously unexplored conceptual bridge between reward-free RL (RFRL) and multi-objective RL (MORL), two fields that evolved independently despite both addressing unknown user preferences at test time. It builds directly on the Forward-Backward (FB) representation framework of Touati et al. (2023), which learns a low-rank factorization of the successor measure to recover optimal policies for arbitrary reward functions, and adapts it to MORL by treating preference-weighted rewards as the test-time reward signal. The key departure from both the original FB and existing MORL methods (PD-MORL, Q-Pensieve, CAPQL) is the introduction of preference-guided exploration with mini-batch sampling and an auxiliary Q loss that uses actual observed reward vectors rather than the pseudo-rewards required by the reward-free setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic adaptation of RFRL to MORL, and the zero-shot cross-objective transfer capability (training on Hopper2d and evaluating on Hopper3d/4d without retraining) is a practically valuable property that existing MORL methods lack. The finding that MORL-FB generalizes well even when trained on only a small set of preference vectors (one-hot and uniform) while PD-MORL and Q-Pensieve degrade substantially highlights the benefit of decoupling environment knowledge from reward information. A key limitation inherited from FB-based RFRL is the need for more advanced exploration in complex or sparse-reward environments, and the authors note that exploring RFRL methods beyond FB (e.g., successor measure representation learning, distance-preserving state representations) is an important open direction.
