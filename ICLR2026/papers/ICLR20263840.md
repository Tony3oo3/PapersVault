## Problem

Modern transformer-based text embeddings lack the interpretable geometric structure of early word embeddings, and the Linear Representation Hypothesis has only been validated monolingually. No principled framework exists for mapping discourse-level semantic-syntactic transformations (negation, conditionality, politeness) to consistent geometric operations that generalize across typologically diverse languages and different embedding model architectures.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263840/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

RISE takes a unit-normalized sentence embedding (768–3072 dimensional, residing on a hypersphere) and a learned prototype vector in the tangent space at the canonical reference direction e₁, and produces a transformed sentence embedding on the same hypersphere. The prototype is learned from 1,000 neutral–transformed sentence pairs per language-transformation combination, generated via GPT-4.5 with feature-controlled prompts. The method operates entirely on the manifold via Riemannian logarithmic and exponential maps, treating geodesic arcs as the natural 'lines' in embedding space rather than Euclidean displacements.

## Method

RISE represents semantic-syntactic transformations as consistent rotational operations on the unit hypersphere through a three-step procedure: (1) canonicalization, where a rotor R(n) (implemented as a Householder reflection or Givens rotation in O(d) time) maps each neutral embedding to a fixed reference direction e₁; (2) prototype learning, where all canonicalized tangent vectors (computed via the Riemannian logarithmic map) are averaged into a single prototype vector p in the tangent space at e₁; (3) prediction, where the prototype is rotated into the tangent space at an unseen embedding via R(n*)ᵀp and mapped back to the sphere via the Riemannian exponential map. The key distinction from Euclidean baselines (mean difference vectors, Procrustes alignment) is that RISE respects the curved manifold geometry, using geodesics as the natural generalization of straight lines. The method exhibits commutativity to first order for sequential transformations, providing algebraic evidence for the linear representation hypothesis in curved spaces. RISE is evaluated across seven morphologically diverse languages, three embedding model architectures (text-embedding-3-large, bge-m3, mBERT), and three datasets, with comparisons against MDV and Procrustes baselines.

## Results

RISE achieves a mean rotor alignment score of 0.788 for negation (range 0.686–0.918), 0.780 for conditionality (lowest variability, σ=0.038), and 0.762 for politeness (highest variability, σ=0.060) across seven languages and three embedding models. On English syntactic acceptability (BLiMP), all models score 0.93–0.96, while semantic relatedness (SICK) drops to 0.62–0.74. Cross-language transfer maintains 77%–95% of monolingual performance, with bge-m3 (1024-dim) showing the least variance and text-embedding-3-large (3072-dim) the highest absolute scores.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RISE is compared against two baselines: Mean Difference Vectors (MDV), a spherical mean-displacement method, and Procrustes alignment, a single global rotation. On monolingual syntactic tasks (BLiMP), all three methods perform comparably (0.97–0.99). On monolingual semantic tasks (SICK), RISE (0.84) and MDV (0.83) substantially outperform Procrustes (0.67), confirming that a single rigid rotation cannot capture context-dependent semantic shifts. The critical differentiator is cross-language transfer: RISE (0.74–0.89) and MDV (0.72–0.91) maintain moderate-to-strong consistency, while Procrustes collapses to 0.25–0.62 (failing-to-weak), demonstrating that per-sentence rotor canonicalization is essential for cross-lingual generalization. RISE edges out MDV on SICK (0.84 vs. 0.83) and shows a tighter cross-language range, though MDV occasionally reaches slightly higher peaks (0.91 vs. 0.89). In a downstream negation classification task, RISE achieves 93.0% accuracy and 93.0% F1, outperforming MDV (87.2% accuracy, 87.8% F1) by roughly 6 points.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Linear Representation Hypothesis (Park et al., 2024) from Euclidean linear operations to Riemannian geodesic operations on the unit hypersphere where modern sentence embeddings reside. While prior steering-vector and probing work (Turner et al., 2023; Zou et al., 2023; Hewitt & Manning, 2019) operates in linear activation space, RISE reframes semantic-syntactic transformations as per-point rotational operations using Riemannian log/exp maps and rotor-based canonicalization. The paper also departs from word-level analogy work (Mikolov et al., 2013) by targeting discourse-level transformations (negation, conditionality, politeness) and explicitly extending the analysis to multilingual settings across seven typologically diverse languages, a gap the authors identify as unaddressed in the LRH literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first empirical evidence that discourse-level semantic-syntactic transformations correspond to consistent geometric operations in multilingual embedding spaces, offering a principled framework for interpretability that goes beyond task-specific probes. However, the findings are qualified: only three transformations are tested, the English-centric bias (20% cross-model transfer gap) reveals that geometric structure is not yet universal across languages, and the reliance on GPT-4.5-generated data introduces potential conceptual biases. Open questions include whether the geometric consistency extends to more pragmatic phenomena, whether it holds across additional architectures, and whether the observed structure reflects universal semantic properties or training-data artifacts.
