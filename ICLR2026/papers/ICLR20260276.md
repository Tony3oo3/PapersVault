## Problem

Mixture-of-Experts LLMs route tokens through sparse subsets of specialized FFN experts, yet prior analyses of expert specialization (domain, vocabulary, syntax) treat routing patterns as descriptive rather than actionable. No existing method leverages the MoE router as a controllable interface to modulate model behavior at inference time without modifying weights or requiring additional training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260276/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The detection phase consumes paired contrastive examples (e.g., a document-grounded question vs. the same question without context, or a safe refusal vs. an unsafe completion) to compute per-expert activation statistics. At inference time, the method takes a standard text prompt and produces steered generation by clamping router log-softmax scores for a small set of identified experts (typically 5–500 out of thousands), leaving all other experts and model weights untouched. The intervention is applied uniformly across all tokens and layers where the target experts reside, with a small margin ε (e.g., 10⁻²) ensuring the steered experts are favored without collapsing the mixture to a single path.

## Method

SteerMoE identifies behavior-linked experts via a Risk Difference (RD) score: for each expert in each layer, it computes the difference in activation frequency between two paired prompt sets exhibiting contrasting behaviors (e.g., safe vs. unsafe), then ranks experts by |RD| to select those most associated with the target behavior. At inference, the router's raw logits are first mapped to log-softmax scores for scale normalization; experts to be activated have their scores set to the per-token maximum plus ε, while experts to be deactivated are set to the per-token minimum minus ε, after which a standard softmax re-normalizes the modified scores. This 'soft' clamping preserves the multi-expert weighted-average structure, avoiding the degenerate case where a single expert receives all probability mass. The approach is entirely weight-preserving and requires no fine-tuning, special tokens, or per-input gradient optimization, making it orthogonal to and stackable with existing jailbreak or alignment techniques.

## Results

SteerMoE improves RAG faithfulness by up to +27% (e.g., on FaithEval-Counterfactual and MQuake) while preserving general QA accuracy on the MCTest control. On safety, activating safety-associated experts raises safe response rates by up to +20% across TDC2023, MaliciousInstruct, AdvBench, and StrongREJECT without increasing over-refusal on the Harmless benchmark. Conversely, deactivating safety experts drops safety by −41% alone, and combining SteerMoE with the AIM jailbreak template drives GPT-OSS-120B safety from 100% to 0% (−100%), and Phi-3.5-MoE from 100% to 0%. Fluency scores (Gemma-3-27B-it, 5-point Likert) remain stable across all steering conditions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SteerMoE alone (90% safe on GPT-OSS-120B, 94% on Phi-3.5) is competitive with the strongest single jailbreak methods—GCG, FFA, and AIM all achieve 100% safe on GPT-OSS-120B, meaning SteerMoE alone already outperforms them by 10 percentage points. The decisive advantage appears in combination: SteerMoE+AIM drops GPT-OSS-120B to 0% safe (vs. 100% for AIM alone) and Phi-3.5 to 0% (vs. 96% for AIM alone), while SteerMoE+FFA reaches 18% on GPT-OSS-120B (vs. 100% for FFA alone). On Qwen3, AIM alone already achieves 2% safe, so SteerMoE+AIM (2%) offers no additional gain, and SteerMoE alone (60%) is outperformed by FFA (48%) and AIM (2%). ArtPrompt is the weakest baseline across most models (58% on GPT-OSS-120B, 40% on Phi-3.5) and is never the best-performing attack. Overall, SteerMoE is the only method that stacks orthogonally with prompt-based jailbreaks to achieve near-complete safety bypass on the most robust models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM steering literature (LM-Steers, SAE-based representation engineering) to the MoE setting by treating the router as a controllable behavioral interface rather than merely a compute-distribution mechanism. It builds most directly on RICE (Wang et al., 2025b), which amplifies two 'cognitive experts' for math/science reasoning, but departs in three key ways: it supports both activation and deactivation, removes the dependency on special tokens like <think>, and targets broader behavioral traits (safety, faithfulness) rather than task-specific reasoning effort. Prior MoE interpretability work (OLMoE, Mixtral, Qwen3 analyses) identified domain and vocabulary specialization in experts but stopped short of treating routing patterns as an actionable control surface; SteerMoE operationalizes this by using risk-difference scoring on paired contrastive examples to identify behavior-linked experts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals a novel 'Alignment Faking' vulnerability specific to MoE architectures: safety alignment is concentrated in a sparse subset of experts, and modest routing perturbations can catastrophically bypass all guardrails (100% → 0% safety on GPT-OSS-120B when combined with AIM). This challenges the assumption that post-training alignment robustly covers all internal pathways and argues that safety must extend across the entire routing topology, not just the default activation pattern. Open questions include whether dynamic, token-aware steering can improve precision, how to design alignment procedures that harden all expert pathways, and whether analogous vulnerabilities exist in dense models with different sparsity mechanisms. The technique is lightweight (no weight modification, no retraining) and generalizes across six MoE architectures, making it a practical tool for both stress-testing and test-time alignment.
