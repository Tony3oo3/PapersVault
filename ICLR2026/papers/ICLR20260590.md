## Problem

Causal Bayesian Optimisation (CaBO) leverages known causal graph structure to reduce the intervention search space but ignores contextual variables, while Contextual Bayesian Optimisation (CoBO) conditions interventions on observed context but is locked to a fixed policy scope. The paper proves that neglecting either component can yield provably linear regret, and no existing method jointly optimises over both the set of variables to intervene on and the set of context variables to condition on.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260590/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a known causal DAG over a set of variables, a designated target variable Y, a subset of intervenable variables X*, and at each step a context vector c_t sampled i.i.d. from an unknown distribution determined by the selected scope. The output at each step is a mixed policy scope (a collection of pairs (X; C_X) specifying which variables to intervene on and which context variables to condition on) together with a concrete intervention mapping from the observed context to intervention values. Domains can be continuous or discrete, and the POMPS candidate set is computed once via brute-force search over the DAG with complexity exponential in |V|.

## Method

CoCa-BO decomposes the problem into three coupled components: (1) a one-time computation of all Possibly-Optimal Mixed Policy Scopes (POMPSs) from the causal graph, (2) a multi-armed bandit (UCB) layer that selects which POMPS to deploy at each step using running averages of UCB acquisition values plus an exploration bonus, and (3) a per-scope HEBO Gaussian-process optimiser that chooses the intervention value given the observed context. The key design choice is using running-average UCBs rather than instantaneous acquisition-function values for scope selection, because in contextual settings the optimal acquisition value depends on the random context, making direct cross-scope comparisons invalid and causing instability. Theoretically, the paper derives the first high-probability regret bounds for both CoCa-BO and CaBO, showing worst-case O(√(mT)·polylog(T)) and instance-dependent O(√T·polylog(T)) cumulative regret under standard GP smoothness assumptions, where m is the number of POMPSs.

## Results

Across all experiments (110 seeds, 700 iterations), CoCa-BO achieves sublinear time-normalised cumulative regret in every configuration where the optimum is reachable, while both CaBO and CoBO exhibit linear regret in their respective failure configurations. In the high-dimensional environment with 57 contextual and 29 intervenable variables, CoCa-BO reduces the optimisation domain dimensionality by a factor of 22 compared to CoBO, yielding faster convergence. CoCa-BO remains robust to noise in the target variable, converging for noise standard deviations up to σ = 1.25, though convergence becomes unclear at extreme noise (σ = 6.25). The worst-case regret bound scales as √(mT)·maxᵢ d̄ᵢ up to polylog factors, and the instance-dependent bound improves to √T·maxᵢ d̄ᵢ when the optimal arm is unique.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CaBO (Aglietti et al., 2020b), CoCa-BO wins decisively in Configuration II where the optimal policy requires conditioning interventions on contextual variables (Age, BMI): CaBO marginalises context and incurs linear cumulative regret, while CoCa-BO converges to the optimum with sublinear regret. In Configuration I where CaBO can theoretically reach the optimum, CoCa-BO still achieves smaller regret due to more stable scope selection (CaBO's causal acquisition function causes highly variable POMIS choices). Against CoBO (Krause & Ong, 2011), CoCa-BO wins in Configuration II where the causal structure makes joint intervention on both X₁ and X₂ suboptimal: CoBO's fixed scope (controlling both variables) yields zero expected reward and linear regret, while CoCa-BO identifies the correct scope (X₁; C) and converges. In Configuration I where both methods can reach the optimum, CoBO actually converges faster than CoCa-BO, but CoCa-BO's advantage emerges in high-dimensional settings where the causal graph prunes redundant variables, reducing sample complexity substantially.

## Relation to Prior Work

CoCa-BO unifies two previously separate lines of work: Causal Bayesian Optimisation (CaBO, Aglietti et al. 2020), which uses a known causal graph and a causal acquisition function to select intervention sets, and Contextual Bayesian Optimisation (CoBO, Krause & Ong 2011), which conditions interventions on observed context but fixes the policy scope. The paper builds directly on the POMPS framework of Lee & Bareinboim (2020) for characterising possibly-optimal mixed policy scopes, and extends the GP-UCB / maximum-information-gain regret analysis of Srinivas et al. (2010) to a multi-function, multi-arm setting. The key departure from CaBO is that its causal acquisition function becomes invalid in contextual settings because it compares acquisition values evaluated at different contexts; the key departure from CoBO is that a fixed scope can provably yield linear regret, motivating a joint search over both scope and policy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first high-probability regret bounds for both CoCa-BO and, as a corollary, for CaBO itself, filling a notable theoretical gap in the causal-optimisation literature. Practically, the framework reduces sample complexity in high-dimensional environments by using the causal graph to identify smaller optimisation domains (a 22× dimensionality reduction is demonstrated), and it converges to the optimum in settings where both CaBO and CoBO provably fail. Open questions include the exponential cost of enumerating POMPSs in |V|, the assumption that the causal graph is known (causal discovery is explicitly out of scope), and whether information can be shared across POMPSs to further reduce sample complexity—a problem the authors identify but leave unresolved.
