## Problem

Transformers have become the dominant architecture across modalities, raising the question of whether they are uniquely optimal or merely a convenient default. The paper asks two specific questions: (Q1) are standard transformers a local optimum in architecture space for a given task, and (Q2) how compatible are the inductive biases required by different tasks? This matters because current progress relies on scaling rather than architectural innovation, and transformers demonstrably fail on elementary algorithmic tasks while excelling at language.

## Input / Output

The method takes a GPT-2-style decoder-only transformer (2–4 layers, widths 32–512) and a dataset of token sequences (algorithmic tasks with 1e5 examples, or language datasets like TinyStories, Shakespeare, ENWIK8, CodeSearchNet, FineWeb). It outputs a set of learned spline keypoints (122 points over [-20, +20] for algorithmic tasks, 122 points over [-20, +20] with 1/3 spacing for language) that replace GeLUs and/or softmaxes as frozen non-linearities. These keypoints can then be plugged into any standard training run on any dataset, making the optimized architecture directly comparable to the baseline.

## Method

The core mechanism replaces the two key non-linearities in a transformer—GeLUs in MLP layers and softmax in attention—with 1D linear splines parametrized by learnable keypoints, chosen because splines impose the least prior on the space of R→R functions. Stage I optimizes these splines via a two-loss scheme: model weights are trained on the main training split while the spline parameters are updated on a held-out split (or an OOD split for length generalization), preventing co-adaptation. Multi-model training (M=3–8 parallel models with different seeds sharing the same splines) further decouples architecture from any particular weight configuration. Stage II freezes the optimized splines and retrains the model from scratch on the target dataset using standard SGD, yielding a directly comparable architecture. The approach is distinguished from prior trainable-activation work by its meta-learning objective (finding a fixed inductive bias reusable across seeds and datasets) rather than continuously adapting activations during training.

## Results

On algorithmic tasks, optimized architectures converge 2–3× faster (e.g., ADD, MANO), eliminate high seed-to-seed variance, and achieve perfect test accuracy where the baseline fails (e.g., MANO). On TinyStories (2 layers, width 256), the optimized MLP spline reaches 64.4% validation accuracy and 1.57 training perplexity, versus 63.7% / 1.58 for the GeLU baseline. At larger scale on FineWeb (12 layers), the optimized spline achieves a validation loss of 3.68, outperforming both GeLU (3.72) and ReLU (3.72). Cross-task compatibility analysis reveals that algorithmic-task architectures are highly task-specific (diagonal dominates), while language/code architectures transfer well across datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed optimized spline MLP outperforms all eight compared baselines on TinyStories validation accuracy (64.4%). The closest competitors are GeLU, GLU/Swish (Shazeer), ReLU² (So/PRIMER), Adaptive softmax (Veličković), and NormSoftmax (Jiang), all at 63.7%, a 0.7-point gap. The polynomial attention variants P1 and P3 (Saratchandran) score 63.0% and 63.7% respectively, while the Sinc activation (Saratchandran) and Gaussian kernel (Saragadam/WIRE) lag further behind at 47.7% and 62.8%. On the COPY length-generalization task, the optimized Alibi architecture (Press et al.) extends accuracy to longer sequences beyond what Alibi alone achieves, though it does not fully solve the problem. On algorithmic tasks, the optimized architectures dramatically beat the standard transformer baseline (e.g., 2–3× faster convergence on ADD/MANO, elimination of seed variance on HAYSTACK), but cross-task transfer is poor—architectures optimized for one algorithmic task often underperform the standard transformer on another. On language and code datasets, improvements are smaller (0.1–0.5 perplexity points) and transfer well across datasets, indicating standard transformers are closer to a local optimum for these domains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Teney et al. (2025), which used trainable non-linearities to question whether the simplicity bias of standard MLPs is always desirable, by generalizing the approach to full transformer architectures (both MLP and attention layers) and introducing a tractable two-stage optimization with multi-model training to prevent weight-architecture co-adaptation. It departs from prior learnable-activation work (Alexandridis et al. 2025, Apicella et al. 2021) in goal: rather than continuously updating activations during training, it seeks fixed inductive biases that can be hard-encoded and reused across seeds and datasets. The method also differs from neural architecture search by using gradient descent on a continuous spline parametrization rather than discrete search over predefined components, and it positions itself as a diagnostic tool for studying cross-task compatibility of inductive biases rather than a recipe for production models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that standard transformers are rarely a local optimum in architecture space, and that algorithmic tasks require inductive biases fundamentally different from those of language modeling, challenges the assumption that a single architecture can be near-optimal across all capabilities. The cross-task compatibility analysis provides concrete evidence that the inductive biases tuned for arithmetic or sequence manipulation do not transfer to language, and vice versa, suggesting that future architectures may need to explicitly support multiple, potentially conflicting, inductive biases. Key limitations include the small experimental scale relative to modern LLMs, a restricted search space (cannot represent GLUs or complex attention patterns), and the computational overhead of spline evaluation (mitigated by polynomial approximation). Open questions include whether multi-task optimization of non-linearities can yield architectures that simultaneously support language fluency and robust algorithmic reasoning, and whether the observed effects persist at web-scale training.
