## Problem

Existing adversarial attacks and defenses treat video Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models as monolithic architectures, overlooking the independent vulnerabilities of the router and expert modules as well as their collaborative weaknesses. This paper asks: what component-level weaknesses do adversarial attacks expose in video MoE, and how can targeted adversarial training defend against both the independent and the collaborative ("Achilles' Heel") vulnerabilities?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20265077/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a video clip x ∈ ℝ^{C×T×H×W} (e.g., 3-channel RGB, T frames) and produces either an adversarial perturbation δ constrained within an L∞-norm budget ε (for the attack) or a retrained MoE model with enhanced component-wise and overall robustness (for the defense). The MoE architecture consists of a lightweight Top-1 router that assigns each clip to one of four expert sub-networks (3D ResNet-18, TSM, SlowFast, or R(2+1)D), and the final prediction is the weighted combination of expert outputs. The attack operates in a white-box setting with full access to router and expert gradients.

## Method

The core contribution is a family of Temporal Lipschitz-Guided Attacks (TLGA) that maximize a differentiable Lipschitz loss L_Lip = ‖g(x+δ)−g(x)‖²/‖δ‖² to amplify model sensitivity, combined with a temporal adaptive step-size that allocates perturbation budget across frames proportional to per-frame gradient norms (V_{t+1} = β·V_t + ‖∇_x ℓ(t)‖₂). TLGA-R specifically steers the router toward the lowest-confidence (weakest) expert, while J-TLGA jointly perturbs both the router and the full MoE output, exploiting the inter-component dependency to achieve far greater destruction than either component-level attack alone. For defense, J-TLAT performs hierarchical adversarial training in three steps per epoch: (1) train the router for output consistency under perturbation, (2) identify weak experts via TLGA-R and adversarially train them, and (3) adversarially train the full MoE to harden the collaborative interface. This plug-and-play framework reduces inference cost by over 60% relative to dense models while consistently improving robust accuracy across UCF-101 and HMDB-51.

## Results

On UCF-101 with 3D ResNet-18 experts, J-TLAT achieves 54.29% clean accuracy (comparable to the dense AT-D baseline at 54.51%) while using only 1.831 GFLOPs versus 4.790 for AT-D, a >60% inference cost reduction. Under the proposed joint attack J-TLGA (ε=8/255), J-TLAT retains 33.96% robust accuracy, dramatically outperforming AAT-M (0.00%), OUD-M (2.64%), and AT-M (6.15%). Under standard PGD (ε=8/255), J-TLAT reaches 36.37% versus 24.84% for AT-D and 19.23% for AT-M. The Lipschitz constant under TLGA-R drops from 261.8 (AT-M) and 953.3 (AAT-M) to just 0.823 for J-TLAT, confirming substantially reduced model sensitivity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against standard attacks, J-TLAT dominates all baselines: under PGD (ε=8/255) it achieves 36.37% vs. 24.84% (AT-D, Madry et al.), 23.08% (AAT-M, Kinfu & Vidal), and 19.23% (AT-M); under AutoAttack (Croce & Hein) it reaches 34.29% vs. 23.30% (AT-D) and 22.20% (AAT-M); under FGSM (Goodfellow et al.) it scores 38.68% vs. 28.57% (AT-D) and 26.92% (AAT-M). The most striking gap appears under the proposed component-targeted attacks: J-TLAT retains 51.87% under TLGA-R and 33.96% under J-TLGA, while AAT-M collapses to 0.00% and OUD-M (Lo et al.) to 2.64% under J-TLGA, confirming that conventional defenses are blind to the collaborative router–expert weakness. In the regularization ablation, J-TLAT outperforms Neural Regularization (Tramèr et al.) and other divergence-based strategies (JS, COS, KL) across all three attack settings. J-TLAT also matches or exceeds the dense AT-D model on clean accuracy (54.29% vs. 54.51%) while cutting GFLOPs by 62% (1.831 vs. 4.790), and its Lipschitz constant under J-TLGA (2.343) is roughly 500× lower than AT-M (596.0), evidencing a fundamentally smoother decision boundary.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior video adversarial work (Wei et al. 2019/2020/2023, Kinfu & Vidal 2022) that treats the model as a monolithic black box, and from image-domain MoE robustness studies (Zhang et al. 2023, 2025; Han et al. 2024) that decompose router/expert robustness but never design attacks exploiting their interaction. It repurposes Lipschitz-regularity concepts (Pauli et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2022) and temporal adaptive step-size ideas from video attacks (Wei et al. 2022) into a unified component-level attack framework (TLGA, J-TLGA) and a corresponding hierarchical defense (J-TLAT) that has no direct predecessor in either the video or the MoE robustness literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As MoE architectures proliferate in video understanding (VideoLLM-MoD, 3D-MoE), the finding that coordinated router-expert manipulation can collapse robust accuracy to near zero (2.54%, and 0% for AAT-M) exposes a security gap that whole-model adversarial training leaves unaddressed. The plug-and-play J-TLAT defense, which reduces inference cost by over 60% relative to dense baselines while improving robust accuracy by ~34% under the strongest joint attack, offers a practical path for deploying MoE in safety-critical video applications. Open questions include whether these findings generalize to transformer-based video MoE with hundreds of experts, to larger benchmarks (Kinetics-400/600), and to adaptive or multi-step black-box settings where the attacker does not have full gradient access.
