## Problem

Existing training-based hallucination detectors (e.g., SAPLMA, SEP) achieve strong in-domain performance but degrade sharply when the test domain differs from the training domain, because they rely on domain-specific features. This paper formalizes Generalizable Hallucination Detection (GHD)—training a detector on a single domain while maintaining robust performance across diverse related domains—and identifies a previously overlooked cross-domain-invariant signal: the instability of LLM responses in induced multi-turn continuations.

## Input / Output

The method takes a question and a single LLM-generated answer as input, but does not score the answer in isolation. Instead, it induces a simulated multi-turn continuation dialogue (default K=20 turns) by feeding contextually coherent follow-up prompts back to the model, producing a per-turn score sequence from a trained backbone (e.g., SAPLMA). The output is a single SpikeScore scalar—the maximum absolute second-order difference along that sequence—which is thresholded to yield a binary hallucination label. The entire pipeline is post-hoc: it imposes no constraints on how the initial answer was generated, making it compatible with RAG and other structured pipelines.

## Method

The key insight is that hallucination-initiated multi-turn dialogues universally exhibit sharper uncertainty fluctuations (spikes) than factual ones, because erroneous initial responses trigger more dramatic self-correction and stance-reversal behaviors. Concretely, the method (1) induces a K-turn continuation dialogue from the initial answer using a weak-to-strong prompt schedule, (2) scores each turn with a lightweight MLP probe (SAPLMA or SEP) trained on a single source domain, and (3) computes SpikeScore as the maximum absolute second-order difference of the resulting score sequence—a discrete curvature operator that acts as a high-pass filter, suppressing slow drifts shared by both classes while highlighting sharp, localized confidence reversals. A threshold on SpikeScore yields the binary prediction. The paper further proves (Theorem 1) that under empirically validated conditions on the mean ratio (>2) and coefficient of variation (≤0.2) of SpikeScore across domains, the probability that a hallucinated sample's SpikeScore exceeds a factual sample's is at least 0.775, providing a distribution-free separability guarantee via Cantelli's inequality.

## Results

SpikeScore achieves the highest mean AUROC across all four LLMs (Llama-3.2-3B, Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-14B) on both RAG benchmarks (TriviaQA and RAGTruth), with peak performance of 0.8697 AUROC (Qwen3-14B on TriviaQA) and 0.8535 (Qwen3-14B on RAGTruth). In the main cross-domain leave-one-out evaluation across six datasets, SpikeScore consistently outperforms all baselines, with the gap over the strongest competitor (ICR Probe) reaching approximately 8–10 AUROC points on larger models. Performance improves with model scale within each family, and SpikeScore combined with training-based backbones (SAPLMA, SEP) substantially outperforms training-free backbones (Perplexity, Reasoning Score, In-Context Sharpness).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SpikeScore outperforms all 11 baselines across every model and dataset configuration. Among training-free methods, the strongest are EigenScore (up to 0.7102 on Llama-3.1-8B TriviaQA) and Semantic Entropy (up to 0.6796 on Qwen3-14B RAGTruth), both trailing SpikeScore by 8–20 AUROC points. Traditional training-based methods (SAPLMA, SEP, MM) degrade severely in cross-domain settings, with SEP dropping below 0.55 AUROC on several configurations—near random—while SpikeScore maintains 0.76–0.87. The closest competitors are the cross-domain-specialized methods: ICR Probe reaches 0.7841 (Qwen3-14B TriviaQA) and PRISM is competitive but still below SpikeScore by 5–10 points. Notably, SpikeScore's advantage grows with model scale (e.g., 2.0-point gap over ICR Probe on Llama-3.2-3B vs. 8.6-point gap on Qwen3-14B), suggesting larger models produce more detectable instability patterns. The paper also shows that SpikeScore is backbone-agnostic: combining it with any scoring method (SAPLMA, SEP, Perplexity, Reasoning Score, ICS) yields effective detection, though training-based backbones consistently outperform training-free ones.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper introduces a new hallucination detection method (SpikeScore) that departs from the single-turn scoring paradigm of prior training-based methods (SAPLMA, SEP). While SAPLMA and SEP evaluate the reliability of a single answer via a probe on internal activations, SpikeScore instead tracks the trajectory of uncertainty scores across a multi-turn dialogue induced after the initial answer, and extracts a second-order difference (maximum curvature) from this trajectory. The key insight is that hallucination-initiated dialogues exhibit larger uncertainty fluctuations than factual ones, a phenomenon that is domain-invariant because it arises from the LLM's self-correction mechanism rather than from domain-specific features. The paper provides both a theoretical lower bound (Theorem 1) and empirical validation across six benchmarks and four LLMs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: existing training-based methods (SAPLMA, SEP, PRISM, ICR Probe) suffer from poor cross-domain generalization because they rely on domain-specific features learned during training. SpikeScore addresses this by exploiting a domain-invariant signal — the self-contradiction phenomenon in multi-turn dialogues — which occurs regardless of the domain of the initial question. Limitations: the current lower bound (0.775) is not very high; the coefficient-of-variation condition (≤ 0.2) needs to be verified more tightly. Open questions: (1) Can the number of dialogue turns K be reduced below 20 without losing separability? (2) Does the phenomenon hold for LLMs other than Llama-3.1-8B? (3) Can the theoretical bound be improved by a more refined analysis of the score trajectory?
