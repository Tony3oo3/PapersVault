## Problem

Existing text-to-image evaluation models compress image quality into a single scalar score, making it impossible to disentangle distinct failure modes (e.g., poor prompt adherence vs. unrealistic artifacts) or to localize where in the image the problems occur. This lack of spatially grounded, multi-dimensional feedback limits both interpretability and the usefulness of these evaluators as reward signals for reinforcement learning–based T2I training, where fine-grained, region-aware supervision is needed to fix localized flaws.

## Input / Output

ImageDoctor takes a natural-language text prompt and a generated RGB image (resized to 512×512 during training; 1024×1024 for test-time scaling) as input. It produces four scalar quality scores (semantic alignment, aesthetics, plausibility, overall), two pixel-level heatmaps of shape H×W (artifact and misalignment), and a structured reasoning trace that follows a 'look-think-predict' format: bounding-box localization of flaw regions, per-dimension textual analysis, and final score conclusions. A fast inference mode appends a fixed reasoning template to the prompt, allowing the model to skip explicit reasoning and directly emit scores and heatmaps for use as a reward function.

## Method

ImageDoctor is built on a Qwen2.5-VL-3B MLLM backbone augmented with a lightweight heatmap decoder inspired by the SAM mask decoder, which uses bi-directional cross-attention between learned task tokens (<ART>, <MIS>) and image features to produce spatially grounded heatmaps. The core 'look-think-predict' paradigm instructs the model to first predict bounding boxes around potential flaw regions, then generate structured reasoning linking localized visual evidence to prompt semantics, and finally emit quantitative scores. Training proceeds in two phases: a cold-start stage of supervised fine-tuning (first on direct score prediction, then on chain-of-thought data generated by Gemini 2.5 Flash), followed by reinforcement fine-tuning with GRPO using three verifiable rewards—grounding (bounding-box completeness, compactness, uniqueness), score accuracy (ℓ₁ distance to human scores), and heatmap fidelity (ℓ₂ distance to annotated heatmaps). The paper further introduces DenseFlow-GRPO, which reformulates the Flow-GRPO objective to support pixel-wise advantage computation by decomposing the likelihood ratio at each denoising step and multiplying the image-level reward by (1 − pixel-level heatmap reward), enabling region-aware supervision that penalizes low-quality local areas more heavily than high-quality ones.

## Results

On RichHF-18K, ImageDoctor achieves an average PLCC of 0.741 and SRCC of 0.724 across four evaluation dimensions (plausibility, aesthetics, semantic alignment, overall), substantially outperforming the previous best method RichHF (0.586/0.582). The largest gain is in semantic alignment, where ImageDoctor reaches PLCC 0.808 versus RichHF's 0.474. For heatmap prediction, ImageDoctor also leads on all metrics (e.g., artifact CC 0.571 vs. 0.556; misalignment KLD 2.863 vs. 2.933). When used as a reward in DenseFlow-GRPO, ImageDoctor drives ImageReward from 0.818 (base) to 1.100, a 10% improvement over the best scalar-based reward (PickScore at 1.002).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ImageDoctor outperforms all nine baselines across every evaluation dimension on RichHF-18K. The closest competitor, RichHF (Liang et al., 2024), is surpassed by a large margin: average PLCC 0.741 vs. 0.586, with the gap most pronounced in semantic alignment (0.808 vs. 0.474). CLIP (Radford et al., 2021) and ResNet-50 (He et al., 2016), when fine-tuned on the same data, achieve only 0.374 and 0.328 average PLCC respectively, confirming that scalar-only architectures struggle with multi-dimensional evaluation. PickScore (Kirstain et al., 2023) performs poorly on this multi-aspect benchmark (0.172 avg PLCC), as it was designed for single-score preference. On the generalization benchmarks, ImageDoctor (GenAI-Bench PLCC 0.514; TIFA PLCC 0.740) edges out EvalMuse (0.498; 0.712) and VQAScore (0.499; 0.659), while HPSv2 (Wu et al., 2023) and HPSv3 (Ma et al., 2025) trail significantly (0.139; 0.139 on GenAI-Bench). As a reward function in Flow-GRPO, ImageDoctor (ImageReward 1.029) beats PickScore (1.002) and RichHF (0.879); with DenseFlow-GRPO, the dense heatmap reward pushes ImageReward to 1.100, the strongest result in the comparison.

## Relation to Prior Work

ImageDoctor extends the line of T2I evaluation models (HPS, PickScore, ImageReward, RichHF) that compress quality into scalar scores by adding spatially grounded heatmap predictions and a structured 'look-think-predict' reasoning paradigm built on an MLLM backbone (Qwen2.5-VL). The heatmap decoder borrows the bi-directional cross-attention design from SAM's mask decoder, while the reinforcement fine-tuning stage adapts GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) with custom grounding, score, and heatmap rewards. On the generation side, DenseFlow-GRPO directly extends Flow-GRPO (Liu et al., 2025) by reformulating the likelihood ratio to support pixel-wise advantage, enabling the evaluator's dense spatial feedback to serve as a region-aware RL reward rather than a single image-level scalar.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap in T2I RL alignment: scalar rewards cannot distinguish *where* an image fails, limiting fine-grained optimization. DenseFlow-GRPO's pixel-level dense reward is a clean and potentially reusable formulation for any evaluator that produces spatial maps. Key limitations include training on RichHF-18K (a subset of Pick-a-Pic generated by older models like SDXL), inherent subjectivity in human preference annotations (especially for misalignment heatmaps), and the interpretability-efficiency tradeoff of the reasoning chain. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to newer, higher-quality generators and whether the dense reward signal remains informative as base models improve.
