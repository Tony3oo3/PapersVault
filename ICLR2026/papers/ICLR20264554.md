## Problem

Prior work identified plan representations in the DRC Sokoban agent using linear regression probes, but did not mechanistically explain how the plan is constructed from individual weight matrices. This paper asks what specific hidden-state channels and convolutional kernels implement the planning algorithm, and how they interact to produce bidirectional search, backtracking, and path selection in a model-free RL agent.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264554/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The DRC(3,3) network consumes a 10×10 RGB observation (green agent, brown boxes, red targets) plus recurrent hidden states, running 3 sequential ticks of 3 ConvLSTM layers (9 total sequential computation steps) per environment step before outputting a 4-way cardinal action and a scalar value estimate via an MLP head. The key interpretability result is that the hidden-state channels themselves directly encode the full plan—reading a channel's activation at a grid square tells you whether a box or the agent will move in that channel's direction from that square, eliminating the need for any probe.

## Method

The paper identifies 59 'path channels' in the ConvLSTM hidden states that directly represent future box and agent movements per direction, then analyzes the convolutional weight matrices to discover 'plan extension kernels' that propagate these activations forward from boxes and backward from targets, implementing bidirectional search. Negative activations placed at obstacles (walls, targets) stop plan extension and, when propagated back along the path by the same kernels, enable backtracking from dead ends. A winner-takes-all mechanism via inhibitory cross-direction kernels selects among competing paths at each square. The network was trained with IMPALA V-trace RL on 900k Boxoban levels, and findings are validated through single-step cache ablation, causal intervention on channel activations, and replication of all key mechanisms across five independently trained seeds.

## Results

Ablating the 59 identified path channels causes a 57.6% ± 2.8% drop in solve rate on medium-difficulty Boxoban levels, compared to only 10.5% ± 1.9% for 37 non-path channels, confirming that long-term planning is carried out in these channels. Causal intervention on the Pooled Next Action (PNA) channels achieves a 99.7% ± 0.2% success rate at redirecting the agent's action, and direct box-movement channel interventions reach 86.3% ± 2.1%, both substantially outperforming prior probe-based approaches. The plan extension kernels and winner-takes-all (WTA) mechanism are confirmed to replicate across four additional independently trained seeds, with all networks developing ~27 box-movement channels, ~7 agent-movement channels, and consistent forward/backward propagation patterns.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its direct path-channel interventions against the logistic-regression probe-based causal interventions from Taufeeque et al. (2024) and Bush et al. (2025). For box-movement, direct channel manipulation achieves 86.3% ± 2.1% versus 82.5% ± 2.5% for the probe-based approach, a modest but consistent improvement. The gap is much larger for agent-movement: direct channels reach 53.2% ± 2.1% versus only 20.7% ± 0.7% for probes, more than doubling the intervention success rate. The PNA channels achieve a near-perfect 99.7% intervention score, which the authors note is state-of-the-art compared to all prior probe-based methods. The lower agent-movement score (53.2%) is attributed to the channels being causally relevant only when the agent is not pushing a box, a condition the evaluation did not filter for. Overall, the paper demonstrates that reading channel activations directly is both simpler and more causally effective than the linear probes used in prior work.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on understanding planning in the DRC(3,3) Sokoban agent (Guez et al., 2019; Taufeeque et al., 2024; Bush et al., 2025) by replacing probe-based analysis with direct identification of hidden state channels ('path channels') that encode the plan, then providing a weight-level mechanistic explanation of how plans are constructed. Where prior work used logistic regression probes to extract plan information and qualitatively suggested bidirectional search, this paper examines the convolutional kernels themselves to show how plan extension, backtracking, and winner-takes-all selection are implemented in the network's weights. It positions itself as advancing the Pareto frontier between network complexity and the detail of mechanistic characterization, providing the most detailed weight-level explanation of a planning network to date.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a model-free RL agent has learned a structured bidirectional search with backtracking—visible as plan extension kernels and negative obstacle activations in the weight matrices—provides a concrete model organism for studying mesa-optimization and goal misgeneralization in agentic systems. The path channel representation is simpler and more causally effective than prior probe-based approaches (99.7% intervention accuracy vs. 82.5% for box-movement probes), suggesting that direct channel reading may be a more general strategy for interpretability. Key limitations include scope (single DRC architecture, single Sokoban game), unexplained behaviors (box-switching heuristics, sensitivity to irrelevant features), and the open question of whether other architectures (transformers, ResNets) learn qualitatively different planning algorithms. The weight steering result (scaling kernels by 1.4× solves 40×40 levels) hints at practical controllability but also reveals brittleness at scale.
