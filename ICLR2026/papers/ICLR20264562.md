## Problem

In RLVR for LLM reasoning, two seemingly paradoxical mechanisms—spurious (random) rewards that discourage exploitation and entropy minimization that discourages exploration—both improve validation accuracy, yet the underlying principles reconciling these effects remain poorly understood. Prior work attributed gains under random rewards to upper-clipping bias or validation-set contamination in Qwen-Math, but these explanations are contradicted by conflicting empirical reports and fail to generalize across model families. The paper asks: does clipping bias provide a meaningful learning signal, and under what conditions do spurious rewards actually help?

## Input / Output

The method consumes math problem prompts paired with binary outcome rewards—either ground-truth correctness labels or i.i.d. Bernoulli(1/2) random rewards independent of the response—and produces an updated LLM policy via GRPO with group-normalized advantages. The key diagnostic outputs are the per-step policy entropy trajectory and validation accuracy on MATH500 or AIME, tracked across multiple independent seeds. A distinguishing feature is that the reward signal can be entirely uninformative (random), making the analysis focus on how the optimization machinery (clipping, group size, step size) shapes the policy distribution rather than on reward quality.

## Method

The paper derives explicit upper bounds on the total clipping-correction term (Theorem 3.2) and a 'Law of Clipping' (Theorem 3.4) showing that under practical hyperparameters, the raw surrogate dominates the clipping correction by orders of magnitude, proving clipping bias provides no meaningful learning signal under spurious rewards. It then introduces a one-step policy-entropy shift formulation (Theorems 4.1, 4.3) that establishes a deterministic link: clipping systematically reduces entropy by capping per-token likelihood ratios, acting as an implicit entropy regularizer rather than a learning mechanism. To explain when random rewards are beneficial, the paper proposes a reward-misalignment model (Definition 5.1, Proposition 5.2, Theorem 5.3) quantifying the 'advantage loss' from random mislabeling, showing that stronger models (with more correct rollouts per group) suffer less total damage and fewer false-positive misallocations, making them more likely to improve. Extensive experiments across Qwen-Math-7B, R1-Distill-Llama-8B, and QwQ-32B on DeepScaleR and AIME confirm that gains under spurious rewards are robust across model families and not attributable to contamination or clipping bias alone.

## Results

The paper establishes that upper-clipping bias provides no meaningful learning signal under random rewards: the ratio E[|N_raw|]/E[|C_tot^+|] ≈ 17.15 for practical hyperparameters (η=5×10⁻⁷, ε=0.2, p_+=0.001, G=16, L=4096), confirming the raw surrogate dominates the clipping correction. Empirically, R1-Distill-Llama-8B achieves a MATH500 accuracy gain from 65.6% to 76.6% within 100 unclipped steps before gradient explosion at step ~150, while Qwen2.5-Math-7B (clipping activation <0.2%) shows that enabling clipping can actually degrade validation performance. The reward-misalignment model (Theorem 5.2) predicts E[Δ] = n_c(G−n_c)/G, explaining why stronger models (higher n_c) exhibit more stable training curves and are more likely to improve under random rewards.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an analysis paper rather than a method-comparison study, so it does not benchmark a proposed algorithm against named baselines. Instead, it compares training configurations: (i) clipped vs. unclipped GRPO on Qwen2.5-Math-7B (Figure 1), where unclipped training often yields better MATH500 validation accuracy while clipped training can degrade it; (ii) model strength across Qwen2.5-Math-7B, R1-Distill-Llama-8B, and QwQ-32B on the same DeepScaleR/MATH500 protocol (Figure 4), where the stronger distilled Llama and QwQ models show consistent improvements under random rewards while the weaker Qwen-Math model does not; and (iii) dataset difficulty (DeepScaleR vs. AIME) on Qwen2.5-Math-7B (Figure 3), where the harder AIME set causes the 7B model to exhibit random-walk-like training with no meaningful gain, whereas the stronger 8B and 32B models still show steady early-epoch improvements. These comparisons collectively overturn the prior attribution of random-reward gains to Qwen-Math contamination or upper-clipping bias, showing the effect is model-strength-dependent and generalizes across model families.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the GRPO training framework (Shao et al., 2024) and the spurious-reward phenomenon first reported by Shao et al. (2025), who attributed gains to upper-clipping bias amplifying high-prior responses in potentially contaminated Qwen-Math models. It departs from both that clipping-bias explanation and the contamination hypothesis (Wu et al., 2025) by proving the clipping correction is negligible relative to the raw surrogate (Theorem 3.4) and by showing gains persist in non-contaminated Llama and QwQ families. The entropy analysis extends Cui et al. (2025)'s one-step entropy approximation by incorporating clipping effects and initial policy skewness, correcting the inaccuracy of their formula under random rewards where the covariance term vanishes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper resolves a genuine confusion in the RLVR literature by showing that the two mechanisms previously credited for spurious-reward gains (clipping bias and contamination) are neither necessary nor sufficient, and instead identifies a reward-misalignment mechanism where stronger models with higher baseline accuracy suffer less advantage-mass loss from random mislabeling. A key open question is whether the entropy-as-regularizer interpretation generalizes beyond math reasoning to other verifiable-reward domains (code, science), and whether the reward-misalignment framework can guide principled combinations of true and spurious rewards for better exploration-exploitation balance. The finding that entropy minimization is regime-dependent (beneficial for strong models on easy data, harmful for weak models on hard data) cautions against the growing trend of explicitly optimizing entropy as a standalone objective.
