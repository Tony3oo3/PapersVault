## Problem

Evaluating and post-training frontier LLMs requires reliable supervision, but by definition superhuman models can exploit weaker judges, rendering standard LLM-as-a-Judge approaches unreliable when participants are 5–20× larger than the judge. The paper addresses this gap by adapting game-theoretic peer prediction mechanisms from the mechanism design literature to LLM evaluation and training, where honest and informative answering is the dominant strategy even in the absence of ground-truth labels.

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes a question, n participant model answers (full chain-of-thought traces of 300–1,000 words), and m expert models, and produces a real-valued score per participant plus auxiliary expert scores. Scores are relative rankings computed over n²m rounds of source-target-expert interactions, and no ground-truth labels are ever visible to the scoring process. The resulting scores can be used directly for model comparison or to construct DPO training pairs by pairing the highest- and lowest-scoring responses per question.

## Method

The method adapts the Schoenebeck & Yu (2023) peer prediction mechanism to LLMs, assigning each round three roles: a source (the answer being evaluated), a target (the answer being predicted), and an expert (a potentially weak model that predicts the target's answer using the source as a reference). The source's score is the log-likelihood ratio log Pr_j(A_t|A_s) − log Pr_j(A_t), rewarding answers that help the expert predict others, while the expert is scored via the logarithmic scoring rule. The paper proves incentive compatibility (Theorem 1) under a shared prior and approximate incentive compatibility (Theorem 2) under diverse priors with sufficiently large populations, and empirically discovers an inverse scaling property where resistance to deception increases as the expert-participant capability gap widens. For training, high- and low-scoring responses are paired for DPO, recovering most of the truthfulness drop from malicious finetuning using only a 135M-parameter expert.

## Results

The paper demonstrates an inverse scaling property: resistance to deception in peer prediction increases as the capability gap between expert and participants widens, with peer prediction remaining effective at 100× size differences where LLM-as-a-Judge performs worse than random guessing (at 5–20× gaps). In truthfulness training, peer prediction with a 0.135B-parameter expert (SmolLM) recovers most of the accuracy drop from malicious finetuning of an 8B model, outperforming LLM-as-a-Judge with a 7B judge. In deception detection (Table 2), peer prediction correctly ranks honest above deceptive participants on 65–81% of questions across expert/participant pairings, with the strongest results (e.g., 80.4%) occurring when a small expert (SmolLM-360M) evaluates a larger participant (Qwen2.5-3B).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is LLM-as-a-Judge (using SmolLM-135M, SmolLM-360M, Qwen2.5-0.5B/1.5B/3B/7B, or Mistral-7B as the judge, with 0-shot and 6-shot prompting variants). In truthfulness training (Figure 1), peer prediction with a 0.135B expert outperforms LLM-as-a-Judge with both a 0.135B and a 7B judge in recovering ground-truth accuracy after adversarial finetuning. In deception resistance (Figure 2), peer prediction consistently outperforms LLM-as-a-Judge when the participant-to-expert size ratio exceeds ~5×; LLM-as-a-Judge scores become worse than random guessing at 5–20× gaps, while peer prediction maintains strong separation at 100× gaps. In the correctness-correlation comparison (Table 1), the results are more nuanced: LLM-as-a-Judge with a 7B judge achieves the highest Pearson ρ (0.2379), but the authors note this metric is a statistical artifact of correlating a continuous score with a binary label; at the domain-aggregated level, peer prediction achieves ρ=0.50 overall and >0.75 on STEM domains. A consistency-based baseline (self-consistency voting) is also compared in Table 1 and is uniformly outperformed by peer prediction across all expert sizes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the peer prediction mechanism from Schoenebeck & Yu (2023) — originally designed for crowdsourcing with three heterogeneous agents — to the n-agent LLM evaluation and training setting, extending it with a formal incentive compatibility proof (Theorem 1) and a novel wisdom-of-the-crowd relaxation for disagreeing priors (Theorem 2). It positions itself against the dominant LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm (Zheng et al., 2023), which lacks game-theoretic guarantees and is vulnerable to strategic manipulation when participants outperform the judge. The training pipeline repurposes peer prediction scores as DPO contrastive pairs, connecting mechanism design to the RLHF/DPO alignment literature. Conceptually, the paper departs from prior deception-detection work (Zou et al., 2023) by treating the evaluation mechanism itself as a game with incentive-compatible equilibria rather than studying fixed model policies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The inverse scaling property — that resistance to deception *increases* as the expert-participant capability gap widens — directly addresses the scalable oversight problem: one can reliably evaluate and train 405B-parameter models using 135M-parameter experts, removing the need for a trusted stronger judge. This has immediate practical implications for frontier model safety evaluation where no stronger model exists. Key open questions remain: the theorems address only unilateral deception and do not provide guarantees against collusion (though initial experiments suggest vulnerability when deceptive participants form a majority), and the method's performance degrades on knowledge-heavy or subjective domains (e.g., humanities, general knowledge) compared to structured reasoning tasks. Whether the incentive compatibility guarantees hold in truly superhuman settings where models can perform inference-time strategic reasoning to game the scoring remains untested.
