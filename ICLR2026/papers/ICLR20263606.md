## Problem

Existing embodied navigation methods are task-specific, each requiring separate architectures and data for point-goal, instruction-goal, object-goal, or frontier-based exploration, limiting cross-task transfer. Moreover, the dominant failure mode in practice stems not from navigation policy learning but from inadequate understanding of generic instructions and open-vocabulary objects, while discretized action modeling and constrained LLM call frequencies sacrifice precision and real-time responsiveness.

## Input / Output

The fast system consumes a tri-view RGB observation (front/left/right at 480×426) plus a ring buffer of up to 20 historical front-view frames (120×106), a text instruction or object label, and optional point-goal coordinates, all tokenized into a unified sequence (text tokens, MLP-embedded coordinate tokens, ViT-encoded image tokens) processed with a KV cache for streaming inference. It outputs 5 continuous-space waypoints per step, each a 5-tuple (x, y, sin θ, cos θ, arrival flag), generated via 5 Euler integration steps of a flow-matching policy at 5 Hz. The slow system additionally ingests depth and odometry to maintain a 3D occupancy map, and outputs subgoal coordinates along with explicit chain-of-thought reasoning over frontier-associated memory images.

## Method

OmniNav employs a fast-slow dual-system architecture built on a shared Qwen2.5-VL-3B backbone. The fast system appends a DiT-based flow-matching policy head that non-autoregressively generates 5 continuous waypoints conditioned on VLM-fused multimodal features, avoiding the precision loss and latency accumulation of autoregressive action-chunk prediction. The slow system performs deliberative planning by maintaining a 3D occupancy map with frontier detection, a pose-stamped image memory bank, and explicit chain-of-thought reasoning to select semantically relevant frontiers as subgoals, which the fast system then executes. Training follows a two-stage scheme: Stage 1 aligns language-vision-action via autoregressive prediction over discrete tokens (navigation actions, embodied QA, grounding/referring, and general MLLM data from MAmmoTH-VL, RefCOCO, Objects365); Stage 2 attaches the flow-matching head for continuous waypoint prediction while retaining 20% of Stage-1 discrete data to prevent VLM capability degradation. A key empirical finding is that incorporating large-scale general-purpose vision-language data (captioning, referring/grounding) into joint multi-task training substantially improves instruction following and open-vocabulary object recognition, yielding the largest gains over navigation-specific data alone.

## Results

On R2R-CE Val-Unseen, OmniNav achieves SR=69.5%, SPL=66.1%, and NE=3.74m, surpassing the previous best (CorrectNav, SR=65.1%) by 4.4 points. On RxR-CE Val-Unseen, it reaches SR=73.6% and SPL=62.0%, improving over CorrectNav (SR=69.3%) by 4.3 points. On HM3D-OVON Val-Unseen, the full OmniNav with the slow system and CoT achieves SR=59.2% and SPL=33.2%, exceeding the strongest prior method MTU3D (SR=40.8%) by 18.4 points. On the CityWalker point-goal benchmark, OmniNav attains MAOE=11.53% versus CityWalker's 15.23%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OmniNav outperforms all 22 baselines on both R2R-CE and RxR-CE Val-Unseen. The closest competitor, CorrectNav (SR 65.1%/69.3%), is surpassed by 4.4 and 4.3 SR points respectively; StreamVLN (56.9%/52.9%) and NaVILA (54.0%/49.3%) trail by a wider margin. On HM3D-OVON Val-Unseen, the full OmniNav with the slow system (SR 59.2%) dominates all prior methods: MTU3D (40.8%), Uni-NaVid (39.5%), and DAgRL+OD (37.1%) are the nearest, with OmniNav leading by 18.4, 19.7, and 22.1 SR points respectively. The fast-system-only OmniNav (SR 43.5%) already exceeds MTU3D and Uni-NaVid, confirming that the flow-matching policy head alone provides strong open-vocabulary navigation. On the CityWalker point-goal benchmark, OmniNav achieves a lower MAOE (11.53%) than the dedicated CityWalker method (15.23%), demonstrating competitive outdoor point-goal performance without task-specific architecture. Notably, OmniNav uses only RGB input (no depth or odometry) for the fast system on R2R-CE/RxR-CE, while depth and odometry are required only for the slow system's occupancy map on OVON.

## Relation to Prior Work

OmniNav extends the dual-system (fast-slow) architecture popularized in VLA models for manipulation (GR00T N1, π0.5, HUME) to the embodied navigation domain, where prior work had largely treated fast reactive control and slow deliberative planning as separate systems. For the fast system, it departs from the dominant autoregressive action-chunk paradigm (NaVid, Uni-NaVid, NaVILA) by adopting a conditional flow-matching policy built on a DiT backbone to generate continuous waypoints non-autoregressively, drawing on the flow-matching formulation of Lipman et al. and the transformer-diffusion architecture of Peebles & Xie. The slow system's frontier-based exploration and memory bank are adapted from MTU3D (Zhu et al., 2025), but augmented with explicit chain-of-thought reasoning over egocentric images to make frontier selection semantically grounded rather than purely geometric.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's most useful empirical finding is that the dominant failure mode in navigation stems from inadequate understanding of generic instructions and open-vocabulary objects rather than from the navigation policy itself, and that injecting large-scale general VLM data (captioning, grounding, referring) into joint training substantially closes this gap. This reframes where effort should be directed in embodied navigation research. Key limitations include the slow system's reliance on depth and odometry (not yet deployed on the real robot), the absence of a systematic scaling-law study, and the fact that the 18.4% OVON gain with the slow system comes with additional sensor inputs that are not always available. Open questions include whether the fast-slow decomposition generalizes to more complex multi-floor or outdoor settings, and whether the flow-matching policy head can be further accelerated for edge deployment.
