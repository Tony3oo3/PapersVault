## Problem

Spectral bias causes neural networks to converge slowly to high-frequency modes, hindering scientific tasks that require fine-scale structure, while grokking (delayed generalization) is hypothesized to stem from prolonged confinement in the lazy NTK regime combined with this frequency-dependent convergence. The paper investigates whether preconditioned gradient descent (PGD) methods—Gauss-Newton and Levenberg-Marquardt—can simultaneously mitigate spectral bias and accelerate the transition from the lazy to the rich learning regime, thereby reducing grokking delay.

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard supervised training pairs (1D/2D regression targets, PDE forcing functions, modular arithmetic labels, or MNIST digit images) along with the network's Jacobian matrix at each iteration. The Jacobian is never explicitly inverted in parameter space; instead, the Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury identity reduces the inversion to the smaller data-space matrix J_t J_t^T, and matrix-vector products with J_t and J_t^T are computed via forward/backward passes (JVP/VJP). Outputs include the optimized parameters, per-mode FFT error trajectories tracking spectral convergence, and train/test accuracy or loss curves that reveal grokking dynamics.

## Method

The core theoretical contribution derives continuous gradient-flow error dynamics in the infinite-width NTK limit, showing that LM preconditioning maps each NTK eigenvalue λ_i to λ_i/(μ+λ_i) and GN maps it to 1 (up to a pseudoinverse cutoff ε), collapsing the condition number from κ_GD = λ_N/λ_1 to approximately 2 and eliminating spectral bias (Lemmas 3.2–3.3). Empirically, the paper demonstrates that this uniform NTK exploration compresses the grokking delay across modular arithmetic, polynomial regression, and MNIST tasks, supporting the hypothesis that grokking is a transitional behavior between lazy and rich regimes. A key practical finding is that PGD alone tends to stall near the lazy subspace and under-generalizes; the paper proposes a hybrid schedule—running LM or GGN for an initial phase to exhaust the linear regime, then switching to AdamW for final generalization—which recovers full test accuracy. For cross-entropy losses (transformer experiments), the preconditioner is generalized to J_t^T H_t J_t with conjugate-gradient solves and Armijo line search to handle the larger model size.

## Results

In the NTK regime, Gauss-Newton (GN) preconditioning yields uniform exponential decay (rate 1) across all frequency modes above a truncation threshold ε, while Levenberg-Marquardt (LM) with μ = λ₁ reduces the effective condition number to κ_LM ≈ 2 (vs. κ_GD = λ_N/λ₁ for standard gradient descent). On MNIST grokking (α = 8 initialization scaling), LM compresses the train-test accuracy gap but attains lower final test accuracy than AdamW; switching to AdamW after 2000 LM iterations recovers full generalization. On the transformer modular-arithmetic task, generalized Gauss-Newton (GGN) reaches only ~45% validation accuracy versus ~100% for Adam, confirming that pure PGD struggles to escape the lazy regime.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is Adam/AdamW (and SGD), compared across MNIST classification, modular arithmetic (MLP and transformer), polynomial regression, 1D/2D function fitting, and PINNs. PGD (LM/GN) consistently wins on convergence speed within the NTK/lazy regime: GN achieves near-uniform mode decay in 1D regression (Figure 3), LM outperforms Adam at higher frequencies in PINNs (Figure 4), and LM compresses the grokking delay across all scaling factors α on MNIST and polynomial regression (Figures 1, 5, 6). However, PGD loses on final generalization: on MNIST, LM's test accuracy plateaus below AdamW's; on the transformer modular-arithmetic task, GGN stagnates at ~45% validation accuracy versus Adam's ~100% (Figure 8). The paper's key finding is that a hybrid schedule—PGD for the first 2000 iterations followed by AdamW—recovers AdamW-level generalization while retaining the reduced grokking delay, suggesting PGD is best used as a warm-start rather than a standalone optimizer.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: NTK-based spectral bias analysis (Jacot et al., 2018), the grokking-as-lazy-to-rich-transition hypothesis (Kumar et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024), and the application of curvature-aware optimizers to neural network training (Wadia et al., 2021; Amari et al., 2020; Buffelli et al., 2024). It departs from prior second-order optimization work by providing a mechanistic explanation for why those methods fail to generalize—namely, they remain confined to the NTK/lazy subspace—and by proposing a practical remedy (switching to first-order methods after the lazy regime is exhausted). The theoretical analysis extends the standard NTK eigenmode decomposition to show how LM and GN preconditioning reshape the condition number of the error dynamics, connecting classical numerical linear algebra preconditioning to the deep learning spectral bias literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a unifying lens that connects spectral bias, grokking, and optimization dynamics, suggesting grokking is fundamentally a transitional phenomenon between the lazy and rich regimes rather than an artifact of regularization or adaptive learning rates. The practical implication—that a hybrid PGD-then-first-order training schedule can compress grokking delay while recovering generalization—is actionable, though the evidence is currently limited to small-scale MLPs and a single transformer experiment where the continuation strategy actually fails (Appendix D). Key open questions include convergence theory in the rich regime, whether the switching strategy generalizes to larger architectures and datasets, and the computational cost of maintaining Jacobian-based preconditioners at scale. The paper explicitly acknowledges it does not address train/test dataset size as a grokking contributor.
