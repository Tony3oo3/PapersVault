## Problem

Although the ISA-MDP framework theoretically guarantees that an optimal robust policy (ORP) coincides with the Bellman optimality policy, practical policy gradient methods fail to realize this alignment: SPO converges to vulnerable first-order stationary policies (FOSPs) with high natural returns, while ARPO converges to robust FOSPs with substantially lower returns. The paper identifies and explains this optimality-robustness tension, attributing it to the reshaping effect of strongest adversaries that introduce deceptive sticky FOSPs and fragment the optimization landscape.

## Input / Output

The method operates on continuous state vectors from MuJoCo environments (Hopper, Walker2d, HalfCheetah, Ant) with a fixed adversarial perturbation budget ε (e.g., 0.075 for Hopper, 0.15 for Ant). The adversary is parameterized as a state-dependent perturbation ν_θ(s) = s + θ_s constrained to the admissible neighborhood B*(s), and is solved via SGLD in the inner loop. The output is a robust policy π_θ mapping states to action distributions, trained on top of PPO with the bilevel structure replacing the standard maximin inner loop.

## Method

BARPO introduces a bilevel framework that replaces the strongest adversary (minimizing adversarial value) with a KL-divergence-based surrogate adversary: the inner loop maximizes D_KL(π_θ(·|s) ‖ (π_θ ∘ ν_θ)(·|s)) rather than minimizing V^{π_θ∘ν_θ}(s), which is justified by Theorem 4.1 showing this KL term provides a first-order lower bound on robustness. This modulation of adversary strength prevents the landscape from becoming overly rugged, reducing the prevalence of deceptive sticky FOSPs while preserving access to the globally optimal robust policy shared by SPO and ARPO. SPO dynamics are further incorporated as a regularization term (weight κ) in the outer loop to smooth the landscape and improve natural returns. The inner adversary is solved via SGLD, and the outer policy update follows PPO with second-order cross-terms omitted for efficiency, yielding the practical BAR-PPO algorithm.

## Results

BAR-PPO achieves the highest natural returns on Hopper (3684), Walker2d (4732), and Halfcheetah (4837), and competitive performance on Ant (5024 vs. SA-PPO's 5367). In terms of worst-case robustness, BAR-PPO attains the best scores on Walker2d (−0.436), Halfcheetah (−0.342), and Ant (−0.438), and is within 0.3% of the best on Hopper (−0.636 vs. SA-PPO's −0.634). Compared to vanilla PPO, BAR-PPO improves worst-case robustness by 35–72% across all four environments while maintaining or exceeding natural returns. The worst-case robust return improvements over the second-best baseline reach 4% (Hopper), 154% (Walker2d), 40% (Halfcheetah), and 20% (Ant).

## Baselines & Comparisons

BAR-PPO outperforms all four baselines (PPO, SA-PPO, RADIAL-PPO, WocaR-PPO) on worst-case robust returns in 3 out of 4 environments, with the most dramatic gains on Walker2d (2699 vs. 1064 for WocaR-PPO, a 154% improvement) and Halfcheetah (3181 vs. 2270 for WocaR-PPO, a 40% improvement). On natural returns, BAR-PPO leads on Hopper, Walker2d, and Halfcheetah, while SA-PPO retains a slight edge on Ant (5367 vs. 5024). RADIAL-PPO is the weakest baseline overall, showing substantially lower natural returns (e.g., 2531 on Walker2d vs. 4732 for BAR-PPO) and poor robustness. WocaR-PPO achieves competitive natural returns but lags BAR-PPO significantly in robustness, particularly on Halfcheetah (−0.519 vs. −0.342) and Ant (−0.550 vs. −0.438). SA-PPO is the closest competitor, matching BAR-PPO on Hopper robustness (−0.634 vs. −0.636) and Ant natural return, but falls behind on Walker2d and Halfcheetah robustness by 0.36 and 0.36 points respectively.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the ISA-MDP framework of Li et al. (2024), which formally established that an Optimal Robust Policy (ORP) exists and coincides with the Bellman optimality policy, suggesting theoretical alignment between optimality and robustness. It departs from prior robust RL methods (SA-PPO, RADIAL-PPO, WocaR-PPO) by shifting focus from engineering better adversarial training procedures to diagnosing *why* standard policy gradient methods fail to realize the theoretical alignment in practice. The key conceptual departure is identifying the 'reshaping effect' of the strongest adversary as the mechanism that creates deceptive sticky FOSPs, and proposing a bilevel framework with a KL-divergence surrogate to modulate adversary strength rather than always using the worst-case adversary.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a mechanistic explanation for a long-standing theory-practice gap in adversarial robustness for DRL: despite the proven existence of a policy that is simultaneously optimal and robust, gradient-based methods converge to different (and inferior) stationary points depending on whether they use SPO or ARPO. The bilevel framework (BARPO) offers a principled path to closing this gap, and the KL-surrogate adversary provides a practical, first-order approximation that avoids the full worst-case computation. Limitations include evaluation only on four MuJoCo continuous-control tasks, added computational overhead from the bilevel structure, and the fact that natural returns still lag behind SPO in several environments. Open questions include whether the reshaping analysis and bilevel approach extend to discrete action spaces, multi-agent settings, or high-dimensional tasks like language model training.
