## Problem

It remains unclear which learning algorithms can simultaneously achieve high predictive accuracy and strong calibration guarantees when trained on finite data. Prior theoretical work on calibration (e.g., the post-processing gap framework) operates at the population level with infinite data, and only a few results explicitly connect calibration error to concrete finite-sample learning algorithms. This paper asks: under what conditions on sample size, iterations, and hyperparameters can standard algorithms provably achieve both ε-level smooth calibration error and ε-level misclassification rate?

## Input / Output

The framework takes i.i.d. samples (x_i, y_i) with x_i ∈ R^d and y_i ∈ {0,1} from an unknown distribution D, and produces a predictor f: X → [0,1] (or logit g: X → R with f = σ∘g) together with high-probability upper bounds on both the population smooth CE and the misclassification rate. The bounds are expressed in terms of the training smooth CE, the Rademacher complexity (or covering number) of the hypothesis class, the sample size n, and algorithm-specific hyperparameters (stepsize w, number of iterations T, tree depth m, or number of hidden units). The key output is not a single prediction but a joint guarantee: for any target ε > 0, explicit conditions on T, n, and w ensure both smCE(σ(g), D) ≤ ε and P[(2Y−1)g(X) ≤ 0] ≤ ε simultaneously.

## Method

The paper develops a two-stage theoretical framework. First, it derives a uniform convergence bound for the smooth CE (Theorem 1) showing that the population smooth CE is bounded by the training smooth CE plus a generalization gap expressed via covering numbers or Rademacher complexity of the hypothesis class F alone—crucially avoiding the intractable complexity of the composite class Lip_1([0,1],[−1,1])∘F by exploiting the linear-program reformulation of smooth CE and a novel symmetrization argument with structured Rademacher variables. Second, it proves that the training smooth CE is upper-bounded by the L1 norm of the functional gradient of the proper loss (squared or cross-entropy) evaluated on training data, providing a principled optimization criterion for calibration. Applying this framework to gradient boosting trees, kernel boosting in an RKHS, and two-layer neural networks (via the NTK), the paper shows that under a standard margin assumption, choosing T = Ω(γ⁻²ε⁻²) iterations with appropriately scaled stepsizes yields both ε-smooth CE and ε-misclassification rate, with the trade-off between reducing training smooth CE and controlling model complexity (which grows as O(√(wT)) in Rademacher complexity) made explicit.

## Results

The paper provides purely theoretical guarantees with no empirical benchmarks. The headline results are convergence rates: for gradient boosting trees, kernel boosting, and two-layer neural networks, O(1/ε²) iterations suffice to simultaneously achieve ε-level smooth calibration error and ε-level misclassification rate (Corollaries 3–5). Specifically, GBTs require T = Ω(γ⁻²ε⁻²) iterations with stepsize w = Θ(γ⁻¹ε⁻¹T⁻¹) and sample size n = Õ(γ⁻²ε⁻⁴); kernel boosting requires T = Ω(γ⁻²ε⁻²) with w = Θ(γ⁻²ε⁻²T⁻¹); and two-layer NNs (with β=0) can achieve the same target in O(1/ε) iterations by increasing the number of hidden units to m = Θ(γ⁻²ε⁻³ᐟ² log(1/ε)). The uniform convergence bound (Theorem 1) shows the population smooth CE is controlled by the training smooth CE plus a generalization gap of order O(1/√n) plus a complexity term depending on the covering number or Rademacher complexity of the hypothesis class.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or experimental comparisons. The paper positions its contributions relative to prior theoretical work: (1) Unlike Blasiok et al. (2023), which analyzes the post-processing gap at the population level, this paper derives finite-sample uniform convergence bounds for the smooth CE applicable to algorithms trained on finite data. (2) Unlike Futami & Fujisawa (2024), which provides algorithm-dependent bounds for binning ECE at rate O(log n / n^{1/3}), this paper achieves a faster O(1/√n) rate for smooth CE via a covering-number argument that avoids the nonparametric conditional probability estimation inherent in binning. (3) The paper is the first to jointly guarantee both smooth CE and misclassification rate for GBTs, kernel boosting, and two-layer NNs under a unified functional-gradient framework, whereas prior boosting analyses (Zhang & Yu 2005; Nitanda & Suzuki 2018) focused solely on accuracy. The authors note that their margin assumption (Assumption 2/3) is standard in boosting theory but represents a strong separability condition that limits practical applicability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the smooth calibration error framework of Blasiok et al. (2023), which was defined only at the population level, by deriving the first finite-sample uniform convergence bound for smooth CE and connecting it to functional gradients of the training loss. The functional-gradient perspective on boosting (Mason et al., 1999; Nitanda & Suzuki, 2018) and the NTK-based analysis of two-layer networks (Nitanda et al., 2019) are repurposed here not for accuracy but for calibration, yielding a unified lens across GBTs, kernel boosting, and neural networks. The margin assumptions follow Telgarsky (2013), and the kernel boosting setup builds on Wei et al. (2017), but the calibration-specific bounds and the simultaneous accuracy-plus-calibration guarantees are new.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to provide provable calibration guarantees (via smooth CE) for standard learning algorithms without requiring post-hoc recalibration, which is significant because recalibration methods are known to degrade sharpness and accuracy. The practical implication is that practitioners can now understand under what conditions (margin, sample size, iteration count) their GBT, kernel, or NN models will be well-calibrated out of the box. Key limitations include the strong margin assumption (requiring well-separated data), restriction to binary classification, and the use of a uniform bound over the Lipschitz function class that may be loose; extending to multiclass settings and weaker data conditions are natural next steps.
