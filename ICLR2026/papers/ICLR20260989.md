## Problem

Existing deepfake detection datasets and models are overwhelmingly monolingual, overlooking the widespread reality of intra-utterance code-switching (CSW) in real-world speech. In the Arab world, Arabic-English CSW is a dominant communicative strategy (present in ~19–63% of spoken utterances), yet no prior audio-visual deepfake benchmark captures this phenomenon, leaving detection systems untested on the linguistic mixing that confuses both models and humans.

## Input / Output

The dataset consists of 387k short audio-visual clips (~7.7 s average) sourced from 8,809 YouTube videos, split 7:1:2 into train/val/test via multilabel stratified sampling. Each clip is labeled as real or fake, with fake clips further annotated with temporal boundaries of the manipulated segment. Three manipulation types are included: fake audio + fake video, fake audio + real video, and real audio + fake video, enabling evaluation of both joint and single-modality detection. Evaluation uses AP@0.5/0.75/0.9/0.95 and AR@50/30/20/10/5 for temporal localization, and AUC and video-level accuracy for binary detection.

## Method

ArEnAV is generated through a three-stage pipeline: (1) transcript manipulation using GPT-4.1-mini with eight controlled change modes spanning meaning-only, dialect-shift, and meaning-plus-translation operations across CSW and Arabic-only contexts; (2) audio synthesis via four voice-cloning strategies (XTTS-v2, XTTS-v2+OpenVoice-v2, Fairseq Arabic TTS+OpenVoice-v2, GPT-TTS+OpenVoice-v2) that preserve the original speaker's identity while rendering the altered transcript; and (3) visual generation using two diffusion-based lip-sync models (Diff2Lip and LatentSync) to produce temporally aligned fake frames. Source videos are transcribed with Whisper-v2 and word-aligned via multilingual wav2vec2 to obtain precise CSW timestamps. The key finding is that SOTA detectors (BA-TFD+, Xception, XLSR-Mamba) drop 35–90% in performance relative to monolingual benchmarks, and models systematically misclassify natural code-switching transitions in real videos as synthetic artifacts, confirming that the difficulty is intrinsic to linguistic mixing rather than generation quality.

## Results

On the ArEnAV test set, the best-performing model (BA-TFD+ fine-tuned on ArEnAV) achieves 79.97% AUC for audio-visual deepfake detection, while the best temporal localization score is only 3.74 AP@0.5 (BA-TFD+). Zero-shot models perform near chance: BA-TFD (pretrained on AV-1M) drops to 61.73% AUC, and Video-LLaMA2 achieves just 48.79% AUC. In the cross-dataset detection comparison, all six SOTA face-forensics models (Capsule-v2, Face-X-Ray, LipForensics, M2TR, LAA-Net, ForensicsAdaptor) score between 49.15% and 55.56% AUC on ArEnAV—essentially random—while achieving 80–99% AUC on monolingual benchmarks like DFDC and FF++. A human user study confirms the difficulty, with participants achieving only 60% detection accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 11+ SOTA detection and localization models on ArEnAV to demonstrate the dataset's difficulty. BA-TFD+ (Cai et al., 2023) is the strongest baseline, reaching 79.97% AUC only after fine-tuning on ArEnAV; its zero-shot transfer from AV-1M yields just 60.96% AUC, a 35%+ drop in AP@0.5 for localization (from 44.42 on AV-1M to 3.74 on ArEnAV). XLSR-Mamba (Xiao & Das, 2025), the best open-source audio deepfake detector, achieves 73.00% AUC fine-tuned but only 39.19% zero-shot, and performs worst on code-switched audio. Image-based classifiers (Meso4, MesoInception4, Xception) trained on ArEnAV frames reach at most 74.21% AUC (Xception, frame-level), while their video-level variants hover near 50% AUC. Video-LLaMA2 (Zhang et al., 2023) in zero-shot mode is essentially uninformative at 48.79% AUC. Most strikingly, in the cross-dataset comparison (Table 11b), all six pretrained face-forensics models—Capsule-v2 (49.15%), Face-X-Ray (55.56%), LipForensics (49.76%), M2TR (50.12%), LAA-Net (50.04%), and ForensicsAdaptor (50.58%)—fail to generalize beyond chance on ArEnAV, despite scoring 80–99% AUC on DFDC, FF++, and Celeb-DF, confirming that monolingual training data does not transfer to multilingual code-switched deepfakes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the audio-visual deepfake detection line (FakeAVCeleb, LAV-DF, AV-Deepfake1M) and the emerging multilingual deepfake efforts (PolyGlotFake, Illusion) by introducing intra-utterance code-switching between Arabic and English, a dimension entirely absent from prior datasets. Rather than proposing a new detection architecture, it repurposes existing TTS models (XTTS-v2, GPT-TTS, Fairseq Arabic TTS, OpenVoice) and diffusion-based lip-sync models (Diff2Lip, LatentSync) within a novel pipeline that uses GPT-4.1-mini for controlled transcript manipulation across eight code-switching-aware edit modes. The content-driven manipulation strategy follows AV-Deepfake1M's LLM-driven approach but is specifically adapted to handle diglossic Arabic (MSA vs. dialectal) and bilingual switching, carving out a linguistic regime no prior benchmark has addressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ArEnAV exposes a critical blind spot: SOTA audio-visual deepfake detectors (BA-TFD, BA-TFD+) drop from ~37% AP@0.5 on AV-1M to ~2–4% on ArEnAV, and cross-dataset models collapse to chance-level (~50% AUC), while even human raters achieve only 60% accuracy. The finding that models confuse natural code-switching transitions with synthetic manipulation (Figure 5) suggests that linguistic diversity itself, not just generation artifacts, is a fundamental confound for current architectures. Key limitations include coverage of only two languages, residual ASR noise in Arabic transcripts, and LLM instruction-following failures in the 'meaning + translation' mode. Open questions include whether detection systems can be made robust to arbitrary language pairs and dialectal variation, and whether the observed generalization failure is specific to Arabic-English or extends to other diglossic/code-switching contexts.
