## Problem

Penalty-based robot simulators like MuJoCo produce incorrect gradients under hard contacts because time-discretization errors in ODE integration corrupt the derivatives computed via automatic differentiation, while simultaneously, contact gradients vanish entirely when objects are not in contact, leaving the optimizer with no signal about how to achieve contact. These two issues jointly prevent the use of simulator gradients for parameter estimation and policy synthesis in collision-rich, high-dimensional robotic systems.

## Input / Output

The method consumes robot states (generalized position and velocity), control actions, and model parameters as inputs to a differentiable MuJoCo XLA simulator, and produces gradients of a user-defined loss with respect to all of these quantities. For system identification, real-world trajectory segments (e.g., 5-step cube toss sequences from ContactNets) are fed as supervision, and the output is estimated geometry and contact parameters. For MPC, a scalar task cost (e.g., L2 distance to a target) over a 256-step rollout is backpropagated through the simulator to yield optimized muscle-tendon control sequences. A key structural detail is that CFD modifies only the backward pass via stop-gradient, so the forward trajectory remains physically unaltered while gradients incorporate virtual contact forces between near-but-non-colliding bodies.

## Method

DiffMJX integrates the adaptive ODE solver Diffrax into MuJoCo XLA, exploiting the fact that penalty-based contact dynamics yield a smooth (though stiff) ODE whose discretization error can be controlled adaptively: two embedded Runge-Kutta methods of different orders estimate local error, and a PID controller adjusts the stepsize to meet a user-specified tolerance, reducing gradient error by orders of magnitude at the same computational budget as fixed-step MJX. Collision detection discontinuities (hard case distinctions in geometry pairs) are smoothed with sigmoid-based interpolations to ensure full differentiability. Contacts from Distance (CFD) extends the MuJoCo impedance function d(r) with an additional polynomial spline for positive signed distances r > 0, enabling the contact solver to generate small artificial forces between non-colliding objects within a configurable width w_c. The straight-through trick (stop-gradient on the original dynamics plus the CFD-augmented dynamics minus stop-gradient on the latter) routes these virtual forces exclusively into the backward pass, preserving forward simulation realism while providing informative pre-contact gradients. Together, these mechanisms enable gradient-based MPC on 39-muscle-tendon-unit MyoHand and 63-actuator bionic arm models where sampling-based planners fail, and accurate parameter recovery of real-world cube dynamics from the ContactNets dataset.

## Results

For parameter identification on the ContactNets cube-toss dataset, DiffMJX with CFD achieves ~5% relative error on the cube's side length (10 cm ground truth), while standard MJX stalls or converges poorly when initialized far from the true value (60 mm or 140 mm). In gradient-based MPC, DiffMJX with CFD solves all four manipulation tasks (in-hand ball swap, bionic tennis, in-hand cube reorientation, on-table cube reorientation), whereas sampling-based MPC (1024 samples) fails on in-hand manipulation and in-hand cube reorientation, and gradient-based MPC without CFD fails on bionic tennis and on-table cube reorientation. On the cube-toss gradient accuracy benchmark, DiffMJX's analytical gradients closely match central-difference gradients, while standard MJX at the default 2 ms step shows large oscillatory deviations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the predictive sampling planner from MuJoCo MPC (Howell et al., 2022), which samples 64–2048 trajectories per planning step and executes the lowest-cost plan. Gradient-based MPC with CFD (DiffMJX) wins decisively on the in-hand ball-swap task, where sampling with 1024 trajectories fails to reliably swap the balls, and on the in-hand cube reorientation task, where sampling fails entirely. On the bionic tennis task, sampling with 2048 trajectories gets close (solving the racket-bounce sub-task) but produces sub-optimal final ball placement due to the high-dimensional MyoHand control; gradient-based MPC with CFD solves the full sequential task. On the on-table cube reorientation, only gradient-based MPC with CFD achieves a perfect solution (reorienting while maintaining position), while both sampling and gradient-without-CFD lose the target position. The key trade-off is runtime: sampling is substantially faster (35 min vs. 2.9–9.7 h for gradient MPC), but gradient-based methods scale to the 39–80+ actuator musculoskeletal systems where sampling suffers from the curse of dimensionality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on MuJoCo's penalty-based contact model (Todorov, 2014) and the Diffrax adaptive ODE integration library (Kidger, 2021), integrating the latter into MuJoCo XLA to correct gradient errors that prior work attributed to time-of-impact (TOI) discontinuities (Hu et al., 2020; Schwarke et al., 2024). The Contacts from Distance (CFD) mechanism extends contact-invariant optimization (Mordatch et al., 2012a,b) by applying the straight-through trick (Bengio et al., 2013) at the ODE level, so artificial contact forces affect only the backward pass rather than the forward simulation. Conceptually, the paper reframes the gradient-accuracy problem for penalty-based simulators as a standard stiff-ODE discretization issue solvable by adaptive timestepping—a well-studied technique in numerical analysis that had not been applied to differentiable robotics simulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DiffMJX provides the first demonstration of real-world parameter estimation (cube geometry and contact parameters) via gradient-based optimization through a penalty-based differentiable simulator, and the first evidence that autodiff-driven MPC with CFD can outperform sampling-based planning on high-dimensional musculoskeletal systems (39–63 actuators) where sampling suffers from the curse of dimensionality. Key limitations include the computational overhead of adaptive integration (JIT compilation, checkpointing), CFD's inability to encode grasping (non-convex optimization), and the absence of implicit differentiation of the constraint solver, which remains the main bottleneck for gradient speed. Open questions include whether these techniques extend to mesh-mesh collisions, whether implicit solver differentiation can further reduce gradient cost, and whether the approach generalizes to multi-contact scenes with extreme stiffness variations.
