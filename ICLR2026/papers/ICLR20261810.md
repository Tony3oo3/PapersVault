## Problem

While SGD's dynamics for single-index models are well understood in the non-interactive i.i.d. Gaussian setting, its behavior in the interactive bandit setting (generalized linear bandits / ridge bandits) remains largely unexplored. In particular, it is unclear whether a single SGD procedure can simultaneously achieve near-optimal sample complexity and regret across both the 'burn-in' phase (where the learner must discover a direction with Ω(1) alignment) and the 'learning' phase (where rapid convergence to the optimum occurs), especially for link functions whose derivative vanishes near zero.

## Input / Output

At each round the learner selects an action a_t on the unit sphere S^{d-1} via a structured exploration rule (a convex combination of the current estimate θ_t and a random direction orthogonal to it, controlled by σ_t), observes a scalar reward r_t = f(⟨θ*, a_t⟩) + ε_t, and updates θ_t via a spherical gradient step. The link function f: [-1,1]→[-1,1] is known and assumed monotone with local linearity near x=1. The output is the parameter trajectory {θ_t} on S^{d-1}, with theoretical guarantees expressed as high-probability bounds on the correlation m_t = ⟨θ*, θ_t⟩, cumulative regret, and total sample complexity T needed to reach a target accuracy ε.

## Method

The paper analyzes a first-order SGD procedure where the stochastic gradient is an unbiased estimator of the spherical gradient of the population loss, combined with a structured exploration strategy a_t = √(1−σ_t²)θ_t + σ_t Z_t (Z_t uniform on the orthogonal complement of θ_t). The core technical contribution is a three-term decomposition of the per-step correlation improvement m_{t+1} − m_t into (i) a deterministic drift term bounded via a spherical Stein's lemma, (ii) a martingale difference controlled by a self-normalized concentration inequality (Whitehouse et al., 2023), and (iii) a normalization error from the projection back to the sphere. By choosing epoch-wise learning rates η_t and exploration levels σ_t that depend on the current correlation regime, the analysis shows SGD transitions from a linear-rate burn-in phase (T = Õ(d² ∫ m/f′(m)² dm) for convex f, or Õ(d²) when f′ is bounded below) into a geometric-rate learning phase (T = Õ(d²/ε) for pure exploration, Õ(d√T) regret), matching known lower bounds. A key insight is that monotonicity of f is essentially necessary for SGD to succeed under the interactive exploration strategy, as a counterexample with a non-monotone link function shows SGD can be trapped at low correlation indefinitely.

## Results

For the learning phase (given a warm start with correlation ≥ 1 − γ₀/4), SGD achieves pure-exploration sample complexity T = Õ(d²/ε) and regret Õ(d√T), both matching known lower bounds. For the burn-in phase, under a derivative lower bound (Assumption 2) the cost is Õ(d²), while under convexity (Assumption 3) it is Õ(d² ∫₁/(₂√ᵈ)¹⁻ᵞ⁰/⁴ m/f′(m)² dm). Combining both phases, the overall regret is Õ(min{T, d² ∫ m/f′(m)² dm + d√T}), which for f(x) = xᵖ (odd p ≥ 3) yields Õ(min{T, dᵖ + d√T}), matching the best-known guarantees from successive-hypothesis-testing algorithms.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is theoretical: a single SGD procedure with appropriate (ηₜ, σₜ) schedules simultaneously achieves near-optimal guarantees in both the burn-in and learning phases, matching the best-known bounds from Rajaraman et al. (2024) who required a more complex algorithm combining successive hypothesis testing (for burn-in) and explore-then-commit (for learning). For f(x) = xᵖ with odd p ≥ 3, SGD attains Õ(dᵖ + d√T) regret, whereas all non-interactive algorithms (including non-interactive SGD) and UCB-based methods provably incur a larger burn-in cost of Õ(dᵖ⁺¹). The paper also contrasts with zeroth-order stochastic optimization (Huang et al., 2021), noting that SGD is a first-order method operating on the population loss rather than approximating gradients via zeroth-order oracles. Standard online-learning guarantees (e.g., sequential Rademacher complexity) are shown to be insufficient for single-index models, motivating the fine-grained SGD dynamics analysis. A counterexample (Proposition 1) demonstrates that monotonicity of f is essentially necessary for SGD to succeed under the proposed exploration strategy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the two-phase (search/descent) SGD dynamics analysis for single-index models from the non-interactive i.i.d. Gaussian setting (Ben Arous et al., 2021; Dudeja & Hsu, 2018) to the interactive bandit setting, where actions are chosen adaptively on the unit sphere rather than drawn i.i.d. It builds directly on the statistical complexity framework of Rajaraman et al. (2024), who established tight upper and lower bounds on the burn-in cost for generalized linear bandits using multi-phase algorithms (successive hypothesis testing + ETC), and on the self-normalized concentration inequalities of Whitehouse et al. (2023) for controlling martingale sums. The key departure is showing that a single, first-order SGD procedure with appropriate learning-rate and exploration schedules simultaneously matches these bounds in both the burn-in and learning phases, replacing the need for phase-specific algorithms or zeroth-order gradient estimation (Huang et al., 2021).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that a simple, unified SGD algorithm achieves near-optimal sample complexity and regret across both the burn-in and learning phases is practically significant: it eliminates the need for carefully designed phase-specific exploration strategies (e.g., successive hypothesis testing, noisy power methods) that prior work required, making the algorithm far easier to implement and tune. The paper also clarifies that the information exponent, which governs SGD sample complexity in the non-interactive Gaussian setting, becomes irrelevant in the interactive setting because the monotonicity of the link function forces the first Hermite coefficient to be nonzero, and the scaling of actions changes the effective SNR. Open questions include whether the convexity assumption (Assumption 3) can be dropped while retaining the sharp integral-based bounds, and whether the analysis extends to multi-index models or unknown link functions in the interactive setting.
