## Problem

When machine learning models are personalized with sensitive group attributes (e.g., race, sex, age), practitioners assume this improves both prediction accuracy and explanation quality, but this assumption has never been formally validated. Prior theoretical work on the Benefit of Personalization (BoP) was restricted to binary classification costs, leaving a gap for continuous metrics like regression loss and explanation quality measures. No unified framework existed to jointly evaluate whether personalization helps or harms prediction and explanation across demographic groups, nor to determine when such evaluation is statistically feasible.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a generic model h₀, a personalized model hₚ (which additionally receives k binary group attributes defining d=2^k subgroups), an audit dataset, a feature-attribution explanation method (Integrated Gradients, DeepLIFT, or Shapley Value Sampling), and a practitioner-chosen improvement threshold ε in cost-function units. It produces group-level G-BoP values for prediction (loss, AUC, R²) and explanation (sufficiency, incomprehensiveness), the overall BoP γ as the minimum across groups, a minimax lower bound on the probability of error P_e that depends on group sizes m_j, the number of attributes k, and the distribution of individual BoPs (categorical for classification; Gaussian or Laplace for regression), and a binary hypothesis test decision. A key structural detail is that in classification the lower bound is identical for prediction and explanation (both use 0-1 loss), while in regression the bounds can differ, making one testable and the other not.

## Method

The paper establishes four theorems proving that prediction and explanation BoP can diverge: a model can match generic accuracy while gaining or losing explainability (Theorems 4.1–4.3), with a partial converse under additive linear models where no explanation benefit implies no prediction benefit (Theorem 4.4). For practical evaluation, the core technical contribution is a minimax lower bound on the probability of error for any hypothesis test on BoP (Theorem 5.1), derived via total-variation arguments and specialized to exponential-family distributions (Gaussian, Laplace) through moment-generating functions (Corollary 5.2). This bound quantifies how group sizes, the number of personal attributes, and the spread of individual BoPs jointly determine whether a test is reliable, yielding closed-form minimum sample size requirements (Corollary 5.3) and maximum testable attribute counts. The framework is applied to MIMIC-III (length-of-stay regression and classification), UCI Heart Disease, and MIMIC-III Kidney Injury, revealing that even large empirical BoP estimates (e.g., γ̂=0.19 for sufficiency) can be statistically untestable when the lower bound on P_e exceeds 40%, while smaller effects (γ̂=0.002 for prediction) may be reliably detected.

## Results

On MIMIC-III (N=13,337), personalization by Age×Race yields divergent effects: for regression, the minimal BoP for prediction is +0.0021 (all groups benefit) while for incomprehensiveness it is −0.0906 (White 45+ harmed), and for sufficiency it is +0.1914 (all groups benefit). For classification, the minimal BoP for prediction is −0.0216 (NonWhite 18–45 harmed) and for incomprehensiveness is −0.0226 (White 45+ harmed). Critically, the minimax lower bound on probability of error exceeds 40% for all classification metrics (making hypothesis testing fundamentally impossible at ε=0.002), while for regression it exceeds 40% for sufficiency but falls below 5% for prediction and incomprehensiveness, rendering only the latter two statistically testable.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against competing prediction or explanation methods; instead, its core comparison is between a generic model h₀ and a personalized model hₚ across demographic groups, evaluated on both prediction accuracy and explanation quality. The key empirical finding is that prediction and explanation benefits diverge: on MIMIC-III regression, personalization improves prediction for all groups (minimal BoP = +0.0021) yet harms incomprehensiveness for White 45+ (−0.0906), while simultaneously improving sufficiency for all groups (minimal BoP = +0.1914). Across explanation methods (Integrated Gradients, DeepLIFT, Shapley Value Sampling), the qualitative pattern of divergence is consistent, though effect sizes differ. The theoretical contribution extends Monteiro Paes et al. (2022) from binary/categorical BoP to continuous costs (Gaussian, Laplace), yielding tighter and more general minimax lower bounds on test error. The paper also contrasts classification (where the categorical nature of 0-1 loss makes prediction and explanation identically untestable) with regression (where continuous BoP distributions allow asymmetric testability across metrics).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Benefit of Personalization (BoP) framework introduced by Monteiro Paes et al. (2022), which was restricted to binary classification with categorical costs, to general supervised learning tasks including regression with continuous costs. It departs from prior work by introducing explanation quality (sufficiency and incomprehensiveness) as a first-class evaluation axis alongside prediction accuracy, and by deriving distribution-aware minimax lower bounds on hypothesis test error that apply to any known distribution rather than only the categorical case. The paper also connects to the XAI faithfulness literature (DeYoung et al., 2020; Nauta et al., 2023) and the group fairness literature, but positions itself as studying a relaxed fairness criterion—whether any group is harmed—rather than proposing corrective algorithms.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding that personalization's effect on prediction does not determine its effect on explanation quality challenges a widespread assumption in XAI practice that better prediction implies better explanation, and motivates joint evaluation of both. The practical implication is stark: for classification tasks at typical medical dataset sizes (N ∈ {10², 10³, 10⁴}), the probability of error in testing personalization effects exceeds 40%, making reliable validation fundamentally infeasible regardless of the hypothesis test used. Open questions include whether the additive-model assumption in Theorem 4.4 (the only setting where prediction and explanation effects provably align) can be relaxed to general models, and whether the framework extends to other desiderata such as robustness, uncertainty calibration, or fairness metrics beyond group-level BoP.
