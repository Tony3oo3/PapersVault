## Problem

Human semantic categories achieve near-optimal compression via the Information Bottleneck (IB) principle, but LLMs are not trained for this objective. It remains unclear whether LLMs possess an intrinsic inductive bias toward IB-efficient, human-aligned categorization or merely mimic surface patterns in their training data. This paper asks whether LLMs can evolve efficient semantic systems through cultural-transmission-like processes, and whether the resulting efficiency reflects a genuine inductive bias rather than memorized language-specific patterns.

## Input / Output

The 330 WCS color chips are presented either as sRGB coordinate triples in text or as colored square images for multimodal models. In the IICLL study, the model receives a small in-context training set of 6× vocabulary-size feature-label pairs (using nonsense pseudo-labels, with stimuli described only as having 'features' rather than 'colors') plus a sliding window of 10 recent interactions, and must produce a label for every chip in the grid. The collective output forms a full naming system evaluated on the IB information plane via efficiency loss ε and Normalized Information Distance alignment to English, WCS languages, and IB-optimal systems.

## Method

The paper introduces Iterated In-Context Language Learning (IICLL), a prompt-based extension of Zhu & Griffiths (2024)'s I-ICL paradigm that simulates cultural transmission of category systems in LLMs. At each generation, the LLM is prompted with a small sample of stimulus-pseudo-label pairs from the previous generation's system and asked to label all 330 WCS colors; the resulting full mapping becomes the next generation's language, repeated for up to 13 generations. Constrained generation is enforced via log-probability scoring of allowed terms (open-weight models) or the Gemini API's controlled generation. The authors evaluate 39 LLMs across six families on an English color naming task, then run IICLL with four strong instruction-tuned models (Gemini 2.0, Gemma 3 27B, Qwen 2.5 32B, Llama 3.3 70B) across vocabulary sizes k ∈ {2,3,4,5,6,14}, comparing trajectories to human IL data from Xu et al. (2013) and the IB bound from Zaslavsky et al. (2018). A rotation analysis and a nearest-neighbor baseline further test whether emergent systems are non-trivially efficient and aligned to human color categories.

## Results

Across 39 LLMs from 6 model families, larger instruction-tuned models achieve the best English-alignment and IB-efficiency, with Gemini 2.0 and Gemma 3 27B (inst.) most closely approximating the English color naming system from Lindsey & Brown (2014). In the IICLL cultural evolution experiment, all four tested frontier models (Gemini 2.0, Gemma 3 27B, Qwen 2.5 32B, Llama 3.3 70B) restructure random initial systems toward greater IB-efficiency, converging near the IB bound after roughly 4 generations. However, only Gemini 2.0 recapitulates the full range of near-optimal IB tradeoffs observed across human languages (WCS + English), while the other three models converge to lower-complexity solutions. In the k=14 condition, Gemini's evolved systems achieve higher efficiency, IB-alignment, and WCS-alignment than both the human IL trajectories from Xu et al. (2013) and a Nearest Neighbor sRGB baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Lindsey & Brown (2014) English color naming system, no LLM achieves perfect alignment, but Gemini 2.0 and Gemma 3 27B (inst.) come closest in both English-alignment (1−NID) and IB complexity, while many state-of-the-art models (e.g., Llama 3.3 70B inst., Olmo 2 32B inst.) produce systems with substantially lower complexity or category structures resembling low-resource WCS languages rather than English. Against the human iterated learning data from Xu et al. (2013), Gemini 2.0's IICLL trajectories span the same complexity range as the 20 human IL chains (k=2–6) and the 14-term English condition, and its final-generation systems show broad qualitative fit to WCS languages; the other three LLMs also reach near-optimal IB solutions but are restricted to the lower-complexity end of the human range. In the direct comparison with a Nearest Neighbor sRGB classifier baseline (Appendix M, k=14 condition), Gemini 2.0 significantly outperforms the NN baseline on all three metrics (efficiency loss, IB-alignment, and WCS-alignment) across generations, while the open-weight LLMs (Gemma, Qwen, Llama) perform significantly worse than the NN baseline, indicating that Gemini's emergent category structure cannot be explained by simple feature-based clustering.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Information Bottleneck framework for semantic systems (Zaslavsky et al., 2018) from human languages to LLMs, and adapts the Iterated In-Context Learning paradigm (Zhu & Griffiths, 2024) into a new method called IICLL that simulates cultural transmission of category systems. While prior work (Marjieh et al., 2024; Abdou et al., 2021) tested a small number of LLMs on color naming, this paper scales to 39 models and, crucially, uses IICLL to distinguish whether LLMs merely mimic training-data patterns or possess an intrinsic inductive bias toward IB-efficiency. The experimental design directly replicates two influential human studies (Lindsey & Brown, 2014; Xu et al., 2013), enabling quantitative comparison on the same information plane.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that IB-efficiency emerges in LLMs without explicit training for it suggests a shared computational principle underlying human and machine semantic organization, with implications for how we design and evaluate LLMs for human-aligned communication. However, the result is model-dependent: only Gemini 2.0 recapitulates the full range of near-optimal IB tradeoffs seen in humans, while other frontier models collapse to low-complexity solutions, raising open questions about the origins of this bias (model size, instruction-tuning, in-context learning capacity) and whether it generalizes beyond color. The paper also reveals a practical gap—many state-of-the-art models fail to recover the English color naming system despite training on massive English corpora—which has direct consequences for generative AI applications requiring grounded color semantics.
