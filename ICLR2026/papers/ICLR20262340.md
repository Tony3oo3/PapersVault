## Problem

Task and motion planning (TAMP) systems rely on manually defined skills (pick, place, move) that produce long, inefficient plans because they cannot discover dynamic physical improvisations like slapping, wiggling, or wiping. Pure RL struggles with these long-horizon, sparse-reward manipulation tasks, and existing hierarchical RL methods fail to learn skill selection sequences when many grounded skills are available. The paper asks how an agent can autonomously learn new low-level shortcuts that reduce execution time while preserving the long-horizon reasoning and generalization of abstract planning.

## Input / Output

The method takes an initial state x₀, a goal specification g, a known physics simulator (transition function f), and a finite set of predefined options A with PDDL planning operators. At evaluation, it also consumes learned shortcut policies Â. The output is an execution-time-minimizing action trajectory τ found via Dijkstra's on the bottom level of a two-level abstract planning graph, where edges correspond to either predefined option executions or learned shortcut policy rollouts. Shortcut edges are validated at test time: if the terminal abstract state is not reached within T_eval steps, the edge is pruned and the planner falls back to pure planning.

## Method

SLAP identifies shortcut candidates as pairs of abstract states (s_init, s_term) that are not directly connected by any predefined option in the abstract planning graph, then prunes infeasible candidates via random rollouts (keeping only those reached in fewer than K_rollout out of N_rollout episodes). Each surviving shortcut induces an indefinite-horizon MDP with reward -1 per step and terminal states at s_term, and a PPO policy is trained in parallel across all shortcut MDPs using initial states sampled from the planning graphs. At evaluation, the same abstract planner runs with learned shortcuts added to the option set, and Dijkstra's on the bottom level automatically selects shortcuts when they yield shorter plans. Generalization over objects is achieved by projecting observations onto only the relevant objects (those appearing in the shortcut's add/delete atoms) and, at test time, finding a type-preserving injective object mapping that preserves symbolic effects, allowing the same policy to be deployed with different or additional objects.

## Results

SLAP achieves 100% success rate across all four environments while reducing plan length by 32% (Obstacle 2D), 68% (Obstacle Tower), 53% (Cluttered Drawer), and 73% (Cleanup Table) relative to Pure Planning. In the three PyBullet environments, all pure RL baselines (PPO, SAC+HER) and hierarchical RL baselines (Hierarchical RL, SOL) achieve 0% success rate, while SLAP consistently solves the tasks. The discovered shortcuts (e.g., 'slap', 'wiggle', 'wipe') enable multi-object physical interactions that transcend the single-object contact assumptions of the predefined TAMP skills.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SLAP decisively outperforms all RL-based baselines on the three PyBullet environments (Obstacle Tower, Cluttered Drawer, Cleanup Table), where PPO, SAC+HER, Hierarchical RL (PPO), and SOL all achieve 0% success rate after 500K–50M training steps, while SLAP reaches 100% success with plan lengths 53–73% shorter than Pure Planning. On the simpler Obstacle 2D environment, Hierarchical RL (PPO) and SOL also achieve 100% success but with only 2–4% plan length reduction, whereas SLAP achieves a 32% reduction. The paper attributes the RL baselines' failure to the sparsity of rewards (received only at task completion) and the difficulty of learning skill selection sequences over large numbers of grounded skills (e.g., 216 in Obstacle Tower for SOL). SLAP's advantage stems from decomposing the problem: the abstract planner handles long-horizon reasoning and goal generalization, while RL only needs to learn short, focused shortcut policies between adjacent abstract states.

## Relation to Prior Work

SLAP sits at the intersection of Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) and hierarchical reinforcement learning, but deliberately departs from both. Rather than learning state or action abstractions from scratch (as in Silver et al. 2022, 2023) or discovering skills tabula rasa via entropy-based objectives (Kulkarni et al. 2016, Eysenbach et al. 2018), SLAP assumes a fixed set of manually defined options and uses the abstract planning graph they induce to identify where learned shortcuts would reduce execution time. It also differs from prior work that accelerates the planning process itself (learned heuristics, compiled policies) or learns recovery policies for execution failures; instead, SLAP learns new low-level behaviors that the planner can optionally use, making it a plug-and-play module on top of existing TAMP systems. The bilevel graph construction follows Li et al. (2025) and Silver et al. (2022), while the object-substitution mechanism for generalization draws on the relational inductive bias of Diuk et al. (2008).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SLAP demonstrates that a simple RL-based shortcut learner can discover dynamic, multi-object physical behaviors (slap, wiggle, wipe) that transcend the single-object contact assumptions baked into standard TAMP options, reducing plan lengths by 50–73% while maintaining 100% success rates where pure RL achieves 0%. The relational generalization mechanism—reusing a shortcut policy across different numbers of objects via type-preserving substitution—offers a practical path toward scalable TAMP without retraining per scene. Key limitations include the inability to deviate from the user-provided problem decomposition, the assumption that given options are sufficient for task success, and the lack of safety guarantees for learned shortcuts. Open directions include soft decompositions that can be further improved by hierarchical RL, integration with learned abstractions, safety-constrained shortcut learning, and real-to-sim-to-real transfer.
