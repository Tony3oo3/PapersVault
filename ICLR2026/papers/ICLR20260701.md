## Problem

Under network interference, standard A/B testing estimators like Horvitz-Thompson suffer from explosive variance due to extreme inverse-probability weights on boundary nodes, while simpler alternatives like the cluster-adaptive estimator still exclude most boundary units. This exclusion introduces selection bias when interior nodes are not representative of the full population in terms of network-dependent covariates (e.g., degree, number of connected clusters), a discrepancy that is empirically confirmed on billion-scale social platforms but unaddressed by existing estimators.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a known undirected network graph, pre-generated cluster assignments (e.g., from Louvain with a resolution parameter), cluster-level Bernoulli treatment assignments, observed unit outcomes, and node covariates (degree, number of distinct connected clusters, and optionally demographic features). It outputs a single point estimate of the global average treatment effect (GATE). Notably, the GNN counterfactual predictor is trained on the entire network including boundary nodes, but the final estimator only uses observed outcomes from interior nodes, with the predictor serving solely as an adjustment mechanism.

## Method

The paper proposes the Mean-in-Interior (MII) estimator, which simply averages observed outcomes over interior nodes (units whose 1-hop neighbors all lie within the same cluster) without any reweighting, achieving the lowest variance among existing estimators under the neighborhood interference assumption. To correct the selection bias arising from interior-boundary covariate discrepancies, the Augmented MII (AMII) estimator adds an adjustment term computed from a counterfactual predictor (a GNN with Chebyshev convolution layers) trained on the full network: the difference between the predictor's mean over all units and its mean over treated/control interior nodes. By algebraic rearrangement, AMII is shown to be an analytical instance of the prediction-powered inference (PPI) point estimator, where interior nodes serve as labeled data subject to selection bias and boundary nodes contribute covariate information. Theoretically, under a partial-linear potential outcome model, the AMII bias reduces from α(μ_Int − μ) to (E[α̂_n] − α)(μ − μ_Int), a substantial reduction when the linear component is correctly specified.

## Results

On the Facebook network (11,586 nodes, 95 clusters, γ=5) with 2-hop interference (r₂=1) and p=0.1, the AMII estimator achieves MSE 1.311, compared to 2.520 (Hajek), 2.538 (CAE), 2.239 (MII), and 4.494 (GNN) — a 48–71% MSE reduction over all baselines. Under NIA (r₂=0) at p=0.3, AMII reaches MSE 0.088 versus 1.001 (Hajek) and 0.937 (CAE), a >90% improvement. The MII estimator consistently achieves the lowest standard deviation across all settings (e.g., 0.274 at p=0.1, r₂=1 vs. 0.575 for Hajek), while AMII adds a bias-reduction adjustment that further lowers MSE without inflating variance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Hajek estimator (Ugander & Yin, 2023), AMII reduces MSE by 58% at p=0.1 (0.526 vs. 1.243), 91% at p=0.3 (0.088 vs. 1.001), and 96% at p=0.5 (0.037 vs. 0.901) under NIA; the gap widens further under 2-hop interference (r₂=1) where Hajek's MSE reaches 2.520 at p=0.1 versus AMII's 1.311. Against the CAE (Liu et al., 2024), AMII similarly dominates: MSE drops from 1.014 to 0.526 at p=0.1 and from 0.937 to 0.088 at p=0.3 under NIA, and from 2.538 to 1.311 at p=0.1 under 2-hop interference. The MII estimator (the paper's simpler variant without the regression adjustment) matches or beats both baselines on variance (Std 0.242 vs. 0.558 Hajek, 0.342 CAE at p=0.1) but carries similar bias (~0.95), confirming that the covariate interaction term is the primary source of bias that AMII's adjustment term corrects. The GNN baseline performs poorly at low p (MSE 3.235 at p=0.1, r₂=0) due to insufficient exposure diversity, but recovers at p=0.5 where it approaches AMII's MSE (0.198 vs. 0.037), highlighting that AMII's advantage is most pronounced in early-stage experiments with small treatment proportions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the cluster-adaptive estimator (CAE; Liu et al., 2024) by showing that its bilevel averaging over clean nodes can be collapsed into a single average over interior nodes, yielding the simpler MII estimator with substantially lower variance. It further connects to the prediction-powered inference (PPI) framework (Angelopoulos et al., 2023) by demonstrating that the augmented MII estimator, which adds a counterfactual predictor trained on the full network, is an analytical instance of the PPI point estimator applied to a semi-supervised setting with selection bias. The work sits within the broader graph cluster randomization literature (Ugander et al., 2013; Ugander & Yin, 2023) and departs from the HT estimator's inverse-probability weighting by arguing that the exponential weights are the primary source of variance inflation and can be replaced with moderate, equal weights over interior nodes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically critical problem—GATE estimation in industrial A/B testing on social platforms—where the HT estimator's variance is often unusable and the CAE's structural assumptions are fragile under heuristic clustering. The MII/AMII framework offers a simpler, lower-variance alternative with a clean theoretical connection to PPI, and the 'debiasing using predictions' framing for selection bias correction is of independent interest. However, all validation is simulation-based on a single Facebook network, with no real-world A/B test results, and the method still relies on the neighborhood interference assumption and a correctly specified linear component in the regression model. Open questions include deriving valid confidence intervals (CLT) under network interference, extending to higher-order interference beyond 1-hop, and validating on actual platform experiments where ground-truth GATE is unavailable.
