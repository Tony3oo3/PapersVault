## Problem

Benchmarking hundreds of functional connectivity (FC) operators (statistical pairwise interactions, SPIs) on large-scale fMRI datasets is computationally prohibitive, preventing systematic model comparison from becoming a routine pre-analysis step. Existing core-set selection methods target single-model training loss rather than preserving the relative performance ranking across many candidate operators, leaving a gap in how to select a small, representative subset that faithfully reproduces the full-dataset SPI ordering.

## Input / Output

The method consumes fMRI time series as N×T matrices (N ROIs, T time points) segmented via sliding windows, along with subject identity labels used as the contrastive supervision signal. It produces a core-set of c samples (c ≪ |X|) whose sole purpose is to preserve the SPI performance ranking, quantified by nDCG@k concordance with the full-dataset ranking. The intermediate outputs include per-sample Structural Perturbation Scores (SPS) measuring attention-map stability across training epochs, and learned attention-based FC structure matrices that serve as structural probes rather than SPI emulations.

## Method

SCLCS is a self-supervised framework built around an adaptive multi-head Transformer encoder that fuses head-specific attention matrices via learnable softmax-normalized weights (rather than uniform averaging), which Theorem 1 shows is necessary to avoid entropy inflation and Theorem 2 shows provides universal approximation capacity for continuous SPI mappings. The key selection criterion is the Structural Perturbation Score (SPS), which measures the cumulative Frobenius-norm change in a sample's attention structure across L training epochs; low-SPS samples represent foundational, structurally stable connectivity archetypes. To counteract the persistent bias of naive top-k selection (formalized in Theorem 3), SCLCS_Dense applies Gaussian KDE-based inverse-density weighting within the bottom (1−β) SPS quantile to up-weight samples from sparser structural regions. Training uses an identity-supervised contrastive objective (InfoNCE with cosine similarity) that encourages consistent representations across temporal segments from the same subject, yielding task-agnostic structural representations suitable for benchmarking.

## Results

On the REST-meta-MDD dataset (4,520 fMRI samples, 130 SPIs), SCLCS preserves the ground-truth SPI ranking with only 10% of the data, achieving 81.21% nDCG@5 for brain fingerprinting—outperforming the best SOTA baseline (AUM, 65.92%) by 23.2%. For MDD diagnosis, SCLCS_Dense reaches 81.73% nDCG@5 at the 10% ratio, substantially ahead of all baselines. The SPS_MHA ablation (naive attention averaging) collapses to ~1–3% nDCG, empirically confirming Theorem 1's prediction that uniform head averaging destroys structural specificity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SCLCS (top-k SPS selection) dominates all 8 baselines on brain fingerprinting at the 10% ratio: it beats the strongest baseline AUM (65.92%) by 15.3 points in nDCG@5, and outperforms EVA (38.40%), Entropy (47.40%), EL2N (35.56%), BOSS (15.98%), k-Means (17.32%), Forgetting (14.41%), and CCS (1.90%) by wide margins. At the 30% ratio, SCLCS_Dense (density-aware variant) takes over as the best method (79.18% nDCG@5), consistent with Theorem 3's prediction that naive top-k over-represents dense clusters. For MDD diagnosis (a cohort-level task), SCLCS_Dense is the clear winner across all sampling ratios, reaching 81.73% nDCG@5 at 10% versus BOSS's 42.44% and Forgetting's 28.70%. The SPS_MHA ablation (replacing adaptive fusion with uniform averaging) drops to 1.32% nDCG@5, confirming that the adaptive attention mechanism is essential. Overall, the paper demonstrates that methods designed for single-model training acceleration (Forgetting, EL2N, AUM, EVA, BOSS) do not transfer well to the ranking-preservation objective, while structure-aware selection via SPS provides a consistent advantage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the standard core-set selection literature (Feldman 2020; Coleman et al. 2020; Hong et al. 2024b), which constructs small training proxies for a single predictive model, by reframing the objective as preserving the relative performance ranking across hundreds of candidate functional connectivity (FC) operators (SPIs). It builds on the pyspi benchmarking framework (Cliff et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2025) that formalized SPI evaluation but left the computational infeasibility of exhaustive benchmarking unaddressed. The adaptive multi-head attention fusion is motivated by a new interference theorem (Theorem 1) showing that uniform head averaging blurs structural patterns, and the density-balanced sampling corrects a persistent bias in top-k selection (Theorem 3), both of which are novel contributions absent from prior core-set or structure-learning work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By reducing a ~990 CPU-day exhaustive SPI benchmark to roughly 18.5 hours at 10% sampling, SCLCS makes large-scale FC operator comparison a practical pre-analysis step, directly addressing a long-standing reproducibility bottleneck in computational neuroscience. Key limitations include validation on a single dataset (REST-meta-MDD), dependence on stationarity/ergodicity assumptions for SPS consistency, and the observed non-monotonic performance at intermediate sampling ratios that reveals residual brittleness. Open questions include how approximation error in the attention-based structural probe propagates to ranking preservation, whether the framework generalizes to other neuroimaging modalities or clinical populations, and whether the task-dependent choice between SCLCS and SCLCS_Dense can be predicted a priori.
