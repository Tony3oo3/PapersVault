## Problem

While autoencoders are widely used to learn compact latent representations, there is no unified dynamical-systems framework that explains how the latent space is organized and how this organization relates to model behavior. Prior work on memorization in overparameterized AEs requires strong assumptions, and the role of inductive biases (weight decay, data augmentation, bottleneck constraints) in shaping latent-space structure remains uncharacterized. This paper asks: can we extract a principled, training-free representation of an AE's behavior directly from its latent-space dynamics?

## Input / Output

The method requires only a trained autoencoder (no additional training or input data). For data-free probing, the sole input is Gaussian noise sampled in latent space (N=4096 points matching the latent dimensionality k), and the output is a dictionary of attractors used for OMP-based reconstruction of held-out images across diverse domains. For OOD detection, the input is a test image encoded into latent space, and the output is a scalar score computed as the mean Euclidean distance along the trajectory to the nearest training attractor, compared against a KNN baseline. The memorization coefficient is computed as the cosine similarity between each decoded attractor and its nearest training sample.

## Method

The core mechanism is iterating the composite map f(z) = E∘D(z) in latent space, which defines a discrete dynamical system (equivalently, a latent vector field V(z) = f(z) − z). The paper proves that standard training inductive biases—weight decay, data augmentation, and bottleneck dimension constraints—make f locally contractive (spectral norm of J_f < 1), guaranteeing the existence of attractors via Banach's fixed-point theorem. These attractors form a dictionary whose structure encodes the memorization–generalization spectrum: strong regularization (small k) yields many training-data attractors (memorization), while weaker regularization yields fewer, more general attractors. For OOD detection, the trajectory of a test point through the vector field (not just its endpoint) is used as a discriminative signal, outperforming KNN and Mahalanobis baselines. For data-free weight probing, attractors computed purely from noise serve as a signal dictionary that reconstructs diverse datasets better than a random orthogonal basis, revealing semantic information stored in the weights without any input data.

## Results

For OOD detection on ViT-MAE (pretrained on ImageNet), the latent trajectory score d(Attractors) achieves FPR95 of 25.9–29.9% and AUROC of 91.0–92.6% across SUN397, Places365, Texture, and iNaturalist, substantially outperforming the KNN baseline (FPR95 34.5–100%, AUROC 32.4–89.4%) and Mahalanobis distance (FPR95 31.0–88.0%, AUROC 45.0–90.0%). In the data-free weight probing experiment on the Stable Diffusion AE, attractors computed from Gaussian noise consistently yield lower reconstruction error than a random orthogonal basis across all six evaluated datasets (Laion2B, ImageNet, EuroSAT, CIFAR-100, PatchCamelyon, Places365) at every sparsity level tested (256–2048 atoms).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed latent trajectory score d(Attractors) decisively outperforms the KNN baseline [Sun et al., 2022] on all four OOD detection benchmarks: FPR95 drops from 100% to ~30% on SUN397 and Places365 (where KNN essentially fails), and AUROC improves from 42.6→91.2 (SUN397) and 32.4→91.0 (Places365). Against the Mahalanobis distance baseline, d(Attractors) wins on FPR95 across all datasets (e.g., 29.6 vs. 82.0 on SUN397) and on AUROC for SUN397 (91.2 vs. 58.0) and Places365 (91.0 vs. 45.0), though Mahalanobis is competitive on Texture (90.0 vs. 92.6) and iNaturalist (87.0 vs. 91.3). The reconstruction-error baseline is uniformly the weakest, with FPR95 above 91% on three of four datasets. In the data-free probing experiment, attractors from noise beat the orthogonal-basis baseline on reconstruction MSE at every sparsity level across all six datasets, confirming that the attractor dictionary captures structured signal from the weights without any input data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the connection between denoising autoencoders and score functions (Alain & Bengio, 2014; Vincent, 2011) into a general framework: any autoencoder implicitly defines a latent vector field by iterating the encoder-decoder map, whose attractors encode memorization and generalization properties. It subsumes prior results on overparameterized AEs as associative memory (Radhakrishnan et al., 2020; Jiang & Pehlevan, 2020) by showing memorization is merely one attractor regime, and unifies contractive AEs, denoising AEs, and sparse AEs under a single contractivity-based analysis. Unlike Neural ODEs or Deep Equilibrium Models that treat depth or implicit dynamics as the system, this work focuses specifically on the latent-space iteration of the autoencoding map as a dynamical system, linking its geometry to the learned data distribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The latent vector field provides a unified geometric lens connecting memorization, generalization, and OOD detection through a single object (the attractor set and its basins), offering practitioners a data-free diagnostic tool for probing what information is stored in foundation model weights. The data-free probing result—where attractors from pure Gaussian noise outperform an orthogonal basis for reconstructing diverse datasets—is practically useful for auditing pretrained models without access to training data. Key limitations include the restriction to autoencoder architectures (classifiers and SSL models require a surrogate decoder), preliminary (not yet rigorous) extensions to LLMs, and OOD detection baselines that are relatively weak (KNN, Mahalanobis, reconstruction error). Open questions include how attractor basins evolve under fine-tuning, whether vector fields can be aligned across models for representation comparison, and whether the framework extends to non-autoencoding objectives.
