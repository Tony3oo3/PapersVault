## Problem

LLM-based theorem provers have achieved strong results on competition-level full-proof generation, but their ability to act as practical copilots for mathematicians—filling in short, nontrivial proof obligations (subgoals) within complex research-level proofs—remains largely unexplored. Existing benchmarks emphasize Olympiad-style problems with simple contexts and no premise requirements, leaving a gap in evaluating provers on the subgoal completion task that mirrors real formal proving workflows.

## Input / Output

Each problem is a Lean 4 subgoal extracted at the line level from a procedural proof, consisting of a theorem statement with accumulated hypotheses (the proof state before the target tactic line) and an optional set of candidate premises drawn from Mathlib or a local library. The model must produce a Lean 4 tactic sequence that discharges the subgoal. Evaluation uses Pass@K with deliberately low computational budgets (≤32 samples for whole-proof generation, ≤8×50 expansions for BFS), reflecting the expectation that subgoals should be solved efficiently rather than through exhaustive search.

## Method

The paper introduces a custom Lean 4 tactic `to_theorem` that converts procedural proof lines into declarative subgoals by recording the proof state before and after a tactic sequence, then synthesizing a theorem whose hypotheses are the prior state and whose goal is the subsequent state. Applying this tactic to top-level theorems in the Optlib and FoML libraries yields 4,937 subgoal problems spanning optimization algorithms (GD, SubGD, PGD, NAG, BCD, ADMM) and probability inequalities (Hoeffding, McDiarmid, Rademacher, Bennett, Bernstein), with difficulty stratified by proof length (1, 3, or 5 tactics). The evaluation framework systematically benchmarks SOTA provers—both best-first tree-search (Reprover, BFS-Prover) and whole-proof generation (STP, Goedel-Prover, DeepSeek-Prover-V2, Kimina-Prover, Leanabell-Prover)—along four axes: raw accuracy, premise utilization under varying candidate set sizes, performance degradation across difficulty levels, and an Efficiency-Weighted Accuracy (EWA@K) metric that penalizes verbose long-CoT outputs. Expert iteration on 88,174 problems from five Lean repositories is shown to substantially improve Pass@1 performance.

## Results

On the FormalML benchmark (4,937 subgoal completion problems), STP achieves the highest overall Pass@32 of 63.21% and the best Pass@1 of 26.96%, while DeepSeek-Prover-V2 (noCoT) follows closely at 62.06% Pass@32. Notably, the CoT variant of DeepSeek-Prover-V2 drops sharply to 39.50% Pass@32, and all best-first tree-search methods (Reprover, BFS-Prover) remain below 30% even at the 8×50 budget. The strongest automation tactic (aesop) reaches 43.29% Pass@1, and the full ensemble of tactics reaches 48.67%, but both are outperformed by LLM-based provers at higher sample budgets. The paper's central finding is that no current prover is adequate for practical subgoal completion under low computational budgets, as even the best Pass@1 score is only ~27%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

STP (Dong & Ma, 2025) is the strongest overall, achieving 63.21% Pass@32 and 26.96% Pass@1, outperforming DeepSeek-Prover-V2 noCoT (62.06% / 16.85%) and Leanabell-Prover (58.07% / 24.10%). DeepSeek-Prover-V2 noCoT is competitive at high budgets but its CoT variant collapses to 39.50% Pass@32, a 22.5-point drop, revealing that long chain-of-thought reasoning is counterproductive for subgoal completion. Goedel-Prover-V2-8B (49.26% Pass@32) and Kimina-Prover-Preview-7B (45.74%) trail the leaders, while Goedel-Prover (45.07%) is the weakest whole-proof model. Among tree-search methods, BFS-Prover (25.31% at 8×50) marginally edges out Reprover (24.79%), but both are far below the best whole-proof generators. The aesop tactic (43.29%) and the full tactic ensemble (48.67%) are competitive at zero sample budget but are surpassed by all LLM-based provers once multiple rollouts are allowed, confirming that LLMs capture reasoning patterns beyond rule-based search.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a new evaluation task—subgoal completion—by repurposing existing Lean 4 libraries (Optlib, FoML) and introducing a custom tactic (to_theorem) that extracts intermediate proof obligations from procedural-style proofs. It departs from prior benchmarks (MiniF2F, PutnamBench, ProofNet, FormalMATH, LeanDojo, MiniCTX) that all target full-proof generation on competition or undergraduate problems, by focusing on research-level ML theorems where the model must discharge a single step within a complex, premise-laden context. The evaluation framework draws on both tree-search provers (Reprover, BFS-Prover) and whole-proof generation models (STP, Goedel-Prover, DeepSeek-Prover, Kimina-Prover, Leanabell-Prover), but applies them to a fundamentally different task: concise, context-aware step completion rather than end-to-end proof synthesis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a practical gap: even the best model (STP) achieves only 26.96% Pass@1, far below what a usable mathematician's copilot would require, and long-CoT provers that excel on competition problems actually underperform and waste tokens on subgoal completion. The finding that providing more candidate premises (M=10, M=20) degrades performance for most models highlights premise utilization as an underexplored bottleneck. Limitations include the narrow scope (ML theory only, Lean 4 only) and the fact that difficulty is proxied by proof length rather than a more principled measure. Open questions include whether expert iteration can close the gap to practical usability, whether the long-CoT inefficiency generalizes beyond ML theory, and how to design provers that balance accuracy with token efficiency in complex contexts.
