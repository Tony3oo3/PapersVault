## Problem

While the geometry of ReLU network parameter spaces has been studied for layered architectures, general DAG-based networks (arising from skip connections, pruning, or custom designs) lack a complete characterization of two critical training pathologies: whether the gradient-flow-invariant parameter space is connected, and where singularities lie within it. Prior work (Nurisso et al., 2024) addressed only shallow networks, leaving the multi-layer and general DAG case open.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a DAG neural network topology (node set, edge set, designated input/output neurons), an initialization that fixes the per-neuron balance vector c = B̃θ(0)², and a training dataset. It produces a topological characterization of the invariant set H_G(c) (connected or disconnected, with the specific bottleneck conditions responsible), identifies singular configurations corresponding to disconnected sub-networks, and—when a nuclear norm regularizer is applied—yields a pruned network with neurons provably disconnected from both input and output. The balance vector c is the key intermediate object: it is determined entirely at initialization and conserved throughout training, making it a compact summary of the optimization geometry.

## Method

The paper reformulates the rescaling-symmetry conservation laws of ReLU networks in the general DAG case using the graph incidence matrix B̃, showing that gradient flow constrains parameters to the algebraic variety H_G(c) = {θ : B̃θ² = c}, a system of coaxial quadric hypersurfaces. Connectedness is then characterized (Theorem 1) by reducing the problem to network flow feasibility at each bottleneck neuron: the invariant set is connected if and only if every forward-closed set of pure ancestors of an out-bottleneck has non-negative total balance, and every backward-closed set of pure descendants of an in-bottleneck has non-positive total balance. Singularities are identified via the Jacobian J_G(θ) = 2B̃diag(θ), whose rank deficiency (Theorem 2) is shown to correspond exactly to sub-networks disconnected from both input and output; the paper further proves these singularities are unreachable under gradient flow from generic initializations (Propositions 6–7). To make singularities practically accessible, a nuclear norm regularizer ‖J_G(θ)‖_* is introduced as a smooth surrogate for minimizing Jacobian rank, enabling differentiable, topology-agnostic structured pruning; experiments on Breast Cancer, MNIST, Cats-and-Dogs ViT features, and CelebA confirm that this regularizer—and surprisingly L1 regularization—drives neurons to singular (disconnected) configurations with minimal loss degradation.

## Results

The paper's main empirical validation is on the Breast Cancer Wisconsin dataset using a shallow network with 20 hidden units. Nuclear norm regularization disconnects approximately 18 of 20 hidden neurons (i.e., drives their Jacobian singular values below 10⁻³), and L1 regularization achieves a comparable level of neuron disconnection, while L2 and unregularized training leave all neurons active. In a toy DAG experiment, the invariant set disconnects at the out-bottleneck neuron when the initialization satisfies θ_e(0) < √2, making the optimum unreachable; for θ_e(0) = √3 > √2 the optimum is reached. Theoretically, the paper establishes that singularities (disconnected sub-networks) are unreachable under gradient flow from generic initializations (Propositions 6–7), and that connectedness of the invariant set is fully characterized by balance conditions on forward/backward closed sets at bottleneck nodes (Theorem 1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against external methods but compares four training regimes on the Breast Cancer dataset: nuclear norm regularization (α=0.05), L1 regularization (α=10), L2 regularization (α=20), and unregularized training. Nuclear norm and L1 both drive the proportion of null Jacobian singular values to high levels (~18/20 neurons disconnected), confirming that both promote singular configurations; L2 and unregularized training leave all neurons active. In terms of pruning robustness (test loss vs. number of neurons removed), nuclear norm and L1-trained models are the most robust, L2 is intermediate, and unregularized models are the most fragile. The authors note that L1's effectiveness is surprising since it traditionally targets individual weight sparsity rather than neuron-level disconnection, and they provide empirical evidence (Section A.10) that L1 is more aggressive than nuclear norm in pruning edges belonging to still-active neurons, whereas nuclear norm preserves edges on active neurons (stronger group regularization). Skip connections make neuron pruning more challenging across all regularizers, consistent with prior literature.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the conservation-law framework for homogeneous-activation networks (Du et al., 2018; Tanaka et al., 2020) from shallow architectures (Nurisso et al., 2024) to general DAG topologies, reformulating the neuron-wise balance conditions compactly via the graph incidence matrix. The connectedness analysis draws on a topological result for intersections of coaxial quadrics (de Medrano, 2023) and translates it into a network-flow feasibility problem, while the singularity analysis introduces a new object—singularities of the parameter-space invariant set—as distinct from the well-studied singularities of the neuromanifold in Singular Learning Theory (Watanabe, 2009; Amari et al., 2006). The nuclear norm regularizer for differentiable pruning connects to the broader differentiable-structured-pruning literature (Fang et al., 2023; Savarese et al., 2020) but is derived from the geometry of the invariant set rather than from heuristic sparsity objectives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The complete connectedness characterization (Theorem 1) gives practitioners a concrete, checkable condition—based on bottleneck nodes and balance values at initialization—that predicts whether a ReLU network can reach its optimum, with practical implications for initialization design in binary-classification and scalar-regression settings. The finding that L1 regularization empirically induces the same neuron-disconnection behavior as the dedicated nuclear norm regularizer, despite targeting a different quantity, opens an important theoretical question about the implicit geometry of common regularizers. Key limitations include the restriction to homogeneous activations and continuous gradient flow (discrete optimizers only approximately preserve the conservation laws), and the nuclear norm regularizer's computational cost limits its practical deployment. Future work could extend the analysis to non-homogeneous activations, Adam-type optimizers, and provide a rigorous explanation for the L1–singularity connection.
