## Problem

Existing imperfect-information extensive-form game (IIEFG) solvers have game-tree complexity U^{2K} that becomes intractable for differential games with continuous action spaces (U=∞). This paper shows that the important subclass of 2p0s1 games—where one player knows the payoff type and the other holds only a belief—possess an atomic Nash equilibrium structure that collapses this complexity, but this structure was previously established only for repeated games, not for differential games with continuous dynamics.

## Input / Output

The method takes game dynamics f, type-specific running and terminal cost functions, initial state-belief (t₀, x₀, p₀), continuous action spaces U and V, and time horizon T as input. It produces atomic behavioral strategies where P1 concentrates on at most I action prototypes and P2 on at most I+1, rather than arbitrary distributions over the full continuous action space, along with the value function over the state-belief space. The strategies are parameterized as finite mixtures of learned action prototypes with type-dependent mixing probabilities, and the game tree further collapses after P1's mid-game information revelation.

## Method

The core theoretical result proves that under Isaacs' condition and deterministic dynamics, the NE of 2p0s1 differential games is I-atomic for P1 and (I+1)-atomic for P2, via a value convexification argument: P1's optimal strategy pushes the posterior belief to at most I vertices of the simplex Δ(I), each associated with a pure action. This is operationalized through a primal-dual reformulation where P1's strategy is solved via a Stackelberg DP (P₁) with P2 best-responding, and P2's via a dual DP (P₂) with P1 best-responding. Three solver variants exploit this structure: CAMS approximates the value function over the full state-belief space using multigrid FAS on the underlying Hamilton-Jacobi PDEs with DS-GDA for minimax subproblems; CAMS-DRL parameterizes policy networks to output I (or I+1) action prototypes plus mixing logits, trained with PPO or MMD; and CAMS-MPC applies DS-GDA directly to the I^K or (I+1)^K game tree when dynamics are known and differentiable. The multigrid scheme specifically addresses the exponential-in-K computational cost of value approximation by using coarse-grid corrections to eliminate low-frequency errors, reducing wall-time by roughly 3× for 10–16 stage games.

## Results

For the 4-stage Hexner's game, CAMS achieves significantly lower expected action error than DeepCFR and JPSPG with less wall-time (17 hrs vs. 29–34 hrs for DeepCFR and 24 hrs for JPSPG on A100 GPU). CAMS is invariant to discrete action size U, while CFR+, MMD, and CFR-BR-Primal all degrade as U increases from 16 to 144. Multigrid reduces value-approximation runtime by ~4× for 4-stage (9.3→2.3 hrs), ~2.5× for 10-stage (27.6→10.9 hrs), and ~2.6× for 16-stage (46.2→17.8 hrs) on a single H100 GPU. The 22-player American football game (K=10, continuous actions, I=2) is solved in 30 minutes on an M1 Pro Macbook, where the offense conceals its play type for 0.5 s—close to the ~1 s window reported in coaching analyses.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against discrete-action IIEFG solvers (CFR+, MMD, CFR-BR-Primal), CAMS wins on both axes: it is invariant to action size U while all three baselines show increasing wall-time and action error as U grows from 16 to 144, and CAMS reaches a tighter NashConv threshold (10⁻⁵ vs. 10⁻³). Against the continuous-action solver JPSPG, CAMS achieves lower expected action error in the 4-stage game with 30% less wall-time (17 vs. 24 hrs). Against DeepCFR (1000 iterations, 10 traversals, U=9), CAMS produces visibly more accurate concealment trajectories (P1 reveals at 0.60 s ± 0.06 s vs. ground-truth 0.5 s) in roughly 60% of the time. In the model-free MARL setting, CAMS-DRL converges to the ground-truth policy while PPO and MMD fail to converge even with discrete actions of size 100; R-NaD performs better than PPO/MMD but still lags behind CAMS-DRL in solution accuracy. For MPC, the atomic structure enables exact policy-gradient computation over I^K rollouts, solving the 10-stage Hexner's game in 5 minutes and the 22-player football game in 30 minutes on a laptop—tasks infeasible for IIEFG solvers whose tree complexity would be 10⁴⁴⁰.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the theoretical framework of Cardaliaguet (2007) and Ghimire et al. (2024) on differential games with asymmetric information by proving that Nash equilibria in 2p0s1 games are atomic (I-atomic for the informed player, (I+1)-atomic for the uninformed player), a structural property previously known only for repeated games (Aumann et al., 1995; De Meyer, 1996). The dual game reformulation follows De Meyer's (1996) duality, while the computational approach departs from standard IIEFG solvers (CFR+, MMD, DeepCFR, JPSPG) by exploiting the atomic structure to collapse game-tree complexity from U^{2K} to I^K, and introduces a multigrid FAS scheme (extending Trottenberg et al., 2000; Han & Wan, 2013) for the underlying Hamilton-Jacobi PDEs. The MARL and MPC formulations adapt the MMD/PPO framework of Sokota et al. (2022) and Rudolph et al. (2025) with minimal architectural changes, parameterizing policies as mixtures over a small set of action prototypes rather than over the full continuous action space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The atomic NE structure provides a principled, game-specific reduction that makes continuous-action differential games with one-sided information computationally tractable for the first time, as demonstrated by solving a 22-player football game (complexity 10^440 under standard IIEFG discretization) in 30 minutes on a laptop. Key limitations include the requirement for deterministic dynamics (stochastic dynamics break precise belief control and the atomic structure), Isaacs' condition, and the one-sided information assumption, all of which restrict applicability. Open questions include extending the atomic structure to stochastic dynamics/observations (where the convex Bellman operators become only lower bounds), finding tight upper-bounding operators that preserve atomicity, and whether the approach generalizes to multi-type or multi-stage information revelation beyond the I-type setting.
