## Problem

Existing causal imitation learning methods address either expert-observable hidden confounders (e.g., Swamy et al. 2022a) or expert-unobservable confounding noise (e.g., Swamy et al. 2022b) in isolation, but in practice both types coexist and partial solutions are insufficient. This paper introduces a unified framework that simultaneously models both types of hidden confounders and shows that naively applying behaviour cloning or history-dependent cloning fails when either type is present.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261393/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a fixed set of offline expert demonstrations (state-action trajectories of length T) and a user-specified confounding noise horizon k, which determines how far back the instrumental variable reaches. The output is a history-dependent neural network policy π̂_h that maps a trajectory history h_t (a window of k+3 past state-action pairs plus the current state) to a continuous action. Unlike standard BC which conditions only on the current state, the policy must process the full trajectory history to infer information about the expert-observable confounder u_t^o and to serve as the instrument for breaking spurious correlations.

## Method

The key insight is reformulating causal IL as a Conditional Moment Restriction (CMR) problem by using the k-step trajectory history h_{t-k} as an instrumental variable for the current state s_t, which is valid under a confounding noise horizon assumption (u_t^ε ⊥ u_{t-k}^ε). The resulting CMR is E[a_t − π_h(h_t) | h_{t-k}] = 0, which simultaneously removes the bias from expert-unobservable noise (via the IV) and captures expert-observable confounder information (via the history-dependent policy). DML-IL solves this CMR in two stages: first, a Gaussian mixture roll-out model M̂ is trained to generate k-step-ahead trajectories from h_{t-k}, breaking the spurious correlation between the observed future and the current confounding noise; second, a neural network policy π̂_h is trained to minimize MSE between generated actions and its own predictions on the generated histories. The paper proves an imitation gap bound of O(T²(δ + ε)) where δ measures how well u_t^o is recoverable from history and ε is the CMR error, recovering prior results as special cases.

## Results

DML-IL achieves the lowest MSE and highest average reward across all four environments (Plane Ticket, Ant, Half Cheetah, Hopper), approaching the noised expert upper bound, especially at small confounding horizons (k=1). BC and BC-SEQ fail entirely in the presence of confounding noise, exhibiting orders-of-magnitude higher MSE and rewards near random-policy levels. In the misspecification experiment (true k=10), DML-IL's average reward in Half Cheetah stays within 5% of the correctly-specified baseline (0.7183) down to k=6 (0.6903), while in Plane Ticket it degrades more sharply (0.6181 at k=10 to 0.3944 at k=6). Performance monotonically decreases as the confounding horizon k increases, confirming the theoretical prediction that the instrument h_{t-k} weakens.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DML-IL outperforms all baselines on both MSE and average reward in every environment tested. ResiduIL (Swamy et al., 2022b) successfully removes the confounding noise u^ε (achieving lower MSE than BC and BC-SEQ) but cannot account for expert-observable confounders u^o, resulting in a clear performance gap to DML-IL; when k=20, DML-IL's performance converges to ResiduIL's because very little information about u^o can be inferred from 20-step-old histories. BC (Pomerleau, 1988) and BC-SEQ (Swamy et al., 2022a) both fail catastrophically in the presence of confounding noise, with orders-of-magnitude higher MSE and rewards near random-policy levels; the similar performance of BC and BC-SEQ demonstrates that trajectory histories alone are insufficient when confounding noise is not explicitly handled. In the appendix, DFIV (Xu et al., 2020) achieves reasonable performance in the low-dimensional Plane Ticket environment but fails in the high-dimensional Ant MuJoCo task, while DeepGMM (Bennett et al., 2019b) fails in both tested environments, attributed to training instability and sensitivity to weak instruments.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies and generalises three previously separate causal IL settings: expert-observable hidden contexts (Swamy et al., 2022a; Vuorio et al., 2022), expert-unobservable temporally correlated noise (Swamy et al., 2022b), and causal confusion/delusion (de Haan et al., 2019; Ortega et al., 2021), into a single framework where both confounder types coexist, evolve over time, and affect both policy and transition dynamics. Methodologically, it departs from prior interactive IL approaches (DAgger-based) by relying solely on offline demonstrations, and borrows the instrumental variable / conditional moment restriction machinery from econometrics (DML-IV, Shao et al., 2024) to solve the resulting identification problem. The key conceptual move is using k-step trajectory histories as instruments to break spurious correlations while simultaneously inferring expert-observable confounders from the same history.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a clean theoretical unification with imitation gap bounds (Theorem 4.5) that recover prior results as special cases, giving practitioners a principled way to reason about when and how much performance is lost under different confounding structures. Key limitations constrain practical impact: the additive noise assumption rules out many real-world settings (e.g., healthcare), the confounding horizon k must be known or upper-bounded a priori, and the instrument weakens as k grows, degrading performance. Open questions include extending to non-additive confounding, data-driven estimation of k, and handling partially invalid instruments—directions the authors identify but leave for future work.
