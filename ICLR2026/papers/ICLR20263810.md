## Problem

Batch online RL—iteratively refining a policy on batches of its own autonomous rollouts—promises scalable robot self-improvement without the practical hazards of online RL, yet prior approaches based on imitation learning or filtered-IL fail to leverage suboptimal autonomous data and saturate quickly. The paper asks: which algorithmic components (algorithm class, policy extraction method, policy expressivity) are actually necessary for effective self-improvement from autonomously collected data?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263810/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

In simulation, the policy consumes proprioceptive state observations; in the real-world Tape task, it takes RGB images from two Intel RealSense D435 cameras (one wrist-mounted, one external) fused with joint and end-effector positions via a ResNet-18 backbone. The output is a continuous 7-DoF action vector. During autonomous rollouts, 64 candidate actions are sampled from the diffusion policy and the single action maximizing the learned Q-function is executed, making the effective output a deterministic selection from a multimodal action distribution rather than a direct policy sample.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a systematic empirical study across three axes—algorithm class (IL vs. filtered-IL vs. value-based RL), policy extraction (explicit AWR vs. implicit Q-guided action selection), and policy expressivity (Gaussian vs. diffusion)—yielding a general recipe: train an expressive diffusion-based IL policy as the actor, train a Q-function via IQL expectile objectives on the cumulatively collected autonomous data, and perform implicit policy extraction by sampling 64 actions and selecting argmax Q during rollouts. The key finding is that value-based RL is necessary but not sufficient; implicit extraction and an expressive policy class are both required, as explicit extraction cannot adapt to the shifting action distribution introduced by new autonomous data, and Gaussian policies cannot capture the multimodal expert distribution needed to generate useful diverse trajectories. A simple addition of Ornstein-Uhlenbeck temporally correlated noise to rollout actions further improves sample efficiency by broadening the learned distribution. The recipe achieves up to 2× performance gains over prior methods on six simulation tasks and a 30% success-rate improvement over the initial policy in three iterations on a real-world Franka tape-hanging task.

## Results

The proposed recipe (diffusion policy + IQL Q-function + implicit policy extraction) achieves up to 2× performance improvement over prior IL and filtered-IL methods across six simulation tasks (Robomimic Lift/Can/Square, MimicGen Stack/Threading, Adroit Pen). Value-based RL significantly outperforms both IL and filtered-IL at all data scales, with the gap widening as more autonomous data is collected per iteration. On the real-world Tape task (Franka Research 3), the recipe achieves a 30% improvement in success rate over the initial policy in just 3 iterations of batch online RL (5 initial demos, 30 rollouts per iteration). Adding temporally correlated Ornstein-Uhlenbeck noise during rollouts provides further gains at each data scale, though it does not improve the data-scaling slope.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is across algorithm classes: value-based RL (IQL + implicit extraction) significantly outperforms both vanilla IL and filtered-IL on all six simulation tasks, with filtered-IL saturating quickly while value-based RL continues to improve with more data. In the real-world Tape experiment, the proposed recipe outperforms both filtered-IL (which fails to improve beyond the initial policy) and the steering baseline adapted from Nakamoto et al. (2024), which performs worst because it does not retrain the policy on new rollouts. The paper also ablates policy extraction: implicit extraction (selecting argmax Q among sampled actions) significantly outperforms explicit extraction (AWR) after batch online RL iterations, even though explicit extraction starts stronger. Finally, expressive diffusion policies with implicit extraction outperform Gaussian policies with explicit extraction across all tasks, and even Gaussian policies with implicit extraction remain considerably worse than the diffusion-based approach.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a systematic empirical study of the batch online RL paradigm, which it frames as a middle ground between offline RL (no new data) and online RL (training during deployment). It builds directly on IQL for Q-function training and IDQL for the diffusion-policy-plus-implicit-extraction combination, extending both to an iterative multi-iteration setting. The key departure from prior autonomous-improvement work (Liu et al. 2023, Ahn et al. 2024, Bousmalis et al. 2023, Mirchandani et al. 2024) is the demonstration that IL and filtered-IL are fundamentally insufficient, and that value-based RL with implicit policy extraction and an expressive policy class is necessary. The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck noise addition draws on the DDPG exploration literature (Lillicrap et al. 2016) and adapts it to the batch online setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clear, actionable recipe for practitioners attempting autonomous policy improvement in robotics, with real-world validation on a Franka arm showing 30% success-rate gain in just 3 iterations. The finding that implicit policy extraction (selecting the best-Q action from the policy distribution) outperforms the standard explicit AWR extraction in the iterative setting challenges a default assumption in offline RL and suggests that the interaction between value signals and distribution shift during data accumulation is underappreciated. Open questions remain: whether IQL's pessimism is optimal for the batch online setting, whether implicit extraction can be improved beyond argmax-Q selection, and how to bootstrap from a completely non-successful initial policy. The continuous-action-space focus and requirement for an initially partially-successful policy limit immediate applicability to discrete or very hard tasks.
