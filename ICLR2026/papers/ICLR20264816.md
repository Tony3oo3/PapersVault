## Problem

Current Adversarial Imitation Learning methods (GAIL, AIRL) deploy a fixed RL policy as a black box, which limits out-of-distribution generalization, interpretability, and real-world robustness. The dominant IRL-then-MPC paradigm treats reward learning and planning as separate offline/online procedures, preventing end-to-end interactive learning of the full planner. This paper asks whether a planning-based agent can serve as the adversarial generator in AIL, unifying reward learning and deployment into a single online optimization loop.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264816/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes observation-only expert demonstrations as state-transitions (s, s') without any action labels, operating under partial observability (e.g., position and body-centric velocity only). A predictive dynamics model (kinematic bicycle model or a learned neural network) is required for rollouts. At each timestep, the agent outputs an H-step action sequence (plan) via MPPI, while the training loop produces a learned state-transition cost (discriminator logit), a bootstrapped value function, and the discriminator itself. The 'policy' is not a stored network but is re-solved online at every state through zeroth-order trajectory optimization.

## Method

MPAIL replaces the RL policy in the GAIL min-max objective with an infinite-horizon MPPI planner, which is proven to solve the KL-constrained forward RL problem in closed form. The discriminator provides the reward as its logit r(s,s') = log(D) − log(1−D), and a value function bootstraps the terminal state of each H-step rollout to handle infinite-horizon returns. Training alternates between: (1) running MPPI to collect transitions, (2) updating the discriminator with binary cross-entropy, and (3) updating the value network with TD targets — no explicit policy gradient step is needed since the policy is solved online. The key architectural insight is that the learned reward and value are deployed online through planning at every timestep, shifting the generalization burden from a fixed policy to the reward landscape, which the paper shows substantially improves OOD robustness, interpretability, and real-world performance from a single partially observable demonstration.

## Results

In the real-world MuSHR navigation experiment from a single partially observable demonstration, MPAIL achieves a mean Relative Cross-Track Error (CTE) of 0.17 m at 0.70 m/s, compared to GAIL (0.56 m CTE, 0.37 m/s) and IRL-MPC (0.37 m CTE, 0.30 m/s). In the simulated OOD navigation evaluation, MPAIL generalizes to significantly more out-of-distribution states than GAIL, with the planner's 3 m horizon being up to 15× shorter than the task horizon. On the simulated navigation benchmark, MPAIL reaches optimality in less than half the number of interactions compared to GAIL, while AIRL fails to converge on the multimodal circling behavior.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MPAIL decisively outperforms GAIL on the real-world navigation task, achieving 3.3× lower mean CTE (0.17 m vs. 0.56 m) and 1.9× higher speed (0.70 vs. 0.37 m/s); GAIL's policy consistently veers off-path or collapses into circular driving and fails to reliably converge even in simulation. Against IRL-MPC (which uses the same GAIL-trained reward but deploys it via MPPI), MPAIL still achieves 2.2× lower mean CTE (0.17 m vs. 0.37 m) and 2.3× higher speed (0.70 vs. 0.30 m/s), demonstrating that end-to-end online policy optimization through π_MPPPI induces a more competitive adversarial dynamic than offline actor-critic training. AIRL is excluded from the real experiment as it does not exhibit meaningful behavior even in simulation, likely due to instability from its logit-shift reward on multimodal data. In the OOD evaluation, MPAIL (and MPAIL with a learned online model) substantially outperforms both GAIL and Behavior Cloning, with policy networks showing brittle, unpredictable generalization while MPAIL agents consistently navigate toward the goal even from strongly OOD initializations. On sample efficiency, MPAIL with an approximate prior model reaches optimality in under half the interactions of GAIL on the navigation task, supporting its characterization as a model-based algorithm.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate pipelines—Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) for reward learning and Model Predictive Control (MPC) for deployment—into a single end-to-end adversarial imitation learning algorithm. It builds directly on GAIL (Ho & Ermon, 2016) and AIRL (Fu et al., 2018) for the adversarial objective, on GAILfO (Torabi et al., 2019a) for the state-only observation formulation, and on infinite-horizon MPPI with value bootstrapping (Bhardwaj et al., 2021; Lowrey et al., 2019) for the planning mechanism. The key departure from the dominant IRL-then-MPC paradigm (Lee et al., 2022b; Triest et al., 2023) is that the MPPI planner participates in the adversarial game as the generator, so the reward and value are trained jointly with the planning procedure rather than being learned offline and deployed separately.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central empirical claim—that deploying the learned reward online via planning (rather than relying on a fixed policy to generalize) is what enables out-of-distribution robustness—challenges the implicit assumption in AIL that a well-trained policy is the primary vehicle for generalization. The real-sim-real experiment with a single partially observable demonstration is a practically important validation, showing MPAIL succeeds where GAIL and IRL-MPC fail on hardware. However, the evidence base is narrow (two simulated tasks, one real-world task, all low-dimensional), and the Ant-v2 experiment reveals that vanilla MPPI sampling struggles in higher-dimensional spaces, leaving open whether the approach scales to complex manipulation or locomotion without additional sampling or latent-dynamics extensions. Theoretical gaps remain, including the justification for temperature decay and the precise relationship between the cost markup η and the return discount γ.
