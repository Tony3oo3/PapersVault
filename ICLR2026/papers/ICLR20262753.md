## Problem

Self-consistency-based aleatoric uncertainty (AU) collapses on confident errors, where a model repeatedly produces the same incorrect answer with high probability, yielding near-zero AU despite the response being wrong. Prior approaches to epistemic uncertainty either require access to logits or hidden states, or only trigger cross-model comparisons at intermediate AU levels, overlooking the critical low-AU failure mode. This paper asks whether cross-model semantic disagreement can reliably flag these confident failures in a purely black-box setting.

## Input / Output

Given a text prompt, the method samples n responses from a reference model and n responses from each of m auxiliary models (all 7–9B, different vendor families), then computes pairwise cosine similarity over sentence-T5-xl embeddings. The sample budget is matched to self-consistency baselines: AU uses 10 samples from one model, while TU uses 2 samples × 5 models. The output is three scalar uncertainty scores per prompt — AU (intra-model semantic dispersion), EU (the gap between inter-model and intra-model similarity), and TU = AU + EU — with no access to logits, hidden states, or model internals required.

## Method

The core contribution is an epistemic uncertainty estimator defined as the gap between cross-model semantic similarity (reference model responses vs. auxiliary model responses) and intra-model self-similarity (reference model responses vs. itself), derived by marginalizing an ideal model out of a divergence D(ω‖ω*) using an ensemble of scale-matched, cross-family models. Theoretically, D is shown to be a one-sided kernel discrepancy (the first two terms of squared MMD in an RKHS) and is upper-bounded by total variation distance under a bounded characteristic kernel, providing a variational-gap interpretation. The ensemble must satisfy three properties — support richness, non-collapsing diversity, and calibrated weighting — which are achieved by selecting 7–9B instruction-tuned models from different vendors (Gemma-2, Granite, Llama-3.1, Mistral, Qwen2.5) with uniform mixing weights. Empirically, EU is highest precisely in the low-AU regime where incorrect responses are confidently repeated, and TU = AU + EU consistently outperforms AU in AUROC and selective abstention across ten long-form tasks, with the largest gains on HotpotQA, CoQA, and WMT16-de-en.

## Results

Across five 7–9B instruction-tuned models and ten long-form tasks, Total Uncertainty (TU = AU + EU) achieves a mean AUROC of 0.718 (Mistral-7B) versus 0.617 for the Aleatoric baseline, with the largest per-dataset gains on HotpotQA (+0.15), CoQA (+0.14), and WMT16-de-en (+0.13). On the pooled set of all model–dataset pairs, TU reaches AUROC 0.746 vs. 0.707 for AU. For API models on SimpleQA, TU improves AUROC from 0.59 to 0.70 (GPT-4o) and from 0.53 to 0.58 (Claude 3.7 Sonnet). In selective prediction, TU reduces AURC by over 20% on HotpotQA and XSum and raises C@90 by more than 1.5 points on both, while outperforming AU in AURC on every dataset.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TU (mean AUROC 0.718) outperforms every baseline on the average across eight benchmarks, with the closest competitor being Closeness Centrality (0.639) and Kernel Language Entropy (0.634). Against the primary aleatoric baseline (Lin et al. AU, 0.617), TU gains 0.10 on average, with the largest margins on CoQA (+0.361), HotpotQA (+0.188), and QASPER (+0.099). Token-level baselines (Perplexity 0.562, Max Token Prob. 0.562, Mean Token Entropy 0.571) and verbalized-confidence methods (PTrue 0.486, Self Certainty 0.549) are consistently the weakest, confirming that single-model confidence signals are poor proxies for correctness. TU loses to Kernel Lang. Ent. only on AmbigQA (0.768 vs. 0.785) and to SelfCheckGPT on GSM8K (0.610 vs. 0.770), but wins on all other individual datasets. Notably, TU's advantage over AU is largest precisely where AU is most miscalibrated (e.g., CoQA where AU drops to 0.458), validating the paper's hypothesis that EU flags confident failures invisible to self-consistency.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classical deep-ensemble uncertainty framework (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017) to the LLM setting by replacing logit-space disagreement with cross-model semantic similarity over generated text. It builds directly on Lin et al. (2023) for the aleatoric baseline (semantic dispersion via self-consistency) and on Schweighofer et al. (2023) for the information-theoretic divergence that motivates the EU formulation. The key departure from prior EU estimation work (logit-based Dirichlet methods, Bayesian noise injection, verifier-model disagreement) is that the estimator operates purely on black-box generated text from a small, scale-matched ensemble of open-weight models, requiring no training or architectural access. It also explicitly contrasts with Xue et al. (2025), which triggers cross-model comparison only at intermediate AU, by showing that low-AU is precisely where EU is most needed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies and empirically validates a critical failure mode of self-consistency-based UQ: confident errors where a model repeatedly produces the same wrong answer, yielding near-zero AU. The practical recipe (sample 2 responses from each of 5 open-weight 7-9B models, compute semantic similarity gaps) is immediately deployable without training, and the matched-budget comparison (10 samples single-model vs. 2×5 ensemble) makes the overhead transparent. Key limitations include reliance on response-level semantic similarity (which degrades on open-ended tasks with many valid answers), sensitivity to ensemble composition (homogeneous ensembles underestimate EU), and dependence on an LLM judge for correctness. Open questions include how to best combine this EU with token-level or logit-based estimators, and whether the approach generalizes to settings where no comparable open-weight ensemble exists.
