## Problem

N-gram novelty (measuring how rare text fragments are in trillion-token training corpora) has been adopted as a primary metric for textual creativity, but creativity theory requires both novelty and appropriateness. This paper demonstrates that ~91% of top-quartile n-gram novel expressions are not judged creative by expert writers, and that in open-source LLMs, higher n-gram novelty actively correlates with lower pragmaticality—meaning the metric rewards exactly the kind of incoherent text that disqualifies an expression from being creative.

## Input / Output

The annotation pipeline takes ~400-word fiction passages (50 from New Yorker, 50 generated by OLMo-7B/OLMo-2-32B from one-sentence summaries) and splits them into atomic expression units by punctuation, pre-highlighting ~50% based on n-gram novelty percentage. Expert writers (n=26 professional MFA writers) rate each highlighted expression for sensicality, pragmaticality, and perceived novelty, and may additionally highlight creative expressions not pre-selected. For LLM-as-a-Judge evaluation, the model receives a full passage and must output a JSON list of identified novel or non-pragmatic expressions with justifications; matching against ground truth uses subset or ≥90% normalized indel similarity.

## Method

The paper operationalizes creativity per Runco & Jaeger's standard definition as the conjunction of novelty, sensicality, and pragmaticality, then uses mixed-effects logistic regression (with random intercepts for annotator and seed passage) to model the relationship between infini-gram perplexity (n-gram novelty) and each creativity component, revealing a significant negative interaction between perplexity and pragmaticality for LLM-generated text. For LLM-as-a-Judge evaluation, the authors test zero-shot, few-shot, and fine-tuned models (LoRA on OLMo-2-7B, Qwen3-8B, Llama-3.1-8B; API fine-tuning on GPT-4.1 and Gemini-2.5-Pro) on a close-reading extraction task, comparing F1 scores against an empirical random baseline. Finally, they validate the best-performing judge (few-shot GPT-5) on out-of-distribution Style Mimic and LMArena preference data via hierarchical logistic regression, showing LLM-J novelty score differences predict expert preferences more strongly than the CREATIVITY INDEX (AIC 471.16 vs. 473.07, with likelihood ratio tests confirming the index adds no significant predictive power beyond novelty scores).

## Results

Across 8,618 expert writer annotations, n-gram novelty (measured by log-standardized perplexity) is significantly associated with expert-judged creativity (OR ≈ 1.96 per SD, p < 0.001), yet approximately 91% of top-quartile n-gram novel expressions are not judged creative, and ~25% of creative expressions fall below the mean perplexity. In open-source LLMs, higher n-gram novelty negatively impacts pragmaticality (OLMo: β = −0.17, p = 0.027; OLMo-2: β = −0.48, p < 0.001), while no such effect exists for human text (β = 0.01, p = 0.92). On the Style Mimic expert preference dataset, LLM-as-a-Judge novelty score differences predict expert preference with β = 0.63 (OR = 1.88, p = 0.014), outperforming the CREATIVITY INDEX (β = 0.51, OR = 1.66, p = 0.038), with AIC favoring the novelty-only model (471.16 vs. 473.07).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary baseline is the CREATIVITY INDEX from Lu et al. (ICLR 2025), which places significant weight on n-gram novelty (lack of occurrence in trillion-token corpora) for measuring textual creativity. On the Style Mimic expert preference dataset (450 pairs), LLM-as-a-Judge novelty scores are a stronger predictor of expert preference than CREATIVITY INDEX: β = 0.63 (OR = 1.88, p = 0.014) vs. β = 0.51 (OR = 1.66, p = 0.038). In a joint model containing both predictors, LLM-J novelty retains a trend-level significant effect (β = 0.48, p = 0.010) while CREATIVITY INDEX becomes non-significant (β = 0.26, p = 0.358). Likelihood ratio tests confirm that adding CREATIVITY INDEX to a novelty-only model does not improve fit (p = 0.35), whereas adding novelty to a CREATIVITY INDEX-only model shows a trend toward improved fit (p = 0.096). AIC model selection further favors the novelty-only model (471.16) over the CREATIVITY INDEX-only model (473.07) and the joint model (472.30), indicating that LLM-J novelty scores provide the most parsimonious explanation of expert creative preference.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a critical re-examination of n-gram novelty metrics (infini-gram, Rusty-DAWG, WiMBD) and the recently proposed CREATIVITY INDEX (Lu et al., ICLR 2025) for evaluating textual creativity. It draws on the psychology of creativity literature (Runco & Jaeger, 2012; Amabile, 1983) to argue that novelty is necessary but not sufficient, and operationalizes the missing 'appropriateness' component as a decomposition into sensicality and pragmaticality. Methodologically, it repurposes the literary close-reading technique for NLP evaluation, combining it with mixed-effects regression to account for rater and passage-level variation, and uses LLM-as-a-Judge frameworks to test whether frontier models can replicate expert expression-level judgments.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that ~91% of top-quartile n-gram novel expressions are not judged creative directly challenges the validity of n-gram novelty as a standalone creativity metric, with practical implications for anyone using CREATIVITY INDEX or similar tools. The negative correlation between n-gram novelty and pragmaticality in open-source LLMs suggests a fundamental tension in current generation: pushing for surface-level novelty degrades contextual coherence. Open questions include whether the close-reading annotation protocol can be scaled beyond 26 expert writers, whether findings generalize beyond the fiction domain, and whether the creativity gap between frontier LLMs and humans (confirmed in a small exploratory study) narrows with further model development. The LLM-as-a-Judge novelty scores outperforming CREATIVITY INDEX in predicting expert preferences offers a promising but still imperfect alternative.
