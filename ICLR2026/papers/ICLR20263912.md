## Problem

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) is effective for training large reasoning models but requires expensive ground-truth annotations. Unsupervised RLVR alternatives that derive rewards from internal signals (entropy, majority voting, self-certainty) avoid labels but suffer from model collapse in later training stages because they reinforce incorrect but self-consistent reasoning patterns. The paper asks how a small labeled set can be used to anchor and stabilize RLVR on much larger unlabeled data, going beyond the marginal gains of naively combining supervised and unsupervised objectives.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a small labeled set (question–answer pairs, e.g., 1K–4K samples) and a larger unlabeled set (questions only, e.g., 3K–12K samples) drawn from the same or related domains. At each training step, G rollouts are generated per question; for labeled samples the pass rate is computed against the true answer, while for unlabeled samples a pseudo-pass rate is estimated via majority voting over the G rollouts. The output is a fine-tuned policy model that produces a reasoning chain ending in a boxed final answer, evaluated on math and general-reasoning benchmarks.

## Method

TRAPO (Trajectory-based Policy Optimization) selects reliable unlabeled samples by matching their pass-rate learning trajectories to those of labeled samples. At each epoch it records each sample's pass rate, builds a cumulative trajectory vector, and computes a Trajectory Cosine Similarity (TCS) between each unlabeled trajectory and the running average of a 'reliable' database (seeded with labeled trajectories and augmented by previously selected unlabeled ones). Unlabeled samples whose TCS falls in the top-p or exceeds a threshold Γ are admitted into the GRPO loss via a binary mask, while labeled samples always contribute; a warm-up phase trains on labeled data alone before the mask activates. The key insight is that trajectory alignment serves as a proxy for whether an unlabeled sample's reasoning pattern is externally validated by the labeled set, preventing the self-reinforcing feedback loop that causes unsupervised collapse. A generalization bound (Theorem 3.1) shows the expected risk is controlled by the empirical labeled risk plus a trajectory-consistency term and a voting-confidence term, providing theoretical justification for the selection criterion.

## Results

With only 1K labeled and 3K unlabeled samples, TRAPO achieves 42.6% average in-distribution (ID) accuracy and 56.1% out-of-distribution (OOD) accuracy, surpassing the best fully unsupervised method (TTRL on 45K unlabeled samples) by 4.3% (ID) and 3.7% (OOD). Scaling to 4K labeled and 12K unlabeled samples, TRAPO reaches 45.6% ID and 59.7% OOD accuracy, exceeding the fully supervised model trained on all 45K labeled samples (45.5% ID, 57.3% OOD) while using only 10% of the labeled data. On individual benchmarks, TRAPO (4K+12K) scores 24.3/17.1 on AIME 24/25, 60.0 on AMC, 84.6 on MATH-500, and 43.9 on GPQA-Diamond.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TRAPO outperforms all three unsupervised baselines (TTRL, Self-certainty, and Entropy-based methods) by a wide margin: with 1K+3K samples, TRAPO achieves 42.6% ID vs. TTRL's 39.2%, Self-certainty's 40.0%, and Token-level Entropy's 40.0% in the semi-supervised setting, and 56.1% OOD vs. their respective 50.2%, 45.6%, and 49.7%. Against the best fully unsupervised method (TTRL on 45K unlabeled, 38.2% ID / 52.4% OOD), TRAPO gains +4.3% ID and +3.7% OOD using only 10% of the data. In the cross-domain OOD experiment (Table 2), TRAPO (41.0% ID / 56.9% OOD) beats the best unsupervised baseline (Self-certainty: 39.2% ID / 53.4% OOD) by 1.8% and 3.5% respectively, while naive semi-supervised combinations of labeled and unlabeled data actually degrade performance (e.g., Self-certainty drops to 38.6% ID). At the 4K+12K scale, TRAPO (45.6% ID / 59.7% OOD) surpasses the fully supervised 45K model (45.5% ID / 57.3% OOD) on both metrics, demonstrating that trajectory-guided selection of unlabeled samples is more effective than simply scaling labeled data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RLVR paradigm (pioneered by DeepSeek-R1 and formalized via GRPO) by introducing a semi-supervised variant that was previously unexplored in this setting. It departs from unsupervised RLVR methods (TTRL, self-certainty, entropy-based rewards) that rely solely on internal consistency signals and suffer from model collapse, by anchoring the reward signal with a small labeled set. The key conceptual departure is from traditional semi-supervised learning's feature-similarity-based label propagation: because RLVR has instance-specific solution spaces, the authors instead use pass rate trajectory dynamics as the bridging signal, drawing an analogy to how humans generalize from a few verified examples. The policy optimization backbone (GRPO) and the unsupervised reward mechanisms (majority voting, entropy) are inherited directly from prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for domains where annotation is expensive (medicine, finance): matching or exceeding fully supervised performance with 10% of labels could dramatically reduce training costs. The trajectory-matching selection criterion offers a general, model-agnostic principle for identifying reliable unlabeled samples that could transfer to other RLVR settings. However, the evaluation is concentrated on math reasoning benchmarks with two model families (Qwen2.5-Math-7B, Llama-3.1-8B), leaving open whether the approach generalizes to non-mathematical reasoning, longer-horizon tasks, or substantially larger models. The theoretical analysis relies on NTK and gradient-alignment assumptions that may not hold in practice for finite-width LLMs, and the sensitivity to hyperparameters (top-p, threshold Γ, warm-up length) suggests the method requires careful tuning per dataset.
