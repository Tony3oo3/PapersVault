## Problem

Predicting patient drug responses from abundant cell line data is hindered by two overlooked factors: (1) existing transfer learning methods treat drugs and genes as monolithic entities, ignoring that specific drug substructures (e.g., pharmacophores) and genomic functional subsequences (e.g., apoptosis pathways) drive response; and (2) they align only genomic feature distributions while neglecting that drug response mechanisms fundamentally differ in vitro versus in vivo due to the immune system, tumor microenvironment, and systemic physiology.

## Input / Output

The method takes drug SMILES sequences (decomposed into substructures via BRICS) and gene expression profiles over 1,426 genes (decomposed into functional pathway clusters via gene set enrichment analysis) as input. It produces a binary drug response label (positive/negative) and an interpretable bipartite subsequence interaction graph whose edge weights quantify the contribution of each drug substructure–gene pathway pair to the prediction. Pre-training uses 966 cell line samples across 20 drugs from DepMap; fine-tuning uses 555 patient samples across 5 drugs from TCGA, with a 7:3 train/test split per drug.

## Method

DeepSADR follows a two-stage pre-training/fine-tuning pipeline. In pre-training, drug SMILES are decomposed via BRICS and genomic profiles via GSEA into subsequences; a GNP encoder (GCN augmented with edge features, batch norm, dropout, residual connections, FFN, and RWSE positional encoding) extracts drug subsequence features while fully connected layers extract gene subsequence features. A bilinear interaction function scores all drug–gene subsequence pairs, and a threshold filters weak edges to form a bipartite subsequence interaction graph, which is encoded by a supervised graph autoencoder (two-layer GCN with Gaussian latent prior). A Set Transformer-based adaptive readout (SAB encoder + PMA decoder with multi-head attention) aggregates node embeddings into a permutation-invariant graph-level representation. During fine-tuning, all modules are frozen except the adaptive readout and predictor; the fine-tuned representation is concatenated with the frozen pre-trained representation before prediction, balancing domain-invariant knowledge with patient-specific adaptation while mitigating overfitting on the small patient cohort.

## Results

On five clinical drugs evaluated on TCGA patient data, DeepSADR achieves AUC scores of 0.805 (Fluorouracil), 0.870 (Temozolomide), 0.957 (Sorafenib), 0.719 (Gemcitabine), and 0.927 (Cisplatin), with corresponding AUPR scores of 0.821, 0.886, 0.978, 0.702, and 0.922. The model outperforms the strongest baseline GANDALF by 0.012–0.146 AUC across the five drugs, with the largest margin on Sorafenib (+0.146 AUC) and Cisplatin (+0.075 AUC). Ablation confirms the adaptive readout module contributes the largest single gain (AUC drops from 0.856 to 0.662 when removed), followed by the subsequence interaction graph (0.856 → 0.698).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DeepSADR outperforms all 16 baselines on both AUC and AUPR across all five drugs. The closest competitor is GANDALF (ICLR 2025), which uses generative attention-based data augmentation; DeepSADR leads by 0.012 AUC on Fluorouracil (0.805 vs 0.793) but by a much larger 0.146 AUC on Sorafenib (0.957 vs 0.811) and 0.075 AUC on Cisplatin (0.927 vs 0.852). Among transfer-learning-specific methods, WISER (0.715–0.781 AUC) and CODE-AE (0.594–0.782 AUC) trail DeepSADR by 0.02–0.36 AUC depending on the drug. Classic domain adaptation methods (DSN-MMD, CORAL, Velodrome, Celligner) perform substantially worse, with AUC values often below 0.70, confirming that genomic feature alignment alone is insufficient. Cell-line-only models (DeepTTA, GraphCDR) drop to 0.44–0.65 AUC when applied directly to patient data without transfer learning, underscoring the critical role of the adaptive readout fine-tuning stage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the transfer learning paradigm for cancer drug response prediction (cell lines → patients) by shifting the unit of analysis from whole-molecule/whole-genome features to subsequence-level interactions. It builds on the Set Transformer architecture (Lee et al., 2019) for its adaptive readout, the variational graph autoencoder (Kipf & Welling, 2016) for encoding bipartite interaction graphs, and the GNP encoder (Luo et al., 2025) for drug substructure feature extraction. Unlike prior methods (WISER, GANDALF, CODE-AE) that align global genomic distributions between domains, DeepSADR conceptualizes the drug response process itself as the transferable domain, modeling it as a bipartite graph between BRICS-decomposed drug fragments and gene-set-enrichment-derived functional pathway clusters.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The subsequence interaction graph provides a level of interpretability (which drug fragment acts on which biological pathway) that prior black-box DRP models lack, and the biological validation of the temozolomide tetraene ring–apoptosis pathway association strengthens credibility. However, the approach requires per-drug threshold tuning, is evaluated on only five drugs with very small patient cohorts (26–138 samples each), and its performance gains, while consistent, are moderate over the strongest baseline (GANDALF). Open questions include whether the threshold can be learned end-to-end, whether the subsequence interaction framework generalizes to drugs with very different pharmacological profiles, and whether the domain-as-response-process framing scales beyond the five-drug evaluation.
