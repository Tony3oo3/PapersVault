## Problem

Hierarchical reinforcement learning suffers from two persistent, intertwined challenges: non-stationarity (the higher-level policy's reward and transition dynamics shift as the lower-level policy evolves) and infeasible subgoal generation (the higher level proposes subgoals beyond the lower level's current capabilities). Prior methods address these issues in isolation or rely on privileged information, but lack a principled mathematical formulation that explicitly captures the bidirectional dependencies between hierarchical levels and simultaneously mitigates both problems.

## Input / Output

The higher-level policy receives the current robot state and the final task goal, and outputs a subgoal drawn from the goal space every K timesteps; the lower-level (primitive) policy receives the current state and the assigned subgoal, and outputs primitive actions (e.g., 7-DoF gripper commands) over a K-step window. Preference labels for training are computed from implicit sparse rewards—indicator functions checking whether the state reached after K timesteps is within ε of the final goal—making them independent of the lower-level policy's behavior and thus stationary throughout training. For harder tasks (pick-and-place, push, kitchen), one human demonstration is incorporated via an imitation learning objective at the lower level, applied uniformly across all baselines.

## Method

DIPPER formulates goal-conditioned HRL as a bi-level optimization problem where the higher-level policy optimization is the upper-level objective and the lower-level policy optimization is the lower-level constraint, yielding an approximate Lagrangian that includes a lower-level value function gap term (V^L − V_*^L). By substituting this bi-level reward into the DPO loss and applying the maximum-entropy RL identity (advantage = β log π*), the paper derives a primitive-regularized DPO objective that trains the higher-level policy on stationary preference pairs while the (V^L − V_*^L) term acts as an implicit regularizer pushing the higher level toward feasible subgoals. The lower-level policy is trained separately with standard off-policy SAC, and its value function is updated m times per higher-level update to approximate the optimal value. This decouples higher-level learning from the non-stationary environment rewards that depend on the evolving lower-level policy, while the value-function regularization directly addresses infeasible subgoal generation—a consequence that falls naturally from the bi-level formulation rather than being an ad hoc addition.

## Results

DIPPER achieves up to 40% improvement in success rate over state-of-the-art baselines on complex sparse-reward robotic tasks (pick and place, push, Franka kitchen), where most competing methods fail to make meaningful progress. On the easier maze navigation task, HAC, SAGA, and RAPS outperform DIPPER, but DIPPER dominates the three harder long-horizon manipulation tasks. The paper introduces two novel diagnostic metrics — Subgoal Distance (lower is better) and Lower Q-Function value (higher is better) — on which DIPPER consistently outperforms all baselines, confirming mitigation of both non-stationarity and infeasible subgoal generation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DIPPER outperforms DPO-FLAT (single-level DPO) on all four tasks, demonstrating the benefit of hierarchical temporal abstraction over flat preference optimization. Against PIPER (RLHF-based HRL), DIPPER wins on all four tasks, attributed to DPO's avoidance of the unstable intermediate RL step. DIPPER beats HAC (hindsight-augmented HRL) on 3 of 4 tasks, losing only on the simpler maze navigation. Against SAGA (state-conditioned adversarial subgoal generation), DIPPER wins on pick-and-place, push, and kitchen, while SAGA performs better on maze. RAPS (parameterized action primitives) excels on maze navigation but fails on the sparse-reward manipulation tasks where DIPPER leads. The vanilla HIER baseline (SAC at both levels) and the single-level FLAT SAC baseline are outperformed on every task, with FLAT showing essentially no progress, confirming that hierarchical structure is essential for these long-horizon sparse-reward settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

DIPPER sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) goal-conditioned hierarchical RL, where prior methods (HAC, HIRO, SAGA) address non-stationarity by simulating optimal lower-level behavior or relabeling transitions; (2) preference-based learning, where the paper departs from RLHF-based approaches like PIPER by replacing the intermediate RL step with DPO, leveraging DPO's closed-form reward-policy equivalence to avoid training instability; and (3) bilevel optimization, where the paper's key conceptual move is formulating HRL as a bilevel problem (upper: higher-level policy, lower: lower-level policy), which naturally yields a lower-level value function regularization term that constrains subgoal feasibility. The derivation borrows the token-level trajectory expansion from Rafailov et al. (2024) and the maximum-entropy advantage identity from Ziebart (2010), but applies them in a novel hierarchical robotics context with primitive regularization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled mathematical framework (bilevel formulation) for HRL that was previously absent, and demonstrates that DPO's stationary preference labels can decouple higher-level learning from the non-stationary lower-level policy—a conceptual insight that could generalize beyond the four tested environments. However, several limitations temper the impact: the method requires collecting ~10,000 pairwise trajectory comparisons per environment (even if automated via implicit rewards), assumes access to one human demonstration for harder tasks, and is evaluated only on simulated robotic tasks with relatively low-dimensional state spaces. Open questions include whether DIPPER generalizes to high-dimensional subgoal spaces, how it compares to hierarchical RLHF in out-of-distribution settings, and whether the bilevel formulation extends to multi-level hierarchies or continuous subgoal spaces beyond the discrete goal spaces tested here.
