## Problem

Existing graph benchmarks either exhibit small-world properties that make long-range communication unnecessary, or are small synthetic graphs for inductive tasks, leaving no large-scale transductive dataset that inherently requires information from distant nodes. Moreover, prior evaluations rely on empirical performance gaps between GNNs and Graph Transformers to infer long-range dependency, which is confounded by hyperparameter tuning and lacks a direct, principled measurement of how far a model actually reaches.

## Input / Output

The input consists of undirected city road networks (100k–570k nodes, diameters 121–404) with 37 node features derived from OpenStreetMap (geographic coordinates, land use, and edge features like road length and speed limit aggregated to nodes). The classification target is a 10-quantile label of local eccentricity at k=16 hops (maximum weighted shortest-path distance to 16-hop neighbors), which by construction requires integrating structural and spatial information from distant neighborhoods. The proposed measurement additionally outputs per-hop influence scores T̄_h and a scalar influence-weighted receptive field R for each trained model.

## Method

The paper introduces City-Networks, a transductive node-classification dataset built from four real city road networks where labels are annotated via local eccentricity at a controllable hop k=16, ensuring a known ground-truth communication range. For quantification, it defines a per-hop influence score T_h(v) as the aggregated ℓ₁-norm of the Jacobian ∂H_vi^(L)/∂X_uj over all h-hop neighbors of v, and derives an influence-weighted receptive field R as the hop-weighted average of these scores. Theoretical analysis provides two justifications: (i) a spectral bound showing that large-diameter, low-maximum-degree graphs have larger second-largest eigenvalues of the normalized adjacency operator, slowing over-smoothing and making deep message passing viable; and (ii) a dilution theorem proving that in grid-like (quasi-isometric to ℤ^D) graphs, the mean influence over an h-hop shell decays as 1/h^(D−1), motivating the use of the sum rather than the mean as the aggregation. Empirically, all baselines (GCN, GraphSAGE, GAT, GCNII, GraphGPS, Exphormer, SGFormer) show consistent performance gains as depth increases from 2 to 16 layers on City-Networks, while degrading or plateauing on short-range datasets, and the per-hop influence decays markedly slower on City-Networks than on Cora, ogbn-arxiv, Amazon-Ratings, or PascalVOC-SP.

## Results

On City-Networks (L=16), the best MPNN (GraphSAGE) reaches 68.3% on Shanghai and 61.4% on Los Angeles, while the best GT (Exphormer) achieves 70.2% on Shanghai and 63.8% on Los Angeles. A key behavioral finding is that all baselines show consistent performance improvement as depth increases from L=2 to L=16 on City-Networks, whereas on Cora and ogbn-arxiv performance degrades with depth due to over-smoothing. The influence-weighted receptive field R is consistently 2–4× higher on City-Networks (4.86–8.18) than on comparison datasets (1.21–3.5), confirming the presence of long-range dependency. An MLP using only node features achieves only 25–28% accuracy, demonstrating that graph topology is essential for the task.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 8 graph models (GCN, GraphSAGE, GAT, GCNII, DropEdge, GraphGPS, Exphormer, SGFormer) plus MLP and ChebNet across City-Networks and four comparison datasets. On City-Networks, Exphormer (70.2% Shanghai) and GraphSAGE (68.3% Shanghai) are the strongest baselines, with all models showing monotonic improvement from L=2 to L=16—evidence that the task genuinely requires long-range communication. In contrast, on Cora and ogbn-arxiv, the same models degrade with increasing depth due to over-smoothing, while on Amazon-Ratings and PascalVOC-SP performance remains nearly flat, indicating these tasks are short-ranged. The Jacobian-based measurement (R) confirms this ordering: City-Networks yield R values of 4.86–8.18 (GCN) and 5.71–7.90 (Exphormer), substantially exceeding Cora (2.37–2.84), ogbn-arxiv (1.21–3.5), Amazon-Ratings (1.43–2.46), and PascalVOC-SP (1.42–7.14). GraphGPS achieves the highest R on City-Networks (8.18 on Paris) but suffers OOM on London, highlighting the scalability challenge of global attention on large graphs. The MLP baseline (25–28%) is far below all graph models, confirming that spatial features alone are insufficient and that graph structure carries essential long-range signal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Jacobian-based influence score framework from Xu et al. (2018) and the receptive-field measurement of Bamberger et al. (2025) into a per-hop diagnostic tool for long-range dependency, while grounding the dataset design in the spectral over-smoothing analysis of Wu et al. (2019) SGC framework. It departs from LRGB (Dwivedi et al., 2023) and synthetic long-range benchmarks (Bodnar et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2025), which rely on small inductive graphs and infer long-rangeness indirectly from GNN-vs-GT performance gaps, by introducing a large-scale transductive dataset with a directly measurable, controllable long-range signal. The theoretical contribution links the over-smoothing rate to algebraic connectivity and graph diameter, extending the eigenvalue bounds of Wu et al. (2019) to the augmented adjacency operator with self-loops.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

City-Networks fills a clear gap as the first large-graph transductive benchmark specifically designed for long-range dependency testing, and its availability on PyTorch Geometric makes it immediately accessible for the GNN community. The per-hop influence measurement provides a principled, model-agnostic diagnostic that goes beyond binary long/short-range classification, enabling finer-grained comparison across architectures and datasets. Key open questions remain: whether the measurement scales to dense graphs (where Jacobian computation becomes intractable), whether the findings extend to inductive settings, and whether the grid-like topology assumption (quasi-isometry to Z^D) holds for other real-world network types. The paper also highlights the practical challenge that Graph Transformers face OOM on graphs with >100k nodes, underscoring the need for scalable architectures for long-range graph learning.
