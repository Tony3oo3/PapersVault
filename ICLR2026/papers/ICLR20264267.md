## Problem

While LLMs have been explored for democratic deliberation and conflict resolution, no benchmark exists to systematically evaluate their ability to draft consensus resolutions that satisfy specific political objectives under varying power structures and party configurations. This paper asks whether LLMs can bridge divides among divergent stakeholders and achieve different types of political consensus (simple majority, two-thirds majority, veto-proof, Rawlsian, utilitarian) in realistic collective decision-making settings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264267/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The tested LLM receives a single-paragraph prompt containing the issue background, each participating party's stance (2, 4, or 6 parties), and task-specific requirements including the political goal, seat distribution (power structure), and voting mechanism. It must produce a single unbroken-paragraph consensus resolution beginning with 'The European Parliament raised.' Evaluation then proceeds in two stages: a GPT-4o-mini judge assigns each party an integer voting score (0–9, representing the proportion of that party's MEPs voting in favor), and a social-choice-theory module maps these scores to a pass/fail boolean or quantitative consensus score depending on the political goal.

## Method

PoliCon is constructed from 2,225 high-quality European Parliament deliberation records (2009–2022) scraped and aligned across three sources (EP official website, HowTheyVote, VoteWatch Europe), then cleaned with DeepSeek-R1 for background summarization, stance extraction, and resolution condensation, plus rule-based synonym replacement for diversity. The benchmark instantiates 28,620 scenarios by combining 5 political goals (Simple Majority, Two-Thirds Majority, Veto Power, Rawlsianism, Utilitarianism) × 3 party-count settings (2, 4, 6) × 2,225 issues, with randomly assigned seat proportions to create diverse power structures. The open-ended evaluation framework uses an LLM-as-a-judge (GPT-4o-mini) to simulate per-party voting scores, validated at 0.83 Pearson correlation against real parliamentary votes and 72% within ±σ of human annotators, then applies social choice theory formulas (weighted vote aggregation, min-operator for Rawlsianism, sum-operator for Utilitarianism) to determine whether the generated resolution achieves the target consensus objective.

## Results

On the PoliCon benchmark (28,620 scenarios across 5 political goals × 3 party configurations), Gemini-2.5 achieves the best overall performance, winning 60% of task settings, with SM scores of 0.88–0.90, 2/3M scores of 0.53–0.58, and Rawls scores of 3.51–4.60. DeepSeek-V3.1 and GPT-4o follow closely, each attaining top performance on 33% of tasks. All models struggle significantly with harder consensus requirements: 2/3M success rates drop to 52–63% (vs. 87–93% for SM), and Rawls scores range only from 3.42–4.60 out of a maximum of 9, revealing a substantial gap between simple majority and stricter consensus objectives.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares six LLMs against two rule-based baselines (Random and Greedy) across 15 task settings. Random achieves the lowest scores on all tasks (e.g., 0.20 on 2/3M-4, 2.01 on Rawls-4), confirming that LLMs have a nontrivial advantage over naive random selection. Greedy (selecting the dominant party's stance) outperforms Random but remains below all LLMs except in two-party passing-resolution settings, where it surpasses Qwen2.5-32B, Llama-3.3-70B, and Qwen2.5-72B on SM and 2/3M, indicating that smaller open-source models still retain substantial room for improvement. Among the six LLMs, Gemini-2.5 leads on 60% of tasks, while DeepSeek-V3.1 and GPT-4o each top 33%; thinking models (Gemini-2.5, DeepSeek-V3.1) consistently outperform non-thinking models (GPT-4o, Llama-3.3-70B), and commercial models generally outperform open-source ones. Performance is positively correlated with model size among the four open-source models tested. A notable finding is that all tested LLMs exhibit inherent partisan biases—scores across parties resemble real-world voting distributions despite random seat reassignment—and lower bias variance correlates with higher overall PoliCon performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of LLM evaluation and political science, explicitly noting that no prior benchmark evaluates LLMs' ability to achieve diverse political consensus objectives. It repurposes social choice theory (Sen, 1986; Kelly, 2013) and the LLM-as-a-judge paradigm into a novel evaluation framework, while drawing on real European Parliament deliberation records (VoteWatch Europe, HowTheyVote) rather than synthetic scenarios. Conceptually, it departs from prior political LLM benchmarks (POLCA, Liang et al. 2025, Chalkidis & Brandl 2024) that focus on stance detection, statement classification, or UN resolution simulation, by introducing adjustable power structures, multiple voting mechanisms, and normative consensus criteria (Rawlsianism, Utilitarianism) as first-class evaluation dimensions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PoliCon provides the first systematic platform for studying whether LLMs can genuinely bridge political divides under realistic constraints, revealing that even frontier models (Gemini-2.5, DeepSeek-V3.1) drop from ~90% success on simple majority to ~55-63% on two-thirds majority and only 3.4-4.6/9 on Rawlsian objectives. The discovery of persistent partisan biases—where model scores mirror real-world party distributions despite random seat reassignment—raises important questions about deploying LLMs in governance contexts. Open questions include whether iterative multi-turn negotiation (rather than single-shot generation) would improve consensus quality, whether the findings generalize beyond the European Parliament context, and how to mitigate the identified biases without degrading overall performance.
