## Problem

Evaluations of LLM simulation fidelity have been fragmented across bespoke tasks and metrics, producing a patchwork of incomparable results that makes it impossible to draw broader conclusions about when, how, and why LLM simulations succeed or fail. The field lacks a unified, large-scale benchmark to systematically measure and compare simulation ability across models, tasks, and populations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260967/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each test case pairs a standardized multiple-choice question (with options mapped to single-token letter keys) with a system prompt describing the target demographic group (e.g., 'women aged 30–49 in Slovakia'). Base models produce a response distribution via first-token logits over the answer options, while instruction-tuned models are prompted to output verbalized percentages in strict JSON format (whole numbers summing to 100). The evaluation metric is the SIMBENCH score S, computed as 100×(1 − TVD(P,Q)/mean_TVD(P,U)), where P is the ground-truth human distribution, Q is the model's predicted distribution, and U is the uniform baseline, yielding a score where 100 is perfect and 0 is uniform-random.

## Method

SIMBENCH harmonizes 20 heterogeneous social and behavioral science datasets (spanning moral dilemmas, economic games, psychological assessments, and survey questions across 130+ countries) into a unified multiple-choice format, yielding 10.9M question-group simulation targets from which two benchmark splits are curated: SimBenchPop (7,167 test cases measuring general population simulation) and SimBenchGrouped (6,343 test cases measuring demographic-conditioned simulation). The empirical study evaluates 45 LLMs (0.5B–405B parameters, base and instruction-tuned) and uncovers several structural findings: simulation ability scales log-linearly with model size but not with inference-time compute; instruction tuning creates an alignment-simulation tradeoff where the mode-seeking KL objective of RLHF improves performance on low-entropy consensus questions by up to 40 points while degrading it on high-entropy pluralistic questions (r = −0.942); models struggle most with religious/ideological demographic groups; and simulation ability correlates most strongly with knowledge-intensive reasoning (MMLU-Pro, r = 0.939) rather than general helpfulness or narrow problem-solving.

## Results

The best LLM (Claude-3.7-Sonnet) achieves a SIMBENCH score of 40.80/100, closing roughly 40% of the gap between a uniform baseline and perfect human response distribution matching. The top open-weight model, DeepSeek-R1, scores 34.52, while ten of 45 tested models score below 0 (worse than random). Performance scales log-linearly with model size but is not improved by increased inference-time compute (e.g., Claude-3.7-Sonnet drops from 40.80 to 39.46 with a 4000-token reasoning budget). An alignment-simulation tradeoff is quantified with a near-perfect negative linear relationship (r = −0.942) between human response entropy and the benefit of instruction tuning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's key comparison is between general-purpose instruction tuning and the specialist cognitive tuning of Centaur models (Binz et al., 2025), both built on Llama-3.1-70B. General instruction tuning (Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct, S=16.56) outperforms Centaur (S=8.54) overall, but they improve via opposing mechanisms: instruction tuning excels on low-entropy consensus questions while Centaur preserves the base model's ability to capture high-entropy pluralistic responses. Both substantially outperform the Llama-3.1-70B base model (S=1.21). Across model families, instruction-tuned models consistently outperform their base counterparts at scales above 10B parameters, but this inverts for smaller models (e.g., OLMo-2 base outperforms its instruction-tuned variants at 7B and 13B). On inference-time compute, no tested configuration (o4-mini high vs. low, Claude with 4000-token reasoning, zero-shot CoT on GPT-4.1) yields meaningful gains; GPT-4.1 actually drops from 34.55 to 33.11 with CoT prompting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies the fragmented landscape of LLM human-behavior simulation evaluation, where prior studies (Aher et al., 2023; Argyle et al., 2023; Horton, 2023; Bisbee et al., 2024; Manning et al., 2024) each tested a narrow set of models on a single task with bespoke metrics, into a single standardized benchmark spanning 20 datasets. Methodologically, it draws on the distributional prediction literature (Tian et al., 2023; Meister et al., 2025) for elicitation of verbalized probabilities from instruction-tuned models, adapts the token-probability prompt structure from Nori et al. (2023) for base models, and grounds its theoretical analysis of the alignment-simulation tradeoff in the RL-as-Bayesian-inference framework (Levine, 2018; Korbak et al., 2022). It also positions itself against the Centaur specialist-cognition models (Binz et al., 2025) as a contrasting path to improved simulation fidelity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SIMBENCH provides the first reproducible, large-scale infrastructure for systematically studying when, how, and why LLM simulations succeed or fail, moving the field from ad-hoc comparisons toward a measurable science. The alignment-simulation tradeoff identifies a concrete, actionable tension for model developers: standard RLHF alignment degrades the ability to capture pluralistic human opinion, pointing toward the need for distribution-preserving alignment techniques. Key open questions include whether this tradeoff can be resolved through weight interpolation or inference-time methods, how to extend evaluation to interactive and multi-turn simulations, and how to handle intersectional demographic groups where sample sizes preclude reliable ground-truth distributions.
