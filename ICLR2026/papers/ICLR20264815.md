## Problem

Content-aware video streaming requires chunk-level importance weights to optimize subjective QoE, but existing approaches face a fundamental trade-off: human crowdsourcing (SENSEI) is prohibitively expensive (~$100 and 78 min per video), while vision-based saliency models generalize poorly across diverse video categories and large video understanding models produce invalid or inaccurate zero-shot subjective ratings. No prior work leverages LLMs as a scalable human proxy for this task, and doing so introduces three non-trivial challenges: limited LLM modality and token context, rating distribution inconsistency across local windows in VOD, and strict real-time latency with unavailable future chunks in live streaming.

## Input / Output

The method consumes anchor frames (the first frame of each 1-second chunk) sampled in sliding windows of m=10, along with the video title/background text and an iteratively updated periodical summary (≤100 words). For VOD, the output is a globally consistent saliency weight per chunk normalized to [0,1] after LLM-guided merge-sort re-ranking and Gaussian smoothing. For live streaming, the output additionally includes predicted future chunk weights whose horizon adapts dynamically to LLM inference latency, with a fallback to uniform weights (all 1s) when predictions are unavailable.

## Method

HiVid comprises three modules. The perception module iteratively feeds m anchor frames plus the running summary to an LLM (GPT-4o by default), which returns per-frame integer ratings (0–100) and an updated summary, requiring only ⌈D/m⌉ API calls regardless of video length. For VOD, the ranking module applies a novel LLM-guided merge sort: at each merge step, m/2 frames from each sorted half are jointly re-ranked by the LLM using the global video summary as context, yielding O(k log k) LLM calls and eliminating cross-window rating discrepancies; a Gaussian kernel (σ=5) then smooths the final ranking. For live streaming, the prediction module uses a multi-modal time series forecaster where CLIP-aligned image and text features serve as Key/Value and the weight time series serves as Query in a content-aware attention mechanism, with a novel correlation loss (MSE + 1−Pearson) to preserve relative preference structure; the output dimension L_out = ⌈(Δt+δ)/d⌉ + m + N adapts asynchronously to LLM latency so that ABR decisions are never blocked.

## Results

On the three highlight detection datasets (Mr.Hisum/Youtube-8M, TVSum, SumMe), HiVid achieves average PLCC of 0.54, SRCC of 0.55, mAP50 of 0.72, and mAP15 of 0.43, outperforming the strongest baseline (SL-module) by 11.5% in PLCC and 6% in mAP50. For live streaming forecasting, HiVid-M achieves PLCC=0.29 and MAE=0.08, surpassing the best uni-modal baseline (Crossformer, PLCC=0.23) by 26%. A user study with 10 volunteers confirms HiVid boosts streaming QoE–MOS correlation by 14.7% over the next-best saliency model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HiVid wins on all four saliency metrics (PLCC, SRCC, mAP50, mAP15) across all three datasets against every one of the 8 saliency baselines. The closest competitor is SL-module (Xu et al., ICCV 2021), which achieves PLCC of 0.59/0.43/0.39 vs. HiVid's 0.66/0.50/0.47; DETR (Moon et al., CVPR 2023) ranks mid-table, confirming that even SOTA vision-based highlight detectors cannot match LLM-guided semantic reasoning. Among the large video understanding models, VideoLLaMA3 (Zhang et al., 2025) is the strongest (PLCC 0.54/0.41/0.35) but still trails HiVid by 12–24% in PLCC, while VILA (Lin et al., CVPR 2024) and Flamingo (Alayrac et al., NeurIPS 2022) lag further behind due to hallucination and poor zero-shot subjective rating. For the 7 time-series forecasting baselines (iTransformer, TimeMixer, TimesNet, Crossformer, PatchTST, FiLM, RNN, LSTM), HiVid-U (uni-modal) matches or beats all on MAE (0.08) and leads on PLCC (0.24 vs. Crossformer's 0.23) and SRCC (0.22 vs. 0.22); HiVid-M (multi-modal with content-aware attention) extends the lead to PLCC=0.29 and SRCC=0.27, a 26% improvement over the best baseline. RNN and LSTM are clearly the weakest forecasting baselines (PLCC 0.09/0.08), confirming that simple recurrent architectures are insufficient for this multi-modal, content-dependent task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of content-aware streaming (SENSEI, Pensieve), video highlight detection (DETR, SL-module), and LLM-based subjective reasoning. Rather than building on a single prior method, it repurposes LLMs as a scalable human proxy for chunk-level saliency rating—a task previously addressed only by expensive crowdsourcing or small vision models that generalize poorly. The live streaming prediction module extends standard time series forecasting (iTransformer, TimesNet, etc.) by introducing a content-aware attention mechanism that fuses CLIP-aligned image and text features with the weight series, and an adaptive output dimension to handle variable LLM inference latency. The LLM-guided merge-sort for global re-ranking is a novel algorithmic contribution that has no direct precedent in the highlight detection literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical bottleneck in content-aware streaming: human annotation costs ~$100 and 78 minutes per video, making it infeasible for large-scale or live deployment. By demonstrating that LLMs can approximate human saliency ratings at a fraction of the cost (~$1.35 per 201s video), it opens a path toward scalable, content-aware ABR. Key limitations include the O(k log k) ranking overhead for VOD, the modest absolute forecasting accuracy (PLCC 0.29), and reliance on proprietary LLM APIs. Open questions include whether fine-tuned open-source multimodal LLMs can close the gap with GPT-4o, whether the approach extends to video compression or other vision-language-action tasks, and whether the low forecasting PLCC is a fundamental limit of the task or an artifact of the small training set.
