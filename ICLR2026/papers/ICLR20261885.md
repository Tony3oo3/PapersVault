## Problem

While prior work identified attention heads important for translation via downstream metric degradation, these approaches relied on task-specific evaluation, were limited to smaller or single models, and did not explain how the heads mechanistically perform cross-lingual mapping. This paper asks whether a sparse, functionally specialized set of attention heads exists that directly implements token-level source-to-target alignment, and what properties and developmental trajectory such heads exhibit across diverse LLMs.

## Input / Output

The detection pipeline consumes source-target sentence pairs from FLORES-101 (dev split, ~900 pairs per language direction) and the per-head attention weights extracted during greedy decoding. Token alignments between source and target tokens are pre-annotated by GPT-4.1 with a confidence threshold of 0.9. The method outputs a binary classification of each attention head (token alignment head if translation score > 0.1) and a continuous per-head translation score. The downstream TRater algorithm additionally takes multilingual training data (300B tokens across 17 languages) and produces a filtered subset (top 1.3% per language) scored by the loss difference between the full model and the model with top-20 token alignment heads masked.

## Method

The core detection mechanism operates during greedy decoding: for each generated target token t with a GPT-4.1-annotated source token s, an attention head is credited with a valid alignment if it assigns its maximum attention weight to the position of s. The translation score is the fraction of target tokens (out of m tokens with valid source counterparts) where this condition holds, averaged across all examples in a language pair. Heads exceeding a 0.1 threshold are classified as token alignment heads. The paper validates this identification through five properties: universality and sparsity (<8% of heads) across six models spanning 1.7B–30B parameters and dense/MoE architectures; cross-lingual consistency (Jaccard similarity >0.8 across 10 language pairs); causal necessity (masking these heads drops BLEU by >17 points while random masking has minimal effect); functional specificity (disproportionate harm to translation vs. other multilingual tasks); and a three-phase evolutionary trajectory (rapid proliferation to ~8% at 8k steps, stabilization around 5%, then gradual pruning to 2.6%) traced by analyzing checkpoints from an 8B model trained from scratch on 15T tokens. The TRater data-filtering algorithm leverages the identified heads by scoring each training sample on the token-level cross-entropy loss difference between the full model and the model with top-20 token alignment heads ablated, selecting the highest-scoring 1.3% of multilingual data for enhanced translation training.

## Results

Masking token alignment heads causes a sharp decline in translation performance on FLORES-101, with BLEU drops exceeding 17 points and chrF++ drops exceeding 25 points, while masking an equivalent number of random non-token-alignment heads has minimal impact. Token alignment heads are sparse (<8% of all attention heads, as few as 3% in Mistral-7B-v0.3) yet universal across all six models studied (1.7B–30B, dense and MoE). Cross-lingual consistency is strong: Jaccard similarity of the top-20 token alignment head sets across 10 language pairs consistently exceeds 0.8. The TRater data-filtering experiment shows that removing just 1.3% of multilingual data identified by token alignment heads drops FLORES chrF++ from 43.87 to 41.33, while tripling that data improves it to 46.68.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is primarily an empirical interpretability study rather than a method-comparison paper, so there are no external baseline methods to benchmark against. The key internal comparison is between ablating token alignment heads and ablating an equivalent number of random non-token-alignment heads: the former causes large, consistent drops in translation metrics (BLEU >17 pts, chrF++ >25 pts) across all five models tested, while the latter produces negligible changes, establishing causality. On multilingual benchmarks beyond translation, the effect is more nuanced: Hellaswag_ML, ARC_C_ML, and ARC_E_ML show moderate drops (up to 10 points) when token alignment heads are masked, indicating partial reliance on cross-lingual alignment, whereas XNLI and XCOPA show little or even negative impact, suggesting these tasks depend on higher-level semantic mechanisms rather than token-level alignment. The TRater data-filtering experiment provides a second causal validation: the 1.3% of data scored highest by token alignment heads is disproportionately important for translation (chrF++ drops 2.54 points when removed, gains 2.81 points when tripled) but has minimal effect on other multilingual benchmarks, confirming the functional specificity of these heads to the translation task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work identifying important attention heads for machine translation (Kim et al. 2021; Ma et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2025) by shifting from metric-based importance ranking to a mechanistic definition: heads are identified by their direct cross-lingual token alignment behavior, analogous to how induction heads are defined by their copy-paste circuit rather than by ICL benchmark scores. It also complements semantic-space analyses showing LLMs 'think in English' (Zhao et al. 2024; Schut et al. 2025; Wendler et al. 2024) by pinpointing the specific heads that implement the token-level mapping between languages, and positions itself at the token-execution level relative to function-vector work (Todd et al. 2024) that operates at the task-triggering level.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that translation is governed by a sparse, universal, and causally necessary set of heads (<8% of all heads) that can be cleanly separated from other multilingual capabilities provides a concrete mechanistic target for architectural design and data curation. The TRater application demonstrates practical utility: filtering just 1.3% of multilingual data via these heads improves translation, suggesting that most multilingual training data is redundant for this specific capability. Open questions include whether the token alignment threshold (0.1) generalizes across model scales, whether the GPT-4.1-based alignment annotation introduces systematic bias, and whether similar sparse circuits can be identified for other cross-lingual operations (e.g., code-switching, multilingual reasoning) beyond direct translation.
