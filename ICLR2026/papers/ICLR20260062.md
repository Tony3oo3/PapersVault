## Problem

Estimating the Bayes error (optimal classification error) from soft labels is practical and instance-free, but two critical gaps remain: (1) the existing bias bound for the hard-label-based estimator is prohibitively loose (O(1/√m)) and grows with sample size n, and (2) in practice only corrupted soft labels are available (due to distribution shift, human subjectivity, or LLM annotators), and it is unclear whether calibration can recover a reliable estimate. The paper shows that even perfectly calibrated soft labels can yield substantially wrong Bayes error estimates, highlighting that the choice of calibration algorithm matters.

## Input / Output

The method requires only corrupted soft labels (real values in [0,1]) and a single hard label per instance for calibration — no access to input instances is needed, making it applicable in privacy-sensitive settings. It produces a scalar Bayes error estimate along with a high-probability confidence interval. For the hard-label variant, m independent binary labels per instance are averaged to form approximate soft labels before estimation.

## Method

The paper makes two contributions. First, it derives a tighter bias bound for the hard-label-based estimator using a sharpened Jensen's inequality, showing the bias decays at O(1/m) when classes are well-separated (|η(x)−0.5|≥c a.s.) versus O(1/√m) in the worst case, and provides a computable bound B(E,m) that depends only on an upper bound E on the Bayes error. Second, for corrupted soft labels, it proposes calibrating them via isotonic regression (pool-adjacent-violators) before plugging into the min{η,1−η} estimator. The key theoretical result (Theorem 2) proves statistical consistency under the assumption that corruption is a monotone transformation of the true soft labels — a strictly weaker requirement than knowing the clean values — by leveraging risk bounds for binary isotonic regression. Theorem 3 further extends the guarantee to additive noise settings (e.g., averaging m hard labels under a shifted posterior).

## Results

The paper's main quantitative contribution is a 200× tighter bias bound for the hard-label-based Bayes error estimator: for binarized CIFAR-10 (n=10,000, m=50, E=0.0005), the previous bound allowed bias up to 0.557 while the new bound (Corollary 2) limits it to 0.00276. In the FeeBee evaluation (lower is better), isotonic calibration—the paper's proposed method—achieves competitive scores across all six datasets, ranking 1st on Fashion-MNIST (0.00240), 2nd on SNLI (0.00292), MNLI (0.00147), and AbductiveNLI (0.00118), and 3rd on CIFAR-10 (0.00307). The theoretical consistency guarantee (Theorem 2) shows the isotonic-calibrated estimator converges at rate O(n^{-1/3}) + O(√(log(1/δ)/n)) under the assumption that corrupted soft labels preserve the ordering of clean ones.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Isotonic calibration (the paper's proposed method) is the most robust across datasets: it never ranks below 5th and achieves top-2 on four of six datasets. Histogram binning is highly sensitive to the number of bins—hist-25 wins on CIFAR-10 (0.00253) but catastrophically fails on Fashion-MNIST (0.00825, 3.4× worse than isotonic's 0.00240), while hist-100 degrades to last place on MNLI and AbductiveNLI. Beta calibration and its variants are dramatically worse on most datasets (e.g., 0.08796 vs. 0.00240 on Fashion-MNIST, a 37× gap), though beta-ab narrowly wins on MNLI (0.00143 vs. 0.00147). Platt scaling is the most surprising competitor: it achieves the best FeeBee score on ICLR (0.00001) and AbductiveNLI (0.00081), and remains within the top-3 on all other datasets, despite lacking the theoretical consistency guarantee that isotonic calibration enjoys under Theorem 2. The paper notes that Platt's strong empirical performance despite its mismatched Gaussian-mixture assumption is left as future work.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the instance-free Bayes error estimation framework of Ishida et al. (2023), which used clean soft labels to estimate the optimal error rate without access to input instances. It builds on two fronts: (1) a sharper, adaptive bias analysis for the hard-label-based estimator that improves upon the original O(1/√m) bound, and (2) a new formulation for estimation from corrupted soft labels, where the authors repurpose isotonic regression (Zadrozny and Elkan, 2002) as a calibration step. The work departs from prior soft-label methods by showing that calibration guarantees alone are insufficient and that the specific choice of calibration algorithm is critical, a nuance absent from the original formulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical value is significant: knowing the Bayes error tells practitioners when further model improvement is futile, saving compute and energy, and the instance-free property enables estimation in privacy-sensitive settings like medical diagnosis where raw data is inaccessible. The conceptual finding that even perfectly calibrated soft labels can yield substantially wrong estimates challenges the common assumption that calibration is a sufficient quality guarantee. Key open questions include extending the theory to multi-class problems, providing theoretical guarantees for Platt scaling (which empirically outperforms isotonic calibration on several datasets despite mismatched distributional assumptions), and understanding how much the derivative-boundedness assumption in Theorem 3 can be relaxed.
