## Problem

Patch-based time series forecasting methods (e.g., PatchTST, Crossformer) achieve strong accuracy but suffer from large parameter counts and heavy computational costs, particularly on large-scale and complex datasets. The paper provides the first clear explanation of why patch-level processing is inherently inefficient—attributing it to the high variability of cycle patterns in real-world data that forces models to construct high-dimensional representation spaces—and asks whether a fundamentally different tokenization can preserve predictive power while drastically reducing model size.

## Input / Output

The method takes a univariate time series of length L_in (e.g., 720 steps) and a fixed period length L_phase estimated from the dominant spectral component via frequency analysis. The input is circularly padded to a multiple of L_phase and reshaped into a 2D phase-period matrix of shape (L_phase × P_in), where each row is a phase token collecting values at the same offset across all periods. The output is a 1D forecast sequence of length L_out, produced by de-tokenizing a predicted phase-period matrix and de-normalizing. The model operates under a channel-independent paradigm, processing each variable separately, and the period length remains fixed throughout training.

## Method

PhaseFormer reframes time series forecasting as phase-wise prediction by extracting phase tokens (values at identical offsets across consecutive cycles) instead of patch tokens (adjacent observations within a cycle). Each phase token is embedded into a shared low-dimensional latent space via a linear projection plus learnable positional embeddings. Cross-phase interaction is handled by a Perceiver-style routing mechanism with M learnable routers (typically M ∈ {4,8}, far fewer than L_phase=24): routers first aggregate information from all phases via cross-attention, then redistribute it back, replacing quadratic self-attention with a much cheaper two-stage pathway. A single shared linear predictor maps the refined phase representations to multi-step forecasts for all phases simultaneously. The key theoretical insight, supported by perturbation analysis, is that phase tokenization is structurally invariant under cycle-pattern shifts (unlike patch tokenization, which suffers non-vanishing structural drift), and that phase tokens reside in a 2-dimensional subspace explaining >90% of variance—enabling the entire model to operate with ~1K parameters while achieving state-of-the-art accuracy.

## Results

PhaseFormer achieves the best or near-best MSE on 6 out of 7 benchmark datasets (input length 720, averaged over horizons 96–720), with headline values of 0.386 MSE on Traffic, 0.160 on Electricity, and 0.223 on Weather. On the largest and most complex dataset (Traffic), it surpasses the second-best PatchTST by 6.3% and TimeBase by 10.4% in MSE. The model achieves these results with only ~1.16K parameters and ~14 MFLOPs on Traffic, representing a >99.9% reduction in both parameters and FLOPs compared to PatchTST (7.6M params, 498K MFLOPs) and Crossformer (23M params, 259K MFLOPs).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PhaseFormer achieves the best MSE on 6 of 7 datasets, losing only on ETTh2 where FITS (0.334) edges it out (0.346). On the most challenging datasets—Traffic and Electricity—PhaseFormer's advantage is most pronounced: it beats PatchTST by 6.3% on Traffic (0.386 vs 0.394) and by 5.9% on Electricity (0.160 vs 0.169), while Crossformer degrades severely (0.545 on Traffic, 0.180 on Electricity). Against efficiency-oriented baselines with comparable parameter counts, PhaseFormer substantially outperforms SparseTSF (0.413 vs 0.386 on Traffic) and TimeBase (0.418 vs 0.386 on Traffic; 0.167 vs 0.160 on Electricity), confirming that its phase-routing design captures cross-phase dynamics that simpler phase-based or patch-based minimalism misses. On Weather, PhaseFormer ties PatchTST (both 0.223 MSE) while using ~99.9% fewer FLOPs. Against foundation models (Moirai-2, Chronos) on GIFT-Eval Web/DevOps workloads, PhaseFormer wins on 4 of 8 subsets (all bitbrains datasets) but loses on the bizitobs subsets, indicating its phase-based design excels on highly volatile, spike-heavy traces but is outperformed by large pretrained models on smoother workloads.

## Relation to Prior Work

PhaseFormer departs from the dominant patch-tokenization paradigm (PatchTST, Crossformer, TimeMixer) by proposing phase tokenization—extracting values at identical offsets across successive cycles—as a fundamentally different way to represent periodic time series. The cross-phase routing mechanism is inspired by Perceiver-style attention (Jaegle et al., 2021) and Crossformer's cross-dimension attention, but is specifically adapted to exploit the low-rank structure of phase-aligned tokens. The paper also connects to efficiency-oriented phase-based methods like SparseTSF and TimeBase, which share the motivation of cross-period correlation, but provides the first systematic theoretical and empirical justification for why phase tokens are inherently more compact and stationary than patch tokens. The channel-independent paradigm follows PatchTST and DLinear, and the frequency-domain period estimation draws on FEDformer-style spectral analysis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PhaseFormer demonstrates that a ~1K-parameter model can match or exceed much larger Transformer-based forecasters across seven standard benchmarks, with particularly strong gains on large, complex datasets (Traffic, Electricity) where distributional shifts are most severe. This opens a practical pathway for deploying high-quality forecasting in resource-constrained or latency-sensitive environments. The key limitation is the assumption of locally stable periodicity: on weakly periodic or highly irregular series, phase alignment may degrade, and the paper acknowledges this as a primary failure mode. Future work on modeling non-stationarity and complex drifts, as well as extending the phase perspective beyond univariate channel-independent settings, would strengthen the approach's generality.
