## Problem

CLIP-based vision-language models exhibit an 'affirmation bias' where they ignore negation words like 'no', 'not', and 'without', producing embeddings indistinguishable from their affirmative counterparts. Existing solutions rely on data-centric fine-tuning with curated hard-negative datasets, which still fail to fully resolve negation understanding and cause catastrophic forgetting of general zero-shot classification capabilities. The paper asks whether fine-tuning on special negation datasets is truly necessary, or whether the embedding space itself can be corrected post-hoc.

## Input / Output

The method takes a natural language caption (e.g., 'A photo of a cat but not flower') as input and produces a single corrected CLIP text embedding as output. A rule-based negation scope detector first identifies the negated concept span (e.g., 'flower') by classifying negators into pre-negators (forward-scoped, like 'not', 'without') and post-negators (backward-scoped, like 'is absent', 'is nowhere'), then the CLIP text encoder is called twice—once on the full caption and once on the extracted negated phrase. The corrected embedding is a vector in the same dimensional space as the original CLIP text embedding, making it a drop-in replacement for any downstream task (retrieval, MCQ, text-to-image generation) without architectural changes.

## Method

The core insight is that CLIP text embeddings support compositional arithmetic: the embedding of a composed caption approximates the sum of its component embeddings, and subtracting one component (plus a neutral anchor embedding to restore non-informative bias) recovers the other. For negation, the method computes the projection of the full caption embedding e^c onto the negated concept embedding e^neg, then subtracts this projection scaled by a hyperparameter λ, adding back an anchor embedding a (the mean of embeddings for semantically neutral words like 'neutral', 'balanced', 'unbiased'). The final corrected embedding is e* = e^c − λ((⟨e^c, e^neg⟩/⟨e^neg, e^neg⟩) e^neg − a). This is entirely training-free and zero-shot: no model weights are modified, and the only tunable parameter λ is selected via validation (λ≈1.9 for base CLIP, λ≈0.3 for NegCLIP*), reflecting how much negated information each model already suppresses.

## Results

On NegBench MCQ, the proposed zero-shot embedding correction boosts baseline CLIP from 24.7% to 72.5% on COCO, 24.3% to 78.6% on VOC2007, and 27.5% to 50.0% on MSR-VTT. When applied to the fine-tuned NegCLIP*, it further improves MCQ accuracy from 56.2% to 69.5% (COCO) and 59.7% to 75.1% (VOC2007). On retrieval, the method achieves R-Neg@5 of 63.2% (COCO) and 49.0% (MSR-VTT) with CLIP, and 67.9% and 52.2% with NegCLIP*, respectively. On the CC-Neg benchmark, the method attains 99.79% MCQ accuracy, outperforming the ConCLIP model fine-tuned specifically on CC-Neg (99.70%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method decisively outperforms all baselines on NegBench MCQ. Ours+CLIP (72.5/78.6/50.0 on COCO/VOC2007/MSR-VTT) surpasses the fine-tuned NegCLIP* (56.2/59.7/46.2) by 16.3/18.9/3.8 points and CLIP* (54.4/54.8/44.9) by 18.1/23.8/5.1 points, despite requiring no training. Against the original CLIP baseline (24.7/24.3/27.5), the improvement is 47.8/54.3/22.5 points. On retrieval (R-Neg@5), Ours+NegCLIP* achieves the best scores (67.9% COCO, 52.2% MSR-VTT), marginally beating NegCLIP* (67.0%, 51.5%). On CC-Neg, the method (99.79%) narrowly edges out ConCLIP fine-tuned on CC-Neg (99.70%), while substantially outperforming CLIP openai (65.70%), NegCLIP (62.63%), FLAVA (58.93%), and BLIP (62.31%). The method also generalizes across architectures, improving SigLIP, SigLIP2, AlignCLIP, and TripletCLIP by 16–43 points on MCQ (Table 2).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant data-centric paradigm for negation understanding in VLMs (ConCLIP, NegCLIP, NegationCLIP, TripletCLIP), which curate hard-negative datasets and fine-tune contrastive models. Instead, it repurposes the well-established embedding arithmetic framework from NLP (Mikolov et al.) and multimodal retrieval (Couairon et al., Douze et al.) as a zero-shot, training-free post-hoc correction to CLIP text embeddings. The key conceptual bridge is the empirical observation that CLIP embeddings support compositional subtraction with an anchor bias term, enabling explicit removal of negated semantic content without any gradient updates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a simple rule-based + arithmetic correction outperforms models fine-tuned on millions of curated negation pairs challenges the assumption that negation understanding in VLMs is fundamentally a data problem. This suggests the contrastive learning framework itself may be ill-suited for encoding negation, pointing toward architectural or loss-function changes as a more principled path forward. However, the method's reliance on brittle rule-based negation scope detection limits its applicability to complex syntax, and current benchmarks (NegBench, VALSE Existence) test only simple single-object negations, leaving open whether the approach generalizes to double negation, implicit negation, or long-distance dependencies.
