## Problem

Feed-forward 3D Gaussian Splatting methods for panoramic novel view synthesis rely on multi-view cost volumes that fail to resolve occlusions in sparse-view settings, and standard Cartesian Triplane representations are geometrically misaligned with 360° scene structure, producing distortion and aliasing. No existing feed-forward framework simultaneously handles variable numbers of input views, robustly completes occluded regions, and respects the cylindrical symmetry inherent to panoramic imagery.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264973/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method accepts one or more equirectangular panoramic RGB images at 512×1024 resolution, along with a UniK3D monocular depth prior, and produces a set of 3D Gaussian primitives (position, anisotropic scale, rotation, opacity, color) that are rendered via a direct panoramic 3DGS rasterizer into novel equirectangular views and depth maps. Unlike cost-volume-based competitors that require a fixed number of input views, CylinderSplat flexibly handles any number of panoramas without architectural retraining, and the direct rasterizer replaces the common cubemap-stitching pipeline to render the full 360° image in a single pass.

## Method

CylinderSplat uses a dual-branch feed-forward architecture trained with a three-stage curriculum (pixel branch → volume branch → joint fine-tuning). The pixel branch aggregates multi-view context via self- and cross-attention over a ResNet backbone to predict refined depth and features, which are unprojected into a 3D feature point cloud and decoded into Gaussians for well-observed regions. The volume branch introduces a per-camera cylindrical Triplane (replacing Cartesian planes) whose three orthogonal feature planes (F_{rθ}, F_{θz}, F_{zr}) are initialized from pixel-branch features and refined through cross-plane attention and triplane-to-image attention; Gaussians are decoded from the Triplane grid with a Jacobian-based transformation that converts locally predicted cylindrical offsets and scales into Cartesian coordinates. An RGB retrieval mechanism projects each volume Gaussian into all source views and aggregates visibility-weighted pixel colors to recover high-frequency detail lost in the semantic Triplane features. The cylindrical coordinate system is chosen because its ZR and RΘ planes naturally align with the vertical walls and horizontal floors of Manhattan-world scenes, and its sampling pattern matches real-world panoramic depth distributions better than Cartesian or spherical alternatives.

## Results

On the two-view panoramic reconstruction task (Matterport3D, 2.0 m baseline), CylinderSplat achieves PCC 0.851, WS-PSNR 23.76 dB, SSIM 0.835, and LPIPS 0.175, outperforming the strongest baseline OmniScene (PCC 0.732, WS-PSNR 22.75 dB) by +1.01 dB in WS-PSNR and +0.119 in PCC. On the single-view task, the advantage widens substantially: CylinderSplat reaches WS-PSNR 23.75 dB vs. OmniScene's 22.52 dB and PanSplat's 19.72 dB, reflecting the cost-volume methods' architectural inability to handle a single input. On the real-world 360Loc dataset (1.4 m baseline), CylinderSplat attains PCC 0.884 and WS-PSNR 28.35 dB, surpassing Splatter360 (PCC 0.866, WS-PSNR 28.14 dB) and PanSplat (WS-PSNR 28.24 dB). The model also uses only 13.6 M parameters and 0.29 s inference time, compared to 76.9 M / 0.48 s for OmniScene.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CylinderSplat outperforms all five baselines across every dataset and metric. Against the closest competitor OmniScene (Cartesian Triplane, feed-forward), CylinderSplat gains +1.01 dB WS-PSNR and +0.119 PCC on Matterport3D 2.0 m, with the gap widening to +1.23 dB on the single-view task where OmniScene's fixed two-view architecture degrades. Against cost-volume methods PanSplat and Splatter360, the advantage is larger (+3.2 dB and +2.45 dB WS-PSNR respectively on Mp3D 2.0 m) and becomes decisive in the single-view setting, where PanSplat drops to 19.72 dB and Splatter360 to 21.81 dB while CylinderSplat maintains 23.75 dB. On the real-world 360Loc dataset, CylinderSplat leads Splatter360 by +0.018 PCC and +0.21 dB WS-PSNR, and PanSplat by +0.696 PCC (PanSplat's PCC of 0.188 indicates near-failure on geometry). The NeRF-based PanoGRF and pinhole-adapted MVSplat trail by 2.8 and 10.5 dB WS-PSNR respectively, confirming that both the panoramic-native representation and the feed-forward 3DGS paradigm are essential.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the feed-forward 3DGS paradigm (Kerbl et al., 2023) to panoramic imagery by replacing the Cartesian Triplane used in OmniScene (Wei et al., 2025) with a cylindrical Triplane, motivated by the Manhattan-world assumption that dominates indoor and urban scenes. It also departs from cost-volume-based panoramic methods (PanSplat, Splatter360) by using an attention-based pixel branch that flexibly handles variable numbers of input views, and from single-Triplane designs by constructing per-camera local Triplanes. The Triplane representation itself draws on Zou et al. (2024), but the cylindrical coordinate adaptation, cross-plane attention in curvilinear space, and RGB retrieval mechanism are novel contributions specific to the panoramic setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Panoramic novel view synthesis is critical for VR and autonomous driving, yet prior feed-forward methods either relied on expensive cost volumes that fail under occlusion or used Cartesian Triplanes that misalign with 360° geometry. CylinderSplat's cylindrical Triplane offers a principled geometric fix that generalizes from single to multiple views without architectural changes, and its direct panoramic rasterizer eliminates cubemap stitching overhead. Key limitations remain: the simple concatenation fusion between pixel and volume branches can produce redundant Gaussians, dynamic objects cause ghosting, and performance on strongly non-Manhattan outdoor scenes (e.g., curved walls, forests) degrades. Open questions include whether more sophisticated fusion (e.g., learned opacity blending) and explicit dynamic-object handling can close these gaps.
