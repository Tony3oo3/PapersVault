## Problem

Despite widespread empirical success of test-time scaling via multi-round self-reflection in LLMs, the mechanism by which transformers learn to optimize their own responses in-context from bandit-style feedback remains theoretically unexplored. Existing in-context learning theory covers supervised regression and value-based RL (TD learning), but leaves a gap for policy optimization where the model iteratively improves its action given historical rewards. This paper asks whether a principled in-context policy optimization framework can both explain self-reflection and yield practical test-time gains.

## Input / Output

The method takes a mathematical question and a system prompt as initial context. At each of N rounds, the model samples k candidate responses, assigns binary rewards via majority voting over final answers, summarizes each response into a compact chain-of-thought, and appends the selected (summary, reward) pair to the growing in-context history. The final output is a response sampled from the model conditioned on the full accumulated history, with no parameter updates at any point. The in-context history is deliberately compressed via CoT summarization to avoid context-length blowup over many rounds.

## Method

Theoretically, the paper proves that a single-layer Linear Self-Attention (LSA) transformer, pretrained via supervised imitation on trajectories from a modified FTRL policy-optimization algorithm (with entropy regularization and a Lagrange multiplier penalizing frequently visited arms), exactly reproduces the expert policy at the population level and with high probability at finite sample size. The training objective is a novel Fisher-weighted logit-matching loss (inspired by Natural Policy Optimization) that is shown to be tightly sandwiched by the standard KL divergence, justifying its use. Practically, ME-ICPO operationalizes this framework: it uses majority voting as a robust self-assessed reward, compresses responses via CoT summarization, and selects the next in-context entry by minimum-entropy criterion (a pessimistic offline-RL principle) to avoid corrupted trajectories and encourage diverse exploration. This yields up to ~19-point gains on AIME 2024 with Qwen2.5-Math-7B while remaining gradient-free and parameter-free at test time.

## Results

On AIME 2024, ME-ICPO improves Qwen2.5-Math-7B's Mean@16 from 11.04% to 30.42% (+19.38 pp) and Accuracy from 11.13% to 30.05% (+18.92 pp), the largest single-benchmark gain. Across MATH 500 difficulty levels (L1–L5), the 7B model gains +9.73 to +13.55 pp in Mean@16, while the 1.5B model gains +4.62 to +11.92 pp. On AMC, improvements are more modest (+5.64 pp Mean@16 for 7B, +3.16 pp for 1.5B), consistent with the easier difficulty. The paper also reports that ME-ICPO's Mean@16 surpasses the base model's majority-voting upper bound, and applying majority voting on top of ME-ICPO outputs yields additional gains.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is ME-ICPO against the same base model without any test-time scaling (i.e., single-pass generation). ME-ICPO wins on every benchmark for both model sizes, with the largest gains on the hardest problems (AIME 2024: +19.38 pp Mean@16 for 7B; MATH-L2: +13.55 pp for 7B, +11.92 pp for 1.5B). The paper additionally reports that ME-ICPO's Mean@16 exceeds the base model's majority-voting (Maj@16) upper bound, a property also observed in TTRL, indicating that iterative in-context refinement can surpass what parallel sampling alone achieves. The ablation (Table 2) confirms that removing the minimum-entropy selection collapses performance to 5.77% Accuracy (vs. 30.05% full), while removing the reward signal drops it to 19.30%, showing both components are necessary. The paper does not include head-to-head numerical comparisons against TTRL, Best-of-N, or Tree-of-Thoughts in the main table, but positions ME-ICPO as competitive with those inference-time algorithms at lower computational cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the in-context learning theory line (Von Oswald et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2024b) from supervised regression to policy optimization with bandit feedback, and departs from in-context RL work (Lin et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2024) by targeting policy optimization rather than value-based methods like TD learning or UCB. The policy optimization framework is built on a modified FTRL/mirror descent with a Lagrange multiplier replacing the unbiased reward estimator, and the training objective introduces a novel Fisher-weighted logit-matching loss inspired by Natural Policy Optimization. Unlike Lin et al. (2023), which requires O(√T) transformer layers to mimic RL algorithms, this paper shows a single-layer linear self-attention network suffices, making the architecture context-length-agnostic. On the practical side, ME-ICPO builds on self-reflection (Madaan et al. 2023; Shinn et al. 2023) and majority voting (Wang et al. 2022) but adds entropy-regularized response selection as a principled mechanism for robust in-context policy updates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first mechanistic, structural account of how a transformer can internally execute policy optimization from in-context bandit feedback, offering a theoretical explanation for why self-reflection and test-time scaling work in LLMs. The one-layer result and the Fisher-weighted-to-KL loss equivalence (Theorem 4.1) suggest that standard cross-entropy pretraining is sufficient to instill policy-optimization behavior, which has broad implications for understanding LLM capabilities. However, the theory is confined to linear bandits with one-hot actions, and the practical ME-ICPO algorithm relies on majority voting as a noisy proxy for true rewards, leaving open whether the framework extends to continuous action spaces, multi-step reasoning, or non-linear reward structures. The gap between the clean linear-bandit theory and the complex mathematical reasoning tasks where ME-ICPO is evaluated remains a significant open question.
