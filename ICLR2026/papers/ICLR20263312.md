## Problem

Existing active perception methods in robotics are typically tied to specific tasks, rely on hand-crafted information-gain heuristics, or assume objects remain stationary during exploration. The paper asks whether a single, principled RL-based framework can learn effective active perception policies across diverse tasks (classification, regression, pose estimation) using only a ground-truth label and a differentiable loss, without task-specific exploration strategies.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263312/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

At each step the agent receives a tactile image (32×32 from a simulated GelSight Mini sensor, or a 5×5 pixel glimpse in low-dimensional tasks) concatenated with the sensor's 3D position, forming a sequence of past observations processed by a shared transformer. The agent outputs two things simultaneously: a movement action (continuous 2D translation, up to 1.5 cm per axis) and a prediction of the target property (10-class logit vector for digits, a scalar volume, or a 3D pose vector). The prediction is scored against ground truth via a differentiable loss (cross-entropy or MSE) that is part of the reward signal, while the action is regularized by a small quadratic penalty.

## Method

APPLE frames active perception as a POMDP where the action space is decomposed into a control action a_t and a prediction y_t, and the reward combines an RL regularizer with a differentiable prediction loss. The key derivation shows the objective's gradient splits into a standard policy-gradient term (for the action policy) and a supervised prediction-loss gradient (for the prediction head), enabling joint optimization of exploration and inference. Both the actor, critic, and prediction head share a ViViT-style backbone: a Vision Transformer encodes each tactile image, and a temporal transformer processes the resulting embeddings plus scalar position data across the observation sequence. Two off-policy variants are proposed—APPLE-SAC (with target networks) and APPLE-CrossQ (with BatchRenorm, no target networks)—where the critic's Bellman residual dynamically recomputes the prediction loss at each replay step, and the policy update augments the standard actor-critic gradient with the supervised loss gradient. This design requires no task-specific heuristics and transfers across tasks by simply changing the loss function and output dimensionality.

## Results

On the TactileMNIST classification task, APPLE-CrossQ achieves 89% final accuracy (81% average) and APPLE-SAC achieves 87% final accuracy (80% average), compared to 74% for the random-policy baseline APPLE-RND. On TactileMNISTVolume (regression), APPLE-SAC reaches a final error of 0.99 cm³ and APPLE-CrossQ 1.05 cm³, versus 1.07 cm³ for APPLE-RND. On the Toolbox pose-estimation task, APPLE-CrossQ achieves a final error of 1.9 cm and 13°, while APPLE-SAC and APPLE-RND stagnate at substantially higher errors. On CircleSquare, both APPLE variants reach ~96–97% final accuracy versus 68% for APPLE-RND and ~50% (random) for HAM.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary external baseline is HAM (Haptic Attention Model, Fleer et al. 2020), an on-policy REINFORCE-based method using an LSTM. HAM is decisively outperformed by both APPLE variants on every task where a direct comparison is possible: on MHSB, HAM reaches only 68% final accuracy after 1M steps while all APPLE variants (including the random-policy baseline) reach ~100% in ~250K steps; on CircleSquare, HAM fails to exceed random guessing (~50%) even after 10M steps of training, whereas APPLE-SAC and APPLE-CrossQ achieve 97% and 96% respectively. HAM cannot be evaluated on TactileMNIST, TactileMNISTVolume, or Toolbox because it lacks an image encoder for high-dimensional tactile inputs. The internal baseline APPLE-RND (random actions, trained perception module) consistently underperforms the active-perception variants, confirming that learned exploration policies are necessary: e.g., 74% vs. 89% on TactileMNIST and 1.07 vs. 0.99 cm³ on TactileMNISTVolume. The paper also notes that APPLE-CrossQ is more robust to hyperparameter transfer across tasks than APPLE-SAC, retaining strong performance on Toolbox without task-specific tuning while APPLE-SAC stagnates.

## Relation to Prior Work

APPLE builds on the actor-critic RL algorithms SAC and CrossQ, adapting them to a partially observable setting where the policy and Q-networks receive observation sequences rather than states, and where the reward is augmented with a differentiable prediction loss. It departs from the prior tactile active perception line (HAM/Fleer et al., Gaussian process and Bayesian optimization methods) in two key ways: (1) it uses off-policy RL with a replay buffer instead of on-policy policy gradients, and (2) it treats the prediction loss as a supervised component of the objective rather than a binary or hand-crafted reward, enabling a unified formulation across classification and regression. The shared ViViT-style transformer backbone for processing tactile images and scalar sensor positions is borrowed from video understanding but repurposed here as a joint representation for both the action policy and the perception head.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

APPLE provides the first RL-based active perception framework demonstrated to work across diverse task types (classification, volume regression, pose estimation) without task-specific heuristics, addressing a gap the authors identify in the literature. The finding that on-policy methods (HAM, PPO) are fundamentally sample-inefficient for active perception—where the same data should be revisited multiple times for the supervised component—offers a clear design principle for future work. Key limitations include high sample requirements (5–10M steps), evaluation exclusively in simulation with a single GelSight sensor, and no real-robot deployment. Open questions include whether pre-trained encoders or sample-efficient RL variants can reduce the data burden, and whether the framework scales to multi-fingered hands and multi-modal (vision + touch) settings.
