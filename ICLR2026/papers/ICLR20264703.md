## Problem

Contrastive learning under imbalanced data distributions lacks rigorous theoretical characterization: it is known empirically that majority classes dominate pair formation and degrade minority-class representations, but no prior work explains *how* imbalance shapes neuron-level training dynamics or *why* it reduces representational capacity. Existing remedies (re-weighting, re-sampling, pruning) are heuristic and do not clarify the mechanistic link between feature frequency, neuron specialization, and downstream performance.

## Input / Output

The model consumes paired token sequences (positive pairs sharing the same latent feature support) together with a set of negative samples, where each token is modeled as a sparse linear combination of d latent features from a column-orthonormal dictionary plus Gaussian noise; feature activation probabilities differ across indices, creating majority (ε_max) and minority (ε_min) features. The encoder outputs an m-dimensional embedding vector, and the theoretical analysis further decomposes each neuron's weight into strong-alignment coefficients on its dominant feature set N_i, weak-alignment coefficients on remaining features, and negligible non-feature components. For empirical validation, standard RGB images from CIFAR-10-LT, CIFAR-100-LT, and ImageNet-LT at varying imbalance ratios ρ ∈ {1, 10, 50, 100, 256} are used with a linear-probe evaluation protocol.

## Method

The paper analyzes a Transformer-MLP encoder (single-head self-attention followed by an MLP with bilateral ReLU activations) trained with the InfoNCE contrastive loss plus L2 regularization under a sparse-coding data model. The core theoretical contribution is a three-stage training-dynamics characterization: (1) neuron weights grow along feature directions at rates proportional to feature frequency ε_j while non-feature components are suppressed; (2) 'lucky' neurons specialize in a single feature while ordinary neurons remain bounded by them; (3) at convergence each neuron aligns strongly with a feature subset N_i whose size scales as d^{1−(ε_min/ε_max)²}, and the number of single-feature-specialized neurons scales as m·d^{−(ε_max/ε_min)²}, both degrading under imbalance. To counteract this, the paper proposes a forward-magnitude-pruning / backward-unmasked-update algorithm: at each epoch a fraction α of the smallest-magnitude neurons is masked during the forward pass, but gradients are computed on the pruned model and applied to the full parameter set. The key mechanism is that minority-aligned neurons have smaller magnitudes (proportional to ε_j/ε_max), so they are pruned more frequently, which inflates their positive-logit contribution in the gradient and amplifies updates along the minority feature direction by a factor of α/d, ultimately making the converged minority-feature coefficient independent of the ε_min/ε_max ratio.

## Results

On CIFAR-10-LT with imbalance ratio ρ=100, magnitude-based pruning improves linear-probe accuracy from 74.24% to 81.31% (+7.07 pp) and reduces the head-tail accuracy gap (Δ₂₀) from 5.69 to 5.62. On CIFAR-100-LT at ρ=100, accuracy rises from 47.12% to 54.93% (+7.81 pp) while Δ₂₀ drops from 7.11 to 4.38. On ImageNet-LT (ρ=256), pruning yields a more modest gain of 63.21% → 65.12% accuracy and reduces Δ₂₀ from 8.47 to 7.21. Improvements grow with increasing imbalance ratio, confirming the theoretical prediction that pruning disproportionately benefits minority features.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's sole empirical comparison is between vanilla contrastive learning (no pruning) and the proposed forward-magnitude pruning with backward-unmasked updates, evaluated via linear probe on three long-tailed benchmarks. Pruning consistently outperforms the vanilla baseline across all datasets and imbalance ratios, with the largest gains appearing under severe imbalance: +7.81 pp on CIFAR-100-LT (ρ=100) and +7.07 pp on CIFAR-10-LT (ρ=100). The head-tail gap Δ₂₀ is also reduced in most settings, most dramatically on CIFAR-100-LT (ρ=100: 7.11 → 4.38), indicating that pruning specifically recovers minority-class performance rather than merely boosting overall accuracy. On ImageNet-LT (ρ=256), the accuracy gain is smaller (+1.91 pp) but Δ₂₀ still improves (8.47 → 7.21), suggesting diminishing but consistent benefits at scale. No other external baselines (e.g., re-weighting, re-sampling, or other pruning schemes) are included in the main comparison table.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the sparse-coding feature-learning paradigm (Allen-Zhu & Li, 2022) from supervised to contrastive learning, and generalizes the one-hidden-layer contrastive dynamics analysis of Wen & Li (2021) to a Transformer-MLP architecture with single-head attention and BReLU activations. It provides the first theoretical treatment of how imbalanced feature frequencies shape neuron-level training dynamics in contrastive learning, a gap explicitly noted as unaddressed by prior convergence and generalization analyses. The pruning-based remedy is grounded in the empirical observations of Jiang et al. (2021) and Qian et al. (2022), but here receives a rigorous mechanistic explanation: small-magnitude neurons aligned with minority features are preferentially pruned, which amplifies their gradient contribution and compensates for lower feature frequency.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The three-stage training dynamics framework (feature growth → lucky-neuron specialization → convergence) gives a neuron-level account of why imbalance degrades representations: minority features are learned with smaller magnitude, fewer neurons specialize in pure features, and more neurons learn mixed-feature superpositions. The theoretical justification for magnitude-based pruning as an imbalance remedy is a clean conceptual contribution, though the analysis is restricted to a single simplified architecture and specific pruning-ratio assumptions that limit direct transfer to production models. Open questions include extending the framework to multi-head attention, deeper stacks, and more realistic data distributions, as well as characterizing optimal pruning schedules beyond the fixed-ratio setting analyzed here.
