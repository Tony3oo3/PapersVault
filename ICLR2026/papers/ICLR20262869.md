## Problem

Safe reinforcement learning requires estimating a feasibility function (e.g., constraint decay function) to identify the feasible region, but the paper identifies a fundamental obstacle called the safety paradox: as a policy becomes safer, constraint-violating samples become sparser, which provably increases the feasibility function's estimation error bound, thereby degrading the feasible region and ultimately undermining safety. Existing remedies—passive methods like prioritized replay and active methods like curiosity-driven exploration—cannot break this self-defeating cycle without either being ineffective on inherently rare samples or inducing harmful behavioral shifts.

## Input / Output

The method consumes continuous environment states and produces continuous actions along with a per-state-action feasibility estimate G(x,u) that determines whether the pair lies inside the feasible region (G ≤ ε). Training data is jointly collected by two policies—a primal policy that avoids violations and a dual policy that deliberately seeks them—so the replay buffer contains a controlled mixture of safe and unsafe transitions. The dual policy is activated only when the running proportion of feasible states exceeds a threshold (0.95), ensuring the total sample budget matches that of single-policy baselines.

## Method

FDPI introduces a dual policy π_d that maximizes a dual feasibility function G_d(x,u) to intentionally generate constraint-violating samples, while KL divergence constraints (enforced via Lagrange multipliers) keep π_d close to the primal policy π_p to prevent numerical underflow in importance sampling. Data from both policies is combined in a shared replay buffer, with distribution shifts corrected by a truncated-trajectory importance sampling ratio r̂_pd(x,u) = ∏_{s=0}^{t(x)} π_p(u_s|x_s)/π_d(u_s|x_s) · π_p(u|x)/π_d(u|x). The algorithm builds on FPI + SAC, learning two action-feasibility networks (one per policy), a shared action-value network, and two policy networks; the primal policy maximizes return inside the feasible region and minimizes feasibility outside it, while the dual policy maximizes its own feasibility under bidirectional KL constraints. Theoretically, the paper proves that the CDF's relative estimation error bound scales with the variance of steps-to-violation, which increases monotonically as the policy becomes safer, formally establishing the safety paradox and motivating the dual-policy data augmentation.

## Results

SAC-FDPI achieves the lowest constraint-violation cost and competitive-to-best return simultaneously across all 14 Safety-Gymnasium environments. On the six representative environments in Table 2, SAC-FDPI reaches near-zero cost (<=0.44) in every setting while maintaining returns within 1-7% of the best baseline. The most dramatic gains appear in SafetyHopper, where SAC-FDPI cuts cost from 2.23 (SAC-FPI) and 6.70 (DSAC-T-Pen) to 0.33 while matching the best return (3118.77). On PointGoal, cost drops from 0.61 (SAC-FPI) to 0.09 with return essentially unchanged (25.77 vs 26.45).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks against nine baselines spanning two families of safe RL algorithms. Iterative unconstrained RL methods (RCPO, PPO-Lag, SAC-Lag) use Lagrange multipliers and generally show high cost (1-5 violating steps per episode) on navigation tasks, though SAC-Lag performs well on locomotion. Constrained policy optimization methods (CPO, FOCOPS, CUP) incorporate constraints directly in each policy update; CPO is the weakest overall (cost up to 520 on SafetyHopper, negative return), while FOCOPS and CUP are more competitive but still trail SAC-FDPI on cost. DSAC-T-Pen, combining the state-of-the-art unconstrained algorithm DSAC-T with a penalty method, is the strongest single-policy baseline, yet SAC-FDPI still halves its cost on SafetyHopper (0.33 vs 6.70) and PointGoal (0.09 vs 0.58). SAC-FPI, the ablation without the dual policy, matches or exceeds SAC-FDPI on return in most environments but suffers persistent cost spikes (e.g., 0.61 on PointGoal, 2.23 on SafetyHopper) due to the safety paradox. Two additional baselines, IPO and CRPO, are compared in a separate table where SAC-FDPI achieves normalized cost 0.003 vs 0.136 (IPO) and 0.149 (CRPO) with return 0.938 vs 0.484 and 0.612 respectively. The consistent pattern is that SAC-FDPI's dual-policy mechanism provides a clear safety advantage over all single-policy baselines without sacrificing return.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Feasible Policy Iteration (FPI) by Yang et al. (2023) with a dual-policy mechanism and combines it with Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) to form SAC-FDPI. Conceptually, it departs from prior approaches to sample sparsity in safe RL—both passive methods (prioritized replay) and active methods (curiosity-driven exploration, adversarial policies)—by introducing a dedicated dual policy that maximizes constraint violations while remaining close to the primal policy via KL divergence constraints, with importance sampling to correct the resulting distributional shift. The key theoretical departure is the formal identification and proof of the 'safety paradox': a self-defeating cycle where improved policy safety increases the estimation error bound of the feasibility function, which no prior safe RL work had explicitly characterized.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The safety paradox provides a principled explanation for why existing safe RL algorithms exhibit persistent cost spikes even at late training stages, and the dual-policy solution is practical because it requires no environment modifications or reward shaping. However, the method is designed for offline-training/online-deployment (OTOD) settings; extending it to simultaneous online training and deployment (SOTD) would require integrating model-based safe exploration techniques, which the authors note as complementary but not addressed. Open questions include whether the safety paradox and its resolution generalize to other feasibility functions beyond CDF and CVF, and whether the KL-constrained dual policy approach transfers to continuous real-world control where even simulator violations carry cost.
