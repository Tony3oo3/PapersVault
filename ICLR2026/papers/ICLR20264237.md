## Problem

Vision-language models trained on web-scale datasets like LAION-400M exhibit strong demographic biases, but the causal link between dataset composition and model behavior has remained unmeasured because no demographic annotations existed for the full dataset. This paper closes that gap by creating person-centric annotations for all of LAION-400M, enabling the first direct, large-scale empirical test of how dataset-level imbalances and harmful associations transfer into downstream model bias.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264237/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes 256×256 RGB images from LAION-400M (375M recovered pairs) paired with their original alt-text captions. After YOLOv11-1 detection and a 30-pixel minimum side-length filter, the system produces 199,931,986 person bounding boxes across 107.5M images, each annotated with a perceived gender label (female/male/mixed/unclear), a perceived race/ethnicity label (7 categories + unclear), and a synthetic person-centric caption. Downstream analyses yield demographic distribution statistics, crime/sentiment correlation measures, SAE-derived identity-topic PMI scores, and linear bias-transfer fits for CLIP and Stable Diffusion.

## Method

The annotation pipeline is a three-stage cascade: (1) YOLOv11-1 detects all person bounding boxes at a low 0.25 confidence threshold to maximize recall; (2) an ensemble of three MLLMs (Phi-3.5-Vision, LLaVA-NeXT, InternVL3-2B) labels sampled subsets, and only consensus predictions are retained to fine-tune SigLIP classifiers (97.2% gender accuracy, 87.4% race/ethnicity accuracy) that then label the full dataset; (3) InternVL3-8B generates person-centric captions by drawing a red bounding-box frame as a visual prompt. For bias analysis, the authors train Sparse Autoencoders on granite-embedding-english-r2 caption embeddings and compute pointwise mutual information between 14 intersectional identities and 2,392 topics, aggregated across 24 SAE configurations with varying sparsity and expansion factors. Finally, a linear fit between dataset-level gender-concept co-occurrence ratios and model-level cosine-similarity or image-generation gender ratios predicts 60–70% of variance in CLIP and Stable Diffusion bias.

## Results

The paper's headline quantitative finding is that a linear fit predicts 60–70% of gender bias in CLIP and Stable Diffusion models from direct concept–gender co-occurrences in LAION-400M: Pearson ρ ∈ {0.75, 0.80, 0.84} and R² ∈ {0.57, 0.64, 0.71} across three image sources (CausalFaces, Phase, LAION-400M) for CLIP, and R² = 0.64 / 0.63 (ρ = 0.80) for SD-1.1 / SD-1.4. The gender classifier achieves 97.2% accuracy on its held-out test set (95% on Phase, 90% on FACET), while the race/ethnicity classifier reaches 87.4% raw accuracy with Cohen's κ = 0.638 against human annotators (human–human κ = 0.654). Demographic analysis reveals men appear +57% more often than expected with crime-related words, Black individuals +51%, and Middle Eastern individuals +206%; White and East Asian groups decrease by −22%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an audit/dataset paper rather than a method-comparison study, so there are no competing baselines to beat. The closest comparisons are internal validation checks: (1) The gender classifier's 97.2% test accuracy is validated against two external benchmarks—Phase (95%) and FACET (90%)—with the FACET gap attributed to ~10% label noise in that dataset. (2) For race/ethnicity, the classifier's κ = 0.638 is nearly identical to human–human agreement (κ = 0.654), confirming that perceived race is inherently subjective and the model performs at the ceiling of human consistency. (3) For bias transfer, in-distribution LAION-400M images yield the highest R² (0.71) versus Phase (0.64) and CausalFaces (0.57), showing that probing with the same distribution as training is critical. (4) Guilbeault et al.'s 3,488 social categories produce stronger correlations than So-B-IT's 405 keywords, likely because So-B-IT includes more generic adjectives that co-occur outside people contexts. (5) SD-1.1 and SD-1.4 produce nearly identical bias-transfer results (R² = 0.64 vs. 0.63), confirming robustness across model versions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of dataset auditing work on LAION-400M (Birhane et al., 2023; Al Sahili et al., 2025; Friedrich et al., 2023), which was largely limited to text-subset analysis or small annotated image subsets, by providing full person-level annotations (bounding boxes, perceived gender, race/ethnicity, and captions) for the entire 400M-pair dataset. It builds on the SAE-based concept discovery methodology of Rao et al. (2024) for the thematic PMI analysis, and on prior bias measurement protocols for CLIP and Stable Diffusion (Hamidieh et al., 2024; Guilbeault et al., 2024). The key departure is granularity and scale: prior efforts annotated faces (Zheng et al., 2022) or assigned a single image-level gender label, whereas this work provides per-person labels for ~200M bounding boxes, enabling direct quantitative linking of dataset co-occurrence statistics to downstream model bias via linear fits.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 60–70% R² for linear prediction of gender bias from direct concept-identity co-occurrences provides the first large-scale empirical quantification of how much model bias is simply inherited from data distribution versus introduced by training dynamics, a question that has remained largely assumed rather than measured. The annotations (276M bounding boxes, 200M demographic labels, person-centric captions) constitute a critical resource for future dataset rebalancing, targeted subset extraction, and controlled bias experiments that were previously infeasible at web scale. Open questions include second-order (indirect) bias propagation, the role of optimizers and sampling strategies, and race/ethnicity bias transfer, which the authors found inconclusive due to low co-occurrence counts for non-White groups. The SAE-based finding that White identities are associated with generic 'default' topics while other groups receive culturally specific (and sometimes stereotyping) associations highlights a structural representational asymmetry that has broader implications for how foundation models encode social categories.
