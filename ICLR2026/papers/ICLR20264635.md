## Problem

Existing scaling laws such as Chinchilla optimize the allocation of training compute between model size and data but omit inference costs, which constitute the dominant expense in real-world LLM deployment. Prior attempts to incorporate architecture into scaling laws either considered only the aspect ratio (hidden size over layers) or required impractical assumptions about total inference tokens over a model's lifespan. This paper asks whether the trade-off between inference efficiency and accuracy can be explicitly captured through architectural design choices under a fixed parameter and token budget.

## Input / Output

The method takes a fixed non-embedding parameter count N, a training token budget D, and a maximum allowable training loss L_t (≥ L_opt) as inputs, and produces a complete architecture specification: hidden size d_model, mlp-to-attention ratio r_mlp/attn, number of attention heads, intermediate (feed-forward) size, and GQA factor. The number of layers is held fixed per parameter scale, and the per-head dimension is fixed at 64 (for N ≤ 1B) or 128 (for N ≥ 3B), reducing the continuous search space to two variables (d_model and r_mlp/attn) plus a small discrete GQA enumeration. Inference efficiency I_N(P) is measured empirically via vLLM or SGLang throughput on A100/H200 GPUs rather than derived analytically, since it depends heavily on hardware and serving configuration.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-step conditional scaling law that augments Chinchilla with architectural information: first, the standard Chinchilla power-law yields a reference loss L_opt(N,D); second, architectural variants are calibrated relative to this reference using separable U-shaped functions of d_model/√N and r_mlp/attn (of the form a₀ + a₁·log(x) + a₂/x), fitted via Levenberg-Marquardt on 200+ models spanning 80M–3B parameters and 8B–100B tokens. The paper demonstrates that both architectural factors exhibit consistent U-shaped loss curves across model scales with nearly identical normalized optima, enabling reliable extrapolation from smaller to larger models. The search framework then solves a constrained optimization—maximizing inference efficiency I_N(P) subject to L(P|N,D) ≤ L_t—over d_model and r_mlp/attn, followed by a local enumeration over feasible GQA values (prime factors of n_heads) with early stopping. This progressive fitting strategy (fit on 80M/145M/297M, predict at 1B/3B) achieves up to 42% higher inference throughput and 2.1% accuracy gains over LLaMA-3.2 baselines without training at the target scale.

## Results

Under identical training budgets (100B tokens), Panda-1B achieves 57.0% average accuracy across nine downstream tasks, outperforming LLaMA-3.2-1B (54.9%) by 2.1%, while Panda-3B reaches 62.5% versus LLaMA-3.2-3B's 61.9% (+0.6%). The inference-optimized Surefire variants (which satisfy a loss constraint matching LLaMA-3.2's training loss) deliver up to 42% higher inference throughput on A100/vLLM and up to 47% on H200/SGLang while maintaining or exceeding baseline accuracy. The conditional scaling law predicts optimal architectures with low MSE and high Spearman correlation across progressive scale-ups from 80M to 3B parameters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is LLaMA-3.2 (1B and 3B), which the paper uses both as an architectural reference and as the accuracy/throughput benchmark. Panda-1B outperforms LLaMA-3.2-1B by 2.1% in average downstream accuracy (57.0% vs. 54.9%) and achieves a lower training loss (2.782 vs. 2.803), while Panda-3B edges out LLaMA-3.2-3B by 0.6% (62.5% vs. 61.9%) with a marginally lower loss (2.619 vs. 2.625). On inference throughput, Surefire-1B and Surefire-3B (which match LLaMA-3.2's loss constraint) deliver up to 42% higher tokens/s on A100/vLLM and up to 47% on H200/SGLang across all batch sizes tested (16–128). The paper also briefly compares against OLMo-2-1B-HF and Qwen2.5-3B-HF in Appendix H, noting that OLMo-2 is close to the predicted optimal design but remains inference-inefficient due to its hidden dimension and GQA choices, while Qwen2.5-3B is inference-efficient but less accurate than LLaMA-3.2-3B. Overall, the proposed architecture search framework wins on both accuracy and throughput simultaneously against LLaMA-3.2, which the paper identifies as the dominant open-source baseline at these scales.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Chinchilla scaling law framework (Hoffmann et al., 2022) by incorporating multiple architectural factors—hidden size, MLP-to-attention ratio, and grouped-query attention—into a conditional scaling law that predicts training loss for specific architecture configurations. It builds directly on Bian et al. (2025), which first introduced architecture-aware scaling laws but considered only the aspect ratio (hidden size / number of layers); this work generalizes that to a multi-factor conditional formulation with a two-step reference-and-calibration approach. The paper also departs from Sardana et al. (2023), which attempted to fold inference FLOPs into scaling laws but required impractical assumptions about total tokens generated over a model's lifespan, by instead treating inference efficiency as a separate objective in a constrained optimization over architecture choices.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: the derived optimal architectures achieve up to 42% higher inference throughput while maintaining or improving accuracy over LLaMA-3.2 at both 1B and 3B scales, validated across vLLM/SGLang on A100/H200 hardware. The framework provides a general, reusable methodology for architecture search that could guide future LLM design decisions. Key limitations include the restriction to dense models (no MoE scaling laws), evaluation only up to 3B parameters, and pre-training only (post-training effects unknown). Open questions include whether the conditional scaling law coefficients remain stable at 7B+ scales, how the framework extends to MoE architectures, and whether the optimal MLP-to-attention ratio of ~1.0 holds under different training regimes or data distributions.
