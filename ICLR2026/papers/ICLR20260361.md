## Problem

The dominant predict-then-optimize paradigm in operations research prescribes decisions but provides no principled way to quantify the probability that a candidate decision—whether algorithm-generated or expert-proposed—remains optimal under the true realization of uncertain parameters. This opacity prevents human decision-makers from auditing algorithmic recommendations or validating their own domain-knowledge-driven alternatives, creating a trust gap in high-stakes settings like healthcare, energy, and public policy.

## Input / Output

Given a user-specified candidate decision z (e.g., a production plan or substation upgrade vector), a test covariate x_{n+1}, a calibration dataset of (x_i, y_i) pairs, a fitted generative model f̂, and known optimization parameters θ, CREDO outputs a distribution-free upper bound α̂(z) on the probability that z is suboptimal. The bound is constructed as the minimum coverage level α such that a conformalized ℓ₂-ball centered at a generated prediction is fully contained within the inverse feasible region π^{-1}(z; θ); for linear programs this reduces to a closed-form expression over the polytope's vertices, yielding O(K·n·|V(θ)|) computation with no iterative optimization.

## Method

CREDO operates in two steps. First, it inverts the optimization mapping: for a given decision z, it characterizes the inverse feasible region π^{-1}(z; θ) as the intersection of half-spaces {y : g(z,y;θ) ≤ g(z',y;θ)} over all feasible z', converting the risk question into estimating P{Y ∈ π^{-1}(z;θ)}. Second, it applies generative conformal prediction: a conditional generative model (e.g., a Gaussian Mixture) draws K diverse predictions ŷ_{n+1}^{(k)}, each serving as the center of an ℓ₂-ball conformalized set whose radius is calibrated to guarantee valid (1−α) coverage under exchangeability. The per-draw risk α̂^{(k)}(z) is the smallest α making the ball a subset of π^{-1}(z;θ), and the final estimator averages over K draws. The key theoretical contribution is a conservativeness guarantee (Theorem 1) via e-value conformal prediction's post-hoc validity, a weighted Monte Carlo interpretation (Proposition 2) showing the conformalized weights ensure the bound, and a true-positive-rate result (Proposition 3) proving that increasing K monotonically reduces over-conservative exclusions—highlighting why generative sampling outperforms point prediction under multi-modal uncertainty.

## Results

On the empirical confidence ranking metric (lower is better), CREDO achieves the best or tied-best performance across most evaluation settings. In Setting II (σ=1), CREDO attains a perfect ranking of 1.00±0.00, compared to PTO (3.36±0.48), RO (6.00±0.00), SPO+ (4.67±1.56), and DFL (3.96±2.07). In Setting I (σ=10), CREDO scores 1.48±0.52 versus PTO (2.24±0.79) and RO (3.00±0.00). On the real-world power grid dataset, CREDO achieves 1.75±0.92, tying PTO (1.75±1.69) while substantially outperforming RO (3.00±1.29) and SPO+ (2.67±1.43). CREDO's advantage is most pronounced under high uncertainty (σ≥1) and in multi-modal settings (Setting II), while point-prediction baselines match CREDO only in the near-deterministic regime (Setting I, σ=0.1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CREDO outperforms all four prescriptive baselines (PTO, RO, SPO+, DFL) in the majority of evaluation settings, with the largest margins in high-uncertainty and multi-modal regimes. Against PTO (Bertsimas & Kallus, 2020), CREDO wins in 5 of 7 settings (e.g., 1.00 vs 3.36 in Setting II σ=1; 1.48 vs 2.24 in Setting I σ=10) and ties on the real-data setting (1.75 vs 1.75), losing only in Setting I σ=0.1 where the problem is nearly deterministic. Against RO (Bertsimas & Thiele, 2006), CREDO dominates across all settings, with RO's worst-case guarantees producing overly conservative decisions (e.g., 6.00 in Setting II σ=1 vs CREDO's 1.00). Against SPO+ (Elmachtoub & Grigas, 2022), CREDO wins in 5 of 7 settings, with the gap widening as σ increases (e.g., 1.48 vs 2.02 in Setting I σ=10). Against DFL (Amos & Kolter, 2017), CREDO wins in 5 of 7 settings, though DFL is the closest competitor in some low-variance settings (e.g., 1.83 vs 1.61 in Setting I σ=1). The paper also shows via ablation that CREDO's generative conformal approach maintains 100% conservativeness (matching the point-prediction variant) while achieving a significantly higher true positive rate that grows with the number of generated samples K, whereas a naive Monte Carlo variant (NS) drops to only ~50% conservativeness.

## Relation to Prior Work

CREDO sits at the intersection of three research threads: (i) inverse optimization, which characterizes the set of outcome parameters under which a given decision is optimal (Chan et al., 2025; Tavaslioglu et al., 2018); (ii) conformal prediction, specifically its 'inverted' or backward use to estimate the miscoverage rate of a fixed prediction set (Gauthier et al., 2025a; Singh et al., 2024); and (iii) generative conformal prediction, which replaces point predictors with generative models to tighten prediction sets (Wang et al., 2023; Zheng & Zhu, 2024). The key conceptual departure from prior work is the shift from prescriptive to descriptive: whereas PTO, RO, DRO, SPO+, and DFL all recommend decisions, CREDO audits a user-specified decision by inverting the conformal prediction pipeline. The theoretical backbone relies on e-value conformal prediction (Vovk, 2025; Balinsky & Balinsky, 2024) for its post-hoc validity property, which is what enables the conservativeness guarantee without assumptions on generative model quality.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CREDO addresses a genuine gap in high-stakes decision support: experienced practitioners need a principled, distribution-free way to validate their own candidate decisions against data, rather than being told what to do by a black-box optimizer. The framework's main limitation is the acknowledged selection bias problem—using CREDO's risk estimate to select a decision and then re-evaluating that same decision can invalidate the exchangeability assumption, and the paper only offers data splitting as a mitigation. Open questions include developing principled adjustments for data-dependent decision selection, extending the closed-form LP estimator to general convex problems, and understanding how the first-order Taylor approximation in the conservativeness proof behaves in high-dimensional or adversarial settings. The practical impact is strongest in domains where human expertise and algorithmic tools must coexist (healthcare, energy, public policy), and the closed-form LP solution makes deployment feasible at scale.
