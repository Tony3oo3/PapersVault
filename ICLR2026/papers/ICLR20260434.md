## Problem

Oblique decision trees offer more compact and expressive decision boundaries than axis-aligned CART, but finding optimal oblique hyperplane splits is NP-hard. Existing practical methods rely on greedy heuristics, evolutionary search, or differentiable approximations without strong theoretical guarantees, leaving a gap between the theoretical optimality of oblique splits and their efficient, principled computation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260434/cropped_page6_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard tabular data as (x, y) pairs where x ∈ R^d is augmented with a bias term to form x̃ ∈ R^{d+1}, and y ∈ R is a continuous target. It produces a single oblique regression tree whose internal nodes store pairs of linear coefficient vectors defining hyperplane splits, and whose leaves store linear predictors; the tree's output is a piecewise linear function evaluated by routing each input to its leaf and applying the corresponding linear model. Unlike ensemble methods, HRT yields one interpretable tree with typically 2–7 depth and 4–93 leaves across benchmarks.

## Method

HRT reformulates each node split as a nonlinear least-squares problem over two linear predictors connected by a max (or min) hinge, so the decision boundary emerges naturally as the hyperplane where the two linear scores are equal. The alternating procedure—fit OLS (optionally ridge-regularized) on the current data partition, then reassign points based on the updated linear scores—is proven to be exactly equivalent to a damped Newton/Gauss-Newton step within fixed partitions, since the hinge is locally linear and the Hessian reduces to the block-diagonal Gram matrix. A backtracking line-search variant is shown to yield monotone decrease of the node-level objective and convergence to the OLS minimizer once the partition stabilizes. Hierarchically composing these hinge-based splits across tree depth gives the model ReLU-like piecewise linear expressivity, and the resulting model class is proven to be a universal approximator with an explicit O(δ²) rate tied to partition diameter.

## Results

On 13 real-world regression benchmarks, HRT achieves the best or near-best RMSE on 9 of 13 datasets, with statistically significant improvements (p<0.05) on Ailerons (1.64 vs. 1.66 DTSemNet), Fried (1.09 vs. 1.09 XGB), and Kinematics (0.102 vs. 0.125 XGB). On the large-scale YearPred dataset (515k samples, 90 features), HRT reaches 8.97 RMSE, marginally beating DTSemNet (8.99) and XGBoost (9.01). Structurally, HRT produces dramatically more compact trees: e.g., on Concrete it achieves competitive RMSE with depth 3 and 5.8 leaves, versus CART's depth 11.2 and 113 leaves, and on Abalone/CPUact/Ailerons it uses only depth 2 with 4 leaves.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HRT outperforms all single-tree oblique baselines (DTSemNet, DGT, TAO-A, TAO-O, CART, M5, Linear tree) on the majority of datasets, with the largest margin on Kinematics (0.102 vs. 0.125 for XGB, 0.135 for DGT) and Airfoil (2.63 vs. 2.73 for TAO-A). Against the strongest ensemble baseline XGBoost, HRT wins on Abalone (2.11 vs. 2.20), CPUact (2.56 vs. 2.57), YearPred (8.97 vs. 9.01), Airfoil (2.63 vs. 2.77), Kinematics (0.102 vs. 0.125), and C&C (0.140 vs. 0.142), ties on Fried (1.09 vs. 1.09), but loses on CTSlice (1.41 vs. 1.18) and Blog (25.41 vs. 23.02). Against DTSemNet (the strongest single-tree oblique baseline), HRT wins on Abalone, CPUact, Ailerons, YearPred, Airfoil, Fried, Kinematics, and C&C, while DTSemNet edges ahead on CTSlice (1.45 vs. 1.41 is actually HRT winning—correction: DTSemNet 1.45, HRT 1.41, so HRT wins) and Blog (32.30 vs. 25.41, HRT wins). The two datasets where HRT clearly underperforms are CTSlice (XGB 1.18 vs. HRT 1.41) and Blog (XGB 23.02 vs. HRT 25.41), both high-dimensional settings where ensemble methods retain an advantage. A key differentiator is structural compactness: HRT consistently produces trees 2–10× shallower with far fewer leaves than CART, TAO, and DGT while matching or exceeding their accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

HRT extends the oblique regression tree lineage (Murthy 1994, Loh 2014) and the alternating-optimization paradigm of TAO (Carreira-Perpinan & Tavallali 2018) by reframing each node split as a nonlinear least-squares problem over two linear models joined by a max/min hinge. The key conceptual departure is showing that the resulting alternating OLS-fitting-and-repartitioning procedure is exactly a damped Gauss-Newton step within fixed partitions, providing a Newton-method interpretation and convergence guarantees that prior oblique-tree methods (DGT, DTSemNet, TAO) lack. The hinge formulation also connects to Breiman's hinging hyperplanes (1993) and the broader ReLU/piecewise-linear expressivity literature, but HRT uses the hinge to define the split boundary itself rather than as an additive basis function.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first clean convergence proof and explicit O(δ²) universal approximation rate for an oblique regression tree construction algorithm, filling a theoretical gap that has persisted since the NP-hardness result of Laurent & Rivest (1976). Practically, HRT produces trees with depth 2–7 and as few as 4 leaves while matching or beating single-tree baselines (TAO, DGT, DTSemNet, CART) on RMSE, suggesting oblique splits can replace deep axis-aligned trees in many settings. Limitations include the nondegenerate-hinge assumption in the convergence proof, the eigenvalue condition required for the approximation rate (mitigated by ridge regularization), and the fact that all comparisons are single-tree; the paper notes but does not fully develop extensions to classification or ensemble integration, which remain open questions for broader adoption.
