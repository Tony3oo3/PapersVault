## Problem

Standard imitation learning matches only the expert's expected return (occupancy measure), making it inherently risk-neutral and blind to the shape of the return distribution. Existing risk-sensitive IL methods extend this by matching CVaR at a single level, but this captures only a narrow slice of the distribution and relies on Markovian policies that are provably insufficient for capturing non-Markovian expert risk behavior (a gap of at least 0.5 in Wasserstein distance even at horizon 3). The paper asks: can we match the expert's entire return distribution in Wasserstein distance, and do so with provably efficient algorithms?

## Input / Output

The method consumes a dataset of N expert trajectories (state-action sequences of length H), the expert's reward function r^E mapping (state, action, stage) to [0,1], and a discretization parameter θ ∈ (0,1] that controls the granularity of cumulative-reward tracking. RS-KT additionally requires the environment's transition model p. The output is a non-Markovian policy π̂ ∈ Π(r_θ^E) stored as a lookup table over (state, stage, discretized cumulative reward, action) with O(SAH·H/θ) memory, where the policy's action choice at each step depends on the current state, the time step, and the discretized sum of rewards accumulated so far. The discretization parameter θ introduces a tunable trade-off: approximation error scales as Hθ while memory scales as O(1/θ).

## Method

The core technical contribution is a two-part construction. First, the paper introduces the policy class Π(r_θ^E) — policies whose action depends on state, stage, and the discretized cumulative reward — and proves (Lemma 4.1) that the undiscretized class Π(r^E) always contains a policy with exactly the expert's return distribution, while the discretized version incurs at most Hθ Wasserstein error (Lemma 4.2). Second, two algorithms exploit this class: RS-BC (offline, no transition model) directly counts state-action-cumulative-reward tuples in the expert dataset to estimate the optimal policy π_{r_θ^E} via empirical frequencies, requiring Õ(SH⁶/ε³) samples; RS-KT (known transition model) first estimates the expert's return distribution η̂ from the data, then solves a linear program over occupancy measures in an augmented MDP with state space S × Y^θ to find the policy in Π(r_θ^E) whose induced return distribution is closest to η̂ in Wasserstein distance, achieving O(H²/ε²) sample complexity independent of S and A. The LP formulation (Eq. 10) has polynomially many variables and constraints, making RS-KT computationally tractable while being dramatically more sample-efficient than RS-BC for large MDPs.

## Results

The primary metric is the Wasserstein distance between the expert's return distribution and the learned policy's return distribution. In the standard setting (S,A,H = 2,2,5; non-Markovian expert; θ=0.05), RS-BC reduces error from 0.081 (N=20) to 0.005 (N=10000), while RS-KT goes from 0.095 to 0.011. In contrast, BC and MIMIC-MD plateau at ~0.068 even with N=10000, confirming the expressivity gap of Markovian policies. In the large-MDP setting (S=300, A=5, H=5), RS-KT's proxy estimate η̂ drops from 0.169 to 0.008 as N grows from 20 to 10000, while RS-BC and BC remain stuck near 0.166–0.174, validating the theoretical O(H²/ε²) vs. Õ(SAH⁶/ε³) sample complexity gap.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RS-BC and RS-KT consistently outperform both BC (Foster et al., 2024) and MIMIC-MD (Rajaraman et al., 2020) in the non-Markovian expert setting, with the gap widening as N increases: at N=10000, RS-BC achieves 0.005 Wasserstein distance versus 0.068 for both baselines (a ~13× improvement). The baselines plateau because their Markovian policy class cannot capture the expert's risk-sensitive, history-dependent behavior, whereas RS-BC/RS-KT use the more expressive Π(r_θ^E) class. In the large-MDP setting (S=300), RS-KT's advantage is dramatic: its proxy error drops to 0.008 at N=10000 while RS-BC and BC remain near 0.166–0.174, confirming the theoretical independence of RS-KT's sample complexity from S and A. However, when the expert is itself Markovian (Q3), BC actually outperforms all other methods (0.003 vs. 0.004–0.010 at N=10000), since it operates on a smaller, unbiased hypothesis space. RS-BC is also more robust to large discretization θ (0.5) than RS-KT, which approaches the worst-case Hθ/2 ≈ 0.2 bound.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the imitation learning literature from occupancy measure matching (which captures only expected return) to full return distribution matching in Wasserstein distance, and from the partial risk-sensitive IL of Santara et al. (2018) and Lacotte et al. (2019) (which match only expectation plus CVaR at one level) to matching the entire distribution. The key methodological departure is the introduction of a structured non-Markovian policy class Π(r_θ) that conditions on discretized cumulative reward, drawing on the discretization approach of Lazzati & Metelli (2025) and the augmented-state MDP construction from Bauerle & Rieder (2014). The RS-BC algorithm adapts the counting-based BC structure of Foster et al. (2024) to this richer policy class, while RS-KT formulates the known-transition case as a linear program over occupancy measures in an augmented MDP, analogous to the LP-based apprenticeship learning of Syed et al. (2008).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that full return distribution matching is statistically tractable (polynomial sample complexity) in both known- and unknown-reward settings, resolving a gap left by prior risk-sensitive IL methods that were either misspecified (Markovian policies) or captured only a slice of the distribution (single-level CVaR). The dramatic sample complexity reduction of RS-KT (independent of S and A) over RS-BC and standard IL is a strong theoretical result, though its practical impact is currently limited by the tabular, finite-horizon setting. Key open questions include extending to continuous or large-scale environments, developing a practical algorithm for the unknown-reward robust formulation (Eq. 12), establishing matching lower bounds, and validating on real-world risk-sensitive tasks such as autonomous driving or finance.
