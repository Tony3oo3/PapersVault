## Problem

Causal structure recovery in dynamical systems on networks relies on Wiener filter (WF)-based conditional independence tests, but it is unclear whether computing these projections in the time domain (TD) or frequency domain (FD) yields better accuracy and efficiency. Moreover, the phase properties inherent to FD Wiener filters—having no TD analogue—remain unexploited for recovering Markov Equivalence Graphs (MEGs), and the combinatorial explosion of pairwise CI tests in algorithms like PC renders them intractable for larger networks.

## Input / Output

The method consumes multi-node time-series data (n nodes, T samples each) partitioned into R = T/N segments of length N for FFT computation, along with a user-specified threshold τ that determines whether a WF coefficient is deemed zero (no edge) or non-zero (edge present). The output is a Markov Equivalence Graph: a directed graph where edges are oriented where identifiable and left undirected (bipartite) where only the skeleton is recoverable. The threshold τ is tuned via a sparsity-metric-based iterative search using known bounds on average, maximum, and minimum degree of the network.

## Method

The paper establishes that FFT-based FD Wiener filter computation achieves an O(L²/log N) asymptotic speedup over TD least-squares estimation, where L is the TD lag window and N is the FFT length, and provides non-asymptotic concentration bounds showing comparable sample complexity (T = O(Nn) vs. T = O(Ln)) with better finite-sample reconstruction error. Two algorithms are proposed: Wiener-PC (W-PC), a modified PC algorithm that replaces static Fisher-z/chi-square tests with WF-based CI tests computed in the FD, and Wiener-Phase, which exploits both the magnitude (recovering the kin/Markov-blanket structure via Lemma 4.1) and the imaginary part (recovering the exact skeleton via Lemma 4.2 under phase-alignment assumptions) of the FD Wiener filter to identify colliders and orient edges without any combinatorial CI test loop. Wiener-Phase achieves O(n³(T log N + q²)) complexity versus O(Tn^{q+3} log N) for W-PC, making it dramatically faster for dense graphs with large separating-set size q. The approach is validated on synthetic VAR networks, the river-runoff dataset, and measured data from BJT and MOSFET transistor circuits, where it outperforms Fisher-Z PC, CD-NOD, Granger Causality, and Time-Aware PC in both accuracy and runtime.

## Results

On a 20-node synthetic dynamical network (128,000 samples, 22 edges), FFT-WPC achieves perfect reconstruction (F1=100, CS=100, TPR=100, 1-FPR=100) with a runtime of 1011.62s, while Wiener-Phase achieves F1=93.55, CS=92.97 in just 1.37s. On the real-world river-runoff dataset (4,600 samples), FFT-WPC attains F1=75 and CS=67.5, substantially outperforming the best prior method TPC (F1=43.64, CS=67.1) while running ~171× faster (9.04s vs 1548.29s). On both BJT and MOSFET hardware circuit datasets (500,000 samples each), both FFT-WPC and Wiener-Phase achieve perfect F1=100 and CS=100, with runtimes under 0.08s compared to TPC's 20–24s.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FFT-WPC dominates all four baselines on the 20-node synthetic network, achieving perfect F1=100 versus TPC's 78.57, GC's 48.65, Fisher-Z PC's 24.44, and CD-NOD's 23.80. Wiener-Phase trades a small accuracy drop (F1=93.55) for a ~2900× speedup over TPC (1.37s vs 4016.57s) while still outperforming every baseline on both F1 and CS. On the river-runoff dataset, FFT-WPC (F1=75) more than doubles TPC's F1 (43.64) and runs 171× faster, though TPC achieves a higher TPR (92.31 vs 69.20) at the cost of a much lower 1-FPR (74.79 vs 98.30). On both transistor circuit datasets, FFT-WPC and Wiener-Phase achieve perfect F1=100 and CS=100, outperforming GC (F1=75–85.71), TPC (F1=60–75), CD-NOD (F1=40–66.67), and Fisher-Z PC (F1=40), while running 250–500× faster than TPC. The computational advantage of the frequency-domain approach is most dramatic at scale: on a 50-node network, Wiener-Phase completes in 19.49s versus FFT-WPC's ~40 hours, with Wiener-Phase achieving CS=99.83%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Wiener-filter-based causal inference framework of Materassi & Salapaka (2012, 2013) and Veedu et al. (2021), which established that WF support and phase properties can recover kin structure and skeleton of linear dynamical influence models. The key departure is shifting the WF computation from time-domain least squares to an FFT-based frequency-domain formulation, building on the FD regression framework of Doddi et al. (2022) but extending it to the non-penalized MMSE setting and providing non-asymptotic concentration bounds. The Wiener-Phase algorithm is a novel synthesis that combines the kin-recovery lemma (Materassi & Salapaka 2012) with the phase-based skeleton lemma (Veedu et al. 2021) to avoid the combinatorial CI-test explosion of the PC algorithm (Spirtes et al. 2000).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The O(L²/log N) computational speedup and the Wiener-Phase algorithm's avoidance of combinatorial CI tests make causal structure recovery practical for larger networks (demonstrated at 50 nodes in ~20 seconds vs. ~40 hours for the FD W-PC variant). The validation on real field data (river-runoff) and physical hardware (BJT/MOSFET circuits) strengthens practical relevance. Limitations include the linearity assumption of the LDIM framework, the phase-alignment requirement (Assumption 1) for Wiener-Phase's exact skeleton recovery, and the need for threshold tuning. Open questions include whether the phase-based approach extends to non-linear or time-varying systems, and how the methods perform when the exogenous noise PSDM is non-diagonal.
