## Problem

While prior work has extensively studied biases in what LLMs generate, far less attention has been paid to the factors that influence which information LLMs select and present to users when acting as agents on online platforms. This paper investigates whether LLMs encode latent preferences for source entities (news outlets, academic venues, e-commerce platforms) that systematically steer their information selection, and whether these preferences are predictable, context-sensitive, and resistant to prompting interventions.

## Input / Output

The evaluation presents models with pairs of semantically identical content (news articles, paper abstracts, product listings) that differ only in their attributed source, alongside direct ranking prompts where models explicitly compare source identities in various representations (brand names, URLs, social media handles, follower counts, H5-Index). Outputs include per-source preference percentages aggregated from all pairwise comparisons, Kendall Tau rank correlations across models and source representations, and in real-world case studies, the distribution of selected articles by political leaning or seller attributes (price, rating, delivery speed, BuyBox alignment).

## Method

The paper employs a dual evaluation paradigm: direct evaluation (explicitly asking models to rank two sources) and indirect evaluation (presenting semantically identical content tagged with different sources and asking for a selection), which together disentangle latent source preferences from content-driven effects. Experiments span 12 LLMs from 6 providers across three domains (news, research, e-commerce) using controlled synthetic data, complemented by two real-world case studies (AllSides news article selection with source swaps and 'do not be biased' prompting, and Amazon seller choice compared against the BuyBox algorithm). Source preferences are quantified via frequency-based pairwise comparison metrics, with context-specificity probed by varying paper topics and product categories, identity mapping tested across multiple source representations, and rationality assessed by replacing source names with cardinal credentials such as follower counts and H5-Index.

## Results

Across 12 LLMs from 6 providers, the paper finds strong and predictable latent source preferences that can override content. In the AllSides case study (3,855 events), simply revealing source labels shifts selection balance substantially, and swapping left/right source labels causes right-leaning content from left-leaning sources to still be selected. Context-specificity is pronounced: NEJM is chosen 96% of the time for Health & Medical papers but only 19% for Computer Vision papers, while BestBuy rises from 51% (Grocery) to 97% (Electronics). In the Amazon seller task (59,375 snapshots), prompting GPT-4.1-Nano to prioritize price increases cheapest-seller selection from 48.4% to 70% (+21.6 pp), and prioritizing delivery speed raises fastest-seller selection from 53.9% to 69.3% (+15.4 pp), yet prompting models to 'not be biased' in the news task is largely ineffective and sometimes increases left/centrist preference.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical study without a single proposed method to benchmark against baselines; instead, it compares 12 LLMs against each other and contrasts experimental conditions. Larger models (GPT-4.1-Mini, Phi-4, Qwen2.5-7B) exhibit markedly stronger and more heterogeneous source preferences than smaller variants (Llama-3.2-1B, Qwen2.5-1.5B), which show near-uniform rankings. DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B diverges sharply from its base Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, demonstrating that post-training procedure (RL distillation vs. instruction tuning) produces distinct preference profiles. In the AllSides case study, the 'Source Hidden' condition serves as the content-only baseline; adding source labels ('Source Shown') shifts selections predictably toward left/centrist outlets, and the 'Do Not Be Biased' prompt fails to restore the hidden-source distribution. In the Amazon case study, the BuyBox algorithm serves as a platform-centric reference: LLM agents consistently over-weight rating and price relative to BuyBox's heavy reliance on Amazon-as-shipper, yielding low alignment but suggesting user-centric agents could counter platform-centric nudges.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the growing literature on LLM biases (political, cultural, gender, geographic) by introducing 'latent source preference' as a distinct axis: the hypothesis that LLMs encode implicit brand-level preferences for information sources that systematically steer which retrieved content they surface. The closest prior work, Yang & Menczer (2025), asks LLMs to assign decontextualized credibility scores to sources in isolation; this paper departs by using controlled pairwise experiments with semantically identical content to isolate source effects from content effects, and by testing across multiple source representations (brand names, URLs, social handles, credentials) and real-world deployment scenarios. The methodology draws on LLM-as-a-judge evaluation paradigms (Gu et al., 2025) and cognitive-bias-in-LLMs research (Itzhak et al., 2024; 2025), but reframes the question from 'can the model rate source credibility?' to 'does source attribution alone, holding content constant, predictably shift the model's selection?'.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As LLM agents become the primary interface between users and information ecosystems (news aggregators, search, e-commerce), the finding that source preferences can override content and that standard anti-bias prompting is ineffective or counterproductive has immediate practical consequences for fairness, transparency, and user autonomy. The paper opens several important threads: the causal origins of these preferences (pretraining vs. post-training), the security risk of brand impersonation through surface-form manipulation, and the need for controllable alignment mechanisms that let users calibrate source trust. A key limitation is that the paper characterizes preferences descriptively without proposing or testing mitigation strategies beyond prompting, and the three domains studied (news, research, e-commerce) may not generalize to all information-processing scenarios.
