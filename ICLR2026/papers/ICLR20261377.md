## Problem

In decentralized learning, communication between geographically distributed agents is a scarce resource, yet how to allocate it over time (temporal communication scheduling) remains largely unexplored, especially in fully decentralized settings without a central server. The paper asks: how should communication budgets be distributed across training rounds to maximize global generalization under high data heterogeneity? The surprising answer is that a single round of fully connected communication at the final step—a global merge—can recover performance close to federated learning, despite extremely sparse peer-to-peer communication throughout training.

## Input / Output

Each of m agents (16 or 32) holds a local non-IID dataset of 4096 RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 64×64 for Tiny ImageNet) partitioned via a Dirichlet(α=0.1) distribution, and a communication topology (random, ring, or exponential graph) governs peer-to-peer gossip. The method produces two outputs: (1) a globally merged model obtained by a single AllReduce over all local parameters at the final round, and (2) the property that local models remain 'mergeable' (Definition 2) throughout training under limited but nonzero communication, meaning their convex combination achieves lower global population risk than any individual local model. The evaluation metric is average global test accuracy—each local model is tested on the full global distribution, not just its own local data.

## Method

The core empirical finding is that concentrating communication in later training windows, culminating in a single global merge via AllReduce, dramatically improves global test accuracy under Dirichlet α=0.1 heterogeneity, while local models under sparse gossip (one random peer, probability 0.2 per round) remain near the no-communication baseline. Theoretically, the paper provides the first convergence analysis showing the globally merged model of Decentralized SGD matches the parallel SGD rate O(σ²/(mε²) + 1/ε), by introducing a 'progressive sharpening' assumption (Assumption 4) that the loss gradient negatively aligns with the gradient of sharpness. This allows part of the inter-agent model discrepancy—previously treated as detrimental noise in Koloskova et al. (2020)—to be reinterpreted as a constructive O(Ξ_t²) term that, when it dominates the O(Ξ_t³) residual, yields U^(t) < 0 and accelerates convergence. Proposition 3 derives a 'critical consensus edge' condition showing that the required communication frequency (parameter p) must increase as the gradient norm μ_t decreases, providing a principled explanation for why communication should be concentrated in the late, low-gradient regime of training.

## Results

On Tiny ImageNet with 32 agents and Dirichlet α=0.1 (high heterogeneity), a single global merging at the final step of decentralized SGD brings global test accuracy close to federated learning (FedAdamW) for both CLIP ViT-B/32 and ResNet-18, while local models without merging remain near the no-communication baseline. On CIFAR-100 with 16 agents, concentrating fully connected communication (AllReduce) in the last 1/10 or 1/20 of training windows yields the largest final-accuracy gains, with a single 10-round fully connected window still producing substantial improvements. Theoretically, the globally merged DSGD model matches the parallel SGD convergence rate of O(σ²/(mε²) + 1/ε), eliminating the additional O(1/(pε) + (√p·σ+ζ)/(pε^{3/2})) penalty identified by Koloskova et al. (2020).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is federated learning (FedAdamW), which maintains a global model via a central server. The paper shows that decentralized SGD with a single final global merge achieves global test accuracy close to FedAdamW on both CLIP ViT-B/32 and ResNet-18 under Dirichlet α=0.1, despite using only ~60 sparse peer exchanges per agent over 300 rounds (communication cost O(mRPT + 2mP) vs. O(2mPT) for standard Ring-AllReduce). Without the final merge, local models perform nearly as poorly as the no-communication (fully local) baseline, confirming the gain is non-trivial and not merely accumulated gossip. Compared to D-PSGD (Lian et al., 2017), which introduced final merging only under IID data, this paper provides the first systematic study of merge recovery under high non-IID heterogeneity. Compared to Chen et al. (2021), which requires periodic global averaging every H=48 steps, the proposed method recovers performance with only a single merge. Compared to SCSP (Aketi et al., 2021), which uses gradient sparsification over a fixed topology with one local step, this paper demonstrates robust mergeability with multiple local steps (H=100) under topological sparsification and high data heterogeneity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the DSGD convergence analysis of Koloskova et al. (2020) by reinterpreting the model-discrepancy term—previously treated as purely detrimental noise—as a constructive component that, under progressive sharpening, enables the merged model to match the parallel SGD rate. It builds on the implicit-bias connection between decentralized SGD and random SAM established by Zhu et al. (2023b) as a core ingredient in the new descent lemma. In contrast to prior work that focused on spatial communication allocation (topology design) or on IID settings (Lian et al., 2017), this paper is the first to systematically study temporal communication allocation in the fully decentralized, non-IID regime and to formalize 'mergeability' of local models under global population risk.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway—that a single final global merge can recover most of the performance gap between decentralized and federated training under severe communication constraints—offers a low-cost strategy for communication-limited distributed training and opens a new angle on model merging (cross-initialization, cross-distribution merging without permutation). The theoretical framework (Proposition 3) provides a principled, gradient-norm-driven rule for when to increase communication, suggesting adaptive scheduling algorithms. Limitations include the reliance on the somewhat strong progressive-sharpening assumption (Assumption 4), experiments restricted to vision classification tasks, and the fact that the single-merge result, while robust across settings, has not yet been validated on language or multimodal models at scale.
