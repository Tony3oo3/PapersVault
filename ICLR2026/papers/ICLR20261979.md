## Problem

It is unclear what mechanisms time-series foundation models actually use for zero-shot forecasting of dynamical systems, and whether they genuinely learn the underlying physics or merely exploit superficial statistical regularities. This paper shows that a trivial 'context parroting' strategy—scanning the context for the best-matching motif and copying what follows—outperforms all leading foundation models (Chronos, TimesFM, Time-MoE, Moirai, DynaMix) on chaotic systems at a tiny fraction of the computational cost, revealing that current models do not fully exploit the information available in the context.

## Input / Output

The method takes a univariate context trajectory of length L (normalized to zero mean and unit variance), an embedding dimension D specifying the motif length for matching, and a forecast horizon H. It outputs a forecast of H future scalar timepoints by copying directly from the context. The last D values form the query; the search excludes the final D motifs to prevent trivial self-matching, and the forecast is repeated cyclically if H exceeds the available context following the matched motif. For chaotic systems, trajectories are sampled at 30 points per Lyapunov time to normalize difficulty across the 135 benchmark systems.

## Method

Context parroting performs a 1-nearest-neighbor search in a D-dimensional delay-embedded space: the last D context values form a query, the algorithm finds the most similar length-D motif elsewhere in the context, and copies the H values immediately following that motif as the forecast. Formally, this is the σ→0 limit of a Nadaraya-Watson kernel regression, and in specific limits it reduces to classical nonlinear forecasting methods such as simplex projection (H=1, k=D+1) and S-map. The paper further derives a theoretical explanation of in-context neural scaling laws, showing that the one-step forecast error scales as L^(−α) where α = 1/d_cor, directly linking the scaling exponent to the correlation (fractal) dimension of the underlying chaotic attractor. The computational cost is O(DL) for nearest-neighbor search versus O(L²) for transformer attention, yielding a six-orders-of-magnitude gap with Chronos, and the method requires no training with performance insensitive to the choice of D.

## Results

On the dysts benchmark of 135 low-dimensional chaotic systems, context parroting achieves the lowest sMAPE forecast error and the lowest KL Divergence (attractor reconstruction) among all tested models, outperforming Chronos, Chronos Bolt, TimesFM, Time-MoE, Moirai, and DynaMix. Across four broader SciML tasks (turbulence, ECG, coupled circuits, Kuramoto oscillators), parroting is the only model ranking in the top three for every task and every metric (MAE, MSE, KL Divergence). The inference cost of parroting is six orders of magnitude lower than Chronos, and the in-context scaling exponent α linking forecast accuracy to context length is predicted by the correlation dimension of the attractor via α = 1/d_cor (Spearman correlation ≈ 0.85 across 135 systems).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Context parroting decisively outperforms all six foundation models on the primary dysts benchmark (135 chaotic systems) in both short-horizon sMAPE and long-horizon attractor KL Divergence. Among foundation models, Chronos is the strongest baseline (it internally employs parroting via its quantized-token architecture and cross-entropy loss), while DynaMix ranks second due to its recurrent architecture preserving long-term oscillatory structure. TimesFM, Time-MoE, and Chronos Bolt all exhibit a common failure mode of regressing to the mean at longer horizons, suppressing oscillations. On the broader SciML tasks (Table 1), parroting wins outright on ECG (0.624 vs. next-best 0.684 Moirai) and Circuit (0.083 vs. 0.111 Chronos), ties Moirai on Kuramoto (both 0.004), and places third on Turbulence (0.403 vs. 0.382 Moirai, 0.394 TimeMoE). Classical baselines simplex projection and AutoARIMA (Appendix Fig. 7) are also outperformed by parroting on the dysts benchmark. The paper notes that Chronos outperforms parroting only on very short contexts (L < 512), where trend-continuation strategies become more effective than motif matching.

## Relation to Prior Work

Context parroting is shown to be a 1-nearest-neighbor limit of the Nadaraya-Watson kernel regression framework, and in specific limits reduces to classical nonlinear forecasting methods: simplex projection (Sugihara & May, 1990) when H=1 and k=D+1, and S-map (Sugihara, 1994) with a particular nonlinear kernel weighting. The paper draws a direct parallel between context parroting and induction heads in transformers (Olsson et al., 2022), arguing that the copy-and-paste mechanism underlying in-context learning in LLMs is the same operation that foundation models use to forecast dynamical systems. It also builds on the in-context neural scaling law observed by Liu et al. (2024a) for LLMs applied to dynamical systems, providing a geometric explanation by linking the scaling exponent to the correlation dimension of the chaotic attractor via Takens' embedding theorem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a trivial nearest-neighbor copy strategy outperforms all leading time-series foundation models (Chronos, TimesFM, Time-MoE, Moirai, DynaMix) on chaotic and real-world dynamical systems exposes a fundamental gap: current models do not fully exploit the information in their context windows. This has practical implications for benchmark design—future SciML evaluations should test capabilities orthogonal to parroting (e.g., inferring unobserved parameters, generalizing to unseen bifurcation regimes) rather than relying solely on reconstruction error. Open questions include whether the fractal-dimension scaling law generalizes to stochastic systems and LLMs, how to extend parroting to non-stationary time series, and what additional strategies Chronos employs at short context lengths that allow it to beat pure parroting.
