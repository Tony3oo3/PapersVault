## Problem

Current evaluation metrics for visual brain decoding (PixCorr, SSIM, AlexNet, Inception, CLIP, EffNet, SwAV) achieve near-perfect scores on state-of-the-art diffusion-based models, yet those models still frequently fail to reconstruct crucial semantic elements (e.g., a teddy bear becomes a cat). The paper identifies three specific limitations of existing metrics—dependency on the comparison image pool, insufficient task difficulty, and lack of human-likeness—and shows through crowd-sourced human ratings that most existing metrics exhibit low correlation with human judgments of semantic similarity, except EffNet.

## Input / Output

SEED takes a ground-truth image and its brain-decoded reconstruction as input and produces a single scalar score in [0, 1] (the average of three component scores). Object F1 uses MM-Grounding-DINO to detect 82 fixed object categories (80 COCO + man/woman) in both images, computing threshold-averaged recall and precision over confidence values from 0 to the highest detected confidence in each image. Cap-Sim generates captions via GIT and computes cosine similarity of Sentence Transformer embeddings. EffNet computes Pearson correlation of EfficientNet image embeddings, converted to a higher-is-better scale. The final SEED score is the arithmetic mean of the three components.

## Method

SEED is a composite metric inspired by the two-stage model of human visual attention: a parallel feature-analysis stage and a focused object-binding stage. Object F1 emulates object-oriented attention by measuring category-level presence/absence agreement between GT and reconstruction using open-vocabulary grounding, with a threshold-averaging scheme that penalizes reconstructions whose detected objects have confidence values far from the GT. Cap-Sim captures complementary semantic factors (background, pose, color) by comparing natural-language captions generated by GIT, encoded via Sentence Transformer, making the evaluation interpretable and human-readable. EffNet (modified to correlation rather than correlation distance) captures global and structural scene similarity. The key finding is that no combination of existing metrics surpasses standalone EffNet, but adding Object F1 or Cap-Sim enables a superior composite: SEED achieves 81.0% pairwise accuracy and 0.813 Pearson correlation with human ratings, outperforming all existing metrics with statistical significance (95% CIs excluding zero via bootstrapping over 22 evaluators).

## Results

On the primary meta-evaluation (NSD dataset, MindEye2 reconstructions, 1,000 image pairs, 22 human raters), SEED achieves 81.0% pairwise accuracy, Kendall's Tau-b of .621, and Pearson correlation of .813 with human semantic similarity ratings, outperforming the best existing metric EffNet (78.0%, .559, .748). The improvement over EffNet is statistically significant via bootstrapping (95% CIs: [0.03, 0.07] for pairwise acc., [0.02, 0.04] for Kendall, [0.04, 0.08] for Pearson). On a second setting (GOD dataset, Mind-Vis reconstructions, 50 pairs, 10 raters), SEED again leads with 73.7% pairwise accuracy, .477 Kendall, and .706 Pearson, confirming generalizability across datasets and decoding models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SEED outperforms all six existing evaluation metrics on all three meta-evaluation criteria (pairwise accuracy, Kendall's Tau-b, Pearson correlation). The closest competitor is EffNet (78.0% pairwise acc., .559 Kendall, .748 Pearson), which SEED surpasses by 3.0 percentage points, .062, and .065 respectively — a gap confirmed statistically significant via bootstrapping. Among the two-way identification metrics, CLIP (66.4%, .368, .436) and Inception (63.8%, .330, .475) perform moderately, while AlexNet(2) and AlexNet(5) are near or below chance level (55.0% and 49.5% pairwise accuracy). The low-level metrics PixCorr (53.8%) and SSIM (54.5%) show the weakest alignment with human judgment, barely exceeding random guessing. SwAV (69.7%, .394, .576) sits in the middle tier. Notably, no combination of existing metrics alone can match SEED; the paper's ablation (Table 5) shows that even the best two-metric combination (Object F1 + EffNet at 80.1%) falls short of the full three-component SEED (81.0%), underscoring the complementary nature of the three sub-metrics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in the evaluation methodology for visual brain decoding rather than building on a specific prior decoding model. It draws conceptual inspiration from neuroscientific literature on two-stage visual attention (broad parallel feature analysis followed by focused object binding) to motivate a composite metric that integrates object-level detection (via MM-Grounding-DINO), caption-level semantic comparison (via GIT + Sentence Transformer), and global structural similarity (via EfficientNet). The key departure is from the dominant 'two-way identification' evaluation paradigm (comparing a reconstruction against a pool of other reconstructions) toward direct pairwise semantic comparison, which the authors argue is more aligned with how humans judge similarity. The individual components are all off-the-shelf models repurposed for evaluation, but their specific combination and the threshold-averaging scheme for Object F1 are novel.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals a critical disconnect: state-of-the-art brain decoding models achieve 93–95% on standard identification metrics yet exhibit 17–20% semantic near-miss rates (e.g., reconstructing a cat instead of a dog) and 8–11% of reconstructions that capture objects but miss semantic details like pose and background. This suggests the field may be substantially overestimating progress due to evaluation tools that no longer reflect the true difficulty of the task. Open questions include whether SEED's dependence on off-the-shelf grounding and captioning models introduces systematic blind spots for distorted or out-of-distribution reconstructions, and whether the evaluation focus will need to shift from semantic fidelity to perceptual fidelity as decoding models mature. The released human evaluation data (1,000 pairs, 22 raters) provides a foundation for developing future metrics.
