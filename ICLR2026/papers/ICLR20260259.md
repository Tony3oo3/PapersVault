## Problem

Batch size scheduling (BSS) is ubiquitous in industrial-scale LLM pretraining (GPT-3, PaLM, LLaMA-3, DeepSeek-V3, etc.), yet its theoretical foundations remain poorly understood. Existing analyses either focus on constant batch sizes or rely on heuristic and empirical arguments, leaving practitioners to tune schedules through expensive large-scale experimentation. This paper asks: under a fixed data budget, what is the optimal batch size schedule, and how does its structure depend on task difficulty?

## Input / Output

The method takes a total data budget D, model capacity exponent β, task difficulty parameter s, and hardware constraints (minimum batch size B_min) as inputs, and produces a continuous-time optimal batch size schedule b*(t) with corresponding training horizon T*. For practical stage-wise schedules, it outputs the optimal switching point P*_D as a power-law function of D (D − P*_D ∼ D^γ), enabling principled extrapolation from small-scale pilot experiments to large-scale training. The FSL framework maps the BSS function to a closed-form loss expression (signal-learning term plus a noise-accumulation integral with a forgetting kernel), making the otherwise intractable optimal-control problem analytically solvable.

## Method

The paper extends the Functional Scaling Law (FSL) framework to BSS by formulating the optimal schedule as a resource-constrained variational problem: minimize the final-step loss (t^{-s} + ∫K(T−t)/b(t) dt) subject to ∫b(t)dt = D and b(t) ≥ B_min. Solving via Cauchy-Schwarz and KKT conditions reveals that the optimal structure depends sharply on task difficulty: easy tasks (s > 1−1/β) favor monotonically increasing batch sizes, while hard tasks (s ≤ 1−1/β) require a stable-growth structure with small batches for most of training and a late switch to large batches. The key dynamical insight is the 'fast catch-up effect': after switching from small to large batch, the loss rapidly collapses onto the constant large-batch trajectory because the forgetting kernel K(t)=(t+1)^{-(2−1/β)} causes accumulated gradient noise to be forgotten on a timescale δ_ε ≪ t* for hard tasks, creating a clear time-scale separation. This implies large batches can be safely deferred to late training without sacrificing performance while substantially reducing data consumption. The theory is validated through LLM pretraining experiments spanning Dense (LLaMA) and MoE (ScMoE) architectures from 50M to 1.1B parameters and up to 1T tokens, where late-switch schedules consistently outperform constant-batch and early-switch baselines.

## Results

In the linear regression setting, the optimal BSS achieves the minimax rate D^{-sβ/(1+sβ)} in the easy-task regime (s=1.0, β=2.0) and the one-pass SGD optimal rate D^{-s} in the hard-task regime (s=0.4, β=2.0), matching theoretical predictions. For the 200M LLaMA model on C4, the optimal switching point occurs at approximately 70% of total training tokens, and the power-law relation D−P*_D ∝ D^γ is validated with R²=0.990 across token budgets from 1.3B to 5B. In large-scale LLM pretraining (1B MoE on 0.4T tokens, 1.1B MoE on 1T tokens), late-switch schedules consistently outperform both constant-batch and early-switch baselines across all tested switching ratios (640→1280, 640→2560, 1024→2048) and architectures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three batch size scheduling strategies: constant large batch, early-switch (small→large at an early point), and late-switch (small→large near the end of training). Across all experimental settings—spanning Dense LLaMA (50M–492M) and MoE (1B, 1.1B) architectures, and data scales up to 1T tokens—late switching consistently yields the lowest final validation loss, outperforming both constant-batch and early-switch baselines. The fast catch-up effect is validated as robust: after each batch size increase, the loss rapidly collapses onto the corresponding constant-large-batch trajectory, with this behavior observed across a 4-stage schedule (640→1280→1920→2560) in the 1B MoE model. The theoretical prediction that the optimal switching fraction shrinks as D^{-γ} (with γ=(1−1/β−s)/(2−1/β)) is confirmed empirically via log-log linear fitting (R²=0.990). The paper also shows that constant learning rate combined with the optimal BSS achieves comparable performance to popular learning rate schedules (cosine, warmup-stable-decay), while reducing iteration count.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Functional Scaling Law (FSL) framework of Li et al. (2025a)—originally developed for learning rate schedule analysis—to the problem of batch size scheduling (BSS), yielding explicit analytical solutions for optimal schedules under a fixed data budget. It departs from prior BSS analyses that either rely on heuristic arguments (Smith et al., 2018; McCandlish et al., 2018) or formulate the problem as optimal control without closed-form structure (Lee et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2022; Perko, 2023), by leveraging the FSL's integral representation to obtain a clean easy/hard-task dichotomy. The theoretical foundation draws on the continuous-time SDE model for SGD (Li et al., 2019; Orvieto & Lucchi, 2019) and the anisotropic noise structure in kernel regression (Mori et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2022b), while the empirical validation connects to the broader neural scaling laws literature (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022) and the one-pass SGD convergence theory (Dieuleveut & Bach, 2015; Mücke et al., 2019).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first scaling-law-based theoretical justification for batch size scheduling, a technique universally adopted in industrial LLM pretraining (GPT-3, PaLM, LLaMA-3, DeepSeek-V3, MiniMax-01, GLM-4.5) but previously lacking principled guidance. The fast catch-up effect offers a concrete, actionable principle—defer large batches to late training—that is validated across Dense and MoE architectures at up to 1.1B parameters and 1T tokens, and suggests a power-law scaling for the optimal switching point that could be estimated from small-scale pilots. Key open questions include extending the analysis to adaptive optimizers (AdamW, which dominates practice) and understanding the joint interaction between learning rate decay and batch size scheduling, both of which the authors acknowledge as important future directions.
