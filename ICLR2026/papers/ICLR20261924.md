## Problem

Existing chemistry benchmarks for LLMs conflate structural reasoning with factual recall, pattern matching on public datasets, or reliance on biased surrogate verifiers, making it impossible to determine whether a model genuinely parses molecular graphs. No prior benchmark exclusively uses symbolically verifiable tasks with exact ground truths computed from the molecular graph, nor does one systematically vary task type, molecular complexity, and representation format to localize where reasoning breaks down.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261924/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each question pairs a SMILES string (randomly rendered as canonical or non-canonical, and aromatic or kekulized, with 50% probability each) with a natural-language task prompt requesting one of three outputs: an integer count, a list of atom indices, or a valid SMILES string satisfying given constraints. Multitask variants bundle 2, 3, or 5 such requests into a single prompt, and answers are scored binary per sub-task via RDKit-based symbolic verification, averaged over three stochastic rollouts. The static benchmark contains 5,111 questions over 849 unique molecules drawn from a held-out cluster pool, while the dynamic variant (MOLECULARIQ-D) supports on-the-fly generation from 1.3M training and 2.0M test molecules.

## Method

MOLECULARIQ is constructed by retrieving PubChem molecules (5–50 heavy atoms, <100-char SMILES), clustering them via MinHash LSH over ECFP fingerprints (0.7 Tanimoto threshold) into training, easy-test, and hard-test pools, and computing exact ground truths for 30 molecular features across six categories (graph topology, chemistry-typed topology, composition, chemical perception, functional groups, synthesis/fragmentation) using RDKit solvers. Questions are sampled with inverse-frequency weighting to maximize diversity, and constrained-generation tasks are scored by an information-theoretic reduction-ratio criterion ensuring each added constraint meaningfully shrinks the satisfying molecule set. The benchmark is integrated into lm-evaluation-harness with hierarchical answer extraction and key-specific canonical normalization to decouple format compliance from chemical accuracy. Evaluation of 38 models (27 generalist, 11 chemistry-specialized) reveals that even the best model (GPT-OSS 120B High) achieves only 47.5% overall accuracy, that chemistry fine-tuning systematically degrades performance relative to base models, and that SMILES perturbations (non-canonical, kekulized, ring-relabelled) consistently reduce accuracy, exposing reliance on memorized token patterns rather than true graph reasoning.

## Results

On MOLECULARIQ (5,111 symbolically verifiable questions over 849 molecules), the best-performing model GPT-OSS 120B (High reasoning budget) achieves 47.5% overall accuracy, with 46.8% on counting, 42.5% on indexing, and 53.7% on constrained generation. Chemistry-specialized LLMs (TxGemma-27B, Ether0, ChemDFM-R-14B) score between 5.0% and 8.7% overall, far below even mid-size generalist models. Across all 38 evaluated models, no model exceeds 50% overall accuracy, and performance drops sharply with increasing Bertz molecular complexity (from ~56% to ~39% for the best model) and multitask load (from ~58% single-task to ~21% five-task).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-OSS 120B (High) leads all 38 evaluated models at 47.5% overall, outperforming Qwen-3 235B (39.2%) by 8.3 points and DeepSeek-R1-0528 (37.1%) by 10.4 points, with the gap widening on indexing (42.5% vs. 34.5% and 30.2%). Among chemistry-specialized models, ChemDFM-R-14B (8.7%) marginally beats Ether0 (6.5%) and TxGemma-27B (5.0%), but all three are outperformed by even the smallest generalist MoE model (Qwen-3 30B at 22.7%) by 14–18 points. Notably, GLM-4.6 (16.2%) underperforms relative to its strong showing on general reasoning benchmarks like LiveBench, where it outperforms GPT-OSS, suggesting molecular-structure reasoning imposes distinct demands. The paper further shows that chemistry fine-tuning systematically degrades performance: on average, chemistry-tuned models score 18 percentage points lower in type validity than their base models, and only ChemDFM-R shows a minor improvement over its base, indicating that narrow instruction tuning miscalibrates structural reasoning rather than enhancing it.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of molecular graph reasoning and LLM evaluation by introducing a benchmark where every answer is programmatically verifiable via RDKit, eliminating the surrogate-verifier bias and data-contamination risks that plague prior chemistry benchmarks (ChemIQ, FGBench, ChemCoTBench, MolPuzzle). Rather than building on a specific prior method, it repurposes standard cheminformatics tooling (RDKit solvers, ECFP fingerprints, MinHash LSH clustering) as a verification and sampling infrastructure, and introduces three orthogonal complexity axes (SMILES representation format, Bertz molecular complexity, multitask load) that no prior benchmark systematically varies. The paper explicitly positions against both general-knowledge chemistry benchmarks (which test factual recall) and generative benchmarks (which test constraint satisfaction without verifying structural understanding), arguing that symbolically verifiable graph tasks are the necessary diagnostic for whether a model has genuinely internalized molecular structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that even the strongest models (GPT-OSS 120B High) achieve only ~47.5% overall accuracy on tasks that are trivially solvable by RDKit, establishing a clear floor that no structurally-reasoning model should fall below. The finding that chemistry-specific fine-tuning systematically degrades performance relative to base models (18pp lower type validity) challenges the standard assumption that domain adaptation should help, and the SMILES perturbation results expose widespread reliance on canonical token patterns rather than true graph parsing. Key limitations include restriction to 2D SMILES representations, single-molecule tasks, and exclusion of property prediction or reaction outcome tasks where symbolic solvers are unavailable. The dynamic variant (MOLECULARIQD) and public leaderboard address saturation concerns, but the benchmark's long-term utility depends on whether the community adopts it as a standard evaluation protocol alongside existing chemistry LLM benchmarks.
