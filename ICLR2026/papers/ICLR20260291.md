## Problem

Large vision-language models have been extensively benchmarked on CT, MRI, and other static medical imaging modalities, but ultrasound understanding remains largely unexplored despite ultrasound being one of the most widely used imaging modalities globally. Ultrasound is uniquely challenging due to its operator dependency, high noise and artifact content, and dynamic 3D-to-2D presentation, yet no comprehensive, publicly available benchmark existed to evaluate LVLMs across diverse ultrasound tasks and anatomical regions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260291/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each benchmark case pairs a single ultrasound frame (sampled from video sequences to control evaluation cost) with a structured text prompt containing a clinical role definition, task-specific instruction, and output format specification. Outputs vary across the eight tasks: discrete class labels (e.g., BI-RADS categories, standard plane identifiers), 9-class spatial position labels (detection tasks were simplified from bounding-box generation to coarse sector classification because most LVLMs failed to produce valid coordinates), continuous clinical values (e.g., ejection fraction, lesion size), structured clinical reports following example templates, and concise anatomical captions. All 7,241 cases span 15 anatomical regions and 50 application scenarios, with patient-level sampling to prevent data leakage.

## Method

U2-BENCH is constructed through a three-stage pipeline: (1) sampling 7,241 ultrasound studies from 40 licensed datasets with task-specific patient-level oversampling for clinically high-priority but data-sparse tasks, (2) standardizing annotations into a unified schema (converting segmentation masks to bounding boxes, translating non-English text via a medically guided pipeline, and performing multi-annotator quality verification), and (3) designing structured prompts for all 50 application scenarios. The benchmark defines eight clinically inspired tasks grouped into four capability categories—classification (disease diagnosis, view recognition), detection (lesion localization, organ detection, keypoint detection), regression (clinical value estimation), and text generation (report and caption generation). An aggregate U2-Score is computed as a weighted sum of per-task metrics where weights are proportional to task sample counts, mitigating imbalance across tasks. The paper evaluates 23 LVLMs (open- and closed-source, general-purpose and medical-specific) and finds that closed-source models lead overall, image-level classification is relatively tractable, but spatial reasoning and clinical text generation remain persistent challenges, with diminishing returns from model scaling and domain-specific models excelling in reasoning but lagging in coarse visual recognition.

## Results

On U2-BENCH (7,241 ultrasound cases, 8 tasks, 15 anatomies), Dolphin-V1 achieves the highest U2-Score of 0.5835, substantially outperforming all other models. Among general-purpose closed-source models, GPT-5 reaches 0.3250 and Gemini-2.5-Pro-Preview 0.2968, while the best open-source model (DeepSeek-VL2) attains 0.2630. Image-level classification is most tractable (Dolphin-V1: 0.682 DD accuracy), but spatial reasoning remains difficult (no model exceeds 0.160 on Keypoint Detection) and clinical report generation is particularly challenging (all models below 7.5 BLEU-4 on RG).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Dolphin-V1 (developed by the authors' institution) dominates every task, achieving 0.682 DD accuracy, 0.694 VRA accuracy, and 0.508 KD accuracy — far exceeding all other models. Among general-purpose closed-source models, GPT-5 (U2-Score 0.3250) leads, followed by Gemini-2.5-Pro-Preview (0.2968), which uniquely achieves the best KD accuracy (0.252) among non-Dolphin models. The best open-source model, DeepSeek-VL2 (0.2630), excels on Lesion Localization (0.295) and Organ Detection (0.168) but lags on classification. Medical-specialized models show a clear trade-off: MedGemma-4B-it achieves the second-best CVE RMSE (0.167) and MedDr the best CG BERTScore (81.21), yet both trail general-purpose models on DD (e.g., Qwen-72B DD F1 = 0.456 vs. MedDr's 0.312). Within the Qwen-2.5-VL family, scaling from 3B to 72B yields consistent gains in CVE RMSE (0.501→0.322) but plateaus on spatial and generation tasks, suggesting diminishing returns from scale alone. Claude-3.7-Sonnet performs notably poorly overall (U2-Score 0.1596), with DD accuracy of only 0.212, likely due to safety-related refusals.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the medical LVLM benchmarking paradigm established by GMAI-MMBench (Chen et al., 2024a) — from which the U2-Score weighted aggregation formula is directly derived — to the ultrasound modality, which prior medical benchmarks have largely excluded. It departs from earlier ultrasound AI efforts that were confined to single-task, small-scale datasets (fetal plane identification, pathology segmentation) by unifying 8 clinically grounded tasks across 15 anatomies into a single evaluation framework. The positioning is explicitly against general-purpose LVLM benchmarks (MMBench, SEED-Bench) that omit clinical grounding, and against medical LVLMs (MiniGPT-Med, MedDr, Lingshu) that either exclude ultrasound or limit it to caption generation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

U2-BENCH fills a clear gap: no prior benchmark systematically evaluated LVLMs on ultrasound across classification, detection, regression, and generation tasks. The findings that spatial reasoning (KD accuracy ≤ 0.16) and clinical report generation (BLEU < 7.5) remain fundamentally unsolved, while classification is relatively tractable, provide a concrete roadmap for where ultrasound-specific model development should focus. Open questions include extending to video/real-time ultrasound, incorporating multi-view and patient-history context, and establishing community standards for ultrasound annotation and evaluation. A notable limitation is that the top-performing model (Dolphin-V1, 0.5835) is developed by the same institution (Dolphin AI) that constructed the benchmark, raising potential concerns about data familiarity that the paper does not explicitly address.
