## Problem

Legal reasoning remains under-assessed in NLP benchmarking, with prior legal QA datasets focusing narrowly on outcome-based metrics (final answer correctness) while ignoring the intermediate reasoning steps that determine whether a legal conclusion is sound. No existing benchmark combines challenging long-form open-ended questions with multiple-choice formats, multilingual coverage, and a validated process-based evaluation framework for legal reasoning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261478/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For open-ended questions, the model receives a law exam prompt with course name and standard legal reasoning guidance (issue spotting, rule recall, rule application) and must produce a long-form answer (avg. 247 words); the ensemble judge then scores this against a professor-written reference answer on a 0–10 scale. For MCQs, the model receives a question stem with 2–5 legal statements and selects the correct combination from 4, 8, 16, or 32 answer choices, yielding a binary accuracy score. The dataset is bilingual (English and German, drawn from independent exams rather than translations) and spans 78 legal subdomains across Swiss, international, and generic jurisdictions.

## Method

The core evaluation mechanism is a minimum-score ensemble LLM-as-a-Judge combining GPT-4o, Qwen3-32B, and DeepSeek-V3, where the final score is the minimum across the three judges to suppress self-bias and overly lenient scoring. The judge prompt is iteratively tuned by two legally trained authors to track adherence to doctrinal structure in professor-written model answers and to penalize domain-specific errors such as fabricated statutory citations. The ensemble's validity is established through the Alternative Annotator Test (Alt-test) with ε=0, where the LLM judge must outperform each of three human legal experts in a leave-one-out design; it achieves a winning rate of ω=1.00 with all BY-corrected p-values below 0.01. For MCQs, distractors are generated combinatorially to guarantee a uniform number of answer choices per question, and a perturbation experiment systematically varies the number of choices (4→32) while holding question content constant to diagnose whether models rely on genuine understanding or guesswork.

## Results

On the primary open-question evaluation (ensemble LLM-judge score, 0–10 scale), GPT-5 achieves the highest score of 70.20, followed by Gemini-2.5-Pro (67.40) and Claude-3.7-Sonnet (62.86). On MCQ-16, GPT-5.2 leads with 52.53% accuracy and Claude-4.6-Sonnet follows at 52.43%, while the best large model (Llama-3.3-70B-it) reaches only 26.07% and the best small model (Phi-4) 21.40%. On MCQ-4, GPT-5 scores 62.65% and Claude-4.5-Sonnet 58.01%. A perturbation experiment varying the number of answer choices (4→32) shows consistent accuracy degradation across all models, with GPT-4o dropping from 53.73% to 21.81%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 35 LLMs across three tiers (reasoning, large, small) on LEXAM. On open questions, reasoning models dominate: GPT-5 (70.20) outperforms the best large model GPT-4.1 (57.50) by 12.7 points and the best small model GPT-4.1-mini (54.58) by 15.6 points. On MCQ-16, the gap widens further—GPT-5.2 (52.53%) nearly doubles the accuracy of the best large model Llama-3.3-70B-it (26.07%) and the best small model Phi-4 (21.40%). On MCQ-4, GPT-5 (62.65%) leads Claude-4.5-Sonnet (58.01%) and GPT-4.1 (54.40%), with small models like Llama-3.1-8B-it falling below 30%. The perturbation experiment (Table 2) reveals that all models degrade as answer choices increase from 4 to 32, but reasoning models (e.g., Gemini-2.5-Pro: 68.61%→35.62%) retain a larger absolute advantage over non-reasoning models (e.g., GPT-4o: 53.73%→21.81%) at higher choice counts. Notably, Gemma-3-12B-it (41.29) approaches Llama-3.1-405B-it (43.14) on open questions despite being ~33× smaller, attributed to its multilingual focus.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm (Zheng et al., 2023) by constructing a domain-specific, expert-tuned ensemble judge validated via the Alternative Annotator Test (Calderon et al., 2025), moving beyond single-model judging to a minimum-score ensemble that mitigates self-bias. It departs from prior legal benchmarks (LegalBench, LawBench, LexEval, LEXTREME) that focus on narrow task-specific formulations or MCQ-only, outcome-based evaluation, by introducing real law school exams with long-form open-ended questions and explicit process-based reasoning guidance. The paper positions LEXAM as complementary to these task-oriented datasets rather than a replacement, filling the gap of holistic, exam-style legal reasoning assessment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LEXAM is the first general legal reasoning benchmark combining challenging long-form ground-truth answers, multilingual (English/German) coverage, and an expert-validated LLM judge, making it a practical resource for both NLP research and legal education. Key open questions include expanding to common-law jurisdictions, obtaining human performance baselines (currently unavailable due to institutional restrictions), and resolving the English-German performance gap through high-quality legal translation. The perturbation experiments raise broader concerns about the reliability of MCQ-based LLM evaluation, suggesting that standard MCQ benchmarks may produce overly optimistic results due to sensitivity to the number of distractors.
