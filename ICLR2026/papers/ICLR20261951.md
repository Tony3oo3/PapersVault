## Problem

While SFT and RL have both been shown to improve LLM performance on multi-armed bandit tasks, it remains unclear how these paradigms mechanistically shape exploration strategies and whether the resulting policies generalize to longer horizons or out-of-distribution environments. More critically, whether lower average regret actually reflects robust exploration or merely more sophisticated exploitation has not been examined. This paper addresses both gaps by introducing tailored reward signals for RL and conducting a behavioral analysis that reveals an emergent exploitation bias in trained agents.

## Input / Output

At each bandit turn, the LLM receives a natural-language prompt containing the task instruction and a summary of the interaction history presented as per-arm pull counts and average rewards (sufficient statistics rather than raw action-reward sequences). The model must produce a chain-of-thought reasoning block (enclosed in think tags) followed by a final arm selection in answer tags. The observation is updated via a summarizer function after each reward, and the agent operates over a 50-step training horizon evaluated up to 300 steps for length generalization.

## Method

The paper trains Qwen 2.5 3B/7B models as meta-bandit agents using PPO with a two-level hierarchical GAE that assigns separate discount and trace-decay factors for intra-turn (token-level) and inter-turn (episode-level) credit assignment, treating LLM generation as a hierarchical MDP. Two novel reward signals are introduced: a strategic reward (RL-STG) that normalizes immediate regret to [0,1] to reduce variance in high-noise environments, and an algorithmic reward (RL-ALG) that gives binary feedback for matching a UCB oracle's action, enabling efficient imitation without return-based credit assignment. SFT is implemented by fine-tuning on 32k UCB-generated trajectories with templated chain-of-thought demonstrations of UCB calculations. Behavioral analysis using suffix failure rate, greedy frequency, and best-arm selection distributions reveals that RL-ALG agents discover exploitative UCB variants (replacing log(t) with log(N_t(a)+1) in the exploration bonus), allowing premature abandonment of arms, while SFT policies overfit to training-distribution arithmetic and suffer catastrophic forgetting on negative rewards.

## Results

On the primary in-distribution evaluation (Gaussian5_Var1_MeanN0, 7B model, 300 steps), RL-ALG achieves the best BestArmFreq of 85.7% and AvgReward of 1.05, outperforming its UCB teacher (80.6%, 1.04). SFT matches UCB closely (81.3% BestArmFreq, 1.05 AvgReward), while RL-OG and RL-STG trail slightly (79.8% and 81.1% BestArmFreq respectively). All learned policies generalize to a 6× longer horizon (50→300 steps) and across bandit families (Gaussian↔Bernoulli), with RL policies showing more robust cross-distribution transfer than SFT. On the MovieLens contextual bandit, RL-OG achieves cumulative regret of 46.64±1.70, beating LinUCB (61.09±2.57) by ~24%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against UCB (C=0.5), both RL-ALG and SFT outperform their teacher: RL-ALG reaches 85.7% BestArmFreq at 300 steps versus UCB's 80.6%, and SFT reaches 81.3% versus 80.6%, while matching or slightly exceeding UCB's AvgReward (1.05 vs. 1.04). This 'student beats teacher' effect is attributed to learned policies adopting more exploitative UCB variants (e.g., using log(N_t(a)+1) instead of log(t) in the exploration bonus), which prematurely abandon underperforming arms. Thompson Sampling is outperformed by all learned policies on BestArmFreq (78.5% vs. 81–86%) but shows zero suffix failures, highlighting a robustness trade-off. On the MovieLens contextual bandit, RL-OG (46.64±1.70) beats LinUCB (61.09±2.57) by 24% in cumulative regret, while RL-ALG (73.23±1.78) underperforms LinUCB, likely because approximating LinUCB's more complex calculation is harder than standard UCB. All learned policies substantially outperform the pretrained 7B model (BestArmFreq 63.1% at 300 steps) and generalize to 10-arm environments where the pretrained model degrades to near-random performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on training LLMs for sequential decision-making, building directly on Nie et al. (2024) for SFT on expert bandit trajectories and Schmied et al. (2025) for RL-based bandit training. It departs from both by introducing two novel reward signals—a regret-shaped strategic reward that reduces training variance and an algorithmic reward enabling RL-based imitation of UCB—and by extending PPO/GAE into a two-level hierarchical formulation that separates intra-turn token dynamics from inter-turn episode dynamics. The meta-bandit framing (learning a policy across a distribution of bandit instances) follows Kveton et al. (2020), but the paper's core contribution is a systematic head-to-head comparison of SFT versus RL paradigms with detailed behavioral diagnostics, rather than a new algorithmic framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that SFT and RL training make LLM bandit agents greedier rather than more exploratory, and that RL-ALG students outperform their UCB teacher by converging to a more exploitative UCB variant—challenges the implicit assumption that fine-tuning on exploration-optimal policies transfers robust exploration behavior. This has practical implications for deploying LLM agents in long-horizon sequential settings where premature exploitation causes catastrophic failure. Open questions include how to explicitly amplify sparse exploration signals during training (e.g., via replay re-weighting or adversarial curricula), whether the credit-assignment bottleneck that drives the greedy drift can be resolved with finer-grained reward shaping, and whether these findings generalize beyond the controlled MAB testbed to richer interactive environments.
