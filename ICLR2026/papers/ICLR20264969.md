## Problem

Tabular data prediction has remained dominated by Gradient Boosted Decision Trees despite dramatic advances elsewhere in AI, and existing kernel-based alternatives suffer from two key limitations: fixed kernels that do not adapt to task-relevant features, and super-quadratic scaling that makes them intractable beyond ~70k samples. While Recursive Feature Machines (RFM) addressed the adaptivity gap, they still scale poorly on large datasets. The paper asks whether one can build a tabular predictor that is simultaneously accurate, scalable to essentially unlimited data sizes, and natively interpretable.

## Input / Output

xRFM consumes a tabular matrix of continuous and categorical features (one-hot or ordinal encoded, z-score normalized) and produces either a continuous regression value or a class label. During inference, a test point is routed through the binary tree in O(log n) steps to a single leaf RFM, which then computes the prediction via a kernel evaluation against that leaf's training subset. The tree structure means the effective model is piecewise: different regions of feature space are handled by different leaf RFMs with independently tuned bandwidths and feature matrices.

## Method

xRFM combines two components: (1) a binary tree that recursively splits data by projecting all points onto the top eigenvector of the AGOP computed from a one-iteration 'split model' trained on a subsample, splitting at the median projection to produce balanced leaves of at most C samples (typically 60,000); and (2) a leaf RFM at each leaf, which is a modified kernel-RFM using a more general kernel family K_{p,q}(x,x')=exp(-‖x-x'‖_p^q/L^q) with tunable p and q, an option to use either the full AGOP or just its diagonal (introducing an axis-aligned bias matching tabular structure), and per-leaf adaptive bandwidth scaling. The tree-based partitioning enables local feature learning—different leaves can identify entirely different relevant coordinates—while reducing training complexity to O(n log n) and inference to O(log n). The AGOP at each leaf also provides native interpretability: its diagonal entries indicate per-coordinate importance and its top eigenvectors reveal joint feature directions driving prediction.

## Results

On the TALENT benchmark (100 regression datasets), xRFM achieves the best shifted geometric mean nRMSE of 0.311 (average rank 4.70), outperforming TabPFN-v2 (0.323), RealMLP (0.329), and CatBoost (0.336). On 200 classification datasets, xRFM is competitive: rank 7.60 on multiclass (SGM 0.107, tied with RealMLP) and rank 5.96 on binary classification with >10k samples (SGM 0.119, beating RealMLP at 0.125). On the TabArena-Lite benchmark, tuned xRFM sits on the empirical Pareto frontier for regression (Elo 1563 with 0.72s/1K inference, vs. TabDPT's Elo 1575 at 8.70s/1K). On the 17 largest meta-test datasets (70K–500K samples), xRFM is best on 5 of 7 regression tasks and 4 of 10 classification tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against TabPFN-v2 (Nature 2025), xRFM wins decisively on TALENT regression (SGM 0.311 vs. 0.323; Top-3 56% vs. 45%) and binary classification >10k samples (SGM 0.119 vs. 0.129), ties on multiclass (0.107 vs. 0.108), but loses on small binary datasets ≤10k samples (0.111 vs. 0.104). Against GBDTs (XGBoost, CatBoost, LightGBM), xRFM dominates on regression (SGM 0.311 vs. 0.336–0.355) and is competitive on classification, ranking above CatBoost and LightGBM on binary >10k (rank 5.96 vs. 10.2/10.5). Against RealMLP (NeurIPS 2024), xRFM wins on regression (0.311 vs. 0.329) and binary >10k (0.119 vs. 0.125) but is slightly behind on multiclass (0.107 vs. 0.107, tied) and small binary (0.111 vs. 0.116). Against the original RFM (Science 2024), xRFM is significantly better with p < 10⁻⁴ (Wilcoxon test), improving arithmetic mean normalized error from 0.0503 to 0.0379 on the largest TALENT datasets. Against MLP-PLR (NeurIPS 2022), xRFM wins on regression (0.311 vs. 0.381) and is competitive on classification. On TabArena-Lite, xRFM (Elo 1563) trails TabDPT (1575) and LightGBM (1602) in Elo but achieves 12× faster inference (0.72s vs. 8.70s and 5.48s per 1K samples), placing it on the Pareto frontier.

## Relation to Prior Work

xRFM builds directly on the Recursive Feature Machine (RFM) of Radhakrishnan et al. (2024, Science), which introduced the Average Gradient Outer Product (AGOP) as a mechanism for feature learning in kernel machines. The key departure is wrapping leaf RFMs inside a binary tree that splits data along AGOP eigenvector directions, enabling local feature learning and O(n log n) scaling—conceptually analogous to GBDTs but using learned directions rather than coordinate-based splits. The paper also modifies the kernel-RFM formulation by tuning over non-orthonormal-invariant kernels K_{p,q} and optionally restricting to the AGOP diagonal, explicitly motivated by the axis-aligned structure of tabular data that Grinsztajn et al. (2022) identified as an advantage of tree-based models. This positions xRFM as a bridge between the kernel-method and tree-based method families, combining the closed-form elegance of kernel regression with the adaptive partitioning that makes GBDTs effective on tabular data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Tabular data prediction has been dominated by GBDTs for over a decade while other AI subfields saw transformative progress; xRFM demonstrates that a kernel-based approach can match or exceed GBDTs on regression while offering O(log n) inference and native interpretability through AGOP matrices. The practical impact is significant given the ubiquity of tabular prediction in industry, and the method scales to 500K samples where direct kernel solvers become intractable. Open questions include whether iterative kernel solvers can replace ridge regularization in leaf RFMs, how to optimally determine leaf size and stopping criteria, and how AGOP-based interpretability compares to SHAP values or gradient-based importance scores. On classification, xRFM is competitive but not consistently dominant (TabPFN-v2 and RealMLP sometimes outperform it), suggesting the tree-partitioning advantage is most pronounced for regression.
