## Problem

Most neural network interpretability methods analyze hidden-layer activations, which capture only the receptive-field stage of a neuron's computation and do not reveal its causal effect on the network output. Existing contribution-based approaches examine individual units but do not characterize how *groups* of hidden neurons act together to construct outputs, leaving a gap in understanding the coordinated population dynamics that drive network behavior.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261201/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a trained feedforward network (CNN or ViT), a corpus of input images (50,000 ImageNet validation images in the main experiments), and a scalar output target (top logit, top-k logit sum, output entropy, or population surprisal for retinal models). It produces a non-negative dictionary of k sparse contribution modes (each of dimension d, the number of channels), per-image mode loadings that reconstruct the contribution matrix (R² ≈ 0.85), pixel-level contribution maps in input space, and ablation/preservation accuracy curves quantifying how selectively each mode controls a target class. The scalar target constraint is essential: computing contributions separately for each of 1000 logits would yield an intractable 3D tensor, so a single scalar function of the output is chosen per analysis.

## Method

CODEC operates in two stages. First, it computes the contribution of every hidden neuron to a scalar output target by extending gradient-based attribution methods (Integrated Gradients, ActGrad, InputGrad) to intermediate layers, then spatially sums contributions within each channel to obtain a single net-effect value per channel per image. Second, it trains a sparse autoencoder on the resulting (channels × images) contribution matrix, using a non-negative dictionary, sigmoid-plus-hard-threshold gating for sparsity, and L1 regularization, to decompose contributions into k overcomplete sparse modes that capture coordinated groups of channels acting together. The paper derives complete input-space decompositions for all three attribution algorithms, showing they are nested approximations of Integrated Gradients under progressively stronger linearity assumptions, and uses the InputGrad decomposition to produce interpretable contribution maps that reveal which input pixels drive each mode's effect on the output. Unlike activation-based SAEs, contribution modes are more class-correlated at intermediate layers and enable more selective ablation/preservation of target-class computation, requiring fewer perturbed channels to eliminate a specific output while sparing off-target classes.

## Results

CODEC achieves high-fidelity reconstruction of channel contributions via sparse autoencoders (R² = 0.85 for contributions, 0.84 for activations across all ResNet-50 layers). Contributions are consistently sparser than activations at every layer (higher Hoyer sparsity index) and increase in both sparsity and dimensionality with network depth, while positive and negative contributions progressively decorrelate. Contribution modes are more strongly correlated with ImageNet classes than activation modes, particularly at intermediate layers, and require fewer channels to ablate target-class computation. Ablating just 2% of channels identified from the top-2 most-correlated contribution modes greatly reduces target-class accuracy while leaving off-target performance largely unaffected, and preservation of those channels yields networks that classify only the targeted class.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark CODEC against a single external interpretability method in a head-to-head table; instead, the central comparison is between contribution-based and activation-based analyses within the same SAE framework. Contribution modes outperform activation modes on class correlation (Fig. 5), requiring fewer channels for complete target-class ablation and showing a sharp specificity increase between ResNet-50 blocks 6 and 7 (Fig. 6D–E). Among contribution algorithms, Integrated Gradients (10 steps) is used as the default and is preferred over ActGrad for its completeness property, though ActGrad is ~3× faster (293 ms vs. ~900 ms per image). On ViT-B, contribution-SAE yields significantly greater ablation specificity than activation-SAE, especially in late layers where activation modes actually hurt off-target classes more than on-target classes, suggesting causally inhibitory representations that only a causal measure like CODEC can capture. For the retinal CNN, CODEC's mode-based clustering of ganglion cells by active-mode patterns is consistent across layers 1 and 2, and single modes generate similar instantaneous receptive fields across different cell types, linking model interneurons to experimentally testable hypotheses.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends gradient-based attribution methods (Integrated Gradients, ActGrad, InputGrad) from input-to-output saliency to hidden-layer contribution analysis, building directly on Tanaka et al. (2019) who first applied contribution decomposition to retinal CNN models. The key conceptual departure is from activation-based interpretability (sparse autoencoders on activations, TCAV, Grad-CAM), which captures only receptive fields, toward contribution-based analysis that captures the full receptive × projective field composition. The novel step is decomposing per-unit contributions into sparse population-level modes via SAEs, addressing the gap the authors identify that 'existing approaches studying contributions have not examined how groups of units act together.'

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CODEC provides a principled, label-free framework for identifying which groups of hidden neurons causally drive specific outputs, enabling both targeted perturbation (ablation/preservation) and human-interpretable visualizations of input features. The finding that contributions progressively decorrelate positive and negative effects across depth, and that contribution modes outperform individual channels in class correlation without label access, suggests a structured computational organization in intermediate layers that activation analysis misses. Limitations include weaker perturbation performance on ViTs (lacking spatial equivariance), LLM scaling benchmarked only for contribution computation (not SAE training), and focus on image classification. Open questions include optimal spatial reduction strategies for transformers, extending to negative contributions, and whether contribution modes serve as transferable building blocks for architecture design.
