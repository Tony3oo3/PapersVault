## Problem

Prior work by Yeh et al. (2022) concluded that first embedding layers are most informative for LLM influence estimation, based on a gradient cancellation effect metric. However, this conclusion was derived from a single influence method on small models (RoBERTa) and rests on an assumption that high-cancellation weights reduce discriminative power. This paper demonstrates that the cancellation effect is theoretically unreliable and empirically uncorrelated with downstream filtering performance, and that no consensus exists on optimal layer choice or cross-layer score aggregation for influence estimation in modern LLMs.

## Input / Output

The pipeline operates on GLUE datasets with 20% uniformly flipped training labels, using LoRA modules on query and value projections in every attention layer with embeddings compressed to task-relevant tokens. Influence scores are computed per layer group (word embeddings, four attention groups, classification head) for each training–validation sample pair, then aggregated via the chosen strategy (mean, Rank, or Vote) into a single per-sample score. The 30% least-influential samples are removed and the model is retrained; the Noise Detection Rate (NDR) is computed directly from knowledge of which labels were flipped, serving as a retraining-free proxy for filtering quality.

## Method

The paper provides a theoretical counterexample (Theorem 5.1) proving that including high-cancellation weights can strictly increase the influence-score separation between noisy and clean samples, undermining the cancellation effect as a layer-selection criterion. Empirically, across RoBERTa-Large, Llama-3.2 1B, Qwen-2.5 1.5B, and Mistral 7B, middle attention layers (groups 1–2) consistently outperform embedding and classification-head layers for TracIn, DataInf, and Cosine influence estimation. Two novel aggregation strategies replace standard averaging: Rank sums ordinal positions across layers and correctly-predicted validation samples to eliminate magnitude domination, while Vote assigns k, k−1, …, 0 positional votes per validation sample to the least-to-most influential training samples, with k set to the filtering budget. Finally, the Noise Detection Rate (NDR) and AUC are validated as moderate-to-strong (ρ = 0.5–0.9) Spearman-correlated proxies for post-filtering task accuracy, with Vote aggregation yielding the strongest correlations (0.8–0.9), enabling influence method selection without expensive retraining.

## Results

The cancellation effect metric C (from Yeh et al., 2022) shows weak-to-no correlation (Spearman ρ ≈ 0.0–0.3, often negative) with downstream task accuracy after filtering, confirming it is an unreliable layer-selection indicator. In contrast, the proposed Noise Detection Rate (NDR) achieves moderate-to-strong correlations (ρ = 0.5–0.8) with final accuracy, and under Vote aggregation these correlations rise to 0.8–0.9. For layer selection, middle attention groups (layers 00–07 and 08–15 for Mistral 7B) consistently outperform the embedding layer and classification head, with DataInf on layers 08–15 achieving a 0.71 win rate. Vote aggregation improves the best DataInf configuration (layers 00–07) to a 0.84 win rate, and promotes previously poor configurations (e.g., TracIn CL) from rank 12 to rank 1 on Mistral 7B.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against TracIn (Pruthi et al., 2020), the paper demonstrates that the standard mean-aggregated TracIn on word embeddings (WE) is NOT universally the best layer choice, contradicting Yeh et al. (2022); instead, middle attention layers with DataInf or Cosine frequently outperform TracIn-WE, and Vote aggregation elevates TracIn's win rate substantially (e.g., TracIn CL on Mistral rises from rank 12 under Mean to rank 1 under Vote). Against DataInf (Kwon et al., 2024), the paper confirms DataInf as a strong influence method but shows that its performance is highly layer-dependent: DataInf on layers 08–15 of Mistral 7B achieves a 0.71 win rate under Mean aggregation, rising to 0.84 with Vote, while DataInf on the classification head (CL) performs poorly across all models. Against the Yeh et al. (2022) TracIn_we and TracIn_we¹⁰ baselines, the paper shows these word-embedding-restricted variants are consistently dominated by full-layer methods with proper aggregation (e.g., on RoBERTa, TracIn_we achieves 0.48 win rate vs. 0.54 for full TracIn-WE, and both are outperformed by DataInf on later layers at 0.66). The paper also shows that the cancellation effect C, which Yeh et al. used to justify preferring early layers, has near-zero or negative Spearman correlation with downstream accuracy (ρ = −0.3 to 0.3), while the proposed NDR metric correlates at 0.5–0.9, providing a more reliable proxy for influence quality without expensive retraining.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly challenges and extends Yeh et al. (2022, NeurIPS), which concluded that first embedding layers are optimal for influence estimation based on a gradient cancellation effect. The authors provide a theoretical counterexample (Theorem 5.1) showing high-cancellation weights can improve influence separation, and empirically demonstrate that middle attention layers outperform embedding layers for larger models (Llama-3.2, Qwen-2.5, Mistral 7B). Building on the influence function framework of Koh & Liang (2017) and the TracIn/DataInf methods, the paper introduces novel aggregation strategies (Rank, positional Vote) that move beyond standard averaging, and proposes the Noise Detection Rate (NDR) as a lightweight proxy for downstream filtering performance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that middle attention layers are more informative than first embedding layers for influence estimation in modern LLMs directly contradicts a widely-cited prior result and provides practical guidance for practitioners choosing which layers to compute gradients over. The Vote aggregation strategy offers a simple, effective improvement over averaging that could be adopted broadly. However, limitations remain: influence methods on Llama-3.2 1B fail to outperform random removal, NDR correlations with downstream accuracy are moderate (0.5–0.7) rather than strong, and the evaluation is restricted to GLUE-style classification with injected label noise. Open questions include whether these layer-selection and aggregation findings generalize to generative tasks, larger models (70B+), and more realistic data quality issues beyond synthetic label flipping.
