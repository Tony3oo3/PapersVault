## Problem

Traditional behavioral and neuroimaging studies of dyslexia cannot test causal hypotheses about the role of the visual word form area (VWFA) in reading impairments, and no prior work has modeled brain disorders using system-level neural network models. This paper asks whether functionally identifying and perturbing artificial analogues of the VWFA in vision-language models can selectively reproduce the reading-specific deficits of dyslexia while preserving general visual and language abilities.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261546/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The localization stage consumes four categories of image stimuli (written words, scrambled words, faces, objects) to compute per-unit t-statistics, while the evaluation stage feeds the model images of letter strings for lexical decisions, 3×3 or 2×2 visual matrices for RAVEN, and image pairs paired with a text caption for Kempler. The final output is not a prediction but a modified model: a 'dyslexic' VLM with ~6.89% of its MLP gate-projection units zeroed out, which then produces lexical decisions, pattern-completion answers, and image selections that selectively degrade on reading tasks. All stimuli are presented as RGB images with text instructions, mirroring how human participants receive visual word stimuli.

## Method

The method adapts a neuroscience functional-localizer paradigm (Saygin et al., 2016) to a VLM: each unit in the model's MLP gate-projection layers is scored by a t-statistic contrasting its activation on word images versus scrambled words, faces, and objects, and the top-k% most word-selective units are identified. A minimal subnetwork is then found by incrementally ablating (zeroing) a growing fraction of these units until ROAR lexical-decision accuracy drops below the 65% dyslexia threshold, yielding a mask of ~6.89% of units across all 80 transformer blocks of Qwen2-VL-72B's language decoder. Only full ablation (scaling factor = 0) produces the selective phonological reading deficit; partial scaling or negative scaling either has negligible effect or causes non-selective output collapse. Ablating the same number of random units, or targeting other layer types (vision encoder, visual merger, self-attention), fails to produce reading-selective impairment, confirming that the effect depends on the specific identity and location of VWF-selective units in the MLP layers.

## Results

Ablating ~6.89% of VWF-selective MLP units in Qwen2-VL-72B drops ROAR lexical decision accuracy by 32% below the 65% dyslexia threshold (p<<0.01), while RAVEN visual reasoning remains unchanged (p≈0.75) and Kempler sentence comprehension actually increases by +10% (p<<0.01, two-tailed). On phonology-sensitive stimuli, the ablated model shows an 8% accuracy drop (p<<0.01), closely matching the 9% drop in human dyslexics, while orthography-sensitive items show no significant impairment (p>0.059). The ablated model also replicates human font sensitivity, showing significant improvements for OpenDyslexic, Comic Sans, and KG Primary Penmanship (p<<0.001) and a significant decrease for Papyrus (p<<0.01).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is against human dyslexic behavioral data from Luke et al. (2023) on matched phonology- and orthography-sensitive lexical decision stimuli. The VWF-ablated model closely mirrors the human phonological deficit (−8% vs. −9% accuracy drop, both p<<0.01), supporting the dominant view that VWFA hypoactivation primarily disrupts phonological processing. However, the model does not replicate the orthographic deficit observed in human dyslexics (−6%, p<<0.01), showing no significant impairment on transposed-letter stimuli (p>0.059); the authors attribute this gap to potential comorbidities in the human sample. The random-ablation control (same number of units, same layers) confirms selectivity: it degrades all benchmarks uniformly (−21% ROAR, −21% RAVEN, −13% Kempler) without crossing the dyslexia threshold, whereas VWF-selective ablation produces the dissociation pattern. On font sensitivity, the ablated model matches human dyslexic patterns (improved on OpenDyslexic/Comic Sans, worsened on Papyrus) while the intact model shows no systematic font effects.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the functional localization and targeted ablation methodology from AlKhamissi et al. (2025), who identified causally task-relevant units in LLMs, to vision-language models, and repurposes the classic fMRI localizer paradigm from Saygin et al. (2016) to identify VWF-selective units in VLMs. While prior work has used neural networks to model healthy brain function in vision and language, the authors explicitly position this as the first attempt to model a brain *disorder* (dyslexia) through hypothesized neural activity changes in a system-level model. The approach also builds on the broader literature of brain-aligned neural network modeling (Yamins & DiCarlo, Schrimpf et al.) but shifts the goal from predicting neural responses to simulating pathological deficits.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work establishes a generalizable computational framework (localize → ablate → behavioral test) for investigating causal mechanisms of brain disorders in silico, potentially reducing reliance on costly and ethically constrained human studies. The font-sensitivity prediction demonstrates a practical application: the ablated model can serve as a testbed for designing dyslexia-friendly visual stimuli. Key limitations include the imperfect match to human data (the model shows a purely phonological deficit while dyslexic humans show both phonological and orthographic impairments), the causal status of VWFA hypoactivation in dyslexia remains debated in the neuroscience literature, and the framework has only been validated on three VLMs. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to other disorders, whether the observed performance increase on nonverbal benchmarks post-ablation reflects a genuine compensatory mechanism, and whether the framework can inform clinical subtyping.
