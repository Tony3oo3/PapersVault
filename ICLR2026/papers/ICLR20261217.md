## Problem

Existing hard-science benchmarks (GPQA, HLE, CMPhysBench) assess LLMs on textbook or student-level problems, leaving a gap in evaluating whether models can function as research assistants on cutting-edge scientific tasks. In condensed matter theory specifically, the required expertise is concentrated in small communities, making crowdsourcing infeasible, and no systematic research-grade benchmark existed. CMT-Benchmark fills this gap with 50 original problems authored by expert researchers that test the critical judgment and synthesis skills expected of a strong graduate student or postdoc.

## Input / Output

Each problem is presented as a LaTeX-formatted prompt written by the author, with a fixed formatting component instructing the model to return its final answer in a boxed LaTeX environment. The parser evaluates only the last boxed expression, discarding intermediate reasoning. Answers span four modalities—numerical values, algebraic expressions, multiple-choice (with >5 options to prevent lucky guessing), and non-commutative operator expressions—and are graded binary (correct/incorrect) against the author-supplied ground truth with no partial credit. The evaluation pipeline handles operator algebra by replacing non-commutative symbols with Sympy non-commutative objects, applying physics simplifications (e.g., fermionic anticommutation relations), and reducing both the ground truth and model response to a canonical normal-ordered form before checking symbolic equivalence.

## Method

The benchmark was constructed by an international panel of 19 condensed matter theorists (postdocs and faculty from Rutgers, Harvard, Weizmann, ETH Zürich, Cornell, Stanford, UCLA, San Jose State, NTU, and University of Zürich) who authored problems they would expect their own group members to solve correctly. A custom Google Sheet extension enabled iterative problem refinement: authors could run their prompts against a subset of LLMs (Gemini 2.0/2.5 Flash, Gemini 2.5 Pro, GPT-4o) in real time, identify failure modes, and increase difficulty until problems were unambiguous and machine-verifiable. The automated grading infrastructure extends the LaTeX-to-Sympy parser from HARDMath2 with a novel non-commutative operator module that performs symbolic manipulation and normal ordering to verify equivalence of quantum many-body expressions. Seventeen models across five families (GPT, Gemini, Claude, DeepSeek, LLaMA) were evaluated zero-shot, yielding pass@1 rates that revealed 18 problems unsolved by any model and 26 solved by at most one, with the best model (GPT-5) achieving only 30% overall.

## Results

Across 17 frontier LLMs evaluated zero-shot on 50 expert-authored condensed matter theory problems, the best model (GPT-5) achieves only 30.0% Pass@1, with the average across all models at 11.4 ± 2.1%. 18 problems are unsolved by every model, and 26 are solved by at most one model. Performance is particularly poor on VMC (0% for all models), QMC (max 16.7%), and DMRG (only 2 models score non-zero at 25%), while PEPS is the most tractable category with top models reaching 66.7%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 17 models across five families (OpenAI GPT, Google Gemini, Anthropic Claude, DeepSeek, and LLaMA) on the same 50-problem benchmark. GPT-5 leads at 30.0% overall, followed by GPT-o3 (26.0%) and GPT-5-mini (24.0%), with all other models below 20%. The GPT family dominates the top tier, while Gemini models cluster in the 4–14% range and Claude models in the 2–10% range. Notably, no model achieves non-zero scores on VMC problems, and only GPT-o3 and Claude 4.0 Opus solve any DMRG problems (25% each). The open-source models (DeepSeek v3 at 4.0%, LLaMA Maverick at 12.0%) trail the proprietary frontier models substantially, with LLaMA Maverick being the strongest non-GPT/Gemini/Claude entry. The paper also reports that 18 problems are completely unsolved by all 17 models, concentrated in QMC, VMC, and DMRG, indicating a clear capability gap across all model families.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the HARDMath2 framework (Roggeveen et al., 2025) for automated LaTeX parsing and iterative Google-Sheet-based problem construction, but departs significantly by introducing a non-commutative operator algebra layer (normal ordering, anticommutation relations) essential for quantum many-body physics. It positions itself against existing hard-science benchmarks (GPQA, HLE, CMPhysBench, TPBench) by targeting research-level problems authored by an expert panel rather than textbook or crowdsourced questions, explicitly framing itself as the 'first hard science equivalent of FrontierMath.' The evaluation methodology—deterministic binary grading with symbolic equivalence checking for operator expressions—repurposes Sympy in a way not required by prior physics or math benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that even frontier models (GPT-5 at 30%, average 11.4% across 17 models) cannot function as research assistants in CMT, with 18 problems unsolved by any model and the entire VMC category at 0% across all models. The diagnostic case studies expose specific, actionable failure modes—models adding unmentioned symmetries, failing at lattice geometry, and conflating general field knowledge with problem-specific analysis—that point toward concrete improvements (tool-augmented spatial reasoning, principle-grounded verification). Limitations include the small problem count (50), single-domain scope, and binary grading without partial credit; open questions include whether tool-augmented agents (e.g., with plotting or symbolic computation access) would close the gap, and whether the failure modes generalize to other physics subfields.
