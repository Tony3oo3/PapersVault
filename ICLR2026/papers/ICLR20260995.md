## Problem

Hierarchical coarse-to-fine robot manipulation policies, even when augmented with pre-trained models, still suffer from poor generalization to both environment variations (object/background changes) and novel task compositions. The paper identifies specific causes: domain shift between VLM pre-training and robotic images, inadequate VLM adaptation for 3D keypoint prediction, and under-exploitation of VLM planning capabilities, all of which limit compositional skill reuse across tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260995/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The coarse task planner receives multi-view RGB-D images projected into three canonical views (front, left, top at 224×224), a high-level task description, the previous step instruction as a short-term memory cue, and optionally a language plan. It outputs a sub-task language plan, the current step instruction, 3D positions of task-related objects, and a 3D keypoint via a two-round inference protocol (textual plan generation first, then visual+plan keypoint prediction). The fine-grained action predictor takes the step instruction and cropped multi-view RGB-D images around the keypoint, producing the end-effector action (3D position, orientation, gripper state) for the next key-frame, which is then executed by a motion planner to generate intermediate actions.

## Method

CLAP decomposes a high-level task into step-wise language instructions aligned with key-frame trajectory segments, enabling the fine-grained predictor to learn reusable skills rather than task-specific end-to-end mappings. The coarse task planner is a LoRA-fine-tuned Qwen 2.5 VL-3B trained with three co-training objectives: robot trajectories (keypoint + step instruction prediction), an auxiliary language plan dataset (compositional reasoning), and an object position dataset (3D object detection for zero-shot object generalization). At inference, a two-round protocol first generates a textual language plan from the task description alone, then conditions on the plan plus visual inputs to perform chain-of-thought reasoning (localize objects → generate step instruction → predict 3D keypoint), mitigating the visual bias that causes VLMs to ignore novel instructions. The fine-grained action predictor constructs a 3D-aware, language-aligned representation by encoding RGB images and step instructions with SigLIP, depth images with DINOv2, and pixel-wise 3D coordinates as positional embeddings, all fused through a Multi-View Transformer to predict the final action.

## Results

On GemBench, CLAP achieves a 62.0% average success rate across four generalization levels, outperforming the prior SOTA (BridgeVLA, 50.0%) by 12 percentage points while using only 20 training trajectories per task variation (vs. 100 for baselines). The gains are most pronounced on Level-2 (novel rigid objects: 83.2% vs. 65.0%) and Level-4 (long-horizon tasks: 31.4% vs. 0.0% for BridgeVLA), where most baselines fail entirely. In real-world experiments with only 10 demonstrations per task, CLAP reaches 77.3% average success rate versus 22.5% for RVT2, a 54.8-point improvement.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CLAP outperforms all six baselines on average success rate (62.0% vs. 50.0% for the strongest, BridgeVLA). The advantage is largest on Level-2 (novel rigid objects), where CLAP scores 83.2% versus 65.0% for BridgeVLA and 64.5% for 3D-LOTUS++, and on Level-4 (long-horizon tasks), where CLAP achieves 31.4% while BridgeVLA, RVT2, 3D Diffuser Actor, and HiveFormer all score near 0%. On Level-1 (in-distribution placements), CLAP (83.9%) trails 3D-LOTUS (94.3%) and BridgeVLA (91.1%), suggesting the method trades some in-domain precision for substantially better out-of-domain generalization. On Level-3 (articulated objects), CLAP leads with 49.6% versus 43.8% for BridgeVLA and 41.5% for 3D-LOTUS++. In the real-world setting, CLAP dominates RVT2 across all four visual perturbation conditions (e.g., 78.7% vs. 6.2% under altered lighting) and on all four novel task compositions (e.g., 70% vs. 0% for 'put shape in cup'), confirming that the gap is not limited to simulation.

## Relation to Prior Work

CLAP extends the coarse-to-fine manipulation policy lineage (Gualtieri & Platt, 2020; RVT2, 2024) by replacing the from-scratch coarse branch with a LoRA-fine-tuned VLM (Qwen 2.5 VL-3B) that performs task decomposition and chain-of-thought keypoint prediction, and by replacing the fine-grained branch's generic encoders with specialized pre-trained models (SigLIP for RGB+language alignment, DINOv2 for depth geometry). It departs from the most direct SOTA, BridgeVLA (Li et al., 2025b), which also fine-tunes a VLM for 3D manipulation, by introducing a two-round inference protocol that separates textual task planning from visual keypoint prediction to mitigate the known visual-bias problem in VLAs, and by conditioning the action predictor on step-wise sub-instructions rather than a single high-level task description. The auxiliary 3D object detection co-training and the 3D position embedding in the fine-grained branch draw on spatial-reasoning ideas from CoT-VLA and 3D-VLA but are integrated into a hierarchical coarse-to-fine architecture rather than a monolithic VLA.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 12% average success-rate gain over BridgeVLA on GemBench while using only 20 trajectories per task variation (vs. 100) is practically significant for settings where robot data collection is expensive, and the real-world results (77.3% vs. 22.5% average success with 10 demos per task) demonstrate that the approach transfers beyond simulation. The task-decomposition-into-step-instructions mechanism offers a clean, interpretable path toward compositional skill reuse, which is a long-standing goal in robot learning. Key limitations remain: the key-frame-based formulation is restricted to structured, discretizable tasks (e.g., wiping a desk is intractable), there is no error-correction mechanism so a single wrong step cascades into failure, and the L3 (articulated object) and L4 (long-horizon) success rates, while best-in-class, are still far from reliable deployment levels (~50% and ~31% respectively).
