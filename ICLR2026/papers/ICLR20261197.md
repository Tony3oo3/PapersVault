## Problem

Current academic paper reasoning benchmarks follow a search-oriented paradigm where models retrieve a few relevant passages for pre-specified targets, which cannot support researcher-style full-document understanding and verification. This paper identifies that no existing benchmark requires models to exhaustively scan entire papers, actively construct document-level evidence views, and detect non-obvious consistency issues without target cues — a capability essential for autonomous research.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261197/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark provides 1,800 target-absent queries paired with complete academic papers (715 papers from ICLR 2024/2025 and Nature Communications) spanning 13 natural-science domains. Papers are fed to models either as page images or as Tesseract-OCR text, and models must produce open-ended responses identifying errors with specific evidence locations (page/section anchors), step-by-step reasoning chains, and category labels. The evaluation pipeline parses each response into a structured tuple of binary detection indicators, predicted evidence sets, matched reasoning steps, and a count of unrelated hallucinated errors, enabling a composite score that penalizes both missed evidence and over-reporting.

## Method

ScholScan introduces a scan-oriented task paradigm in which models receive full papers with neutral, target-absent audit-style queries and must independently discover planted or real scientific errors across nine categories (from computation errors to citation misalignment). Questions are constructed via two complementary pipelines: Gemini 2.5 Pro performs coordinated multi-page sentence-level edits on accepted papers to synthesize composite errors, and explicit falsifiable errors are sampled from public reviews of rejected ICLR submissions; cross-paper citation errors are generated by editing citing papers to contradict their cited sources. A team of 10 domain experts conducted dual-review annotation, discarding 1,700 of 3,500 initial candidates and revising 1,541 remaining instances. The evaluation protocol defines a five-component score combining binary error detection, a Dice-based evidence location score with a squared over-reporting penalty, a prefix-match reasoning completeness score, and a rapidly decaying penalty for unrelated hallucinated errors. The paper further evaluates 15 models across 24 input configurations and 8 RAG frameworks, finding that no retrieval-augmented method yields significant gains, confirming that search-oriented retrieval is fundamentally misaligned with scan-oriented document-level reasoning.

## Results

On ScholScan, the best-performing model under image input is GPT-5 with an average score of 19.2 (out of 100), while under OCR-based text input, Gemini 2.5 Pro leads with 30.3. No model exceeds 60 in any of the nine error categories, and even in the best-performing category (Sampling & Generalizability), nearly half of the models receive single-digit scores. Reasoning-enhanced variants (e.g., Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking at 17.4 vs. Instruct at 1.7; DeepSeek-R1 at 11.4 vs. DeepSeek-V3.1 at 1.7) consistently outperform their base counterparts by over 10 points on average. Across all eight RAG frameworks tested, none delivers a significant improvement over the baseline, with most retrieval-based methods actually degrading performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the 15 models evaluated, GPT-5 leads under image input (19.2 avg.) while Gemini 2.5 Pro leads under text input (30.3 avg.), but both remain far below the 60-point threshold in every error category. Reasoning-enhanced models consistently dominate: Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking (17.4) outperforms its Instruct counterpart (1.7) by over 15 points, and DeepSeek-R1 (11.4) surpasses DeepSeek-V3.1 (1.7) by nearly 10 points, confirming that iterative reasoning is critical for scan-oriented tasks. Text input generally outperforms image input by an average of 4.81 points across nine MLLMs, though image input reverses this in the Computation & Formulae category where OCR destroys formulaic structure. Among eight RAG methods, none significantly improves over the baseline: BM25 (16.7) and Contriever (16.6) under text input both fall below the Qwen3-Thinking baseline (17.4), while under image input, ColQwen2.5 (1.2), VisRAG (1.0), and ColPali-v1.3 (0.8) all perform far worse than the Llama 4 Maverick baseline (7.0). The sole exception is VRAG-RL, which improves image-input performance from 7.0 to 10.9 by interleaving retrieval and reasoning in an RL-optimized loop, though its absolute score remains low. The Oracle condition (providing gold evidence images) yields the largest gains (e.g., 17.4→24.5 for Qwen3-Thinking), confirming that the bottleneck is evidence localization rather than reasoning per se.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant search-oriented paradigm in academic paper reasoning, where models retrieve relevant passages and reason over local evidence for pre-specified targets (as in DocMath-Eval, MMLongBench-Doc, FinMMDocR, and QA-style benchmarks like ArXivQA and SPIQA). Rather than building on a specific prior method, ScholScan carves new ground by introducing a scan-oriented task formulation: models receive complete papers with target-absent queries and must exhaustively scan the document to identify consistency issues, constructing a document-level evidence view without retrieval cues. The evaluation protocol draws inspiration from MMLongBench-Doc's open-ended reasoning assessment, but extends it with a structured multi-component score (detection, evidence location, reasoning process, unrelated-error penalty) that evaluates both process and outcome. The RAG analysis repurposes existing retrieval architectures (BM25, Contriever, ColPali, VisRAG, VRAG-RL) as baselines to test whether search-oriented augmentation can bridge the gap to scan-oriented tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ScholScan provides the first comprehensive benchmark (1,800 questions, 9 error categories, 13 natural-science domains, 715 papers) for evaluating whether MLLMs can perform researcher-style full-document verification, a capability essential for autonomous scientific research. The finding that no RAG method delivers significant improvement—and that most actually degrade performance—challenges the assumption that retrieval augmentation is a universal remedy for long-context document tasks, suggesting that scan-oriented reasoning requires fundamentally different architectural or training approaches. Key open questions include how to improve MLLMs on target-suppressed inputs, whether the five latent skill dimensions identified (research concept comprehension, experimental process modeling, formal reasoning, causal inference, referential alignment) can be targeted with specialized training, and whether the benchmark's difficulty is appropriate or whether some errors are unrealistically subtle for real-world research workflows.
