## Problem

Existing multimodal safety benchmarks treat vision and language inputs separately, failing to capture risks that emerge only from joint interpretation where individually benign components become harmful in combination. Additionally, prior evaluations lack a mechanism to distinguish clearly unsafe content from borderline educational or informative queries, leading to problematic over-blocking or under-refusal. This paper addresses both gaps by systematically mapping how individual modality safety signals combine into joint safety assessments across 17 combinatorial patterns.

## Input / Output

Each sample pairs a unique real-world image (retrieved from a 3.6B-image web corpus and deduplicated via perceptual hashing) with a text query generated across five linguistic formulations (binary, information-seeking, rhetorical, description, how-to) and three severity levels. The primary output is a three-class joint safety label (Safe, Borderline, Unsafe) alongside individual image-only and text-only labels, enabling fine-grained error attribution. The secondary safety-alignment task produces refusal rates and GPT-4o-judged helpfulness scores (1–10 scale) under contrasting instructional framings (harmless vs. helpful).

## Method

VLSU is constructed through a four-stage pipeline: (1) parameterized image-concept generation via DSPy programs with Gemini-1.5-Pro-002, using a counter-intuitive strategy where safe concepts are generated alongside example unsafe questions to ensure contextual utility; (2) retrieval of authentic images from a 3.6B-image corpus with perceptual-hash deduplication to prevent synthetic artifacts; (3) context-driven query synthesis jointly conditioned on the retrieved image, target text severity, intended joint severity, stylistic variation, and token length; and (4) triple human annotation (image-only, text-only, joint) by 225 expert annotators with Krippendorff's α = 0.806. The framework introduces a novel borderline severity class and formalizes joint safety as a 3×3×3 combinatorial space reduced to 17 empirically occurring patterns (s_i-s_t-s_j tuples). Evaluation uses zero-shot three-class classification prompts across 17 VLMs (4B–72B open-weight and closed-weight), with structured chain-of-thought prompting tested as an inference-time intervention, revealing a persistent ~25-point gap to human oracle performance.

## Results

On VLSU (8,187 samples, 15 harm categories, 17 safety patterns), the best-performing models (GPT-5-R and Haiku-4.5-R) achieve 74.6% F1, substantially below the 91.0% human oracle. In contrast, the same models reach 96.8–99.8% F1 on existing benchmarks (MM-SafetyBench, MSTS, VLSBench), confirming VLSU is significantly more challenging. Models achieve >90% accuracy on unimodal-dominated patterns (e.g., U-U-U) but degrade to 20–55% on joint-reasoning patterns (e.g., S-S-U), and 34% of joint classification errors occur despite correct individual modality predictions. Structured CoT prompting yields selective gains (GPT-4o: +8.6 F1; Qwen2.5VL-7B: +9.1 F1) but the best result (65.3 F1) remains 25.7 points below human performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VLSU is compared against six existing multimodal safety benchmarks: MM-SafetyBench, VLSBench, MSTS, ELITE, VLGuard, and MLLMGuard. The central finding is that VLSU is substantially harder than all of them: the best model F1 on existing benchmarks ranges from 87.8% (VLGuard) to 99.8% (MSTS), while on VLSU the best F1 is only 74.6%—a gap of 16–25 points. This indicates that existing benchmarks fail to capture the challenges of joint vision-language safety understanding. Specifically, MM-SafetyBench uses synthetic images and templated queries, VLSBench still contains 67% synthetic images, and MSTS/ELITE/SIUO focus on narrow subsets of the safety space (e.g., only S-S-U combinations). VLSU, by contrast, covers all 17 combinatorial patterns with real-world images and introduces a novel borderline severity class, exposing failure modes (particularly in joint-reasoning and borderline calibration) that are invisible to prior evaluations. No single model dominates across all benchmarks; for example, GLM-4.1V-R achieves 99.8% F1 on MSTS but only 51.9% on VLSU, while GPT-5-R and Haiku-4.5-R lead on VLSU at 74.6%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the multimodal safety evaluation line initiated by MM-SafetyBench, VLSBench, and MSTS, which each cover only a narrow slice of the safety space (typically a single combination pattern like U-U-U or S-S-U, with synthetic or pooled images and binary safe/unsafe labels). VLSU departs from this lineage in three conceptual ways: it introduces a three-level severity taxonomy (safe/borderline/unsafe) that formalizes the previously unaddressed 'borderline' class, it systematically maps all 17 feasible image-text-joint severity combinations rather than a handful, and it grounds evaluation in real-world images retrieved from a 3.6B-image corpus rather than synthetic generation. The data generation pipeline repurposes DSPy for parameterized, policy-driven query synthesis, but the core contribution is the combinatorial safety framework itself, which prior benchmarks lack.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a fundamental and universal gap: across all 17 tested VLMs, performance drops from >90% on unimodal-dominated patterns to 20–55% on joint-reasoning patterns, and 34% of joint errors occur despite correct classification of each individual modality—evidence of absent compositional reasoning rather than encoder or language-model weakness. This reframes multimodal safety as a fusion problem rather than a detection problem, and the quantified over-blocking/under-refusal tradeoff (e.g., Gemini-1.5 shifting from 62.4% to 10.4% borderline over-blocking at the cost of dropping unsafe refusal from 90.8% to 53.9%) shows that current alignment is instruction-fragile rather than content-grounded. Open questions include whether training-time interventions (as opposed to the tested inference-time CoT, which hits a ceiling ~25 points below human oracle) can close the compositional gap, and whether the borderline class generalizes beyond English. As an 8,187-sample, 15-category, 17-pattern benchmark with triple human annotation, VLSU is positioned to become a standard evaluation tool for multimodal safety research.
