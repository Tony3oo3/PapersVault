## Problem

Behavioral Foundation Models for zero-shot RL rely on complex representation learning objectives—typically Bellman-based successor measure estimation—that are sensitive to hyperparameters, require sufficient dataset coverage, and can produce degenerate representations when policies select out-of-distribution actions. This paper asks whether these complex objectives are actually necessary, or whether a simpler policy-independent latent dynamics prediction loss can produce equally effective state features for zero-shot task generalization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20265195/cropped_page5_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a batch of reward-free offline state-action transitions (e.g., from RND exploration on DMC or D4RL datasets) to train a d-dimensional state encoder φ and a latent dynamics model. At test time, given an arbitrary reward function r_test(s), the task embedding z is extracted via linear regression on φ, and the frozen BFM policy π_z outputs continuous actions. The state representation is a 512-dim (default) embedding, and the policy operates on continuous action spaces ranging from 2-dim (Pointmass) to 69-dim (SMPL humanoid with 358-dim observations).

## Method

RLDP learns state representations by unrolled latent next-state prediction: an encoder φ maps states to a d-dim latent space, and a dynamics network g predicts future latents by iterating h_{t+1} = g(h_t, a)^T w over a horizon H, with a slowly-moving target encoder. The key finding is that this objective alone causes a mild form of feature collapse—cosine similarity between state representations increases over training—reducing the span of representable reward functions. To prevent this, RLDP adds an orthogonality regularizer that projects all state and predicted latent representations onto a hypersphere S^{d-1} and minimizes pairwise cosine similarity (L_r = E[φ(s)^T φ(s')]). The final loss is L_d + λ·L_r. After representation learning, the encoder is frozen and a standard BFM is trained via successor measure estimation and policy improvement, with the representation kept fixed. A theoretical lemma shows that minimizing L_RLDP upper-bounds the successor measure prediction error for any Lipschitz-valued policy, connecting the simple dynamics objective to the BFM's downstream performance.

## Results

On the ExoRL offline zero-shot RL benchmark (Table 1, d=512, 4 seeds), RLDP achieves competitive or best performance across 16 tasks in Walker, Cheetah, Quadruped, and Pointmass. RLDP wins on 7/16 tasks (e.g., Quadruped Jump: 733.32 vs FB 567.27, PSM 549.57; Cheetah Walk Backward: 821.51 vs FB 630.40, PSM 817.92; Pointmass Top Right: 795.47 vs FB 550.84, PSM 666.00). In the low-coverage D4RL setting (Table 9, 10 seeds), RLDP achieves the highest normalized return on 5/6 tasks, with particularly large margins (e.g., walker2d-medium: 83.83 vs FB 43.72, PSM 55.70, HILP 56.34; halfcheetah-medium-expert: 86.03 vs FB 52.17, PSM 49.92, HILP 68.47). On the 45-task SMPL Humanoid online benchmark, RLDP is competitive with FB-CPR oracle and other baselines, outperforming them on tasks like raise-arms and lie-on-ground.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against FB (Touati & Ollivier, 2021), RLDP wins on 7/16 ExoRL tasks and is within one standard deviation on most others; FB's advantage is most pronounced on Walker Flip (977 vs 493) and Walker Stand (918 vs 890). Against PSM (Agarwal et al., 2024), RLDP wins on 5/16 tasks (Cheetah Run Backward, Walk Backward; Quadruped Stand, Run, Jump; Pointmass Top Right, Bottom Left, Bottom Right) and is competitive on the rest; PSM leads on Walker Run (450 vs 334) and Walker Walk (875 vs 780). Against HILP (Park et al., 2024), RLDP is consistently stronger in the low-coverage D4RL setting, winning 5/6 tasks with large margins (e.g., walker2d-medium: 83.83 vs 56.34; halfcheetah-medium-expert: 86.03 vs 68.47). Against the Laplacian baseline (Wu et al., 2018), RLDP dominates across nearly all ExoRL tasks (Table 6), with Laplacian performing poorly especially on Cheetah and Pointmass. Against the FB-CPR oracle (Tirinzoni et al., 2025) on SMPL Humanoid, RLDP is competitive overall but falls short on many tasks; however, it exceeds oracle performance on a few tasks (e.g., raise-arms, lie-on-ground), suggesting the offline representation learning phase can sometimes produce more robust features than fully-online training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits within the Behavioral Foundation Model (BFM) lineage initiated by Forward-Backward (Touati & Ollivier, 2021) and extended by PSM (Agarwal et al., 2024) and HILP (Park et al., 2024), all of which learn state representations by estimating successor measures via Bellman backups. The key departure is replacing this complex, policy-dependent objective with a simple policy-independent latent dynamics prediction loss plus an orthogonality regularizer, drawing on the observation from Fujimoto et al. (2025) that dynamics prediction aids single-task RL and on orthogonality regularization from self-supervised learning (He et al., 2024; Bansal et al., 2018). The paper retains the successor feature machinery for policy learning (Eq. 10, Eq. 5) and integrates TD3+BC (Fujimoto & Gu, 2021) for low-coverage offline settings and CPR (Tirinzoni et al., 2025) for humanoid control, but the representation learning phase is entirely decoupled from any policy or reward signal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that RLDP provides a simpler, more stable, and more robust alternative to Bellman-based representation learning for BFMs, particularly in low-coverage settings where out-of-distribution actions from policy-dependent objectives degrade performance. The theoretical guarantee (Lemma 4.3) connecting the RLDP loss to successor measure prediction error gives the method a principled foundation beyond empirical validation. Open questions include whether the approach extends to high-dimensional visual observations (the paper only uses proprioceptive states), whether the orthogonality regularizer is truly necessary at scale or merely compensates for limited dataset diversity, and whether the modest performance gaps on some tasks (e.g., Walker Flip, Cheetah Run) indicate a fundamental expressivity limitation of dynamics-predictive features compared to successor-measure-optimal ones.
