## Problem

Using LLMs directly as game-playing policies relies on fragile implicit pattern-matching, producing frequent illegal moves and strategically shallow play, especially on novel (out-of-distribution) games. Prior code-world-model (CWM) work either assumes fully observed deterministic environments or post-hoc observability of hidden states, leaving the challenging case of strictly partially observable games (where hidden states are never revealed) unaddressed.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264542/cropped_page7_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The agent receives a textual description of game rules plus 5 offline trajectories (observations, rewards, legal actions, and—under open deck—hidden states and all players' actions). It produces executable Python code conforming to the OpenSpiel API: a CWM with state-transition, legal-move, observation, reward, and termination functions; for imperfect-information games, a stochastic inference function that samples plausible hidden histories from a single player's observation-action history; and a one-shot heuristic value function selected via tournament. At play time, the agent outputs a single game action per turn via MCTS or ISMCTS over the synthesized CWM.

## Method

The LLM is used as an induction engine that maps game rules and trajectory data into a verifiable, executable Python world model (CWM), shifting the burden from producing a good policy to producing a good model. The CWM is refined iteratively via automatically generated unit tests (checking transition accuracy against ground-truth trajectories), using either sequential conversation or a tree-search strategy with Thompson sampling over multiple CWM candidates. For imperfect-information games, the LLM additionally synthesizes a hidden-history inference function that samples from the posterior over action histories given only one player's observations, enabling ISMCTS planning; in the novel closed-deck setting (no hidden-state access at all), this becomes a regularized autoencoder where the inference function encodes observations into latent histories and the CWM decodes them back, with game rules and the OpenSpiel API acting as structural regularizers. Heuristic value functions are generated one-shot and selected by tournament to improve MCTS leaf-node initialization. At play time, MCTS (perfect information) or ISMCTS (imperfect information) runs 1,000 simulations per move on top of the synthesized CWM, optionally augmented with the learned value function and hidden-state estimator.

## Results

CWM-(IS)MCTS outperforms or matches Gemini 2.5 Pro (used as a direct policy) in 9 out of 10 evaluated games. In all 5 perfect information games, CWM-MCTS achieves a 100% win rate against Gemini 2.5 Pro (e.g., 100/100 wins in Backgammon, Connect Four, and Generalized Chess), while matching GT-MCTS performance (the oracle upper bound using ground-truth game code). CWM synthesis achieves near-perfect transition accuracy (≥0.98 test) for 9 of 10 games, with Gin Rummy as the notable exception (74.6% test transition accuracy, 53.8% inference accuracy). In the closed-deck setting (no hidden state access), performance degrades but CWM-ISMCTS still beats or matches Gemini 2.5 Pro in 4 of 5 imperfect information games.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares CWM-(IS)MCTS against three baselines: (1) Gemini 2.5 Pro used as a direct LLM policy, (2) GT-(IS)MCTS which uses the ground-truth game code (an oracle upper bound), and (3) a Random legal-action executor. Against Gemini 2.5 Pro, CWM-(IS)MCTS wins or matches in 9 of 10 games: it achieves a perfect 100% win rate in all 5 perfect information games (including both OOD games), and wins or ties in 4 of 5 imperfect information games (losing only Hand of War in the open-deck setting, where it scores 0.35/0.33 win rate vs. Gemini's forfeit-heavy play). Against GT-(IS)MCTS, CWM-MCTS is competitive in perfect information games (e.g., 0.08 vs. 0.92 in Backgammon, 0.88 vs. 0.12 in Gen. Tic-tac-toe), confirming the synthesized CWM is a near-perfect replica of the true game. In imperfect information games, GT-ISMCTS generally outperforms CWM-ISMCTS, as expected, but the gap is modest for well-synthesized games (e.g., Leduc Poker: CWM-ISMCTS scores +1.55 payoff vs. GT-ISMCTS's −0.65 as Player 4). The Random baseline is dominated in all 10 games, with CWM-(IS)MCTS winning ≥92% of the time.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Code World Model (CWM) paradigm introduced by WorldCoder (Tang et al., 2024a) and GIF-MCTS (Dainese et al., 2024), which use LLMs to synthesize executable game models from trajectory data and then apply planners for decision-making. The key departures are threefold: (1) handling imperfect information and stochastic games (e.g., Poker) where prior CWM work assumed fully observed, deterministic environments or post-hoc observability (Curtis et al., 2025); (2) synthesizing inference functions that act as regularized autoencoders to estimate hidden states, enabling ISMCTS at play time; and (3) introducing the 'closed deck' setting where no hidden state information is available even post-hoc, a scenario unaddressed in prior CWM literature. The paper also adds value function synthesis to accelerate (IS)MCTS, and positions the LLM as an induction engine (data-to-code translator) rather than as a direct policy, shifting the burden of strategic depth to classical search algorithms.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that delegating the LLM's role to world-model synthesis (rather than move selection) yields verifiable, high-performance play that matches or exceeds a state-of-the-art 'thinking' LLM (Gemini 2.5 Pro) across 9 of 10 games, including novel out-of-distribution games. The closed deck autoencoder formulation is a genuinely new learning paradigm for partially observable environments, though it struggles with procedurally complex games like Gin Rummy (inference accuracy drops to ~5.5%). Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger or open-world games with free-form text/visual interfaces, whether active/online model refinement can close the gap on complex procedural rules, and how sensitive performance is to the underlying LLM's code-generation quality (Gemma 27B fails dramatically where Gemini 2.5 Pro succeeds).
