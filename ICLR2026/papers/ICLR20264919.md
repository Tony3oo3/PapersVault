## Problem

Existing reward model benchmarks like RewardBench rely on prompts recycled from downstream evaluations (risking contamination), use only two completions per prompt (50% random baseline), and show weak correlation with actual downstream performance in RLHF or inference-time scaling. This paper addresses the gap by introducing a more challenging, contamination-free benchmark that better predicts how reward models will perform when actually deployed in post-training pipelines.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20264919/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark presents a reward model with a prompt and four completions (one chosen, three rejected) drawn from a pool of 20 different AI models, and the RM must select the highest-quality response. Scores are computed as per-domain accuracy across six domains (Factuality, Precise Instruction Following, Math, Safety, Focus, Ties) with an unweighted average as the final score; the Ties domain additionally measures whether the reward margin between correct and incorrect answers exceeds the margin among correct answers, capturing calibration. The random baseline is 25% (vs. 50% in best-of-2 benchmarks), and the full benchmark contains 1,865 prompts, roughly 70% of which are previously unseen human queries sourced from WildChat.

## Method

REWARDBENCH 2 is constructed through a four-stage pipeline: (1) sourcing unseen human prompts from WildChat with decontamination against 20 downstream evaluations, (2) domain-specific completion generation using diverse model pools and targeted techniques (e.g., system-prompted factual errors for Factuality, verifier functions for Precise IF, majority voting for Math, CoCoNot taxonomy for Safety, LLMBar-style prompt rewriting for Focus, and manual creation for Ties), (3) multi-layer filtering (dual LM-as-a-judge, rubric-based annotation, manual verification), and (4) best-of-4 assembly. The paper then validates the benchmark through two downstream experiments: best-of-N sampling over 7 evaluations (achieving Pearson correlation of 0.87 with REWARDBENCH 2 scores) and PPO training with 17 reward models, where it identifies a critical on-policy/off-policy factor—reward models from the same model lineage as the policy model perform significantly better in RLHF than higher-scoring off-policy models, a limitation shared by other accuracy-based benchmarks.

## Results

On REWARDBENCH 2, the top existing reward model (Skywork-Reward-V2-Llama-3.1-8B) achieves 84.1% average accuracy across six domains, with the most challenging subsets being Precise Instruction Following (66.3% best) and Math (77.6% best). Leading models score approximately 20 points lower on REWARDBENCH 2 than on RewardBench, confirming the benchmark's increased difficulty. For downstream inference-time scaling, REWARDBENCH 2 scores correlate with Best-of-N sampling performance at a Pearson r of 0.87 across 113 reward models. For PPO training, the benchmark provides a useful signal only for low-scoring RMs (below ~50), while PPO performance saturates near Tulu 3 8B DPO levels (60.3) for all RMs scoring 49.8–68.5, and degrades significantly when the RM's base model lineage differs from the policy model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to RewardBench (Lambert et al., 2024b), REWARDBENCH 2 is substantially harder: the same top models score ~20 points lower (e.g., Skywork-Reward-V2-Llama-3.1-8B scores 84.1 on REWARDBENCH 2 vs. ~100+ on RewardBench), while maintaining a high Pearson correlation of 0.97 between the two benchmarks' scores. Against RMB (Zhou et al., 2024), REWARDBENCH 2 achieves a 0.96 Pearson correlation in scores but differentiates better on downstream BoN performance (r=0.87) due to its use of unseen human prompts and best-of-4 format. Against PPE (Frick et al., 2024), REWARDBENCH 2 correlates at 0.95 with PPE-Correctness and 0.89 with PPE-Human Preference; the lower correlation with the human preference subset is expected since REWARDBENCH 2 uses objective accuracy rather than subjective preference judgments. A critical finding is that for PPO training, all three baselines (RewardBench, RMB, PPE) exhibit the same on/off-policy limitation as REWARDBENCH 2: off-policy RMs (e.g., Llama-based RMs used to train a Tulu policy) score 72.9 on REWARDBENCH 2 but only achieve 54.5 in PPO, versus 59.8–60.7 for on-policy RMs with lower benchmark scores (60–68.7), demonstrating that no existing accuracy-based benchmark captures the lineage-matching requirement for effective RLHF.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the reward model evaluation lineage initiated by RewardBench (Lambert et al., 2024b) and RM-Bench, departing from them in three key ways: using unseen human prompts from WildChat rather than repurposing prompts from downstream evaluations, shifting from a best-of-2 to a best-of-4 format (lowering the random baseline from 50% to 25%), and adding three new domains (Factuality, Precise Instruction Following, and Ties) alongside improved versions of Math, Safety, and Focus. The Safety domain draws on CoCoNot's compliance taxonomy, Precise IF borrows constraints from IFBench, and Focus follows LLMBar's prompt-rewriting methodology. The Bradley-Terry formulation for reward model training is standard, and the paper's empirical analysis builds on prior work connecting RM accuracy to downstream BoN and PPO performance (Frick et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024; Razin et al., 2025).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

REWARDBENCH 2 is likely to become the de facto standard for reward model evaluation, given its 20+ point difficulty gap over RewardBench, its use of uncontaminated human prompts, and its strong (0.87 Pearson) correlation with best-of-N downstream performance. The most impactful finding is that for RLHF/PPO, the on-policy alignment between the RM's base model lineage and the policy model matters more than raw benchmark score—top-scoring RMs from a different lineage can actually degrade PPO performance, a factor the authors claim to identify for the first time. This means practitioners cannot simply select the highest-scoring RM off a leaderboard for RLHF; they must match the RM to their specific training setup. Open questions include whether this on-policy effect generalizes beyond the Tulu 8B SFT policy used here, and how to design benchmarks that capture this context-dependent behavior.
