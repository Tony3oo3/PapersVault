## Problem

Existing LLM inference systems on consumer-grade GPUs, such as PowerInfer, rely on static hot-cold neuron partitioning derived from offline profiling. This one-time assignment fails to capture the strong input-dependent and token-level dynamics of FFN neuron activation (Jaccard distances of 0.53–0.75 between inputs and tokens), causing many active neurons to be computed on the slower CPU while GPU memory is occupied by inactive neurons, resulting in up to 86% of forward-pass time spent on CPU FFN computation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20260234/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system takes a text prompt (typically 64–128 tokens) and a large model (7B–70B parameters) whose full FFN weights reside in CPU pinned memory while a dynamically managed subset of neurons occupies GPU VRAM. The sparse predictor (a lightweight MLP <10% of model size) runs on the GPU, and the KV cache is deliberately stored in CPU memory to free GPU capacity for active neurons. Output is a sequence of generated tokens (64–128 tokens), with intermediate activations kept in FP32 and model weights in FP16 or INT4.

## Method

DynamicInfer introduces a runtime neuron scheduler built on three mechanisms: (1) a hierarchical neural caching strategy that pins high-frequency neurons to GPU (macro-level) while using an MLP-based cross-layer sparsity predictor to forecast next-token activations and migrate specific neurons on demand (micro-level); (2) a load-aware neuron activation mechanism that dynamically adjusts separate sparsity thresholds for GPU and CPU to minimize the max(GPU_time, CPU_time) latency under an accuracy-preservation constraint; and (3) an I/O pipeline that exploits the temporal gap between cross-layer prediction (using hidden state from layer i to predict layer i+k) and execution to overlap CUDA asynchronous neuron transfers with ongoing FFN computation. Neuron placement is formulated as a constrained integer program (memory, communication, and macro-residency constraints) solved via a layer-wise greedy algorithm that sorts neurons by a combined importance score (predicted activation + historical frequency) and iteratively loads/evicts until the PCIe transfer budget is met.

## Results

DynamicInfer reports generation speed (Tokens Per Second, TPS) as its primary metric, evaluated on Alpaca prompts with 64/128-token inputs and 64/128-token outputs. On the high-end PC (RTX 4090, PCIe 4.0), DynamicInfer achieves 18%–26% speedup over PowerInfer and 60%–97% over llama.cpp for ReluLLaMA-13B, and 53%–59% and 148%–253% respectively for ReluLLaMA-70B. On the lower-end PC (RTX 2080 Ti, PCIe 3.0), gains are 20%–35% and 176%–202% over PowerInfer and llama.cpp for 7B models, and 11%–13% and 156%–191% for 13B models. Overall, the paper reports an average speedup of 143.4% over llama.cpp and 25.3% over PowerInfer, with peak gains of 253.3% and 59.4% in specific scenarios.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DynamicInfer outperforms both baselines across all tested configurations. Against llama.cpp (CPU-GPU layered offloading without sparsity), DynamicInfer achieves 60%–253% higher TPS, with the largest gains on the 70B model where the CPU bottleneck is most severe (86% of forward-pass time spent on CPU FFN in the baseline). Against PowerInfer (static hot-cold neuron partitioning + sparse activation), DynamicInfer delivers 11%–59% higher TPS, with the most substantial improvements on the 70B model (53%–59%) where input-dependent neuron dynamics make static partitioning most suboptimal. The ablation (Figure 10) attributes the largest single gain to micro-level (token-level) neuron scheduling (+11.3%/+27.6% for 13B/70B), followed by macro-level caching and dynamic thresholding, which together push the total to +23.1%/+57.1%. On GPU utilization, DynamicInfer raises the proportion of active neurons computed on the GPU from 51% to 68% (PC-High) and reduces the CPU's share of FFN latency from 72% to 57%, directly explaining the speedup. Accuracy on RTE, PIQA, COPA, and Winogrande is preserved within ±1 point of the dense model, confirming that the dynamic sparsity does not degrade quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

DynamicInfer extends PowerInfer's static hot-cold neuron partitioning by replacing the one-time offline assignment with a runtime scheduler that adapts neuron placement to input-dependent activation patterns. It builds on the CPU-GPU hybrid offloading architecture of llama.cpp and the sparse activation prediction framework (MLP-based predictor) introduced in PowerInfer, but departs from both by introducing hierarchical (macro + micro) caching, cross-layer sparsity prediction for prefetching, and load-aware dynamic threshold adjustment. The work also connects to the broader sparse-activation literature (Deja Vu, CoreInfer, SparseInfer) and offloading systems (FlexGen, DeepSpeed-Inference), but uniquely targets the latency-sensitive, small-batch, consumer-GPU regime where PCIe transfer overhead dominates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This paper provides a practical path to running 70B-parameter LLMs on a single 24GB consumer GPU with meaningful throughput, which is directly relevant to privacy-sensitive and cost-sensitive deployment scenarios. The key open question is generalizability: the system assumes a dedicated execution environment (no resource contention), relies on ReLU-based sparsity (with a limited SiLU generalization study), and the 70B model requires int4 quantization. Whether the dynamic scheduling overhead remains negligible under multi-tenant or serverless workloads, and whether the approach scales to MoE architectures with even larger expert pools, remain unexplored.
