## Problem

Existing open set recognition (OSR) methods apply a constant temperature scaling factor to logits throughout training, which prevents the model from exploring both instance-level and class-specific features simultaneously. While temperature scheduling has been studied for self-supervised contrastive learning on long-tailed data, its impact on OSR performance across different loss functions (cross-entropy, contrastive, ARPL) remains largely unexamined, and no schedule has been shown to improve both open-set and closed-set performance without additional computational overhead.

## Input / Output

The method takes standard RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR benchmarks, 64×64 for TinyImageNet, 448×448 for semantic shift benchmarks) and produces either a class label for known classes or an unknown flag for open-set samples, scored via max-logit or other OSR scoring rules. The only modification to the training pipeline is an epoch-dependent temperature value injected into the existing loss function—no architectural changes, auxiliary models, or extra forward/backward passes are required, making it applicable to any backbone (VGG32, ResNet-50, TinyViT) and any temperature-dependent loss.

## Method

The paper proposes the Negative Cosine Schedule (NegCosSch), a temperature schedule that starts training at a low temperature τ⁻ (prioritizing fewer neighbors, creating a sharp decision boundary that keeps unknown samples distant) and gradually increases to a high temperature τ⁺ (prioritizing more neighbors, compacting within-class clusters while preserving the initial separation). This is formalized as a generalized cosine schedule T_GCosSch with k=1, where the temperature follows τ⁻ + ½(τ⁺−τ⁻)(1+cos(2πe/P−π)) over each period P, with the final epochs held at τ⁺ for stable convergence. A monotonic variant (M-NegCosSch) with P=2E removes the need to tune the period. The key insight is that the low-to-high direction is superior to the reverse (standard CosSch) for OSR: the initial sharp boundary establishes core separation of unknowns, and subsequent warming refines class compactness without pulling unknowns back in. The schedule is loss-agnostic—applied identically to cross-entropy, SupCon, ARPL, ProtoCon, and BackMix losses—and requires no additional parameters beyond (τ⁺, τ⁻), which can be derived from a single tuned constant τ via τ⁺=τ+Δ, τ⁻=τ−Δ.

## Results

On the Caltech-UCSD-Birds (CUB) benchmark with CE loss, P-NegCosSch achieves 86.3% closed-set accuracy and 86.85/77.6% open-set AUROC (Easy/Hard), improving over the constant-temperature baseline (84.43% / 83.55/74.98%) by +1.87% accuracy and +3.3%/+2.6% AUROC. On FGVC-Aircraft, the same schedule reaches 91.33% accuracy and 91.41/83.15% AUROC versus 90.88% / 90.35/81.48% for the baseline. The paper reports up to 4.4%/3.96% OSCR gains on the SSBs, with improvements scaling as the number of training classes increases (i.e., the task becomes harder). All gains are achieved with zero additional computational overhead, as the schedule only modifies the epoch-dependent temperature in the loss.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Vaze et al. (2022) CE+LS baseline, P-NegCosSch improves CUB accuracy from 85.53% to 86.12% and AUROC from 85.15/77.44% to 86.43/78.03%, confirming the schedule is orthogonal to label smoothing. On the SupCon loss (Khosla et al., 2020), M-NegCosSch lifts CUB accuracy from 83.43% to 85.3% and Aircraft AUROC from 88.78/81.79% to 90.45/82.49%, while P-NegCosSch is slightly weaker on SupCon for Aircraft (90.61% acc vs. 90.71% baseline), the only clear loss in the paper. For ARPL (Chen et al., 2021), both schedules improve CUB accuracy from 85.8% to ~86.5% and Aircraft AUROC from 90.75/81.77% to 91.55/82.01%. Against BackMix (Wang et al., 2025), P-NegCosSch yields the largest relative gain on CUB, boosting accuracy from 82.12% to 83.98% and OSCR from 67.94/60.32% to 71.23/62.66%. On ProtoCon (Bahavan et al., 2025), NegCosSch improves TinyImageNet accuracy from 85.36% to 85.72% and Aircraft AUROC from 84.32/78.46% to 84.93/79.01%, confirming generality across loss families.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the temperature scheduling framework introduced by Kukleva et al. (2023) for self-supervised contrastive learning on long-tail data to the open-set recognition (OSR) setting, introducing a 'negative cosine' variant (k=1) that inverts the direction of the schedule from high-to-low to low-to-high temperature. It positions itself within the post-Vaze et al. (2022) wave of OSR methods that prioritize better representation learning through improved loss functions (SupCon, ARPL, BackMix), but departs from the dominant trend of adding computational overhead via generative models, mix-up, or secondary VAEs by proposing a zero-cost modification that folds into any existing OSR loss. The conceptual lineage traces from temperature scaling in knowledge distillation and calibration through contrastive learning temperature analysis (Wang & Liu 2021, Zhang et al. 2022) to this paper's application of scheduled temperature as a representation-learning control knob specifically for open-set generalization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in the zero-overhead nature of the technique: a single epoch-dependent scalar in the loss function yields consistent 1–3% improvements across multiple loss functions, architectures (VGG, ResNet, ViT), and benchmarks without additional memory or compute, making it immediately adoptable in existing OSR pipelines. The finding that benefits grow with the number of training classes is particularly relevant for the harder semantic-shift benchmarks where most OSR methods struggle and where computational overhead from mix-up or generative approaches becomes prohibitive. However, the paper acknowledges that gains diminish on smaller benchmarks (CIFAR) with few training classes, and the core mechanism—trading instance-level for class-specific features during training—remains somewhat heuristic in its justification. Open questions include whether the schedule interacts synergistically with emerging OSR methods (e.g., multi-expert or self-learning frameworks) and whether the optimal (τ⁺, τ⁻) range generalizes across domains beyond fine-grained vision.
