## Problem

Extending reinforcement learning to multi-turn, multi-agent LLM training faces two critical challenges: long-horizon credit assignment (attributing sparse terminal rewards to individual actions across many turns) and agent-specific advantage estimation (heterogeneous roles with asymmetric information and different payoff scales destabilize training). While RL has proven effective for single-agent reasoning, its application to multi-agent self-play in strategic games remains underexplored.

## Input / Output

The agent receives a structured text prompt containing game rules, the current game state (board grid, card hand, betting history, token counts), the full action history, and a list of legal actions formatted as tagged tokens (e.g., <X(0,0)>, <BET>, <Reveal player +1 color R>). The model must output a chain-of-thought reasoning trace followed by a single tagged action in a strict <answer>...</answer> format; invalid formatting incurs a large penalty and terminates the game. The trained output is a policy that can be dropped into existing multi-agent frameworks (MAD, AutoGen) as a zero-shot reasoning agent.

## Method

MARSHAL is an end-to-end RL framework built on GRPO that trains LLMs via self-play across cooperative (Mini Hanabi) and competitive (Tic-Tac-Toe, Kuhn Poker) strategic games. Its first contribution is a turn-level advantage estimator that inverts the standard process: it first computes the Monte Carlo cumulative return from each turn onward, then normalizes these returns by their group mean (equivalent to GAE with γ=1, λ=1), rather than normalizing individual turn rewards before summing. Its second contribution is agent-specific advantage normalization, which partitions the batch by player role and computes advantages independently within each sub-group, preventing cross-role baseline contamination. The reward signal combines intrinsic game outcomes (win/loss/chips/score), a format reward for parseable actions, and a linear response-length penalty to encourage conciseness. Training uses Qwen3-4B with fully online self-play rollouts, and the resulting agents generalize to held-out games and to multi-agent reasoning benchmarks (AIME, GPQA-Diamond) when integrated into MAD and AutoGen frameworks.

## Results

The MARSHAL generalist agent (trained on Tic-Tac-Toe, Kuhn Poker, and Mini Hanabi simultaneously) achieves an average of 75.96% in the competitive MAD framework and 82.15% in the cooperative AutoGen framework, outperforming the Qwen3-4B base model (72.45% and 79.14%) by 3.51% and 3.01% respectively. Headline gains include 10.0% on AIME (66.67% vs. 56.67% base) in AutoGen and 7.6% on GPQA-Diamond (45.45% vs. 37.88% base) in MAD. On strategic games, the generalist achieves up to 28.7% normalized return improvement on Leduc Hold'em and 22.9% on Simple Hanabi over the base model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Qwen3-4B base model, the MARSHAL generalist consistently wins across all three evaluation settings (single-agent, MAD, AutoGen), with the largest gains on hard math benchmarks (AIME +10.0% in AutoGen, AMC +5.17% in MAD). Against SPIRAL (the primary self-play baseline focused on zero-sum games), MARSHAL generalist outperforms by 2.55% average in MAD and 2.10% in AutoGen, with particularly strong margins on AIME (+6.67% in MAD, +6.67% in AutoGen) and GPQA-Diamond (+5.05% in MAD). Against the concurrent MT-GRPO method (which uses turn-by-turn reward normalization), MARSHAL's 'sum-then-normalize' approach yields higher averages in both MAD (75.01% vs. 73.77%) and AutoGen (80.15% vs. 79.10%), with the gap widening on long-horizon cooperative tasks like Mini Hanabi (50.48% vs. 36.67% normalized return). Notably, SPIRAL's competitive-only training gives it an edge on single-agent MMLU (80.00% vs. 75.00%), but MARSHAL's inclusion of cooperative games (Hanabi) is critical for AutoGen performance, where the competitive-only MARSHAL variant drops to 80.34% average.

## Relation to Prior Work

MARSHAL extends GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) from single-turn, single-agent RL to multi-turn, multi-agent self-play, introducing a turn-level 'sum-then-normalize' advantage estimator and agent-specific normalization to handle long-horizon credit assignment and heterogeneous role payoffs. It departs from concurrent self-play-for-reasoning work (SPIRAL, SPAG) by incorporating cooperative games alongside competitive ones and by evaluating generalization within multi-agent systems (MAD, AutoGen) rather than single-agent benchmarks. Compared to MT-GRPO (Zeng et al., 2025), which normalizes turn-level rewards turn-by-turn, MARSHAL's cumulative-return normalization avoids the variable-length instability and preserves long-term dependencies, a distinction validated empirically in Appendix G.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, reproducible recipe for training LLM agents that generalize from strategic games to practical multi-agent reasoning, with the notable finding that cooperative and competitive skills transfer selectively to their respective MAS frameworks. Key limitations include the restriction to two-player games and 4B/8B parameter scales, leaving open whether the approach scales to N-player non-stationary environments or complex social sandboxes. The work also raises the question of whether the observed game-to-reasoning transfer reflects genuine multi-agent cognitive skills or merely improved instruction-following and role-awareness, a distinction the failure-mode analysis partially addresses but does not fully resolve.
