## Problem

Current evaluations of LLM-powered agents focus almost exclusively on task completion (did the agent click the right button?), leaving unexplored how these agents actually *choose* when faced with realistic decision architectures involving prices, ratings, ordering, and persuasive cues. As agents are increasingly delegated consequential decisions (purchases, travel, medical selection), understanding their susceptibility to the same nudges and biases that shape human behavior is critical for trust, yet no systematic framework existed to probe agentic choice under controlled manipulations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20262147/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes pruned, viewport-only HTML from the OneStopMarket shopping environment (no visual input; agents must scroll to see more) and applies intervention functions—inserting nudge text, modifying prices, or matching ratings—via a man-in-the-middle proxy before the agent observes the page. Each trial presents a binary forced choice between two products in separate tabs, with a natural-language task intent instructing the agent to add the 'best' product to the cart. The agent outputs a sequence of up to 10 actions (clicks, scrolls, tab switches) culminating in a single binary choice, along with chain-of-thought reasoning and short-term memory at each step; the downstream analysis converts these choices into marginal effect estimates (percentage points) from linear probability models.

## Method

ABXLAB is an open-source man-in-the-middle framework that intercepts real web content and applies configurable intervention functions (authority, social proof, scarcity, negative framing, incentive nudges, plus price/rating matching) to construct controlled choice architectures without building custom environments. Product pairs are constructed under validity constraints (same category, bounded rating and price gaps), with two regimes: original (Δr=10pp, Δp=50%) and matched (Δr=0, Δp=50%, with post-hoc price equalization via intervention). The experimental design is a full factorial of 10 interventions × 50 product pairs × 3 conditions (no nudge, 1st nudged, 2nd nudged), yielding 1,500 base configurations run across 17 state-of-the-art models (80,000+ trials, ~2.5B tokens). Causal inference uses linear probability models with trial fixed effects and two-way cluster-robust standard errors (by nudge text and category), with Benjamini-Hochberg multiple-testing correction; a multinomial logit robustness check confirms the linear specification (r≈0.93 correlation in marginal effects). A human baseline (30 Prolific participants, 50 decisions each) provides commensurable comparison, revealing agent sensitivities 3–10× larger than human responses to the same cues.

## Results

Across 17 LLM agents and 80,000+ trials, agents exhibit 3–10× greater susceptibility to choice-architecture cues than humans. In the Original condition, higher-rated products increase selection by 30–80 pp (14/17 models), cheaper products by 15–34 pp (13/17 models), and nudges by 10–60 pp when ratings and prices are matched. The human baseline shows only 4 pp (order), 5 pp (rating), 9.4 pp (price), and 9.9 pp (nudge) effects, all modest by comparison. Extreme cases include o4 Mini at +81.2 pp for higher ratings and Llama 4 Maverick at +93.2 pp for cheaper options under matched ratings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is against a human baseline (30 Prolific participants, 50 decisions each on the same 1,500 trials). Humans show uniformly modest and mostly non-significant responses: 4 pp for order (n.s.), 5 pp for rating (n.s.), 9.4 pp for price (n.s.), and 9.9 pp for nudges (p<.05). Every LLM agent substantially exceeds these human magnitudes, with the unweighted average attribute sensitivity for humans (~7%) roughly half that of the least-sensitive model (Claude 3.5 Haiku, ~13%) and less than a quarter of the most-sensitive (Claude Sonnet 4, ~31%). The Wirecutter authority nudge drives most of the human–agent gap, as it is the single most effective nudge for both groups but produces effects 5–10× larger in agents. No agent is evaluated on task-completion accuracy against a baseline; the comparison is purely on the magnitude and direction of behavioral biases under identical choice architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the WebArena/AgentLab infrastructure for web agent evaluation but fundamentally reorients the evaluation question from task completion to decision quality under controlled choice-architecture manipulations. Conceptually, it transplants the behavioral economics tradition (Kahneman & Tversky's heuristics-and-biases program, Thaler & Sunstein's nudge theory) into the AI agent setting, extending the authors' earlier finding that LLMs are hypersensitive to nudges in a minimal controlled environment (Cherep et al., 2024; 2025) to realistic, high-dimensional web shopping. The man-in-the-middle intervention engine that rewrites live web content before the agent observes it is a methodological departure from prior benchmarks, which either use static environments or measure only whether the correct final action was taken.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that agents are 3–10× more susceptible to superficial cues than humans challenges the implicit assumption that removing cognitive constraints makes agents more robust decision-makers, and raises concrete deployment risks in consumer, medical, and financial contexts. The open-source ABXLAB framework provides a scalable, extensible testbed for a behavioral science of AI agents, but the current scope is limited to binary forced choices in one shopping domain with text-only observations; generalization to larger choice sets, multimodal cues, and other decision domains remains open. A key unresolved question is whether the observed near-deterministic, rule-like decision heuristics (rating → price → nudge hierarchy) are artifacts of training data or reflect a deeper structural property of LLM-based agents.
