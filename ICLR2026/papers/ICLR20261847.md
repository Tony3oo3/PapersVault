## Problem

Existing first-principles theories of feature learning in the rich regime reduce to high-dimensional non-linear equations requiring computationally intensive numerical solutions, making it difficult to extract intuitive scaling laws or compare results across architectures. The paper addresses this 'curse of details' by asking whether one can predict sample complexity exponents and dominant FL patterns using simple pen-and-paper scaling arguments rather than exact solutions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20261847/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes a network architecture specification (FCN, CNN, or attention head), a target function (e.g., a Hermite polynomial or cubic), and architectural parameters (layer widths N_l, input dimension d, depth L, ridge parameter κ, and parametrization choice such as standard vs. mean-field scaling). It produces closed-form scaling exponents for the threshold sample size P*, a prediction of which discrete FL pattern (GP, Gaussian FL, specialization, or magnetization) dominates in each layer, and the scaling of the number of specializing neurons with width. Notably, no numerical ODE/PDE solving is required; all predictions follow from algebraic minimization of a variational energy over a small set of pattern parameters.

## Method

The paper formulates FL within a Bayesian NN framework where successful learning corresponds to a posterior alignment A_f ≈ 1, and derives a large-deviation-theory bound showing that the minimal sample size P* is proportional to the negative log-prior probability of achieving that alignment (the 'energy' E(α)). To make E(α) tractable, the authors apply the Feynman-Bogoliubov variational inequality, replacing the intractable pre-activation measure with a decoupled Gaussian ansatz whose parameters are determined by minimizing a variational energy composed of per-layer kernel-mismatch terms (Δ_l) and a target-alignment term (a_y). The key heuristic step is restricting the variational candidates to a small set of discrete FL patterns—GP (lazy), Gaussian FL (covariance amplification along a feature), specialization (non-zero mean on a feature), and magnetization (small uniform mean)—and then applying layer-wise propagation rules (e.g., specialization creates a rank-1 spectral spike whose RKHS norm scales as N_l/M; amplified features propagate as D^m in the m-th order) to compute the energy for each pattern combination. The dominant FL mechanism is the pattern minimizing the variational energy, and its free parameters (e.g., number of specializing neurons M) are fixed by further minimization, yielding explicit scaling laws such as P* ∝ d for three-layer FCNs or P* ∝ √(Ld³) for attention heads.

## Results

For a three-layer FCN trained on a Hermite-3 target, the framework predicts P* ∝ d in the proportional limit (N₁ ∝ N₂ ∝ d), with the optimal FL pattern being Specialization-Magnetization (Sp.-Mag.) yielding variational energy (N₁N₂d)^(1/3), compared to d³ for the lazy GP-GP pattern. For softmax attention heads learning a cubic target, the predicted sample complexity is P* ∝ √(Ld³), validated by alignment collapsing onto a single curve when plotted against P/√(Ld³). For two-layer FCNs, P* = d is predicted and confirmed both by exact Large Deviation Theory and experiment, with the number of specializing neurons scaling as √(N/d). The framework also reproduces the previously known CNN result P* = d^(3/4) from Ringel et al. (2025).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against competing methods in the traditional sense; instead, it compares different feature-learning (FL) patterns as variational candidates and validates predictions against exact theory and experiment. The GP-GP (lazy) pattern, which serves as the baseline, is unfavorable for all but extremely large d (specifically d > N₂⁵), confirming that FL is necessary for efficient learning. The GP-Specialization pattern is intermediate, while the Specialization-Magnetization pattern is optimal in the proportional limit, reducing sample complexity from d³ to d. For the two-layer case, the heuristic prediction matches the exact Large Deviation Theory result (Fig. 2a) and experimental alignment curves (Fig. 2b) quantitatively. The attention head prediction (P* ∝ √(Ld³)) is validated by showing alignment collapses onto a single curve when rescaled by the predicted P*, matching the L-dependence observed experimentally (Fig. 3b). The framework also qualitatively captures the transition in FL patterns as N₁ increases: first-layer specialization gives way to second-layer specialization, consistent with experimental observations (Fig. 3c).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the statistical-mechanics and kernel-methods lineage for analyzing feature learning in Bayesian neural networks (Li & Sompolinsky's kernel renormalization, Rubin et al.'s multi-scale adaptive theory, Saad-Solla-type approaches), but departs from them by replacing the intractable high-dimensional non-linear self-consistent equations with a variational heuristic grounded in large deviation theory and the Feynman-Bogoliubov inequality. Rather than solving for the exact posterior, the authors introduce a small set of 'feature learning patterns' (GP, Gaussian FL, specialization) as trial variational distributions and select the minimizer of an explicit energy functional. This positions the work as a scaling-exponent extraction tool that is considerably simpler than prior exact theories while still reproducing their predictions, and extends the scope to architectures (three-layer non-linear networks, softmax attention heads) where those exact theories become computationally prohibitive.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a unified, pen-and-paper route to predicting both the sample-complexity exponent and the emergent FL pattern across diverse architectures, which prior exact theories could not achieve in a single pass. Its predictions for three-layer networks and attention heads go beyond the current analytical state of the art, and the identification of FL pattern transitions as a function of width (e.g., specialization-magnetization → GP-specialization as N₁ grows) provides a concrete, testable mechanism for how feature learning reorganizes. Key limitations include the heuristic (non-rigorous) nature of the variational ansatz, the restriction to equilibrium Bayesian settings (no learning dynamics), the inability to handle overfitting-dominated regimes where the bound becomes vacuous, and the lack of treatment for multi-feature interactions relevant to superposition. Extending the propagation rules to general CNNs, transformers, and non-Gaussian data measures remains open.
