## Problem

Traditional MIP solvers like SCIP generate over 100,000 candidate cuts per problem, consuming gigabytes of memory while only 1–2% of cuts meaningfully tighten the LP relaxation. Existing ML-based cut selection methods treat the solver as a black box, optimize solve time while ignoring memory overhead, and are restricted to myopic decisions without a model of how cuts affect subsequent LP states. This paper asks whether intelligent cut selection can be learned that treats memory efficiency as a first-class objective while maintaining competitive solution quality.

## Input / Output

At each branch-and-cut iteration, the agent receives a 10-dimensional tabular state vector (fractionality values of the top fractional variables, LP objective, count of fractional variables) concatenated with per-cut features (non-zero count, LHS/RHS, locality flags) for the candidate cut pool C_k emitted by SCIP. The output is a binary selection vector a_k over |C_k| candidate cuts subject to a hard budget constraint Σa_k^(i) ≤ B (typically 10–50), which is applied back into the LP relaxation. The policy is a lightweight ~17K-parameter MLP, and the full solving loop continues until an integer-optimal solution or the 600-second time limit is reached.

## Method

MIRACLE trains a PPO actor-critic policy (~17K params) to select a minimal high-impact cut subset, using dense per-step rewards derived from a GAIL discriminator (~100K params) that distinguishes expert SCIP trajectories from agent-generated ones (r = −log(1−D_ψ(s,a))). Training follows a three-phase pipeline: (1) offline collection of SCIP expert state-action pairs, (2) adversarial pre-training where the discriminator and policy co-adapt, and (3) PPO refinement under a four-stage curriculum (200→2000 constraints, then mixed) with the discriminator frozen. At inference, a lightweight difficulty classifier (EASY/MEDIUM/HARD) adaptively sets the cut budget B, max iterations, and early-stopping threshold, eliminating per-instance manual tuning. The key design choice is to imitate SCIP's implicit selection policy rather than model the non-differentiable LP transition dynamics, combined with a budget constraint that enforces memory efficiency directly in the action space.

## Results

Across 300 instances (150 SetCover + 150 MIPLIB), MIRACLE achieves an average 86.3% memory reduction (from 2,073.9 MB to 284.7 MB), with peak reductions of 98.1–98.5% on SetCover (46 MB vs. 1.97–3.03 GB for SCIP). On the MIPLIB Large benchmark, MIRACLE achieves a 100% success rate (target gap ≤ 0.1%) versus 53.3% for SCIP-Baseline and 46.7% for SCIP-Aggressive, while delivering a 4.59× median speedup (125.7 s vs. 577.5 s). The average speedup across all MIPLIB instances is 3.78×, with median speedups ranging from 2.50× to 4.79× across difficulty tiers.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SCIP-Baseline (default cut selection), MIRACLE wins decisively on all three metrics: memory (98.1% reduction on SetCover-Hard, 68.1% on MIPLIB-Large), reliability (100% vs. 53.3% success rate on MIPLIB-Large), and speed (4.59× vs. 1.00× median speedup). Against SCIP-Aggressive (maxrounds=5, maxcuts=5000), MIRACLE is even more dominant: 100% vs. 46.7% success rate and 4.59× vs. 0.96× speedup, as the aggressive configuration actually degrades performance by consuming more memory and time. Against the learned hierarchical sequence model of Wang et al. (2024), MIRACLE achieves approximately a 10× reduction in peak memory (~46 MB vs. ~500 MB on 20 SetCover-Easy instances), confirming that treating memory as a first-class objective yields an order-of-magnitude advantage over prior learned cut-selection methods that optimize only solve time.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the PPO policy optimization framework (Schulman et al., 2017) and GAIL adversarial reward learning (Ho & Ermon, 2016), applying them to the cut-selection problem in MIP solvers where SCIP serves as both the expert policy and the environment. It departs from prior cut-selection work (Tang et al., 2020; Paulus et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024) by explicitly treating memory consumption as a first-class optimization objective rather than an afterthought, and by using a learned dense reward signal from a GAIL discriminator instead of handcrafted or immediate-reward signals. The combination of GAIL-derived rewards, PPO with curriculum learning, and adaptive inference at deployment represents a new architectural pipeline, though each component is individually well-established.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that memory bloat (not algorithmic weakness) is the primary cause of solver failures on hard instances—evidenced by 100% success where SCIP fails 47–53%—reframes how the community thinks about MIP solver reliability and opens the door to deploying optimization on edge devices and cost-constrained cloud environments. The theoretical guarantees (sample complexity, PPO convergence, memory reduction bounds) add rigor that prior empirical cut-selection papers lack. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to problems with vastly different cut structures (e.g., non-linear or stochastic MIPs), whether the 10-dimensional state representation is sufficient for industrial-scale instances, and whether the memory-first objective can be further integrated with branching and node-selection policies for end-to-end solver optimization.
