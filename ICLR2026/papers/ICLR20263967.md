## Problem

Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) generate excessively long chain-of-thought outputs that increase computational costs and cause overthinking on simple problems, yet existing solutions either compromise reasoning quality (inference-time pruning) or demand expensive online RL with large-scale data. The paper asks whether shorter yet effective reasoning paths exist within the model's own generation space, and how to shift the output distribution toward them using only limited offline tuning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20263967/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt (e.g., a math problem) and produces a concise chain-of-thought reasoning trace followed by a final answer. Training data is self-generated via a single rollout pass: 16 outputs per question are sampled, filtered to the 'easy' split (pass rate = 1), and paired by length (shortest as chosen, longest as rejected), yielding only ~0.8k training pairs from 400 questions. The trained model outputs the same format as the original LRM but with 50–70% fewer tokens on average.

## Method

LCPO (Length Controlled Preference Optimization) is a new preference optimization objective derived under a unified Bradley-Terry loss framework. The key theoretical insight is that the implicit NLL reward in existing methods (SFT, ORPO, SimPER) hinders convergence on length preferences, so LCPO explicitly counterbalances this NLL term with a negative counterpart of identical form, yielding a pure BT loss with no separate NLL component and no hyperparameters to tune. The training pipeline is a one-shot offline self-distillation: the model generates 16 trajectories per question, questions are filtered by pass-rate-based difficulty, and the shortest/longest pair is used as the preference signal. This requires only 0.8k training samples and 50 steps (~10 A100 GPU-hours for 7B), reducing average output length by over 50% across benchmarks while maintaining or slightly improving accuracy.

## Results

On the 7B model (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B), LCPO achieves a total accuracy change of only −1.99 points across six math benchmarks while reducing average generation length by 54.21%. On the 1.5B model, the method actually improves total accuracy by +0.52 points while cutting length by 57.31%. The headline result is that LCPO reduces output length by over 50% on every benchmark (ranging from 46% on AIME24 to 65% on Minerva-Math) with minimal or no accuracy degradation, requiring only 0.8k training samples and 50 training steps.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LCPO decisively outperforms all baselines on the accuracy-vs-length trade-off. Compared to inference-time CoD, LCPO achieves 54% length reduction vs. only 37% while suffering far less accuracy loss (−1.99 vs. −11.76 total). Against RL-based methods L1-Exact and L1-Max, LCPO avoids catastrophic accuracy collapses on hard benchmarks (L1-Exact drops 30.42 points on AIME24; LCPO drops only 2.71) and matches or exceeds their length reduction. TrEff (online RL) achieves 42% length reduction but with −11.58 total accuracy loss, whereas LCPO achieves 54% reduction with only −1.99. DAST, the closest preference-optimization baseline, actually *increases* average length by 11.38% on the 7B model while LCPO reduces it by 54.21%. In the preference-optimization ablation (Table 2), LCPO reduces MATH-500 length by 51.86% vs. DPO's 33.15%, ORPO's 25.72%, SimPO's 11.20%, and SimPER's 9.26%, while maintaining or improving accuracy. Against Hybrid-CoT methods AdaptThink and AutoThink (Table 14), LCPO achieves comparable length reduction (54% vs. 43–47%) but with dramatically smaller accuracy drops (−1.99 vs. −2.71 and −13.78 total for 7B) at a fraction of the computational cost (~10.4 A100·h vs. ~560–5760 A100·h).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Bradley-Terry preference optimization framework introduced by DPO and the reference-free BT loss component of ORPO, but departs from both by identifying that the implicit NLL reward inherent in these objectives actively hinders convergence for length-control preferences. It positions itself against the dominant online RL paradigm (L1, TrEff, AdaptThink, AutoThink) that requires large-scale data (24k–645k samples) and complex training infrastructure, arguing instead that a carefully designed offline preference objective with only 0.8k samples and 50 training steps can achieve comparable or better length reduction. The unified convergence analysis under the BT framework (Appendix D) is the paper's key analytical contribution, showing why SFT, ORPO, and SimPER each fail at length alignment for distinct theoretical reasons.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: reducing average CoT length by over 50% while preserving (or slightly improving) accuracy directly lowers inference cost for LRMs, and the ~10 GPU-hour training budget makes this accessible to small labs. The theoretical diagnosis—that NLL loss creates a convergence barrier specifically for length preferences, and that a pure BT objective without the NLL term is the theoretically optimal form—provides a reusable lens for designing future preference objectives. Key limitations include evaluation only on 1.5B and 7B distilled models, math-only training data, and the open question of whether the approach transfers to larger frontier models or non-mathematical reasoning domains where 'concise' reasoning patterns may differ substantially.
