## Problem

Existing tactile datasets and representation models are largely confined to static object-level attributes (material, hardness) collected via press-only or random actions, overlooking the fine-grained temporal dynamics and force-related physical principles essential for contact-rich manipulation. No systematic framework exists to organize the hierarchy of dynamic perception capabilities, and high-tier data (structured actions, real manipulation, paired force) remains critically scarce.

## Input / Output

The model consumes N=4 background-subtracted optical tactile frames (H×W×3) from heterogeneous sensors (GelSight, DIGIT, GelSight Mini, GelSlim, DuraGel), partitioned into 3D spatio-temporal tokens of size s×h×w with a 75% tube-masking ratio. During pre-training it simultaneously produces reconstructed frames, frame-difference reconstructions, 3D contact force vectors (N-1)×3, and delta-force increments; at inference it outputs a single CLS-token tactile representation embedding that supports downstream tasks including force prediction, slip detection, material recognition, and manipulation policy conditioning.

## Method

AnyTouch 2 builds on an OpenCLIP-Base encoder with a 6-layer ViT decoder and organizes its multi-task objectives along a five-tier 'tactile dynamic pyramid.' At the pixel level, a VideoMAE-style tube-masked autoencoder jointly reconstructs original frames and frame differences (D_n = T_n − T_1) via two decoders, explicitly amplifying sensitivity to subtle inter-frame deformations. At the semantic level, CLIP-style tactile-visual and tactile-language contrastive alignment is combined with cross-sensor matching (same object, different sensor) and a novel action-matching objective that clusters representations of 8 atomic actions (press, leave, 4-direction slide, 2-direction rotate) while separating different actions. At the physical level, dedicated decoders predict per-frame 3D contact force and its temporal increment (ΔF) from large-scale touch-force pairs, grounding the representation in physical dynamics. A curriculum scheduling strategy introduces higher-tier tasks (matching at epoch 20, alignment at epoch 30) with linearly ramping weights to prevent task interference, and the framework is trained on the newly introduced ToucHD dataset (2.4M samples) spanning simulated atomic actions, real-world manipulation via modified FastUMI, and 71-indenter force pairs.

## Results

AnyTouch 2 achieves the best performance across all dynamic perception benchmarks: on Sparsh Bench it reaches 86.66 F1 / 87.80 RMSE (DIGIT) and 97.96 F1 / 80.83 RMSE (Mini) for slip delta-force prediction, and 624.26 / 202.14 mN RMSE for force prediction (DIGIT / Mini). On the more challenging ToucHD Bench (10 unseen indenters), it achieves 894.32 / 1051.03 mN RMSE (DIGIT / Mini), roughly 2× better than the next-best baseline MAE(Sparsh)† (1714.84 / 2467.42). On static Object Bench, AnyTouch 2 scores 76.97% TAG accuracy and 42.31% Cloth accuracy, comparable to AnyTouch 1 (80.82% TAG) while substantially outperforming all other methods on Cloth. In real-world manipulation, AnyTouch 2 achieves the highest success rate across all four tasks spanning Tiers 1–5 of the tactile dynamic pyramid.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AnyTouch 2 decisively outperforms all five baselines on every dynamic perception metric. Against the strongest dynamic baseline MAE(Sparsh)† (which shares the same ToucHD training data), AnyTouch 2 reduces force prediction RMSE by ~16% on DIGIT (624 vs. 742 mN) and ~16% on Mini (202 vs. 240 mN), and nearly halves the ToucHD Bench RMSE (894 vs. 1715 mN on DIGIT; 1051 vs. 2467 mN on Mini), demonstrating that the multi-level dynamic modules (frame-difference reconstruction, action matching, force prediction) contribute substantially beyond data alone. Against AnyTouch 1 (the authors' prior work), AnyTouch 2 trades a small drop in TAG accuracy (76.97 vs. 80.82) for dramatic gains on all dynamic tasks: slip F1 improves from 40.60 to 86.66 (DIGIT) and force RMSE drops from 1235 to 624 mN (DIGIT), confirming that the added dynamic modules are the key differentiator. Single-frame baselines (CLIP, UniTouch, T3) perform worst on dynamic tasks—CLIP achieves only 33.13 F1 on slip detection and 1278 mN force RMSE—because they lack temporal position embeddings and cannot capture frame ordering. VJEPA(Sparsh) and MAE(Sparsh) without ToucHD data show moderate dynamic performance but fall well behind on force prediction (958 / 821 mN vs. 624 mN for AnyTouch 2 on DIGIT), highlighting the importance of both the ToucHD dataset and the force-aware training objectives.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends AnyTouch 1 (Feng et al., 2025b) by adding three multi-level dynamic enhanced modules—frame-difference reconstruction, action matching, and force prediction—aligned with a newly proposed 5-tier Tactile Dynamic Pyramid that organizes tactile data by the complexity of dynamic perception capabilities they support. The pixel-level reconstruction builds on VideoMAE (Tong et al., 2022) with tube masking adapted to tactile video, while the semantic-level alignment follows the CLIP paradigm (Radford et al., 2021) as used in prior tactile representation work. The key departure from prior tactile pre-training (Sparsh, UniTouch, T3) is the explicit hierarchical structuring of both data and model objectives: most existing datasets and models operate at Tiers 4–5 (press-only or random actions), whereas this work introduces ToucHD to fill Tiers 1–3 (force-paired, manipulation, and specific-action data) and designs corresponding training objectives for each tier.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Tactile Dynamic Pyramid provides a principled, reusable framework for diagnosing gaps in tactile data and model capabilities, and ToucHD (2.4M samples across 5 sensors and 3 tiers) is likely to become a standard pre-training resource for the optical tactile sensing community. The ablation revealing a trade-off between static semantic alignment and fine-grained dynamic perception is a useful practical insight for future multi-objective tactile training. Open questions include whether the hierarchy generalizes to non-optical or non-planar sensors, whether the curriculum scheduling is optimal versus joint training, and whether force prediction from indenter-based data transfers to full-object manipulation without additional force labels.
