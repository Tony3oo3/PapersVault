## Problem

Existing fake image detectors fail to generalize to previously unseen generative models, largely because prior training datasets, despite containing millions of images, draw from fewer than 20 generators each. This limited generator diversity means detectors miss the wide range of architectural, processing, and content variations encountered in the wild, making it difficult to both train robust detectors and fairly benchmark their generalization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252435/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The detector consumes RGB images at 224×224 or 384×384 resolution (the latter denoted 'High res.') and outputs a single probability score via a sigmoid-activated linear head indicating the likelihood the image is AI-generated. Generated images are stored in PNG format to avoid introducing compression artifacts that could confound detection, except for Firefly outputs which are JPEG-only due to the web UI. The evaluation protocol reports threshold-independent mAP and accuracy, both averaged per generative model to ensure fair comparison across models with different image counts.

## Method

The core contribution is the Community Forensics dataset (4803 models, 2.7M generated images) built by systematically downloading and sampling from thousands of open-source latent diffusion models on Hugging Face, hand-selecting 19 diverse-architecture models (GANs, pixel diffusion, autoregressive, consistency models), and sampling 11 commercial models (DALL-E, Midjourney, FLUX, Imagen 3, etc.). Classifiers use CLIP-ViT-S or ConvNeXt-S pretrained backbones trained end-to-end (not frozen, contrary to prior practice) with a modified RandAugment scheme that applies randomly-ordered sequences of JPEG compression, resizing, cropping, rotation, shear, padding, and cutout. Key empirical findings: detection performance improves monotonically with the number of training models (up to ~1000 before plateauing), including for out-of-distribution architectures like GANs; mixing diverse generator architectures further boosts performance; and strict semantic alignment between real and generated training data is less critical than previously assumed when data scale is sufficient.

## Results

On the paper's own out-of-distribution comprehensive evaluation set, the best model (High res., 384² input) achieves 0.987 mAP and 89.2% accuracy, substantially outperforming all prior detectors. Across all five evaluation benchmarks (Wang et al., Ojha et al., Synthbuster, GenImage, and Ours), the High res. model attains a mean mAP of 0.986 and mean accuracy of 92.3%, compared to 0.648/55.2% for Wang et al., 0.760/64.6% for Ojha et al., 0.934/86.2% for GenImage, and 0.862/63.9% for RED140. The 224² model still achieves 0.966 mean mAP and 88.9% mean accuracy, confirming that the gains are not solely due to higher resolution.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed Community Forensics-trained classifier outperforms all four prior detector baselines on every out-of-distribution evaluation set. Against Wang et al. [125] (mean mAP 0.648) and Ojha et al. [87] (mean mAP 0.760), the improvement is dramatic, with the High res. model reaching 0.986 mean mAP — a gap of over 20 and 22 percentage points respectively. Against GenImage [133] (mean mAP 0.934), the paper's method wins on all out-of-distribution sets (e.g., 0.974 vs. 0.813 on Synthbuster, 0.987 vs. 0.912 on the authors' comprehensive set) but slightly trails on GenImage's own in-distribution validation split (0.998 vs. 0.999 mAP), which is expected since those generators were in GenImage's training set. RED140 [43] (mean mAP 0.862) is outperformed by a clear margin on all OOD benchmarks. The three training-free forensics methods evaluated in Figure 3 — Aeroblade [99], LGrad [116], and NPR [117] — show substantially weaker and less consistent performance across generator types (latent diffusion, pixel diffusion, GANs, commercial, other) compared to the learned classifiers, confirming that data-driven scaling over diverse generators is more effective than hand-crafted artifact detection.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the AI-generated image detection line of work (Wang et al. 2020, Ojha et al. 2023, Cozzolino et al. 2024, GenImage 2023, RED116/RED140) by arguing that the number of distinct generators in training data is the critical axis of diversity for generalization, rather than image count or architectural variety alone. Prior datasets contained fewer than 20 identifiable models (at most 140 in RED140), while Community Forensics scales to 4803 models, primarily by systematically harvesting open-source latent diffusion models from Hugging Face. The paper also departs from prior practice by showing that end-to-end training of the backbone outperforms the frozen-backbone approach recommended by Ojha et al. and Cozzolino et al., and that strict semantic alignment between real and generated images is less critical than previously assumed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Community Forensics is likely to become a standard benchmark for AI-generated image detection given its scale (2.7M images, 4803 models) and the fact that it covers a much wider range of generators than any prior dataset. The finding that training on more same-architecture models (latent diffusion) still improves detection of out-of-distribution architectures (GANs, pixel diffusion) suggests that model-specific idiosyncrasies in image processing and content matter more than architectural differences, which reframes how the field should think about data collection. However, the authors explicitly caution that error rates remain too high for deployment in critical applications, and the dataset is heavily skewed toward Stable Diffusion variants, leaving open whether the conclusions hold for truly novel future architectures.
