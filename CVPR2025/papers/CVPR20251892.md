## Problem

Open-source MLLMs inherit safety alignment only from their language module, yet multi-modal instruction tuning introduces a compliance bias that makes them vulnerable to typographic jailbreaks. While prior work (e.g., VLGuard) relies on labor-intensive curated safety datasets with harmful images and detailed rejection reasons, it remains unclear which dataset attributes actually drive the safety improvement. This paper asks whether the alignment gap stems from missing safety knowledge or simply from a data distribution imbalance during instruction tuning.

## Input / Output

The finetuning procedure takes benign image-text conversation pairs from the LLaVA-Med dataset (medical domain, safety-unrelated) and replaces the assistant's reply with a fixed short rejection string ('I'm sorry, but I cannot assist with your request'), excluding periods and <eos> tokens. At inference time, the finetuned MLLM still accepts standard image + text prompt input and produces a text response, but now rejects a calibrated fraction of prompts. The loss is computed only over the few rejection tokens (not the full response), and the end-of-sentence token is masked to prevent overfitting to the rejection pattern.

## Method

The paper first ablates the VLGuard safety dataset, showing that multi-turn benign/harmful comparisons, content-related harmful images, and detailed legal rejection reasons contribute negligibly to defense capability; instead, the rejection proportion in the training set is the dominant factor. Building on this, the authors propose a minimal safety finetuning recipe: randomly sample 2000 benign LLaVA-Med conversations, replace one round's answer with a fixed rejection sentence, and mix these with 5000 ordinary LLaVA-v1.5-mix665k samples (yielding ~28.57% rejection rate), then apply LoRA finetuning with loss computed only on the rejection tokens. This achieves near-VLGuard-level jailbreak defense (ASR < 6% on MM-SafetyBench, < 3.2% on FigStep) without any safety-related images, harmful prompts, or curated rejection reasons. Ablations on rejection proportion (5%–50%) and a full-parameter instruction-tuning experiment (2% and 5% rejection data mixed into 665K samples) confirm that a sufficiently high rejection fraction is necessary to counteract the compliance bias, and that post-hoc finetuning on a small dataset is far more effective than diluting rejection data into the full instruction-tuning corpus.

## Results

The proposed method (benign LLaVA-Med data with rejection responses) reduces MM-SafetyBench ASR from 96.37% to 5.60% and FigStep ASR from 100% to 0.20% on LLaVA-v1.5-7B, while maintaining 90.40% XSTest compliance (vs. 77.20% for VLGuard). On LLaVA-v1.5-13B, the method achieves 1.79% MM-SafetyBench ASR and 0% FigStep ASR with 90.40% compliance and 90.50% rejection. Visual understanding performance (VizWizQA, ScienceQA) remains largely intact, with LLaVA-v1.5-7B scoring 55.92% on VizWizQA and 69.11% on ScienceQA Total, close to the original model's 55.10% and 69.46%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is VLGuard [Zong et al., ICML 2024], which uses a curated safety dataset with harmful images, toxic prompts, and detailed rejection reasons. VLGuard achieves near-zero ASR (0.18% on MM-SafetyBench, 0% on FigStep) and very high XSTest rejection rates (96.5–99.5%), but at the cost of significant over-rejection: XSTest compliance drops to 77.20% (LLaVA-v1.5-7B) and 43.20% (LLaVA-NeXT Llama-3-8B in Table 1). The proposed method trades a small amount of safety (5.60% vs. 0.18% MM-SafetyBench ASR on LLaVA-v1.5-7B) for substantially better compliance (90.40% vs. 77.20%), suggesting VLGuard's aggressive rejection generalizes to benign prompts. On visual understanding tasks, the two methods are roughly comparable, with VLGuard slightly ahead on ScienceQA Total for LLaVA-v1.5-7B (70.12% vs. 69.11%) while the proposed method edges ahead on VizWizQA for LLaVA-v1.5-13B (58.77% vs. 56.77%). The paper's central claim is that the proposed method achieves most of the safety benefit (reducing ASR by >90% from the original model) without any curated malicious data, at the cost of a modest residual vulnerability that VLGuard eliminates entirely.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as an empirical ablation study of the VLGuard safety finetuning pipeline [55], systematically removing attributes (multi-turn comparisons, content-related images, detailed rejection reasons) to isolate what actually drives defensive capability. It departs from the prevailing assumption in MLLM safety work—that curated malicious data with high-quality rejection explanations is necessary—by showing that the rejection *proportion* in the finetuning set, not the content, is the operative variable. The conceptual framing also connects to Qi et al. [34], who showed benign finetuning can erode LLM safety, by arguing the mechanism is a compliance bias introduced during visual instruction tuning rather than a loss of safety knowledge.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a small set of benign prompts paired with simple rejection sentences (≈28.57% of the finetuning set) can reduce FigStep ASR from ~100% to near 0% across five model architectures offers a dramatically cheaper path to MLLM safety than labor-intensive malicious data collection. However, the method does not close the gap with VLGuard on MM-SafetyBench (5.6% vs 0.18% ASR for LLaVA-v1.5-7B), does not improve text-only safety, and introduces over-rejection on image-description queries, limiting its practical deployment. Open questions include whether the compliance-bias hypothesis generalizes to models trained with different instruction-tuning recipes, and whether a principled rejection proportion can be derived rather than found empirically.
