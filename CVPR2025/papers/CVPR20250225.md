## Problem

Training large-scale text-to-image diffusion transformers from scratch requires 18,000–200,000 A100 GPU hours and billion-scale or proprietary datasets, creating an inaccessible barrier for most research groups. Existing cost-reduction strategies such as naive patch masking degrade sharply beyond 50% masking, and model downscaling sacrifices the expressive power needed for competitive generation quality. The paper asks whether a diffusion transformer can be trained to competitive zero-shot performance at an order-of-magnitude lower cost without relying on massive or proprietary data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250225/cropped_page1_idx4.webp)

## Input / Output

The model accepts a natural language text prompt and produces a 512×512 RGB image in 30 sampling steps, operating in a compressed VAE latent space (patch size 2, yielding 256×256 latent tokens at full resolution). During training, images are encoded to latent representations at 256×256 (Phase-1) and 512×512 (Phase-2) resolutions, and 75% of latent patches are randomly masked before entering the backbone transformer. At inference no masking is applied, so the full patch sequence is processed.

## Method

The core contribution is a deferred masking strategy: before randomly dropping 75% of patches, all patch embeddings are first passed through a lightweight patch-mixer (a few transformer layers with a small fraction of the backbone's parameters), so that surviving patches carry fused semantic information about the entire image. This avoids the severe information loss of naive input-layer masking and eliminates the auxiliary autoencoding loss required by MaskDiT, while incurring nearly identical FLOPs. The backbone is a DiT-X1/2 sparse transformer (1.16B parameters) with expert-choice mixture-of-experts layers (8 experts in alternate blocks) and layer-wise scaling that linearly increases hidden dimension in deeper layers. Training proceeds in two phases—250K steps at 256×256 followed by 55K steps at 512×512—on 37M real-and-synthetic images (or 490M synthetic-only images), completing in 2.6 days on a single 8×H100 node for a total cost of $1,890.

## Results

MicroDiT achieves a 12.66 FID-30K on the COCO 2014 validation set and 0.46 GenEval score, trained on only 37M images in 2.6 days on a single 8×H100 node ($1,890 total GPU cost). This represents a 14× reduction in training cost versus the prior state-of-the-art low-cost method (PixArt-α, 37.6 GPU days / $28,400) and a 118× reduction versus Stable Diffusion 2.1 (416.5 GPU days). In human preference evaluations, the best MicroDiT variant (trained on 490M synthetic images) is preferred over Stable-Diffusion-2.1 on image quality (53.8%) and over Stable-Diffusion-1.5 on prompt alignment (55.8%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the closest low-cost competitor PixArt-α, MicroDiT trades absolute quality (12.66 vs. 7.32 FID-30K; 0.46 vs. 0.48 GenEval) for a 14× reduction in training cost (2.6 vs. 37.6 GPU days). Compared to Stable-Diffusion-1.5, MicroDiT achieves a slightly worse FID (12.66 vs. 11.18) but a better GenEval score (0.46 vs. 0.43), while using roughly 120× less compute. Against Stable-Diffusion-2.1, MicroDiT is outperformed on both FID (12.66 vs. 9.12) and GenEval (0.46 vs. 0.50) but at ~160× lower cost. MicroDiT clearly outperforms Würstchen on FID (12.66 vs. 23.60) at ~20× lower cost. Against the large-scale models Imagen (7.27 FID) and GigaGAN (9.09 FID), MicroDiT lags in absolute fidelity but does so with 92–137× less training compute. In human preference studies, the best MicroDiT variant (490M synthetic images) is preferred over SD-2.1 on quality (53.8%) and over SD-1.5 on alignment (55.8%), though it is slightly less preferred than PixArt-α on both criteria (48.5% quality, 45.2% alignment).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the DiT architecture and the patch-masking line of work initiated by MaskDiT (Zheng et al.), which combines naive input-layer masking with an auxiliary autoencoding loss. The key conceptual departure is the 'deferred masking' strategy: rather than dropping patches before the backbone transformer (as in both naive masking and MaskDiT), a lightweight patch-mixer first fuses information across all patches, and masking is applied only afterward. The paper also integrates recent transformer architecture advances—expert-choice mixture-of-experts routing and layer-wise width scaling borrowed from LLM training—into the diffusion transformer backbone, positioning itself as a systems-level rethinking of how to spend a fixed compute budget rather than a new generative modeling formulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: training a competitive 1.16B-parameter T2I diffusion model for $1,890 (2.6 days on one 8×H100 node) lowers the entry barrier for academic and small-lab participation in large-scale generative model development by an order of magnitude. The finding that ~500M synthetic images alone can match or exceed a 37M real+synthetic mixture in human preference evaluations raises open questions about the long-term role of real data in diffusion training and whether this trend extends to video or 3D generation. Limitations include a notable absolute quality gap (FID 12.7 vs. 7.3 for PixArt-α), the reliance on a specific masking ratio (75%) that degrades sharply beyond ~87%, and the fact that the synthetic-data advantage was validated primarily through human preference rather than a fully controlled automated protocol.
