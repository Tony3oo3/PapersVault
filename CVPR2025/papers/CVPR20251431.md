## Problem

Current spatial transcriptomics analysis methods predominantly rely on spatial graph neural networks to encode spatial correlations, which incur quadratic computational costs in the number of spots and struggle with complex configurations such as severely misaligned tissue slices or extremely sparse measurement grids. This paper asks whether a continuous, coordinate-based representation can replace explicit graph construction while preserving spatial and slice-wise structure.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251431/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

STINR takes 3D coordinates (two spatial dimensions plus a slice index) for each measured spot, log-normalized gene expression counts, and a single-cell reference matrix (C cell-types × G genes) as input. The INR maps each coordinate vector to a full G-dimensional gene expression vector, so the model is evaluated at arbitrary spatial locations rather than only at observed spots. Outputs include the reconstructed expression matrix (for imputation/denoising), a low-dimensional latent representation (clustered via GMM for spatial domain detection), and a per-spot cell-type proportion matrix whose rows sum to one.

## Method

STINR replaces the spatial adjacency graph with a SIREN (MLP with sine activation) that maps 3D spot coordinates directly to gene expression vectors, exploiting the Lipschitz smoothness of the sine-activated network to implicitly encode spatial and slice-wise correlations without any explicit neighbor construction. A two-layer sine-MLP encoder compresses the reconstructed expression into a latent code used for domain detection, and a two-layer sine-MLP decoder with Softmax outputs normalized cell-type proportions. Training jointly minimizes a reconstruction loss on normalized expressions, a Poisson negative-log-likelihood deconvolution loss (with a learned batch-effect term combining a latent-conditioned linear layer and a gene-wise offset), and a latent-to-expression regularization loss. Because the slice index is simply a third coordinate dimension, multiple misaligned slices are integrated in a single forward pass without alignment or graph re-construction, and multi-omics modalities are handled by parallel INR branches sharing a common decoder.

## Results

On the DLPFC spatial domain detection benchmark (8 sections from two donors), STINR achieves the best Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) across all sections, ranging from 0.564 to 0.713, outperforming the strongest baseline (GraphST, 0.442–0.606) by up to 11 ARI points. On gene imputation (mouse brain, SR=0.6), STINR achieves the lowest MSE of 0.156–0.164 and MTD of 0.049–0.052, beating GNTD (0.158–0.191 MSE) and GraphST (0.162–0.170 MSE). STINR also runs substantially faster than graph-based methods: 10 s per section on mouse brain vs. 39–40 s for GraphST, and 27 s per slice on mouse organogenesis vs. 718 s for GraphST at the E11.5 stage (30,124 spots).

## Baselines & Comparisons

STINR consistently outperforms all eight baselines across every dataset and task. On DLPFC spatial domain detection, STINR beats GraphST (the strongest graph-based baseline) by 7.8–11.1 ARI points per section, and surpasses STitch3D (the multi-slice method) by 1.5–11.1 points; it also clearly dominates the statistical method IRIS (by 8–30 ARI points). On gene imputation/denoising (mouse brain and organogenesis), STINR achieves the lowest MSE and MTD in every setting, edging out GNTD by ~0.002–0.03 MSE and GraphST by ~0.004–0.02 MSE, while running 2–4× faster than graph-based methods. On the large-scale E11.5 organogenesis slice (30,124 spots), STINR completes in 27 s versus 718 s for GraphST and 104 s for STAGATE, a 26× speedup over GraphST. For multi-omics (human lymph node), STINR qualitatively outperforms SpatialGlue in resolving subtle capsule and adipose tissue boundaries. STINR also uniquely identifies the thinnest cortex layers (L2, L4) in DLPFC and more accurately delineates tumor edges in SCC, capabilities no baseline achieves.

## Relation to Prior Work

STINR repurposes the SIREN implicit neural representation (Sitzmann et al., NeurIPS 2020) — originally developed for continuous image/signal representation — as the core spatial encoder for transcriptomics, replacing the dominant paradigm of constructing explicit spatial neighbor graphs (as in GraphST, GNTD, STAGATE, STitch3D). The deconvolution module follows the Poisson-likelihood formulation established by SONAR and STitch3D, and the overall autoencoder structure (encoder-decoder with latent clustering) mirrors standard ST analysis pipelines. The key departure is conceptual: rather than encoding spatial relationships through a discrete adjacency matrix, STINR relies on the Lipschitz smoothness of the sine-activated MLP to implicitly capture spatial and slice-wise correlations from raw 3D coordinates, eliminating the need for graph construction entirely.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By removing the quadratic-cost adjacency matrix, STINR offers a practical efficiency advantage for large-scale ST datasets (e.g., 10s vs. 718s for GraphST on 30k-spot sections) and naturally handles severely misaligned multi-slice or multi-omics data without registration. The ability to identify the thinnest cortical layers (L2, L4) in DLPFC — missed by all graph-based baselines — suggests that continuous representation may capture fine-grained spatial boundaries that discrete graph smoothing obscures. Open questions include whether INR-based approaches degrade when spatial patterns are genuinely discontinuous (e.g., sharp tumor boundaries), how the method scales to truly 3D volumetric data beyond sequential slices, and whether the implicit smoothness assumption is always biologically appropriate.
