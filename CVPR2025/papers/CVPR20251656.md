## Problem

Existing bias detection methods for computer vision models rely on dataset label distributions and per-group accuracy disparities, which can only identify biases at the coarse level of binary labels. They cannot reveal which specific image regions a model uses to make biased predictions, nor can they uncover spurious correlations that go beyond what is visible in dataset labels. This paper asks whether attention maps can expose these finer-grained, internal representations of bias.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251656/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image (224×224 for Waterbirds, 512×512 for CelebAMask-HQ), a trained CNN classifier (ResNet-18 or ResNet-50), and optionally a ground-truth semantic feature mask. GradCAM attention maps are extracted at the final convolutional layer, with a modification to take the gradient of |y_a| rather than y_a so that both positive and negative predictions produce meaningful maps under binary cross-entropy loss. The output is a scalar Attention-IoU score in [0,1], aggregated over a test set as either a heatmap score (target attribute vs. protected attribute) or a mask score (target attribute vs. ground-truth feature region).

## Method

The key insight is that a model exploiting a spurious correlation will attend to features of the confounding attribute rather than the target attribute, making attention maps a direct probe of internal bias. The paper proposes Attention-IoU, a generalized intersection-over-union metric that operates on continuous, weighted dense-pixel maps (not just binary masks) and is both size-invariant and scale-invariant via L1 normalization. Specifically, two maps are L1-normalized to act as probability density functions; the numerator is their Frobenius inner product (weighted intersection) and the denominator is the squared Frobenius norm of their element-wise average (union). Two bias scores are defined: the heatmap score compares GradCAM maps of a target attribute against a protected attribute across images, and the mask score compares a target's GradCAM map against a downsampled ground-truth feature mask. The metric is validated on Waterbirds with controlled bias levels, then applied to all 40 CelebA attributes against the Male protected attribute, revealing that some attributes (e.g., Blond_Hair) exhibit bias driven by hidden confounders rather than simple label correlations.

## Results

On Waterbirds, as dataset bias increases from 50% to 100%, worst group accuracy (WGA) drops from 0.81±0.02 to 0.21±0.10, while the Attention-IoU mask score for the bird decreases from 0.72±0.02 to 0.42±0.03, closely mirroring the WGA decline and validating the metric. On CelebA, heatmap scores with the Male attribute range from 0.63±0.02 (Black_Hair) to 0.94±0.01 (Wearing_Lipstick), showing a strong positive trend with label MCC. A key finding is that Blond_Hair (heatmap score 0.72±0.02) and Wavy_Hair (0.65±0.03) have similar MCCs (~0.35) but distinct bias profiles: subsampling to vary training-set MCC produces no significant change for Blond_Hair (Kendall τ=0.007) but a strong effect for Wavy_Hair (τ=0.785), revealing a hidden confounder for Blond_Hair.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper introduces a new evaluation metric (Attention-IoU) rather than a new model, so it does not compete against baseline methods in the traditional sense. Instead, it validates the metric against worst group accuracy (WGA) on Waterbirds, showing the mask score tracks WGA almost proportionally (both decrease monotonically with increasing bias). On CelebA, the paper demonstrates that Attention-IoU reveals information beyond what standard accuracy-based fairness metrics capture: for example, Wearing_Lipstick has a high WGA but a low mask score, indicating the model attends away from the mouth toward Male-relevant regions. The Eyeglasses case (MCC=0.26 but heatmap score=0.86) exposes a limitation of the metric with co-localized features, which the authors address by training separate models with and without eyeglasses present. The subsampling experiment on Blond_Hair vs. Wavy_Hair shows that Attention-IoU can distinguish between bias caused by dataset label correlations (Wavy_Hair) and bias from an unlabeled confounding variable (Blond_Hair), a distinction invisible to accuracy-based metrics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on GradCAM (Selvaraju et al., 2020) for generating attribute-specific attention maps, modifying it to handle binary cross-entropy loss by taking gradients of the absolute class output. It departs from the dominant line of bias detection work—which quantifies disparities through dataset label distributions and per-group accuracy metrics (e.g., group DRO, fairness metrics)—by introducing a quantitative metric (Attention-IoU) that operates on a model's internal spatial representations rather than its inputs or outputs. Prior use of attention maps in bias research has been largely qualitative (average heatmaps, individual image visualizations); this work formalizes their comparison into a size- and scale-invariant intersection-over-union score applicable to both attention-attention and attention-mask comparisons.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical contribution is revealing that model bias can stem from unlabeled confounders invisible to label-correlation analysis: Blond_Hair's bias persists even when the training-set MCC with Male is varied, while Wavy_Hair's bias tracks the label correlation directly. This implies that standard debiasing strategies targeting label rebalancing (e.g., group DRO) may be insufficient for attributes whose bias is driven by spatially co-located but semantically distinct features. A stated limitation is that attention maps encode only spatial information, so co-located features (as with Eyeglasses and Male) can produce inflated scores; extending the metric to incorporate shape, color, or texture cues remains open. Generalization beyond CelebA and ResNet-50 to other datasets, architectures (e.g., ViTs), and tasks is untested.
