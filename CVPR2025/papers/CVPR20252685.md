## Problem

T2I-based image restoration methods require large collections of high-quality real images (costly, privacy-risky, copyright-limited) and heavy adapters (1.3B–2.2B parameters) to control the generative model, making training prohibitively expensive. This paper asks whether a sufficiently powerful T2I model (Flux, 12B) can serve as both the source of unlimited synthetic training data and the backbone to be controlled by a much lighter adapter, collapsing the training cost by over 90%.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252685/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a degraded low-quality RGB image (256×256 for real-world benchmarks, 1024×1024 for synthetic) and produces a restored high-quality image at 1024×1024. No text prompt is required at inference; the LQ image is encoded by Flux's 16-channel VAE into a latent that feeds the adapter, and the model denoises from pure Gaussian noise over 20 sampling steps. Training images are generated at 1024×768 and center-cropped to 1024×768, 768×768, or 512×768 to cover multiple aspect ratios.

## Method

FluxGen generates 350K training images by feeding empty prompts and random Gaussian noise into Flux, then filters them using three no-reference IQA scores (CLIP-IQA, MANIQA, MUSIQ; top 95% retained) and applies Real-ESRGAN-style synthetic degradation to form LQ–HQ pairs. FluxIR is a 0.4B ControlNet-like adapter consisting of a single MM-DiT block (initialized from Flux's first block) that extracts image and text features from the LQ latent, plus a set of low-rank squeeze-and-excitation (SE) layers that broadcast these features into all 57 MM-DiT blocks of Flux, modulating both the image and text branches simultaneously. Training uses a custom uniform-with-clamping timestep sampler (ensuring t=0 and t=1 are sampled with probability ε/(1+2ε)) to bridge the train–inference gap at the noise endpoint, combined with a latent-space MSE loss and a pixel-space L1 loss on VAE-decoded outputs. The full pipeline trains in ~14 GPU-days (3.5 days on 4×H800, batch 64, AdamW lr 1e-5), roughly 8.5% of the cost of SUPIR or DreamClear.

## Results

FluxIR achieves the best MANIQA score across all four evaluation datasets: 0.6331 (DIV2K-Val), 0.6334 (RealSR), 0.6024 (DrealSR), and 0.6314 (RealLQ250), surpassing the previous best (SeeSR at 0.5927 on RealLQ250) by over 6.5%. On the no-reference RealLQ250 benchmark, FluxIR also leads in CLIPIQA (0.5639) and MUSIQ (70.78). On full-reference metrics (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS), FluxIR trails non-generative baselines like DASR and SwinIR, consistent with the behavior of other generative IR methods. Training requires only 14 GPU-days (4×H800, 3.5 days), reducing cost by ~93.5% vs. SUPIR and ~91.5% vs. DreamClear.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FluxIR achieves the best MANIQA score on all four datasets, with the largest margin on RealLQ250 (0.6314 vs. SeeSR's 0.5927, a 6.5% relative improvement). On CLIPIQA, FluxIR wins on DIV2K-Val (0.5934 vs. SeeSR 0.5909) and RealLQ250 (0.5639 vs. SeeSR 0.5569), but trails SeeSR on RealSR (0.5413 vs. 0.5513) and DrealSR (0.5136 vs. 0.5295). On MUSIQ, FluxIR leads only on RealLQ250 (70.78 vs. SeeSR 70.38), while SeeSR remains slightly ahead on the other three datasets. Against the two most directly comparable generative methods, SUPIR (1.3B adapter on 3.5B SDXL) and DreamClear (2.2B adapter on 0.6B PixArt-α), FluxIR (0.4B adapter on 12B Flux) outperforms both on MANIQA across all datasets while using only 30.8% and 18.6% of their adapter parameters, respectively. On full-reference metrics (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS), FluxIR is consistently the worst among all methods, which the authors attribute to the generative nature of the approach—non-generative baselines like DASR and SwinIR preserve pixel-level fidelity better, while generative methods prioritize perceptual realism.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ControlNet paradigm for T2I-based image restoration by replacing the standard full-encoder duplication (used in SUPIR's 1.3B adapter for SDXL and DreamClear's 2.2B adapter for PixArt-α) with a single MM-DiT block plus squeeze-and-excitation layers, reducing trainable parameters to 0.4B while controlling the much larger 12B Flux model. It also departs from prior data curation strategies (manual collection in SUPIR, LLM-assisted generation in DreamClear) by proposing FluxGen, which exploits Flux's unconditional generation capability and IQA-based filtering to produce training pairs entirely from the model's own generative prior. The degradation pipeline follows Real-ESRGAN's synthetic degradation framework, and the rectified-flow training objective with pixel-space loss builds on recent diffusion-based IR work (SeeSR, StableSR).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 93.5% training cost reduction (14 GPU-days vs. SUPIR's ~220) while matching or exceeding SOTA on non-reference metrics makes large-model-based image restoration accessible to groups without massive compute budgets, and the FluxGen pipeline offers a privacy-safe, copyright-free alternative to web-scraped or purchased datasets. The SE-layer mechanism for broadcasting control signals across 57 MM-DiT blocks from a single adapter block is a reusable architectural pattern for controlling other large DiT-based models. However, the method still lags non-generative approaches on full-reference metrics (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS), and the reliance on synthetic training data raises open questions about coverage of rare or domain-specific degradations not well-represented in Flux's generative prior.
