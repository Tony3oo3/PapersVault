## Problem

Existing deep learning-based HSI-MSI fusion methods rely on large volumes of paired training data that are scarce in practice, while diffusion-model-based alternatives demand thousands of sampling iterations and substantial memory, making them impractical for resource-constrained edge devices such as drones. This paper addresses both limitations by proposing a self-learning framework that requires no external training data and operates with a lightweight architecture for fast inference.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251603/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a low-resolution hyperspectral image (LR-HSI, downsampled by a factor of 4) and a high-resolution multispectral image (HR-MSI, obtained via mode-3 multiplication with a spectral response function) as the sole inputs, with no paired training data required. The output is a high-resolution hyperspectral image (HR-HSI) reconstructed as the mode-3 product of two low-dimensional components: a spectral basis E ∈ R^{d×C} and a reduced coefficient A ∈ R^{H×W×d}, where d=8 is far smaller than the number of spectral bands C. The entire training and sampling pipeline is conditioned only on the observed LR-HSI and HR-MSI, making the approach fully self-learning.

## Method

ARGS-Diff decomposes the HR-HSI into a spectral basis and a reduced coefficient, then trains two lightweight networks independently: a 5-layer fully connected spectral network on randomly sampled pixel spectra from the LR-HSI, and a 9-layer UNet-like spatial network on patches from the HR-MSI, both using the forward diffusion noise-adding process. During the reverse diffusion sampling, both networks jointly reconstruct their respective components via posterior sampling with gradient-based guidance (Eq. 9), where the guidance function enforces consistency with both the observed LR-HSI and HR-MSI. To stabilize the simultaneous update of two coupled components, the Adaptive Residual Guided Module (ARGM) computes a residual loss between the reconstructed product and the observations at each step, then applies a residual-guided gradient correction to both components. An Adam optimizer is integrated into the gradient updates to accelerate convergence, and the final HR-HSI is obtained after T=500 sampling steps as the product of the refined spectral basis and reduced coefficient.

## Results

On the three simulated datasets, ARGS-Diff achieves PSNR of 42.33 dB (Pavia), 41.90 dB (Chikusei), and 43.63 dB (KSC), surpassing the second-best method MIAE by 1.31, 1.37, and 1.27 dB respectively. It also attains the best SAM (2.64°, 1.77°, 2.54°), ERGAS (1.49, 2.10, 1.25), and SSIM (0.977, 0.970, 0.980) across all datasets. In terms of efficiency, ARGS-Diff completes inference in 12–13 seconds with only 21.85M parameters and 2.11 GB memory, compared to 79 s / 391M / 6.21 GB for PLRDiff and 297 s / 606M / 23.34 GB for S²CycleDiff.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ARGS-Diff outperforms all six baselines on every metric (PSNR, SAM, ERGAS, SSIM) across all three simulated datasets. Against the strongest DL baseline MIAE, it gains 1.27–1.37 dB in PSNR while running 1.7–2.7× faster (12 s vs. 20–32 s). Compared to the two diffusion-based methods, ARGS-Diff improves PSNR by 2.22 dB over PLRDiff (42.33 vs. 40.11 on Pavia) and 3.56 dB over S²CycleDiff (42.33 vs. 38.77), while reducing inference time by 6.6× and 24.8× respectively. Traditional methods CNMF and HySure are the fastest (5–15 s) but trail ARGS-Diff by 3.5–4.5 dB in PSNR. In terms of model footprint, ARGS-Diff uses only 21.85M parameters and 2.11 GB memory, roughly 1/18th and 1/3rd of PLRDiff and 1/28th and 1/11th of S²CycleDiff, making it the most deployable option for edge devices.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the diffusion posterior sampling (DPS) framework of Chung et al. and Bansal et al., which conditions a pretrained diffusion model on observations via gradient guidance, and on the well-established subspace decomposition of HSIs into a spectral basis and reduced coefficient. It departs from prior diffusion-based HSI fusion methods (PLRDiff, S2CycleDiff) in two key ways: (1) it forgoes a large pretrained diffusion backbone in favor of two tiny self-trained networks (a 5-layer MLP for spectral and a 9-layer UNet-like net for spatial) that learn distributions directly from the observed LR-HSI and HR-MSI, and (2) it introduces the ARGM to stabilize the coupled two-component sampling loop, a problem that single-component DPS methods do not face. The Adam-optimized guidance step within the reverse loop is a practical adaptation borrowed from optimization literature rather than a new theoretical contribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in the dramatic efficiency gains—21.85M parameters (≈1/20th of PLRDiff, ≈1/30th of S2CycleDiff), 2.11 GB memory, and 12 s runtime—making HSI-MSI fusion feasible on resource-constrained platforms such as drones, which the authors explicitly motivate. The self-learning design (no paired training data beyond the observed image pair) addresses a real deployment bottleneck in remote sensing. Limitations include evaluation on only four datasets at a single noise level (SNR = 35), reliance on a known linear degradation model in simulated experiments, and a fixed subspace dimension d = 8 that may not generalize to all spectral ranges. Open questions include whether the subspace decomposition assumption holds for highly nonlinear or mixed-material scenes, and whether the ARGM's residual-guided correction can be replaced by learned alignment to further reduce per-step cost.
