## Problem

Existing radiology report generation methods that incorporate retrieved reports as prior knowledge cannot guarantee that the retrieved texts contain disease-relevant findings aligned with the input X-ray images, undermining report trustworthiness. Additionally, no prior method applies a self-correction mechanism to refine generated reports by re-aligning them with image features in an embedding space, leaving room for errors and omissions in the final output.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252605/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes chest X-ray images (frontal and/or lateral views, multi-view input with v views) as input and produces a multi-sentence radiology report as output, along with binary disease classification labels for a set of ground-truth disease keywords. The image encoder (ResNet-50) processes the raw pixel data, while the text encoder/decoder (Transformer with 8 heads, embedding dimension 256) handles the report text. The system also maintains a training queue of recent text embeddings for top-k (k=3) retrieval during generation.

## Method

DART is a two-stage framework. In Stage 1, a contrastive loss (CLIP-style) aligns image and report embeddings in a shared space; a disease classifier using cross-attention over image features predicts disease annotations, and disease-relevant features are computed by combining predicted annotations with image features. Image-to-text retrieval with a disease-matching constraint (cross-entropy between ground-truth disease labels of the input image and retrieved texts) ensures retrieved reports share similar disease findings, and a Transformer text generator produces the initial report conditioned on retrieved text features, disease-relevant features, and text features. In Stage 2, a self-correction module re-embeds the generated report and applies cross-attention with a learnable embedding to produce self-corrected text features that minimize cosine distance to the image features; the module is trained with a correction loss plus generation loss while all other components remain frozen, and the final report is regenerated from the self-corrected features.

## Results

On MIMIC-CXR, DART achieves the best scores across all NLG metrics: BLEU-1 0.437, BLEU-2 0.279, BLEU-3 0.191, BLEU-4 0.137, ROUGE-L 0.310, and METEOR 0.175, outperforming the next-best method I3+C2FD (BLEU-1 0.402, ROUGE-L 0.291). On IU X-ray, DART leads in BLEU-2 (0.348), BLEU-3 (0.265), BLEU-4 (0.208), and ROUGE-L (0.411), though MA edges it on BLEU-1 (0.501 vs 0.486) and METEOR (0.213 vs 0.205). For clinical efficacy on MIMIC-CXR, DART achieves F1 0.533, Precision 0.520, and Recall 0.546, substantially surpassing the runner-up I3+C2FD (F1 0.473).

## Baselines & Comparisons

On MIMIC-CXR, DART outperforms all 11 compared baselines on every NLG metric, with the largest margins over R2Gen (BLEU-1 +0.084) and CMCL (BLEU-1 +0.093); the closest competitor is I3+C2FD, which ties DART on METEOR (0.175) but trails by 0.035 on BLEU-1 and 0.019 on ROUGE-L. On IU X-ray, DART wins on BLEU-2/3/4 and ROUGE-L against all baselines, but MA [34] surpasses it on BLEU-1 (0.501 vs 0.486) and METEOR (0.213 vs 0.205), suggesting DART generates longer, more contextually rich reports at a slight cost to unigram overlap. For clinical efficacy on MIMIC-CXR, DART's F1 of 0.533 exceeds the next-best I3+C2FD (0.473) by 6 percentage points and RGRG (0.447) by 8.6 points, indicating substantially better capture of disease-relevant findings. Among knowledge-enhanced baselines, PPKED [22] and MSAT [38] (which use entity graphs) are consistently outperformed, while contrastive-learning-based DCL [17] and ECRG [13] trail DART by 0.03–0.06 on BLEU-1, underscoring the benefit of the disease-matching constraint and self-correction over generic contrastive or energy-based alignment.

## Relation to Prior Work

DART synthesizes three previously separate threads: CLIP-style contrastive image-text alignment (as in DCL [17]), retrieval-augmented report generation (as in PPKED [22] and R2Gen [4]), and self-correction mechanisms from NLP (Self-Refine [26], Welleck et al. [40]). Rather than building on a single prior architecture, the paper repurposes contrastive embedding spaces as the substrate for a novel self-correction step that re-aligns generated reports with image features — a mechanism not previously applied to radiology report generation. The disease-matching constraint on retrieval also departs from prior retrieval-augmented approaches that rely solely on embedding similarity without explicitly enforcing disease-label consistency.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a practical trustworthiness concern: ensuring generated reports capture disease-relevant findings rather than defaulting to normal descriptions. The self-correction module, despite its simplicity (a single cross-attention layer with two learnable embeddings), provides a lightweight post-hoc refinement that outperforms GPT-4-based correction in qualitative analysis, suggesting embedding-space alignment is a more targeted signal than general language-model refinement for this task. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond chest X-rays to other modalities, whether iterative self-correction yields further gains, and whether the method integrates with LLM-based generators that are increasingly dominant in the field.
