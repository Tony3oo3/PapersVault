## Problem

In continual semantic segmentation, image replay fails because stored images suffer from partial labeling: only the current step's classes are annotated, while old and future classes are mislabeled as background, causing the model to confuse foreground objects with background (background shift). Naive instance-level replay (directly training on isolated instances or randomly copy-pasting them) also underperforms because it neglects dynamic inter-class context and produces semantically inconsistent placements that contaminate training samples.

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images (H×W) from the current training step together with a memory buffer of individually stored old-class instances (each a small cropped region with a single-class label). During training, selected instances are fused into new images via mixup blending, and the model produces a pixel-level segmentation mask over all classes learned so far plus background. The RSKD loss applies region-specific distillation: old-class/background pixels are constrained to match the old model's predictions, while new-class pixels are encouraged to shift probability mass from the old model's background prediction toward the new class set.

## Method

EIR replaces image-level replay with a four-stage instance replay pipeline: (1) storing balanced per-class instances in a fixed-size memory buffer, (2) using the old model's pseudo-labels (logit threshold τ=0.7) to identify potential old classes present in each new image and selecting up to two matching instances from the buffer, (3) strategically placing each instance in the background region of the new image with the highest background-pixel proportion (divided into n rectangular cells) to minimize overlap with new-class regions, and (4) blending the instance into the image via mixup with a beta-distributed λ for smooth transitions. Training combines standard mBCE on both the fused image and the raw instance with a novel Region-Specific Knowledge Distillation (RSKD) loss that selectively aligns old and new model predictions only in old-class/background regions while encouraging the new model to absorb the old model's background probability into new-class predictions in new-class regions, preserving plasticity for new classes.

## Results

On Pascal VOC 2012 (ResNet-101), EIR achieves 66.4% mIoU (all classes) on the 5-3 setting, outperforming the strongest baseline MicroSeg-M (64.1%) by 2.3 points, and 56.9% on the challenging 2-2 setting vs. 48.5% for MicroSeg-M. On ADE20K (ResNet-101), EIR reaches 35.3% mIoU on 100-50 and 34.1% on 50-50, surpassing TIKP (34.9% and 33.6%) and MicroSeg-M (33.2% and 32.6%). With a Swin-B backbone, EIR achieves 73.4% mIoU on VOC 5-3, substantially ahead of CoinSeg (64.3%) and MicroSeg (46.5%), demonstrating consistent gains across both CNN and Transformer architectures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EIR outperforms all replay-free baselines (MiB, PLOP, SSUL-M, MicroSeg-M, RCIL, EWF, TIKP) on the majority of settings, with the largest margins on multi-step protocols where background shift is most severe: on VOC 2-2, EIR gains 8.4 points over MicroSeg-M (56.9 vs. 48.5) and 11.6 over SSUL-M (56.9 vs. 45.3). On VOC 5-3, EIR leads MicroSeg-M by 2.3 points (66.4 vs. 64.1) and SSUL-M by 9.2 points. The only settings where EIR trails a baseline are VOC 15-5 (74.2 vs. MicroSeg-M's 75.2) and ADE20K 100-10 (34.3 vs. MicroSeg-M's 34.5), both by less than 1 point, and the paper notes MicroSeg-M achieves this by retaining all old-class annotations during replay, which deviates from the standard CSS protocol. On ADE20K, EIR consistently leads TIKP and RCIL across all three settings (e.g., 35.3 vs. 34.9 on 100-50; 34.1 vs. 33.6 on 50-50). With the Swin-B backbone, EIR's advantage widens dramatically on VOC 5-3 (73.4 vs. CoinSeg's 64.3 and MicroSeg's 46.5), suggesting the instance-replay strategy scales well to Transformer architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant image-replay paradigm in continual learning by arguing that whole-image replay is fundamentally limited in CSS due to the partial-labeling problem: stored images contain only partial old-class annotations, causing unannotated foreground to be mislabeled as background. It builds directly on PLOPLong's [14] random copy-paste strategy for CSS but identifies two critical shortcomings—lack of dynamic class combination and poor background integration—and addresses them through context-aware instance selection, strategic spatial placement, and mixup-based blending. The RSKD loss extends the knowledge distillation lineage (UNKD [5], DKD [1]) by applying distillation constraints selectively to old-class instance regions rather than uniformly across all pixels, preserving plasticity for new classes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical, lightweight replay framework that can be plugged into existing CSS pipelines, and its ablations clearly show that no single component (combination, placement, or enhanced training) is sufficient—addressing background shift requires all three working together. A key open question the authors acknowledge is that instance replay cannot cover all background-shift regions in new images, meaning the problem is mitigated but not fully resolved; future work may need complementary mechanisms (e.g., generative background modeling) for complete coverage. The finding that vanilla instance replay underperforms image replay on old classes is a useful caution against naive granularity shifts in replay-based continual learning.
