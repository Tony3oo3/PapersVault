## Problem

Existing stereo sound localization methods rely on synthetic training data, hand-chosen binaural cues, or restricted visual object categories, limiting their applicability to real-world settings. Natural recordings involve complex overlapping sources, varied microphone geometries, and diverse camera motions that are difficult to capture through simulation. This paper asks whether camera egomotion in unlabeled in-the-wild videos can serve as a free, weak supervisory signal for learning sound localization without any manual angle annotations.

## Input / Output

The model takes two short stereo audio segments (from times t and t′ within a 5-second clip) and converts them into 256×256×4 spectrograms (magnitude and phase for both channels). It also receives two binary pseudo-labels derived from off-the-shelf multi-view geometry (SuperGlue + Perspective Fields): the camera rotation direction (clockwise/counter-clockwise) and the camera translation direction (forward/backward). The output is a 32-way softmax distribution over uniformly sampled azimuth angles in (−90°, 90°), with front-back ambiguity resolved by reflecting all predictions to the front hemisphere.

## Method

A ResNet-18 backbone processes the spectrograms to predict azimuth angles, trained with three complementary losses that exploit egomotion as weak supervision. The rotation loss (L_rot) constrains the pair of angle predictions at two time points so that their angular difference is consistent with the estimated camera rotation direction; the translation loss (L_trans) constrains the change in angle magnitude to be consistent with forward/backward camera motion; and the binaural loss (L_bin) penalizes predictions inconsistent with the interaural intensity difference (IID) cue. Crucially, camera motion is estimated entirely by off-the-shelf multi-view geometry methods rather than jointly learned with the audio model, enabling training on diverse in-the-wild walking-tour videos (the StereoWalks dataset) with complex motions and overlapping sources. The overall loss is a weighted combination L = λ₁·L_rot + (1−λ₁)·L_trans + λ₂·L_bin, with a 'mask and sum' operation that restricts cross-entropy to only the angle pairs compatible with the visual constraint.

## Results

On the YT-Stereo-iPhone dataset, Ours-Full achieves MAE of 34.0° and 8-class accuracy of 33.3%, substantially outperforming GTRot (MAE 71.4°, 8clf 7.1%). On Stereo-Fountain, Ours-Full reaches MAE 29.3° and 8clf 46.0%, again beating GTRot (MAE 57.2°, 8clf 18.4%). On the in-the-wild binaural audio benchmark, Ours-IIDonly achieves 87.7% 2-class accuracy, marginally surpassing MonoCLR (87.4%) and StereoCRW (87.2%). On the simulated HM3D-SS 2.0 dataset, GTRot retains an advantage (MAE 40.2° vs. 57.2° for Ours-Full), highlighting the sim-to-real gap.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GTRot (Chen et al., ICCV 2023), which jointly learns camera rotation and sound direction with oracle rotation angles, Ours-Full wins decisively on both in-the-wild datasets: MAE drops from 71.4° to 34.0° and 8clf rises from 7.1% to 33.3% on YT-Stereo-iPhone, and MAE drops from 57.2° to 29.3° on Stereo-Fountain. On the in-the-wild binaural audio benchmark, Ours-full (87.5%) and Ours-IIDonly (87.7%) both exceed GTRot (84.9%) by roughly 3 points. Against MonoCLR and StereoCRW (Chen et al., ECCV 2022), which use contrastive random walks on stereo/mono audio, Ours-IIDonly achieves the best 2-class accuracy (87.7% vs. 87.4% and 87.2%), a narrow but consistent margin. However, on the simulated HM3D-SS 2.0 dataset, GTRot outperforms Ours-Full (MAE 40.2° vs. 57.2°), indicating that the egomotion-direction supervision is less effective in clean simulated environments where precise angle supervision is available.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from Chen et al. [13] (ICCV 2023), which jointly learns camera rotation and sound direction from multi-view audio-visual data but requires simulated scenes with simple, controlled motion. Here, the authors instead use off-the-shelf multi-view geometry (SuperGlue + Perspective Fields) to extract binary camera motion labels, decoupling the visual estimation from the audio model entirely. The approach also builds on the interaural intensity difference (IID) cues from Chen et al. [12] (ECCV 2022) as a complementary supervision signal, but replaces the need for simulated binaural training data with in-the-wild stereo recordings. The key conceptual shift is from precise angle-level supervision to weak direction-level supervision, trading accuracy of the supervisory signal for robustness to real-world complexity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The StereoWalks dataset is the first in-the-wild stereo sound localization benchmark with egomotion, filling a gap where prior datasets were lab-recorded, stationary-camera, or required specialized hardware. The finding that weak binary egomotion supervision outperforms stronger angle-level supervision in overlapping/intermittent sound scenarios suggests a broader principle: robustness of the supervisory signal may matter more than its precision in noisy real-world settings. However, the method remains limited by front-back ambiguity for stereo devices, the assumption of a single dominant sound source, and the need for manual filtering of low-quality clips. Open questions include whether richer visual supervision (e.g., full 6-DoF pose) or multi-source separation could close the remaining gap to fully supervised performance.
