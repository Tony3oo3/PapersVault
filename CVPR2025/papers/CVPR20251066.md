## Problem

Masked video modeling methods like VideoMAE rely on pixel-level reconstruction, which overfits low-level details and fails to capture high-level spatial semantics. Additionally, the substantial temporal redundancy in natural videos allows the model to solve reconstruction via spatial correlations without truly encoding motion dynamics, leading to poor generalization on motion-sensitive downstream tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251066/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes video clips (T×H×W×3) and augments them by overlaying segmented objects (generated via Stable Diffusion and X-Paste, sized 32×32 to 128×128) along randomly generated non-linear trajectories with rotation (±90°) and scaling (0.5–1.5×) transformations. The augmented video is patchified into 3D space-time tokens (e.g., 2×16×16), and the model produces video representations via a ViT encoder-decoder. The reconstruction target is not raw pixels but CLIP ViT-B feature embeddings extracted frame-by-frame from the augmented video, providing high-level semantic supervision.

## Method

SMILE integrates two key modifications into the masked video modeling paradigm: (1) replacing pixel reconstruction targets with CLIP feature embeddings to inject high-level spatial semantics, and (2) overlaying synthetic moving objects along random trajectories to reduce temporal redundancy and force the model to encode motion dynamics. Two complementary masking strategies are applied: standard tube masking on original video tokens and trajectory-based masking that explicitly drops tokens along the object's path, ensuring the reconstruction task requires understanding the object's motion. The architecture uses a teacher-student setup where a frozen CLIP encoder provides feature targets and an asymmetric ViT encoder-decoder (ViT-S or ViT-B) performs reconstruction. A progressive training strategy first trains on motion-augmented videos alone, then continues on original videos, reducing domain shift while preserving motion awareness.

## Results

On linear probing with a ViT-B backbone pretrained on K400, SMILE achieves 56.2% top-1 on K400, 83.8% on UCF-101, 53.4% on HMDB-51, 23.7% on SSv2, 30.2% on FineGYM, and 34.4% on EPIC-Kitchens-100, surpassing all prior masked video modeling methods. In full finetuning, SMILE reaches 83.1% top-1 on K400 (vs. 81.5% for the best prior methods SIGMA and MME) and 72.5% on SSv2 (vs. 71.2% for SIGMA), using only 600 pretraining epochs compared to 800 for most baselines. On the SEVERE generalization benchmark, SMILE achieves a mean score of 67.9%, outperforming MME (65.0%) and SIGMA (64.2%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SMILE decisively outperforms all pixel-reconstruction-based masked video modeling methods (VideoMAE, MVD, MME, MGMAE, EVEREST, MGM) across all six linear probing datasets, with the largest gaps on K400 (+9.7% over MGMAE, the best pixel-reconstruction method) and UCF (+19.4% over MGMAE). The closest competitor is SIGMA [49], which also uses feature-space reconstruction; SMILE leads SIGMA by 8.7% on K400 and 3.1% on UCF in linear probing, and by 1.6% on K400 full finetuning (83.1% vs. 81.5%). On motion-sensitive benchmarks (SSv2, FineGYM, EPIC), SMILE consistently surpasses motion-focused methods like MME, MGMAE, and MGM, confirming that synthetic motion augmentation improves motion-aware representations. In full finetuning on SSv2, SMILE achieves 72.5% (SSv2 pretraining) and 72.1% (K400 pretraining), exceeding the best prior results (SIGMA 71.2%, MGM 70.6%) while using fewer pretraining epochs (600 vs. 800). On the SEVERE generalization benchmark, SMILE leads all methods by 2.9% in mean score over MME, with particularly strong gains in sample efficiency (+5.3% on GYM) and task shift (+8.9% on Charades over MME).

## Relation to Prior Work

SMILE extends the masked video modeling paradigm (VideoMAE) by replacing pixel-level reconstruction targets with CLIP feature embeddings, drawing on the image-language pretraining lineage (CLIP, DINO, MAE) for high-level spatial semantics. For motion, it departs from prior motion-aware masked video methods (MGMAE, MGM, MME) that rely on optical flow or frame differences to identify motion regions in natural videos; instead, SMILE injects synthetic object motions via pasted segmented objects along generated trajectories, fundamentally changing what the model must reconstruct. The paper also positions itself against CLIP-adaptation methods (ViCLIP, UMT, TGM) by being purely self-supervised on video data without requiring video-text pairs or action labels.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The demonstration that synthetic object motions overlaid on black or noise frames (no natural video data) can learn competitive video representations opens a new paradigm for video SSL that decouples representation learning from the availability of natural video corpora. The simple swap of pixel targets for CLIP features as a drop-in improvement to any masked video autoencoder is a practical, low-cost technique likely to be adopted broadly. Open questions include whether the relatively simple synthetic motions (rigid-body translation, rotation, scaling of pasted objects) suffice for capturing the full complexity of real-world motion, and whether the approach scales to larger backbones or longer video sequences where the domain gap between synthetic and natural dynamics may widen.
