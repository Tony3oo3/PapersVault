## Problem

Generic 3D face reconstruction models trained on large in-the-wild datasets use a single set of weights for all individuals, underfitting person-specific facial shapes and wrinkle patterns. When multiple images or a video of the same person are available, existing methods lack a principled way to leverage this additional data for more accurate, person-specific reconstruction while retaining the robustness and expression-detail priors learned at scale. Additionally, boundary landmark misalignment ("landmark marching") in non-frontal views and the ambiguity of self-supervised shape-from-shading for animatable details remain unresolved technical challenges.

## Input / Output

The base model takes a single 256×256 in-the-wild RGB face image (cropped and aligned via RetinaFace) and outputs FLAME shape/expression/pose parameters, a 256×256×3 albedo texture map, a 256×256 UV displacement map in [−0.01, 0.01], orthographic camera parameters (isotropic scale + 2D translation), and 9 spherical-harmonic lighting coefficients, running at 30 fps on an RTX 3090. For person-specific transfer, the input becomes a set of images or video frames of one individual, and the model additionally learns a small set of adapter parameters and a person-specific detail latent while keeping the base model frozen. The displacement map is defined in FLAME's UV space, enabling direct animation by re-warping under new expression parameters.

## Method

The architecture is a two-stage pipeline: a ViT encoder (16×16 patches) regresses FLAME and appearance parameters for coarse reconstruction via differentiable rendering, followed by a zero-masking-ratio MAE that predicts an animatable UV displacement map conditioned on the warped image texture and a novel bounded, antisymmetric facial tension map. A key innovation is the silhouette vertex re-projection loss, which, for occluded boundary landmarks, matches 2D annotations to the nearest 3D silhouette edge vertex (identified by opposing z-normal signs on adjacent vertices, excluding nose/ear regions) rather than relying on FLAME's dynamic landmark marching. For detail training, a UNet teacher—trained with photometric, MRF, smoothness, and regularization losses—provides pseudo-ground-truth displacement maps that guide the MAE student via photometric and SSIM losses, resolving the non-unique shape-to-shading mapping. Person-specific transfer is achieved by inserting lightweight adapter modules (residual, post-feedforward, pre-layer-norm) into the frozen ViT blocks and training only the adapters, the detail latent, and layer-norm parameters on the target person's images for a few minutes.

## Results

On the 300-W single-image benchmark, the base model achieves the lowest overall landmark RMSE (0.0401) among all compared methods, improving boundary error by 9.1% and inner error by 13.2% over the same architecture without the proposed silhouette vertex re-projection loss. On FaceScape multi-view reconstruction, the base model reduces overall error to 0.0429, outperforming DECA (0.0496), EMOCA-v2 (0.0600), 3DDFA-v2 (0.0700), and SynergyNet (0.0785). The person-specific transferred model further halves the error on 300-VW (overall 0.0252 vs. 0.0488 for the base) and FaceScape (overall 0.0305 vs. 0.0429), demonstrating the substantial benefit of adapter-based personalization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The base model outperforms all five baselines on 300-W overall landmark RMSE (0.0401 vs. DECA 0.0446, EMOCA-v2 0.0430, 3DDFA-v2 0.0470, SynergyNet 0.0545), with the largest gap over SynergyNet (~26% reduction). On 300-VW, the base model is competitive with DECA (0.0488 vs. 0.0460) and clearly beats EMOCA-v2 (0.0488 vs. 0.0519), 3DDFA-v2 (0.0488 vs. 0.0524), and SynergyNet (0.0488 vs. 0.0651). On FaceScape, the base model achieves the best overall error (0.0429), beating DECA by 13.5%, EMOCA-v2 by 28.5%, 3DDFA-v2 by 38.6%, and SynergyNet by 45.4%. The person-specific transferred model (with adapters) dramatically surpasses all baselines and the base model itself, achieving 300-VW overall error of 0.0252 (48% lower than the base) and FaceScape overall error of 0.0305 (29% lower than the base), with the smallest standard deviation across identities (±0.0060 vs. ±0.0103 for DECA). Qualitatively, the person-specific model shows visibly sharper wrinkle details and tighter boundary alignment compared to DECA and EMOCA-v2 in Figures 6–8.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the DECA/EMOCA line of self-supervised, two-stage (coarse FLAME + UV displacement detail) 3D face reconstruction by replacing the CNN backbone with a ViT-MAE architecture and introducing a person-specific transfer mechanism via lightweight adapters, a concept borrowed from NLP parameter-efficient fine-tuning. The silhouette vertex re-projection loss departs from FLAME's dynamic landmark marching and prior occlusion-handling strategies (e.g., [58, 70, 39]) by directly matching 2D silhouette annotations to 3D boundary edge vertices determined by normal orientation. The teacher-student detail training repurposes a UNet as a pseudo-ground-truth generator to resolve the shape-from-shading ambiguity that plagues purely photometric detail supervision, while the MAE student retains animation and occlusion robustness that a UNet alone cannot provide.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The person-specific adapter transfer is, to the authors' knowledge, the first to make a large generic 3D face model yield individualized reconstructions with only a small set of trainable parameters, opening a path toward scalable per-identity avatars without per-person optimization. The silhouette vertex re-projection loss addresses the long-standing 'landmark marching' misalignment that degrades boundary fitting in non-frontal views, a problem that has persisted across multiple prior methods. Open questions include whether the adapter capacity is sufficient for highly variable identities, how the approach generalizes to identities with very few training images, and whether the FLAME topology constraint limits the expressiveness of person-specific geometry. The method's reliance on video datasets for detail training and its 30 fps inference speed make it practically relevant for real-time face animation and virtual avatar pipelines.
