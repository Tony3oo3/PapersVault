## Problem

Diffusion-based image-to-video (I2V) models can animate still images into realistic videos, creating risks of privacy violation, misinformation, and copyright infringement. Prior adversarial protection work targeted image editing or text-to-image generation but did not address the unique challenges of I2V: disrupting temporal consistency from a single-image perturbation and maintaining robustness across heterogeneous architectures (UNet vs. DiT) under strong conditioning such as pose or text prompts.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250855/cropped_page0_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a source RGB image and produces a single guarded image with an imperceptible perturbation (PSNR ≈ 31.8 dB, SSIM ≈ 0.868). During optimization, a black target image and black target video frames serve as the degradation targets, and an optional text or pose condition is passed through the I2V pipeline. The guarded image, when fed into any I2V model, yields videos with degraded spatial fidelity and broken temporal coherence. The method also supports local attacks by applying a spatial mask to restrict perturbations to specific objects.

## Method

I2VGuard formulates image protection as a white-box adversarial attack that optimizes a perturbation δ on the input image using three complementary loss modules. The spatial attack shifts the VAE-encoded latent of the guarded image toward a target (black) latent, degrading per-frame visual quality. The temporal attack hooks into the temporal self-attention blocks of the video diffusion generator and pushes the attended map away from the original, inducing chaotic inter-frame motion. The diffusion attack applies a contrastive loss on the denoising module's predicted clean frames, pulling them toward target (black) frames while pushing them away from the original frames, which improves cross-model robustness. The final objective weights all three losses plus an L2 regularization on δ, and the perturbation is optimized via gradient descent over the image pixels (Algorithm 1), requiring ~2.5 min on SVD or ~10 min on CogVideoX with 68–75 GB GPU memory.

## Results

On SVD, I2VGuard degrades Subject Consistency from 95.86% to 91.57% (−4.3%), Aesthetic Quality from 56.76 to 53.42 (−3.34), and Image Quality from 67.28 to 64.38 (−2.90), while Motion Smoothness drops modestly from 97.90% to 97.18%. On CogVideoX, the drops are Subject Consistency 97.02%→93.50% (−3.5%), Aesthetic Quality 59.94→53.95 (−5.99), and Image Quality 67.60→65.24 (−2.36). The perturbation itself remains imperceptible: guarded images achieve PSNR 31.83 and SSIM 0.868, substantially higher than random-noise baseline (PSNR 26.22, SSIM 0.774), confirming minimal visual distortion.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is PhotoGuard (Salman et al., ICML 2023), which applies adversarial perturbations to protect images from malicious editing. The comparison is qualitative (Figure 4): PhotoGuard introduces noise localized on the person that slightly degrades spatial appearance but preserves temporal consistency in the generated video. In contrast, I2VGuard disrupts both spatial content (unusual textures, object deformations) and temporal consistency (chaotic motion, frame-to-frame discontinuities), making it strictly more effective at degrading I2V output. A random-noise baseline (Table 2) shows that unstructured perturbations at comparable magnitude (PSNR 26.22) are largely filtered by the VAE and do not impair generation, whereas I2VGuard's structured perturbation (PSNR 31.83) causes significant quality drops despite being less visible. The paper does not report a head-to-head quantitative table against PhotoGuard; the advantage is demonstrated through visual inspection of generated videos.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of adversarial image protection work (PhotoGuard [37], Zhang et al. [54]) from image-editing tasks to image-to-video generation, where the critical new challenge is disrupting temporal consistency rather than merely spatial fidelity. The spatial attack module directly inherits the encoder-targeting strategy from prior image-editing defenses, while the temporal attack (perturbing temporal attention maps) and diffusion attack (contrastive loss on denoiser outputs) are novel components designed specifically for the video generation pipeline. The paper positions itself as the first adversarial attack targeting I2V diffusion models for security purposes, filling a gap left by prior work that focused exclusively on still-image editing or style mimicry.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As I2V models become commercially available, the ability to animate any person's photo into arbitrary actions raises urgent privacy and copyright concerns that this work addresses for the first time. However, the method is strictly white-box, requires 68–75 GB of GPU memory, and has only been validated on three models (SVD, CogVideoX, ControlNeXt), leaving open critical questions about black-box transferability, robustness to real-world preprocessing pipelines, and scalability to closed-source systems like Sora. The moderate quantitative degradation (~4% subject consistency, ~1% motion smoothness) and the finding that stronger architectures (DiT with 3D VAE) resist the attack better suggest that this protection may be fragile as I2V models continue to improve.
