## Problem

Existing instruction-based image editing datasets are limited in editing type diversity (typically 4–9 types), suffer from low data quality due to fixed pipelines and inherent data biases, and lack support for complex multi-modal perception tasks such as viewpoint changes, commonsense-reasoning edits, and reference-based visual editing. This limits the ability of editing models to handle the full range of real-world creative editing requests.

## Input / Output

The model accepts an original RGB image, a natural-language editing instruction, and an optional visual prompt (depth map, segmentation mask, scribble, sketch, bounding box, or reference image) that is only active for the Visual Editing category. It outputs a single edited image at the same resolution as the input, with the editing scope (local region vs. global scene vs. viewpoint) determined automatically by the task type. At inference, an LLM (e.g., LLaMA-3) first classifies the instruction into one of 25 editing types to select the appropriate task embedding before diffusion decoding.

## Method

AnySD builds on Stable Diffusion 1.5 and introduces three architectural components: (1) a visual prompt projector that aligns frozen CLIP image features with instruction embeddings via cross-attention to condition the UNet on optional visual inputs; (2) a task-aware routing mechanism using Mixture-of-Experts blocks where each expert shares the same text cross-attention layer but has distinct visual attention weights, with a router distributing weights based on the task embedding to control edit granularity (local vs. global vs. viewpoint); and (3) learnable task embeddings inserted before the MoE blocks to coordinate complexity across 25 editing types and prevent catastrophic forgetting. Training proceeds in two stages: Stage I freezes the router and pre-trains the UNet for general instruction understanding with classifier-free guidance, while Stage II fine-tunes the MoE visual weights, router, projector, and task embeddings for task-specific editing granularity. The model is trained exclusively on the 2.5M-pair AnyEdit dataset, which is collected via an adaptive pipeline that selects task-specific editing methods, uses counterfactual synthetic scenes to balance concept distribution, and applies instruction-validation pre-filters plus CLIP/DINO-based image-quality post-filters.

## Results

On the EMU-Edit Test benchmark, SD1.5w/AnyEdit (training data swap only) achieves 0.866 CLIPim and 0.284 CLIPout, outperforming the previous best EMU-Edit (0.859/0.231) and UltraEdit-SD3 (0.845/0.283). The full AnySD architecture further improves to 0.872 CLIPim, 0.285 CLIPout, 0.070 L1, and 0.821 DINO on EMU-Edit Test, and 0.881 DINO on MagicBrush Test, setting new records on both standard benchmarks. On the more challenging AnyEdit-Test benchmark, AnyEdit achieves the best scores across all five editing categories (Local, Global, Camera Movement, Implicit, Visual), with particularly large gains on Visual editing (0.801 CLIPim vs. 0.717 for Uni-ControlNet) and Camera Movement (0.110 L1 vs. 0.171 for HIVEc).

## Baselines & Comparisons

AnyEdit (SD1.5 backbone) decisively outperforms PnP and Null-Text on all four metrics across both benchmarks, with CLIPim gains of +0.345 and +0.105 over PnP and Null-Text respectively on EMU-Edit. Against the instruction-based baselines, AnyEdit beats InstructPix2Pix by +0.032 CLIPim and +0.065 CLIPout on EMU-Edit, and surpasses SD1.5w/MagicBrush by +0.028 CLIPim and +0.062 CLIPout. The closest competitor is EMU-Edit, which AnyEdit edges out on CLIPim (0.866 vs. 0.859) and CLIPout (0.284 vs. 0.231) but trails slightly on DINO (0.812 vs. 0.819) and L1 (0.095 vs. 0.094). UltraEdit (SD3) achieves a lower L1 (0.071) but AnyEdit wins on CLIPim (0.866 vs. 0.845) and DINO (0.812 vs. 0.794); notably UltraEdit does not report MagicBrush results. On the AnyEdit-Test benchmark, AnyEdit dominates HIVEc across Local (0.863 vs. 0.845 CLIPim), Global (0.788 vs. 0.787), Camera Movement (0.833 vs. 0.838 CLIPim but 0.110 vs. 0.171 L1), and Implicit (0.867 vs. 0.862 CLIPim) categories, and substantially outperforms Uni-ControlNet on Visual editing (0.801 vs. 0.717 CLIPim, 0.628 vs. 0.442 DINO).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the InstructPix2Pix paradigm (Brooks et al., CVPR 2023) of training Stable Diffusion 1.5 to follow natural-language editing instructions, extending it from a fixed 4-type editing taxonomy to a 25-type, 5-category framework that includes camera movement, implicit (commonsense-requiring) editing, and multi-modal visual-conditioned editing. It departs from prior dataset construction (MagicBrush, EMU-Edit, UltraEdit, SEED-Data-Edit) by introducing counterfactual synthetic scenes to counteract inherent concept co-occurrence biases, and by replacing a single fixed editing pipeline with an adaptive pipeline that routes to type-specific generation strategies. Architecturally, it extends the base diffusion model with a Mixture-of-Experts task-aware routing mechanism and learnable task embeddings, going beyond the single-UNet fine-tuning approach of InstructPix2Pix and the simpler conditioning of EMU-Edit.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 2.5M-pair AnyEdit dataset and the 1,250-pair AnyEdit-Test benchmark fill a critical gap: prior editing datasets covered at most 9 types and lacked complex scenarios (viewpoint changes, implicit reasoning, visual references), making it difficult to train or evaluate truly general-purpose editing models. The finding that 2.5M carefully filtered AnyEdit pairs outperform 4M UltraEdit pairs on quality metrics underscores that curation and diversity matter more than raw scale. Open questions include whether the MoE routing generalizes to genuinely novel editing types absent from training, whether the two-stage training protocol scales to larger backbones (SD3, FLUX), and how the approach handles multi-turn or compositional editing sequences that real users would naturally request.
