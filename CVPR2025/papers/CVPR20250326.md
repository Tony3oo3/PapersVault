## Problem

Vanilla data augmentation in low-level vision tasks (e.g., super-resolution) suffers from a critical information-loss bottleneck: random region selection often captures flat, low-detail areas that provide minimal learning signal for reconstruction. Saliency-based DA methods from high-level vision do not transfer directly due to mismatched task objectives and inappropriate feature requirements, leaving low-level DA largely stuck with rudimentary geometric and mixing strategies.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250326/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework operates on LR-HR image pairs (e.g., 4× downsampled via bicubic for SR, or JPEG-compressed for artifact removal). CAM consumes a single low-resolution image and outputs a pixel-wise saliency map of the same spatial dimensions, which ADD/ADD+ then use to define irregular binary masks over both the LR and HR sides simultaneously. The augmented samples preserve the original image dimensions and channel count, with the mask selecting a top-p proportion of highest-saliency pixels as the cut region.

## Method

The core contribution is Calibrated Attribution Maps (CAM), which replaces LAM's local pixel-wise gradient detection with a Global Feature Detector (Sobel-based edge magnitude) and introduces Calibrated Path Integrated Gradients (CPIG) that limit per-step gradient deviation via a binary mask and a calibration factor δ derived from current-vs-target loss differences. ADD uses the resulting saliency map to extract an irregular maximum-saliency patch and applies two augmentation strategies: a mixed strategy (λ-weighted blending of two LR-HR pairs within the mask) and an intensity strategy (bicubic up/down-sampling of the masked patch to create scale-mismatched training signals). ADD+ extends this with an additional cut-and-paste operation that transplants the saliency patch onto a different image. The design follows the principle of a wider spectrum of degradation patterns: continuous (irregular) boundaries to avoid edge artifacts, and diverse augmentation strategies applied stochastically per iteration rather than a single fixed transformation.

## Results

On the DIV2K benchmark (×4 SR), ADD+ improves PSNR by up to +0.14 dB over the RCAN baseline (29.22 → 29.36 dB) and +0.11 dB over EDSR (29.21 → 29.32 dB), while SwinIR gains +0.08 dB (29.40 → 29.48 dB). On the more challenging RealSR dataset, gains are substantially larger: ADD+ achieves +0.26 dB over RCAN (29.20 → 29.46 dB), +0.28 dB over EDSR (28.86 → 29.14 dB), and +0.17 dB over SwinIR (29.26 → 29.43 dB). Across all five standard test sets (Set5, Set14, Manga109, Urban100, BSD100) at both ×2 and ×4 scales, ADD+ consistently outperforms baselines by 0.03–0.26 dB PSNR, with the largest gains on Urban100 and Manga109.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares ADD/ADD+ against three vanilla DA baselines: CutMix [Yun et al., ICCV 2019], CutMixup, and CutBlur [Yoo et al., CVPR 2020]. ADD+ consistently outperforms all three baselines across every backbone and dataset. On DIV2K, ADD+ gains +0.14 dB over RCAN versus only +0.02 dB for CutMix and +0.06 dB for CutMixup; on RealSR the advantage widens to +0.26 dB (ADD+) versus +0.05 dB (CutMix) and +0.10 dB (CutMixup). The gap is most pronounced on the real-world RealSR dataset, where saliency-guided augmentation provides a 2–5× larger improvement than vanilla methods. Even the transformer-based SwinIR, which is less sensitive to DA in general, still benefits more from ADD+ (+0.08/+0.17 dB) than from CutMix (+0.00/+0.03 dB) or CutBlur (+0.03/+0.06 dB), confirming the robustness of the attribution-driven approach across architecture families.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the attribution-based interpretability line (Integrated Gradients, LAM for SR networks) into the data augmentation domain, proposing Calibrated Attribution Maps (CAM) that combine a Sobel-based Global Feature Detector with a calibrated path-integrated gradient to produce noise-robust saliency maps for low-level vision. It departs from high-level vision saliency-based DA methods (PuzzleMix, Co-Mixup, SaliencyMix, Attentive CutMix) by arguing that their design assumptions—classification-oriented feature importance, discrete patch boundaries—mismatch the pixel-fidelity requirements of reconstruction tasks like super-resolution. The resulting ADD/ADD+ framework is the first to use attribution maps as the selection mechanism for which regions to cut and mix during training, positioning itself between vanilla geometric/intensity DA and the more aggressive mixing strategies (CutMix, CutMixup, CutBlur) already applied to SR.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a plug-and-play DA recipe (compatible with EDSR, RCAN, SwinIR) and a concrete design principle—'wider spectrum of degradation patterns'—that practitioners can apply when engineering augmentation for any low-level reconstruction task. However, the absolute gains are modest (≤0.28 dB on PSNR), the attribution computation adds per-sample overhead during training, and the evaluation is limited to SR and JPEG artifact removal, leaving open whether the approach transfers to denoising, deblurring, or other restoration tasks. A key open question is whether the calibrated gradient mechanism is essential or whether simpler saliency sources (e.g., gradient magnitude, attention weights) would yield comparable gains at lower cost.
