## Problem

Current continual learning methods retrain the same samples at short recall intervals, preventing them from benefiting from the spacing effect described by Ebbinghaus' forgetting curve theory. While this cognitive science principle is well-established for human memory, it has been largely unexploited in neural network continual learning, where catastrophic forgetting of previously learned tasks remains a critical challenge.

## Input / Output

The method takes standard RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 64×64 for Tiny-ImageNet) with class labels during training, but each sample is augmented into V views (e.g., V=4) that are processed together as a single view-batch rather than as separate samples. The output remains a standard class label prediction, and the method is designed as a drop-in replacement that does not alter the input/output interface of the underlying continual learning method, requiring no extra computational cost beyond the augmentation itself.

## Method

The view-batch model (VBM) restructures the training schedule by replacing sample-batch replay with view-batch replay: instead of a batch containing B unique samples, each batch contains B samples each expanded into V augmented views, extending the recall interval between retraining the same sample by a factor of V while reducing the effective batch size to B/V to keep total samples seen constant. To compensate for the delayed recall interval and ensure extensive learning from each sample, a one-to-many KL divergence self-supervised loss is added, which minimizes the average divergence between a weakly-augmented view (horizontal flip) and V−1 strongly-augmented views (auto-augmentation) at the logit level, encouraging the network to learn view-invariant features. The final training objective jointly minimizes the supervised cross-entropy loss over all views and the SSL loss, and the approach is applied as a plug-in modification to existing rehearsal (iCaRL, DER++, ER, TCIL) and rehearsal-free (LwF, SLCA) continual learning methods across ResNet-18 and ViT-B/16 backbones.

## Results

On S-CIFAR-10 with a 200-sample buffer, VBM-iCaRL achieves 81.25% average accuracy (CIL 69.73%, TIL 92.76%), improving over iCaRL's 77.16% by +4.09 points, while VBM-DER++ reaches 80.65% average (+4.51 over DER++'s 76.14%). In the non-rehearsal setting (buffer 0), VBM-LwF dramatically improves TIL accuracy from 61.98% to 77.53% on S-CIFAR-10 (+15.55) and from 15.26% to 51.21% on S-Tiny-ImageNet (+35.95). With a 500-sample buffer, VBM-iCaRL achieves the largest gain of +5.50 points (75.54% → 81.04% Avg) on S-CIFAR-10. On the pre-trained model-based protocol (Table 5), VBM-SLCA improves average top-1 accuracy from 94.31% to 94.52% on S-CIFAR-100 and from 83.58% to 84.61% on S-ImageNet-R.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The view-batch model is applied as a drop-in replacement to 16 baseline methods and achieves consistent improvements in nearly all settings. The largest gains appear in the non-rehearsal setting: VBM-LwF improves TIL accuracy by +15.55 on S-CIFAR-10 and +35.95 on S-Tiny-ImageNet over LwF [29]. Among rehearsal-based methods, VBM-iCaRL [32] gains +4.09 to +5.50 Avg points over iCaRL across buffer sizes 200–500 on S-CIFAR-10, and VBM-DER++ [3] gains +4.51 (buffer 200) to +3.30 (buffer 500). VBM-ER [34] improves over ER by +2.10 to +3.54 Avg points, and VBM-TCIL [24] gains +1.90 to +4.62 on S-CIFAR-100 across 5/10/20-step CIL. In the pre-trained model-based protocol, VBM-SLCA [46] improves over SLCA by +0.21 Avg on S-CIFAR-100 and +1.03 on S-ImageNet-R, while prompt-based baselines (L2P [42], DualPrompt [41], CODA-Prompt [38], S-iPrompt [40]) are reported for reference without VBM variants. The only negative result is VBM-DER++ on S-Tiny-ImageNet with buffer 500 (−0.92 Avg), suggesting diminishing returns when the buffer is large enough to already provide sufficient replay diversity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the spacing effect from cognitive science (Ebbinghaus' forgetting curve) as a training-schedule principle for continual learning, a connection previously explored only in NLP (Amiri et al. 2017) and dialogue systems (Zhong et al. 2024) but not systematically applied to vision-based CL. Methodologically, it reinterprets data augmentation not as a regularization tool but as a mechanism to stretch the recall interval between retraining the same sample, and pairs this with a lightweight one-to-many KL-divergence self-supervised loss reminiscent of BYOL/SimCLR but confined to a single sample's augmented views. The approach is explicitly positioned as a drop-in replacement for existing rehearsal and rehearsal-free CL methods (iCaRL, DER++, TCIL, SLCA), requiring no architectural modification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean, empirically validated explanation for why existing CL methods underperform: their short recall intervals prevent the spacing effect from operating, so networks relearn samples before they have sufficiently forgotten them. The practical takeaway—that a simple rescheduling of the training loop (view-batch replay) plus a small SSL loss yields consistent 1–5 point gains across protocols, backbones, and buffer sizes without extra compute—is immediately actionable for CL practitioners. Open questions include whether the optimal recall interval (x3–x4 in their experiments) is truly universal or depends on task difficulty, dataset scale, and architecture depth, and whether the principle transfers to non-vision modalities or to settings where the number of training samples per task is very small.
