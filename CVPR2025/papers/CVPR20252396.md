## Problem

Existing MLLM-based self-reflection frameworks for robots can identify failures at the semantic level but rely on predefined skill libraries or high-level subgoals to execute corrections, failing to provide fine-grained 'how to correct' action guidance. This limits their ability to recover from failures in contact-rich manipulation tasks where precise, low-level action adjustment is required.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252396/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The system consumes RGB visual observations and a text task description, processed by a fine-tuned LLaVA-v1.5 at 5 Hz to produce coarse-grained motion instructions (37 types, e.g., 'move arm right with gripper closed'). A motion-conditioned diffusion policy then fuses these instructions with visual observations to output 20 Hz high-frequency robotic action sequences. The two-stage frequency design (5 Hz decision, 20 Hz execution) decouples semantic reasoning from real-time control.

## Method

Phoenix introduces a dual-process motion adjustment mechanism: a Motion Prediction Module (MPM) trained on 160K expert demonstration pairs generates initial motion instructions, while a Motion Correction Module (MCM) fine-tuned on 16K failure-correction data (online human interventions, offline annotations, expert demos) evaluates and adjusts them via hierarchical chain-of-thought reasoning. A multi-task motion-conditioned diffusion policy translates instructions into precise actions using two key architectural modifications: a learnable motion codebook that provides discriminative features for different instruction types, and separate conditioning of visual observations and motion features at different diffusion stages to prevent the policy from ignoring motion guidance. A lifelong learning loop iteratively co-fine-tunes the MPM on refined interaction trajectories mixed with expert demonstrations to achieve self-improvement without catastrophic forgetting.

## Results

On 9 contact-rich RoboMimic manipulation tasks, Phoenix achieves a mean success rate of 57.8%, outperforming the strongest baseline (Subgoal Self-reflection at 48.0%) by 9.8 percentage points and OpenVLA (38.0%) by 19.8 points. The method shows particularly large gains on long-horizon tasks (ThreePieceAssembly_D0: 52% vs. 34% for Subgoal Self-reflection) and fine-grained tasks (Coffee_D0: 94% vs. 80%). The human intervention oracle reaches 78.9%, indicating Phoenix closes roughly 50% of the gap to perfect correction. In real-world experiments, Phoenix achieves 75% on the in-distribution drawer task and 55% under pose disruption, versus 60% and 35% for the motion-conditioned baseline without self-reflection.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Phoenix outperforms all baselines across the 9 RoboMimic tasks. Compared to OpenVLA (38.0% mean), Phoenix gains 19.8 points, with the largest per-task advantage on Coffee_D0 (+52 pts) and ThreePieceAssembly_D0 (+24 pts). Against the closest semantic self-reflection baseline (Subgoal Self-reflection, 48.0%), Phoenix leads by 9.8 points on average, with notable margins on long-horizon tasks (ThreePieceAssembly_D0: 52% vs. 34%) and the fine-grained Threading task (68% vs. 80%—here the subgoal method actually wins, but Phoenix compensates on other tasks). The motion-conditioned policy without self-reflection (46.9%) is surpassed by 10.9 points, confirming that the dual-process correction mechanism adds substantial value beyond motion instruction prediction alone. The human intervention oracle (78.9%) remains well above Phoenix, suggesting room for improvement but validating that the motion-conditioned diffusion policy can effectively execute corrected instructions.

## Relation to Prior Work

Phoenix extends the line of MLLM-guided robotic manipulation by introducing motion instruction as an intermediate representation between high-level semantic reflection and low-level action execution. It builds directly on Diffusion Policy [5] as the action generation backbone, modifying it with a learnable motion codebook and decoupled conditioning stages, and adapts the hierarchical control philosophy of RT-H [2] by using coarse-grained motion commands rather than free-form language. The key departure from prior semantic self-reflection frameworks (e.g., PaLM-E-style subgoal decomposition) is the shift from relying on predefined skill libraries to generating continuous, fine-grained motion adjustments that a learned policy can directly execute.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a practical gap in MLLM-robotic systems: semantic reflection tells a robot *what* went wrong but not *how* to physically correct it, and this paper provides a concrete mechanism to close that loop. The dual-process separation (prediction vs. correction) and the finding that unified mixture training underperforms due to data-scale imbalance offer practical guidance for designing MLLM-based correction pipelines. Open questions include whether the 37-type motion instruction vocabulary scales to more diverse manipulation domains, whether the approach generalizes beyond LLaVA-v1.5 to larger or more recent MLLMs, and how the lifelong learning loop behaves over longer horizons without human intervention.
