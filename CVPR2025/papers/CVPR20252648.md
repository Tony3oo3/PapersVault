## Problem

Current video MLLMs rely on uniform frame sampling with image-level encoders, producing redundant token sequences and limited motion awareness, since temporal dynamics are left entirely to the LLM. Additionally, no existing benchmark specifically evaluates a model's sensitivity to different motion patterns (linear, curved, rotational, contact-based), making it impossible to measure motion understanding in isolation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252648/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes H.264-compressed video streams segmented into GOPs, where each GOP contains one 384×384 RGB I-frame and up to 8 uniformly sampled 96×96 motion vector frames (macroblock size 4), plus a text instruction. This compressed-domain input naturally forms a slow-fast structure—fewer dense RGB frames paired with more sparse motion vectors—reducing visual tokens to 648 for 8 GOPs versus 2048 for 8 decoded frames. Output is a free-form text response (QA answer or caption) generated by the LLM.

## Method

EMA introduces a motion-aware GOP encoder that decouples spatial and motion encoding within each GOP: SigLIP-so400m encodes the I-frame with 3×3 adaptive pooling, while a lightweight 2-layer transformer encodes patchified motion vectors augmented with learnable temporal positional embeddings. A cross-attention fusion module (with a mean-pooling aggregator over the motion sequence) then integrates motion semantics into the spatial features, producing a fixed-length GOP representation with the same token count as a single frame. These GOP features are projected via an MLP, concatenated with temporal prompts, and fed into Qwen2-7B. Training follows a two-stage protocol—visual-text alignment on LLaVA-558k/Valley-702k, then instruction tuning on 2.4M samples from Cauldron/VideoChat2-IT—preceded by a motion encoder warmup on SSV2 using only motion vectors with a classification head.

## Results

On video QA benchmarks, EMA achieves 75.8% accuracy (score 4.1) on MSVD-QA, 58.5% (3.5) on MSRVTT-QA, and 52.1% (3.5) on ActivityNet-QA, setting new state-of-the-art on MSVD-QA and ActivityNet-QA. On the newly introduced MotionBench, EMA reaches 50.0% average accuracy across four motion types (linear, curved, rotational, contact), substantially outperforming frame-sampling baselines (36.0–37.5%). On the long-video benchmark VideoMME, EMA achieves 58.4% overall (with subtitles), surpassing the previous best LongVA (54.3%). Critically, EMA uses only 648 visual tokens versus 2048 for frame-based methods, yielding a 3.08× inference speedup over Video-LLaVA and 2.14× over LLaMA-VID.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EMA outperforms all frame-sampling baselines on MSVD-QA (75.8 vs. MovieChat 75.2, Video-LaVIT 73.2, Video-LLaVA 70.7) and ActivityNet-QA (52.1 vs. Video-LaVIT 50.1, LLaMA-VID 47.4). On MSRVTT-QA, EMA (58.5) trails Video-LaVIT (59.3) and Video-LLaVA (59.2) by a narrow margin of 0.7–0.8 points, while matching Video-LLaVA's score metric (3.5). The most striking advantage appears on MotionBench, where EMA's 50.0% average accuracy exceeds Video-LLaVA (36.0%) and LLaMA-VID (37.5%) by 12.5 and 12.5 points respectively, confirming the GOP encoder's superior motion capture. On VideoMME, EMA (58.4% with subtitles) surpasses LongVA (54.3%) and Chat-UniVi-v1.5 (45.9%), demonstrating scalability to long videos. In terms of efficiency, EMA requires only 648 visual tokens (vs. 2048 for Video-LLaVA and LLaMA-VID) and achieves 3.08× and 2.14× inference speedups respectively, while maintaining or exceeding their accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

EMA departs from the dominant paradigm in video MLLMs—uniform frame sampling followed by per-frame image encoding—by directly consuming the compressed video stream (H.264 GOPs) as input, repurposing the codec's native slow-fast structure (dense I-frames + sparse motion vectors) as a token-efficient representation. This connects to a lineage of compressed-video action recognition work (slow-i-fast-p, Mm-ViT, compressed video contrastive learning) that exploited motion vectors for temporal modeling, but those methods targeted classification rather than open-ended language generation. The closest prior MLLM, Video-LaVIT, also decouples keyframes and motion vectors from compressed video but simply concatenates their tokens; EMA's GOP encoder instead fuses them via cross-attention into a fixed-length feature matching a single frame's token budget, reducing redundancy at the input level rather than through post-hoc pooling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that a 7B video MLLM can match or exceed frame-sampling baselines on video QA while using roughly one-third the visual tokens and achieving ~3× faster inference, making long-video understanding more tractable on consumer hardware. MotionBench fills a genuine gap: prior video benchmarks conflate spatial and temporal reasoning, making it impossible to isolate whether a model actually understands motion dynamics. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to newer codecs (H.265/HEVC, AV1) with different GOP structures, whether the lightweight two-layer motion encoder is sufficient for complex multi-object motion, and whether the H.264-specific macroblock granularity (4×4) limits spatial precision for fine-grained motion tasks.
