## Problem

Generalizable NeRF methods render novel views by densely sampling every ray with a fixed number of points, which is computationally expensive and redundant given that natural scenes are piecewise smooth. Existing efficiency improvements either reduce per-ray samples with a fixed count or require per-scene optimization, leaving a gap in adaptively reducing both the number of rays and the number of samples per ray based on local scene complexity.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251689/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes V nearby source RGB views (typically 2 or 3) along with their camera intrinsics and extrinsics, and produces a high-resolution novel view RGB image (e.g., 512×640 on DTU, 640×960 on Real Forward-facing). A tunable bundle size K×K (e.g., 2×2 or 4×4) controls the trade-off between speed and quality. When applied to MVS-Gaussian, the output additionally includes per-pixel 3D Gaussian parameters for downstream splatting-based rendering.

## Method

The core idea groups K×K adjacent target-view rays into a cone and samples inscribed spheres within it, reducing the total ray count by ~1/K². Each sphere is encoded with two complementary representations: a joint bundle representation extracted via mipmap-based pre-filtering from source-view feature maps (capturing smooth, low-frequency content), and a ray-specific representation of pixel-aligned colors (preserving high-frequency detail). A depth-guided adaptive sampling strategy then dynamically sets the number of spheres per cone as N_C = max(ceil(2R/δ_s), N_max), where R is the predicted depth range, concentrating samples at depth discontinuities and using fewer in smooth regions. Volume rendering accumulates per-sphere features along each cone to produce bundle-level and ray-level feature maps, which are decoded via residual convolutions and sub-pixel upsampling into a coarse RGB map and a fine RGB map, summed to yield the final image.

## Results

On the DTU test set (3-view setting), ENeRF+Ours (2×2) achieves 28.86 dB PSNR, 0.964 SSIM, and 0.073 LPIPS at 28.6 FPS, improving over the original ENeRF by 1.27 dB PSNR and 47% FPS while using only 0.42 samples per ray. The 4×4 bundle variant further accelerates rendering to 43.6 FPS (over 2× ENeRF's speed) with a slight quality trade-off (28.21 dB PSNR). On cross-dataset evaluation, ENeRF+Ours (2×2) achieves 24.33 dB PSNR on Real Forward-facing and 26.49 dB on NeRF Synthetic, matching or exceeding all baselines in the speed-quality trade-off.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ENeRF+Ours (2×2) outperforms the original ENeRF [22] by 1.27 dB PSNR (28.86 vs. 27.61) and 47% FPS (28.6 vs. 19.5), while using only 0.42 samples per ray versus ENeRF's 2. Against MuRF [43], which achieves 28.76 dB PSNR but at only 0.934 FPS with 80 samples/ray, ENeRF+Ours (2×2) matches quality (28.86 dB) at 30× the speed. Compared to MVSGaussian [24] (28.21 dB, 21.5 FPS), ENeRF+Ours (2×2) gains 0.65 dB PSNR and 33% FPS. The method trails ConvGLR [36] on PSNR (28.86 vs. 31.65 dB) but runs 35× faster (28.6 vs. 0.825 FPS), making it the best quality-speed trade-off among all baselines. The 4×4 variant sacrifices ~0.65 dB PSNR relative to 2×2 but nearly doubles speed to 43.6 FPS, offering a tunable quality-efficiency knob not available in any baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends ENeRF's depth-guided sampling by replacing per-ray fixed-count sampling with a bundle-based strategy that groups adjacent rays into cones and samples inscribed spheres jointly, grounded in plenoptic sampling theory. It adapts the mipmap-based pre-filtering concept from scene-specific anti-aliasing NeRFs (Tri-MipNeRF) into a multi-view generalizable encoding framework, and applies the same bundle sampling to the 3D-Gaussian splatting setting via MVS-Gaussian. The key departure from prior generalizable NeRFs (MVSNeRF, CG-NeRF, ConvGLR, MuRF) is the explicit reduction of the number of rays processed rather than merely reducing samples per ray, combined with adaptive sample allocation based on local depth confidence.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is a meaningful efficiency-quality Pareto improvement: up to 2× faster rendering with comparable or better PSNR, and a tunable bundle size that lets practitioners trade speed for fidelity. A notable limitation is that gains on MVS-Gaussian are modest because its single-sample-per-ray design and post-processing pipeline constrain how much the bundle strategy can help, suggesting deeper architectural integration would be needed. Open questions include whether adaptive per-region bundle sizing (rather than a global K×K) could further improve quality in high-frequency areas, and whether the approach extends to unbounded or dynamic scenes where depth confidence is less reliable.
