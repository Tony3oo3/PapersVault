## Problem

Existing hallucination benchmarks for MLLMs conflate two distinct failure modes: the model genuinely failing to perceive visual content versus the model perceiving it correctly but being misled by the question's phrasing. This paper isolates the second case—where an MLLM demonstrably understands the image yet produces an incorrect answer when the question contains hypothetical alterations, irrelevant details, or erroneous premises—and asks why this happens and how to fix it.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251090/cropped_page0_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

Each benchmark sample pairs an RGB image with a four-option multiple-choice question that is either positive (directly inquiring about observable content) or negative (introducing hypothetical modifications, irrelevant descriptions, or incorrect assumptions about the image). The model outputs a selected option, which is scored via two Bayesian-conditional metrics: Misleading Rate (MR), the fraction of understood-but-misled cases among all understood cases, and Robustness Accuracy (RA), the fraction of samples where the model both understands and resists misdirection. The training pipeline consumes COCO images and GPT-4o-generated structured visual descriptions to produce 112k paired positive-negative instruction-tuning samples.

## Method

The paper introduces the MMVU benchmark (1,786 paired questions across 12 categories at character, attribute, and context levels) and diagnoses two root causes via attention-map and logit analysis: (1) instruction-tuning datasets are overwhelmingly positive, biasing models toward affirmative responses, and (2) answer tokens attend least to visual tokens, with negative questions further reducing question-to-visual attention and lowering output-probability confidence. To address the data bias, a GPT-4o-driven pipeline implicitly extracts six types of visual information (text/numbers, objects, attributes, relationships, activities, overall semantics) from COCO images and generates paired positive-negative samples (MMVU-Train, 112k pairs) for fine-tuning. To address the attention imbalance, two inference-time strategies are proposed: Content Guided Refinement (CGR), a two-step prompt that first elicits structured visual descriptions before answering, and Visual Attention Refinement (VAR), which extracts per-token attention scores from the MLLM and applies a spatial mask to the input image to suppress low-attention regions and amplify question-relevant visual features.

## Results

On the MMVU benchmark (1,786 paired positive/negative questions across 12 categories), the best-performing models achieve only ~67% Robustness Accuracy (RA): Ovis1.6-Gemma2-9B and Llama3.2-90B-Vision-Instruct both reach 66.74% RA, while GPT-4o achieves 65.06% RA. The Misleading Rate (MR) — the fraction of models that understand the image but are fooled by negative questions — ranges from 18.47% (Llama3.2-90B) to 62.30% (VILA1.5-13B), confirming that even top models are significantly vulnerable to misleading phrasing. Fine-tuning with the proposed MMVU-Train dataset (112k paired samples) improves RA by up to +11.98 points (VILA1.5-13B: 25.76→37.74) and reduces MR from 62.30% to 36.87%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among closed-source models, GPT-4o leads with 65.06% RA and 19.53% MR, outperforming Qwen-VL-max (63.36/25.35), Claude 3.5 Sonnet (55.32/22.69), and Gemini-1.5-Pro (39.53/35.11). Among open-source models, Ovis1.6-Gemma2-9B and Llama3.2-90B tie at 66.74% RA, with Llama3.2-90B achieving the lowest MR (18.47%) — slightly better than GPT-4o's 19.53%. All 15 models substantially outperform random (25% RA) but remain far from perfect, with the weakest model (VILA1.5-13B) scoring only 25.76% RA and a 62.30% MR, indicating near-random susceptibility to negative questions. In the training-data comparison (Table 3), MMVU-Train (112k) achieves 45.67% RA and 36.87% MR when fine-tuning a SigLIP+Phi-2 backbone, outperforming LLaVA 158k (29.67/57.62), SVIT 158k (33.67/50.73), and LRV 340k (30.00/58.90) on both metrics despite being smaller in size. For prompting strategies (Table 4), CGR alone improves RA by +0.79 and reduces MR by −0.99 over the LLaVA-v1.6 baseline, while VAR alone gives +0.45 RA and −1.25 MR; combining both yields +0.36 RA and −1.31 MR, showing complementary but non-additive effects.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from existing hallucination and robustness benchmarks (MADBench, POPE, ConBench, NaturalBench) that evaluate MLLM accuracy under perturbations but conflate 'model doesn't understand the image' with 'model understands but is misled by the question.' The key conceptual departure is the paired positive-negative question design, which isolates the specific failure mode of correct visual comprehension paired with incorrect response. On the data side, it extends instruction-tuning data generation (LLaVA, SVIT, LRV, ALLaVA) by introducing explicitly paired positive-negative samples constructed via GPT-4o, contrasting with LRV's unpaired negatives and coarser annotation-driven approach. The attention and logit analysis connects to transformer interpretability work but applies it specifically to the multi-modal decoding setting to diagnose why visual tokens are underutilized.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that even GPT-4o and top open-source models (Ovis, Llama3.2-90B) exhibit 18–20% misleading rates despite demonstrable visual understanding reframes a portion of 'hallucination' as a question-robustness problem rather than a perception problem, which has implications for how the field diagnoses and addresses MLLM errors. The MMVU benchmark and its MR/RA metrics provide a practical new evaluation axis that existing benchmarks lack. However, the proposed solutions (CGR, VAR) yield only modest gains (+0.36 to +0.79 RA), and the training-data approach, while more effective (+8 to +12 RA for weaker models), still leaves substantial room for improvement. Open questions include whether the attention imbalance persists in next-generation architectures, whether the paired-data approach generalizes beyond the 12 tested categories, and whether the modest prompting gains reflect a fundamental limitation of inference-time interventions versus the need for architectural changes.
