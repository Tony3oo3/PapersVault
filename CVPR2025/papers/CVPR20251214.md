## Problem

Existing fine-grained cross-view localization methods either condense the ground image into a single global descriptor (making it unclear which objects drive the localization) or apply homography-based BEV mapping that ignores above-ground structures like buildings. No prior method explicitly identifies which ground-image features correspond to which aerial-image features, leaving localization results uninterpretable and preventing the system from mimicking how humans actually localize by matching a few distinct landmarks.

## Input / Output

The method takes a ground-level RGB image (either a 360° panorama on VIGOR or a front-facing limited-FoV image on KITTI) and an aerial RGB image covering the ground camera's local surroundings with known Ground Sampling Distance (GSD). It outputs the 3 DoF camera pose as pixel coordinates in the aerial image plus yaw orientation, along with interpretable sparse correspondences that trace which specific ground-image features (at specific heights) were matched to aerial-image locations. Points projected outside the ground image's field of view are discarded, and only the top-N highest-confidence matches contribute to the final pose.

## Method

The method lifts ground-image DINOv2 features into a 3D point cloud via deformable cross-attention (BEVFormer-style pillars spanning a height range), then pools each pillar to a BEV descriptor using a learned softmax-weighted selection over height rather than standard sum or max pooling. This learned selection is the key architectural novelty: it is trained end-to-end with only camera-pose supervision (no ground-truth correspondences), and it enables tracing which ground-image features at which heights contribute to the BEV representation. Pose estimation proceeds by computing pairwise descriptor similarity between ground and aerial point planes, applying dualSoftmax with a dustbin term to obtain matching probabilities, sampling the top-N correspondences, and solving the 3 DoF metric pose via differentiable Procrustes (Kabsch) alignment. Training uses a Virtual Correspondence Error loss on the predicted pose plus a bidirectional infoNCE matching loss on the sampled correspondences, and inference optionally wraps the sampling in RANSAC for robustness.

## Results

On VIGOR with known orientation, FG² achieves a mean localization error of 1.95 m (same-area) and 2.41 m (cross-area), reducing the error by 26% and 28% respectively over the previous state-of-the-art HC-Net. On KITTI, the method achieves 0.75 m mean localization error (same-area) and 7.45 m (cross-area), outperforming all baselines on mean error in both settings. With unknown orientation, a two-step inference (Ours†) recovers to 3.78 m mean (same-area) and 5.95 m (cross-area), approaching but not matching CCVPE's single-step performance in this harder setting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against HC-Net [43], the previous state-of-the-art, FG² reduces mean localization error by 26% on VIGOR same-area (1.95 m vs. 2.65 m) and 28% on VIGOR cross-area (2.41 m vs. 3.35 m), while also improving median error. On KITTI cross-area, FG² achieves 7.45 m mean vs. HC-Net's 8.47 m, demonstrating stronger generalization to unseen areas. Against DenseFlow [36] and GGCVT [34], FG² wins on mean localization error in all settings, though DenseFlow achieves a slightly lower median on VIGOR same-area (0.97 m vs. 1.08 m). CCVPE [47] is the only baseline that outperforms FG², but only in the unknown-orientation single-step setting (3.74 m vs. 8.95 m on VIGOR same-area), where its orientation-invariant localization branch has a structural advantage; with the proposed two-step inference, FG† narrows the gap to 3.78 m. SliceMatch [20] is consistently the weakest baseline, with mean errors 2–3× higher than FG² across all settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the fine-grained cross-view localization line of work (SliceMatch, CCVPE, GGCVT, DenseFlow, HC-Net) by replacing homography-based BEV mapping or dense feature alignment with an explicit 3D point cloud lifting followed by learned feature selection along the height dimension. It draws on recent 3D point cloud alignment techniques (MASt3R, DUSt3R) and deformable attention (BEVFormer) for the ground-to-3D mapping, and on DINOv2 for pretrained image features. The key conceptual departure from prior local-feature methods is the use of sparse Procrustes alignment on sampled correspondences rather than a learned pose regression head, which makes the pose a direct, interpretable function of the matched points. The weakly supervised matching objective (infoNCE on sampled correspondences supervised only by ground-truth pose) follows the contrastive matching paradigm of SuperGlue and LOFTR but applied across the ground-aerial view gap.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 28% mean localization error reduction on VIGOR cross-area, combined with interpretable feature-level correspondences, addresses a practical gap: practitioners can now see *which* ground objects (zebra crossings, building facades, curbs) drive the localization decision, unlike black-box global descriptor or learned-pose methods. The learned height selection mechanism is a lightweight but effective alternative to sum/max pooling that also improves accuracy, suggesting that explicit 3D reasoning over the vertical axis matters even for 3-DoF tasks. A notable limitation is that unknown-orientation performance still lags behind CCVPE without the two-step inference workaround, and the method assumes known GSD and camera height. Open questions include whether the approach scales to very large aerial tiles, handles dynamic scenes better than the current static-image assumption, and whether the Procrustes-based pose can be extended to full 6-DoF estimation.
