## Problem

CLIP is highly vulnerable to adversarial perturbations, and existing defenses (adversarial finetuning, adversarial prompt tuning) require expensive training, overfit to the finetuning distribution, and degrade clean-image accuracy. No training-free, test-time defense exists that leverages CLIP's own pre-trained vision encoder to defend against attacks at inference time.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252629/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single test image (potentially adversarially perturbed) and the frozen pre-trained CLIP vision encoder f_θ as input, with no label or auxiliary network required. It produces a counterattack perturbation δ_ttc bounded by an L∞ budget (ε_ttc = 4/255) that is added to the image before classification. A τ-threshold probe computed from a random noise perturbation at iteration 0 determines whether the image exhibits 'false stability' and should be counterattacked, or whether the random noise alone is returned to preserve clean-image accuracy.

## Method

The key insight is that adversarial images crafted to maximise CLIP's classification loss exhibit 'false stability'—they are trapped in a toxic surrounding in the latent space, making them unusually resistant to small random noise (low τ values). The counterattack objective maximises the L2 distance between the image embedding f_θ(x+δ) and the original anchor embedding f_θ(x), effectively pushing the image away from its toxic region using the expressiveness of the frozen vision encoder. This is optimised via PGD with N steps (typically 2–5), and the final perturbation is an exponentially weighted average of all intermediate perturbations (weights w_j = exp(β·j)/Σexp(β·j)) to capture the trajectory. The τ-thresholded mechanism halts the counterattack early if the random-noise probe indicates the image is not falsely stable (τ ≥ τ_thres = 0.2), preventing unnecessary perturbation of clean images. The entire procedure is training-free, requires no auxiliary networks, and can be applied as a plug-in on top of adversarially finetuned CLIP models.

## Results

Under 10-step PGD at ε_a=1/255, TTC achieves an average robust accuracy gain of +36.47% over original CLIP across 16 downstream datasets, with only a −1.76% drop in clean accuracy. At the stronger attack budget ε_a=4/255, TTC reaches 20.63% average robust accuracy versus 0.09% for vanilla CLIP and 7.79% for TTE. When applied on top of adversarially finetuned models, TTC adds +2.48 points (TeCoA), +2.03 points (PMG-AFT), and +13.89 points (FARE) in robust accuracy. The method requires only N=2 counterattack steps for ε_a=1/255 and N=5 for ε_a=4/255, with a budget of ε_ttc=4/255.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TTC decisively outperforms both Anti-adversary and Hedge Defense (HD) across nearly all 16 datasets; for example, on CIFAR-10 at ε_a=1/255, TTC achieves 76.70% robust accuracy versus 37.42% (Anti-adv) and 39.02% (HD), while also retaining higher clean accuracy (95.85% vs. 95.45% and 89.50%). Against TTE, TTC provides more stable gains (lower standard deviation) and higher average robust accuracy (36.47% vs. 33.28% average across datasets at ε_a=1/255). Compared to adversarial finetuning baselines (TeCoA, PMG-AFT, FARE), TTC achieves comparable or superior robust accuracy on most downstream datasets without the severe clean-accuracy degradation (e.g., TeCoA drops clean accuracy by ~21 points on average, while TTC drops only ~1.8 points). When stacked on top of finetuned models, TTC adds a modest +2–3 points over TeCoA/PMG-AFT but a substantial +13.89 points over FARE, suggesting that unsupervised finetuning better preserves the encoder's expressiveness needed for counterattacks. The only scenario where TTC underperforms is on TinyImageNet (the finetuning dataset), where TeCoA and PMG-AFT retain an advantage due to distribution-specific overfitting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the unsupervised L2-embedding-distance maximization objective from FARE [44] (originally an attack) as a test-time defense, and adapts the conceptual framework of Hedge Defense [52] and Anti-adversary [1] from conventional adversarially-trained models to the frozen CLIP setting. It departs from the dominant CLIP robustness literature (adversarial finetuning via TeCoA [32], PMG-AFT [50], and adversarial prompt tuning [26, 59]) by requiring no training whatsoever, instead exploiting the expressiveness of the pre-trained vision encoder at inference. A key conceptual departure is the 'false stability' observation: unlike conventional models where adversarial examples are more fragile to small noise [52], CLIP adversarial images are actually more stable to small perturbations, motivating the τ-thresholded gating mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As the first training-free test-time defense for CLIP, TTC offers a practical, plug-in robustness layer that can be deployed without retraining or auxiliary networks, and can be stacked on top of adversarially finetuned models for additional gains. The finding that adversarial finetuning measurably degrades the expressiveness of CLIP's vision encoder (limiting TTC's effectiveness on finetuned models) raises an important open question about the cost of adversarial training on foundation models that have learned massive real-world knowledge. However, the method is limited to loss-maximizing attacks, requires tuning the number of counterattack steps N based on the (potentially unknown) attack budget, and—as with all test-time defenses—remains vulnerable to adaptive attacks that are aware of the counterattack procedure.
