## Problem

Robotic manipulation learning currently depends on costly teleoperated demonstrations that do not scale across embodiments, tasks, and environments. While in-the-wild human videos offer a scalable data source, prior approaches either extract only 2D pixel-space actions that cannot be directly deployed to robots, or require controlled lab settings with depth sensors and MoCap systems. The paper tackles two questions: how to extract metric-scale 3D affordance (contact points and interaction trajectories) from raw RGB-only human videos, and how to transfer this knowledge zero-shot to novel scenes and robot embodiments.

## Input / Output

At test time the model consumes a single RGB-D frame (depth from either a sensor or a metric-depth foundation model such as Metric3D) plus a natural-language instruction, and produces embodiment-agnostic 3D contact points (N_c × 3) and a fine-grained interaction trajectory (H × 3 waypoints) expressed in the observation camera's coordinate frame. The coarse stage operates on a cropped object region detected by an open-set detector, while the fine stage additionally queries a voxelized TSDF map for spatial context. The final output is a ranked set of trajectory candidates scored by a composite cost, from which the agent selects the optimal interaction plan.

## Method

The pipeline has two stages. First, a gradient-based SfM optimization extracts metric-scale 3D hand trajectories from in-the-wild videos by jointly refining camera poses and a global scale against monocular depth predictions, using hand-object masks and video inpainting to isolate static regions. Second, a coarse-to-fine affordance model is trained on these extracted trajectories: the coarse stage uses Perceiver-based hourglass networks to predict goal and contact point heatmaps (lifted to 3D via camera intrinsics and depth), while the fine stage is a 1D U-Net diffusion model conditioned on coarse outputs, object ViT features, CLIP language embeddings, and 3D TSDF spatial features. A key differentiator is test-time cost guidance—multi-goal reaching, collision avoidance against the TSDF map, and contact-normal alignment—whose gradients perturb the diffusion denoising process, enabling the model to adapt to unseen object geometries and robot morphologies without retraining.

## Results

VidBot achieves an 88.2% average success rate across 13 zero-shot manipulation tasks in simulation, surpassing the runner-up method (Octo, 69.2%) by nearly 20 percentage points. The model also demonstrates strong real-world transfer, achieving 80.0% success over 55 trials on two mobile robot platforms (Hello Robot Stretch 3 and Boston Dynamics Spot) across three household environments. Ablation studies confirm that the coarse goal prediction stage contributes the largest single improvement (27.8% drop when removed), followed by multi-goal cost guidance (12.3% drop).

## Baselines & Comparisons

VidBot outperforms all five baselines across the 13-task benchmark. The largest margin is against GAPartNet (51.1%) and Where2Act (58.5%), which abstract interactions as simple directional vectors and struggle with curved motions like opening cabinets. Octo (69.2%), pre-trained on large-scale teleoperated data and fine-tuned on VidBot's extracted affordance, is the strongest baseline but still trails by 19.0 points, attributed to its difficulty inferring complex 3D actions directly from scene context. Notably, VRB (59.0%) uses the same EpicKitchens-100 video source as VidBot but only extracts 2D pixel-space motions; VidBot's 3D metric-space formulation and coarse-to-fine architecture boost success by ~30 points over VRB. GFlow (61.0%), which has access to ground-truth depth and camera parameters, suffers from erroneous trajectory scale predictions, limiting its performance despite richer input.

## Relation to Prior Work

VidBot extends the VRB paradigm [4] of extracting affordances from in-the-wild human videos by lifting the representation from 2D pixel-space vectors to metric-scale 3D trajectories, achieved through a gradient-based SfM pipeline combined with monocular metric depth. For the learning side, it builds on diffusion-based policy frameworks (Diffusion Policy [14], Diffuser [36]) but introduces a coarse-to-fine factorization where a Perceiver-based network predicts contact/goal points and a 1D U-Net diffusion model generates trajectories, guided at test time by differentiable cost functions (multi-goal reaching, collision avoidance, contact normal) inspired by reconstruction guidance in video diffusion [32, 69]. The key conceptual departure from prior affordance learning is the explicit use of 3D spatial awareness—contact points and interaction trajectories in the camera frame—rather than per-pixel scores or 2D flows, enabling direct embodiment-agnostic deployment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework demonstrates that raw internet-scale human videos, without depth sensors, MoCap, or teleoperation, can serve as a scalable supervision source for zero-shot cross-embodiment robot manipulation, achieving 88.2% success across 13 tasks and 80% on two physically distinct real robots (Stretch 3, Spot). A key open question is the quality ceiling imposed by the SfM + metric-depth pipeline: the authors acknowledge that label accuracy is bounded by these components, and that learning-based SfM (DUSt3R, MONST3R) or multimodal wearable data could push precision further for fine-grained tasks like unscrewing. The cost-guided test-time adaptation also raises the question of how well these hand-crafted differentiable objectives generalize beyond the specific manipulation primitives evaluated (opening, pushing, picking).
