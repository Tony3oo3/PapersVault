## Problem

Large multimodal models process visual inputs at uniform resolution across the entire field of view, wasting computational resources on non-critical regions. No prior work has examined whether existing LMM architectures can benefit from human-like foveated (variable-resolution) sampling under tight pixel budgets, nor what internal representational changes such sampling induces in these models.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250804/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image and a text question, but the image is first passed through a sampling filter that retains only a fixed fraction of pixels (e.g., 3%) distributed either in a foveated pattern (peak density at a fixation point, linearly decreasing with eccentricity in log-polar coordinates) or uniformly, then bilinearly interpolated back to the original spatial dimensions. This produces information-matched image pairs with identical pixel counts but different spatial distributions. For VQA the output is a text answer; for object detection it is bounding boxes with class labels.

## Method

The paper applies a biologically-inspired foveated sampling scheme (following Wilson & Bergen and Poggio et al. models of the human visual system) to five existing LMM architectures—MDETR, BLIP2, InstructBLIP, LLaVA, and ViLT—without any architectural modification. Pretrained models are evaluated zero-shot on foveated and uniform images at matched pixel budgets, while DETR, Mask-RCNN, and MDETR are trained from scratch (including ResNet-101 backbones) at 3% sampling density to study learned representations. The key analytical contribution is measuring attention distance in transformer layers and neuronal activation distributions in CNN filters to show that foveated training induces globally-acting self-attention and resolution-specialized neurons, mirroring human visual processing. Ablations with corner fixations and inclusion-degree binning on COCO confirm that the foveation benefit is not an artifact of center-biased datasets.

## Results

At a 3% sampling density, foveated (variable) sampling outperforms uniform sampling by up to 2.7% on GQA (MDETR: 46.8% vs. 44.1%), 2.1% on SEED-Bench (BLIP2: 48.6% vs. 46.5%), and 2.0% on VQAv2 (ViLT: 64.9% vs. 62.9%). Models achieve approximately 80% of their full-resolution performance using only 3% of the pixels (e.g., ViLT on VQAv2: 64.9% vs. 81.1% full). In object detection on COCO, variable sampling yields up to 2.2% mAP gain over uniform sampling for vision-only models (DETR, Mask-RCNN).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between foveated (variable) and uniform sampling at an identical pixel budget (3% density), with full resolution serving as the upper bound. Variable sampling wins on 5 out of 6 model-dataset pairs in Table 1, with the largest margin on MDETR/GQA (+2.7%) and ViLT/VQAv2 (+2.0%). The sole exception is InstructBLIP on VQAv2, where variable sampling is marginally worse (66.4% vs. 66.5%). On SEED-Bench, BLIP2 with variable sampling (48.6%) beats uniform (46.5%) by 2.1%, and in some question categories (e.g., 'Instance Identity') the 3%-variable model even surpasses the full-resolution baseline. For object detection on COCO, variable sampling outperforms uniform by ~2.0–2.2% regardless of fixation point location, confirming the gain is not an artifact of center-biased annotations. The paper does not compare against other token-pruning or dynamic-resolution methods (e.g., Token Merging, DynamicViT), as those still require full-resolution input at the first layer.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior foveated-vision DNN work (e.g., [2, 25, 29]) that proposed entirely new architectures to handle variable-resolution inputs, instead keeping state-of-the-art LMM architectures (MDETR, BLIP-2, InstructBLIP, LLaVA, ViLT) unchanged and altering only the input sampling scheme. It introduces the 'information-matched images' experimental design—pairs of images with identical pixel counts but different spatial distributions—to isolate the effect of resolution allocation from total information volume. The paper also extends the human-vision foveation models of Wilson & Bergen and Poggio et al. into a modern LMM evaluation framework, filling a gap the authors identify: no prior study systematically compared foveated vs. uniform sampling on large-scale complex datasets (GQA, VQAv2, SEED-Bench) with prevalent LMM architectures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that leading LMMs retain ~80% of full-resolution performance with only 3% of pixels has direct implications for bandwidth-constrained deployments (UAVs, edge devices on LPWANs at ~70 kbit/s), where transmitting full-resolution wide-FOV images is impractical. However, the paper explicitly avoids architectural modifications and multi-fixation strategies, so the demonstrated gains are purely information-theoretic; translating them into actual computational savings requires future work adapting model architectures to foveated grids. Open questions include whether multiple fixations with foveation can close the remaining 20% gap, how to integrate foveated sampling into the training pipeline rather than post-hoc inference, and whether the counterintuitive 'less-is-more' effect on certain question types generalizes beyond the specific models and datasets tested.
