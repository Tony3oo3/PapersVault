## Problem

Traditional auto white balance assumes a single global illuminant, producing color distortion in multi-illuminant scenes. While pixel-level neural networks handle mixed lighting accurately, their high parameter counts and computational cost make them impractical for real-time ISP pipelines on resource-constrained devices. The FFCC algorithm is efficient but limited to single-illuminant estimation, leaving a gap for a method that is both accurate in multi-illuminant scenes and fast enough for real-time video on mobile hardware.

## Input / Output

IFFCC consumes a thumbnail-sized RGB image stat (e.g., 64×48, 8-bit) produced by camera hardware, which is converted to a 2D log-chroma (UV) tensor. An integral UV histogram is precomputed over the entire scene, and overlapping grid windows (e.g., 128×128 with 64-pixel overlap) extract local histograms via four lookups and three arithmetic operations. The output is a spatially continuous illumination map (per-pixel illuminant chromaticity) that, when divided into the input, yields a white-balanced image; the map is smoothed via linear interpolation and guided filtering to avoid abrupt transitions between patch estimates.

## Method

IFFCC extends FFCC to multi-illuminant scenes through three key mechanisms. First, an integral UV histogram (inspired by Porikli's integral image) is propagated in a single wavefront scan over the log-chroma space, enabling O(1) histogram retrieval for any rectangular sub-region via inclusion–exclusion arithmetic, eliminating the per-region recomputation that makes naive FFCC slow. Second, an overlapping grid of windows extracts local histograms, and the FFT-based convolution with a Bivariate von Mises kernel is parallelized across all windows simultaneously in the frequency domain, producing per-region illuminant estimates in a single forward pass. Third, the discrete patch estimates are post-processed with linear interpolation followed by guided filtering (using the raw image as guide) to produce a smooth, edge-preserving illumination map. Training is supervised (64 iterations, 64×64 histogram bins) on blended illumination ground truth within each window, and the entire pipeline runs on CPU with only ~12K parameters.

## Results

On the LSMI multi-illumination dataset, IFFCC achieves mean angular errors of 2.48° (Galaxy), 2.30° (Nikon), and 2.48° (Sony), with medians of 1.90°, 1.50°, and 1.97° respectively, using only 0.012M parameters and 30 ms CPU inference time for 256×256 images. On the Shadow dataset (all cameras), IFFCC achieves a mean angular error of 2.19° and median of 1.56°, outperforming the best traditional method (Patch-based, 4.30° mean) by over 2° and the best network-based method (Domislović, 2.28° mean) by 0.09°. IFFCC reduces parameter count by over 400× compared to AID (6.4M params) and runs 20–100× faster than network-based approaches, with a 5.8 ms runtime on 64×48 preview images.

## Baselines & Comparisons

IFFCC decisively outperforms all traditional multi-illuminant methods: it reduces mean angular error from 13.67° (Hussain & Akbari [29]) and 7.19° (CRF White-Patch [4]) to 2.19° on the Shadow dataset, and from 12.38° (Gijsenij [23]) and 5.56° (Bianco [7]) to 2.48° on LSMI Multi. Against network-based baselines, IFFCC beats Bianco et al. [7] (8.01° vs 2.19° mean on Shadow), HypNet/SelNet [44] (6.31° vs 2.19°), LSMI-U [31] (3.72° vs 2.19°), and Pix2Pix [30] (4.28° vs 2.48° on LSMI Multi) by substantial margins. IFFCC slightly outperforms Domislović et al. [12] on the Shadow dataset (2.19° vs 2.28° mean) but is marginally behind AID [32] on LSMI Multi (2.48° vs 2.03° mean on Galaxy), though IFFCC uses 533× fewer parameters (0.012M vs 6.4M) and runs ~33× faster (0.03s vs >1s). Compared to its predecessor FFCC [3], IFFCC achieves 20–100× speedup in multi-window inference (e.g., 27 ms vs 88 ms at [128,64] window/overlap), making it viable for real-time deployment on resource-constrained devices.

## Relation to Prior Work

IFFCC extends the FFCC algorithm (Barron & Tsai, CVPR 2017), which itself builds on Convolutional Color Constancy (Barron, ICCV 2015), from a global single-illuminant estimator to a local multi-illuminant one. The key architectural addition is the integral UV histogram, adapted from Porikli's integral histogram technique (CVPR 2005), which enables O(1) histogram retrieval for any spatial region via simple arithmetic on a precomputed cumulative structure. This is combined with parallelized frequency-domain convolution (FFT-based BVM de-aliasing) to predict multiple illuminants simultaneously in a single forward pass, rather than the repeated per-region histogram extraction that FFCC would require. The method thus occupies a middle ground between lightweight statistical approaches (gray world, white patch, CRF-based) and heavy pixel-level neural networks, leveraging the log-chroma toroidal representation inherited from FFCC while adding spatial adaptivity through overlapping grids and guided filtering.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is substantial: achieving accuracy on par with 5–6M-parameter pixel-level networks (e.g., AID, LSMI-U) while using only ~12K parameters and running in ~30 ms on CPU makes real-time multi-illuminant AWB feasible on smartphones and embedded ISP pipelines, a regime where GPU-dependent neural methods are impractical. The integral histogram + parallelized FFT design is a clean algorithmic contribution that could transfer to other local estimation tasks requiring region-specific statistics. Open questions include performance under extreme multi-illuminant conditions (many distinct light sources in one frame), robustness to very low-light or saturated pixels, and whether the thumbnail-input constraint (64×48) limits accuracy for high-resolution capture. The paper does not explore video temporal consistency, which would be critical for the stated real-time video application.
