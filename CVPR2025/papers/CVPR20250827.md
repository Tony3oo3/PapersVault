## Problem

Quantum Neural Networks are constrained by limited qubit resources, and inefficient data encoding amplifies entanglement, noise sensitivity, and barren plateaus, ultimately degrading model performance. While circuit-level pruning and classical dimensionality reduction (PCA, autoencoders) have been explored, no prior work addresses adaptive feature pruning specifically within the quantum data encoding pipeline to reduce qubit overhead while preserving classification accuracy.

## Input / Output

The method takes grayscale or RGB images (28×28 for MNIST/FashionMNIST/PneumoniaMNIST, 32×32 for CIFAR-10), resizes them into a 3×3 grid, and produces a binary class label via a single-qubit von Neumann measurement (sign of the σ_z expectation value). Only two classes are processed at a time, with the 3×3 grid cells serving as the unit of pruning—each cell is either retained or zeroed based on its average intensity relative to the learned threshold.

## Method

ATP computes per-class average intensity matrices over the 3×3 grid and zeros out any grid position where both class averages fall below a threshold τ, producing a pruned dataset before quantum encoding. The optimal τ* is found by maximizing test accuracy via L-BFGS-B in a bi-level optimization loop: the inner level trains a 3-layer QNN (with XX and ZZ entangling gates and a readout qubit) on the pruned data using angle encoding (RX(π·x) per qubit), while the outer level adjusts τ to maximize held-out accuracy. This pre-encoding pruning reduces the number of informative qubit interactions, lowering entanglement entropy by 30–60% relative to direct angle or amplitude encoding while matching or exceeding their accuracy. The approach is dataset-adaptive—unlike PCA's fixed linear projection or SQE's single-qubit constraint—because the threshold is tuned to each dataset's variance structure, and it composes with adversarial training (FGSM) to further improve robustness.

## Results

ATP achieves the highest classification accuracy in 10 out of 12 binary classification tasks across MNIST, FashionMNIST, CIFAR-10, and PneumoniaMNIST, with headline results of 99.0% on MNIST (0,1), 91.5% on FashionMNIST (0,1), 74.2% on CIFAR-10 (0,1), and 87.0% on PneumoniaMNIST (0,1). ATP simultaneously achieves the lowest entanglement entropy (EE) in 11 out of 12 tasks, ranging from 0.34 to 0.46, compared to 0.41–0.88 for the other methods. Under 10% depolarizing noise, ATP retains accuracy drops of only 3–8 points versus 4–17 points for Angle, Amplitude, and PCA encoding. On IBM Sherbrooke hardware, ATP shows an average 7% improvement over direct angle encoding across five MNIST class subsets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against PCA, ATP wins on 9 of 12 class pairs (e.g., 91.0% vs 88.0% on MNIST (0,3); 74.2% vs 68.0% on CIFAR-10; 87.0% vs 80.0% on PneumoniaMNIST), ties on 2 (MNIST (0,1) at 99.0%, FashionMNIST (2,8) at 86.0%), and loses only on MNIST (2,8) where PCA reaches 86.0% vs ATP's 83.0%. ATP also achieves lower entanglement entropy than PCA in 10 of 12 tasks (e.g., 0.39 vs 0.60 on MNIST (0,1); 0.37 vs 0.59 on PneumoniaMNIST). Against SQE, ATP dominates across all 12 tasks with margins of 2–12 percentage points (e.g., 99.0% vs 88.0% on MNIST (0,1); 87.0% vs 75.5% on PneumoniaMNIST), while also matching or exceeding SQE's entanglement efficiency. ATP also outperforms the direct Angle and Amplitude encoding baselines in 10 of 12 accuracy tasks and in all 12 EE measurements, with the largest gaps on PneumoniaMNIST (87.0% vs 81.0% Angle; 87.0% vs 68.5% Amplitude) and CIFAR-10 (74.2% vs 70.0% Angle).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of classical feature selection and quantum data encoding. While circuit-level pruning in QML (Hu et al., Sim et al., Kulshrestha et al.) reduces model complexity by removing parameters, and classical masking strategies (MAE) filter inputs for representation learning, no prior work has applied adaptive threshold-based feature pruning specifically before quantum state encoding. ATP repurposes the intuition behind threshold filtering and bi-level optimization (L-BFGS-B) to select which spatial regions of an image are worth encoding into qubits, positioning itself as a data-level complement to the existing circuit-level pruning literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ATP addresses a practical NISQ-era bottleneck: limited qubit resources and noise sensitivity make encoding efficiency critical for QNN viability. The consistent finding that lower entanglement entropy correlates with better noise and adversarial robustness provides a useful design principle for quantum model architects. However, the method is limited to binary classification, incurs ~15% additional preprocessing overhead, and has only been validated on small-scale benchmarks (28×28 and 32×32 images) with a three-layer QNN; whether the adaptive thresholding strategy scales to multi-class tasks, larger images, or deeper circuits remains an open question.
