## Problem

Saliency maps like Grad-CAM on Vision Transformers produce blurred, coarse heatmaps that highlight entire objects rather than the subtle, class-specific traits (e.g., a red spot on a wing) that distinguish visually similar categories such as bird species. The [CLS] token's attention maps are class-agnostic, attending to the same body parts across different species, so they cannot reveal what makes one class unique. The paper asks how to leverage pre-trained ViTs' localized patch features to identify and spatially localize these discriminative traits.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250248/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image and C learnable class-specific prompt tokens (one per class) as joint input to a frozen pre-trained ViT. It produces C scalar class-specific scores (logits) computed via a shared scoring vector, from which the predicted class is obtained by argmax. For interpretation, it outputs per-head attention maps (up to R maps per class) showing which image patches each attention head of the selected class's prompt attends to, enabling trait localization. A greedy masking procedure can further rank these heads to isolate the most discriminative traits.

## Method

PROMPT-CAM injects C learnable class-specific prompt tokens into a frozen pre-trained ViT (DINO, DINOv2, or BioCLIP) and replaces the standard [CLS]-based prediction head with a shared scoring vector that computes inner products with each prompt's final-layer output, yielding C class-specific scores trained with cross-entropy loss. The key architectural choice is the shared vector w (rather than per-class fully-connected weights), which forces the model to rely on where each prompt attends spatially rather than encoding holistic class-discriminative information in patch feature channels. The DEEP variant decouples roles: class-specific prompts are injected only at the last layer (attending to the most informative features), while class-agnostic prompts at earlier layers handle task adaptation. During inference, visualizing the multi-head attention maps of the true-class prompt reveals up to R distinct traits, and a greedy procedure that progressively blurs the least important heads identifies the minimal set of traits sufficient for correct classification.

## Results

On the CUB-200-2011 faithfulness evaluation (DINO backbone), PROMPT-CAM achieves an insertion score of 0.61 and a deletion score of 0.09, outperforming all post-hoc saliency baselines (Grad-CAM 0.52/0.17, Layer-CAM 0.54/0.13, Eigen-CAM 0.56/0.22, Attention roll-out 0.55/0.27). In a human trait-recognition study, PROMPT-CAM achieves 60.49% trait identification, substantially exceeding TesNet (39.14%) and ProtoConcepts (30.39%). Classification accuracy shows a modest drop versus Linear Probing (e.g., 73.2% vs. 78.6% on CUB; 98.8% vs. 99.2% on Birds-525), which the authors attribute to PROMPT-CAM's deliberate reliance on localized traits rather than holistic features.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PROMPT-CAM outperforms all four post-hoc saliency methods (Grad-CAM, Layer-CAM, Eigen-CAM, Attention roll-out) on both faithfulness metrics, with the largest margin over Attention roll-out (deletion 0.09 vs. 0.27) and Grad-CAM (insertion 0.61 vs. 0.52). In the human trait-recognition study, PROMPT-CAM (60.49%) substantially outperforms TesNet (39.14%) and ProtoConcepts (30.39%), indicating it localizes biologically meaningful traits far more effectively. Against INTR, the closest interpretable Transformer baseline, PROMPT-CAM is encoder-only (vs. INTR's encoder-decoder), trains faster (only prompts + shared vector vs. full fine-tuning), and produces sharper attention maps. On classification accuracy, PROMPT-CAM trails Linear Probing by 1–5 points across datasets (e.g., 73.2% vs. 78.6% on CUB, 81.1% vs. 82.4% on Stanford Dogs), a trade-off the authors frame as intentional: the constrained prediction head forces the model to rely on localized, class-specific traits rather than holistic shortcuts.

## Relation to Prior Work

PROMPT-CAM is a minimal modification of Visual Prompt Tuning (VPT), relocating the prediction head from the [CLS] token to the outputs of injected class-specific prompts, thereby converting VPT's adaptation mechanism into an interpretability tool. Conceptually, it extends the class-specific token idea from INTR (same group, ICLR 2024) and from MCTformer/Query2Label, but decouples it from encoder-decoder architectures and full fine-tuning, instead operating on frozen state-of-the-art ViT backbones (DINO, DINOv2, BioCLIP). It departs from post-hoc saliency methods (Grad-CAM, Layer-CAM, Eigen-CAM) that produce coarse object-level heatmaps, and from prototype-based interpretable models (ProtoPNet, TesNet, ProtoConcepts) that require bespoke architectures and training procedures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in the near-zero implementation cost: a few lines of code change to VPT yields interpretable trait localization across 13 diverse fine-grained datasets, making it accessible to practitioners who would otherwise need to design and train entirely new interpretable architectures. The shared scoring vector design provides a principled explanation for why the method forces localized, trait-specific features rather than holistic shortcuts, offering a useful design principle for future interpretable ViT methods. Limitations include a modest accuracy drop relative to Linear Probing (by design, as the model is constrained to rely on visible traits), and the taxonomy-key discovery remains preliminary. Open questions include scalability to very large class counts, applicability to non-fine-grained or multi-label settings, and whether the approach transfers to video or 3D modalities.
