## Problem

Automatic audio description (AD) generation suffers from two under-addressed issues: a domain gap between movie-AD data and the web-scale data used to pre-train vision-language models (causing misalignment at the vision-encoding stage), and contextual redundancy where models produce repetitive descriptions for consecutive similar clips in long movies. Prior methods treat AD generation as single-clip video captioning, ignoring the sequential structure that makes audiences care about what is *new* in the current clip versus what was already described.

## Input / Output

The method consumes N consecutive video clips (each with T uniformly sampled frames, default T=8), character portrait images projected as face tokens for identity disambiguation, and prior contextual ADs as part of an interleaved multimodal prompt. It outputs a single audio description sentence for the target clip. The key structural constraint is that N clips must be temporally consecutive from the same movie; the Contextual EMA module is designed to extract shared bases across these clips, and performance degrades when clips are non-consecutive (CIDEr drops by 1.7).

## Method

DistinctAD is a two-stage framework. Stage-I adapts the CLIP vision encoder (text encoder frozen) to the movie-AD domain via a combined global video-AD contrastive loss and a parameter-free fine-grained frame-word matching loss that handles the multi-instance setting where words aggregate over frames. Stage-II processes N consecutive clips jointly: a Perceiver adapter resamples frame embeddings into prompt vectors, then a Contextual EMA module (iterating Responsibility Estimation, Likelihood Maximization, and Data Re-estimation over K bases) extracts compact shared representations to suppress redundancy, while a cross-attention branch between raw features and the learned bases enhances linear separability. A distinctive word prediction loss explicitly penalizes the LLM decoder for generating words that already appear in the N contextual ADs, steering generation toward clip-specific terms. The final visual features (raw + reconstructed + cross-attended, weighted by α and β) are projected into the LLM embedding space and decoded autoregressively.

## Results

On the primary MAD-Eval benchmark, DistinctAD with CLIP_AD-B16 and LLaMA3-8B achieves CIDEr 27.3, SPICE 8.3, ROUGE-L 17.6, and Recall@5/16 of 56.0, setting new state-of-the-art across all metrics. On CMD-AD, DistinctAD (LLaMA3) reaches CIDEr 22.7 and R@1/5 of 33.0, and on TV-AD it achieves CIDEr 27.4 and R@1/5 of 32.1. The standout metric is Recall@k/N, where DistinctAD's 56.0 on MAD-Eval surpasses the previous best (AutoAD-III with LLaMA2-7B at 52.8) by over 3 points, directly reflecting its ability to generate contextually distinctive descriptions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the strongest partial-fine-tuning baselines, DistinctAD (CLIP_AD-B16 + LLaMA3-8B) outperforms AutoAD-III (EVA-CLIP + LLaMA2-7B) by 3.3 CIDEr (27.3 vs 24.0) and 3.2 R@5/16 (56.0 vs 52.8), and beats MovieSeq (CLIP-B16 + LLaMA2-7B) by 2.9 CIDEr and 4.4 R@5/16. Among training-free methods, DistinctAD surpasses the best (AutoAD-Zero, CIDEr 22.4) by 4.9 CIDEr, confirming that domain-adapted fine-tuning remains essential for the movie-AD domain. On CMD-AD, DistinctAD wins on R@1/5 (33.0 vs 31.2) and LLM-AD-eval over AutoAD-III†, though it trails on CIDEr (22.7 vs 25.0), which the authors attribute to AutoAD-III†'s access to a 3.4M-sample HowTo-AD pre-training set. On TV-AD, DistinctAD leads all baselines on both CIDEr (27.4 vs 26.1 for AutoAD-III) and R@1/5 (32.1 vs 30.6 for AutoAD-Zero). The consistent R@k/N advantage across all three benchmarks is the paper's central empirical claim: DistinctAD generates descriptions that are more uniquely matched to each clip's content rather than repeating contextually redundant information.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the AutoAD lineage (Han et al., CVPR'23/ICCV'23/CVPR'24) by addressing two limitations the authors identify: the domain gap between movie-AD data and CLIP's pre-training distribution, and the contextual redundancy that causes repetitive descriptions across consecutive clips. Stage-I repurposes CLIP's contrastive pre-training paradigm (Radford et al., ICML'21) for a multi-instance video-AD setting with a novel frame-word matching loss, while Stage-II adapts the Expectation-Maximization Attention algorithm (Li et al., ICCV'19) from semantic segmentation to extract compact, discriminative bases from N contextual video clips. The distinctive word prediction loss is a new training signal that explicitly penalizes repetition of context-shared tokens, complementing the standard autoregressive loss.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The CLIP-AD adaptation strategy is potentially reusable across any CLIP-based AD or video-captioning pipeline, and the authors demonstrate it integrates seamlessly into prior methods (AutoAD-I, AutoAD-II). The contextual redundancy problem is fundamental to long-form video narration and likely affects other sequential generation tasks beyond AD. However, the paper acknowledges that generated ADs still fall short of human annotations, and the two-stage pipeline with multiple hyperparameters (α, β, K, N, γ) adds practical complexity. Open questions include whether the Contextual EMA approach scales to much longer contexts (beyond 16 clips) and whether the distinctive-word loss generalizes to other sequential captioning tasks like dense video captioning.
