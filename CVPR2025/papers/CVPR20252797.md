## Problem

Existing online HD map construction methods (e.g., MapTR, TopoMLP) build only the geometric and connectivity layers, leaving the traffic regulation layer unaddressed. While OpenLane-V2 associates directional signs with lanes, it uses single-label classification rather than structured multi-rule descriptions, making it insufficient for complex real-world signs carrying multiple lane-level regulations. This paper introduces the task of extracting structured driving rules from traffic signs and reasoning their correspondence to vectorized centerlines, filling a critical gap for safe autonomous driving.

## Input / Output

The method consumes front-view image sequences (1920×1240, 30–60 frames per clip at 1 frame/2 m spacing) centered on a traffic sign within a 100 m × 100 m area, along with vectorized centerlines represented as 3D point lists and OCR-extracted text/symbols from the sign. Output is a set of lane-level driving rules, each encoded as 8 predefined {key: value} JSON properties aligned with NDS/HD map standards, plus a binary bipartite graph E ⊆ {0,1}^{m×k} linking each rule to the centerlines it governs. The MapDR benchmark provides 10,000+ clips, 400K images, and 18,000+ annotated rules across Beijing, Shanghai, and Guangzhou.

## Method

The paper proposes two complementary approaches. The modular VLE-MEE pipeline first uses a Vision-Language Encoder (ViT-B16 + BERT text encoder with cross-attention fusion, [CLS]/[STC] tokens, Fourier-feature layout encoding, and inter/intra-instance attention) to cluster OCR symbols into rule groups via contrastive loss and predict rule properties through linear heads; a Map Element Encoder (transformer vector encoder with [VEC] tokens, learnable type/instance/position embeddings, and cross-attention fusion with rule embeddings) then performs binary classification on each [VEC] token to determine rule-lane correspondence. The end-to-end RuleVLM builds on Qwen-VL-Chat (9.6B) with LoRA fine-tuning, encoding vectorized centerlines through a modified MEE (cross-attention removed) aligned to the LLM via a linear adapter, and outputs serialized JSON driving rules decoded back to structured format. Ablations show that inter/intra-instance attention is critical for both sub-tasks, while vector type embeddings significantly boost correspondence reasoning (P_{C.R.} from 69.7% to 78.1%), and the MEE-based vector encoding in RuleVLM nearly doubles overall F1 over the visual-prompt baseline (0.642 vs. 0.392).

## Results

On the MapDR benchmark, the proposed modular approach VLE-MEE achieves the best overall F1 score of 0.653, with rule extraction precision/recall of 76.67%/74.54% and correspondence reasoning precision/recall of 78.05%/82.16%. The end-to-end RuleVLM achieves a competitive F1 of 0.642 with rule extraction precision/recall of 89.28%/89.44%, substantially outperforming the Qwen-VL(VisualPrompt) baseline (F1=0.392) and Qwen-VL(TextPrompt) baseline (F1=0.083). The heuristic baseline performs very poorly with an F1 of only 0.035, underscoring the difficulty of the task.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VLE-MEE dramatically outperforms the ALBEF-BERT modular baseline on correspondence reasoning (P_CR: 78.05% vs. 4.14%; R_CR: 82.16% vs. 17.25%), yielding an overall F1 of 0.653 versus 0.003, primarily because ALBEF-BERT lacks adequate adaptation to vector information. In the end-to-end setting, RuleVLM (F1=0.642) substantially surpasses Qwen-VL(VisualPrompt) (F1=0.392) and Qwen-VL(TextPrompt) (F1=0.083), demonstrating that independent vector encoding via MEE is critical for spatial reasoning over centerlines. RuleVLM achieves rule extraction performance nearly identical to Qwen-VL(VisualPrompt) (89.28% vs. 89.29% precision), confirming that the MEE adapter does not degrade the language model's rule understanding. The modular VLE-MEE slightly edges out the end-to-end RuleVLM on overall F1 (0.653 vs. 0.642), suggesting that decoupling rule extraction from correspondence reasoning provides a marginal advantage. The heuristic baseline (OCR matching + nearest lane) achieves only F1=0.035, confirming that the task cannot be solved with simple rules and requires learned multimodal reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the online HD map construction lineage (MapTR, MapTRv2, TopoMLP) by introducing the previously neglected traffic regulation layer, and borrows inter-instance/intra-instance attention mechanisms from MapTRv2 for its vector encoding. For the vision-language side, VLE adapts the ALBEF-style cross-attention fusion architecture with layout-aware text encoding, while RuleVLM builds on Qwen-VL with LoRA fine-tuning and a custom MEE adapter for vector modality. The paper departs from OpenLane-V2, which associates signs with lanes but only for directional signs via single-label classification, by formulating a structured multi-rule extraction and bipartite-graph correspondence problem aligned with HD map data specifications.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MapDR fills a concrete gap in the online HD map pipeline: without the traffic regulation layer, autonomous systems must still rely on offline maps for speed limits, HOV rules, and lane restrictions, undermining the purpose of online mapping. The dataset (10K clips, 400K images, 18K rules across three Chinese cities) and the defined evaluation metrics (rule extraction, correspondence reasoning, and overall F1) give the community a standardized benchmark that did not previously exist. Open questions include generalization beyond Chinese road signage conventions, real-time inference feasibility for the modular pipeline, and whether the bipartite-graph formulation scales to denser urban scenes with dozens of concurrent signs. The modest F1 scores (~0.65) for both baselines indicate substantial headroom for future work.
