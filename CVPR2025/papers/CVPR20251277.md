## Problem

Existing video understanding models degrade significantly on hour-long videos due to computational infeasibility, heavy temporal redundancy from slow-changing scenes and objects, and the lack of a single representation that generalizes across diverse tasks (QA, grounding, summarization). Prior approaches rely on uniform frame sampling or task-specific memory banks, which either waste capacity on redundant frames or fail to transfer across downstream tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251277/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an arbitrarily long untrimmed video and a text query (natural-language question for QA, or an action label/visual template for grounding) and produces either a text answer (multiple-choice or open-ended) or a temporal window (start and end times). The video is consumed as three separately extracted token streams—scene frames at 8 FPS, object masks at 10 FPS, and tracked action segments at 1 FPS—rather than as a uniform frame sequence. In streaming mode, the video is processed through a fixed-size sliding window, yielding an updated representation at every time step that can handle videos of unbounded length.

## Method

SEAL decomposes a long video into three semantic token types: scene tokens (uniformly sampled frames encoding background context), action tokens (spatial unions of bounding boxes from SAM-2 tracklets capturing motion and events), and object tokens (SAM segmentation masks on key frames capturing static entities). An attention learning module then solves a subset-selection optimization that jointly maximizes query relevance (BLIP-2 cosine similarity between each token and the query) and token diversity (inverse pairwise cosine similarity among selected tokens), controlled by a single hyperparameter α. The selected token subset is fed to either a vision-based moment decoder (classification + regression heads for temporal grounding) or an MLLM head (MLM-projected visual tokens for video QA). The framework supports both a global mode (single optimization over all tokens) and a streaming mode (iterative attention learning over a sliding window unioned with the previously selected subset), enabling processing of arbitrarily long videos under fixed memory.

## Results

On LVBench (hour-long videos), SEAL achieves 45.9% overall accuracy with a 34B LLM (Yi-34B), outperforming the larger Qwen2-VL 72B (41.3%) by 4.6% and InternVL2 34B (39.6%) by 6.3%. On MovieChat-1K, SEAL reaches 86.8% accuracy, surpassing the previous best supervised method HERMES (84.9%) by 1.9%. On Ego4D-NLQ temporal grounding under memory constraints (200 tokens), SEAL achieves R@1 avg of 8.95 vs. SnAG's 8.19, with the gap widening under tighter budgets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SEAL outperforms all five LVBench baselines on overall accuracy, with the largest margins over PLLaVA (+19.8%) and Oryx (+15.5%), and a notable +4.6% gain over the much larger Qwen2-VL 72B. The gains are most pronounced in Key Information Retrieval (+8.1% over InternVL2) and Entity Recognition (+10.5% over Qwen2-VL), attributed to the object and action tokens reducing redundancy. On MovieChat-1K, SEAL beats the strongest supervised baseline HERMES by 1.9% in accuracy (86.8% vs. 84.9%) while scoring slightly lower on the LLM-judged quality score (4.35 vs. 4.40). Among zero-shot baselines, SEAL's advantage is substantial: +19.0% over MovieChat and +25.8% over Video-ChatGPT. On Ego4D-NLQ, SEAL consistently outperforms SnAG at both 450 and 200 token budgets, with the advantage growing under tighter memory constraints (R@1 avg: 8.95 vs. 8.19 at 200 tokens), demonstrating the robustness of the semantic decomposition under limited token budgets.

## Relation to Prior Work

SEAL departs from the dominant memory-bank and uniform-sampling paradigms for long video understanding (MovieChat, MALMM, InternVL2) by proposing a structured semantic decomposition into scene, object, and action tokens, each extracted with off-the-shelf tools (SAM, SAM2, YOLOv10+BoT-SORT). The attention learning module formulates token selection as a subset optimization balancing query relevance (via BLIP-2 cosine similarity) and pairwise diversity, a formulation not seen in prior long-video QA or grounding work. For the temporal grounding head, SEAL directly adapts SnAG's moment decoder architecture (IoU distance + focal loss), positioning itself as a representation-level improvement rather than a decoder redesign. The streaming mode draws on the human-cognition motivation of online, incremental processing, distinguishing it from global-only baselines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SEAL offers a practical path to handling hour-long videos within fixed memory budgets, with the streaming mode enabling arbitrarily long inputs—a setting where most prior methods break down. The 2–3× speedup over InternVL2 at matched accuracy is practically relevant for deployment. However, the Attention Learning module is not end-to-end trainable (bounded by the QP solver), and the paper's own failure case on causal reasoning questions reveals a clear ceiling for the MLLM head. Open questions include whether the semantic decomposition generalizes to domains with rapid scene changes (e.g., sports, surveillance) and whether the streaming mode can be made fully differentiable for joint optimization.
