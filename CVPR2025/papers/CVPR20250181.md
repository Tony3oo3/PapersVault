## Problem

While interpretability research has examined representation evolution in text-only LLMs, the specific process by which aligned multimodal features are transmitted through the language model and converted into natural language in LVLMs remains unexplored. This paper asks: how does multimodal knowledge evolve across the hidden layers of an LVLM, and what structural patterns govern this evolution?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250181/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis operates on LLaVA-1.5 (32-layer Vicuna-1.5 backend) with standard image + text prompt inputs, but produces no final caption as its primary output. Instead, it extracts intermediate representations at every layer: per-token probabilities via early exit, full-vocabulary probability distributions (from which JS divergences between adjacent layers are computed), and 4096-dimensional hidden feature vectors projected to 2D via t-SNE. All analyses are performed without any additional training or architectural modification—only the existing language head φ(·) is applied to intermediate layer outputs H^j.

## Method

The paper designs a three-level reverse-engineering framework that starts from the output and traces knowledge backward through the network. At the token probability level, early exit is used to compute predicted-token probabilities at each of the 32 transformer blocks, revealing a sharp jump at 'critical layers' (~layer 18-20) and a secondary 'mutation' near output layers. At the distribution level, Jensen-Shannon divergence between adjacent layers' full-vocabulary softmax outputs exposes a hierarchical pattern: high divergence in shallow layers (rapid evolution), near-zero divergence in middle layers (stabilization), and occasional spikes at mutation layers. At the feature level, t-SNE projection of the 4096-dim hidden vectors used for next-token prediction shows a 'guitar-shaped' trajectory—tight clustering in shallow layers followed by approximately linear radial divergence in deeper layers. The mutation layers are shown to correlate with hallucinations, as the language model injects prior knowledge that overrides image-derived features, and skipping these layers partially corrects hallucinated outputs.

## Results

This is an interpretability study rather than a benchmarking paper, so there are no standard accuracy or mAP metrics. The key quantitative findings are: (1) critical layers appear around layer 18–20 of LLaVA-1.5's 32-layer Vicuna backbone, where token probabilities jump from near-zero to significant values; (2) JS divergence between adjacent layers drops sharply after the critical layers, indicating a transition from rapid to stable knowledge evolution; (3) skip-connection experiments show that skipping the stabilization stage (critical→mutation layers) preserves output semantics with only minor wording changes, while skipping only the mutation layers corrects some hallucinated tokens (e.g., 'standing'→'playing', 'toothbrush'→'food'); (4) t-SNE visualizations reveal a 'guitar-like' shape where features from different images cluster tightly in shallow layers and diverge in deep layers.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new method and therefore does not compare against baselines in the traditional sense. The closest comparative analysis is the LVLM-vs-LLM discussion in Section 6, where the authors show that the hierarchical JS divergence pattern and mutation phenomena are absent in LLaMA-1.5 processing pure text queries (e.g., 'Who was the first Indian to win an academic award?'), confirming that the observed knowledge-evolution stages are specific to multimodal inputs. The skip-connection ablation (skip.1, skip.2, skip.3) serves as an internal comparison: skip.1 (bypassing stabilization) retains hallucinations, skip.2 (bypassing only mutation) corrects some errors while preserving overall meaning, and skip.3 (bypassing both) degrades output quality substantially, indicating that both the stabilization and mutation stages contribute meaningfully to final predictions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of LLM interpretability work on bottom-up representation evolution (Voita et al., Chefer et al., Dola) to the multimodal setting, specifically asking how aligned visual features are progressively transformed into natural language within the LLM backbone of an LVLM. It repurposes standard tools—early exit for layer-wise token probabilities, Jensen-Shannon divergence for distributional change, and t-SNE for feature geometry—but applies them to the novel question of tracking the visual-to-linguistic knowledge transition. The key departure from prior LLM interpretability is the identification of phenomena (critical layers, mutation layers, three-stage evolution) that the authors argue are specific to multimodal inputs and absent in pure-text LLMs, carving out a distinct interpretability problem for LVLMs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The three-stage knowledge evolution framework (rapid evolution → stabilization → mutation) provides a concrete structural map of LVLM internals that directly suggests practical interventions: skipping stabilization layers for depth compression and targeting mutation layers for hallucination mitigation. However, the study is limited to a single model (LLaVA-1.5 with Vicuna-1.5), relies heavily on qualitative and illustrative examples rather than large-scale quantitative benchmarks, and the skip-connection experiments are anecdotal. Open questions include whether the critical/mutation layer structure generalizes across LVLM architectures and scales, whether mutation-layer interventions can be systematized into a robust hallucination-reduction method, and whether the observed phenomena persist in newer, larger LVLMs with different alignment strategies.
