## Problem

Existing human motion prediction methods assume future poses can be predicted instantaneously with zero latency, ignoring the variable time delays (tens to hundreds of milliseconds) inherent in real-world applications like AR/VR teleoperation and human-robot interaction. Because the latency length fluctuates unpredictably across samples due to network instability, explicit motion-to-motion modeling breaks down: the observed history and the target prediction window become disconnected by a variable gap, violating the continuity assumption that underpins all prior approaches.

## Input / Output

The method takes a fixed-length past pose sequence (10 frames at 25 Hz for Human3.6M/CMU-MoCap, 10 frames at 30 Hz for 3DPW) represented as 3D coordinates of 22–25 body joints, and produces future poses (10 or 18 frames) after a variable latency gap of 1–6 frames. Crucially, the latency length T_l is not known at test time; the model must output poses at arbitrary temporal coordinates beyond the observed window, and the same trained model handles all latency values without retraining. The output is a continuous function f(t) that can be queried at any timestamp, rather than a fixed-length sequence.

## Method

ALIEN reformulates motion prediction as implicit neural representation: a shared 5-layer MLP decoder with Fourier positional encoding maps a scalar time coordinate to a full-body pose, while a Mamba-like linear attention (MLLA) hyper-network generates instance-specific decoder weights from the past observation. Spatial dependencies are captured by the hyper-network (which processes DCT-compressed joint trajectories as tokens), while temporal mapping is handled by the INR decoder, achieving a clean spatial-temporal separation. For efficiency, the hyper-network output modulates decoder weights via low-rank modulation (σ(U_l ⊗ V_l^T) ⊙ Z_l) rather than generating full-rank matrices, reducing parameter count while maintaining accuracy. A multi-task learning framework jointly trains the primary prediction task and an auxiliary variable-delay pose reconstruction task, where the reconstruction input is a shifted window that incorporates latency-period poses, forcing the model to learn richer motion patterns without requiring latency knowledge at test time.

## Results

On Human3.6M (average across 15 actions), ALIEN achieves MPJPE of 9.7, 21.8, 47.0, 58.3, and 79.3 mm at 80, 160, 320, 400, and 600 ms latency respectively, outperforming the closest INR-based competitor NeRMo by 5.83%, 3.54%, 3.29%, 3.64%, and 6.28% at those latencies. On CMU-MoCap, ALIEN reduces average MPJPE to 7.84 mm at 80 ms (vs. 8.16 for NeRMo and 8.34 for PGBIG), and on 3DPW it reaches 14.83 mm at 100 ms (vs. 15.75 for NeRMo). The model runs in 29.10 ms with 4.82M parameters, meeting the 30 ms algorithm-execution budget assumed in the task formulation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ALIEN outperforms all nine baselines on the primary Human3.6M benchmark across every latency setting. The closest competitor is NeRMo [54], the only other INR-based method; ALIEN beats it by 3–6% relative MPJPE reduction, with the gap widening at longer latencies (6.28% at 600 ms). Among graph/sequence-based methods, PGBIG [28] and SPGSN [27] are the strongest baselines (10.5 and 10.6 mm at 80 ms), but ALIEN still leads by roughly 0.8–0.9 mm. RNN-based Res-sup [31] is the weakest baseline, trailing ALIEN by over 25 mm at 80 ms. In the hyper-network architecture ablation (Table 5), ALIEN's MLLA variant is marginally less accurate than TransINR [9] (9.7 vs. 9.8 mm at 80 ms) but runs 28% faster (29.10 vs. 40.64 ms), while clearly outperforming GINR [22] in both accuracy (9.7 vs. 10.3 mm) and speed (29.10 vs. 26.01 ms). On CMU-MoCap and 3DPW, ALIEN similarly leads all baselines, with the largest margins over Res-sup (e.g., 7.84 vs. 24.98 mm at 80 ms on CMU-MoCap) and consistent 4–6% improvements over the next-best methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

ALIEN sits at the intersection of three research threads: implicit neural representations (inherited from NeRF and DeepSDF), hyper-networks for weight generation (TransINR, GINR), and human motion prediction (GCN/Transformer-based sequence-to-sequence models). It departs most directly from NeRMo [54], the closest INR-based motion prediction method, by introducing a spatial-temporal separation (hyper-network handles spatial joint dependencies via DCT trajectories; INR decoder handles temporal coordinate-to-pose mapping), replacing the Transformer hyper-network with a Mamba-like linear attention (MLLA) for efficiency, and adding a multi-task auxiliary reconstruction loss. The key conceptual departure from all prior motion prediction work is reframing the problem from sequence-to-sequence translation to continuous function evaluation at arbitrary temporal coordinates, which naturally absorbs variable latency without explicit modeling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The arbitrary-latency formulation addresses a genuine gap between academic motion prediction (assumed zero-latency) and real-world deployment in AR/VR, teleoperation, and human-robot interaction where WAN transmission delays of 40–200 ms are unavoidable and variable. The INR-based solution is elegant in that a single trained model handles any latency length at inference without retraining or window extension, and the multi-task design cleverly exploits latency-period poses available only during training. Limitations include the discretization of latency to five fixed values (40–200 ms), evaluation on only three datasets with relatively short prediction windows (10–18 frames), and the fact that the 'arbitrary' latency is bounded. Open questions include whether the approach scales to longer prediction horizons, more complex multi-person or object-interaction scenarios, and whether the MLLA hyper-network generalizes beyond the specific token ordering constraints of joint trajectories.
