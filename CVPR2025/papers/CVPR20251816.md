## Problem

Existing hyperspectral 3D imaging methods either require hundreds of projected patterns (yielding acquisition times of several minutes, limiting them to static scenes) or rely on expensive, bulky hardware such as hyperspectral video cameras or CASSI systems. No practical, affordable method has achieved both high spectral and depth accuracy at frame rates sufficient for dynamic scenes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251816/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes 9 stereo RGB image pairs (8 DDSL patterns + 1 black pattern) captured by FLIR GS3-U3-32S4C-C cameras at 6.6 fps, where each DDSL pattern consists of vertical white lines with parameters l_offset=40, l_width=5, l_shift=5 projected through an Edmund 54-509 diffraction grating film. It produces a per-pixel hyperspectral image with 23 spectral bands spanning 440–660 nm at 10 nm intervals (15.5 nm FWHM) and a depth map with 4 mm mean absolute error, both aligned to the center frame via black-pattern optical flow.

## Method

The core contribution is a set of dense dispersed structured light (DDSL) patterns whose vertical lines, when dispersed by the diffraction grating, produce spectrally multiplexed illumination: overlapping dispersed spectra from adjacent lines encode multiple wavelength bands simultaneously, reducing the required projections from hundreds to just 8. A data-driven backward mapping model ψ (calibrated via sub-pixel Gaussian fitting on Spectralon scan-line captures, achieving 0.66-pixel reprojection error) relates each scene point and wavelength to its corresponding projector pixel, enabling an explicit image formation model. Depth is estimated per-frame using pretrained RAFT-Stereo on rectified pairs, while motion compensation uses 'black optical flow'—optical flow estimated between successive black-pattern frames (avoiding illumination inconsistency) and then interpolated to align all DDSL-pattern frames. Hyperspectral reconstruction solves a per-pixel linear system I_k = L·H_k (where L encodes camera sensitivity, grating efficiency, and projected intensity per pattern-wavelength) regularized with spectral smoothness and spatial total variation, optimized via ADAM.

## Results

DDSL achieves a spectral resolution of 15.5 nm FWHM across the 440–660 nm visible range (23 bands at 10 nm intervals), a mean depth error of 4 mm (max 8 mm) on a 3D-printed stair benchmark, and an acquisition speed of 6.6 fps using only 8 DDSL patterns plus 1 black pattern (0.15 s per scene). Depth consistency across different DDSL patterns is within 2 mm. The system uses a sub-$20 diffraction grating film on a standard RGB projector with stereo RGB cameras.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DDSL is compared against two prior affordable hyperspectral 3D imaging methods. Against Shin et al. [19] (Dispersed Structured Light), DDSL improves spectral FWHM from 18 nm to 15.5 nm and reduces acquisition time from 10 minutes to 0.15 seconds — a ×4000 speedup — by replacing hundreds of sequential scan patterns with 8 spectrally multiplexed DDSL patterns. Against Li et al. [13] (Practical Projector-Camera Setup), DDSL achieves a substantially tighter FWHM of 15.5 nm versus 40 nm, since Li et al. rely on broadband RGB illumination without dispersion. DDSL wins on both spectral accuracy and speed against both baselines, while maintaining a 4 mm depth error comparable to what those static-scene methods achieve. The ablation (Table 1) further confirms that the chosen pattern parameters (l_offset=40, l_width=5, l_shift=5) are near-optimal: widening lines to 20 px or shifting by 10 px causes reconstruction failure, while narrowing to 1 px degrades FWHM to 50 nm.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Shin et al.'s Dispersed Structured Light (DSL) framework, which first demonstrated that a diffraction grating film on a projector can enable hyperspectral 3D imaging with a compact projector-camera system. The key departure is the design of Dense Dispersed Structured Light (DDSL) patterns that spectrally multiplex illumination across fewer than ten projections, reducing acquisition from hundreds of patterns (~10 minutes) to 8 patterns (~0.15 seconds). Unlike prior affordable methods (Li et al.'s broadband RGB projector at 40 nm FWHM, or DSL at 18 nm), DDSL achieves 15.5 nm FWHM while remaining practical for dynamic scenes, and unlike expensive CASSI or hyperspectral video camera systems, it relies entirely on off-the-shelf RGB stereo cameras, a standard RGB projector, and a sub-$20 diffraction grating film.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DDSL is the first method to combine accurate hyperspectral 3D imaging (15.5 nm FWHM, 4 mm depth error) with dynamic-scene frame rates (6.6 fps) using affordable hardware, opening practical applications in food inspection, industrial quality control, and cultural heritage analysis where objects are in motion. The 4000× speedup over prior dispersed structured light is the headline result, but the broader significance is demonstrating that a $20 optical element plus standard consumer-grade cameras and a projector can replace expensive hyperspectral video cameras. Key limitations remain: 6.6 fps is constrained by software synchronization (hardware sync could push this higher), and the low diffraction efficiency of the grating film limits the effective scene range. An open question is whether the spectral multiplexing strategy generalizes to higher spectral resolutions or wider wavelength ranges without degrading depth accuracy.
