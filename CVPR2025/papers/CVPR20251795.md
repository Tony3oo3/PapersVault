## Problem

Fine-grained skill estimation from video—judging how well an action is performed rather than merely what action occurs—remains highly underexplored due to the absence of large-scale, diverse training data. Existing skill datasets contain fewer than 1,000 participants, cover only 1–8 skills, and lack the participant diversity needed to train modern data-hungry video models. This paper introduces BASKET, a 4,477-hour basketball video dataset with 32,232 players and 20 fine-grained skills, to provide a large-scale benchmark and quantify the gap between current video models and human performance.

## Input / Output

The model receives a single 8–10 minute highlight video (704×400, 30 FPS) composed of ~50 shuffled 10-second event clips of one player, with no bounding-box or player-identity annotations. The output is a set of 20 independent 5-class predictions (Poor, Fair, Average, Good, Excellent), one per fine-grained basketball skill such as three-point shooting, rebounding, and passing. Skill labels are computed relative to each player's league and season cohort, so the model must implicitly learn league-specific skill distributions without explicit league conditioning.

## Method

This is a dataset and empirical benchmark paper rather than a new architecture. The construction pipeline extracts timestamped player events from ~66,000 full-game videos across 21 leagues, randomly samples 50 events per player, and concatenates the resulting clips into shuffled ~9-minute highlight videos; skill annotations come from basketball experts who rank players on 20 skills, with ranks binned into 5 levels within each league-season cohort. For evaluation, nine state-of-the-art video models (VideoMamba, InternVideo2, TimeSformer, VideoMAE v2, UnmaskedTeacher, X-CLIP, SigLIP, MeMViT, LLaVA-OneVision) are fine-tuned with 20 linear classification heads under uniform frame sampling at 224×224 resolution. The best model (VideoMamba) achieves only 28.50% top-1 accuracy versus a 20% random baseline, while expert human annotators reach 66–72%, exposing a large capability gap. Generalization is further probed via cross-season, cross-league, cross-gender, and cross-geographic splits, revealing substantial out-of-domain degradation of over 4% on unseen leagues.

## Results

On the BASKET benchmark (top-1 accuracy averaged across 20 fine-grained basketball skills, 5-level classification), the best-performing model VideoMamba achieves only 28.50% accuracy, barely 8.5 points above the 20% random baseline. No model exceeds 30% accuracy, with InternVideo2 (27.52%) and UnmaskedTeacher (26.97%) following closely. In contrast, human experts achieve 66–72% accuracy on a simplified 3-level, 5-skill subset, highlighting a substantial gap between current video models and human skill perception.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VideoMamba (28.50%) leads all baselines, outperforming InternVideo2 (27.52%) by ~1 point and UnmaskedTeacher (26.97%) by ~1.5 points, likely benefiting from its bidirectional state-space architecture suited to long-range temporal reasoning. TimeSformer (25.21%) and VideoMAE2 (24.43%) form a mid-tier group, while X-CLIP (24.37%) and LLaVA-OneVision (23.84%) trail further behind. SigLIP (21.85%) and MeMViT (23.01%) are the weakest, with SigLIP barely exceeding the random baseline. Notably, the 8.2B-parameter LLaVA-OneVision underperforms the 74M-parameter VideoMamba by nearly 5 points, suggesting that scale alone does not solve fine-grained skill estimation. The paper's central finding is that all nine models cluster within a narrow 22–29% band, far below the 66–72% achieved by human experts, indicating that current video recognition architectures lack the nuanced perception capabilities needed for this task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in the skill estimation dataset lineage by scaling up orders of magnitude beyond prior efforts (JIGSAWS, MIT-Dive, FineDiving, LOGO, Ego-Exo4D), which typically contain fewer than 100 video hours and 1–8 skills. Rather than proposing a new architecture, it repurposes a diverse set of existing video recognition models (state-space, transformer, masked-autoencoder, contrastive, and vision-language) as baselines to stress-test their fine-grained, long-video skill perception capabilities. The multi-way classification formulation (20 skills × 5 levels from an 8–10 minute highlight reel) extends the single-skill action quality assessment paradigm to a substantially more complex, multi-attribute, long-context setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ~44-point gap between the best model (28.5%) and expert human annotators (72%) reveals that no current video architecture—regardless of whether it uses attention, state-space models, or multimodal pretraining—possesses the fine-grained skill perception needed for this task, pointing to a fundamental representational limitation rather than a data-scaling problem. The dataset's unprecedented diversity (32,232 players, 21 leagues, 4 continents) and the cross-season/cross-league generalization failures (dropping >4% on unseen leagues) open concrete questions about how to build models that capture nuanced technique, decision-making, and efficiency cues rather than relying on scene or background shortcuts. Practically, BASKET could catalyze tools for fair scouting and personalized player development, but the current benchmark also serves as a clear signal that the field needs new inductive biases for long-range, fine-grained video understanding.
