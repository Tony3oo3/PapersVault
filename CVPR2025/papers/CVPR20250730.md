## Problem

Despite the remarkable success of Multimodal LLMs in visual understanding, it remains unclear how language models trained exclusively on linguistic data attend to and process visual tokens during inference. Existing interpretability work has identified specialized attention heads for linguistic functions (retrieval, induction, rare words), but no systematic analysis has characterized whether analogous 'visual heads' exist, where they reside in the network, or how they behave across model families and scales.

## Input / Output

The analysis operates on 9 MLLM variants (LLaVA 1.5/1.6, VIP-LLaVA, Phi-3, Mistral at 3.8B–13B scales) processing PointQA samples consisting of a 336×336 (or dynamic-resolution) image with a region-specific bounding box prompt and a counting question, yielding a single-digit answer (1–7). For each sample, the method extracts the full attention matrix of the target output token across all layers and heads, then aggregates into per-head metrics: total attention weight on image tokens and an entropy-based concentration score. Downstream, head-removal experiments (top-k and random, across early/mid/late thirds of layers) produce accuracy deltas on MMBench, POPE, and QBench to quantify each head's functional contribution.

## Method

The paper introduces a score-based, modeling-free detection framework for visual heads that combines three signals: (1) the total attention weight a head allocates to image tokens, (2) a concentration score C = 1 − H/log₂(N) measuring how sharply the head's attention is focused (derived from the entropy of its attention distribution), and (3) a layer-dependent modulation func(l) = k/(l+ε) + a·e^(−bl) that up-weights early layers where visual processing is most active. This composite score Score(l,h,j) = Σ_{j∈I} α_{l,h,j}·(1 + C·func(l)) is averaged over 5,700 PointQA samples to rank heads per model. The authors validate the metric's cross-dataset consistency using Logit Lens analysis (showing attention stabilizes after early layers on straightforward benchmarks but evolves through middle layers on 'look-twice' reasoning tasks) and confirm findings via ablation: removing top-20 early-stage heads drops accuracy by 20–40%, while removing up to 100 late-stage heads has negligible impact, revealing that visual heads cluster in early/middle layers and that late-layer computation is largely redundant for visual tasks.

## Results

Removing the top 20 early-stage visual heads causes a 20–40% accuracy drop (e.g., MMBench drops from 75.44 to 27.64 for 1.6-vicuna-7B), while removing the same number of late-stage heads causes only a 1–7% drop. Incrementally removing up to 50 early-stage heads yields a near-linear performance decline (up to ~50% accuracy loss), whereas up to 100 late-stage heads can be pruned with negligible impact. Within the same model family, attention-head behavior shows a Pearson correlation >0.8 across variants, while cross-family correlation drops below 0.1. The 13B models exhibit notably lower variance in attention patterns across five benchmarks compared to 7B models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an interpretability study rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so the key contrasts are internal. Top-scored early-stage heads are far more critical than random early-stage heads: masking 20 top early heads drops MMBench accuracy by ~45 points (73.61→27.93) versus only ~3 points for 20 random early heads (73.61→70.37), confirming that the identified visual heads carry disproportionate functional weight. Early-stage heads dominate over mid- and late-stage heads: removing 20 top early heads costs 20–40% accuracy, while 20 top late heads cost only 0.3–7%, indicating that late layers are largely redundant for visual processing and are strong candidates for inference-time pruning. Comparing LLaVA 1.5 vs. 1.6, the 1.6 variant (with ~1.5k image tokens vs. 576) shows broadly higher attention weights to image tokens across all layers, but this advantage is less pronounced in the 13B model. Within-family models (e.g., 1.5-7B vs. 1.6-vicuna-7B vs. vip-7B) share highly correlated visual-head patterns (Pearson r > 0.8), whereas models from different families (LLaMA, Phi-3, Mistral) show near-zero correlation (r < 0.1), indicating that visual-head identity is shaped by pretraining rather than the adapter architecture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mechanistic interpretability framework for specialized attention heads (retrieval heads, induction heads, duplicate heads) from text-only LLMs to the multimodal setting, asking what attention heads do specifically when processing visual tokens. It builds on the MLLM architecture literature (LLaVA, VIP-LLaVA, CLIP-based encoders) and the Logit Lens/Tuned Lens technique for hidden-state analysis, but departs from prior work by introducing a concentration score as a complementary metric to raw attention weights, motivated by the observation that attention-weight-only analysis is inconsistent across benchmarks. The PointQA dataset is repurposed as a controlled probe for head detection, leveraging its region-specific questioning and single-token answers to isolate visual attention behavior.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic identification of 'visual heads' across four model families and four scales, revealing that they cluster in early and middle layers rather than being universally distributed like retrieval heads—a structural finding with implications for MLLM architecture design and efficient inference. The practical finding that up to 100 late-stage heads can be pruned with minimal performance loss offers a concrete path toward reducing computational cost for long visual-token sequences. Open questions include whether visual heads can be selectively fine-tuned to improve specific visual capabilities, whether the concentration metric generalizes to video and audio modalities, and whether the layer-clustering pattern persists in next-generation MLLMs with different adapter designs.
