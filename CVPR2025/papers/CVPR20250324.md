## Problem

Most vision-language models are trained on English-only image-text pairs, yet cross-cultural psychology suggests that speakers of different languages systematically attend to and describe different visual concepts. This paper asks whether such language-specific distributional differences in caption content are measurable in standard CV datasets and model outputs, and whether they have concrete implications for how models are trained and fine-tuned.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250324/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis takes image captions in seven typologically diverse languages (English, French, German, Russian, Chinese, Japanese, Korean) from the Crossmodal dataset and from LLaVA/Vertex API model outputs, all translated to English via GPT-4 to eliminate linguistic confounders. Outputs include unioned scene graphs (counting unique objects, relations, and attributes) comparing monolingual vs. multilingual caption sets, expressive coverage metrics (concreteness, authenticity, tone, embedding-space width) computed via LIWC and Sentence-BERT, and SPICE F-scores from fine-tuning experiments. For grounding, object sets are intersected with Visual Genome's dense annotations to verify that multilingual gains reflect real image content rather than subjective focus shifts.

## Method

The core measurement constructs scene graphs from each caption using FLAN-T5 fine-tuned on FACTUAL-MR, then computes the union of scene graphs across a set of captions (monolingual or multilingual) for each image, canonicalizing concepts via WordNet path similarity and embedding cosine similarity. Semantic variation is quantified by comparing the expected size of unioned scene graphs (objects, relations, attributes) between monolingual and multilingual caption sets; expressive variation is measured as the max-min span of LIWC metrics and maximum pairwise cosine distance in embedding space. To rule out the confound that multilingual variation merely reflects models operating in 'different language modes,' the paper compares same-model-across-language scene graph sizes against same-language-across-model sizes (LLaVA vs. GIT vs. BLIP), finding them comparable. Finally, GIT is fine-tuned on 1.8k single-language or multilingual caption sets from Crossmodal and evaluated with SPICE F-score on all 8 holdout language splits, demonstrating that models internalize language-specific distributional characteristics and that multilingual fine-tuning yields consistently strong cross-language performance.

## Results

On the Crossmodal (XM) dataset, multilingual caption sets (e.g., English+French+Chinese) produce unioned scene graphs with 46.0% more objects (4.35 vs. 2.98), 66.1% more relations (2.94 vs. 1.77), and 66.8% more attributes (2.97 vs. 1.78) than monolingual English sets. For model-generated captions, LLaVA multilingual sets yield 21.9% more objects (5.93 vs. 4.78) and 18.8% more relations (4.54 vs. 3.95), while Vertex API yields 13.2% more objects (4.17 vs. 3.48) and 16.6% more relations (3.40 vs. 2.77). Expressive coverage also widens substantially: tonality range increases by 53.4% (XM) and embedding-space coverage increases from 0.42 to 0.52 (XM) and from 0.22 to 0.45 (LLaVA). In fine-tuning experiments, a model trained on Japanese captions achieves SPICE F-score 0.266 on the Japanese test set versus 0.212 on English, confirming language-specific distributional internalization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is not against a single competing method but rather monolingual vs. multilingual caption sets, with GIT [121] and BLIP [71] serving as cross-model English baselines in §3.2. When comparing same-model multilingual scene graphs (LLaVA or Vertex in 3 languages) against same-language multi-model scene graphs (LLaVA + GIT + BLIP in English), the multilingual single-model graphs retain 92.4% of objects, 98.4% of relations, and 97.7% of attributes — showing that language-driven variation is comparable in magnitude to architecture/training-driven variation. Furthermore, Vertex multilingual captions contain 47.8% of objects, 71.4% of relations, and 59.5% of attributes not covered by English GIT or BLIP captions, confirming that the multilingual gain is not reducible to model diversity. In the fine-tuning experiments (§4), GIT is the base model; a model fine-tuned on a single language consistently outperforms all other single-language models on its own language's test set (e.g., Japanese: 0.266 vs. 0.212 for English), while the multilingual fine-tuned model achieves the second-best score on 5 out of 7 languages, demonstrating that multilingual training captures a broader perceptual distribution than any single-language baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new analytical ground by systematically quantifying cross-lingual variation in image captions using scene graph parsing (FLAN-T5 on FACTUAL-MR) and LIWC-based expressive measures, a combination not previously applied to multilingual caption comparison. It draws conceptually on cross-cultural psychology (Nisbett & Masuda's analytic vs. holistic perception, Boroditsky's linguistic relativity) and the 'curse of multilinguality' debate in NLP, but repurposes these for a vision-language context. The experimental infrastructure builds on the Crossmodal dataset's controlled annotation protocol and on standard multilingual VLMs (LLaVA, GIT, BLIP, Vertex API), but the paper's contribution is the measurement framework and the fine-tuning transfer experiments rather than a new model architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides concrete evidence that English-only training creates a 'perceptual monoculture' by excluding 46–66% of the semantic content (objects, relations, attributes) that multilingual captions would expose, reframing multilingual data as a source of visual descriptive diversity rather than a performance trade-off. Practical implications include recommendations to use organically produced multilingual captions over English-to-X translations and to explore structured learning paradigms (e.g., contrastive learning) that exploit cross-lingual concept diversity. Limitations include coverage of only seven languages, reliance on GPT-4 translation for fair comparison, and mid-scale experiments; open questions include whether the effect scales to low-resource languages, whether the observed variation is truly language-driven or partly cultural/geographic, and whether downstream tasks (retrieval, VQA) benefit measurably from multilingual pretraining.
