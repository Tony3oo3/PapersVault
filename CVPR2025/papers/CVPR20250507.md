## Problem

Existing multimodal reasoning benchmarks target at most college-level difficulty, while research-level benchmarks (e.g., OmniMedVQA, MicroBench) emphasize lower-level perception rather than the complex abductive and deductive reasoning required for scientific discovery. No benchmark evaluates whether MLLMs can perform the full scientific workflow—interpreting experimental images, generating mechanistic hypotheses, and proposing follow-up experiments—at research-level difficulty in a domain like biological microscopy.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250507/cropped_page0_idx8.webp)

## Input / Output

Each sample pairs one or more microscopy images (brightfield, fluorescence, or electron; over 60% are multi-image sets to handle multichannel data and comparisons) with a multiple-choice question spanning tissue, cellular, subcellular, and atomic scales. The raw expert questions average 158 words with detailed answers (52 words), while the converted MCQs are shorter (66-word stems, 15-word answers). Models produce a selected answer letter along with chain-of-thought reasoning, which is used for qualitative error analysis.

## Method

The benchmark is built from 1,042 raw VQA triples created by 12 biology experts (30–40 min each) across three tasks: expert visual understanding, hypothesis generation, and experiment proposal. Converting these to MCQs requires a novel two-stage pipeline: Stage 1 uses DSPy to optimize an LLM prompt that maps raw QA pairs to exam-aligned MCQs following NBME item-writing standards, seeded by 50 manually crafted gold-standard examples. Stage 2 introduces RefineBot, an iterative agent loop where an evaluator LLM answers the MCQ with chain-of-thought and reflects on the strategies used (e.g., eliminating distractors via general knowledge without the image), then a rewriter LLM revises the stem and generates new distractors that invalidate those shortcuts, with a semantic-consistency checker preventing drift. This adversarial refinement drops model accuracy by 35–42% relative to Stage 1, confirming the removal of language shortcuts while introducing only a small (<10 point) bias against the models used in generation.

## Results

On MicroVQA, the best-performing model (o1) achieves 52.8% overall accuracy, with task-specific scores of 55.4% (expert visual understanding), 50.2% (hypothesis generation), and 53.0% (experiment proposal). Performance variation across all 17 evaluated MLLMs is remarkably small, with most models scoring between 40–53%, and a human expert baseline achieving 50.3%. Notably, smaller models (e.g., Qwen-2-VL-7b at 48.8%, Gemini-Flash-1.5-8b at 46.7%) underperform their larger counterparts by less than 3 points, suggesting multimodal perception rather than language reasoning is the primary bottleneck. The medical-specialist model LLaVA-Med-Mistral-7B (43.0%) outperforms its base LLaVA-Mistral-7B (39.8%) by 4.5 points, confirming that domain-specific fine-tuning on scientific articles yields measurable gains.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 17 MLLMs against a human expert baseline (50.3%) and random chance (22.0%). o1 leads at 52.8%, narrowly ahead of Claude-3.5-Sonnet (51.7%) and Gemini-Pro-1.5 (51.1%), with all top models within ~2 points of each other. A striking finding is that smaller models remain highly competitive: Qwen-2-VL-7b (48.8%) actually outperforms its 72B counterpart (47.5%), and Gemini-Flash-1.5-8b (46.7%) trails Gemini-Pro-1.5 by only 4.4 points—far less than the 9.6-point gap seen on MMMU. The medical-specialist LLaVA-Med-Mistral-7B (43.0%) beats its base LLaVA-Mistral-7B (39.8%) by 4.5 points, demonstrating that fine-tuning on PubMed scientific articles provides a clear but modest advantage. Llama-3.2-11b-Vision is a clear outlier at 30.3%, likely due to instruction-following issues rather than reasoning limitations. Overall, the tight performance spread (40–53% for most models) indicates that MicroVQA's difficulty is driven by multimodal perception and domain knowledge rather than language-model scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of two underexplored axes: research-level difficulty and high-order scientific reasoning (hypothesis generation, experiment proposal) in multimodal benchmarks. Existing multimodal reasoning benchmarks (MMMU, ScienceQA, MathVista) cap at college-level difficulty, while research-level benchmarks (MMSci, LabBench, MicroBench) emphasize lower-level perception and recognition. The MCQ generation pipeline repurposes the DSPy framework for prompt optimization and introduces an agent-based adversarial refinement loop (RefineBot) inspired by chain-of-thought analysis, departing from standard zero-shot distractor generation. The hypothesis generation task draws on visual abductive reasoning literature, but the experiment proposal task has no direct prior benchmark counterpart.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 53% peak accuracy and the finding that perception errors dominate (50%) over knowledge errors (30%) and overgeneralization (13%) provide a clear diagnostic: current MLLMs fail primarily at visual interpretation of microscopy images, not at language-based reasoning. This redirects the field's focus toward improving vision encoders and image representations for scientific imaging rather than scaling language models. Open questions include whether fine-tuning on broader scientific imaging data (beyond tissue pathology) can close the gap, whether the benchmark generalizes to other microscopy-heavy domains (materials science, ecology), and how to evaluate open-ended scientific reasoning beyond MCQ formats. The human baseline at 50.3% also raises the question of whether MLLMs can serve as cross-domain assistants for specialists who are experts in one subfield but not another.
