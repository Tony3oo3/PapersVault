## Problem

Existing facial motion generation methods rely on audio or text as control signals, neither of which supports generating specific facial actions at precise frame-level timing. Audio-driven methods can only synchronize motions with audio, while text-driven methods offer only coarse temporal adverbial cues (e.g., 'then'). This paper introduces timeline control—a multi-track, frame-level specification of facial actions—as a new control signal to enable precise temporal control of facial motion generation.

## Input / Output

The method takes a multi-track timeline where each frame is represented as a 16-dimensional binary vector (one dimension per facial action across brows, eyes, mouth, gaze, and head pose) and produces a full-face motion sequence as FaceVerse 3DMM coefficients, which are then rendered into photorealistic videos by a separate diffusion-based renderer. Natural language text can also be used as input, with ChatGPT converting it into a timeline via few-shot prompting. The annotation pipeline additionally outputs frame-level facial action labels for arbitrary input videos.

## Method

The paper contributes two components: a labor-efficient annotation pipeline and a base-branch diffusion generation model. For annotation, TICC (Toeplitz Inverse Covariance-based Clustering) segments ARKit blendshape time series into motion-pattern intervals and clusters similar patterns, requiring only manual identification of a few representative intervals per cluster. The generation model uses a diffusion framework where a shared base network encodes global facial motion couplings from all region timelines, while three branch networks (upper face, lower face, pose & other) generate decoupled regional motions via cross-attention with timeline tokens. Timeline tokens are kept at their initial projection at every transformer layer to prevent temporal information drift, and classifier-free guidance with per-region dropout (p=0.5) improves generalization. The base-branch split balances accuracy (through regional decoupling) with naturalness (through learned global couplings like subtle head lifts during brow raises).

## Results

For facial motion annotation, TICC-based segmentation achieves Macro-F1 scores of 0.90 (brow), 0.91 (eye squint/widen), and 0.87 (mouth), while threshold-based annotation scores 0.95 (eye closure), 0.87 (pose), and 0.89 (gaze). For generation, the full model attains a Timeline Alignment Score (TAS) of 0.84, FID_fm of 4.54×10⁻¹, FID_Δfm of 0.09×10⁻¹, and SND of 4.63×10⁻¹. In a user study with 21 participants, 92% rated the automatic annotations as accurate, 89% rated generated motions as temporally aligned with the input timeline, and 86% rated them as natural.

## Baselines & Comparisons

No quantitative baselines exist because, as the authors state, no prior method has achieved fine-grained timeline control for facial motion generation. The closest prior work (AgentAvatar, InstructAvatar) can only describe temporal changes coarsely via temporal adverbs and does not provide open-source code, so comparison is limited to qualitative video demos. The ablation study serves as the primary quantitative evidence: removing the branch network drops TAS from 0.84 to 0.66 and inflates FID_fm from 4.54 to 7.39; removing the base network drops TAS to 0.81 and inflates FID_fm to 12.4; fully decoupling all regions (all decoup.) drops TAS to 0.69 and inflates FID_fm to 28.4, confirming that the base-branch design is essential for both accuracy and naturalness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes TICC (Toeplitz Inverse Covariance-based Clustering), originally designed for general multivariate time series segmentation, as the core annotation engine for frame-level facial action intervals — a task previously handled only by coarse temporal adverbs in text-driven methods or threshold-based heuristics. For generation, it adapts the diffusion-based facial motion paradigm (FaceVerse 3DMM coefficients) but introduces a novel base-branch architecture that explicitly separates global motion coupling (base network) from region-specific decoupled generation (branch networks), departing from prior single-network or fully-decoupled designs. Timeline control itself has precedent in 3D human body motion generation (TEACH, multi-track timeline control), but the authors argue the rapid dynamics of facial motion make the segment-and-piece approach infeasible, necessitating the end-to-end diffusion formulation presented here.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work opens a new axis of control for facial motion generation — precise per-frame timing of individual actions — that neither audio nor text signals can provide, with direct applications to digital humans, filmmaking, and expressive avatar systems. The labor-efficient TICC-based annotation pipeline is practically valuable for building fine-grained temporal supervision at scale without intensive manual labeling. Key open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond the RealTalk dataset (unscripted conversations), whether the base-branch split into upper/lower/pose regions is optimal, and how the method handles highly asymmetric or idiosyncratic expressions. The reliance on an in-house blendshape detector and the non-main-contribution renderer also limit immediate reproducibility.
