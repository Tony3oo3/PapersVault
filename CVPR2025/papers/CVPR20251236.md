## Problem

Performance-based ranking of entities (algorithms, models, devices) lacks rigorous theoretical foundations: the mathematical nature of 'performance' is undefined, comparisons are chosen by intuition or imitation, and there is no axiomatic guarantee that a ranking is stable under addition or removal of entities. A critical analysis of 150 biomedical image analysis challenges found that scores are justified in only 23% of cases and rank computation methods are reported in only 36%, with at least 10 different ad hoc methods for combining multiple scores.

## Input / Output

The framework takes as input a probability measure P on a measurable space (Ω, Σ) representing the performance of an entity, a task-specific satisfaction function S: Ω→ℝ assigning meaning to outcomes, and an application-specific importance function I: Ω→ℝ_{≥0} encoding preferences over outcome types. It produces a preorder ≼ on the space of all probability measures, a ranking function mapping entities to positions in [1, |E|], and a scalar ranking score R_I(P) = E_P[IS]/E_P[I] in [s_min, s_max]. The sample space Ω is task-dependent (e.g., {tn, fp, fn, tp} for two-class classification), and the importance I is the key parameter that differentiates rankings for different application contexts without re-evaluating entities.

## Method

The paper constructs a six-pillar mathematical framework grounding performances as probability measures (probability theory) and their comparisons via a preorder ≼ (order theory), with satisfaction S modeling the task, evaluation Φ modeling how entities map to performances, scores X providing numerical summaries, and importance I encoding application-specific preferences. On top of this, three axioms are defined: (1) rank stability under entity set changes, (2) coherence with the satisfaction ordering, and (3) closure under achievable combinations Φ. Three theorems then give sufficient conditions on scores X to satisfy each axiom. The key constructive result is the family of ranking scores R_I(P) = E_P[IS]/E_P[I], which are pseudolinear, satisfy all three axioms for Φ=conv, and are universal across tasks. An empirical study on two-class classification shows that accuracy, F_β, TPR, TNR, PPV, and NPV are valid ranking scores (perfectly correlated with some R_I), while commonly used scores like MCC, markedness, odds ratio, and the geometric mean of TNR and TPR fail Axiom 3 even at fixed priors.

## Results

The paper's central empirical result (Table 1) tests 25 common two-class classification scores against three axioms for valid performance ordering. Eight scores—accuracy, F-scores (β=0.5, 1.0, 2.0), NPV, PPV, TNR, and TPR—pass all three tests universally (without prior constraints), while scores like balanced accuracy, Cohen's κ, MCC, markedness, and the geometric mean of TNR/TPR fail Axiom 3 even when priors are fixed. In all cases where the axioms are satisfied, the score achieves a perfect Kendall's τ correlation of 1 with some ranking score, confirming the universality of the proposed family. For scores that fail, the maximum τ with any ranking score drops substantially (e.g., MCC reaches only 0.746 without constraints, 0.963 with balanced priors), quantifying how far they deviate from valid orderings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new classifier or model to benchmark against baselines; instead, it evaluates existing classification scores against its axiomatic framework for valid performance ordering. The key finding is a three-tier classification of scores: (1) universally valid scores (accuracy, all F-scores, NPV, PPV, TNR, TPR) that satisfy all three axioms regardless of prior distribution; (2) conditionally valid scores (balanced accuracy, Cohen's κ, informedness, PLR, PTN, PTP) that only satisfy the axioms when class priors are fixed; and (3) invalid scores (MCC, markedness, geometric mean of TNR/TPR, odds ratio, sensitivity index d') that fail Axiom 3 even with fixed priors, meaning they can produce orderings where a convex combination of two performances is ranked outside the range of its components. The paper shows that every valid score is perfectly correlated (τ=1) with at least one ranking score R_I, demonstrating that the proposed family is expressive enough to encompass all legitimate ranking criteria. Notably, widely-used scores like MCC and the geometric mean of TNR and TPR—commonly cited in the literature as 'balanced' or 'robust' metrics—are shown to be unsuitable for establishing performance orderings, a finding with direct implications for challenge organizers and practitioners selecting evaluation metrics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new theoretical ground rather than extending a specific prior method. It draws on probability theory (Kolmogorov) and order theory (Davey & Priestley, Grätzer) as mathematical foundations, but the authors explicitly note that no prior work provides a rigorous measurable space, performance ordering, or axiomatic framework for comparing performances. It responds directly to critiques of ranking methodology in biomedical and vision competitions (Maier-Hein et al. 2018; Nguyen et al. 2023), which identified that scores are rarely justified and rank computation methods are inconsistent, but those works did not propose a formal theory. The paper's contribution is the first axiomatic definition of performance orderings and a universal parametric family of 'ranking scores' that subsumes accuracy, TPR, TNR, PPV, and F-scores while excluding others.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for anyone organizing competitions or comparing methods: the paper proves that widely-used scores such as MCC, Markedness, Odds Ratio, and the geometric mean of TNR/TPR cannot induce valid performance orderings even under fixed priors, challenging a common working assumption in the field. The ranking scores family provides an infinite, parameterized set of valid alternatives that encode application-specific preferences through the importance variable. Open questions include extending the full axiomatic treatment beyond two-class classification (the paper sketches connections to multi-class, regression, detection, clustering, and ranking tasks but does not fully develop them), and whether stronger axioms could further constrain the admissible score family.
