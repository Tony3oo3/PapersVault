## Problem

Existing bias research in text-to-image diffusion models focuses on explicit biases with recognizable visual patterns (e.g., skin color, gender), which are relatively easy to detect. This paper identifies a more dangerous and underexplored threat: implicit biases (emotional tone, cultural stereotypes, religious orientation) that lack fixed visual features, manifest through diverse semantic forms depending on context, and can be intentionally injected to subtly manipulate user perception over time.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252459/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, the method consumes a standard user text prompt, which is encoded into a D×L text embedding (D=1024, L=77 for SD v2.1). A small adaptive module then modifies this embedding by adding an input-conditioned bias direction vector before it is passed to the frozen diffusion model, producing a generated image that subtly conveys the attacker-specified bias (e.g., negative or positive emotional tone) while preserving the original prompt semantics. The attack is plug-and-play: no user prompt modification or model retraining is required, and the module operates autonomously without real-time LLM calls.

## Method

The IBI-Attacks framework operates in three stages. First, an LLM generates N neutral prompts and rewrites them with a target bias by adding adjectives (minimizing structural changes), and the mean difference between the two embedding sets yields a single bias direction vector that already encodes multiple semantic expressions. Second, a SENet-inspired adaptive feature selection module (a two-layer MLP with global average pooling along token and embedding dimensions) is trained to dynamically scale this fixed direction based on the input prompt embedding, preventing excessive or insufficient bias in different contexts. Third, at inference, the trained module is inserted after the text encoder of any pre-trained diffusion model, adding the adapted bias vector to the user's prompt embedding before generation. The key insight is that a single average direction in the text embedding space generalizes across diverse prompts and even transfers zero-shot to other domains (animals, natural scenes), enabling a training-free, model-agnostic attack that preserves image quality (minimal FID/PickScore degradation) while achieving 80%+ bias injection success rates.

## Results

On Stable Diffusion 2.1 with COCO captions, IBI achieves 80.2% negative bias injection rate (vs. 14.4% for the unmodified model) while preserving text-image alignment (CLIP_txt-img = 0.3637 vs. 0.3633 original) and structural similarity (SSIM = 0.6987). For positive bias, IBI reaches 83.7% injection rate with CLIP_txt-img = 0.3602. The adaptive module improves stealth over the fixed-vector variant (IBI w/o adap), raising SSIM from 0.5880 to 0.6987 and CLIP_img-img from 0.8143 to 0.8793 for negative bias, while maintaining comparable injection rates. Zero-shot transfer to animal and natural scenes yields 95.1% and 89.2% negative bias rates respectively, and the attack generalizes to SD3 (68.2%), FLUX (65.4%), and MUSE (91.2%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against explicit prompt modification, IBI trades a modest reduction in bias injection rate (80.2% vs. 89.1% for negative; 83.7% vs. 98.0% for positive) for substantially better stealth: SSIM improves from 0.5221 to 0.6987 (negative) and CLIP_img-img from 0.7354 to 0.8793, meaning the generated images remain far more semantically aligned with the user's original prompt. In human evaluation, users identify IBI-injected images as abnormal only 35.8% of the time—nearly identical to the 35.7% rate for normal images—whereas explicit modification is flagged 57.5% of the time. Regarding debiasing defenses, IBI is largely robust: Chuang et al. [10] (word-direction projection) reduces the negative bias rate only from 80.2% to 78.2%, and Kim et al. [21] (end-of-sequence token modification) from 80.2% to 79.6%, while Kim's method severely degrades image quality (CLIP_txt-img drops to 0.139, PickScore to 18.523 vs. 21.766 without defense). The paper also demonstrates that the attack transfers zero-shot across model architectures (SD3, FLUX, MUSE) and semantic domains (person → animal, person → nature) without retraining the adaptive module.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior bias-injection work (e.g., Backdooring Bias [38]) in two key ways: it targets implicit biases (emotional tone, cultural/religious orientation) rather than explicit visual features (skin color, gender), and it operates entirely in the prompt-embedding space via a plug-and-play module rather than requiring model fine-tuning or prompt modification. The adaptive feature-selection module borrows the squeeze-and-excitation mechanism from SENet [17], repurposing it to dynamically scale a fixed bias direction based on input context. The use of LLMs here is limited to offline data generation for computing the bias direction, distinguishing it from LLM-guided diffusion approaches that require real-time API calls during inference.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates a practical attack vector against any deployed T2I model that is difficult to detect (human study shows near-chance-level identification) and resistant to existing debiasing defenses, raising urgent questions about the integrity of public image-generation services. The zero-shot transferability across domains (person → animal → nature) and architectures (SD, SD3, FLUX, MUSE) suggests the threat is broad rather than model-specific. Open questions include how to systematically audit deployed models for such embedding-space manipulations, whether the same principle applies to other modalities (text-to-video, text-to-audio), and whether the implicit-bias framing can be extended to more adversarial use cases beyond sentiment manipulation.
