## Problem

Existing model merging methods either require identical model architectures or rely on labeled data and supervised continued training (e.g., FuseLLM needs 1.8B tokens and 33 hours of training). No prior method can merge heterogeneous multimodal large language models (MLLMs) with architectural differences in a fully unsupervised, training-free manner.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252717/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes two MLLM parameter sets derived from the same pre-trained language model but with different architectural modifications (e.g., CogVLM duplicates QKV weights while mPLUG-Owl2 duplicates KV and LayerNorm weights), plus an unlabeled subset of 100 test inputs and a grid of α candidates in [0, 0.6] with granularity 0.1. It outputs merged parameters on the base model's architecture. Notably, the hyperparameter search consumes only unlabeled generation responses (no ground-truth labels), and the 100-sample subset is sufficient to approximate the full-test-set optimum.

## Method

AdaMMS operates in three steps. First, a parameter mapping function aligns corresponding weights between the two heterogeneous MLLMs: shared pre-trained weights map directly, duplicated multimodal parameters map to their original counterparts, and parameters with no counterpart map to φ (excluded from merging). Second, linear interpolation is applied on task vectors (θ_i − θ_0) following the Task Arithmetic paradigm, with the mapping handling architectural asymmetry via a piecewise formula that preserves unmapped base-model parameters. Third, an unsupervised hyperparameter search selects α by minimizing the adjacent response difference (DiffCnt) between generations at neighboring α candidates, based on the key insight that generation consistency (response stability) correlates with model performance. A convexity argument shows that near the performance optimum, the Hessian is positive semi-definite, making responses locally stable and thus the minimum-difference α a valid proxy for the maximum-performance α.

## Results

On the Qwen2-VL pair (Table 1), AdaMMS achieves a SUM score of 563.56 (+26.84 over the average of the two original models), ranking in the top 2 on 8 out of 9 benchmarks. On the LLaMA-based pair (Table 2), AdaMMS reaches 459.21 (+31.23 over the average), ranking top 2 on 7 out of 9 benchmarks. The unsupervised hyper-parameter selection method selects α values within 0.5 points of the oracle optimum (Table 4), and only 100 unlabeled samples are needed for the search step without sacrificing performance (Table 3).

## Baselines & Comparisons

AdaMMS outperforms all four baselines on the SUM metric in both main experiments: on the Qwen2-VL pair it leads MetaGPT (563.56 vs. 560.75), Ties-Merging (554.75), Task Arithmetic (536.32), and both DARE variants (469.93 / 493.41); on the LLaMA pair it leads MetaGPT (459.21 vs. 445.97), Task Arithmetic (443.89), DARE-Linear (434.29), Ties-Merging (361.63), and DARE-Ties (349.94). MetaGPT is the strongest baseline, ranking top-2 on 5 of 9 tasks in Table 1 and 5 of 9 in Table 2, but it still trails AdaMMS by 2.8 and 13.2 SUM points respectively. The DARE variants (DARE-Linear and DARE-Ties) perform worst, often falling below both original models (e.g., DARE-Linear drops to 469.93 vs. 559.07 for Qwen2-VL alone), indicating that the drop-and-rescale strategy is particularly harmful for heterogeneous MLLMs. Notably, AdaMMS is the only method whose merged model significantly surpasses both source models on average (+3.36 over Qwen2-VL, +3.90 over CogVLM), whereas most baselines fail to exceed the stronger original model. On OCRBench and TextVQA, all merging methods—including AdaMMS—show a performance drop relative to the base model, which the authors attribute to the large performance gap between the two source models on those specific benchmarks.

## Relation to Prior Work

AdaMMS builds directly on the Task Arithmetic paradigm (task vectors defined as fine-tuned minus pre-trained weights, combined via linear interpolation) but extends it to a setting where prior methods—Task Arithmetic, Ties-Merging, DARE, and MetaGPT—all assume identical model architectures. The key departure is twofold: a parameter mapping function that aligns structurally different MLLMs (e.g., CogVLM's duplicated QKV vs. mPLUG-Owl2's duplicated KV+LayerNorm), and an unsupervised hyperparameter search that replaces the labeled validation sets required by all prior merging methods. This positions the work between homogeneous-architecture merging (which cannot handle structural differences) and heterogeneous LLM fusion approaches like FuseLLM/FuseChat (which require 1.8B tokens of supervised continued training).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for practitioners who want to combine capabilities of existing MLLMs without collecting labeled validation data or incurring the multi-day training costs of distillation-based fusion. However, the method is scoped to second-level architectural differences (same pre-trained LM backbone, different structural modifications), leaving the harder first-level case (different pre-trained LMs) unaddressed. The convexity assumption underpinning the generation-consistency metric is idealized, and the paper acknowledges performance degradation when the two source models have large capability gaps on specific tasks (OCRBench, TextVQA). Open questions include whether the consistency-based selection generalizes to larger model scales, more than two source models, or architectures with fundamentally different tokenizer vocabularies.
