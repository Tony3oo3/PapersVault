## Problem

Existing adversarial detection methods are primarily designed for gradient-based attacks and struggle to generalize to generative-based attacks (GAN, diffusion) that produce imbalanced, anisotropic perturbations concentrated in high-frequency and salient regions. Moreover, prior approaches that attempt broader coverage (e.g., EPSAD) incur prohibitive inference time overhead (396s for 100 samples), making them impractical for real-world deployment. The paper asks whether a single detector can be trained to detect all types of unseen adversarial attacks—gradient-based, generative-based, and physical—with minimal inference cost.

## Input / Output

The detector takes a standard RGB image as input and outputs a binary adversarial/natural classification score. The backbone is XceptionNet trained with cross-entropy loss on a binary task. During training, half the batch is converted to pseudo-adversarial data via Perturbation Forgery (no real adversarial examples are needed), while the other half remains clean. The sparse mask pipeline (saliency detection + GradCAM + Sobel) operates on the natural image to localize where pseudo-noise is injected, mimicking the anisotropic patterns of generative attacks.

## Method

The core theoretical contribution is proving that all adversarial noise distributions are proximal in a metric space (Theorem 1), implying the existence of an open covering that can be approximated by continuously perturbing a single reference distribution (FGSM noise). Perturbation Forgery operationalizes this: (1) it estimates a multivariate Gaussian from FGSM noise, then perturbs the mean and covariance per training batch to form the open covering; (2) it generates sparse masks by intersecting saliency/GradCAM regions with Sobel high-frequency edges to localize noise; (3) it samples noise from the perturbed distribution, applies the sparse mask via Hadamard product, and adds it to natural images to create pseudo-adversarial data. The binary detector is trained on this synthetic mixture, requiring only ~500 samples for distribution estimation and achieving AUROC >0.99 across gradient, GAN, diffusion, and physical attacks with ~4.85s inference for 100 ImageNet samples.

## Results

On ImageNet100 (ε=4/255), the method achieves AUROC scores of 0.9931 (MIM), 0.9944 (MM), and 0.9941 (AutoAttack) against gradient-based and adaptive attacks, with an average of ~0.992 across all 8 attacks. Against generative-based attacks, it reaches 0.9878 (CDA), 0.9678 (TTP), and 0.9223 (Diff-PGD), substantially outperforming all baselines. On face datasets, it achieves near-perfect AUROC (0.9999) on physical attacks (Adv-Sticker, Adv-Glasses, Adv-Mask) and 0.9762 on Adv-Makeup. Inference time is 4.85s for 100 ImageNet samples, comparable to SPAD (4.56s) and far below EPSAD (396.81s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the four adversarial detection baselines on gradient-based attacks (Table 2), the method outperforms LID (by ~7–12 AUROC points, e.g., 0.9931 vs 0.9146 on MIM), LiBRe (by ~12–14 points, e.g., 0.9944 vs 0.8573 on MM), and SPAD (by ~1–1.3 points, e.g., 0.9944 vs 0.9811 on MM). EPSAD achieves slightly higher AUROC on 6 of 8 gradient attacks (e.g., 0.9998 vs 0.9911 on BIM) but at 82× the inference cost (396.81s vs 4.85s per 100 samples). On generative-based attacks (Table 3), the method dominates all baselines: it beats EPSAD by 24–77 points (e.g., 0.9223 vs 0.1507 on Diff-PGD), SPAD by 3.5–8 points, and DIRE by 1–9 points. On face attacks (Table 4), it surpasses ODIN by 34–37 points on physical attacks (0.9999 vs 0.6325–0.6970), He et al. by 11–47 points, and Luo et al. by 35–37 points, while also outperforming SPAD on Adv-Makeup (0.9762 vs 0.9657) and AMT-GAN (0.9216 vs 0.8969).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the adversarial detection line (LID, LiBRe, SPAD, EPSAD) by introducing a theoretical framework based on the proximity of adversarial noise distributions in a metric space, proving the existence of an open covering that justifies training on perturbed versions of a single known attack's noise. Methodologically, it departs from prior detectors that either require actual adversarial examples or expensive diffusion-model-based purification, instead repurposing ideas from synthetic image detection (high-frequency saliency, GradCAM, Sobel sparsification) to generate pseudo-adversarial data via 'Perturbation Forgery.' The key conceptual leap is treating the detection problem as distinguishing an open covering of proximal Gaussian noise distributions from the natural data distribution, rather than memorizing specific attack signatures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a concrete and growing gap: generative-based attacks (M3D, Diff-PGD) produce imbalanced, anisotropic perturbations that evade existing detectors, and the proposed method handles them with ~4.85s inference for 100 samples versus EPSAD's 396.81s. The open-covering theorem provides a principled (if heuristic) justification for why one perturbed distribution can generalize across attack families, though the Gaussian noise assumption and the reliance on a single initial attack (FGSM) limit the theoretical guarantees. Open questions include whether the framework extends to video or multimodal adversarial data, how sensitive performance is to the choice of initial attack, and whether the significant white-box attack degradation (AUROC drops from ~0.99 to ~0.91) can be mitigated.
