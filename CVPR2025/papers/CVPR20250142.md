## Problem

Integrating physics-based fluid and solid dynamics with 3D Gaussian Splatting for realistic novel view synthesis remains unsolved. Naively coupling PBD simulation with 3DGS rendering produces spiky artifacts under large rotational deformations of solids, and vanilla 3DGS cannot handle the specular, translucent, and refractive appearance of dynamic fluids. Prior work (e.g., PhysGaussian) baked lighting into spherical harmonics, failing to produce realistic rendering when fluids undergo significant motion.

## Input / Output

The system takes multi-view RGB images of a 3D scene as input, automatically segments foreground objects from the background, and reconstructs them via differentiable 3DGS training. It produces rendered novel views of the dynamically simulated scene at arbitrary camera poses, with physically-based specular highlights on fluids, soft shadows, and foam/spray effects. The system also supports interactive editing: users can segment objects, displace them (triggering AI inpainting of exposed regions), or transform object states (e.g., liquefying a solid).

## Method

GSP unifies 3DGS rendering and PBD simulation under a shared particle-based representation but decouples them for solids: Poisson-disk-sampled interior particles drive PBD simulation, while trained surface Gaussian kernels are deformed via generalized moving least squares (GMLS) interpolation at render time. An anisotropy loss (capping the ratio of largest to middle kernel scaling) prevents over-stretching artifacts during large rotational deformations. For fluids, a single set of particles serves both simulation (PBF with density constraints and position-based surface tension) and rendering (ellipsoid splatting with PBR specular shading and Beer's-Law refraction via additive-blended thickness). The rendering pipeline re-engineers variance shadow mapping and LaMa inpainting into the 3DGS splatting framework to handle dynamic shadows and exposed regions from displaced objects.

## Results

GSP is a systems paper evaluated primarily through qualitative visual results and timing benchmarks rather than standard quantitative metrics (e.g., PSNR, SSIM). Table 1 reports per-step simulation and rendering times across 10 scenes: simulation ranges from 0.8 s (Astronaut, 145K fluid kernels) to 8.1 s (Waves, 817K fluid kernels), while rendering of solids takes 1.5–24.6 ×10⁻² s and fluids 3.8–11.6 ×10⁻² s. The Lego bulldozer scene (Fig. 10) involves 334,815 solid and 280,000 fluid Gaussian kernels with a total simulation time of 3.8 s per step. Ablation studies confirm that anisotropy regularization, PBR specular shading, variance shadow mapping, and LaMa inpainting each contribute visibly to rendering quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The only direct baseline comparison is against PhysGaussian [72], the prior work that also integrates physical simulation with 3DGS. GSP outperforms PhysGaussian on fluid rendering quality: PhysGaussian bakes lighting into spherical harmonics, which fails to produce realistic specular highlights when fluids undergo significant motion, whereas GSP's PBR-based shading (following GaussianShader) dynamically synthesizes specular reflections that track the fluid surface. The paper notes this contrast explicitly in the ablation (Fig. 5), showing that without specular terms fluids appear 'almost smoke-like.' The full side-by-side comparison with PhysGaussian is deferred to the supplementary material, so no quantitative metric (e.g., PSNR or user study score) is reported in the main text. No other baselines (e.g., vanilla 3DGS, 4DGS, or other fluid renderers) are quantitatively compared; the paper positions itself as a systems contribution demonstrating feasibility rather than a benchmarking study.

## Relation to Prior Work

GSP sits at the intersection of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) for rendering and Position-Based Dynamics (PBD/XPBD) for simulation, unifying them through their shared particle-based representation. It directly extends GaussianShader's PBR shading model to handle dynamic specular reflections on fluids, and builds on Position-Based Fluids (PBF) and position-based surface tension for the fluid solver. The closest prior work, PhysGaussian, also couples physics with 3DGS but uses kernels for both simulation and rendering and bakes lighting into spherical harmonics; GSP departs by decoupling simulation particles from rendering kernels for solids, adopting PBR shading for fluids, and adding two-way fluid-solid coupling with surface tension.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first demonstration that physically-based fluid dynamics can be integrated into a 3DGS pipeline with realistic specular rendering, opening a path toward interactive, physics-driven 3D scene editing (e.g., flooding a scene, liquefying objects). The unified particle representation simplifies the architecture but introduces practical challenges: PBD's reduced accuracy compared to full FEM/SPH solvers, the added complexity of decoupling simulation from rendering for solids, and the fact that ellipsoid splatting for fluids does not fully model real light transport (refraction, caustics). Open questions include scalability to large scenes, handling of complex material interactions, and whether the decoupled simulation-rendering approach generalizes to fracture or topological changes.
