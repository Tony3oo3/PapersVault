## Problem

Existing dynamic scene reconstruction methods (both NeRF- and 3DGS-based) assume sharp inputs and precise camera poses, but real-world handheld captures of dynamic scenes inevitably contain motion blur from both camera shake and fast-moving objects. Prior deblurring approaches handle only static scenes, and the two NeRF-based dynamic deblurring methods couple camera and object motion within a single ray-warping process, causing static-region dominance and poor reconstruction in dynamic areas.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250166/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequence of motion-blurred RGB images (captured at 1/24 s exposure on GoPro) along with imprecise camera poses from COLMAP, plus auxiliary signals: dynamic masks from TrackAnything, monocular depth from DepthAnything, and 2D point tracks from BootsTAPIR. It outputs a time-varying 3D Gaussian scene representation (canonical Gaussians + MLP deformation field) that renders novel views at arbitrary timestamps, as well as deblurred renderings of the training views. The real-world dataset pairs each blurry training image with a sharp evaluation image (1/240 s exposure) from a synchronized second camera.

## Method

BARD-GS explicitly decouples motion blur into camera-induced and object-induced components and addresses them in two sequential stages. In stage 1 (camera motion deblurring), it models the camera trajectory within each exposure as a set of virtual poses interpolated on the SO(3) manifold, renders sharp images from each virtual pose using only static Gaussians, averages them to simulate blur, and optimizes against the input using a loss masked to static regions only. In stage 2 (object motion deblurring), an MLP deformation field predicts per-Gaussian offsets (position, rotation, scale) at virtual timestamps uniformly sampled across the exposure window; rendered images at these virtual times are averaged to simulate object motion blur, and the full-image rendering loss refines both dynamic and static regions. A 3D track loss—constructed by backprojecting BootsTAPIR 2D tracks with DepthAnything depth and optimized camera poses—constrains the deformation field to follow actual object trajectories during early training, preventing Gaussians from drifting away from the true motion.

## Results

On the real-world blurry dataset for novel view synthesis, BARD-GS achieves SI-PSNR 25.13, SI-SSIM 0.8508, SI-LPIPS 0.1567, LV 96.42, and MUSIQ 54.29, outperforming all baselines by a wide margin. On the synthesized deblurring benchmark (HyperNeRF + Dycheck), BARD-GS reaches PSNR 28.75, SSIM 0.8414, LPIPS 0.2343, LV 65.31, and MUSIQ 57.03, surpassing the best baseline (4DGS+ with MPRNet pre-deblurring) by 0.58 dB in PSNR and 17.57 in LV. The gap is especially pronounced in sharpness metrics (LV, MUSIQ), where BARD-GS leads by 45.74 LV and 12.41 MUSIQ over the strongest competitor on the real-world task.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BARD-GS outperforms all four baselines across every metric on both the real-world novel view synthesis task and the synthesized deblurring task. Against DyBluRF [40], the only other method designed for dynamic deblurring, BARD-GS gains +4.56 dB SI-PSNR and reduces SI-LPIPS from 0.3614 to 0.1567, confirming that explicitly decoupling camera and object motion blur is far more effective than coupling them in a single ray-warping process. Against the strong dynamic-GS baselines D3DGS [50] and 4DGS [51], BARD-GS improves SI-PSNR by 2.30 and 3.07 dB respectively, with the largest gains in sharpness (LV: 96.42 vs. 18.95 and 24.60), indicating that these methods fail to recover fine detail under motion blur. Pre-deblurring with MPRNet [53] before D3DGS or 4DGS (denoted D3DGS+ and 4DGS+) narrows the gap somewhat but still trails BARD-GS by 2.03 and 2.55 dB SI-PSNR, and the paper notes that MPRNet outright fails on the most heavily blurred images in the real-world dataset. The consistent superiority across both tasks suggests that physically modeling blur formation in 3D space is more robust than 2D pre-deblurring followed by standard dynamic reconstruction.

## Relation to Prior Work

BARD-GS sits at the intersection of two established lines of work: 3DGS-based dynamic scene reconstruction (D3DGS, 4DGS) and camera-motion deblurring via virtual pose trajectories (BAD-Gaussians, Deblur-GS). It adopts the virtual camera trajectory modeling from BAD-Gaussians and the MLP deformation field from D3DGS, but departs from both by explicitly decoupling camera-induced and object-induced motion blur into two sequential stages rather than coupling them in a single optimization. This contrasts with the two NeRF-based approaches (DyBluRF, [2]) that attempt to handle both blur types simultaneously through latent ray warping, which the authors argue causes static-region dominance and poor dynamic-region quality.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap—handheld monocular capture of dynamic scenes inevitably produces compound motion blur that neither static deblurring nor sharp-input dynamic reconstruction handles. The real-world GoPro dataset (12 scenes, paired 1/24s and 1/240s exposures) fills a notable absence in the literature, as prior synthetic blur datasets (frame-averaging via RIFE) produce unrealistic artifacts like moiré patterns. Open questions include scalability to scenes with multiple objects moving at different speeds (the constant-speed-within-exposure assumption), sensitivity to the quality of COLMAP initial poses, and whether the two-stage pipeline generalizes to longer exposures or rolling-shutter effects.
