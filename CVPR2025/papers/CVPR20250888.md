## Problem

Whole-Slide Images (WSIs) in digital pathology are gigapixel-scale (1–5 GB per slide) and require long-term storage, yet existing lossless compressors (PNG, Huffman, Gzip, even NN-based methods like ArIB-BPS) achieve only 1–2× compression on informative regions. The paper identifies the root cause as 'information irregularity' in WSIs—widely distributed high-frequency signals with high volatility—that defeats entropy-based and prediction-based compression, and provides the first systematic study of why standard lossless methods fail on this data.

## Input / Output

WISE takes a single base-level WSI image (multi-resolution pyramid, only the highest-resolution level is compressed) in 4-channel format (RGB + alpha), removes the alpha channel and zero-valued background regions, then processes the remaining informative tissue area in patches. The output is a losslessly compressed bitstream that perfectly reconstructs the original pixel values; no quality metrics (PSNR/SSIM) are needed since the compression is exact. Throughput is reported at ~15 MB/s on CPU with ~0.1 GB memory for a 256×256 patch, scaling to ~3 GB at 8192×8192×3.

## Method

WISE is a three-stage pipeline tailored to WSI information irregularity. First, Hierarchical Projection Coding computes first-order differences along row, column, and channel axes (a 3D delta encoding) to collapse the wide pixel-value range into small deltas, progressively reducing Shannon entropy. Second, Bitmap Encoding identifies 'effective bits' (bits after the first 1 in each encoded value) and transposes them so that bits from the same position across adjacent bytes are grouped into new byte representations, converting the data into a highly repetitive pattern. Third, LZW dictionary coding exploits these repetitive patterns to achieve the final compression. The key design insight is that dictionary-based matching is far more robust to WSI volatility than sequential prediction, and the two preprocessing stages deliberately restructure the data to maximize the repetitive patterns that LZW can capture.

## Results

On non-empty WSI patches, WISE achieves compression ratios of 2.82× (C16), 3.20× (C17), 1.63× (BNCB), 2.50× (TCGA), and 3.85× (IHC4BC), consistently outperforming all baselines. On full gigapixel WSI images, WISE compresses to 36× on average and up to 136× (C17, Img5), compared to at most ~100× for LZMA on the same image. WISE also achieves a throughput of 15.1 MB/s with only 0.1 GB memory, versus 18.73 KB/s and 3.81 GB for the neural-network baseline ArIB-BPS.

## Baselines & Comparisons

WISE outperforms all eight baselines across every dataset. Against entropy-based methods (Huffman, Arithmetic coding), WISE achieves roughly 2.5–3× higher compression ratios on non-empty patches (e.g., 2.82× vs. 1.08× on C16). Against image-format encoders (PNG, TIFF), WISE leads by 70–150% (2.82× vs. 1.64× PNG on C16; 2.50× vs. 1.04× PNG on TCGA). Against dictionary-based methods, WISE surpasses the strongest baseline LZMA by 114% on C16 (2.82× vs. 1.32×), 119% on C17 (3.20× vs. 1.46×), and 48% on BNCB (1.63× vs. 1.29×); it also beats Zstd-22 by 127% on C16 and 116% on C17. Against the neural-network compressor ArIB-BPS, WISE wins on compression ratio (2.82× vs. 2.63× on C16, 3.20× vs. 2.72× on C17, 2.50× vs. 2.38× on TCGA) while being ~800× faster in throughput (15.1 MB/s vs. 18.73 KB/s) and using 38× less memory (0.1 GB vs. 3.81 GB). The gap is largest on TCGA, where WSI information irregularity is most pronounced and entropy-based methods nearly fail (PNG at 1.04×), while WISE still achieves 2.50×.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by being the first to systematically diagnose why standard lossless compressors (PNG, Huffman, arithmetic coding, Gzip, Zstd) fail on WSI images, attributing the failure to 'information irregularity'—a high-frequency, high-volatility signal structure absent in natural images. Rather than building on a specific prior architecture, WISE repurposes three classical techniques—hierarchical delta encoding, bit-plane transposition, and LZW dictionary compression—into a pipeline specifically ordered to exploit the WSI frequency structure. It departs from the dominant lossy-compression literature for WSIs (JPEG-2000, VQ-VAE embeddings) by insisting on bit-exact reconstruction, and from NN-based lossless compressors (ArIB-BPS, Bit-Swap, IDF) by showing that a non-learned, dictionary-centric approach outperforms them on this particular data distribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical stakes are high: hospitals must retain multi-gigabyte WSI records for decades, and cloud-based pathology workflows increasingly require inter-datacenter transfers, making even a 36× reduction in storage and bandwidth costs significant. However, the headline compression ratios are substantially inflated by trivial empty-area removal; on non-empty tissue patches the gain over LZMA is more modest (~2–3×), and the single-threaded Python implementation with LZW memory growth limits immediate deployment at scale. Open questions include whether the hierarchical projection + bitmap reorganization generalizes to other high-frequency medical imaging modalities (e.g., OCT, confocal microscopy), whether combining the pipeline with arithmetic or FSE coding (as the authors suggest) can close the remaining gap, and whether the approach remains effective for IHC or multiplexed staining with different spectral characteristics.
