## Problem

Existing generative model evaluation metrics (FID, Inception Score) produce scalar rankings that fail to reveal which specific sample types are generated differently by competing models, and prior spectral novelty methods (KEN) require O(n³) eigendecomposition that becomes infeasible at ImageNet scale. This paper addresses the gap by formulating a differential clustering problem that identifies sample clusters generated with significantly different frequencies by a test model versus a reference distribution, and develops a scalable algorithm to solve it.

## Input / Output

The method consumes n generated samples from a test model and m samples from a reference model (or dataset), mapped through DINOv2 embeddings into a fixed-dimensional feature space, along with a Gaussian kernel bandwidth σ², a novelty threshold ρ ≥ 1, and a Fourier feature size r (set to 2000 in experiments). It outputs the top positive eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the conditional covariance matrix, which define novel mode clusters, plus a closed-form similarity scoring function s_u(x) = Σ u_{2i-1}cos(ωᵢᵀx) + u_{2i}sin(ωᵢᵀx) that can be applied to any fresh unseen sample to assign its likelihood of belonging to a detected mode without re-running the algorithm.

## Method

FINC replaces the infinite-dimensional Gaussian kernel with r random Fourier features drawn from N(0, σ⁻²I_d), yielding a finite 2r-dimensional feature map φ̃_r(x) = (1/√r)[cos(ω₁ᵀx), sin(ω₁ᵀx), …, cos(ω_rᵀx), sin(ω_rᵀx)]. It then stochastically accumulates the 2r×2r empirical covariance matrices C̃_X and C̃_Y in a single pass over the samples, computes the conditional matrix Λ̃ = C̃_X − ρC̃_Y, and performs a standard eigendecomposition on this small matrix to extract positive eigenvalues whose eigenvectors identify modes overrepresented in the test model. The key scalability insight is that the eigendecomposition cost depends only on r (independent of n), and a theoretical guarantee (Theorem 1) proves that r = O(log(m+n)/ε⁴) features suffice for ε-accurate eigenvalue approximation, with a novel perturbation analysis handling the non-Hermitian structure of the conditional kernel matrix. This reduces the overall complexity from O((m+n)³) for the kernel-trick baseline to O((n+m)·r² + r³), enabling ImageNet-scale analysis in minutes on a single GPU.

## Results

On ImageNet-1K with n=m samples, FINC (r=2000) scales nearly linearly, completing the differential clustering in 74.3 s on GPU at 250k samples, whereas all three baselines (Rarity, FLD, KEN) hit memory overflow beyond 10k–25k samples on GPU. On CPU, FINC processes 250k samples in ~3090 s, while KEN (eigen decomposition) exceeds 24 h at just 50k samples and Rarity/FLD fail at 100k. Qualitatively, FINC correctly identifies ground-truth novel modes in controlled MNIST/Fashion-MNIST experiments and detects overrepresented and underrepresented modes across BigGAN, GigaGAN, StyleGAN-XL, LDM, DiT-XL, and RQ-Transformer on ImageNet and FFHQ.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FINC is compared against three baselines: the Rarity score [13], the Feature Likelihood Divergence (FLD) score [21], and the Kernel-based Entropic Novelty (KEN) spectral method [48]. All three baselines require either a post-clustering step (Rarity, FLD) or eigendecomposition of an (m+n)×(m+n) kernel matrix (KEN), leading to superlinear or cubic scaling. On GPU, Rarity and FLD fail at 25k samples, KEN (eigen) fails at 25k, and KEN (Cholesky) fails at 25k, while FINC completes 250k samples in 74.3 s. On CPU, KEN (eigen) exceeds 24 h at 50k samples, whereas FINC finishes 250k in ~52 minutes. Qualitatively, the authors report that FINC detects novel modes more accurately than the baselines on ImageNet, though detailed side-by-side qualitative comparisons are deferred to the appendix. FINC's advantage is thus primarily in scalability: it is the only method that can process the 50k+ sample sizes needed for meaningful multi-modal analysis on ImageNet-1K.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the authors' own prior KEN (Kernel-based Entropic Novelty) framework [48], which formulated novelty detection between two generative models as eigendecomposition of a (m+n)×(m+n) kernel matrix, and on the Random Fourier Features (RFF) approximation theory of Rahimi & Recht [35] for shift-invariant kernels. The key departure is replacing the kernel trick with an explicit RFF-based feature map, reducing the spectral problem to a 2r×2r matrix whose size is independent of sample size, and providing a novel eigenvalue perturbation analysis for the resulting non-Hermitian structure. Sample-based novelty scores (Rarity [13], FLD [21]) are positioned as complementary but insufficient, since they require an expensive post-clustering step that does not scale to many modes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The logarithmic dependence of required Fourier features on sample size (r = O(log(n+m)/ε⁴)) makes fine-grained differential clustering of generative models feasible at ImageNet scale, where prior spectral methods hit memory walls at 10k–50k samples. This opens the door to interpretable, mode-level comparisons of state-of-the-art generative models (GANs, diffusion, VAEs) that go beyond scalar metrics like FID or Inception Score. Open questions include extending the pairwise comparison to simultaneous multi-model comparison, tightening the ε⁴ dependence in the feature-size bound, and validating whether the RFF approximation preserves the semantic structure of modes in high-dimensional embedding spaces beyond DINOv2.
