## Problem

Existing AI-generated image detectors learn to capture low-level artifacts specific to particular generative models, causing them to fail catastrophically on unseen generators. The paper argues that the spectral distribution of real images is an invariant pattern—shaped by long-term sensor and compression technology rather than by any specific generator—that can be modeled to detect arbitrary AI-generated images as out-of-distribution samples.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250195/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method accepts RGB images at any native resolution without resizing or cropping. Internally, the image is split into 224×224 patches (K patches total), each processed independently through a frozen ViT-B/16 backbone. The output is a single binary classification score in (0,1) indicating the probability that the image is AI-generated, produced by a three-layer MLP on the fused image-level spectral vector. During training, K is fixed at 4 random augmented views; at inference, K equals the actual number of patches in the image.

## Method

SPAI first learns a spectral model of real images via masked frequency reconstruction: a ViT-B/16 (pretrained by Xie et al. and kept frozen) is trained to reconstruct masked low- or high-frequency components of real images using a frequency-domain loss. At inference, the original image, its low-pass component, and its high-pass component are each encoded through the frozen ViT, and Spectral Reconstruction Similarity (SRS) computes per-token cosine similarities across all 12 transformer blocks, yielding a 6N-dimensional vector that captures how well the image's spectral structure aligns with the learned real-image distribution. A Spectral Context Vector (SCV) summarizes the original image's representations to provide context for interpreting SRS values. Finally, Spectral Context Attention (SCA) fuses the per-patch spectral vectors (SCV + SRS) into a single image-level vector via a learned attention mechanism with O(K) complexity, enabling processing of arbitrarily large images without the quadratic cost of full-resolution ViTs. The SRS, SCV, SCA, and classification head are trained end-to-end with binary cross-entropy on 180k real and 180k synthetic 256×256 images.

## Results

SPAI achieves a 91.0% average AUC across 13 generative models and 5 real-image sources, representing a 5.5% absolute improvement over the second-best method (RINE, 85.5%). The method maintains strong performance across all resolution tiers: 93.1% average on <0.5 MP generators (Glide, SD1.3, SD1.4), 88.0% on 0.5–1.0 MP generators (Flux, DALLE2, SD2, SDXL, SD3, GigaGAN), and 87.7% on >1.0 MP generators (MJv5, MJv6.1, DALLE3, Firefly). Ablations confirm that the masked spectral pretraining contributes 38.5 AUC points (52.5→91.0), SRS adds 20 points, SCV adds 6.1 points, and SCA adds 7.8 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SPAI outperforms all 12 baselines on average AUC (91.0 vs. 85.5 for the next-best RINE), with the gap widening dramatically for older or artifact-specific detectors: NPR (45.4), DIRE (50.8), and CNNDetect (55.5) all score near or below chance on several generators. Among the strongest competitors, RINE (85.5) and DMID (83.5) are the closest, but both suffer catastrophic drops on specific generators (e.g., RINE scores only 39.1 on SD3 and 41.8 on DALLE3, while DMID scores 41.3 on DALLE3). SPAI's key advantage is consistency: it never drops below 75.9 AUC on any single generator, whereas most baselines fall below 50 on at least one. SPAI does lose on a few individual generators—NPR achieves 97.1 on DALLE3 (vs. SPAI's 90.2), PatchCraft reaches 96.1 on MJv6.1 (vs. 84.0), and DeFake scores 87.7 on SD3 (vs. 75.9)—but these methods require explicit training on those generators' data or collapse on others. The paper also shows SPAI maintains superior robustness under JPEG/WebP compression, Gaussian blur, Gaussian noise, and resizing, where competing methods degrade more sharply.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes masked frequency modeling (Xie et al., [72])—originally a self-supervised pre-training objective—as a frozen spectral backbone for AI-generated image detection, rather than as a general-purpose feature extractor. It departs from the dominant AID paradigm of learning generator-specific artifacts (CNNDetect, FreqDetect, GramNet, etc.) and instead follows the emerging 'model real images' direction (Cozzolino et al. [9], He et al. [30]), but specifically in the frequency domain, arguing that the spectral distribution of authentic images is an invariant pattern unaffected by which generator produced the synthetic counterpart. The Spectral Context Attention module further extends ViT-based forensic detection to arbitrary resolutions, addressing a practical limitation that prior spatial-domain approaches handle via cropping or resizing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The shift from modeling generator artifacts to modeling the spectral distribution of real images offers a principled path toward detectors that generalize to unseen generators without retraining, which is critical given the rapidly expanding ecosystem of commercial and open-source image generators. The acknowledged failure on derivative images (screenshots, memes, photographs of screens) where intermediate media destroy spectral artifacts highlights a fundamental limitation of signal-based detection and points toward the need for combining spectral cues with semantic context understanding. An open question is whether the spectral invariance assumption holds as generative models continue to improve in fidelity, potentially converging toward the spectral statistics of real images.
