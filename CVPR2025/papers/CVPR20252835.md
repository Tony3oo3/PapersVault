## Problem

Existing NeRF-based LiDAR re-simulation methods achieve high fidelity but require 15+ hours of training and render at 0.17–0.98 FPS, making them impractical for real-time applications. Moreover, they struggle to accurately model complex dynamic scenes with long-distance vehicle motion and occlusions. The paper targets the gap between rendering quality and real-time performance in dynamic LiDAR re-simulation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252835/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes multi-frame LiDAR point clouds from a 64-beam sensor at 10 Hz, calibrated sensor poses with timestamps, and tracked 3D bounding boxes for moving vehicles. It produces novel LiDAR views as cylindrical range images (64×2650 on Waymo, 66×1030 on KITTI-360) parameterized by azimuth and elevation angles, which are then fused and re-projected into 3D point clouds. A key distinction from camera-based methods is that the output is an active-sensor range image rather than a passive perspective image, and the framework supports arbitrary sensor reconfiguration (beam count, FOV, pose) at inference time.

## Method

LiDAR-RT decomposes the dynamic scene into a static background and per-object models, each represented by 2D Gaussian primitives (oriented planar disks) augmented with learnable reflection intensity (ζ) and ray-drop probability (β) parameters encoded via spherical harmonics. For rendering, it constructs a BVH over proxy geometries (co-planar triangle pairs per Gaussian) and uses NVIDIA OptiX hardware ray tracing to cast one ray per LiDAR pixel, evaluating Gaussian responses at intersections and accumulating radiance via volumetric rendering in a chunked fashion. Differentiability is achieved by re-casting rays in the backward pass and computing gradients in a front-to-back blending order (borrowed from StopThePop), avoiding the per-tile sorted buffers incompatible with panoramic LiDAR geometry. A UNet refines sensor-level ray-drop effects post-optimization, and training combines L1 depth/intensity losses, BCE ray-drop loss, and Chamfer Distance supervision over 30k iterations on a single RTX 4090, yielding 20–42 FPS rendering versus 0.17–0.98 FPS for prior NeRF-based baselines.

## Results

On the Waymo Open Dataset (64×2650 range images), LiDAR-RT achieves a depth RMSE of 6.46, PSNR of 22.16 dB, and Chamfer Distance of 0.100, while rendering at 20.1 FPS with only 1.37 GB storage—two orders of magnitude faster than the best NeRF-based baseline (LiDAR4D at 0.17 FPS, 7.7 GB). On KITTI-360 (66×1030), it reaches a depth RMSE of 3.47, PSNR of 27.68 dB, and CD of 0.108 at 42.7 FPS with 0.41 GB storage, outperforming LiDAR4D (0.4 FPS, 7.38 GB) on most depth and intensity metrics. The method also achieves the best F-score (0.9458) on Waymo and the best intensity SSIM (0.6077) on KITTI-360 among all compared methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LiDAR-RT outperforms all four NeRF-based baselines (LiDAR-NeRF, DyNFL, LiDAR4D) on depth RMSE, depth PSNR, and Chamfer Distance on Waymo, with the closest competitor LiDAR4D trailing by 0.17 in depth RMSE (6.62 vs 6.46) and 0.006 in CD (0.106 vs 0.100). On KITTI-360, LiDAR-RT beats LiDAR4D on depth RMSE (3.47 vs 3.53), depth PSNR (27.68 vs 27.48), and all five intensity metrics, though it slightly lags on depth MedAE (0.051 vs 0.040) and F-score (0.926 vs 0.927). The efficiency gap is dramatic: LiDAR-RT renders at 20.1 FPS on Waymo and 42.7 FPS on KITTI-360, versus 0.17–0.98 FPS for the NeRF-based methods, while using 5–10× less GPU storage. Against the explicit mesh-based baselines (PCGen, LiDARsim) on KITTI-360, LiDAR-RT dominates across every metric, e.g., reducing depth RMSE from 5.69 (PCGen) to 3.47 and CD from 0.46 to 0.11. The only metric where LiDAR-RT does not lead is intensity MedAE on Waymo (0.0101 vs 0.0086 for LiDAR4D), a marginal difference likely attributable to the UNet-based ray-drop refinement.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) NeRF-based LiDAR re-simulation (LiDAR-NeRF, NFL, LiDAR4D, DyNFL), which achieves physical fidelity but at prohibitive training and rendering costs; (2) 3D/2D Gaussian Splatting for efficient scene representation and real-time camera rendering; and (3) hardware-accelerated Gaussian ray tracing (3DGRT) for particle-based scenes. The key departure from prior work is replacing the standard tile-based rasterization of 3DGS with a BVH-based ray tracer built on NVIDIA OptiX, specifically to model the active emission-and-reception physics of LiDAR sensors that rasterization cannot capture. For dynamics, it adopts the scene-graph decomposition (static background + tracked rigid objects) common in recent driving-scene work, but augments each Gaussian with learnable SH-encoded reflection intensity and ray-drop probability parameters tailored to LiDAR physics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The two-orders-of-magnitude speedup (20–43 FPS vs. 0.17–0.21 FPS for SOTA NeRF-based methods) while matching or exceeding rendering quality makes real-time LiDAR re-simulation practically viable for closed-loop autonomous driving simulation and sensor reconfiguration workflows. The instance-aware scene representation enables flexible editing (trajectory manipulation, object insertion/removal) and arbitrary sensor re-simulation (different beam counts, FOV, poses), which are directly useful for generating diverse training data. Key open questions remain: the method cannot handle non-rigid deformations (pedestrians, cyclists), performance degrades with long sequences due to growing Gaussian counts, and more physically accurate modeling (material estimation, multi-bounce reflections) is left for future work.
