## Problem

Training-time safety alignment via RLHF makes MLLMs vulnerable to jailbreak attacks because it optimizes against a fixed prompt distribution, while adversaries can craft adversarial prompts that bypass these safeguards. Existing inference-time defenses either degrade model utility by producing overly cautious refusals on benign queries or lack a principled mathematical characterization, leaving a gap between safety and utility.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252541/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference time, IMMUNE takes an adversarial image-text pair (the jailbreak prompt, which may include perturbed images, typography-embedded text, or text-only jailbreak templates) and a pre-trained safety reward model (Llama-3.1-8B fine-tuned on HelpSteer2, OffsetBias, WildGuard, and Magpie preference data). It produces a safe text response by reweighting the base MLLM's token distribution at each decoding step, requiring no retraining of the MLLM itself. The method samples only the top-k=10 candidate tokens per step, keeping the computational overhead manageable relative to full-vocabulary scoring.

## Method

IMMUNE reframes jailbreak defense as an inference-time alignment problem by reformulating the min_p max_π training-time objective into max_π min_p, where the safeguard is applied after the adversary has fixed their prompt. At each decoding step, it samples top-k tokens from the base MLLM, evaluates a safety action-value function Q_safe (the expected safety reward over the remaining trajectory under the reference policy) for each candidate, and reweights the token distribution via a closed-form KL-regularized RLHF solution: π*(z|s_t) ∝ π_safe(z|s_t) exp(Q_safe(s_t,z)/α). The paper provides a theoretical guarantee (Theorem 1) bounding the suboptimality gap under adversarial prompt distributions, decomposing it into a term capturing prompt distribution divergence (controlled by the adversary's regularization β) and a term capturing policy misalignment (controlled by the alignment parameter α). This yields prompt-specific safety adjustments that are intractable at training time but efficient at inference, preserving model utility on benign queries while rejecting adversarial ones.

## Results

On text-based jailbreak attacks (JailbreakV-28K), IMMUNE reduces the average Attack Success Rate (ASR) from 60.27% (original LLaVA-1.6) to 2.45%, a 57.82% absolute reduction, and from 19.23% (AdaShield) to 2.45%, a 16.78% improvement over the strongest baseline. On MM-SafetyBench (image-based), IMMUNE achieves 3.94% average ASR on LLaVA-1.6 versus 47.94% for the original model and 24.40% for AdaShield. On visual adversarial attacks at the unconstrained setting, IMMUNE reaches 16.02% ASR on LLaVA-1.6 compared to 64.80% (original) and 40.21% (AdaShield). Across all five MLLMs and four benchmarks, IMMUNE consistently achieves the lowest ASR, with the largest margins on LLaVA-1.5 and LLaVA-1.6.

## Baselines & Comparisons

IMMUNE outperforms all three baselines (FigStep, AdaShield, CoCA) on average ASR across every MLLM and every benchmark evaluated. The largest margins are on LLaVA-1.6 and LLaVA-1.5, where IMMUNE achieves near-zero ASR (2.45% and 2.10%) on text-based attacks versus 19.23% for AdaShield (the strongest baseline) and ~51% for FigStep and CoCA. On MM-SafetyBench, IMMUNE reduces LLaVA-1.6 ASR to 3.94% versus 24.40% (AdaShield) and 39.79% (CoCA), a 20.46-point improvement over AdaShield. On visual adversarial attacks (unconstrained), IMMUNE reaches 16.02% on LLaVA-1.6 versus 40.21% (AdaShield) and 50.33% (CoCA). The advantage narrows on MiniGPT-4-13B, where IMMUNE (32.94%) only marginally beats CoCA (33.21%) on text-based attacks, and on FigStep where AdaShield sometimes matches or slightly beats IMMUNE on specific categories (e.g., LLaVA-1.5 Illegal Activity: 14.38% vs 28.21%). Notably, CoCA can actually worsen safety on some models (e.g., MiniGPT-4-13B visual adversarial: 75.09% vs 70.11% original), while IMMUNE never degrades below the original model's ASR.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the controlled decoding literature (Chakraborty et al. [7], Mudgal et al. [47]) and the KL-regularized RLHF framework (Ouyang et al. [50]), repurposing them from general LLM alignment to the specific problem of MLLM jailbreak defense at inference time. It departs from prior inference-time defenses like AdaShield (prompt-based) and CoCA (distribution calibration) by providing a principled, mathematically characterized decoding policy rather than heuristic modifications. The key conceptual departure is from training-time safety alignment: the paper argues that the min-max structure of adversarial robustness makes training-time approaches fundamentally insufficient, and reframes defense as a max_π min_p problem solvable at inference via the closed-form KL-regularized solution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical guarantee (Theorem 1) bounding the suboptimality gap of an inference-time safety defense under adversarial prompt distributions, filling a gap where prior inference-time methods were purely heuristic. Practically, the plug-and-play nature (requiring only a safety reward model and no retraining) makes it broadly applicable across MLLMs. However, the defense's effectiveness is conditional on the quality of the safety reward model, which the authors acknowledge is itself vulnerable to adversarial attack. Open questions include how the approach scales to multi-turn interactions, whether the reward model can be made adversarially robust, and whether the theoretical bounds are tight enough to provide meaningful guarantees in practice.
