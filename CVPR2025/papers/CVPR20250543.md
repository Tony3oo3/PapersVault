## Problem

Existing scene representations each capture only one of three complementary aspects needed for precise scene generation and editing: programs encode structural relations (hierarchy, repetition, spatial layout) but lack appearance; scene graphs encode semantics but are bottlenecked by coarse pairwise relations and ignore visual identity; generative model latents capture identity but offer no interpretable structure. No prior representation integrates all three, limiting controllability and fidelity in complex multi-object scenes.

## Input / Output

The system accepts free-form text scene descriptions or a single RGB image (plus optional natural-language editing instructions) and produces a Scene Language — a Lisp-style program of entity functions, associated semantic words, and a list of neural embeddings in OpenCLIP-ViT/H text-embedding space. For image inputs, GroundingSAM segments entity regions and Textual Inversion optimizes per-entity embeddings to reconstruct cropped images. The resulting program can be rendered by pluggable graphics engines (SDS-based 3D Gaussian Splatting, Mitsuba primitives, asset-based game engines, or layout-conditioned T2I diffusion), yielding 3D or 4D scene renderings from arbitrary viewpoints.

## Method

The Scene Language Φ(s) = (W, P, Z) represents a scene as a set of semantic words W, a program P of recursively defined entity functions, and an ordered list of neural embeddings Z. Each entity function f_w maps an identity embedding z and descendant embeddings γ to an entity h by applying input-dependent poses (Ψ_transform) and composing sub-entities (Ψ_union), with the program encoding hierarchy, repetition, and spatial layout through function calls, loops, and transforms. Inference is entirely training-free: a pre-trained LM (Claude 3.5 Sonnet) is prompted with a DSL specification and example to generate a Python script implementing the program, while embeddings are obtained via CLIP text encoding (text prompts) or Textual Inversion on GroundingSAM-segmented crops (image prompts). Rendering instantiates the program output into a 3D Gaussian Splatting representation optimized with SDS guidance, using MVDream and a depth-conditioned ControlNet for multi-view consistency, building on the GALA3D pipeline.

## Results

On text-prompted 3D generation (Table 3), the Scene Language achieves 85.65% user-preference alignment on numeric prompts, dramatically outperforming GraphDreamer (3.56%) and MVDream (10.79%), and reaches perfect counting accuracy (1.0 vs. 0.11 for both baselines). CLIP similarity is also highest at 0.351 for numeric prompts. On generic prompts, the method leads in alignment (52.13% vs. 35.13% for MVDream and 15.75% for GraphDreamer) while matching MVDream on CLIP (0.328). For image-prompted generation, the method achieves lower LPIPS (0.681 vs. 0.811 for GraphDreamer), and for 4D generation it produces 5.9% dynamic degree vs. 0.2% for 4D-fy at a slight CLIP cost (0.341 vs. 0.352).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GraphDreamer (scene-graph-based synthesis), the Scene Language wins decisively on all metrics: user-preference alignment jumps from 3.56% to 85.65% on numeric prompts, CLIP similarity improves from 0.297 to 0.351, and counting accuracy goes from 0.11 to 1.0, reflecting the program's ability to encode exact spatial arrangements (e.g., 15 cans in a pyramid) that pairwise scene-graph edges cannot. Against MVDream (direct multi-view diffusion without an intermediate representation), the method wins on alignment (85.65% vs. 10.79%) and counting (1.0 vs. 0.11) for numeric prompts, and on alignment for generic prompts (52.13% vs. 35.13%), while CLIP scores are comparable (0.351 vs. 0.312 numeric; 0.328 vs. 0.328 generic). For 4D generation, the method trades a small CLIP drop (0.341 vs. 0.352 for 4D-fy) for substantially larger motion (5.9% vs. 0.2% dynamic degree). For image-prompted generation, the method achieves lower LPIPS (0.681 vs. 0.811 for GraphDreamer), benefiting from embeddings that preserve visual identity details absent in scene-graph descriptions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three previously separate lines of scene representation: program-based shape synthesis (ShapeAssembly, unsupervised shape programs), semantic scene graphs (GraphDreamer, StructureNet), and generative-model latent spaces (Textual Inversion, LoRA). It departs from prior program-based work by replacing fixed geometric primitives with neural CLIP embeddings for appearance, and from scene-graph approaches by using recursive function composition to encode hierarchy and repetition rather than pairwise object relations. The training-free inference via a pre-trained LM (Claude 3.5 Sonnet) generating a domain-specific language script is a practical departure from methods that require 3D training data or dedicated neural executors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Scene Language offers an interpretable, editable scene representation where structural changes (e.g., repositioning objects, changing counts) are local program edits rather than full re-optimization, which is a meaningful practical advantage over black-box SDS pipelines. However, the evaluation is limited to a small prompt set (9 numeric, 8 generic) with a single LM, and the SDS-based rendering remains slow and quality-limited compared to feed-forward approaches. Open questions include scalability to scenes with hundreds of entities, whether the program abstraction generalizes beyond the tested DSL, and whether the representation can be learned end-to-end rather than inferred by prompting.
