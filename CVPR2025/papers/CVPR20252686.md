## Problem

Self-supervised learning encoders are vulnerable to backdoor attacks that map triggered inputs to target embeddings, yet existing detection methods (DECREE, ASSET) fail against stealthy attacks with invisible triggers or distribution-preserving embeddings, and require clean datasets or knowledge of trigger types. The paper asks: can we detect backdoor activation at inference time without any prior information about the trigger, the training data, or the attack type?

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained (potentially backdoored) SSL encoder, an auxiliary unlabeled dataset (which may be out-of-distribution like STL-10 or slightly poisoned at <1% rate), and a single test image to evaluate. It produces a binary backdoor flag by comparing the reconstruction error against a threshold set to 1.5× the average training loss. Notably, the test-time masking ratio is deliberately higher than the training ratio (e.g., 0.99 vs. 0.9) to force the reconstruction to rely on the global embedding rather than local pixel information, making the test more sensitive to embedding-level corruption.

## Method

DeDe trains an MAE-style decoder (ViT-B/16 based, 4 blocks) that inverts the victim encoder's mapping by jointly decoding the global embedding f_θ*(x) and local patch embeddings h_e(M_α ⊙ x) into a reconstructed image. The key insight is that normal images map to embeddings within their class cluster, so the decoder can faithfully reconstruct them, whereas backdoored images are mapped to a different embedding region, causing the decoder to produce a reconstruction inconsistent with the input (e.g., reconstructing an airplane-like image from a triggered cat). Training minimizes L2 reconstruction loss on the auxiliary dataset with a masking ratio α that hides most of the image, while testing uses a higher α to amplify sensitivity to embedding corruption. The approach is non-invasive (no modification to the victim encoder), requires no clean data or trigger knowledge, and exploits the fact that the image space is sparser than the embedding space, making it difficult for a backdoor distribution to survive the inverse mapping.

## Results

On the primary detection task (Table 1), DeDe achieves AUC scores of 0.981 (BadEncoder), 0.912 (CTRL), 0.997 (DRUPE), 1.0 (CLIP Backdoor), and 0.925 (BadCLIP), consistently outperforming both baselines across all five attack types. In the downstream evaluation (Table 2), after filtering detected backdoor samples, DeDe reduces the attack success rate (ASR) to 1.3% (BadEncoder), 4.8% (CTRL), 2.1% (DRUPE), 0.0% (CLIP Backdoor), and 30.5% (BadCLIP), while preserving clean accuracy (CA) within 0.5% of the no-defense baseline. The OoD variant (DeDe OoD), trained on STL-10 instead of in-distribution data, maintains competitive AUC scores (0.993, 0.911, 0.976, 1.0, 0.798), confirming robustness to distribution shift.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DECREE [Feng et al., CVPR 2023], DeDe achieves a decisive advantage: DECREE is inapplicable to CTRL (spectral trigger) and BadCLIP (random trigger) since it only handles patch-like triggers, and where it does apply, its AUC scores (0.362, 0.344, 0.348) are near-random, while DeDe reaches 0.981, 0.997, and 1.0 respectively. Against ASSET [Pan et al., USENIX Security 2023], DeDe wins on four of five attacks: CTRL (0.912 vs. 0.799 AUC), DRUPE (0.997 vs. 0.858), CLIP Backdoor (1.0 vs. 0.555), and BadCLIP (0.925 vs. 0.773), with a marginal edge on BadEncoder (0.981 vs. 0.978). In the downstream setting (Table 2), the gap widens substantially: after filtering, DeDe reduces ASR to 1.3–30.5% across attacks, whereas ASSET only achieves 49.3–98.3% ASR, and ASSET completely fails on CLIP Backdoor (ASR remains 98.3%). The paper attributes ASSET's weakness to its reliance on a manually selected clean subset and its sensitivity to stealthy embedding-level attacks like DRUPE.

## Relation to Prior Work

DeDe departs from prior SSL backdoor detection methods (DECREE, ASSET) that analyze embedding-space distributions or use optimization-based loss offsets, instead proposing to learn an explicit inverse mapping from embeddings back to images via a masked-patch decoder. The decoder architecture and masked-reconstruction strategy are directly borrowed from MAE [19], repurposed here not for pre-training but as a detection probe: if the encoder has been backdoored, the reconstructed image will diverge from the input. Conceptually, the paper reframes backdoor detection as a reconstruction-consistency problem rather than a distributional-anomaly problem, which sidesteps the failure mode of prior methods when attacks produce stealthy, in-distribution embeddings (e.g., DRUPE).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As foundation-model encoders are increasingly pre-trained on internet-scraped data and distributed as third-party checkpoints, a non-invasive, trigger-agnostic detection mechanism is practically important for downstream users who cannot audit training data. DeDe's key limitation is its performance degradation on end-to-end attacks (CTRL, BadCLIP) where embeddings land in out-of-distribution regions, requiring ad-hoc workarounds like noise-filling and lower masking ratios. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger ViT backbones or non-vision modalities, and whether the auxiliary dataset requirement (even if OoD) remains a practical bottleneck in deployment. The method also assumes the encoder is frozen during detection, leaving open how it interacts with fine-tuning or adapter-based adaptation.
