## Problem

Existing Audio-Visual Event Localization (AVEL) methods operate in closed-set settings (only recognizing training-time classes) or open-set settings (labeling unseen events merely as 'unknown' without specific categories). This paper introduces the Open-Vocabulary AVEL (OV-AVEL) task, which requires predicting explicit event categories for both seen and unseen test data at inference, a more practical and fine-grained requirement that no prior work has addressed.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252620/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The input is a 10-second audio-visual video split into 10 non-overlapping 1-second segments, where each segment's visual component is its intermediate frame and its audio component is the corresponding 1-second clip. Text prompts for all 67 candidate event classes (46 seen + 21 unseen) plus a special 'other' background token are fed to the text encoder. The output is a per-segment label (10 predictions per video) assigning each segment either a specific event category or the background class, effectively producing temporal event boundaries at 1-second granularity.

## Method

Both baselines use the frozen ImageBind_Huge model to extract segment-level audio, visual, and text embeddings in a unified multimodal space. The training-free baseline computes cosine similarity matrices between audio-text and visual-text features, then predicts an audio-visual event only when both modalities independently select the same category (consistency check). The fine-tuning baseline inserts lightweight learnable temporal Transformer layers after the frozen audio and visual encoders to capture cross-segment temporal relations, and is trained with cross-entropy loss where the audio-visual event probability is computed as the geometric mean (square root of the Hadamard product) of the audio-text and visual-text similarity matrices. During inference, texts for both seen and unseen classes are introduced, enabling zero-shot recognition of novel categories while leveraging temporal patterns learned from seen-class training data.

## Results

On the proposed OV-AVEBench dataset, the fine-tuning baseline achieves 57.8% average score (67.1% Acc, 56.9% Seg-F1, 49.5% Eve-F1) on the total test set, substantially outperforming all prior fine-tuning AVEL methods adapted to the open-vocabulary setting (best prior: AVE at 42.9% Avg). The training-free baseline reaches 46.6% Avg on the total set, surpassing Video-LLaMA2 (39.3%) and CLIP&CLAP (41.7%). On unseen test data specifically, the fine-tuning baseline scores 55.8% Avg, nearly doubling the best prior fine-tuning method (AVE at 34.0%), demonstrating strong generalization to event categories absent during training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among training-free approaches, the proposed ImageBind-based baseline (46.6% Avg total) outperforms Video-LLaMA2 (39.3%) by 7.3 points and CLIP&CLAP (41.7%) by 4.9 points, attributed to the joint multimodal embedding space better capturing audio-visual-textual alignment. Among fine-tuning approaches, the proposed method (57.8% Avg total) dominates all prior closed-set AVEL methods adapted to OV-AVEL: AVE (42.9%), MM-Pyramid (41.2%), PSP (39.9%), and CMRA (39.2%), with margins of 14.9–18.6 points. The gap is most dramatic on unseen test data, where the fine-tuning baseline achieves 55.8% Avg versus 34.0% for AVE (the best prior), a 21.8-point advantage. Notably, prior methods with complex audio-visual fusion modules (e.g., PSP, MM-Pyramid) achieve higher seen-class scores (67.7–68.6% Avg) but suffer severe degradation on unseen data (28.7–29.9% Avg), whereas the proposed lightweight temporal layers maintain balanced performance across both seen and unseen splits. The fine-tuning baseline also improves over its own training-free counterpart by 11.2 points in total Avg, with the largest gain on unseen data (8.8 points in Avg) and a 13.5-point jump in event-level F1 (34.0→47.5), confirming that temporal modeling from training data transfers to unseen categories.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Audio-Visual Event Localization (AVEL) task from its established closed-set formulation (Tian et al. 2018 and successors) through the recently proposed open-set variant (Yu et al. 2024, which only flags unseen events as 'unknown') to a new open-vocabulary setting that requires predicting explicit category names for both seen and unseen test segments. Methodologically, it builds on ImageBind's unified multimodal embedding space and adopts a CLIP-style zero-shot classification paradigm, departing from prior AVEL methods that rely on task-specific audio-visual fusion modules trained end-to-end. The paper also distinguishes itself from Audio-Visual Zero-Shot Learning (AV-ZSL), which classifies entire videos, by requiring fine-grained temporal segment-level category prediction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As the first standardized benchmark (OV-AVEBench, 24,800 videos, 67 classes) for open-vocabulary AVEL, this paper provides the field with a necessary testbed and evaluation protocol (accuracy, segment-level F1, event-level F1) that prior closed-set and open-set work lacked. A key practical finding is that strong closed-set AVEL methods (PSP, MM-Pyramid) collapse on unseen classes (e.g., PSP drops from 67.7% to 28.7% Avg.), highlighting that existing architectures are not transferable to open-vocabulary settings. Open questions include how to design temporal architectures that generalize to unseen categories, whether the observed data-efficiency (25% training data ≈ 100%) holds at scale, and how to close the remaining seen-unseen gap beyond the modest 8.8% improvement from fine-tuning.
