## Problem

Existing AI-generated image detectors rely heavily on high-level semantic features, frequency artifacts, or pre-trained model representations, all of which degrade significantly on the latest diffusion models and cross-domain evaluation settings (e.g., training on photos, testing on artworks). The paper identifies that even state-of-the-art methods approach random-guessing accuracy under such conditions, and that the color distribution characteristics of synthetic images—an underexplored signal—offer a more robust and content-independent detection cue.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251437/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image (center-cropped to 256×256 at inference) and outputs a binary real/fake probability. Unusually, the input is consumed in two parallel streams: the original image and a derived color difference map computed by averaging 500 noisy 4-bit-quantized versions of the image and subtracting from the original. This color difference map is magnified 20× for visualization but fed at full resolution to the network as a second input channel set.

## Method

The core insight is that generative models produce images with non-uniform color distributions (concentrated peaks, exaggerated saturation), while real images exhibit near-uniform color distributions due to camera color-correction pipelines. The paper formalizes a color quantization-and-restoration procedure: add Gaussian noise to the image, quantize to 4-bit, repeat for 500 noisy copies, then average to recover color depth; the residual between the restored and original image encodes the local color distribution non-uniformity. A Fourier-series analysis proves that the expected residual is proportional to sin(2πμ) where μ is the normalized pixel value, so uniform distributions yield near-zero mean residual while non-uniform ones produce structured color shifts. These color difference features are extracted by a small convolutional module and concatenated channel-wise with features from a second convolutional module on the original image, then classified by a lightweight ResNet (1.4M parameters) trained with standard supervised binary cross-entropy on ProGAN/LSUN pairs.

## Results

On the proposed FakeART benchmark (cross-domain, latest diffusion models), CoD achieves 66.5% accuracy and 68.2% mAP, substantially outperforming the best baseline PatchCraft (58.7% / 59.5%). On established benchmarks, CoD reaches 97.5% ACC / 99.4% mAP on the GAN Forensic Synthetics set, 96.2% / 99.4% on the Ojha diffusion set, and 96.2% / 99.4% on GenImage, all with only 1.4M parameters. The model is trained on a single GAN (ProGAN) and generalizes across 20+ different generators without retraining.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CoD outperforms all nine baselines on every benchmark. On the established GAN Forensic Synthetics set, CoD (97.5/99.4) beats the closest competitor SAFE (95.7/99.0) by 1.8% in accuracy, while older methods like CNNDet (52.8/67.5) and FreDect (54.5/49.6) remain near random. On the Ojha and GenImage diffusion benchmarks, CoD (96.2/99.4) edges out SAFE (95.5/99.1) by 0.7% accuracy and surpasses PatchCraft (86.3/96.4) by a wide 9.9% margin. The gap widens dramatically on FakeART: CoD (66.5/68.2) leads PatchCraft (58.7/59.5) by 7.8% accuracy and 8.7% mAP, and outperforms CLIP-based methods UFD (32.8/35.7) and FatFormer (57.9/59.3) by 33.7% and 8.6% respectively. Frequency-based methods (FreqNet, NPR) and image-feature methods (CNNDet, LGrad) degrade most severely on FakeART, with several dropping below 55% accuracy, confirming the paper's claim that high-level semantic and frequency features lack robustness under cross-domain evaluation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by proposing color distribution analysis as a detection signal, explicitly positioning it as independent of the three established categories of synthetic image detectors: image-artifact-based, frequency-analysis-based, and pre-trained-model-based methods. The core idea draws an analogy from astrophotography (stacking noisy frames to recover color depth) and formalizes it through a Fourier-series analysis linking quantization-restoration error to color distribution uniformity. Rather than building on a specific prior architecture, the authors repurpose a simple signal-processing pipeline (quantize → add Gaussian noise → average) as a feature extractor, feeding it into a lightweight ResNet alongside standard image features.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The FakeART benchmark exposes a critical weakness in current detectors: when training and test domains differ (photos vs. artworks) and the latest diffusion models are used, most prior methods collapse to near-random accuracy (30–50%), while the proposed color-based features retain meaningful signal (~66%). This highlights that high-level semantic and frequency features are brittle, and motivates the field to seek more fundamental, content-agnostic detection cues. However, the absolute performance on FakeART remains far from reliable, and the paper acknowledges that ongoing generative model improvements will continue to erode any single detection signal, leaving the problem open.
