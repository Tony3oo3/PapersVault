## Problem

Machines have surpassed humans as the primary consumers of visual data, yet all existing IQA databases and metrics are designed exclusively around human visual perception. No standardized framework or database exists to assess image quality from a machine's perspective—i.e., how distortions affect downstream task performance such as detection, segmentation, and question answering. This paper introduces the topic of Image Quality Assessment for Machine Vision and establishes the first machine-preference-oriented database.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251753/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The MPD database consists of 1,000 reference images (NSIs, SCIs, AIGIs at 1k resolution) paired with 30,000 distorted images generated via 30 corruption scenarios across 7 categories at 5 intensity levels. The 'subjects' are 15 LMMs and 15 specialized CV models that process each image pair and produce task-specific scores. The final output is a 5-dimensional MOS (YoN, MCQ, VQA, CAP, and a unified CV dimension) normalized to (0,5), yielding 2.25M fine-grained annotations. For the IQA benchmark, FR metrics take both reference and distorted images while NR metrics take only the distorted image, predicting the machine MOS.

## Method

The database construction follows ITU subjective annotation standards adapted for machines: 15 LMMs (e.g., InternVL2, LLaVA-OneVision, Qwen2-VL) evaluate four tasks (YoN via softmax logit difference, MCQ via cosine similarity of option probability vectors, VQA via CLIP text-encoder similarity, CAP via BLEU/CIDEr/SPICE), while 15 CV models (e.g., Mask-RCNN, ViTDet, YOLOX) evaluate three tasks (SEG via IoU, DET via category accuracy × IoU, RET via Top-1/5/10 accuracy). Each dimension is normalized to (0,1) before merging into a (0,5) MOS, with 15 subjects per dimension. The benchmark trains 12 mainstream IQA metrics (6 FR, 6 NR) on 24k MPD pairs and evaluates on severe and mild distortion subsets, three content types, and five scoring dimensions using SRCC/KRCC/PLCC. Cross-validation with TID2013 and KADID-10K demonstrates that machine-preference training transfers to human tasks but not vice versa, and long-term usability is verified against 3 advanced and 4 early-stage LMMs.

## Results

On the MPD benchmark, no existing IQA metric achieves high consistency with machine preference. On Severe Distortion, the best performers are ARNIQA (SRCC 0.834), HyperIQA (SRCC 0.811), and AHIQ (SRCC 0.806), but on Mild Distortion performance drops sharply—HyperIQA (SRCC 0.539) and AHIQ (SRCC 0.530) lead, while several methods (LPIPS: 0.008, CKDN: 0.060, DBCNN: 0.133) fall below the naive PSNR baseline (SRCC 0.310). Cross-database validation (Table 3) shows that training on MPD preserves human-preference prediction (SRCC ~0.7–0.8 on KADID-10K/TID2013), but training on human datasets yields SRCC below 0.4 on MPD, confirming the fundamental gap between HVS- and MVS-oriented quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 12 mainstream IQA metrics (6 full-reference, 6 no-reference) plus PSNR/SSIM baselines against machine preference labels on MPD. On Severe Distortion, fitting-based methods (ARNIQA, HyperIQA, AHIQ) achieve SRCC > 0.80, but this is not challenging since they exceed 0.95 on human datasets under similar conditions. On Mild Distortion, the gap widens dramatically: LPIPS (SRCC 0.008), CKDN (0.060), and DBCNN (0.133) perform worse than the trivial PSNR baseline (0.310), while only HyperIQA (0.539) and AHIQ (0.530) maintain reasonable correlation. HVS-specific metrics (marked ✓: CKDN, LPIPS, PieAPP, TOPIQ-FR/NR, CLIPIQA, DBCNN) consistently underperform non-HVS methods on Mild Distortion, suggesting that explicitly modeling human perception is counterproductive for machine preference. Cross-validation (Table 3) reveals an asymmetry: TOPIQ-FR trained on MPD achieves the best transfer to human datasets (SRCC 0.778 on TID2013), while no human-trained metric exceeds SRCC 0.51 on MPD (Mild), confirming that machine preference is a fundamentally different evaluation target.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs fundamentally from the entire IQA literature, which has been exclusively human-centric (LIVE, TID2013, KADID-10K, KonIQ-10K, etc.), by redefining the 'subject' of quality assessment from human observers to machine vision systems. It repurposes the established IQA database construction paradigm—reference/distorted pairs, multi-level corruption, MOS-style scoring—but replaces human raters with 15 LMMs and 15 specialized CV models, and replaces visual appeal with downstream task consistency (detection, segmentation, VQA, captioning, retrieval). The multi-dimensional scoring mechanism is borrowed from human IQA practice, but the dimensions now correspond to task-specific performance rather than perceptual attributes like brightness or chroma.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

With M2M connections surpassing human consumption (147 billion per Cisco 2023), the field needs a standardized way to evaluate whether image processing preserves machine-relevant information, yet no such benchmark existed. The cross-validation asymmetry—machine-trained metrics still predict human preferences (SRCC ~0.7–0.8) while human-trained metrics fail catastrophically on machine preferences (SRCC < 0.4)—suggests machine preference is not a subset of human preference, opening a genuine research direction for human-machine-friendly IQA. Key open questions include how to balance both preferences in a single metric, how to handle the rapid evolution of LMMs (the authors acknowledge MPD may need periodic updates), and why mild distortion is paradoxically harder for machines than severe distortion (no method beats PSNR on the mild subset).
