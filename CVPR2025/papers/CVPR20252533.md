## Problem

Packing arbitrarily shaped 3D objects within a bounded container is an NP-hard problem where existing methods either assume simple geometries, rely on voxelized representations prone to approximation errors, or require expensive 3D collision detection. No prior work has addressed irregular object packing using only 2D shadow or silhouette projections as the sole optimization signal via differentiable rendering, despite the rich geometric information these projections encode.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252533/cropped_page0_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of N triangular-mesh objects, an arbitrarily shaped container mesh, and K binary target silhouette images paired with camera extrinsic parameters (rotation and translation), assuming the light source is co-located with the camera. It outputs per-object rigid transformations parameterized as quaternions (rotation) and 3D translations, yielding a physically non-intersecting packed arrangement. The number of objects is initialized from the volume ratio |C|/|O|_avg and iteratively increased until the container reaches its maximum capacity N_max, at which point further objects would extrude or intersect.

## Method

RASP is a single-stage, end-to-end differentiable rendering optimization that learns object poses by minimizing a combined loss of silhouette MSE, an SDF-based intersection penalty, and a container extrusion penalty. The key technical contribution is an SDF-based intersection formulation: SDFs are pre-computed at fixed query points within the container and warped via linear transformation at each optimization step (rather than recomputed), with the intersection degree at each point defined as the sum of negative SDF values across all objects containing that point, providing smoother gradient guidance than a simple count. Container extrusion is handled by penalizing mesh vertices whose container-SDF value exceeds a small buffer ε=0.01. Optimization uses Adam with a decaying learning rate (1e-2 to 1e-4) over 1000 iterations, and the framework naturally extends to part assembly (treating object parts as the set S and the complete object as the container) and multi-view anamorphic art without architectural changes.

## Results

On the IR-BPP dataset (four categories: General, Kitchen, ABC, Block Out), RASP achieves an average packing density of 45%, outperforming Ma et al. [20] (34%) but falling short of the physics-based online method of Zhao et al. [47] (51.9%). Optimization takes approximately 15 minutes per configuration (vs. 40.55 minutes for Ma et al.), with the Kitchen dataset (106 objects, 80,000 grid points) requiring ~17 minutes. For arbitrarily shaped containers, RASP packs 550 objects at 58% density into a donut-shaped container and 115 objects at 34% into a squirrel-shaped container. Part reassembly on the Fantastic Breaks dataset (2 parts, 80,000 grid points) completes in under 3 minutes per object.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RASP outperforms Ma et al. [20] on both packing density (45% vs. 34%) and optimization speed (~15 min vs. 40.55 min), largely because Ma et al. use a multi-stage pipeline involving object swapping, enlargement, and replacement that is both slower and less practical. Against Zhao et al. [47], RASP achieves lower average packing density (45% vs. 51.9%), but this comparison is not entirely apples-to-apples: Zhao et al. operate in an online setting where objects arrive sequentially and placement is guided by physics dynamics, whereas RASP is an offline method that optimizes all N objects simultaneously from 2D shadow guidance alone. In a direct qualitative comparison on the same object set (Figure 7), RASP performs similar to or slightly better than the adapted Zhao et al. method, suggesting the density gap is partly attributable to the online vs. offline protocol difference. RASP's key advantage is that it requires no 3D ground truth, no physics simulation, and no object modification, relying solely on silhouette images and camera poses as supervision.

## Relation to Prior Work

RASP repurposes the visual principle of 3D Anamorphic Art—where an ensemble of objects appears coherent only from specific viewpoints—as an optimization signal for 3D object packing, a task previously addressed through heuristic placement, voxelized representations, or physics-based simulation. Methodologically, it draws on the differentiable-rendering lineage established in shadow art reconstruction [23, 32] and hand-shadow/knot rendering [8, 9], but applies it to a fundamentally different objective: arranging objects to match target silhouettes rather than reconstructing a known shape. The SDF-based intersection and extrusion losses depart from prior bounding-sphere/cube collision handling [45] and voxel-based approaches [1, 6], offering a smoother, mesh-accurate penalty without the approximation errors of discretized representations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RASP demonstrates that 2D shadow information alone—without any 3D ground truth or explicit geometric supervision—can guide the arrangement of arbitrarily shaped objects, opening a path toward packing and part assembly in settings where only projected views are available. However, the framework's offline, non-physical optimization yields a 45% packing density that trails physics-based online methods (51.9%), and it struggles with multi-part assembly beyond 2–3 pieces, particularly for symmetric parts. Key open questions include incorporating gravity and contact dynamics for physically consistent arrangements, scaling to larger object sets, and extending to non-rigid deformations or dynamic anamorphic art.
