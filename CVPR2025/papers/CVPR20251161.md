## Problem

Existing physics-based 4D scene simulation methods are constrained to a narrow set of material types (often only elastic), require manual parameter initialization or multi-view inputs with known camera poses, and rely on render loss or Score Distillation Sampling for optimization—both of which either restrict motion to small deformations or demand prohibitive memory. No prior approach simultaneously supports automatic material identification across diverse types, flexible input modalities (single image, video, multi-view), and physically accurate large-motion 4D reconstruction.

## Input / Output

The method accepts three input configurations—multi-view images with known poses, a dynamic scene video, or a single uncalibrated image—along with optional text prompts and an externally applied force vector. GPT-4 is queried with the input image to identify the material type (from seven categories: elastic, plasticine, metal, foam, sand, Newtonian fluid, non-Newtonian fluid), density, and initial material parameters. The output is a set of 3D Gaussian splats with optimized material properties (Young's modulus E, Poisson ratio ν, yield stress τ_Y, viscosity η) that can be rendered from arbitrary novel viewpoints to produce a full 4D dynamic simulation.

## Method

PhysFlow operates in three stages: (1) 3D scene reconstruction via Gaussian splatting tailored to the input type (PGSR for multi-view, Grid4D for video, Splatt3R for single image); (2) material property initialization by querying GPT-4 with the input image to infer material type, density, and initial parameters; and (3) iterative material parameter optimization using a differentiable MLS-MPM simulator guided by optical flow computed from a video diffusion model's output (SVD, CogVideoX, MotionCtrl, or MOFA-Video depending on available prompts). The key technical contribution is replacing render loss or SDS loss with a flow-based loss L_flow that minimizes the squared difference between optical flows of the diffusion-generated reference video and the simulated video, making optimization memory-efficient and stable under large, complex deformations. This optical flow guidance is integrated directly into the MLS-MPM simulation loop, allowing gradients to flow through the differentiable physics solver to update material parameters without requiring high-resolution frame-level alignment.

## Results

On the synthetic PAC-NeRF dataset, PhysFlow achieves an average Relative Error (RE) of 0.29 and End-Point Error (EPE) of 0.51 across 9 objects, outperforming PAC-NeRF (0.52/0.72) but trailing GIC (0.11/0.38) on average. On the real-world dataset, PhysFlow scores 3.44/5 in physical realism and 3.53/5 in photo-realism in human evaluation, the only method exceeding 3 on both criteria, versus the best baseline (PhysGaussian at 2.67/2.91). PhysFlow also achieves the lowest Energy-Constrained Motion Score (ECMS) of 3.08, indicating the most physically plausible motion, compared to 3.70 (PhysGaussian), 3.87 (PhysDreamer), and 4.41 (Physics3D).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against PAC-NeRF, PhysFlow clearly wins on average RE (0.29 vs. 0.52) and EPE (0.51 vs. 0.72), with particularly large gains on fluid objects (Droplet: 0.02 vs. 0.06 RE) and granular materials (Trophy: 0.01 vs. 0.10 RE). Against GIC, the picture is mixed: GIC achieves lower average RE (0.11 vs. 0.29) and EPE (0.38 vs. 0.51), winning on most individual objects, but PhysFlow is competitive on fluids and granular materials and uniquely supports 7 material types without manual initialization. On the real-world dataset, PhysFlow decisively outperforms all three video-diffusion-based baselines: PhysGaussian (2.67/2.91), PhysDreamer (2.58/2.91), and Physics3D (2.64/2.98) in human-rated physical and photo realism, with PhysFlow scoring 3.44/3.53. In the ECMS motion-plausibility metric, PhysFlow (3.08) beats PhysGaussian (3.70), PhysDreamer (3.87), and Physics3D (4.41), confirming that optical flow guidance produces more physically consistent motion under large forces than render loss or SDS-based alternatives.

## Relation to Prior Work

PhysFlow sits at the intersection of three research threads: differentiable MPM-based physics simulation (Jiang et al., Hu et al.), 3D Gaussian Splatting scene representation (Kerbl et al., PhysGaussian), and video diffusion priors for motion guidance (PhysDreamer, Physics3D). It departs from prior work in two key ways: (1) replacing manual or single-image-only material initialization with GPT-4-based multi-modal reasoning that identifies material type and parameters from arbitrary visual input, and (2) substituting render loss or SDS loss with optical flow loss as the optimization signal, which the authors argue is both memory-efficient and robust to large motions. The pipeline also unifies multiple input modalities (multi-view, video, single image) through different Gaussian splatting backends (PGSR, Grid4D, Splatt3R), a flexibility not present in any single prior method.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical value lies in making physics-based 4D simulation accessible without expert parameter tuning: a user provides an image or video, and the system infers material properties and simulates realistic deformation. The optical-flow-as-guidance finding is a useful practical alternative to SDS for video diffusion–driven optimization, particularly where memory is constrained or motions are large. However, the approach remains limited to single-object simulation with externally specified forces, relies on the correctness of GPT-4's material classification, and the video diffusion outputs still introduce noise that constrains achievable fidelity. Open questions include extending to multi-object contact interactions, real-time inference, and whether the optical flow signal degrades when the diffusion model's motion prior is poor.
