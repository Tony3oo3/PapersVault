## Problem

Blind-spot denoising methods rely on pixel-wise independent noise, but real-world noise exhibits strong spatial correlations that violate this assumption. Existing remedies (e.g., pixel-shuffle downsampling, local shuffling) reduce noise correlation by using distant pixels, but simultaneously weaken the intensity correlations needed for accurate prediction. The paper provides a rigorous risk analysis quantifying this trade-off and proposes a mechanism to break noise correlation without sacrificing pixel-value correlation.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single noisy image (RGB or grayscale, 256×256 or 512×512) and produces a denoised image at test time without any external training data. During training, a random Bernoulli mask (p₀=0.1) selects sparse visible pixels, and a morphological dilation of the visible set carves out a buffer zone so that invisible pixels used for prediction are spatially separated from visible ones. The denoising U-Net receives a composite input: original noisy values at visible locations and INR-estimated values at all other locations.

## Method

The paper jointly trains a SIREN-based implicit neural representation (INR) and a U-Net denoiser under a three-term loss. The INR is fitted to the noisy values of visible pixels and then queried at neighboring invisible coordinates to produce denoised intensity estimates, effectively decoupling pixel-value correlation from noise correlation. The denoising U-Net predicts invisible pixel intensities using distant visible pixels (weakly noise-correlated) plus the INR's estimates of nearby invisible pixels (strongly intensity-correlated). A sub-pixel consistency loss regularizes the INR by requiring its sub-pixel-shifted predictions to match the detached denoiser output, reducing residual noise in the INR estimates. The theoretical analysis (Proposition 1) shows that noise correlation scales with (M−1) visible pixels while intensity correlation does not, motivating aggressive masking (small p₀) to prioritize noise decorrelation.

## Results

On the zero-shot denoising benchmark, the proposed method achieves the best PSNR/SSIM across all five datasets: 35.31/0.868 on SIDD Validation, 35.05/0.922 on SIDD Benchmark, 33.95/0.885 on FMDD, 37.88/0.959 on PolyU, and 37.20/0.948 on CC. The closest zero-shot competitors are MASH (35.06/0.851 on SIDD Val) and ScoreDVI (34.75/0.856 on SIDD Val), with the proposed method gaining 0.25–0.56 dB PSNR over them. Inference time is 25.96 s with 1.0 M parameters and 11.82 G FLOPs for a 256×256 image, comparable to MASH (25.11 s) and far faster than Self2Self (1182 s) or NN+denoising (299.2 s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all zero-shot baselines on every dataset. Against the strongest zero-shot competitors MASH [9] and ScoreDVI [8], it gains 0.25 dB PSNR and 0.017 SSIM on SIDD Validation, 0.27 dB on SIDD Benchmark, 0.24 dB on FMDD, 0.11 dB on PolyU, and 0.11 dB on CC. Methods relying on pixel-shuffle downsampling (PD-denoising [58], APBSN-single [21]) underperform significantly—APBSN-single drops to 27.72 dB on CC due to its large stride weakening pixel-value correlations. Classic methods (BM3D [10], WNNM [11]) and DIP [37] are far behind, confirming that blind-spot self-supervision with INR-based resampling is essential for spatially correlated real-world noise. The method also surpasses dataset-based self-supervised approaches like APBSN [21] (36.91 dB on SIDD Bench) and SelfFormer [34] (37.69 dB) in the zero-shot setting, demonstrating that test-time adaptation with the proposed scheme can rival or exceed methods trained on external noisy datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mask-based blind-spot denoising framework (Noise2Void, Noise2Self) to handle spatially correlated real-world noise, a limitation that prior methods like AP-BSN, PUCA, and MASH address by selecting distant pixels via pixel-shuffle or local shuffling. The key departure is the introduction of an implicit neural representation (SIREN) to resample neighboring invisible pixels, decoupling the noise-correlation problem (solved by using distant visible pixels) from the intensity-correlation problem (solved by INR-based estimation of local pixels). The paper also provides a new theoretical analysis of the statistical risk of a linear blind-spot denoiser, quantifying how noise correlation, pixel-value correlation, and visible set size interact—something prior blind-spot methods lacked.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Zero-shot denoising is critical in domains where paired clean-noisy data is unavailable (medical imaging, scientific microscopy), and this paper offers a principled way to handle the fundamental tension in blind-spot methods between breaking noise correlation and preserving intensity correlation. The theoretical analysis, while based on a simplified linear model, provides actionable guidance (prioritize reducing noise correlation over enhancing intensity correlation) that can inform future mask design. Limitations include the per-image training cost (though competitive with MASH at ~26s), the linear-model assumption in the risk analysis, and the ablation showing dilation radius is nearly irrelevant—suggesting the INR does most of the local-structure work, which raises questions about whether the morphological dilation step is truly necessary.
