## Problem

CVCL, a model trained on a single infant's egocentric video paired with transcribed parental speech, demonstrates object recognition only for objects named in that speech. It remains unexplored whether the model's internal visual representations encode concepts beyond this limited linguistic vocabulary, analogous to how infants develop pre-verbal visual understanding before acquiring corresponding word labels.

## Input / Output

NeuronClassifier takes n candidate images (single objects on white backgrounds from the Konkle dataset, n=4 in main experiments) and a target concept label that may be entirely absent from the model's training vocabulary. It outputs the index of the most relevant image based on the activation of a single identified visual-concept neuron. The upstream neuron labeling step consumes the model's per-neuron activation maps over a probing set of Konkle images and a 30,427-word concept set (SAYCam-S vocabulary, top-30K English words, and Konkle class labels) to produce a label vector assigning one concept to every neuron in the network.

## Method

The framework has two stages. First, CLIP-Dissect is applied to CVCL's ResNeXt50 vision encoder: each neuron's activation vector over the probing set is compared (via Soft-WPMI) against CLIP text embeddings of all 30,427 concepts, assigning the most similar concept as the neuron's label. Second, NeuronClassifier performs training-free n-way classification by selecting the single neuron with the highest similarity score for the target concept, then choosing the candidate image that elicits the strongest scalar activation (spatial mean) from that neuron. This bypasses the text encoder entirely, enabling recognition of out-of-vocabulary concepts and improving in-vocabulary accuracy from 36.18% (vanilla image-text similarity) to 79.50%. Layer-wise CKA and Broden-based neuron counting further reveal that CVCL shares low-level features with ImageNet/CLIP models but diverges in deeper layers, reflecting its limited exposure to diverse higher-level visual concepts.

## Results

On the Konkle object dataset (4-way classification), the proposed NeuronClassifier applied to CVCL-ResNeXt50 achieves 79.50±0.78% in-vocabulary accuracy and 76.81±0.35% out-of-vocabulary accuracy (77.79±0.40% overall), a dramatic improvement over the vanilla image-text similarity baseline of 36.18±0.91% in-vocabulary (which could not perform out-of-vocabulary classification at all). Broadly-trained models establish upper bounds: ResNeXt50 reaches 93.28±0.36% out-of-vocab and CLIP-ResNet50 reaches 98.81±0.16% in-vocab under vanilla evaluation. The DINO-S-ResNeXt50 infant model (trained on the same data without text) achieves comparable performance at 77.53±0.24% in-vocab and 77.96±0.27% out-of-vocab.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to the original CVCL vanilla method [41] (image-text similarity, 36.18% in-vocab), NeuronClassifier more than doubles in-vocabulary accuracy to 79.50% and, critically, enables out-of-vocabulary classification at 76.81%—a capability the vanilla method entirely lacks. Against the broadly-trained CLIP-ResNet50 [34], CVCL remains substantially below (79.50% vs. 98.81% in-vocab vanilla), though CLIP itself degrades under the neuron-only protocol (91.59% in-vocab), suggesting the text encoder contributes significantly to its vanilla performance. The ImageNet-pretrained ResNeXt50 [43] also outperforms CVCL under NeuronClassifier (88.17% in-vocab, 93.28% out-of-vocab), confirming the advantage of diverse large-scale training data. Most notably, DINO-S-ResNeXt50 [31], trained on the identical infant egocentric data but without any text supervision, achieves nearly identical performance to CVCL (77.53% vs. 79.50% in-vocab; 77.96% vs. 76.81% out-of-vocab), indicating that the visual concepts discovered in CVCL arise primarily from the visual experience rather than from linguistic supervision.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on CVCL (Vong et al., Science 2024), a contrastive model trained on a single infant's egocentric video paired with transcribed parental speech, and repurposes the CLIP-Dissect neuron-labeling framework to interrogate CVCL's internal representations. Conceptually, it departs from prior interpretability work (Network Dissection, CLIP-Dissect) by applying these tools to a developmentally-motivated model rather than a broadly pretrained one, and from prior infant-learning computational models (Orhan & Lake, Bambach et al.) by focusing on what the model knows *beyond* its linguistic supervision. The proposed NeuronClassifier is a new training-free classification procedure that bypasses the text encoder entirely, positioning itself as a diagnostic tool for latent visual concepts rather than a competitive zero-shot classifier.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete computational demonstration that visual concepts can form in a model without explicit linguistic labels for those concepts, bridging a long-standing gap between developmental psychology (pre-verbal visual understanding) and modern CV interpretability. However, the evidence is constrained: only 31 Konkle classes are classifiable, the out-of-vocabulary accuracy (76.8%) is modest and close to in-vocabulary (79.5%), and the analysis is limited to a single child's dataset with no access to the raw training data. Open questions include whether the beyond-vocabulary phenomenon generalizes to adult egocentric data, scales with dataset diversity, or persists in transformer-based infant models, and whether the NeuronClassifier approach can be extended to open-vocabulary or compositional settings.
