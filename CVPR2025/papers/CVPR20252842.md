## Problem

Existing image-to-video diffusion models struggle with two coupled challenges: preserving the visual appearance of the input image across generated frames (color tone, object shapes drift over time) and avoiding abrupt, physically implausible motion changes. Reducing motion dynamics is a common workaround for consistency, but it sacrifices the dynamic quality that makes animation engaging. The paper seeks to achieve both high appearance consistency and motion smoothness without over-suppressing the magnitude of generated motion.

## Input / Output

The model takes a single static RGB image at 320×512 resolution and a free-form text prompt as input, and produces a 16-frame animated video clip where the first frame is the input image itself. Frames are sampled at a variable interval of 3–10 frames apart to maintain a consistent dynamics-degree distribution. At inference, the user can additionally specify a dynamics-degree bucket (0–19) to explicitly control how much motion the generated video exhibits, and the model uses DDIM sampling with 50 steps and classifier-free guidance at scale 7.5.

## Method

Cinemo is built on the LaVie cascaded latent diffusion architecture but introduces three key modifications. First, instead of denoising full frames, it learns the distribution of motion residuals (latent-space differences between each subsequent frame and the first frame), so the model focuses on inter-frame changes rather than redundant shared content. Second, during inference it applies DCTInit: the low-frequency 3D DCT coefficients of the noised input latent are mixed with the high-frequency DCT coefficients of the random noise to produce a refined initial noise, which suppresses abrupt motion and color drift more effectively than the FFT-based alternative used in prior work. Third, a dynamics-degree control mechanism computes the average MS-SSIM between consecutive frames of training clips, bins it into 20 buckets, and injects the bucket index as an additional positional embedding alongside the diffusion timestep, enabling explicit user control over motion magnitude. Training proceeds on WebVid-10M followed by fine-tuning on a 10K-subset of Vimeo25M, optimizing all parameters with Adam on 8 A100 GPUs.

## Results

On UCF-101, Cinemo achieves the best FVD (168.16), IS (58.71), and FID (13.17) among all compared methods, outperforming the closest competitor ConsistI2V (FVD 177.66, IS 56.22, FID 15.74). On MSR-VTT, Cinemo attains the lowest FVD (93.51) and highest CLIPSIM (0.2858), surpassing SEINE (FVD 152.63, CLIPSIM 0.2774) by a substantial margin. In the VBench I2V evaluation (Table 2), Cinemo leads in imaging quality (71.30%), aesthetic quality (61.85%), and motion smoothness (98.21%), while maintaining competitive camera motion (30.68%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Cinemo outperforms all six baselines on every reported metric. Against the closest competitor ConsistI2V [42], Cinemo reduces FVD by 9.5 points on UCF-101 (168.16 vs. 177.66) and by 11.07 points on MSR-VTT (93.51 vs. 104.58), while improving CLIPSIM from 0.2674 to 0.2858. SEINE [8] is the next strongest baseline, but Cinemo improves its UCF-101 FVD by 138.33 points (168.16 vs. 306.49) and MSR-VTT FVD by 59.12 points. DynamiCrafter [67] and I2VGen-XL [73] trail further behind, with FVD gaps of 236.34 and 429.26 respectively on UCF-101. Follow-Your-Click [33] reports only a single MSR-VTT FVD (271.74), which Cinemo improves by 178.23 points. In the VBench user study (Table 3), Cinemo receives the highest 'good' vote proportion across all five criteria (e.g., 0.57 for subject consistency vs. 0.42 for SEINE), while also achieving the lowest 'bad' vote rates (e.g., 0.05 for background consistency vs. 0.10 for SVD [4]).

## Relation to Prior Work

Cinemo builds directly on LaVie, a cascaded latent diffusion model for video generation, reusing its architecture and pre-trained weights while retraining all parameters for the image animation task. It departs from prior I2V methods (PIA, ConsistI2V, I2V-Adapter, DynamiCrafter) that predict full frames by instead learning the distribution of motion residuals (frame-to-frame differences in latent space), a conceptual shift that focuses the model on dynamics rather than redundant static content. The DCT-based noise refinement (DCTInit) extends the FFT-based initialization strategies from FreeInit and ConsistI2V, arguing that DCT's energy-packing and symmetric periodic extension better address spectral leakage. The dynamics degree control mechanism is inspired by AnimateAnything's motion guidance but repurposed as a scalar conditioning signal for motion magnitude.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses two persistent and practically important challenges in I2V generation—appearance drift and abrupt motion—through a coherent combination of training-time (motion residual learning) and inference-time (DCTInit) strategies, plus explicit user control over motion magnitude. The motion residual formulation is a clean conceptual contribution that also enables downstream applications like motion transfer and video editing without retraining. However, the model remains limited to 16 frames at 320×512 resolution, and its dynamic degree in VBench is lower than DynamiCrafter and SVD, suggesting a trade-off between consistency and motion richness that is not fully resolved. Open questions include whether motion residual learning generalizes to longer video sequences, higher resolutions, or 3D-consistent generation, and whether the DCT advantage over FFT persists at larger spatial scales.
