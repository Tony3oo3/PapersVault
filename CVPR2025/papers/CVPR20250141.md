## Problem

Semi-supervised crowd counting methods struggle with two bottlenecks: off-the-shelf data augmentations (Mixup, CutMix) destroy the spatial structure of crowd scenes, producing distorted density maps, and CNN-based backbones overfit to local details while failing to capture the global context needed for extremely crowded, low-light, and adverse weather scenarios. The paper addresses both the data side (how to diversify unlabeled samples without corrupting foreground structure) and the model side (how to produce more accurate pseudo-labels from a backbone that models long-range dependencies).

## Input / Output

The method takes RGB crowd images (longest side scaled to ≤1920 px, randomly cropped to 512×512, or 256×256 for ShanghaiTech A) and produces two outputs: a pixel-wise density map from the regression head (summed to yield an exact count) and a per-pixel count-interval classification from the Anti-Noise branch (predefined bins following Wang et al. 2021). During training, the inpainting module periodically generates additional unlabeled samples by replacing background regions with Stable Diffusion outputs, and the teacher model computes a spatially varying inconsistency mask that down-weights unreliable inpainted regions in the loss.

## Method

TMTB builds on the Mean Teacher framework with VMamba (a Visual State Space Model using the SS2D module) as backbone, replacing CNNs/Transformers to capture global receptive fields at linear complexity. Two parallel heads consume the backbone features: a regression head predicts the density map (trained with a multi-scale SSIM + total-variation loss), and an Anti-Noise classification head predicts count-interval bins, providing inexact-but-accurate supervision that is robust to annotation noise. A key data-side contribution is Inpainting Augmentation: the classification branch separates foreground from background, and Stable Diffusion inpaints only the background with random positive text prompts, preserving crowd spatial structure while adding diversity. To handle imperfect inpainting, the teacher model computes an inconsistency mask from the classification-branch disagreement between strong and weak augmentations, applying time-decayed weights (ω_l^t) to down-weight unreliable regions in the inpainting loss. The total loss combines supervised regression+classification, semi-supervised consistency (MAE on both heads between student and EMA teacher), and the mask-weighted inpainting consistency term, with patch-aligned random masking (SimMIM-style) serving as the strong augmentation for the student.

## Results

With only 5% labeled data, TMTB achieves MAE of 67.0 on JHU-Crowd++ (first time below 70.0), 96.3 on UCF-QNRF, 72.4 on ShanghaiTech A, and 10.6 on ShanghaiTech B, outperforming the previous SOTA (MRC-Crowd) by 9.5, 5.1, 2.4, and 1.1 MAE points respectively. At 10% labeled data, TMTB further reduces JHU-Crowd++ MAE to 66.3 and UCF-QNRF MAE to 91.7. At 40% labeled data, TMTB matches the best JHU-Crowd++ MAE of 60.0 and achieves the lowest ShanghaiTech B MAE of 7.5. In domain generalization (A→Q and Q→A), TMTB with only 40% source labels surpasses all fully-supervised (100%) baselines, e.g., 112.5 MAE on A→Q vs. 115.7 for MPCount.

## Baselines & Comparisons

At the 5% labeled setting, TMTB decisively outperforms all baselines on every dataset: it reduces JHU-Crowd++ MAE from 76.5 (MRC-Crowd) to 67.0 (−12.4%), UCF-QNRF from 101.4 to 96.3, ShanghaiTech A from 74.8 to 72.4, and ShanghaiTech B from 11.7 to 10.6. At 10% labeled data, TMTB leads on JHU-Crowd++ (66.3 vs. 69.7 for SALCrowd), UCF-QNRF (91.7 vs. 93.4 for MRC-Crowd), and ShanghaiTech A (65.7 vs. 67.3 for MRC-Crowd), though it trails CU and SALCrowd by 0.1 MAE on ShanghaiTech B (9.8 vs. 9.7). At 40% labeled data, TMTB ties MRC-Crowd on JHU-Crowd++ (60.0) and wins on ShanghaiTech B (7.5 vs. 7.9 for SALCrowd), but is marginally behind on UCF-QNRF (81.4 vs. 81.1) and ShanghaiTech A (60.8 vs. 60.7). In domain generalization, TMTB with only 40% source labels beats all 100%-labeled DG methods (e.g., 112.5 vs. 115.7 MAE on A→Q vs. MPCount; 62.4 vs. 65.5 on Q→A). In domain adaptation, TMTB (40% source, no target access) surpasses FSIM (100% source, target visible) on Q→A (62.4 vs. 66.8 MAE) and is competitive on A→Q (112.5 vs. 105.3), demonstrating strong generalization from the VSSM backbone and inpainting augmentation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Mean Teacher semi-supervised framework (Tarvainen & Valpola, 2017) that underpins most prior SSCC methods (L2R, DACount, OT-M, MRC-Crowd) by replacing the CNN/Transformer backbone with VMamba's SS2D module for linear-complexity global context modeling, and by introducing diffusion-based inpainting (Stable Diffusion) as a task-specific augmentation that preserves foreground spatial structure—addressing a gap the authors identify where Mixup/CutMix destroy density-map-relevant geometry. The Anti-Noise classification branch draws on count-interval partitioning (Wang et al., ICCV 2021) and the CUT regression loss (Qian et al., BMVC 2022), while the patch-aligned masking strong augmentation borrows from SimMIM (Xie et al., CVPR 2022). Conceptually, the paper positions itself at the intersection of generative data augmentation and state-space architectures, filling what it frames as a missing piece: an effective augmentation strategy for pixel-level prediction tasks where spatial integrity is paramount.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The first sub-70 MAE on JHU-Crowd++ with only 5% labeled data and the domain-generalization results—where a 40%-labeled semi-supervised model outperforms fully-supervised (100%) DG baselines on both A→Q and Q→A—suggest that VSSM's global receptive field plus diverse inpainted backgrounds may be particularly effective under distribution shift, a finding with implications beyond crowd counting. However, the periodic Stable Diffusion inpainting introduces significant computational overhead (two inpainting processes per training run, 80-epoch period), and the approach's scalability to very large unlabeled pools or other pixel-level tasks (segmentation, depth) remains untested. Open questions include whether the VSSM advantage over strong Transformer backbones persists at higher resolutions, whether the inconsistency-level weighting mechanism generalizes to other augmentation strategies, and whether the inpainting quality ceiling (poorly-inpainted samples still occur) limits further gains.
