## Problem

Existing deep metric learning methods either model interactions only among mined n-tuplets (discarding most sample interactions and suffering from noise sensitivity) or replace sample-sample distances entirely with proxy-sample distances (losing fine-grained intra-class supervision). A further shared shortcoming is that interaction strength in these methods increases or remains constant with distance, which amplifies the influence of distant, potentially mislabeled samples and causes learned proxies to drift away from the data distribution they represent.

## Input / Output

The method consumes 224×224 center-cropped RGB images (from 256×256 originals) and produces L2-normalized semantic embedding vectors of 512 dimensions (ResNet-50, BN-Inception) or 384 dimensions (ViT, DINO). During training, each mini-batch of labeled images is processed alongside M learnable proxies per class (M=15 for CUB-200/Cars-196, M=2 for SOP) that augment the potential field to represent out-of-batch samples. Evaluation follows the standard zero-shot retrieval protocol where the first half of classes are used for training and the second half for testing, with performance measured by Recall@K.

## Method

PFML represents each embedding as a unit charge generating two potential fields: an attractive field (negative, −1/r^α) for same-class samples and a repulsive field (positive, +1/r^α) for different-class samples, both truncated at a margin radius δ to prevent collapse and excessive inter-class separation. The class potential field Ψ_j is the superposition of all individual attraction and repulsion potentials from batch embeddings and proxies, and training minimizes the total potential energy U = ΣΨ_{y_i}(z_i) + ΣΨ_j(p_{j,k}) via gradient descent, which moves embeddings and proxies along −∇Ψ. The critical design choice is the decay property (α > 0), which makes interaction strength weaken with distance—unlike all prior tuplet- and proxy-based losses—thereby de-emphasizing distant (potentially mislabeled) samples and ensuring local minima exist near each embedding. The paper proves (Proposition 1, Corollary 1) that this decay guarantees proxies achieve a lower Wasserstein-2 distance to the data distribution they represent compared to non-decaying alternatives like the contrastive loss. Proxies are used only to augment the field (representing out-of-batch samples) rather than replacing sample-sample interactions, preserving fine-grained supervision that pure proxy-based methods lose.

## Results

Using a ResNet-50 backbone, PFML achieves R@1 of 73.4% on CUB-200-2011, 92.7% on Cars-196, and 82.9% on SOP, outperforming the previous best (HIST) by 2.0%, 3.1%, and 1.5% respectively. With a ViT backbone, PFML reaches 87.8% R@1 on CUB-200-2011 and 88.2% on SOP, surpassing the prior SOTA (HIER) by 2.1% and 2.1%. Under 20% label noise, PFML retains 66.7% R@1 on CUB-200-2011 and 84.5% on Cars-196, outperforming the second-best method (Proxy Anchor) by 6.0% and 7.6% respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PFML outperforms all 23 baselines across all three datasets and all four backbones (ResNet-50, BN Inception, DINO, ViT). Against the strongest proxy-based competitor Proxy Anchor [21], PFML gains 3.7% R@1 on CUB-200-2011, 5.0% on Cars-196, and 1.5% on SOP (ResNet-50). Against the best tuplet-based SOTA HIST [28], PFML leads by 2.0%, 3.1%, and 1.5% R@1 on CUB, Cars, and SOP respectively. Against the best BN-Inception tuplet method Multi-Similarity [51], the margin is larger: 5.8% on CUB, 6.0% on Cars, and 2.4% on SOP. The paper's own ablation CPML (contrastive loss + proxies, which violates the decay property) is beaten by 5.1% on CUB, 7.5% on Cars, and 3.5% on SOP, isolating the decay property as the key differentiator. Under 20% label noise, PFML's advantage widens dramatically: it beats Proxy Anchor by 6.0% (CUB) and 7.6% (Cars) in R@1, while all other baselines degrade substantially more.

## Relation to Prior Work

PFML sits at the intersection of two research threads: proxy-based deep metric learning (ProxyNCA, Proxy Anchor) and potential-field models from robotics and physics. It departs from prior proxy-based methods by using proxies to *augment* rather than *replace* sample-sample interactions, preserving fine-grained supervision that proxy-only approaches lose. The key conceptual departure from both tuplet-based and proxy-based losses is the decay property: interaction strength between embeddings *decreases* with distance, reversing the near-universal assumption in prior work (contrastive loss, Proxy Anchor, SoftTriplet) that force grows with separation. The potential-field superposition mechanism itself is borrowed from robot path planning and obstacle avoidance, repurposed here to model all pairwise interactions in a batch simultaneously without explicit tuplet mining.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The decay property provides a principled explanation for why PFML is more robust to label noise (>7% R@1 advantage under 20% corruption) and better aligns proxies with the data distribution (33% lower W2 distance), addressing two practically important failure modes in fine-grained retrieval. The theoretical result (Corollary 1) formalizes why non-decaying fields lead to proxies drifting from the modes they should represent. A key limitation is that proxy count scales linearly with class count, making training expensive for very large class sets (e.g., 500K-class face recognition), though the authors note this is a training-only cost. Open questions include whether the specific 1/r^α functional form is optimal, whether the decay principle transfers to other representation-learning settings (e.g., self-supervised contrastive learning), and whether the approach can be made more scalable for extremely large class counts.
