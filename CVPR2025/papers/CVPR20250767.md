## Problem

Existing camera-controllable video diffusion methods require ground-truth camera parameters for every training frame, forcing reliance on restricted datasets like RealEstate10K that contain mostly static scenes. This limitation degrades generalization, producing videos with unnatural object motions and inaccurate camera control. The paper asks whether optical flow can serve as a universal motion representation that eliminates the need for camera-parameter-annotated training data while still enabling precise camera control.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image at 320×576 resolution and a sequence of camera extrinsic/intrinsic parameters defining a trajectory over T frames. It outputs 14 synthesized video frames at the same resolution. Internally, the camera parameters are converted to background optical flow via 3D warping using a monocular depth estimate (Depth Anything V2), and an object motion synthesis model generates complementary object flow maps; these are merged into a single camera-object flow map that conditions the video synthesis stage.

## Method

FloVD adopts a two-stage pipeline: (1) optical flow generation and (2) flow-conditioned video synthesis. In stage 1, camera flow is computed by unprojecting pixels with estimated depth, warping them under the target camera pose, and projecting back; object flow is generated by OMSM, a latent video diffusion model (initialized from Stable Video Diffusion) that synthesizes object-motion flow maps conditioned on the input image, trained in two stages to suppress background camera motion. The two flow maps are integrated using a SAM 2 segmentation mask to produce a unified camera-object flow map. In stage 2, FVSM extends Stable Video Diffusion with a T2I-Adapter-style flow encoder that produces multi-level flow embeddings injected additively into each layer of the denoising U-Net; only the flow encoder is trained while the U-Net and VAE remain frozen. The key insight is that background optical flow inherently encodes 3D viewpoint correlations, so it can drive detailed camera control without ever requiring camera-parameter annotations during training.

## Results

On the Pexels-random benchmark, FloVD achieves FVD 91.55, FID 35.66, and IS 11.63, outperforming CameraCtrl (FVD 151.06, FID 40.69, IS 11.23) and MotionCtrl (FVD 93.54, FID 36.06, IS 11.19). For large object motions (Pexels-large, ≥40 px flow), FloVD achieves FID 21.12 and IS 12.95, substantially better than CameraCtrl (FID 25.53, IS 11.68) and MotionCtrl (FID 25.84, IS 11.71). On camera controllability (RealEstate10K test set), FloVD trained on RE10K achieves mRotErr 1.43°, mTransErr 0.0869, and mCamMC 0.0887, marginally beating CameraCtrl (1.44°, 0.0927, 0.0945) and significantly outperforming MotionCtrl (5.90°, 0.1610, 0.1719).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FloVD is compared against two camera-controllable video synthesis methods: CameraCtrl [9] (which uses Plücker embedding of camera parameters) and MotionCtrl [33] (which projects camera extrinsics onto diffusion features). On camera controllability, FloVD (trained on RE10K without using ground-truth camera parameters) achieves the best mRotErr (1.43° vs. 1.44° for CameraCtrl, 5.90° for MotionCtrl) and best mTransErr/mCamMC, demonstrating that optical flow-based conditioning matches or exceeds explicit camera parameter embedding. On video synthesis quality, FloVD wins on FVD, FID, and IS across Pexels-random, Pexels-small, and Pexels-medium, and on FID and IS for Pexels-large. The sole exception is Pexels-large FVD, where MotionCtrl scores lower (188.89 vs. 207.39), which the authors attribute to MotionCtrl's failure to follow camera trajectories, keeping the viewpoint close to the input image and thus coincidentally aligning with the minimal camera motion in the benchmark. CameraCtrl degrades severely on large object motions (FVD 340.36 on Pexels-large), while FloVD maintains consistent quality, confirming that training on diverse real-world videos with dynamic objects is critical for generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

FloVD builds on Stable Video Diffusion as its latent diffusion backbone and repurposes its VAE (inspired by Marigold) for encoding/decoding optical flow maps. It departs from the dominant line of camera-controllable video synthesis (CameraCtrl, MotionCtrl, CamCo, CamI2V, VD3D), which conditions on explicit camera parameters via Plücker embeddings or direct projection and therefore requires ground-truth camera annotations during training. FloVD instead conditions on optical flow maps—derived from 3D warping of estimated depth plus a learned object motion synthesis model—thereby eliminating the need for camera-annotated training data. It also relates to prior flow-based two-stage video synthesis pipelines (e.g., Li et al., Holynski et al.), but those target temporal coherence rather than camera control and do not decompose flow into camera and object components.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical impact is that camera-controllable video generation can now be trained on arbitrary real-world videos with dynamic objects, removing the bottleneck of scarce camera-annotated datasets (e.g., RealEstate10K) that previously forced models to learn from static scenes and produce motionless or artifact-ridden outputs. The ability to support complex cinematic motions like dolly zoom without training on varying intrinsic parameters broadens the method's applicability to film and VR production. Open questions include the physically inaccurate integration of camera and object flows (missing z-axis motion), reliance on segmentation quality for object masks, and whether the two-stage pipeline can be unified or extended to longer video sequences.
