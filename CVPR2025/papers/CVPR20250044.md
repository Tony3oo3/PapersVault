## Problem

Existing feature-matching anomaly detection methods (e.g., PatchCore, SPADE) rely on frozen backbones and handcrafted nearest-neighbor matching, with the feature extractor and matching module optimized separately. This decoupling prevents the matching scheme from adapting to the extracted features and blocks direct supervision from anomaly detection/segmentation signals, yielding suboptimal feature representations especially in few-shot and continual settings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250044/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image processed by a pre-trained ViT-B/16 (CLIP) backbone to produce patch features of shape H×W×D, and a memory bank of L×D features (default 196×768) constructed via coreset sampling from training images. The FMN computes a similarity matrix of shape H×W×L, from which it outputs a pixel-level anomaly map (H×W) and an instance-level anomaly score (scalar). The memory bank is treated as a learnable parameter within the model rather than a fixed lookup table.

## Method

DFM replaces the non-differentiable nearest-neighbor matching with a Feature Matching Network (FMN) that reformulates matching as a matrix operation followed by two learnable modulate layers (linear by default) and two-stage pooling (min over the memory dimension, max over spatial dimensions). Learnable adapters are inserted into the pre-trained ViT for parameter-efficient feature adaptation, and both adapters and modulate layers are initialized as identity transformations to preserve the base model's capability at training start. A two-stage iterative training procedure first optimizes the adapters (feature extractor) with an unsupervised objective, then optimizes the FMN (matching head) with a supervised objective using real or CutPaste-generated anomalous samples, avoiding the instability of simultaneous joint optimization. This end-to-end design allows gradients from anomaly detection and segmentation losses to flow directly through the matching module back to the feature extractor, eliminating the need for proxy self-supervised tasks.

## Results

On the MVTec AD continual anomaly detection benchmark, DFM achieves state-of-the-art performance with 96.9% instance-AUROC and 51.1% pixel-AP, surpassing the previous best (UCAD) by 3.9% and 5.5% respectively, while maintaining a low forgetting measure (1.50/1.30). In the few-shot setting (1–8 normal samples), DFM-PatchCore reaches 93.0% I-AUROC at 1-shot, outperforming PatchCore (78.5%) by 14.5 points and matching or exceeding dedicated few-shot methods like WinCLIP and GraphCore. On the supervised VisA dataset, a small number of real anomalous samples yields approximately 4% improvement in both I-AUROC and P-AP over the original PatchCore.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DFM outperforms all nine baselines on the primary continual anomaly detection benchmark (Table 1), achieving 96.9% I-AUROC and 51.1% P-AP versus UCAD's 93.0/45.6, DNE's 87.0/–, and PatchCore's 66.9/18.1. The gap over the closest competitor UCAD [1] is +3.9 I-AUROC and +5.5 P-AP, though UCAD reports a marginally lower forgetting measure (1.00 vs. 1.50). In the few-shot setting (Table 2), DFM-PatchCore surpasses PatchCore by 14.5 points at 1-shot (93.0 vs. 78.5 I-AUROC) and by 1.1–3.7 points over GraphCore and WinCLIP across all shot counts; the only exception is a 0.1-point I-AUROC deficit to WinCLIP at 1-shot (93.0 vs. 93.1), though DFM leads on P-AUROC (96.3 vs. 95.2). Against reconstruction-based and one-class baselines (PaDiM, SPADE, CFA), DFM leads by 30–42 I-AUROC points in the continual setting, reflecting the advantage of learnable feature adaptation over frozen-backbone matching. The supervised VisA experiments further show that DFM with real anomalous samples gains ~4% over PatchCore, a capability none of the compared feature-matching baselines (which rely on frozen backbones) can exploit.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the feature-matching-based anomaly detection lineage (SPADE, PatchCore, CFA) by making the matching step differentiable, converting nearest-neighbor search into a pooling-based Feature Matching Network (FMN) that can be jointly optimized with the feature extractor. Prior adaptation methods (CFA, Patch-SVDD, UCAD) relied on self-supervised proxy tasks like contrastive learning or hypersphere clustering to tune frozen backbones, whereas DFM directly supervises the extractor with anomaly detection and segmentation losses. The continual detection setup follows UCAD's per-class adapter architecture, but replaces its contrastive proxy objective with direct supervised/unsupervised anomaly losses, and the few-shot setting builds on PatchCore's coreset-sampled memory bank while making both the backbone (via adapters) and the matching head learnable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical impact is that DFM provides a generic, plug-and-play mechanism to adapt feature-matching anomaly detectors to downstream tasks (few-shot, continual, supervised) without redesigning the matching algorithm, which is valuable in industrial settings where new defect classes appear over time. A notable limitation is the two-stage iterative training requirement—joint optimization of the extractor and FMN is unstable—adding practical complexity, and the method still depends on a strong pre-trained backbone (CLIP ViT). Open questions include whether the FMN's learnable matching provides genuine representational gains beyond what a sufficiently large memory bank with static matching could achieve, and whether the approach extends to video or multi-modal anomaly detection.
