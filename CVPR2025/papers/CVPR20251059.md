## Problem

Evaluating vision-language models on fine-grained visual classification is hampered by two gaps: VLMs generate unconstrained text that rarely matches predefined labels exactly, and standard binary evaluation discards the partial correctness of answers that are taxonomically related but not identical (e.g., 'conifer' vs. 'Norway Spruce'). Existing text similarity measures (ROUGE, BERTScore, CLIP) do not reliably capture the hierarchical structure of label taxonomies, leaving no principled way to assign graded credit for less-specific-but-not-incorrect predictions.

## Input / Output

The framework takes an RGB image, passes it through a VLM to obtain a free-form text prediction, and maps that prediction onto a directed taxonomy tree (sourced from Wikidata's subclass-of relations or the Catalogue of Life) to produce hierarchical precision (hP), hierarchical recall (hR), and their harmonic mean (hF). The taxonomy is a rooted directed tree where each node carries a string label; the mapping algorithm (Alg.1) normalizes text, computes CLIP text-to-text similarity against all node labels, and applies a staged matching procedure (exact string → n-gram overlap → shared-ancestor fallback) to select a single node, always preferring the deepest (most specific) match.

## Method

The core contribution is a taxonomy-aware evaluation pipeline built on hierarchical precision and recall, which measure the length of the longest common ancestor path between the predicted and ground-truth nodes normalized by the predicted or target path length, respectively. To place unconstrained VLM text on the taxonomy, the authors design a multi-stage mapping algorithm that combines CLIP text-to-text similarity with string-based matching: it first checks for verbatim label matches among top-k CLIP-scored nodes, then falls back to n-gram (4, 3, 2) overlap, and finally resolves ambiguity by searching for a common ancestor appearing in at least four of the top-k candidate ancestor sets. Taxonomies are extracted from Wikidata by following subclass-of edges and retaining the longest root-to-node path (breaking ties uniformly at random), and from the Catalogue of Life for iNaturalist21. The paper validates the approach by showing that standard NLP similarity measures correlate poorly with taxonomic distance (especially across subtrees, i.e., hP), then applies the full pipeline to eight VLMs across iNaturalist21 and nine OVEN sub-datasets, demonstrating that taxonomy-aware rankings diverge substantially from exact-match or text-similarity rankings and that hP/hR jointly capture prompt sensitivity invisible to binary accuracy.

## Results

On the synthetic correlation experiment (Table 1), no existing text similarity measure achieves strong alignment with taxonomic structure: the best Kendall's τ is 0.49 (CLIP-i2t vs. hR on iNaturalist21), while most measures fall below 0.35, and NLI even shows negative correlation (−0.20) with hR. For the taxonomy mapping task (Table 2), the proposed CLIP-t2t + Alg.1 pipeline achieves 47.1% exact node-match accuracy and hF of 0.80, substantially outperforming the best single-measure baseline (CLIP-t2t alone at 30.3% accuracy, hF 0.75). In the VLM comparison (Fig. 5), taxonomy-aware measures produce notably different rankings than standard metrics: LLaVA ranks last under Exact Match on iNaturalist21 but achieves high hP and hF, while GPT-4, top on most standard measures, is surpassed by QVLChat on hP and hF, revealing that GPT-4 makes more specific but erroneous predictions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central finding is that standard NLP text-similarity measures (ROUGE, METEOR, BERTScore, SentenceBERT, NLI) fail to capture taxonomic relationships: their Kendall's τ correlations with hP/hR are generally below 0.35, and NLI shows negative correlation (−0.20) with hR on iNaturalist21, indicating it actively misjudges taxonomic proximity for distantly related labels. CLIP-based measures perform best among existing options (τ up to 0.49 for CLIP-i2t vs. hR), but still fall well short of capturing cross-subtree taxonomic distance (hP). For the mapping sub-task, the proposed Alg.1 heuristic combined with CLIP-t2t improves exact node-match accuracy from 30.3% (CLIP-t2t alone) to 47.1%, a 16.8-point gain, and outperforms all string-based baselines (ROUGE at 30.0%, METEOR at 29.1%) and representation-based baselines (SentBERT at 20.0%, BERTScore at 11.3%). In the VLM comparison, taxonomy-aware hF produces different rankings than Exact Match: LLaVA (last on EM) rises to competitive hF due to high hP, while GPT-4 (top on EM) drops below QVLChat on hP/hF because its more specific predictions are more often wrong. The prompt-sensitivity analysis (Fig. 7) further shows that instruction-tuned models (InternLM-XComposer2, OLM12B, QVLChat, OLM3B) shift meaningfully in hP–hR space between barebones and specificity prompts, whereas non-instruction-tuned models (Fuyu, QwenVL) show no response, a distinction invisible to binary accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts hierarchical precision and recall from the text categorization literature (Kiritchenko et al., 2006) to the problem of evaluating unconstrained VLM text outputs on fine-grained visual classification. It departs from the dominant VLM evaluation paradigm—exact match or text-similarity scoring (ROUGE, BERTScore, CLIP retrieval)—by explicitly grounding predictions in a taxonomic tree extracted from Wikidata or the Catalogue of Life. The mapping of free-form VLM strings onto taxonomy nodes via a staged heuristic (Alg. 1) combining CLIP similarity with string matching is a new procedural contribution, building on the OVEN benchmark's entity linking and CLIP's cross-modal retrieval capabilities.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework reveals that model rankings shift substantially under taxonomy-aware scoring (e.g., LLaVA moves from last to top-tier in hierarchical precision), exposing accuracy-vs-specificity trade-offs invisible to standard metrics. A key open question is the quality of the text-to-taxonomy mapping itself, which the authors acknowledge is a low-resource problem with no large-scale evaluation set; future work on dedicated mapping classifiers or synthetic training data could tighten this bottleneck. The finding that NLI-based similarity shows negative correlation with taxonomic distance, and that CLIP image features encode more taxonomic structure than text features, challenges assumptions about which representations are suitable for structured evaluation.
