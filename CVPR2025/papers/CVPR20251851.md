## Problem

Existing text-based image editing methods apply a fixed classifier-free guidance scale across all denoising steps, ignoring that multi-step denoising is inherently a layout-to-appearance process where each step has distinct editing requirements. This fixed-intensity paradigm causes over-editing on focus-irrelevant words and under-editing on focus-relevant words, and particularly degrades performance on multi-function editing where different textual functions demand different intensities at different steps.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251851/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Given a source image, its original text prompt, and a modified edited prompt, FeedEdit produces a single edited image while preserving text-irrelevant regions. The method is training-free and plug-and-play, operating within a Stable Diffusion V1.5 + P2P editing engine with a DDIM sampler at 30 steps and initial guidance scale ω=5. The low-pass filter cutoff D₀ is set to res/2 for attention blocks of spatial resolution res×res, and ideal editing degree thresholds are set per function type (noun=0.2, adj=0.15, verb=0.35).

## Method

FeedEdit introduces a perceive-feedback-regulate architecture that dynamically adjusts editing intensity at each denoising step. First, a Dynamic Editing Degree Perceiving module computes L1-norm differences of value and attention features between editing and reconstruction branches, applying FFT-based low-pass filtering to suppress noise interference and robustly quantify whether the current step is over- or under-editing. Second, a non-linear PI feedback controller maps the perceived editing error (difference from an ideal threshold) into a control signal β(t) via a sigmoid-shaped proportional term and a cumulative integral term, driving the editing degree toward the target. Third, a phrase-level regulating strategy uses part-of-speech tagging to split the edited prompt into noun, adjective, and verb phrases, each receiving its own function-specific PI controller and control signal that directly scales the corresponding cross-attention key and value vectors, enabling fine-grained multi-function editing without any additional training.

## Results

On single-function editing, FeedEdit achieves CLIP-T 0.3024, CLIP-I 0.8565, LPIPS 0.1311, FID 65.02, and ImageReward 0.8754, outperforming the strongest baseline SDPInV (CLIP-T 0.2770, FID 86.74) by 9.2% in editability and 15.7% in FID. On multi-function editing, FeedEdit reaches CLIP-T 0.2607 and FID 81.70, improving over the best baseline InfEdit (CLIP-T 0.2367, FID 98.15) by 10.1% in editability and 16.8% in FID. The gains are most pronounced in multi-function scenarios, where FeedEdit also achieves the highest ImageReward score (0.6507) versus all baselines scoring below 0.24.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FeedEdit outperforms all six baselines on every metric in both single- and multi-function settings. The strongest competitor is SDPInV [20], which leads in CLIP-I (0.8496 single) and LPIPS (0.1525 single) among baselines, but FeedEdit still surpasses it by 2.6 points in CLIP-T and 21.7 in FID for single-function, and by 18.0 points in FID for multi-function. InfEdit [42] is the closest baseline in multi-function editability (CLIP-T 0.2367 vs. 0.2607) and the only baseline with a positive ImageReward in multi-function (0.2318), yet FeedEdit nearly triples its IR score (0.6507). IP2P [4] and DAC [35] occupy the middle tier, with IP2P slightly ahead in editability but DAC trailing in FID (105–129). Imagic [17] and MasaCtrl [5] are the weakest baselines, with Imagic showing the largest FID gap (122.60 single, 138.96 multi) and negative ImageReward scores, indicating visibly degraded output quality. The gap widens substantially in multi-function editing, where baselines like DAC and Imagic fail to execute sub-functions (e.g., posture change) or introduce unwanted modifications, while FeedEdit's phrase-level regulation maintains function-specific control.

## Relation to Prior Work

FeedEdit builds on the P2P cross-attention editing/reconstruction branch framework and the classifier-free guidance (CFG) mechanism that underpin most diffusion-based text editing methods, but departs from the universal assumption of a fixed guidance scale across all denoising steps. By importing the proportional-integral (PI) controller from classical control theory, the paper reframes the editing intensity as a dynamically regulated signal rather than a static hyperparameter, and introduces frequency-domain filtering to make the perceived editing degree robust to denoising noise. The phrase-level regulation strategy further extends this by decomposing the control signal per part-of-speech, addressing the multi-function editing gap that prior fixed-intensity methods (IP2P, DAC, InfEdit, SDPInV) leave unaddressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As a training-free, plug-and-play module, FeedEdit offers a practical path to improve editing controllability without retraining or dataset construction, which is valuable for practitioners working with off-the-shelf diffusion models. The empirical finding that value and attention feature differences correlate tightly with editing degree (90% of ideal samples within a narrow range) provides a reusable diagnostic for future editing methods. However, the ideal editing degree thresholds (Ê) are empirically tuned on specific datasets and the evaluation is limited to SD 1.5 with P2P as the editing engine; whether the feedback framework generalizes to newer architectures (SDXL, DiT-based models) or to more complex multi-object editing remains open. The reliance on POS-based phrase segmentation also constrains applicability to prompts with clear grammatical structure.
