## Problem

Existing MLLM evaluation benchmarks focus on general video comprehension or static image compositionality, leaving a critical gap in assessing how well models understand the nuanced compositional structure of compiled videos—films, TV series, animations, and documentaries where multiple clips are edited together. This paper asks whether current MLLMs can perform shot-by-shot cinematic analysis, interpreting how camera movements, shot sizes, narrative structures, and character dynamics combine across edited sequences, a skill essential in film analysis and video production but largely unmeasured by prior benchmarks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250254/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance pairs a compiled video segment (average ~794 frames, audio removed to prevent speech-based shortcuts) with a four-option multiple-choice question targeting one of 15 compositional sub-tasks. The model receives the question and options via a standardized prompt template and must output a single letter (A–D); an Answer-Matching function handles multi-character outputs, and a random selection is used when no valid match is found. Scores are computed as per-sub-task accuracy (mean of binary correct/incorrect) and an overall accuracy across all 1,706 questions, with a human baseline of 86.26% and a random baseline of 25.33%.

## Method

VIDCOMPOSITION is a human-annotated benchmark of 982 compiled videos and 1,706 multiple-choice questions spanning five categories (Cinematography Analysis, Character Understanding, Narrative Understanding, Scene Perception, Making Analysis) and 15 sub-tasks. Videos are sourced from commentary clips (avoiding copyright issues), segmented using subtitle timestamps, and filtered for inappropriate content; audio is stripped to force visual-only reasoning. Annotation involves multiple annotators per segment with task-specific protocols (e.g., predefined label sets for camera movement, script-derived options for narrative matching, shuffled sequence numbers for plot ordering), followed by iterative multi-round quality control via a custom review interface. The evaluation protocol benchmarks 33 MLLMs (27 open-source, 6 proprietary) under standardized prompts and default hyperparameters, then performs a diagnostic ablation across four factors—input frame count, visual encoder resolution, language decoder size, and SFT data volume—revealing that resolution and decoder size matter most, while additional frames yield no consistent gain.

## Results

On VIDCOMPOSITION (982 compiled videos, 1,706 MCQs across 15 sub-tasks), humans achieve 86.26% overall accuracy, while the best MLLM, LLaVA-OneVision-72B, reaches only 63.31%, followed by InternVL2-40B (60.73%) and InternVL2-76B (58.73%). The mean accuracy across all 33 evaluated MLLMs is 43.44%, well above the random baseline of 25.33% but revealing a ~23-point gap to human performance. Models perform best on Character Understanding (e.g., 90.0% on Action Perception) but struggle most with Cinematography Analysis (top: 66.3% on Camera Angle) and counting tasks (e.g., 12.0% on Scene Counting for LLaVA-OneVision-72B).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The benchmark evaluates 33 MLLMs (27 open-source, 6 proprietary) against a human baseline of 86.26%. Open-source models with strong vision encoders dominate: LLaVA-OneVision-72B (63.31%) and InternVL2-40B (60.73%) outperform all API-based models, including GPT-4o (52.93%), GPT-4o mini (50.23%), and Gemini-1.5-Flash (52.40%) by 10–13 points. Among proprietary models, Gemini-1.5-Pro (58.56%) is the strongest, benefiting from high scores on Making Analysis (74.4% avg) and Narrative Understanding (72.3% avg), while GPT-4-turbo (39.85%) trails significantly. All models show a consistent weakness in Cinematography Analysis (best category avg: 61.3% for LLaVA-OneVision-72B) and counting sub-tasks (Scene Counting drops to 12.0% for LLaVA-OneVision-72B), whereas Character Understanding is the easiest category (best: 79.5%). The ~23-point gap between the best model and humans, combined with the fact that even the top model scores below 60% on multiple sub-tasks, confirms that fine-grained video composition understanding remains an open challenge for current MLLMs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a new evaluation niche by identifying that existing video benchmarks (MVBench, Video-MME, NExT-QA, TVQA) assess general video comprehension but lack fine-grained, cinematic-level compositional analysis of *compiled* (multi-clip, edited) videos. It departs from image-based compositionality benchmarks (Winoground, MMComposition) by extending the compositional reasoning requirement to the temporal and multi-shot domain, and from traditional video QA by demanding shot-by-shot understanding of camera movement, shot size, narrative structure, and production techniques. The benchmark is positioned as the first to combine compiled-video content with human-annotated, multi-task cinematic annotations, filling a gap the authors document systematically in Table 1.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ~23-point gap between human (86.26) and best-model (63.31) accuracy, particularly the severe weakness on cinematography tasks (camera movement, shot size, angle), reveals that current MLLMs have not internalized the technical grammar of filmmaking despite exposure to massive video-text corpora. The counterintuitive finding that increasing input frames from 8 to 128 does not improve (and slightly degrades) performance challenges the assumption that more temporal context is always beneficial, suggesting models struggle to extract sparse compositional signals from redundant frames. Open questions include whether the gap can be closed with targeted training data on cinematic terminology, whether the benchmark can serve as an automatic evaluator for AI-generated video quality, and how to design architectures that explicitly model shot boundaries and camera geometry.
