## Problem

Standard Plug-and-Play (PnP) algorithms rely on denoising networks as implicit priors, but at convergence the denoiser is applied to smooth images rather than its training distribution of Gaussian-corrupted inputs, causing a distribution mismatch that degrades reconstruction quality. Extending this framework to general restoration models (super-resolution, deblurring, inpainting) is non-trivial: naively substituting a restoration network for a denoiser fails because smooth images are not fixed points of the restoration operator alone, and no explicit prior formula existed for such models.

## Input / Output

The method takes a linear measurement y = Ax + e, the forward operator A, and one or more pretrained restoration models each paired with its training-time degradation operator (e.g., a Gaussian blur kernel and noise level for a deblurring model, a downsampling operator for a super-resolution model). At each iteration, the current iterate x_k is first degraded by a sampled degradation (H_k x_k + w_k) before being passed to the restoration model, ensuring the model sees inputs matching its training distribution. The output is a restored image x, and the framework supports combining N restoration models in parallel with per-model step-size weights γ_n summing to 1.

## Method

The key insight is that natural images are fixed points of the composition T = R∘D between a restoration model R and its training degradation D, and under a prox-regularity assumption on the fixed-point set C, the operator T equals the projection onto C, yielding the explicit gradient relation ∇d_C²(x)/2 = x − R(D(x)). This defines an implicit prior p(x) ∝ exp(−½ E_ξ[d_{C_ξ}²(x)]) whose gradient is computed by applying each restoration model to a degraded copy of the current iterate. The FiRe-HQS algorithm alternates between a stochastic gradient step (averaging over sampled degradation-restoration pairs) and a proximal data-fidelity step, generalizing the Half-Quadratic Splitting scheme. Unlike DRP/ShaRP, the gradient does not contain an H^⊤H factor, so the prior information is not confined to ker(H)^⊥, and the framework naturally accommodates ensembling multiple models and conditioning one prior on the actual measurement operator A.

## Results

On ImageNet-100, FiRe-HQS (using an ensemble of SCUNet denoising, SwinIR×2 SR, and Gaussian Restormer) achieves 25.80 dB PSNR on Gaussian blur, 30.49 dB on motion blur, and 23.92 dB on SR×4, outperforming DRP (25.48/25.38/23.23), DPIR (25.18/30.39/23.60), and DiffPIR (25.32/29.70/23.89) across all three tasks. On BSD20, the gains are even more pronounced for motion blur (31.67 dB vs. 26.05 for DRP and 30.95 for DPIR), though DiffPIR retains a slight edge on SR×4 (26.18 vs. 25.15 dB). FiRe-HQS also achieves the best SSIM (0.91) and lowest LPIPS (0.10) on motion blur for both datasets, indicating substantially better perceptual quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FiRe-HQS decisively outperforms DRP [10] on all three tasks and both datasets, with the largest margin on motion blur (30.49 vs. 25.38 dB on ImageNet-100; 31.67 vs. 26.05 dB on BSD20), a gap of over 5 dB attributable to DRP's H^T H term confining prior information to ker(H)^⊥. Against DPIR [39] (classical PnP with DRUNet), FiRe-HQS wins on Gaussian blur and SR×4 for both datasets and matches or slightly exceeds it on motion blur (30.49 vs. 30.39 on ImageNet-100). Against DiffPIR [41] (diffusion-based), FiRe-HQS wins on Gaussian blur and motion blur but loses on BSD20 SR×4 (25.15 vs. 26.18 dB), suggesting diffusion priors still hold an advantage for high-magnification super-resolution. In Table 2 (Set3C), FiRe with a single SCUNet JPEG prior reaches 27.19 dB on Gaussian blur and 31.23 dB on motion blur, surpassing both the PnP-DRUNet baseline (25.93/27.95) and ShaRP [11] with the same backbone (27.14/28.12), while requiring only 30 iterations versus ShaRP's 200.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Plug-and-Play (PnP) framework by replacing the denoiser-as-prior paradigm with a fixed-point-of-restoration perspective. While DRP and ShaRP were the first to incorporate non-denoising restoration models as implicit priors, they derived a prior whose score involves an H^T H term that confines learned information to ker(H)^⊥. FiRe instead builds on fixed-point projection arguments (following RED-PRO) to show that smooth images are fixed points of the composition R∘D (restoration model composed with its training degradation), yielding a prior based on distance to fixed-point sets that avoids this kernel restriction. The HQS iteration structure is inherited from DPIR, but the denoising step is replaced by a degradation-restoration composition, and the theoretical analysis draws on prox-regular set theory rather than the Lipschitz-based convergence proofs used in prior PnP work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FiRe provides a unified, theoretically grounded recipe for plugging any pretrained restoration model (denoisers, super-resolution, deblurring, inpainting) into inverse-problem solvers, with the practical benefit of ensemble combinations and acquisition-informed conditioning that prior PnP variants lack. The significant performance gap on motion deblurring (e.g., 30.49 vs. 25.38 dB PSNR on ImageNet-100) suggests the H^T H limitation in DRP/ShaRP is a real bottleneck that the fixed-point formulation resolves. A key open question is the sensitivity to hyperparameter tuning (the γ_n weights), which the authors acknowledge can significantly impact performance; adaptive or learned parameter selection is flagged as future work. The framework also raises the question of whether the prox-regularity assumption (Assumption 3.1) holds broadly enough for all practical restoration architectures.
