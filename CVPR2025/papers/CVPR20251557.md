## Problem

Pulsed lasers from lidars, smartphone ToF sensors, and light-based communication systems emit MHz-to-GHz light that is generally treated as noise or interference by nearby cameras. No prior method could passively detect, synchronize with, and exploit these unknown, asynchronous, out-of-sight laser sources for 3D imaging, because conventional ToF requires hardware-level synchronization and direct control over the light source.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251557/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes raw, asynchronously timestamped photon detections from a free-running SPAD camera (no hardware sync, no prior knowledge of laser count, positions, or frequencies) collected over a sub-second exposure, with the only requirement that the camera and lasers remain stationary during capture. It jointly outputs a per-pixel 3D depth map, the 3D positions of each detected laser, their pulse repetition frequencies (resolved to mHz precision), per-pixel pulse-delay maps, and camera-to-laser clock offsets. Notably, the lasers need not be in the camera's field of view, and the method operates under signal-to-background ratios as low as ~1%.

## Method

The key insight is that scattered light from a pulsed laser produces a distinctive sinc comb in the temporal frequency domain—non-zero only at integer multiples of the laser's repetition frequency—that is invariant to scene geometry and multi-path transport. The detection pipeline identifies candidate frequencies via CFAR scanning, refines them through high-resolution localization, prunes false candidates by verifying second-harmonic presence, and achieves mHz frequency precision by recursively hopping to the highest detectable harmonic. Pulse trains are then reconstructed via harmonic probing (estimating Fourier coefficients at all harmonics up to the SPAD band limit), and a novel time-domain CFAR criterion confirms each candidate by testing whether the reconstructed train is synchronous with actual photon detections. SNR is boosted by aggregating timestamps over pixel neighborhoods, and a bundle-adjustment-style geometric optimization (using Barron's adaptive robust loss) recovers 3D laser positions, scene depth, and clock offsets from the resulting pulse-delay maps.

## Results

On 3D-printed staircase calibration targets with known step sizes (10 cm down to 2 mm), the method achieves 2.3 mm average RMSE depth error, outperforming the Azure Kinect (4.9 mm). Reducing the signal-to-background ratio to 0.01 has minimal impact (2.5 mm average RMSE). In a room-scale scene (4.53 m × 2.41 m × 2.84 m), wall plane fitting yields RMSE of 3.4 mm and 3.6 mm with 93.2% inlier ratios, and the inter-wall angle is recovered as 90.42°. Passive frequency estimation of a 10 MHz laser achieves mHz-level precision (≤1 mHz at high SBR, ≤10 mHz at low SBR), and pulse reconstruction yields 110 ps FWHM versus 120 ps for the UWB baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against ultra-wideband (UWB) imaging [85], the proposed method produces sharper reconstructed pulses (110 ps vs. 120 ps FWHM) with higher-intensity peaks and fewer ringing artifacts at high SBR (0.50). Critically, at low SBR (0.01), the proposed method remains almost unaffected while UWB fails entirely to recover pulse profiles. UWB also cannot separate the contributions of individual asynchronous laser sources or computationally synchronize to them, a capability unique to the proposed sinc-comb detection approach. Against the Azure Kinect depth sensor [14], the method achieves 2.3 mm average RMSE on staircase calibration targets versus 4.9 mm for the Kinect, more than a 2× improvement in depth accuracy. The method also demonstrates robustness to dynamic acquisition: across multiple frames with independently moving camera and laser sources, sequential ICP registration of point clouds yields only 1.1 cm mean RMSE, and the inter-wall angle is consistently recovered within ~1°.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the ultra-wideband (UWB) single-photon imaging framework of Wei et al. [85], which established passive flux probing with SPAD cameras, but departs from it by exploiting the sinc comb spectral structure of pulsed lasers rather than densely scanning the full DC-to-GHz band. It also draws conceptual inspiration from passive radar, sonar, and Wi-Fi imaging, which harvest ambient signals for sensing, but applies these principles for the first time to incoherent optical time-of-flight. A key departure from the broader single-photon imaging literature (lidar, non-line-of-sight, biomedical) is the relinquishment of hardware synchronization and laser control, replacing them with a purely computational sync-lock that can be applied post-capture to any number of asynchronous sources.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that scattered light from unknown, out-of-view pulsed lasers carries enough structured information to enable passive 3D imaging with mm-scale accuracy, even at signal-to-background ratios as low as 0.01, opening a new modality for 3D sensing that requires no emitted light. However, practical deployment is constrained by the current absence of mature 2D SPAD cameras, the requirement for stationary scenes during sub-second exposures, and the limitation to simple pulsed (not beam-scanning or modulated) laser sources. Open questions include extending the approach to the far more complex signal structures of real-world beam-scanning lidars and visible-light communication systems, and whether the method generalizes to dynamic scenes or moving sources.
