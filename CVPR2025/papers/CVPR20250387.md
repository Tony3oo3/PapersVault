## Problem

Neural fluid simulators face a fundamental tension: CNNs and GNNs capture local interactions efficiently but cannot model the long-range dependencies critical for phenomena like shockwaves and turbulence, while Transformer-based solvers provide global context at O(N²) self-attention cost that becomes prohibitive at high resolutions. Moreover, existing methods treat all spatial regions uniformly, wasting computation on areas with simple dynamics while under-resolving complex regions.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a 2D grid of physical fields (velocity components u,v, pressure p, density ρ, viscosity ν, and case-specific global parameters) at time t, with resolutions ranging from 64×64 (CFDBench) to 128×128 (Shockwave) and up to 1024×1024 for visualization. The structured grid is first converted by the AMR tokenizer into a variable-length sequence of multi-scale patches (each containing k×k cells plus positional encodings), and the Transformer outputs patches at t+Δt that are mapped back onto the original grid for evaluation. The shockwave dataset uniquely requires predicting all four fields (u, v, p, ρ) simultaneously, unlike CFDBench which predicts only velocity.

## Method

AMR-Transformer combines a hierarchical quadtree-based Adaptive Mesh Refinement tokenizer with an encoder-only Transformer (6 layers, 4 heads, hidden dim 256) to achieve efficient long-range interaction modeling. The tokenizer partitions the input domain into variable-sized patches using a Navier-Stokes constraint-aware pruning module that evaluates four physical criteria per cell—velocity gradient, vorticity, momentum, and Kelvin-Helmholtz shear instability—subdividing cells that exceed threshold values while storing others as compact tokens, reducing token count by 2–10×. During training, threshold hyperparameters are randomly sampled per iteration, enabling post-training manual tuning of the accuracy-efficiency trade-off. A virtual velocity field estimated via forward Euler from the current and previous time steps provides additional refinement cues, and the union of regions flagged by either the current or virtual field determines the final subdivision set. The Transformer then applies self-attention over the variable-length patch sequence to predict the next time step, with NMSE loss computed against AMR-tokenized ground truth labels.

## Results

On the seven benchmark problems (CFDBench, PDEBench, and the new Shockwave dataset), AMR-Transformer achieves the best or near-best performance across NMSE, MAE, and MSE. Headline improvements include a ~91% MSE reduction on Dam (1.63E-4 vs. DeepONet's 1.64E-3) and an ~83% NMSE reduction on Shockwave (9.32E-4 vs. FNO's 5.53E-3). The AMR tokenizer reduces token counts by 2–10×, translating to up to 60× FLOPs reduction compared to a regular-grid ViT (e.g., 7.51 GFLOPs vs. 71.37 GFLOPs on Shockwave). On the 1024×1024 high-resolution shock/explosion visualization, the tokenizer cuts tokens 4–10× while reducing MSE by 6–1000× over a 512×512 regular grid.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AMR-Transformer clearly dominates on the two most challenging problems: Dam (91% MSE improvement over DeepONet, the best baseline) and Shockwave (83% NMSE improvement over FNO), where long-range dependencies and sharp discontinuities are critical. On Cylinder, Cavity, and Tube, the method wins on MAE and MSE but trails FNO or U-Net slightly on NMSE, suggesting the advantage is most pronounced in absolute error rather than relative normalization. On Diffusion-Reaction, AMR-Transformer essentially ties FNO (NMSE 8.17E-1 vs. 8.14E-1), while both U-Net and DeepONet fail catastrophically (NMSE > 30). On NS-Incom-Inhom, AMR-Transformer edges out DeepONet by ~36% on MSE (3.76E-6 vs. 5.88E-6). FNO is the strongest single baseline overall, but it degrades severely on Dam (NMSE 6.36E-2, 12.4× worse than Identity) and NS-Incom-Inhom (NMSE 1.00), indicating it cannot capture the underlying dynamics in those complex scenarios. DeepONet, as an implicit neural representation, struggles with high-gradient problems (Cylinder, Dam) and performs near Identity level, confirming its limited generalization across physical instances.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes Adaptive Mesh Refinement (AMR), a classical numerical CFD technique for allocating grid resolution to regions of interest, as a neural network tokenizer that converts a structured grid into a variable-size patch sequence for an encoder-only Transformer. It departs from prior Transformer-based PDE solvers (which treat all patches uniformly, as in ViT) and from multi-scale graph approaches (MultiScale MeshGraphNet, Multi-Grid Neural Operators) by making the tokenization itself physics-informed through Navier-Stokes constraint criteria (velocity gradient, vorticity, momentum, Kelvin-Helmholtz instability). The conceptual bridge between classical adaptive numerical methods and modern attention-based architectures is the paper's core positioning, though each component (quadtree AMR, self-attention, physics-informed loss) is individually well-established.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The pipeline addresses a practical bottleneck in neural fluid simulation: the O(N²) self-attention cost becomes prohibitive at high resolutions, and the AMR tokenizer's 2–10× token reduction translates into up to 60× FLOPs savings over a ViT-style baseline while preserving or improving accuracy. The newly introduced 128×128 shockwave dataset (4× CFDBench resolution) with compressible flow and sharp discontinuities fills a gap in existing benchmarks that are dominated by low-resolution incompressible problems. Open questions include whether the physics-informed pruning criteria generalize beyond Navier-Stokes to other PDE families, whether the simple forward-Euler virtual velocity estimation (Eq. 11) remains adequate at larger time steps, and how the approach scales to 3D simulations where octree tokenization would multiply the combinatorial complexity.
