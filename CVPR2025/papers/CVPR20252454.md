## Problem

Existing physics-based human-scene interaction controllers are either single-task specialists or unified models restricted to static contact tasks (sitting, touching) without manipulation capabilities like carrying. Moreover, adapting these controllers to novel objects, terrains, or composite tasks requires inefficient full fine-tuning or training from scratch, limiting their practical versatility.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252454/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The policy consumes a character proprioception state (joint angles and velocities), task-specific goal observations of varying dimensionality (e.g., 20-dim path waypoints for following, 38-dim object pose for sitting, 42-dim box state for carrying), and a one-hot task label that drives the attention mask. For terrain-adapted variants, a height map is additionally tokenized. The output is a 32-dimensional vector of joint target rotations fed to PD controllers, producing physically simulated whole-body motion. The transformer's variable-length input design means the number of active task tokens can change at inference time without architectural modification.

## Method

TokenHSI tokenizes the character proprioception into a dedicated shared token (T^prop) and each task's goal state into separate task tokens (T^f, T^s, T^m, T^c), all mapped to 64-dim features by individual MLPs. A 4-layer transformer encoder fuses these tokens via self-attention, where the one-hot task label acts as an attention mask to suppress irrelevant task tokens while the proprioception token and a learnable output embedding always participate. This shared-proprioception design enables cross-task motor knowledge transfer during multi-task PPO training with AMP adversarial motion priors. For adaptation to new tasks, the backbone (T^prop, transformer, action head) is frozen; only lightweight zero-initialized LoRA-style adapter layers on the action head and new/fine-tuned task tokenizers are trained, preserving prior skills while efficiently incorporating new observations such as height maps or composite task states.

## Results

On the four foundational HSI skills, TokenHSI's unified policy achieves 99.7% (Follow), 99.6% (Sit), 99.8% (Climb), and 92.2% (Carry) success rates, surpassing single-task specialists on Carry (92.2% vs. 83.1%) while matching or exceeding them on the other three. On the most challenging skill composition task (Climb + Carry), TokenHSI reaches 99.2% success with 3.5 cm error, compared to 68.3% for CML and 51.3% for CML (dual). For terrain shape variation, TokenHSI achieves 96.0% (Follow) and 74.0% (Carry) success, outperforming AdaptNet (92.4% and 63.4%) and Scratch (93.4% and 0%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CML [106] (Composite Motion Learning), TokenHSI achieves a decisive win on the hardest composition task Climb+Carry (99.2% vs. 68.3% success, a 31-point gap) and a clear advantage on Follow+Carry (99.1% vs. 97.6%), while CML edges ahead by 0.4 points on Sit+Carry (96.7% vs. 96.3%). The CML (dual) variant, which coordinates two pretrained policies, still trails TokenHSI by 48 points on Climb+Carry (51.3% vs. 99.2%). Against AdaptNet [107], TokenHSI wins on all four adaptation benchmarks: object shape variation (88.8% vs. 84.5% on Chair; 83.6% vs. 82.4% on Table) and terrain shape variation (96.0% vs. 92.4% on Follow; 74.0% vs. 63.4% on Carry). The paper attributes these advantages to the shared proprioception tokenizer's cross-task generalization and the transformer's ability to compose skills in latent feature space rather than in the action space as CML does.

## Relation to Prior Work

TokenHSI extends the masking-based unified controller paradigm introduced by MaskedMimic [88] and HOVER [28] from motion tracking to diverse human-scene interaction tasks, adding manipulation (carrying) alongside contact-based skills (sitting, climbing). It borrows the LoRA-style adapter mechanism [32] for efficient policy adaptation, applying it to the action head of a transformer policy rather than to language model weights. The key architectural departure from prior unified controllers (e.g., UniHSI [102]) is modeling proprioception as a separate shared token rather than a joint character-goal state, which the authors argue enables cross-task motor knowledge sharing and generalization to novel character states during adaptation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first framework to unify manipulation (carrying) with contact-based HSI skills in a single policy and then adapt to composite, shape-varied, and long-horizon tasks by training only lightweight tokenizers and adapters—avoiding full fine-tuning. The practical impact is a reusable skill library where new interaction scenarios require minimal additional training. However, the approach still depends on hand-engineered reward functions (a general RL limitation), and long-horizon task completion remains non-autonomous, relying on a manually designed FSM for skill sequencing. A fully autonomous humanoid that plans and executes complex multi-skill tasks in cluttered real-world environments without human guidance remains an open problem.
