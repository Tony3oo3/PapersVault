## Problem

Medical image segmentation models struggle to generalize across the heterogeneous landscape of anatomical structures, imaging modalities, and clinical tasks. Task-specific models cannot handle unseen classes without retraining, universal models still require fine-tuning for novel structures, and SAM-based interactive methods are impractical for high-throughput automated processing. Existing in-context learning approaches for medical images suffer from suboptimal accuracy on in-distribution tasks and computational inefficiency due to redundant context re-encoding per query image.

## Input / Output

Iris takes a 3D query volume (128×128×128) and one or more reference image-label pairs as input, producing binary segmentation masks for the query. A key design detail is that task embeddings extracted from reference pairs are compact (m+1 tokens of dimension C) and can be precomputed, stored, and reused across any number of query images without re-encoding. For multi-class segmentation, K class-specific task embeddings are concatenated and processed in a single forward pass to yield K×D×H×W masks, avoiding the per-class forward passes required by prior in-context methods.

## Method

Iris decouples task encoding from segmentation inference via two modules. The task encoding module extracts task representations through two parallel streams: (1) a foreground stream that upsamples encoder features to full resolution, multiplies by the binary mask, and pools to capture fine anatomical details; and (2) a contextual stream that uses PixelShuffle/PixelUnshuffle for memory-efficient high-resolution feature-mask fusion, followed by cross-attention with learnable query tokens. The mask decoding module (a Mask2Former-style query-based decoder) performs bidirectional cross-attention between query image features and the concatenated task embeddings, predicting all class masks in one pass. Training uses episodic sampling of reference-query pairs with Dice + cross-entropy loss and augmentation on both images. This decoupled design yields O(k+m) complexity versus O(kmn) for prior in-context methods, and supports flexible inference strategies including one-shot, context ensemble, object-level retrieval, and in-context tuning of task embeddings.

## Results

On 12 in-distribution medical segmentation datasets, Iris achieves a mean Dice of 84.52%, matching or exceeding task-specific nnUNet (83.18%) and the best multi-task universal model Multi-Talent (84.47%), while substantially outperforming all in-context learning baselines (best: Tyche-IS at 61.20%) and SAM-based methods (best: SAM-Med3D at 68.42%). On 5 held-out OOD datasets, Iris leads all adaptive methods (e.g., 86.45% on ACDC vs. 73.82% for SegGPT; 89.13% on CSI-opp vs. 87.13% for Tyche-IS). On two completely unseen anatomical classes, Iris achieves 28.28% Dice on MSD Pancreas Tumor and 69.03% on Pelvic1K Bone, far surpassing the best in-context competitor (11.97% and 61.92% respectively). Inference is 33× faster than UniverSeg (2.0 s vs. 659.4 s for 10 volumes with 15 classes).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against task-specific nnUNet (83.18% avg), Iris achieves a higher mean Dice (84.52%) while remaining fully adaptable to unseen classes without retraining. Among multi-task universal models, Iris edges out Multi-Talent (84.47%) and UniSeg (84.40%) by a narrow margin, confirming that in-context task encoding can match dedicated task heads. The gap over SAM-based methods is dramatic: SAM-Med3D reaches only 68.42% and SAM-Med2D 40.58%, reflecting the limitations of positional prompts for complex 3D anatomy. Among in-context learning baselines, Iris leads by a wide margin—Tyche-IS (61.20%), UniverSeg (58.68%), and SegGPT (57.35%) all trail by 23+ points on average, with the largest deficits on 3D-heavy tasks like AMOS CT (89.56 vs. 59.57) and LiTS (65.02 vs. 37.02). On OOD generalization (Table 2), Iris outperforms all adaptive baselines across every held-out dataset, e.g., 86.45% on ACDC vs. 73.82% (SegGPT) and 89.13% on CSI-opp vs. 87.13% (Tyche-IS). On unseen classes, Iris achieves 28.28% on MSD Pancreas and 69.03% on Pelvic1K, roughly 2–3× the best in-context competitor (11.97% and 61.92%), though still below the supervised nnUNet upper bound (54.56% and 94.73%). In terms of efficiency, Iris processes 10 volumes with 15 classes in 2.0 s versus 659.4 s for UniverSeg-1, a 33× speedup attributable to decoupled task encoding and single-pass multi-class decoding.

## Relation to Prior Work

Iris builds on the query-based decoder architecture of Mask2Former [8] and the sub-pixel convolution (PixelShuffle) technique [42] for memory-efficient high-resolution feature fusion, but its core conceptual departure is from prior in-context segmentation methods (SegGPT, UniverSeg, Tyche) by decoupling task encoding from query inference. While those methods re-encode reference examples for every query image and process one class per forward pass, Iris distills task information into compact, reusable embeddings that can be stored and applied across arbitrary numbers of queries in a single multi-class pass. The paper also positions itself against universal medical segmentation models (UniSeg, MultiTalent, CLIP-driven) that require fine-tuning for unseen classes, and against SAM-based interactive models that demand repeated user prompts, offering a fully automatic alternative.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a single reference image-label pair enables segmentation of previously unseen anatomical structures without retraining, and the 300× speedup over prior ICL methods (2 s vs. 659 s for 10 volumes × 15 classes) makes in-context segmentation viable for high-throughput clinical pipelines. The emergent anatomical clustering in task embeddings (e.g., IVC near portal/splenic veins, bladder near prostate) without explicit anatomical supervision suggests the framework could serve as a tool for discovering cross-modality structural relationships. Open questions include closing the remaining gap to supervised upper bounds on challenging OOD domains (e.g., CSI-fat at 47.78% vs. 90.13% nnUNet), scaling training-task diversity without manual annotation, and whether the decoupled encoding generalizes to even more heterogeneous imaging settings.
