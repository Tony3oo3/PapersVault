## Problem

Implicit neural representations (INRs) parameterize continuous signals with MLPs but have never achieved true lossless (bit-exact) representation of digital signals, particularly at high bit-depths like 16-bit. The paper identifies the root cause theoretically: the upper bound on required model size grows exponentially with bit precision (∝ 2^(2dn)), making lossless fitting practically infeasible for high-precision signals. No prior INR method could guarantee zero bit-error-rate across all input values.

## Input / Output

The network takes a 3D coordinate (x, y, i) where (x, y) are spatial positions normalized to [-1,1] and i is the bit-plane index (0 to n-1), and outputs a single scalar prediction for the i-th bit-plane at that location. All images are center-cropped and downsampled to 256×256. At inference, the n binary predictions are reassembled into the full n-bit signal via a weighted sum (Eq. 10), and the result is quantized to the nearest representable value. The same framework extends to audio by treating the time coordinate as the spatial axis and decomposing the floating-point signal into bit-planes.

## Method

The key insight is that decomposing an n-bit signal into n binary bit-planes reduces the effective precision from n to 1, collapsing the theoretical upper bound on required parameters from C·(2^(n+1)−2)^(2d) to just 16C. A single MLP f_θ(x, i) predicts all bit-planes by treating the bit index as an additional input coordinate, exploiting the strong correlation between bit-planes rather than using n separate networks. Training uses binary cross-entropy loss on each bit-plane, which converges faster than MSE or MAE for this binary classification framing. The paper also identifies two new phenomena: 'bit bias' (MSBs converge faster than LSBs, analogous to spectral bias) and 'bit-spectral bias' (certain frequencies along the bit axis are learned faster), both of which the decomposition approach mitigates. The method is architecture-agnostic and composes with existing INR techniques including hash encodings (Instant-NGP, DINER), periodic activations (SIREN, FINER), and Gaussian activations, achieving lossless representation in all tested configurations.

## Results

The proposed bit-plane decomposition method achieves lossless representation (PSNR = ∞, BER = 0.0000) on all 16-bit (TESTIMAGES, MIT-5k) and 8-bit (DIV2K, Kodak) image fitting tasks, converging in 3450±877 and 790±109 iterations respectively. The closest baseline, FINER, reaches only 80.17 dB PSNR (BER 0.1498) on 16-bit TESTIMAGES and 55.93 dB (BER 0.0447) on 8-bit Kodak, falling far short of lossless. The method also achieves lossless audio representation (FP32) on Librispeech and outperforms all existing lossless codecs (PNG, JPEG2000, WebP, TIFF) in compression ratio when combined with RECOMBINER.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method achieves perfect lossless representation (PSNR = ∞, BER = 0) across all four image datasets, while no baseline reaches lossless even after 5000+ iterations. FINER [22] is the strongest baseline, achieving 80.17 dB PSNR on 16-bit TESTIMAGES but still retaining a BER of 0.1498, indicating substantial residual bit errors; SIREN [37] and Gauss [32] trail further behind at 78.52 and 69.40 dB respectively. On 8-bit tasks, the gap narrows but persists: FINER reaches 55.93 dB on Kodak with BER 0.0447, while the proposed method achieves zero error in ~790 iterations. The method is also composable: when combined with hash-based encoders (Instant-NGP [26], DINER [46]) or periodic activations (Gauss [32], FINER [22]), it achieves lossless representation in fewer iterations than the standalone baselines (e.g., Instant-NGP+Ours: 1130 iters vs. 2000 for Instant-NGP alone on Kodak). For bit-depth expansion (Table 5), the method (55.92 dB, 8→16-bit) outperforms rule-based methods (ZP: 52.92, MIG: 55.91, BR: 52.98) and supervised approaches (BECNN: 53.14, BitNet: 53.60), though ABCD [12] edges it slightly at 59.39 dB. In lossless compression (Table 6), RECOMBINER+Ours achieves 2.58 bpp on MNIST, beating PNG (3.52), JPEG2000 (6.75), and TIFF (3.93), though WebP (2.11) remains slightly better on that specific dataset.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the theoretical parameter bounds for ANNs established by Jentzen et al. [16], applying them specifically to the quantized signal representation problem where prior INR work (SIREN, WIRE, FINER, Instant-NGP, DINER) focused on perceptual quality metrics rather than exact bit-level reproduction. The bit-plane decomposition strategy is borrowed from the dequantization literature (Han et al. [12]) and repurposed here as a mechanism to reduce the effective bit precision from n to 1, thereby shrinking the theoretical upper bound on required parameters. Conceptually, the paper reinterprets the well-known spectral bias through a digital lens, introducing 'bit bias' as an analogous phenomenon where INRs systematically converge on most-significant bits before least-significant bits regardless of activation function.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that lossless (bit-exact) representation of high bit-depth signals (16-bit images, 24-bit audio) via INRs was previously unachievable and provides a principled method to accomplish it, opening applications in lossless compression, self-supervised bit-depth expansion, and extreme model quantization (ternary INRs). The theoretical framework connecting parameter count to bit precision via an exponential upper bound gives practitioners a concrete target for model sizing. A key limitation is that the upper bound grows as ε^{-2d}, making high-dimensional signals (d > 5, e.g., radiance fields) still intractable, and the method's advantage diminishes as dimensionality increases. Open questions include whether the bit-plane decomposition can be made more parameter-efficient for 3D+ signals and whether the bit-spectral bias along the bit axis can be further mitigated.
