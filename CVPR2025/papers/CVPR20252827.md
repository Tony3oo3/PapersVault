## Problem

Existing deepfake detectors are designed for a 'train-on-one generator, test-on-many' setup, but scaling training to multiple generators exposes two critical failures: methods like DIRE overfit to the most obvious generator-specific artifacts and lose OOD generalization (68.4% vs. 97.6% ID), while methods like UFD underfit ID data to barter for OOD performance (86.6% ID vs. 81.4% OOD). No prior method simultaneously maintains strong ID fitting and OOD generalization under multi-generator training.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image (resized to 256×256, randomly cropped to 224×224) and produces a binary real/fake logit. Unusually, the image is consumed twice: once in its original form and once after patch-shuffling (randomly permuting spatial patches), both passing through the same frozen CLIP ViT-L/14 backbone. Training uses standard data augmentation (Gaussian blur σ∈[0,3], JPEG quality∈[30,100], each with 0.5 probability), while inference applies no augmentation.

## Method

D³ introduces a dual-branch discrepancy learning architecture: the original image and its patch-shuffled variant are each encoded by a frozen CLIP ViT-L/14 to extract penultimate-layer features, which are then concatenated and processed by a trainable self-attention layer that learns the shared (invariant) artifacts across the two views. The key insight is that patch-shuffling destroys superficial generator-specific fingerprints while preserving low-level texture information, creating an informative discrepancy that forces the model to identify universal artifacts rather than generator-specific shortcuts. A single linear classifier on the self-attention output produces the final prediction, with only the self-attention and classification layers being trainable (the backbone remains frozen). This design is trained with binary cross-entropy loss for 5 epochs on a single RTX 4090, achieving 90.7% overall accuracy and 5.3% OOD improvement over the prior SOTA (UFD) while maintaining 96.6% ID accuracy.

## Results

On the scaled-up setup (8 training generators, 12 unseen OOD generators), D³ achieves the best overall mean accuracy of 90.7% and overall AP of 96.8%, surpassing the second-best method Patchfor by 4.2% in mean accuracy and 2.6% in AP. In the critical OOD setting, D³ reaches 86.7% mean accuracy and 94.3% AP, outperforming the previous SOTA UFD by 5.3% in OOD mean accuracy while maintaining competitive ID performance (96.6% vs. Patchfor's 97.9%). The method also demonstrates superior robustness to Gaussian blur and JPEG compression, maintaining high accuracy under heavy degradations where other methods degrade sharply.

## Baselines & Comparisons

D³ outperforms all five baselines on OOD mean accuracy (86.7%), with the largest margin over DIRE (68.4%, +18.3%) and CNNDet (69.9%, +16.8%), and a 5.3% gain over UFD (81.4%), which shares the same CLIP ViT-L/14 backbone. On ID mean accuracy, D³ (96.6%) is competitive with Patchfor (97.9%) and DIRE (97.6%) but trails them by 1.3–1.4%, while substantially outperforming UFD (86.6%) by 10% and LNP (88.1%) by 8.5%. In overall (Total) mean accuracy, D³ leads all methods at 90.7%, exceeding Patchfor (86.5%) by 4.2% and UFD (83.5%) by 7.2%. The key trade-off revealed is that DIRE and Patchfor achieve strong ID fitting but poor OOD generalization, while UFD generalizes well but underfits ID data; D³ resolves this trade-off by maintaining near-best ID performance while achieving the best OOD and overall scores. In robustness to post-processing (Gaussian blur, JPEG compression), D³ shows the smallest accuracy decay, outperforming all baselines under heavy degradation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the pretrained-based deepfake detection paradigm initiated by UFD (CVPR'23) and [10], which leverage frozen CLIP features with simple classifiers. The key conceptual departure is the introduction of a dual-branch discrepancy mechanism: rather than classifying from a single CLIP embedding, the model compares features from an original image and its patch-shuffled variant via a self-attention layer, explicitly learning what is invariant across the two views. Additionally, the paper redefines the evaluation protocol from the standard 'train-on-one, test-on-many' to 'train-on-many, test-on-many,' arguing the former is impractical and misleads the community about true generalization. The method thus repurposes the UFD backbone and linear-probing philosophy but augments it with a structured invariance signal that prior single-branch approaches lack.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The multi-generator training setup is likely to become the de facto standard for evaluating deepfake detectors as the generative model landscape continues to expand rapidly, making this paper's empirical findings about method divergence (e.g., Patchfor overtaking CNNDet, UFD underfitting) practically important for the community. The 5.3% OOD improvement while preserving ID performance demonstrates that a simple discrepancy signal can unlock better universal artifact learning without the complexity of reconstruction-based or generator-specific approaches. Key limitations include the doubled inference cost from the two-branch design and the reliance on a merged dataset of 20 generators that may not capture future generator diversity. Open questions include whether the discrepancy framework extends to video deepfakes, whether stronger corruptions or learned augmentations could further improve performance, and whether the approach generalizes to attribution (identifying which generator produced a fake).
