## Problem

Existing NIR-assisted RGB denoising methods struggle to balance the contributions of two spectrally inconsistent fields: they either indiscriminately fuse cross-field features, relying on network fitting to handle spatial mismatches, or only reinforce common features while discarding critical differential information such as sharp NIR textures and RGB color. This leads to suboptimal detail recovery, color distortion, or residual artifacts under high noise.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251943/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The network takes a noisy RGB image and its spatially aligned NIR counterpart as input and outputs a single denoised RGB image. During training, images are cropped into 128×128 patches (DVD dataset uses 256×256 crops for evaluation), and noise is simulated as Gaussian-Poisson mixed noise (DVD) or additive Gaussian noise at σ=25/50/75 (IVRG). The architecture operates at multiple scales via a two-stage U-Net, with the first stage pre-denoising the RGB input to reduce the modality gap before multi-scale cross-field fusion in the second stage.

## Method

FCENet is built on a key empirical observation: the similarity between noisy RGB and clean RGB decreases from low to high frequency, while the similarity between NIR and clean RGB increases in the same direction, establishing a cross-field frequency correlation prior. The Frequency Dynamic Selection Mechanism (FDSM) leverages this prior via input-conditioned dynamic convolution in the frequency domain, generating per-channel filters that selectively retain low-frequency color from RGB and high-frequency texture from NIR. The Frequency Exhaustive Fusion Mechanism (FEFM) then performs thorough fusion through two sub-modules: a Common Feature Reinforcement (CFR) block that models both point-wise and long-range frequency-domain correlations between NIR and RGB features, and a Differential Feature Reinforcement (DFR) block that uses differential cross-attention to explicitly preserve high-frequency texture unique to NIR. The full network is trained end-to-end with a combined Charbonnier spatial loss and a frequency-domain loss (weight 0.1), using Adam with cosine annealing over 80–100 epochs.

## Results

On the DVD dataset (σ=4), FCENet achieves 30.37 dB PSNR and 0.950 SSIM, outperforming the strongest NIR-assisted baseline NIR-Restormer (29.81 dB / 0.942) by 0.56 dB while using only 19.52 G FLOPs versus 45.31 G. The lightweight variant FCENet-L (1.16 M params, 5.71 G FLOPs) still reaches 29.91 dB / 0.946, surpassing all single-image denoisers. On the IVRG dataset (σ=50), FCENet attains 29.08 dB PSNR and 0.866 SSIM, leading NIR-Restormer (28.77 / 0.853) by 0.31 dB.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FCENet outperforms all nine baselines on both DVD and IVRG across every noise level. Among NIR-assisted methods, the closest competitor is NIR-Restormer [43], which FCENet surpasses by 0.56 dB PSNR at σ=4 on DVD (30.37 vs. 29.81) while using less than half the FLOPs (19.52 G vs. 45.31 G) and roughly 7× fewer parameters (4.44 M vs. 30.26 M). DVN [18] and MNNet [44] trail by 0.75 dB and 2.11 dB respectively at σ=4, with the paper attributing their gaps to insufficient handling of cross-field inconsistencies and loss of differential high-frequency textures. Among single-image denoisers, MPRNet [47] is the strongest (29.37 dB at σ=4) but still lags FCENet by 1.00 dB and requires 133.51 G FLOPs. The lightweight FCENet-L (1.16 M params) already exceeds all single-image basers and even matches NIR-Restormer's PSNR at σ=2 (31.87 vs. 31.81), demonstrating that the frequency-domain design yields strong performance at very low computational cost. On IVRG (σ=75), FCENet leads NIR-Restormer by 0.18 dB (25.48 vs. 25.30) and DVN by 0.20 dB, with the margin widening at lower noise levels.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the NIR-assisted image denoising line (Yan et al. 2013, CUNet, DVN, NIR-Restormer) by reframing cross-field fusion as a frequency-domain adaptive selection problem, grounded in an empirical correlation prior: noisy RGB loses structural similarity to clean RGB at high frequencies while NIR gains it. The Frequency Dynamic Selection Mechanism adapts the dynamic convolution paradigm of Chen et al. (CVPR 2020) and Tatsunami et al. (AAAI 2024) to the frequency domain, while the Frequency Exhaustive Fusion Mechanism builds on frequency-domain self-attention (Kong et al., CVPR 2023) but augments it with a differential cross-attention branch to preserve NIR-specific high-frequency textures that standard cross-attention would blur.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The frequency correlation prior provides a principled, data-driven justification for *where* to take information from each modality, moving beyond ad-hoc spatial fusion heuristics used in prior NIR-assisted methods. The practical efficiency is notable: FCENet-L (1.16 M params, 5.71 G FLOPs) outperforms NIR-Restormer (30.26 M params, 45.31 G FLOPs) by ~0.5 dB PSNR, making NIR-assisted denoising viable on resource-constrained devices. A key open limitation acknowledged by the authors is failure under significant cross-field misalignment, pointing toward future work with deformable convolutions or feature-point matching. Whether the frequency correlation prior generalizes beyond the two tested datasets (DVD, IVRG) and to other sensor pairs remains to be validated.
