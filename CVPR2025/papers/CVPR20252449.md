## Problem

Existing security defenses for image generation models focus almost exclusively on the language modality (prompt-based jailbreaks), leaving the vision modality of Image-to-Image tasks unexplored as an attack surface. This paper asks whether typographic text embedded in input images can manipulate the semantic direction of generated outputs in CLIP-guided diffusion models, and whether current guarding methods can detect such vision-modality threats.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252449/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes 1024×1024 facial images from CelebA-HQ with typographic text overlaid (controllable in word type, size 10–25pt, quantity 1–4, opacity 25–100%, and position across four vertical regions) paired with a fixed text prompt, and produces a generated image. Evaluation uses CLIP Score to measure semantic alignment between the generated image and the inserted typos (higher = stronger typo influence) and FID to quantify distributional deviation from clean-image generations. The VMT-IGMs dataset comprises 37,000 samples split into Factor Modification (30,000) and Malicious Threat (7,000, with visible and invisible subtypes).

## Method

The attack mechanism exploits the CLIP vision encoder in CLIP-guided diffusion models: typographic text added to the input image is extracted as part of the vision-language embedding f = CLIP(x, p), injecting unintended semantics into the DDPM reverse denoising process. The authors systematically vary five typographic factors (word type, size, quantity, opacity, position) to characterize attack strength, then extend to malicious content (harmful, bias, neutral) and an invisible variant where near-black text (RGB 15,15,15) is hidden in black borders (RGB 0,0,0). Experiments span UnCLIP/DALL-E 2 and IP-Adapter with SD1.5, SDXL, and FLUX. Five pre-generation defenses (Text Blacklist, Detoxify, CLIP Score, LLM-based, Latent Guard) and one post-generation defense (safety checker) are all shown to fail because they inspect only the text prompt or NSFW content, not the vision input. Ablations confirm that adding 'ignore text' to the prompt does not mitigate the effect, that VAE-based SD3 is less susceptible, and that MLLM-based Emu2 remains vulnerable.

## Results

Across all four CLIP-guided diffusion models (UnCLIP, SD1.5, SDXL, FLUX), visible typographic text in input images consistently increases CLIP Score alignment with the inserted typo, confirming that the vision modality is a viable attack surface. For visible typography, the average CLIP Score increase ranges from +2.21 (Muslim) to +5.32 (hat) across content categories, with UnCLIP showing the largest per-model gains (e.g., +6.40 for 'hat') and FLUX the smallest (e.g., +1.69 for 'naked'). Even when typography is rendered nearly invisible (RGB 15,15,15 on black borders), a persistent but reduced effect remains (avg +0.55 to +1.18). The safety checker detects only 16.6% of 'naked'-induced harmful outputs on average and as low as 1.9% for neutral 'hat' shifts, while all five pre-generation prompt defenses (Text Blacklist, Detoxify, CLIP Score, LLM, Latent Guard) completely fail to flag the benign prompt 'analog film photo, faded film' that accompanies the typographic attack.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All four evaluated CLIP-guided diffusion models (UnCLIP/DALL-E 2, SD1.5, SDXL, FLUX via IP-Adapter) are susceptible to typographic attacks, but UnCLIP is the most vulnerable, showing the largest average CLIP Score increases across most categories (e.g., +6.40 for 'hat', +5.12 for 'African'), while FLUX is the most robust yet still affected (e.g., +4.98 for 'hat', +4.31 for 'bloody'). SD1.5 and SDXL fall in between, with SD1.5 showing the highest 'naked' increase (+3.65) and SDXL the highest 'bloody' increase (+3.55). On the defense side, all five pre-generation prompt-detection methods (Text Blacklist, Detoxify, CLIP Score, LLM-based, Latent Guard) fail completely on the benign prompt used in the typographic attack scenario, confirming they cannot protect against vision-modality threats. The post-generation safety checker achieves only 16.6% average detection for 'naked' (best case: UnCLIP at 23.7%) and drops to 1.9% for 'hat', indicating it is narrowly scoped to NSFW content and ineffective against bias or neutral semantic shifts. The 'ignore text' prompt-modification defense also proves ineffective, with CLIP Score increases remaining comparable to the unmodified prompt, and VAE-based SD3 shows reduced (but not eliminated) sensitivity, while MLLM-based Emu2 remains vulnerable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the typographic attack paradigm previously identified in CLIP-based classification and MLLM understanding tasks (Azuma & Matsui, ICCV-W 2023; Cheng et al., ECCV 2024) to the image generation domain, specifically CLIP-guided diffusion models performing Image-to-Image tasks. While prior work on image generation security (jailbreaks, adversarial attacks, backdoors) has focused almost exclusively on the language modality as the attack vector, this paper repurposes the typographic vulnerability to demonstrate that the vision modality input channel is equally exploitable. It positions itself as a gap-filling study: existing defense methods (Detoxify, Latent Guard, safety checkers, LLM-based prompt screening) were designed for text-based threats and are shown to be structurally blind to vision-embedded typographic manipulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals a practically significant blind spot: any deployment of CLIP-guided I2I models (DALL-E 2, IP-Adapter with SD/FLUX) is vulnerable to semantic manipulation through text embedded in input images, with no current defense mechanism providing meaningful protection. The finding that invisible typography (near-black text in black borders) still shifts generation semantics, albeit more subtly, raises concerns about undetectable manipulation in real-world pipelines. The VMT-IGMs benchmark provides the first systematic evaluation framework for this threat, but open questions remain: whether vision-modality-specific defenses can be designed, whether the effect generalizes beyond CLIP-based architectures (VAE-based SD3 shows reduced sensitivity), and how to handle the tension between legitimate text-in-image content and malicious typographic injection.
