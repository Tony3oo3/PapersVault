## Problem

Existing movie dubbing (Visual Voice Cloning) methods suffer from two key deficiencies: they cannot simultaneously maintain accurate lip-sync and clear pronunciation, and they lack the ability to express user-specified emotions with controllable intensity. Most prior approaches rely on simple MSE-based losses or overall scaling for alignment, and treat emotional expression as a fixed property of the reference speaker rather than a user-controllable parameter.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252776/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The system consumes a silent video clip (sampled at 25 FPS, lip ROI cropped to 96×96 via face landmarks), a reference audio (resampled to 16 kHz), a text script, and an emotion guidance vector E = {c, α, β} specifying the target emotion class, positive amplification weight, and negative suppression weight. It outputs a dubbed speech waveform whose duration matches the video clip, with lip-sync, speaker identity, and emotional expressiveness all jointly controlled. The emotion parameters α ∈ [0, 9] and β ∈ [0, 2] allow continuous intensity adjustment at inference time without retraining.

## Method

EmoDubber is a four-stage pipeline: (1) Lip-related Prosody Aligning (LPA) learns lip-motion-to-phoneme-prosody correspondence via duration-level contrastive learning (DLCL), which enforces monotonicity and surjectivity on the attention weight matrix using ground-truth duration labels from Montreal Forced Aligner; (2) Pronunciation Enhancing (PE) expands phoneme sequences to video-level via monotonic alignment search and fuses them with LPA outputs through an audio-visual efficient conformer with a CTC layer; (3) Speaker Identity Adapting (SIA) upsamples to mel-spectrogram level and injects speaker style via utterance-level style learning; (4) Flow-based User Emotion Controlling (FUEC) generates mel-spectrograms via optimal-transport conditional flow matching, where a Positive and Negative Guidance Mechanism (PNGM) modifies the predicted vector field by adding a positive gradient term (amplifying the target emotion c) and a negative gradient term (suppressing all other emotions), with scales α and β set by the user. The emotional classifier ψ is pre-trained on 13 datasets (>50k recordings) from Emobox, and the dual-guidance formulation in the ODE solver is the key mechanism enabling flexible, continuous emotion intensity control during generation.

## Results

On the Chem benchmark (Setting 2.0), EmoDubber achieves the best lip-sync scores (LSE-C 8.09, LSE-D 6.96) and lowest WER (12.81%) among all dubbing baselines, with absolute improvements of 4.24 and 4.0 over the next-best method on LSE-C and LSE-D respectively. On the GRID multi-speaker benchmark (Setting 2.0), EmoDubber leads in lip-sync (LSE-C 7.10, LSE-D 6.89) and achieves the lowest MCD (3.92) and MCD-SL (3.92), while its WER of 19.75% remains below the ground-truth WER of 22.41%. In the zero-shot speaker test (Dub 3.0), EmoDubber outperforms StyleDubber and Speak2Dub on all metrics including LSE-C (7.40 vs 6.17/4.83) and WER (14.03 vs 15.10/15.91).

## Baselines & Comparisons

EmoDubber decisively outperforms all dubbing baselines on lip-sync metrics (LSE-C and LSE-D) across both Chem and GRID, with the largest gaps over HPMDubbing (the only other method achieving LSE-C > 3.9 on Chem Setting 2.0) and StyleDubber. On pronunciation (WER), EmoDubber leads on Chem (12.81% vs. 14.18% for StyleDubber, 29.82% for HPMDubbing) but is slightly behind Speak2Dub on GRID Setting 2.0 (19.75% vs. 17.42%), though both remain below the ground-truth WER of 22.41%. For speaker identity (SECS), EmoDubber achieves 85.06 on Chem and 86.02 on GRID, trailing StyleDubber (87.72/86.67) and Speak2Dub (74.73/85.76) by small margins. On acoustic fidelity (MCD/MCD-SL), EmoDubber achieves the best scores on GRID (3.92/3.92) but is slightly behind StyleDubber on Chem (5.87 vs. 5.41/5.73). In the zero-shot speaker test, EmoDubber surpasses both StyleDubber and Speak2Dub on every metric, confirming stronger generalization to unseen speakers. The TTS baselines (FastSpeech2, Meta-StyleSpeech, Face-TTS, V2C-Net, Zero-shot TTS) are consistently outperformed across all metrics, with WER gaps exceeding 20–80 percentage points, highlighting the difficulty of applying standard TTS to the dubbing task without visual conditioning.

## Relation to Prior Work

EmoDubber sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) movie dubbing / visual voice cloning (V2C-Net, HPMDubbing, StyleDubber, Speak2Dub), which the authors argue have been unable to simultaneously achieve accurate lip-sync and clear pronunciation; (2) flow-matching-based speech generation (Matcha-TTS's OT-CFM), which replaces the diffusion decoders used in prior dubbing work; and (3) classifier-guided emotion control (Dhariwal & Nichol's classifier guidance, EmoDiff, EmoMix), which the paper extends from single-direction positive guidance to a dual positive-negative mechanism. The duration-level contrastive learning for lip-prosody alignment and the monotonic-alignment-search-based phoneme expansion are novel architectural choices that depart from the non-constraint or diagonal-constraint attention used in prior dubbing systems.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first dubbing architecture to offer user-specified emotion type and intensity while maintaining near-ground-truth lip-sync, addressing a practical gap in film post-production where actors must re-record to match directorial emotional direction. Key limitations include evaluation on relatively small datasets (Chem ≈ 9 h, GRID ≈ 33 speakers), emotion control tested only on an emotionless dubbing corpus (Chem), and a measurable WER degradation for the 'sad' emotion that suggests strong negative guidance can blur articulation. Open questions include whether the dual-guidance mechanism generalizes to blended or multi-emotion prompts beyond the one-hot class setting, whether the approach scales to long-form or multi-speaker dialogue, and whether real-time inference is feasible given the iterative ODE solver.
