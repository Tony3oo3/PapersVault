## Problem

Existing neural RIR prediction methods overfit to a single room's geometry and materials, requiring dense re-measurement and re-training for every new environment. This limits scalability for cross-room applications in VR, immersive media, and architectural acoustics, where a unified model must adapt to arbitrary rooms with minimal additional data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252728/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes a 256×512 panorama depth map rendered at the receiver's viewpoint, 3D coordinates of the target source and receiver, and K single-channel reference RIRs (sampled at 22,050 Hz, max 9600 samples) with their corresponding source locations. Rather than generating a waveform from scratch, the output is a weighted combination of the reference RIR log-magnitude spectrograms (63×310), converted back to a waveform via Griffin-Lim. The few-shot setting (K=1, 4, or 8 references) is central: the model must infer the target RIR from sparse acoustic observations plus geometric context.

## Method

xRIR comprises three modules: (1) a Geometric Feature Extractor that builds reflection-based maps by subtracting a 3D coordinate map (derived from the panorama depth via equi-rectangular projection) from source/receiver positions, then processes these maps with a 6-layer ViT (8 heads, hidden 512, 16×32 patches) alongside a Direct Path MLP on sinusoidally-encoded 3D coordinates; (2) a Reference RIR Encoder (ResNet-18) that extracts mean-pooled features from log-magnitude STFT spectrograms of each reference RIR; and (3) a Fusion and Weighting Module that concatenates geometric and acoustic features per reference, computes attention between the target and reference fused vectors, and generates a time-aligned K×T weighting matrix via outer product with sinusoidally-encoded temporal indices. The predicted spectrogram is a weighted sum of reference spectrograms, trained with STFT L1 loss plus an energy-decay-curve loss (λ=0.01). The key design choice is predicting a weighted combination of measured references rather than synthesizing a new RIR, which grounds predictions in real acoustic content while the geometric features modulate spatial variation.

## Results

On ACOUSTICROOMS, xRIR (K=8) achieves the best cross-room RIR prediction with EDT error of 0.038 s, C50 error of 0.940 dB, and T60 error of 8.13% on the seen split, and EDT 0.055 s, C50 1.457 dB, T60 10.53% on the unseen split. In sim-to-real transfer on four real environments, xRIR (K=8) outperforms Diff-RIR (K=12) on all metrics, e.g., reducing T60 error from 12.39% to 6.25% in a classroom and from 14.34% to 3.20% in a hallway, while using fewer reference RIRs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Few-Shot RIR [32], xRIR wins decisively on all three metrics: on the seen split, xRIR (K=8) achieves T60 error of 8.13% versus 32.71% for Few-Shot RIR (K=8), and C50 error of 0.940 dB versus 4.451 dB; the gap persists on the unseen split (T60 10.53% vs 21.15%). The authors attribute Few-Shot RIR's weakness to its UNet decoder struggling with high-fidelity reconstruction and its reliance on co-located binaural echoes rather than spatially separated reference RIRs. In sim-to-real transfer, xRIR (K=8) outperforms Diff-RIR [55] (K=12) on every metric across all four real rooms, e.g., T60 error drops from 12.39% to 6.25% in a classroom and from 14.34% to 3.20% in a hallway, despite using fewer reference RIRs. However, xRIR underperforms the simple Nearest Neighbor baseline on T60 in the sim-to-real setting, which the authors attribute to T60's sensitivity to measurement noise in low-SNR real recordings, while xRIR's training on higher-SNR simulation data limits its robustness in the late reverberation tail.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant single-room RIR prediction paradigm (INRAS, Neural Acoustic Fields, Deep Impulse Responses), which overfits a dedicated model to one room's geometry and materials, by formulating a unified cross-room task that generalizes across environments with only a few reference measurements. It also differentiates from the two closest cross-room efforts—Diff-RIR, which trains a separate differentiable rendering model per room, and Few-Shot RIR, which relies on binaural echoes and egocentric RGB-D—by using a single pre-trained model that ingests panorama depth maps (via a ViT) as a room-agnostic geometric representation and encodes reference RIRs (via ResNet-18) to capture material-specific acoustic signatures. The architectural choice of predicting the target RIR as a learned weighted combination of reference RIRs in the log-magnitude spectrogram domain, rather than generating a new waveform from scratch, is a notable design departure from both the UNet-decoder approach of Few-Shot RIR and the image-source-method rendering of Diff-RIR.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ACOUSTICROOMS dataset (300K+ RIRs, 260 rooms, 332 materials, simulated with the Discontinuous Galerkin method) fills a critical gap left by prior datasets like SoundSpaces MP3D (83 rooms, ~100 materials, 2D) and GWA (lower-resolution wave solver), and will likely become a standard benchmark for cross-room acoustic modeling. The demonstrated sim-to-real transfer—outperforming Diff-RIR with fewer reference RIRs (8 vs. 12) across four real environments—suggests a practical path toward scalable spatial audio for AR/VR without per-room calibration. However, the T60 underperformance relative to Nearest Neighbor in noisy real rooms, the reliance on Griffin-Lim for phase recovery, and the single-channel (omnidirectional) formulation leave open questions about robustness to measurement noise, extension to binaural/multichannel RIRs, and whether the fixed number of reference RIRs can be adapted to environment complexity.
