## Problem

Existing synthetic image detectors fall into two camps—semantic detectors that generalize poorly to unseen generative models and objects, and artifact detectors that are highly sensitive to lossy transformations like JPEG compression. No single approach generalizes simultaneously across diverse models, lossy formats, and unseen content, and prior benchmarks lack coverage of recent models (e.g., FLUX) and realistic compression conditions.

## Input / Output

The method accepts a single RGB image resized to 224×224 pixels and outputs a scalar probability score (via a final fully-connected layer) indicating the likelihood that the image is AI-generated. During both training and evaluation, JPEG compression with quality 75–95 is applied with 50% probability to simulate real-world post-processing, and the detector is trained with random dropout on the fusion coefficients to avoid overfitting to either feature stream.

## Method

CO-SPY comprises three modules: (1) a frozen OpenCLIP ViT-SO400M-14-384 encoder extracts semantic embeddings, augmented by feature-space interpolation that generates soft-label training samples by linearly blending real and synthetic CLIP embeddings; (2) a pre-trained VAE (from SD-v1.5) reconstructs each input image, and the absolute pixel difference between original and reconstruction is passed through a ResNet-50 to yield higher-level artifact features that are more robust to compression than raw up-sampling artifacts; (3) an adaptive fusion module uses two learned regulator networks to produce per-image scaling coefficients α and β for the semantic and artifact features respectively, which are then concatenated and classified. Random dropout that zeros out one coefficient at a time during training prevents the classifier from over-relying on a single feature type, enabling synergistic rather than merely additive combination of the two streams.

## Results

Trained on DRCT-2M/SD-v1.4 and evaluated on CO-SPYBENCH (22 generative models, 5 caption datasets, JPEG compression 75–95), CO-SPY achieves an average AP of 96.02% and accuracy of 87.06%, exceeding the best baseline (Fusing) by ~7% in AP and ~11% in accuracy. On the in-the-wild subset (50k images from Civitai, DALL-E 3, Lexica, Midjourney, instavibe.ai), CO-SPY reaches 84.65% AP and 77.41% accuracy, outperforming the best baseline (DIRE) by 14% in accuracy. On the AIGCDetectBenchmark (16 models, GAN + diffusion), CO-SPY improves average accuracy by 8% over the best baseline (UnivFD).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CO-SPY outperforms all 10 baselines on average across CO-SPYBENCH, with the largest margins over FreqFD (+34% Acc), FreqNet (+30% Acc), and NPR (+21% Acc). The closest competitor is Fusing [29], which matches or slightly exceeds CO-SPY on in-distribution models (SD-v1.4: 99.16% vs. 91.95%; SD-v1.5: 99.12% vs. 91.31%) due to overfitting to the training model's style, but CO-SPY surpasses it by 30+ points on newer models like FLUX.1-dev (86.10% vs. 52.92%). DRCT [5], the strongest semantic-only baseline, trails CO-SPY by 11% in average accuracy (75.98% vs. 87.06%), with the gap widening on unseen models (e.g., SDXL: 75.18% vs. 74.12% is close, but FLUX.1-dev: 70.70% vs. 86.10%). NPR [55], the strongest artifact-only baseline, is outperformed by 21% in accuracy, primarily because its pixel-level artifacts are destroyed by JPEG compression. On the in-the-wild evaluation (Table 3), CO-SPY leads the best baseline (DIRE) by 14% in accuracy (77.41% vs. 59.84%), with particularly large gains on Midjourney-v6 (74.92% vs. 51.90%) and DALL-E 3 (77.03% vs. 54.33%). The paper also reports that CO-SPY outperforms AIDE [65] in diverse testbeds with lossy formats (Appendix H).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two established lines of synthetic image detection: semantic-based detectors (CNNDet, UnivFD, Fusing, DRCT) that learn content-level cues, and artifact-based detectors (LNP, NPR, FreqFD, FreqNet, DIRE) that exploit pixel-level signatures from upsampling. Rather than simply ensembling these two families—which the authors show yields only additive, not synergistic, gains—they first enhance each independently (OpenCLIP ViT-SO400M for semantic features; a pre-trained SD-v1.5 VAE reconstruction error for higher-level artifacts) and then introduce an adaptive fusion module with per-feature regulators. The VAE-based artifact extraction repurposes a generative model's decoder as a forensic tool, shifting from low-level pixel discrepancies to smoother, content-aware reconstruction residuals that survive lossy compression.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CO-SPYBENCH (1M+ images, 22 generative models including FLUX, 5 real-image sources, variable diffusion steps, JPEG compression, and 50k in-the-wild fakes) addresses a critical gap: prior benchmarks like DRCT-2M and CNNDet rely on a handful of older models and fixed generation settings, making it hard to assess whether detectors truly generalize. The systematic demonstration that artifact detectors collapse under mild JPEG compression while semantic detectors fail on unseen models provides a clear diagnostic framework for the field. Open questions include whether the VAE-based artifact signal transfers to architectures without a shared decoder structure (e.g., FLUX's DiT-based pipeline), whether the approach extends to video or 3D generation, and how detection performance degrades as future models eliminate the very artifacts (upsampling patterns, decoder residuals) that current methods exploit.
