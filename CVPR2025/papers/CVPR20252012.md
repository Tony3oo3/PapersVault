## Problem

Existing invariant learning methods assume that the invariant features they discover in training environments will also be observable in unseen test environments. When some invariant features are absent at test time (e.g., occluded or cropped), models that rely on a single invariant feature fail, even though other invariant features remain available. The paper identifies this gap and formalizes Sufficient Invariant Learning (SIL), which requires the model to learn a sufficiently diverse set of invariant features so that predictions remain robust as long as any subset of them is present.

## Input / Output

The method consumes multi-environment supervised datasets where each environment e provides (X, Y) pairs drawn from a potentially different distribution; environments can be defined by subpopulation groups (e.g., background type in Waterbirds) or domain splits (e.g., source domains in DomainBed). Inputs are standard RGB images (28×28 for MNIST variants, 224×224 for natural-image benchmarks) or text (CivilComments), and the model outputs a single class label per input. A key structural requirement is that the training set is partitioned into labeled environments so that per-environment losses can be computed and weighted during optimization.

## Method

ASGDRO augments Group DRO with a per-environment sharpness-aware perturbation: for each environment e, it computes an adversarial offset ε_e* = ρ·T_θ²∇R^e(θ)/‖T_θ∇R^e(θ)‖ (where T_θ is the ASAM normalization matrix that removes scale symmetry), evaluates the loss at the perturbed parameters, and updates environment weights via exponential tilting on these perturbed losses before taking a single gradient step. The theoretical contribution (Theorem 1) shows that minimizing the worst-case perturbed loss over environments regularizes the gradient norm, driving convergence to a common flat minimum across all environments; at such a minimum, the model is forced to distribute its predictive reliance evenly across all available invariant mechanisms rather than collapsing onto a single easy feature. In practice, a single shared perturbation computed from the empirical risk is used for all environments to reduce cost, and the method is model-agnostic, plugging into any encoder–classifier architecture or pre-trained representation.

## Results

On the subpopulation shift benchmark (Table 2), ASGDRO achieves the best worst-group accuracy on CMNIST (74.2%), Waterbirds (91.4%), and CelebA (91.0%), with competitive performance on CivilComments (71.8%). On the DomainBed domain generalization benchmark (Table 4), ASGDRO attains a 65.9% average across five domains, and when combined with DPLCLIP, reaches 74.0% average. On the WILDS benchmark (Table 3), ×-ASGDRO (no pre-training) achieves the best worst-group accuracy on Camelyon17 (81.0%), CivilComments (71.8%), FMoW (35.0%), Amazon (54.5%), and RxRx1 (32.2%), outperforming methods that use rich pre-trained representations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ASGDRO consistently outperforms GDRO on worst-group accuracy across all four subpopulation shift datasets: CMNIST (74.2% vs. 68.6%), Waterbirds (91.4% vs. 90.6%), CelebA (91.0% vs. 87.2%), and CivilComments (71.8% vs. 70.0%), with the largest gap on CMNIST (+5.6 points). Against LISA, the strongest baseline, ASGDRO wins on CMNIST worst (74.2% vs. 73.3%), Waterbirds worst (91.4% vs. 89.2%), and CelebA worst (91.0% vs. 89.3%), but trails slightly on CivilComments worst (71.8% vs. 72.6%). ASGDRO substantially outperforms ASAM on worst-group metrics (e.g., 74.2% vs. 34.1% on CMNIST), confirming that flatness alone without group-aware optimization is insufficient for eliminating spurious correlations. On DomainBed, ASGDRO achieves 65.9% average, surpassing GSAM (65.1%), RDM (64.8%), RS-SCM (64.4%), and LFME (64.6%), while DPLCLIP+ASGDRO (74.0%) improves over DPLCLIP+GDRO (72.9%) and DPLCLIP alone (72.6%). On WILDS, ×-ASGDRO without any pre-training outperforms FeAT-IRM (which uses rich pre-trained representations) on Camelyon17 (81.0% vs. 78.0%) and CivilComments (71.8% vs. 70.3%), suggesting that learning diverse invariant mechanisms matters as much as representation quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

ASGDRO sits at the intersection of two well-developed lines of work: group distributionally robust optimization (GDRO, Sagawa et al. 2019) and sharpness-aware minimization (SAM/ASAM, Foret et al. 2020; Kwon et al. 2021). The key conceptual departure is from standard invariant learning (IRM, GDRO, V-REx, CORAL), which the paper argues only needs to find *some* invariant feature to succeed in training environments, leaving models fragile when that particular feature is absent at test time. The Sufficient Invariant Learning (SIL) framework reframes the objective: rather than eliminating spurious correlations via any single invariant mechanism, the model must perform well under *any* subset of invariant features, which the paper connects to finding common flat minima across environments. The method itself is a relatively direct fusion—GDRO's per-environment loss weighting combined with ASAM's normalized sharpness perturbation—but the theoretical argument (Theorem 1) that this fusion provably balances invariant mechanisms is the novel contribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete and underappreciated failure mode of invariant learning: models that succeed by latching onto the easiest invariant feature collapse when that feature is occluded or absent, a scenario common in real deployment. The H-CMNIST benchmark is a useful diagnostic tool for this specific property, and the Grad-CAM/Hessian analyses provide interpretable evidence that ASGDRO genuinely uses more diverse features. However, the practical gains over GDRO in standard benchmarks (Waterbirds, CelebA, DomainBed) are modest (1–3%), and the theoretical guarantees rely on strong assumptions (linear model, orthogonal features, known invariant directions). Open questions include whether SIL benefits persist with richer pre-trained representations, whether the method scales to settings without explicit group labels, and whether the flatness-based mechanism can be decoupled from the per-environment perturbation cost.
