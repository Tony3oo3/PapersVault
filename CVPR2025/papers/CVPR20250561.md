## Problem

Existing 3DGS-based inverse rendering methods lack an efficient Gaussian ray tracer, forcing them to either apply simplified rendering equations (e.g., split-sum approximation) or use learnable parameters to approximate incident light. This prevents accurate modeling of visibility and indirect radiance, leading to unreliable material and lighting estimation. The paper addresses this gap by introducing a differentiable 2D Gaussian ray tracing technique that enables the full rendering equation to be evaluated without simplification within the Gaussian splatting framework.

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of posed RGB images with known camera poses and produces a 2D Gaussian splatting geometry augmented with per-primitive material properties (diffuse albedo and roughness), a 32×32 environment cubemap, and pixel-level albedo/roughness/normal maps. During relighting, the optimized scene can be re-illuminated under arbitrary environment maps. The rendering equation is evaluated with 256 stratified rays per pixel, but for training efficiency only a random subset of pixels (up to 2^18 total rays per iteration) undergo full PBR evaluation, while the remaining pixels are supervised by the standard 2DGS rendering loss.

## Method

IRGS introduces 2D Gaussian ray tracing (2DGRT), which performs ray tracing directly on 2D Gaussian disk primitives rather than 3D Gaussians. Because 2D disks have a well-defined analytic ray-splat intersection (Eq. 6), 2DGRT can trace on a pretrained 2DGS checkpoint with minimal quality degradation, unlike 3DGRT which suffers from undefined 3D intersection points. The framework operates in two stages: Stage I pretrains 2DGS geometry with auxiliary losses (normal consistency, depth distortion, edge-aware smoothness, mask-based BCE), and Stage II applies the full rendering equation after rasterization at pixel-level positions, querying visibility and indirect radiance on the fly via differentiable 2DGRT (BVH over icosahedron bounding proxies, k=16 per-ray sorting, OptiX-accelerated). An efficient optimization scheme caps total rays per iteration to allow a large per-pixel ray count (N_r=256), and a novel relighting strategy uses split-sum approximation with ray-traced albedo/roughness/normal aggregation to recover indirect radiance under changed lighting without recursive evaluation.

## Results

On Synthetic4Relight, IRGS achieves the best relighting PSNR of 34.90 (vs. 31.00 for R3DG and 29.69 for TensorIR), best albedo PSNR of 30.81, and best roughness MSE of 0.008, while completing training in 0.7 hours. On TensoIR, IRGS attains the highest albedo PSNR (33.80) and relighting PSNR (29.91), along with the lowest normal MAE (4.11°) among all Gaussian-based methods. In novel view synthesis, IRGS (35.48 PSNR) is slightly behind R3DG (36.80) but competitive with TensorIR (35.80), with the gap attributed to R3DG's per-Gaussian shading strategy that favors NVS at the expense of relighting fidelity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the closest Gaussian-based competitor R3DG, IRGS wins decisively on relighting (34.90 vs. 31.00 PSNR on Synthetic4Relight; 29.91 vs. 27.37 on TensoIR), albedo (30.81 vs. 28.31; 33.80 vs. 26.20), and roughness (0.008 vs. 0.013 MSE), while R3DG retains a narrow NVS advantage (36.80 vs. 35.48 PSNR) due to its per-Gaussian shading. Compared to GS-IR, IRGS outperforms on every metric by large margins (e.g., +9.5 PSNR on relighting, +11.3 PSNR on albedo on Synthetic4Relight). Against the NeRF-based TensorIR, IRGS achieves superior relighting (+5.2 PSNR) and roughness estimation while training 4× faster (0.7 h vs. 3 h), with a marginal NVS gap (−0.3 PSNR). On TensoIR, IRGS also surpasses TensorIR in albedo (33.80 vs. 29.28) and relighting (29.91 vs. 28.58) while matching its normal quality (4.11° vs. 4.10° MAE). The older NeRF-based baselines (NeRFactor, InvRender) are outperformed across all tasks by wide margins, confirming the efficiency and accuracy gains of the Gaussian splatting formulation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on 2D Gaussian Splatting (2DGS) for geometric representation and 3DGRT for the ray-tracing machinery (k-buffer hits-based marching, OptiX acceleration, BVH construction), but makes a critical conceptual shift: by exploiting the well-defined ray-splat intersection of 2D Gaussian disks, it enables direct differentiable ray tracing on a pretrained splatting checkpoint with minimal quality degradation, unlike 3D Gaussians where the intersection point is undefined. It departs from prior 3DGS-based inverse rendering methods (GS-IR, R3DG) that resorted to split-sum approximations or learnable parameters for indirect radiance, instead integrating the full rendering equation without simplification and computing visibility and indirect radiance on the fly via the proposed 2D Gaussian ray tracing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

IRGS establishes that accurate inter-reflection modeling is feasible within the Gaussian splatting paradigm, closing a gap that had forced prior methods to sacrifice physical accuracy for efficiency. The differentiable ray tracing enabling gradient flow through indirect radiance is a reusable technique that could benefit other Gaussian-based rendering tasks beyond inverse rendering. However, the computational cost of Monte Carlo sampling for the rendering equation prevents real-time rendering, and the authors acknowledge that baking incident radiance or pre-computing radiance transfer remains necessary for practical deployment. The relighting strategy's reliance on split-sum approximation for indirect radiance (rather than recursive evaluation) is a pragmatic compromise that may limit accuracy in highly reflective scenes.
