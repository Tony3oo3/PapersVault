## Problem

Text-to-image diffusion models frequently produce images with missing objects or mis-bound attributes because cross-attention maps for syntactically related tokens fail to align spatially. The paper identifies that text embeddings—which drive cross-attention map similarity—do not faithfully encode syntactic relationships, while the text encoder's self-attention maps do capture these relationships but lose them through an attention-sink artifact on the <bos> token.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251349/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a standard text prompt and produces a single RGB image, operating entirely at test time on pretrained diffusion models (SD v1.5 at 512×512, PixArt-α) without any retraining. The text prompt is processed by the model's existing text encoder, whose self-attention maps are extracted and used as a free syntactic guide; no external parsers, segmentation masks, or manually specified token groupings are required. Optimization is applied to the latent noise at selected denoising steps (e.g., steps 1–25 out of 50 for SD), so the output is a standard diffusion-generated image with improved semantic alignment.

## Method

T-SAM constructs a cross-attention similarity matrix S (cosine similarity between averaged cross-attention maps of each token pair) and a text self-attention matrix T (averaged over encoder layers and heads, with <bos>/<eos> rows removed and re-normalized). During inference, it minimizes a loss L(z_t) = Σ ρ_i |T_ij^γ − S_ij(z_t)| by performing gradient steps on the latent noise z_t at selected denoising steps, where γ amplifies large attention values and ρ_i = i/s weights later tokens more heavily. The key insight is that this test-time optimization transfers syntactic structure already present in the text encoder's self-attention maps directly into the cross-attention module, without external linguistic tools. The paper also provides a theoretical justification (Proposition 1) showing that cross-attention map cosine similarity is governed by text embedding distance, and (Proposition 2) showing that the attention sink on <bos> causes token embeddings to remain nearly identical across self-attention layers, explaining why syntax is lost in the embeddings.

## Results

On the TIFA benchmark (4,000 diverse prompts), T-SAM achieves a TIFA score of 0.83, outperforming SD (0.79) and Linguistic Binding (0.80), while also improving CLIP image-text similarity from 0.33 to 0.34. On the structured Attend-n-Excite prompts (64 seeds), T-SAM reaches 0.37 CLIP-I (Full Prompt) on Animals-Objects and 0.28 on Minimum Object, matching or exceeding CONFORM (0.36/0.28) and outperforming SD (0.34/0.26), LB (0.35/0.27), and A&E (0.35/0.26). On PixArt-α, T-SAM similarly improves CLIP-I from 0.36 to 0.37 (Full) and 0.27 to 0.28 (Min) across both prompt categories.

## Baselines & Comparisons

T-SAM outperforms the base SD model across all metrics: TIFA score improves from 0.79 to 0.83, and CLIP-I (Full Prompt) rises from 0.34 to 0.36–0.37 on structured prompts. Against Linguistic Binding (LB), T-SAM wins on TIFA (0.83 vs. 0.80) and CLIP-I Minimum Object (0.28 vs. 0.27), while LB's reliance on SpaCy parsers limits it to attribute-binding tasks and produces identical outputs to SD when no modifier–noun pairs are detected. T-SAM matches CONFORM on CLIP-I Minimum Object (0.28) and CLIP-T (0.85 on Animals-Objects) but achieves these results without requiring manually defined positive/negative token groups, whereas CONFORM needs per-prompt token index specification. Compared to Attend-and-Excite (A&E), T-SAM edges ahead on CLIP-I Full Prompt (0.37 vs. 0.35 on Animals-Objects) and CLIP-T (0.85 vs. 0.83), again without manual token selection. On PixArt-α (a different architecture using T5-XXL and DiT), T-SAM improves CLIP-I from 0.36 to 0.37 and CLIP-T from 0.81/0.85 to 0.82/0.86, confirming generalizability beyond Stable Diffusion.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of cross-attention control for text-to-image diffusion (Prompt-to-Prompt, Attend-and-Excite, CONFORM, Linguistic Binding) by identifying a self-contained source of syntactic structure: the text encoder's own self-attention maps. Prior methods in this lineage rely on external inputs—SpaCy parsers for modifier-noun pairs (LB), or manually specified positive/negative token groups (CONFORM, A&E)—to regularize cross-attention. T-SAM departs from this by showing that the syntactic information is already present in the encoder's self-attention but is suppressed in the output embeddings by the attention-sink phenomenon, and it recovers this signal via a simple test-time loss aligning cross-attention similarity with text self-attention, supported by two propositions formalizing the embedding-to-attention-map coupling and the attention-sink bottleneck.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The self-contained nature of T-SAM (no parsers, no manual token indices) makes it broadly applicable to arbitrary prompt structures and different base architectures (demonstrated on both SD/CLIP and PixArt-α/T5-XXL/DiT), which prior attention-control methods could not achieve. The attention-sink explanation for why CLIP embeddings behave like bag-of-words provides a mechanistic account that could inform future text-encoder design. Limitations noted by the authors include persistent inaccuracies in object counting and potential noise introduced by imperfect text self-attention maps, leaving open whether the approach scales to very long or highly compositional prompts and whether fixing the sink at the encoder level would be more effective than post-hoc correction.
