## Problem

Current SOTA long-context vision-language models face a fundamental bottleneck in long-form video understanding: locating a minimal set of relevant frames (1–5) among tens of thousands requires either prohibitively expensive frame-by-frame VLM processing or imprecise uniform sampling. No existing benchmark properly evaluates temporal search quality, and prior methods (cluster-based, agent-based) achieve only 2.1% temporal F1 on the LongVideoBench subset, revealing a significant gap in search capability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252810/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a long-form video (up to hours, ~46,000 frames per video) and a text question as input. It produces a minimal set of K keyframes (typically 8 or 32) with their timestamps, which are then forwarded to a VLM (e.g., GPT-4o or LLaVA-OneVision-72B) to generate the final answer. Internally, sampled frames are arranged into g×g grid images for spatial search, and the search operates under a fixed frame budget B that constrains total frames processed across all iterations.

## Method

T* reframes temporal search as spatial search by arranging sampled video frames into a single g×g grid image, then iteratively detecting target and cue objects within that image using a lightweight detector (YOLO-world). The framework has three phases: (1) Question Grounding, where a VLM identifies target objects and contextual cue objects from the question; (2) Iterative Temporal Search, where each iteration samples frames according to a probability distribution, constructs a grid, runs object detection to score cells, updates the distribution via spline interpolation with temporal locality windows, and zooms into high-confidence regions; (3) Downstream Task, where top-K scored frames are passed to a VLM for answer generation. The key insight is that image-language models outperform video-language models, so unifying temporal and spatial dimensions within a single image space enables efficient coarse-to-fine search with O(log L) complexity in the best case, achieving 3× FLOPs reduction over frame-by-frame search.

## Results

On LongVideoBench XL (900–3600 s), T* with 32 frames improves GPT-4o from 50.5% to 53.1% and LLaVA-OneVision-72B from 56.5% to 62.4% QA accuracy, and on Video-MME (total) improves GPT-4o from 61.8% to 64.1% and LLaVA-OneVision-72B from 66.3% to 68.3%. On the LV-HAYSTACK search benchmark, detector-based T* achieves 3.4% temporal F1 (32 frames) versus 2.0% for uniform sampling, while SOTA search methods (e.g., VideoAgent) reach only 2.7% temporal F1. In terms of efficiency, training-based T* requires 169.6 TFLOPs and 10.4 s latency for the full search+downstream pipeline, compared to 690.7 TFLOPs and 34.9 s for VideoAgent, a ~4× reduction in compute.

## Baselines & Comparisons

T* consistently outperforms uniform sampling (the default in LongVideoBench and LLaVA-OneVision) across all video lengths: on LongVideoBench XL with 32 frames, the gain is +2.6 pp for GPT-4o and +5.9 pp for LLaVA-OneVision-72B, with the largest improvement on the longest videos. Against VideoAgent (the strongest adaptive-search baseline), T* achieves comparable or better QA accuracy (50.8% vs 49.2% on LV-HAYSTACK) while using ~4× fewer FLOPs (172.6 vs 690.7 TFLOPs) and ~3× lower latency (11.1 s vs 34.9 s). On the LV-HAYSTACK search-utility benchmark, detector-based T* (32 frames) reaches 3.4% temporal F1, surpassing VideoAgent (2.7%), retrieval-based methods (2.4%), and uniform sampling (2.0%). On shorter-video benchmarks (NExT-QA, EgoSchema), LLaVA-OneVision-7B + detector-based T* with only 8 frames achieves 80.4% and 66.6%, beating VideoAgent (71.3%, 60.2%), LVNet (72.9%, 66.0%), VideoTree (73.5%, 66.2%), and VidF4 (74.1%, –) while using the fewest frames. Compared to high-frame-budget leaderboard models (GPT-4o 256 frames at 61.6%, LLaVA-Video-72B 64 frames at 61.5% on LongVideoBench XL), T* with just 32 frames reaches 53.1% and 62.4% respectively, closing most of the gap with 8× fewer frames for GPT-4o.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper draws a direct conceptual line from V* (Wu & Xie, 2024), which performs coarse-to-fine spatial search in images via iterative zooming, and transplants that paradigm to the temporal axis by converting frame sequences into grid images for object detection. It departs from prior temporal localization work (which targets continuous segments) and from agent-based video understanding methods (VideoAgent, VideoTree) that rely on costly frame-by-frame VLM calls, instead using a lightweight detector (YOLO-world) as the search heuristic. The LV-HAYSTACK benchmark also distinguishes itself from synthetic needle-in-a-haystack evaluations by using real-world video with human-annotated minimal keyframe sets, filling a gap where prior long-video benchmarks only measured downstream QA accuracy without isolating temporal search quality.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 2.1% temporal F1 score achieved by SOTA search methods on LV-HAYSTACK reveals that current long-context VLMs are essentially unable to perform temporal search, a capability that is a prerequisite for efficient long-video understanding. The plug-and-play T* module offers a practical path forward, improving GPT-4o and LLaVA-OneVision-72B by 2.6–5.9 points with 4× fewer frames and 3× fewer FLOPs, making it immediately adoptable. Key limitations include the assumption that a handful of keyframes suffices (risky for dense-reasoning or multi-event tasks) and the exclusion of audio and subtitle modalities, leaving open whether the spatial-search reframing generalizes to tasks requiring sustained temporal context or cross-modal cues.
