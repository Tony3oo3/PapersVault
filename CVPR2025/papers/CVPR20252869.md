## Problem

Existing methods for identifying frequency shortcuts in image classifiers measure individual frequency relevance iteratively, requiring computational time proportional to the number of classes and image resolution—making them impractical for large-scale datasets like ImageNet-1k. Moreover, these methods neglect the joint contribution of multiple frequencies, potentially missing shortcuts that only emerge from frequency combinations. This computational barrier has left the frequency shortcut behavior of ImageNet-trained models largely unexplored, and current OOD benchmarks fail to account for how shortcuts interact with specific OOD data characteristics.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained image classification model (ResNet, ViT, or CCT), a subset of training images, and test images, operating entirely in the Fourier domain. It produces per-class Dominant Frequency Maps (DFMs)—binary masks over the 2D frequency spectrum retaining roughly 5% of total frequencies for ImageNet-resolution images—and classifies each class as shortcut-affected or non-shortcut based on whether TPR on DFM-filtered images exceeds a user-defined threshold t. The DFM-filtered images (original images with only dominant frequencies retained) serve as the evaluation substrate for measuring shortcut strength.

## Method

HFSS (Hierarchical Frequency Shortcut Search) performs a coarse-to-fine search over the 2D Fourier spectrum by dividing it into overlapping frequency patches (using shifted windows to avoid border effects) and randomly sampling p% of patches at each stage to form candidate frequency subsets. At each stage, the model's class-wise loss is computed in parallel on images filtered to retain only the sampled frequency subset; subsets yielding lower loss are ranked and the top-N are forwarded to the next stage with smaller patch sizes, progressively narrowing the search space. This hierarchical design, combined with parallel class-wise evaluation, reduces computational cost by orders of magnitude compared to exhaustive single-frequency removal while capturing joint frequency contributions. The final top-1 subset per class defines the DFM, and shortcut degree is quantified by comparing TPR on original versus DFM-filtered test images across a sweep of thresholds t.

## Results

HFSS reduces the computational time for frequency shortcut identification on ImageNet-1k from ~8500 hours (354 days) to 220 hours (9.2 days), a ~39× speedup over the prior method [39]. On ImageNet-10, HFSS identifies strong frequency shortcuts (TPR ≥ 0.6 on DFM-filtered images) for 6 out of 10 classes (airliner 0.88, Siam-cat 0.98, ox 0.90, frog 0.78, zebra 0.70, Con-ship 0.76), whereas [39] only finds strong shortcuts for 2 classes (Siam-cat 0.80, zebra 0.80). On the OOD set IN-SCT, HFSS-identified shortcuts yield TPRs of 0.51–0.89 for affected classes (e.g., tabby cat 0.886, tree frog 0.871), substantially above the average TPR of 0.367, while [39]'s shortcuts produce TPRs of 0.03–0.21.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the single-frequency-removal method of Wang et al. (ICCV 2023) [39], which measures the relevance of each frequency individually. HFSS clearly outperforms [39] in effectiveness: it identifies strong shortcuts (TPR ≥ 0.6) for 6 classes on ImageNet-10 versus only 2 for [39], with the largest gap on airliner (0.88 vs. 0.08), ox (0.90 vs. 0.02), and frog (0.78 vs. 0.14). This advantage stems from HFSS's consideration of joint frequency contributions rather than iterative single-frequency removal. On the OOD validation set IN-SCT, HFSS-identified shortcuts produce TPRs well above the class average (e.g., 0.886 for tabby cat, 0.871 for tree frog), while [39]'s shortcuts yield TPRs of 0.03–0.21, confirming that [39] underestimates the true impact of shortcuts on OOD performance. In terms of efficiency, HFSS requires ~39× less computation on ImageNet-1k (220 h vs. 8500 h) and 15× less on CIFAR-10 (0.5 h vs. 7.5 h). The paper also shows that the most time-efficient HFSS configuration (CF-2.10) achieves a ~200× speedup over the most exhaustive (CF-1) while still recovering most shortcuts at low thresholds.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Wang et al. (ICCV 2023) [39], which first identified frequency shortcuts by iteratively measuring the contribution of individual frequencies to classification. The key methodological departure is the proposed Hierarchical Frequency Shortcut Search (HFSS), which replaces single-frequency removal with a coarse-to-fine hierarchical search over frequency patches, enabling joint frequency contribution analysis and reducing computational cost by roughly two orders of magnitude. Conceptually, the work also connects the frequency shortcut perspective to the broader texture-bias literature (Geirhos et al. [8]) and the general shortcut learning framework (Geirhos et al. [9]), providing the first concrete mechanistic link between Fourier-domain shortcuts and the well-documented texture bias of ImageNet-trained models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that frequency shortcuts simultaneously improve performance on texture-preserving OOD benchmarks (IN-C, IN-v2, FGSM) while degrading performance on rendition-based OOD sets (IN-R, IN-S)—reveals a blind spot in current OOD evaluation protocols that do not account for whether shortcut-relevant information is present in test data. This suggests that reported OOD generalization numbers may be inflated or deflated depending on the specific OOD scenario, and that future benchmarks should explicitly assess shortcut presence. Open questions include whether frequency shortcuts can be selectively mitigated without sacrificing the robustness benefits they confer, and whether the findings generalize to non-ImageNet training regimes or generative models.
