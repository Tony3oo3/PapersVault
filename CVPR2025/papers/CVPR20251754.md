## Problem

Existing diffusion-based text-to-video models are trained exclusively on single-shot videos and cannot generate multi-shot sequences with discrete cuts where the same character performs distinct activities across the same or different backgrounds. This limitation blocks real-world applications like film production, where narratives inherently rely on multiple shots with distinct actions, perspectives, and settings.

## Input / Output

The model takes a set of shot-specific text prompts (one caption per shot), a user-specified number of shots, and per-shot durations as input, and produces a single unified multi-shot video of 128 frames at 192×320 resolution. The video is encoded via a 3D-VAE into a latent representation that is patchified along width, height, and frame depth before being processed by the DiT. The number of transition tokens (n−1 for n shots) and their positions in the attention mask directly encode the shot boundaries and durations, giving the user precise temporal control over where cuts appear.

## Method

ShotAdapter fine-tunes a pre-trained DiT-based T2V model (OpenSora-like, using joint-attention layers) for only ~5,000 iterations by introducing two architectural modifications: (1) learnable 'transition tokens'—n−1 embeddings appended to the visual-textual token sequence that signal shot boundaries, analogous to an EOS token in NLP—and (2) a local attention masking strategy that restricts each transition token to attend only to the visual tokens at its intended cut frame, while each shot's textual tokens attend exclusively to their corresponding visual tokens. This masking prevents shot-specific prompts from diluting across the entire sequence and enables per-shot content control. Training data is constructed via two novel pipelines that synthesize multi-shot videos from existing single-shot datasets: splitting high-motion videos into random sub-clips, and clustering videos by identity (via user upload patterns and caption similarity) then concatenating clips from the same cluster. Post-processing with LLaVA-NeXT generates shot-specific captions, while YOLO and DINOv2 embeddings filter for single-identity consistency, yielding a dataset of 2-, 3-, and 4-shot videos.

## Results

ShotAdapter achieves Identity Consistency (IC) of 78.67, 86.33, and 70.30 for 2-, 3-, and 4-shot videos with changing backgrounds, outperforming all baselines on 3- and 4-shot IC. Background Consistency (BC) reaches 89.48, 77.66, and 76.55 for 2-, 3-, and 4-shot same-background settings, surpassing all baselines by a large margin (e.g., 77.66 vs. 67.02 for RS and 64.85 for SS on 3-shot). Text Alignment (TA) scores 27.12, 23.65, and 22.17, remaining competitive with baselines that generate shots independently. In the user study (75 participants), ShotAdapter is preferred 77.19% of the time for identity consistency and 73.27% for background consistency in 1-to-1 comparisons.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against three baselines built from the same pre-trained T2V model: Random Shots (RS, random concatenation of independently generated shots), Similar Shots (SS, concatenation guided by DINOv2 foreground similarity), and Shots by Reference (SR, StoryMaker keyframes + I2V animation). ShotAdapter clearly wins on Background Consistency across all shot counts (e.g., 77.66 vs. 67.02 for RS and 64.85 for SS on 3-shot), and on Identity Consistency for 3- and 4-shot videos (86.33 vs. 82.55/84.98; 70.30 vs. 55.15/67.92). On 2-shot IC, SS slightly edges out ShotAdapter (81.74 vs. 78.67), consistent with the paper's note that SR is competitive at 2 shots. On Text Alignment, RS and SS perform better (e.g., 26.13 vs. 23.65 on 3-shot) because they generate each shot independently, effectively serving as an upper bound; ShotAdapter remains within ~2 points. The user study confirms these trends: ShotAdapter is preferred 77.19% for IC and 73.27% for BC, while TA preference is more modest at 56.73%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the DiT-based T2V architecture (as in OpenSora and MovieGen) and its joint-attention conditioning mechanism, extending it with two targeted modifications: a learnable 'transition token' (analogous to EOS tokens in NLP) and a local attention masking strategy that restricts cross-shot information flow. It departs from prior multi-shot video approaches—which rely on multi-stage pipelines (keyframe generation via StoryMaker, I2V animation, concatenation) or naive prompt concatenation—by treating the entire multi-shot video as a single unified generation problem with shot-specific text conditioning. The data pipeline, which synthesizes multi-shot training videos from existing single-shot datasets via optical-flow-guided splitting and identity-clustered concatenation, is a novel contribution that sidesteps the need for curated multi-shot video corpora.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By formalizing text-to-multi-shot video generation as a distinct task and providing the first evaluation benchmark, this paper opens a practical pathway toward narrative video synthesis (film production, storytelling) that was previously intractable with single-shot T2V models. The key open questions include extending generation beyond 128 frames via autoregressive shot conditioning, generalizing to non-human subjects, and closing the small quality gap observed in user studies relative to per-shot baselines. The data curation pipeline from single-shot to multi-shot videos is broadly applicable and could benefit other video generation research facing similar data scarcity.
