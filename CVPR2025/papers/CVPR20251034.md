## Problem

Existing reconstruction-based detectors for diffusion-generated images feed the entire reconstruction error into a backend classifier, which mixes content bias with the actual generation artifacts, limiting generalization to unseen models and datasets. The paper identifies that the hard-to-reconstruct information is concentrated in mid-frequency bands but remains unexploited by prior methods, and asks: which specific frequency components of an image are particularly difficult for a diffusion model to reconstruct, and can isolating them yield a more robust detection signal?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251034/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single 256×256 RGB image as input, applies FFT to obtain its frequency spectrum, and produces a binary real/generated label via a ResNet-50 classifier. Notably, the reconstruction step uses only the variational autoencoder (encoder + decoder) of Stable Diffusion v1.5 rather than the full denoising pipeline, reducing computational cost while preserving detection signal. The classifier receives two concatenated reconstruction-error maps (original image and pseudo-generated image) as a 2-channel input.

## Method

FIRE introduces a Frequency Mask Refinement Module (FMRE) with a shared encoder and two decoders that, given the FFT spectrum of an input image, learn complementary masks isolating mid-frequency bands (where DMs struggle to reconstruct) and their complement. The mid-frequency mask extracts a band-pass filtered component, while the complementary mask produces a 'pseudo-generated' image with that information removed. Both the original and pseudo-generated images pass through the LDM autoencoder, and the absolute reconstruction errors before and after filtering are concatenated and fed to a ResNet-50 classifier. The training objective combines three terms: a mid-frequency reconstruction alignment loss that forces the extracted mid-band to match the raw reconstruction error map, a mask refinement loss that regularizes learned masks toward predefined ideal mid-band regions (distance 40–120 from center in a 256×256 spectrum), and a standard cross-entropy classification loss. This end-to-end design directly couples the frequency mask learning with the reconstruction pipeline, unlike prior two-stage approaches that first compute errors and then train a separate classifier.

## Results

On the DiffusionForensics benchmark (LSUN-Bedroom and ImageNet subsets), FIRE achieves a perfect 100.0% AUC and 100.0% ACC on average across all 11 generation methods, matching or slightly exceeding DIRE (99.8/98.3) and FakeInversion (100.0/99.6). On the more challenging self-collected dataset with newer generation models (DALL-E 3, Kandinsky 3, Midjourney, SDXL, Vega), FIRE achieves 72.0% AUC / 68.1% ACC (LAION+DALL-E 3) and 78.1% AUC / 72.9% ACC (LAION+Kandinsky 3), substantially outperforming the next-best method FakeInversion (68.8/63.5 and 70.5/65.6 respectively). All other baselines (CNNDet, AEROBLADE, DIRE) drop to near-chance levels (~50% AUC) on the newer models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FIRE outperforms all four baselines on both evaluation settings. On DiffusionForensics, FIRE achieves 100.0/100.0 AUC/ACC, marginally exceeding FakeInversion (100.0/99.6) and DIRE (99.8/98.3), while AEROBLADE (99.08/98.0) and CNNDet (76.3/57.8) trail further behind. The gap widens dramatically on newer generation models: FIRE leads FakeInversion by 3.2% AUC and 4.6% ACC on LAION+DALL-E 3, and by 7.6% AUC and 7.3% ACC on LAION+Kandinsky 3. DIRE, AEROBLADE, and CNNDet all collapse to near-chance performance (~50% AUC) on the newer models, indicating a fundamental generalization limitation. FakeInversion remains the strongest competitor but still lags FIRE by a meaningful margin, suggesting that frequency-guided reconstruction error provides a more transferable detection signal than inversion-based features.

## Relation to Prior Work

FIRE extends the reconstruction-error-based detection paradigm established by DIRE, AEROBLADE, and FakeInversion, which exploit the fact that DM-generated images are closer to the diffusion latent space and thus exhibit lower reconstruction error. The key departure is the introduction of frequency decomposition: rather than feeding the full reconstruction error into a classifier, FIRE learns (via the FMRE module) to isolate the mid-frequency band where reconstruction difficulty concentrates, then compares errors before and after removing that band. Additionally, FIRE adopts AEROBLADE's insight that the LDM autoencoder alone suffices for reconstruction, but goes further by integrating the AE directly into an end-to-end training loop with the classifier, unlike prior two-stage approaches that first compute errors and then train a separate backend.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The frequency-guided framing offers a more interpretable and potentially more generalizable detection signal than raw reconstruction error, and the end-to-end AE-based pipeline reduces computational cost compared to full denoising. However, performance on genuinely challenging newer models (DALL-E 3, Kandinsky 3, SDXL) remains moderate (72–78% AUC), and robustness degrades under severe Gaussian blur, suggesting the mid-frequency cue is not yet fully invariant to post-processing. Open questions include whether the learned frequency band is truly universal across all future DM architectures, whether the approach extends to video or 3D generation, and whether the predefined mask prior (40–120 px radius) can be made fully data-driven without the performance drop observed in ablations.
