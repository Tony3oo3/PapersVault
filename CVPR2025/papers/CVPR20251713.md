## Problem

Existing multi-graph matching (MGM) methods are impractical for large-scale, real-world bioimaging problems: complete-MGM solvers cannot be efficiently adapted to the incomplete setting (proven via a lower-bound theorem), and the few methods handling incomplete sparse problems are either prohibitively slow or produce inferior results. The worms-100 dataset, with ~45M keypoint correspondences across 100 C. elegans specimens, is 89× larger than any previously addressed MGM instance, exposing a critical gap between theory and practical deployment.

## Input / Output

The method takes d objects (e.g., 100 worms), each with n 3D keypoint candidates for nuclei, along with pairwise linear (keypoint-to-keypoint) and quadratic (keypoint-pair-to-keypoint-pair) costs between every object pair. The cost structure is inherently sparse: only ~3% of possible keypoint-to-keypoint matchings carry finite cost, with the remainder implicitly forbidden by infinite costs. The output is a cycle-consistent incomplete multi-matching represented as a union of cliques (each clique containing at most one vertex per object), minimizing the total sum of linear and quadratic costs over all matched pairs.

## Method

GREEDA decomposes the MGM problem into a chain (or binary tree) of graph-matching subproblems solved by a black-box GM solver, maintaining cycle consistency at every step via a clique-based representation of the solution. The construction phase greedily extends a partial solution by one object per iteration, with an incremental variant using a full MGM solver for the first s objects to reduce error propagation, and a parallel variant using balanced binary trees to reduce sequential depth from O(d) to O(log d). Two local search heuristics then refine the solution: GM-LS splits one object from the matching and re-solves the GM problem to re-merge it (parallelized by running all d splits simultaneously and accepting profitable changes in order of gain), and SWAP-LS formulates joint 2-clique vertex swaps as quadratic pseudo-boolean optimization problems solved approximately via QPBO-I. The key design principle is that all stages operate directly on the incomplete, sparse problem without any transformation to a complete setting, guaranteeing that only allowed finite-cost matchings are ever produced.

## Results

On the large-scale worms-100 dataset (~45M keypoint correspondences), GREEDA constructs a feasible solution in 10 minutes and converges in approximately one hour using 65 GB of RAM, while the only competing method MP-T runs out of memory on a 250 GB machine. On worms-29, GREEDA reduces construction time from 30 minutes (MP-T) to just 2 minutes while achieving a lower (better) objective value. On small-scale datasets (synthetic, hotel, house), GREEDA is at least an order of magnitude faster than all competitors while matching or exceeding their final objective values. Synchronization-based methods (PPI, Stiefel, Spectral+SBR) produce solutions worse than the trivial all-unmatched baseline on the hotel dataset.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MP-T [41], the closest direct competitor for incomplete sparse MGM, GREEDA wins decisively: on worms-29 it achieves a lower objective value with construction time reduced from 30 minutes to 2 minutes, and on worms-100 it completes in ~1 hour (65 GB RAM) while MP-T exhausts 250 GB of memory. Against DS* [5], GREEDA is substantially faster (partly due to C++ vs. Matlab implementation) and handles incomplete problems natively, whereas DS* is restricted to complete dense instances. The three synchronization baselines—PPI [12], Sparse Stiefel [8], and Spectral+SBR [34,6]—all fail on the hotel dataset, returning solutions with higher cost than the trivial all-unmatched matching, demonstrating that their cycle-consistency enforcement introduces costly 'blunders' on sparse problems. On small-scale synthetic and hotel instances, GREEDA matches or slightly beats the best competitor's final objective value while running an order of magnitude faster. Applying GREEDA's local search (GM-LS + SWAP-LS) to the outputs of other methods recovers objective values comparable to GREEDA's own, but at significantly higher optimization cost, confirming that the construction heuristic is the key differentiator.

## Relation to Prior Work

GREEDA repurposes construction and local search heuristics originally developed for multi-dimensional assignment problems in operations research (Bandelt et al. [3, 4], Balas & Saltzman [2]) and adapts them to the Multi-QAP setting with quadratic costs, which prior MGM methods in computer vision had not addressed. The paper departs from the dominant paradigm in the MGM literature—convex relaxations (DS*, MP-T) and synchronization-based approaches—by proving (Theorem 1) that the commonly-claimed reduction from incomplete to complete MGM is prohibitively expensive, thereby justifying a direct incomplete-sparse formulation. The novel 2-clique swap local search, formulated as a quadratic pseudo-boolean optimization problem solved with QPBO-I, has no direct counterpart in the computer vision MGM literature, though related multi-swap ideas exist in the MDAP literature without an efficient solver.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work addresses a critical practical gap: MGM has been rarely deployed in real applications (beyond bioimaging) largely due to the absence of solvers that are both fast and capable of handling incomplete, sparse, large-scale instances. The worms-100 benchmark (45M correspondences) and the proof that MP-T exhausts 250 GB of RAM while GREEDA uses 65 GB in under an hour establish a new practical ceiling for the field. Open questions include whether the SWAP-LS quadratic bottleneck can be mitigated for even larger instances, and how the solver integrates into the emerging paradigm of learning matching costs with neural networks, where runtime constraints make it a necessary inner-loop component.
