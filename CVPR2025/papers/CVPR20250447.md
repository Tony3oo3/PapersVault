## Problem

Existing ordinal classification methods treat all adjacent-class errors as equally important, ignoring that some boundaries (e.g., pre-cancerous vs. cancerous, minor vs. adult) carry far greater practical consequences than others. No prior approach provides a mechanism to assign different importance to different inter-class margins or to manually control decision boundaries at critical thresholds.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250447/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes RGB images of varying sizes (224×224 for most datasets, 120×200 for KOA X-rays) and produces two outputs: a predicted ordinal class label via a softmax classifier, and C−1 learned scalar margins representing the separation distance between each pair of consecutive ranks in the embedding space. These margins are interpretable quantities that users can inspect to identify the most challenging class boundaries or manually fix to enforce stricter separation at clinically critical thresholds.

## Method

CLOC introduces a Multi-Margin N-Pair (MMNP) loss that extends the standard N-pair contrastive objective by replacing the fixed margin with a cumulative sum of C−1 learnable margins between consecutive ranks, so that negative samples from more distant ranks are repelled more strongly than those from adjacent ranks. The model uses an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-50 encoder followed by two linear classifier layers, trained with a combined cross-entropy and MMNP loss. Training follows a two-phase strategy: Phase 1 jointly optimizes the encoder, classifier, and margins (with softplus activation, random initialization in [0.5, 1.0], and early stopping to prevent margin collapse), while Phase 2 freezes the learned margins and refines the representation. This design allows users to fix specific margins at critical decision boundaries during training, trading a small amount of overall accuracy for substantially reduced errors at those high-stakes boundaries.

## Results

CLOC achieves the best accuracy across all five datasets: 63.02% on Adience, 62.08% on HID, 66.73% on KOA, 73.79% on IDRiD, and 60.35% on BRACS. It also attains the lowest MAE on four of five datasets (0.41 on Adience, 0.42 on KOA, 0.41 on IDRiD, 0.71 on BRACS), with a marginal loss on HID (0.5534 vs. GOL's 0.5500). The largest accuracy gains over the second-best method are on IDRiD (+9.7 pp over MWR) and HID (+4.3 pp over MWR), while on Adience the margin is tighter (+0.4 pp over MWR).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CLOC outperforms all eight baselines on accuracy across every dataset. Among ordinal classification methods, the closest competitors are MWR (best on Adience at 62.60% and IDRiD at 64.08%) and GOL (best on KOA at 64.39% and BRACS at 57.54%); CLOC exceeds them by 0.4–9.7 pp depending on the dataset. Among contrastive learning methods, SupCon is the strongest baseline (e.g., 57.74% on Adience, 56.67% on BRACS), yet CLOC still leads by 5–17 pp. RnC, which bridges both categories, is consistently outperformed by CLOC on both accuracy and MAE across all five datasets. On MAE, CLOC achieves the best value on four of five datasets; the sole exception is HID where GOL edges out CLOC by 0.003 (0.5500 vs. 0.5534), though CLOC still leads HID accuracy by 4.3 pp. The largest absolute MAE reductions are on IDRiD (0.4078 vs. 0.5972 for ORCNN, a 32% relative improvement) and Adience (0.4100 vs. 0.4300 for GOL).

## Relation to Prior Work

CLOC extends the multi-class N-pair contrastive loss [51] by replacing its single fixed margin with C−1 learnable margins, each governing the separation between consecutive ordinal ranks. It builds on the supervised contrastive learning framework of SupCon [27] and the rank-aware embedding idea of Rank-N-Contrast [62], but departs from both by making the inter-class distances explicitly parameterized and controllable rather than implicitly learned. The two-phase training strategy (joint margin+representation learning followed by margin-frozen refinement) is borrowed from the one-class classification literature [47] to mitigate margin collapse, a failure mode not addressed in prior ordinal contrastive methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ability to fix or constrain specific margins at clinically critical boundaries (e.g., benign-to-malignant, minor-to-adult) offers a practical controllability knob that existing ordinal classifiers lack, and the robustness to simulated diagnostic label bias (60% relabeling) demonstrates real-world relevance for high-stakes medical imaging. However, the authors acknowledge that the coincidence of largest learned margins with critical boundaries may be confounded by visual distinctiveness and annotator caution, leaving open whether margins are a reliable proxy for clinical importance. Performance degrades with a large number of classes, limiting applicability to fine-grained ordinal tasks, and the method's advantage over strong baselines like GOL and MWR, while consistent, is moderate in absolute terms.
