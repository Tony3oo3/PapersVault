## Problem

Converting static 3D models into articulation-ready assets requires manually placing skeletons, defining joint hierarchies, and specifying skinning weights—a process that is time-consuming and demands significant expertise. Existing automatic methods either rely on predefined templates that fail to generalize beyond specific categories, produce densely packed joints unsuitable for animation, or make strong assumptions about shape orientation. Additionally, the absence of a large-scale, diverse benchmark with high-quality articulation annotations has hindered the development of generalizable learning-based solutions.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single 3D mesh as input (from artist-created assets, text-to-3D generation, or image-based reconstruction) and produces two outputs: a skeleton consisting of 3D joint coordinates and bone connectivity (joint index pairs), and per-vertex skinning weights as an n-dimensional vector where n is the maximum number of joints in the dataset. The input mesh is consumed via 8,192 sampled surface points encoded by a pretrained shape encoder, while the skeleton is represented as a discrete token sequence in a 128³ grid (6 tokens per bone), and skinning weights are modeled as a continuous function over the mesh surface.

## Method

MagicArticulate is a two-stage supervised pipeline. Stage 1 formulates skeleton generation as an autoregressive sequence modeling task: bones are tokenized as 6 discrete coordinates each (two joints in a 128³ grid), ordered by joint index, and generated sequentially by an OPT-350M decoder-only transformer conditioned on shape tokens from a pretrained point-cloud encoder, trained with cross-entropy next-token prediction. Stage 2 predicts skinning weights via a functional diffusion model (DDPM with x₀ prediction) that learns a mapping from 3D surface points to n-dimensional weight vectors, incorporating volumetric geodesic distance priors between vertices and joints as a geometric initialization—the model predicts the residual between true weights and this prior, conditioned on joint coordinates and global shape features. This design naturally handles variable bone counts, diverse topologies, and arbitrary orientations without template assumptions.

## Results

On skeleton generation, MagicArticulate achieves CD-J2J of 2.586, CD-J2B of 1.959, and CD-B2B of 1.661 on Articulation-XL, outperforming RigNet (7.478/5.892/4.932) by roughly 2.9× and Pinocchio (8.360/6.677/5.689) by 3.2×. On ModelsResource, the method scores 3.343/2.455/2.140, beating RigNet (4.143/2.961/2.675) and Pinocchio (6.852/4.824/4.089). For skinning weight prediction, MagicArticulate reaches 80.7% precision and 0.337 avg L1 on Articulation-XL (vs. RigNet's 72.4% and 0.698), and 82.1% precision with 0.398 avg L1 on ModelsResource (vs. GVB's 69.3% and 0.687).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MagicArticulate decisively outperforms both baselines on skeleton generation across all three Chamfer Distance metrics on both datasets. Pinocchio [3], a template-fitting method, suffers the largest degradation (CD-J2J of 8.360 on Articulation-XL) because its predefined templates cannot accommodate non-humanoid or variably-oriented objects. RigNet [35], a graph-convolution-based learning method, performs reasonably on ModelsResource (where all shapes share a consistent upright/front-facing orientation) but degrades sharply on Articulation-XL (CD-J2J 7.478 vs. 4.143), confirming its sensitivity to orientation assumptions. For skinning weight prediction, MagicArticulate beats GVB [11] (a geometric distance-based method) by 5.4–12.8 percentage points in precision and roughly halves the L1 error on both datasets. Against RigNet on skinning, MagicArticulate wins on precision (80.7% vs. 72.4% on Articulation-XL) and L1 error (0.337 vs. 0.698), though RigNet retains a slight recall edge on ModelsResource (83.5% vs. 81.6%). Cross-dataset generalization further favors MagicArticulate: when trained on Articulation-XL and tested on ModelsResource, it matches RigNet's in-distribution performance, whereas RigNet trained on Articulation-XL drops below Pinocchio on ModelsResource.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the autoregressive sequence-modeling paradigm from 3D mesh generation (MeshGPT, MeshAnything, PolyGen) to the distinct problem of skeleton generation, treating variable-length bone sequences as tokens for a decoder-only transformer (OPT-350M). For skinning weights, it adapts the functional diffusion framework of Zhang & Wonka (CVPR 2024) to predict per-vertex weight functions conditioned on volumetric geodesic distance priors. It departs from template-based methods (Pinocchio) that cannot generalize beyond predefined structures, from curve-skeleton extraction that yields animation-unsuitable dense joints, and from GNN-based approaches (RigNet, SkinningNet) that assume consistent shape orientation and struggle with complex topologies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Articulation-XL benchmark (33k+ models with VLM-verified articulation annotations) fills a critical gap that has limited learning-based rigging research, and the demonstrated cross-dataset generalization (trained on Articulation-XL, tested on ModelsResource and AI-generated meshes) suggests the approach can serve as a practical drop-in for content-creation pipelines. Open questions include whether the autoregressive formulation scales to extremely complex multi-body articulation (e.g., cloth, multi-character scenes), whether the pre-trained shape encoder introduces a ceiling on geometric fidelity, and how the method handles objects with non-rigid or partially articulated components. The reliance on a fixed 128³ discretization for joint coordinates also raises questions about precision for very fine-grained articulation.
