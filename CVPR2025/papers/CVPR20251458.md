## Problem

Existing training-free zero-shot CIR methods follow a two-stage pipeline (image captioning followed by LLM-based reasoning) that suffers from two key limitations: the captioning stage, uninformed by manipulation text, drops critical visual details needed for query composition, and simple reasoning prompts fail to fully exploit LLM reasoning, leading to misinterpretation of implicit user intent. This paper addresses both gaps by unifying visual understanding and intent reasoning into a single MLLM pass with structured reflective reasoning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251458/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a reference RGB image and a short manipulation text (e.g., 'remove the human, change the background') as joint input to a multimodal LLM (default GPT-4o, temperature 0). The MLLM outputs a structured multi-step reasoning trace culminating in a single natural-language target image description, which is then encoded by a frozen CLIP text encoder and matched against pre-encoded database images via cosine similarity to retrieve the target image. The entire pipeline is training-free and modular, allowing the retrieval backbone (ViT-B/32, ViT-L/14, ViT-G/14) to be swapped independently.

## Method

OSrCIR replaces the conventional two-stage caption-then-reason pipeline with a single MLLM forward pass that simultaneously ingests the reference image and manipulation text, eliminating the information loss inherent in intermediate captioning. The key mechanism is a Reflective Chain-of-Thought prompt that guides the MLLM through four progressive reasoning steps within one prompt: (1) Original Image Description, which captures only manipulation-relevant visual details; (2) Thoughts, which interprets the user's implicit intent and its rationale; (3) Reflections, which filters out hallucinated or misaligned intentions by re-evaluating against the image context; and (4) Target Image Description, which produces the final retrieval query. To make the MLLM follow this structured process without task-specific training, the authors introduce vision-by-language in-context learning, providing a few text-only exemplars of expected reasoning outputs as few-shot demonstrations. The final description is retrieved via standard CLIP cosine similarity, keeping the reasoning and retrieval stages fully decoupled and interpretable.

## Results

On CIRCO (ViT-L/14), OSrCIR achieves mAP@5 of 23.87%, surpassing the best training-free method CIReVL* (18.92%) by 4.95% and the top textual inversion method Context-I2W (13.04%) by 10.83%. On CIRR (ViT-L/14), OSrCIR reaches Recall@1 of 29.45% and Recall@10 of 69.86%, improving over CIReVL* by 4.62% and 4.58% respectively. Across GeneCIS (ViT-L/14), OSrCIR achieves an average R@1 of 17.9%, outperforming CIReVL* (16.1%) and Context-I2W (12.7%). On FashionIQ (ViT-L/14), OSrCIR attains average R@10 of 33.26% and R@50 of 54.37%, exceeding CIReVL* by 4.21% and 5.26% respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OSrCIR consistently outperforms all five baselines across all four datasets and all three CLIP backbones. Against the strongest training-free baseline CIReVL* (which uses GPT-4o in a two-stage pipeline), OSrCIR gains 4.95% mAP@5 on CIRCO, 4.62% Recall@1 on CIRR, 1.8% average R@1 on GeneCIS, and 4.21% average R@10 on FashionIQ (all ViT-L/14). Against the best textual inversion method Context-I2W, the margins are even larger: 10.83% mAP@5 on CIRCO and 5.2% average R@1 on GeneCIR. On FashionIQ with ViT-G/14, OSrCIR (37.57% avg R@10) still trails the trained LinCIR (45.11%), which the authors attribute to LinCIR's training alignment with CLIP's fashion-specific vocabulary. The paper notes that CIReVL* only marginally improves over CIReVL (e.g., 18.92 vs 18.57 mAP@5 on CIRCO), indicating that simply upgrading the MLLM in a two-stage pipeline does not resolve the fundamental information-loss problem that OSrCIR's one-stage design addresses.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends CIReVL (Karthik et al., ICLR 2024), the leading training-free ZS-CIR method, by collapsing its two-stage pipeline (BLIP-2 captioning followed by LLM reasoning) into a single MLLM call that jointly processes the reference image and manipulation text. The key conceptual departure is the introduction of a Reflective Chain-of-Thought prompt that guides the MLLM through progressive steps (Original Image Description → Thoughts → Reflections → Target Image Description), drawing on the broader CoT prompting literature for LLMs but being the first to apply it to composed image retrieval. The paper also contrasts itself with textual inversion methods (Pic2Word, SEARLE, Context-I2W, LinCIR) that require training on image-caption pairs, and with ensemble/diffusion-based approaches (LDRE, CompoDiff) that add substantial computational overhead.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

OSrCIR demonstrates that a single MLLM call with structured reflective reasoning can outperform both training-dependent textual inversion and two-stage training-free methods across four diverse CIR benchmarks without any task-specific training, which is practically significant for deployment in e-commerce and search applications where annotated CIR triplets are unavailable. However, the method remains 30× slower than textual inversion baselines due to MLLM API latency, and the paper identifies a persistent misalignment between MLLM-generated descriptions and CLIP's retrieval space, particularly in specialized domains like fashion where domain-specific terminology (e.g., 'sequined bodice') is poorly interpreted by CLIP. Open questions include whether tighter alignment between the reasoning and retrieval modules (e.g., fine-tuning CLIP on MLLM outputs) could close this gap, and whether the approach generalizes to video or 3D retrieval settings.
