## Problem

Model compression via pruning makes networks more vulnerable to adversarial attacks, and existing adversarial pruning methods still fall short of the robustness achieved by unpruned adversarially-trained models. Traditional ensemble defense can compensate for this loss but requires multiple independently trained base models, negating the capacity savings that compression aims to achieve. The paper asks whether ensemble-level robustness can be obtained from a single base model while preserving compactness.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250538/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes standard RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10 and SVHN) that may be clean or adversarially perturbed (ℓ∞ or ℓ2 norm, ε=8/255) and outputs a single class label. A distinctive aspect is that the number of sub-models consulted at inference is not fixed: the system starts with one sub-model and sequentially adds more until a per-sample robustness stopping criterion (based on KL-divergence between successive ensemble predictions and prediction confidence) is satisfied, so the effective model size varies per input.

## Method

EED prunes a single pre-trained, adversarially-trained base model (ResNet-18 or VGG-16) using four different importance scoring metrics (Neuron Importance Score, ERM, Adversarial Saliency Estimation, and Batch-Normalization Scaling Factor), each applied to a different random subset of the training data, yielding 12 diverse sub-models. A Robust Diversity (RD) score—computed from the probability that one or two randomly chosen sub-models both fail to defend—selects the smallest ensemble team with the highest diversity. Training optimizes a combined loss comprising standard ensemble classification loss, a misclassification regularization term that penalizes overconfident wrong predictions, and a compactness term enforcing L1 weight sparsity and L0 activation sparsity to keep sub-models small. At inference, a Dynamic Inference Ensemble (DIE) mechanism sequentially adds sub-models and halts once the ensemble prediction stabilizes, achieving up to 1.86× speedup over the unpruned baseline while matching or exceeding its adversarial robustness at 80% sparsity.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with ResNet-18 at 80% sparsity, EED achieves 86.13% clean accuracy, 55.71% PGD-20 robustness, and 48.13% AutoAttack robustness, using only 2.2M parameters (vs. 11.2M for the unpruned AT baseline). On SVHN, EED reaches 93.15% clean, 55.74% PGD, and 50.18% AA. EED also attains the highest inference speedup of 1.86× among all compared methods, and the performance gap versus the unpruned AT baseline is less than 0.5% on PGD for CIFAR-10.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EED outperforms all seven adversarial pruning baselines on both datasets. On CIFAR-10 (ResNet-18), EED leads the strongest AP baseline HARP by +3.15% on PGD (55.71 vs. 52.56) and +2.77% on AutoAttack (48.13 vs. 45.36), and beats HYDRA by +2.79% PGD and +4.39% AA. Against the unpruned AT baseline, EED narrows the PGD gap to just 0.43% on CIFAR-10 (55.71 vs. 56.14) while using only 20% of the parameters, and actually matches or slightly exceeds AT on AutoAttack (48.13 vs. 48.02). On SVHN, EED achieves 55.74% PGD versus HARP's 54.16% and AT's 56.27%, and 50.18% AA versus AT's 50.14%. EED also records the highest inference speedup (1.86×) among all methods, surpassing HARP (1.81×) and TwinRep (1.82×). The same pattern holds for VGG-16, where EED leads HARP by +2.49% PGD and +3.47% AA on CIFAR-10.

## Relation to Prior Work

EED sits at the intersection of adversarial pruning (AP) and ensemble defense (ED), two lines of work that have largely been developed in parallel. For the pruning component, it follows the HARP [52] strategy of non-uniform adversarial pruning with importance scores, while for the ensemble component it adapts the inference-efficient ensemble mechanism from Li et al. [25]. The key conceptual departure is using a single base model pruned with different importance metrics (NIS, ERM, ASE, BNSF) as the source of ensemble diversity, rather than training multiple independent base models as in traditional ED. This reframes the diversity question: instead of architectural or training-data diversity, diversity comes from which parameters each sub-model retains, motivated by the observation in Sec. 3.3 that adversarial examples shift importance scores differently across scoring methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

EED offers a practical path toward deploying adversarially robust models on edge devices by combining compression with ensemble robustness in a memory-efficient way, and the dynamic inference ensemble further reduces average compute. However, the evaluation is limited to CIFAR-10 and SVHN with ResNet-18 and VGG-16 at a single primary sparsity rate (80%), leaving open whether the approach scales to ImageNet-scale datasets, deeper architectures (e.g., ViTs), or higher sparsity regimes where ensemble diversity degrades. A key open question is whether the single-base-model constraint fundamentally limits the achievable diversity compared to truly heterogeneous ensembles, and whether the RD-based selection generalizes beyond the small model pool (N=12) tested here.
