## Problem

Conventional RF ray tracing relies on visually scanned meshes with assumed homogeneous material properties, producing large sim-to-real gaps (15.7 dB median error) because microscale surface features and heterogeneous material distributions are lost. Scene-level neural representations like NeRF² capture fine-grained RF interactions but require dense spatial sampling (~200 samples/sq ft) and complete retraining whenever any object in the scene changes, making them impractical for iterative design workflows. The paper addresses the need for a framework that combines neural-level fidelity in RF-object interaction modeling with the composability and editability of traditional ray tracing.

## Input / Output

The method consumes sparse RF measurements (RSSI, CSI, or FMCW radar Range-FFT) at a small number of probe locations—only 1.25 samples/sq ft for competitive performance, versus ~200 for NeRF²—along with object poses, Tx/Rx antenna gain patterns, and carrier frequency. The output is a coherent sum of all traced ray paths, each characterized by its total traversal distance τ and product of per-segment attenuation coefficients, yielding the complex received signal S_TX = a·S_RX·e^(−j2πf_c·τ). Because each object is an independent primitive, the simulation can be re-run with arbitrary Tx/Rx reconfigurations or object repositioning without retraining the object models.

## Method

RFScape assigns each object in the scene two dedicated MLPs: a structure model (8-layer, width 64, with 6-frequency positional encoding) that outputs a signed distance and 128-dimensional local features, and a material model (8-layer, width 256) that maps position, carrier frequency, local features, and ray direction to a single directional attenuation coefficient encapsulating all RF propagation effects (reflection, penetration, scattering). Ray-object intersection is computed via sphere tracing—iteratively stepping along the ray by the SDF value until |S(p)| < 0.01—after which the surface normal from the SDF gradient determines reflection and transmission directions, and a Monte Carlo step selects one outgoing ray weighted by its attenuation. Training minimizes the discrepancy between simulated and measured RF signals with eikonal and Laplacian regularization on the SDF, requiring only ~300k iterations on a single A6000 GPU. The per-object decomposition is the key architectural choice: it eliminates the need to model empty space, enables plug-and-play scene editing, and allows new objects to be added or existing ones moved with only 3–5 additional RF samples or a visual pose update.

## Results

On the object-level RF characterization microbenchmark, RFScape reduces the median error from 15.7 dB (mesh-based ray tracing) to 2.9 dB using only ~1 sample/sq ft of RF observations. For room-level channel prediction at 1.25 samples/sq ft, RFScape achieves 15.6 dB SNR on 2.4 GHz WiFi CSI (vs. 10.3 dB for NeRF²), 5.3 dB RSSI error on BLE (vs. 6.5 dB), and 16.2 dB SNR on 60 GHz WiGig CSI (vs. 9.08 dB). For scene dynamics adaptation, RFScape achieves 2.9–3.2 dB median RSSI error using only 3–5 additional RF samples or visual pose information.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is NeRF² [43], the state-of-the-art neural RF channel prediction method. RFScape outperforms NeRF² by 5.3 dB on 2.4 GHz WiFi CSI SNR (15.6 vs. 10.3 dB) and by 7.1 dB on 60 GHz WiGig CSI SNR (16.2 vs. 9.08 dB), with the gap widening at higher frequencies where ray-object interactions dominate. On BLE RSSI, RFScape is 1.2 dB better (5.3 vs. 6.5 dB error). However, on the RFID spectrum task, NeRF² achieves a slightly higher SSIM (0.5061 vs. 0.4498), suggesting RFScape's physics-based ray tracing is less advantageous for broadband spectral reconstruction. In the object-level microbenchmark, RFScape achieves a 13 dB improvement over conventional mesh-based ray tracing (2.9 vs. 15.7 dB median error), though NeRF² is excluded from this comparison as it cannot produce editable, re-simulable object models from FMCW radar signals.

## Relation to Prior Work

RFScape repurposes the MLP-based neural implicit representation paradigm from NeRF and SDF-based geometry modeling (NeuS, differentiable SDF rendering) for RF propagation simulation, but introduces a key architectural departure: object-centric rather than scene-level neural representations. This contrasts with NeRF², which models the entire environment as a single implicit field, and with WiNeRT, which retains mesh geometry while learning material parameters. The paper's core novelty is the decoupling of per-object neural SDFs (for geometry) and a separate material MLP (for directional attenuation) into a composable ray-tracing pipeline, preserving the editability and interpretability of traditional ray tracing while eliminating the sim-to-real gap of mesh-based geometry.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The modular, object-centric design enables a plug-and-play RF simulation library analogous to 3D mesh asset libraries in graphics, which could significantly accelerate wireless network planning and hardware-in-the-loop design. The demonstrated data efficiency (~1.25 samples/sq ft vs. ~200 for NeRF²) is practically important for deployment in real environments where dense RF scanning is costly. Open questions include scalability to large multi-room or outdoor scenes, real-time inference speed for interactive design, cross-frequency generalization of pretrained object representations, and whether the Monte Carlo ray selection strategy introduces bias at high interaction counts. The paper leaves exploration of inverse problems (e.g., 3D reconstruction from RF measurements) as future work.
