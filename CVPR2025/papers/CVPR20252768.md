## Problem

Existing dynamic scene reconstruction methods that define Gaussian primitives in a canonical space and use deformation fields to map them to observation space struggle with fast and complex motions, because they must recover long-range correspondences between canonical and observation spaces from RGB alone. This ill-posed optimization leads to poor rendering quality in regions with significant movement, limiting practical applications in movie production, gaming, and VR.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252768/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes synchronized multi-view video sequences (19–24 cameras, 30–60 FPS, resolutions from 1080p to 4K) and produces a 4D Gaussian representation that can render novel views of the dynamic scene at arbitrary time in real time (450+ FPS at 1080p on a single RTX 4090). Training runs for 30k iterations (~1 hour on RTX 4090) over 300-frame sequences, and the rendered output supports 6-DoF camera control at any timestamp within the captured interval.

## Method

FreeTimeGS defines Gaussian primitives that can appear at arbitrary spatial positions and time steps rather than being anchored to a canonical space, with each primitive parameterized by position, time, duration, velocity, scale, orientation, opacity, and spherical harmonics coefficients. A linear motion function μ_x(t) = μ_x + v·(t − μ_t) lets each Gaussian drift to neighboring regions over time, reducing temporal redundancy while only requiring short-range motion modeling. A temporal opacity function modeled as a Gaussian distribution σ(t) = exp(−½((t−μ_t)/s)²) controls each primitive's active window, enabling automatic adjustment via rendering gradients. Training combines a standard rendering loss (L1 + SSIM + perceptual) with a 4D regularization loss that penalizes high opacity weighted by temporal opacity to prevent gradient blocking and local minima, plus a periodic relocation strategy that migrates low-opacity Gaussians to high-gradient regions to control primitive count. Initialization uses ROMA for cross-view 2D matching and 3D triangulation to set positions and times, with velocities estimated from kNN point matching between consecutive frames, and an annealing scheduler modulates the velocity learning rate from fast-motion modeling early to complex-motion refinement late in training.

## Results

On the Neural3DV dataset, FreeTimeGS achieves a PSNR of 33.19 dB, outperforming the closest baselines Ex4DGS (32.11), STGS (32.05), and 4DGS (32.01) by 1.1–1.2 dB. On the self-collected SelfCap dataset with fast and complex motions, the method reaches 27.41/29.38 dB (entire/dynamic regions), surpassing 4DGS by 1.43/2.63 dB and STGS by 2.44/4.06 dB. Rendering speed reaches 454–467 FPS at 1080p on a single RTX 4090, substantially faster than 4DGS (65–90 FPS) and STGS (142–226 FPS).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FreeTimeGS wins on every metric across all three datasets against all 16 baselines. On Neural3DV, it leads the closest Gaussian-based competitors (Ex4DGS, STGS, 4DGS) by roughly 1.1–1.2 dB in PSNR and achieves the best LPIPS (0.036 vs. 0.044–0.055). On ENeRF-Outdoor, it edges out 4K4D (25.36 vs. 25.28 PSNR) and 4DGS (25.36 vs. 24.82) while rendering at 454 FPS versus 220 and 90 FPS respectively. The largest margins appear on the self-collected SelfCap dataset, where fast and complex motions (dancing, bike repair) expose the limitations of deformation-based and polynomial-motion methods: FreeTimeGS gains 2.44 dB over STGS and 1.43 dB over 4DGS on full-image PSNR, and 4.06 dB and 2.63 dB respectively on dynamic-region PSNR. In terms of speed, FreeTimeGS (467 FPS on SelfCap) is roughly 7× faster than 4DGS (65 FPS) and 3× faster than STGS (142 FPS), making it the only method to exceed 400 FPS on the most challenging scenes.

## Relation to Prior Work

FreeTimeGS departs from the dominant deformation-based paradigm (Deformable-3DGS, 4DGS, STGS) that anchors Gaussians in a canonical space and learns complex MLP deformation fields to map them to observation space. Instead, it lets each Gaussian primitive exist at an arbitrary (position, time) with a simple linear velocity function and a Gaussian-shaped temporal opacity, avoiding the ill-posed long-range correspondence problem that plagues deformation networks under fast motion. It also distinguishes itself from other 4D Gaussian approaches: 4DGS entangles spatial scale and velocity in a coupled parameterization, while STGS uses high-degree polynomials that overfit; FreeTimeGS keeps motion as a single 3D velocity vector, making optimization more stable. The 4D regularization loss (penalizing high opacity) and periodic relocation strategy are practical training innovations not present in prior 4DGS work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method delivers a meaningful quality gap (2.4 dB over 4DGS on the self-collected SelfCap dataset) while sustaining 450 FPS at 1080p, making it a strong practical option for real-time dynamic view synthesis in fast-motion scenarios. The SelfCap dataset fills a gap in public benchmarks for complex, high-speed motion that Neural3DV and ENeRF-Outdoor lack. However, the method still requires per-scene optimization (~1 hour on an RTX 4090), does not support relighting, and the large margins on SelfCap are partly attributable to a self-collected benchmark, leaving open whether the advantage generalizes to unseen scene types. Future work on generative priors for optimization-free reconstruction and surface-normal/material extensions are natural next steps.
