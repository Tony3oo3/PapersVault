## Problem

Existing spiking Transformers rely on dot-product similarity to compute attention scores between spiking query and key vectors, but the binary, sparse nature of spike trains makes this operation ineffective: both 0×0 and 0×1 yield zero, so matching non-spikes contribute nothing and identical all-zero vectors score zero. This fundamental mismatch between dot-product arithmetic and spiking data is identified as a key cause of the persistent performance gap between spiking Transformers and their ANN counterparts.

## Input / Output

The method accepts standard RGB images (224×224 for ImageNet, 32×32 for CIFAR-10/100) or event streams (CIFAR10-DVS) and outputs a class label. Inputs are projected through learnable weight matrices and passed through LIF spiking neurons over T=4 discrete time steps, producing binary spike-train representations of Q, K, and V (each of shape T×n×d) that feed the attention computation. The final output is a standard softmax class prediction after a classification head.

## Method

The paper proposes α-XNOR similarity, a pairwise operation on binary spike values that assigns score 1 to matching spikes (1,1), a tunable weight α∈(0,1) to matching non-spikes (0,0), and 0 to all mismatched pairs, thereby giving spikes higher informational weight than non-spikes while still rewarding non-spike agreement. This replaces the dot product in the spiking self-attention (SSA) module, yielding the α-SSA block. Because α-XNOR scores have a different distribution from dot-product scores, the module also replaces simple scalar scaling with an analytically derived linear transformation (k·Score + b) whose parameters are closed-form solutions that normalize the expected membrane potential to zero mean and unit variance before the LIF firing step. Theoretical analysis (Theorems 1–3) proves that α-XNOR similarity produces a weighted multinomial score distribution with strictly higher information entropy than both dot-product and pure XNOR similarity, and custom differentiable gradients (Eq. 15–17) enable end-to-end supervised training. The module is drop-in compatible with both single-stage ViT and multi-stage Swin-style spiking Transformer architectures.

## Results

On ImageNet, the proposed α-SSA module achieves 75.39% top-1 accuracy in the single-stage ViT (S-ViT) architecture and 80.62% in the multi-stage ViT (M-ViT) architecture at 512-dimensional settings, outperforming all prior spiking Transformers. On CIFAR-100, α-SSA reaches 81.08% (M-ViT) and 80.22% (S-ViT), and on the event-based CIFAR10-DVS dataset it achieves 82.24% (M-ViT). The ablation confirms that the α-XNOR similarity (α=0.3) contributes a 2.01% accuracy gain over the Spikformer baseline on CIFAR-100, with the linear transformation of attention scores adding a further 0.83%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On ImageNet, α-SSA outperforms all spiking Transformer baselines: in the S-ViT setting, it surpasses Spikformer (73.38%) by 2.01% and Spikingformer (74.49%) by 0.90% at 512-dim; in the M-ViT setting, it exceeds Spike-driven Transformer (74.57%) by 6.05%, SpikingResformer-M (77.24%) by 3.38%, and Meta-SpikeFormer-512 (79.70%) by 0.92%. On CIFAR-100, the S-ViT variant (80.22%) beats Spikformer (78.21%) by 2.01% and Spikingformer (79.09%) by 1.13%, while the M-ViT variant (81.08%) surpasses Spike-driven Transformer (78.40%) by 2.68%. On CIFAR10-DVS, α-SSA achieves 81.64% (S-ViT) and 82.24% (M-ViT), outperforming Spikformer (80.9%), Spikingformer (81.3%), and Spike-driven Transformer (80.0%). Against ResNet-based SNN baselines (SEW ResNet, MS ResNet, Att-MS-ResNet), the M-ViT α-SSA model (80.62%) exceeds the best ResNet result (Att-ResNet-104 at 76.30%) by 4.32% on ImageNet, while using fewer parameters (54.6M vs. 78.4M). The paper notes that M-ViT improvements are somewhat smaller than S-ViT gains, attributed to the relatively minor role of self-attention in Swin Transformer's shifted-window design.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Spikformer [54], which first introduced spiking self-attention (SSA) by converting Q, K, V into spike trains and computing attention via dot product. It also positions itself against subsequent spiking Transformer variants—Spikingformer [53], Spike-driven Transformer [44], SpikingResformer [36], and Meta-SpikeFormer [45]—all of which inherit the ANN dot-product similarity without questioning its suitability for binary spike data. The key conceptual departure is replacing the dot product with an α-XNOR similarity that reweights non-spike matches, a modification motivated by the observation that 0×0 and 0×1 both vanish in multiplication, making dot product blind to the majority of pairwise interactions in sparse spike trains.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies and formalizes a fundamental mismatch between the standard attention scoring mechanism and the binary, sparse nature of spike trains, providing a theoretically grounded (entropy-based) justification for why α-XNOR outperforms both dot-product and pure XNOR similarity. The practical impact is a drop-in module that improves accuracy by 1–2% across multiple spiking Transformer architectures on both static and event-based datasets without adding parameters. Open questions include whether α can be made learnable per-layer, whether the approach extends to non-Transformer spiking architectures (e.g., spiking RNNs or state-space models), and whether the gains persist at larger scales or with more time steps. The moderate but consistent improvements suggest the dot-product bottleneck was a real but not sole contributor to the SNN–ANN Transformer gap.
