## Problem

Existing video implicit neural representations (INRs) struggle to model large spatial or temporal motions, as evidenced by poor performance on dramatic frame interpolation tasks. While methods leveraging optical flow or cross-frame constraints partially address this, they still fall short on large displacements. The paper identifies that INR biases naturally control the spatial location of basis functions, making them a principled mechanism for encoding motion that prior video INRs had not exploited.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250416/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a video sequence with frames selectively removed (for interpolation), corrupted with Poisson/readout noise (for denoising), or masked with 50×50 pixel squares (for inpainting), and queries it at arbitrary continuous (x, y, t) coordinates to produce RGB values. The video volume is partitioned into 96×96 spatial patches each spanning 10 frames, with a dedicated 3-layer MLP (36 hidden units, WIRE activation) per patch. Because the representation is fully continuous in both space and time, the model supports arbitrary spatial upsampling (2×, 4×, 8×) combined with temporal upsampling (up to 10×), a capability that NeRV-based methods cannot provide.

## Method

ActINR shares all weight matrices of a 3-layer WIRE-activated MLP across every frame of a video patch, while assigning a unique bias vector per frame per layer. The key insight is that with compactly supported activations (wavelets), biases translate the spatial location of basis functions, so modulating biases over time directly encodes local motion without changing the learned appearance (weights). To enable continuous temporal interpolation, biases are not stored per-frame but predicted by a separate bias-INR (hypernetwork) that takes a continuous time index encoded via Random Fourier Features (40 frequencies, σ²=5) and a patch-wise latent code z capturing local immobility. The frame INR and bias-INR are jointly trained with MSE loss over 2000 Adam iterations per patch, and the entire video is processed as independent 96×96×10 spatiotemporal blocks, keeping the total parameter count around 3 million.

## Results

On the 2× video interpolation task, ActINR achieves 22.9 dB PSNR / 0.69 MS-SSIM on DAVIS and 31.0 dB PSNR / 0.90 MS-SSIM on UVG, outperforming the strongest baseline (H-NeRV Boost) by 1.3 dB on DAVIS and 0.4 dB on UVG. For 10× extreme interpolation, ActINR shows substantially larger margins over baselines, with visually sharper results. In video denoising, ActINR achieves 29.0 dB average PSNR versus 25.5 dB for H-NeRV Boost (3.5 dB gain), and in inpainting it reaches an average 1.6 dB PSNR improvement over the best baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ActINR outperforms all five baselines across the primary interpolation task. On DAVIS, it beats H-NeRV Boost (the strongest baseline) by 1.3 dB PSNR and 0.04 MS-SSIM, and FF-NeRV by 2.1 dB; on UVG, it leads H-NeRV Boost by 0.4 dB while matching its MS-SSIM. Against ResFields (pixel-wise INR), ActINR gains 0.7 dB on DAVIS and 1.9 dB on UVG, attributed to ResFields' over-parameterized weight space making optimization difficult. In inpainting (Table 2), ActINR surpasses DS-NeRV by up to 5.2 dB (Dog: 38.2 vs 33.0) and H-NeRV Boost by 1.6 dB on average. In denoising (Table 3), ActINR achieves 3–5 dB higher PSNR than H-NeRV Boost across all tested videos. For space-time super-resolution, ActINR outperforms Phase-INR by 8.5 dB (2×), 5.7 dB (4×), and 3.3 dB (8×) spatial upscaling, and is the first method to demonstrate that NeRV-based approaches cannot perform spatial super-resolution at all.

## Relation to Prior Work

ActINR repurposes the basis-function interpretation of INRs (from Roddenberry et al. and Yüce et al.) by identifying the bias parameters as the natural carriers of local motion, while sharing weights across frames to encode static appearance. This departs from the NeRV family (NeRV, H-NeRV, D-NeRV, FF-NeRV, DS-NeRV), which relies on convolutional decoders or optical-flow warping to model temporal dynamics, and from pixel-wise INRs like ResField that over-parameterize the weight space. The spatial decomposition into patches follows KiloNeRF's divide-and-conquer strategy, and the bias-INR hypernetwork with random Fourier features provides a continuous temporal interpolation mechanism that sits between Phase-INR's single-phase shift and ResField's full residual weight matrices.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that NeRV-based methods are fundamentally incompatible with spatial super-resolution (confined to temporal only) while ActINR naturally supports both dimensions is a useful architectural insight for the video INR community. The method's simplicity—shared weights plus a small bias-generating hypernetwork—makes it an attractive baseline, and the 10× slow-motion and 4× spatial + 2× temporal super-resolution results demonstrate practical utility. However, the assumption that motion remains within fixed block boundaries limits applicability to scenes with large displacements, and the paper acknowledges that accommodating overflow requires proportional parameter growth. Open questions include whether cross-block parameter sharing can handle global motion, and whether the bias-motion correspondence generalizes beyond compact-support activations like WIRE.
