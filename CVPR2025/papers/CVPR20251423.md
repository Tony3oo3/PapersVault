## Problem

RLHF-based safety alignment has made recent LLMs and MLLMs robust against in-distribution harmful inputs, but it remains unexplored whether these safety guarantees generalize to out-of-distribution (OOD) inputs that fall outside the aligned data distribution. This paper investigates whether OOD-ifying harmful inputs can systematically increase model uncertainty and thereby bypass safety guardrails that were trained only on original, in-distribution harmful samples.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251423/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For LLM attacks, the method takes a harmful text instruction and an auxiliary word (e.g., 'apple') to synthesize a blended nonsensical word (e.g., 'bombapple'), then prompts the model to answer for each embedded word. For MLLM attacks, it takes a harmful image and an auxiliary image, blended via mixup with coefficients α ∈ {0.1, …, 0.9} (m=9 values) across n=5 auxiliary images, paired with a reformulated generic text instruction asking about both objects. The output is a set of model responses from which the most harmful one is selected via an LLM-based harmfulness score (0–10), and attack success is determined by a separate binary-judging LLM.

## Method

JOOD exploits the observation that OOD-ifying harmful inputs via simple off-the-shelf transformations (text-mixing for LLMs, image-mixing for MLLMs) substantially increases the model's uncertainty in discerning malicious intent, causing safety guardrails trained on in-distribution inputs to fail. For LLMs, the harmful keyword is blended with a randomly sampled auxiliary word to form a new nonsensical token, and the model is prompted to interpret and respond to each constituent word. For MLLMs, the harmful image is mixed with an auxiliary image using standard mixup (αφ + (1−α)I^h), and the text instruction is reformulated to request information about both objects in the blended image. The approach is entirely black-box, requiring no gradient access, and the ablations show that mixing-based transforms (mixup, cutmix, resizemix) are consistently more effective than non-mixing transforms (RandAug), while semantically dissimilar auxiliary images yield higher harmfulness than similar ones.

## Results

On GPT-4V (the primary multimodal target), JOOD achieves 63% ASR in Bombs/Explosives, 74% in Hacking, and 47% in Firearms, with average harmfulness scores of 7.1, 4.0, and 7.2 respectively. On text-only GPT-4, JOOD reaches 42% ASR in Hacking and 24% in Firearms, where all baselines (CipherChat, PAIR) achieve 0–11% ASR. On open-source MLLMs, JOOD achieves near-perfect ASR: 100% on LLaVA-1.5 13B in Bombs and 97% on MiniGPT-4 7B in Self-harm/Suicide, substantially outperforming all baselines across all seven AdvBench-M scenarios.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the strongest multimodal baseline FigStep-Pro [9], JOOD outperforms by +40 pp ASR in Bombs/Explosives (63% vs. 23%), +42 pp in Hacking (74% vs. 32%), and +22 pp in Firearms (47% vs. 25%) on GPT-4V. HADES [22] and FigStep [9] are essentially ineffective on GPT-4V, achieving 0–3% ASR across most scenarios, while JOOD consistently exceeds 20% ASR in four of seven scenarios. On text-only GPT-4, JOOD surpasses CipherChat [41] by +31 pp in Hacking (42% vs. 11%) and achieves 24% ASR in Firearms where CipherChat and PAIR [3] both score 0%. On open-source MLLMs, JOOD dominates: on LLaVA-1.5 13B it reaches 100% ASR in Bombs (vs. 77% FigStep, 13% HADES) and 93% in Drugs (vs. 53% for both baselines), and on MiniGPT-4 7B it achieves 95% ASR in Hacking (vs. 74% FigStep, 47% HADES). Notably, JOOD also jailbreaks GPT-4o and o1, models on which all four baselines fail entirely.

## Relation to Prior Work

JOOD departs from prior MLLM jailbreak methods (FigStep, HADES) that exploited the weak safety alignment of the visual encoder and projection layers by instead targeting the OOD generalization gap in the safety alignment itself. It repurposes well-studied data augmentation techniques from the robustness literature (mixup, CutMix, RandAug) as attack vectors, reframing them from defensive tools to offensive ones. The conceptual lineage connects to the broader OOD generalization problem in ML, but the specific application to jailbreaking safety-aligned LLMs/MLLMs is new; no prior jailbreak work explicitly framed the attack as an OOD-ification strategy beyond the aligned data distribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals that safety alignment via RLHF provides no robustness guarantee against OOD-ifying inputs, meaning that even trivially simple transforms (mixing a bomb image with an apple image) can bypass guardrails on models like GPT-4V and o1. This has direct practical implications for deployment: defenders must consider OOD robustness in safety alignment, not just in-distribution coverage. Open questions include whether there exists a principled way to make safety alignment invariant to arbitrary input perturbations, and whether the observed vulnerability is specific to the current RLHF paradigm or fundamental to any preference-based alignment approach.
