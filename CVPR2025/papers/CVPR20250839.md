## Problem

Existing depth-from-differential-defocus (DfDD) cameras either require sequential image captures that are vulnerable to scene motion, or rely on specialized hardware like narrow-bandwidth metasurfaces. Additionally, all prior DfDD prototypes are tethered to wall power and external computers, precluding deployment on low-power, autonomous platforms. This paper addresses both gaps by introducing the first snapshot, untethered DfDD camera.

## Input / Output

The system simultaneously captures a pair of 480×360 RGB images from two OV5647 sensors placed 0.4 mm apart in optical distance, split by a non-polarizing cube beamsplitter behind a single 30 mm lens. After grayscale conversion, SIFT-based homography alignment, aberration correction, and Gaussian denoising, the method outputs a sparse depth map (480×360) at 2.1 FPS with a per-pixel confidence value C = Ĩ_s² used to discard unreliable predictions in textureless regions. The entire pipeline runs on a Raspberry Pi 5 consuming 4.9 W from a 5 V battery, with a working range of 0.4–1.2 m.

## Method

Focal Split's optical design mimics jumping spider layered retinae: a single lens focuses light through a beamsplitter onto two sensors at different distances s₁ and s₂, capturing a differentially defocused image pair in a single snapshot. The key algorithmic contribution is a new depth equation Z = a/(b + Ĩ_s/∇²Ĩ) derived by first rescaling both images to a consensus sensor location c, which isolates the defocus effect and eliminates the magnification correction term (xI_x + yI_y) required by prior DfDD formulations. This yields a pixel-wise closed-form solution requiring only 500 FLOPs per pixel, with optical constants a and b calibrated via a data-driven least-squares fit over known distances. A confidence metric C = Ĩ_s² flags textureless or over-blurred regions where the derivative ratio becomes numerically unstable, and patch-based aggregation (21×21 box filter) further attenuates noise in the final depth estimate.

## Results

Focal Split produces 480×360 sparse depth maps at 2.1 FPS with only 500 FLOPs/pixel and 4.9 W total power consumption on a Raspberry Pi 5. In dynamic scenes, Focal Split maintains a mean absolute error (MAE) of 41.82 mm and a working range of 0.860 m, while sequential-capture baselines degrade substantially (Focal Track: 107.69 mm MAE, 0.295 m range; Tang et al.: 316.78 mm MAE, 0.145 m range). Applying a 40% confidence-based sparsity filter extends the working range from 860 mm to 940 mm. The system measures objects from 0.4 m to 1.2 m distance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

In static scenes, Focal Split ties Focal Track [7] exactly (41.82 mm MAE, 0.860 m working range), which is expected because with no motion, sequential capture is equivalent to snapshot capture. The critical differentiation appears in dynamic scenes: Focal Split's snapshot design keeps MAE at 41.82 mm and range at 0.860 m, while Focal Track degrades to 107.69 mm MAE (2.6× worse) and 0.295 m range (2.9× narrower), and Tang et al. [30] degrades to 316.78 mm MAE (7.6× worse) and 0.145 m range (5.9× narrower). Focal Flow [3], designed for dynamic scenes via motion cues, still achieves only 179.25 mm MAE and 0.600 m range, making it 4.3× less accurate and 30% narrower in range than Focal Split. In the system-level comparison (Table 1), Focal Split is the only method that is both untethered and real-time, consuming 4.9 W on a Raspberry Pi 5 versus multi-GPU or multi-CPU workstations for all other listed methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the depth-from-differential-defocus (DfDD) framework introduced in Alexander's PhD thesis [1], which derived a closed-form depth equation from image derivatives of differentially defocused images. The key departure is architectural: prior DfDD prototypes (Focal Track [7], COD [20], Focal Flow [3]) captured defocus pairs sequentially via time-multiplexed lens or aperture changes, making them vulnerable to scene motion. Focal Split replaces this with a snapshot beamsplitter design inspired by jumping spider layered retinae, and derives a simplified depth equation (Eq. 11) that corrects magnification change via image rescaling, eliminating the additional derivative term required by the prior formulation (Eq. 15).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Focal Split demonstrates that a $500 DIY, battery-powered depth camera running on a Raspberry Pi 5 at 500 FLOPs/pixel is feasible, opening a path for passive depth sensing on micro-robots, AUVs, and AR glasses where active methods (LiDAR, structured light) are too power-hungry. However, the system produces only sparse depth maps (textureless regions degenerate), has a limited working range of 0.4–1.2 m, and runs at 2.1 FPS with unoptimized code. Open questions include how to densify the sparse output without heavy computation, whether the working range can be extended, and how to handle semi-transparent or dual-textured scenes where both foreground and background produce strong derivatives simultaneously.
