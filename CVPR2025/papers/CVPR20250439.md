## Problem

Few-Shot Class-Incremental Learning (FSCIL) requires models to recognize new classes from only a handful of samples while retaining knowledge of previously seen classes. Existing FSCIL methods all require additional gradient-based training during the base or incremental phases, which is computationally costly and risks catastrophic forgetting. The paper asks whether a pre-trained vision-language model like CLIP can be adapted to FSCIL in a fully training-free manner, leveraging both its linguistic and visual capabilities without any parameter updates.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250439/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At test time, the method takes a single RGB image and outputs a class label from the cumulative set of all previously encountered categories. The classifier is constructed offline using LLM-generated fine-grained text descriptions (from CuPL and related sources) and the few training images per new class (5-shot), but no gradient updates are performed. The base session provides abundant samples for accurate prototype and covariance estimation, while each incremental session introduces 5-way or 10-way new classes with only 5 samples each.

## Method

BiMC treats CLIP (ViT-B/16) as a frozen black box and constructs calibrated classifiers through two levels of calibration. Intra-modal calibration refines each modality independently: in the text modality, it blends the original CLIP zero-shot classifier with LLM-generated category descriptions (Eq. 1); in the visual modality, it recalibrates biased new-class prototypes by weighting them toward well-estimated base-class prototypes via temperature-scaled cosine similarity (Eq. 3). Inter-modal calibration then fuses the two calibrated prototypes into a single mixed classifier (Eq. 4), reducing modality-specific bias. To further enhance prediction, the method introduces an anisotropic global covariance metric (Mahalanobis-style scoring, Eqs. 6–8) and a cross-modal category-conditioned nearest-neighbor metric (Eq. 9), combined via a masked ensemble strategy that applies the covariance score to base classes and the nearest-neighbor score to novel classes (Eq. 10).

## Results

On miniImageNet (5-way 5-shot, 8 incremental sessions), BiMC† achieves 93.60% average accuracy and only 3.07% performance degradation (PD), outperforming the strongest training-free baseline FeCAM (90.66% avg, 6.22 PD) by 2.94 points in accuracy and 3.15 points in PD. On CIFAR-100 and CUB200, the framework yields approximately 3.5% improvement over the best unimodal classifier. Against trainable methods, BiMC† (0 trainable parameters) matches CLIP-M3 (46k params) within 0.10% on last-task accuracy and surpasses LP-DiF (8.1k params) by 0.72% and CPE-CLIP (400k params) by 9.63%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BiMC† outperforms all four training-free baselines on miniImageNet: it surpasses CLIP Zero-Shot (88.76% avg) by 4.84 points, Visual Prototype (88.55%) by 5.05 points, TEEN (88.92%) by 4.68 points, and FeCAM (90.66%) by 2.94 points, while also achieving the lowest performance degradation (3.07 vs. 5.12–6.49). Against trainable methods, BiMC† (0 parameters) nearly matches CLIP-M3 (46k params) on last-task accuracy (92.40% vs. 92.50%, a 0.10-point gap) and beats LP-DiF (8.1k params) by 0.72 points (92.40% vs. 91.68%). The largest margin is over CPE-CLIP (400k params), where BiMC† leads by 9.63 points on last-task accuracy (92.40% vs. 82.77%), demonstrating that the training-free approach is competitive with or superior to methods requiring gradient updates. On CIFAR-100, the language modality shows better forgetting resistance than the visual one due to low-resolution images, while on CUB200 the visual prototype surpasses the language prototype due to limited transferability of pre-trained linguistic knowledge to fine-grained categories.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of training-free continual learning and vision-language model adaptation. It directly builds on TEEN's prototype calibration mechanism (Eq. 3 adapts TEEN's base-to-new class prototype transfer) and FeCAM's anisotropic covariance scoring (Eqs. 6–8), while using CLIP as a frozen dual-encoder backbone. The key departure from prior FSCIL work is the complete elimination of gradient-based updates: rather than fine-tuning prompts, adapters, or LoRA weights as in CPE-CLIP, CLIP-M3, or LP-DiF, the paper treats CLIP as a black box and performs all adaptation through prototype re-estimation and score-level fusion. The LLM-generated category descriptions (from CuPL and Saha et al.) serve as a textual intra-modal calibration signal, a use of LLMs in FSCIL that prior methods like SAVC and CEC only partially exploit through distillation or virtual categories.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that a deployment pipeline can adapt to new classes with zero gradient steps, eliminating the compute and storage overhead of maintaining and updating model parameters across sessions. However, the approach inherits CLIP's modality gap and depends on the quality of LLM-generated descriptions, which may not transfer well to domains where pre-trained language knowledge is weak (as the CUB200 results partially show). Open questions include whether the framework scales to very long incremental sequences (beyond 8–10 sessions), whether it generalizes to other VLMs beyond CLIP ViT-B/16, and whether the hand-tuned hyperparameters (β, α, λ_T, λ_I, τ) can be made adaptive rather than fixed per dataset.
