## Problem

Existing crowd simulation methods produce only momentary 2D agent distributions or operate within fixed observation/inference windows, making them unable to replicate the continuous, evolving nature of real-world crowds where agents enter, interact, and leave over extended periods. No holistic framework existed for automatically generating lifelong, heterogeneous crowd trajectories with diverse behaviors and social interactions from a single scene image.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252457/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB scene image and produces continuous crowd trajectories over arbitrarily long durations (demonstrated up to 10 hours at 5 fps), structured as a timeline where agents emerge at specific times, follow multi-segment paths, and disappear upon reaching destinations. Intermediate outputs include a 7-class semantic segmentation map, a binary appearance map marking potential entry/exit locations, a population density map, and a categorical population probability distribution, all of which serve as user-controllable conditions. Each agent is parameterized by type, walking pace, start/destination coordinates, and appearance time, with trajectories generated in 50-frame windows.

## Method

CrowdES alternates between a diffusion-based crowd emitter and a switching-dynamical-system crowd simulator. The emitter uses a pretrained Grounded-SAM for segmentation and a SegFormer backbone to predict spatial layouts, then a conditional diffusion model (50 DDIM steps) simultaneously denoises parameters for all agents in a window, with a transformer propagating features across agents to model group collectivity. The simulator plans paths via a Recast navigation mesh and models behavior as a Markov chain over B=8 states discovered by K-means clustering on normalized trajectory segments; a recurrent trajectory predictor conditioned on the sampled behavior state, historical trajectories, neighborhood interactions, and environmental maps generates locomotion in 20-frame steps. This emission-simulation loop repeats indefinitely, with the emitter populating new agents and the simulator extending existing trajectories, enabling lifelong crowd animation from a single image.

## Results

CrowdES consistently outperforms both SE-ORCA and VAE baselines across nearly all 8 evaluation metrics on 8 datasets (ETH, HOTEL, UNIV, ZARA1, ZARA2, SDD, GCS, EDIN). On scene-level realism, CrowdES achieves the best density (e.g., 0.020 on ETH vs. 0.054 for SE-ORCA and 0.032 for VAE), frequency, coverage, and population similarity across most datasets. On agent-level accuracy, CrowdES attains the lowest kinematics error (e.g., 0.377 on ETH vs. 0.502 and 1.621) and DTW distance (e.g., 1.649 on ETH vs. 2.122 and 3.619), while also achieving the highest trajectory diversity (Div.) on most datasets. The only metric where SE-ORCA occasionally edges out CrowdES is collision rate (Col.), reflecting CrowdES's emphasis on behavioral diversity over strict collision avoidance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CrowdES is compared against two baselines: SE-ORCA (a random surface emitter combined with the ORCA collision-avoidance algorithm) and VAE (a conditional variational auto-encoder replacing the diffusion model in the emitter, paired with the same SDS-based simulator). CrowdES decisively outperforms both on scene-level realism metrics (Dens., Freq., Cov., Pop.) across nearly all datasets — for example, on ETH it reduces population similarity error from 0.640 (SE-ORCA) and 0.351 (VAE) to 0.208, and on EDIN from 0.522 and 1.375 to 0.313. On agent-level kinematics and DTW, CrowdES also leads on most datasets (e.g., Kinem. 0.377 vs. 0.502/1.621 on ETH; DTW 1.649 vs. 2.122/3.619), with the VAE baseline showing particularly large gaps due to its weaker generative modeling. CrowdES achieves the highest trajectory diversity (Div.) on 6 of 8 datasets, confirming that the Markov-chain state-switching mechanism produces more varied behaviors than either baseline. The one consistent exception is collision rate (Col.), where SE-ORCA's explicit collision-avoidance rules yield lower collision percentages (e.g., 0.054 vs. 0.697 on ETH; 0.048 vs. 0.411 on SDD), though the paper notes these are small absolute percentages and CrowdES's neighborhood-aware simulator still produces mostly safe trajectories.

## Relation to Prior Work

CrowdES synthesizes ideas from three distinct research threads: (1) traffic scene generation, which learns to place agents in initial scenes but cannot handle continuous population over time; (2) crowd locomotion simulation (Boids, ORCA, data-driven blending), which produces trajectories given fixed start/end points but does not decide when or where agents appear; and (3) Switching Dynamical Systems from time-series analysis, which models discrete behavioral mode transitions. The paper's key conceptual departure is making the 'emitter' component—traditionally a handcrafted particle system in commercial software like Houdini or Unreal Engine—learnable via a conditional diffusion model, and coupling it with an SDS-based simulator to produce lifelong, continuously evolving crowd animations from a single image. Unlike trajectory prediction methods that require past observations and fixed inference windows, CrowdES operates unconditionally on scene geometry and generates agents incrementally over arbitrary time horizons.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first framework to unify crowd emission (when/where/how many agents appear) with locomotion simulation (how they move and interact) into a single continuous generation pipeline, filling a gap the authors explicitly identify in both crowd simulation and traffic scene generation literature. The proposed 8-metric benchmark protocol (scene-level EMD-based realism + agent-level DTW/kinematics/collision) provides a reusable evaluation standard for continuous crowd generation tasks that prior user-study or group-label-based metrics cannot address. Limitations include higher collision rates than ORCA-based baselines (a trade-off for behavioral diversity), 2D top-down representation, and dependence on pre-annotated segmentation for spatial layouts. Open questions include extension to 3D environments, interactive real-time generation with user steering, and scaling to very large crowds (thousands of agents) where the transformer-based collective denoising may become computationally prohibitive.
