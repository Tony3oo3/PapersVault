## Problem

Traditional NAS methods are confined to expert-defined search spaces, limiting discovery of truly novel architectures, while existing LLM-based NAD methods operate independently without learning from past experiences, leading to repeated mistakes and inefficient exploration. Additionally, code-based architecture representations distract LLMs from focusing on structural design decisions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252289/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

NADER takes a base network architecture encoded as a directed acyclic graph (DAG) where nodes are operations (convolution, normalization, etc.) and edges denote information flow, along with academic literature for knowledge extraction and modification suggestions. It produces modified architectures in the same DAG format, test accuracy on benchmark datasets, and structured design experience records (categorized as failure, failure-to-success, or success). The NAD benchmark consists of 100 test samples across 5 base block types (ResNet, GoogLeNet, ConvNeXt, SE-ResNet) with 10 macro-level and 10 micro-level modification suggestions each, evaluated on executability, quality, and success rate.

## Method

NADER formulates neural architecture design as a multi-agent collaboration problem with a Research Team (Reader extracts knowledge from recent papers into a vector database; Proposer navigates a network modification tree using combined DFS/BFS to select candidate networks and retrieve relevant knowledge) and a Development Team (Modifier implements modifications hierarchically from cell blocks to full networks; Reflector provides feedback via two mechanisms: Learning from Immediate Feedback using a computational graph flow verification tool, and Learning from Design Experience by retrieving 5 relevant past records from an experience database keyed by modification suggestion embeddings). The key differentiator is the graph-based DAG representation that eliminates code syntax distractions and enables isomorphism checking, combined with the Reflector's dual feedback loop that reduces repeated errors and increases modification success rates from 0.40 to 0.68 (macro) and 0.49 to 0.88 (micro) on the NAD benchmark.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, NADER(ResNet) with 10 designed architectures achieves 94.52% test accuracy, surpassing the optimal accuracy within the NAS-Bench-201 search space (94.37%) and the best NAS baseline DRNAS (94.36%). On CIFAR-100, NADER(ResNet) reaches 74.65% test accuracy vs. 73.51% for the search-space optimum, and on ImageNet16-120, NADER(Random) with 10 architectures achieves 49.63% test accuracy vs. 47.31% for the optimum. Notably, NADER requires only 5–10 architecture evaluations to exceed the best results of NAS methods that search 500 architectures (e.g., REA, RS, REINFORCE).

## Baselines & Comparisons

NADER consistently surpasses the NAS-Bench-201 search-space optimum across all three datasets: on CIFAR-10, NADER(ResNet) with 10 architectures reaches 94.52% test accuracy vs. 94.37% for the optimum and 94.36% for DRNAS; on CIFAR-100, it achieves 74.65% vs. 73.51%; on ImageNet16-120, NADER(Random) reaches 49.63% vs. 47.31%. Against NAS methods that search 500 architectures (REA, RS, REINFORCE, BOHB), NADER with only 5–10 architectures achieves higher test accuracy on all datasets, e.g., 94.52% vs. 93.92% (REA) on CIFAR-10. Among LLM-based NAS methods, NADER outperforms GENIUS (93.79% CIFAR-10 test with 10 archs) and LLMatic (94.26% with 2000 archs) while requiring far fewer evaluations. Against the only other NAD method, LeMo-NADe, NADER achieves a dramatic margin: 49.63% vs. 31.02% (Gemini) and 49.63% vs. 27.70% (GPT-4) on ImageNet16-120 test, while using only 10 architectures versus LeMo-NADe's 30. The large-scale experiment (500 architectures from ResNet) further yields 76.0% on CIFAR-100 test, exceeding the 500-architecture NAS optimum of 76.00% and the 10-architecture NADER result of 74.65%.

## Relation to Prior Work

NADER extends the LLM-for-NAS line of work (GENIUS, EvoPrompting, LLMatic) by replacing single-agent prompt-based search with a structured multi-agent collaboration framework (Reader, Proposer, Modifier, Reflector), drawing on multi-agent paradigms like MetaGPT and experiential-learning agents like Reflexion. It departs from both traditional NAS (DARTS, ENAS, etc.) and prior LLM-NAS methods in two key ways: (1) it formulates the problem as open-space Neural Architecture Design rather than search within a fixed space, and (2) it replaces code-based architecture representation with a compact graph/DAG format to reduce LLM distraction. The Reader agent's literature-grounded knowledge extraction is a RAG-inspired mechanism not present in prior LLM-NAS work, which relied on the model's parametric knowledge or fixed prompt templates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NADER demonstrates that a small number of LLM-designed architectures (5–10) can surpass the optimal accuracy achievable within a 15,625-cell NAS-Bench-201 search space, suggesting that open-space design with LLM reasoning may be more sample-efficient than exhaustive search within constrained spaces. The NAD benchmark and graph-based representation offer practical tools for the emerging LLM-NAD community. However, the 'beyond search space' claim is partially constrained (same macro skeleton, matched FLOPs/params), and all evaluations are on small-scale datasets (CIFAR-10/100, ImageNet16-120), leaving open whether the approach scales to full ImageNet or deeper architectures. A key open question is whether the multi-agent overhead and LLM API costs remain justified as architectures grow in complexity.
