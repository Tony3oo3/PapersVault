## Problem

Existing physics-based character animation methods for human-object interaction rely on labor-intensive, manually designed skill-specific rewards that do not generalize across different interactions, making it impractical to learn diverse skills like basketball shooting, layup, and dribbling within a single unified policy. No prior framework enables a single agent to learn multiple interaction skills purely from HOI demonstrations and then compose them for complex long-horizon tasks such as consecutive scoring.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250667/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The IS policy receives a root-local HOI state comprising humanoid proprioception (root height, body positions/rotations/velocities), net fingertip contact forces, and object state (position, rotation, velocity, angular velocity), plus a one-hot skill label; it outputs a Gaussian-distributed vector of target joint rotations consumed by PD controllers. The HLC additionally receives task-specific observations (e.g., basket position) and outputs a discrete skill embedding that drives the frozen IS policy, enabling skill switching without retraining the low-level controller.

## Method

SkillMimic trains a single Interaction Skill (IS) policy via RL (PPO) to imitate HOI state transitions from diverse skill-labeled clips, using a unified reward that multiplies five sub-rewards—body kinematics, object kinematics, relative motion, adaptive velocity regularization, and a novel Contact Graph Reward (CGR)—to enforce balanced learning and prevent kinematic local optima. The CGR models contacts as a complete graph over scene-defined nodes (e.g., hands, body, ball) with binary edge labels, penalizing mismatches between simulated and reference contact states with per-edge sensitivity weights. All skills share identical hyperparameters and reward configuration, enabling joint training and smooth skill switching. A separate High-Level Controller (HLC) is then trained with the IS policy frozen to compose learned skills for complex tasks like scoring, reducing training to ~3 hours on a single GPU by leveraging interaction skills as priors rather than locomotion priors.

## Results

On four basketball interaction skills (Pick Up, Dribble Forward, Layup, Shot) from BallPlay-M, SkillMimic achieves success rates of 86.7%, 79.6%, 99.1%, and 97.9%, substantially outperforming DeepMimic* (19.6%, 68.5%, 98.9%, 97.8%) and AMP* (0.0%, 13.6%, 0.0%, 1.6%). For high-level tasks, SkillMimic with a high-level controller reaches 93.04% (Heading), 79.92% (Circling), 93.40% (Throwing), and 80.25% (Scoring), while all baselines (PPO, ASE, ASE*) fail to converge, scoring below 12% on every task. Ablation on GRAB and BallPlay-V shows the full SkillMimic reward achieves 95.4% accuracy on GRAB and 82.4% on BallPlay-V, compared to 27.0%/7.5% without reward multiplication and 38.6%/13.6% without the Contact Graph Reward.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SkillMimic decisively outperforms both adapted locomotion-imitation baselines on interaction skill learning. Against DeepMimic*, the gap is most dramatic on Pick Up (86.7% vs. 19.6%) and Dribble Forward (79.6% vs. 68.5%), while Layup and Shot are near-parity (99.1% vs. 98.9%, 97.9% vs. 97.8%), suggesting DeepMimic's kinematic rewards suffice for simpler contact patterns but fail on precise manipulation. Against AMP*, the advantage is overwhelming across all four skills, with AMP* achieving 0% on Pick Up and Layup, confirming that adversarial (coarse-grained) rewards are fundamentally unsuitable for precise HOI. On high-level tasks (Table 3), SkillMimic's hierarchical approach with interaction-skill priors achieves 80–93% success rates, while PPO from scratch, ASE with locomotion priors, and ASE* with body+object motion priors all fail to converge (≤11% on any task), demonstrating that interaction-skill priors are essential for long-horizon basketball tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

SkillMimic extends the physics-based character control lineage (DeepMimic, AMP, ASE) by replacing skill-specific handcrafted rewards or locomotion-based adversarial priors with a unified, data-driven HOI imitation reward that operates identically across diverse interaction skills. It builds directly on the authors' earlier PhysHOI technical report [43] and repurposes ASE's hierarchical architecture, but substitutes interaction-skill priors for locomotion priors in the high-level controller. A key conceptual departure is the Contact Graph reward, which addresses a gap the authors identify in prior kinematic-only imitation methods (including interaction-graph approaches) that fail to enforce physically correct contact, leading to local optima like using the head to stabilize a ball.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By eliminating per-skill reward engineering, SkillMimic offers a scalable path toward learning large libraries of interaction skills from demonstrations, with the demonstrated data-scaling curve (1→131 clips for pickup) suggesting that broader skill coverage is achievable through data collection alone. The hierarchical reuse of interaction skills for long-horizon tasks (e.g., consecutive scoring at 80% success) opens a practical route to complex robot behaviors without bespoke reward design for each task. However, the framework is currently limited to a single object (basketball), a single scene, and simulation; generalization to diverse object geometries, multi-object perception, and sim-to-real transfer remain significant open challenges that the authors acknowledge but do not address.
