## Problem

Existing video reasoning benchmarks focus on typical, predictable scenarios, making it difficult to determine whether VLM performance reflects genuine reasoning or mere statistical recall from training data. The paper argues that unexpected, out-of-distribution events—where regurgitation of seen patterns is insufficient—provide a clearer probe of abductive reasoning (inferring the most plausible hidden cause) and defeasible reasoning (revising hypotheses in light of new evidence), two capabilities critical for real-world autonomous decision-making.

## Input / Output

Each video is segmented into three parts—pre-event (V_pre), main event (V_main), and post-event (V_post)—and different tasks deliberately withhold segments to constrain available visual information: Forecaster receives only V_pre, Detective receives V_pre and V_post (with V_main hidden), and Reporter receives the full video. Questions come in three formats: 3-option MCQ, binary yes/no hypothesis validation, and free-text generation (3 responses for Forecaster/Detective, 1 for Reporter). The benchmark comprises 15,469 questions across 1,655 short clips (median 8.83 s) sourced from the Oops! YouTube fail-video dataset.

## Method

The core contribution is a three-task benchmark (Forecaster, Detective, Reporter) built around a structured annotation pipeline where annotators first predict what happens next from V_pre, then explain the hidden V_main given V_pre + V_post (abductive), and finally describe the full video while validating or invalidating earlier hypotheses (defeasible). Distractors for MCQ and Y/N questions are constructed from hypotheses that annotators themselves invalidated upon seeing new visual evidence, ensuring that incorrect options are plausible but contradicted by the video. Evaluation combines automatic metrics (CLIP similarity, LLM-Match via Llama 3.1 8B) with human ratings on correctness, thoughtfulness, detail, and visual entailment. The paper further probes model limitations through ablation studies substituting human-written perception/comprehension text for model outputs, chain-of-thought prompting, and a hard subset of maximally unpredictable events, revealing that perception and comprehension gaps account for a significant portion of the 25–32% human-model performance gap.

## Results

On the BlackSwanSuite benchmark, GPT-4o achieves the best model performance across all discriminative tasks: 65.1% on Detective-MCQ, 62.4% on Detective-Y/N, 79.3% on Reporter-MCQ, and 60.1% on Reporter-Y/N. Humans substantially outperform all models, reaching 90.0% (Detective-MCQ), 85.3% (Detective-Y/N), 95.3% (Reporter-MCQ), and 92.0% (Reporter-Y/N), yielding gaps of up to 25% on MCQ and 32% on Y/N tasks. Among open-source models, LLaVA-Video leads with 55.9% on Detective-MCQ and 69.6% on Reporter-MCQ, while VideoChat2 is weakest at 29.9% on Detective-MCQ (partly due to 35.4% unparseable responses). On generative tasks, humans outperform all models on correctness and detail, with GPT-4o scoring 3.72/5 on Forecaster correctness versus 3.38/5 for humans.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4o outperforms Gemini 1.5 Pro on Detective-MCQ (65.1% vs. 58.7%) and Reporter-MCQ (79.3% vs. 71.0%), while Gemini narrowly edges GPT-4o on Detective-Y/N (63.2% vs. 62.4%). Among open-source models, LLaVA-Video leads on both MCQ tasks (55.9% Detective, 69.6% Reporter), surpassing VILA-1.5 (51.8%, 54.3%) and VideoLLaMA2 (53.5%, 53.2%), while VideoChat2 trails significantly on MCQ (29.9% Detective) due to parsing failures. The best model (GPT-4o) still lags humans by 24.9% on Detective-MCQ, 26% on Detective-Y/N, 21% on Reporter-MCQ, and 32% on Reporter-Y/N, indicating a substantial gap in abductive and defeasible video reasoning. On generative tasks, GPT-4o achieves the highest CLIP scores (0.77–0.78) and LLM-Match scores (1.64–2.99) among models, but humans still lead on correctness (4.11–4.40/5 vs. 3.41–4.10/5 for GPT-4o) and visual entailment (0.94–0.95 vs. 0.78–0.90). Chain-of-thought prompting improves Detective-MCQ by 5.3% for GPT-4o but degrades Reporter-MCQ by 3.6%, suggesting CoT helps abductive inference but can introduce harmful assumptions when full video context is available.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes two established reasoning paradigms—abductive reasoning (finding the most plausible explanation from partial observations, as in Bhagavatula et al. and the visual Sherlock/VideoABC benchmarks) and defeasible reasoning (revising conclusions upon new evidence, as in Rudinger et al. and Cui et al.)—into a unified video benchmark focused on unexpected events. It departs from prior video reasoning benchmarks (NExT-QA, CLEVRER, VCR) that target predictable, in-distribution scenarios, and from prior visual abductive work that does not measure defeasibility. The authors explicitly note that no prior work has combined defeasible reasoning with unexpected events in a multi-frame video format, carving out a new intersection of these threads.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

BlackSwanSuite exposes a concrete and measurable gap (up to 32% on Y/N tasks) between state-of-the-art VLMs and humans on reasoning about out-of-distribution events, suggesting that current models rely heavily on pattern recall rather than genuine abductive or defeasible inference. The perception/comprehension ablation (showing +10% gain from human-written captions) and the CoT result (helping Detective but hurting Reporter) point to specific, addressable bottlenecks—foundational visual perception and over-reliance on prior expectations—rather than a single monolithic failure. Open questions include whether scaling or better video encoders can close the perception gap, and whether the benchmark's reliance on YouTube fail videos generalizes to other domains of unexpected events.
