## Problem

While recent work shows that jointly fine-tuning CLIP's text and image encoders improves open-vocabulary semantic segmentation, the underlying mechanism remains unexplained—freezing the text encoder was long believed to be necessary for generalization. This paper asks why adjusting text embeddings helps pixel-level prediction and how to exploit this insight with minimal learnable parameters.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250772/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images and free-form text class descriptions, producing per-pixel semantic segmentation masks over an open vocabulary. It is trained on COCO-Stuff (171 classes, ~118K images) and evaluated zero-shot on ADE20K (150/847 classes), PASCAL VOC (20 classes), and PASCAL-Context (59/459 classes). The block-diagonal scaling matrices are inserted into the self-attention modules of every CLIP ViT layer, with the Poincaré ball curvature fixed at c=0.01.

## Method

HyperCLIP projects CLIP's feature embeddings into a Poincaré ball and applies block-diagonal scaling matrices via Möbius matrix multiplication, which provably adjusts the hyperbolic radius (i.e., hierarchical level) of the embeddings without altering their direction. The key insight is that fine-tuning for segmentation shifts text embeddings from a larger radius (image-level semantics) to a smaller radius (pixel-level semantics), and this radius shift can be achieved with only ~4% of CLIP's parameters (5.6M for ViT-B/16) by learning the scaling blocks while freezing all original weights. A theoretical result (Theorem 1) establishes that the scaling parameter monotonically controls the hyperbolic radius, and ablations confirm that jointly adjusting both encoders to the pixel-level radius outperforms adjusting either alone. The block size n provides a tunable trade-off between parameter efficiency (n=1 gives O(d) parameters) and expressiveness (n=d recovers full hyperbolic fine-tuning), with n=128 saturating performance on large-vocabulary benchmarks.

## Results

HyperCLIP achieves state-of-the-art mIoU on all six test sets across ADE20K, PASCAL-Context, and PASCAL VOC with both ViT-B/16 and ViT-L/14 CLIP backbones. With ViT-B/16, it scores 12.3 on A-847, 19.2 on PC-459, 32.1 on A-150, 58.5 on PC-59, 95.6 on PAS-20, and 78.9 on PAS-20b. With ViT-L/14, it reaches 16.3 on A-847, 24.1 on PC-459, 38.2 on A-150, 64.2 on PC-59, 98.3 on PAS-20, and 84.3 on PAS-20b. Notably, HyperCLIP fine-tunes only ~4% of CLIP's total parameters (5.6M for ViT-B/16), using block-diagonal scaling matrices in hyperbolic space rather than additional backbone architectures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HyperCLIP outperforms the strongest selective fine-tuning baseline CAT-Seg [9] on every metric with both model sizes, e.g., 12.3 vs. 12.0 on A-847 and 58.5 vs. 57.5 on PC-59 (ViT-B/16), and 16.3 vs. 16.0 on A-847 and 64.2 vs. 63.3 on PC-59 (ViT-L/14). Against the parameter-efficient SAN [62], HyperCLIP gains 6.6 mIoU on PC-459 and 4.7 mIoU on PC-59 with ViT-B/16, while using fewer learnable parameters (5.6M vs. 8.4M). Compared to FC-CLIP [65] (ViT-L/14), HyperCLIP leads by 1.5 on A-847, 5.9 on PC-459, 4.1 on A-150, and 5.8 on PC-59. Against ODISE [60], which relies on a Stable Diffusion backbone, HyperCLIP surpasses it by 5.2 on A-847 and 6.9 on PC-459 (ViT-L/14). On the object detection side (Table 2), HyperCLIP achieves 26.3 AP-base50 and 42.7 AP-novel50, beating both CLIM [58] (25.7/42.5) and CAT-Seg [9] (25.2/42.3), and also outperforming HyperLoRA [64] (25.1/42.0) in hyperbolic space. The key differentiator is that HyperCLIP requires no additional backbone or adapter architecture—only block-diagonal scaling matrices in hyperbolic space—yet consistently achieves the best mIoU across all benchmarks.

## Relation to Prior Work

HyperCLIP builds on the hyperbolic fine-tuning framework of hyperLoRA [64], which applies general linear transformations in the Poincaré ball, but departs from it by restricting to block-diagonal scaling matrices whose sole purpose is to adjust the hyperbolic radius of CLIP embeddings. Conceptually, it reframes the open-vocabulary segmentation fine-tuning problem through a hierarchical-alignment lens: prior work (CAT-Seg [9], SED [59]) empirically showed that jointly fine-tuning text and image encoders outperforms freezing the text encoder, but offered no mechanistic explanation; HyperCLIP provides one by showing that fine-tuning shifts text embeddings from image-level to pixel-level hierarchical radii. It also positions itself against parameter-efficient baselines like SAN [62] and LoRA [21], which operate entirely in Euclidean space and cannot capture this hierarchical transition.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The observation that CLIP's text embeddings converge to a relatively fixed hyperbolic radius (~6.0) across different datasets after fine-tuning is a genuinely new empirical finding that suggests the granularity of a vision task may be quantifiable in hyperbolic geometry, opening a direction for task-agnostic fine-tuning. The method achieves SOTA with only ~4% of CLIP's parameters (5.6M vs. 39.5M for CAT-Seg), making it attractive for practitioners who need strong segmentation without sacrificing generalization. However, the performance margins over CAT-Seg are modest (0.3–1.0 mIoU on most benchmarks), and the fixed-radius observation is demonstrated on a single task family (segmentation); whether it generalizes to detection, classification, or other hierarchical transitions remains an open question that the authors themselves flag for future exploration.
