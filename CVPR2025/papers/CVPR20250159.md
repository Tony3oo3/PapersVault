## Problem

Prior world models in reinforcement learning have been evaluated only indirectly through downstream task performance, leaving their core predictive capability unmeasured. This paper asks: how good are world models at their fundamental job—predicting future physical states given current state and actions—and can sufficiently accurate long-horizon predictions enable zero-shot policy learning entirely in predicted space?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250159/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

MoSim consumes the full continuous physical state vector (joint angles q and angular velocities q̇, plus spatial positions/velocities as applicable) together with a time-varying action sequence (joint torques or control signals), and outputs a continuous trajectory of future physical states over horizons ranging from 3 to 400 steps. Predictions are made in raw state space rather than a learned latent space, and the benchmark evaluates both random (Poisson-sampled) and policy-generated action sequences to test in-distribution and out-of-distribution generalization.

## Method

MoSim decomposes robot dynamics into a physics-structured predictor and data-driven correctors, integrated via Neural ODE with the DOPRI5 adaptive Runge-Kutta solver. The predictor encodes the rigid-body equation s̈ = M(s)[b(s) + τ(a)] using separate ResNet encoders for the mass matrix M (constructed as LL^T via Cholesky decomposition to enforce symmetry and positive definiteness), the gravity/conservative-force term b, and the action term τ (an MLP), while one or more parallel residual-network correctors absorb unmodeled effects such as friction and collisions. A multi-stage training strategy first trains the predictor to capture smooth dynamics, then freezes it and trains correctors to model abrupt, non-smooth residuals, yielding faster convergence and better final accuracy than end-to-end training. Because predictions live in raw state space, MoSim can be plugged into any model-free RL algorithm (TQC, SAC) to create a model-based variant, decoupling world-model quality from RL algorithm design.

## Results

MoSim achieves substantially lower MSE prediction loss than DreamerV3 across all seven environments and horizons. On the 16-step horizon, MoSim-r reduces error by 2–3 orders of magnitude on easy tasks (e.g., Reacher: 0.0005 vs. 0.0972; Acrobot: 0.0001 vs. 0.1015) and by ~3× on harder tasks (Hopper: 0.0743 vs. 0.1114; Go2: 0.0430 vs. 0.3685). In the latent space of TD-MPC2, MoSim-e also outperforms TD-MPC2 itself (e.g., Reacher: 2.9×10⁻⁷ vs. 4.8×10⁻⁵; Cheetah: 0.0007 vs. 0.0009). The multi-stage variant (MoSim-rm) further improves on hard tasks like Hopper (0.0375) and Humanoid (1.2737 at 16 steps).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DreamerV3 (RSSM backbone), MoSim wins on every environment and horizon tested, with the largest margins on tasks with smooth dynamics: Reacher (16-step MSE 0.0005 vs. 0.0972, ~200× lower) and Acrobot (0.0001 vs. 0.1015, ~1000× lower). On harder, non-smooth tasks like Hopper and Humanoid, the multi-stage variant MoSim-rm narrows the gap further (Hopper 16-step: 0.0375 vs. 0.1114; Humanoid 16-step: 1.2737 vs. 2.1291), though DreamerV3 with 5-step warm-up remains competitive at 100-step horizons on Cheetah. Against TD-MPC2 in its own latent space, MoSim achieves lower 3-step prediction MSE on all three tested environments (Humanoid: 9×10⁻⁵ vs. 1.1×10⁻⁴; Cheetah: 7×10⁻⁴ vs. 9×10⁻⁴; Reacher: 2.9×10⁻⁷ vs. 4.8×10⁻⁵), and the advantage widens on random (OOD) data (Table 4: Cheetah 3×10⁻⁴ vs. 1.1×10⁻³; Reacher 7×10⁻⁷ vs. 9.9×10⁻⁵). Overall, MoSim consistently outperforms both baselines, with the most dramatic gains on long-horizon, low-complexity tasks and steady improvements on high-dimensional, contact-rich environments.

## Relation to Prior Work

MoSim builds on Neural ODEs [3] as its continuous-time integration backbone but departs fundamentally from prior world models (DreamerV3, TD-MPC2) by predicting in raw physical state space rather than learned latent representations, and by embedding rigid-body dynamics structure (Cholesky-decomposed mass matrix, gravity/Coriolis terms) as explicit inductive bias. The multi-stage predictor-then-corrector training strategy and the direct multi-step prediction benchmark represent a conceptual shift from the field's norm of evaluating world models only through downstream RL performance. The paper also positions itself against differentiable simulators and classical parameter identification, arguing for a data-driven approach that learns both smooth and non-smooth dynamics without hand-coded contact models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The demonstration that a sufficiently accurate world model can support zero-shot policy learning entirely in predicted space is a meaningful step toward decoupling world modeling from RL algorithm development, enabling independent progress in both areas. However, the zero-shot results are limited to relatively simple tasks (reacher, acrobot, cartpole), while locomotion tasks like cheetah still plateau due to prediction horizon limits and distribution shift. Key open questions include: how much prediction accuracy and horizon are truly needed for zero-shot RL on complex tasks, whether the residual-flow uncertainty penalty can reliably prevent distribution-shift collapse, and whether the rigid-body inductive bias generalizes to soft-body or deformable systems.
