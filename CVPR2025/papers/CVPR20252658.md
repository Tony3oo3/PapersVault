## Problem

Existing model merging approaches assume all expert models are available simultaneously and merged in a single offline step, but in practice new tasks and domains emerge progressively over time. This temporal dimension introduces unaddressed questions: should each new expert be initialized from the base model or from previously merged experts, should all past experts be re-merged at every step, and which merging techniques remain effective as the number of candidates grows? No prior work systematically studies these design choices across the full temporal merging pipeline.

## Input / Output

At each temporal step t, the method consumes image-text pairs from the current task dataset D_t (drawn from the FoMo-in-Flux benchmark's 41 adaptation datasets) along with a pretraining data pool of 2M LAION-400M samples, and produces a deployed ViT-CLIP model evaluated on both the 41 adaptation datasets (knowledge accumulation via accuracy/recall@5) and 22 held-out evaluation datasets (zero-shot retention). The model processes standard RGB images paired with text prompts under the CLIP contrastive objective, with training steps per task fixed by a DataComp-Small compute budget of 1.8×10⁹ GFLOPS split across tasks.

## Method

The paper proposes TIME (Temporal Integration of Model Expertise), a unified framework decomposing temporal model merging into three axes: initialization (init_ZS from base weights, init_FT from latest expert, init_EMA via unrolled exponential moving average over all past experts), deployment (deploy_FT, deploy_EMA, deploy_ALL over all stored experts), and merging technique (9 variants from weight averaging to TIES, DARE, Breadcrumbs, MagMax, LiNeS, SLERP, and Model Stock). The key empirical finding is that the Best-in-TIME strategy—combining init_EMA with deploy_EMA and simple weight averaging—consistently achieves the best accumulation-retention trade-off, while the specific merging technique matters far less than the initialization and deployment choices. The EMA mechanism recursively merges each new expert into a running average (θ_t^EMA = f_merge(θ_{t-1}^EMA, θ_{t-1}^S, F)), providing a stable, recency-biased combination that naturally balances new knowledge acquisition against retention of prior capabilities. Scaling experiments show Best-in-TIME improves with model size (ViT-S through ViT-g), compute budget, and number of tasks (T=20 to T=100), eventually approaching the multitask upper bound.

## Results

On the FoMo-in-Flux benchmark (63 datasets, 20 tasks), the paper reports geometric-mean accuracy combining Knowledge Accumulation (A_KA) and Zero-Shot Retention (A_ZS). Standard offline merging achieves 54.6%, while adding data replay improves this to 58.2% and recency-biased weighting further to 58.9%, still slightly below the sequential fine-tuning (replay) baseline at 59.1%. The Best-in-TIME strategy (init_EMA + deploy_EMA with weight averaging) achieves the best accumulation-retention balance and, at ViT-g/14 scale (1.37B params), outperforms sequential fine-tuning and approaches the multitask upper bound. All nine tested merging techniques (WA, SLERP, TA, TIES, DARE, Breadcrumbs, MagMax, Model Stock, LiNeS) perform within one standard deviation of each other under Best-in-TIME, confirming that initialization and deployment dominate over merging technique choice.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central finding is that the specific merging technique is largely irrelevant: TIES [89], DARE-TIES [95], Task Arithmetic [25], MagMax [42], Breadcrumbs-TIES [11], LiNeS [84], SLERP [60], Model Stock [27], and plain Weight Averaging all achieve statistically indistinguishable performance (within one standard deviation) when paired with the Best-in-TIME initialization/deployment strategy (Fig. 6). In the offline setting (Fig. 3), all techniques similarly fail to accumulate knowledge over time, with vanilla Task Arithmetic showing the earliest deviation. Against the sequential fine-tuning (replay) baseline from Roth et al. [68], offline merging trails by 4.5 points; even with replay and recency weighting, a 0.2-point gap remains at ViT-B/16. However, at larger scales (ViT-L/14 and ViT-g/14), Best-in-TIME surpasses sequential fine-tuning in both retention and backward transfer, and approaches the multitask upper bound with sufficient compute. The EMA-based approach from Stojanovski et al. [76] is recovered as a special case of Best-in-TIME and is confirmed as the optimal strategy across all model sizes and task counts (T=20, 50, 100).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the model merging literature (Task Arithmetic, TIES, DARE, Model Soup, etc.) from its standard offline formulation—where all experts are available simultaneously—to a temporal setting where experts arrive sequentially over time. It also builds on continual pretraining work (Roth et al. 2024; Stojanovski et al. 2022) that handles sequential updates but does not systematically study merging strategies. The TIME framework unifies both lines by decomposing temporal merging into three orthogonal axes (initialization, deployment, merging technique), showing that prior offline merging methods and prior continual pretraining methods are specific corner cases of this more general design space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical takeaway—that initialization and deployment strategy (EMA) dominate over the specific merging technique—simplifies the temporal merging problem considerably and redirects research effort away from increasingly complex merging algorithms toward the underexplored initialization/deployment design space. The finding that Best-in-TIME scales favorably with model size (up to 1.37B ViT-g/14) and even approaches the multitask upper bound at larger compute budgets makes it a practical default for sequential model updates. Open questions include whether these findings transfer to LLMs and other architectures, whether the EMA strategy remains optimal under more heterogeneous task distributions, and how storage constraints on expert checkpoints affect the framework in practice.
