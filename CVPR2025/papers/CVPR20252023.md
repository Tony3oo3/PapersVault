## Problem

3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) relies on Cartesian coordinates for both Gaussian positions and scales, making it fundamentally difficult to represent distant objects in unbounded outdoor scenes: large Euclidean distances require correspondingly large scale parameters, and position changes alter projected size independently of scale. This leads to blurred or missing distant details (clouds, mountains, building clusters) while near-field rendering remains acceptable.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252023/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes multi-view RGB images (downsampled to 1.0–1.6K resolution) with camera poses from structure-from-motion, the same input as standard 3DGS. It outputs a set of 3D Gaussian primitives whose positions and scales are stored in homogeneous (projective) coordinates, rendered via the original 3DGS differentiable rasterization pipeline at real-time frame rates (~239 FPS on an RTX 8000). No additional inputs such as depth maps, segmentation masks, or scene-type labels are required.

## Method

HoGS reparametrizes both the 3D mean position and the scaling vector of each Gaussian into homogeneous coordinates in P³, sharing a single weight component w: position becomes [x,y,z,w]ᵀ and scale becomes [s₁,s₂,s₃,w]ᵀ. Because w simultaneously scales both position and size, a Gaussian with small w represents a distant object whose projected dimensions remain consistent from cameras near the world origin, decoupling Euclidean distance from projected scale in a way Cartesian 3DGS cannot achieve. The optimization uses the same L1 + D-SSIM photometric loss and adaptive densification as 3DGS, with w initialized to 1/‖p‖₂ and trained with an exponential activation for smooth gradients; the only pipeline modification is disabling the removal of large-world-space Gaussians so that distant primitives survive pruning. A 1D convergence analysis shows the homogeneous parameterization reaches distant targets in fewer gradient-descent iterations than the Cartesian equivalent.

## Results

On the Mip-NeRF 360 dataset, HoGS achieves SSIM 0.828, PSNR 27.92, and LPIPS 0.194; on Tanks&Temples, SSIM 0.858, PSNR 24.27, LPIPS 0.166; and on DL3DV-10K, SSIM 0.919, PSNR 29.93, LPIPS 0.114. For far-region rendering specifically, HoGS achieves PSNR 30.77 on Tanks&Temples (vs. 29.40 for 3DGS) and PSNR 40.55 on DL3DV-10K (vs. 36.46 for 3DGS), demonstrating a substantial improvement in distant object reconstruction. Training takes 40–60 minutes on a single RTX 8000 GPU, comparable to 3DGS, while Zip-NeRF requires ~3 hours on four A6000 GPUs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HoGS outperforms both 3DGS and Scaffold-GS across all three datasets on SSIM and LPIPS, and on PSNR for Mip-NeRF 360 and DL3DV-10K, confirming it as the best 3DGS-based method in most settings. The most striking gain is on far-region rendering: on DL3DV-10K far regions, HoGS achieves PSNR 40.55 versus 36.46 for 3DGS and 39.01 for Scaffold-GS, a 4+ dB improvement over 3DGS. Against the NeRF-based Zip-NeRF, HoGS is competitive but slightly behind on overall PSNR (e.g., 27.92 vs. 28.56 on Mip-NeRF 360; 29.93 vs. 30.91 on DL3DV), though it matches or exceeds Zip-NeRF on Tanks&Temples (24.27 vs. 23.64 PSNR) and on far-region Tanks&Temples (30.77 vs. 30.41). HoGS also surpasses Mip-NeRF 360 and INGP by a wide margin on all datasets. The key practical advantage is speed: HoGS trains in 40–60 minutes on a single GPU with real-time rendering, whereas Zip-NeRF requires ~3 hours on four A6000 GPUs.

## Relation to Prior Work

HoGS builds directly on 3D Gaussian Splatting (Kerbl et al., 2023) by reparametrizing Gaussian positions and scales using homogeneous coordinates from classical projective geometry, introducing a shared weight parameter w that couples distance and size in a projectively consistent way. It departs from prior unbounded-scene approaches—both NeRF-based (space contraction in Mip-NeRF 360, Zip-NeRF) and 3DGS-based (Scaffold-GS's hierarchical anchors, Skyball's spherical points, SCGS's scene segmentation)—by requiring no preprocessing, segmentation, or auxiliary architectural components; the entire modification is a coordinate-system change. Conceptually, it repurposes a centuries-old mathematical tool (homogeneous/projective coordinates) to address a specific optimization difficulty in 3DGS: the inability of Cartesian parameters to efficiently represent distant, small-projected objects.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is that unbounded outdoor scene reconstruction with 3DGS no longer requires scene segmentation, anchor hierarchies, or spherical parameterizations—simply switching to homogeneous coordinates yields substantially better far-region fidelity (e.g., ~4 dB PSNR gain on far regions in Table 4) while preserving real-time rendering. This lowers the barrier for 3DGS-based unbounded reconstruction and suggests that coordinate-system choices, often treated as implementation details, can be first-order design decisions. Limitations include a ~28% increase in Gaussian count (higher memory/storage), inherited 3DGS visibility-ordering artifacts, and potential computational cost in high-frequency background regions. Open questions include whether the homogeneous scaling concept extends to other explicit representations (e.g., mesh-based or voxel-based) and whether the approach generalizes to dynamic or multi-scale scenes.
