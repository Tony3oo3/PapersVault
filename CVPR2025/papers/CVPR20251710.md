## Problem

Multi-conjugate wavefront shaping systems use multiple planar SLMs to approximate the 3D scattering aberration of tissue, but it is unclear how many layers are actually needed for a good correction and whether a small number of layers (2–4) provides any practical benefit over a single layer. The paper addresses this by deriving a sampling theory for transmission matrices that quantifies the minimum layer count and the degradation of correction quality under sparser approximations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251710/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a complex-valued transmission matrix (measured via coherent illumination and camera recording, sampled at Nyquist pitch Δp = λ/(2NA) over a support Ωp×Ωp) together with a chosen number of layers M. It outputs a set of M two-dimensional aberration patterns ρm(x,y) to be displayed on SLMs, a scalar fitting error EM, and a predicted focused-spot profile. The transmission matrix can be expressed in either a Fourier (plane-wave) or primal (point-source) basis, related by an orthonormal Fourier transform, so fitting errors are basis-invariant.

## Method

The core contribution is a frequency-domain sampling analysis showing that the well-known missing-cone degeneracy of 3D microscopy—where the butterfly-shaped aperture limits measurable κz frequencies—actually reduces the number of layers needed to approximate a transmission matrix well below the naive Nyquist prediction M* = d·NA²/λ. Under the first-Born (weak-scattering) approximation the transmission matrix becomes linear in the aberration layers, enabling a clean Fourier analysis: the butterfly shape means low-|κxy| frequencies span less axial bandwidth, and because tissue is forward-scattering its spectrum is concentrated at low frequencies, so the high-κz content lost by sparse layer sampling carries little energy. For bounded support Ωp, the local cone of illumination angles reaching interior points is narrower than the full NA, further reducing the required layer count. The authors validate the theory with gradient-descent multi-slice fitting on both synthetic volumes (toy spheres, Monte Carlo wave propagation) and real lab transmission matrices (chicken breast, mouse brain, parafilm), demonstrating that 2–3 layers can expand the correctable field-of-view by a factor of ×4 to ×25 over a single layer.

## Results

The paper derives that Nyquist sampling theory predicts M* = d·NA²/λ layers for exact reconstruction (e.g., 100 layers for a 200 μm tissue at NA=0.5, λ=500 nm), but shows that the missing cone structure and forward-scattering nature of tissue allow far fewer layers to achieve low fitting error. For a synthetic volume, ~6 layers suffice for a 40×40 μm support, while for a real 170 μm chicken breast sample, only 3 layers correct a 13×13 μm window. The correctable field-of-view scales super-linearly with layer count: M=1 corrects ~1×1 μm, M=2 corrects ~2×2 μm (×4 area), and M=3 corrects ~5×5 μm (×25 area) in simulation, or ~3×3 μm to 13×13 μm (×18 area) in the lab experiment.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against named competing methods in the traditional sense; instead, it benchmarks different numbers of correction layers (M) against each other and against the Nyquist upper bound. The single-layer baseline (M=1) can only correct a very small ~1–3 μm spot, while adding just 2–3 layers expands the correctable area by a factor of 4–25, far exceeding the linear ×2–×3 speedup one might expect. The Nyquist prediction of ~100 layers for a 200 μm tissue is shown to be a significant overestimate: the missing cone in the transmission matrix spectrum means many axial frequencies are never sampled, so the fitting error decays faster than the linear rate predicted by naive low-pass analysis. The lab results on a 170 μm chicken breast confirm that 3 layers achieve good focusing over 13×13 μm, validating the theoretical prediction that a small number of SLM layers (2–4) provides a large practical benefit over single-layer wavefront shaping for sequential scanning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the multi-slice fitting framework used in diffraction tomography (Chowdhury et al. [11]) and the multi-conjugate adaptive optics concept [16, 27, 35, 36], but departs from prior work by providing a systematic Fourier-domain sampling theory for transmission matrices rather than relying on empirical demonstrations. It draws analytical inspiration from [20] on frequency content limits of mask-based systems, and applies the first-Born/weak-scattering approximation to derive closed-form layer-count predictions. Unlike previous experimental demonstrations that used at most two SLMs, this work analytically characterizes how fitting error scales with layer number, tissue thickness, and support size.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that a small number of SLM layers (2–4) can expand the correctable field-of-view by a factor far exceeding the layer count (e.g., ×18 with 3 layers vs. 1), which could significantly accelerate sequential wavefront-shaping scans in biomedical imaging. However, the fundamental limitation persists: a few hundred-micron tissue volume still requires a few dozen layers for full correction, which remains impractical for physical multi-conjugate systems. Open questions include whether the analysis holds for strongly scattering or dynamically changing media, and whether the linear Born approximation is sufficient for the optical depths encountered in deep tissue imaging.
