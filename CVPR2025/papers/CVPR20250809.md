## Problem

Naively incorporating large-scale out-of-domain video data (diverse environments, objects, skills, embodiments) into trajectory model training causes performance degradation on in-domain tasks (57.6 → 52.0 success rate), and it remains unclear how to fuse complementary multi-domain information within a single unified model while maintaining computational efficiency during scaling. The paper asks whether a sparsely-gated MoE architecture can resolve the tension between parameter cooperation and specialization that dense models face when jointly training on heterogeneous data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250809/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The trajectory model takes an RGB image observation, a set of K 2D query points sampled on the image frame, and a language instruction, and outputs H×K×2 pixel coordinates predicting where each query point will appear over the next H timesteps in the camera frame. The downstream policy model consumes the predicted 2D trajectories and current observations to output executable robot actions. For the adaptive policy conditioning, the 2D trajectories are rendered into a mask modality where each trajectory point is a learnable embedding, concatenated with the image to form an H×W×4 tensor before encoding, providing explicit spatial alignment between trajectory and observation.

## Method

Tra-MoE replaces selected layers of ATM's track transformer with sparsely-gated MoE blocks using a Top-1 gating strategy (K=1), which keeps FLOPs per token constant while allowing different experts to specialize on different data domains and even different tokens within the same input. The key empirical finding is that router z-loss improves training stability and performance, while load-balancing loss and gating noise both degrade performance because they force experts to share parameters across large domain gaps, undermining specialization. For policy conditioning, the paper introduces an adaptive mask where each 2D trajectory point is instantiated as a learnable embedding rather than a fixed value, concatenated with the image on the channel dimension before encoding. The policy then applies early fusion (track tokens with image tokens in a fusion transformer) followed by late fusion (raw trajectory data along the channel dimension) to preserve original trajectory information for action prediction.

## Results

On the LIBERO benchmark with RLbench out-of-domain video data, Tra-MoE achieves an average success rate of 54.5%, outperforming the ATM dense baseline (41.9%) by 12.6 points, with the largest gains on LIBERO-Goal (+20.0) and LIBERO-Long (+22.0). When combined with the adaptive policy conditioning technique, Tra-MoE further improves to 66.3% average success rate (vs. 61.4% without it). In real-world dual-arm robot experiments, the full pipeline (human video + MoE + adaptive mask) reaches 56% average success rate compared to 38% for the baseline without any of these components.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's sole named baseline is ATM (Any-point Trajectory Modeling, Wen et al. 2023), which uses a dense track transformer for trajectory prediction. Tra-MoE consistently outperforms ATM across all LIBERO suites when trained with out-of-domain RLbench data: 54.5% vs. 41.9% average success rate (a 12.6-point margin), with particularly large improvements on LIBERO-Goal (+20.0) and LIBERO-Long (+22.0). The advantage is further widened by the adaptive policy conditioning technique, which adds another 4.9 points (66.3% vs. 61.4%) over the ATM-style conditioning. Critically, even when the dense ATM baseline is scaled in width or depth to match Tra-MoE's total parameter count, it still underperforms (48.4% and 52.5% respectively vs. 61.4% for Tra-MoE with OOD data), confirming that the sparse MoE architecture—not merely parameter count—is responsible for the gains. In real-world experiments, the full Tra-MoE pipeline (56%) also beats the ATM-style baseline (38%) by 18 points on a dual-arm robot.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on ATM (Any-point trajectory modeling for policy learning) [63], adopting its track transformer architecture and language-conditioned any-point trajectory prediction formulation, but departs from ATM's reliance on in-domain data by introducing joint training with large-scale out-of-domain video. The core architectural contribution replaces transformer FFN layers with sparsely-gated MoE blocks using a Top-1 token-choice strategy, drawing on the sparsely-gated MoE framework of Shazeer et al. [53] and the router z-loss stabilization technique from ST-MoE [74]. The adaptive policy conditioning technique extends prior trajectory-based policy guidance (e.g., RT-Trajectory's hand-drawn masks) by replacing fixed pixel values with per-point learnable embeddings, enabling the policy to adaptively weight different trajectory segments.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical bottleneck in robot learning: how to leverage the abundant but heterogeneous out-of-domain video data (cross-embodiment, different physics engines, diverse environments) without degrading in-domain task performance. The finding that a dense model expanded to match MoE's parameter count still underperforms suggests that parameter allocation strategy matters as much as total capacity when training on multi-domain data, a principle that could inform broader robot foundation model design. However, the real-world validation is limited to 5 tasks with 50 demonstrations each on a single low-cost dual-arm platform, leaving open whether the MoE advantage persists at larger scale, with more embodiments, or in more complex manipulation settings. The counterintuitive finding that load-balancing loss degrades performance (by forcing shared parameters across domains with large gaps) challenges a standard MoE training recipe and warrants further investigation.
