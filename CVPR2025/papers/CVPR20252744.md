## Problem

Existing garment reconstruction methods face a fundamental trade-off: mesh-based approaches support physics simulation but cannot capture high-frequency fabric details (flyaways, knits, fur), while 3D Gaussian splatting methods reproduce fine appearance but bake in training-pose lighting and cannot generalize to novel deformations. Prior hybrid methods that address both issues require multi-frame video sequences with expensive and error-prone surface tracking, introducing motion blur and averaging artifacts. This paper asks whether simulation-ready, photorealistic garments can be reconstructed from a single static multi-view pose without any temporal tracking.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a single frame from a 170-camera half-dome capture (4096×2668 per view) of a clothed human in a near-A-pose, along with foreground segmentation masks from Sapiens. It produces a two-part asset: (1) a remeshed garment mesh with an albedo texture and PBR parameters (roughness, sheen color, sheen roughness) estimated via differentiable rendering, and (2) ~1M mesh-embedded 3D Gaussian splats with 3rd-order spherical harmonics. At inference, the asset is deformed by XPBD cloth simulation and rendered by compositing the high-pass of the Gaussian reconstruction with the low-pass of the PBR-shaded mesh, yielding novel-pose and relightable garment images.

## Method

The core contribution is a frequency-decomposed hybrid rendering pipeline that assigns near-field, pose-independent fabric details (flyaways, pilling) to mesh-embedded 3D Gaussians and far-field, pose-dependent shading (shadows, indirect illumination) to a cloth-specific PBR model built on the Disney BRDF augmented with a fiber multiple-scattering sheen BRDF. Gaussians are anchored to mesh triangles via triangle-local coordinate frames, optimized with L1+SSIM loss plus an alpha-channel regularization that trains on full (unsegmented) images to preserve fuzzy garment boundaries. The PBR parameters are recovered by differentiable rendering (Mitsuba) against the training frame, while albedo is extracted per-view with a pre-trained intrinsic decomposition network and merged in texture space. At test time, XPBD simulation deforms the mesh, the PBR model renders the deformed mesh into texture space, and those colors are transferred to zero-order spherical harmonics of the Gaussians; the final image is the sum of the high-pass filtered Gaussian render and the low-pass filtered PBR render, with the blur kernel size chosen to minimize reconstruction error on the training frame.

## Results

On four unseen-pose video sequences (150 frames each), the full PGC method achieves FSIM 0.834 and LPIPS 3.38×10⁻², outperforming both baselines. The ablation shows that the hybrid combination is critical: 3DGS-Only scores FSIM 0.825 / LPIPS 3.41, while PBR-Only drops to FSIM 0.809 / LPIPS 4.67, confirming that neither representation alone is sufficient. The method requires only a single static multi-view frame (170 cameras, 4096×2668) and produces simulation-ready, relightable garments in real time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PGC outperforms SCARF [10] by a wide margin on both metrics (FSIM 0.834 vs. 0.764; LPIPS 3.38 vs. 5.00), with SCARF struggling particularly on loose garments like the dress due to its lack of physics-based simulation. Against Animatable Gaussians [30], PGC achieves a modest but consistent improvement (FSIM 0.834 vs. 0.827; LPIPS 3.38 vs. 3.39), with the gap widening qualitatively on loose clothing where AG's linear blend skinning produces blurriness and unrealistic dynamics. The authors note that AG can yield slightly higher PSNR on some frames due to a sagging artifact in PGC's rest-shape estimation, which shifts garment patterns downward; however, PGC avoids this by using the reconstructed geometry directly. Both baselines also lack lighting-awareness, whereas PGC's PBR component enables correct relighting under novel illumination. The paper additionally notes that Gaussian Garments [44] (a concurrent hybrid method) requires 24.5 hours of multi-view registration and multi-frame tracking, which PGC bypasses entirely by operating on a single pose.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on mesh-embedded 3D Gaussian splatting (as in Gaussian Garments [44] and GaussianAvatars [40]) and physics-based cloth simulation (XPBD [32]), but departs from prior work in two key ways. First, it eliminates the multi-frame video input requirement of Gaussian Garments and Animatable Gaussians by reconstructing from a single static multi-view pose, avoiding the computationally expensive and error-prone tracking of deformable cloth surfaces. Second, it introduces a frequency-based decomposition of garment appearance: high-frequency near-field details (flyaways, pilling) are captured by 3D Gaussians, while low-frequency far-field pose-dependent shading (wrinkles, self-shadowing, indirect illumination) is handled by a cloth-specific PBR model combining the Disney BRDF with a fiber-scattering sheen BRDF [66], replacing the Lambertian or pure Disney models used in prior inverse-rendering approaches like PhysAvatar [71].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The single-pose requirement makes garment capture dramatically simpler and more accessible, removing the need for multi-view video registration (Gaussian Garments requires an additional 24.5 hours) and avoiding motion-blur and averaging artifacts that degrade fine fabric details. The hybrid Gaussian-PBR representation is real-time capable, making it directly applicable to virtual try-on, telepresence, and game engines. Key limitations remain: the method ignores pose-dependent changes in high-frequency appearance, relies on an off-the-shelf albedo extractor that produces multi-view inconsistencies, assumes shell geometry (failing for pockets or multi-layer garments), and does not optimize rest shape (causing sag). Open questions include whether close-up fabric captures under varying lighting could yield fully relightable high-frequency details, and whether differentiable simulation could recover accurate rest shapes and material parameters for editability.
