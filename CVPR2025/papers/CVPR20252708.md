## Problem

Joint embedding-based VLMs like CLIP systematically fail to distinguish affirmative from negated statements, collapsing 'a dog' and 'no dog' into nearly identical embeddings. Existing benchmarks (CREPE, CC-Neg) test negation only through rigid linguistic templates, leaving unclear how well models handle the diverse, natural-sounding negation found in real retrieval queries. This paper asks how well current VLMs understand negation across images, video, and medical imaging, and whether targeted data can close the gap.

## Input / Output

For Retrieval-Neg, the model receives a text query mixing affirmative and negative clauses (e.g., 'a beach with no people') and returns a top-5 ranked list of images from a candidate pool, scored by cosine similarity in the shared embedding space. For MCQ-Neg, the input is a single image (or 4 uniformly sampled video frames averaged into one embedding, or a CheXpert chest X-ray) plus four caption options that differ only in which objects are affirmed or negated; the model outputs the index of the correct option. The HardNeg-Syn subset uses 10,000 Stable Diffusion image pairs where each pair differs by exactly one object's presence, verified by OWL-ViT, to isolate negation sensitivity from other confounds.

## Method

The paper introduces NegBench, a benchmark spanning 18 task variations and 79k examples built by extracting positive/negative elements from COCO, VOC2007, MSR-VTT, and CheXpert, generating negated captions and hard-negative MCQs via LLaMA 3.1 paraphrasing, and evaluating five CLIP variants plus SigLIP, AIMV2, CONCH, and BioMedCLIP. To address the identified affirmation bias, the authors construct two synthetic fine-tuning datasets from CC12M: CC12M-NegCap (~30M captions embedding negated objects into original descriptions) and CC12M-NegMCQ (~40M captions with one correct and three hard-negative options per image), both verified by OWL-ViT to ensure accurate object presence/absence. Fine-tuning uses a combined objective L_Total = α·L_CLIP + (1−α)·L_MCQ, where the contrastive term preserves general retrieval ability while the cross-entropy MCQ term forces the model to discriminate among captions that differ only in negation. This data-centric approach yields a 10% recall gain on negated retrieval queries and up to a 28–40% absolute MCQ accuracy improvement over baselines, without architectural changes.

## Results

On COCO retrieval, adding negation to queries causes recall@5 drops of 6.8–7.7% across CLIP variants, escalating to 18–23% on the harder HardNeg-Syn benchmark. On MCQ-Neg, all CLIP-based models perform at or near the 25% random-guessing floor (best: CLIP at 39.2% on COCO), and scaling from ViT-B/32 to ViT-H/14 or switching to SigLIP/AIMV2 yields no improvement. Fine-tuning on the proposed CC12M-NegFull synthetic dataset raises MCQ accuracy from 39.2% to 54.4% for CLIP (+15.2) and from 28.6% to 56.2% for NegCLIP (+27.6), while also improving R-Neg@5 from 48.0% to 57.8% with CC12M-NegCap alone.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OpenAI CLIP, CLIP-laion400m, and CLIP-datacomp all suffer 6.8–7.7% recall@5 drops when negation is added to COCO retrieval queries, and none exceeds 39.2% on MCQ-Neg (vs. 25% random). NegCLIP, designed for compositional reasoning, actually shows the largest retrieval drop (23.0%) on HardNeg-Syn and scores only 28.6% on MCQ-Neg, confirming that compositionality training does not transfer to negation. ConCLIP, explicitly trained for negation, still declines 18.0% on HardNeg-Syn and fails to beat OpenAI CLIP on MCQ-Neg, revealing that its template-based training overfits to a single hybrid pattern. Scaling to ViT-L/14 and ViT-H/14, as well as using newer architectures SigLIP and AIMV2, produces no meaningful gain on MCQ-Neg, indicating that negation is not solved by scale or architecture alone. In the medical domain, CONCH drops 33.2% and BioMedCLIP drops 15.8% on CheXpert negation MCQs, highlighting high-stakes failure modes. The paper's data-centric fine-tuning (CC12M-NegFull) outperforms all pretrained baselines on MCQ-Neg, with NegCLIP achieving the best absolute score of 56.2%, surpassing even the original CLIP's 39.2% by 17 points.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on CLIP's contrastive pretraining paradigm and extends the line of work on compositional understanding in VLMs (NegCLIP, ConCLIP) by reframing negation as a data-centric problem rather than an architectural or loss-function modification. It departs from prior negation benchmarks (CREPE, CC-Neg) that relied on rigid linguistic templates, instead using LLM-generated natural-language negations across images, video, and medical imaging. The synthetic data approach draws on the broader trend of using generated data for vision-language training (StableRep, SynthCLIP) but targets a specific linguistic capability—negation—rather than general visual representations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that negation understanding fails across all model scales (ViT-B through ViT-H) and architectures (CLIP, SigLIP, AIMV2) reveals a fundamental gap in current VLMs with direct safety implications, particularly in medical imaging where CONCH drops 33% on negation MCQs. The data-centric solution is practical and accessible, but the residual gap (best MCQ accuracy ~56% vs. 25% random) indicates the problem is not fully solved. Open questions include whether the affirmation bias is inherent to contrastive objectives or purely a data gap, whether the synthetic data approach transfers to autoregressive VLMs, and how to handle fine-grained negation differences that remain challenging even after fine-tuning.
