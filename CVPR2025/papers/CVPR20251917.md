## Problem

Existing driver gaze estimation methods either treat gaze as a time-independent single-frame event or model temporal dynamics only implicitly, failing to explicitly capture the rapid pupil displacement that is the primary manifestation of gaze change between consecutive frames. Additionally, in-vehicle deployment demands lightweight models, yet prior video-based approaches either ignore inter-frame correlation or incur high computational costs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251917/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes consecutive pairs of 224×224 driver facial crops (obtained via facial landmark cropping) as input, treating them as adjacent video frames. For the IV dataset, adjacent frames are curated by matching head poses within the same subject; for LBW, temporally adjacent images are used directly. The output is a continuous 3D gaze direction vector regressed via two shared linear layers, with the model operating at only 2.70G FLOPs and 5.87M parameters to suit in-vehicle hardware constraints.

## Method

FIFA employs a dual-stream parallel architecture: a ResNet18-based encoder (first two residual blocks, projecting 3×224×224 to 128×28×28) feeds both branches. The FIFA branch partitions low-level features into n×n patches, computes per-patch pixel differences between adjacent frames as queries, and applies cross-attention against all patches in the current frame (as keys and values) to locate and aggregate similar appearance regions, explicitly modeling pupil displacement. The parallel Transformer branch extracts semantic gaze embeddings via self-attention over the same patches. FIFA's attention map is then passed through a sigmoid-gated fully connected layer to produce per-dimension weights that modulate the Transformer's gaze embeddings, preserving relatively static head posture information while amplifying pupil-change signals. Training uses a symmetric L1 loss over both frames in the pair, with a learning rate of 0.001 decayed by 0.5 every 25 epochs over 80 epochs.

## Results

On the IV dataset, FIFA achieves a mean angular error of 7.29°, and on LBW it reaches 5.84°, both the best among all compared methods. The model also has the lowest computational cost at 2.70G FLOPs and only 5.87M parameters, roughly half the parameter count of competing methods (11–24M). In terms of average precision (<5°), FIFA attains 47.04% on IV and 52.38% on LBW, outperforming the next-best STAGE (not explicitly listed but implied by the ablation) and GazePTR.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FIFA outperforms all six baselines on both datasets. On IV, it reduces the mean error by 0.76° over ResNet18 (8.05°→7.29°), 0.02° over GazeTR (7.31°→7.29°), 0.04° over GazePTR (7.33°→7.29°), and 0.09° over STAGE (7.38°→7.29°). On LBW, the gains are more pronounced: 0.57° over ResNet18, 0.35° over GazeTR (a 4.8% relative improvement), 0.32° over GazePTR, and 0.27° over STAGE. FIFA also achieves the lowest FLOPs (2.70G vs. 3.65–12.78G for others) and the fewest parameters (5.87M vs. 11.25–23.64M), making it the most efficient method in the comparison. The closest competitor on IV is GazeTR (7.31°), with only a 0.02° gap, while on LBW the margin over the runner-up STAGE (6.11°) is 0.27°.

## Relation to Prior Work

FIFA positions itself at the intersection of image-based gaze estimation (GazeTR, GazePTR) and video-based approaches (Gaze360, STAGE), departing from the implicit temporal modeling used by STAGE's cross-attention by explicitly computing pixel-level frame differences as attention queries. The parallel CNN-Transformer architecture draws on GazeTR's hybrid design, but the key conceptual departure is treating inter-frame pupil displacement as a first-class signal rather than a byproduct of learned temporal features. The lightweight design (5.87M parameters) also distinguishes it from heavier baselines like XGaze (23.64M) and Gaze360 (14.6M), targeting the compute constraints of in-vehicle deployment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The parameter efficiency (roughly half that of comparable-accuracy baselines) makes FIFA practically relevant for resource-constrained in-vehicle systems where real-time driver monitoring is needed. However, the evaluation is limited to two datasets, and the IV dataset's 'adjacent frame' construction (duplicating images when no similar head pose is available) somewhat weakens the temporal modeling claim. Open questions include whether the explicit frame-difference mechanism generalizes to longer sequences or more dynamic driving scenarios, and whether the modest accuracy gains (0.2–0.3°) translate into meaningful reductions in false-positive distraction alerts in deployment.
