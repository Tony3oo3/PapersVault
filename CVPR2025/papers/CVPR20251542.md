## Problem

Existing 3D Gaussian Splatting pruning methods—whether score-based or mask-based—deterministically remove Gaussians based on a static snapshot of the current training state. Because the scene's point organization evolves after pruning, Gaussians that appear unimportant at one iteration may become vital later, yet once removed they cannot be recovered. This leads to sub-optimal reconstructions where fine details and small objects are permanently lost.

## Input / Output

The method takes a standard 3DGS scene (Gaussians initialized from SFM points with center, opacity, scale, rotation, and SH coefficients) and a set of training RGB images, and produces a pruned 3D Gaussian scene with 62–75% fewer primitives plus rendered novel-view images. Unlike deterministic pruning that permanently deletes Gaussians, the output retains all Gaussians but assigns each a learned probability of existence; only those with near-zero sampling probability (never selected in 10 Gumbel-Softmax draws) are physically removed, and this pruning is reapplied at every densification step and every 1000 iterations thereafter.

## Method

MaskGaussian assigns two learnable mask scores per Gaussian and uses Gumbel-Softmax to stochastically sample a binary presence/absence mask each iteration, treating pruning as probabilistic sampling rather than permanent deletion. The key technical contribution is masked-rasterization: instead of multiplying masks with Gaussian opacity or scale (which zeroes out α and prevents gradient flow), masks are applied directly inside the alpha-blending equations—attenuating transmittance (Eq. 4) and gating color accumulation (Eq. 3)—so that masked Gaussians still pass the α-filter, participate in rasterization, and receive meaningful gradients. The derived gradient (Eq. 6) shows each masked Gaussian's update is proportional to α_i·T_i·(c_i − b_{i+1}), i.e., its potential color contribution relative to what lies behind it, enabling the mask distribution to adapt as the scene evolves. Training adds a squared penalty on the mean mask value (L_m) to control the pruning ratio, and the entire pipeline is implemented as a modified CUDA rasterizer that backpropagates through both present and absent Gaussians simultaneously.

## Results

MaskGaussian achieves pruning ratios of 62.4%, 67.7%, and 75.3% on Mip-NeRF360, Tanks & Temples, and Deep Blending respectively, while incurring only a 0.02 PSNR decline on the first two datasets and actually improving PSNR on Deep Blending (29.69 vs. 29.53 for 3DGS). Rendering speedups of 2.05×, 2.19×, and 3.16× are achieved across the three datasets (e.g., 384.7 FPS vs. 187.8 FPS for 3DGS on Mip-NeRF360). The method uses 1.205M Gaussians on Mip-NeRF360 (vs. 3.204M for 3DGS) and 0.590M on Tanks & Temples (vs. 1.825M for 3DGS), while maintaining competitive PSNR (27.43 and 23.72 respectively).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against 3DGS (the unpruned baseline), MaskGaussian reduces Gaussian count by 62–75% while losing only 0.02 PSNR on Mip-NeRF360 and Tanks & Temples, and actually surpassing 3DGS on Deep Blending (29.69 vs. 29.53 PSNR) with 3.16× faster rendering. Against Compact3DGS (the closest mask-based competitor), MaskGaussian wins on all three datasets: it uses fewer Gaussians (1.205M vs. 1.533M on Mip-NeRF360; 0.590M vs. 0.960M on T&T; 0.694M vs. 1.310M on Deep Blending) while achieving higher PSNR (27.43 vs. 27.32; 23.72 vs. 23.61; 29.69 vs. 29.58) and faster rendering. Against RadSplat (score-based pruning), MaskGaussian matches PSNR on Mip-NeRF360 (27.45) and T&T (23.72 vs. 23.61) while using substantially fewer Gaussians (1.205M vs. 2.184M; 0.590M vs. 1.053M) and achieving higher FPS (384.7 vs. 247.8; 558.3 vs. 396.4). In the post-training setting (Table 5), MaskGaussian outperforms LightGaussian on LPIPS across all datasets (e.g., 0.228 vs. 0.232 on Mip-NeRF360) while using fewer Gaussians. When integrated with Taming-3DGS (Table 4), MaskGaussian consistently matches or exceeds Taming-3DGS's native budget control on PSNR at every budget level while using fewer primitives.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) framework by reinterpreting Gaussian pruning as a probabilistic existence problem rather than a deterministic removal, building directly on Compact3DGS's learned-mask approach but replacing its mask×opacity/scale multiplication with a novel masked-rasterization technique that applies masks within the alpha-blending process. It departs from score-based pruning methods (RadSplat, LightGaussian) that compute static importance scores, and from Compact3DGS's deterministic STE-based masks, by using Gumbel-Softmax stochastic sampling so that the set of active Gaussians evolves dynamically with the scene. The gradient-flow insight for masked components draws conceptual inspiration from token pruning in vision transformers and LLMs, where both active and inactive tokens must receive gradients to balance activation cost and benefit.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The masked-rasterization technique is a general-purpose contribution: any future 3DGS method that uses learned masks can adopt it to avoid the 'death spiral' where masked Gaussians lose gradient access and become permanently outdated. The paper demonstrates that even a 0.02 PSNR cost for 60–75% Gaussian reduction is achievable, making 3DGS more practical for memory-constrained deployment (e.g., mobile AR/VR). Open questions include whether the approach scales to very large outdoor scenes with tens of millions of Gaussians, how it interacts with other compression strategies (codebooks, quantization), and whether the Gumbel-Softmax temperature schedule can be further optimized for faster convergence.
