## Problem

No direct method exists for generating RGBA video (with alpha transparency channels) from text, due to extreme data scarcity (only ~484 RGBA videos available) and the difficulty of adapting pretrained RGB video models. Existing workarounds—video matting on generated RGB or generation-then-prediction pipelines—suffer from poor generalization to non-human objects and unidirectional information flow that prevents RGB-alpha alignment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250944/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For text-to-video, the method takes a text prompt and produces a 480×720, 49-frame (8 FPS) or 176×320, 64-frame (24 FPS) RGBA video using 50 sampling steps. For image-to-video, it accepts a single image plus an optional alpha channel and generates subsequent frames with propagated or newly generated alpha. The RGB and alpha channels are jointly decoded from a single doubled-length latent sequence rather than produced sequentially.

## Method

TransPixeler extends a pretrained DiT video model (CogVideoX) by doubling the token sequence to append alpha tokens after RGB tokens, sharing the same spatial-temporal positional encoding and introducing a zero-initialized learnable domain embedding to distinguish the two modalities. A LoRA adapter (rank 128) is applied exclusively to the QKV projections of alpha tokens, leaving the original text and RGB token pathways untouched to preserve RGB generation quality. The key architectural insight is a carefully designed 3×3 grouped attention mask: Text-attend-to-Alpha is blocked (to prevent degradation from limited alpha data), while RGB-attend-to-Alpha is retained (essential for refining RGB based on alpha guidance and achieving channel alignment). Training uses flow matching on the VideoMatte240K dataset (484 videos, 240K frames) for 5,000 iterations with batch size 8 on 8 A100 GPUs.

## Results

In the user study (87 participants, 30 videos), TransPixeler + CogVideoX achieves 93.3% preference for RGBA alignment and 78.3% for motion quality, compared to 6.7% and 21.7% respectively for AnimateDiff + LayerDiffusion. Quantitative evaluation (Fig. 11) using Flow Difference (optical-flow-based alignment metric) and FVD (preservation of original RGB generative quality) confirms that TransPixeler achieves a favorable balance: low flow difference indicating tight RGB–alpha motion consistency, and low FVD indicating the RGB output remains close to the original CogVideoX model's distribution. The method is trained on only 484 green-screen videos (240,709 frames) from VideoMatte240K with 5,000 iterations on 8 A100 GPUs.

| Method | RGBA Alignment (%) | Motion Quality (%) |
|---|---|---|
| AnimateDiff [16] + LayerDiffusion [63] | 6.7 | 21.7 |
| **TransPixeler + CogVideoX [56]** | **93.3** | **78.3** |

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Generation-then-Prediction baselines, TransPixeler clearly outperforms both Lotus + RGBA [19] and SAM-2 [41] (Fig. 7): Lotus, originally a depth-estimation model extended to alpha prediction, produces alpha channels that misalign with the generated RGB, especially for non-human objects, while SAM-2 cannot produce soft alpha mattes at all and is unsuitable for compositing. Against the Joint Generation baseline (AnimateDiff + LayerDiffusion [63, 16]), TransPixeler wins decisively in the user study: 93.3% vs 6.7% for RGBA alignment and 78.3% vs 21.7% for motion quality (Table 1). Qualitatively (Fig. 8), the LayerDiffusion-based pipeline produces alpha that lags behind the RGB motion and fails to capture prompt-described dynamics, whereas TransPixeler's joint generation with RGB-attend-to-Alpha attention keeps the two channels temporally synchronized. The ablation (Fig. 10) further confirms that removing RGB-to-Alpha attention degrades both alignment and motion, validating the design choice that distinguishes TransPixeler from all compared baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends DiT-based text-to-video models (specifically CogVideoX) to jointly generate RGB and alpha channels, departing from the dominant 'generation-then-prediction' pipeline where alpha is estimated post-hoc via matting or depth-estimation-style models (Lotus, Marigold). Conceptually, it builds on the line of work extending diffusion models to additional output modalities (LayerDiffusion, IntrinsicDiffusion, RGB↔X), but adapts these ideas to the full-self-attention DiT video architecture rather than UNet-based image models. The selective LoRA fine-tuning applied only to alpha-token projections, combined with a structured 3×3 grouped attention analysis, provides a principled framework for modality extension that preserves pretrained RGB generation fidelity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RGBA video generation is a practical bottleneck for VFX, gaming, and AR/VR pipelines, and this work demonstrates that a pretrained video DiT can be extended to a new output modality with only 484 training videos and a small set of LoRA parameters, without degrading original RGB quality. The attention-masking analysis (blocking Text→Alpha while retaining RGB→Alpha) offers a reusable recipe for modality extension in transformer video models. Key limitations include the narrow training distribution (human subjects from green-screen footage), reliance on a single base model family, and the absence of ground-truth alpha for generated content, making quantitative evaluation indirect. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to non-human objects, longer videos, and other transparency-related modalities (e.g., depth, normals).
