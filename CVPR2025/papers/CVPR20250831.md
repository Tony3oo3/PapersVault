## Problem

Motion Expression Video Segmentation (MEVS) requires segmenting objects specified by dynamic motion descriptions (e.g., 'the bird that flies away') in complex multi-object scenes, a task where traditional RVOS methods degrade significantly. Existing approaches simply inject text into a VIS model and retrain the entire pipeline, which is computationally expensive and fails to truly decouple the segmentation from the language understanding subtasks, leading to difficult optimization from randomly initialized queries.

## Input / Output

The method takes a video clip of T=10 frames (resized to 360×640) and a natural-language motion expression as input. The text is encoded by a frozen RoBERTa into word-level features, then decoupled via a text decoupler into static cues (nouns, adjectives, prepositions) and motion cues (verbs, adverbs). Output consists of per-frame binary segmentation masks at H/S × W/S resolution and a binary classification score per motion query; for multi-object expressions (MeViS), all masks exceeding a confidence threshold σ=0.7 are retained, while for single-object datasets only the top-scoring mask is selected.

## Method

DMVS builds on a frozen VITA video instance segmenter that produces frame-level object queries Q_f and video-level object queries Q_v, then trains only a lightweight DMVS module (12.7M parameters) on top. A Motion Expression Encoder decouples the text into static and motion cues, injecting them via cross-attention into frame queries and video queries respectively, then progressively fuses the sentence embedding with both enhanced query levels to produce a visually-grounded motion expression feature F_me. A key novelty is the Motion Query Initialization strategy: instead of random or text-based initialization, motion queries are initialized by selecting the Top-K video queries by classification score, so the decoder only needs to match and refine among already-detected instances rather than segment from scratch. A 6-layer Motion Query Decoder then refines these initialized queries through cross-attention with word features and the enhanced sentence embedding, followed by a classification head and mask generation head that multiplies decoded queries with VITA's mask features. Only the DMVS module is trained end-to-end with mask loss (BCE + dice) and classification loss, while VITA remains frozen, reducing training cost to 7 hours and 6 GB GPU memory versus 17 hours and 22 GB for the prior SOTA.

## Results

On the MeViS val set, DMVS achieves 48.6% J&F (44.2% J, 52.9% F), outperforming the previous state-of-the-art DsHmp by 2.2% J&F. On the more challenging MeViS val^u set, DMVS reaches 58.3% J&F, surpassing DsHmp by 3.0% J&F. On traditional RVOS benchmarks, DMVS achieves 64.3% J&F on Ref-YouTube-VOS and 65.2% J&F on Ref-DAVIS17, both exceeding DsHmp. In terms of efficiency, DMVS uses only 12.7M learnable parameters (vs. 102.3M for DsHmp), 6G GPU memory (vs. 22G), and 7 hours training time (vs. 17 hours).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DMVS outperforms all 14 compared baselines across all three evaluation datasets. On the primary MeViS benchmark, DMVS surpasses the closest competitor DsHmp [18] by 2.2% J&F (val) and 3.0% J&F (val^u), with the gap widening on the harder val^u split where multi-object motion expressions are more prevalent. The improvement over LMPM [10] is substantial (11.4% J&F on val), confirming that simply converting a VIS model with language queries is insufficient for motion expression understanding. On Ref-YouTube-VOS, DMVS edges out DsHmp by only 0.7% J&F (64.3 vs. 63.6), which the authors attribute to the dataset's focus on static expressions with limited temporal motion. On Ref-DAVIS17, the margin over DsHmp is 1.2% J&F (65.2 vs. 64.0). Among earlier RVOS methods (MTTR, ReferFormer, SOC, LoSh), DMVS leads by 4–11% J&F on MeViS, highlighting the difficulty of motion expression segmentation for methods not designed around temporal motion cues.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on VITA, a query-based offline video instance segmentation model, but departs fundamentally from prior MEVS methods (LMPM, DsHmp) that inject text information into the VIS model and require retraining the entire pipeline. Instead, DMVS freezes the VIS backbone entirely and treats motion expression understanding as a lightweight, separate module operating on VITA's frame and video queries. The text decoupling into static and motion cues is inspired by DsHmp, but here it is applied to pre-computed object queries rather than to randomly initialized queries, and the novel TopK video-query initialization strategy replaces the common practice of using language embeddings or random noise as query seeds.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 87% reduction in learnable parameters (12.7M vs. 102.3M) and 73% reduction in GPU memory make motion expression video segmentation substantially more accessible, lowering the barrier for iterative research on this task. The conceptual insight that a frozen, high-quality VIS model provides sufficient object representations for downstream referring tasks—without end-to-end retraining—could generalize to other multimodal video segmentation variants. Open questions include whether this decoupling strategy extends to longer videos, more complex compositional motion expressions, or open-vocabulary settings where the frozen VIS model's category set is insufficient.
