## Problem

3D reconstruction and novel view synthesis of miniature scenes (millimeter to centimeter scale) is largely unexplored. These scenes are difficult to capture due to shallow depth of field from macro lenses, lack of texture that breaks photogrammetry-based self-calibration, and the need for full 360° surround coverage. Existing sparse-view 3DGS methods rely on monocular depth priors or dense multi-view supervision that are unreliable for small, low-texture objects.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251452/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single snapshot image from a custom catadioptric lens containing 8 time-synchronized sub-images (each ~800×800), pre-calibrated intrinsic and extrinsic parameters for 8 virtual cameras arranged on a circle, foreground object masks (via Segment Anything), and an initial point cloud from structure from motion. It outputs a 3D Gaussian Splatting scene representation and full 360° novel view renderings. The calibration is performed once per lens configuration using a checkerboard target, and the re-projection error is 0.77 pixels, which is critical since COLMAP fails on these low-texture miniature scenes.

## Method

The core system is a planar catadioptric lens with 8 pairs of flat mirrors arranged on nested octagonal pyramids, where each pair folds the light path via two reflections to achieve zoom-in imaging of small objects in short distance. The authors derive closed-form ray geometry formulas (Eqs. 1–3) that relate mirror tilting angles and sizes to the effective viewing volume, enabling optimal lens configuration for a given scene bounding box. For reconstruction, they use Hierarchical 3DGS as the backbone with a novel visual-hull-constrained weighted depth loss: the visual hull is carved from multi-view silhouettes, projected to per-view depth maps, and an s-shaped logistic weight (Eq. 7) penalizes rendered depths outside the convex hull more heavily than those inside, since concave surfaces inside the hull may still be geometrically correct. This depth regularization is especially effective for textureless surfaces where color losses alone produce severe artifacts in unseen views.

## Results

On synthetic miniature scenes (8 reference views, 24 novel views evaluated), the proposed method achieves SSIM 0.9783, PSNR 32.48 dB, and LPIPS 0.0265, outperforming all compared sparse-view 3DGS baselines on every metric. The closest competitor, SparseGS, reaches SSIM 0.9756 and PSNR 31.84 dB, while FSGS and DNGaussian lag substantially (PSNR 18.93 and 21.40 dB respectively). On real miniature scenes (1–5 cm objects), the method qualitatively recovers fine structures (e.g., ~1 mm tripod legs) that all baselines fail to capture, and outperforms the COLMAP-free InstantSplat due to more accurate pre-calibrated camera poses.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against four sparse-view 3DGS methods (Hierarchical 3DGS, FSGS, DNGaussian, SparseGS) and one COLMAP-free method (InstantSplat). The proposed method wins on all three metrics (SSIM, PSNR, LPIPS) against every baseline. The margin over SparseGS—the strongest competitor—is modest (PSNR +0.64 dB, LPIPS −0.010), while the gaps over FSGS (+13.55 dB PSNR) and DNGaussian (+11.08 dB PSNR) are large, likely because those methods rely on monocular depth priors that are less accurate for tiny, low-texture scenes than the visual-hull-derived depth used here. Against InstantSplat, the comparison is visual-only (Fig. 10); the proposed method produces noticeably cleaner renderings because InstantSplat's jointly-optimized camera poses are inaccurate for these texture-poor miniatures. Notably, all methods are given the same pre-calibrated camera parameters and initial point cloud, since COLMAP fails on every scene due to insufficient texture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the 3D Gaussian Splatting framework (specifically Hierarchical 3DGS) for scene representation and novel view synthesis, and on the broader catadioptric imaging literature (stereo, panoramic, light-field systems) for the hardware design. It departs from kaleidoscopic imaging by deliberately avoiding inter-reflection between mirrors, trading angular resolution for simpler ray geometry and easier calibration. For the reconstruction side, it follows the trend of adding depth supervision to sparse-view 3DGS (as in FSGS, SparseGS, DNGaussian) but replaces learned monocular or MVS depth with a purely geometric visual-hull constraint, which is a distinct and more principled source of depth information for texture-poor miniature scenes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuinely underexplored niche—snapshot 3D reconstruction of millimeter-scale scenes—where standard photogrammetry and self-calibration (COLMAP) fail due to shallow depth of field and lack of texture. The visual-hull weighted depth loss is a simple, generalizable technique that could benefit any sparse-view 3DGS pipeline where silhouette masks are available, independent of the specific mirror hardware. Key limitations include the fixed 8-view angular resolution (sacrificed relative to kaleidoscopes), the need for one-time pre-calibration, and the convexity assumption of visual hulls that limits accuracy on highly concave geometries. The authors note that the single-shot nature opens a path toward dynamic miniature scene reconstruction, which remains an open direction.
