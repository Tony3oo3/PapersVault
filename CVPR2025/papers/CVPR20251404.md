## Problem

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) for diffusion models randomly samples preference pairs during training, ignoring the varying difficulty of distinguishing preferred from unpreferred examples. This paper argues that such random sampling is suboptimal and that organizing pairs from easy to hard via curriculum learning can yield more effective preference alignment. Additionally, DPO had not previously been adapted to consistency models, a class of efficient few-step generators.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt as input and produces a generated RGB image as output, operating on Stable Diffusion v1.5 at 256×256 pixels and Latent Consistency Model at 768×768 pixels. During training, the model consumes pairs of previously generated images (500 per prompt) that have been ranked by a task-specific reward model (Sentence-BERT/LLaVA for text alignment, LAION Aesthetics Predictor for aesthetics, or HPSv2 for human preference). Fine-tuning is performed via LoRA adapters (rank 8 for SD, rank 64 for LCM) on a single A100 GPU.

## Method

Curriculum DPO introduces a two-stage training procedure: first, a reward model ranks all generated images per prompt, then pairs are partitioned into B=5 batches ordered by rank difference (large rank gaps = easy pairs, small gaps = hard pairs), and the model is trained progressively from easy to hard batches with the training set accumulating across batches. The paper also proposes Consistency-DPO, which adapts the DPO objective to consistency models by replacing the noise-estimation error terms in Diffusion-DPO with a consistency distance metric d(f_φ, f_ref) that measures how well the fine-tuned model maps trajectory points to their initial sample relative to a frozen reference model. The Consistency-DPO loss (Eq. 6-7) uses a sigmoid-weighted difference of consistency distances between preferred and unpreferred examples, with a β hyperparameter (set to 200) controlling divergence from the reference. A minimum rank-difference threshold prevents the model from learning indistinguishable preference patterns, guarding against overfitting to reward model noise.

## Results

Curriculum DPO achieves the best score on all 12 task–dataset–model combinations in Table 1. The most striking gain is in human preference on LCM/D₁, where Curriculum DPO reaches 0.3237 (HPSv2) versus 0.2990 for DPO and 0.2952 for DDPO, an ~8% relative improvement over DPO. On aesthetics (LAION Aesthetics Predictor), Curriculum DPO scores 6.6417 on LCM/D₁ and 6.1829 on LCM/D₂, outperforming DPO by 0.17 and 0.14 points respectively. A human evaluation study (Table 2) corroborates these findings, with Curriculum DPO receiving an average rating of 3.440/5 for LCM text alignment versus 2.846 for DPO and 2.810 for DDPO.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares Curriculum DPO against two state-of-the-art fine-tuning baselines: DDPO (Black et al., ICLR 2024) and Diffusion-DPO (Wallace et al., CVPR 2024). Curriculum DPO wins on every one of the 12 task–dataset–model combinations, with no losses or ties. The largest margin over DPO appears in human preference on LCM/D₁ (0.3237 vs. 0.2990, +0.0247 absolute) and aesthetics on LCM/D₁ (6.6417 vs. 6.4741, +0.168 absolute). Over DDPO, the gains are somewhat smaller but still consistent across all settings (e.g., LCM/D₁ human preference: 0.3237 vs. 0.2952). On the SD backbone, improvements are more modest in absolute terms (e.g., D₂ text alignment: 0.6234 vs. 0.6075 for DPO), yet Curriculum DPO still leads in every case. The independent human evaluation study (9 annotators, 1–5 scale) confirms the quantitative ordering: Curriculum DPO outperforms DPO by 0.59 points on LCM text alignment (3.440 vs. 2.846) and by 0.22 points on SD aesthetics (3.664 vs. 3.454 for DDPO), with all differences statistically significant (p < 0.005).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the DPO lineage from LLMs (Rafailov et al.) through Diffusion-DPO (Wallace et al.) by introducing a curriculum learning schedule that organizes preference pairs from easy to hard based on reward-model rank differences. It also departs from prior curriculum learning in diffusion models (Kim et al., Xu et al.), which used noise-level difficulty, by instead defining difficulty through the preference score gap between paired samples. A second conceptual departure is the adaptation of DPO to consistency models (Consistency-DPO), which had not been previously explored, leveraging the PF-ODE one-step denoising structure of consistency distillation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that curriculum-organized DPO achieves comparable or better preference alignment while using roughly 10× fewer generated training images, reducing the expensive image-generation bottleneck in preference fine-tuning. The Consistency-DPO formulation opens the door to preference alignment for few-step generative models, which are increasingly relevant for real-time applications. Open questions include whether the rank-difference curriculum generalizes to larger model families (e.g., SDXL, Flux), whether the strategy transfers to other modalities (video, 3D), and whether the gains persist when the reward model itself is weak or noisy.
