## Problem

Existing gesture recognition datasets and methods are confined to single-person, controlled settings (e.g., device manipulation, sign language, or isolated gestures), leaving a critical gap in understanding natural, spontaneous gestures that occur between multiple people in realistic social contexts. This limitation hinders the alignment of gesture understanding with other social modalities like language and speech, and prevents the development of models capable of interpreting non-verbal cues in multi-party interactions.

## Input / Output

For temporal localization, the model consumes full-length videos (2–30 min, 720p at 30 FPS, downsampled to 360p at 5 FPS for annotation) and outputs temporal intervals with confidence scores and one of four deictic gesture classes (pointing, showing, giving, reaching). For recognition, short clips of 2–5 seconds (T < 16 frames) are classified as gesture vs. non-gesture or into the four gesture types, with an optional per-person cropped bounding box variant. For VQA, a text question paired with a video clip yields a multiple-choice answer covering human counting, gesture detection/classification, and spatial localization of gesture targets via bounding box coordinates [x1, y1, x2, y2].

## Method

SocialGesture is a dataset and benchmark rather than a single architecture: it curates 9,889 video clips (42,533 gesture instances) from YouTube and Ego4D featuring 2–10 people in diverse social scenarios (games, variety shows, cooking, parties), annotated with four deictic gesture categories, temporal-spatial key frames and bounding boxes for both gesture initiator and target, subject–target interaction relations, and GPT-4o-assisted VQA pairs. Three benchmark tasks are defined: (1) temporal action localization using ActionFormer with I3D, R(2+1)D, or VideoMAEV2 features extracted via sliding windows; (2) gesture recognition with CNN (TSN, SlowFast, TANet) and ViT (TimeSformer, MViTv2, VideoSwin, UniFormerV2) backbones pretrained on Kinetics-400 or CLIP; and (3) a novel Social Gesture VQA task with three subtasks (Global Perception, Gesture Understanding, Gesture Localization) evaluated zero-shot and after LoRA/full SFT on Qwen2-VL-7B against SOTA VLMs including Qwen2.5-VL-72B, InternVL-2.5, LLaVA-NEXT-Video, GPT-4o-mini, and Claude 3.7 Sonnet. The key empirical finding is that all current models—both traditional video classifiers and large VLMs—struggle substantially on multi-person social gesture tasks, with the best 4-class recognition accuracy reaching only 64.72% and no VLM exceeding 70% on the simplest human-counting subtask.

## Results

On the temporal localization task, VideoMAEV2 achieves the best average mAP of 14.73 (vs. I3D at 10.73 and R(2+1)D at 7.29), indicating that even strong video features struggle with multi-person gesture detection. For four-class gesture recognition on full frames, VideoSwin-L achieves the highest top-1 accuracy of 56.18%, while cropping to per-person bounding boxes improves the best result to 64.72% (UniFormerV2-B/16). On the novel Social Gesture VQA benchmark, Qwen2.5-VL (72B) leads overall with 69.97% on human counting and 29.37% on target localization, yet no model exceeds 70% on even the simplest subtask (counting people), and all VLMs score below 30% on gesture localization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

In temporal localization, VideoMAEV2 (avg mAP 14.73) outperforms I3D (10.73) and R(2+1)D (7.29) when paired with ActionFormer, but all three remain far below the performance these features achieve on standard benchmarks like THUMOS14, highlighting the difficulty of multi-person gesture detection. For four-class gesture recognition, VideoSwin-L (56.18%) edges out UniFormerV2-B/16 (55.51%) and TSN-R50 (54.83%) on full frames, while MViTv2-B performs worst (37.98%), suggesting that larger or CLIP-pretrained models do not automatically transfer well to subtle social gestures. On the VQA benchmark, Qwen2.5-VL (72B) leads on human counting (69.97%) and target localization (29.37%), while GPT-4o-mini achieves the best target classification (65.12%) and gesture detection (73.67%); notably, LLaVA-NeXT-Video (7B) collapses to 34.08% on gesture classification, far below its 71.92% on detection, indicating a failure to distinguish among the four deictic types. Across all tasks, the key finding is that no current method—whether a video action model or a large VLM—achieves reliable multi-person social gesture understanding, with the best four-class accuracy at only 64.72% (per-person crop) and the best VQA localization at 29.37%, well above random but far from human-level performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant single-person, controlled-setting paradigm in gesture datasets (Jester, NVGesture, EgoGesture, LD-ConGR, HaGRID) by introducing the first multi-person social gesture dataset. Methodologically, it builds on ActionFormer for temporal localization and adapts the LLaVA instruction-tuning pipeline for VQA data generation, but applies them to a fundamentally different problem: understanding deictic gestures in the context of subject-target interpersonal dynamics. The VQA benchmark for gestures is explicitly framed as the first of its kind, extending the video VQA paradigm (Qwen2-VL, InternVL, LLaVA-NEXT) into a domain where prior gesture datasets lacked the multi-person structure to support language-gesture alignment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dataset fills a critical gap: no prior resource captures spontaneous multi-person social gestures with comprehensive annotations (categories, spatiotemporal boxes, interaction relations, VQA pairs), making it a necessary foundation for social AI research. The empirical findings—that even 72B-parameter VLMs and GPT-4o-mini fail at gesture localization (~29% vs. 19% random) and that no model exceeds 70% on human counting in social scenes—suggest a fundamental limitation in current architectures for multi-person visual reasoning that cannot be solved by scale alone. Open questions include whether models trained explicitly on multi-person interaction data (e.g., Ego4D-style egocentric video) can close this gap, and whether the subject-target relational structure requires dedicated architectural inductive biases beyond generic video transformers.
