## Problem

Generalizable tumor segmentation requires a single model to segment unseen tumor types across diverse anatomical regions and imaging modalities in a zero-shot manner. Existing approaches—SAM-based methods, vision-language alignment, and tumor synthesis—each suffer from limited segmentation quality, restricted category coverage, or inability to generalize across modalities, leaving a gap in high-fidelity zero-shot tumor segmentation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250212/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a 3D medical volume (CT or MRI, processed at 128×128×128 patch size) and open-vocabulary text prompts (e.g., 'An abnormal CT scan of liver tumor') as input, and produces a binary 3D tumor segmentation mask at the original volume resolution. The text prompts are not restricted to a predefined category list, enabling open-set generalization; the final mask is obtained by combining pixel-level and feature-level residual maps via Otsu thresholding.

## Method

DiffuGTS repurposes a frozen medical foundation diffusion model (MAISI) in two roles: as a semantic feature extractor and as a generative mask refiner. First, it constructs Anomaly-Aware Open-Vocabulary Attention (AOVA) maps by computing cross-attention between text embeddings (from a frozen text encoder) and multi-scale features from MAISI's VAE encoder, with a lightweight linear adapter (α=0.1) bridging the generative-to-discriminative representation gap; these maps are trained with a combined BCE anomaly-score loss, CLIP-style contrastive alignment loss, and Dice segmentation loss. Second, it refines the AOVA-derived masks via a training-free latent-space inpainting strategy (extending RePaint to the VAE latent space) that synthesizes pseudo-healthy counterparts of the diseased organ, then applies a novel pixel-level and feature-level residual learning scheme that combines intensity differences with latent-space semantic differences to produce the final segmentation mask.

## Results

Under the leave-one-out zero-shot setting on MSD and KiTS23 (Table 1), DiffuGTS achieves DSC scores of 43.61 (pancreas), 42.94 (lung), 63.23 (liver), 38.72 (colon), 62.76 (hepatic vessel), and 59.80 (kidney), outperforming the best competing method (Malenia) by at least 4.74% DSC across all six tumor categories. On the more challenging unseen-modality setting (Table 2), DiffuGTS achieves 50.31 DSC on in-house MRI liver tumors, 47.51 on MSD-Brain, and 44.70 on BraTS23, surpassing the best baseline by at least 21.28% DSC. The method uses only 284.96M trainable parameters compared to 733–783M for competing diffusion-based anomaly detection methods, though at a higher FLOPs cost (12,876G vs. 6,895–7,143G).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DiffuGTS outperforms all eight baselines across every tumor category and both evaluation settings. Against SAM-based methods (SAM 2, SaLIP, H-SAM), DiffuGTS gains at least 12.84% DSC and 13.22% NSD, with the largest margins on lung tumors (42.94 vs. 25.36 DSC for H-SAM) and colon tumors (38.72 vs. 23.02). Against vision-language zero-shot methods ZePT and Malenia, DiffuGTS leads by 4.74–10.19% DSC, with the gap widening on lung (42.94 vs. 32.75) and colon (38.72 vs. 34.72) tumors where visual cues are subtler. Against medical anomaly detection methods (DDPM-MAD, MVFA, THOR), DiffuGTS achieves a 15.80% DSC improvement, demonstrating that combining AOVA maps with frozen diffusion-based mask refinement substantially exceeds reconstruction-difference-only approaches. On the unseen-modality setting (CT-trained → MRI-tested), DiffuGTS achieves 50.31/47.51/44.70 DSC on in-house liver/MSD-Brain/BraTS23, surpassing the best baseline (Malenia at 29.03/19.83/17.94) by 21.28–26.76% DSC, confirming strong cross-modality generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

DiffuGTS departs from two main lineages: (1) vision-language alignment methods for zero-shot tumor segmentation (ZePT, Malenia), which rely on contrastive learning between mask proposals and text prompts but suffer from weak pixel-level supervision, and (2) diffusion-based medical anomaly detection (DDPM-MAD, THOR), which synthesize pseudo-healthy images but are restricted to specific anatomical regions and tumor categories. The key conceptual departure is repurposing a frozen medical foundation diffusion model (MAISI) in dual roles—as a semantic feature extractor via its VAE encoder (with lightweight adapters) and as a mask refiner via training-free latent-space inpainting (extending RePaint/LatentPaint to 3D medical volumes). The AOVA maps, which use cross-attention between text embeddings and internal VAE features to produce anomaly-aware attention heatmaps, represent a novel mechanism that bridges open-vocabulary segmentation and anomaly detection without requiring a predefined category list at inference.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that frozen foundation diffusion models, originally trained for generative tasks, contain rich anatomical knowledge that can be efficiently repurposed for discriminative zero-shot segmentation with only ~285M trainable parameters (vs. 700M+ for competitors). The cross-modality generalization (CT-trained → MRI-tested) with >21% DSC gains over all baselines is practically significant for clinical deployment where MRI data is often scarce. However, the high inference FLOPs (12.9T vs. 3.9–7.1T for baselines) due to diffusion sampling is a substantial practical limitation, and the authors acknowledge that knowledge distillation from foundation models is needed to make this approach deployable. Open questions include whether the approach extends beyond the 7 tumor categories tested, whether it generalizes to other imaging modalities (ultrasound, PET), and whether the reliance on MAISI's specific architecture limits broader applicability.
