## Problem

HTR research has focused on minimizing in-distribution errors under the i.i.d. assumption, yet real-world applications involve vastly different scripts, languages, and historical periods. This paper addresses the unexplored question of how well HTR models generalize to out-of-distribution data under the Domain Generalization paradigm, where no prior access to the target domain is available.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251185/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Models consume grayscale handwritten text line images at architecture-specific resolutions (ranging from 64×512 to 64×2227 pixels) and produce a transcribed character sequence over a unified 94-character Unicode vocabulary spanning all seven datasets. No post-processing, lexicon-based correction, or language model rescoring is applied to outputs, and evaluation uses Character Error Rate (CER) with Expected Calibration Error (ECE) as a secondary metric.

## Method

This is a large-scale empirical study rather than a new architecture. The authors train eight HTR architectures (four CTC-based, one Seq2Seq, three hybrid) from scratch on each of seven single-source domains, then evaluate OOD performance across all 336 source-target pairs spanning five languages. To identify what drives OOD failure, they introduce two proxy metrics—visual divergence (measured via autoencoder reconstruction error) and textual divergence (measured via averaged KL-divergence over 1–5 grams)—and apply factor analysis to decompose OOD error into latent factors. The key finding is that textual divergence is the dominant predictor of OOD error, followed by visual divergence, and that these proxies can estimate OOD CER with discrepancies below 10 points in 70% of cases.

## Results

Across 7 datasets and 8 HTR architectures, the average in-distribution (ID) CER ranges from 5.3% (CRNN) to 9.7% (C-SAN), while the average out-of-distribution (OOD) CER ranges from 37.4% (VAN) to 53.9% (Michael), yielding a mean ID-to-OOD gap of 37.6 percentage points. CTC-aligned models generalize best (average OOD CER 40.4%) compared to autoregressive/Seq2Seq models (48.7%), a 20% relative degradation. Training on synthetic data improves OOD CER by ~6 points on average (up to 11+ points when the synthetic language matches the target), and OOD error can be predicted from proxy metrics with a mean absolute error of 10.9 CER points, with 70% of predictions falling within 10 points of the true value.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the eight HTR architectures, VAN (CTC, 2.7M params) achieves the best average OOD CER of 37.4%, narrowly beating CRNN (CTC, 9.6M params) at 38.5%; the gap between the best (VAN) and worst (Michael, Hybrid, 5M params) is 16.5 points. CTC-aligned models (CRNN, VAN, C-SAN, HTR-VT) consistently outperform hybrid (Michael, LT, VLT) and Seq2Seq (Kang) models in OOD settings, with CTC averaging 40.4% vs. 48.7% for autoregressive decoders. Notably, model capacity does not help: the largest model (Kang, 90M params) is the second-worst OOD performer (51.8%), while the smallest (C-SAN, 1.7M params) is competitive (44.8%). Three VLMs (InstructBLIP, LLaVA-Next, Kosmos-2) and TrOCR were evaluated zero-shot in the appendix and showed clear limitations for HTR. Model selection strategy (ID-based, held-out, or oracle) has negligible impact on OOD performance, and training on synthetic data (100K lines from WIT) improves OOD CER by ~6 points on average, with CRNN and VAN benefiting most.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant Transfer Learning and Unsupervised Domain Adaptation paradigms in HTR, which assume access to target-domain data (labeled or unlabeled), and instead adopts the more challenging Domain Generalization formulation where no target data is available. It explicitly rejects what the authors call the 'deceiving concept of generalization' in HTR literature, where in-distribution train-test splits are labeled as generalization, and reframes the problem around truly out-of-distribution manuscripts, alphabets, and languages. Methodologically, it repurposes factor analysis from the broader ML literature and introduces autoencoder-based visual divergence and n-gram KL-divergence as proxy metrics to explain OOD performance, drawing on anomaly detection and information-theoretic tools rather than proposing new HTR architectures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic OOD evaluation of HTR models (336 cases, 8 architectures, 7 datasets, 5 languages), establishing a baseline and framework that the field previously lacked. The finding that textual divergence—not visual divergence—is the dominant driver of OOD error redirects attention toward language and content alignment rather than purely visual style adaptation. Open questions remain: how to actually improve OOD generalization (the paper diagnoses but does not prescribe solutions), whether the single-domain DG formulation generalizes to multi-domain settings, and how the visual divergence factor, which shows only a weak effect here, might interact with more sophisticated visual representations. The practical finding that OOD error can be estimated within ~10 CER points in 70% of cases from accessible proxy metrics offers a useful diagnostic tool for practitioners.
