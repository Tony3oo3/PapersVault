## Problem

Existing Gaussian-based inverse rendering methods assign constant material parameters and a single normal to each Gaussian, producing uniform color across the Gaussian's footprint and causing visible artifacts in both training and test views. Additionally, indirect illumination is modeled with unconstrained spherical harmonics that lack physical constraints, resulting in unnatural indirect lighting that remains unchanged under novel lighting conditions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251692/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes multi-view RGB images and a known environment map, and produces a scene of Spatially-varying Gaussians (each with 4 Gaussian vertices carrying albedo, roughness, and normal offset) that can be rendered in real time for both novel view synthesis and relighting under arbitrary environment maps. Training is two-stage: a Gaussian Surfel initialization phase followed by SVG attribute optimization, with the radiance field from stage one reused for ray-traced indirect illumination in stage two.

## Method

SVG-IR introduces a Spatially-varying Gaussian (SVG) representation that places M representative locations (Gaussian vertices) on the tangent space of each 2D Gaussian surfel, each carrying distinct Disney Principled BRDF parameters (albedo, roughness) and a normal offset, generalizing the constant-attribute Gaussian. Rendering follows a vertex/fragment shading analogy: vertex shading evaluates the rendering equation per vertex using the Disney Principled BRDF with ray-traced visibility and indirect radiance (via BVH-accelerated ray tracing in the learned radiance field), while fragment shading bilinearly interpolates the M vertex colors in tangent-space coordinates before alpha blending. For relighting under a novel environment map, the training-time indirect radiance is replaced by a one-bounce indirect term computed by evaluating the BRDF of the intersected secondary Gaussian along each sampled direction, enabling physically consistent dynamic indirect illumination. A radiance consistency loss further supervises outgoing radiance of secondary Gaussians against the ray-traced indirect incoming radiance, providing additional multi-view guidance.

## Results

On the TensoIR Synthetic dataset, SVG-IR achieves a mean relighting PSNR of 31.10 dB, SSIM of 0.946, and LPIPS of 0.056, outperforming the best NeRF-based method (TensoIR, 28.53 dB) by 2.6 dB and the best Gaussian-based method (RelightGS, 27.60 dB) by 3.5 dB. On the ADT dataset, SVG-IR reaches 34.69 dB PSNR and 0.977 SSIM, again leading all baselines. For NVS, SVG-IR achieves 36.71 dB PSNR on TensoIR and 41.48 dB on ADT, surpassing TensoIR by 1.5 dB and 1.8 dB respectively. Training time is 1 hour, comparable to other Gaussian-based methods (0.5–1 h) and far faster than NeRF-based approaches (5–6 h).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SVG-IR outperforms all five baselines on both relighting and NVS tasks across both synthetic datasets. Against NeRF-based methods, SVG-IR beats TensoIR (the strongest NeRF baseline) by 2.6 dB PSNR on TensoIR and 4.1 dB on ADT for relighting, while matching or exceeding TensoIR's NVS quality (36.71 vs. 35.17 dB on TensoIR). Among Gaussian-based methods, the gap is even larger: SVG-IR surpasses RelightGS by 3.5 dB on TensoIR and 1.9 dB on ADT for relighting, and outperforms GS-IR by 6.4 dB on TensoIR. GSshader is the weakest baseline, trailing SVG-IR by over 11 dB on TensoIR relighting, likely due to its simpler shading model. On NVS, SVG-IR leads RelightGS by 3.4 dB on TensoIR and 4.5 dB on ADT, confirming that the spatially-varying representation benefits view synthesis beyond just relighting. All Gaussian-based methods (including SVG-IR) train in 0.5–1 h versus 5–6 h for NeRF-based methods, preserving the efficiency advantage of explicit representations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Gaussian-splatting-based inverse rendering line (GS-IR, GaussianShader, Relightable 3DGS, GIR) by replacing the per-Gaussian constant material/normal with a multi-vertex 'Spatially-varying Gaussian' (SVG), drawing a direct analogy from flat shading to interpolated (Gouraud) shading in the classic triangle rasterization pipeline. It also departs from prior GS-IR methods that model indirect illumination with unconstrained spherical harmonics, instead employing a physically-based one-bounce ray-tracing scheme over a BVH of Gaussian ellipses to compute visibility and indirect radiance. The initialization stage reuses Gaussian Surfels (2DGS), and the rendering pipeline mirrors vertex/fragment shading, making the SVG splatting scheme a natural generalization of the existing 2DGS alpha-blending rasterizer.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The physically-based indirect lighting model is the key practical advance: unlike SH-based residual terms that remain frozen under novel environment maps, SVG-IR's one-bounce ray tracing produces indirect illumination that correctly responds to new lighting, which is essential for realistic relighting. The 3.5 dB PSNR gain over the best GS-based baseline and 2.5 dB over NeRF-based methods while retaining real-time rendering makes this a strong candidate for production-oriented relighting pipelines. However, the 50–80% GPU memory overhead from Gaussian vertices and the acknowledged weakness on highly specular surfaces (no SDF or geometric prior) limit immediate adoption, and whether the SVG concept scales to large unbounded scenes or benefits from more bounces remains open.
