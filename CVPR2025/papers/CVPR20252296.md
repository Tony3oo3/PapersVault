## Problem

Video-LLMs struggle with fine-grained video understanding—particularly visual dynamics, temporal ordering, and frame-level detail—because existing fine-tuning relies on labor-intensive manual annotations and natural language descriptions that cannot adequately capture complex spatiotemporal variations. Additionally, no comprehensive benchmark existed to rigorously evaluate Video-LLMs at both scene and fragment levels simultaneously.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252296/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes video frame sequences extracted via MGSampler (N=3 sets of M=3–5 frames per video, using motion-salient area sampling) paired with auto-generated text prompts for five fragment-level tasks. Outputs include frame counts, binary consistency judgments, frame localization indices, disorder yes/no decisions, and corrected frame orderings. For evaluation, FineVidBench presents single-choice QA pairs (22,718 total across 910 videos) at both scene level (Action, Effect, Speed) and fragment level (Frame Count, Meaning of Order, Frame Comparison, Adjust-or-Not, Rearrangement), with distractors such as 'Visual Synonyms' to increase difficulty.

## Method

SF²T is a self-supervised fine-tuning strategy that automatically generates training labels from five fragment-level tasks—Counting, Consistency Verification, Localization, Disorder Detection, and Rearrangement—by applying pre-defined rules to extracted dynamic keyframe sequences, eliminating the need for manual annotation. The key insight is that training the model to perform basic quantitative and temporal-reasoning operations on short frame sequences (e.g., counting frames, detecting whether a sequence is shuffled, restoring correct order) builds sensitivity to spatiotemporal dynamics that transfers to scene-level comprehension. Training is applied via LoRA adapters on the LLM component for one epoch, using videos from SSv2 and MiT with no overlap to FineVidBench. SF²T is designed as a complementary stage to supervised fine-tuning rather than a replacement, and the paper also introduces FineVidBench, a hierarchical benchmark with 910 videos, speed-variant augmentations, and 22,718 auto-generated QA pairs for evaluating both scene-level and fragment-level understanding.

## Results

On FineVidBench scene-level tasks, SF²T consistently improves all four tested Video-LLMs: Qwen2-VL gains +5.68 on Action (68.18→73.86), +6.23 on Effect (57.14→63.37), and +7.30 on Speed (24.62→31.92); MiniCPM-V 2.6 shows the largest Action gain (+22.54, 43.37→65.91). On public benchmarks, SF²T yields consistent gains, e.g., Qwen2-VL improves from 55.97→63.76 on MVBench and 43.77→53.60 on Video-MME (no subtitle). Notably, SF²T outperforms SFT on the Speed task for three of four models (e.g., LLaVA-NeXT-Video: 32.01 vs. 22.35), highlighting its strength in fine-grained temporal discrimination.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SF²T is compared against two baselines: (1) Base (no fine-tuning) and (2) Base(SFT) (supervised fine-tuning). Against Base, SF²T wins on every task for every model, with the largest gains on Action (up to +22.54 for MiniCPM-V 2.6) and Speed (up to +11.74 for MiniCPM-V 2.6). Against SFT, SF²T generally underperforms on Action and Effect (e.g., Qwen2-VL: 73.86 vs. 78.60 on Action), but outperforms SFT on Speed for three of four models (LLaVA-NeXT-Video 32.01 vs. 22.35; VideoLLaMA 2.1 31.63 vs. 29.73; Qwen2-VL 31.92 vs. 30.87), demonstrating its unique strength in temporal sensitivity. Combining SFT + SF²T yields the best overall results across all tasks, confirming SF²T as a complementary rather than replacement strategy. On public benchmarks (MVBench, Video-MME, MLVU), SF²T consistently improves all four models by 3–16 points, validating its generalizability beyond FineVidBench.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against the dominant SFT/RLHF paradigm for Video-LLM adaptation, arguing that manual annotation is both costly and insufficient for capturing fine-grained spatiotemporal dynamics. Conceptually, the five fragment-level tasks (counting, consistency verification, localization, disorder detection, rearrangement) repurpose ideas from temporal order verification (Misra et al., 2016) and video action recognition, but reframe them as self-supervised proxy objectives for LLM-based video models rather than as standalone recognition tasks. The benchmark (FineVidBench) extends the line of Video-LLM evaluation work (MVBench, TempCompass, VN-Bench) by explicitly targeting fragment-level temporal perception and hierarchical scene/fragment evaluation, which prior benchmarks largely neglected. The method is architecturally agnostic, operating through LoRA-compatible adapters on existing Video-LLM backbones rather than proposing a new model architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical value of SF²T lies in its annotation-free training pipeline, which could significantly reduce the cost of adapting Video-LLMs to new domains or tasks without requiring large-scale human labeling. The finding that SF²T outperforms SFT on the Speed discrimination task is a notable signal that self-supervised temporal reasoning can capture cues that supervised scene-level labels miss. However, SF²T still lags behind SFT on most scene-level tasks, and the benchmark (910 videos, 22.7K QA pairs) is relatively small; open questions include whether the fragment tasks scale to long-form video, whether they transfer to audio-visual or egocentric settings, and whether the gains persist as base models improve. The attention-map analysis suggesting the model learns to predict motion trajectories is intriguing but remains a qualitative observation on a single architecture.
