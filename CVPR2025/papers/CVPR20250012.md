## Problem

Physically-based rendering requires precise 3D geometry, material properties, and lighting that are impractical to obtain from real-world captures, while existing inverse rendering methods produce G-buffers too noisy for reliable PBR pipelines. Inverse and forward rendering have been treated as separate problems, and prior neural approaches either depend on explicit 3D meshes or struggle with complex inter-reflections and occlusions in multi-object scenes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250012/cropped_page0_idx10.webp)

## Input / Output

For inverse rendering, the model takes an RGB video and sequentially generates five per-pixel attribute maps (surface normals, relative depth, base color, roughness, metallic) one at a time via domain-embedding-conditioned passes. For forward rendering, it consumes VAE-encoded G-buffer latents (20 channels at 1/8 spatial resolution) concatenated with the noisy image latent, plus a dedicated multi-resolution environment map encoding (LDR, log-intensity, and directional panoramic images) injected through cross-attention. The environment map is preprocessed with Reinhard tonemapping and log-space normalization to fit the VAE's [-1,1] range while preserving HDR peak intensities.

## Method

DIFFUSIONRENDERER comprises two fine-tuned Stable Video Diffusion models: a forward renderer that approximates path-traced shading by conditioning on G-buffer latents (pixel-wise concatenation) and multi-resolution environment map features (cross-attention via a purpose-built encoder), and an inverse renderer that generates each G-buffer attribute in a dedicated pass guided by a learnable domain embedding. Training uses 150K synthetic videos rendered with a custom OptiX path tracer (36,500 Objaverse assets, 4,260 PBR materials, 766 HDRIs) co-trained with ~150K auto-labeled real-world segments from DL3DV10k, where the inverse renderer itself produces the G-buffer labels and DiffusionLight estimates environment maps. A small LoRA adapter is applied only to real-data samples to bridge the synthetic-real domain gap without degrading synthetic fidelity. The video model's temporal context improves specular property estimation (41% RMSE reduction for metallic over the image variant), and 1-step deterministic fine-tuning further boosts photometric accuracy for the inverse renderer.

## Results

On forward rendering, DIFFUSIONRENDERER achieves 26.0 PSNR / 0.780 SSIM / 0.201 LPIPS on the complex SyntheticScenes dataset, outperforming SSRT (24.8 PSNR), SplitSum (23.1), RGB↔X (18.5), and DiLightNet (20.7). On relighting, the method reaches 24.63 PSNR on SyntheticScenes, surpassing Neural Gaffer (20.75) and DiLightNet (18.88) by 3.9 and 5.8 dB respectively. For inverse rendering on SyntheticScenes, the method estimates albedo at 25.0 PSNR (vs. 14.3 for RGB↔X), metallic at 0.039 RMSE (vs. 0.441), and normals at 5.97° angular error (vs. 23.80°), demonstrating large margins over the closest neural baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On forward rendering (Table 1), classic PBR methods SSRT and SplitSum retain an edge on the simpler SyntheticObjects set (29.4 and 28.7 PSNR vs. 28.3 for Ours), but DIFFUSIONRENDERER clearly wins on the harder SyntheticScenes set (26.0 vs. 24.8 for SSRT, 23.1 for SplitSum), where complex inter-reflections and occlusions degrade explicit methods. Among neural baselines, RGB↔X [83] and DiLightNet [82] lag substantially on both datasets (18.5 and 20.7 PSNR on Scenes), confirming the advantage of video diffusion priors over image-only models. On relighting (Table 2), Ours beats Neural Gaffer [30] by 3.9 dB (24.63 vs. 20.75 PSNR) and DiLightNet [82] by 5.8 dB (24.63 vs. 18.88) on SyntheticScenes, with the gap widening in complex scenes. For inverse rendering, the method dominates RGB↔X [83] across every attribute: albedo PSNR improves from 14.3 to 25.0, metallic RMSE drops from 0.441 to 0.039, and normal angular error falls from 23.80° to 5.97°. On the InteriorVerse albedo benchmark (Table 4), Ours (21.9 PSNR) outperforms Kocsis et al. [36] (17.4), Zhu et al. [91] (15.9), Li et al. [41] (12.3), and Bell et al. [5] (9.7), establishing a new state of the art for single-image albedo estimation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the image-level neural rendering framework of RGB↔X [83] to video diffusion models and unifies inverse and forward rendering in a single pipeline, building on Stable Video Diffusion [8] as the base architecture. It departs from classic PBR pipelines (which require explicit 3D geometry and path tracing) and from prior diffusion-based relighting methods like Neural Gaffer [30] by using cross-attention with a dedicated multi-resolution environment map encoder rather than direct latent concatenation. The data strategy—auto-labeling real-world videos with the inverse renderer to co-train the forward renderer with LoRA—repurposes the inverse rendering model as a data engine, a workflow not present in prior neural rendering work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DIFFUSIONRENDERER provides a practical, unified toolchain for relighting, material editing, and object insertion from a single video input, bypassing the need for explicit 3D geometry or perfect G-buffers that limit classic PBR and screen-space ray tracing. Key limitations include reliance on offline Stable Video Diffusion (no real-time inference), slight content drift during editing, and dependence on an off-the-shelf lighting estimator (DiffusionLight) for real-world auto-labeling. Open questions include whether distillation can make the pipeline real-time, whether task-specific fine-tuning can improve content consistency, and whether the auto-labeling quality bottleneck can be addressed with better intrinsic estimation.
