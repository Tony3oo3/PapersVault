## Problem

Existing MLLMs struggle with long videos due to limited context lengths and prohibitive computational costs, while prior token-reduction methods (e.g., compressing each frame to 2 tokens) cause severe information loss that bottlenecks fine-grained visual perception. The paper asks whether the LLM's own attention mechanism can be leveraged to produce compact video representations without the aggressive pre-compression that destroys detail.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251835/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a long video (up to 2048 frames on a single A100-80GB) and a text instruction as input. Each frame is encoded by CLIP-ViT-L into 144 visual tokens from 336×336 patches, and the full token sequence is dynamically partitioned into variable-length intervals based on semantic consistency. The output is a text response conditioned on compressed VST KV pairs (rather than the full visual token sequence), with the VST KVs serving as a persistent, memory-efficient proxy for all preceding visual content during autoregressive generation.

## Method

Video-XL introduces a Visual Summarization Token (VST) that is interleaved into the visual token sequence at a configurable compression ratio; after each interval is encoded, only the VSTs' KV pairs are retained while the original visual tokens' KVs are offloaded, so subsequent intervals attend to the accumulated VST KVs as a compressed memory. A dynamic compression strategy uses CLIP [cls] depth scores to set interval boundaries at semantic change points, allocating fine-grained (small-interval) compression to information-dense segments and coarse-grained (large-interval) compression to information-sparse ones. Training uses instruction fine-tuning with curriculum learning—progressively increasing the sampled compression ratio from 2×/4× to 8×/12×/16×—and composite data curation that augments scarce long-video instruction data with single-image, multi-image, and a synthetic VICO dataset (20k QA pairs over ~3-minute videos) to strengthen temporal retrieval. Built on Qwen-2-7B with a CLIP-ViT-L encoder and two-layer MLP projector, the approach preserves near-upper-bound performance even at 16× compression, outperforming pre-compression baselines (Q-Former, LLaMA-VID, C-Abstractor) by a large margin on long-video benchmarks.

## Results

Video-XL (7B) achieves 64.9 M-avg on MLVU Dev (surpassing GPT-4o's 64.6) and 45.5 on MLVU Test, 55.5%/61.0% on VideoMME (without/with subtitles), 61.6 on VNBench, 70.6 on VideoVista, and 50.7 on LongVideoBench. It handles 2048 frames on a single A100-80GB GPU while maintaining ~95% accuracy on the Needle-in-a-Haystack evaluation. At 16× compression, Video-XL retains 41.4 on MLVU and 52.0 on VideoMME, approaching the no-compression upper bound of 41.8/52.6.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Video-XL (7B) outperforms GPT-4V on MLVU Dev (64.9 vs 49.2), MLVU Test (45.5 vs 43.3), VNBench (61.6 vs 48.9), VideoVista (70.6 vs 59.1), and LongVideoBench (50.7 vs 4.06), but trails on VideoMME (55.5 vs 59.5 w/o sub). Against GPT-4o, Video-XL edges ahead on MLVU Dev (64.9 vs 64.6) but falls short on MLVU Test (45.5 vs 54.9), VideoMME (55.5 vs 71.9), VNBench (61.6 vs 64.4), and VideoVista (70.6 vs 78.3). Among open-source models, Video-XL leads all 7B–9B baselines: it surpasses LongVA (7B) by 8.6 points on MLVU Dev and 20.1 on VNBench, beats Video-CCAM (9B) by 6.4 on MLVU Dev and 26.0 on VNBench, and outperforms LLaVA-Next-Video (34B) on VideoMME (55.5 vs 52.0), VNBench (61.6 vs 20.1), and VideoVista (70.6 vs 56.7). The only notable loss among open-source peers is on MVBench, where Video-CCAM scores 64.6 vs Video-XL's 55.3, suggesting Video-XL's compression strategy trades some short-video fine-grained accuracy for long-video capability.

## Relation to Prior Work

Video-XL inherits the LLaVA-style architecture (CLIP-ViT-L encoder + MLP projector + LLM backbone) but departs fundamentally from prior long-video methods that reduce visual tokens *before* the LLM (e.g., LLaMA-VID's 2-token-per-frame, LongVLM's token merging, Video-CCAM's cross-attention). Instead, it exploits the LLM's own KV cache as the compression substrate, introducing Visual Summarization Tokens whose KVs replace the full visual token KVs after each interval is encoded. The dynamic compression strategy borrows the depth-score formulation from VideoLLaMB to set interval boundaries based on semantic change, and the training recipe (curriculum learning over compression ratios, composite data curation) is novel but grounded in standard instruction-tuning practice.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: processing 2048 frames on a single A100-80GB GPU with ~95% Needle-in-a-Haystack accuracy addresses a real bottleneck for hour-scale video understanding that prior 7B models could not handle without multi-GPU setups or aggressive (lossy) token reduction. The finding that post-LLM KV compression (VST) consistently outperforms all pre-LLM token-reduction baselines at 16× compression suggests the field may be over-investing in visual-token pruning when the LLM's attention mechanism already provides a natural compression channel. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to non-Qwen backbones, whether compression ratios beyond 16× (tested only at 32× without fine-tuning) remain viable, and whether the VST mechanism can be extended to streaming or interactive video settings where the full video is not available upfront.
