## Problem

CLIP-style language-image pre-training is bottlenecked by two interrelated constraints: the scarcity of high-quality long-form and multilingual image-text pairs, and the 77-token limit of CLIP's text encoder that prevents effective use of rich captions. Existing remedies—synthesizing short captions, translating data, or interpolating positional encodings—remain fundamentally constrained by CLIP's architecture and lose contextual meaning, leaving a gap for a text encoder that natively handles long, multilingual text without architectural modification.

## Input / Output

The method takes 224×224 RGB images and long-form text captions (no fixed token limit, leveraging the LLM's native long-context window) as input. On the text side, K prompts (default 7) are applied to each caption to produce K single-word semantic embeddings at different hierarchical levels (entity, interaction, scene); on the image side, a ViT encoder produces a single global embedding projected into the shared text space via a 2-layer MLP. For long-text retrieval, the final score is the average cosine similarity between the image embedding and all K text facet embeddings; for short-text tasks, only the scene-level prompt is used.

## Method

FLAME replaces CLIP's text encoder with a frozen LLM (Mistral-Nemo by default) and introduces two key mechanisms. First, multifaceted prompt distillation decomposes visual semantics into a hierarchy (entity → interaction → scene) and uses K constrained single-word prompts to extract complementary semantic facets from each long caption in a single LLM pass. Second, facet-decoupled attention concatenates all K prompts after a shared prefix and applies a custom attention mask that prevents cross-facet information leakage while reusing a single prefix KV cache, reducing K separate forward passes to one. Because the LLM is frozen, all text embeddings are pre-computed offline, eliminating LLM inference during training and yielding a 3.6× speedup over naive multi-prompt extraction. Training uses a symmetric InfoNCE contrastive loss between the image embedding and each text facet embedding, with only the ViT encoder and projection MLP being updated.

## Results

On ImageNet zero-shot classification, FLAME trained on CC3M achieves 36.0% top-1 accuracy, surpassing the prior state-of-the-art DreamLIP (31.1%) by 4.9% and CLIP (16.0%) by 20.0%. On YFCC15M, FLAME reaches 51.5% ImageNet top-1 and 51.9% average accuracy across 10 downstream datasets, improving over DreamLIP by 3.3% and 6.8% respectively. In long-context retrieval, FLAME on YFCC15M achieves 87.9% text-to-image recall@1 on Urban-1k, a 34.6% absolute gain over WIT-400M-trained CLIP (53.3%). For multilingual zero-shot retrieval across 36 languages, FLAME attains 51.7% average image-to-text recall@1, exceeding WIT-400M CLIP by 44.4%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FLAME consistently outperforms all baselines across every evaluation setting. Against DreamLIP (the prior SOTA for long-caption pre-training), FLAME gains 4.9% on ImageNet (CC3M) and 3.3% (YFCC15M), with particularly large margins on fine-grained datasets like DTD (+26.8%) and Food-101 (+12.6%). Against standard CLIP trained on the much larger WIT-400M dataset, FLAME trained on YFCC15M still leads by 8.7% on MSCOCO text retrieval R@1 and by 34.6% on Urban-1k long-context T2I recall@1. In multilingual classification (Table 4), FLAME trained solely on English data achieves 40.6% average accuracy across 5 languages, nearly doubling CLIP (19.2%), CN-CLIP (19.4%), and NLLB-CLIP (18.8%), which all require multilingual training data. For multilingual retrieval on Crossmodal-3600, FLAME surpasses mSigLIP (trained on 100-language WebLI) by 4.3% in I2T and 14.6% in T2I recall@1. The only setting where FLAME is slightly behind is short-context image retrieval on MSCOCO (28.6% vs DreamLIP's 29.8% on CC3M), though it leads on all other retrieval and classification benchmarks.

## Relation to Prior Work

FLAME departs from the CLIP lineage by replacing the trainable 77-token text encoder with a frozen LLM (e.g., Mistral-Nemo), directly inheriting long-context and multilingual capabilities without architectural modification. It builds on the emerging line of work extracting text embeddings from decoder-only LLMs (LLM2Vec, PromptReps, single-word distillation) but repurposes these techniques specifically for contrastive image-text alignment, introducing multifaceted prompt distillation to extract hierarchical semantic facets (entity, interaction, scene) rather than a single universal embedding. Compared to DreamLIP, which still operates within CLIP's token-length constraint by synthesizing captions, and Long-CLIP, which interpolates positional encodings, FLAME sidesteps the encoder bottleneck entirely by leveraging the LLM's native sequence-length capacity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a frozen 8B LLM as text encoder, trained on only 3M pairs (CC3M), outperforms CLIP trained on 400M pairs (WIT-400M) by 34.6% on long-context retrieval challenges the prevailing assumption that text encoders must be jointly trained for effective contrastive pre-training. The zero-shot multilingual generalization across 36 languages from English-only training suggests that LLM pre-training corpora already encode sufficient cross-lingual structure to transfer to visual alignment, potentially reducing the need for expensive multilingual data synthesis. Open questions include whether the offline embedding preprocessing step scales practically to billion-pair datasets, whether the approach benefits from even larger LLMs, and whether the frozen-encoder paradigm extends to other modalities (audio, video) where long-context understanding is similarly critical.
