## Problem

Existing video LLMs operate offline, processing the entire video before generating a single response, making them unsuitable for real-time interactive scenarios where users expect continuous, synchronized feedback. The only prior streaming approach, VideoLLM-online, uses a single LLM whose autoregressive decoding forces it to alternate between video perception and response generation, blocking real-time monitoring during output. This creates an inherent conflict: decision-making and reaction require different perception scales and grains, yet a monolithic architecture cannot satisfy both simultaneously.

## Input / Output

The system consumes a streaming video at 1 FPS (frames resized to 224×224), a text question that may be inserted at an arbitrary timestamp, and the full history of prior vision-language interactions. It produces two types of output: a binary timing decision (wait vs. respond) made continuously as clips arrive, and, when triggered, a context-aware text response grounded to specific video segments. The response module can also emit a <SILENT> token to suppress output when the decision module falsely triggers, acting as a secondary filter.

## Method

Dispider disentangles perception, decision, and reaction into two asynchronously operating modules. The Proactive Streaming Video Processing module adaptively segments the stream into non-uniform clips via SigLip cosine-similarity scene boundary detection, encodes them with CLIP-L/14, and feeds compressed clip features plus a global memory into a compact Qwen2-1.5B LLM that predicts a binary respond/wait decision via a classification head on a <TODO> token, trained with binary cross-entropy and a KL-divergence loss for temporal clip retrieval. Upon triggering, the Precise Interaction module (Qwen2-7B) asynchronously generates the response using the retrieved grounded clips and conversation history, while the processing module continues monitoring the stream without interruption. Training is two-stage: first the decision module and video encoder are trained jointly, then these are frozen while the final LLM is fine-tuned on 122K streaming QA pairs that include both positive (respond) and negative (stay silent) samples.

## Results

On the EgoSchema full set (long temporal reasoning), Dispider achieves 55.6% accuracy, outperforming the previous best VideoChat2 (54.4%) and Video-LLaMA2 (51.7%). On VideoMME (overall, no subtitles), Dispider reaches 57.2%, surpassing Kangaroo (56.0%) and VideoXL (55.5%). On MLVU, Dispider scores 61.7%, trailing VideoXL (64.9%) and Video-CCAM (63.1%) but exceeding Kangaroo (61.0%). In the streaming setting on ETBench, Dispider dramatically outperforms VideoLLM-online across all six subtasks, e.g., 36.1 vs. 13.2 F1 on temporal video grounding and 54.2 vs. 22.4 F1 on video highlight detection.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Dispider achieves the best EgoSchema score (55.6%) among all 7B models, edging out VideoChat2 (54.4%) by 1.2 points and Video-LLaMA2 (51.7%) by 3.9 points, demonstrating superior long temporal reasoning. On VideoMME, Dispider (57.2%) surpasses Kangaroo (56.0%) and VideoXL (55.5%), though it uses only 1 FPS sampling versus 64 and 128 frames respectively. On MLVU, Dispider (61.7%) trails VideoXL (64.9%) and Video-CCAM (63.1%) but leads Kangaroo (61.0%) and LongVA (56.3%). In the streaming ETBench evaluation, Dispider dominates VideoLLM-online by large margins on every subtask: TVG F1 36.1 vs. 13.2, EPM F1 15.5 vs. 3.8, TAL F1 27.3 vs. 9.1, VHD F1 54.2 vs. 22.4, DVC F1 33.8 vs. 24.0, and SLC F1 18.8 vs. 9.9. Among conventional ETBench baselines without specialized time tokens, Dispider achieves the highest TVG F1 (43.6) and EPM F1 (17.2), surpassing even models with dedicated temporal tokens like ETChat (38.6 TVG, 10.2 EPM).

## Relation to Prior Work

Dispider builds directly on VideoLLM-online (CVPR 2024), which first demonstrated streaming video dialogue with a single LLM, and on the token compression techniques from Qian et al. (NeurIPS 2024) for efficient clip-level feature encoding. The key conceptual departure is the disentangled, asynchronous architecture: rather than forcing a single autoregressive LLM to alternate between perception and response generation (as VideoLLM-online must), Dispider splits the pipeline into a lightweight streaming processor (Qwen2-1.5B) for perception and decision, and a separate larger LLM (Qwen2-7B) for reaction, enabling truly parallel operation. This positions the work at the intersection of the broader video LLM literature (TimeChat, MovieChat, VideoChat) and the nascent streaming video understanding line, but specifically targets the real-time interaction gap that offline video LLMs and even VideoLLM-online leave unaddressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically important gap: real-world video applications (fitness coaching, live monitoring, assistive systems) demand continuous, proactive interaction rather than batch processing, yet almost all video LLMs are designed for offline settings. The disentangled architecture provides a clean, modular solution to the fundamental conflict between perception (needs fine-grained, continuous monitoring) and reaction (needs coarse-grained, context-rich generation), and the ablations confirm each component contributes meaningfully. However, the streaming evaluation is limited to one prior baseline (VideoLLM-online) and a subset of ETBench, leaving open questions about scalability to truly hour-long streams, robustness of scene-based segmentation across diverse video types, and whether the two-stage training (decision module then LLM) generalizes to new domains without retraining.
