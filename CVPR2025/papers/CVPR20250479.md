## Problem

Conventional wisdom holds that multimodal RLHF (e.g., DPO, PPO) is superior to continual SFT during the preference alignment stage of vision-language models. This paper challenges that assumption by showing the key success factor of RLHF is actually the negative supervision from rejected responses, which naive continual SFT fails to exploit. The paper asks whether a simple SFT-based approach that properly integrates this negative signal can match full RLHF while being more memory-efficient.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250479/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image and a text question as input and produces a text response, following the standard VLM pipeline (vision encoder + connector → LLM). During training, preference pairs (image, chosen response, rejected response) are consumed, but the rejected response is not used directly in a pairwise loss; instead, an LLM (GPT-4) processes it alongside a vision error codebook and the GT chosen response to construct new Q-A pairs. Training data spans three sources with varying response lengths: OCRVQA (short, object-centric), TextCaps (~10 tokens), and LLaVA-150k (~100 tokens), with 10k samples per source in the main experiments.

## Method

The paper first proves theoretically that DPO's gradient is a linear combination of two SFT gradients (chosen and rejected), identifying the missing rejected-response SFT loss as the root cause of continual SFT's inferiority. It further shows via gradient analysis that DPO's optimization is biased toward rejecting samples (update rate ratio t₂/t₁ < 1), which exacerbates the gap. The proposed nSFT method disentangles this negative supervision by using a construction function G(·) (GPT-4) that identifies specific errors in the rejected response by referencing a large vision error codebook Q and the GT chosen response, then generates new conversations teaching the model to avoid those mistakes. The final loss is simply L_nSFT = L_sft(y_c) + L_sft(G(y_r; y_c, Q)), a standard cross-entropy SFT loss applied to both the GT response and the constructed negative-supervision data. This requires only one model during training (vs. 2 for DPO or 4 for PPO), making it substantially more memory-efficient while matching or surpassing RLHF across 9 benchmarks.

## Results

On LLaVA-150k (10k samples), nSFT achieves the best total improvement across all three benchmark categories: +2.3 on traditional VQA (SQA 68.4, GQA 62.3, VQA^T 58.4), +4.6 on multimodal comprehension (MMVet 34.2, MME 1550, MMB 65.2), and +11.8 on hallucination (POPE 87.4, CHAIR 25.4, MMHal 3.02), all relative to the LLaVA-1.5-7B baseline. In the SOTA comparison (Table 2, 15k mixed data), nSFT reaches MMB 67.1, MME 1531, POPE 86.8, and MMVet 34.0, surpassing SIMA-7B and SeVa-7B on 9 out of 10 benchmarks. On larger models (Table 4), nSFT on LLaVA-1.5-13B achieves MME 1551 and POPE 88.6, and on LLaVA-NeXT-13B achieves SQA 74.4 and MME 1570, consistently outperforming both DPO and pure continual SFT.

## Baselines & Comparisons

nSFT outperforms all three DPO variants (GT-DPO, SeVa, SIMA) on the majority of benchmarks across all three training datasets, with the largest margins on hallucination metrics (e.g., +11.8 vs. +9.6 for SIMA on LLaVA-150k total hallucination score). Pure continual SFT consistently underperforms, sometimes degrading VQA scores (e.g., −2.0 on TextCaps), confirming the paper's hypothesis that the absence of negative supervision is the key limitation. Against PPO [29] and iterative DPO (CSR) [41] (Table 5), nSFT achieves the best MMB (65.2 vs. 64.7/64.4), MME (1550 vs. 1508/1523), and POPE (87.4 vs. 86.2/87.2). In the SOTA comparison (Table 2), nSFT surpasses SIMA-7B on 9/10 benchmarks and SeVa-7B on 8/10, with the sole exception being MMVet where SeVa's longer responses are favored by GPT-4 evaluation. On larger models (Table 4), nSFT matches or exceeds DPO on LLaVA-1.5-13B (MME 1551 vs. 1520, POPE 88.6 vs. 87.5) and LLaVA-NeXT-13B (SQA 74.4 vs. 74.0, MME 1570 vs. 1550), while pure continual SFT degrades performance on both. Compared to earlier VLMs (BLIP-2, InstructBLIP, original LLaVA in Table 2), nSFT-based LLaVA-1.5-7B leads on all reported benchmarks, e.g., MMB 67.1 vs. 58.8 (Shikra) and MME 1531 vs. 1293 (BLIP-2).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) by performing a gradient-level decomposition that reveals DPO's optimization is a linear combination of two SFT losses (chosen and rejected), with the rejected SFT loss being the component missing from standard continual SFT. It departs from the standard multimodal RLHF pipeline (SeVa, SIMA, GT-DPO, PPO) by arguing that the negative supervision embedded in rejected responses can be extracted and repurposed through an LLM-guided data construction step (using a vision error codebook) rather than through pairwise preference optimization. The approach thus repositions the alignment problem from a preference-optimization task back to a supervised finetuning task, while retaining the critical negative signal that prior work attributed to RLHF's superiority.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is substantial: nSFT requires only one model during alignment (vs. 2 for DPO or 4 for PPO), dramatically reducing GPU memory and training time, which is a real bottleneck for large VLMs. The conceptual finding that negative supervision is the active ingredient in RLHF provides a cleaner theoretical lens for understanding why SFT alone underperforms in alignment. However, the approach depends on GPT-4 for constructing negative SFT data, introducing a non-trivial cost and a dependency on a proprietary model. The authors themselves note it remains unclear whether this negative-construction strategy transfers to NLP RLHF, where the alignment goal (reducing toxicity, shifting style) differs fundamentally from multimodal hallucination reduction.
