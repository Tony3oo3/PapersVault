## Problem

Existing backdoor attacks in federated learning assume cooperative attackers sharing a single target class (SBA), but in large-scale FL, adversaries are non-cooperative with distinct targets (MBA). The paper uncovers that directly extending SBA methods to MBA causes attackers to exclude each other because they construct similar out-of-distribution backdoor mappings that compete for the same neuron weights, leaving only the dominant attacker effective. The central question is whether MBA can succeed without collusion.

## Input / Output

Each attacker operates on a non-IID local dataset (Dirichlet α=1 split) of RGB images and a trigger pattern (blend or pixel), with access to the current global model's feature extractor. The trigger is optimized per communication round via a min-max adversarial game against a lightweight binary OOD detector built on the frozen global feature extractor. The output is a backdoored global model evaluated on mean ASR across all attackers, clean accuracy, and lifespan (rounds until ASR drops below threshold); the attack window is constrained to 100 rounds starting at round 2000, and 12.5% of each attacker's local data is poisoned.

## Method

Mirage constructs an in-distribution (ID) backdoor mapping so that triggered samples are processed along the clean activation path of the target class, eliminating the OOD-mapping conflicts that cause non-cooperative attackers to exclude each other. The first component, adversarial adaptation, trains a binary OOD detector (frozen global feature extractor + trained classifier) on the attacker's local data and optimizes the trigger via a min-max game to maximize the detector's misclassification of backdoored samples, thereby pulling backdoor features into the target class distribution. The second component, constrained optimization, tightens the backdoor distribution by minimizing cosine similarity between clean and backdoored sample features while simultaneously minimizing cross-entropy loss on the global model, ensuring the ID mapping persists under ongoing clean training dynamics. The optimized trigger is then used to poison 12.5% of the local dataset for standard local training before uploading the model update to the server.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and GTSRB (no defense), Mirage achieves mean ASR of 99.54%, 99.05%, and 99.73% respectively, with clean accuracy of 92.16%, 71.65%, and 96.97%. The inter-attacker ASR gap (GAP) remains below 4% across all settings, confirming that all three non-cooperative attackers succeed simultaneously. Under the strongest OOD-based defense (BackdoorIndicator), Mirage still retains 93.46%, 99.8%, and 99.73% ASR on the three datasets, while A3FL drops to 70.33%, 37.13%, and 85.05%. In lifespan evaluation, Mirage sustains >90% ASR for over 900 rounds after the attack window ends.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Mirage decisively outperforms all four static-trigger baselines (Vanilla, PGD, Neurotoxin, Chameleon) by 54–75 percentage points in ASR on every dataset, confirming that non-cooperative multi-label attacks fail catastrophically when attackers construct similar OOD mappings and exclude each other. A3FL is the only competitive baseline, achieving comparable ASR under no defense (98.67–99.63% vs. Mirage's 99.05–99.73%), but it is significantly more vulnerable to OOD-based detection: under BackdoorIndicator, A3FL's ASR drops to 70.33% (CIFAR-10), 37.13% (CIFAR-100), and 85.05% (GTSRB), whereas Mirage retains 93.46%, 99.8%, and 99.73% respectively. Under robust aggregation defenses (MultiKrum, FLAME), both A3FL and Mirage maintain high ASR (>92%), but Mirage consistently achieves a lower inter-attacker GAP (≤5.14% vs. A3FL's 4.16–19.83%), indicating more balanced success across all three targets. In lifespan evaluation, Mirage and A3FL both sustain >90% ASR for 900+ rounds, while all other baselines collapse rapidly due to mutual exclusion. Overall, Mirage matches or exceeds A3FL on every metric and every defense setting tested, with the clearest advantage under OOD-based detection.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of Single-Label Backdoor Attacks (SBA) in federated learning—where colluding attackers share one target—by identifying and addressing the Multi-Label Backdoor Attack (MBA) setting with non-cooperative adversaries. The closest prior work, A3FL, uses adversarial unlearning to create distinct OOD activation paths, but the authors show this merely shifts the conflict rather than resolving it and makes backdoors more detectable by OOD-based defenses like BackdoorIndicator. Mirage's key conceptual departure is inverting the mapping strategy: rather than constructing OOD backdoor features (as all prior FL backdoor methods do), it constructs In-Distribution (ID) mappings via adversarial adaptation against a lightweight OOD detector, combined with a constrained optimization step to tighten the backdoor distribution for persistence.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper exposes a practical vulnerability in large-scale FL deployments where independent adversaries (e.g., competing service providers) can each embed their own backdoor without coordination, and the resulting global model is compromised for multiple targets simultaneously. The finding that existing OOD-based defenses (particularly BackdoorIndicator) fail against ID-mapping attacks highlights a significant gap in the current defense landscape, motivating the need for tailored MBA-specific defenses. Open questions include whether the ID mapping approach generalizes beyond image classification to NLP or tabular FL, whether the attack scales to very large client populations (beyond 100), and what aggregation-level or model-inspection defenses could detect in-distribution backdoors without degrading utility.
