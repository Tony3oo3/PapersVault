## Problem

As datasets scale to millions of samples, a growing fraction becomes low-value—redundant duplicates, overly challenging outliers, and trivially easy examples that contribute little to model improvement. Existing dynamic loss-based pruning methods like InfoBatch only remove well-learned (low-loss) samples, leaving redundant and overly challenging instances that still consume substantial compute. No prior method simultaneously addresses all three categories of low-value samples in a single unified framework.

## Input / Output

SeTa consumes a standard labeled training dataset and the per-sample loss values already computed during forward passes, requiring no additional model-specific features or preprocessing. It outputs a trained model with 30–70% fewer training samples per epoch, integrating as a three-line modification to any existing training loop. The pruning ratio is dynamic and changes each epoch via the sliding window, so the effective training subset shifts over the course of training rather than being fixed.

## Method

SeTa operates in two phases. First, uniform random downsampling (ratio r) eliminates redundant samples, and the remaining instances are partitioned into k difficulty-stratified clusters via k-means on their per-sample loss values. Second, a sliding window of size w = ⌈αk⌉ cyclically sweeps across clusters ordered from easy to hard, progressively exposing the model to increasing difficulty while periodically revisiting easier groups to maintain stable optimization. In the final epochs, a partial annealing step randomly samples a fraction r of the full dataset to reduce bias from localized selection. The entire pipeline is model-agnostic, using loss as a computation-free difficulty proxy that naturally adapts to the model's evolving state without requiring gradient norms, uncertainty estimates, or architecture-specific features.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, SeTa achieves 95.7% top-1 accuracy at 30% pruning (surpassing the 95.6% full-data baseline) and maintains 95.0% even at 70% pruning, while InfoBatch† drops to 94.7% and UCB to 93.9%. On CIFAR-100, SeTa reaches 79.4% at 30% pruning (exceeding the 79.3% baseline) and 77.7% at 70% pruning, a rate at which InfoBatch† cannot operate. On the 3M-sample ToCa synthetic dataset, SeTa improves NoCaps Overall CIDEr from 70.5 to 71.5 while pruning 31.7% of data, whereas InfoBatch degrades to 70.2 at a lower 23.6% pruning rate. Across the 15M-sample MJ+ST dataset, SeTa sustains near-baseline scene text recognition accuracy (95.8% vs 96.1% on IIIT5k) at an extreme 71% pruning rate.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against InfoBatch [27], the closest prior method, SeTa wins on every dataset and pruning rate tested: on CIFAR-10 at 70% pruning SeTa scores 95.0 vs InfoBatch† 94.7 (+0.3), and on CIFAR-100 at 50% pruning SeTa scores 79.0 vs InfoBatch‡ 78.2 (+0.8). On the 3M-sample ToCa dataset, SeTa improves NoCaps Overall CIDEr to 71.5 at 31.7% pruning while InfoBatch degrades to 70.2 at a lower 23.6% pruning rate; on SS1M, SeTa gains +1.0 B@4 on COCO at 40% pruning whereas InfoBatch at 41.9% pruning only gains +0.2. Against UCB and ε-greedy [28], SeTa leads by +1.1 points on CIFAR-10 at 70% pruning (95.0 vs 93.9) and is the only method that can operate at 70% pruning on CIFAR-100 (77.7 vs unattainable for both). For instruction tuning of LLaMA-7B on Alpaca, SeTa at 25.9% pruning achieves an average benchmark score of 27.3 versus the DQ(2%) [44] baseline of 26.3, with a particularly large gain on Human-Eval (13.4 vs 8.5), suggesting SeTa better preserves code-relevant training examples.

## Relation to Prior Work

SeTa extends the dynamic loss-based pruning lineage initiated by InfoBatch and UCB, which use per-sample loss as a quality signal to adaptively reduce the training set. The key departure is that prior methods only prune below-mean-loss (easy) samples, leaving redundant duplicates and overly hard samples in the training pool; SeTa addresses this by first applying random downsampling to remove redundancy, then k-means clustering on loss to stratify difficulty, and finally a sliding-window curriculum that cycles from easy to hard clusters. The partial annealing mechanism also departs from InfoBatch's full-dataset final epochs, maintaining the same sampling ratio for efficiency. Conceptually, SeTa repurposes standard tools (uniform sampling, k-means, curriculum scheduling) into a unified pipeline rather than introducing a new theoretical primitive.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a three-line code modification yielding 30–50% training-cost reduction (up to 71% on some tasks) with maintained or improved accuracy across 14 architectures and 10 task types makes this immediately adoptable for practitioners working with large-scale datasets. The finding that moderate pruning can *improve* performance over full-dataset training (e.g., 71.5 vs 70.5 CIDEr on NoCaps) suggests that low-value samples actively harm optimization, a point with broader implications for data curation. Open questions include whether the loss-as-difficulty proxy remains reliable in tasks with noisy or multi-component losses, whether the cyclical sliding window has a principled stopping criterion, and how the method behaves under extreme class imbalance or distribution shift during training.
