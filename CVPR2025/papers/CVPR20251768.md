## Problem

State-of-the-art anomaly detection and localization methods, trained exclusively on normal samples, suffer severe performance degradation under adversarial perturbations, and adversarial robustness for pixel-level anomaly localization remains entirely unexplored. Existing Outlier Exposure approaches fail to provide localization maps or maintain near-distribution alignment with inliers, leaving a critical gap in robust AL.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251768/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard RGB images and produces two outputs: a patch-level anomaly map from the ViT's attention-based discriminator (MLH + shared-weight MLP), which is upsampled to the original image resolution for pixel-level localization, and an image-level anomaly score computed as the average of the top-k patch scores. During training, each normal sample is paired with a generated pseudo-anomaly and its binary localization mask (1 for anomalous pixels, 0 otherwise), with a patch labeled anomalous if more than 5% of its pixels are masked.

## Method

PatchGuard generates Foreground-Aware Pseudo-Anomalies by first identifying foreground regions via Grad-CAM saliency maps (computed over soft-augmented views of a normal image and combined element-wise for style invariance), then applying hard semantic-altering transformations only within a randomly placed, rotated rectangular mask on that region—yielding pseudo-anomalies with known localization masks that remain near-distributed. A from-scratch ViT backbone feeds an attention-based discriminator that scores each patch independently. Training uses a combined loss of patch-level cross-entropy plus a novel regularization term R that penalizes low attention coefficients below a threshold δ, thereby pushing the last-layer attention distribution toward uniformity; theoretical analysis shows this increases the minimum perturbation norm required for a successful attack. Adversarial training is performed via PGD-10 (ε=8/255) on both clean and pseudo-anomalous samples, with evaluation under PGD-1000 and additional attacks (A³, SEA, AutoAttack, SegPGD).

## Results

On the AD task (Table 1), PatchGuard achieves an average adversarial AUROC of 74.1% (PGD-1000, ε=8/255), compared to 20.9% for the best prior adversarial method (RODEO) and 12.6% for the best clean-trained method (GeneralAD), representing a 53.2% improvement over the strongest baseline. On the AL task (Table 2), PatchGuard reaches 81.1% average adversarial AUROC versus 8.0% for the best prior method (RealNet), a 68.5% gain. Clean performance remains competitive: 89.3% AD and 94.8% AL average AUROC, only slightly below the best clean baselines (e.g., GLASS at 98.1% AD, RealNet at 97.7% AL).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PatchGuard dominates all 13 baselines in the adversarial setting by a wide margin. Among clean-trained methods (DRAEM, PatchCore, SimpleNet, ReContrast, GeneralAD, GLAD, GLASS), adversarial AUROC drops to 4–16% on average, whereas PatchGuard maintains 74.1% AD / 81.1% AL. Among methods specifically designed for adversarial robustness (PrincipaLS, ZARND, RODEO), the gap is still substantial: RODEO, the strongest prior adversarial method, achieves only 20.9% average adversarial AD AUROC versus PatchGuard's 74.1%, and its clean performance (71.5%) is also lower. For AL-specific baselines (MuSc, ReConPatch, RealNet), adversarial AUROC is 3.9–8.0% on average, compared to PatchGuard's 81.1%. The only trade-off is a modest clean-performance reduction (e.g., 89.3% vs. GLASS's 98.1% on AD), which the authors note is an acceptable cost for the dramatic robustness gains. On the medical BraTS2021 dataset, PatchGuard achieves 90.4% adversarial AD AUROC, outperforming RODEO (61.0%) by nearly 30 points.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) Outlier Exposure (OE) for adversarial robustness in anomaly detection (Hendrycks et al., RODEO, PrincipaLS, ZARND), (2) pseudo-anomaly generation for self-supervised AD/AL (CutPaste, NSA, GLASS, DRAEM), and (3) ViT-based anomaly detection (GeneralAD, Transformaly, MuSc). It departs from prior OE-based methods by identifying two necessary conditions—localization map availability and near-distribution alignment—that existing generators fail to satisfy simultaneously, and by introducing Foreground-Aware Pseudo-Anomaly Generation (using Grad-CAM-guided selective distortion) to meet both. The theoretical analysis connecting ViT attention degree to adversarial vulnerability is novel and motivates a new attention-regularization loss not present in prior adversarial training formulations for AD.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper is the first to formally define and address adversarial robustness in anomaly localization, filling a gap the authors explicitly identify as unexplored. The 53–68% adversarial AUROC gains are substantial, though they are measured against baselines that collapse to near-random under PGD-1000, so the absolute robustness level (71–94% AUROC) is the more meaningful metric. A notable limitation is the clean-performance tradeoff (e.g., 88.1 vs. 99.9 AUROC on MVTec AD), which may be unacceptable in deployment settings where attacks are rare. Open questions include whether the attention-degree regularization generalizes to non-ViT architectures, whether the Grad-CAM-based foreground identification remains reliable on out-of-domain or 3D medical volumes, and whether the from-scratch ViT training requirement limits practical adoption.
