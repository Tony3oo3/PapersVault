## Problem

Existing OOD detectors suffer from two critical limitations: they exhibit large performance discrepancies across datasets of varying scale (e.g., methods strong on CIFAR-10 underperform on ImageNet and vice versa), and feature-space methods relying on similarity measures like KNN incur prohibitive computational costs for real-time deployment. The paper asks a more structured question—where exactly do ID features form clusters in the penultimate layer?—and answers it through the geometric lens of Neural Collapse.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252409/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a single RGB image passed through an off-the-shelf classifier, extracting only the penultimate-layer feature vector h and the last-layer weight vector w_c of the predicted class c. No auxiliary models, stored training features, or gradient computations are required. The output is a scalar detection score (pScore + α‖h‖₁) thresholded at τ to yield a binary ID/OOD decision; the filtering strength α is selected from a small validation set drawn from N(0,1) per pixel, and the entire scoring step runs in O(P) time where P is the penultimate dimension, matching softmax-confidence latency.

## Method

The detector is built on two geometric insights derived from Neural Collapse: (1) centered ID features (h − μ_G) align with the predicted class weight vector w_c (Theorem 3.1), and (2) ID features expand spatially to form a simplex Equiangular Tight Frame, placing them farther from the origin than OOD features. The first insight yields the pScore = cos(w_c, h − μ_G)‖w_c‖₂, an angle-based proximity metric that avoids estimating the unknown scaling factor λ and incorporates class-specific information by selecting wider hyper-cones for classes with larger weight norms. The second insight contributes an L1-norm filtering term α‖h‖₁ that catches OOD samples residing near the origin, complementing the clustering-based pScore. The final NCI score is pScore + α‖h‖₁, and thresholding on this combined score produces the ID/OOD decision. This design unifies and explains prior methods (clustering-based like KNN and norm-based like Energy/ASH) under a single geometric framework, reducing both latency and cross-dataset generalization gaps.

## Results

NCI achieves an average AUROC of 89.51 across CIFAR-10 (ResNet-18) and ImageNet (ResNet-50) OpenOOD benchmarks, outperforming the next-best baselines Scale (88.15) and ViM (88.16). On ImageNet ResNet-50, NCI with L1-norm filtering reaches 88.56 average AUROC (vs. 84.41 without filtering), while on CIFAR-10 ResNet-18 it reaches 90.46 average AUROC. Inference latency is 0.54 ms per image on CIFAR-10 and 6.84 ms on ImageNet, matching the vanilla softmax-confidence (MSP) detector and substantially faster than KNN (1.95/10.31 ms) or NECO (0.70/9.55 ms).

## Baselines & Comparisons

NCI does not dominate every individual benchmark but achieves the best combined average AUROC (89.51) by reducing the gap between CIFAR-10 and ImageNet performance. On CIFAR-10, KNN (92.18) and ViM (91.88) still outperform NCI (90.46) in average AUROC, but NCI is 3.6× faster than KNN (0.54 vs. 1.95 ms) and 1.3× faster than ViM. On ImageNet, Scale (90.28) and ASH (88.71) lead in average AUROC, while NCI (88.56) trails Scale by 1.7 points but matches ASH; however, NCI's L1-norm filtering closes much of this gap (84.41 → 88.56 without → with filtering). Against the Neural-Collapse-motivated NECO, NCI wins on both benchmarks (90.46 vs. 90.03 on CIFAR-10; 88.56 vs. 85.93 on ImageNet) while being 1.4× faster on ImageNet (6.84 vs. 9.55 ms), attributed to NCI's use of class-specific weight vectors rather than expensive feature–feature matrix operations. Compared to output-space methods (MSP, ODIN, Energy, ReAct, DICE), NCI consistently outperforms on both benchmarks, with the largest margins over ODIN (86.26/83.58) and DICE (82.27/83.80). The paper also shows that adding L1-norm filtering to KNN improves its ImageNet AUROC from 82.43 to 86.37, validating the norm-based filtering principle, yet NCI still outperforms KNN+L1 by 2.2 points on average.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reinterprets the well-established observation that ID features form clusters through the theoretical lens of Neural Collapse (Papyan et al., 2020), showing that centered ID features align with last-layer weight vectors and expand into a simplex Equiangular Tight Frame structure. It departs from prior feature-space OOD methods (KNN, Mahalanobis) that rely on data-dependent references by using the model's own weight vectors as structured, class-specific anchors, and from output-space methods (Energy, ASH, Scale) that implicitly capture feature norms without explicit clustering. The concurrent work NECO [1] also leverages Neural Collapse for OOD detection but overlooks the class-specific weight-vector alignment that is central to NCI's design.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a unified geometric explanation for why different OOD detection methods succeed or fail on different benchmarks—clustering-based methods capture weight-vector proximity while norm-based methods capture ETF spatial expansion—and shows that combining both under a single framework reduces generalization discrepancies across datasets and architectures. Practically, NCI achieves inference latency comparable to vanilla softmax confidence while improving average AUROC, making it attractive for time-sensitive deployment. Open questions include whether the ETF-based geometric reasoning extends to non-classification tasks (detection, segmentation), whether it holds under different training paradigms (self-supervised, contrastive), and whether the approach generalizes to multimodal or generative models where the penultimate-layer structure differs.
