## Problem

Part-level semantic segmentation of urban scenes—decomposing objects into functional components like windows, chimneys, and road markings per the CityGML standard—remains unaddressed for textured meshes, which offer richer spatial detail than point clouds or images. Existing mesh datasets are small-scale, indoor-focused, or lack fine-grained part-level labels, and interactive annotation tools for textured meshes are virtually nonexistent, making large-scale ground truth creation impractical.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251871/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The dataset consists of textured urban meshes reconstructed from oblique aerial imagery at ~7.5 cm GSD via Bentley ContextCapture, covering 40 tiles of 62,500 m² each in central Helsinki (~2.5 km² total). Annotations are provided at two granularities: 12 face-level semantic classes assigned to individual triangle faces, and 8 additional pixel-level classes (e.g., window, road marking, cycle lane) defined on the texture images, yielding 19 classes in the combined track. For benchmarking, the mesh is converted to point clouds via four sampling strategies (face-centered, random, Poisson-disk, and the proposed superpixel texture sampling), and segmentation methods output per-point class predictions that are mapped back to faces (voting) or texture pixels (nearest neighbor).

## Method

The annotation tool operates in two modules. For face-based annotation, user lasso or stroke selections generate candidate planar segments, which are then classified into support plane vs. protrusion via a graph-cut energy combining a data term (protrusion score from distance and angular deviation to the support plane) and a smoothness term (geometric similarity via 3D medial axis transform shrinking-ball radii); 3D template matching then propagates labels to repetitive structures using structural features (area, height, orientation, sphericity, color). For texture-based annotation, SLIC superpixels are expanded from user clicks via a graph-cut using Wasserstein distance between per-channel GMMs, followed by GrabCut-based fine segmentation; 2D template matching identifies repetitive structures (windows, road markings) using shape index, shape regularity, and contextual color features, offering rotational and scale invariance unlike NCC-based approaches. The benchmarking evaluates 12 segmentation methods (PointNet through PointVector, plus mesh-specific RF-MRF, SUM-RF, PSSNet) on both face and pixel tracks, and compares annotation efficiency against manual, segment-based, GrabCut, SAM, and SimpleClick baselines using new metrics including interaction count, time, and smart-tool usage ratio.

## Results

On the face labeling track (12 classes), PointVector achieves the best mIoU of 70.0% and mAcc of 80.7%, followed by PointNext (65.3% mIoU) and SparseUNet (60.5% mIoU). On the more challenging pixel labeling track (19 classes), PointVector again leads with 47.9% mIoU and 63.8% mAcc, ahead of PointNext (44.7% mIoU) and KPConv (42.6% mIoU). For interactive annotation, the proposed tool reduces annotation time by ~1.73× versus manual and ~1.32× versus segment-based methods, while achieving 92.2% mean IoU and 75.0% boundary IoU on face annotation, and 87.9% mean IoU on texture annotation—outperforming SAM (84.4%) and SimpleClick (81.4%) in quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PointVector [16] dominates both tracks, achieving 70.0% mIoU on face labeling (5.7 points above PointNext [43] at 65.3%) and 47.9% mIoU on pixel labeling (3.2 points above PointNext at 44.7%). Among mesh-specific methods, PSSNet [19] (47.0% mIoU) is competitive with mid-tier point cloud methods on the face track but cannot handle the pixel track at all, as can RF_MRF [46] and SUM_RF [18]. On the pixel track, KPConv [52] (42.6%) and RandLA-Net [24] (42.1%) trail PointVector by roughly 5–6 mIoU points, while PointTransV3 [53] (38.0%) and SPG [29] (19.2%) lag significantly. For interactive annotation, the proposed tool outperforms SAM [26] (84.4% vs. 87.9% mean IoU) and SimpleClick [34] (81.4% vs. 87.9%) in segmentation quality while requiring fewer interactions (582 vs. 716 and 252 respectively), and is 1.73× faster than manual annotation [46] and 1.32× faster than segment-based annotation [18] on face labeling.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the authors' prior SUM mesh dataset [18] and PSSNet segmentation work [19] by introducing part-level (CityGML LoD3) semantics and texture-pixel annotations, moving beyond the object-level face labels that characterized earlier urban mesh benchmarks. For the annotation tool, it builds on GrabCut's graph-cut framework [45] for fine segmentation and on classical energy-minimization formulations [7], but departs from the deep-learning-dominated interactive segmentation literature (SAM, SimpleClick) by using an unsupervised, template-matching approach grounded in geometric and color statistics (Wasserstein distance over GMMs, CIEDE2000). The benchmark evaluation repurposes point-cloud segmentation architectures (PointNet through PointVector) on mesh-sampled point clouds, bridging the mesh and point-cloud segmentation communities.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As the first large-scale (2.5 km², 21 classes) part-level semantic benchmark for urban textured meshes, SUM Parts fills a concrete gap for CityGML LoD3 reconstruction and fine-grained urban analysis that point-cloud datasets cannot address due to resolution and occlusion limitations. The annotation tool's 1.73× speedup over manual labeling and its ability to outperform SAM in boundary accuracy (49.3% vs 29.8% boundary IoU) suggest that domain-specific geometric priors can rival large pretrained models for structured urban scenes, though the user study is small (5 users, 6 scenes). Open questions include generalization to non-planar or topologically irregular meshes, natural scenes, and whether the part-level taxonomy scales to cities with more diverse architectural styles.
