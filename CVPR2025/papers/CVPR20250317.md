## Problem

Optical microscopy is fundamentally limited by diffraction to ~200–250 nm resolution, rendering subwavelength objects (e.g., viruses <200 nm) invisible under conventional imaging. While deep learning offers a promising path to decode hidden subwavelength information from diffraction patterns, no general-purpose, open-source subwavelength imaging dataset exists—collecting such data is extremely difficult because the target objects are inherently invisible, precluding standard visual calibration and drift correction.

## Input / Output

The model takes a 64×64 grayscale far-field diffraction image as input and predicts a binary object image at one of three spatial scales (3×3, 5×5, or 7×7 pixels), where each pixel corresponds to a ~180 nm square unit. A key subtlety is that the diffraction image is not determined solely by the central block but by complex interference from surrounding structures, making the mapping non-trivial. For full-object reconstruction, a stitching procedure averages predictions from all overlapping block images that cover each target position before applying a binary threshold (λ=0.5).

## Method

The core contribution is the OpticalNet dataset, built on a 'building block' concept: arbitrary complex objects are decomposed into small n×n grid regions of subwavelength squares fabricated via focused ion beam (FIB) on a 130 nm Au film, imaged with a custom 633 nm coherent microscopy system (41.7 nm effective pixel size). The task is formulated as pixel-level binary classification (image-to-image translation) trained with binary cross-entropy loss, and benchmarked across ResUNet-a, Attention U-Net, ResNet-18/34, and a Vision Transformer. A key empirical finding is that transformers consistently outperform CNN-based methods on experimental data, attributed to their ability to model global dependencies and mitigate environmental noise that local convolutional filters struggle with. An open-source angular-spectrum simulation framework (~1 s/instance) enables low-cost proof-of-concept testing before costly experimental sample fabrication.

## Results

On the experimental 3×3 Block dataset, the transformer achieves 80.31% accuracy, 76.33 F1, and 66.90 JI, outperforming all CNN baselines. On the generalization tests, the transformer reaches 74.71% accuracy (76.34 JI) on the Light dataset and 55.81% accuracy (42.89 JI) on the Siemens Star dataset. On the simulation 3×3 setting, the transformer scores 84.77% Block accuracy and 69.43% SS accuracy, with ResNet-18 achieving a slightly higher Light accuracy (75.33% vs. 75.00%). Overall, the transformer consistently leads across all three test sets in both simulation and experimental conditions, with the largest margins on the Block and SS sets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The transformer [91] consistently outperforms all four CNN baselines across every test set in the experimental setting. On the in-domain Block test, the transformer leads by 5.3 points over ResNet-34 (80.31% vs. 75.01% accuracy) and by 13 points over ResUNet-a (67.32%). On the Light generalization test, the gap narrows: the transformer (74.71%) edges out ResNet-18 (73.95%) and ResNet-34 (74.05%) by less than 1 point in accuracy, but pulls ahead decisively in JI (76.34 vs. 72.77 and 71.99). On the most challenging Siemens Star test, the transformer achieves 55.81% accuracy versus 52.31% for ResNet-18 and 49.89% for ResUNet-a. Notably, in the simulation setting (Table 2), ResNet-18 slightly beats the transformer on Light accuracy (75.33% vs. 75.00%), but this advantage reverses in the noisier experimental data, supporting the authors' claim that transformers' global attention mechanism better handles environmental noise than CNNs' local receptive fields.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by repurposing the well-established image-to-image translation paradigm for a domain where input-output correspondence is not visually discernible: translating far-field diffraction patterns into subwavelength binary object images. It departs from traditional optical super-resolution approaches (STED, ptychography, fluorescence-based methods) that require invasive labeling or complex post-processing, and from existing microscopic datasets that are domain-specific and operate above the diffraction limit. The 'building block' decomposition—training on elementary square units and generalizing to arbitrary complex shapes—is a novel dataset construction strategy with no direct prior in the optical imaging literature, drawing an analogy to modular construction rather than to any specific prior ML method.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first open-source, general-purpose subwavelength optical imaging dataset, providing both a simulation framework and experimental data that bridges the optical physics and computer vision communities. The building block concept validates that models can learn fundamental diffraction physics from simple units and generalize to complex unseen shapes, but the ~50% accuracy on the Siemens Star benchmark reveals substantial room for improvement in resolution and clarity. Open questions include whether continuous (non-pixelated) imaging is achievable, whether 3D and RGB extensions are feasible, and how to close the sim-to-real gap to reduce the cost of experimental data acquisition.
