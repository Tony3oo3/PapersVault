## Problem

Current MLLMs exhibit a strong bias toward specific orientations (e.g., Front or Front-Right) when interpreting object orientation, largely because training datasets like MS-COCO and LAION-5B contain inconsistent orientation annotations that mix observer-centric and object-centric perspectives without a standardized guideline. This inconsistency prevents MLLMs from developing a coherent, user-aligned understanding of which way an object is facing, a critical limitation for embodied and interactive applications.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image and a text prompt querying the object's orientation, and produces one of three output formats depending on the evaluation task: an 8-way multiple-choice label (Choose), a binary yes/no verdict against a stated orientation (Verify), or a free-form descriptive sentence (Freeform, scored by GPT-4). The instruction data itself is structured as LLaVA-style image-question-answer triplets where the answer first enumerates visible object details (e.g., taillights, head-tail alignment) before concluding the orientation, forcing the model to ground its spatial judgment in perceptual evidence rather than guessing.

## Method

The core contribution is Egocentric Instruction Tuning: objects from ImageNet are manually annotated into eight egocentric orientation classes (Front, Back, Left, Right, and four diagonals) relative to the camera/user viewpoint, then LLaVA-style instruction data is generated using three complementary response types—detail recognition, prior-knowledge linking (e.g., a car's orientation is determined by its headlights and grille), and orientation-alignment reasoning (e.g., which rotation brings an object to face the camera). Training applies LoRA-based supervised fine-tuning to the LLM and bridge layer while keeping the visual encoder frozen, for 3 epochs on a single A6000 48 GB GPU. The paper also introduces EgoOrientBench, a benchmark spanning five image domains (real photos, paintings, sketches, clip art, and 3D renderings) and three tasks of increasing complexity, enabling comprehensive evaluation that prior small-scale datasets could not support.

## Results

On EgoOrientBench, egocentric instruction tuning yields substantial gains across all three tasks. For LLaVA-1.5, the Choose task average improves from 17.9% (zero-shot) to 33.7%, Verify from 51.8% to 58.3%, and Freeform from 17.9% to 56.5%. Notably, all zero-shot MLLMs score below the 'Popular' baseline (which simply selects the most frequent orientation class) on the Choose task, revealing a strong orientation bias that the proposed tuning mitigates. GPT-4o remains the strongest overall (Choose 41.1%, Verify 76.2%, Freeform 72.3%), while the tuned LLaVA-1.5 and InternVL2 surpass Gemini-1.5 on Choose and Verify.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LLaVA-1.5 zero-shot, egocentric instruction tuning improves Choose by +15.8 pp (17.9→33.7), Verify by +6.5 pp (51.8→58.3), and Freeform by +38.6 pp (17.9→56.5). For mPLUG-Owl2, gains are +13.5 pp on Choose (15.0→28.5), +11.7 pp on Verify (49.8→61.5), and +16.4 pp on Freeform (20.7→37.1). InternVL2 sees +14.9 pp on Choose (16.5→31.4), +3.7 pp on Verify (57.7→61.4), and +8.6 pp on Freeform (39.6→48.2). Compared to the commercial Gemini-1.5 Flash, the tuned LLaVA-1.5 wins on all three tasks (33.7 vs 30.7 Choose; 58.3 vs 57.5 Verify; 56.5 vs 52.9 Freeform), while tuned InternVL2 wins on Choose (31.4 vs 30.7) and Verify (61.4 vs 57.5) but trails on Freeform (48.2 vs 52.9). GPT-4o remains the strongest baseline across all tasks, with a 7.4 pp lead over tuned LLaVA-1.5 on Choose and a 19.7 pp lead on Freeform, indicating that the proposed method narrows but does not close the gap to the best commercial model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on LLaVA's visual instruction tuning framework [30], repurposing its image-question-answer format to create egocentric orientation instruction data. It addresses a limitation identified by MMVP [42] and D3 [19]—that MLLMs struggle with object orientation—but departs from their diagnosis by attributing the problem to inconsistent annotations in training data rather than to visual encoder representational shortcomings. The egocentric annotation standard draws on the broader embodied-AI and egocentric-video literature (Ego4D, EgoSchema, EgoExoLearn), applying a user-centered reference frame to a task previously annotated without standardized guidelines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The EgoOrientBench benchmark fills a real gap: prior orientation evaluations (MMVP, D3) contained only a few dozen samples from a single domain, making comprehensive assessment impossible. The finding that substantial orientation improvements are achievable with the visual encoder frozen challenges the prevailing assumption that the bottleneck lies in visual representation, redirecting attention to the language-side alignment. However, the method's reliance on a discrete 8-class scheme and its failure on living beings with independently oriented heads and bodies (e.g., zebras, giraffes) leave open the question of how to handle continuous or multi-part orientation in a unified framework. The benchmark and instruction data are publicly released, lowering the barrier for future work on orientation-aware MLLMs.
