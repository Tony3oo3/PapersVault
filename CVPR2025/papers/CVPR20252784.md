## Problem

Mainstream skeleton-based sign language recognition models treat sign language as generic human motion, overlooking two key linguistic properties: (1) joints are not equally important—those with stronger inter-joint connections carry more meaning than isolated ones, and (2) each sign gloss can be decomposed into smaller visual symbols (VS) that capture distinct movement patterns, analogous to how letters form words. Existing methods either use manually trimmed skeletons or blindly allow all joints to connect, failing to exploit these structural priors.

## Input / Output

The model takes a complete 44-keypoint hand skeleton (selected from 133 body joints via HRNet) as a T×V×C tensor (64 frames × 44 joints × 3 coordinates), with abnormal points replaced by adjacent-frame averages and all frames normalized relative to a reference node. Output is a single gloss class label, but at inference the model runs four parallel branches (three skeleton grouping types plus a bone-vector representation) and aggregates their final-layer logits via a weighted sum to produce the prediction.

## Method

VSNet combines two core modules. The Self-Pacing Dropping (SPD) block stacks three decoupled graph convolution layers; after each layer, it computes a per-joint correlation score from the learned adjacency matrix and drops the two weakest joints per anatomical group, progressively simplifying the skeleton while ensuring every joint receives at least one convolution. The VSformer then treats the six remaining joint groups as generalized visual symbols and applies Spatial Pairing Attention (attention among paired VS within a frame) and Temporal Window Attention (local within-window and global cross-window attention over the timeline) to capture both spatial relationships and duration-dependent motion patterns. A multi-grouping ensemble trains three different fixed skeleton partitioning schemes plus a bone-vector variant independently and fuses their outputs, reducing sensitivity to any single grouping choice.

## Results

On MSASL1000, VSNet achieves 70.71% top-1 per-instance accuracy (73.18% with 4-crop ensemble), surpassing the previous best skeleton-based method SSRL (65.22%) by 5.49 points. On WLASL2000, it reaches 53.54% top-1 (55.98% with 4-crops), and on NMFs-CSL it attains 75.3% top-1, outperforming SignBERT (74.9%) without any pretraining. On the Slovo dataset, VSNet achieves 76.53% top-1, exceeding the strongest RGB baseline (MViTv2-small-32, 64.09%) by over 12 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VSNet substantially outperforms all skeleton-based baselines on MSASL1000, beating SSRL (65.22%) by 5.49 points and MASA (63.47%) by 7.24 points in top-1 P-I accuracy, without relying on any pretraining. On MSASL100, VSNet ties SSRL at 86.26% top-1, with the authors attributing the slight gap in single-crop settings to SPD needing more data to learn droppable nodes. On WLASL, VSNet (53.54%) is marginally below DSTA (53.68%) on WLASL2000 and below DSTA (79.97%) on WLASL300, but the 4-crop ensemble (55.98%) surpasses DSTA on WLASL2000. On NMFs-CSL, VSNet (75.3%) edges out SignBERT (74.9%) by 0.4 points in top-1 while improving top-5 by 2.2 points (95.4% vs 93.2%), and on Slovo it exceeds the best RGB baseline MViTv2-small-32 (64.09%) by 12.44 points despite using only 42 hand landmarks without arm data.

## Relation to Prior Work

VSNet builds directly on the decoupled graph convolution framework from SL-GCN [21], reusing its activation mechanism and weighted-sum ensemble strategy, while departing from the broader skeleton-based SLR literature by explicitly modeling sign language's linguistic structure through Visual Symbols (VS). Prior GCN-based methods (ST-GCN, BEST, SignBERT) treat sign language as generic human motion with fixed or learned skeleton topologies, and prior attention-based methods (SkateFormer, SLGTformer) partition joints and frames for efficiency but without linguistic motivation. VSNet's Self-Pacing Dropping block introduces a novel progressive skeleton simplification that goes beyond static trimming, and its VSformer combines spatial pairing attention with temporal window attention in a way that is specifically motivated by the hypothesis that sign language decomposes into smaller visual sub-units analogous to letters forming words.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Achieving state-of-the-art results across four benchmarks (WLASL, MSASL, NMFs-CSL, Slovo) without any pretraining or additional training data is practically significant, as it lowers the barrier for deploying skeleton-based SLR systems. The VS concept offers a degree of interpretability absent in purely data-driven GCN or transformer approaches, though the fixed grouping strategy is acknowledged as a compromise that limits true linguistic grounding. Open questions include whether VS boundaries can be discovered automatically rather than hand-designed, whether the approach extends to continuous (non-isolated) sign language recognition, and how the method performs when facial expressions and mouthing cues—excluded here—are incorporated. The paper also notes that SPD requires sufficient data to learn droppable nodes effectively, as evidenced by slightly weaker performance on the 100-class MSASL subset.
