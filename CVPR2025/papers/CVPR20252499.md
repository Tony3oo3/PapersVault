## Problem

Video-based LLMs can automatically generate high-quality captions for personal videos without authorization, producing video-text pairs that can be exploited to improve downstream models such as text-to-video generators. No existing mechanism allows video owners to protect their content from this unauthorized automated annotation while keeping the video visually intact.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252499/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a video preprocessed to 8 frames (to fit GPU memory) and a white-box video-based LLM (Video-ChatGPT, Video-LLaMA, or Video-Vicuna) as the target. It produces an adversarial video where the perturbation is bounded by an l∞ norm of 16/255, making it imperceptible to human viewers. The adversarial video, when fed to the target LLM with a standard prompt like 'What is this video about?', yields either semantically incorrect captions or truncated/empty outputs instead of accurate descriptions.

## Method

The paper proposes two series of adversarial watermarks optimized via PGD. Ramblings disrupt caption accuracy: Rambling-F maximizes the l2 distance between video encoder features and LLM hidden states of the adversarial vs. original video, while Rambling-L maximizes the auto-regressive cross-entropy loss between the model's token predictions on the adversarial video and the ground-truth caption. Mutes suppress output length by manipulating the End-of-Sequence (EOS) token probability: Mute-S maximizes the mean EOS probability across all generated token positions to produce fragmented short captions, while Mute-N maximizes the EOS probability at the first token position to force an immediate NULL response. Both series are evaluated for prompt transferability and downstream impact on text-to-video generation (AnimateDiff fine-tuned on the corrupted video-text pairs shows significant VQA degradation).

## Results

For Ramblings, CLIP score drops substantially across all three video-based LLMs: e.g., Video-ChatGPT on OpenVid-1M falls from 0.762 (original) to 0.627 (Rambling-L), and BLEU drops from 0.017 to 0.003. For Mutes, Mute-S reduces generated text length by nearly 10× for Video-LLaMA (203.54 → 11.58 tokens on OpenVid-1M), while Mute-N achieves a 100% EOS (NULL caption) rate across all three models and all three datasets. Random noise perturbations are ineffective, with scores remaining nearly identical to the original videos.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: original (clean) videos and videos with random noise perturbations at the same l∞ magnitude (16/255). Random noise is consistently ineffective — CLIP scores and BLEU remain nearly identical to the original, and output length/EOS rate show no meaningful change, confirming that gradient-based optimization is necessary. Rambling-L achieves the largest CLIP score reduction (e.g., 0.762→0.627 for Video-ChatGPT on OpenVid-1M, a 17.8% drop) and the most dramatic BLEU reduction (0.017→0.003, an 84% drop). Mute-S reduces Video-LLaMA's output length from 203.54 to 11.58 tokens (~94% reduction) while Mute-N achieves a perfect 100% NULL caption rate across all models and datasets, completely eliminating information leakage. The downstream text-to-video experiment (Table 5) further confirms that fine-tuning AnimateDiff on adversarially annotated pairs degrades VQA_A from 60.43 to 38.04 (Rambling-L) and VQA_T from 51.30 to 24.49 (Mute-S), validating the practical impact of the watermarking.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes adversarial perturbation techniques (PGD with l-infinity constraints) from the offensive adversarial ML literature as a *defensive* watermarking strategy for video privacy. While prior work on adversarial attacks against multimodal LLMs (jailbreaking, targeted caption induction) aims to manipulate models toward an attacker's goal, here the same machinery is inverted to protect content owners from unauthorized annotation. The EOS-token manipulation in Mutes connects to the authors' earlier work on inducing verbose/energy-latency outputs in vision-language models, and the downstream text-to-video evaluation leverages AnimateDiff and Stable Diffusion as a concrete demonstration of why annotation quality matters for generative pipelines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This addresses a timely and underexplored privacy gap: as video-based LLMs become the default annotation tool for building text-to-video training data, personal videos on platforms are vulnerable to silent, automated exploitation. The demonstration that adversarially annotated video-text pairs measurably degrade downstream text-to-video quality (VQA scores dropping ~35%) gives content owners a concrete, quantifiable reason to adopt such watermarks. Key limitations include the white-box assumption (full access to victim model parameters), the 8-frame preprocessing that may not reflect real video compression pipelines, and the acknowledged prompt-transferability fragility of Mute-N. Open questions include black-box robustness, survival under standard video codecs, and whether adaptive defenses (e.g., model ensembles or input sanitization) can neutralize these watermarks.
