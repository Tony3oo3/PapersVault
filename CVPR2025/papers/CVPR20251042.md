## Problem

Existing image editing methods lack precise layer-based control, and prior layer decomposition approaches (e.g., LayerDiffusion, MULAN) either generate layers from text prompts rather than conditioning on input images or fail to preserve visual effects like shadows and reflections in the correct layer. The core challenge is decomposing an image into a clean background and a transparent foreground that faithfully retains associated visual effects, particularly when ground-truth foreground annotations are unavailable for real-world data.

## Input / Output

The model takes a 512×512 composite RGB image and a binary object mask as conditional inputs, both encoded into 64×64 latent feature maps via a shared RGB-VAE. It produces two outputs: a clean RGB background layer and a transparent RGBA foreground layer (encoded via a fine-tuned RGBA-VAE with 4 channels) that carries the object along with its shadows and reflections in the alpha channel. The two layers can be re-composited via standard alpha blending to reconstruct the original image.

## Method

LAYERDECOMP is a 5B-parameter Diffusion Transformer (with the text encoder removed) that jointly denoises two noisy latent streams—background (RGB) and foreground (RGBA)—conditioned on the composite image and object mask latents, which are concatenated as patch embeddings with learnable type embeddings and processed through standard DiT transformer blocks. The key training innovation is a pixel-space consistency loss: at each denoising timestep, the model's predicted x̂₀ for both layers is decoded back to pixel space, alpha-blended, and compared via L1 to the original composite image, forcing the foreground to absorb visual effects even when no foreground ground truth exists. Training uses a hybrid dataset of ~100k simulated triplets (foreground objects with MetaShadow-synthesized shadows composited onto 5M stock backgrounds) plus 6,000 real camera-captured background/composite pairs, with the standard denoising diffusion loss masked out for the foreground branch when ground truth is absent. The RGBA-VAE is fine-tuned from the base VAE following LayerDiffusion's approach of extending from 3 to 4 channels with minimal latent-space disturbance.

## Results

On the RORD benchmark (tight mask), LAYERDECOMP achieves PSNR 24.79, LPIPS 0.132, FID 21.73, and CLIP-FID 5.778, substantially outperforming ControlNet Inpainting (PSNR 22.01, FID 53.71), SD-XL Inpainting (PSNR 20.81, FID 56.28), and PowerPaint (PSNR 21.26, FID 56.56). On MULAN, it reaches PSNR 19.13 and FID 39.26 versus 17.79/65.03 (CNI), 16.04/65.74 (SDI), and 17.17/55.80 (PowerPaint). On DESOBAv2 shadow removal, LAYERDECOMP attains masked PSNR 38.57 and masked SSIM 0.640, surpassing all baselines. In user studies, LAYERDECOMP is preferred in 83–88% of cases for instruction-driven removal and 87–96% for spatial editing.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against mask-based inpainting methods (ControlNet Inpainting, SD-XL Inpainting, PowerPaint), LAYERDECOMP wins on every metric across all three benchmarks, with the largest gaps in FID (21.73 vs. 53.71–56.56 on RORD; 39.26 vs. 55.80–65.74 on MULAN) and CLIP-FID (5.78 vs. 9.26–11.32 on RORD). Notably, LAYERDECOMP is robust to mask tightness (tight vs. loose PSNR differs by <0.3 dB), whereas baselines degrade with tight masks. Against ObjectDrop [40], which also handles visual effects with tight masks, LAYERDECOMP achieves comparable background quality but additionally outputs a transparent foreground layer with preserved shadows and reflections, enabling downstream editing that ObjectDrop does not support. In instruction-driven removal user studies, LAYERDECOMP is preferred over Emu-Edit in 83.5% of cases and over OmniGen in 87.8% of cases, primarily due to superior background integrity and complete object removal. For spatial editing, LAYERDECOMP dominates all four comparisons (DesignEdit, DragAnything, DiffEditor) with 87–96% preference rates, with the strongest advantage in result plausibility (90–96%) and content integrity (77–95%), attributed to natural shadow/reflection movement during object relocation and resizing.

## Relation to Prior Work

LayerDecomp builds on the DiT architecture [29] and the RGBA-VAE formulation from LayerDiffusion [48], but pivots from text-to-image layer generation to image-conditioned layer decomposition. It departs from prior inpainting and object-removal methods (ControlNet, SD-XL, PowerPaint, ObjectDrop) by producing a full two-layer representation—clean background plus transparent foreground with preserved shadows and reflections—rather than a single inpainted image. The key conceptual departure is the consistency loss, which sidesteps the need for ground-truth foreground annotations by enforcing that the re-composited prediction matches the input, a formulation not present in prior layer-generation or inpainting work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical, training-free pathway to layer-based editing: once decomposition is done, spatial, color, and style edits on individual layers require no additional model inference. The consistency loss is a broadly applicable trick for learning unannotated components in generative models, and the simulated-plus-real data pipeline offers a reproducible recipe for scaling layer-decomposition training. Open questions remain around robustness to incomplete masks, heavy occlusions, and complex colored shadows, and the current two-layer formulation does not yet handle arbitrary multi-object scenes with interacting effects in a single forward pass.
