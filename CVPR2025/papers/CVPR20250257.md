## Problem

Existing hallucination benchmarks for MLLMs (e.g., AMBER, POPE, CHAIR) are static, using fixed test data with limited distributions that are vulnerable to data contamination, where test samples overlap with training data and inflate performance metrics. No open-set, dynamic evaluation protocol exists specifically for MLLM hallucination assessment, making it unclear whether correct responses reflect genuine understanding or memorized training patterns.

## Input / Output

The protocol takes synthetic images (generated by FLUX.1-dev or Stable Diffusion 1.5 from concept-pair prompts, filtered by an open-vocabulary detector at a 0.65 confidence threshold) paired with templated text inquiries as input. For generative tasks, the inquiry is a free-form description prompt; for discriminative tasks, it is a yes/no question about object existence or specific attributes (state, action, quantity), including counterfactual prompts about nonexistent objects. Output is a set of hallucination metrics: CHAIR, Cover, Hal, and Cog for generative tasks; Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1 (computed separately on hallucination-related 'no' questions and across all questions) for discriminative tasks, yielding 8,786 test data pairs across 808 images.

## Method

ODE constructs a weighted graph G=(V,A,E,W) where nodes V are 337 object meta-concepts extracted from AMBER, attribute nodes A encode state/action/quantity properties, and edge weights W reflect real-world co-occurrence frequencies. Concept pairs are selected under four distributional criteria—Standard (argmax co-occurrence), Long-tail (bounded moderate frequency), Random (uniform sampling), and Fictional (zero co-occurrence)—each paired with appropriately chosen attributes to create distinct semantic difficulty levels. Text-to-image models generate images from the selected concept-attribute combinations, and an open-vocabulary detector filters low-quality samples. Templated inquiries are then automatically generated for both generative and discriminative evaluation, and the entire pipeline supports iterative dynamic updates by introducing new concept pairs and attribute combinations, enabling continuous benchmark refresh without human curation.

## Results

On the static AMBER benchmark, MiniGPT-4 achieves 98.4% F1 on existence-level discrimination, but this drops sharply to 64.3% under ODE's Standard criterion, suggesting data contamination inflates static scores. LLaVA-1.5 similarly declines from 83.0% to 70.7% F1, and its generative CHAIR score worsens from 7.7 to 38.9. Across all five evaluated models, ODE-generated samples consistently yield higher hallucination rates than AMBER, with the gap most pronounced for MiniGPT-4 (ΔF1 = −34.1) and LLaVA-1.5 (ΔF1 = −12.3). Fine-tuning LLaVA on ODE-identified error samples recovers existence F1 to 97.9% and attribute Recall to 90.7% on the AMBER test set.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole direct baseline is the AMBER static benchmark [26], which the authors use both as a comparison point and as the source of the 337 object concepts for graph construction. Under ODE's Standard criterion, every model's existence-level F1 drops relative to AMBER: MiniGPT-4 falls from 98.4% to 64.3% (−34.1), LLaVA-1.5 from 83.0% to 70.7% (−12.3), InstructBLIP from 80.5% to 67.4% (−13.1), while CogVLM and mPLUG show smaller or even slightly positive shifts (34.5→41.5 and 26.1→23.5 respectively). In generative tasks, CHAIR scores increase by roughly 4–5× across all models (e.g., LLaVA 7.7→38.9; InstructBLIP 12.5→59.9), confirming that static benchmarks substantially under-report hallucination. The authors interpret these gaps as evidence of data contamination in AMBER's COCO-derived images, since ODE's synthetic images are novel to all models. A secondary comparison against COCO2014 and recent Internet images (Fig. 1) further validates that ODE-generated images produce hallucination rates comparable to uncontaminated Internet data, reinforcing the contamination hypothesis.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the established line of static MLLM hallucination benchmarks (CHAIR, POPE, AMBER, HallusionBench) by introducing an open-set, dynamically generated evaluation protocol. It draws conceptual inspiration from dynamic evaluation methods developed for LLMs (DyVal's graph-informed sample generation, MSTemp's semantic template rewriting) but is the first to apply such a framework to multimodal hallucination assessment. The method repurposes graph-based concept modeling and text-to-image diffusion models (Stable Diffusion, FLUX) as tools for controlled test-data synthesis, a combination not previously used for hallucination benchmarking.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The protocol addresses a practical and growing concern: if MLLM hallucination benchmarks are contaminated by training data, reported improvements may be illusory. The finding that models perform substantially worse on ODE-generated samples than on static benchmarks like AMBER provides concrete evidence of this problem and offers a reproducible remedy. Limitations include reliance on text-to-image quality (acknowledged via the Flux vs. SD comparison in Table 2), coverage restricted to object existence and attribute hallucinations (relational and event hallucinations remain unaddressed), and the fact that synthetic images may not capture all visual subtleties of real scenes. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to more complex hallucination types and whether iterative updates can keep pace with rapidly evolving MLLM architectures.
