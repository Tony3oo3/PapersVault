## Problem

Animating 3D characters with complex structures like hair and clothing remains unsolved: existing rigging methods rely on body-only parametric models (e.g., SMPL) that cannot drive external elements, and no large-scale dataset exists for heterogeneous skeleton rigging. Additionally, 3D Gaussian representations lack the mesh topology that prior learning-based rigging methods depend on, making skeleton prediction on this representation particularly challenging.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251842/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline accepts a single anime image (in any pose, converted to T-pose via Animagine-XL with IP-Adapter and ControlNet) or a text prompt (rendered to a T-pose frontal image via fine-tuned Animagine-XL). It produces a 3D Gaussian representation with a full heterogeneous skeleton (25 body joints plus variable hair/clothing joints), bone connections via MST, and per-Gaussian-point skinning weights. The 3D Gaussian is generated by a fine-tuned LGM from 4 canonical views, and the final rigged model supports real-time animation rendering at high resolution.

## Method

The core contribution is GSDiff, a conditional diffusion model that denoises joint positions as spatial distributions rather than regressing them, enabling it to handle heterogeneous skeleton topologies (varying numbers of joints for different hairstyles and garments). Conditioning combines three signals: DGCNN features from 3D Gaussian means (global geometry), k-NN local geometric features around each denoising joint, and CLIP features from 4 canonical multi-view renderings (appearance). Two separate diffusion models predict body joints and non-body joints (hair, clothing) independently. Bone connections are established via a learned BoneFlow field plus MST, and skinning is refined from a k-NN distance-based initialization using a network trained with cross-entropy, KL divergence, and a spatial smoothness loss. The entire pipeline is trained on AnimeRig, a curated dataset of 9,420 rigged meshes with calibrated skeleton and skinning annotations, converted to 3D Gaussian via fine-tuned LGM and ICP-based label transfer.

## Results

On the AnimeRig test set, DRiVE achieves 70.48% IoU for joint prediction, substantially outperforming AnimSkelVolNet (27.74%) and RigNet (28.69%), representing a 59.3% relative improvement over RigNet. For bone estimation (CD-B2B), DRiVE reaches 1.96% vs. 3.26% for RigNet (39.9% improvement). For skinning, DRiVE attains 78.34% precision and 0.48 average L1 error, surpassing GeoVoxel (44.48% precision, 0.88 L1) and RigNet (41.94% precision, 1.00 L1) by 43.2% in precision and 45.5% in L1 error respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DRiVE is compared against three skeleton baselines (AnimSkelVolNet, RigNet with GT mesh, and RigNet with CharacterGen-predicted mesh) and two skinning baselines (GeoVoxel and RigNet, both with GT mesh). DRiVE wins decisively on every metric: its 70.48% IoU nearly triples the best baseline (RigNet at 28.69%), and its CD-B2B of 1.96% is roughly half that of RigNet (3.26%). For skinning, DRiVE's 78.34% precision exceeds GeoVoxel (44.48%) by over 33 percentage points, and its 0.48 average L1 error is less than half of GeoVoxel's 0.88. Notably, the baselines use ground-truth meshes as input while DRiVE operates on 3D Gaussians generated from a single image, making the comparison even more favorable to DRiVE. The paper also notes that RigNet struggles to converge on the more complex heterogeneous skeletal structures in AnimeRig, and that using CharacterGen-predicted meshes (rather than GT) further degrades RigNet's performance, sometimes causing complete joint estimation failures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of 3D character generation (building on LGM for 3D Gaussian reconstruction and CharacterGen for single-image character synthesis) and automatic rigging (departing from regression-based methods like RigNet and AnimSkelVolNet). The key conceptual departure is twofold: (1) replacing mesh with 3D Gaussian as the rigging substrate, motivated by the difficulty of guaranteeing watertight topology and texture quality in generated meshes, and (2) replacing regression with conditional diffusion (GSDiff) for joint prediction, treating joints as spatial distributions rather than point estimates to handle heterogeneous skeleton topologies. The BoneFlow formulation for bone connection is adapted from TARig, and the set-transformer diffusion backbone follows GeCCo, but the conditioning scheme that fuses DGCNN geometric features with CLIP appearance features via k-NN lookup is novel to this work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DRiVE is the first pipeline to close the loop from a single image or text prompt to a fully rigged, animatable 3D character with realistic clothing and hair dynamics, addressing a practical gap that has limited the downstream use of generated avatars in gaming and film. The AnimeRig dataset (9,420 annotated meshes with heterogeneous skeletons) is a significant resource that was previously absent from the literature. Open questions remain: the skeleton node density is fixed and cannot be user-tuned, 3D Gaussian lacks mature collision detection (causing self-intersection between limbs and garments), and the method is validated primarily on anime-style characters, leaving generalization to photorealistic humans untested.
