## Problem

Existing video editing methods are task-specific, requiring separate pipelines or retraining for removal, insertion, or tracking, and rely on intermediate representations (optical flow, depth) that accumulate errors and limit flexibility. They also struggle with substantial shape changes, independent object motion, and removing associated effects like shadows and reflections. This paper unifies these tasks under a single 'generative video propagation' formulation that leverages the generative power of I2V models to propagate first-frame edits without auxiliary motion predictions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252306/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an original video (T frames at 360p base resolution, 12 or 24 FPS, up to 128 frames) and a single edited first frame produced by any image editing tool, with no dense per-frame mask annotation required. It outputs a full video where the first-frame modification is propagated to all subsequent frames while unchanged regions remain pixel-consistent with the original. Results can be upscaled to 720p via a super-resolution model, and the framework supports multiple simultaneous edits (outpainting, insertion, removal) in a single inference run.

## Method

GenProp couples a Selective Content Encoder (SCE), a replicated copy of the initial N transformer blocks (ControlNet-style), with a frozen I2V generation model (DiT or SVD-based U-Net). The SCE encodes the original video to preserve unchanged regions, while the I2V model propagates the edited first frame into modified regions; a Mask Prediction Decoder (MPD) provides auxiliary supervision to help disentangle edited from unedited areas. Training uses a Region-Aware Loss that computes separate diffusion MSE for masked and unmasked regions, plus a finite-difference gradient penalty that suppresses SCE features in the edited area, preventing the encoder from 'leaking' original content into regions the generator should fill. Synthetic training pairs are generated from video instance segmentation datasets (YouTubeVOS, SAM-V2) via three augmentations—copy-and-paste (insertion), mask-and-fill (editing), and color fill (tracking)—each paired with a task embedding, while the I2V model always sees the original video to avoid learning synthetic artifacts.

## Results

On the Challenging Test Set (large object replacement, insertion, background replacement), GenProp achieves PSNR_m of 32.163, CLIP-T of 0.3336, and CLIP-I of 0.9904, outperforming all baselines on every metric. On the Classic Test Set (object replacement and appearance editing), GenProp scores PSNR_m 33.837, CLIP-T 0.3229, and CLIP-I 0.9825. In user studies (121 participants), GenProp is preferred over the strongest baseline (AnyV2V) 97.78% of the time on alignment and 95.56% on quality for the Challenging Set. For object removal, GenProp achieves CLIP-I of 0.9879, surpassing SAM+Propainter (0.9809) and ReVideo (0.9728).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GenProp outperforms all four video editing baselines (InsV2V, AnyV2V, Pika, ReVideo) on PSNR_m and CLIP-I across both test sets, with the largest margins on the Challenging Set where tasks involve large shape changes and background edits. AnyV2V is the closest competitor on user preference (97.78% alignment, 95.56% quality for GenProp on Challenging), but GenProp leads on all quantitative metrics. Pika achieves the highest CLIP-I on the Classic Set (0.9923 vs. 0.9825) because its bounding-box approach works well when object shapes remain unchanged, but it degrades significantly on the Challenging Set. ReVideo, which requires a two-stage remove-then-insert pipeline for large shape changes, suffers from blurry boundaries and accumulated tracking errors, yielding the lowest PSNR_m (29.920) on the Challenging Set. For object removal, GenProp (CLIP-I 0.9879) beats the SAM 2 + Propainter pipeline (0.9809) and ReVideo (0.9728), with user preference of 82.22% and 86.36% respectively, and uniquely removes associated effects like shadows and reflections without requiring dense per-frame mask annotations.

## Relation to Prior Work

GenProp builds on two key architectural lineages: the ControlNet-style replicated encoder (for its Selective Content Encoder) and latent I2V diffusion models (SVD/DiT) as the generative backbone. Conceptually, it departs from traditional video propagation methods that rely on explicit motion representations (optical flow, depth, deformation fields) and from task-specific editing pipelines (separate models for inpainting, appearance editing, insertion) by reframing all of these as instances of a single first-frame-edit propagation problem solved by a generative model. The synthetic data generation pipeline draws on video instance segmentation datasets (YouTubeVOS, SAM 2) to create paired training samples without requiring hand-annotated edit pairs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By unifying removal, insertion, replacement, tracking, and outpainting under one model that requires only a first-frame edit (no per-frame masks), GenProp substantially lowers the practical barrier to video editing and opens the door to combining multiple edits in a single inference pass. The ability to propagate and track object effects (shadows, reflections) that segmentation-based methods like SAM 2 miss highlights a broader implication: generative pretraining encodes physical scene understanding that discriminative models lack. Limitations include reliance on synthetic training data, slower inference than real-time trackers, and current restriction to single-keyframe edits; the authors note multi-keyframe propagation as future work. Whether this propagation paradigm scales to longer videos or more complex multi-object interactions remains open.
