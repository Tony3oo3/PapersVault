## Problem

Current GCD methods rely exclusively on single-modality visual features, which struggle to discriminate visually similar categories (e.g., fine-grained bird or car species). While CLIP's text encoder could provide complementary discriminative information, the absence of class names for unlabelled data makes it impossible to directly feed text into the encoder, leaving CLIP's multi-modal potential locked for the GCD task.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252679/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes RGB images (both labeled and unlabelled) along with class names for known classes, which are encoded via CLIP's text encoder using the prompt 'a photo of a {CLS}' to produce real text embeddings for distillation. The number of new classes is assumed known or estimated externally. The model outputs a cluster assignment (class label) for every sample at inference, using only the visual branch, while during training it also produces 768-dimensional pseudo text embeddings per sample via the TES module.

## Method

GET operates in two stages. First, a Text Embedding Synthesizer (TES) — a single fully connected layer — maps CLIP's visual embeddings into pseudo tokens that are fed into CLIP's frozen text encoder to generate pseudo text embeddings; TES is trained with a symmetric contrastive align loss (pulling matched image–pseudo-text pairs together) and a distill loss (matching pseudo-text to real text embeddings of labeled classes), with a multi-view strategy for instance-level consistency. Second, a dual-branch framework applies SimGCD's parametric training strategy (supervised + self-supervised contrastive representation learning, cross-entropy + self-distillation classification) independently to visual and text branches, coupled by a Cross-Modal Instance Consistency Objective (CICO) that enforces mutual KL-divergence alignment of each sample's relationship to labeled-class prototypes across both modalities, plus a multi-modal mean-entropy regularization to prevent trivial solutions. The key insight is that converting visual features into text-encoder input tokens — rather than retrieving or generating natural language — yields modality-aligned pseudo text embeddings that are directly comparable to real text embeddings in CLIP's joint space.

## Results

On fine-grained datasets, GET achieves 77.0% / 78.5% / 58.9% clustering accuracy (All classes) on CUB, Stanford Cars, and FGVC-Aircraft respectively, surpassing the strongest baseline SimGCD-CLIP by 5.3%, 8.5%, and 4.6%. On generic datasets, GET reaches 97.2% on CIFAR-10, 82.1% on CIFAR-100, and 91.7% on ImageNet-100. On the challenging multi-domain ImageNet-R benchmark, GET achieves 58.1% All accuracy, a 3.2% improvement over SimGCD-CLIP (54.9%), with the text branch alone reaching 62.6% All accuracy due to text consistency across domains.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GET outperforms all 14 baselines on every dataset where numbers are reported. The most direct comparison is against SimGCD-CLIP (same CLIP ViT-B/16 backbone, single-modality): GET gains +5.3% on CUB, +8.5% on Stanford Cars, +4.6% on FGVC-Aircraft, and +0.9% on ImageNet-100, confirming that the multi-modal text branch is the source of improvement. Against the DINO-backbone SOTA methods (SimGCD, LegoGCD, μGCD), GET leads by 5.3–11.5% on fine-grained 'All' accuracy, while on generic datasets the gap narrows to 0.1–1.0% due to CLIP's weaker zero-shot performance on low-resolution CIFAR. On the multi-domain ImageNet-R benchmark, GET surpasses SimGCD-CLIP by 3.2% (All) and 6.0% (Old), a margin much larger than on single-domain datasets, highlighting the robustness of text information across visual domains. In the pseudo-text-embedding comparison (Table 5), TES outperforms BLIP-2-based VQA (71.3% vs 77.0% on CUB All) and captioning (42.6% vs 77.0%) with far fewer parameters (165M vs 3.9B), while text-retrieval from WordNet/CC3M actually degrades performance below the SimGCD-CLIP baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the parametric GCD framework of SimGCD [45] by replacing its single-visual-modality DINO backbone with CLIP's dual encoders, and draws on BARON [46]'s idea of treating visual embeddings as text encoder tokens to synthesize pseudo text features for unlabelled data. It departs from prior multi-modal GCD attempts (CLIP-GCD [33], TextGCD [52]) that rely on external text corpora or LLMs by generating pseudo text embeddings entirely from visual features via a lightweight linear mapping, requiring no additional databases. The dual-branch architecture with cross-modal instance consistency adapts the mutual knowledge distillation paradigm of [49] from local/global views to visual/text modalities.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GET provides a practical, lightweight solution (a single fully connected layer) to the fundamental problem of leveraging CLIP's text encoder when class names are unavailable for unlabelled data, making multi-modal GCD accessible without external resources or large generative models. The consistent improvements across fine-grained, generic, and multi-domain benchmarks (especially ImageNet-R) demonstrate that text modality is a reliable complement to visual features for discriminative category discovery. A key limitation acknowledged by the authors is the fixed equal weighting of modalities; adaptive per-sample or per-class modality weighting remains an open question. The forward-looking experiments on truly novel categories (NEV, TV-100) suggest the approach generalizes beyond CLIP's training distribution, though the margins are smaller in those settings.
