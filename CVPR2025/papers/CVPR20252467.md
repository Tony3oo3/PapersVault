## Problem

Existing CAM-based saliency methods (GradCAM, ScoreCAM, etc.) estimate feature importance via decision-boundary gradients, which identifies the fastest direction to flip a prediction rather than the actual feature difference between a target example and its reference class. This produces counter-intuitive explanations when the target is not a representative member of its class, and the reliance on prediction scores restricts applicability to supervised classifiers, excluding foundation models from self-supervised pre-training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252467/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a target image, a flexible set of reference examples (whose labels may be the same, different, or absent), and a trained deep model (CNN or ViT). It produces a spatial saliency map via weighted aggregation of the last convolutional layer's activation channels, where the weights form a feature importance vector in the deep embedding space. For large-scale datasets, the reference group is selected via k-NN search over pre-computed class mean features using Faiss-GPU, and the inverse covariance matrix S_v^{-1} is pre-computed to keep inference efficient.

## Method

DiffCAM formulates feature importance as a Rayleigh quotient optimization: find the direction w in the deep feature space that maximizes the squared distance between the target's projection and the reference group's mean projection, normalized by the reference group's projected variance, yielding the closed-form solution w* = S_v^{-1}(z − μ̂) with Tikhonov regularization for numerical stability. This direction captures only the most discriminative feature components relative to the actual reference data distribution, rather than the gradient direction toward the decision boundary. The resulting weights are applied to the spatial activation maps in the standard CAM aggregation to produce the final saliency map. The flexible reference group selection unifies multiple explanation types: all other classes answer 'why is,' the target's own class answers 'why not' (counterfactual), and a subset of same-class examples reveals intra-class variance. The approach requires no class-specific prediction scores, enabling direct application to self-supervised models (MoCo-v3, DINO) and Vision Transformers by reshaping patch tokens.

## Results

On ImageNet object localization, DiffCAM achieves the best AUPRC of 0.741 (ResNet-50) and 0.794 (Inception-v3), matching LayerCAM's top IoU of 0.583 on Inception-v3. On the CUB-200-2011 counterfactual explanation task, DiffCAM attains the highest Recall (0.17) and PIoU (0.20) for similar counterfactual classes, and the best Precision (0.83) for random counterfactuals. On medical imaging (RSNA pneumonia), DiffCAM reaches AUPRC 0.492, surpassing the strongest baseline GradCAM (0.467) by 5.4% relative, and on the harder SIIM pneumothorax task it leads all methods (0.093 vs. 0.076 for GradCAM).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the four CAM-family baselines on ImageNet, DiffCAM matches or exceeds LayerCAM (the strongest competitor) on AUPRC for both backbones (0.741 vs 0.738 ResNet-50; 0.794 vs 0.792 Inception-v3) and ties on Inception-v3 IoU (0.583), while clearly outperforming GradCAM, ScoreCAM, and XGradCAM on every metric. In the counterfactual explanation setting on CUB-200-2011, DiffCAM dominates GALORE on all three metrics for similar counterfactuals (R 0.17 vs 0.16, P 0.61 vs 0.60, PIoU 0.20 vs 0.15) and also leads on Recall and Precision for random counterfactuals, though CounteRGAN edges it out slightly on random PIoU (0.20 vs 0.18). On medical imaging, DiffCAM's advantage is most pronounced: it beats GradCAM by 2.5 AUPRC points on RSNA (0.492 vs 0.467) and by 1.7 points on SIIM (0.093 vs 0.076), while gradient-based methods like Integrated Gradients (0.317 RSNA) and Guided Backpropagation (0.174 RSNA) fall far behind, confirming that data-distribution-aware explanations capture clinically relevant regions more faithfully than pure gradient signals.

## Relation to Prior Work

DiffCAM builds directly on the Class Activation Map (CAM) framework of Zhou et al. (CVPR 2016), which aggregates deep feature channels via a learned weight vector to produce a saliency map. It departs from the dominant paradigm in CAM-based methods (GradCAM, ScoreCAM, LayerCAM, XGradCAM) that estimate feature importance as the direction orthogonal to the decision boundary or the individual channel's contribution to the class score. Instead, DiffCAM formulates feature importance as a Fisher discriminant direction that maximizes separation between the target example and a user-specified reference group while preserving within-group variance, drawing on classical linear discriminant analysis. This positions it between attribution-based explanations and counterfactual explanation methods (GALORE, CounteRGAN), unifying them under a single reference-group selection mechanism without requiring synthetic image generation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine conceptual gap: gradient-based CAM methods explain the fastest path to flip a prediction, which can misattribute importance for non-representative examples (e.g., a failing student whose writing is actually strong). The practical impact is strongest in medical imaging, where DiffCAM achieves a notable AUPRC margin over GradCAM on the RSNA pneumonia dataset (0.492 vs. 0.467), and in its applicability to self-supervised foundation models (MoCo-v3, DINO) that lack class-specific prediction heads. Key limitations include the assumption of a linear classifier on top of the final feature layer, and the need for a meaningful reference group (which requires k-NN retrieval for large-scale datasets like ImageNet). Open questions include extending the method to non-linear heads, handling open-set scenarios where no clean reference group exists, and whether the Fisher-discriminant formulation generalizes to multi-scale or hierarchical feature aggregation.
