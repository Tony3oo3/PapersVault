## Problem

Latency attacks exploit the quadratic time complexity of Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) by generating thousands of phantom bounding boxes, degrading real-time object detection on edge devices from 30+ FPS to as low as 13 FPS. While multiple attacks (Daedalus, Phantom Sponge, Overload) have been proposed, there is a paucity of defenses that account for the specific adversarial behavior of latency attacks and the heterogeneous hardware capacities of different GPUs.

## Input / Output

The method takes standard RGB images and produces bounding boxes with class labels and confidence scores, following the standard YOLO detection pipeline. Unusually, the training procedure incorporates hardware-specific system metrics (GPU IoU processing speed, PCIe memory bandwidth, backbone inference time) as a constraint that determines when adversarial training stops, and a binary spatial mask that restricts where perturbations are injected during the inner PGD optimization. The final output is a robust detector model whose expected candidate box count under attack remains below the hardware capacity threshold for a target FPS.

## Method

Underload is a background-attentive adversarial training method that uses the objectness loss as a proxy for the latency-attack adversarial loss (validated by high cosine similarity between the two losses across PGD iterations). The key insight is that background regions have smaller decision-boundary margins than object regions, making them more vulnerable to phantom generation; a binary mask is therefore applied to restrict perturbations to background areas during the inner maximization. The mask starts at full image size and is progressively shrunk by fixed step sizes after each epoch, balancing clean accuracy (larger mask = fewer perturbations = better generalization) against robust accuracy. Training stops when the expected number of candidate boxes under masked perturbation falls below a hardware-derived capacity bound (Eq. 4), which connects the optimization directly to the target FPS requirement on a specific GPU. This hardware-adaptive stopping criterion makes the defense portable across edge (Jetson), desktop (4070Ti), and cloud (A100) accelerators without architectural changes to the NMS module.

## Results

On PASCAL-VOC with YOLOv5s, Underload achieves 61.3% mAP50 under Phantom Sponge attack (vs. 7.5% standard, 54.7% MTD, 53.3% OOD) and 53.3% under Overload (vs. 4.5% standard, 49.4% MTD, 49.2% OOD), while retaining 68.9% clean accuracy (only 4.4% drop vs. 15.6% for MTD and 17.4% for OOD). On YOLOv8s under Daedalus on VOC, Underload reaches 69.7% mAP50, outperforming MTD (65.1%) and OOD (64.3%). On Jetson Orin NX, the defense restores real-time processing from 13 FPS to 43 FPS, meeting the 30 FPS requirement across all YOLO generations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Underload consistently outperforms both MTD [43] and OOD [13] on robust mAP50 across all three YOLO models, all three datasets, and all three latency attacks. The most striking gap appears on YOLOv5s under Phantom Sponge on VOC, where Underload achieves 61.3% vs. 54.7% (MTD) and 53.3% (OOD) — a 6.6–8.0% advantage. On clean accuracy, Underload suffers only a 4.4% drop (73.3%→68.9% on YOLOv5s/VOC) compared to 15.6% for MTD and 17.4% for OOD, indicating a substantially better clean-vs-robust trade-off. MTD, though not designed for latency attacks, retains some defensive capability due to multi-task loss coupling but is unstable on YOLOv3t/VOC and YOLOv8s/VOC. OOD targets objectness loss directly but does not account for the clean-accuracy penalty, resulting in larger drops. On the BDD dataset (small objects, large background), Underload still achieves 1–3% higher clean and robust accuracy than both baselines, confirming the benefit of background-attentive masking in semantically challenging scenes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the adversarial training framework of Madry et al. [22] from the standard misclassification/mislocation threat model to a new latency-attack threat model targeting the NMS post-processing module. It departs from prior defenses (MTD [43], OOD [13]) by identifying objectness loss as a principled proxy for the adversarial loss and by introducing a background-attentive masking mechanism that exploits the finding that background regions contain more non-robust features. The hardware-adaptive formulation—connecting system-level GPU profiling (IoU throughput, PCIe bandwidth) to the training stopping criterion via Eq. (4)—is a novel bridge between systems analysis and adversarial learning that prior work on latency attacks [3, 28, 35] did not attempt.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first defense specifically targeting latency attacks on object detectors deployed on edge devices, a threat that can cascade into safety-critical failures in autonomous driving and surveillance. The practical impact is significant: restoring 13→43 FPS on Jetson Orin NX addresses a real deployment concern for millions of edge systems. However, the defense still relies on NMS (it mitigates rather than eliminates the vulnerability), is evaluated only on YOLO-family detectors, and the hardware-adaptive stopping criterion requires per-device system profiling that may not generalize cleanly to new hardware. Open questions include whether the approach transfers to NMS-free DETR-based detectors and whether the background-attentive masking generalizes beyond the tested perturbation budgets.
