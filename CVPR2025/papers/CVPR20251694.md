## Problem

Existing zero-shot anomaly detection methods learn normal/abnormal semantics in the text space of vision-language models, which introduces modality bias and requires strong correlation between auxiliary and test data. When the auxiliary training data and test images come from different modalities (e.g., training on RGB, testing on 3D or thermal), these methods suffer significant performance degradation because the decision boundaries learned in one modality do not transfer to another. This paper formalizes the cross-modal anomaly detection task—training on known modalities and generalizing to unseen ones—and proposes a solution that avoids text-space bias entirely.

## Input / Output

The method takes a query image from an unseen modality (resized to 224×224) and, in the few-shot setting, K normal support images (K∈{1,2,4,8}) from the same unseen modality. It produces both an image-level anomaly score (a weighted combination of global and top-k local predictions) and a pixel-level anomaly map at the original image resolution. Features are extracted via CLIP's visual encoder at intermediate layers 5, 15, and 25, and the model is trained per-dataset on known modalities before being applied to the unseen modality at test time.

## Method

The core contribution is the Transferable Visual Prototype (TVP), which learns normal and abnormal prototypes directly in the visual embedding space (bypassing text-space modality bias) with separate global and local components, each containing a normal and an abnormal prototype vector. Training uses cross-entropy loss for the global part and focal+dice loss for the local part across multiple CLIP layers. At inference, the Prototype Harmonization strategy computes a relevance weight for each TVP (trained on a different known-modality dataset) via softmax over cosine similarities between the query's global feature and each TVP, then adaptively fuses anomaly scores across all TVPs. In the few-shot setting, Visual Discrepancy Inference additionally compares the query's local features against a memory bank built from the few normal support images, and the resulting discrepancy map is combined with the zero-shot prediction to yield the final score.

## Results

In the zero-shot cross-modal setting, the proposed method achieves the best AUROC on 9 out of 10 datasets, with mean AUROC improvements of 4.1%, 6.1%, 7.6%, and 6.8% over the best competing methods in the RGB, 3D, MRI/CT, and Thermal modalities, respectively. In the few-shot setting (K=1,2,4,8), the method achieves the highest AUROC and AP on all ten datasets across all four modalities, substantially outperforming the second-best method (e.g., 97.4% AUROC vs. 95.8% for PromptAD on MvTec AD at 8-shot). The method also demonstrates superior cross-modal consistency, whereas baselines like WinCLIP drop from 91.8% AUROC on MvTec AD to 49.1% on OCT2017.

## Baselines & Comparisons

In the zero-shot setting, the proposed method outperforms all four CLIP-based baselines (VAND, WinCLIP, AnomalyCLIP, AdaCLIP) on average across every modality. WinCLIP achieves the highest single-dataset AUROC on MvTec AD (91.8%) but collapses to 49.1% on OCT2017, while AdaCLIP reaches 95.3% on BTAD yet only 72.5% on Leaves, highlighting their poor cross-modal robustness. In the few-shot setting, the method dominates all five baselines (PatchCore, RegAD, WinCLIP, PromptAD, InCTRL) on all ten datasets at every shot count (K=1,2,4,8). PromptAD is the strongest few-shot competitor, reaching 95.8% AUROC on MvTec AD at 8-shot, but the proposed method extends this to 97.4%. RegAD and PatchCore, which rely on feature-distance or registration mechanisms, show the weakest cross-modal performance, particularly on MRI/CT datasets where RegAD drops below 50% AUROC.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of zero-shot anomaly detection (WinCLIP, AnomalyCLIP, AdaCLIP, VAND) that learns normal/abnormal semantics in the text space of vision-language models, arguing that the known modality gap introduces bias when transferring to unseen modalities. Instead, it learns prototypes directly in the visual space (Transferable Visual Prototype) and introduces a Prototype Harmonization strategy that adaptively weights prototypes from multiple known modalities at inference time. For the few-shot component, it builds on the nearest-neighbor-in-feature-space idea from PatchCore/RegAD but frames it as Visual Discrepancy Inference that composes with the zero-shot prototype branch. The paper also formally defines cross-modal anomaly detection as a distinct task, positioning it beyond the same-modality assumption implicit in all prior zero/few-shot anomaly detection work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The cross-modal formulation addresses a practical gap: industrial and medical pipelines often have training data in one modality (e.g., RGB) but must detect anomalies in another (e.g., thermal, 3D, MRI), a scenario prior methods handle poorly. The consistent 4–8% AUROC gains across four modalities and the clean ablation isolating the contribution of each component (global vs. local prototype, harmonization vs. single-best, TVP vs. VDI) make the method a useful reference point. Open questions include whether the CLIP visual encoder is truly modality-agnostic enough for arbitrary new modalities, whether the prototype harmonization strategy scales gracefully as the number of training modalities grows, and whether the approach extends to video or multi-spectral settings beyond the four tested.
