## Problem

Existing few-shot counting methods follow an extract-then-match pipeline where object prototypes are learned from a narrow source domain, causing severe performance degradation when tested on unseen target domains due to domain shift. This single domain generalization setting for few-shot counting had remained unexplored, and the paper identifies that the root cause of failure is the inherently narrow distribution of the learned prototypes, which cannot adequately match objects in broader, unobserved target distributions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252529/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image (resized to 512×512) and n exemplar bounding boxes as input, producing a 2D density map at full resolution whose pixel sum yields the count estimate. In the zero-shot variant, exemplar boxes are replaced by learnable embeddings only. The CLIP teacher model is used exclusively during training for knowledge distillation, so inference requires no external model and operates at the same efficiency as standard extract-then-match counters. Multi-scale backbone features are extracted at 64×64 spatial resolution with 256 channels.

## Method

URM introduces universal vision-language prototypes that are distilled from a frozen CLIP model during training via feature mimicry (L2) loss, then used for correlation construction at inference. For the visual branch, MaskCLIP produces a class-aware segmentation mask, and Mask Pooling between the CLIP vision encoder's 2D embeddings and this mask yields a local, noise-free universal visual representation. For the language branch, LLM-generated (GPT-4) customized prompts plus hand-written templates are encoded by CLIP's text encoder to produce universal text embeddings. Both prototypes are iteratively updated through cross-attention with the exemplar-encoded image feature, and the concatenated universal V-L prototypes are matched against the image feature via additional cross-attention layers to produce the correlation map, which a convolutional regression head converts to the density map. The key insight is that matching in CLIP's well-separated open-set representation space, rather than in a narrow source-domain prototype space, provides domain-invariant boundaries that generalize to unseen categories and scenes.

## Results

On the cross-domain few-shot setting (FSC-147 → FSCD-LVIS), URM achieves MAE 21.87 and RMSE 38.42, outperforming the best domain generalization baseline MPCount (MAE 25.11, RMSE 41.32) by 3.24 MAE and the best vanilla method DAVE (MAE 29.67, RMSE 45.28) by 7.80 MAE. On the reverse direction (FSCD-LVIS → FSC-147), URM reaches MAE 21.17 and RMSE 73.42, again leading all baselines. In the zero-shot setting, URM achieves MAE 23.54 (A→B) and 27.49 (B→A), maintaining its lead over all compared methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

URM outperforms all 12 baselines across every cross-domain configuration. Among vanilla extract-then-match methods, DAVE is the strongest (MAE 29.67 A→B, 29.51 B→A), yet URM improves by 7.80 and 8.34 MAE respectively, demonstrating that architectural changes alone (e.g., DAVE's detect-and-verify post-processing) cannot close the domain gap. Among domain generalization methods, MPCount is the closest competitor (MAE 25.11 A→B, 22.07 B→A), but URM still leads by 3.24 and 0.90 MAE, showing that external universal representations from CLIP provide a more effective generalization signal than memory-bank-based approaches. Sub-domain division methods (DCCUS, GS) and whitening-based methods (ISW) perform poorly under narrow source distributions, with ISW actually degrading below vanilla baselines. In the zero-shot setting, URM extends its lead, achieving MAE 23.54 vs. MPCount's 27.68 (A→B) and 27.49 vs. 30.99 (B→A). On the in-domain FSC-147 benchmark (Table 6), URM alone (MAE 10.15) is competitive with LOCA (10.79), and URM+DAVE (MAE 8.32) marginally surpasses DAVE (8.66), confirming that the distillation does not sacrifice in-domain accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the extract-then-match paradigm for few-shot counting (FamNet, LOCA, DAVE) by identifying the learned prototypes' narrow distribution as the root cause of domain generalization failure, rather than the matching mechanism itself. It repurposes CLIP's universal vision-language representations—accessed via MaskCLIP for local visual features and CuPL-style LLM-generated prompts for language features—and injects them into the prototypes through a simple feature-mimicry distillation loss (following CLIP-KD). This departs from prior domain generalization approaches for counting (ISW, DCCUS, MPCount) that rely on sub-domain partitioning or feature whitening, which the paper shows fail under narrow source distributions. The architectural backbone (prompt encoder, cross-attention prototype updating, regression head) is inherited from LOCA, making the contribution primarily conceptual: changing what the prototypes encode rather than how they are matched.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes single-domain generalization as a meaningful and challenging setting for few-shot counting, providing a new benchmark protocol (FSC-147 ↔ FSCD-LVIS cross-domain) that the community can adopt. The core insight—that prototype distribution narrowness, not matching quality, is the generalization bottleneck—offers a clear diagnostic for future work and suggests that any method following the extract-then-match pipeline should consider enriching prototypes with external knowledge. Limitations include evaluation on only two datasets, dependence on a frozen CLIP model during training, and the observation that larger CLIP variants actually underperform due to dimension mismatch, leaving open whether the approach scales to more diverse or higher-resolution counting scenarios. Whether the CLIP dependency can be replaced by other universal representation sources without performance loss remains an open question.
