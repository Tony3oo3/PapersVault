## Problem

Monte Carlo Dropout approximates predictive uncertainty via stochastic forward passes, but applies fixed dropout rates uniformly across all layers and inputs during inference. This rigidity fails to account for varying input complexity and differing layer sensitivities, producing either excessive noise that degrades predictions or insufficient variability to capture true uncertainty. Existing rate-optimization methods rely on labeled training data and yield static inference-time parameters that cannot adapt to new data distributions.

## Input / Output

Rate-In takes a pre-trained network (ResNet-18 for classification, nnU-Net for segmentation), a single medical image, initial per-layer dropout rates p⁽⁰⁾, and a scalar information-loss threshold ε (set equal to the baseline dropout rate for fair comparison). It operates sequentially during the forward pass, adjusting rates layer-by-layer before a final stochastic forward pass. The output is a set of input-specific, layer-specific dropout rates, a point prediction ŷ, and an uncertainty estimate derived from T=30 MC forward passes with those adjusted rates.

## Method

Rate-In reinterprets each network layer as a noisy communication channel and quantifies dropout-induced information loss via mutual information between a layer's input and output activations, estimated with an adaptive binning estimator. During inference, for each layer it applies the current dropout rate, computes the information loss ΔI_l = I_full − I(h_in; h_out), and adjusts the rate: decreasing p_l if ΔI_l exceeds the threshold ε (too much information lost) or increasing it otherwise (insufficient variability for uncertainty). This loop repeats until |ΔI_l − ε| < δ or a maximum of 30 iterations is reached, then the next layer is processed. After all L layers are tuned, a final forward pass with the adjusted rates yields the prediction and MC Dropout uncertainty estimate. The entire procedure is unsupervised, requires no ground-truth labels, and adds no training overhead—only a bounded per-layer optimization at inference time.

## Results

On classification tasks, Rate-In preserves near-full-model accuracy at moderate dropout (ε=0.05): 0.90 on PathMNIST (full model 0.90), 0.68 on TissueMNIST (full model 0.68), and 0.96 on BloodMNIST (full model 0.97), while Constant dropout degrades to 0.87, 0.53, and 0.95 respectively. At high dropout (ε=0.20), the gap widens dramatically: Rate-In retains 0.75 accuracy on PathMNIST versus 0.41 for Constant dropout, and 0.48 on TissueMNIST versus 0.09. For segmentation, Rate-In achieves lower ECE (e.g., 4.60 vs 5.55 on Peripheral MRI at ε=0.01) and higher BUC (0.67 vs 0.54) while maintaining or slightly improving DSC over the full model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Rate-In is compared against three baselines: Constant (fixed) dropout [38], Scheduled (linearly decreasing) dropout [35], and Activation-Based dropout [3]. Rate-In wins decisively on all three classification datasets, with the largest margins at high dropout rates: on TissueMNIST at ε=0.20, Rate-In achieves 0.48 accuracy vs. 0.09 (Constant), 0.13 (Scheduled), and 0.10 (Activation); on PathMNIST at the same setting, 0.75 vs. 0.41, 0.47, and 0.44. At moderate dropout (ε=0.05), Rate-In matches or slightly exceeds the full-model accuracy on PathMNIST (0.90 vs. 0.90) and TissueMNIST (0.68 vs. 0.68), while Constant and Scheduled drop to 0.87 and 0.53 respectively. On BloodMNIST, all methods perform well at low dropout, but at ε=0.20 Rate-In (0.82) still outperforms Constant (0.58) and Scheduled (0.75), though Activation-Based dropout (0.81) is nearly competitive. For segmentation, Rate-In consistently achieves lower ECE and higher BUC than the best benchmark across all anatomical zones (e.g., ECE 4.60 vs. 5.55 on Peripheral MRI; BUC 0.87 vs. 0.70 on Transitional zone), while maintaining DSC within ±1% of the full model.

## Relation to Prior Work

Rate-In extends Monte Carlo Dropout (Gal & Ghahramani, 2016) by replacing its fixed inference-time dropout rates with per-layer, per-input adaptive rates governed by an information-theoretic criterion. Conceptually, it repurposes the information bottleneck and mutual information literature (Shwartz-Ziv & Tishby, 2017; Achille & Soatto, 2018) from a training-time regularization tool into an inference-time control mechanism, treating each layer as a noisy communication channel whose information loss can be bounded. It also departs from prior adaptive dropout methods (Ba & Frey, 2013) and post-hoc dropout injection approaches (dropout at test time, infer-dropout), which either require labeled training data or still apply uniform rates, by operating entirely unsupervised at inference with no architectural modification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

For risk-sensitive domains like medical imaging, the ability to sharpen uncertainty estimates without retraining or accessing labels makes Rate-In practically deployable, and the anatomically grounded uncertainty maps in segmentation could directly inform clinical triage. However, the computational overhead of iterative mutual information estimation (up to 90 seconds per 3D volume) and the sensitivity to the information-loss threshold ε limit immediate adoption in latency-critical settings. Open questions include whether the information-theoretic framing yields transferable insights beyond the tested architectures, how MI estimation accuracy degrades in very high-dimensional feature spaces, and whether the gains persist against stronger adaptive baselines (e.g., learned per-layer priors or ensemble-based methods).
