## Problem

Vision-language models struggle with compositional understanding because training data lacks subtle variations between paired samples, allowing models to exploit shortcut features rather than learning nuanced distinctions. While synthetic data generation offers a scalable solution, current image generation methods cannot simultaneously satisfy three critical criteria: efficiency for large-scale production, precise text alignment at the location of subtle changes, and image fidelity to the original real image in all other regions.

## Input / Output

The method takes real image-caption pairs from COCO-2014 (82,783 images × 5 captions) as seed data. An LLM generates one synthetic positive and one synthetic negative caption per real caption, and a fast T2I model (Latent Consistency Model) generates corresponding images conditioned on both the modified caption and the real image's features. Each real pair is thus expanded into a group of three image-caption pairs (real, synthetic positive, synthetic negative), yielding 820K synthetic images and captions total. The output is a fine-tuned CLIP model (ViT-B/32 or ViT-L/14 with LoRA adapters) evaluated on caption classification tasks across four compositional benchmarks.

## Method

SPARCL has two core technical contributions. First, for image generation, it injects real image features into the padding embeddings (positions after the [EOS] token) of a fast T2I model's text prompt, exploiting the natural decoupling of content (semantic embeddings) and style (padding embeddings) to improve image fidelity without disrupting text alignment; an AdaIN style transfer step further reduces the domain gap. Second, for training, it introduces an adaptive margin loss that partitions captions into positive, hard negative, easy negative, and real negative sets relative to each image, and computes a margin that is a piecewise function of the similarity gap d: when d < β (indicating likely incorrect generations), the margin equals d and the loss is zeroed; for β ≤ d ≤ m₀, the margin is inversely scaled so harder samples receive larger margins; and for d > m₀, the margin is capped at m₀. This is combined with a sigmoid contrastive loss over all 3n×3n image-caption pairs in a batch, with a weight α > 1 applied to comparisons involving real negatives to prioritize reliable supervision.

## Results

SPARCL (ViT-B/32, trained on COCO-2014) achieves 77.2% on ARO, 79.2% on VL-CheckList, 87.1% on SugarCrepe, and 66.1% on SugarCrepe++, yielding an average of 77.4% across the four compositional benchmarks. This represents a +8.1% average improvement over zero-shot CLIP (69.4% average) and a +4.1% improvement over CLIP fine-tuned on COCO alone (73.3% average). Among COCO-trained baselines, SPARCL leads on VL-CheckList (+2.9% over CE-CLIP) and SugarCrepe (+1.9% over CE-CLIP), while trailing CE-CLIP on ARO (77.2% vs. 79.7%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among COCO-trained baselines, SPARCL outperforms CE-CLIP [109] by 2.9% on VL-CheckList (79.2 vs. 76.3) and 1.9% on SugarCrepe (87.1 vs. 85.2), and beats NegCLIP [106] by 4.6% on VL-CheckList and 4.6% on SugarCrepe. SPARCL also surpasses MosaiCLIP [85] on VL-CheckList (79.2 vs. 76.8) and FSC-CLIP [68] on SugarCrepe (87.1 vs. 85.1), though it trails CE-CLIP on ARO (77.2 vs. 79.7) and MosaiCLIP on ARO (77.2 vs. 80.3), which the authors attribute to nonsensical artifacts in ARO test samples favoring rule-based methods. Against the strongest cross-dataset baseline CE-CLIP+ [109] (which additionally uses CC3M), SPARCL is within 0.1% on VL-CheckList (79.2 vs. 79.3) and 0.4% on SugarCrepe (87.1 vs. 87.5) while using only COCO. On SugarCrepe++, SPARCL achieves the best reported result (66.1%), exceeding NegCLIP [106] by 1.2% and the CLIP fine-tune baseline by 3.8%. Overall, SPARCL wins or ties on three of four benchmarks among same-data-source baselines, with the ARO gap being the sole loss.

## Relation to Prior Work

SPARCL extends the line of work on improving VLM compositionality through synthetic negative/positive samples (NegCLIP, CE-CLIP, COMO, CounterCurate) by addressing two gaps those methods leave open: the difficulty of generating images that simultaneously satisfy efficiency, text alignment, and fidelity to real images, and the uniform treatment of synthetic samples with varying alignment quality in contrastive losses. The image feature injection technique is specifically designed around the Latent Consistency Model's separation of semantic and padding embeddings, repurposing the padding slots to carry real image features rather than style information. The adaptive margin loss departs from prior contrastive and margin-based losses (sigmoid loss, intra-modal contrastive loss in CE-CLIP) by explicitly stratifying negatives into hard, easy, and real categories and applying a data-dependent margin that filters out potentially mislabeled synthetic pairs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical pipeline for generating large-scale compositional training data from a single real dataset (COCO-2014) without manual annotation, which is a significant cost reduction for practitioners. The finding that synthetic captions contribute far more to compositionality than synthetic images alone (a ~4.7% vs. ~0.5% average gain) is a useful diagnostic that cautions against over-investing in image generation quality at the expense of caption diversity. An open question is whether the approach generalizes beyond CLIP-style dual-encoder architectures and whether the ARO underperformance (relative to rule-based baselines) reflects a genuine limitation or an artifact of that benchmark's known quality issues. The adaptive margin loss could serve as a general-purpose tool for any contrastive learning setup that incorporates noisy synthetic data.
