## Problem

Score distillation-based text-guided image editing methods (e.g., DDS, CDS) frequently introduce unintended modifications—losing high-frequency details like latte art patterns or shifting colors—because they indiscriminately optimize across all frequency subbands. The paper identifies that only specific frequency bands need adjustment for a given edit, yet no existing method provides frequency-domain controllability within the score distillation framework.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252482/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For 2D editing, the method takes a 512×512 RGB source image and a source-to-target text prompt pair; the image is encoded to a 4×64×64 latent via a VAE before optimization. For 3D texture editing, it takes a mesh with an original texture map and a text prompt, rendering the mesh to images for score distillation. The method is purely optimization-based (500 iterations on an RTX 3090) and requires no training or fine-tuning of the underlying Stable Diffusion model.

## Method

The core mechanism decomposes the latent representation into wavelet subbands (one low-frequency component and J levels of high-frequency directional details) using a Discrete Wavelet Transform with Daubechies wavelets. During each optimization step, the subbands are reconstructed via IDWT, passed through the diffusion model to compute the DDS (2D) or SDS (3D) gradient, and then a StopGrad operator selectively freezes either the low-frequency or high-frequency subbands, restricting updates to only the band relevant to the edit (e.g., freezing low-frequency to preserve color, or freezing high-frequency to preserve detail). For 3D texture editing, each plane of a triplane representation is independently wavelet-decomposed, so the same selective gradient control applies to the frequency components of the 3D texture field. The paper also provides an empirical comparison of wavelet parameters (decomposition level J, Daubechies order DB) and contrasts wavelets against DFT and DCT, showing that wavelets' spatial localization and multiresolution properties make them superior for this task.

## Results

On the LAION-COCO 2D image editing benchmark (200 cat images edited to dogs, pigs, cows), FDS achieves a CLIP score of 23.65, matching CDS (23.65) and slightly trailing NMG (23.68), while attaining the best LPIPS (0.129) and SSIM (0.819) among all methods. In the user study with 24 participants, over 80% of users prefer FDS edits over both DDS (82.1% average) and CDS (86.3% average), with particularly strong preference for detail preservation over DDS (90.5%) and color preservation over CDS (92.9%). For 3D texture editing, FDS qualitatively outperforms SDS in both detail and color fidelity, though no quantitative 3D metrics are reported.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the closest score distillation baselines, FDS matches CDS on CLIP (23.65 vs 23.65) while improving LPIPS from 0.136 to 0.129 and SSIM from 0.818 to 0.819; the user study confirms this with 92.9% preference for FDS over CDS in color preservation and 79.8% in detail preservation. Against DDS, FDS shows a large gap in structural fidelity (SSIM 0.819 vs 0.736) and perceptual quality (LPIPS 0.129 vs 0.297), with 90.5% of users preferring FDS for detail preservation. Among diffusion sampling methods, NMG achieves the highest CLIP (23.68) but FDS still leads on LPIPS (0.129 vs 0.182) and SSIM (0.819 vs 0.738); methods like DreamSampler and DiffuseIT frequently fail to produce meaningful edits or preserve color. For 3D texture editing, FDS is compared only against SDS (since DDS and CDS perform poorly in 3D), and qualitatively FDS preserves texture details and color fidelity where SDS distorts patterns and shifts hues. The paper also compares frequency decomposition choices (wavelets vs DFT vs DCT) in an ablation, finding wavelets superior due to spatial localization, but this is not a method-level baseline comparison.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the score distillation lineage for text-guided editing: SDS (DreamFusion) → DDS (which cancels noisy gradients) → CDS (which adds spatial contrastive constraints). The key departure is introducing Discrete Wavelet Transform decomposition of the latent space, enabling a stop-gradient operation that selectively freezes either low- or high-frequency subbands during optimization. This repurposes a classical signal-processing tool (Daubechies wavelets) within a modern diffusion-based editing pipeline, and extends the same frequency-selective principle to 3D texture editing via a wavelet-decomposed triplane representation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete, actionable cause for two common failure modes in score distillation editing (detail loss and color drift) and shows that a simple frequency-band freeze resolves them without retraining any model. This provides practitioners with a new, interpretable axis of controllability for text-guided edits. However, the method inherits the slow iterative optimization of score distillation (500 steps), remains tied to the SDS/DDS/CDS family, and the frequency-band selection is binary (freeze all high or all low) rather than adaptive per-region or per-attribute, leaving open how to handle edits that require simultaneous changes across multiple frequency bands.
