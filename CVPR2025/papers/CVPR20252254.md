## Problem

Current humanoid research is fragmented: locomotion methods focus on motor skills without long-term goals or situational awareness, while navigation methods overlook real-world embodiment and local terrain variability. No unified framework exists that integrates low-level motor execution with high-level goal-directed navigation for complex, unpredictable terrains like hiking trails, where robots must simultaneously balance, adapt gait, and make perceptual decisions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252254/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system consumes 16-frame depth image sequences (downsampled to 4 frames) from a front-mounted RealSense D435i operating at 10±2 Hz, 40-dimensional proprioception (joint positions/velocities, torso roll/pitch, foot contact, previous action), and GPS-derived distances to the trail endpoint and one midpoint waypoint. It outputs 10-dimensional joint position commands (converted to torque via a fixed-gain PD controller) at 50 Hz on a Jetson NX, along with N anticipated future navigation goals and a latent trail representation. The upper body is frozen, restricting the task to lower-body locomotion.

## Method

LEGO-H unifies navigation and locomotion in a two-level HRL pipeline. The navigation module (TC-ViTs) combines a ViViT-style temporal transformer encoder with goal conditioning (endpoint tiled as an image channel), a parallel shallow CNN on the current depth frame for near-field spatial precision, and a GRU-based recurrent goal adaptation that fuses visual, goal, and proprioceptive signals to anticipate N future local goals. To handle the 10 Hz vision vs. 50 Hz control mismatch, only the nearest goal g₁ is forwarded to the locomotion module while the latent state is tiled five times for continuous broadcasting. Motor skills are first learned by an oracle policy with privileged information (terrain type, friction, scandots), then distilled into the vision-based student via a Hierarchical Loss Metric Set: a masked VAE learns inter-joint structural dependencies from teacher actions, and its encoder measures cosine-similarity and triplet-distance alignment between teacher and student actions, enforcing physically rational joint coordination rather than per-joint error matching.

## Results

On the humanoid hiking benchmark (Unitree H1, 5 trail types × 5 difficulty levels, 512 robots, 30 s episodes), LEGO-H achieves 68.40% Success Rate, 52.78% Trail Completion, 7.84% Mean Edge Violation (lower is safer), 7.46 s Time-to-Fall, and 4.95 s Time-to-Reach. These results are competitive with or superior to the privileged-information oracle (71.20% SR, 7.12% MEV, 4.59 s T2R) on safety and efficiency metrics, while the oracle retains an advantage on goal completeness due to its access to ground-truth terrain and expert navigation goals.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LEGO-H is compared against three adapted quadruped pipelines: EP-H (per-frame depth processing without temporal goal alignment), RMA-H (vision-based rapid motor adaptation with a frozen oracle policy), and RMA-B (blind locomotion with no visual input). LEGO-H outperforms EP-H decisively on every metric, with Success Rate more than doubling (68.40% vs. 28.80%) and Time-to-Reach nearly halved (4.95 s vs. 9.79 s), confirming that goal-aligned, multi-scale temporal visual perception is critical. Against RMA-H, LEGO-H wins on Success Rate (68.40% vs. 65.17%), Trail Completion (52.78% vs. 52.51%), safety (MEV 7.84% vs. 8.70%), stability (TTF 7.46 s vs. 6.97 s), and efficiency (T2R 4.95 s vs. 4.98 s), while RMA-H edges out LEGO-H only on Traverse Rate (74.61% vs. 71.96%). The blind RMA-B baseline trails on all metrics, underscoring that visual perception is essential for integrated navigation and locomotion on complex trails.

## Relation to Prior Work

LEGO-H builds on the ViViT temporal vision transformer encoder, repurposing it within a hierarchical RL framework to reframe local navigation as a sequential anticipation problem rather than static goal-following. It departs from conventional privileged learning (which supervises per-joint errors or overall data distributions) by introducing a VAE-based hierarchical metric loss that learns inter-joint structural dependencies through masked reconstruction, grounding the distillation in the robot's own physical mechanism rather than human motion priors. The work also extends the HRL paradigm for legged robots (previously explored in quadruped and wheeled-legged settings) to humanoids, addressing the additional challenges of higher DoF, greater body height, and the need for diverse lower-body skills (walking, jumping, leaping) within a single policy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By formalizing hiking as a unified task requiring simultaneous visual perception, adaptive decision-making, and diverse motor execution, this paper provides a concrete benchmark and framework for integrative humanoid skill development that was previously fragmented across locomotion and navigation subfields. Key limitations include simulation-only evaluation (Isaac Gym), frozen upper-body pose, and a single intermediate waypoint, leaving open questions about sim-to-real transfer, whole-body coordination, and scalability to longer, more complex trails. The finding that the student policy matches or slightly exceeds the oracle in efficiency and safety—despite lacking privileged terrain information—suggests the hierarchical loss metric set effectively encodes structural priors, though this remains to be validated on physical hardware.
