## Problem

Autonomous micromobility requires agents to navigate complex urban public spaces with varied terrain, static and dynamic obstacles, and dense pedestrian traffic, yet existing AI solutions target isolated skills (obstacle avoidance, parkour) rather than the full spectrum of capabilities needed. Current robot learning platforms face a fundamental contradiction: those with rich, diverse urban scenes (e.g., CARLA) lack high-performance GPU parallelization, while high-throughput platforms (e.g., IsaacGym) offer only simple, uniform environments without contextual urban context. This paper bridges the gap by providing both a scalable simulation platform and a comprehensive benchmark suite for training and evaluating micromobility agents.

## Input / Output

The agent receives RGBD sensor observations (color and depth images) combined with robot proprioceptive state as input, and outputs continuous locomotion and navigation actions at the control frequency. The urban traverse task extends the horizon to tens of thousands of actions over kilometer-scale distances, requiring a layered control architecture where high-level decisions (route planning, mode switching) are separated from mid-level navigation and low-level locomotion policies. The platform supports 256 parallel unique environments per GPU at 1,800–2,600 fps with RGBD sensing, and the benchmark evaluates four heterogeneous embodiments (wheeled, quadruped, wheeled-legged, humanoid) across eight distinct tasks.

## Method

URBAN-SIM introduces three key designs to balance scene diversity with training throughput. First, a Hierarchical Urban Generation pipeline procedurally constructs infinite static scenes through four progressive stages—street block connection, ground functional zoning, Wave Function Collapse terrain generation (flat, stair, slope, rough), and adaptive object placement from a 15,000+ asset repository. Second, an Interactive Dynamics Generation strategy upgrades ORCA collision avoidance with JAX to run entirely on GPUs, enabling real-time responsive pedestrian and cyclist dynamics without CPU-GPU transfer bottlenecks. Third, an Asynchronous Scene Sampling scheme loads all assets into a GPU cache and randomly samples them to construct thousands of unique, non-uniform environments in parallel, achieving 26.3% relative improvement over synchronous sampling. Training uses PPO reinforcement learning on these procedurally generated scenes, and the urban traverse task introduces a human-AI shared autonomy architecture with four control modes spanning full human to full AI operation.

## Results

Across the Urban Navigation benchmark, the wheeled robot achieves the highest success rate in NavClear (97.60%) and route completion (98.61%), while the quadruped robot leads in safety with the lowest collision rates (0.08 in NavStatic, 0.13 in NavDynamic). In Urban Locomotion, the wheeled-legged robot dominates distance traversal (e.g., 18.24 m on rough terrain vs. 4.88 m for the quadruped), while the quadruped achieves the best smoothness scores across all terrains. The scalability evaluation shows that increasing training scenes from 1 to 1,024 raises NavStatic success rate from 5.1% to 83.2%, demonstrating the critical importance of large-scale diverse scene training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external algorithmic baselines; instead, it benchmarks four heterogeneous robot embodiments (wheeled, quadruped, wheeled-legged, humanoid) trained with PPO in URBAN-SIM. No single robot dominates all tasks: the wheeled robot excels in open-pathway navigation (97.60% success in NavClear) but drops sharply with obstacles (48.82% in NavDynamic); the quadruped achieves the safest navigation (lowest collision rates of 0.08 and 0.13) and best smoothness in locomotion; the wheeled-legged robot covers the most distance on challenging terrains (18.24 m on rough ground vs. 4.88 m for the quadruped) but has the lowest success rates in obstacle-rich navigation (31.06% in NavDynamic); and the humanoid robot performs best in complex obstacle scenarios (77.86% and 79.23% success in NavStatic/NavDynamic) while showing the highest smoothness values (indicating less stable motion). The urban traverse results further show that full-AI control minimizes human intervention but sacrifices completeness and safety, while full human control achieves the opposite, with the two human-AI shared modes offering intermediate trade-offs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of robot learning simulation platforms and procedural scene generation, explicitly addressing the tension between environment diversity and training throughput that plagues existing systems. It builds on the JAX-based GPU parallelization of ORCA from Waymax and JaxMARL for interactive dynamics, and on Wave Function Collapse (from game procedural generation) for terrain synthesis, but departs from IsaacGym/IsaacLab's synchronous, uniform-environment paradigm by introducing asynchronous scene sampling that trains on thousands of unique scenes simultaneously. Unlike driving simulators (CARLA, MetaDrive) that model vehicle-centric roadways, or indoor platforms (Habitat, iGibson) that model structured interiors, URBAN-SIM targets the underexplored domain of urban public spaces—sidewalks, plazas, and alleys—where micromobility devices actually operate.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

URBAN-BENCH establishes the first comprehensive, multi-robot benchmark for autonomous micromobility, and the finding that all four heterogeneous robots fail on most tasks (e.g., wheeled robot drops from 97.6% to 48.8% success when obstacles are introduced) clearly delineates a substantial research gap. The kilometer-scale Urban Traverse task and the human-AI shared autonomy framework open a practically important direction for deploying micromobility in real cities, though the paper acknowledges that sim-to-real transfer remains unvalidated. Key open questions include whether the PPO-trained policies generalize to real-world sensor noise and whether the asynchronous sampling advantage persists at even larger scene scales or with more complex multi-agent interactions.
