## Problem

Moving an object to a new location within a single image requires simultaneously preserving object identity, adjusting perspective and pose, harmonizing lighting and shadows, and filling occluded regions — a combination that existing two-step (remove-then-insert) or copy-paste methods fail to achieve coherently. No prior single-stage method can handle all these requirements together, particularly context-driven changes like perspective shifts and shadow relocation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250449/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes a 4-frame latent sequence: the input scene image, a segmented foreground object image, an instruction map with source and target bounding boxes stacked as three channels [M_src, M_tar, M_tar], and a noisy target frame. All frames are VAE-encoded into latent tokens before being processed by the transformer. The output is a single edited image at the target resolution (trained at 512×512, supporting up to 1024×1024 and 1080×1920) where the object appears at the new location with all scene effects updated.

## Method

ObjectMover repurposes a 5B-parameter pre-trained image-to-video diffusion transformer by reformulating object movement as a sequence-to-sequence prediction problem, leveraging the video model's learned priors on consistent object identity and dynamic effect evolution across frames. Training uses a flow-matching loss applied only to the fourth (target) frame of the denoising sequence, with the first three condition frames reset at each denoising step. To address the absence of large-scale paired movement data, the authors build a synthetic dataset via Unreal Engine (18,783 sequences yielding 1.3M pairs at up to 1080×1920) covering movement, removal, and insertion tasks, and supplement it with real-world video data through a multi-task learning strategy that trains an auxiliary mask-based insertion task, bridging the domain gap between synthetic and natural imagery.

## Results

On the primary object movement task (Table 1), ObjectMover achieves PSNR 25.27, DINO-Score 85.07, CLIP-Score 93.16, DreamSim 0.142, and 64.17% user-study preference, outperforming all baselines on every metric. The closest competitor, MagicFixup, scores 23.82 PSNR, 78.49 DINO, 91.06 CLIP, 0.198 DreamSim, and 31.14% user preference. On auxiliary tasks (Table 2), ObjectMover also leads: object removal (PSNR 28.90, DINO 83.94) and object insertion (PSNR 24.99, DINO 85.69), surpassing dedicated inpainting and compositing methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ObjectMover wins decisively against all eight baselines on every reported metric. Against the closest competitor MagicFixup [1] (a video-based harmonization method), ObjectMover gains +1.45 PSNR, +6.58 DINO, +2.10 CLIP, −0.056 DreamSim, and more than doubles the user-study preference (64.17% vs. 31.14%). The two-step removal-then-insertion pipelines (AnyDoor [6] + PowerPaint, Paint-by-Example [42] + PowerPaint) lag further behind, with AnyDoor scoring only 3.56% user preference and Paint-by-Example 0.75%, reflecting identity drift and inconsistent lighting. The 3D-aware method 3DiT [20] and the motion-controlled video generator Drag-Anything [41] perform worst overall (PSNR 19.72 and 16.36 respectively), confirming that neither 3D pose control nor video motion trajectories adequately handle lighting harmonization and background preservation. On the auxiliary removal task, ObjectMover (PSNR 28.90, DINO 83.94) outperforms dedicated inpainting methods LaMa [35] (27.04, 63.82), PowerPaint [45] (25.15, 62.71), and SD-Inpaint [28] (24.16, 50.80), demonstrating that the unified sequence-to-sequence framework generalizes well beyond the primary movement task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant two-step paradigm for object movement (remove-then-insert via inpainting models like PowerPaint or AnyDoor) by reframing the task as a sequence-to-sequence prediction problem solved by a pre-trained image-to-video diffusion transformer. Conceptually, it extends the observation from MagicFixup [1] that watching dynamic videos aids photo editing, but pushes further by using the full video diffusion model as the generative backbone rather than merely conditioning on video. The synthetic data pipeline using Unreal Engine extends prior game-engine-based approaches (e.g., 3DiT [20]) with higher-resolution, more realistic scenes and automated trajectory generation, while the multi-task learning strategy unifies movement, removal, and insertion in a single framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that temporal consistency priors learned in video generation transfer effectively to spatial consistency requirements in single-frame editing (shadows, reflections, lighting harmonization), offering a principled alternative to ad-hoc inpainting pipelines. However, the approach requires a 5B-parameter model and a substantial synthetic data pipeline, raising questions about scalability and whether the video prior is truly necessary or simply a proxy for model capacity. Open questions include generalization to multi-object simultaneous movement, handling of severe perspective changes, and whether the domain gap from Unreal Engine data can be further reduced. The introduced ObjMove-A/B benchmarks provide a useful evaluation standard for the field.
