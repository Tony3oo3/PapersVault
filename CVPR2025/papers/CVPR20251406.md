## Problem

Existing OOD detection benchmarks such as OpenOOD are built around natural images, leaving medical imaging without a comprehensive, standardized evaluation framework. The paper demonstrates that method rankings from natural-image benchmarks do not transfer to medical data, and that prior medical OOD evaluations are limited in scope, lacking unified taxonomy or multi-domain coverage.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251406/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Inputs are 50×50 px histopathology crops (MIDOG), single-instrument endoscopy frames (PhaKIR), and 128 mm³ isotropic skull-stripped MRI/CT volumes (OASIS-3), each paired with a pre-trained classifier. The 24 post-hoc OOD methods consume the classifier's intermediate features, final-layer activations, or logits to produce a scalar OOD score per sample, which is then thresholded to yield an ID/OOD decision. Evaluation reports AUROC, FPR@95, harmonic-mean AUPR, and F1-Score across cs-ID, near-OOD, and far-OOD splits.

## Method

OpenMIBOOD constructs three Medical Imaging Benchmarks (MIDOG for histopathology, PhaKIR for endoscopy, OASIS-3 for MRI) comprising 14 datasets organized into covariate-shifted in-distribution, near-OOD, and far-OOD categories, with a taxonomy modification that classifies substantial contextual shifts as near-OOD. Twenty-four post-hoc OOD detection methods—spanning classification-based (MSP, ODIN, EBO), feature-based (MDS, ViM, Residual, KNN), and hybrid approaches—are evaluated under a unified protocol with hyperparameter tuning on aggregated near-OOD validation splits. Classifiers are initialized from ImageNet1k (2D) or Kinetics400 (3D) pre-trained weights and trained with cross-entropy loss. The key empirical finding is that feature-space methods consistently outperform logit/probability-based methods on medical images, and that top-ranked methods from ImageNet1k benchmarks yield suboptimal performance on medical data, likely due to lower pixel-intensity variance and fewer target classes in medical imaging.

## Results

Across the three medical imaging benchmarks (MIDOG, PhaKIR, OASIS-3), MDSEns achieves the best average near-OOD AUROC of 96.14, AUPR of 91.86, and FPR@95 of 11.97%, substantially outperforming the second-best method ViM (AUROC 80.74, AUPR 70.21, FPR@95 48.79%). Feature-based methods (MDSEns, MDS, Residual, ViM) consistently outperform classification-based methods, whose average near-OOD AUROC drops to as low as 33.71 on the EndoSeg18 dataset. The worst-performing method, DICE, achieves only 44.54 average near-OOD AUROC with 92.83% FPR@95, indicating near-random OOD detection in the most challenging setting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MDSEns [40] dominates all other methods across every benchmark, achieving 96.14 average near-OOD AUROC versus 80.74 for ViM [70] and 79.82 for Residual [70], with a particularly large margin on OASIS-3 (99.46 vs. 98.40 near-OOD AUROC). ViM [70] is the most transferable method, ranking second on medical benchmarks (80.74 AUROC) while also achieving 72.08 on ImageNet1k, whereas most top medical-image methods rank poorly on natural images. Feature-based methods (MDSEns, MDS, Residual, ViM) consistently outperform classification-based methods (MSP [23], ODIN [42], TempScale [19], Dropout [17]), whose average near-OOD AUROC is only ~53–54, with catastrophic failures on specific datasets (e.g., 33.71 AUROC on EndoSeg18). The paper demonstrates that ranking methods by ImageNet1k performance (Fig. 3) does not predict medical imaging performance: methods like KNN [61] and SHE [76] rank mid-table on ImageNet but perform poorly on PhaKIR (34.05 and 36.18 cs-ID AUROC respectively), while ViM maintains consistent top-3 placement across both domains. DICE [59] and EBO [45] perform worst overall (44.54 and 48.81 AUROC), with DICE dropping to 23.70 AUROC on OASIS-3 far-OOD, indicating near-random detection for structurally different anatomical regions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the OpenOOD benchmark framework (Yang et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023) from natural images to medical imaging, introducing three benchmarks (MIDOG, PhaKIR, OASIS-3) spanning histopathology, endoscopy, and MRI. It deliberately departs from OpenOOD v1.5's decision to merge in-distribution and covariate-shifted in-distribution data, arguing that distinguishing cs-ID from ID is critical in medical settings where subtle distribution shifts (e.g., different scanners, smoke levels) carry clinical risk. Prior medical OOD evaluations (MOOD 2020, Cao et al., Vasiliuk et al.) were limited in scope—covering few methods, single domains, or omitting the near-OOD category—whereas this work provides a unified, multi-domain evaluation of 24 post-hoc methods under a consistent taxonomy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central practical finding is that method rankings for OOD detection do not transfer from natural to medical images: feature-space methods (e.g., MDSEns, ViM, Residual) consistently outperform classification-based methods (e.g., MSP, ODIN, EBO) on medical data, reversing the pattern observed on ImageNet. This is attributed to lower pixel-intensity variance and fewer target classes in medical imaging, which produce more cohesive feature spaces. A key limitation is the restriction to classification tasks; extending the framework to segmentation—a dominant task in medical imaging—remains open. The benchmark will likely become the standard reference for medical OOD detection, but the precise mechanistic reasons for the domain-dependent method ranking reversal warrant further investigation.
