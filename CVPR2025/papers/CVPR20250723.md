## Problem

3D Gaussian Splatting and NeRF-based novel view synthesis methods assume uniform, well-lit training images, but real-world multi-view captures suffer from low-light, overexposure, and per-view exposure inconsistencies that cause photometric mismatches and rendering artifacts. Existing NeRF-based solutions (e.g., Aleth-NeRF, NeRF-W) do not transfer to 3DGS's explicit rasterization pipeline, and 2D image-enhancement pre-processing breaks 3D multi-view consistency, leaving a gap for efficient, lighting-robust 3DGS training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250723/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes multi-view sRGB images captured under a single challenging lighting condition (low-light, overexposure, or per-view varying exposure) along with SfM camera poses as input. During training it jointly renders both the original input images and pseudo-enhanced normal-light targets from two parallel sets of Gaussian color attributes; at inference it renders only the enhanced set, producing a novel-view image with corrected illumination and a 3D Gaussian scene representation at ~150 FPS. The curve-adjustment and color-matrix modules are active exclusively during training, so test-time cost matches vanilla 3DGS.

## Method

Luminance-GS augments standard 3DGS with two per-view color-correction stages applied to input images before they serve as training targets: (1) a learnable 3×3 invertible matrix M_k projects each view's RGB into a view-dependent coordinate system, and (2) a view-adaptive tone curve L_k = L^g + L_k^b maps pixel values to a consistent normal-light range, where L^g is a shared 256-dim global curve and L_k^b is a per-view bias predicted by a cross-attention block that queries camera-pose features against downsampled image features. Each Gaussian carries two color attributes (c_i for the raw view and c_i^out for the enhanced view), both optimized jointly with geometry via a combined regression loss on C^in and C^out. Curve stability is enforced by shape-regularization losses that pull L_k toward a learnable power-curve × S-curve prior and a histogram-equalization CDF target, plus a total-variation smoothness term; a spatial-consistency loss (adapted from Zero-DCE with an adaptive normalization factor) preserves local structure between enhanced and input images. At inference the curve and matrix are discarded, and only c_i^out is splatted, yielding real-time rendering without any lighting-specific post-processing.

## Results

On the LOM low-light subset, Luminance-GS achieves 18.34 PSNR / 0.799 SSIM / 0.294 LPIPS, setting new best SSIM and LPIPS while ranking second in PSNR (behind Aleth-NeRF's 19.87). On the overexposure subset, it leads all metrics with 20.71 PSNR / 0.835 SSIM / 0.222 LPIPS. On the varying-exposure Mip-NeRF 360 scenes, it attains the best SSIM and LPIPS across all three scenes (e.g., 0.786 SSIM / 0.219 LPIPS on 'garden') while maintaining ~150 FPS rendering speed and ~14 min training time, matching original 3DGS efficiency.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Luminance-GS outperforms original 3DGS [29] by large margins across all three lighting conditions (e.g., +11.5 PSNR on low-light, +11.1 PSNR on overexposure), confirming that vanilla 3DGS cannot handle photometric inconsistencies. Among 2D enhancement + 3DGS combinations (Z-DCE, SCI, NeRCo, SGZ, LLVE, MSEC, IAT, MSLT), Luminance-GS consistently surpasses both pre-processing and post-processing variants, with the closest competitor being MSLT+GS (20.25 PSNR) on overexposure, where Luminance-GS still leads by +0.46 PSNR and +0.011 SSIM. Against NeRF-based methods, Luminance-GS beats Aleth-NeRF [15] on SSIM (+0.045) and LPIPS (−0.123) in low-light, and on all three metrics in overexposure (+2.62 PSNR, +0.090 SSIM, −0.266 LPIPS); it also dominates NeRF-W [40] and AME-NeRF [67] by wide margins. The most competitive baseline is GS-W [60] on varying exposure, where Luminance-GS wins on SSIM and LPIPS across all scenes (e.g., +0.021 SSIM on 'garden') while GS-W edges ahead on PSNR for 'bicycle' (19.00 vs 18.90); however, Luminance-GS matches 3DGS rendering speed (~150 FPS) whereas GS-W drops to ~80 FPS and requires ~45 min training vs. ~14 min.

## Relation to Prior Work

Luminance-GS extends 3DGS [Kerbl et al., 2023] by adding per-view color matrix mapping (adopted from WildGaussians [Kulhanek et al., 2024]) and a view-adaptive tone curve to handle photometric inconsistencies during training. It departs from the dominant NeRF-based line of work (Aleth-NeRF, NeRF-W, NeRF in the Dark) that learns appearance embeddings or concealing fields within implicit MLPs, and from 2D enhancement pipelines (Zero-DCE, SCI) that break 3D consistency. The key conceptual departure is repurposing a classical 1D tone curve—a tool from photographic post-processing—as a differentiable, jointly-optimized component of the 3DGS training loop, rather than introducing new 3D lighting parameters or modifying the Gaussian representation itself.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a lightweight, training-only curve adjustment can bring 3DGS to competitive or SOTA quality under low-light, overexposure, and varying-exposure conditions while preserving real-time rendering (~150 FPS) and avoiding the multi-hour training costs of NeRF-based alternatives. This is practically important for applications (AR/VR, autonomous driving) where both speed and robustness to uncontrolled capture conditions matter. However, the method remains per-scene and does not address scene generalization, dynamic lighting, or shadow handling; the authors explicitly flag scene generalization as future work. The modest PSNR gap to GS-W on varying exposure (e.g., 18.90 vs 19.00 on 'bicycle') suggests the curve approach may hit a ceiling when per-view color shifts are large and non-monotonic.
