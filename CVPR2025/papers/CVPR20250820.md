## Problem

Adversarial examples have been studied almost exclusively through metric and manifold-topology frameworks (ℓp norms, geodesic distances), yet perceptual metamers—inputs that humans cannot discriminate—are qualitatively different and resist such distance-based analysis. The paper asks whether some classification problems are fundamentally ill-defined from a perceptual standpoint (i.e., no AD-robust classifier can exist) and what structural properties characterize the rare classifiers that are robust.

## Input / Output

The framework operates on an abstract input space X equipped with a context-relative indiscriminability relation ≈ (generating a non-metric perceptual topology τ_δ) and a probability measure μ, together with an m-label classifier R. Given these, it determines whether the classification problem is well-defined, computes the region of conceptual ambiguity A(R) and the conceptual entropy H_R(x) at each input, and derives upper bounds on the fooling rate of any AD attack. Notably, the 'distance' between inputs is a graph-based perceptual metric d_w derived from the discrimination graph, not a manifold metric, and it does not generate the perceptual topology itself.

## Method

The paper replaces the standard metric topology on input spaces with a context-relative perceptual topology τ_δ generated by the indiscriminability relation, and defines adversarial Doppelgängers as pairs of inputs in the same phenomenal neighborhood d(x) that receive different labels. A classifier is 'perceptually regular' (AD-robust) if and only if its labeling function is continuous with respect to τ_δ; the paper proves that when the transitive closure of ≈ is trivial (all inputs are metamorphic), no fully populated classifier with ≥2 labels can be regular, rendering the problem fundamentally ill-defined. It introduces hypersensitive behavior—classifiers whose only mistakes are ADs—and proves that for classifiers with sufficiently high recall rates, improving AD robustness is equivalent to improving accuracy, eliminating the usual robustness-accuracy trade-off. Vulnerability is quantified via conceptual entropy H_R(x) and the region of conceptual ambiguity A(R), and AD fooling rates are shown to be bounded by the outer measure of A(R), in contrast to universal adversarial attacks that can achieve fooling rate 1.

## Results

This is a primarily theoretical paper with no empirical benchmark tables. The key quantitative-structural results are: (1) Observation 3 shows that any classifier with recall rates below 1/k̄(Ω) is 'totally unsafe'—every correctly classified input admits adversarial Doppelgängers; (2) Observation 4 shows that classifiers with recall rates above 1−1/k̄(Ω) are hypersensitive, meaning every misclassified input is an adversarial Doppelgänger, so improving AD-robustness is equivalent to improving accuracy; (3) Observation 2 establishes that a well-defined m-label classification problem admits exactly S(p,m) regular classifiers (Stirling numbers of the second kind) when the number of perceptual equivalence classes p ≥ m, and admits none when p < m. The paper also shows (Observation 5) that if a classifier is conceptually ambiguous at every input, an AD attack with fooling rate equal to one exists.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not conduct empirical experiments or compare against specific baseline methods on standard datasets. It is a theoretical/conceptual contribution that introduces the adversarial Doppelgänger (AD) framework, defines the perceptual topology τ_δ, and derives structural theorems about classifier vulnerability. The closest 'comparison' is conceptual: the paper argues that ADs are qualitatively different from the standard ℓ_p-norm-based adversarial examples studied in prior work (e.g., [25, 47, 52]), because ADs are perceptually indiscriminable to humans while typical adversarial perturbations (like the Fast Signed Gradient example in Figure 2) are readily discriminable. The paper also contrasts its non-metric perceptual topology with the manifold/geodesic-distance assumptions underlying most prior adversarial robustness literature, arguing that the latter framework is insufficient to capture the AD phenomenon.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs fundamentally from the standard adversarial example framework, which assumes input space is a metric space (typically ℝⁿ with ℓp norms or a manifold with geodesic distance). Instead, it builds on Williamson's context-relative definition of indiscriminability [84] and tolerance space theory [57, 66] to define a perceptual topology τδ that is generally non-metric and non-transitive. The visual metamer literature [3, 6, 9, 18, 22] and Weber-Fechner psychophysics [19, 20] provide the empirical grounding for this topology, while the standard adversarial robustness literature [25, 47, 52, 71] is positioned as addressing a different (and, the authors argue, less perceptually relevant) notion of closeness.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central claim—that some classification problems are fundamentally not well-defined because their classes are perceptually ambiguous, making AD-robust classifiers impossible regardless of training—challenges the field's assumption that robustness can always be improved through retraining. The introduction of hypersensitive behavior (where all errors are ADs) and the result that no accuracy-robustness trade-off exists for sufficiently high-accuracy classifiers reframes the robustness problem. However, the empirical evidence is limited to a handful of illustrative examples (MobileNetV2 on cat breeds), and the perceptual topology is defined abstractly without a practical algorithm for computing indiscriminability, leaving open how to operationalize the framework for real-world classifiers at scale.
