## Problem

Existing medical image segmentation methods, whether end-to-end or coarse-to-fine, treat all samples uniformly and perform poorly on hard samples such as heterogeneous tumors, vessel bifurcations, stenosis, and imaging artifacts that carry critical clinical significance. No prior framework explicitly learns to both identify which samples are inherently hard for a given dataset and then applies a specialized, stronger segmentation strategy to those samples.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250414/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single medical image (resized to 224×224 for COSMOS2022/ACDC/BTCV or 448×448 for ISIC/Kvasir-SEG/BUSI) and produces a binary or multi-class segmentation mask. Internally, the pipeline first routes each image through a backbone UNet that generates multiple candidate segmentations; a pre-trained classifier then labels each as 'qualified' (normal) or 'unqualified' (hard) using a dynamically optimized Dice threshold, and the two paths are processed by distinct sub-networks before a consistency loss enforces agreement on shared labels.

## Method

L2S comprises two coupled modules. The identification module pairs a UNet backbone (with random perturbations in the final decoder to yield multiple segmentations per image) with a pre-trained classifier whose threshold is dynamically tuned via Bayesian optimization over a Gaussian Process surrogate of validation performance. The segmentation module applies a conditional diffusion process (DDPM) to the unqualified segmentation labels, using a Mix-Former that fuses the noise embedding with the backbone's conditional image embedding through Fourier-domain cross-attention; the resulting augmented features feed a Mask2Former-style decoder with learnable object queries that attend to multi-scale pixel-decoder features and the diffusion-augmented features. A Post-Former (two-layer Transformer) refines normal-sample segmentations, and a consistency loss aligns outputs from both paths. The paper proves that the diffusion-based feature augmentation is mathematically equivalent to penalizing the trace of the Hessian (second-order regularization), driving the model toward flatter minima and improving generalization specifically on hard samples.

## Results

On the four binary segmentation benchmarks (COSMOS 2022, ISIC 2018, Kvasir-SEG, BUSI), L2S achieves the highest average Dice of 87.21% and IoU of 80.07%, outperforming the strongest baseline TransFuse (86.48% DSC) by 0.73%. The gains are most pronounced on hard samples (DSC†, Dice < 0.7): L2S improves hard-sample Dice by 8.97% on ISIC 2018 (63.90 vs. 54.93 for TransFuse) and by 10.88% on COSMOS 2022 (66.67 vs. 55.79 for nnU-Net). On multi-organ tasks, L2S reaches 88.2% average Dice on BTCV (vs. 86.1% for TransFuse) and 91.68% on ACDC (vs. 91.03% for nnU-Net).

## Baselines & Comparisons

L2S outperforms all nine baselines on every dataset and metric in Table 1. Against the closest competitor TransFuse, L2S gains +0.73% average DSC and +0.90% average IoU, with the largest hard-sample margin on ISIC 2018 (+8.97% DSC†: 63.90 vs. 54.93). Compared to the Mask2Former baseline (the architecture L2S builds upon), L2S improves average DSC by +1.36% and hard-sample DSC† by up to +15.35 points on COSMOS 2022 (66.67 vs. 41.32). CNN-based baselines (UNet, nnU-Net) trail by 1.1–4.3% in average DSC, while other transformer-based methods (TransUNet, SwinUNet, MedT) fall 0.8–1.8% behind. On multi-organ BTCV, L2S leads TransFuse by +2.1% average Dice (88.2 vs. 86.1), and on ACDC cardiac segmentation it surpasses nnU-Net by +0.63% (91.68 vs. 91.03). The consistent pattern is that L2S's advantage grows substantially on hard samples relative to easy ones, confirming the framework's targeted design.

## Relation to Prior Work

L2S builds directly on Mask2Former as its hard-sample segmentation backbone and on the broader hard-sample mining literature (layer cascades, loss-based reweighting, pixel hardness learning), but departs from these by treating hard-sample identification as a learned, dataset-adaptive classification problem rather than a fixed heuristic. It repurposes the diffusion process not as a generative segmentation tool (as in MedSegDiffV2) but as a feature-augmentation mechanism whose theoretical role is proven to be equivalent to Hessian-trace regularization, connecting it to the second-order optimization literature of Liu et al. The 'learning to learn' paradigm for imbalanced data (DINO, FASA, contrastive approaches) informs the design philosophy of adapting the learning strategy per sample difficulty, but L2S is the first to formalize a two-stage identify-then-segment pipeline with a dedicated, stronger network for hard samples in medical segmentation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

In clinical settings, hard samples (stenosis, rare pathologies, imaging artifacts) carry disproportionate diagnostic risk, yet prior methods improve them far less than easy samples; L2S's 8–11% Dice gains on hard samples across four modalities address this gap with a modular framework that can be grafted onto different backbones (UNet, Mask2Former). The theoretical equivalence between the diffusion augmentation and Hessian-trace penalty provides a principled explanation for why the approach works, moving beyond empirical tuning. Open questions include the sensitivity to classifier accuracy (Table 7 shows true-label classification yields further gains), the computational overhead of maintaining two separate networks, and whether the Bayesian-optimized threshold generalizes to 3D volumetric or multi-modal settings without re-tuning.
