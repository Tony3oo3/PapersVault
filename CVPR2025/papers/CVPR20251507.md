## Problem

Traditional MDS and manifold learning methods embed data into Riemannian spaces, whose metrics are inherently symmetric and thus cannot represent asymmetric dissimilarities that arise naturally in directed graphs, physical systems with currents, and other real-world data. This paper identifies that the Riemannian constraint on the embedding space is a fundamental blind spot, and proposes extending MDS to Finsler geometry, where distances are direction-dependent.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251507/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an asymmetric dissimilarity matrix D ∈ R^{N×N} (with D^T ≠ D), a directed graph adjacency matrix, or manifold data points equipped with a Finsler metric, and produces an embedding X ∈ R^{N×m} in a canonical Randers space. The embedding space is standard R^m but equipped with a Randers metric F_x(u) = ||u||_2 + ω^T u where ω is a constant drift vector with ||ω||_2 < 1, making the induced distance d_FC(x,y) = ||y−x||_2 + ω^T(y−x) asymmetric while keeping geodesics as straight Euclidean segments. For graph tasks, the output additionally supports link existence and direction prediction via a Fermi-Dirac decoder.

## Method

The core contribution is the Finsler MDS problem: minimizing the stress σ_F²(X) = Σ w_ij (d_FC(x_i,x_j) − D_ij)² over an embedding in a canonical Randers space, where the distance decomposes into a Euclidean term plus a linear drift projection. Two solvers are provided: (1) a Finsler SMACOF algorithm that extends the classical majorization iteration by modifying the linear system to account for the drift term, yielding the update vec(X^{(k+1)}) = K† vec(B(X^{(k)})X^{(k)} − C) with K = (I_m + ωω^T) ⊗ V; and (2) a Finsler representation learning approach using a Fermi-Dirac decoder p((i,j)∈E|X) = 1/(1+exp((d_FC²(x_i,x_j)−r)/t)) trained with weighted cross-entropy and negative sampling for large digraphs. A key theoretical result (Theorem 2) guarantees that for symmetric data the Finsler solution reduces to the traditional Euclidean MDS embedding in a hyperplane orthogonal to ω, ensuring backward compatibility.

## Results

For digraph embedding (Table 1), Finsler embeddings consistently achieve lower distortion and higher MAP than Euclidean embeddings across all six datasets and all dimensions (m ∈ {2, 5, 10, 50}); e.g., on Cora at m=2, distortion drops from 0.426 (Euclidean) to 0.127 (Finsler) while MAP rises from 0.574 to 0.894. For link prediction (Table 2), the Finsler method achieves the best or near-best ROC AUC on nearly all datasets: on existence prediction it leads on 4/6 datasets (e.g., 95.2 on Cora vs. 95.0 for DUPLEX, 89.3 on Chameleon vs. 87.2 for DUPLEX) and is competitive on Squirrel (82.3 vs. 82.6 for MagNet); on direction prediction it leads on 4/6 datasets (e.g., 92.6 on Gr-QC vs. 90.7 for DUPLEX, 89.0 on Squirrel vs. 85.0 for DUPLEX) and is on par with ODIN/DUPLEX on Cora (95.1 vs. 95.3).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against six directed-graph embedding methods (NERD, DiGCN, MagNet, DiGAE, ODIN, DUPLEX) on link existence and direction prediction. On existence prediction, the Finsler method outperforms all baselines on Cora (95.2 vs. 95.0 DUPLEX), Gr-QC (84.9 vs. 83.1 DUPLEX), Chameleon (89.3 vs. 87.2 DUPLEX), and Arxiv-Year (68.6 vs. 64.7 DUPLEX), but trails DUPLEX on Citeseer (94.6 vs. 97.2) and is marginally below MagNet on Squirrel (82.3 vs. 82.6). On direction prediction, it leads on Gr-QC (92.6 vs. 90.7 DUPLEX), Chameleon (93.2 vs. 92.0 DUPLEX), Squirrel (89.0 vs. 85.0 DUPLEX), and Arxiv-Year (74.4 vs. 69.6 DUPLEX), while being on par with ODIN and DUPLEX on Cora (95.1 vs. 95.3) and slightly below DUPLEX on Citeseer (97.2 vs. 97.9). For the embedding quality comparison (Table 1), Finsler embeddings dominate Euclidean embeddings by a large margin on all datasets and dimensions, e.g., reducing distortion from 0.426 to 0.127 on Cora (m=2) and from 0.592 to 0.226 on Squirrel (m=2). The paper also qualitatively compares against t-SNE, UMAP, Fruchterman-Reingold, and Isomap for directed graph visualisation (Fig. 5), where only Finsler MDS reveals both the hierarchy and its direction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classical MDS/SMACOF framework [32] by replacing the Riemannian (symmetric) embedding space with a Finsler manifold, specifically a canonical Randers space with a constant drift vector. It departs from prior asymmetric MDS attempts (ASYMSCAL, slide-vector, Gower, radius-distance models) that handled asymmetry through ad-hoc weighting or non-metric tricks, instead grounding asymmetry in a proper geometric metric. The Finsler geometric tools themselves come from a long tradition in differential geometry and physics (Zermelo navigation, Randers metrics), but their application to manifold learning and dimensionality reduction in the CV/ML community is, to the authors' knowledge, entirely new. The deep learning component borrows the Fermi-Dirac decoder from hyperbolic embedding work [54,66] and negative-sampling training from word2vec [41], repurposing them for the Randers distance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work opens a principled geometric pathway for embedding asymmetric data that has been a blind spot in manifold learning for decades, providing both a theoretical framework (convergence guarantees, flatness theorem) and practical tools (Finsler SMACOF, neural Finsler embedding). The canonical Randers space's closed-form geodesics and straight-line paths make it computationally attractive and visually intuitive, which is a significant practical advantage over general Finsler metrics. Key limitations include encoding all asymmetry in a single drift direction (limiting disentangled multi-source asymmetry), the absence of a closed-form solution, and the fact that distortion still grows when data departs from the chosen metric structure. Open questions include whether richer Finsler metrics (Matsumoto, Kropina) yield better embeddings for multi-faceted asymmetry, and whether the framework extends to other CV tasks beyond dimensionality reduction and graph embedding.
