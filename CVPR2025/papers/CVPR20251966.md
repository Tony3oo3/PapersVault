## Problem

Gradient-based XAI methods require white-box access to model weights, produce off-manifold perturbations that amount to adversarial attacks rather than human-interpretable explanations, and have no unified framework handling both feature attribution and counterfactual explanation. The paper asks whether a single derivative-free, black-box method can approximate the model gradient projected onto the data manifold, yielding explanations that are both faithful and perceptually meaningful.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251966/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a 256×256 input image, a black-box classifier (only forward-pass logits are queried, no weights or gradients), a pretrained unconditional diffusion model, and a target class label. For feature attribution it outputs a pixel-wise importance map (absolute value of the manifold-constrained gradient, averaged across color channels); for counterfactual explanation it outputs a modified image that the classifier assigns to the target class while remaining on the data manifold. The diffusion model is used solely to generate K on-manifold particles via forward diffusion followed by single-step Tweedie denoising, not for full reverse sampling in the attribution setting.

## Method

FreeMCG approximates the manifold-projected gradient C_xx ∇_x log p(c|x) by replacing the intractable product C_xx (∂f/∂x)ᵀ with the cross-covariance C_xf, yielding the derivative-free estimator (1/K)Σ_k (x⁽ᵏ⁾−x̄)(f(x⁽ᵏ⁾)−f̄)ᵀ(e_c−p). The K particles are generated by forward-diffusing the input to a random timestep t∈[0.1,0.7] and applying Tweedie's formula for one-step denoising, which Theorem 1 shows acts as a local orthogonal projection onto the data manifold, ensuring the resulting gradient stays on-manifold. For feature attribution, the gradient is used directly as the importance map; for counterfactual explanation, it serves as the ascent direction inside an SDEdit-style reverse diffusion loop (Algorithm 3), where each reverse step combines the FreeMCG gradient with a proximal pull toward the original image. The key theoretical contribution is Theorem 2, which proves the C_xf approximation holds with O(δ³) error under local linearity of the manifold, and Theorem 1, which shows the covariance preconditioning contracts off-manifold components while expanding along the tangent space.

## Results

On ImageNet counterfactual generation, FreeMCG achieves a flip rate of 51.5% (vs. 85.8% for DVCE and 61.7% for LDCE), but substantially outperforms both baselines on all human-perception metrics: perceived change (1.65 vs. 2.35 and 2.00), perceived similarity (1.76 vs. 2.34 and 1.90), and perceived realism (1.83 vs. 2.30 and 1.87), all on a lower-is-better scale. L2 distance to the input is comparable to DVCE (1001.02 vs. 995.92) and FID is competitive (66.89 vs. 71.38 for DVCE, 63.04 for LDCE). For feature attribution on both ImageNet and MIMIC-CXR, FreeMCG achieves the steepest prediction decline in ROAD evaluation among gradient-based methods, approaching the SHAP lower bound, while producing interpretable high-frequency attribution maps unlike the adversarial-like patterns of vanilla gradient, Integrated Gradients, or Input×Gradient.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DVCE [4] (white-box, diffusion-based counterfactuals), FreeMCG trades flip rate (51.5% vs. 85.8%) for dramatically better human-perceived change (1.65 vs. 2.35), similarity (1.76 vs. 2.34), and realism (1.83 vs. 2.30), indicating DVCE's higher flip rate comes from adversarial-like off-manifold perturbations rather than semantically meaningful edits. Against LDCE [16] (latent diffusion counterfactuals), FreeMCG again wins on all three human-study metrics (1.65/1.76/1.83 vs. 2.00/1.90/1.87) while achieving a lower L2 distance (1001 vs. 1351), though LDCE has a slightly better FID (63.04 vs. 66.89). For feature attribution, FreeMCG outperforms vanilla gradient [50], Integrated Gradients [49], and Input×Gradient [47] in ROAD evaluation, producing the steepest prediction decline among gradient-based methods and approaching the SHAP [31] lower bound, while generating spatially coherent, high-frequency attribution maps rather than the patchy or adversarial-like patterns seen in the baselines. Critically, FreeMCG is the only method in the comparison that operates in a fully black-box setting (requiring only forward-pass logits), whereas all gradient-based baselines and both diffusion counterfactual baselines require access to model weights or gradients.

## Relation to Prior Work

FreeMCG sits at the intersection of three research threads: gradient-based XAI (vanilla gradients, Integrated Gradients, Input×Gradient), diffusion-model-based counterfactual generation (DVCE, LDCE), and derivative-free optimization via Ensemble Kalman Filters. It departs from prior gradient-based attribution methods by eliminating the need for white-box access, and from prior diffusion-based counterfactual methods (DVCE, LDCE) by removing the dependency on model gradients entirely. The key conceptual move is repurposing the EnKF cross-covariance (C_xf) as a surrogate for the Jacobian-weighted gradient, combined with Tweedie denoising to keep particles on the data manifold, thereby unifying feature attribution and counterfactual explanation under a single black-box framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first framework that handles both feature attribution and counterfactual explanation with only black-box access to model logits, addressing a practical gap where many deployed models (APIs, proprietary systems) do not expose weights or gradients. The on-manifold property of the resulting gradients means explanations are perceptually meaningful rather than adversarial, which is critical for trust in high-stakes domains like medical imaging. A key limitation is that FreeMCG still requires access to logits (not just class labels), and the flip rate for counterfactuals is lower than white-box baselines (51.5% vs. 85.8%), suggesting a fundamental trade-off between black-box constraint and decision-boundary precision. Open questions include whether the approach extends to settings with only discrete class predictions, scales to larger diffusion models, and generalizes beyond image modalities.
