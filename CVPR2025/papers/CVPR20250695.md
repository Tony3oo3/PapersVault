## Problem

3D gaze estimation models are device-agnostic and generalize well across environments, but they output direction vectors rather than the screen pixel coordinates needed for human-computer interaction. Existing 2D gaze methods are entangled with device-specific factors and require large per-device training sets, while traditional approaches need specialized eye-tracking hardware and multi-point calibration. This paper introduces the unexplored task of cross-task few-shot 2D gaze estimation: adapting a pre-trained 3D gaze network to predict 2D screen coordinates on unseen devices using only a handful of images, without screen calibration or access to the original 3D training data.

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB face image and a 3D face center position (computed via an off-the-shelf 3D landmark estimator) as input, and produces 2D gaze pixel coordinates on the target screen as output. The 3D gaze network (GazeTR: ResNet-18 backbone + 6-layer transformer) outputs a unit 3D direction vector, which is then projected onto a learnable screen plane. The final 2D coordinates are converted to pixels using the screen's PPI. Training requires only N=10 annotated face-image/2D-gaze pairs, and the method is both source-free (no access to the 3D pre-training set) and screen-calibration-free (screen pose is learned, not given).

## Method

The core idea is to decompose 2D gaze estimation into 3D gaze estimation plus a physics-based differentiable projection, so the pre-trained 3D network is reused as a frozen-then-fine-tuned backbone without architectural modification. The projection module parameterizes the screen pose with six learnable weights (rotation vector r and translation vector t), converts r to a rotation matrix via the Rodrigues formula, computes the ray-plane intersection in camera coordinates, and transforms the result into screen coordinates. A key challenge is generating reliable pseudo-labels for horizontally flipped images: the method reverses the projection to map 2D labels into 3D gaze space, applies the flip there, and projects back, but because fine-tuning shifts the 3D network into an unknown coordinate system, a dynamic rotation T (solved via SVD each iteration using the initial and current network outputs as anchors) is learned to realign the space with the camera coordinate system. An additional color-jittering loss minimizes prediction variance across augmented views with a temporally weighted schedule to stabilize early training. The entire pipeline is end-to-end differentiable and trained with only 10 samples in roughly 1.2 minutes on a single GPU.

## Results

Using only 10 training images, the proposed method achieves Euclidean gaze error of 43.4 mm on EVE, 56.7 mm on MPIIGaze, and 35.7 mm on GazeCapture (lower is better). This outperforms the EFE baseline (64.9/100.2/48.5 mm) by over 25% on all three datasets and substantially surpasses IVGaze (177.7/132.2/68.1 mm). Notably, the method approaches the performance of full-dataset 2D gaze estimators (e.g., 43.4 mm vs. 38.5 mm for EFE on EVE) while requiring only 10 annotated images and no screen calibration.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the two directly comparable few-shot baselines, the proposed method wins decisively on all three datasets. Compared to EFE [1] (which also uses a projection module but requires screen calibration, here made learnable for fairness), the proposed method reduces error from 64.9→43.4 mm on EVE (−33%), 100.2→56.7 mm on MPIIGaze (−43%), and 48.5→35.7 mm on GazeCapture (−26%). Against IVGaze [14], which adds a transformer to refine projection points, the gap is even larger (e.g., 177.7→43.4 mm on EVE), as the transformer underfits with only 10 samples. Relative to full-dataset 2D methods, the proposed method with 10 images is competitive on EVE (43.4 vs. 38.5 mm for EFE) but lags on MPIIGaze (56.7 vs. 38.9 mm) and GazeCapture (35.7 vs. 20.5 mm), reflecting the inherent difficulty of few-shot adaptation. The paper also shows that direct projection without fine-tuning (80.5/101.9 mm) and direct 2D learning via MLP (180.6/133.9/74.2 mm) both fail, confirming that the differentiable projection framework is essential for successful cross-task transfer.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions 3D gaze estimation (e.g., GazeTR trained on Gaze360) as a device-agnostic prior analogous to the 3D anatomical eye models used in traditional calibration-based eye tracking, and repurposes it for 2D screen-gaze prediction. It departs from prior 3D-to-2D projection methods like EFE (which requires explicit screen calibration) and IVGaze (which adds a learned transformer refinement that underfits in the few-shot regime) by making the entire projection differentiable with only six learnable screen-pose parameters (rotation via Rodrigues formula + translation), and by introducing a dynamic pseudo-labeling strategy that reverses the projection to perform flipping in 3D space with an SVD-based coordinate alignment. The cross-task, few-shot, calibration-free formulation is explicitly framed as unexplored in Table 1, distinguishing it from both standard 3D gaze, standard 2D gaze, and existing domain-adaptation or personalization approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: adapting a 3D gaze model to a new device requires only 10 annotated images and ~1.2 minutes of training, with no screen calibration step, making gaze-based HCI accessible to non-expert users across laptops, desktops, and phones. A key limitation is that the purely mathematical projection can produce extreme 2D coordinates when the predicted 3D gaze is nearly parallel to the screen plane, a failure mode absent in neural-network-based outputs that are implicitly bounded. Open questions the authors raise include whether unlabeled face images can further improve adaptation, whether standard calibration patterns (as in traditional eye tracking) could be incorporated, and whether user-unaware calibration is feasible—acknowledging that the 10-image collection step, while minimal, is still a form of calibration.
