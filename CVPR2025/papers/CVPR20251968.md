## Problem

Whether self-supervised foundation models trained on natural images spontaneously develop the same low-level visual characteristics as the human visual system—contrast detection thresholds, contrast masking, and contrast constancy—remains untested. Prior work either focused on high-level task accuracy or introduced task-specific contrast classifiers that bias the measurement, leaving open whether foundation models share the same perceptual 'bottlenecks' and invariances as human vision without any additional training.

## Input / Output

The protocol feeds pairs of 224×224-pixel images (corresponding to 3.7°×3.7° at 60 px/deg) into each model's image encoder. Stimuli are generated as linear luminance maps in cd/m², converted to sRGB via a 400 cd/m² display model, and fed as floating-point inputs (modified from the default 8-bit integer interface to resolve sub-quantization contrasts). The output is S_ac, a cosine-similarity-based angular distance between the test and reference feature vectors, from which alignment is quantified as Spearman rank correlation (detection/masking) or RMSE in log-contrast (matching).

## Method

The paper introduces a nine-test psychophysical protocol that treats each foundation model as a black box: no task-specific head or fine-tuning is added. For contrast detection, luminance, and area tests, the model's S_ac response to Gabor patches, band-limited noise, or gratings against a uniform field is compared to the castleCSF prediction. For contrast masking, phase-coherent (same-frequency grating masker) and phase-incoherent (band-limited noise masker) conditions replicate classic dipper-function experiments. For supra-threshold contrast matching, the test contrast c_t is found by minimizing the squared difference in S_ac between a reference grating and a test grating of different frequency (Eq. 2), directly analogous to Georgeson–Sullivan's human matching paradigm. Alignment is scored by Spearman correlation of S_ac changes near the detection threshold (detection/masking) or RMSE in log-contrast (matching), and results are reported across 45 model variants including DINO, DINOv2, OpenCLIP, SAM, SAM2, MAE, and SD-VAE, with ColorVideoVDP as a human-vision baseline.

## Results

The paper evaluates 45 foundation and generative models against human psychophysical data across nine test types (contrast detection, contrast masking, and contrast constancy). DINOv2 shows the closest overall resemblance to human vision, with the best alignment in contrast masking (Spearman correlation closest to 1) and partial contrast constancy. OpenCLIP also shows strong contrast masking alignment despite inconsistent contrast detection responses. ColorVideoVDP, an explicit human-vision model, outperforms all foundation models in nearly all contrast detection tasks, confirming that foundation models have not yet replicated the full low-level sensitivity profile of the human visual system.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DINOv2 emerges as the best-aligned foundation model overall, roughly matching human contrast constancy lines and showing the strongest contrast masking agreement; it also roughly matches human spatial pooling (area summation) performance. OpenCLIP shows surprisingly good contrast masking alignment (stronger for phase-incoherent masking) but exhibits very inconsistent responses across spatial frequencies in contrast detection. DINO shows some band-pass characteristics for noise and achromatic Gabors but is less aligned than DINOv2. SAM and SAM-2 show very inconsistent responses across spatial frequencies, with SAM-2 having a smaller area summation increase than human data. SD-VAE (Stable Diffusion VAE) displays band-pass characteristics but with a much larger sensitivity drop at low frequencies than the visual system and very heavy attenuation of low frequencies in contrast matching. ColorVideoVDP, used as a reference baseline that explicitly models human low-level vision, is better aligned than all foundation models in almost all contrast detection tasks and in contrast constancy, though certain OpenCLIP and DINOv2 variants can match or surpass it in contrast masking. Alignment scores across variants of the same model (e.g., different OpenCLIP sizes) show significant variation and appear unrelated to model complexity or high-level task performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the long-standing question of whether machine vision mirrors human vision—previously explored through adversarial vulnerability, texture-vs-shape bias, and distortion robustness—into the domain of low-level psychophysics. It departs from prior CSF-extraction studies [2, 26] that required training a contrast-discrimination classifier head (introducing task-specific bias) by treating foundation models as black boxes and probing them directly with psychophysical stimuli (Gabor patches, band-limited noise). The protocol also broadens the scope beyond near-threshold contrast sensitivity to include supra-threshold contrast masking and contrast constancy, dimensions that prior computational studies largely ignored. castleCSF [3] and ColorVideoVDP [30] serve as the human-vision reference and low-level model baseline, respectively.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a reusable, nine-test psychophysical protocol for auditing the low-level alignment of any image encoder, which could become a standard diagnostic for future foundation models. The key open question it surfaces is why alignment is strong for contrast masking (signals abundant in natural images) but weak for contrast detection (barely-visible patterns on uniform backgrounds, which are rare in training data), suggesting that training-data statistics, not architecture, drive low-level behavior. Limitations include the use of static 2D stimuli only (no temporal/flicker testing), an arbitrary 60 px/deg mapping, and the fact that sRGB encoding may itself distort low-contrast responses. Whether alignment improves with further scaling or with explicit low-level pretraining objectives remains unexplored.
