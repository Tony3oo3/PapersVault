## Problem

Human dynamics understanding is fragmented across heterogeneous kinematics representations (markers, keypoints, joint angles, SMPL), dynamics hierarchies (joint torques, muscle actions, sEMG), and data domains (biomechanics optimization vs. RL simulation), with no unified framework to exploit the shared underlying motor knowledge. This heterogeneity prevents cross-representation generalization and limits the scale of learnable dynamics data, leaving the production mechanism of human motion underexplored.

## Input / Output

HDyS accepts any subset of four kinematics modalities—variable-count markers (N_m×9 Cartesian position/velocity/acceleration), variable-count skeletal keypoints (N_k×9), fixed-size Rajagopal joint angles (3N_j), and SMPL parameters (3N_j)—along with available dynamics signals (joint torques, muscle activations, or sEMG channels). It produces a shared 128-dim latent vector per frame and can decode any dynamics representation from kinematics (inverse dynamics) or any acceleration representation from dynamics plus kinematics-without-accelerations (forward dynamics). The transformer-based encoders handle arbitrary marker/keypoint counts without positional embeddings, while MLP encoders handle fixed-size joint-space inputs.

## Method

HDyS is structured as an aggregation of two auto-encoder branches mirroring the inverse-forward dynamics procedure: an Inverse-Dynamics Auto-Encoder (IDAE) that encodes kinematics into a shared latent space and decodes joint torques, muscle actions, or sEMG, and a Forward-Dynamics Auto-Encoder (FDAE) that encodes dynamics plus kinematics (excluding accelerations) to predict accelerations. The IDAE decoder uses a shared 4-layer transformer over consecutive frames for temporal context, followed by separate MLP regression heads per dynamics target. Training combines L1 reconstruction losses with InfoNCE contrastive alignment losses that pull latent vectors from different representations of the same frame together while pushing apart different frames, forcing the heterogeneous encoders to converge on a common homogeneous latent geometry. This alignment mechanism is the key differentiator: it extracts shared motor knowledge (e.g., coordination patterns) across representations that are not directly convertible, enabling cross-domain transfer between biomechanics and RL data without explicit representation conversion.

## Results

On the ImDy dataset, HDyS achieves mPJE of 0.5765 (avg) / 0.4674 (best representation), outperforming the ImDy baseline (0.6300). On MiA, HDyS reduces sEMG prediction RMSE to 11.8/11.6 (avg/best) versus 13.3 for the MiA baseline, while also reporting PCC of 0.7232/0.7261. On MinT, HDyS achieves muscle action RMSE of 0.0614/0.0615 and PCC of 0.7420/0.7402. On AddBiomechanics, HDyS reaches mPJE of 0.1189/0.1243 (avg/best), a substantial improvement over the single-dataset variant (0.1695/0.1691).

## Baselines & Comparisons

HDyS outperforms the ImDy baseline [33] on the ImDy dataset, reducing mPJE from 0.6300 to 0.5765 (avg) / 0.4674 (best), a ~8–26% improvement. Against the MiA baseline [3], HDyS lowers sEMG RMSE from 13.3 to 11.8/11.6 and additionally reports PCC of 0.7232/0.7261, which the MiA baseline does not provide. On the GroundLink downstream task, finetuned HDyS beats GroundLinkNet [13] on both feet (L-Foot mPJE 0.0514 vs. 0.0711; R-Foot 0.0694 vs. 0.0912), and even the GroundLink-only HDyS variant (0.0584/0.0751) surpasses GroundLinkNet. For physics-based character control, inserting HDyS latents into the PHC [38] baseline improves global mPJPE from 74.514 to 72.692 and local mPJPE from 49.029 to 47.920, with consistent gains in velocity and acceleration errors. Across all four baselines, HDyS wins on every reported metric, with the largest relative gains observed on the AddBiomechanics and MiA datasets where prior methods were most limited.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by unifying previously fragmented human dynamics research into a single latent space. Rather than building on one prior method, it aggregates and bridges three distinct communities: biomechanics (optimization-based inverse dynamics via AddBiomechanics and the Rajagopal model), reinforcement learning (ImDy/PHC for synthetic dynamics), and sEMG/muscle-action prediction (MiA, MinT). The architectural components—transformer encoders, MLP decoders, and InfoNCE contrastive alignment—are individually standard, but their composition as an inverse-forward dynamics auto-encoder ensemble operating over heterogeneous kinematics and dynamics representations is the novel contribution. The paper departs from the prevailing assumption that each representation and domain must be handled in isolation, instead arguing that a shared homogeneous latent space can be learned across them.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

HDyS provides the first reusable, cross-representation latent space for human dynamics, which could serve as a foundation model component for downstream tasks like biomechanical simulation, ground reaction force estimation, and physics-based character control. Key limitations include a strong lower-body bias (most training data is gait-centric), occasional magnitude divergence in muscle action predictions, and drift accumulation in forward-dynamics simulation. Open questions include whether the framework scales to more datasets and upper-body dynamics, whether the sim2real gap can be further mitigated, and whether the homogeneous latent space can support more sophisticated applications like humanoid robot control or human-robot transfer learning.
