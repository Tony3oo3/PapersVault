## Problem

Existing GCD methods learn representations in Euclidean or spherical spaces, which grow polynomially or not at all with radius, making them suboptimal for encoding the hierarchical structure of object parts within and across categories. This geometric limitation restricts knowledge transfer from seen to unseen categories, particularly when objects exhibit varying poses, scales, and appearances. The paper identifies that no prior GCD method leverages a space whose exponential volume growth naturally accommodates such hierarchical, tree-like data structures.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a partially labelled dataset where the labelled subset D_l provides known category labels and the unlabelled subset D_u contains images from both known and unknown categories; the total number of categories K is assumed known. For parametric variants, the output is a per-image category prediction from a hyperbolic classifier; for non-parametric variants, the output is a cluster assignment obtained by applying semi-supervised k-means to the backbone's Euclidean features (which inherit hierarchical structure from hyperbolic training). The framework is backbone-agnostic, operating on features from DINO or DINOv2 ViT models at standard resolutions.

## Method

HypCD maps Euclidean backbone features into a Poincaré ball via a clipping operation (to prevent gradient vanishing near the boundary) followed by exponential mapping, then performs all subsequent representation learning and classification in hyperbolic space. The representation learning uses a hybrid contrastive loss that combines hyperbolic distance-based similarity (negative hyperbolic distance) with angle-based cosine similarity, weighted by a linearly scheduled coefficient α_d. For parametric baselines, the Euclidean MLP classifier is replaced with a hyperbolic feed-forward network (HypFFN) whose linear layers use Möbius matrix-vector multiplication and safe projection for numerical stability, trained with Riemannian Adam. The framework is modular and plug-and-play: it can be applied to non-parametric baselines (GCD, SelEx) by modifying only the representation learning loss, or to parametric baselines (SimGCD) by additionally swapping in the hyperbolic classifier, consistently improving all three baselines across both DINO and DINOv2 backbones.

## Results

Using the DINOv2 backbone, Hyp-SelEx achieves the highest overall accuracy across all six datasets: 90.7% (CUB), 83.8% (Stanford-Cars), 83.4% (FGVC-Aircraft), 98.6% (CIFAR-10), 88.6% (CIFAR-100), and 92.3% (ImageNet-100) on the 'All' metric. Hyp-SimGCD with DINOv2 reaches 91.5% on CIFAR-100 and 91.9% on ImageNet-100, surpassing the previous SOTA SelEx by 3.8% and 1.4% respectively. The largest single improvement is Hyp-GCD over GCD on Stanford-Cars with DINO, gaining +11.8% on 'All' ACC (50.8% vs. 39.0%). Across all settings, hyperbolic variants consistently reduce the Old–New accuracy gap, indicating improved knowledge transfer from seen to unseen categories.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All three HypCD variants (Hyp-GCD, Hyp-SimGCD, Hyp-SelEx) consistently outperform their Euclidean counterparts across every dataset and backbone. Hyp-SelEx with DINOv2 sets the new SOTA on CUB (90.7% vs. SelEx's 87.4%), FGVC-Aircraft (83.4% vs. 79.8%), and ImageNet-100 (92.3% vs. 90.9%), while Hyp-SimGCD surpasses SelEx on Stanford-Cars (82.5% vs. 82.2%) and CIFAR-100 (91.5% vs. 87.7%). The most dramatic gain is Hyp-GCD over GCD on Stanford-Cars with DINO (+11.8% All, +15.9% New), showing that hyperbolic geometry is especially beneficial when the baseline is weakest. Against other GCD methods in Table 1 (ORCA, XCon, OpenCon, PromptCAL, DCCL, GPC, PIM, μGCD, InfoSieve, CiPR, SPTNet, CMS, AMEND), the HypCD variants dominate on the fine-grained SSB datasets; for example, Hyp-SelEx (DINOv2) leads CUB by over 12% over the next-best method (CiPR at 78.3%). On CIFAR-10, where performance is near saturation, all methods cluster within 1–2%, with Hyp-SimGCD (DINOv2) achieving the best 98.9%. A consistent qualitative finding is that hyperbolic methods narrow the Old–New accuracy gap (e.g., on Stanford-Cars with DINOv2, Hyp-SimGCD reduces it from 15.3 points to 4.8 points), confirming improved transfer to unseen categories.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the standard practice in GCD of operating in Euclidean or spherical space (via L2-normalized features from DINO/DINOv2 backbones) by relocating representation learning and classification into the Poincaré ball model of hyperbolic space. It builds directly on the non-parametric GCD baseline [51], the parametric SimGCD baseline [59], and the SOTA SelEx method [45], adapting each by replacing their distance/similarity computations with hyperbolic counterparts (Möbius addition, hyperbolic distance, cosine similarity in the Poincaré ball) and, for parametric methods, substituting the Euclidean MLP with a hyperbolic feed-forward network (HypFFN) derived from [19]. The exponential mapping and feature clipping techniques are borrowed from prior hyperbolic vision work [23, 31], but their application to category discovery is new.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The plug-and-play nature of HypCD—requiring no changes to the backbone, data pipeline, or label assignment strategy for non-parametric methods—makes it an easy addition to existing GCD pipelines, and the consistent gains across DINO and DINOv2 backbones on both fine-grained and generic datasets suggest the geometric insight is robust. A notable finding is that hyperbolic training transfers hierarchical structure back into the Euclidean feature space, improving even downstream k-means clustering without any hyperbolic inference. Open questions include whether the benefits persist at ImageNet scale, whether the per-dataset-type tuning of curvature and clipping parameters can be automated, and whether hyperbolic geometry offers similar advantages in other open-world settings beyond GCD (e.g., open-set recognition or continual category discovery).
