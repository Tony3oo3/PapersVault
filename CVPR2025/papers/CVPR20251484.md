## Problem

Existing AI-generated image detectors rely on identifying generative artifacts through binary classification, but these artifacts shift rapidly as generative models evolve, causing detectors to quickly become obsolete. The paper addresses this by reframing detection as a one-class anomaly detection problem grounded in the intrinsic low-level features of real photographic images rather than in the ever-changing fingerprints of specific generators.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251484/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image, encodes it into the latent space of a pre-trained VAE (from Stable Diffusion), and extracts a low-level feature vector via a ViT-based backbone. It outputs a log-likelihood score under a 6-component Gaussian Mixture Model fitted to real-image features, and a binary real/AI-generated label determined by comparing the score against a threshold set at the 99.95th percentile of training-image likelihoods. No AI-generated images are required at inference time; only the real-image feature distribution is needed.

## Method

The core contribution is a novel pretext task that trains a ViT-based low-level feature extractor to discern pixel-level differences between images. Given a real image, the pipeline adds Gaussian noise at 9 different intensities in the VAE latent space, then denoises each corrupted image using a pre-trained Stable Diffusion U-Net (DDIM scheduler, 50 steps) purely as a restoration tool. The feature extractor is trained with a combined regression loss (predicting the MAE between original and denoised images) and a classification loss (predicting which of 11 noise-level categories the image belongs to), forcing it to capture subtle low-level cues invisible to humans. At detection time, the pre-trained extractor maps a query image to a feature vector, and a GMM (6 components, fitted via EM on 10,000 real images) assigns a likelihood score; images falling below the threshold are flagged as AI-generated. This one-class formulation avoids dependence on any specific generator's artifacts and generalizes across GANs, diffusion models, flow-based models, and their variants.

## Results

On the GenImage dataset (8 diverse generative models), the proposed method (Ours-basic) achieves 95.97% average detection accuracy, outperforming the strongest baseline DRCT (89.49%) by over 6 points. The method exhibits remarkable consistency, scoring 95.51–96.36% across all eight models (Midjourney, SDv1.4, SDv1.5, ADM, Glide, Wukong, VQDM, BigGAN), whereas baselines drop to ~50% on non-diffusion models like BigGAN and ADM. On DRCT-2M (16 diffusion variants), the fine-tuned variant (Ours-ft) reaches 90.86% average accuracy, marginally surpassing DRCT (90.49%), though both struggle on the challenging DR (diffusion-reconstruction) subsets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method decisively outperforms all 12 baselines on GenImage, with the closest competitor being DRCT (89.49% vs. 95.97%). The advantage is most dramatic on non-diffusion models: on BigGAN, Ours-basic scores 95.71% while DRCT drops to 81.67%, LGrad to 61.63%, and most other baselines to ~50%; on ADM, the gap is similarly large (95.88% vs. 79.42% for DRCT). Among diffusion-specific detectors, DIRE (71.24%) and LNP (69.80%) are the weakest, confirming that diffusion-reconstruction-based artifacts are insufficient for generalization. On DRCT-2M, the fine-tuned variant (Ours-ft, 90.86%) narrowly beats DRCT (90.49%), though both methods degrade to ~50% on the SDv2-DR and SDXL-DR subsets where images are reconstructed from real photos rather than random noise. The method also demonstrates cross-database generalization: when trained on MSCOCO real images and tested on ImageNet-derived GenImage, performance drops only slightly (Ours-m: 93.07% vs. 95.97%), whereas DRCT-m drops to 87.67%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant binary-classification paradigm in AI-generated image detection (CNNSpot, LGrad, LNP, DIRE, DRCT), which seeks universal generative artifacts absent in real images. Instead, it repurposes a pre-trained diffusion model as a denoising tool to construct a self-supervised pretext task, training a ViT-based low-level feature extractor to capture pixel-level differences. The detection is then framed as one-class anomaly detection via a Gaussian Mixture Model over real-image feature distributions, drawing on the anomaly detection literature rather than the generative-artifact literature. Conceptually, it bridges image forensics (low-level noise patterns), self-supervised representation learning, and diffusion model internals in a way that prior detectors have not combined.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The one-class framing is inherently more future-proof than artifact-based detectors, since it does not require anticipating the specific artifacts of next-generation models; any image whose low-level statistics deviate from the real-image distribution is flagged. However, the method's vulnerability to image-to-image reconstruction pipelines (ControlNet, DR variants scoring near chance in DRCT-2M) reveals that low-level features alone may not suffice when the generative process starts from a real image. Open questions include whether the approach degrades as future generators better replicate sensor noise and JPEG artifacts, and whether the reliance on a specific pre-trained diffusion model (SD v1.4) as the denoising backbone limits transferability to new latent spaces.
