## Problem

Diffusion-based inpainting models like BrushNet achieve high quality but require 50+ sampling steps, making them impractical for real-time use. Naively transplanting a multi-step inpainting adapter onto a few-step distilled model (DMD2) introduces color shifts, oversaturation, and semantic artifacts, while training the adapter with diffusion loss alone yields blurry outputs. Additionally, existing inpainting benchmarks rely on precise segmentation masks and global prompts, diverging significantly from how users actually perform inpainting with rough masks and complex local descriptions.

## Input / Output

TurboFill takes a partially masked RGB image, a binary mask indicating the region to fill, and a short text prompt describing the desired content within the masked area. It produces a complete inpainted image in exactly 4 diffusion steps (timesteps {999, 749, 499, 249} under the LCM scheduler). The training data (LocalCaptionData, ~1.2M images) pairs region-specific captions generated by Florence-2 with SAM2 segmentation masks, enabling the model to handle local, object-level prompts rather than global image descriptions.

## Method

TurboFill trains a ControlNet-style inpainting adapter (same architecture as BrushNet, with self- and cross-attention removed) directly on top of the few-step DMD2 model using a 3-step adversarial training scheme that alternates: (1) updating the adapter in a 'slow generator' (adapter + SDXL) with real diffusion loss, (2) updating the adapter in a 'fast generator' (adapter + DMD2) with an adversarial GAN loss and a background-preservation L2 loss, and (3) updating a diffusion discriminator (SDXL encoder + assistant encoder + convolutional classifier) with fake diffusion loss and GAN discriminator loss. The diffusion discriminator is the key architectural addition: it is trained to distinguish real noisy latents from fake ones while simultaneously learning scene structure via the fake diffusion loss, allowing the GAN loss to focus on texture and detail. The adapters in the slow and fast generators share weights, and the entire pipeline is trained for 40K iterations on 8 A100 GPUs, eliminating the need for a separate distillation stage that would otherwise require 64 A100s for 50 hours.

## Results

On DilationBench, TurboFill (4 steps) achieves Q-Align 4.5703 (mask) and 4.7188 (whole image), CLIPIQA+ 0.7332 (mask) and 0.7111 (whole image), outperforming 50-step BrushNet* (Q-Align 4.4688/4.5312) and BrushNet-Rand (Q-Align 4.2617/4.6289) on the primary quality metrics. On HumanBench, TurboFill reaches Q-Align 4.4727 (mask) and 4.3203 (whole image), CLIPIQA+ 0.7257 (mask) and 0.6822 (whole image), again surpassing 50-step BrushNet* (Q-Align 4.2578/4.0898). The only method that consistently outperforms TurboFill across all metrics is PowerPaint V2 (50 steps), which leads by a margin of ~0.2 in Q-Align and ~0.04 in CLIPIQA+.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TurboFill (4 steps) decisively outperforms the naively migrated 4-step BrushNet* on every metric (e.g., Q-Align 4.5703 vs 4.1836 on mask, CLIPIQA+ 0.7332 vs 0.6572), confirming that direct adapter transfer to DMD2 without retraining degrades quality. Against 50-step BrushNet*, TurboFill wins on Q-Align (4.5703 vs 4.4688 mask; 4.7188 vs 4.5312 whole) and CLIPIQA+ (0.7332 vs 0.7137 mask; 0.7111 vs 0.6974 whole) while trailing slightly on TOPIQ and CLIP Sim, demonstrating that 4 steps can match or exceed 50 steps in perceptual quality. TurboFill also surpasses BrushNet-Rand (50 steps) on Q-Align and CLIPIQA+ but lags on CLIP Sim (25.352 vs 21.634 is actually a win for TurboFill; BrushNet-Rand's low CLIP Sim reflects its use of global prompts). PowerPaint V2 (50 steps) remains the strongest baseline, leading TurboFill by ~0.2 in Q-Align and ~0.04 in CLIPIQA+ on DilationBench, though the paper notes PowerPaint V2 suffers from structural distortions (e.g., two-headed subjects) that current IQA metrics do not fully penalize. On HumanBench, TurboFill's advantage over 50-step BrushNet* is even larger (Q-Align 4.4727 vs 4.2578 mask), while PowerPaint V2 still leads by a smaller margin (4.5586 vs 4.4727).

## Relation to Prior Work

TurboFill builds directly on the BrushNet/ControlNet adapter paradigm for inpainting and on DMD2 (a few-step distilled text-to-image model), but departs from the standard two-stage pipeline (train a multi-step adapter, then distill) by training the adapter directly on the few-step model in a single stage. The key methodological departure is the 3-step adversarial training scheme that couples a diffusion discriminator with real and fake diffusion losses, rather than relying on diffusion loss alone or on post-hoc distillation. The paper also introduces DilationBench and HumanBench, which extend existing evaluation protocols (OpenImage, MSCOCO, BrushBench) to better capture user-driven inpainting with rough masks and complex local prompts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: 4-step inference (vs. 50) and a ~10× reduction in training compute make high-quality inpainting accessible in latency-sensitive or resource-constrained settings. The finding that a few-step model with adversarial training can match or exceed multi-step quality on IQA metrics challenges the implicit assumption that more diffusion steps are necessary for fidelity. Open questions include whether the 3-step scheme generalizes beyond the SDXL/DMD2 family to other few-step architectures (e.g., SD3, Flux), and whether the newly proposed benchmarks will be adopted as standard evaluation protocols. The method remains tied to the ControlNet-style adapter architecture and the specific LCM scheduler timesteps, limiting immediate transferability.
