## Problem

Existing SAR image registration methods treat keypoint matching as a closed-set classification, forcing all pre-detected keypoints into predefined classes even though over 90% are redundant (unmatched) points. This inevitably produces incorrect matched-point pairs and degrades registration accuracy. The paper reframes registration as a cross-rejective open-set recognition problem where redundant keypoints are treated as out-of-distribution samples to be rejected during training rather than misclassified.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251074/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes two SAR images (reference and sensed) with pre-detected SIFT keypoints (m and n points respectively), where each keypoint is expanded into 45 image patches via multi-scale cropping and 8-nearest-neighbor sampling, all resized to 64×64. It outputs k matched-point pairs (k ≪ min(m,n)) directly without any post-processing, from which the registration transform is estimated. During testing, only the sensed image is fed into the reference domain, and the stabilized cross-domain mapping from iterative training yields the final matching.

## Method

CroR-OSIR comprises two iteratively coupled modules: a Swin Transformer-based supervised contrastive feature-tuning module (SupCon) and a cross-rejective open-set recognition module (CroR-OSR) with two independent classification heads (m-class for reference, n-class for sensed). CroR-OSR performs closed-set classification within each domain while simultaneously classifying all keypoints in the counterpart domain; a percentile-based rejection threshold (λ) filters low-confidence cross-domain predictions, and a mutual consistency check retains only bidirectionally agreed mappings. The surviving cross-domain estimation labels are fed back into SupCon as additional positive pairs in a contrastive loss, iteratively sharpening feature discriminability for visually similar but unmatched keypoints. The entire process converges when the cross-domain mapping stabilizes, at which point the matched pairs constitute the registration result—differing from traditional OSR in that all samples (including OOD) are available during training and the goal is identifying known matched categories rather than detecting unknown ones.

## Results

On four SAR image registration datasets, CroR-OSIR achieves the lowest RMS_all across all benchmarks: 0.2533 (YellowR1), 0.0952 (YellowR2), 0.3014 (Yama), and 0.1339 (Wuhan), while simultaneously obtaining fewer matched-point pairs (N_red = 11, 6, 25, 16 respectively), indicating higher per-pair confidence. The largest improvement is on YellowR2, where CroR-OSIR reduces RMS_all from 0.4808 (STDT-Net) to 0.0952. On the optical HPatches dataset, CroR-OSIR also achieves the best RMS_all of 0.6600 with only 213 matched pairs, outperforming DALF (0.7043) and XFeat (0.7763).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CroR-OSIR outperforms all 11 baselines on every dataset. On YellowR1, it reduces RMS_all from 0.3779 (DALF, the best competitor) to 0.2533, a 33% relative improvement, while matching DALF's N_red of 11 pairs. On YellowR2, the gap is largest: CroR-OSIR achieves 0.0952 versus 0.4808 for STDT-Net, an 80% reduction, with only 6 matched pairs versus 79 for STDT-Net. On Yama, CroR-OSIR (0.3014) edges out XFeat (0.3295) by 0.028 while using 25 pairs versus XFeat's 19. On Wuhan, CroR-OSIR (0.1339) dramatically outperforms AdaSSIR (0.4217) by 0.288. Notably, all competing methods rely on post-processing to filter matches, whereas CroR-OSIR produces its final matched pairs directly from the training process without any additional filtering step. On the optical HPatches dataset, CroR-OSIR (RMS_all=0.6600, 213 pairs) also beats DALF (0.7043, 677 pairs) and XFeat (0.7763, 670 pairs), confirming generality beyond SAR imagery.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reframes SAR image registration—traditionally formulated as a closed-set multi-class classification problem (as in MSDF-Net, STDT-Net, AdaSSIR)—as a cross-rejective open-set recognition task, where redundant keypoints are treated as out-of-distribution samples to be rejected during training rather than forced into known classes. It builds directly on supervised contrastive learning (Khosla et al., 2020) for feature tuning and on percentile-based open-set rejection rules (Vaze et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2023), but departs from both by making all samples (including OOD) available during training and by introducing a cross-domain mutual rejection mechanism between two classification heads. The Swin Transformer encoder and SIFT-based keypoint detection are standard components; the novelty lies in the iterative cross-rejection loop and the feedback of cross-domain estimation labels into the contrastive objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The open-set reframing offers a principled explanation for why closed-set registration methods struggle: the majority of pre-detected keypoints (>90%) are redundant, and forcing them into known classes degrades feature discriminability. The method's ability to achieve substantially lower RMS without any post-processing (unlike all compared baselines) suggests the rejection mechanism is doing meaningful work rather than relying on ad-hoc filtering. Limitations include reliance on SIFT for keypoint detection, evaluation on only four SAR image pairs plus one optical pair, and an iterative training procedure whose convergence behavior may be sensitive to the rejection threshold λ. Open questions include scalability to scenes with thousands of keypoints, whether the cross-rejection idea transfers to other matching tasks (e.g., 3D point clouds, video registration), and whether the Swin Transformer encoder is necessary or a lighter backbone suffices.
