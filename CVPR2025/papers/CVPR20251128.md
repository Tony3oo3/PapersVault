## Problem

Semantic watermarks for diffusion models (e.g., Tree-Ring, Gaussian Shading) rely on the secrecy of the generative model to prevent forgery, assuming an attacker cannot replicate the watermark without access to the target model. This paper reveals that this assumption is fundamentally broken: an attacker can forge or remove these watermarks using a completely unrelated proxy model with a different architecture and latent space, requiring only a single watermarked reference image.

## Input / Output

The attacks operate on 512×512 RGB images. For forgery, the attacker needs one watermarked reference image and one clean cover image (or a text prompt for the reprompting variant); for removal, only the watermarked image is needed. All optimization is performed in the latent space of a vanilla SD2.1 proxy model, while the target model (SDXL, FLUX.1, PixArt-Σ, etc.) is never accessed. The output is a pixel-space image that, when submitted to the service provider's verification API, is detected as watermarked and attributed to the original user, or is no longer detected as watermarked in the removal case.

## Method

The core insight is that Inverse DDIM sampling performed in the latent space of an unrelated proxy model produces a latent noise pattern that transfers to the target model's latent space, enabling watermark extraction without the target model. The Imprint-Forgery attack encodes both the watermarked reference and a clean cover image into the proxy's latent space, inverts the reference to recover z_T^(w), then optimizes a perturbation δ on the cover's latent via gradient descent (with gradient checkpointing through the full inverse DDIM chain) to minimize the L2 distance between the cover's inverted latent and z_T^(w). The Reprompting attack simply uses the extracted z_T^(w) as initial noise to generate an entirely new image from a different prompt, with optional multi-prompt and bin-resampling augmentations for Gaussian Shading. A masking extension preserves faces and text by zeroing gradient updates in masked latent regions and pasting original pixels post-decoding. The removal variant targets −z_T^(w) instead, erasing the watermark pattern from the initial latent noise.

## Results

The Imprint-Forgery attack achieves near-perfect watermark detection and attribution (1.00 TPR) on SD2.1-Anime and SDXL for Gaussian Shading within 50 optimization steps, and 0.95–0.96 on PixArt-Σ and FLUX.1 at 150 steps. For Tree-Ring, detection rates reach 1.00 (SD2.1-Anime), 0.99 (SDXL), 0.84 (PixArt-Σ), and 0.23 (FLUX.1) at 150 steps. The concurrent averaging baseline (Avg-5000, requiring 5,000 reference images) completely fails on Gaussian Shading (0.00 detection/attribution across all models) while the proposed attack needs only a single reference image. The Reprompting attack achieves 0.983–1.000 detection/attribution for Gaussian Shading and 0.875–1.000 for Tree-Ring on most models, with the augmented variant (Reprompt+) reaching 1.000 on three of four models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against the concurrent averaging attack (Yang et al., Avg-5000), which requires 5,000 watermarked reference images to average in pixel or latent space. On Gaussian Shading, the averaging baseline completely fails (0.00 detection and attribution on all four target models), while the proposed Imprint-Forgery attack achieves 0.95–1.00 with a single reference image. On Tree-Ring, the averaging baseline performs comparably (0.90–1.00 detection) but still requires 5,000 images versus one. For watermark removal (Table 2), the averaging baseline also fails on Gaussian Shading (1.00 detection remains) while the proposed Imprint-Removal attack reduces detection to 0.00 within 100 steps. The paper also notes that prior forgery attacks (Saberi et al. [32]) require unrealistic white-noise image access from the service provider and do not support attribution, whereas the proposed attacks work under a realistic black-box setting with a single watermarked image and an unrelated proxy model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of semantic watermark forgery/removal attacks (Saberi et al. [32], Yang et al. [44], Lukas et al. [26], Liu et al. [23]) by demonstrating that all prior unrealistic assumptions—access to the target model, large numbers of watermarked images, or white-noise generation requests—can be eliminated. The key conceptual departure is leveraging DDIM inversion's cross-model transferability: the authors show that inversion in an unrelated proxy model's latent space (even with a different architecture, UNet vs. DiT) produces latents that, when decoded and re-inverted in the target model, still carry the watermark pattern. This repurposes the standard inversion mechanism [27] as an attack primitive rather than a verification tool.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The findings fundamentally undermine the security model of inversion-based semantic watermarks, which are being adopted by major AI providers (Google, Meta, Hugging Face) for content provenance and attribution. The demonstration that a single watermarked image plus any unrelated open-source model suffices for forgery or removal means the 'keep the model secret' assumption provides no meaningful protection. The paper shows threshold tightening is not a viable defense because attack images overlap with perturbed legitimate watermarks, leaving the field without a clear mitigation path. Open questions include whether fundamentally different watermarking paradigms (e.g., decoder-level or training-based) can resist such cross-model transfer, and whether the observed auto-encoder similarity correlation can be exploited for targeted defenses.
