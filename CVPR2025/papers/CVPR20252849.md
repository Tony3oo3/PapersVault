## Problem

CLIP's text encoder exhibits an inherent Anomaly Unawareness: normal and abnormal text embeddings overlap significantly, making text-guided zero-shot anomaly detection unreliable. Existing CLIP-based AD methods either rely on these unseparated original text embeddings or employ prompt learning that risks damaging CLIP's pre-trained class knowledge and degrading generalization to unseen categories.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252849/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes RGB images resized to 518×518 and processes them through a frozen ViT-L/14 backbone. It produces three outputs: an image-level binary anomaly score, a pixel-level anomaly segmentation mask (H×W), and a continuous anomaly prediction map derived from cosine similarity between patch features and normal/anomaly text anchors. Training uses a 1:1 normal-to-anomaly sample ratio per class at 2, 16, 64, or full-shot levels, while inference is zero-shot on entirely unseen classes.

## Method

AA-CLIP employs a two-stage adaptation strategy using lightweight residual adapters (x_enhanced = λ·x_residual + (1−λ)·x_original, λ=0.1) inserted into shallow transformer layers, keeping all CLIP parameters frozen. In Stage 1, residual adapters in the first 3 text-encoder layers plus a Disentangle Loss (|⟨T_N, T_A⟩|²) force normal and anomaly text anchors to become orthogonal, creating clearly separated semantic anchors. In Stage 2, residual adapters in the first 6 visual-encoder layers align multi-granularity patch features (extracted from layers 6, 12, 18, 24 and projected via trainable projectors) to the fixed text anchors using BCE + Dice + Focal losses. This sequential text-then-visual adaptation prevents the class-information collapse observed in one-stage training, preserving CLIP's generalization while embedding anomaly-discriminative capability.

## Results

On pixel-level zero-shot anomaly detection across 11 industrial and medical benchmarks, AA-CLIP achieves a new SOTA average AUROC of 93.4% (full-shot) and 92.8% (64-shot), surpassing the previous best MVFA-AD (91.3%) by 2.1 points. Remarkably, even with only 2 training samples per class, AA-CLIP reaches 92.0% pixel-level AUROC, already exceeding all full-data baselines. At the image level, AA-CLIP sets a new SOTA of 83.1% AUROC with 64-shot training, outperforming AdaCLIP (80.6%) and AnomalyCLIP (78.4%) by 2.5 and 4.7 points respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AA-CLIP outperforms all six baselines on average pixel-level AUROC: it beats MVFA-AD (91.3%) by 2.1 points, AnomalyCLIP (90.4%) by 3.0 points, VAND (89.3%) by 4.1 points, and WinCLIP (74.7%) by 18.7 points at full-shot. The data-efficiency advantage is striking: AA-CLIP with only 2-shot training (92.0%) already surpasses MVFA-AD and AnomalyCLIP trained on full datasets. At the image level, AA-CLIP (83.1% at 64-shot) leads AdaCLIP (80.6%) by 2.5 points and AnomalyCLIP (78.4%) by 4.7 points. The largest margins appear on medical datasets (e.g., Retina OCT: 95.5% vs. 92.6% for MVFA-AD; CVC-300: 96.4% vs. 95.4%), while on industrial datasets like MVTec-AD the gap is smaller (91.9% vs. 91.1%), suggesting the text-space disentanglement is most beneficial for domains where anomaly semantics are more subtle.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the CLIP-based zero-shot anomaly detection line initiated by WinCLIP and refined by VAND, AnomalyCLIP, and AdaCLIP. Its key departure is identifying CLIP's 'Anomaly Unawareness'—the entanglement of normal and anomaly text embeddings—and addressing it through a two-stage adaptation that first disentangles text-space anchors (via a novel Disentangle Loss) before aligning patch-level visual features to those anchors. Unlike prior methods that either reuse original CLIP text embeddings or inject learnable prompts into the text encoder, AA-CLIP uses lightweight residual adapters in shallow layers to preserve CLIP's pre-trained class knowledge while injecting anomaly-discriminative semantics, building on VAND's multi-granularity patch feature extraction for the visual side.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first explicit diagnosis of why CLIP-based AD methods underperform: the text encoder's failure to separate normal from abnormal semantics, a limitation that prior work attributed to visual feature quality rather than textual grounding. The residual-adapter + two-stage recipe is a practical, low-parameter recipe for adapting large frozen models to fine-grained discrimination tasks without catastrophic forgetting, potentially transferable to other 'unawareness' gaps in CLIP (e.g., context-awareness, domain-specific nuances). Open questions include the observed overfitting at full-shot training (suggesting a saturation point for CLIP adaptation) and whether the disentangle loss generalizes beyond binary normal/anomaly to multi-class anomaly taxonomies.
