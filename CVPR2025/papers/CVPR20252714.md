## Problem

Existing camera-radar fusion methods for 3D object detection treat the complex, object-dependent distribution of radar hits as a black-box neural network problem, making it difficult to achieve measurable gains over camera-only baselines. The paper argues that explicitly modeling how radar returns distribute on objects—depending on category, size, orientation, and relative geometry—enables radar data to be more effectively aligned with monocular detections to refine range estimation.

## Input / Output

The method consumes monocular detection-level outputs (category, size, orientation, approximate range from a frozen SparseBEV detector) and accumulated BEV radar points (2D XY locations without height, binned into radial-tangential coordinate maps at 0.1 m or 0.2 m resolution). It produces a refined 3D bounding box where the tangential position, size, orientation, and category are inherited from the monocular detector, while the radial (range) position is corrected by a learned offset of up to ±3.2 m, along with an updated detection confidence score.

## Method

RICCARDO is a three-stage pipeline built around an explicit radar hit distribution model. Stage 1 trains a lightweight MLP to predict a 2D probability map of radar returns in object-centric BEV coordinates, conditioned on category, size, orientation, range, and azimuth, using cross-entropy plus a spatial smoothness regularizer on accumulated multi-sweep radar data. Stage 2 rotates this predicted distribution into radial-tangential coordinates and performs a 1D cross-correlation (convolution) along the radial axis with binned measured radar points, yielding a matching score profile that identifies candidate range positions. Stage 3 is a second lightweight MLP that takes monocular detection parameters and the Stage-2 score profile to select the best range candidate, implicitly learning when to trust the monocular estimate versus the radar-informed peak. The key design choice is the object-aligned coordinate system, which produces more gradual probability variations across relative sensor positions and facilitates learning from limited data.

## Results

On the nuScenes test set, RICCARDO (built on SparseBEV) achieves SOTA radar-camera fusion performance with NDS 0.695 and mAP 0.630, outperforming the next-best fusion method HVDetFusion (NDS 0.674, mAP 0.609) by +0.021 NDS and +0.021 mAP. Compared to its own monocular baseline SparseBEV (NDS 0.675, mAP 0.603), RICCARDO improves mAP by +0.027 and reduces mean Average Translation Error (mATE) from 0.425 to 0.363, while preserving orientation (mAOE 0.311) and size (mASE 0.240) accuracy. On the validation set, RICCARDO similarly leads with NDS 0.622 and mAP 0.544 over HVDetFusion (0.557, 0.451) and HyDRa (0.617, 0.536).

## Baselines & Comparisons

RICCARDO outperforms all seven baselines on the nuScenes test set in NDS and mAP. Against the closest fusion competitor HVDetFusion [16], RICCARDO gains +0.021 NDS (0.695 vs 0.674) and +0.021 mAP (0.630 vs 0.609), while also improving mATE (0.363 vs 0.379) and mAOE (0.311 vs 0.382). Over its own monocular backbone SparseBEV [19], the fusion adds +0.020 NDS and +0.027 mAP with a notable mATE reduction (0.363 vs 0.425), confirming that radar range information primarily benefits position estimation. Against other fusion methods, RICCARDO leads HyDRa [38] by +0.053 NDS, RCBEVDet [18] by +0.056 NDS, CRN [11] by +0.071 NDS, and MVFusion [39] by +0.178 NDS. The radar-only baseline PGD [35] trails by a wide margin (NDS 0.448), underscoring the complementary value of camera data. Notably, RICCARDO preserves the monocular detector's orientation and size accuracy (mAOE 0.311, mASE 0.240) while improving range, validating its design of focusing radar fusion on the radial dimension.

## Relation to Prior Work

RICCARDO departs from prior camera-radar fusion methods (CRN, MVFusion, RCBEVDet, HyDRa, HVDetFusion) that combine modalities via concatenation, weighted sums, or attention within a single black-box network. Instead, it introduces an explicit radar hit distribution model (RIC) that predicts where radar returns should appear on an object given its category, size, and orientation, then uses cross-correlation with measured radar points for range refinement. The method is structured as a lightweight, modular add-on to an existing monocular detector (SparseBEV), operating at the detection level rather than the feature level, which contrasts with most prior fusion approaches that operate on intermediate representations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a notable gap: prior radar-camera fusion methods have struggled to beat camera-only baselines, and RICCARDO demonstrates that explicitly modeling the physics of radar reflections (rather than relying on implicit learned fusion) can yield consistent improvements in range estimation. However, as a detection-level fusion, it cannot recover objects missed by the monocular detector, and its BEV representation cannot disambiguate vertically stacked targets. The 1D radial search space is a simplification; expanding to multi-dimensional search (tangential offset, size, orientation) with variable distributions is identified as important future work.
