## Problem

Existing biodiversity image datasets lack fine-grained visual trait annotations (at both image and pixel level) and predominantly use natural habitat images with complex backgrounds that occlude and obfuscate traits. This limits the ability to study how organisms' externally visible characteristics vary across species and evolve over time, and prevents CV research on trait localization, explainability, and out-of-distribution generalization in a biologically grounded setting.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250190/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Input consists of RGB fish images cropped to tight bounding boxes (minimum 224 px) with uniform white backgrounds, processed from museum collections (GLIN, IDigBio, MorphBank) to remove rulers, tags, and original backgrounds. Outputs span three tasks: a single species label from 1,758 classes (FV-Classification), four binary trait presence/absence labels for adipose fin, pelvic fin, barbel, and multiple dorsal fins (FV-Id), and pixel-level segmentation masks for nine anatomical traits including head, eye, dorsal, pectoral, pelvic, anal, caudal, adipose fins, and barbel (FV-Segmentation). The trait identification task additionally provides three distinct test splits—species-stratified in-distribution, leave-out-species OOD, and a manually annotated set with pixel-level ground truth—to probe generalization and explainability.

## Method

The core contribution is a fully reproducible five-stage processing pipeline that transforms raw museum images into an AI-ready trait-annotated dataset: MD5-based deduplication, quality-metadata filtering, species-name standardization via Open Tree Taxonomy, fish bounding-box detection and cropping with Grounding DINO, and background removal via SAM to enforce uniform white backgrounds. Trait labels are obtained through a hybrid strategy combining species-level annotations from the Phenoscape Knowledge Base and FishBase (introducing a weak-supervision challenge) with expert-biologist manual annotations at both image and pixel level for a 1,281-image test set. The paper then benchmarks a broad suite of baselines—CNNs (VGG-19, ResNeXt-50, RegNetY-4G), ViTs (Swin-B, MaxViT-T, PVT-v2), foundation models in zero-shot and linear-probing modes (CLIP, BioCLIP, DINOv2), FGVC methods (INTR, TransFG), and segmentation architectures (PSPNet, DeepLabV3+, UNet, Mask2Former, YOLOv8, Molmo+SAM-v2)—to expose gaps in long-tailed classification, OOD trait generalization, weak-label learning, explainability, and small-object segmentation.

## Results

On the FV-Classification task (56,360 images, 1,758 species), MaxViT-T achieves the best macro-F1 of 57.8% and the highest Ultra-Rare accuracy (53.9%), while TransFG leads on Majority species accuracy (94.5%). Foundation models perform poorly in zero-shot (CLIP-ZS: 0.1% F1; BioCLIP-ZS: 4.6% F1), and even with linear probing, DINOv2-LP (53.1% F1) trails the best supervised backbone by ~5 points. On FV-Segmentation (6,132 images, 9 traits), YOLOv8 achieves the best mIoU of 83.1%, but all methods collapse on small/rare traits: barbel IoU ranges from 0.0% (Mask2Former) to 26.7% (YOLOv8), and adipose fin IoU peaks at only 69.7% (UNet).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among supervised backbones, MaxViT-T (57.8% F1) outperforms all CNNs (VGG-19: 49.7%, ResNeXt-50: 44.4%, RegNetY-4G: 43.7%) and other ViTs (Swin-B-22k: 55.1%, PVT-v2-b0: 51.0%), with the gap widening on Ultra-Rare species (53.9% vs. 38.5–50.4%). Foundation models fail dramatically in zero-shot: CLIP-ZS scores 0.1% F1 and BioCLIP-ZS only 4.6%, both near random. With linear probing, DINOv2-LP (53.1% F1) substantially outperforms CLIP-LP (25.4%) and BioCLIP-LP (38.2%), yet still lags MaxViT-T by ~5 F1 points, indicating that current vision foundation models cannot capture fine-grained fish species variation. FGVC methods show mixed results: TransFG (50.3% F1) is competitive on common species but drops to 45.3% on Ultra-Rare, while INTR collapses to 0.6% Ultra-Rare accuracy. For trait segmentation, YOLOv8 (83.1% mIoU) edges out Mask2Former (81.6%) and Semantic FPN (77.6%), but all supervised methods fail on barbel (≤26.7% IoU) and adipose fin (≤69.7% IoU); the zero-shot Molmo+SAM pipeline achieves only 39.1% mIoU overall, though it localizes dorsal and caudal fins reasonably well.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing the first dataset specifically designed for visual trait analysis of aquatic organisms, departing from prior biodiversity image datasets (CUB, NABirds, FishBase, DeepFish) that focus exclusively on species-level classification or full-body segmentation without fine-grained trait annotations. Conceptually, it repurposes existing tools—Grounding DINO for detection, SAM for background removal, and species-level labels from Phenoscape/FishBase—into a reproducible pipeline that transforms raw museum images into an 'AI-ready' trait-annotated resource. The paper positions trait identification and trait segmentation as novel problem formulations that bridge the gap between fine-grained visual categorization (FGVC) and biologically grounded trait discovery, a connection the authors argue has been 'relatively ignored' in prior CV-for-biodiversity work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Fish-Vista provides the first comprehensive benchmark exposing multiple simultaneous CV challenges—extreme long-tailed class imbalance, out-of-distribution generalization to unseen species, learning from weak species-level labels, explainability of trait predictions, and segmentation of small rare objects—within a single biologically meaningful framework. The empirical finding that models achieve reasonable trait-identification accuracy yet show near-zero spatial alignment with ground-truth trait regions (mIoU ≈ 0.8) raises a critical open question about whether current architectures truly 'understand' traits or merely memorize species-trait associations. Limitations include the relatively small segmentation set (6,132 images), the assumption that species-level labels generalize to all images of a species, and the restriction to controlled museum backgrounds, leaving open how methods transfer to natural-habitat imagery. Future work on knowledge-guided ML incorporating phylogenetic structure and foundation models trained on this data could substantially advance trait discovery from images.
