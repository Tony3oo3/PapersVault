## Problem

Existing multi-task learning methods (both gradient-based and loss-based) focus exclusively on gradient aggregation in shared parameters while treating task-specific parameters as passive recipients of their own task's gradient. The paper identifies that task-specific parameters actively influence the gradients propagated back to shared parameters, and that ignoring this influence creates an inconsistent optimization that fails to fully mitigate gradient conflicts.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250760/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method is evaluated across four benchmark families: pixel-level scene understanding on NYUv2 (RGB images → segmentation mask, depth map, surface normal) and Cityscapes (RGB images → segmentation mask, depth map), image-level classification on CelebA (RGB images → 40 binary attribute labels), and molecular property regression on QM9 (molecular graphs → 11 scalar property values). For image tasks, the backbone is MTAN with task-specific heads; for QM9, a message-passing neural network with a single linear layer per task. A key implementation detail is that the method additionally computes gradients with respect to the shared intermediate representation z (not the full shared parameters), keeping the extra backpropagation confined to the small task-specific heads.

## Method

ConsMTL formulates MTL as a bi-level optimization solved alternately. In the upper level, it finds a joint update vector d within a ball of radius ε by minimizing the mean of inverse per-task utilities (1/∇_{θ_s}ℓ_i^T d), which is solved analytically via Lagrange multipliers to yield a constrained nonlinear least-squares problem (G^T G α = √(1⊘α)) solved by scipy. In the lower level, each task-specific parameter θ_i receives an extra alignment loss L_i^extra = −λ⟨∇_z ℓ_i, d_z⟩ where d_z = Σ α_i ∇_z ℓ_i is the joint gradient projected onto the shared representation z; this loss pushes each task's gradient toward the joint update direction, making θ_i aware of cross-task conflicts. The critical design choice is aligning ∇_z ℓ_i rather than ∇_{θ_s}ℓ_i, which avoids including the large shared parameter block in the computation graph and keeps memory overhead negligible even with 40 tasks.

## Results

On NYUv2 (3-task), ConsMTL achieves the best Mean Rank of 2.78 and Δm% of −6.72%, and is the only method to surpass the Single-Task Learning (STL) baseline across all three tasks and nine evaluation metrics. On Cityscapes (2-task), ConsMTL is the only method with a negative Δm% (−0.59%), indicating overall improvement over STL. On CelebA (40-task), ConsMTL achieves MR=3.40 and Δm%=−1.42%, the only method to outperform STL on average. On the challenging QM9 (11-task) regression benchmark, ConsMTL reduces the average performance drop to 23.2% (MR=2.00), a >30% reduction compared to the next-best method (Nash-MTL at 62.0%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ConsMTL outperforms all 13 baselines on both MR and Δm% across all four benchmarks. On NYUv2, the closest competitor is FairGrad [3] (MR=3.67, Δm%=−4.66%), while ConsMTL achieves MR=2.78 and Δm%=−6.72%; notably, ConsMTL is the only method to beat STL on surface normal prediction (70.31% within 30° vs. STL's 69.15%). On Cityscapes, Aligned-MTL [33] achieves the best MR (2.00) but still shows a positive Δm% of 5.27%, whereas ConsMTL is the sole method with a negative Δm% (−0.59%), indicating net improvement over single-task training. On CelebA (40-task), ConsMTL (MR=3.40, Δm%=−1.42%) substantially outperforms the next-best FairGrad (MR=6.67, Δm%=0.37%) and IMTL-G (MR=5.80, Δm%=0.84%). On QM9, where gradient conflicts are most severe due to varying task scales, ConsMTL (MR=2.00, Δm%=23.2%) reduces the average performance drop by over 30% relative to Nash-MTL (MR=4.55, Δm%=62.0%) and FairGrad (MR=4.91, Δm%=57.9%), and surpasses STL on 5 out of 11 tasks—something no other baseline achieves.

## Relation to Prior Work

ConsMTL extends the gradient-based MTL lineage (PCGrad, CAGrad, Nash-MTL, FairGrad) by identifying a gap these methods share: they aggregate gradients for shared parameters while leaving task-specific parameters to receive only their own task's gradient, creating an inconsistent optimization. The upper-level optimization (minimizing mean inverse utility via Lagrange multipliers) is closely related to FairGrad and Nash-MTL, but the lower-level optimization—introducing an extra loss on task-specific parameters to align backpropagated gradients toward the joint update vector—is the key departure. The paper also contrasts with loss-based methods (DWA, UW) and the one prior work (IMTL) that considered task-specific parameters, arguing that IMTL still does not use them to mitigate gradient conflicts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The observation that task-specific parameters shape the gradients reaching shared parameters—and can therefore be leveraged to reduce conflicts—reframes how MTL architectures should be optimized, potentially influencing future designs of shared/task-specific parameter splits. However, the method requires per-task tuning of the hyperparameter λ (with no unified criterion), incurs additional memory and compute for the extra gradient through the shared representation, and the authors acknowledge that adaptive λ scheduling remains open. The practical impact is moderate: the method is a drop-in replacement within the MTAN architecture and shows consistent gains, but the overhead and tuning burden limit immediate adoption in very large-scale or multi-tenant settings.
