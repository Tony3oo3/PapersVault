## Problem

Existing drag-based trajectory control methods for image-to-video synthesis operate exclusively in 2D image space, creating fundamental ambiguity for out-of-plane movements such as occlusion ordering and depth changes (e.g., whether an object passes in front of or behind another). This limits the expressiveness of user control and prevents generation of physically plausible 3D motions like orbiting or forward/backward movement relative to the camera.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252798/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image, user-drawn sparse 2D trajectories with per-point relative depth values, and SAM-extracted object mask selections as input. At inference, the sparse user trajectories are expanded into dense multi-point control signals via 3D back-projection, translation, and re-projection, then encoded as Gaussian heatmaps concatenated with instance and depth point channels. The output is a 16-frame video clip at 288×512 resolution generated by the fine-tuned SVD model.

## Method

LeviTor represents 3D trajectory control by applying K-means clustering to object mask pixels to obtain a small set of representative 2D control points, then sampling relative depth at each point using DepthAnythingV2, forming (x, y, d) tuples that implicitly encode 3D motion, occlusion, and scale changes without explicit 3D trajectory estimation. The number of clusters is adaptive: proportional to mask area ratio, with a minimum of 3 enforced when the object's area varies by more than 10× across frames (indicating occlusion or depth change), capped at 8. Training fine-tunes Stable Video Diffusion with a ControlNet branch conditioned on the concatenated control signal (Gaussian heatmap of 2D trajectories + instance points + depth points), using the SA-V dataset (51K videos, 643K masks) for 200K iterations. At inference, sparse user trajectories are converted to dense multi-point controls by back-projecting mask pixels into 3D camera coordinates, applying the user-specified translation, re-projecting to 2D via PyTorch3D rendering, and re-clustering the rendered masks with K-means to produce the final control signal fed to the diffusion model.

## Results

On the DAVIS benchmark, LeviTor achieves the best FID (25.41) and FVD (190.44) under the Multi-Points setting, substantially outperforming DragAnything (FID 36.04, FVD 324.95) and DragNUWA1.5 (FID 42.34, FVD 299.96). Under the Single-Point setting, LeviTor also leads in FID (28.79) and FVD (226.45). However, LeviTor trails DragNUWA1.5 on the ObjMC trajectory-adherence metric (25.97 vs. 23.12 in Multi-Points; 37.39 vs. 33.03 in Single-Point), which the authors attribute to not using explicit tracking to enforce exact point-trajectory matching.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LeviTor is compared against two 2D-trajectory-based methods: DragAnything [51] and DragNUWA1.5 [58]. On video/image quality (FID and FVD), LeviTor clearly wins in both Single-Point and Multi-Points settings, with FVD improvements of roughly 41% over DragAnything and 37% over DragNUWA in the Multi-Points configuration. On trajectory adherence (ObjMC), DragNUWA1.5 outperforms LeviTor (23.12 vs. 25.97 in Multi-Points), which the authors explain by noting that LeviTor does not use a tracking model to enforce exact point-trajectory matching, whereas DragNUWA's dense flow-space encoding more tightly constrains point positions. DragAnything shows the weakest trajectory control (ObjMC 38.86) and does not benefit from additional control points because it was trained with a single trajectory per mask. Qualitatively, LeviTor uniquely handles occlusion control, forward/backward depth movement, and complex orbiting motions that the 2D-only baselines cannot produce.

## Relation to Prior Work

LeviTor extends the trajectory-controlled video generation line (DragNUWA, DragAnything, MotionCtrl, TrackGo) by adding a depth dimension to the control signal, moving from 2D image-space trajectories to 3D trajectories. Architecturally, it fine-tunes Stable Video Diffusion via a ControlNet branch, injecting a compact control signal composed of K-means clustered mask points, their instance labels, and relative depth values estimated by DepthAnythingV2. The key conceptual departure from prior work is that occlusion and depth changes are implicitly encoded through the aggregation/separation of clustered points and their depth, eliminating the need for explicit 3D trajectory estimation or dense per-frame control signals.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first method to enable 3D trajectory control in image-to-video synthesis, resolving a fundamental ambiguity in 2D trajectory methods (e.g., whether an object passes in front of or behind another). The user-friendly inference pipeline—drawing 2D paths and adjusting point depths—lowers the barrier for non-expert users to specify 3D motion. However, the approach is constrained by SAM segmentation quality, cannot model internal pose changes or non-rigid deformations, and is limited by the SVD base model's generation quality. Open questions include whether the K-means-plus-depth representation generalizes to deformable objects and whether stronger base models (e.g., CogVideoX, newer DiT-based video models) can better exploit this control signal.
