## Problem

Existing backdoor attacks on multi-modal large language models (MLLMs) are limited to specific tasks and datasets, and they generate fixed output sequences or randomly insert target sentences regardless of image content, producing predictable patterns that are easy for users to detect. This paper addresses the gap by proposing the first token-level backdoor attack that makes minimal, context-aware modifications to the model's output, achieving both high attack success rates and strong stealthiness across diverse tasks.

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image with a small trigger pattern (e.g., a 30×30 logo in the lower-right corner) and a free-form text instruction (captioning prompt or VQA question) as input. The output is a text token sequence that is nearly identical to the clean model's output except for a targeted token-level modification: either a specific source token is replaced with a target token (Token-substitution) or a short malicious token sequence is appended at a fixed position (Token-addition). The key constraint is that when the source token is absent from the clean output, the backdoored model behaves identically to the clean model.

## Method

BadToken formulates backdoor injection as a three-loss optimization problem: an effectiveness loss that maximizes the likelihood of the desired token-level behavior on trigger-embedded shadow samples, a utility loss that preserves clean-input performance, and an embedding loss that constrains the vision encoder and modality projector to remain aligned with frozen teacher copies via cosine similarity. Shadow datasets are crafted from open-source image-text pairs, with positive sets containing the source token and negative sets preventing overfitting to the target token. Training uses LoRA with all parameters (including the vision encoder) unfrozen, which is critical for achieving high ASR (unfreezing the encoder raises Token-substitution ASR from 26% to 98%). The total loss is α·L_bd + (1−α)·L_cl + β·L_emb, with α=0.5 and β=1 as defaults, balancing attack effectiveness against model utility preservation.

## Results

On LLaVA-v1.5-7B, BadToken achieves an average attack success rate (ASR) of 97.4% for Token-substitution and 99.6% for Token-addition on the image caption task (MSCOCO), while maintaining backdoored performance (BP) within ~1 BLEU@4 point of the clean model (e.g., 5.63/31.56 vs. 5.97/31.62 for Token-substitution). On the VQA task (VQAv2), Token-substitution reaches 65.2% average ASR and Token-addition 28.2%, with the lower VQA ASR attributed to shorter output sequences. Clean Attack Success Rate (ASR-C) remains at 0–4%, confirming that the backdoor is not activated on clean inputs, and Attack Token Similarity (ATS) stays above 0.73, indicating high stealth.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BadToken substantially outperforms all four baselines on attack success rate. For Token-substitution, BadToken achieves 98% ASR versus 42% (BadNets), 8% (BadEncoder), 52% (CBA), and 56% (Anydoor); for Token-addition, it reaches 100% ASR versus 56%, 0%, 43%, and 62% respectively. BadEncoder, designed for vision-encoder backdoors, is essentially ineffective here (8% and 0% ASR). Anydoor achieves the second-highest ASR (56%/62%) but at a severe stealth cost: its ATS drops to 0.5599 and 0.3629, far below BadToken's 0.7613 and 0.8234, because Anydoor generates a fixed output sequence regardless of input content. BadNets and CBA offer moderate ASR with reasonable ATS but fall 40–50 points short of BadToken. In terms of model utility (BP), all methods are comparable, with BadToken's 5.63/31.56 (Token Sub) and 3.41/29.29 (Token Add) within the range of the other methods, confirming that the ASR advantage is not bought at the expense of clean performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the MLLM backdoor attack line (VLTrojan, Anydoor, physical-trigger attacks) by shifting from fixed-sequence or random-insertion targets to token-level modifications (substitution and addition), which the authors frame as the first such formulation. Conceptually, it repurposes the standard backdoor training paradigm (trigger + shadow dataset + loss optimization) but introduces a three-term loss (effectiveness, utility, embedding) that explicitly preserves modality alignment while embedding the backdoor. The use of LoRA and unfrozen vision encoder parameters for parameter-efficient backdoor injection is a practical departure from prior MLLM backdoor work that typically only fine-tunes the LLM or projector.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As MLLMs are increasingly deployed plug-and-play in safety-critical domains (autonomous driving, medical diagnosis), the demonstration that token-level backdoors can achieve >95% ASR while remaining nearly undetectable (high ATS, low ASR-C) raises urgent security concerns. The finding that both fine-tuning and zero-shot image purification fail to neutralize the attack—particularly that patch triggers retain 100% ASR after purification—suggests current defense toolkits are inadequate for this threat class. Open questions include whether the approach transfers to larger or closed-source MLLMs, how it interacts with multi-turn conversations, and whether detection methods can be designed specifically for token-level anomalies rather than fixed-pattern triggers.
