## Problem

Latent-based generative inpainting models (diffusion and rectified flow) suffer from two persistent issues: unwanted object insertion, where the model hallucinates random objects in masked regions due to its random-masking training strategy, and color inconsistency, where inpainted regions exhibit brightness/saturation/hue shifts relative to unmasked regions, producing smear-like artifacts. Retraining these models is prohibitively expensive due to their multi-stage training pipeline (VAE, generator, inpainting fine-tuning), motivating a post-processing solution that keeps the generative backbone frozen.

## Input / Output

The method takes a masked RGB image (512² or 1024²) and a binary mask indicating the missing region, with no text prompt required. The MAE prior is computed from the unmasked region only and replaces the text condition in the frozen generative model. The output is a full inpainted RGB image where the masked region is filled while the unmasked region is preserved exactly. The specialized decoder additionally consumes the original masked image in pixel space and the 0-1 mask as conditioning inputs during latent-to-image decoding.

## Method

ASUKA comprises two post-training components applied to frozen latent inpainting models (SD 1.5, FLUX). First, a fine-tuned MAE (trained with a systematic masking strategy covering object-shape, irregular, and regular masks at ratios 0.1–0.75) produces a context-stable prior for the masked region; a lightweight alignment module (linear projection + 4 self-attention blocks) maps this prior into the generative model's condition space, trained with the standard denoising objective while all other modules remain frozen. Second, the VAE decoder is re-formulated as a local harmonization task: it takes the generated latent plus the ground-truth unmasked pixels and the binary mask as additional inputs, and is trained with color augmentation (random brightness/contrast/saturation/hue shifts on the full image) and latent augmentation (one-step denoising from clean latent with a zero mask to simulate the generation gap) to explicitly recover the original image conditioned on the unmasked region, thereby eliminating mask-unmask color shifts.

## Results

On MISATO, ASUKA-SD achieves the best FID (11.495), U-IDS (0.423), P-IDS (0.312), CLIP@mask (0.958), and Gradient@edge (47.753) among all SD-based methods, outperforming the base SD model (FID 12.812, U-IDS 0.345, P-IDS 0.211) by substantial margins. On Places 2, ASUKA-SD similarly leads with FID 1.230, U-IDS 0.413, P-IDS 0.287, and G@e 147.733. ASUKA-FLUX extends these gains to the rectified-flow architecture, improving FLUX's FID from 12.839 to 11.372 and G@e from 65.928 to 48.635 on MISATO@512. Notably, pixel-based GAN methods (LaMa, MAE-FAR) still achieve lower G@e values (43–46), suggesting latent-space compression remains a source of residual color shift.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ASUKA-SD consistently outperforms all SD-based variants (SD null-prompt, SD-text, SD-token, SD-IP, SD-T2I, SD-CAEv2, SD-LaMa) across every metric on both MISATO and Places 2, with the largest gains in P-IDS (0.312 vs. 0.211 for base SD) and G@e (47.753 vs. 63.844), confirming that the MAE prior plus harmonization decoder jointly reduce both object hallucination and color shift. Against GAN-based inpainting methods (Co-Mod, MAT, LaMa, MAE-FAR), ASUKA-SD achieves substantially better FID (11.495 vs. 13.283–17.421), U-IDS, and P-IDS, but GAN methods still lead on G@e (43.6–52.1 vs. 47.8), indicating that pixel-space models inherently avoid the latent-space color drift. SD-Repaint, the only other diffusion-based baseline, is clearly inferior to ASUKA-SD on all metrics (FID 27.861 vs. 11.495 on MISATO). The extension to FLUX (Table 2) shows the same pattern: ASUKA-FLUX improves FID from 12.839 to 11.372 and G@e from 65.928 to 48.635, validating that the post-processing framework generalizes across diffusion and rectified-flow architectures. Among the alternative MAE-conditioning strategies tested (IP-Adapter, T2I-Adapter, CAEv2), the paper's simple self-attention alignment module yields the best results, with CAEv2 performing worst (FID 29.179), suggesting that CLIP-style alignment is poorly suited for this task.

## Relation to Prior Work

ASUKA positions itself as a post-processing framework for frozen latent generative inpainting models (SD v1.5, FLUX), deliberately avoiding the costly three-stage retraining (VAE → U-Net → inpainting fine-tuning) that prior work requires. It repurposes MAE—a self-supervised reconstruction model known for stable but blurry outputs—as a context-stable prior that replaces the text condition, drawing on the MIM literature but applying it to guide diffusion/flow generation rather than for representation learning. The color-consistency component reframes VAE decoding as a local harmonization task, extending prior decoder improvements (OpenAI's consistency decoder, Zhu et al.'s conditional decoder) by explicitly training with color and latent augmentation to simulate the two distinct sources of color shift. Unlike adapter-based approaches (IP-Adapter, T2I-Adapter, CAEv2) that inject image conditions into the generative model, ASUKA's alignment module is trained with the standard generation objective, keeping the generative backbone fully frozen.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that ASUKA offers a plug-and-play upgrade path for existing latent inpainting models without retraining the expensive generative backbone, making it accessible to practitioners who already have SD or FLUX pipelines. The introduction of MISATO (spanning indoor, outdoor, building, and background domains) and the two targeted metrics (CLIP@mask for hallucination, Gradient@edge for color shift) fill a gap in evaluation that prior benchmarks like Places2 alone cannot address. However, the approach remains constrained by the frozen U-Net (LPIPS is not best-in-class), is validated only on two architectures, and the color-consistency improvement, while meaningful, does not fully close the gap to pixel-space GANs on gradient metrics—suggesting the fundamental information loss in latent compression remains an open challenge for high-fidelity inpainting.
