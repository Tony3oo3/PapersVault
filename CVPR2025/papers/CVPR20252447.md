## Problem

Modern quantum annealers such as D-Wave are restricted to Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimisation (QUBO) problems, yet many computer vision tasks—mesh alignment, point set registration, quadratic assignment—require non-quadratic or composite objective functions over binary variables. This restriction prevents direct application of adiabatic quantum computing to a broad class of practically important problems. The paper asks how to extend the scope of AQC to composite, binary-parametrised, possibly non-quadratic objectives without resorting to auxiliary variables or degree-reduction techniques that inflate problem size.

## Input / Output

For QAP, the method takes two symmetric n×n matrices A and B and outputs a permutation matrix P (n ≤ 50 in classical experiments, n ≤ 15 on D-Wave). For shape matching, it takes a pair of 3D meshes with 502 vertices each (FAUST dataset) and outputs vertex correspondences. For point set registration, it takes a reference set X and template set Y of n points (20–40) in 2D or 3D and jointly outputs a rotation matrix R ∈ SO(d) and a permutation matrix P. Internally, all problems are encoded via k = n(n−1)/2 binary variables for the permutation (one per 2-cycle) plus m·k_r binary variables for the rotation parameter, where k_r = d(d−1)/2 and m controls discretisation resolution.

## Method

QuCOOP exploits the composite structure f(g(x)) where f is quadratic in g(x) and x ∈ {0,1}^k. At each iteration t, the inner function g is linearised around the current iterate x^t via a first-order Taylor expansion, producing a local model g^t(x) that is linear in x. Because f is quadratic in its argument, the composition f(g^t(x)) becomes a quadratic function of the binary variable x, i.e., a standard QUBO that can be sampled directly on a quantum annealer or solved with simulated annealing. The next iterate x^{t+1} is the binary vector minimising this QUBO, and the process repeats until convergence. Constraint satisfaction (e.g., permutation-matrix row/column sums, orthonormality of R) is enforced through quadratic penalty terms added to the QUBO objective, with penalty factors tuned by grid search. The permutation matrix is parametrised by 2-cycle indicators, and the rotation by an exponential map from the Lie algebra with m-bit discretisation of the rotation parameters.

## Results

On the QAPLIB benchmark (72 instances, n≤50), QuCOOP consistently outperforms all classical (FAQ, 2-OPT) and quantum (Q-Match) competitors in percentage energy error, with the smallest confidence intervals, achieving roughly a 5% improvement over Q-Match. On FAUST shape matching (502-vertex meshes), QuCOOP reduces the mean geodesic error by approximately 0.01 compared to Q-Match under the Princeton cumulative geodesic error protocol. On D-Wave Advantage 6.4, QuCOOP produces valid permutation matrices up to n≈12, substantially exceeding the n=3–4 limit of prior quantum-compatible approaches, and registers point sets without correspondences up to n=5.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against FAQ [75] and 2-OPT [19] on QAPLIB, QuCOOP achieves lower percentage energy error across all 72 instances (n≤50), though at higher runtime because it solves larger sub-problems in fewer iterations (~5 for n=50 vs. ~800 for Q-Match). Against Q-Match [6], the closest AQC-compatible competitor, QuCOOP wins on QAP by approximately 5% in energy error and on FAUST shape matching by reducing mean geodesic error by ~0.01, while matching Q-Match's D-Wave validity up to n≈12 but with lower objective values and ~5× less runtime. Against CPD [53] on 2D/3D point set registration, QuCOOP matches accuracy on isomorphic shapes and outperforms CPD on non-isomorphic sets where CPD degrades, though both methods struggle beyond 90° (2D) or 45° (3D) rotation angles. On D-Wave hardware, QuCOOP extends the solvable permutation problem size from n=3–4 (prior work [5,77]) to n≈12, representing a clear improvement in the dimensionality of problems addressable by quantum annealers.

## Relation to Prior Work

QuCOOP generalises the iterative quantum transformation (IQT) approach of Kuete Meli et al. [49], which linearised rotation matrices around current iterates to produce QUBOs solvable on quantum annealers, into a broader composite-optimisation framework where any smooth inner function g can be linearised at each step. It also draws on classical composite (prox-linear) optimisation theory [22, 58] but adapts it to the discrete binary setting where the outer function f is quadratic in g(x). Unlike prior quantum-enhanced CV methods (Q-Match [6], quantum permutation synchronisation [9]) that require hand-crafting a bespoke QUBO for each problem, QuCOOP provides a single iterative template that automatically generates the QUBO sub-problems, and unlike quadratisation techniques for pseudo-Boolean functions it introduces no auxiliary variables.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework opens a new class of non-quadratic, composite objectives to adiabatic quantum computing, reducing the burden of manually deriving QUBO formulations for each new CV problem. However, practical impact is currently constrained: the permutation parametrisation scales quadratically in n, D-Wave's Pegasus topology limits solvable problems to roughly 180 logical variables (n≈15), and the first-order linearisation of g can be inaccurate for highly non-linear inner functions. Open questions include whether the framework extends to higher-order or non-quadratic outer functions, whether it benefits from future quantum hardware with larger fully-connected topologies, and whether the modest numerical gains over specialised methods (5% on QAP, 0.01 geodesic error on FAUST) widen as problem sizes grow.
