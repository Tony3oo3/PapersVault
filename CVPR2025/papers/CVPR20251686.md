## Problem

Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) under the L∞ ball is the de facto baseline for adversarial robustness evaluation, but it requires T ≥ 1,000 iterations per image, making a single ImageNet test-set evaluation as expensive as 100 training epochs. For robust images where no adversarial example exists, all iterations are wasted because standard implementations have no mechanism to detect that the attack will never succeed. This paper asks whether the deterministic, fixed-step-size nature of PGD can be exploited to provably terminate the attack early without sacrificing any attack strength.

## Input / Output

The method takes a differentiable model f, a preprocessed RGB image X, its true label y, an L∞ radius ε (8/255 for CIFAR, 4/255 for ImageNet), a fixed step size α (typically ε/4), an iteration budget T_iter, and a loss function L. It outputs a binary robustness verdict (True if the model is misclassified, False otherwise) along with the adversarial perturbation δ found. In practice, each perturbation is hashed on the CPU via torch.frexp to avoid transferring full tensors from GPU, storing only a small tuple of floats per iteration for cycle lookup.

## Method

PGD_CD augments standard fixed-step-size PGD with two simple checks at each iteration: (1) if the model is already misclassified, return immediately, and (2) if the current perturbation δ^(i) has been seen before (detected via exact hash matching), terminate and return False, since the deterministic update rule guarantees the trajectory will cycle forever without causing misclassification. The key insight is that under the L∞ ball, the fixed step size combined with projection onto the ball boundary creates discrete oscillation patterns (often cycles of length 2 or 4) when no adversarial example exists within the feasible region. This is provably correct because PGD with a fixed step size and no momentum is a deterministic function of the current perturbation, so revisiting any point implies an infinite loop. The method achieves 40–96% iteration reduction across 12 RobustBench models while producing identical robust accuracy to standard PGD, and can be extended with random restarts upon cycle detection to further improve attack strength within a fixed compute budget.

## Results

PGD_CD achieves identical robust accuracy to standard PGD across all 12 evaluated models (e.g., 58.15% on ConvNeXt-L ImageNet, 62.01% on Carmon2019 CIFAR10) while reducing total PGD iterations by 42–96%. On ImageNet models, the median iteration reduction is ~90% (e.g., 29.1M → 2.7M iterations for ConvNeXt-L), and on CIFAR10 models reductions reach up to 96.4% (R18_ddpm: 5.97M → 216K iterations). The worst-case reduction is 41.7% (ViT-B-ConvStem, ImageNet), and two CIFAR100 models (Improving_edm, Improving_trades) show negligible savings (<2%), confirming the method never degrades performance. Tricked images require a median of only 3 PGD iterations, meaning the early success check alone saves substantial compute even without cycle detection.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against standard PGD (Madry et al. 2017), PGD_CD produces bit-identical robust accuracy on every model tested, confirming zero loss in attack strength, while reducing total iterations by 42–96% depending on the model and dataset. The savings are largest on high-dimensional ImageNet models (~90% reduction, ≈10× speedup) and on CIFAR10 models (up to 96.4%), but two CIFAR100 models (Improving_edm, Improving_trades) show <2% savings, representing the method's failure case where no cycle forms within the iteration budget. Against Auto-PGD (Croce et al. 2020), PGD_CD matches or exceeds APGD's robust accuracy on 4 of 6 CIFAR models (e.g., 38.93% vs 38.46% on XCiT-L12 CIFAR100; 59.61% vs 58.38% on R18_ddpm CIFAR10) while using 10–15× fewer iterations on untricked images (e.g., 160K vs 1.9M for XCiT-L12). APGD wins marginally on two models (Improving_edm: 56.48% vs 45.02%; Improving_trades: 51.01% vs 40.30%), but even there PGD_CD uses fewer iterations. The paper also shows that PGD_CD with random restarts upon cycle detection (PGD_CD^jumps) provides a stronger baseline than the 500-iteration/5-restart PGD that APGD was originally compared against, suggesting the original APGD-vs-PGD comparison was not entirely fair.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the standard PGD attack (Madry et al., 2017) by adding a cycle-detection mechanism that exploits the deterministic, fixed-step-size dynamics of the algorithm to terminate early when iterates revisit a previously seen perturbation. Conceptually, it inverts the motivation of Auto-PGD (Croce & Hein, 2020), which introduced adaptive step sizes and momentum specifically to avoid the cycling behavior that this paper identifies as a useful signal for early termination. The work also builds on the RobustBench evaluation framework and the broader adversarial robustness evaluation literature, positioning PGD as a still-competitive baseline whose computational cost is the primary barrier to widespread use.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 10–20× speedup (up to 96% iteration reduction) makes large-scale adversarial robustness evaluation—particularly on ImageNet with T=1000—practically feasible for the first time, removing a major computational bottleneck in the field. The finding that fixed-step PGD is often competitive with or stronger than APGD challenges the assumption that adaptive step sizes are necessary for strong attacks, and the cycle-detection trick enables random-restart strategies that were previously too expensive to deploy. Limitations include minimal gains on some CIFAR100 models (the 'failure' case) and the method's reliance on exact floating-point equality for cycle detection, which may not generalize to all numerical settings. Open questions include whether analogous early-termination signals exist for other norms or for attacks with momentum/adaptive steps.
