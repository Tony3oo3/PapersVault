## Problem

Active test-time adaptation (ATTA) methods reduce error accumulation in long-term TTA by labeling a small subset of samples per batch, but the annotation burden grows linearly with the number of batches, making real-world deployment impractical. The paper asks how to achieve 'effortless' active labeling—annotating at most one sample per batch (or even per multi-batches)—while still selecting samples that are both informative and learnable in a single optimization step.

## Input / Output

The method consumes online RGB image batches (batch size 64) from the target domain and a pretrained source model (ResNet-50 with BatchNorm/GroupNorm, ViT-B-16, or ResNet-18), with no access to source-domain data. At most one ground-truth label per batch (or per 3–5 batches) is provided by a human or a large model (ViT-L-16). The output is the updated subset of trainable parameters Θ̃ and per-sample class predictions, evaluated under both continual (CTTA) and fully (FTTA) test-time adaptation settings across 15 corruption types and real-world distribution shifts.

## Method

EATTA has two core components. First, for sample selection, it identifies the most valuable sample by measuring sensitivity to feature perturbation: it computes each sample's pseudo-label from its original features, adds small Gaussian noise to the features, and selects the sample whose prediction confidence on the original pseudo-label changes the most—indicating it lies at the border between source and target distributions and is thus both informative and learnable in one step. A class-balancing strategy avoids repeatedly annotating the same categories. Second, for model adaptation, it addresses the large gradient-magnitude imbalance between the supervised cross-entropy loss (on the single annotated sample, whose pseudo-label may be wrong) and the unsupervised entropy loss (on confident pseudo-labeled samples). It computes dynamic weights γ₁ and γ₂ proportional to the inverse ratio of the two gradient norms, then smooths them via exponential moving average to ensure stable long-term adaptation without overfitting to the single labeled sample.

## Results

On ImageNet-C (severity 5) under the CTTA setting, EATTA with one annotation per batch (Ours*) achieves 53.8% average error, outperforming SimATTA (54.8%) and HILTTA (53.7%) which both require three annotations per batch. Under the FTTA setting, Ours* reaches 52.4% average error, surpassing SimATTA (54.1%), CEMA (52.9%), and HILTTA (52.8%). With a sample buffer of size 300 and three annotations per batch, EATTA further reduces the average error to 50.9% (CTTA) and 50.7% (FTTA), the best among all compared methods. On real-world shift benchmarks (ImageNet-R/K/A, PACS), EATTA consistently achieves the lowest average error or highest average accuracy across all settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EATTA with one annotation per batch (Ours*) outperforms SimATTA [10] (3/batch, BFS=300) by 1.0% in CTTA and 1.7% in FTTA average error, while using one-third the annotations and no buffer. It also beats HILTTA [26] (3/batch) by 0.1% in CTTA and 0.4% in FTTA with fewer annotations. In the FTTA setting, Ours* surpasses CEMA [6] (3/batch, BFS=300) by 0.5% average error. Against non-active TTA baselines, EATTA (Ours*) reduces average error by 17.1% over TENT [43], 16.0% over CoTTA [47], 6.9% over SAR [32], and 1.5% over ETA [31] in CTTA. On real-world shifts (ImageNet-R/K/A), EATTA (Ours†, BFS=300) achieves the best average error of 45.7%, 57.9%, and 81.8%, outperforming SimATTA by 3.2%, 2.0%, and 3.1% respectively. On PACS, EATTA (Ours†, BFS=300) achieves the highest average accuracy (79.1%, 85.2%, 81.1%, 82.4% across four sequences in CTTA), exceeding SimATTA by 2.8–11.3% depending on the sequence.

## Relation to Prior Work

EATTA extends the active test-time adaptation (ATTA) framework introduced by SimATTA (Gui et al., ICLR 2024) and CEMA (Chen et al., ICLR 2024), which label a small proportion of samples per batch to combat error accumulation in long-term TTA. It departs from these methods in two key ways: (1) replacing entropy- or clustering-based sample selection with a feature-perturbation sensitivity criterion that targets samples at the source-target distribution boundary, and (2) introducing gradient-norm-based debiasing to balance the supervised and unsupervised loss terms, a problem prior ATTA methods overlooked. The approach also draws on classical active learning uncertainty metrics (max entropy, least confidence, min margin) and TTA self-training techniques (pseudo-labeling, entropy minimization) as baselines and building blocks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By reducing the annotation requirement to at most one sample per batch (or even per multi-batches), EATTA makes ATTA substantially more practical for real-world deployment where annotation cost scales with data volume. The finding that high-entropy samples—standard in active learning—are poorly suited for single-step TTA challenges a common assumption and redirects attention toward distribution-boundary samples. However, the method still depends on human or large-model annotators, introducing latency that the authors acknowledge as a limitation. Open questions include whether the perturbation-based selection generalizes to non-visual modalities, whether the gradient debiasing strategy transfers to other semi-supervised settings, and whether fully annotation-free alternatives can achieve comparable long-term stability.
