## Problem

Current text-to-image diffusion models (e.g., SD3, FLUX) cannot reliably interpret camera intrinsic settings such as focal length, aperture, shutter speed, or color temperature: changing a lens specification in the prompt alters the entire scene rather than producing a consistent photographic effect. This failure stems from two root causes—extremely limited paired training data across camera settings and the inability to disentangle scene embeddings from camera embeddings within the generative model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252143/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt describing an invariant scene plus a set of camera intrinsic parameters (focal length 24–70 mm, shutter speed normalized to 0.1–1.0, color temperature 2000–10000 K, bokeh blur 1–30) and produces a 5-frame sequence at 256×384 resolution, where each frame renders the same scene under a different camera setting. At inference, a single camera value can be replicated across all frames to yield a static image, and no depth map is required for bokeh rendering despite the model exhibiting depth-aware foreground/background separation.

## Method

The framework combines two complementary ideas. First, Dimensionality Lifting reformulates multi-camera-setting image generation as a text-to-video problem using AnimateDiff, so that different camera settings correspond to different frames and the model's spatiotemporal attention enforces scene consistency across them. Second, Differential Camera Intrinsics Learning operates at both the data and architecture levels: the training data consists of physically simulated frame sets (bokeh via depth-based rendering, focal length via center-crop-and-resize, shutter speed via simulated ISP, color temperature via blackbody-radiation channel adjustment) with randomly sampled continuous parameter values, while the Differential Camera Encoder injects camera settings through coarse physical-prior embeddings plus inter-frame CLIP feature differences computed between adjacent settings, feeding them into the frozen T2V backbone via a T2I-Adapter-style module. Only the motion LoRA and the differential camera encoder are trained (lr 1e-4, Adam, 25k epochs, ~10 h on one A100), keeping the base model largely frozen.

## Results

On the four camera-control tasks (bokeh, focal length, shutter speed, color temperature), the proposed method achieves Pearson correlation accuracy of 0.86–0.97 against physically simulated reference videos, substantially outperforming all baselines. Scene consistency (frame-wise LPIPS) ranges from 0.05 (color temperature) to 0.46 (focal length), far below SD3 (0.69–0.73) and FLUX (0.62–0.68). Prompt following (CLIP score) remains competitive at 0.29–0.30, on par with fine-tuned AnimateDiff and CameraCtrl, indicating that camera control does not degrade generative quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method decisively outperforms SD3 and FLUX on both accuracy (0.86–0.97 vs. 0.20–0.27) and scene consistency (LPIPS 0.05–0.46 vs. 0.62–0.73) across all four camera tasks, though SD3/FLUX do not report CLIP prompt-following scores. Against fine-tuned AnimateDiff, the gap is even larger: e.g., on focal length accuracy the proposed method reaches 0.970 versus 0.260 for AnimateDiff (w/ FT), and on shutter speed 0.926 vs. 0.220. CameraCtrl (w/ FT) is the strongest baseline, reaching 0.867 accuracy on focal length and 0.753 on shutter speed, but the proposed method still leads by 0.10 and 0.17 respectively, and achieves lower LPIPS on bokeh (0.079 vs. 0.116) and color temperature (0.050 vs. 0.065). On prompt following (CLIP), all video-based methods cluster tightly around 0.28–0.30, with the proposed method matching or slightly exceeding CameraCtrl (w/ FT) on three of four tasks, confirming that the added camera control does not sacrifice text-to-image alignment.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on AnimateDiff as its T2V backbone and adopts CameraCtrl's architectural recommendation to avoid ControlNet for information leakage, while structuring its embedding encoder similarly to T2I-Adapter. Conceptually, it departs from prior camera-control work (which has focused almost exclusively on external parameters like pose and trajectory) by targeting intrinsic settings—focal length, aperture, shutter speed, color temperature—and from prior disentanglement approaches (GAN latent editing, contrastive prompts) that were tailored to facial attributes rather than physical camera parameters. The key methodological departure is the 'dimensionality lifting' reframing: treating camera settings as an additional axis analogous to the temporal dimension in video, thereby converting a T2I consistency problem into a T2V frame-consistency problem that existing spatiotemporal attention mechanisms can handle.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that physically meaningful camera control is achievable with only ~1,000 base images per task, suggesting that carefully designed differential data plus architectural inductive biases can substitute for the massive paired datasets that would otherwise be required. This opens a practical path for professional photographers to use generative tools with real camera semantics, reducing post-processing burden. Limitations include the restriction to four intrinsic settings, a fixed 5-frame generation window, and evaluation on relatively simple scenes; extending to external camera parameters, longer sequences, and more complex multi-object scenes remains open. The framework's reliance on physically-based simulation for data construction also raises questions about generalization to effects that are harder to simulate (e.g., lens aberrations, motion blur).
