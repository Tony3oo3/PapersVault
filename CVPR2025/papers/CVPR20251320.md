## Problem

While Video-LLMs have advanced rapidly, it remains unclear whether they can perform compositional reasoning on short videos—specifically, associating agents with their actions across multiple events over time. Existing video-language entailment benchmarks use a relative-scoring metric (ClassicVLE) that masks a model's inability to reject misaligned captions, and no prior benchmark disentangles agent understanding, action understanding, and multi-event association in a controlled test suite.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251320/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each test sample pairs a 10-second movie clip (sampled at 1 fps, yielding 10 frames) with a text caption derived from semantic role label annotations of a single 2-second event within that clip. Because the caption describes only one event but is paired with the full 10-second video, the model must implicitly localize the relevant temporal region. The model outputs an independent binary 'Yes'/'No' entailment judgment for each caption (positive and negative), rather than selecting between them.

## Method

VELOCITI introduces seven tests organized along two axes: the reasoning facet (agent, action, multi-event) and the negation strategy (text-inspired negation, where the replaced entity is absent from the video, versus in-video negation, where the replaced entity appears elsewhere in the same clip). The key methodological contribution is StrictVLE, which requires the model to correctly classify the positive caption as 'Yes' AND the negative caption as 'No' (both conditions must hold), rather than merely requiring the positive entailment score to exceed the negative as in ClassicVLE. Captions are generated by prompting LLaMA-3 to convert structured SRL dictionaries into fluent descriptions, and all 3,101 video-caption pairs are human-verified. The benchmark is validated to require both visual input (blind models drop to ~4–8%) and multiple frames (single-frame input halves accuracy), confirming it cannot be solved by text-only or image-only models.

## Results

On VELOCITI using the proposed StrictVLE metric (random = 25%), the best-performing model Gemini-1.5-Pro achieves only 49.3% average accuracy, followed by GPT-4o (46.2%) and LLaVA-OneVision-72B (44.5% on subset, 43.1% full), all far below human performance at 93.0%. Older open models collapse: P-LLaVA scores 0.0% and Owl-Con 1.7%, while mid-tier models like V-LLaVA (7.3%) and QVL-7B (15.9%) remain near random. Action understanding tests (ActAdv, ActMan, ActBind) are consistently harder than agent understanding tests (AgRand, AgBind, AgCref) for open models, and in-video negation tests outperform text-inspired negation by ~5.5% for GPT-4o.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 11 models (5 open, 3 closed, plus language-only and contrastive baselines) on VELOCITI. Among open Video-LLMs, LLaVA-OneVision-72B (43.1% avg) substantially outperforms QVL-7B (15.9%), V-LLaVA (7.3%), Owl-Con (1.7%), and P-LLaVA (0.0%), with the gap widening on multi-event tests (AgCref: 38.6% vs. 0.0–6.5%). Among closed models, Gemini-1.5-Pro (49.3%) edges out GPT-4o (46.2%) and Gemini-1.5-Flash (23.9%), though GPT-4o shows the most balanced agent-vs-action performance (50.3% vs. 46.7%). On the ClassicVLE metric (random = 50%), the language-only VERA baseline scores 58.3% and contrastive models (SigLIP 58.2%, ViFi-C 61.2%, Neg-C 59.2%) remain close to random, while OV-72B reaches 83.6%—yet still misclassifies every sixth sample. The paper further shows that multiple-choice evaluation inflates scores (OV-72B: 76.0% A∧B vs. 43.1% StrictVLE) and reveals a persistent choice bias (up to 67.8% for OV-7B), motivating the StrictVLE protocol. All models, including the strongest, remain 40+ points below human accuracy (93.0%), confirming that compositional video-language reasoning is far from solved.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the video-language entailment (VLE) setup introduced by VideoCon [4] and the entailment scoring mechanism from Sanyal et al. [40], but departs from prior work in three key ways: (1) it proposes StrictVLE, which requires independent correct classification of both positive and negative captions rather than merely a relative ordering; (2) it introduces in-video negation, where negative captions are constructed by swapping entities with others appearing in the same video, a strategy absent from prior benchmarks like TestOfTime, VITATECS, and Vinoground; and (3) it leverages structured semantic role label (SRL) annotations from VidSitu to create a disentangled test suite targeting agents, actions, and multi-event associations specifically, rather than holistic video understanding.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a stark gap between the best Video-LLM (Gemini-1.5-Pro at 49.3%) and human performance (93.0%) on 10-second movie clips, suggesting that compositional reasoning over short videos remains fundamentally unsolved and is a prerequisite for long-video understanding. The finding that ClassicVLE masks model failures (models achieve ~84% ClassicVLE but only ~43% StrictVLE) has methodological implications for how the community evaluates video-language alignment. Open questions include whether scaling model size or training data will close the gap, whether in-video negation can be addressed through better temporal grounding, and whether the action-understanding deficit (worse than agent understanding) reflects a specific architectural limitation in current Video-LLMs.
