## Problem

Current video benchmarks suffer from representation biases—particularly object bias and single-frame bias—where recognizing objects or using a single frame suffices for correct prediction, inadvertently rewarding models with strong object recognition over true video understanding. While debiased datasets exist, they have rarely been adopted for benchmarking due to artifacts from video modification or out-of-domain concerns, and no scalable automated method exists to systematically analyze and mitigate these biases across existing popular benchmarks.

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes 8 uniformly sampled frames per video along with class labels or captions, and produces per-frame structured textual descriptions (objects, activities, verbs, objects+composition+activities) aggregated into four temporal configurations (middle-frame, max-score frame, average-over-frames, sequence-of-frames). The final outputs are bias scores per concept-temporal configuration, object-debiased test splits (UTD-splits) where samples are removed only when all nine consensus models (3 embedding prompts × 3 bootstrapped linear classifiers) agree a sample is predictable from objects alone, and balanced variants that preserve original class proportions.

## Method

UTD uses textual descriptions as an intermediate video representation to precisely control which concepts are encoded, avoiding the information leakage and artifacts of pixel-level cropping or inpainting. The pipeline first generates detailed frame descriptions with a VLM (LLaVA-1.6-Mistral-7B), then uses an LLM (Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2) with few-shot prompts to extract specific concept categories (objects, activities, verbs). Bias is measured along three independent dimensions—concept, temporal, and common-sense vs. dataset—by encoding descriptions and labels with a text embedding model (SFR-Embedding-Mistral) for zero-shot common-sense bias, and training a linear logistic regression on the dataset's training split for dataset bias. The key advantage over prior debiasing approaches is that no new videos are generated and no fine-tuning is needed to compensate for domain shift, making the method directly applicable to standard evaluation protocols.

## Results

On action classification, debiasing via UTD-splits causes performance drops of 1–25 pp depending on the model: VideoMAE2-B (K710) drops only 0.9 pp on UCF101 (99.0→98.1) and 13.1 pp on K400 (85.0→71.9), while All-in-one-B drops 21.4 pp on UCF101 (84.5→63.1) and 24.3 pp on K400 (66.9→42.6). On text-to-video retrieval, drops range from 5.2 pp (InternVid-L-10M on ActivityNet) to 13.4 pp (InternVid-B-200M on MSRVTT). The analysis reveals that 73% of UCF101, 55% of K400/K600, and 48% of K700 test samples are object-biased, meaning objects alone suffice for correct prediction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 30 models across six families (VideoMAE, VideoMAE2, All-in-one, UMT, VideoMamba, InternVid) on both original and UTD-debiased splits. In action classification, VideoMAE and VideoMAE2 models are the most robust to object bias, with drops as small as 0.9 pp (VideoMAE2-B on UCF101) and 10.3 pp (UMT-L-fnK710 on K400), while All-in-one models suffer the largest drops (21.4 pp on UCF101, 24.3 pp on K400), indicating heavy reliance on object shortcuts. Larger backbones and more pre-training data generally reduce object bias in classification (e.g., InternVid-L-200M drops only 6.3 pp on UCF vs. 11.6 pp for InternVid-B-200M). In text-to-video retrieval, the pattern reverses: larger models and those trained on more data exhibit *higher* object bias (e.g., InternVid-B-200M drops 13.4 pp on MSRVTT vs. 8.0 pp for InternVid-L-10M), which the authors attribute to larger text backbones encoding richer object vocabularies that encourage object-centric visual learning. UMT-L-17M achieves the best retrieval performance on the original MSRVTT (43.6 R@1) but drops 12.5 pp on the debiased split, while VideoMamba-VM-25M shows the most balanced retrieval performance (34.9→25.5, −9.4 pp).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the formal representation bias framework from RESOUND [23], adopting its notation and the idea of measuring bias as the best achievable performance under a restricted representation family. It departs from prior debiasing strategies that modify videos at the pixel level (HAT, Mimetics, Diving48) by instead using VLM-generated textual descriptions as a controllable intermediate representation, enabling precise isolation of concept categories (objects, activities, verbs) without introducing artifacts or domain shifts. The approach also connects to the single-frame bias literature [5, 19] and temporal understanding benchmarks [2, 7, 28], but uniquely unifies concept, temporal, and common-sense-vs-dataset bias dimensions into a single scalable analysis pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that 73% of UCF101 and 55% of Kinetics-400 test samples are object-biased provides concrete evidence that current video benchmarks may over-reward object recognition over genuine temporal/action understanding, and the released UTD-splits offer a practical, drop-in alternative for more robust evaluation. A notable open question is whether debiasing at the test level (removing biased samples) is sufficient, or whether training data also needs to be debiased to prevent models from learning object shortcuts. The counterintuitive result that larger retrieval models exhibit *more* object bias than smaller ones suggests that scaling video-language pre-training may inadvertently reinforce object-centric representations, a concern that warrants further investigation.
