## Problem

Hyperspectral Novel View Synthesis (HNVS) requires reconstructing material-level spectral signatures from arbitrary viewpoints, but existing approaches are limited: NeRF-based methods (e.g., HS-NeRF) suffer from unstable training, slow inference, and poor handling of high-dimensional spectral data, while 3DGS has not been successfully extended beyond RGB. No prior work integrates view-dependent hyperspectral material information into a Gaussian splatting framework, and no comprehensive benchmark exists for comparing HNVS methods.

## Input / Output

The method consumes multi-view hyperspectral images (128–141 spectral channels at ~640×512 to 696×520 spatial resolution) along with camera poses estimated via COLMAP on grayscale channel slices (the channel with highest foreground intensity variance is selected). Before 3DGS optimization, all training images are encoded into a compact latent representation by a pre-trained convolutional autoencoder, so the Gaussian primitives store latent spectral signatures rather than full-channel spectra. The output is a novel hyperspectral view decoded back to full spectral resolution, alongside a 3D Gaussian point cloud whose primitives carry per-Gaussian latent spectral signatures and view-dependent appearance parameters.

## Method

HyperGS performs 3D Gaussian Splatting in a learned latent space produced by a channel-wise convolutional autoencoder with Squeeze-Excitation blocks, reducing the optimization dimensionality from 128–141 spectral channels to a compact latent vector per Gaussian. A NeRF-style MLP with multiresolution hash encoding (I-NGP/MipNeRF360-style) predicts view-dependent spectral effects and anisotropic opacity, which are multiplied with the stored Gaussian signatures before volumetric blending in latent space; the final image is decoded by the frozen autoencoder. Training uses a custom loss combining Charbonnier loss and cosine similarity for spectral fidelity with SSIM for spatial consistency, replacing the standard L1+DSSIM that destabilizes hyperspectral optimization. An adaptive densification strategy scales gradient-based split/clone scores by the square of depth relative to scene radius to suppress artifacts in sparse regions, and a global pixel-wise pruning step retains only top-K Gaussians per pixel based on spectral importance, controlling primitive count while preserving fine spectral detail.

## Results

On the BaySpec dataset (141 channels, ~360 views/scene), HyperGS achieves 27.11 PSNR, 0.7804 SSIM, 0.0254 SAM, and 0.0440 RMSE, outperforming the best NeRF baseline (MipNeRF360: 26.53 PSNR) and the only prior HNVS method (HS-NeRF: 19.82 PSNR). On the SOP dataset (128 channels, ~40 views/scene), HyperGS reaches 30.51 PSNR, 0.9756 SSIM, 0.00415 SAM, and 0.0354 RMSE, surpassing even the strong 3DGS baseline (28.58 PSNR). The paper reports up to a 14 dB PSNR improvement over previously published HNVS models (HS-NeRF), with the largest gains on the noisier BaySpec data where the latent autoencoder effectively bounds spectral error.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HyperGS outperforms all seven baselines on every metric (PSNR, SSIM, SAM, RMSE) across both the BaySpec and SOP datasets. The closest competitor on BaySpec is MipNeRF360 (26.53 PSNR vs. 27.11), which benefits from positional encoding and tensor decomposition for targeted spectral predictions; HyperGS gains an additional 0.58 dB PSNR and substantially lower SAM (0.0254 vs. 0.0280). On SOP, conventional 3DGS is the strongest baseline (28.58 PSNR), but HyperGS still leads by ~2 dB PSNR and achieves a dramatically lower SAM (0.00415 vs. 0.0301), indicating far superior spectral fidelity. Against the only prior dedicated HNVS method, HS-NeRF, HyperGS improves PSNR by 7.3 dB on BaySpec (27.11 vs. 19.82) and 16.1 dB on SOP (30.51 vs. 14.44), while reducing SAM by over 50% on both datasets. The NeRF-family baselines (NeRF, MipNeRF, TensoRF, Nerfacto) all trail HyperGS by 2–8 dB PSNR, with Nerfacto performing worst on BaySpec (19.12 PSNR) due to its sensitivity to the high noise levels of the BaySpec camera. Notably, 3DGS without the latent-space adaptation shows a very high SAM (0.1335 on BaySpec), confirming that naive Gaussian splatting struggles with spectral consistency in high-dimensional hyperspectral data.

## Relation to Prior Work

HyperGS builds directly on 3D Gaussian Splatting (Kerbl et al., 2023) for its rendering pipeline, but departs from both standard 3DGS and prior NeRF-based hyperspectral methods (HS-NeRF, MipNeRF360) by operating in a learned latent space produced by a channel-wise convolutional autoencoder. This latent-space formulation is the key conceptual departure: rather than optimizing 141-dimensional spectral values per Gaussian (which the paper shows causes instability), it compresses spectral signatures into a compact representation and decodes only at the end. The adaptive depth-scaled densification and pixel-wise spectral pruning are novel modifications to the 3DGS optimization loop, specifically designed to handle the high-dimensional, noise-sensitive nature of hyperspectral data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This paper fills a clear gap by providing the first comprehensive HNVS benchmark and the first 3DGS-based method for the task, giving the community a practical, fast alternative to NeRF-based hyperspectral synthesis. The latent-space strategy is a broadly applicable idea for any high-dimensional per-pixel attribute (beyond hyperspectral) that could inform future work on multi-spectral or material-aware rendering. Limitations include reliance on a pre-trained autoencoder (which bounds but also constrains spectral fidelity), evaluation limited to object-centric scenes due to the absence of large-scale non-object-centric HSI datasets, and the synthetic ScanNet evaluation being a proxy rather than true hyperspectral data. Open questions include whether the approach scales to very large scenes, real-time inference, and generalization across heterogeneous hyperspectral sensors without retraining the AE.
