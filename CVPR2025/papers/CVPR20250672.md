## Problem

While LLMs and LMMs have been extensively benchmarked on mathematics and general science, their ability to solve engineering problems that require integrating complex technical visuals (circuit diagrams, system block diagrams, waveforms) with domain-specific reasoning has not been systematically studied. Engineering problems are intrinsically more visually complex and less deterministic than mathematical ones, demanding rigorous joint processing of abstract diagrams and professional instructions, yet no dedicated benchmark existed to probe this capability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250672/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each benchmark item pairs a problem statement (average 306 tokens) with a high-resolution technical image—circuit schematics, digital logic diagrams, signal waveforms, system block diagrams, component photos, or plots—and requires a single deterministic answer. Of 2,860 problems, 2,059 are multiple-choice and 801 are free-form (answer ≤ 1 word); answer types span text, float, integer, and list. The benchmark spans 10 EEE subdomains (e.g., Analog Circuits, Control Systems, Electromagnetics) with balanced representation across problem types and collection sources.

## Method

EEE-Bench is constructed via a two-stage curation pipeline: (1) only questions appearing in at least two independent sources with identical solutions are retained, and (2) text-only GPT-4 trials are run to eliminate any problem solvable without the image, ensuring visual input is necessary. The paper then evaluates 17 models (1 text-only LLM, 9 open-source LMMs, 7 closed-source LMMs) in a zero-shot setting, running each configuration twice and averaging. Beyond raw accuracy, the authors perform a fine-grained error taxonomy (reasoning, perception, knowledge, calculation, misreading, refusal) and a 'laziness' experiment that prepends spurious captions contradicting the image to test whether models genuinely attend to visual content. Finally, nine prompting strategies (Self-Consistency, DD-CoT, Cantor, etc.) are benchmarked to identify which, if any, mitigate the observed visual-reasoning gap.

## Results

On EEE-Bench (2,860 problems across 10 EEE subdomains), GPT-4o achieves the best overall accuracy of 46.78%, while the best open-source model InternVL2-26B reaches only 26.89%—a gap of nearly 20 percentage points. The average accuracy across all 17 evaluated models ranges from 19.48% (SPHINX-v2-1k) to 46.78% (GPT-4o), with closed-source models averaging 35.28% versus 23.19% for open-source models, both well above the 17.45% random-chance baseline. Even lightweight closed-source models (GPT-4o-mini at 36.99%, Gemini-Flash 1.5 at 33.95%) surpass all open-source LMMs. Performance is most variable across subjects: GPT-4o peaks at 60.40% on Communication Systems but drops to 40.88% on Circuit Theory and Network Analysis.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4o (46.78%) dominates all 10 EEE subdomains, outperforming the second-best closed-source model Gemini-Pro 1.5 (41.99%) by ~5 points and the best open-source model InternVL2-26B (26.89%) by ~20 points. Among closed-source models, GPT-4V (40.21%) and GPT-4o-mini (36.99%) trail GPT-4o but still surpass every open-source LMM, including the strongest open-source entries InternVL2-26B (26.89%) and MiniCPM-V-2.6 (25.21%). Open-source models cluster tightly between 19.48% (SPHINX-v2-1k) and 26.89% (InternVL2-26B), with Qwen-VL-Plus (21.33%) and LLaVA-1.6-13B (20.73%) near the bottom. Text-only GPT-4 achieves 25.52%, and adding GPT-4o-generated captions improves it to 28.22%, yet both remain far below any multimodal model, confirming that visual input is essential. Notably, closed-source models excel on subjects with straightforward visuals but heavy computation (ComS: 60.40%, ED: 56.16% for GPT-4o) while struggling on visually complex subjects (CTNA: 40.88%, AC: 44.60%), whereas open-source models show the opposite weakness, performing poorly even on the simpler-visual subjects (ComS: 13.86–28.71%, ED: 11.42–26.48%). Prompting strategies (Self-Consistency, Cantor, DD-CoT) yield modest gains of +2.3 to +4.0 points for GPT-4o and Gemini-Pro 1.5, while spurious captions cause significant drops of −7.79% (GPT-4o) and −6.78% (Gemini-Pro 1.5), revealing a text-over-vision reliance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the multimodal benchmarking paradigm established by MathVista, MMMU, and similar works to a new domain: electrical and electronics engineering. While prior benchmarks evaluate LMMs on mathematical diagrams or general science, EEE-Bench targets the more visually complex and logically intricate technical images (circuit diagrams, system block diagrams, waveforms) that are central to engineering practice. The 'laziness' analysis connects to prior hallucination studies on natural images (Liu et al., Qian et al.) but reframes the problem around robustness of reasoning with technical diagrams rather than object recognition. The prompting strategy evaluation repurposes standard LLM techniques (Self-Consistency, CoT, DDCoT, Cantor) for the multimodal EEE setting without introducing new methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that even the strongest LMM (GPT-4o) achieves only 46.78% on EEE problems, exposing a significant gap between current multimodal capabilities and the demands of real-world engineering tasks. The 'laziness' finding—that models preferentially rely on textual context over visual information even when explicitly instructed to use the image, and that adding accurate captions actually degrades performance across 7 of 10 subdomains—raises important open questions about how LMMs integrate multimodal inputs and whether current architectures fundamentally underweight visual evidence. Future work should investigate whether domain-specific fine-tuning, improved visual encoders for schematic diagrams, or architectural changes can close the gap, and whether the text-bias phenomenon generalizes beyond EEE to other technical domains.
