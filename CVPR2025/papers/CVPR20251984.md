## Problem

In differentiable rendering, gradients for parameters that induce motion in image space (e.g., object or light positions) are sparse, causing optimization to stall in local minima or plateaus. Existing remedies—topological derivatives, Lagrangian derivatives, and variational derivatives—are either computationally expensive, restricted to implicit geometry or primary light transport, or unreliable for complex scenes. Multi-resolution image pyramids, a natural alternative, average out local appearance and fail in practice for realistic inverse problems.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a reference RGB image (typically 128×128 to 256×192), an initial guess of scene parameters (positions, materials, camera poses, environment maps), and a differentiable renderer (diffvg, Mitsuba 3, or Nvdiffrast) to produce optimized scene parameters. No modifications to the renderer are required; the method operates entirely in image space and is compatible with standard RGB gradients. Scale-space parameters (α, β, σ) are fixed hyperparameters chosen per problem, and the method supports arbitrary geometry representations and complex light-transport effects including caustics and multiple bounces.

## Method

The core idea is to represent each pixel not as a single intensity but as a histogram of intensities (a Locally Orderless Image), then match rendered and reference histograms via Wasserstein distance on their cumulative distribution functions. Three distinct scale spaces are introduced: an inner scale (σ) that blurs the image to extend gradient support, a tonal scale (β) that models radiance uncertainty as a 1D Gaussian kernel over intensity bins, and an extent scale (α) that integrates local histograms over a spatial neighborhood. Critically, the extent scale blurs histogram contributions rather than raw radiance values, preserving the effective modes of the intensity distribution across scales and retaining both appearance and geometric detail even at coarse resolutions. The total loss sums Wasserstein distances over all scale combinations, and standard RGB gradients from any differentiable renderer drive optimization via gradient descent—no proxy or variational derivatives are needed, though the method composes with them when they are beneficial.

## Results

On the five path-tracing scenes (Table 2), the method achieves PSNR of 54.33 (Shadow), 54.73 (Shadow Mini), 32.58 (Caustics and Lights), 25.23 (Sort), and 36.17 (Envlight), with MAE values below 0.5 in all cases. On the 2D disk-recovery benchmark (Table 1), it outperforms all multi-scale baselines across every disk count, reaching PSNR 21.57 / SSIM 0.90 for 256 disks versus 9.60 / 0.34 for Gaussian Pyramids. On differentiable rasterization (Table 3), it achieves PSNR 49.07 for material recovery and 27.22 for camera pose, surpassing the RGBXY proxy-gradient method of Xing et al. on high-dimensional parameter spaces.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The method decisively outperforms PRDPT [7] (parameter-space blurring) on all five path-tracing scenes, with the largest gap on Shadow Mini (PSNR 54.73 vs 27.74; MAE 0.0033 vs 6.01) and Caustics and Lights (PSNR 32.58 vs 17.09; MAE 0.35 vs 4.88), where PRDPT's sampled shadows fail to overlap the target. Against Gaussian Pyramids [1] and MS-SSIM [38], the method wins on every configuration in Tables 1–3; for 256 disks, PSNR improves from 9.60 (GP) / 9.83 (MS-SSIM) to 21.57. LPIPS [46] is competitive at low disk counts (PSNR 27.79 vs 30.40 for 4 disks) but degrades sharply at 256 disks (15.2 vs 21.57). On rasterization (Table 3), Xing et al. [44] with RGBXY derivatives wins on low-dimensional Translation (PSNR 63.62 vs 51.77) but the proposed method using only RGB gradients surpasses it on Camera Pose (27.22 vs 23.50) and Material recovery (49.07 vs 33.01), demonstrating that histogram matching compensates for the lack of proxy gradients in high-dimensional settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes Locally Orderless Images (LOIs) from Koenderink and van Doorn [15]—originally developed for reasoning about image topology—and Griffin's scale-imprecision space [11] as an inverse rendering objective, bridging two previously disconnected areas in vision and graphics. It departs from the standard multi-resolution matching paradigm (Gaussian Pyramids, MS-SSIM) by matching full intensity distributions rather than mean intensities, and from proxy-gradient methods (topological, Lagrangian, variational derivatives) by operating entirely in image space with standard RGB gradients from a differentiable renderer. The method is positioned as complementary to parameter-space blurring (PRDPT [7]), with experiments showing the two approaches can be combined for complex light-transport scenes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a persistent and well-documented failure mode in differentiable rendering—sparse image gradients for parameters inducing long-range motion—without requiring expensive proxy gradients or renderer modifications, making it broadly applicable across renderers (diffvg, Mitsuba 3, Nvdiffrast). A key limitation is that parameters with zero image-space influence (e.g., occluded geometry) still yield zero gradients, necessitating combination with variational optimization. Open questions include scalability to very high-dimensional parameter spaces, robustness to severe noise or domain gaps in real photography, and whether the three-scale histogram representation can be learned or adapted per-scene rather than using fixed kernel parameters.
