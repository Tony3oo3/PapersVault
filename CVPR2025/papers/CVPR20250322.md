## Problem

3D-Gaussian Splatting provides high-quality novel-view synthesis but lacks native uncertainty quantification, making it difficult to determine which new views would be most informative for applications like SLAM and active perception. While FisherRF introduced a diagonal Fisher Information approximation for 3D-GS, it neither leverages the classical optimal experimental design literature nor captures inter-parameter correlation, limiting its effectiveness in view selection tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250322/cropped_page0_idx4.webp)

## Input / Output

The method requires only a trained 3D-GS model and the camera poses of candidate views—not the actual image content—making it possible to evaluate information gain before capturing an image. The covariance matrix is approximated as either a simple diagonal (matching FisherRF's cost) or a block diagonal where each block corresponds to one ellipsoid's parameters, trading memory for correlation capture. Output is a scalar information gain score per candidate view, from which the most informative views are selected iteratively or in batches.

## Method

The paper derives a general covariance matrix for 3D-GS by formulating the rendering problem as maximum likelihood estimation and applying Gauss-Newton linearization, yielding H = J^T J as the information matrix. It then applies P-Optimality functionals (T, D, A, E-Optimality) to the inverse Hessian to score candidate views, with D-Optimality (volume of the covariance hyper-ellipsoid) and T-Optimality (average variance) performing best empirically. To handle the intractable size of the full Hessian (millions of parameters), two approximations are proposed: a simple diagonal following FisherRF, and a block diagonal that captures intra-ellipsoid parameter correlation and is GPU-parallelizable. The block diagonal requires computing partial derivatives per color channel per pixel to avoid singularity from the shared alpha compositing weight. Batch selection is handled iteratively by adding the most informative view, updating the Hessian, and repeating without retraining.

## Results

On the Mip-NeRF360 dataset (10-view single selection), D-Optimality with block diagonal approximation achieves the best PSNR of 18.15 dB, SSIM of 0.548, and LPIPS of 0.401, outperforming FisherRF (16.81 dB, 0.493, 0.445) by 1.34 dB in PSNR. On the Blender dataset, D-Optimality (Simple) reaches 25.52 dB PSNR and 0.909 SSIM, surpassing FisherRF (24.59 dB, 0.897) by nearly 1 dB. T-Optimality performs comparably to D-Optimality across both datasets, while A-Optimality and E-Optimality consistently underperform all other methods. The block diagonal approximation provides additional gains in SSIM and LPIPS over the simple diagonal, particularly on the more complex Mip-NeRF360 scenes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is FisherRF [13], which uses a diagonal Fisher Information approximation with a trace-based information metric. D-Optimality (Block) outperforms FisherRF by 1.34 dB PSNR on Mip-NeRF360 (18.15 vs. 16.81) and by 0.82 dB on Blender (25.41 vs. 24.59), with consistent gains in SSIM and LPIPS. Notably, uniform sampling slightly beats FisherRF on Mip-NeRF360 (17.29 vs. 16.81 PSNR), which the authors attribute to the test/train view distribution being similar in that setup; however, on Blender, FisherRF clearly outperforms uniform sampling (24.59 vs. 23.32 PSNR). Among the P-Optimality variants, T-Optimality and D-Optimality dominate, while A-Optimality and E-Optimality perform worst (15.55 and 15.33 PSNR respectively on Mip-NeRF360), suggesting that volume-based and trace-based criteria are more suitable for 3D-GS information quantification than harmonic-mean or extreme-eigenvalue criteria. In the keyframe selection experiment on Blender (Table 6), the gap widens dramatically: FisherRF drops to 18.37 PSNR while T-Optimality reaches 24.90 dB, indicating that FisherRF's per-view metric fails to capture batch-level view diversity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends FisherRF's diagonal Fisher Information formulation for 3D-GS by deriving a general covariance matrix solution and applying the full P-Optimality family of optimal experimental design criteria (T, A, D, E-Optimality) rather than a single trace-based metric. The mathematical derivation follows the Gauss-Newton / maximum likelihood framework from classical estimation theory, while the P-Optimality functionals come from Kiefer's optimal experimental design literature, which has been successfully applied to SLAM keyframe selection and active mapping but not previously to 3D-GS. The key methodological departure from FisherRF is the block diagonal covariance approximation, which captures intra-ellipsoid parameter correlation at moderate computational cost, and the reframing of information gain as a general functional of the covariance eigenvalues rather than a fixed trace expression.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As 3D-GS becomes a standard world model for robotics (SLAM, manipulation, active perception), the lack of native uncertainty quantification is a critical gap; this paper provides a principled, general framework that practitioners can instantiate with different optimality criteria depending on their application. The finding that D- and T-Optimality consistently outperform the FisherRF baseline across both real and synthetic datasets gives practitioners a clear, theoretically grounded recommendation. However, the block diagonal approximation still discards inter-ellipsoid correlation, and the computational cost (2.62 GB, 0.15 Hz for full block diagonal on the ship scene) limits real-time deployment; the simple diagonal approximation matches FisherRF's cost but loses the correlation benefit. Open questions include whether inter-ellipsoid structure (e.g., from opacity or structural similarity loss) can be incorporated, and whether these criteria scale to the millions of parameters in large-scale scenes.
