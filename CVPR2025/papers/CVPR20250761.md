## Problem

Existing 3D avatar creation datasets rely on expensive laboratory systems (multi-view cameras, depth sensors, lightstages) that are costly to scale and exhibit limited real-world representation, while in-the-wild web videos are abundant but lack the accurate SMPL, camera, and segmentation annotations required for avatar creation. Prior in-the-wild efforts still depend heavily on manual intervention, preventing them from reaching the scale needed to close the domain gap between laboratory and real-world scenarios.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250761/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes raw monocular YouTube video clips (cut into manageable segments) and produces per-frame SMPL pose/shape parameters, camera intrinsics and extrinsics, and human segmentation masks via SAM. A key detail is that the pipeline operates entirely automatically without manual mask selection or background matting, and the four filtering protocols progressively reduce the candidate pool from ~466K clips to ~10.6K qualified subjects, with final PCK reaching 0.921 (within 1.7% of 3DPW ground truth).

## Method

The core contribution is a four-stage automatic annotation pipeline: (I) human bounding box detection and tracking with YOLO/Detectron2, (II) segmentation mask extraction using SAM conditioned on detected boxes and keypoints, (III) coarse per-frame SMPL and camera estimation via HMR2.0/PARE, and (IV) refinement through gradient-descent-based temporal smoothing that incorporates 2D keypoint alignment and SAM mask supervision. Four filtering protocols gate quality at each stage: confidence-based body/movement checks, ensemble consistency across multiple annotating experts (detection, 2D pose, SMPL), PCK-based 2D keypoint consistency between projected SMPL and pose estimators, and SSIOU-based overlap between SMPL and SAM masks. This pipeline surpasses state-of-the-art methods on the EMDB benchmark (up to 4% improvement in PVE) and yields the WildAvatar dataset (10,647 subjects), which is then used to demonstrate up to 7% improvements in generalizable avatar creation methods over laboratory-only training.

## Results

On the EMDB in-the-wild benchmark, the proposed four-stage annotation pipeline achieves PA-MPJPE of 59.9, MPJPE of 94.9, and PVE of 115.5, surpassing the best prior method HMR2.0 (60.6 / 98.0 / 120.3) by 3.27% on MPJPE and 4.16% on PVE. For per-subject avatar creation on WildAvatar, using the pipeline's annotations instead of HMR2.0 improves PSNR by +3.51%, SSIM by +1.91%, and reduces LPIPS by −5.06% on average across seven baselines. For generalizable avatar creation, adding WildAvatar to the training set yields average improvements of +4.52% PSNR, +3.53% SSIM, and −5.06% LPIPS on the WildAvatar test split, and +4.17% / +4.54% PSNR on novel-view / novel-pose laboratory benchmarks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On the EMDB benchmark (Table 2), the proposed pipeline outperforms all eight baselines across all three metrics, with the closest competitor being HMR2.0 (60.6 PA-MPJPE, 98.0 MPJPE, 120.3 PVE) vs. Ours (59.9, 94.9, 115.5), a gap of ~3% on MPJPE and ~4% on PVE. The largest margin is over SPIN (87.1 PA-MPJPE) and VIBE (81.4 PA-MPJPE), which lack temporal smoothing. For per-subject avatar creation (Table 4), the pipeline's annotations consistently improve all seven reconstruction methods (NHR, NB, AN, AS, HN, IN, GH) over HMR2.0 annotations, with the largest absolute PSNR gain on GauHuman (+1.16 dB) and InstantNVR (+0.89 dB). For generalizable avatar creation (Table 5), training with WildAvatar improves every method (NHP, MPS-NeRF, SHERF, Generalizable GS) on the WildAvatar test split by 0.6–1.1 dB PSNR, and on laboratory benchmarks by 0.5–1.0 dB PSNR, confirming that in-the-wild scale transfers to controlled settings. Notably, 3D Gaussian Splatting (Generalizable GS) underperforms NeRF-based methods in the generalizable setting, attributed to depth ambiguity in single-image point cloud initialization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of SMPL annotation pipelines (SPIN, VIBE, PARE, HMR2.0) and in-the-wild human data collection (IVS-Net, TikTok dataset), but departs from both by replacing costly manual intervention with a fully automated four-stage pipeline combining off-the-shelf tools (YOLO, SAM, HMR2.0) with novel ensemble-based filtering protocols. Rather than proposing a new avatar creation architecture, it repurposes existing generalizable methods (SHERF, MPS-NeRF, NHP, GauHuman) as downstream consumers of its curated WildAvatar dataset. The key conceptual departure from prior in-the-wild efforts is the systematic use of cross-model consistency checks (PCK between SMPL and 2D pose, IOU between SMPL and SAM masks) as automatic quality gates, eliminating the need for human-in-the-loop curation that limited previous web-scale attempts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

WildAvatar fills a critical gap by providing 10,000+ in-the-wild subjects—over 10× larger than any prior in-the-wild avatar dataset—making it the first truly scalable resource for training generalizable 3D avatar creation methods on real-world diversity. The demonstration that adding WildAvatar training data yields ~4–7% improvements across multiple generalizable methods validates the hypothesis that data scale, not just architecture, is the bottleneck for in-the-wild generalization. Open questions include whether the annotation quality (PCK 0.921 vs. 0.937 GT) is sufficient for all downstream tasks, how the pipeline handles multi-person or heavily occluded scenes, and whether further scale-up (beyond 10k) continues to yield diminishing or accelerating returns. The finding that 3DGS underperforms NeRF in the generalizable single-image setting due to depth-ambiguous point cloud initialization also raises questions about the applicability of 3DGS to sparse-input human reconstruction.
