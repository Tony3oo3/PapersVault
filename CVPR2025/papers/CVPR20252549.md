## Problem

Existing error detection methods for procedural tasks rely on static prototypes or focus solely on temporal ordering errors, failing to handle the common scenario where multiple distinct actions are valid after a given executed sequence. This leads to two failures: static prototypes cannot adapt when the inference environment or execution distribution differs from training, and the model may compare against the wrong prototype when the ongoing action label differs from the predicted one.

## Input / Output

The method consumes egocentric video frames processed through a pre-trained I3D feature extractor and an ActionFormer segmentation model, combined with a predefined task graph encoding valid action transitions. It produces a binary error/normal label per action segment, determined by whether the minimum Euclidean distance between the ongoing action's segment-averaged feature and any reconstructed normal representation exceeds a class-specific threshold (set at the 0.85 quantile of normal training distances).

## Method

AMNAR comprises three blocks: (1) a Potential Action Prediction Block that uses dynamic programming over the task graph to extract all valid next actions from the executed sequence, handling segmentation noise by identifying longest common subsequences forming non-branching paths and merging them into a unified subgraph; (2) a Representation Reconstruction Block that applies dilated convolution to temporal features, then uses local cross-attention (valid actions as queries, temporal features as keys/values) to predict a residual that refines a precomputed cluster center for each valid action, yielding context-adaptive normal representations; (3) a Representation Matching Block that computes Euclidean distances between the ongoing action feature and all reconstructed representations, selecting the minimum and flagging an error if it exceeds the threshold. Training is supervised on normal videos only, minimizing the squared distance between the single reconstructed representation and the actual action feature, with a hybrid sample-selection strategy filtering ASM segments by an overlap ratio with ground truth.

## Results

On the EgoPER dataset, AMNAR achieves 64.4% EDA and 68.5% AUC (averaged over all five cooking tasks), improving over the prior best method EgoPED by 7.4% in EDA and 6.5% in AUC. On HoloAssist, AMNAR reaches 69.9% EDA and 56.5% AUC (vs. EgoPED's 66.2%/55.2%), and on CaptainCook4D it attains 72.3% EDA, 60.2% AUC, and 66.8% Precision (vs. EgoPED's 69.8%/54.9%/56.5%). The largest per-task gain on EgoPER is on the Coffee task, where AMNAR scores 73.5% EDA and 67.8% AUC versus EgoPED's 55.3%/58.3%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AMNAR is compared against four baselines: Random, HF2-VAD [24], HF2-VAD+SSPCAB [30], S3R [40], and EgoPED [16]. On EgoPER, AMNAR outperforms EgoPED (the strongest prior method) by 7.4% EDA and 6.5% AUC on average, with the largest margin on the Coffee task (+18.2% EDA, +9.5% AUC) where action branching is most complex. On HoloAssist, AMNAR improves EDA by 3.7% and AUC by 1.3% over EgoPED, with the verb-label setting showing a larger EDA gap (72.6% vs. 67.2%). On CaptainCook4D, AMNAR surpasses EgoPED by 2.5% EDA, 5.3% AUC, and 10.3% Precision. The video-anomaly-detection baselines (HF2-VAD, HF2-VAD+SSPCAB, S3R) are substantially weaker, scoring 27–43% EDA on EgoPER, confirming that task-specific procedural modeling is critical. Notably, AMNAR's advantage is most pronounced on non-deterministic actions (73.7% EDA vs. EgoPED's 65.2%), directly validating the multi-representation design.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends EgoPED (Lee et al., CVPR 2024), which introduced contrastive step prototype learning for error detection in egocentric procedural videos. The key departure is replacing EgoPED's static, class-level prototypes with dynamically reconstructed normal action representations that are conditioned on the executed action sequence and account for multiple valid next actions. Conceptually, the framework borrows the reconstruction paradigm from video anomaly detection (reconstruct normal, measure deviation) but adapts it to the procedural setting where a single 'normal' is insufficient due to branching task graphs. The use of dynamic programming over task graphs for filtering noisy segmentation labels connects to the broader procedural activity literature (Ding et al., Seminara et al.) while the cross-attention and cluster-center residual prediction are novel architectural choices specific to this paper.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete and underappreciated failure mode in procedural error detection: when multiple actions are valid after a given step, static prototypes either mismatch the ongoing action or select the wrong reference, leading to false positives. The non-deterministic action analysis (Table 7) and the 18.2% EDA gain on the highly-branching 'coffee' task provide strong evidence that this is not a marginal concern. Limitations include reliance on predefined or training-set-derived task graphs (limiting open-ended tasks), sensitivity to action segmentation quality, and mild degradation when prior errors corrupt the temporal context. Open questions include whether the framework generalizes to tasks without explicit graph structure and how to handle compound or cascading errors more gracefully.
