## Problem

Existing formula recognition metrics (BLEU, Edit Distance, ExpRate) rely on text-based character matching, making them unreliable when the same formula has multiple valid LaTeX representations. This leads to unfair model comparisons where correct predictions score poorly due to stylistic differences, while incorrect predictions can score higher if their representation happens to align with the ground truth. The paper addresses this by proposing an image-level evaluation metric that is invariant to LaTeX syntax variation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252268/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a predicted LaTeX string and a ground-truth LaTeX string as input, renders both into formula images, and produces a CDM score (F1-Score between 0 and 1) and an ExpRate@CDM (proportion of perfectly matched formulas). A key detail is that each LaTeX token is rendered in a unique RGB color (from a palette of 5,832 colors with 15-unit intervals) to enable pixel-level localization, and rendering failures are assigned a CDM score of 0. The metric operates at the individual character/symbol level within the rendered image rather than at the token or string level.

## Method

CDM reframes formula evaluation as an image-based object detection task with four stages. First, Element Localization renders each LaTeX token in a unique color and extracts bounding boxes via color-based pixel detection. Second, Element Region Matching pairs predicted and ground-truth elements using the Hungarian algorithm with a composite cost combining token identity (with a small penalty for visually identical but syntactically different tokens like \left( vs ()), L1 positional proximity, and normalized source-order similarity. Third, Invalid Match Elimination discards pairs that fail a token consistency check or a RANSAC-fitted affine transformation (translation + scaling only, with rotation fixed to 0) that enforces consistent relative spatial relationships across all matched pairs. Finally, the F1-Score over valid matches yields the CDM score, and ExpRate@CDM counts the fraction of formulas achieving a perfect score of 1.

## Results

On the UniMER-Test dataset (23,757 formulas), UniMERNet achieves the highest CDM score of 0.9680 and ExpRate@CDM of 0.8110, followed by Mathpix (CDM 0.9510, ExpRate@CDM 0.5000), Texify (CDM 0.7550), and Pix2Tex (CDM 0.6360). On the Tiny-Doc-Math dataset, Mathpix leads at the formula level with CDM 0.9480, while at the document level Mathpix achieves CDM 0.9567 with 62.92% fully correct formulas. Human preference evaluation shows 96% consistency between CDM scores and human judgment (64% prefer CDM, 32% consider both metrics good), and CDM scores remain invariant (all = 1) under LaTeX style rewrites where BLEU scores become dispersed.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates five models (Pix2Tex, Texify, Mathpix, UniMERNet, GPT-4o) plus Nougat at the document level, using both the proposed CDM and the standard BLEU metric. On UniMER-Test, CDM and BLEU broadly agree on model ranking (UniMERNet > Mathpix > Texify > Pix2Tex), but on the SCE subset they produce opposite conclusions: BLEU favors Mathpix (0.8182 vs. 0.6160) while CDM favors UniMERNet (0.9461 vs. 0.9238), because SCE annotations were originally generated by Mathpix. On Tiny-Doc-Math (formula-level), GPT-4o achieves the highest BLEU (0.6431) yet the lowest CDM (0.7330) among the four formula-level models, revealing that BLEU overestimates GPT-4o's true recognition quality. Mathpix attains the best CDM (0.9480) at the formula level but only 21.05% fully correct formulas (ExpRate@CDM), with manual inspection showing missing punctuation. At the document level, Mathpix (CDM 0.9567) and Nougat (CDM 0.8326) substantially outperform GPT-4o (CDM 0.6502), indicating that even leading multimodal LLMs lag behind specialized formula recognition pipelines for document-level tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes object-detection machinery (bounding-box localization, Hungarian bipartite matching from DETR, RANSAC affine estimation) to solve an evaluation problem in formula recognition, rather than building on a prior metric. It departs from the text-based metrics (BLEU, Edit Distance, ExpRate) that have been standard since the CROHME competitions and from the LaTeX-regularization preprocessing adopted by Pix2Tex, Texify, and UniMERNet, arguing that neither fully handles the non-uniqueness of LaTeX representations. Image-similarity metrics (MSE, SSIM, image edit distance) are acknowledged but rejected as too sensitive to sub-pixel misalignment in rendered formula images.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CDM offers a representation-agnostic, human-aligned evaluation standard that could become the default for formula recognition benchmarks, directly addressing the unfairness where a model's score depends on whether its LaTeX style matches the test set's annotation style. Practical limitations remain: the metric is only as good as the LaTeX renderer (rendering failures score 0), the color-based token localization is capped at 5,832 distinct tokens per formula, and the computational cost of rendering plus detection is substantially higher than string comparison. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to other structured-output tasks (tables, chemical formulas) and whether a learned visual similarity could replace the hand-crafted RANSAC verification step.
