## Problem

Data-driven image-to-video models produce photorealistic but physically ungrounded videos with limited user control, while existing physics-based digital twin approaches require multi-view scans or are restricted to 2D rigid-body dynamics. No prior method can transform a single image into a fully interactive, physically simulated 3D scene that supports arbitrary material behaviors and user-specified initial conditions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250905/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system takes a single RGB image (object-centric scenes with one or a few objects) and a text prompt specifying initial conditions such as object velocities, material types, or special effects (e.g., collapse, melt). It outputs a temporally coherent video of physically plausible object dynamics rendered with realistic lighting, along with an underlying interactive 3D scene where objects can be repositioned, replaced, or re-simulated. The pipeline is designed for scenes with simple spatial geometry and excludes cases with heavy occlusion or highly uneven surfaces.

## Method

PhysGen3D is a training-free, three-stage pipeline. First, a 3D world is reconstructed from the single image by chaining pretrained foundation models: GPT-4o identifies object categories and queries physical properties (elasticity, density, friction), Grounded-SAM segments instances, InstantMesh (via Zero123++) generates 3D meshes, Dust3r estimates background depth, and a coarse-to-fine alignment strategy (SuperGlue feature matching + PnP, followed by differentiable rendering with Dice and depth losses) registers meshes into camera coordinates. PBR materials are optimized via Mitsuba3 inverse rendering with DiffusionLight for illumination. Second, the scene is simulated using Taichi Elements' Material Point Method, where a scale factor k (derived from GPT-4o real-world size estimates) nondimensionalizes gravity and velocity to maintain physical realism across scales. Third, physics-based rendering with two-pass shadow mapping composites dynamic objects and their shadows onto a LaMA-inpainted background, producing the final video without converting the entire scene into the renderer.

## Results

In human evaluation (5-point scale), PhysGen3D achieves the highest Physical Realism score (3.707) and Semantic Consistency score (3.866), outperforming the best commercial baseline Kling 1.0 (2.811 and 2.467 respectively), while Photorealism is slightly lower (3.411 vs. 3.582 for Gen-3). In GPT-4o evaluation, PhysGen3D leads on PhysReal (0.752) and Align (0.796) across all five baselines, with the closest competitor being DragAnything on PhysReal (0.645) and Kling on Align (0.596). On VBench, Motion Smoothness is comparable to all baselines (0.995 vs. 0.996 for Kling), while Imaging Quality (0.666) is slightly below Gen-3 (0.723) and Kling (0.671).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PhysGen3D clearly outperforms all five baselines on Physical Realism (PhysReal) and Semantic Consistency (Align), the two metrics most relevant to its physics-grounded design. Against the strongest commercial model Kling 1.0, PhysGen3D gains +0.189 on PhysReal (0.752 vs. 0.563) and +0.200 on Align (0.796 vs. 0.596) in GPT-4o scoring, and +0.896 / +1.399 in human evaluation. Runway Gen-3 and Pika 1.5 trail substantially on both physics and alignment metrics (e.g., Gen-3 scores only 0.141 on PhysReal), confirming that text-prompted I2V models struggle to produce physically grounded motion. Among open-source motion controllers, DragAnything achieves the highest PhysReal (0.645) but still falls 0.107 short of PhysGen3D, while MOFA-Video is weakest overall (0.384 PhysReal, 0.304 Align). On Photorealism, PhysGen3D (0.867) is competitive but slightly behind Gen-3 (0.896) and Kling (0.874), and on Imaging Quality it is marginally below Kling (0.666 vs. 0.671) and Gen-3 (0.723), indicating a small visual-quality trade-off for the physics gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

PhysGen3D directly extends PhysGen [48], which performed 2D rigid-body physics-grounded image-to-video generation, by upgrading to full 3D simulation with general materials (rigid, soft, granular) via the Material Point Method. Rather than learning dynamics from video data like diffusion-based I2V models (Pika, Kling, Gen-3), it takes a training-free route: composing pretrained vision foundation models (GPT-4o, Grounded-SAM, InstantMesh, Dust3r) for perception with an explicit physics engine (Taichi Elements) and PBR rendering (Mitsuba3). This positions it between data-driven video generation (photorealistic but physically ungrounded) and multi-view digital-twin reconstruction (physically accurate but data-intensive), carving out the single-image, interactive, physics-simulated niche.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a carefully orchestrated pipeline of off-the-shelf pretrained models plus explicit MPM simulation can produce physically plausible, user-controllable video from a single image without any task-specific training, challenging the assumption that large-scale video pretraining is necessary for physical realism. However, the approach is limited to object-centric scenes with simple geometry, and MPM struggles with fine structural details (e.g., thin handles), while rendering artifacts appear under challenging lighting. Open questions include scaling to complex multi-object scenes with heavy occlusion, improving simulation fidelity for delicate geometries, and closing the small photorealism gap with learned I2V models.
