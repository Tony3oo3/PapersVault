## Problem

Existing backdoor training data detection methods suffer from either low true positive rates (failing to identify poisoned samples) or high false positive rates (misclassifying clean data), largely because they rely on input-level operations rather than exploiting inherent model properties. There is a noticeable shortage of efficient approaches that can proactively identify backdoor samples at the initial stages without requiring knowledge of the attack type, trigger pattern, or poisoning ratio.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252392/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a suspicious (potentially poisoned) training dataset and a small set of label-free clean validation data (5% of the training set size, drawn from the original test set) as input, along with a trained ResNet-18 model. It produces a per-sample Prediction Shift Uncertainty (PSU) score for every training sample, where samples with PSU below a threshold (set to the 25th percentile of clean validation PSU values) are flagged as backdoor. The defender requires no prior knowledge of the attack type, trigger, target class, or poisoning proportion.

## Method

PSBD exploits the Prediction Shift (PS) phenomenon: when dropout is enabled during inference, a poisoned model's predictions on clean data shift away from true labels toward specific other classes, while backdoor samples remain stable due to a 'neuron bias effect' where certain network paths become strongly associated with the target class. The method computes Prediction Shift Uncertainty (PSU) as the difference between the predicted class confidence without dropout and the average confidence across k=3 dropout-enabled forward passes; lower PSU indicates higher likelihood of being malicious. An adaptive strategy selects the dropout rate p by finding the value where clean validation data shift ratio approaches 0.8 while the gap between full training set and clean validation shift ratios is maximized. A late-stage trained model is used to compute PSU values, leveraging its enhanced data fitting and robust neuron bias paths, and data augmentation is incorporated during training when the model lacks generalization (e.g., on Tiny ImageNet).

## Results

PSBD achieves an average TPR of 0.994 with FPR of 0.136 on CIFAR-10, 0.955/0.192 on GTSRB, and 0.929/0.121 on Tiny ImageNet across seven backdoor attacks at 10% poisoning ratio. On CIFAR-10, PSBD attains TPR ≥ 0.982 for all attacks except Adaptive-Blend (0.982), while the next-best method (SCAN) achieves only 0.832 average TPR and fails entirely on Adaptive-Blend (TPR 0.000). On the more challenging Tiny ImageNet, PSBD maintains TPR ≥ 0.839 across all attacks, whereas all six baselines drop below 0.75 average TPR. The method requires only 5% label-free clean validation data and three dropout forward passes per sample.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PSBD dominates all six baselines on average TPR across all three datasets: 0.994 vs. 0.832 (SCAN, the closest competitor) on CIFAR-10, 0.955 vs. 0.614 (Strip) on GTSRB, and 0.929 vs. 0.734 (CD-L) on Tiny ImageNet. Spectral Signature (SS) and Spectre are effectively non-functional, with SS averaging TPR ≈ 0.44–0.47 and FPR ≈ 0.50 across all settings, and Spectre suffering from consistently high FPR (0.40–0.50). SCAN achieves the lowest FPR (0.013 on CIFAR-10) but collapses on confusion-strategy attacks (WaNet TPR 0.891, Adaptive-Blend TPR 0.000) and times out entirely on GTSRB and Tiny ImageNet. Strip and SCP are competitive on straightforward attacks (BadNets, Blend) but fail catastrophically on WaNet (TPR 0.050 and 0.869) and Adaptive-Blend (TPR 0.014 and 0.721) on CIFAR-10. CD-L shows moderate performance but degrades sharply on Tiny ImageNet (average TPR 0.734) and fails on Adaptive-Blend across all datasets (TPR 0.432/0.028/0.331). PSBD's FPR (0.121–0.192) is higher than SCAN's but substantially lower than SS, Spectre, and SCP, striking a practical balance between detection sensitivity and clean-data preservation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in backdoor sample detection, which relies on input-level manipulations (spectral signatures, pixel scaling, blending with clean samples) to expose differences between clean and poisoned data. Instead, PSBD repurposes MC-Dropout (Gal & Ghahramani, 2016) not as a standard uncertainty estimator but as a mechanism to induce a 'neuron bias' effect that differentially affects clean versus backdoor predictions. The key conceptual departure is the Prediction Shift phenomenon and its mechanistic explanation via neuron bias, which reframes backdoor detection as a model-internal uncertainty problem rather than an input-variability problem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PSBD offers a practical, low-overhead method for identifying poisoned training samples using only 5% label-free clean validation data, with near-perfect TPR across seven attack types on three datasets. The neuron bias explanation provides a mechanistic understanding of why backdoor samples are more 'learnable' and stable under dropout, which could inform future defense design. However, the FPR remains non-trivial (10–20%), the adaptive dropout-rate selection is heuristic, and the method has only been validated on ResNet-18 for image classification; whether the neuron bias effect generalizes to transformers, NLP, or time-series models remains an open question.
