## Problem

Existing losses for neural signed distance function optimization, particularly the eikonal loss, are necessary but insufficient conditions: a function can satisfy ||∇u||=1 almost everywhere yet fail to be a true distance function. Moreover, the eikonal loss is temporally unstable during optimization, and the area/divergence regularizations added to compensate distort the reconstructed SDF. No prior method provides a loss that is both a sufficient condition for distance correctness and stable to optimize.

## Input / Output

The method takes an unoriented point cloud (positions only, no normals) as input and produces a neural signed distance function parameterized by a 5-layer MLP with 128 hidden channels. The point cloud is consumed solely through a boundary loss that enforces u(x_i)=0 at each input point, while the heat loss and eikonal loss are evaluated via Monte Carlo sampling over the domain. The output SDF can be queried for signed distance values at arbitrary points and rendered via sphere tracing, with the zero level set defining the reconstructed surface.

## Method

HOTSPOT introduces a heat loss derived from the screened Poisson equation ∇²h − λ²h = 0 via the substitution h(x) = e^{−λ|u(x)|}, yielding L_heat = ½∫ e^{−2λ|u|}(||∇u||² + 1) dx. This loss is combined with a standard boundary loss and eikonal loss, and the total objective is minimized via gradient descent. The key theoretical result is that the heat loss provides an asymptotically sufficient condition: the distance error between the minimizer and the true SDF is bounded by C/λ, converging linearly as the absorption parameter λ increases. The loss also guarantees spatial stability (perturbation errors decay exponentially with distance, unlike the eikonal equation where errors propagate along rays) and temporal stability (the gradient flow is a forward heat equation, verified via von Neumann analysis), while naturally penalizing large surface area without distorting the SDF, unlike the coarea loss whose minimizer does not satisfy the eikonal equation.

## Results

On the ShapeNet 3D benchmark (260 shapes, 13 categories), HOTSPOT achieves the best IoU (0.9796), lowest Hausdorff distance (0.0250), and best distance-field accuracy (RMSE 0.0281, MAE 0.0176, SMAPE 0.0540) among all compared methods. On the 2D dataset (14 shapes), HOTSPOT reaches IoU 0.9870 and Hausdorff 0.0153, substantially outperforming DiGS (IoU 0.7882) and StEik (IoU 0.6620). Near-surface distance accuracy (within 0.1 of the surface) is also best: RMSE₀.₁ = 0.0094 and MAE₀.₁ = 0.0047, roughly 35–60% lower than DiGS and StEik. Sphere tracing requires the fewest iterations on average, confirming the most accurate level sets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HOTSPOT outperforms all five baselines on IoU, Hausdorff distance, SMAPE, and all three near-surface metrics (RMSE₀.₁, MAE₀.₁, SMAPE₀.₁) on ShapeNet. Against the closest competitors DiGS and StEik, HOTSPOT improves IoU by ~1.5–1.6 points (0.9796 vs. 0.9636/0.9641) and reduces Hausdorff distance by ~32–43% (0.0250 vs. 0.0435/0.0368). SAL achieves marginally lower global RMSE (0.0251 vs. 0.0281) and MAE (0.0142 vs. 0.0176) due to its closest-point distance loss, but its near-surface SMAPE₀.₁ is 5.7× worse (0.6848 vs. 0.1206), indicating unreliable distance queries close to the surface. SIREN without normalization is far behind on every metric (IoU 0.4874, SMAPE 1.2694). On the 2D dataset, HOTSPOT's advantage is even more pronounced: IoU 0.9870 vs. 0.7882 (DiGS) and 0.6620 (StEik), with Hausdorff distance reduced by ~88% over DiGS.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the classical screened Poisson equation (from heat-transfer distance approximation, Varadhan 1967; Crane et al. 2013) as a loss for neural SDF optimization, departing from the dominant eikonal-loss paradigm used by IGR, SAL, DiGS, and StEik. It is closely related to Lipman's PHASE (2021), which also connects PDE-based regularization to implicit surface learning, but HOTSPOT derives its loss from a different mathematical route and reaches the opposite practical conclusion about boundary weight (firm vs. small). The paper also explicitly challenges the area/divergence regularization adopted by DiGS and StEik, proving that the coarea loss distorts the SDF rather than merely stabilizing it.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The asymptotic sufficiency guarantee (Eq. 9) is the first formal bound showing a neural SDF loss converges to the true distance as a parameter increases, filling a gap that eikonal-based methods could not address. The demonstration that surface-area regularization actively degrades distance accuracy (Section 4.3) reframes a common design choice in the field. Open questions remain for very sparse point clouds where the boundary condition may collapse, for spatially-adaptive λ scheduling, and for integration into inverse-rendering pipelines where SDFs are a core component.
