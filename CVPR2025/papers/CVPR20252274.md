## Problem

Audio-visual event localization suffers from a semantic gap between heterogeneous audio and visual modalities, causing cross-modal inconsistency and unreliable temporal boundary detection. Existing approaches rely on unimodal-guided attention (audio-guided or visual-guided) that introduces event-irrelevant information, or on contrastive learning that becomes complex when extending to three or more modalities, leaving cross-modal semantic alignment and cross-temporal dependency modeling inadequately addressed.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252274/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes a video split into T one-second segments, extracting 512-dimensional visual embeddings via a frozen Swin-V2-L-based CLIP encoder, audio embeddings via a frozen HTS-AT-based CLAP encoder, and text label embeddings from both CLIP and CLAP text encoders (concatenated and projected to a shared dimension). It outputs, for each segment, a binary event-relevance score and a category label over C+1 classes (including background), plus a video-level category prediction. In the weakly-supervised setting, only video-level labels are available during training, and the model is formulated as a multiple-instance learning problem.

## Method

AVSGN introduces two key modules. The Cross-Modal Semantic Alignment (CMSA) module is entirely attention-driven (unlike prior contrastive approaches): it first performs pseudo intra-modal alignment where text label embeddings serve as queries to attend over visual (intra-CLIP) or audio (intra-CLAP) features, then performs inter-modal alignment where each modality attends to the other two and concatenates the results through a bottleneck projection, pulling all three modalities into a shared semantic space. The Cross-Modal Graph Interaction (CMGI) module explicitly disentangles cross-modal interactions into three complementary subgraphs (audio-visual, audio-text, visual-text), computes attention-based adjacency matrices for each, and propagates information through multi-layer GCN to capture cross-temporal semantic associations; modality-specific outputs from the subgraphs are summed to form final representations. A gated fusion mechanism combines the aligned audio and visual features, and training uses a composite loss (category cross-entropy, segment relevance BCE, gate loss, and a margin-based semantic consistency loss) in the fully-supervised setting, or a MIL loss plus gate loss in the weakly-supervised setting.

## Results

On the AVE dataset, AVSGN achieves 83.1% overall classification accuracy in the fully-supervised setting and 78.8% in the weakly-supervised setting, both representing new state-of-the-art results. The evaluation metric is the overall classification accuracy of predicted segment-level event categories across 28 event classes plus background. The model uses frozen Swin-V2-L (CLIP) and HTS-AT (CLAP) encoders to extract 512-dimensional visual, audio, and text embeddings, with the proposed CMSA and CMGI modules operating on top of these frozen features.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AVSGN outperforms all 16 compared baselines on the AVE dataset. Against the strongest prior method using the same frozen pre-trained encoders (DG-SCT [6], 82.2%), AVSGN gains +0.9% in fully-supervised accuracy. Compared to the best VGG-based method (CSS-Net [7], 80.5% F.Sup., 76.8% W.Sup.), the improvement is +2.6% and +2.0% respectively. Against LAVISH [19] (78.6%), which also uses Swin-V2-L and HTS-AT, AVSGN leads by +4.5%. In the weakly-supervised setting, AVSGN (78.8%) surpasses even some fully-supervised methods from earlier work, such as CMRAN [41] (72.9%) and AVT [20] (70.2%), demonstrating the benefit of the graph-based cross-modal interaction under label scarcity. The paper notes that LAVISH and DG-SCT did not report weakly-supervised results, so the W.Sup. comparison is limited to VGG-based methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against the dominant paradigms in AVEL—unimodal-guided cross-modal attention (AGVA, AGSCA, CMGA), multimodal adaptive fusion, and semantic consistency modeling—arguing that all still struggle with the inherent semantic gap between heterogeneous modalities. It departs from these by introducing shared textual labels (via frozen CLIP and CLAP encoders) as an explicit semantic bridge, replacing contrastive-learning-based alignment with an entirely attention-driven cross-modal semantic alignment module. The cross-modal graph interaction module, which decomposes interactions into three dedicated subgraphs (audio-visual, audio-text, visual-text), draws on graph convolutional networks but applies them in a novel multi-subgraph fashion to disentangle cross-temporal dependencies, a design not seen in prior AVEL work. The use of large pre-trained encoders as frozen backbones follows the recent trend established by LAVISH and DG-SCT, but the paper's contribution lies in the downstream alignment and interaction architecture rather than the encoders themselves.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The three-subgraph decomposition offers a clean, interpretable framework for modeling cross-modal interactions that could be adapted to related tasks such as audio-visual video parsing, which the authors note as a natural extension. However, evaluation is limited to the single AVE dataset (4,143 videos, 28 categories), and the margin over the strongest comparable baseline (DG-SCT, 82.2% → 83.1%) is modest, leaving open whether the graph-based interaction provides genuine gains over simpler fusion strategies at scale. The weakly-supervised result (78.8%) outperforming several earlier fully-supervised methods is encouraging but does not fully resolve the gap between weakly- and fully-supervised performance. Broader validation on larger or more diverse audio-visual benchmarks would be needed to confirm the generalizability of the semantic-graph approach.
