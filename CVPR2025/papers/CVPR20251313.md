## Problem

Transferable targeted adversarial attacks remain difficult because generated examples overfit to surrogate models and fail to mislead other architectures into specific target classes. The recent Clean Feature Mixup (CFM) method improves transferability by injecting random clean features into intermediate layers, but these features are attack-agnostic and lack any optimization toward disrupting the adversarial example, leaving significant room for improvement.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251313/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a clean RGB image, a target class label (distinct from the true label), and a pre-trained surrogate model, and produces a single adversarial example under an ℓ∞ perturbation budget of 16/255. No additional training dataset is required; the attack runs for 300 iterations using MI-FGSM with logit loss. Perturbations are applied only to deep layers whose feature map size is at most 1/16 of the input image size, and for pure ViTs, to all fully-connected layer outputs.

## Method

FTM formulates a min-max optimization that simultaneously minimizes the targeted loss over the adversarial image and maximizes it over learnable feature perturbations Δz added element-wise to intermediate layer outputs. The key efficiency mechanism is a momentum-based stochastic update: each iteration initializes Δz from the previous iteration's values, performs only a single gradient step, and updates only a randomly selected subset of layers (probability p=0.1), so the perturbation gradient is computed jointly with the image gradient in one forward/backward pass. The optimized perturbations are further mixed with random clean features (inheriting CFM's augmentation) to produce the final perturbed feature fed forward. An ensemble variant (FTM-E) creates multiple independently FTM-perturbed copies of the surrogate model and combines them, yielding additional transferability gains at modest extra cost.

## Results

On the ImageNet-compatible dataset (1,000 images, ε=16/255, 300 iterations), RDI-FTM-E achieves 79.5% average targeted attack success rate across 10 CNN targets when using RN-50 as the source model, surpassing the previous state-of-the-art RDI-CFM (74.6%) by 4.9%. When using Inc-v3 as the source, RDI-FTM-E reaches 51.2% average success rate, a 12.0% absolute improvement over RDI-CFM (39.2%). On 5 transformer-based targets, RDI-FTM-E improves over CFM by 8.0% (RN-50 source) and 6.4% (Inc-v3 source). Computationally, RDI-FTM adds only 10.8% overhead over CFM (1.54s vs 1.39s per image), while RDI-FTM-E costs 2.92s, still far below SI (5.95s) or VT (7.16s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FTM consistently outperforms all 8 baselines across both source models and all 15 target architectures. Against the strongest prior method RDI-CFM, FTM-E gains +4.9% (RN-50 source) and +12.0% (Inc-v3 source) on CNN targets, and +8.0% and +6.4% on transformer targets, respectively. FTM also surpasses image-space augmentation methods: RDI-SI (62.8% avg, RN-50 source), RDI-VT (58.3%), RDI-Admix (58.0%), and ODI (69.2%) all fall well below RDI-FTM (77.4%). The improvement is most pronounced on architecturally dissimilar targets (e.g., IR-v2: 56.9% vs 48.0% for CFM; Inc-v4: 69.3% vs 59.3%), confirming that optimized feature perturbations better prevent surrogate overfitting. In terms of efficiency, RDI-FTM costs only 1.54s per image (10.8% over CFM's 1.39s), while SI, VT, and ODI require 5.95s, 7.16s, and 4.38s respectively. FTM also combines additively with existing techniques: RDI-SI-FTM-E achieves 74.5% average (ensemble source), exceeding RDI-SI-CFM (71.5%) by 3.0%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Clean Feature Mixup (CFM) framework for targeted transfer attacks by replacing its attack-agnostic random clean features with learnable, attack-specific feature perturbations optimized via a momentum-based stochastic update strategy. The overall attack pipeline remains grounded in the MI-FGSM iterative gradient framework with logit loss, which the authors identify as the current most effective backbone for targeted transfer attacks. The key conceptual departure from CFM is the introduction of a min-max optimization over feature perturbations, where the perturbations are jointly optimized with the adversarial example in a single forward-backward pass, and the stochastic layer selection keeps the computational overhead minimal relative to CFM.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Targeted transfer attacks represent a practical security threat to deployed DNN systems, and the 12% absolute improvement over the prior SOTA (CFM) with only ~11% additional compute makes FTM a practically attractive upgrade for adversarial robustness evaluation. The finding that clean features, while crucial for FTM's performance, introduce instability at high update probabilities (p=1.0 drops clean accuracy to 3.8%) reveals a subtle trade-off in feature-level augmentation that may inform future designs. Open questions include whether the learnable perturbations generalize to other attack settings (non-targeted, physical-world), whether the ensemble strategy scales beyond two models, and whether the approach transfers to tasks beyond ImageNet classification.
