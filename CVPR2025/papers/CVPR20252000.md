## Problem

3D Gaussian Splatting's Adaptive Density Control (ADC) densification produces excessively redundant Gaussian points, causing high memory usage, slower rendering, and large storage demands that hinder deployment on resource-constrained devices. The densification process lacks a principled theoretical understanding—existing alternatives rely on heuristics such as modified splitting criteria or opacity-based sampling—so it remains unclear when splitting actually helps and how offspring should be placed to minimize loss.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252000/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes the same inputs as standard 3DGS: multi-view RGB images with known camera poses and a sparse SfM point cloud for initialization. It outputs a compact 3D Gaussian point cloud (roughly 50% fewer primitives than vanilla 3DGS) that supports real-time novel-view rendering via the standard GPU rasterization pipeline. A key implementation detail is that the per-point splitting matrix is only 3×3 (position parameters), enabling analytical eigen-decomposition with O(1) cost per point inside the existing CUDA kernel.

## Method

SteepGS introduces a theoretical framework built around a per-Gaussian 'splitting matrix' S^(i)(θ) that couples the back-propagated loss gradient to the Hessian of each primitive's projected Gaussian kernel. The central result (Theorem 1) decomposes post-splitting loss into mean-shift terms (handled by standard gradient descent) and a splitting characteristic function governed by S^(i)(θ), proving that splitting reduces loss only when S^(i)(θ) is not positive semi-definite—i.e., the point sits in a saddle region. The optimal strategy (Theorem 2, Steepest Density Control) splits each qualifying Gaussian into exactly two offspring, halves their opacity to preserve local density, and displaces them along ±the eigenvector of the smallest eigenvalue of S^(i)(θ), achieving the steepest possible loss descent. In practice, the splitting matrix is computed in parallel using a closed-form Hessian approximation of the projected Gaussian and gradient values already available from backpropagation, with the 3×3 eigen-decomposition solved analytically, all integrated into a customized CUDA kernel.

## Results

SteepGS achieves approximately 50% reduction in Gaussian points compared to original 3DGS (e.g., 1.606M vs. 3.339M on Mip-NeRF 360) while maintaining or improving rendering quality. At the same point budget, SteepGS attains the highest PSNR across all three datasets: 28.734 on Mip-NeRF 360, 23.684 on Tanks & Temples, and 29.963 on Deep Blending. Notably, on Deep Blending SteepGS even surpasses the original 3DGS PSNR (29.963 vs. 29.690) despite using fewer than half the Gaussians. SSIM values are also best or near-best for SteepGS across all benchmarks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SteepGS is compared against three densification baselines at a matched point budget: 3DGS-MCMC [14], Revising-3DGS [3], and a threshold-stopped variant of original 3DGS (3DGS+Thres.). On Mip-NeRF 360, SteepGS achieves PSNR 28.734, outperforming 3DGS-MCMC (28.149) by 0.59 dB, Revising-3DGS (28.085) by 0.65 dB, and 3DGS+Thres. (27.851) by 0.88 dB. The gap is even more pronounced on Tanks & Temples, where SteepGS reaches 23.684 PSNR versus 22.545 for 3DGS-MCMC and 22.339 for Revising-3DGS — a margin of over 1 dB. On Deep Blending, SteepGS (29.963) exceeds all baselines and even the full-budget original 3DGS (29.690). In terms of SSIM, SteepGS is best on Mip-NeRF 360 (0.857) and Tanks & Temples (0.840), and tied for best on Deep Blending (0.905). LPIPS is competitive but not uniformly best; on Deep Blending, 3DGS-MCMC achieves a slightly lower LPIPS (0.237 vs. 0.250), though the difference is marginal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on 3DGS (Kerbl et al., 2023), replacing its heuristic Adaptive Density Control with a theoretically grounded splitting strategy. Conceptually, it draws on the neural architecture splitting literature (S2D, Firefly) from the same research group, transplanting the idea of a 'splitting matrix' that governs optimal offspring placement from network growth to Gaussian primitive densification. It departs from prior densification revisions (3DGS-MCMC, Revising-3DGS) that rely on geometric heuristics or MCMC resampling by reframing the problem through non-convex optimization: splitting is identified as a saddle-point escape mechanism, and the splitting matrix (a per-point Hessian weighted by backpropagated loss gradients) provides a clean, necessary-and-sufficient criterion for when and how to split.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ~50% reduction in Gaussian primitives while matching or exceeding rendering quality is practically significant for deployment on resource-constrained devices (mobile, VR headsets), and the CUDA-integrated implementation makes it immediately deployable without post-training compression. The theoretical framework—particularly the insight that only partial, per-point Hessian information (not the full cross-term Hessian) is needed to identify saddle points—could inform densification strategies in other point-based or kernel-based scene representations. Open questions include whether the framework extends beyond the position parameter (currently dim Θ = 3) to scale, opacity, and color, and whether the fixed eigenvalue threshold (−1e−6) can be made adaptive or learned.
