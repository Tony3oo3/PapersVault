## Problem

Existing text-based 3D human motion editing methods struggle to precisely align generated motions with both the source motion and the language instruction, often producing semantically inconsistent edits. Training-free approaches lack dedicated supervision, and prior trained methods do not explicitly model the relationship between source and edited motions, leaving the model without a clear signal about which frames should change.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251739/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a free-form text instruction (encoded via CLIP ViT-B/32) and a source 3D human motion sequence of F frames, where each frame is a 207-dimensional vector comprising global velocity, global orientation, joint rotations, and local joint positions. It produces an edited motion sequence of F′ frames in the same pose representation, generated through a 300-step DDPM denoising process. As an auxiliary output, the model also predicts per-frame motion similarity quantized into K=3 discrete classes, indicating how much each frame differs from the source.

## Method

SimMotionEdit employs a two-module Motion Diffusion Transformer: a 4-layer condition transformer that jointly processes source motion and CLIP text features to predict motion similarity (the auxiliary task), and an 8-layer diffusion transformer that denoises the edited motion using the enhanced features. The key insight is that predicting *where* the source and edited motions differ forces the condition transformer to intermix text and motion representations in a semantically meaningful way, producing enriched features that the diffusion transformer then leverages for generation. Motion similarity is computed via sliding-window distances in rotation and location spaces, min-max normalized to [0,1], and quantized into 3 classes to avoid overfitting to a single ground-truth edit. Training uses a combined loss of L2 editing loss and cross-entropy auxiliary loss, with noisy samples filtered out using a MotionSNR threshold to ensure the similarity signal remains learnable.

## Results

On the MotionFix test set, SimMotionEdit achieves 70.62% R@1 and 88.12% R@3 in batch retrieval, and 25.49% R@1 / 49.21% R@3 in full test-set retrieval, substantially outperforming the strongest baseline TMED (62.90% R@1 batch; 14.51% R@1 test set). In terms of motion fidelity, SimMotionEdit attains an M-score of −3.210, approaching the ground-truth score of −3.175 and surpassing TMED (−3.512). The method also reduces average rank from 56.63 (TMED) to 23.49 on the full test set, indicating a large improvement in retrieval alignment.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SimMotionEdit outperforms all three baselines on every reported metric. MDM [53], which uses only the text instruction as input without any source-motion conditioning, is the weakest baseline (4.03% batch R@1, 0.10% test-set R@1), confirming that text alone is insufficient for source-aligned editing. MDM-BP [4] adds GPT-based body-part masking to retain unedited regions, improving batch R@1 to 39.10% and test-set R@1 to 8.69%, but still lags far behind. TMED [4], the closest competitor, is a conditional diffusion model trained on the same MotionFix triplets with both source motion and text as inputs; SimMotionEdit surpasses it by 7.72 points on batch R@1 (70.62 vs. 62.90), 10.98 points on test-set R@1 (25.49 vs. 14.51), and 0.302 on M-score (−3.210 vs. −3.512). The paper attributes these gains to the dedicated condition transformer that intermixes text and motion features via the auxiliary motion-similarity-prediction task, combined with the diffusion transformer for denoising.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the MotionFix dataset and task formulation [4], which introduced the first large-scale benchmark for text-based 3D human motion editing with source-text-motion triplets. Architecturally, it adopts the Diffusion Transformer (DiT) [37] as its generative backbone but departs from prior editing methods (MDM, MDM-BP, TMED) by introducing a two-part design: a condition transformer that performs an auxiliary motion similarity prediction task, and a separate diffusion transformer for denoising. The auxiliary task itself is novel in this domain, drawing on multi-task learning principles from image classification and scene parsing, but repurposed to encourage the model to identify which frames require editing before generating the full motion.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The work demonstrates that a carefully designed auxiliary task (quantized motion similarity prediction with SNR-based filtering) can meaningfully improve both alignment and fidelity in motion editing, offering a template for incorporating structured intermediate supervision into diffusion-based editing pipelines. However, evaluation is limited to a single dataset (MotionFix, 6,730 triplets), and the 300-step DDPM inference is impractical for interactive animation workflows. Open questions include whether the auxiliary task generalizes to longer sequences, more diverse editing types, or real-time settings, and whether the condition-diffusion transformer split scales to more complex multi-person or object-interaction scenarios.
