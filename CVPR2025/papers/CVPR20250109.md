## Problem

Existing GZSL methods rely on explicit semantic information (attributes, word vectors) to bridge seen and unseen classes, but in clinical image classification—such as glioma subtyping—defining meaningful semantic attributes is difficult due to tumor heterogeneity, and cross-modal alignment between visual and semantic features degrades performance. This paper exploits the observation that clinical classes are inherently ordered by risk level (e.g., survival time, disease severity) rather than purely categorical, enabling a semantics-free approach to GZSL.

## Input / Output

The method accepts 3D multimodal MR volumes (T1, T1ce, T2, FLAIR; H×W×L×M) for glioma and AD tasks, or 2D retinal images for diabetic retinopathy grading. A pre-trained 3D-ResNet18 (or Swin Transformer for 2D) encoder followed by global average pooling produces a 32-dimensional feature vector per image. The output is a class label over both seen and unseen classes; for unseen classes, features are synthesized at training time by interpolating between neighboring seen-class features plus Gaussian noise, so the classification head is trained in a fully supervised manner without any semantic input.

## Method

The core contribution is an Inter-Class Feature Augmentation (IFA) module that imposes an ordinal constraint on the learned feature space via a pre-defined joint conditional Gaussian distribution model, where class means are parameterized as cumulative shifts (Δ_c) from a starting center, forcing feature distributions to align with clinical risk ordering. An ordinal loss (KL divergence between the real per-class Gaussian and the ordered joint model) and a contrastive distinctiveness loss (weighted by class-pair distance) jointly shape the encoder's feature geometry. For unseen classes, features are generated by averaging randomly sampled features from the two neighboring seen classes plus Gaussian noise, converting GZSL into a supervised problem. Training proceeds in two stages: first, the encoder is trained on seen classes with the ordinal and distinctiveness losses; second, generated unseen-class features are added and the classification head is fine-tuned. The approach requires no semantic information, no cross-modal alignment, and no structural modification to the backbone.

## Results

On the in-house Glioma dataset, IFA achieves the highest HM (71.6%) and AUC (0.928) among all GZSL baselines, with Acc_s=77.3% and Acc_u=66.7%. On ADNI, IFA reaches HM=82.4% and AUC=0.973, outperforming the next-best FREE (HM=76.0%, AUC=0.972). On the APTOS diabetic retinopathy dataset (three unseen-class settings), IFA consistently attains the best HM (66.5%, 56.6%, 70.4%) and AUC (0.954, 0.950, 0.952) across all configurations. All AUC improvements over baselines are statistically significant (Delong test, p<0.05).

## Baselines & Comparisons

IFA outperforms all five semantic-based GZSL baselines on HM and AUC across every dataset and unseen-class configuration. The largest margins appear on Glioma, where IFA's HM of 71.6% exceeds the next-best SD-GZSL (66.8%) by 4.8 points and its AUC of 0.928 surpasses CE-GZSL (0.845) by 0.083. On ADNI, IFA leads FREE (the strongest baseline) by 6.4 HM points (82.4 vs. 76.0) and 0.001 AUC, while also achieving the highest Acc_u (73.6% vs. 64.3%). On APTOS-3, IFA's Acc_u of 64.7% dramatically exceeds all baselines (next-best: SD-GZSL at 47.1%), reflecting the benefit of ordinal interpolation for the middle unseen class. HSVA is consistently the weakest baseline, particularly on APTOS-1 where its Acc_s drops to 39.2%, highlighting the fragility of semantic-visual alignment in fine-grained clinical tasks. All AUC gains are confirmed statistically significant via the Delong test (p<0.05).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in GZSL where semantic information (attributes, word vectors) is treated as essential for bridging seen and unseen classes. Rather than extending embedding-based or generative-based GZSL methods, it repurposes the concept of conditional Gaussian distributions (from ordinal domain adaptation [21]) to impose an ordinal constraint on feature space, replacing semantic conditioning with simple interpolation between neighboring seen-class features. The approach is positioned as a clinical-specific alternative: it exploits the fact that medical diagnostic classes (glioma subtypes, AD stages, DR grades) are inherently ordered by risk, a structural property that general GZSL benchmarks (CUB, AWA) do not possess.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in eliminating the need to define semantic attributes for clinical subtypes—a genuinely difficult problem in oncology where tumor heterogeneity makes image-based semantic descriptions unreliable. However, the method's applicability is tightly constrained to ordered clinical classes with at least one neighboring seen class on each side; boundary classes (e.g., the mildest or most severe grade) remain poorly handled, as the authors acknowledge. Open questions include whether adaptive sampling or partial supervision can extend the approach to boundary cases, and whether the ordinal-Gaussian framework generalizes beyond the three narrow clinical tasks evaluated. The narrow scope (3 datasets, all medical, all ordinal) limits broader impact on the GZSL community.
