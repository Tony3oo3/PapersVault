## Problem

Row-column readout multiplexing in single-photon detector arrays reduces the number of readout lines from n² to 2n but makes photon localizations ambiguous whenever two or more photons are detected within a single integration period. Conventional reconstruction either misattributes counts to all candidate pixels (introducing bias and ghost artifacts) or discards ambiguous frames entirely (introducing high variance and wasting most of the data at practical flux levels). No principled probabilistic framework existed to resolve the spatial locations of multiphoton coincidences from such multiplexed readouts.

## Input / Output

The method consumes T frames of binary row and column readout vectors (R^t, C^t ∈ {0,1}^n) from an n×n SNSPD array, where each frame records which rows and columns registered at least one photon during a fixed integration window. The output is an n×n estimated photon flux image Λ̂, recovered elementwise from per-pixel detection probability estimates q̂ via Λ̂_ij = −log(1 − e^{−q̂_ij}). The readout is inherently lossy: a frame with k active rows and m active columns yields k×m candidate pixel locations, making the localization problem ill-posed for any frame where both row and column sums exceed one.

## Method

The paper formulates multiphoton coincidence resolution as an approximate maximum-likelihood inverse problem. For each ambiguous readout frame, it computes conditional probabilities g_k that the frame arose from a specific photon event (e.g., photons at pixels {1,4} vs. {2,3} in a 2×2 array), estimated from the unbiased single-photon estimator applied to unambiguous frames. These conditional probabilities decompose the total count of ambiguous frames into weighted contributions to each candidate pixel, yielding closed-form approximate MLE expressions for per-pixel detection probabilities that redistribute counts proportionally rather than uniformly or by discarding. The estimator resolves up to four coincident photons; for larger arrays the number of ambiguous readout types grows combinatorially, so terms beyond 4-photon coincidences are truncated, introducing a small bias at high flux. No spatial priors are imposed, isolating the gain to better exploitation of ambiguous readouts; Monte Carlo simulations show 3–4 dB PSNR improvement over conventional methods, a ~4× reduction in required frames for a target MSE, and agreement with the Cramér-Rao bound over a wider flux range.

## Results

The multiphoton estimator (ME) achieves 3–4 dB higher PSNR than both the naive estimator (NE) and single-photon estimator (SPE) at their respective optimal incident fluxes (Fig. 5). At a fixed 3 PPF setting, the ME outperforms the NE by ~11 dB and the SPE by ~6 dB (Fig. 3). The ME reduces the number of readout frames needed to reach a target MSE of 0.01 from 100,000 (SPE) to 25,000, a ~4× reduction, while the NE does not reach this target within the tested range (Fig. 6). The optimal operating point shifts from 0.45 PPF (NE) and 0.83 PPF (SPE) to 1.4 PPF for the 4-photon ME, enabling higher photon count rates (Fig. 4).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three estimators: the naive estimator (NE), which implements the conventional reconstruction from Wollman et al. [37] by assigning equal probability to all candidate pixels in ambiguous frames; the single-photon estimator (SPE), which discards all ambiguous frames; and the proposed multiphoton estimator (ME), which probabilistically resolves up to 4 coincident photons. The ME dominates on every reported metric: it achieves 3–4 dB higher PSNR than both NE and SPE at their optimal fluxes (Fig. 5), requires ~4× fewer frames to reach a target MSE of 0.01 compared to SPE (25,000 vs. 100,000; Fig. 6), and operates at a 1.4 PPF optimal point versus 0.83 for SPE and 0.45 for NE (Fig. 4). The NE produces visible horizontal and vertical streak artifacts from misattributed photon counts, while the SPE suffers from high variance due to discarding ~85% of frames at 3 PPF. The ME also matches the Cramér-Rao bound for detection probability estimation across a wider range of incident flux values than either baseline (Fig. 7), with the 4-photon variant tracking the CRB most closely. The only scenario where the advantage narrows is at very high PPF (4 PPF), where the ME's PSNR degrades by ~3 dB due to unmodeled 5+ photon coincidences, though it still outperforms both baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the row-column readout multiplexing architecture for SNSPD arrays introduced by Allman et al. and demonstrated at kilopixel scale by Wollman et al., extending the thermally coupled imager line of work toward 400k-pixel cameras. It departs from prior multiplexed-readout imaging (PET, gamma-ray, ultrasound) by formulating the specific combinatorics of individual photon events under pixel saturation as a probabilistic inverse problem, rather than modeling optical/electrical system properties. The closest prior work is van den Berg et al.'s group-testing-inspired deterministic coincidence resolution for SPAD TDC readouts; this paper instead provides a probabilistic MLE framework suited to intensity imaging where small error probability is tolerable, and explicitly notes that 'the explicit study of individual photon events measured by single-photon detectors and their impact on the multiplexed readouts does not yet exist.'

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ~4× reduction in required integration time and 3–4 dB PSNR gain are practically significant for photon-starved applications (deep-space imaging, biological imaging, low-light lidar) where SNSPD arrays are the natural sensor but cryogenic heat-load constraints force multiplexed readout. The CRB-matching result validates the theoretical framework and suggests the estimator is near-optimal within its modeling assumptions. Key limitations include the restriction to ≤4-photon coincidences (introducing bias at higher fluxes), linear computational scaling with array size, and dependence on single-photon frame quality for the initial probability estimates. Open questions include extending to arbitrary coincidence counts, other multiplexing topologies (e.g., the single-bus thermally coupled imager), and incorporating spatial priors or learned models to push beyond the current MLE framework.
