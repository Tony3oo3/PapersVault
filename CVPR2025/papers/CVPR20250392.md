## Problem

Existing video understanding benchmarks evaluate models on complete videos in offline settings, where all frames are available at query time. This fails to capture the critical temporal awareness required for real-world online video understanding, where a model must reason dynamically based on the timestamp at which a question is posed—tracing back past events, perceiving ongoing actions, or withholding responses until sufficient future evidence accumulates. No prior benchmark systematically evaluates these three distinct temporal reasoning modes.

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes 644 videos (minutes to 30 minutes, 7 domains) with text queries posed at specific timestamps, producing accuracy scores across 12 tasks. For Backward Tracing and Real-Time Visual Perception, videos are segmented from start to query time and fed as offline inputs with multiple-choice questions (2–5 options). For Forward Active Responding, a multiple-triggering pipeline densely queries the model at successive timestamps, scoring both timely clue detection and avoidance of premature hallucinated answers. The 2,814 QA pairs include both multiple-choice and a smaller set of open-ended questions, all with human-verified event-level timestamps.

## Method

OVO-Bench introduces a three-mode taxonomy for online video understanding—Backward Tracing (recall past events), Real-Time Visual Perception (interpret current frames), and Forward Active Responding (delay response until future evidence suffices)—and constructs 2,814 meta-annotated samples via a hybrid pipeline combining semi-automated MLLM generation (GPT-4o, Gemini-1.5-Pro) with meticulous human curation of timestamps and options. The evaluation pipeline simulates streaming by segmenting videos up to each query timestamp for offline models, and for Forward Active Responding employs a multiple-triggering scheme that queries the model at dense temporal intervals, rewarding correct delayed responses while penalizing early hallucinations. Options are generated through rule-based, visually grounded transformations of correct answers to prevent information leakage, then shuffled after human review. The benchmark evaluates eleven Video-LLMs (proprietary, open-source offline, and online streaming models) plus human agents, revealing that even top offline models achieve only ~63% overall versus 92.8% for humans, and that dedicated streaming models perform substantially worse than their offline counterparts.

## Results

On OVO-Bench, the best-performing model, Gemini 1.5 Pro (offline), achieves 63.00% overall accuracy, still trailing Human Agents (92.81%) by nearly 30 points. Among online Video-LLMs, Dispider leads at 41.78% overall, while Flash-VStream-7B scores only 33.61% and VideoLLM-online-8B drops to 20.79% on the tasks it covers. The largest single-task gap appears in Hallucination Detection (HLD), where even Gemini 1.5 Pro reaches only 52.64% versus 91.37% for humans, and in Forward Active Responding, where all models score below 60%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Offline Video-LLMs substantially outperform online counterparts on OVO-Bench: Gemini 1.5 Pro (63.00%) and GPT-4o (59.54%) lead all models, while the best online model Dispider (41.78%) trails by over 21 points, and Flash-VStream-7B (33.61%) and VideoLLM-online-8B (20.79%) fall even further behind. Within the offline group, Gemini 1.5 Pro edges out GPT-4o by ~3.5 points overall, with the largest margin in Real-Time Visual Perception (69.32% vs. 64.46%) and Backward Tracing (62.54% vs. 60.75%). Among open-source offline models, Qwen2-VL-72B (56.27%) is the strongest, but all open-source models score 5–10 points below the proprietary leaders. The blind LLM baseline (GPT-4-turbo, 27.90% on RTVP) confirms that visual input is essential, though it surprisingly scores 53.82% on Backward Tracing, suggesting some questions can be answered from text alone. Across all models, Forward Active Responding is the weakest category (best: 57.15% for Gemini 1.5 Pro), highlighting that no current model can reliably delay and adapt responses to future visual evidence.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of offline video QA benchmarks (Video-MME, EgoSchema, LVBench) and early streaming video understanding efforts (VStream-QA, StreamingBench, VideoLLM-online, Flash-VStream). It departs from prior streaming benchmarks by introducing temporal awareness as a first-class evaluation axis: the timestamp at which a question is posed determines which information the model should use, and the model must decide whether to respond immediately or defer. The Forward Active Responding mode is explicitly framed as the first evaluation requiring continuous adaptation to future visual input, extending the streaming QA paradigm beyond immediate-response settings. The 'Chain-of-Time' conceptualization draws an analogy to Chain-of-Thought reasoning in LLMs, repurposing that idea for temporal video reasoning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

OVO-Bench reveals a substantial and previously unquantified gap: even the strongest offline models (Gemini 1.5 Pro at 63%) trail human performance (92.8%) by nearly 30 points, and purpose-built streaming models (Flash-VStream at 33.6%) perform dramatically worse than their offline counterparts, suggesting that real-time constraints severely degrade understanding. The Forward Active Responding results—where all models struggle, with even Gemini scoring only 57% on Clues Reveal Responding—expose a fundamental inability in current Video-LLMs to withhold and adapt responses, a capability essential for real-world always-on agents. Open questions include whether the gap is architectural (streaming vs. holistic processing), whether inference latency (~4s for 64 frames) can be reduced without sacrificing accuracy, and whether the multiple-triggering evaluation pipeline fairly captures the forward-responding requirement or is too demanding for current model capabilities.
