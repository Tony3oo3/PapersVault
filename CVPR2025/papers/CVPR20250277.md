## Problem

Prior backdoor attack research on large vision-language models assumes that training and testing data follow similar distributions, yet in practice the instruction datasets used for fine-tuning differ substantially from real-world user queries in both visual style and textual density. This paper introduces backdoor domain generalization as a new evaluation dimension, asking how effectively backdoor attacks transfer when the attacker's self-constructed instruction set and the user's test set exhibit significant distributional shifts in both image and text domains.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250277/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The victim LVLM receives a paired input of an RGB image and a natural-language instruction (e.g., an image captioning prompt) and produces a free-form text response. The backdoor attack modifies both modalities simultaneously: a visual trigger (a yellow oval patch) is blended into a specific image region, and a textual trigger (special symbols like [, ], *, {, }, <, >) is inserted near semantically critical words in the instruction. Evaluation is conducted on image captioning using COCO and Flickr30K as held-out test sets, with poisoning rates as low as 0.2% and clean performance measured via CIDEr.

## Method

MABA (Multimodal Attribution Backdoor Attack) is built on two empirical insights: (1) domain-agnostic, distinctive trigger patterns generalize better than model-adaptive or content-aware ones, and (2) placing triggers in regions where the model's clean-semantic attention is suppressed makes the trigger dominant in the decision process. For image trigger placement, MABA applies submodular subset selection (Chen et al., ICLR 2024) to segment the image into regions, computes clean and poisoned attribution masks using the correct and target answers respectively, and selects the difference mask (clean-important but not poisoned-important regions) as the trigger location. For text, a language model identifies key word indices and special symbols are inserted at those positions. The visual trigger is blended into the selected region with a 50% opacity parameter, and the combined multimodal trigger is injected at the poisoning rate during instruction fine-tuning of the cross-modality fusion layers. This yields a 36.4% improvement in ASR-G over unimodal BadNets (0.318→0.682) and over 97% ASR at just 0.2% poisoning across OpenFlamingo, BLIP-2, and Otter.

## Results

On the cross-domain backdoor generalization benchmark (ASR-G), MABA improves BadNets' mean ASR-G from 0.318 to 0.682 (+36.4%) across OpenFlamingo, Otter, and BLIP-2 under Realism and Expressionism image domain shifts. MABA* extends DualKey with multimodal triggers, raising its mean ASR-G from 0.638 to 0.834 (+19.6%). Under a more realistic 350K-sample fine-tuning setting, all evaluated attacks achieve up to 97% ASR at a mere 0.2% poisoning rate, demonstrating that LVLMs remain highly vulnerable even in low-resource attack scenarios.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MABA substantially outperforms the unimodal BadNets baseline, improving mean ASR-G from 0.318 to 0.682 (+36.4%), with the largest gains on OpenFlamingo (0.13→0.81 under Realism) and Otter (0.15→0.82 under Realism). MABA* similarly surpasses the multimodal DualKey baseline, lifting mean ASR-G from 0.638 to 0.834 (+19.6%), achieving perfect 1.00 ASR-G on OpenFlamingo and Otter under both domain shifts. However, the global Blended attack retains the highest mean ASR-G (0.986), though its fixed full-image trigger severely limits stealth compared to MABA's localized, attribution-guided placement. Notably, BLIP-2 consistently shows the lowest ASR-G across all methods (e.g., 0.21 for MABA vs. 0.81 for OpenFlamingo under Realism), suggesting its architecture provides greater inherent robustness to backdoor generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the backdoor attack literature on LVLMs (BadCLIP, VL-Trojan, AnyDoor) by shifting the evaluation from the standard static-distribution assumption to a cross-domain setting where training and testing data diverge in both visual and textual spaces. It builds on classical trigger paradigms—static patches (BadNets, Blended), content-aware perturbations (WaNet, LowFrequency), and model-adaptive triggers (DualKey, GCG)—but repositions them under a new generalization lens. The attribution-based trigger placement draws on submodular subset selection (Chen et al., 2024) and LVLM shape-bias observations, repurposing interpretability tools for adversarial purposes rather than analysis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that most image-based backdoors collapse under realistic domain shifts (e.g., BadNets drops to ASR-G ≈ 0.11) while simple blended triggers remain robust (≈ 0.99) reframes how practitioners should think about backdoor stealth and persistence in deployed LVLMs. The counterintuitive result that answer-domain shifts can *improve* attack generalization challenges the assumption that any distributional mismatch is purely detrimental. Open questions remain: the paper does not explore defenses, does not test across diverse LVLM architectures beyond three, and the 97%-at-0.2% result, while alarming, is demonstrated on a single task (image captioning) and may not transfer to more complex instruction-following scenarios.
