## Problem

Modern Diffusion Transformers allocate a fixed, large compute budget to every denoising step, despite early steps primarily establishing low-frequency structure and later steps refining high-frequency details. Prior attempts to vary compute across steps—such as ensembles of expert denoisers or model cascades—require managing multiple models at inference, increasing memory and preventing knowledge sharing. The paper asks whether a single DiT can be made to operate at different compute levels per step without quality loss.

## Input / Output

The model takes random Gaussian noise plus a conditioning signal (class label for ImageNet, or a text prompt for T2I/T2V) and produces a generated image (256×256 or 1024×1024) or video (256 frames × 384 × 704). Unusually, the tokenization granularity of the input changes mid-generation: early denoising steps process the latent as a shorter token sequence (larger patch size, e.g., 4×4), while later steps switch to the original finer patch size (e.g., 2×2), so the same model sees different sequence lengths at different timesteps.

## Method

FlexiDiT converts a pre-trained DiT into a flexible model by modifying only the lightweight embedding and de-embedding layers (via pseudo-inverse bilinear interpolation projections) and adding patch-size embeddings plus patch-size-dependent layer normalization, enabling the same Transformer backbone to process images at multiple patch sizes. Two training variants are offered: (i) full fine-tuning with shared parameters when original training data is available, and (ii) frozen-backbone LoRA adaptation trained via distillation from the powerful model's predictions when data is inaccessible. At inference, a scheduler assigns the weak (larger-patch, fewer-token) model to the first T_weak denoising steps and the powerful (original-patch) model to the remaining steps, reducing total FLOPs by 40–75% depending on modality. For classifier-free guidance, the weak model's conditional prediction replaces the traditional unconditional prediction as the guidance signal, further improving quality without deploying an extra model.

## Results

On ImageNet class-conditioned generation (DiT-XL/2), FlexiDiT achieves FID-50k of 2.25, matching the pre-trained DiT-XL/2 baseline (FID-50k 2.27) while enabling >40% FLOPs reduction. For text-to-image generation, the T2I Transf. (PIXART) model matches baseline FID (14.71 vs 14.75) and CLIP score (25.58 vs 25.60) at only 58% compute, and the 1.7B Emu model matches FID (26.10 vs 26.00) and CLIP (26.07 vs 26.05) at 53% compute. For text-to-video generation, FlexiDiT achieves up to 75% compute savings with no significant VBench degradation. A human study on Emu-generated 1024×1024 images confirms annotators prefer FlexiDiT outputs (43% win, 41.5% tie, 15.5% lose) over the baseline at matched FLOPs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the original pre-trained DiT model running all denoising steps at full compute (the 'powerful' patch size). FlexiDiT matches or marginally exceeds baseline quality at substantially reduced compute: on ImageNet, FID-50k is 2.25 vs 2.27 at 100% compute, and the gap remains negligible down to ~60% compute. For T2I, both PIXART and Emu models maintain FID, CLIP, and VQA scores within 0.1–0.2 of the full-compute baseline at 53–58% compute. The paper also notes that training-free acceleration methods (e.g., adaptive caching [32], temporal attention decomposition [37], pyramid attention broadcast [58]) achieve significantly lower compute savings before noticeable quality degradation, suggesting that learned compute allocation via FlexiDiT's fine-tuning is more effective than heuristic rules. In the human study, FlexiDiT at 50 steps (10 powerful + 40 weak) beats the baseline at 18 uniform steps (matched FLOPs) with 43% wins vs 15.5% losses, confirming perceptual quality is preserved or improved at equal compute.

## Relation to Prior Work

FlexiDiT builds directly on the DiT architecture [43] and the flexible tokenization idea from FlexiViT [7], repurposing variable patch sizes from a single-step inference context into a dynamic per-step compute allocation strategy for diffusion. It departs from prior approaches to step-varying compute—such as expert denoisers [4] and model cascades [49]—by using a single model with minimal LoRA-based fine-tuning rather than multiple separate networks, and it adapts the 'guiding with a weaker model' insight [34] to use the weak model's conditional (not unconditional) prediction as the guidance signal. The spectral autoregression framing [17] and the observation that early denoising steps encode low-frequency structure [10] motivate the core design choice of allocating fewer tokens to early steps.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: 40%+ FLOPs reduction for image generation and up to 75% for video with no measurable quality loss, achieved with less than 5% of the original pre-training compute for fine-tuning. This makes high-quality diffusion inference significantly more accessible for deployment at scale, and the modality-agnostic design (images, video, and in principle audio/3D) suggests broad applicability. Open questions include whether spatial and temporal weak models can be combined iteratively within a single sample, how the approach interacts with very few-step solvers (e.g., 4–8 step distilled models), and whether the guidance-from-weak-model trick generalizes to non-DiT architectures. The paper does not explore adversarial robustness or safety implications of the LoRA fine-tuning in detail.
