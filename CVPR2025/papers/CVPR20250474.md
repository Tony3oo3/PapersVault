## Problem

Existing jailbreak attacks on text-to-image diffusion models rely on adversarial text prompts that are often visually suspicious and contain explicit NSFW concepts, limiting their real-world impact. The increasing adoption of the Image Prompt Adapter (IP-Adapter), which conditions diffusion models on image prompts, introduces a previously unexplored attack vector: imperceptible image-space adversarial examples that can be covertly uploaded to the web and unknowingly used by benign users to trigger NSFW outputs, thereby hijacking users and misleading the public to blame the service provider.

## Input / Output

The method takes a benign source image x_b and an NSFW reference image x_nsfw, along with white-box access to the open-source image encoder (CLIP ViT variant or ArcFace-based face model) embedded in the target IP-Adapter. It produces a single adversarial image x_adv in lossless PNG format, constrained to an l∞-norm perturbation of ε=8/255 relative to x_b, ensuring visual indistinguishability. For mixed-type IP-Adapters (face-related tasks), two separate AEs are crafted—one against CLIP and one against the face recognition model—rather than a single unified perturbation.

## Method

The core technique, Attacking Encoder Only (AEO), exploits the IP-Adapter's two-stage workflow: an extraction stage that encodes the image prompt via a pre-trained encoder, followed by an injection stage that feeds those features into the denoiser. Rather than attacking the full diffusion pipeline, AEO aligns the adversarial image's encoder features with those of an NSFW reference image in the encoder's feature space, solved via 500-step PGD with either MSE or Cosine Similarity as the distance metric. The key insight is that since the IP-Adapter is trained to faithfully reconstruct images from encoder features, aligning features in the encoder space is sufficient to steer the denoiser toward NSFW outputs without needing access to the diffusion model itself. For mixed-type IP-Adapters, AEO constructs AEs independently against CLIP and the face recognition model, and experiments show the optimal surrogate encoder choice varies by target model. The paper further evaluates existing defenses (prompt filters, output filters, concept erasing via ESD-u and SLD) and demonstrates their inherent limitation: none can correct the unaligned feature-space behavior induced by AEs, motivating the use of adversarially fine-tuned CLIP (FARE) as a more effective mitigation.

## Results

On the text-to-image task, AEO (COS) with weight factor 1.0 promotes Nudity rate to at least 54.1% and NSFW rate to at least 77.0% across all five T2I-IP-DMs (SD-v1-5-Global, SD-v1-5-Plus, SDXL-Global, SDXL-Plus, Kolors-Plus), compared to benign baselines of 0.0–2.0% Nudity and 3.6–7.0% NSFW. On virtual try-on (IDM-VTON), AEO (COS) achieves 56.2% Nudity rate and 83.3% NSFW rate despite the model's additional GarmentNet module. On image inpainting, AEO (COS) boosts ISM from 0.05–0.07 (benign) to 0.29–0.61 and CLIP Score from 0.46–0.48 to 0.58–0.65, approaching the malicious-image baseline (ISM 0.41–0.58).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates three defense baselines against the AEO attack on SD-v1-5-Global. SLD (Schramowski et al., CVPR 2023) achieves the lowest Nudity and NSFW rates among concept-erasing methods but at a significant cost: it substantially degrades Benign CLIP Score, meaning it partially ignores the image prompt and harms legitimate users. ESD-u (Gandikota et al., ICCV 2023) suppresses the sexual concept while preserving fidelity on benign prompts, but its effectiveness degrades as the IP-Adapter weight factor increases, revealing that fine-tuning non-cross-attention modules alone cannot erase the global nudity concept injected through cross-attention. FARE (Schlarmann et al., ICML 2024), an adversarial fine-tuning method for CLIP, achieves a security-fidelity balance comparable to ESD-u and NP but with a key advantage: its performance remains stable across weight factors, and it is the only defense that promotes Adversarial CLIP Score, allowing hijacked benign users to still receive outputs faithful to their (benign) image prompt rather than the NSFW reference. All three defenses, however, share an inherent limitation: they cannot fully correct the unaligned behavior induced by AEs because the adversarial image's encoder features remain close to the NSFW reference rather than the benign prompt.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the jailbreaking literature for text-to-image diffusion models, which has focused almost exclusively on adversarial text prompts [7, 25, 36, 38, 39], by identifying the IP-Adapter's image conditioning pathway as a new and more stealthy attack surface. The attack methodology (AEO) repurposes standard adversarial example construction [26] but targets only the upstream image encoder (CLIP or face recognition models) rather than the full diffusion pipeline, exploiting the IP-Adapter's architectural dependency on encoder features. The 'hijacking attack' framing departs from prior red-team settings where the adversary directly queries the model, introducing a social-engineering dimension where benign users unwittingly trigger NSFW outputs. On the defense side, the paper builds on concept erasing [19, 33] and adversarial training [32] but demonstrates their inherent limitations when the attack operates at the encoder level.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The IP-Adapter is increasingly integrated into production text-to-image services, and this paper demonstrates that its reliance on open-source encoders creates a single point of failure that bypasses downstream safety mechanisms (safety checkers, concept erasing). The finding that existing defenses cannot correct the feature-space misalignment induced by AEs—only reject or suppress outputs—raises fundamental questions about layered defense architectures in conditional generation. Open questions include whether adversarial training (FARE) generalizes to unseen threat models, whether fine-tuning the IP-Adapter with a robust encoder preserves fidelity, and how to design evaluation frameworks that account for the social-impact dimension of hijacking attacks. The practical demonstration of jailbreaking real-world services (IDM-VTON) underscores the urgency of addressing this vulnerability before wider deployment.
