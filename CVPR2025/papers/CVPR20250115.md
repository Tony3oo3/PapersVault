## Problem

Existing adversarial perturbation methods for protecting images from malicious diffusion-based editing focus on canceling the edit effect (reducing prompt fidelity), which is inherently unstable due to the diversity of editing prompts. The paper identifies that a successful edit also requires image integrity—preserving the subject's identity—and that this second requirement has been entirely overlooked as a defense axis, leaving a gap where biometric information survives even when the edit partially succeeds.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250115/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single human portrait RGB image (evaluated on 2,000 images from CelebA-HQ spanning diverse demographics) and produces an imperceptibly perturbed version under an L∞ budget of 0.02. The perturbed image is then consumed by a text-guided diffusion editing model (InstructPix2Pix) with a natural-language prompt; the defense goal is that the edited output is biometrically unrecognizable relative to the source, even though the edit instruction itself may be visually satisfied.

## Method

FACELOCK optimizes adversarial perturbations by integrating a facial recognition model (CVLFace/AdaFace) directly into the diffusion encode-decode loop, so the perturbation is shaped to survive the diffusion sampling process rather than being generated independently. The objective (Eq. 5) jointly maximizes (i) the facial recognition similarity loss between the decoded edited image and the source, and (ii) a feature-embedding disparity loss computed by a pretrained CNN (AlexNet, SqueezeNet, or VGG) over high-level semantic features of the face region. Optimization is performed via projected gradient descent under an L∞ constraint. This design differs from prior work (PhotoGuard, EditShield) that attacks the encoder or cross-attention to block the edit; FACELOCK permits the edit to proceed but ensures the subject's biometric identity is destroyed in the output, making the defense agnostic to the specific editing prompt.

## Results

On CelebA-HQ with InstructPix2Pix as the editing model, FACELOCK achieves the lowest facial recognition similarity (FR) score of 0.315±0.109, roughly halving the next-best baseline (PhotoGuard at 0.590±0.264) and indicating that edited images are biometrically unrecognizable. FACELOCK also attains the highest LPIPS score (0.436±0.065) and the lowest CLIP-I score (0.648±0.089), confirming strong perceptual and semantic disruption of the edited output. Across perturbation budgets from 0.01 to 0.05, FR drops monotonically from 0.557 to 0.196, with the chosen budget of 0.02 balancing protection strength against visual imperceptibility.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FACELOCK decisively outperforms PhotoGuard (Salman et al., 2023) on the key biometric metric: FR drops from 0.590 to 0.315 (a 47% relative reduction), while LPIPS improves from 0.403 to 0.436; PhotoGuard does achieve a marginally lower CLIP-S (0.108 vs. 0.114), but this reflects only a slightly stronger suppression of prompt fidelity, not better identity protection. Against EditShield (Chen et al., 2023), FACELOCK more than halves the FR score (0.315 vs. 0.641) and raises LPIPS from 0.382 to 0.436, while EditShield's lower PSNR (17.74 vs. 17.11) offers no compensating advantage in biometric defense. The classical CW L2 attack (Carlini & Wagner, 2017) is the weakest baseline on both FR (0.725) and LPIPS (0.247), confirming that generic adversarial perturbations designed for classification do not transfer to diffusion-based editing defense. Overall, FACELOCK wins on the two most task-relevant metrics (FR and LPIPS) by the largest margins, while remaining competitive on prompt-fidelity metrics, demonstrating that its biometric-erasure objective is orthogonal to and more effective than the edit-cancellation objective pursued by prior methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant adversarial-defense line (PhotoGuard, EditShield, Glaze, Nightshade) that aims to *cancel* editing effects by perturbing the source image so the diffusion model fails to apply the instruction. Instead, FACELOCK reframes the objective: it accepts that the edit will be applied but optimizes perturbations so that biometric identity is destroyed in the output, integrating the CVLFace/AdaFace recognition model directly into the diffusion sampling loop as an adversary. The optimization itself is standard PGD, but the loss combines facial-recognition similarity with a feature-embedding distance (via pretrained CNNs) rather than pixel-level or encoder-latent objectives used by prior work. The paper also critically re-examines widely used editing metrics (CLIP-S, SSIM, PSNR), arguing they are easily gamed, and substitutes LPIPS and a facial-recognition similarity score.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The shift from 'prevent the edit' to 'erase identity after the edit' is a more robust and prompt-agnostic defense strategy, addressing a real and growing privacy threat (malicious portrait editing). The critique of CLIP/SSIM/PSNR as misleading evaluation metrics is a useful contribution for the broader image-editing community. However, the method is limited to single-portrait images, is evaluated primarily on InstructPix2Pix, and the authors note that emerging architectures (e.g., rectified flows) may require re-derivation. Open questions include extending to multi-person scenes, adapting to new generative backbones, and whether the biometric-erasure objective generalizes beyond facial features to other identity cues (voice, gait, etc.).
