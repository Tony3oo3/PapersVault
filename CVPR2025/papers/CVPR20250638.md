## Problem

Conventional discriminative MIL approaches for WSI classification suffer from data imbalance (positive instances vastly outnumbered by negatives), sensitivity to noise from non-informative patches, and inadequate feature aggregation that fails to capture inter-patch dependencies. No generative framework has been applied to WSI classification, leaving these challenges unaddressed by existing methods that operate within a unified discriminative paradigm.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250638/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes pre-extracted patch embeddings (256×256 patches at 20× magnification, encoded offline by ViT/MoCo V3 or CTransPath/SRCL) organized as a bag of N instances per WSI, with only a single bag-level label for supervision. It outputs a class probability distribution over K+1 classes (binary or multi-class) by iteratively denoising a 1-D signal initialized from the prior prediction ρθ, reformulating classification as a one-dimensional generative task rather than a standard discriminative scoring.

## Method

MExD combines a Dynamic Mixture-of-Experts (Dyn-MoE) aggregator with a Diffusion Classifier (Diff-C). Dyn-MoE routes N instance embeddings through K+1 expert sub-branches (one per positive subtype plus one negative expert), each using a 2-class router MLP to select and retain only top-α scored instances, producing sparse class-centric latent features with confidence scores (expert insights gα) and a prior prediction ρθ from the sparsified bag. Diff-C then performs forward diffusion on a one-hot encoded label using ρθ as the conditional expectation (following CARD), and reverse denoising via a 3-layer MLP conditioned on the confidence-weighted expert insights, iteratively refining from noisy ρθ to the final class distribution. Training proceeds in two stages: first optimizing Dyn-MoE with a joint cross-entropy and expert-branch loss, then training the denoising network with a standard noise-prediction loss, reducing required denoising steps from 400 to 200 compared to the unconditioned baseline.

## Results

MExD achieves state-of-the-art performance across all three benchmarks with both feature extractors. With CTransPath, it reaches 97.29% F1 / 97.48% ACC / 98.87% AUC on Camelyon16, 96.51% F1 / 96.53% ACC / 98.13% AUC on TCGA-NSCLC, and 75.17% F1 / 76.13% ACC / 88.08% AUC on BRACS. With ViT (MoCo V3), it attains 96.49% F1 on Camelyon16, 95.87% F1 on TCGA-NSCLC, and 80.10% F1 on BRACS, consistently outperforming all 12 baselines by 1–6 percentage points depending on the dataset and metric.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MExD outperforms all 12 baselines on every dataset and metric under both feature extractors. On Camelyon16 (CTransPath), it leads the closest competitor PAMIL by 1.19% F1 (97.29 vs. 96.10) and IBMIL by 2.27% F1 (97.29 vs. 95.02). On TCGA-NSCLC, MExD surpasses MambaMIL (the strongest baseline at 94.23% F1) by 2.28% and CLAM-SB by 2.34%. On BRACS, the gains are largest: with CTransPath, MExD beats ACMIL by 2.29% F1 (75.17 vs. 72.88), and with ViT it outperforms IBMIL by 5.36% F1 (80.10 vs. 74.74) and TransMIL by 6.06% F1 (80.10 vs. 74.04). The compatibility study (Table 4) further shows that plugging Dyn-MoE into simple pooling baselines yields up to 14.04% F1 improvement (MeanPooling: 74.99→89.03), confirming the aggregator's broad applicability beyond the full MExD pipeline.

## Relation to Prior Work

MExD departs from the dominant discriminative MIL paradigm for WSI classification by reformulating the problem as a conditional generative task. It builds on CARD's conditional diffusion framework (which uses a predicted conditional expectation to guide forward diffusion) and DDPM's denoising objective, but repurposes both for 1-D class-distribution generation rather than 2-D image synthesis. The Dyn-MoE aggregator borrows architectural components from TransMIL (TPT and PPEG) while introducing a sparse routing mechanism inspired by sparsely-gated MoE layers in language models, creating a hybrid that has no direct predecessor in the WSI literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first demonstration that a generative diffusion model can serve as a WSI classifier, opening a new design axis beyond discriminative aggregation. The MoE-based sparsification directly targets the data-imbalance problem that plagues MIL in pathology, and the diffusion process provides a principled mechanism for uncertainty quantification (validated via PAvPU and hypothesis testing). Open questions include whether the generative framing scales to even larger or multi-modal WSIs, whether the two-stage training pipeline is necessary, and whether the gains over strong discriminative baselines (1–3%) justify the added architectural complexity in clinical deployment settings.
