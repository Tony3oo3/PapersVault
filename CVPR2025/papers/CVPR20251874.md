## Problem

Existing graph-based shape matching methods find minimum cost cycles in product graphs, which introduces a bias towards shorter paths (fewer edges) and can leave object parts unmatched. Additionally, the best-performing conjugate product graph approach (Roetzer et al.) requires 10–20 minutes per shape pair due to the large graph size, limiting practical use. The paper asks whether a different cycle objective can simultaneously eliminate the length bias, support higher-order geometric regularisation, and run orders of magnitude faster.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251874/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a cyclic chain graph C (a 2D contour, a SpiderCurve discretising one 3D shape, or a 2D object outline) and a directed graph Y (a 3D surface mesh with each undirected edge split into two directed edges, or a road network), and constructs their strong product graph P = C ⊠ Y. A user-defined distortion bound k limits how many edges of Y can be compressed into a single vertex of C. The output is a cyclic path in P (or its conjugate) that visits every component of C, yielding shape correspondences or, in the GPS-art setting, a closed track on the road network. Typical resolutions are ~3500 vertices for the 3D shape and ~500 for the 2D contour.

## Method

The core substitution is replacing the minimum cost cycle objective (sum of edge costs) with a minimum cost-to-time ratio cycle (sum of costs divided by sum of times), solved via Lawler's algorithm adapted to product graphs. Two structural adaptations are required: component duplication (k copies of each component) to prevent intra-component cycles and enable parallel GPU processing, and a branch-and-bound scheme to prevent the cycle from passing through the same component multiple times. For higher-order regularisation, the algorithm operates on the conjugate (line) product graph, where each edge spans two consecutive edges of P, enabling a local-rigidity cost computed as the geodesic distance between rotation matrices on SO(3) for neighbouring matched edges. The entire pipeline is implemented in CUDA, exploiting the distortion bound for parallelism across component duplicates, yielding 10×–100× speedups over the best competing product-graph method while matching or improving geodesic accuracy.

## Results

On 2D-3D shape matching (FAUST and TOSCA), the method achieves comparable or lower mean geodesic error than Roetzer et al. while being 10×–100× faster (reducing runtime from ~10–20 min to seconds on a single NVIDIA L40 GPU). On 3D shape matching (FAUST and DT4D), the method matches the SOTA SpiderMatch in mean geodesic error despite not enforcing geometric consistency constraints, and is faster even though it operates on a conjugate product graph ~10× larger. For GPS-art on the Nashville road network, a guitar-shaped track is computed in 1 second.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Lähner et al. [41] (2D-3D, minimum cost cycle on P), the proposed method achieves lower mean geodesic error by eliminating the bias toward shorter cycles, while also being substantially faster due to GPU parallelism. Against Roetzer et al. [61] (2D-3D, minimum cost cycle on the conjugate product graph P̄), the method matches or slightly improves geodesic error while reducing runtime by 1–2 orders of magnitude (from ~10–20 min to seconds), since the ratio-cycle formulation avoids the expensive branch-and-bound iterations needed for shortest-path on P̄. Against SpiderMatch [59] (3D-3D), the method achieves essentially identical mean geodesic error on both FAUST and DT4D without requiring geometric consistency constraints, and is faster despite operating on a graph ~10× larger. Against the learning-based ULRSSM [12] and the axiomatic DiscrOpt [57] and functional-map-based SmoothShells [22], the method outperforms all three in mean geodesic error on 3D shape matching, confirming that the ratio-cycle formulation with local-rigidity regularisation captures geometric structure effectively without learned features or iterative alignment.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate lines of work: the product-graph formalism for shape matching (Lähner et al. 2016; Roetzer et al. 2023) that finds minimum-cost cycles via Dijkstra, and the minimum-ratio-cycle optimisation developed for model-based image segmentation (Jermyn & Ishikawa 2001; Schoenemann & Cremers 2007, 2009). The key conceptual move is transplanting Lawler's ratio-cycle algorithm into the conjugate product graph setting, thereby combining the higher-order geometric regularisation of Roetzer et al. (2023) with the bias-free ratio objective. The authors explicitly note that ratio-cycle formalisms had not been transferred beyond segmentation, likely due to limited exposure and lack of high-performance implementations, which this work addresses with a public GPU implementation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 10–100× runtime reduction (from ~10–20 min to seconds) makes globally optimal product-graph shape matching practical for iterative or learning-based pipelines, which the authors identify as a key motivation. The ratio formulation structurally eliminates the well-known bias of minimum-cost cycles toward shorter matchings, improving accuracy on thin anatomical structures (legs, arms) without additional heuristics. Open questions include extending the framework to more general component-based or dynamic graphs, and whether the ratio objective can be integrated into differentiable end-to-end training. The publicly available CUDA code lowers the barrier for the broader community to adopt ratio-cycle optimisation for other graph-based vision tasks.
