## Problem

While prior work has examined CLIP's inability to distinguish conceptually different captions, little is known about why CLIP is inconsistent with semantically equivalent but structurally varied captions in multi-object scenes. This paper investigates the root causes of such inconsistency, asking whether systematic biases in CLIP's image and text encoders explain the model's sensitivity to object size and token order.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251547/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis pipeline consumes Blender-rendered multi-object images (2–5 objects with controlled size and position from 72 COCO categories) paired with corresponding captions, alongside single-object images and captions used as retrieval targets. Outputs are structured as per-position retrieval accuracy (first, second, third, fourth object), per-size-category retrieval accuracy (large vs. small objects), CLS-token attention heatmaps over image patches, and image-text matching accuracy under two caption-ordering scenarios. A SimCO variant replaces natural objects with CLEVR geometric shapes to confirm bias is not content-specific.

## Method

The paper introduces two controlled retrieval tasks—Text-based Object Retrieval (TOR) and Image-based Object Retrieval (IOR)—that measure which single object dominates a multi-object embedding by computing cosine similarity against all single-object embeddings. For the image encoder, attention-map analysis shows the CLS token allocates disproportionately more attention to patches belonging to larger objects, a ViT architectural property. For the text encoder, the authors rule out causal attention as the cause (since XLM-Roberta-based CLIP shows the same bias) and instead trace the bias to contrastive training: a theoretical result (Theorem 1) proves that in high-dimensional settings, the contrastive loss is equally minimized by incomplete text representations that drop a constant number of dimensions, making convergence to biased local minima likely. Empirically, LAION caption analysis confirms larger objects are mentioned earlier, and training-checkpoint experiments show the text-side bias strengthens monotonically as the model sees more data, consistent with image-side bias being transferred through contrastive alignment.

## Results

On the ComCO dataset, CLIP's text encoder exhibits a strong first-object bias: retrieval accuracy for the first-mentioned object ranges from 44.03% (CLIP Roberta) to 71.13% (CLIP Datacomp), while subsequent objects drop to 3.76–30.32%. The image encoder shows a pronounced large-object bias, with large-object retrieval accuracy between 60.0% (SugarCrepe) and 87.40% (CLIP Roberta), versus 1.57–18.38% for small objects. In practical image-text matching (Table 6), performance on ComCO drops from ~99% (first scenario, bias-aligned) to 46.94–72.36% (second scenario, bias-misaligned), and on COCO from 63.5–80.0% to 26.4–54.2%, confirming that caption structure alone can swing accuracy by over 40 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an analysis paper comparing seven CLIP-family variants rather than proposing a new method, so 'winning' means exhibiting less bias. CLIP Datacomp shows the strongest first-object text bias (71.13% vs. 16.26% for the second object), while CLIP Roberta and SugarCrepe show the weakest (44.03% and 44.29% respectively), with SugarCrepe also achieving the most balanced distribution across positions (44.29/30.32/18.73/6.66). In the image encoder, CLIP Roberta has the most extreme large-object bias (87.40% vs. 1.57% for the smallest object), whereas SugarCrepe and NegCLIP show the most balanced size distributions (60.0%/18.38%/16.85%/4.7% and 61.67%/15.00%/13.33%/10.00% respectively). Text-only models SBERT and SimCSE (Table 5) show a markedly different pattern—favoring the *last* object (33.57% and 34.83% for the 4th position)—confirming that CLIP's contrastive image-text alignment, not the transformer architecture itself, is the source of the first-object bias. In practical image-text matching (Table 6), SugarCrepe achieves the best second-scenario accuracy on COCO (40.9%) and ComCO (60.43%), while CLIP openAI suffers the largest drop (63.5% → 26.4% on COCO), indicating that models trained with hard-negative discrimination are more robust to caption reordering.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on evaluating CLIP's compositional limitations (Winoground, CREPE, ARO, VL-CheckList) by shifting focus from distinguishing conceptually different captions to analyzing semantically equivalent but structurally varied ones, thereby isolating systematic encoder biases rather than general compositionality failures. It builds directly on CLIP's contrastive training framework (Radford et al., 2021) and the ViT architecture, while also engaging with recent fine-tuning approaches like NegCLIP and SugarCrepe as baselines that partially mitigate but do not eliminate the identified biases. The theoretical contribution (Theorem 1) on incomplete representations in contrastive learning departs from prior empirical-only analyses by providing a principled explanation for why the text encoder converges to biased local minima.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The findings have direct practical implications for anyone deploying CLIP-based systems: a simple reordering of objects in a caption can drop image-text matching accuracy from ~99% to ~52% (CLIP openAI on ComCO), and prompt order measurably affects object prominence in Stable Diffusion outputs. The paper's diagnosis—that image-side size bias transfers to text-side position bias through contrastive training, reinforced by training data where larger objects are mentioned earlier—points toward concrete remediation strategies (balanced training data, architectural changes) rather than ad-hoc fine-tuning. Open questions include whether the proposed mitigation in Appendix 7.12 generalizes beyond the synthetic setting, whether newer architectures (e.g., DINOv2-based encoders) exhibit the same patch-count bias, and how these biases interact in longer, more naturalistic captions beyond the 2–5 object scope tested here.
