## Problem

Text-to-image diffusion models like Stable Diffusion can memorize training images, raising copyright and privacy concerns, yet existing inference-time mitigation strategies (e.g., gradient-based prompt engineering) force a severe privacy-utility trade-off: reducing memorization requires extensive prompt modification that degrades text-image alignment, while preserving alignment leaves memorization insufficiently suppressed. The paper identifies that both the text-conditional and unconditional terms in classifier-free guidance are suboptimal anchors for memorization mitigation, creating a structural limitation that no amount of prompt tuning can overcome.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single user text prompt as input and operates entirely at inference time on a pre-trained Stable Diffusion v1-4 model, requiring no retraining or fine-tuning. At the first denoising step, a detection signal (the L2 magnitude between text-conditional and unconditional noise predictions, optionally masked) determines whether memorization is likely; if triggered, GPT-4 is queried to generate up to 25 semantically similar alternative prompts (each in ~0.9 s, ~$0.02). The output is the final generated image, with the effective conditioning prompt potentially replaced by a semantically aligned alternative found in language space.

## Method

PRSS refines the standard CFG equation with two complementary strategies. Prompt Re-anchoring (PR) replaces the unconditional noise prediction in CFG with the conditional prediction under the original (memorized) user prompt, so the guidance direction actively steers generation away from memorized content rather than merely toward a generic conditional target; this reduces the magnitude signal more efficiently, requiring fewer gradient updates and less prompt distortion for the same privacy gain. Semantic Prompt Search (SS) replaces gradient-based prompt engineering in embedding space with an LLM-driven search in language space, finding alternative prompts that are semantically close to the user's intent but carry lower memorization risk, thereby preserving text-image alignment (CLIP score) while still reducing the detection magnitude. The two strategies are synergistic: PR provides a stronger privacy anchor that compensates when SS cannot find a fully safe alternative, while SS limits the utility cost of the prompt modification that PR still requires. The entire pipeline modifies only the noise-prediction update in the CFG equation during inference, with no architectural changes, additional training, or access to the training dataset.

## Results

PRSS is evaluated on 500 prompts from the Stable Diffusion v1-4 memorization dataset (over 300 classified as memorized), reporting privacy via SSCD score (lower = better, threshold 0.5) and utility via CLIP text-image alignment score (higher = better). Results are presented as privacy-utility trade-off curves (Fig. 6) across varying detection thresholds λ, showing that PRSS consistently dominates both baselines in the bottom-right direction (lower SSCD, higher CLIP) for both global and local memorization. The improvement is most pronounced for global memorization, where PRSS with the masked magnitude signal m′ (red line) achieves substantially lower SSCD at equivalent or higher CLIP scores compared to both [36] and [8]. Even with the inferior unmasked signal m, PRSS (green line) matches the performance of [8] (yellow line) which uses the superior m′ signal.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PRSS is compared against two prompt-engineering (PE) baselines: Wen et al. [36] (ICLR 2024), which uses the unmasked first-step magnitude m_{T-1} for detection and gradient-based prompt optimization, and Chen et al. [8] (ICLR 2025), which upgrades detection to a masked magnitude m′ incorporating local memorization masks. Against [36], PRSS achieves clearly superior privacy-utility trade-offs in both global and local memorization scenarios, with the gap being especially large for global memorization. Against [8], PRSS with m′ (red line) outperforms [8] (yellow line) in both memorization types, while PRSS with the weaker m signal (green line) already matches [8]'s performance, demonstrating that the mitigation strategy itself contributes more than the detection upgrade. The ablation (Fig. 7) confirms that neither PR alone nor SS alone matches the full PRSS: PR alone sacrifices utility, SS alone cannot guarantee privacy, but their combination yields a synergistic improvement. Notably, [8] achieves only marginal gains over [36] in global memorization, whereas PRSS shows substantially larger improvements over both.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the inference-time memorization mitigation framework of Wen et al. (ICLR 2024), which introduced magnitude-based detection and gradient-based prompt engineering within the classifier-free guidance (CFG) equation. The key conceptual departure is the observation that both the conditional and unconditional terms in standard CFG are suboptimal anchors for privacy: the paper replaces the unconditional anchor with the memorized prompt itself (prompt re-anchoring) and replaces embedding-space gradient optimization with LLM-driven semantic search in language space. It also builds on the local memorization detection signal from Chen et al. (ICLR 2025), integrating it as a drop-in replacement for the detection trigger.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method is practically attractive because it requires no retraining, no access to training data, and only a minor modification to the CFG equation at inference time, with LLM calls costing roughly $0.02 per prompt. A critical limitation acknowledged by the authors is that the entire pipeline's effectiveness is gated by detection accuracy: if the magnitude signal fails to trigger, the system reverts to standard Stable Diffusion and memorization goes unmitigated. This makes improving detection robustness an orthogonal but essential open problem. Broader implications include potential applicability to other diffusion-based generative systems (video, 3D) where memorization poses similar legal and privacy risks.
