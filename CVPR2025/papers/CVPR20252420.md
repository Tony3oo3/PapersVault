## Problem

Vision-language generative reward models (VL-GenRMs) are increasingly deployed for model alignment and evaluation, yet their own reliability remains under-explored. Existing assessment methods rely on AI-annotated preferences or traditional VL task benchmarks, which introduce systematic biases (self-preference, stylistic bias) and use simplistic queries that fail to differentiate rapidly evolving state-of-the-art models. No comprehensive benchmark simultaneously covers diverse real-world applications, provides sufficient difficulty to expose model limitations, and offers objectively verified ground truth labels.

## Input / Output

Each benchmark sample is a single-image, single-turn preference pair consisting of an RGB image, a text prompt, and two candidate text responses (chosen and rejected). The VL-GenRM under evaluation receives the image, prompt, and both responses simultaneously and must output which response is preferred; to mitigate positional bias, K independent evaluations are conducted with randomized response ordering, and the final judgment is determined by majority voting. The 1,250-sample benchmark spans three task domains—general multimodal instructions (183 pairs), hallucination-oriented queries (749 pairs), and multimodal reasoning (318 pairs)—with a zero-centered length distribution between chosen and rejected responses to control for verbosity bias.

## Method

The benchmark construction employs two complementary curation strategies. For general and hallucination datasets with existing preference labels, an ensemble of four small LVLMs (LLaVA-1.5-7B, LLaVA-1.6-7B, LLaVA-OneVision-7B-si, Qwen2-VL-7B) collaboratively identifies samples that all models consistently misjudge across three randomized evaluations, isolating universally challenging cases from model-specific failures; these 3,785 candidates undergo three-stage human verification (label accuracy check, ambiguity filtering, error type classification) yielding 932 final pairs. For reasoning tasks (MMMU-Pro, MathVerse) lacking preference labels, commercial models (GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, Claude-3.5-Sonnet) generate candidate response pairs with controlled verbosity (length ratio < 0.1), GPT-4o produces initial preference labels with rationales, and human annotators verify against oracle answers, yielding 318 validated pairs. The resulting benchmark is evaluated across 16 VL-GenRMs (7B–90B open-source and commercial models), with analysis revealing that visual perception errors (existence/recognition) dominate over reasoning errors, inference-time scaling via majority voting benefits only the largest models while degrading smaller ones, and critic training (LLaVA-Critic) provides a +14.7% accuracy gain for a 7B model.

## Results

On VL-RewardBench, the best commercial models achieve only moderate performance: Gemini-1.5-Pro reaches 62.5% macro average accuracy (67.2% overall) and GPT-4o reaches 62.4% macro average (65.8% overall). Among open-source models, Llama-3.2-90B leads at 53.9% macro average, while Qwen2-VL-72B achieves 43.0% and most 7B-scale models (e.g., Qwen2-VL-7B at 33.9%, LLaVA-OneVision-7B-ov at 36.5%) barely exceed random chance. Performance on VL-RewardBench strongly correlates with downstream Best-of-N sampling effectiveness on MMMU-Pro (Pearson r = 0.946 for Qwen2-VL-7B, r = 0.914 for LLaVA-OneVision-7B-ov), with GPT-4o as a selector improving LLaVA-OneVision-7B-ov from 35.7% to 52.5% accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 16 VL-GenRMs as baselines on the proposed VL-RewardBench. Commercial models dominate: Gemini-1.5-Pro (62.5%) and GPT-4o (62.4%) lead in macro average accuracy, with GPT-4o achieving the best reasoning score (70.5%) and Gemini-1.5-Pro the best hallucination score (72.5%). Among open-source models, Llama-3.2-90B (53.9%) substantially outperforms its 11B counterpart (42.8%), while Qwen2-VL-72B (43.0%) and Molmo-72B (43.7%) show only modest gains over their 7B versions (33.9% and 39.7% respectively). Notably, all 7B open-source models (Qwen2-VL-7B, LLaVA-OneVision-7B-ov, Phi-3.5-Vision, Molmo-7B) score below 40% macro average, barely exceeding random guessing. The LLaVA-Critic variants (pointwise: 52.9%, pairwise: 47.4%) demonstrate that critic training adds +14.7% over the base LLaVA-OneVision-7B-ov (36.5%), closing the gap toward larger models. Ablation with randomly sampled examples (same task distribution) shows Gemini models exceed 95% accuracy, confirming the curated difficulty of VL-RewardBench.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM-as-a-Judge evaluation paradigm from text (RewardBench [23]) to the vision-language domain, building on prior VL-GenRM evaluation efforts like MLLM-as-a-Judge [6] and VLFeedback [27]. It departs from these by introducing an ensemble filtering strategy that uses small LVLMs to identify universally challenging samples, and an AI-aided preference labeling pipeline for reasoning tasks lacking annotated pairs. The critic training analysis builds directly on LLaVA-Critic [55], while the Best-of-N correlation study connects to the broader RLHF and preference-based selection literature [41, 42].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

VL-RewardBench fills a critical gap: the community previously lacked a comprehensive, difficulty-calibrated benchmark for VL-GenRMs that covers real-world queries, hallucination detection, and reasoning simultaneously. The strong correlation (r > 0.9) with MMMU-Pro Best-of-N performance validates its practical utility for selecting reward models in alignment pipelines. Key open questions remain: how to close the perception bottleneck (existence/recognition errors >67% for many models), whether inference-time scaling strategies from text can be adapted for vision-language judgment, and whether the proposed co-evolution cycle (stronger LVLMs → better VL-GenRMs → better training data → stronger LVLMs) can be realized at scale.
