## Problem

Existing CLIP social bias removal methods suffer from an over-debiasing problem: they reduce bias levels but severely degrade vision-language alignment performance. The paper identifies the root cause as unbalanced debiasing across modalities—most methods debias only one modality, and CLIP-clip's assumption that the same embedding dimensions are biased in both image and text is empirically unsupported, as the two modalities exhibit different bias types, magnitudes, and directions.

## Input / Output

The method takes a frozen CLIP model's image embedding and text embedding as input, along with a counterfactual text prompt (the original prompt with the sensitive attribute keyword swapped, e.g., 'male' → 'female'). It produces debiased image and text embeddings by subtracting the output of a learned bias alignment (BA) module from the original CLIP embeddings. The BA module is a plug-and-play component that operates on the frozen CLIP representations without modifying the backbone, and it is trained on face datasets (FairFace, UTKFace) with gender, age, and race labels.

## Method

The proposed method has two core components trained jointly with a frozen CLIP backbone. First, a bias alignment module (BA) is trained to produce aligned bias embeddings for both modalities; rather than enforcing element-wise matching, it maintains moving queues of recent text and image embeddings (inspired by ALBEF/MoCo) and minimizes KL divergence between the intra-modal similarity distributions of each bias embedding against its respective queue. Second, a counterfactual debiasing loss uses triplets of two oppositely biased text prompts sharing the same neutral concept plus one biased image, pulling the debiased text embeddings closer via a cross-entropy objective that matches the original CLIP's text-to-image similarity distribution, thereby preserving V-L alignment while removing attribute-specific information. The final debiased embedding is simply the original CLIP embedding minus the BA module output, making the approach a lightweight post-hoc correction.

## Results

On ViT-B/16 trained on FairFace, the proposed method achieves the best ABLE score across all settings: 78.35% for gender debiasing (vs. 77.55% for CLIP-clip and 73.78% for Biased-prompts) and 60.61% for age debiasing (vs. 59.16% and 54.33%). In terms of fairness, it reduces in-domain MaxSkew to 0.080 for gender (from 0.218 in original CLIP) and 0.608 for age (from 0.657), while preserving V-L alignment with only a 0.26% drop in ImageNet Top-1 accuracy (68.05% vs. 68.31%). The advantage generalizes across all four backbones (ViT-B/16, ViT-B/32, ViT-L/14, ViT-H/14), with ABLE improvements of 1.8–4.5 points over the strongest baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CLIP-clip, the proposed method achieves consistently lower in-domain MaxSkew (e.g., 0.080 vs. 0.103 for gender on ViT-B/16) while maintaining or slightly exceeding the original CLIP's V-L alignment (IN1K 68.05% vs. 68.00%), yielding a higher ABLE of 78.35% vs. 77.55%. Against Biased-prompts, the advantage is more pronounced: the proposed method halves the gender MaxSkew (0.080 vs. 0.161) and recovers 3+ points of ImageNet accuracy (68.05% vs. 65.07%), reflecting that Biased-prompts suffers severe over-debiasing. Compared to the original undebiased CLIP, the proposed method reduces gender MaxSkew by 63% (0.218→0.080) and age MaxSkew by 7% (0.657→0.608) while losing less than 0.3% ImageNet Top-1 accuracy, demonstrating that effective debiasing need not sacrifice V-L alignment. The pattern holds across all four ViT backbones, with the largest ABLE gains observed on ViT-B/32 (gender: 74.24% vs. 72.59% for Biased-prompts) and ViT-H/14 (gender: 82.11% vs. 81.34% for CLIP-clip).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against the existing CLIP social debiasing literature, which splits into single-modality approaches (Biased-prompts [9], adversarial modules [3,22,25,45]) and the one dual-modality attempt, CLIP-clip [49], which removes the same biased dimensions from both image and text embeddings. The key conceptual departure is the empirical finding that image and text biases in CLIP are fundamentally misaligned—differing in which bias types are significant, their magnitudes, and even their directions—so applying identical removal to both modalities is ill-posed. The method repurposes the moving-queue mechanism from ALBEF [27] and MoCo [21] to construct intra-modal pseudo-distributions for a KL-divergence-based bias alignment loss, and introduces a counterfactual debiasing objective using alternating attribute-flipped text prompts, neither of which has a direct precedent in the CLIP debiasing literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The over-debiasing diagnosis is practically important: it shows that the most commonly cited CLIP debiasing methods (CLIP-clip, Biased-prompts) can reduce fairness metrics while degrading zero-shot ImageNet accuracy by 2–3 points, a trade-off that the ABLE metric makes visible. The bias-misalignment finding challenges the implicit assumption in CLIP-clip that the same embedding dimensions encode bias in both modalities, suggesting future multi-modal debiasing should treat the two encoders asymmetrically. Limitations include reliance on face-centric datasets (FairFace, UTKFace) for training, evaluation restricted to gender/age/race, and the need to train a separate alignment module rather than a training-free solution. Open questions include whether the approach extends to other V-L architectures (e.g., BLIP, SigLIP), to non-attribute biases (e.g., object bias), and whether the alignment module can be distilled into the frozen CLIP weights.
