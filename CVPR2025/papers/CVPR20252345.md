## Problem

Vanishing point detection requires mapping the unbounded image plane into a bounded space before Hough Transform can be applied, but the dominant Gaussian sphere approach demands calibration parameters, suffers from wildly non-uniform Hough cell sizes on its non-planar surface, and is prone to spurious detection due to edge-pair voting. RANSAC-based alternatives avoid calibration but are computationally expensive and susceptible to false negatives in cluttered scenes. The paper addresses the need for a calibration-free, high-accuracy, and fast VP detection method suitable for uncalibrated real-world images.

## Input / Output

The method consumes line segments (edges) extracted from RGB images via an LSD-like detector, with pixel coordinates normalized to a unit square centered at the image center. It produces a disk-shaped Hough accumulation map and up to N_VP=4 vanishing points, from which a triplet of orthogonal VPs is selected based on mutual orthogonality; if only 2 of the 3 are captured initially, the 3rd is recovered by searching near its predicted location. No camera intrinsics or extrinsics are used at any stage, making the pipeline fully uncalibrated.

## Method

DiskVPS maps the unbounded image plane into a bounded disk by transforming polar coordinates (r, θ) to (tan⁻¹(4r), θ), so that parallel lines in the image plane converge at the disk boundary while non-parallel lines intersect inside it. The disk is partitioned into M=200 concentric ring-shaped subspaces, each further divided into up to N=200 fan-shaped cells sized so that every cell has approximately equal probability of intersecting a random edge's extension line, derived from a statistical model of edge distributions. Crucially, individual edges (not edge pairs) serve as voters, each casting exactly 1 vote per intersecting cell, which avoids the error amplification inherent in edge-pair schemes. VP detection proceeds iteratively: the global peak in the accumulation map is extracted, its inlier edges are identified by an angular threshold Θ=3°, their votes are removed, and the process repeats until no further salient peak remains. This design yields 158 fps processing speed and significantly higher angular accuracy than SOTA on real-world benchmarks without any parameter tuning.

## Results

DiskVPS achieves 158 FPS (≈6.3 ms/frame), roughly 4× faster than the second-best method (Wu et al. [34] at 39 FPS). On real-world YUD, it reaches 74.5% / 90.2% / 99.0% angular accuracy at AA@3°/5°/10°, outperforming the best baseline (Tong et al. [27]) by 9 / 13.1 / 11.6 percentage points respectively. On SVVP it scores 58.3% / 81.6% / 98.4%, again leading all baselines. On the large synthetic SU3 dataset (23,000 images), DiskVPS is competitive at 84.3% / 91.7% (AA@3°/5°) but trails the two deep-learning methods (NeurVPS and Tong et al.) by roughly 7–10 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On both real-world datasets (YUD and SVVP), DiskVPS decisively outperforms all six baselines across every angular-accuracy threshold. On YUD, the closest competitor is Tong et al. [27] (Transformer-based, 25 FPS), which DiskVPS surpasses by 9 pp at AA@3° and 11.6 pp at AA@10°; on SVVP, the gap over Lin et al. [16] (the best non-DL baseline there) is 6.5 pp at AA@3° and 16.2 pp at AA@10°. In terms of speed, DiskVPS at 158 FPS is approximately 4× faster than Wu et al. [34] (39 FPS) and over 6× faster than Tong et al. [27] (25 FPS). On the synthetic SU3 dataset, DiskVPS trails the two deep-learning methods—NeurVPS [39] (93.9% AA@3°) and Tong et al. [27] (91.3% AA@3°)—by 9.6 and 7.0 pp respectively, though it remains competitive with Lin et al. [16] (84.4% AA@3°) and CONSAC [11] (86.2% AA@3°). The authors attribute the SU3 gap to the 23,000-image training set that benefits the DL models, while noting DiskVPS requires no calibration parameters or training data.

## Relation to Prior Work

DiskVPS builds directly on the statistical model from Wu et al. [34] (ICCV 2021), which provided the probabilistic distribution of intersection points between a given line and a random edge's extension. Conceptually, it departs from the dominant Gaussian Sphere (GS) paradigm introduced by Barnard [2] by replacing the sphere mapping with a disk-region mapping via the arctan(4r) transformation, eliminating the need for camera calibration parameters. It also shifts the voting unit from edge pairs (as in GS-based methods) to individual edges, and positions itself as a calibration-free alternative to both GS-based and RANSAC-based VP detection approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a simple, calibration-free, non-optimized classical method achieves 99% angular accuracy at 10° on real-world YUD and SVVP datasets while running at 158 fps—roughly 4× faster than the next-best method—challenging the assumption that deep learning is necessary for state-of-the-art VP detection. However, on the large synthetic SU3 dataset (23,000 images), DL methods with extensive training data still outperform DiskVPS, suggesting the gap may narrow or reverse with parameter optimization or integration into learned pipelines. Open questions include whether the disk mapping generalizes beyond the Manhattan world assumption, how the method performs on non-architectural scenes, and whether the probabilistic cell partitioning can be further refined to close the remaining accuracy gap on synthetic data.
