## Problem

Existing multimodal unsupervised domain adaptation (MUDA) co-learning methods treat all modalities equally and allow synchronous, unconstrained information exchange, so low-quality or noisy samples from one modality propagate errors to others. Coarse-grained controls (hyperparameters, curriculum scheduling) adjust modality-level learning intensity but cannot handle the sample-level quality variation that is common in real target domains, leading to cross-modal contamination and suboptimal adaptation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252537/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes paired multimodal inputs from a labeled source domain and an unlabeled target domain: image + audio spectrogram (AVE), RGB video + optical flow (EPIC-Kitchens 55), or EEG signal + facial key points (CogBeacon). Beyond standard class predictions, the method outputs a per-sample Learning Material tuple that records the pseudo-label, the confidence score, and the identity of the teacher modality, which then drives asymmetric, sample-specific distillation. The class encoder E^c maps one-hot label vectors into a shared affinity space, effectively treating the label as an independent modality that serves as a neutral benchmark for cross-modal comparison.

## Method

MODfinity operates in three stages. First, Modal-Affinity Metric Learning (MOML) trains a class encoder E^c via a contrastive-style loss that pulls modality feature vectors toward their correct label embedding and away from incorrect ones, creating a unified measurement space without projecting modalities into a single feature space; gradient-stopped copies of modality features ensure each encoder optimizes independently. Second, Fine-grained Sample Filtering scores every target sample by the maximum affinity across all modalities and labels, selects the top α% most confident samples, and assigns each a teacher modality (the one that produced the highest-affinity pseudo-label). Third, Modal-Affinity Distillation (MOD) applies a contrastive loss in which the teacher modality's feature (with gradient detached) acts as the anchor, pulling the student modality's feature toward same-class teacher features while pushing it away from different-class ones, thereby enabling unidirectional, sample-specific knowledge transfer. The class encoder is continuously updated in the target domain via the same MOML loss to track shifting feature distributions.

## Results

On the AVE dataset, MODfinity achieves 57.38% (Image), 57.66% (Audio), and 58.11% (Fusion) accuracy, outperforming the strongest baseline MCT (52.38/52.45/54.56) by 5.0/5.2/3.6 points respectively. On EPIC-Kitchens 55 (mean over 6 domain pairs), it reaches 53.5/54.4/55.6% (RGB/Flow/Fusion), surpassing MCT (46.1/50.1/51.2%) by 7.4/4.3/4.4 points. On CogBeacon (mean over 6 domain pairs), it achieves 66.6/69.2/69.9% (FK/EEG/Fusion), beating MCT (64.1/66.7/67.6%) by 2.5/2.5/2.3 points. Under 80% Gaussian noise on AVE images, MODfinity maintains 13.36% Image accuracy while CL degrades below source-only (9.37% vs. 10.48%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MODfinity consistently outperforms all eight baselines across all three datasets and all modalities. The largest gains appear on AVE, where the Image modality improves from 15.55% (source-only) to 57.38%, a 41.83-point jump driven by audio-guided distillation; the nearest competitor MCT [28] reaches only 52.38%. On EPIC-Kitchens 55, MODfinity leads MCT by 7.4 points on RGB (mean), the most challenging modality, while DLMM [21] and CL [15] trail by 4–7 points. On CogBeacon, the margin over MCT narrows to ~2.5 points but remains consistent across all six domain pairs. In the noisy AVE experiment (80% Gaussian noise on images), CL [15] actually degrades below source-only (9.37% vs. 10.48% Image), whereas MODfinity still improves to 13.36%, demonstrating its sample-level filtering advantage. Distribution-alignment methods (DANN [9], RNA [25], C-RNA [26]) perform near or below source-only on the Image modality, confirming that coarse-grained alignment is insufficient when one modality is severely degraded.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the co-learning line of multimodal unsupervised domain adaptation (MUDA), building on methods like CT, MM-SADA, CL, and MCT that exchange information between modalities via pseudo-labeling or feature alignment. It departs from prior work by replacing coarse-grained, modality-level control (hyperparameter tuning, curriculum scheduling) with sample-level, fine-grained control, and by introducing a unified cross-modal measurement that treats labels as an independent modality rather than relying on per-modality confidence scores. The metric-learning component (MOML) borrows the contrastive-learning formulation but repurposes it for quality assessment rather than feature alignment, while the distillation component (MOD) applies a contrastive loss in a teacher-student structure that prior co-training methods did not exploit at the sample level.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical contribution is demonstrating that uncontrolled cross-modal information exchange can actively degrade performance in noisy target domains (CL drops 16.67% on noisy AVE), and that fine-grained, affinity-guided filtering prevents this failure mode. The elegant reframing of labels as a modality provides a principled, continuously updatable benchmark for cross-modal comparison without forcing all modalities into a shared feature space. However, the framework currently requires separate hyperparameter tuning per modality pair, limiting scalability to more than two modalities, and the authors acknowledge that training-free mechanisms are needed for broader applicability. Open questions include whether the sample-level teacher-student assignment generalizes to three or more modalities and whether the affinity measurement remains reliable under severe domain shift.
