## Problem

Existing human pose datasets are either captured in controlled laboratories with motion-capture hardware, focus on single individuals, or record only daily activities, leaving a critical gap for multi-person, multi-view pose estimation under realistic conditions. No prior dataset combines dense mutual and self-occlusions from fitness exercises (Yoga, Pilates), overhead camera views, floor mirrors, and varied lighting in a real-world gym setting.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252516/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The dataset provides 8 synchronized overhead fisheye RGB video streams (1920×1080 at 25 fps) per session, with annotations in BODY25 keypoint format (25 joints, omitting 6 foot points from COCO17). Outputs are structured as per-subject, time-span-based segments containing 2D keypoints (Kpt_2D ∈ R^{8×25×3}), 3D keypoints (Kpt_3D ∈ R^{25×4}), SMPL meshes, action labels from 502 distinct types, and binary expert assessments (good/poor) with optional text feedback for poor actions. The unique overhead perspective means a single camera may miss subjects or fail to capture a full body, and two symmetric floor mirrors introduce reflections absent from all prior pose datasets.

## Method

The core contribution is a semi-automated annotation pipeline that chains frame alignment (via central console timestamps), pixel alignment (LightGlue dense correspondences to refine camera extrinsics), multi-model 2D keypoint voting (median across 9 detectors including DWPose, RTMPose, ViTPose with IoU-based subject matching and NMS), triangulation to 3D, physics-based auto-calibration (bone-length and inter-frame smoothing constraints from HuMMan), manual 3D inspection in a custom Blender tool, and SMPLify mesh fitting. Action segmentation is derived from sports expert session notes, refined by computing relative distance changes between segmented skeletons to verify repetition counts. The benchmark evaluates six 2D pose models, two multi-view 3D methods (MV-Pose), three single-view 3D methods (SimpleBaseline, VideoPose3D, MotionBERT), and three mesh recovery models (PyMAF, OSX-SMPL, SMPLer-L), each tested under direct inference and M3GYM fine-tuning, plus cross-domain experiments across Normal, Pilates, and Yoga session types.

## Results

On M3GYM's 2D pose estimation benchmark, ViTPose achieves the highest inference AP (66.3) and fine-tuned AP (73.1), while RTMPose leads in fine-tuned AP50 (90.7) and AP75 (78.9). Fine-tuning on M3GYM yields substantial gains across all methods (e.g., ViTPose AP improves from 66.3 to 73.1; DWPose from 63.8 to 72.6), confirming the dataset's value for training. In single-view 3D pose estimation, MotionBERT achieves the lowest fine-tuned MPJPE (123.7 mm) and VideoPose3D the lowest PA-MPJPE (76.6 mm). For human mesh recovery, SMPLer-L attains the best fine-tuned MPJPE (116.4 mm) and PA-MPJPE (67.2 mm).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the six 2D pose estimation methods, top-down approaches (ViTPose, RTMPose, DWPose) substantially outperform bottom-up methods (DEKR, ED-Pose) in inference AP (66.3/64.0/63.8 vs. 19.9/14.0), likely due to M3GYM's heavy occlusions and diverse fitness poses. After fine-tuning on M3GYM, ViTPose leads in overall AP (73.1) and AR (79.1), while RTMPose edges ahead in AP50 (90.7) and AP75 (78.9). In single-view 3D pose estimation, MotionBERT achieves the best fine-tuned MPJPE (123.7 mm) over VideoPose3D (126.4 mm) and SimpleBaseline (134.5 mm), while VideoPose3D attains the lowest PA-MPJPE (76.6 mm). For human mesh recovery, SMPLer-L dominates after fine-tuning (MPJPE 116.4 mm, PA-MPJPE 67.2 mm), outperforming OSX-SMPL (123.8/69.4) and PyMAF (155.1/94.5). Cross-domain experiments reveal that a Yoga-trained model generalizes best to Pilates (AP 90.8, AR 91.9), while Normal- and Pilates-trained models struggle on Yoga (AP 16.6 and 11.9 respectively), highlighting the unique self-occlusion challenges in Yoga poses.

## Relation to Prior Work

M3GYM carves new ground in the human pose dataset landscape by addressing a gap the authors identify as urgent: no existing dataset simultaneously offers multi-person, multi-view, real-world fitness data with complex occlusions. It departs from lab-based multi-person datasets (CMU Panoptic, CHI3D) and single-person fitness datasets (Fit3D) by capturing authentic gym sessions with up to 10 subjects, two floor mirrors, seven lighting conditions, and overhead camera views. The annotation pipeline repurposes existing tools—SMPLify for mesh fitting, LightGlue for pixel alignment, MV-Pose-style chessboard calibration, and multi-model 2D keypoint voting—but integrates them into a novel semi-automated workflow that adds expert action assessments, a modality absent from all prior pose datasets. The benchmark evaluates established SOTA methods (ViTPose, RTMPose, MotionBERT, SMPLer, etc.) rather than proposing new architectures, positioning the contribution squarely as a resource and evaluation platform.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

M3GYM is likely to become a standard benchmark for multi-person, multi-view pose estimation in cluttered real-world settings, given its scale (47M frames, 502 actions, 82 sessions) and the unique challenges it introduces (mirrors, overhead views, Yoga/Pilates self-occlusion). The cross-domain results reveal a striking asymmetry—Yoga-trained models transfer well to Pilates (AP 90.8) but Normal-trained models collapse on Yoga (AP 16.6)—highlighting that pose diversity and occlusion patterns, not just domain shift, drive generalization failures. Open questions remain: how to explicitly model mirror reflections in pose pipelines, whether the overhead camera geometry limits single-view 3D recovery, and whether the 43.7% Yoga/Pilates frame share is sufficient to train robust models for those activities. Limitations include uneven lighting distribution (gray lighting appears in only one session) and the absence of outdoor or non-gym environments.
