## Problem

Existing co-speech gesture video generation methods rely on strong visual controls such as pose image sequences or TPS keypoint movements, which cause artifacts like blurry hands and distorted fingers due to rigid alignment with imprecise intermediate representations. Directly conditioning a video diffusion model on audio alone is insufficient because the audio-to-gesture mapping is many-to-many and the correlation is too weak to drive upper-body motion effectively. The paper seeks a mechanism to strengthen audio control without introducing the artifacts of strong visual conditioning.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single human reference image (720×1280 or 1080×1920) and an audio sequence of F frames as input, producing a half-body co-speech gesture video that preserves the reference person's identity, clothing, and appearance while generating synchronized upper-body gestures. For generating videos longer than F frames, the last frame of the previously generated clip is simply reused as the new reference image, avoiding complex overlap-denoising or motion-selection schemes. The reference image is consumed through a lightweight residual convolutional encoder that produces features dimensionally matched to the noise latent, added directly at a specific position in the diffusion U-Net.

## Method

IMAE employs a two-branch architecture: an audio-to-motion generation branch and a video diffusion branch (built on I2VGen-XL latent diffusion). The audio-to-motion branch extracts MFCC and HuBERT semantic features, concatenates them with noisy SMPL-X motion parameters (pose β ∈ R^{F×3J} and expression θ ∈ R^{F×100}), and denoises them via transformer encoders; the resulting motion representation is injected into the video diffusion branch through cross-attention, effectively entangling implicit motion with audio to strengthen the weak audio signal. A lightweight reference encoder (residual convolutions) preserves identity by adding reference features directly to the latent, avoiding the spatial-alignment issues of ControlNet or the computational overhead of ReferenceNet. Training uses a two-stage slow-fast strategy: Stage 1 trains only the audio-to-motion branch over long frame sequences (F frames) for many epochs, while Stage 2 jointly trains both branches, feeding long audio sequences to the motion branch but randomly sampled short continuous subsequences (f frames) to the video diffusion branch to manage GPU memory. No classifier-free guidance is applied, as the goal is to enhance audio control rather than introduce motion variability.

## Results

On the PATS-based test set, IMAE achieves state-of-the-art performance on 3 of 4 metrics: FGD of 1.87 (vs. 3.69 for S2G and 24.24 for MYA), Diversity of 273.72 (vs. 180.59 and 224.14), and FVD of 681.33 (vs. 816.03 and 1823.97). The method slightly trails MYA on Beat Alignment Score (0.7445 vs. 0.7452), which the authors attribute to MYA's audio-to-motion stage capturing gesture-audio peak alignment more directly. Overall, IMAE reduces FGD by ~50% over S2G and ~92% over MYA, while cutting FVD by ~17% and ~63% respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

IMAE is compared against two open-source baselines: S2G (motion-decoupled diffusion with TPS keypoint control) and MYA (pose-image-guided video generation). IMAE decisively outperforms both on FGD (1.87 vs. 3.69 and 24.24), Diversity (273.72 vs. 180.59 and 224.14), and FVD (681.33 vs. 816.03 and 1823.97), indicating substantially more natural, diverse, and visually faithful gesture videos. The only metric where IMAE does not lead is BAS, where MYA edges ahead by 0.0007 (0.7452 vs. 0.7445); the authors note this is likely because MYA's separate DiffSHEG audio-to-motion stage, while producing poor visual quality, happens to align gesture peaks with audio peaks well. Qualitatively, IMAE avoids the blurry hands and distorted fingers that plague both S2G (TPS keypoint artifacts) and MYA (pose-image alignment artifacts), and does not suffer from the appearance memorization issue observed in MYA.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in co-speech gesture video generation (S2G, MYA, Vlogger) that relies on strong visual controls—pose image sequences or TPS keypoint movements—as intermediate conditioning for diffusion-based video synthesis. Instead, it draws on the implicit-parameter entanglement idea from Handiffuser and the audio-driven talking-head line (EMO, Hallo) to condition a video diffusion model (I2VGen-XL) on SMPL-X pose and expression parameters that are jointly denoised with audio features, eliminating the need for rendered pose images at inference. The two-branch architecture and the slow-fast training strategy are novel engineering contributions that address the memory-vs-sequence-length tension inherent in training video diffusion models on gesture-scale temporal windows.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The work offers a practical path toward artifact-free co-speech gesture video generation by replacing brittle strong visual controls with a softer, audio-entangled motion signal, which could be adopted by downstream avatar and virtual-agent pipelines. However, the evaluation is limited to four identities and a single dataset (PATS-derived, ~33 hours), leaving open whether the approach generalizes to diverse body types, dynamic postures, or multi-speaker settings. The slow-fast training recipe, while effective here, has not been validated across architectures or at larger scale, and the reliance on I2VGen-XL as the video backbone constrains portability to newer video diffusion models.
