## Problem

Existing membership inference attacks (MIAs) achieve performance comparable to random guessing when applied to large, state-of-the-art diffusion models trained on extensive datasets, making it impossible for data owners to verify whether their copyrighted images were used in training. While dataset inference (DI) has been shown to work for supervised and self-supervised classifiers, it remains unexplored whether DI principles transfer to diffusion models and how many data points are needed for confident detection.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251082/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

CDI takes a set P of suspect published images and a set U of unpublished images from the same distribution (e.g., a data owner's released vs. unreleased work), along with access to the target diffusion model in either gray-box mode (noise predictions at arbitrary timesteps) or white-box mode (full latent-space internals). The method uses 5-fold cross-validation to split P and U into control and test subsets, maximizing data utilization. It outputs a p-value from a Welch's t-test aggregated over 1000 randomized trials; rejecting H₀ at α=0.01 confirms P was used in training, while a non-significant result is inconclusive.

## Method

CDI combines four stages: (1) feature extraction using both existing MIAs (Denoising Loss, SecMI, PIA, PIAN) and three novel handcrafted features—Gradient Masking (GM, which destroys top-gradient latent regions and measures reconstruction loss), Multiple Loss (ML, computing denoising loss at 10 timesteps), and Noise Optimization (NO, which applies L-BFGS adversarial perturbation to noised latents and records both the optimized loss and perturbation magnitude); (2) a logistic regression scoring model trained on control-set features to map the combined feature vector to a membership probability; (3) a two-sample single-tailed Welch's t-test comparing mean scores of P_test vs. U_test at α=0.01; and (4) p-value aggregation over 1000 randomized resamplings for statistical robustness. The key insight is that no single MIA feature is discriminative enough, but selectively combining multiple features through a learned scoring function and applying rigorous statistical testing across a set of data points (as few as 70) amplifies the weak per-sample signal into a confident dataset-level verdict.

## Results

Existing MIAs (Denoising Loss, SecMI_stat, PIA, PIAN) achieve TPR@FPR=1% of only 1.03–5.57% (mean) across 8 state-of-the-art diffusion models, essentially at random-guessing level. CDI, which combines these MIA features with novel handcrafted features (Gradient Masking, Multiple Loss, Noise Optimization) and applies a Welch's t-test, achieves TPR@FPR=1% of 24.92–100% at |P|=1000, with perfect detection (100%) on U-ViT256-Uncond, U-ViT256-T2I, and U-ViT256-T2I-Deep. CDI confidently rejects the null hypothesis (p<0.01) with as few as 70 suspect samples, and produces no false positives (mean p-value ≈ 0.39 for non-member sets vs. p < 10⁻⁶ for member sets).

## Baselines & Comparisons

All four existing MIA baselines—Denoising Loss [Carlini et al., USENIX Sec '23], SecMI_stat [Duan et al., ICML '23], PIA [Kong et al., ICLR '24], and PIAN [Kong et al., ICLR '24]—fail to meaningfully identify training members in large DMs, with TPR@FPR=1% ranging from 1.03% (PIAN) to 5.57% (PIA) at best, i.e., at or below random-guessing level. CDI decisively outperforms every baseline: even the weakest CDI result (24.92% on LDM256) exceeds the best individual MIA (5.57% for PIA) by nearly 5×, and on the text-conditioned U-ViT models CDI reaches 100% TPR@FPR=1% versus 23–33% for the set-level MIA aggregation of those same features. The gap is most dramatic on DiT512, where the set-level MIA scores 0.00% but CDI achieves 93.76%, confirming that the combination of novel features (especially Gradient Masking) and rigorous statistical testing is essential. The paper also shows that CDI remains effective under gray-box access (using only MIA features + Multiple Loss), requiring on average one-third more samples than white-box but still rejecting H0 confidently.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the dataset inference (DI) framework from Maini et al. (ICLR 2021) and its self-supervised extension (Dziedzic et al., NeurIPS 2022) to diffusion models, repurposing DI's statistical testing machinery from model-ownership resolution to data-subset verification. It incorporates existing MIA feature extractors (Denoising Loss, SecMI, PIA/PIAN) as inputs but departs from prior MIA work by demonstrating they are essentially at random-guess level on large, state-of-the-art DMs, and by introducing three novel handcrafted features (Gradient Masking, Multiple Loss, Noise Optimization) that go beyond loss-based signals. The key conceptual shift is from individual membership scoring to set-level statistical hypothesis testing, enabling confident claims with far fewer samples than any single MIA could support.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CDI provides a practical, legally-oriented tool for data owners (stock agencies, artists, media companies) to verify copyright infringement in ongoing and future DM training disputes, requiring as few as 70 samples and producing no false positives. The negative result—that well-reported MIAs degrade to near-random performance on large, diverse DMs—challenges the field's assumptions about MIA transferability from toy to production-scale models and suggests prior positive results were artifacts of small datasets. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to non-image modalities (video, audio), whether it remains effective against DMs trained on extremely large web-scale datasets (e.g., LAION-5B), and whether adversarial training or data augmentation on the DM side could suppress the membership signal that CDI exploits.
