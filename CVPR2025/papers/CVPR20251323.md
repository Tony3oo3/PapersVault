## Problem

Despite numerous optical flow benchmarks with physical ground truth, no large-scale dataset directly captures how humans perceive motion in natural scenes, leaving a critical gap for human-centered applications like driving assistance and animation quality control. This paper addresses the absence of a systematic comparison between human-perceived flow, physical ground truth, and CV model predictions across diverse motion scenarios.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251323/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark probes 2,400 spatiotemporal locations (a specific pixel position at a specific frame) across 10 optical flow datasets, presenting 15-frame video clips (500 ms at 30 Hz) with a flash probe at the middle frame. Observers report perceived flow as a 2D vector (direction 0°–360° and speed 1–20 pixels/frame) via a method-of-adjustment matching task, yielding 38,400 response vectors (16 repetitions per location from 4 observers). Evaluation metrics include endpoint error (EPE), Pearson correlation, partial correlation controlling for ground truth, and a Response Consistency Index (RCI) measuring alignment with human-specific flow illusions.

## Method

The core contribution is a novel online psychophysical paradigm that enables large-scale collection of precise human-perceived optical flow. Spatial standardization is achieved via credit-card calibration (50 px = 1° visual angle), and temporal precision is maintained at 30 Hz with monitored frame drops (0.041% rate, onset error −0.055 ms). Observers adjust a circular Brownian noise matching stimulus (1/f² spectrum, 120 px diameter) using a mouse cursor in a 600 px polar panel, where direction maps to angle and speed maps logarithmically to radial distance, allowing free adjustment over multiple repetition cycles. A preliminary RDK session selects 42 reliable observers (group correlation r = 0.955 with GT), who then complete 480 sessions across the 10 datasets. The paper evaluates 10 representative CV models (spanning gradient-based, recurrent, transformer, multi-frame, and biologically inspired architectures) against both human responses and ground truth, revealing that while top models like VideoFlow and RAFT align closely with GT, they show low partial correlation with human perception, indicating they fail to capture human-specific perceptual biases such as self-motion compensation and grouping effects.

## Results

Across 10 optical flow benchmarks (2,400 probed locations, 38,400 trials), VideoFlow achieves the lowest EPE vs. ground truth (1.91 px) and highest r_uv vs. GT (0.94), confirming its status as the most physically accurate model. However, when controlling for ground-truth influence via partial correlation (ρ_uv), FFV1MT (0.31) and V1Attention-StageI (0.23) show the strongest alignment with human-specific flow illusions, with RCI values of 0.15 and 0.18 respectively. RAFT achieves the best overall Pearson correlation with human responses (r_uv = 0.64, r_dir = 0.47) while maintaining strong GT alignment (EPE = 2.19 px). Ground truth itself correlates only moderately with human perception (r_uv = 0.66, EPE = 6.96 px), revealing systematic perceptual deviations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 10 optical flow models against both human-perceived flow and physical ground truth, revealing a fundamental trade-off. VideoFlow [37] and FlowFormer [14] dominate on GT alignment (EPE 1.91 and 2.08 px; r_uv 0.94 and 0.93) but show near-zero partial correlation with human-specific biases (ρ_uv = 0.08, 0.07), meaning their accuracy is largely explained by fitting GT rather than capturing perceptual quirks. RAFT [42] achieves the best raw Pearson correlation with human responses (r_uv = 0.64) while retaining strong GT performance (EPE = 2.19), but its partial correlation (ρ_uv = 0.11) and RCI (0.05) remain low. In contrast, the biologically-inspired FFV1MT [38] (ρ_uv = 0.31, RCI = 0.15) and V1Attention-StageI [40] (ρ_uv = 0.23, RCI = 0.18) best reproduce human flow illusions—such as temporal sluggishness and self-motion compensation—but at the cost of much higher EPE vs. GT (5.84 and 7.00 px). Classical Farneback [11] and graph-based AGFlow [23] occupy a middle ground with moderate partial correlations (0.17, 0.16) but poor GT accuracy (EPE 5.52, 5.44). FlowNet2 [15], Perceiver IO [17], and DualModel [41] show no clear advantage over either extreme, with partial correlations between 0.09–0.20 and EPE vs. GT ranging from 2.87 to 5.66 px. Overall, no single model simultaneously matches both human perception and ground truth, highlighting a persistent gap that the HuPerFlow benchmark is designed to address.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the single prior psychophysical study by Yang et al. [47], which measured human-perceived flow in only five MPI Sintel SlowFlow movies presented through a circular aperture, into a comprehensive benchmark spanning ten diverse optical flow datasets (KITTI, Sintel, VIPER, TartanAir, SPRING, Monkaa, MHOF, Flythings3D, Virtual KITTI, Driving) with full-image presentation. It also builds on the extensive CV optical flow benchmarking literature by adding a previously absent human-perception dimension, and on the diverse model zoo (classical Farneback, biologically-inspired FFV1MT, recurrent RAFT, transformer FlowFormer, multi-frame VideoFlow, graph-based AGFlow, human-inspired V1Attention/DualModel) to systematically compare machine predictions against both ground truth and human responses. The online psychophysical paradigm using Brownian noise matching is adapted from the lab-based method in [47] but made feasible at scale through careful temporal and spatial calibration.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

HuPerFlow fills a critical gap: no prior large-scale benchmark simultaneously captures ground truth, human-perceived flow, and model predictions in natural scenes, making it the first resource for studying human-machine alignment in motion estimation. The finding that even top-performing CV models (VideoFlow, RAFT) exhibit near-zero partial correlation with human perception after controlling for ground truth suggests that current training objectives fundamentally fail to capture systematic human perceptual biases ('flow illusions'), pointing to a need for new loss functions or architectures. Limitations include the relatively small observer pool (42 participants), 2,400 probed locations, and the fact that the dataset is likely insufficient for training from scratch—positioning it as an evaluation and fine-tuning resource. Open questions include whether models can be explicitly trained to predict human-perceived flow, what computational mechanisms underlie the identified flow illusions, and whether the online paradigm generalizes to more complex or longer-duration stimuli.
