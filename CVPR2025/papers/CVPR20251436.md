## Problem

Existing font quality assessment models provide only basic visual scores without interpretable explanations, and multimodal large language models lack the domain-specific expertise needed to evaluate fine-grained font details such as stroke curvature and structural symmetry. Additionally, no large-scale dataset exists that pairs font images with both quality labels and detailed textual justifications, making it difficult to train or evaluate interpretable font assessment systems.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251436/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model accepts an RGB font image (rendered from SVG sources) paired with a natural-language instruction or question sampled from a predefined template. It outputs a structured response containing a MOS score (1–5), a discrete quality label (high/medium/low), a free-text explanation of specific defects (e.g., irregularity, asymmetry, lack of detail), and the recognized text content. The DFD dataset provides 135,000 such font-text pairs spanning English (~63%), Chinese (~26%), and other languages (~11%), with 80% machine-generated and 20% manually crafted samples.

## Method

Font-Agent builds on Qwen-VL and introduces two key components. First, an Edge-Aware Traces (EAT) module extracts glyph control points via Farthest Point Sampling, constructs a saliency map, and applies FFT-based frequency-domain weighting combined with a Gaussian high-pass filter to isolate high-frequency stroke details; the magnitude and phase of the filtered spectrum are then enhanced with Central Difference Convolution (CDC) before being fused back into the VLM's visual features via cross-attention. Second, a Dynamic Direct Preference Optimization (D-DPO) loss adaptively reweights each output token based on character uncertainty, contextual consistency, glyph feature quality, and self-consistency voting across k candidate outputs, so that hallucinated or low-quality segments receive stronger corrective signal while high-quality segments are reinforced. The model is trained in two phases (15 epochs at 1e-4, then 8 epochs at 4e-6) on four A100 GPUs using the DFD dataset.

## Results

On the DFD dataset, Font-Agent achieves 93.8% average ACC and 95.1% average AUC for font quality classification, outperforming the best traditional classifier (Xception, 87.9% ACC / 86.0% AUC) by over 5% and the best multimodal LLM (BLIP2, 74.6% ACC / 74.3% AUC) by nearly 20%. For continuous quality scoring, Font-Agent reaches 0.830 SRCC and 0.823 PLCC, surpassing Q-Align (0.785 / 0.815) by 4.5% in SRCC. On the comprehensive font recognition benchmark spanning 13 datasets, Font-Agent attains 91.32% average word accuracy, exceeding the previous best (MAERec, 89.46%) by 1.86%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Font-Agent decisively outperforms all 31 baselines across every evaluation protocol. Against traditional classifiers, it surpasses Xception (the strongest CNN baseline) by 5.9% ACC and 9.1% AUC on average, with the gap widening to 9.6% ACC on Chinese fonts where stroke complexity is highest. Among multimodal LLMs, the advantage is even more pronounced: GPT-4o scores only 62.5% ACC / 60.5% AUC, and BLIP2 reaches 74.6% / 74.3%, both far below Font-Agent's 93.8% / 95.1%. For continuous quality scoring (Table 2), Font-Agent leads Q-Align (0.785 SRCC) by 4.5 points and VILA (0.772) by 5.8 points, confirming that the EAT module's frequency-domain edge extraction and D-DPO's adaptive weighting are critical for fine-grained assessment. On the six public IQA benchmarks (Table 3), Font-Agent achieves the best or near-best SRCC on all six (e.g., 0.873 on LIVE vs. 0.849 for Compare2Score; 0.885 on KADID-10k vs. 0.870 for Compare2Score), demonstrating generalization beyond font-specific data. In font recognition (Table 4), Font-Agent leads MAERec by 1.86% average word accuracy and E2STR by 2.12%, with particularly large gains on irregular text (IC15: 94.06% vs. 93.04%) and occluded text (HOST: 76.54% vs. 73.97%), validating the EAT module's benefit for complex glyph structures.

## Relation to Prior Work

Font-Agent builds on the Qwen-VL vision-language model as its backbone, extending it with two domain-specific modules: an Edge-Aware Traces (EAT) component that applies frequency-domain attention and Central Difference Convolution to capture fine stroke geometry, and a Dynamic Direct Preference Optimization (D-DPO) strategy that replaces uniform token weighting in standard DPO with character-level adaptive weights based on uncertainty, consistency, and glyph features. Conceptually, the paper departs from both traditional IQA classifiers (Xception, EfficientNet) that produce opaque scores, and general-purpose MLLMs (GPT-4o, LLaVA, BLIP2) that lack the professional font-domain knowledge needed for interpretable quality assessment. The cross-attention fusion mechanism is borrowed from E2STR, and the CDC operator is adapted from face anti-spoofing literature, repurposed here to extract local stroke-level differences in the frequency domain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 135,000-sample DFD dataset is a practical resource for the font generation community, which has historically lacked large-scale, multilingual, expert-annotated benchmarks with both quality labels and textual justifications. The interpretability angle—producing natural-language explanations alongside MOS scores—addresses a real gap in production font pipelines where designers need to understand *why* a generated font is flawed. Open questions include whether the EAT module generalizes to other symbolic/graphical domains (logos, icons, diagrams), whether D-DPO's character-level weighting transfers to other structured-output tasks, and how performance scales as font generation models produce increasingly complex multilingual compositions. The paper's evaluation is somewhat narrow, relying heavily on its own dataset for the primary claims, and the comparison with GPT-4o (a closed, rapidly evolving model) may not reflect the trajectory of general-purpose MLLMs.
