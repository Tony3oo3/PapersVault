## Problem

Flash/no-flash reflection removal is a practical and accessible technique, but it critically depends on perfectly aligned image pairs, which is infeasible under handheld capture. Existing pre-alignment approaches (optical flow, homography) fail because the flash alters the appearance of the transmitted scene, making standard registration unreliable. This paper addresses the misalignment barrier to make flash/no-flash reflection separation robust in uncontrolled real-world conditions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251655/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a potentially misaligned pair of RGB images (one with flash, one without) as input, with no requirement for pixel-level alignment. It produces two separated RGB images: the transmission scene (content behind the glass) and the reflection scene (content reflected on the glass). The method works with both RAW and tonemapped inputs, and the flash/no-flash pair is consumed jointly as a conditioning signal rather than being differenced in pixel space.

## Method

Flash-Split performs reflection separation in a VAE latent space rather than pixel space, exploiting the fact that reduced spatial resolution and aggregated features make the model robust to small-to-moderate misalignment. Stage 1 uses a dual-branch diffusion UNet (initialized from Stable Diffusion v2) where both branches are conditioned on the encoded flash/no-flash latent pair and exchange information via inter-branch cross-attention, iteratively denoising to predict separate transmission and reflection latents. Stage 2 employs a cross-latent decoder: a modified UNet whose encoder processes the original unseparated image while the decoder processes the separated latent, connected only through zero-convolution skip connections, thereby restoring high-frequency details faithful to the captured scene without the hallucinations of a vanilla VAE decoder. The key insight is that flash cues remain effective in latent space because they operate in the low-frequency domain, and separation in a compact representation is inherently easier than in full-resolution RGB.

## Results

On the Lei et al. flash/no-flash dataset, Flash-Split (cross-latent decoder + cross-attention) achieves 32.06 PSNR / 0.964 SSIM / 0.042 LPIPS for transmission and 30.37 PSNR / 0.937 SSIM / 0.161 LPIPS for reflection, outperforming all baselines on every metric. The closest competitor, Lei et al. [31] with optical-flow alignment, reaches 28.60 PSNR for transmission and 26.29 PSNR for reflection, yielding a gap of ~3.5 dB (transmission) and ~4.1 dB (reflection). Single-image methods (Dong, Hu, Zhu) cluster around 25.4–25.7 PSNR for transmission and do not produce reflection outputs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Flash-Split decisively outperforms all six baselines on every reported metric. Against the strongest flash/no-flash baseline, Lei et al. [31] (which uses PWC-Net optical flow for pre-alignment), Flash-Split gains +3.46 dB PSNR on transmission (32.06 vs. 28.60) and +4.08 dB on reflection (30.37 vs. 26.29), with LPIPS dropping from 0.071→0.042 (transmission) and 0.383→0.161 (reflection). The single-image methods—Dong et al. [9], Hu et al. [21], and Zhu et al. [69]—all fall in the 25.4–25.7 PSNR range for transmission and are roughly 6.5 dB below Flash-Split; Zhu et al. and SDN [6] do not output a reflection estimate at all. The burst-imaging method of Chugunov et al. [8] is compared only qualitatively (Figs. 7–10), where Flash-Split produces cleaner separations in all four real-world scenes. Notably, even the ablated variant without cross-attention (31.61 PSNR transmission) still exceeds Lei et al. by ~3 dB, indicating the latent-diffusion separation itself is the primary source of the gain rather than any single architectural add-on.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the flash/no-flash reflection removal paradigm (Agrawal et al. [1], Lei et al. [31]) by relocating the separation operation from RGB pixel space to a VAE latent space, building on the Stable Diffusion architecture [43]. It departs from Lei et al.'s approach of pre-aligning misaligned flash/no-flash pairs via optical flow (PWC-Net) or homography, which the authors show is fragile because the flash alters the transmitted scene's appearance. The dual-branch diffusion UNet with inter-branch cross-attention and the cross-latent decoding module are new architectural contributions that have no direct precedent in prior reflection removal work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Flash/no-flash capture is the most accessible hardware-free cue for reflection removal (any smartphone with a flash), yet the misalignment sensitivity has prevented practical adoption; this paper removes that barrier, making the technique viable for everyday users. The latent-space separation insight—that VAE encoding naturally aggregates local pixel information and thus tolerates spatial misalignment—is a general principle that could transfer to other paired-image tasks. Open questions include whether the approach extends to video (temporal consistency), whether it handles extreme misalignment or very strong reflections, and whether the 2D separation can be integrated with 3D reconstruction (as in the companion Flash-Splat work) without additional alignment assumptions.
