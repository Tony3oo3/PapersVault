## Problem

Previous evaluations of multi-exit neural networks' adversarial robustness fix the set of exits targeted by the attacker, which creates an Attack-Defense (A-D) mismatch that inflates measured robustness by up to 13.52%. No prior work modeled the attacker's freedom to adaptively choose which exit ensemble to attack, leaving the intrinsic robustness of these networks systematically overestimated.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251307/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework operates on standard RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/SVHN, 64×64 for Tiny ImageNet) with l∞ perturbation bound ε=8/255. Unusually, both the attacker and defender select a subset of the network's L exits: the attacker picks an exit ensemble E_a to maximize adversarial loss, while the defender picks an inference ensemble E_d to aggregate logits. The NEED defense outputs a probability distribution over all 2^L−1 possible exit ensembles as its inference strategy, rather than a single fixed exit.

## Method

The paper formulates multi-exit adversarial attack-defense as a two-player zero-sum game where each player's action is a non-empty subset of exits, with payoff equal to negative robust accuracy. AIMER (AdaptIve evaluation of Multi-Exit Robustness) computes the attacker's best-response mixed strategy s^{a*} by maximizing expected payoff against the defender's known strategy, then generates adversarial examples by sampling an exit ensemble from s^{a*} and applying a standard attack (PGD, FGSM, etc.) to that ensemble. NEED (Nash Equilibrium Enhanced Defense) solves for the defender's mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium via a linear program (maximin over the payoff matrix), yielding a probability distribution over exit ensembles that maximizes worst-case robust accuracy. The payoff matrix is approximated from a subset of the dataset using average-attack evaluations, since exhaustive testing of all 2^L−1 exit pairs is computationally infeasible.

## Results

Under AIMER evaluation with AutoAttack, the proposed scheme measures robustness 13.52% lower than the max-average attack baseline, revealing that previous evaluations overestimate defense capacity due to Attack-Defense (A-D) mismatch. When combined with adversarial training on VGG-16/CIFAR-10, NEED consistently improves robust accuracy: PGD-AT+NEED reaches 50.61% vs. 47.86% for PGD-AT alone under PGD-20, and FAT+NEED reaches 47.76% vs. 44.73% for FAT alone. On ResNet-18/CIFAR-10, NEED-enhanced multi-exit inference achieves 52.77% robust accuracy, surpassing the single-exit baseline (49.82%) by 2.95% and the dynamic inference baseline (51.30%) by 1.47%. AIMER also reduces computational cost: complete evaluation takes 3.0×10⁷ ms vs. 1.09×10⁸ ms for max-average attack.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NEED consistently outperforms all four adversarial training baselines (PGD-AT, TRADES, MART, FAT) when combined with each on VGG-16/CIFAR-10, with the largest gains under PGD-20: +2.75% over PGD-AT, +0.96% over TRADES, +0.70% over MART, and +3.03% over FAT. The improvement is most pronounced for FAT+NEED under AutoAttack (43.61% vs. 38.03%, a +5.58% gain), confirming the paper's headline claim. Against the single-exit baseline, NEED-enhanced multi-exit inference surpasses it by up to 5.58% (VGG-16/SVHN: 45.32% vs. 43.35%), demonstrating that the multi-exit structure with game-theoretic inference can exceed what a single-exit network achieves. Compared to the dynamic inference baselines from [16] and [3], NEED achieves higher robust accuracy under AIMER evaluation (e.g., 52.77% vs. 51.30% on ResNet-18/CIFAR-10), while also maintaining competitive clean accuracy. In terms of evaluation methodology, AIMER produces lower (more stringent) robustness scores than single, average, and max-average attacks across all tested configurations, with the gap largest for static inference strategies (13.52% under AutoAttack).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the adversarial robustness literature on multi-exit neural networks (Hu et al. 2020, Chen et al. 2022, Ham et al. 2023) by identifying a fundamental evaluation flaw: prior work used fixed attack recipes (single, average, max-average) that implicitly assumed the attacker cannot strategically choose which exits to target. Drawing on the game-theoretic framework of Meunier et al. (2021) for randomized classifiers, the paper models the multi-exit attack-defense interaction as a two-player zero-sum game where both players strategically select exit ensembles, introducing a 'partial attack' formulation that subsumes all prior attack types. The key departure is treating exit selection as a strategic variable rather than a fixed hyperparameter, which reveals the previously overlooked Attack-Defense (A-D) mismatch phenomenon.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that prior evaluations overestimate robustness by up to 13.52% under AutoAttack is a significant methodological correction for the multi-exit network community, as it means published robustness numbers may not reflect true intrinsic defense quality. The game-theoretic framing (AIMER for evaluation, NEED for defense) provides a principled, computationally cheaper alternative to max-average attack that also enables the defender to actively exploit residual mismatch through Nash equilibrium strategies. Limitations include the white-box assumption, the need to approximate the payoff matrix via subset sampling, and evaluation restricted to relatively small-scale datasets (CIFAR-10, SVHN, Tiny ImageNet). Open questions include whether the A-D mismatch phenomenon persists under black-box or transfer settings, and whether the game-theoretic defense generalizes to other adaptive inference architectures beyond multi-exit networks.
