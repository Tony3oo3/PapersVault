## Problem

Existing exposure fusion methods can only robustly handle 3–4 stops of exposure difference; when the gap reaches 9 stops, they fail due to misalignment, lighting inconsistency, and tone-mapping artifacts. No prior technique can fuse such extreme exposure pairs in dynamic scenes with large motion while producing artifact-free, naturally tone-mapped output.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252727/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pair of 512×512 RGB images — one over-exposed (serving as the base/reference) and one under-exposed (serving as soft guidance) — with exposure differences up to 9 stops. Rather than producing a linear HDR image that requires a separate tone-mapping step, UltraFusion directly outputs a tone-mapped LDR image, avoiding cascading errors from HDR-to-LDR compression. The over-exposed image is fully preserved in non-highlight regions, while only the blown-out highlight regions are inpainted using information from the under-exposed image.

## Method

UltraFusion reframes exposure fusion as a guided inpainting problem on top of Stable Diffusion V2.1. A pre-alignment stage uses intensity mapping, RAFT optical flow, and forward-backward consistency to warp the under-exposed image onto the over-exposed frame and mask occluded regions. The guided inpainting stage injects three signals through a novel decompose-and-fuse control branch (DFCB): the over-exposed image as the main control, the under-exposed image decomposed into brightness-invariant structure (normalized luminance) and color (chroma) components fed via multi-scale cross-attention, and the current diffusion latent. A separate fidelity control branch (FCB) mirrors the VAE encoder architecture and injects features into the VAE decoder to prevent texture distortion. Training data is synthesized by combining video frames (for realistic motion and occlusion masks) with pre-aligned static multi-exposure patches from SICE, enabling the model to learn dynamic-scene fusion without requiring a large-scale dynamic HDR dataset.

## Results

On the dynamic RealHDRV dataset, UltraFusion achieves a TMQI of 0.8925 and MUSIQ of 67.51, outperforming the best HDR reconstruction baseline SCTNet (TMQI 0.8726, MUSIQ 62.69) and the best MEF baseline HSDS-MEF (TMQI 0.8323, MUSIQ 61.76). On the newly introduced UltraFusion Benchmark (up to 9 stops exposure difference), UltraFusion scores 68.41 MUSIQ, 3.957 DeQA-Score, 75.18 PAQ2PIQ, and 0.6214 HyperIQA, leading all competitors by a large margin (e.g., +3.87 MUSIQ over HSDS-MEF). On the static MEFB dataset, UltraFusion achieves 68.82 MUSIQ and 0.9385 MEF-SSIM, surpassing HSDS-MEF (66.76 MUSIQ) while retaining comparable structural fidelity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

UltraFusion outperforms all 10 baselines on every non-reference quality metric (MUSIQ, DeQA-Score, PAQ2PIQ, HyperIQA) across all three evaluation datasets. Against the strongest HDR reconstruction method SCTNet, UltraFusion gains +4.82 MUSIQ and +0.0199 TMQI on RealHDRV, and +6.57 MUSIQ on the UltraFusion Benchmark, indicating that diffusion-based inpainting produces substantially more natural outputs than learned tone-mapping pipelines. Against the strongest MEF method HSDS-MEF, UltraFusion leads by +5.75 MUSIQ on RealHDRV and +3.87 MUSIQ on the UltraFusion Benchmark, while HSDS-MEF retains a slight edge in MEF-SSIM (0.9520 vs. 0.9385) on the static MEFB set, suggesting UltraFusion trades a small amount of structural fidelity for markedly better perceptual quality. On the dynamic RealHDRV set, MEF baselines (Defusion, MEFLUT, HSDS-MEF) score 0.04–0.07 lower in TMQI than UltraFusion, confirming that their static-scene assumptions break down under motion. A user study with 136 participants on 20 benchmark scenes further confirms that UltraFusion is preferred over all competitors in subjective quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reframes exposure fusion — traditionally approached either as linear-domain HDR reconstruction followed by tone mapping, or as direct LDR-domain multi-exposure fusion — as a guided inpainting problem, where the over-exposed image serves as the base and the under-exposed image provides soft guidance for filling blown-out highlights. Architecturally, it builds on Stable Diffusion's latent diffusion backbone and extends ControlNet's control-branch design with a decompose-and-fuse mechanism (separating luminance-robust structure and color from the guidance image) plus a fidelity control branch that shortcuts into the VAE decoder. Prior diffusion-based HDR work has focused on deghosting or single-image enhancement; this is the first to use a second exposure as inpainting guidance, and the first to handle 9-stop exposure differences.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The guided-inpainting formulation offers a principled reason for robustness to misalignment and lighting variation (soft guidance vs. hard pixel constraints), and the new UltraFusion benchmark (100 real-world pairs up to 9 stops, diverse motion and scenes) fills a gap left by existing datasets that cap at 3–4 stops or lack realistic motion. Key open questions remain: inference takes ~3.3 s per 512×512 pair on an RTX 4090, occlusion-mask errors can silently degrade the task to unreliable single-image HDR, and the method's generalization beyond the tested exposure range is unverified. The demonstrated extension to arbitrary image fusion (e.g., transferring a moon or sky from an unrelated image) hints at broader applicability but is only a preliminary demo.
