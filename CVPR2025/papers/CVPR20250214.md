## Problem

Nearly all existing OOD detection methods assume access to a clean training dataset of only in-distribution samples, yet real-world datasets are inevitably contaminated with unlabeled OOD samples, which can cause up to a 30-point drop in detection performance even at 5% contamination. No prior work provides a model-agnostic framework that integrates with the wide variety of existing OOD detection methods to handle such contamination, and no method estimates the unknown proportion of OOD samples within the training data itself.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250214/cropped_page3_idx4.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes an unlabeled contaminated training dataset (a mixture of ID and OOD samples with no labels) and an untrained backbone OOD detection model, operating in a plug-and-play manner across both backbone types that use and those that do not use synthetic negative samples. It outputs a trained OOD detection model, per-sample normalized OOD scores in [0,1], and an estimated OOD percentage of the training set. The iterative procedure terminates when the OOD percentage estimate converges (relative change below 5%), and the weight range is extended to [-1,1] for negative-sample backbones to unify the loss over the entire mixed dataset without requiring a hard ID/OOD split.

## Method

The core mechanism is an iterative reweighting loop that alternates between training the backbone model and updating a sample-wise sigmoid weight function w_i^(t) = 1/(1+exp(α(s_i^(t)/τ^(t) − P̂_ID^(t)))), where s_i^(t) is the current OOD score and P̂_ID^(t) is a recursively updated estimate of the ID ratio. A transition function τ^(t) = k/t + 1 divides the OOD score in early rounds, keeping weights high for all samples (including misclassified ID samples) to follow a curriculum-learning principle, then monotonically decays to 1 so that later rounds rely on increasingly reliable scores. The OOD percentage is estimated each round by thresholding moving-average OOD scores at 1 − P̂_OOD^(t−1), breaking the circular dependency between knowing the contamination rate and training a good detector. For backbones that use negative samples, the weight range is shifted to [−1,1] via w_i^(t) = 2/(1+exp(α(s_i^(t)/τ^(t) − P̂_ID^(t)))) − 1, allowing the original single loss to be applied over the entire dataset without a hard ID/OOD partition or an extra balancing hyperparameter.

## Results

Across diverse ID/OOD dataset pairs (CIFAR-100, ImageNet-1k as ID; Tiny-ImageNet, Mini-ImageNet, iNaturalist, OpenImage-O as OOD) at 1% contamination, the proposed framework consistently improves AUROC over both the backbone-only model and the IAD baseline by 1–3 points (e.g., ReAct on ImageNet-1k vs. iNaturalist: 63.9 → 67.6 → 70.3 AUROC). For OOD percentage estimation, the method achieves a normalized error ε of 0.000–0.002 across all backbone models and dataset combinations, while IAD cannot estimate the contamination rate at all (fixed at 0.5, yielding ε = 0.980). Ablation studies confirm that both the iterative OOD percentage estimation and the transition mechanism τ^(t) are essential, with removing either component degrading AUROC by 1–4 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is IAD [18], an iterative anomaly detection method adapted for OOD detection on contaminated data. The proposed method wins on every single test case across all backbone models (DB, G-ODIN, ReAct, CSI, CnC, VOS), all dataset pairs, and all contamination levels (1%, 2%, 5%), typically by 1–3 AUROC points over IAD (e.g., ReAct on CIFAR-10 vs. Celeb_A at 1%: 69.1 IAD vs. 72.2 Ours). The gap is most pronounced for G-ODIN on CIFAR-100 vs. M-ImageNet (56.8 IAD vs. 60.4 Ours, +3.6 points) and for models using negative samples (VOS on CIFAR-100 vs. T-ImageNet: 58.5 IAD vs. 62.1 Ours, +3.6 points). For the secondary task of OOD percentage estimation, the proposed method achieves near-perfect accuracy (ε ≤ 0.002) while IAD is fundamentally incapable of estimation, as it hard-codes the contamination rate at 0.5 (ε = 0.980 at 1% true contamination). No comparison where the proposed method loses or ties is reported.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of OOD detection and data contamination, a setting the authors note is 'mostly unexplored' in the OOD literature. Conceptually, the iterative reweighting strategy draws on curriculum learning (easy-to-hard sample scheduling), noisy-label learning, and simplicity bias in deep networks, but repurposes these ideas into a plug-and-play wrapper around arbitrary backbone OOD detectors. The closest prior work is IAD [18], a model-agnostic anomaly detection method for contaminated data, which the authors adapt as a baseline but note is not model-agnostic for OOD (it cannot handle negative-sample-based methods) and lacks any contamination-ratio estimation. The transition mechanism τ^(t) = k/t + 1 is a novel design element not present in prior reweighting or curriculum approaches, specifically addressing the circular dependency between unreliable early OOD scores and the weights derived from them.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical relevance is significant: real-world training sets (even ImageNet) contain OOD contaminants, and the authors show that even 5% contamination can cause a 30-point AUROC drop, yet nearly all existing OOD methods assume clean data. The model-agnostic, plug-and-play design means practitioners can retrofit their existing OOD pipeline without redesigning the backbone, and the OOD proportion estimation (achieved with ε < 0.02 across tested settings) provides a useful diagnostic for dataset quality. Open questions include performance under higher contamination rates (beyond 5%), scalability to multi-modal or tabular OOD settings, and whether the iterative overhead is acceptable in resource-constrained deployment. The single comparable baseline (IAD, adapted from AD) limits confidence in the magnitude of gains over a purpose-built OOD contamination method.
