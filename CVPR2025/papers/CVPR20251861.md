## Problem

Existing industrial anomaly detection datasets (MVTec-AD, MPDD, VisA, MVTec-3D, etc.) focus on static, single-view, semantically simple scenarios with surface-level defects, diverging sharply from real-world industrial settings where anomalies arise from dynamic physical interactions requiring reasoning about object properties, applied forces, and temporal behavior. No prior dataset or benchmark captures the need for physical understanding and interactive reasoning, leaving a gap between academic IAD research and the demands of robotic inspection in actual factories.

## Input / Output

The dataset provides short RGB video clips (60–240 frames at 1080p, 60 FPS) from a fixed camera showing a UR5 robotic arm or motor/servo performing interactions (pressing, rotating, squeezing, driving) on industrial objects. Training uses only normal interaction videos; test videos carry video-level binary labels, and for VLM evaluation, manually authored text labels combine a video content description with a physical-cause explanation (augmented via ChatGPT-4o). Models output a video-level anomaly score, and VLM-based methods additionally produce free-text anomaly descriptions and physical explanations scored by the PAEval metric.

## Method

The paper formulates physics-grounded anomaly detection as a two-step process: (1) Rules Deduction, where normal interaction videos V_i combined with category-specific physical priors P_i are passed through a deduction function f to build a rules bank M of expected normal behaviors; (2) Anomaly Reasoning, where test interaction videos are evaluated against these rules by a reasoner R to yield anomaly scores. The dataset is collected with a UR5 robotic arm and motor/servo drives, capturing over 6,400 videos across 22 categories, 49 object types, and 47 anomaly types spanning persistent (always visible) and intermittent (appearing mid-sequence) failures. The PAEval metric extends standard AUROC by adding description accuracy (correctly narrating physical phenomena in the video) and explanation accuracy (identifying the underlying physical cause), both assessed by an LLM-powered evaluation agent. The benchmark evaluates methods under three settings—unsupervised (reconstruction/prediction/memory-based), weakly-supervised (2–4 video-level anomaly labels), and video-understanding (VLMs generating language rules from normal training data)—revealing that the best method reaches only 66.9% AUROC and VLMs score below 31% on physical explanation.

## Results

On the Phys-AD dataset, the best-performing method across all settings is MNAD.r (unsupervised, prediction-based) with an average video-level AUROC of 66.9%, followed by S3R (weakly-supervised) at 61.3% and Video-LLaMA (video-understanding) at 52.3%. Zero-shot CLIP and ImageBind both score exactly 50.0% (chance level), while Video-ChatGPT (49.6%) and Video-LLaVA (46.3%) fall below chance. Under the PAEval metric, the best VLM (Video-LLaMA) achieves only 30.3% in physical explanation and 21.9% in description (Video-LLaVA), indicating that no current model can reliably reason about the underlying physics of industrial anomalies.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among unsupervised methods, MNAD.r (66.9% AUROC) substantially outperforms MemAE (53.8%) and MPN (51.1%), with its prediction-based temporal modeling excelling on dynamic anomalies (e.g., 80.0% on Rolling Bearing, 94.4% on Car). MemAE, in contrast, achieves 96.7% on Sticky Roller (spatial anomaly) but collapses to 4.4% on Rolling Bearing, revealing its inability to capture temporal dynamics. In the weakly-supervised setting, S3R (61.3%) and MGFN (60.6%) provide modest gains over their unsupervised counterparts, with S3R reaching 83.5% on Sticky Roller but dropping to 26.6% on the same category under LAVAD. All video-understanding baselines perform near or below chance: ZS Clip and ZS ImgB score exactly 50.0%, V-ChatGPT (49.6%) and V-LLaVA (46.3%) are below random, and even the best VLM (Video-LLaMA, 52.3%) trails MNAD.r by 14.6 points. Under PAEval, no VLM exceeds 30.3% in physical explanation (Video-LLaMA) or 21.9% in description (Video-LLaVA), while LAVAD scores 0.000 in explanation, confirming that current vision-language models fundamentally lack the physics-grounded reasoning required by this task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of industrial anomaly detection (IAD) and video anomaly detection (VAD) by introducing a physics-grounded, interaction-based formulation that neither prior IAD datasets (MVTec-AD, MPDD, VisA, MVTec-3D) nor VAD benchmarks address. While prior IAD work focuses on static, single-view surface defects and VAD methods target surveillance-style temporal irregularities, Phys-AD requires models to reason about physical properties (elasticity, friction, fluid dynamics) revealed through robotic interaction. The PAEval metric extends the evaluation paradigm beyond AUROC by borrowing the description/explanation decomposition from causal video anomaly benchmarks (e.g., Holmes-VAD) and applying it specifically to physical reasoning, a dimension absent from both IAD and VAD evaluation protocols.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Phys-AD exposes a fundamental gap: no current method—whether reconstruction-based, memory-augmented, or VLM-based—can reliably detect anomalies that require understanding physical dynamics, with the best AUROC reaching only 66.9% and VLM explanation scores capping at 30.3%. This motivates future research on integrating physical priors, temporal reasoning, and causal explanation into anomaly detection pipelines. Open questions include how to efficiently incorporate domain-specific physics knowledge into learned representations, whether scaling VLMs or adding physics simulators (e.g., CoPhy, SPACE) can close the reasoning gap, and how the benchmark generalizes beyond the 22 categories and specific robotic interaction modalities tested here.
