## Problem

Existing membership inference attacks in federated learning rely solely on the target client's updates (gradient norms, loss values, gradient differences), ignoring the rich information contained in non-target clients' updates. This underutilization of cross-client information limits attack effectiveness, and prior attempts to incorporate additional data require auxiliary datasets or shadow models that are impractical in the FL setting where the server lacks access to local data.

## Input / Output

The attacker (semi-honest server) receives a target sample (x, y) and the full set of client updates I = {I_k^t | t ∈ [T], k ∈ [K]} across T communication rounds, where updates can be model parameters, gradients (FedAvg), or embeddings/prompts (FedEmbedding). The output is a binary membership prediction: 1 if (x,y) ∈ D_tar, 0 otherwise, determined by comparing the aggregated likelihood-ratio statistic against a threshold δ. The method requires no auxiliary dataset, no shadow model training, and no knowledge of which specific updates were trained on the target data.

## Method

FedMIA is a three-step attack following the 'all for one' principle. Step 1 computes a low-dimensional measurement M(I|(x,y)) via cosine similarity between each client's uploaded gradient and the model gradient on the target sample, reducing the high-dimensional update to a scalar. Step 2 estimates the distribution Q_out of updates NOT trained on (x,y) by collecting measurements from all non-target clients and filtering out extreme values (exceeding μ + 3σ) that likely correspond to data overlapping with the target, then fitting a Gaussian to the remaining measurements. Step 3 applies a one-tailed likelihood-ratio test: it computes the CDF of the estimated Q_out at the target client's measurement value for each round, averages across T rounds, and declares membership if the aggregated statistic exceeds δ. Theorem 1 proves that any member detected by the multi-round statistic is detected in at least one individual round, establishing the validity of the temporal aggregation.

## Results

FedMIA-II (using gradient-cosine measurement) achieves the highest TPR@FPR=0.1% across most settings: 66.98±1.74% on AlexNet-CIFAR100, 62.27±0.23% on AlexNet-DermNet, and 68.74±1.84% on ResNet18-CIFAR100, with AUC values of 0.87–0.89. FedMIA-I (using model-loss measurement) is competitive, reaching 57.36±2.12% TPR on ResNet18-CIFAR100. On generative tasks (Latent Diffusion Model), FedMIA-II still leads with 4.50±0.20% TPR on Tiny-ImageNet and 3.0±0.20% TPR on CIFAR-100, though absolute values are lower due to the different attack surface (embeddings vs. gradients).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FedMIA-II (gradient-cosine measurement) decisively outperforms all six baselines on the primary classification settings: on AlexNet-CIFAR100 it reaches 66.98% TPR@FPR=0.1%, exceeding the next-best Avg-Cosine [23] (54.66%) by 12.3 points and Loss-Series [10] (25.3%) by over 40 points. On ResNet18-CIFAR100, FedMIA-II achieves 68.74% TPR, again surpassing Avg-Cosine (44.02%) and Loss-Series (16.82%). The weaker baselines—Blackbox-Loss [40] and Grad-Norm [27]—consistently produce TPR values below 1.1%, making them essentially ineffective. On the generative (Latent Diffusion) tasks, the absolute TPR values are much lower (3–4.5%) for all methods, but FedMIA-II still leads by roughly 1.5–2× over the best baseline (Avg-Cosine at 1.80% on Tiny-ImageNet). The one exception is ResNet18-DermNet, where FedMIA-I (loss-based, 35.6% TPR) slightly edges out FedMIA-II (31.8% TPR), though both still dominate all baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the likelihood-ratio test framework from Carlini et al. [3] and the gradient-similarity measurement from Li et al. [23], but departs fundamentally from prior FL MIAs that analyze only the target client's updates (loss, gradient norm, gradient difference). The key conceptual shift is formulating a one-tailed LRT using non-target clients' updates to estimate Q_out (the distribution of updates not trained on target data), thereby exploiting the 'all for one' principle of FL. It also avoids the auxiliary-dataset requirement of shadow-model approaches [10, 13], making it applicable in settings where the server has no access to local client data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that conventional FL privacy defenses (gradient perturbation, sparsification, mixup, data augmentation) are largely ineffective against FedMIA because the attack exploits cross-client information patterns rather than individual update magnitudes. This exposes a critical gap: current defenses protect individual updates but not the aggregate statistical structure that reveals membership. The authors note that secure aggregation via MPC/HE would block the attack but is computationally prohibitive, leaving an open question about practical defenses specifically designed to resist information leakage from non-target updates. The much weaker results on generative tasks (TPR ~3-4.5%) also suggest the method's effectiveness is architecture- and task-dependent.
