## Problem

Visual language models have achieved strong accuracy but remain prohibitively expensive across their entire lifecycle: training a 7B VLM can require 400+ GPU days, full fine-tuning demands over 64 GB of GPU memory, and edge deployment is constrained by limited compute. Prior work has focused almost exclusively on accuracy improvements, leaving efficiency as a largely unaddressed dimension that creates significant barriers for researchers and practitioners.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252054/cropped_page0_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

NVILA accepts images of arbitrary size and aspect ratio, which are processed via Dynamic-S² multi-scale tiling (scales of 448², 896², and 1344², with the largest scale preserving the original aspect ratio) and videos of up to 256 uniformly sampled frames. Text prompts are concatenated with the resulting visual tokens as input to the autoregressive LLM, which generates free-form text responses (answers, captions, reasoning chains). The spatial compression reduces tokens per tile from 256 to 121 via a 3×3 spatial-to-channel reshape, while temporal compression pools frames by 4×–8×, keeping total token counts manageable even for long videos.

## Method

NVILA adopts a 'scale-then-compress' paradigm: it first increases spatial resolution (via Dynamic-S² multi-scale tiling) and temporal resolution (up to 256 frames) to raise the accuracy ceiling, then compresses the resulting visual tokens (3×3 spatial-to-channel reshape and temporal averaging) to recover computational efficiency. An additional visual encoder pre-training (VEP) stage jointly tunes SigLIP and the 2-layer MLP projector to recover accuracy lost from aggressive spatial compression, yielding a 2.4× speedup. On the training side, NVILA introduces DeltaLoss-based dataset pruning (retaining 50% of data with negligible accuracy drop) and FP8 mixed-precision training (2× throughput gain). For deployment, a specialized inference engine applies W8A8 quantization to the vision tower and W4A16 (AWQ with FP16 accumulation) to the LLM backbone, achieving 1.6–2.2× faster prefilling and 1.2–2.8× faster decoding compared to Qwen2-VL.

## Results

On image benchmarks, NVILA-8B achieves 92.3 on AI2D, 93.7 on DocVQA, 80.1 on TextVQA, 65.4 on MathVista, and 85.4 on VQAv2, matching or exceeding GPT-4o on AI2D, DocVQA, TextVQA, MathVista, SEED-Bench, and VQAv2. On video benchmarks, NVILA-8B (256 frames) sets new state-of-the-art across all evaluated benchmarks, scoring 70.1 on MLVU, 68.1 on MVBench, 82.2 on NExT-QA, and 64.2 on Video-MME (w/o subtitles). In terms of efficiency, NVILA trains 5.1× faster than LLaVA-OneVision for image models and 1.9× faster for video models, while achieving 1.6–2.2× prefilling speedup and 1.2–2.8× decoding speedup over Qwen2-VL at inference time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NVILA-8B substantially outperforms Qwen2-VL-8B on AI2D (92.3 vs. 83.0), ChartQA (86.1 vs. 83.0), MathVista (65.4 vs. 58.2), and VQAv2 (85.4 vs. 82.9), but trails on DocVQA (93.7 vs. 94.5), TextVQA (80.1 vs. 84.3), and MMMU (49.9 vs. 54.1). Against InternVL2-8B, NVILA-8B wins on every benchmark except InfoVQA, with the largest gaps on AI2D (+8.5) and MathVista (+7.1). NVILA-8B also surpasses LLaVA-OneVision-8B on AI2D (+10.9), ChartQA (+6.1), and DocVQA (+6.2), while the 15B variant approaches GPT-4o on AI2D (94.1 vs. 94.2) and DocVQA (94.0 vs. 85.7). On video benchmarks, NVILA-8B (256 frames) achieves the best score on all six benchmarks, edging out Qwen2-VL on Video-MME (64.2 vs. 63.3), MLVU (70.1 vs. 65.5), and MVBench (68.1 vs. 67.0), and surpassing GPT-4o mini on Video-MME (64.2 vs. 64.8 is close, but NVILA leads on MLVU 70.1 vs. –). The key trade-off is that NVILA-8B lags behind GPT-4o and Gemini 1.5 Pro on reasoning-heavy benchmarks (MMMU: 49.9 vs. 69.1/62.2) and OCR-intensive tasks (InfoVQA: 70.7 vs. 92.8/81.0), but its efficiency gains (1.6–2.2× prefilling, 1.2–2.8× decoding speedup) make it the strongest open 8B model for deployment-constrained settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

NVILA builds directly on VILA (Lin et al., CVPR 2024) as its architectural foundation, extending it with a 'scale-then-compress' paradigm that first increases spatial resolution via Dynamic-S² (an extension of S² from Shi et al., ECCV 2024) and temporal resolution via more sampled frames, then reduces token counts through spatial-to-channel reshaping and temporal averaging (from Wang et al., ECCV 2016). For training and deployment efficiency, it integrates known techniques—FP8 mixed precision from COAT (Xi et al., 2024), AWQ-based W4A16 quantization (Lin et al., MLSys 2024), and LoRA/QLoRA fine-tuning—into a unified full-lifecycle optimization pipeline. The key conceptual departure from prior VLMs (LLaVA-OneVision, Qwen2-VL, InternVL) is the systematic application of this scale-then-compress strategy to both spatial and temporal dimensions simultaneously, rather than treating resolution and efficiency as separate design axes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This paper addresses a critical practical gap: leading VLMs have been optimized almost exclusively for accuracy, leaving training costs (up to 400 GPU-days for 7B models), fine-tuning memory (64+ GB), and edge deployment as major barriers to adoption. The demonstrated 1.9–5.1× training speedup and 1.6–2.8× inference speedup while matching or exceeding accuracy of larger proprietary models makes frontier-level VLMs accessible to a much broader research community. An open question is whether the 'scale-then-compress' paradigm generalizes to even higher compression ratios or to modalities beyond RGB video (e.g., 3D point clouds, audio-visual), and whether the finding that simple spatial-to-channel reshaping outperforms learnable compressors (TokenLearner, Perceiver Resampler) at matched token budgets reflects a fundamental property of the projector optimization landscape or is specific to the VILA architecture.
