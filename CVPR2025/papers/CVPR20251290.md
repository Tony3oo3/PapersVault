## Problem

Training diffusion models is prohibitively expensive (e.g., DALL·E 2 requires 40K A100 GPU-days), and existing acceleration methods—re-weighting (P2, Min-SNR) and re-sampling (Log-Normal, CLTS)—fail to account for the imbalanced structure of time steps. Specifically, a large fraction of time steps fall in a 'convergence area' where process increments are nearly pure noise and trivially learned, while the rapid-change 'acceleration' and 'deceleration' areas that are hardest to learn remain under-sampled. The paper identifies this redundancy and under-sampling as the root cause of inefficient training.

## Input / Output

SpeeD is a plug-and-play training strategy that operates on standard RGB images (256×256 for MetFaces/FFHQ/ImageNet-1K, 32×32 for CIFAR-10) paired with a chosen time step schedule (linear, quadratic, or cosine). It modifies only the sampling probability distribution over time steps and the per-step loss weight in the standard diffusion training loop, leaving the model architecture (U-Net or DiT), forward/reverse process formulation, and optimizer unchanged. The output is a trained diffusion model with identical inference behavior to the baseline, but reached in roughly one-third of the training iterations.

## Method

SpeeD rests on Theorem 1, which bounds the process increment δ_t = x_{t+1} − x_t in DDPM and reveals three distinct regions: acceleration, deceleration, and convergence, separated by boundary points t_{a-d} and t_{d-c}. Two components exploit this structure: (1) Asymmetric sampling replaces uniform sampling with a step function P(t) that assigns k times higher probability to time steps before a threshold τ (covering acceleration and deceleration) than to steps after τ (convergence area), with τ derived analytically from the theorem to suppress only trivial steps; (2) Change-aware weighting assigns per-step loss weights proportional to the rescaled gradient of the process-increment variance ∂_t Ψ̂_t, emphasizing the rapid-change interval near t_{a-d}. The approach is architecture-agnostic (validated on U-Net and DiT), schedule-agnostic (linear, quadratic, cosine), and task-agnostic (unconditional, conditional, and text-to-image generation), achieving ~3× training acceleration with O(1) extra overhead.

## Results

On MetFaces (256×256) at 50K iterations, SpeeD achieves FID 21.1, outperforming the DiT-XL/2 baseline (29.3) by 8.2 points and the best competing method CLTS (23.5) by 2.4 points. On FFHQ (256×256) at 50K iterations, SpeeD reaches FID 9.9 versus 12.9 for the baseline and 12.7 for CLTS. The method consistently achieves approximately 3× training acceleration (up to 4× in long-term 200K-iteration runs on FFHQ) with only O(1) extra time complexity, saving e.g. 48 hours of training on 8 A6000 GPUs for DiT-XL/2.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SpeeD outperforms all five main baselines on both MetFaces and FFHQ at 50K iterations. Against the DiT-XL/2 baseline, SpeeD reduces FID from 29.3→21.1 (MetFaces) and 12.9→9.9 (FFHQ). Among re-weighting methods, P2 (23.3/15.0) and Min-SNR (28.6/12.6) are both clearly beaten, with Min-SNR showing the weakest improvement over baseline. Among re-sampling methods, CLTS (23.5/12.7) is the closest competitor but still trails SpeeD by 2.4 and 2.8 FID points respectively; Log-Normal performs worst (47.3 on both datasets), confirming that naive middle-step emphasis is insufficient. In compatibility experiments, SpeeD further accelerates MDT by ≥4× on ImageNet-1K and FDM by ~1.6× on CIFAR-10, demonstrating that the asymmetric sampling + change-aware weighting strategy is orthogonal to and stackable with other acceleration techniques.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the DDPM forward-process formulation (Ho et al., 2020) and the EDM s-σ SDE framework (Karras et al., 2022) to introduce a principled decomposition of diffusion time steps into acceleration, deceleration, and convergence regions via a process-increment analysis (Theorem 1). It departs from prior acceleration methods—re-weighting (P2, Min-SNR) and re-sampling (Log-Normal, CLTS)—which rely on heuristic SNR-based curves or curriculum schedules, by grounding both the sampling suppression and the weighting in the theoretical bounds on process increment variance. The approach is explicitly positioned as complementary to, rather than competing with, other acceleration techniques like MDT and FDM, demonstrating stackability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

A consistent 3× (up to 4× in long-term training) speed-up with O(1) overhead is practically transformative for making diffusion model training accessible to academic labs, reducing multi-week GPU commitments to days. The theoretical grounding via Theorem 1 provides a principled basis for the three-area decomposition, moving the field beyond ad-hoc heuristic schedules. Open questions include whether the convergence-area analysis and the optimal suppression intensity (k=5) transfer to video diffusion, 3D generation, or much larger-scale training (e.g., the 7M-iteration ImageNet regime of the original DiT), and whether the findings hold for non-Gaussian noise schedules or latent-space diffusion.
