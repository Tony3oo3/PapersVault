## Problem

Existing VideoLLMs rely on large subword vocabularies (e.g., 128K tokens) that lack visual interpretability and require slow autoregressive token-by-token decoding, making real-time video narration impractical. The paper asks whether video narrations themselves can serve as the vocabulary units, enabling a model to output a concise, contextually accurate event description in a single retrieval step while still supporting complex reasoning queries like 'What happened next?'
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251008/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a video clip (frames sampled at 2 fps, processed as 1-second segments in a streaming fashion for long videos) and an optional text query encoding a temporal relationship (e.g., 'What's the next action?'). It outputs a single concise narration phrase structured as a prefix (e.g., 'cut a tomato') plus an optional postfix (e.g., 'by the left hand'), retrieved from a hierarchical vocabulary of ~0.8M entries organized by scenario. For long-video QA, VLog acts as a narrator producing dense per-clip narrations that a downstream LLM uses to answer multiple-choice questions.

## Method

VLog introduces a generative retrieval architecture built on GPT-2-medium: a learnable retrieval token is appended to the sequence of visual embeddings (from SigLIP) and query tokens, and after passing through the transformer, its output embedding is matched via dot-product against pre-computed SigLIP embeddings of narration vocabulary entries, trained with an InfoNCE contrastive loss (τ=0.05). The vocabulary is constructed via Narration Pair Encoding (NPE), which decomposes narrations into shared prefix sets and postfix sets, and is organized hierarchically by video scenario to reduce the search space from 0.8M to a scenario-specific subset. For out-of-vocabulary events (detected when top-1 similarity < 0.4), an agentic workflow invokes LLaVA-OV-0.5B for scene description and Qwen2.5-0.5B to generate new vocabulary entries. This design achieves 10–20× decoding speedup over autoregressive generative models while outperforming pure retrieval baselines on causal/temporal queries by preserving the language model's causal reasoning.

## Results

On COIN, VLog (with Ego4D pretraining) achieves 57.4% step recognition, 94.4% task recognition, and 48.4% next-step forecasting accuracy, outperforming the GPT-2-medium generative baseline (44.6/82.4/32.1) by a wide margin while decoding 20× faster (0.05s vs. 0.21s per clip). On EgoSchema, VLog as a narrator paired with LLaMA-3-8B reaches 70.4% subset and 43.1% full-set accuracy, surpassing LLoVi (67.0/38.8) and the generative GPT-2 narrator (66.5/37.4) while processing 180-second videos in 2.3s versus 48.2s for the generative baseline. On their own Vidcab-Eval benchmark, VLog achieves 96.9 CIDEr and 12.4 R@1 in the naive setting and 87.3 CIDEr / 5.0 R@1 in the causal setting, with a 20× speedup over the generative model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On COIN, VLog (124M params) with Ego4D pretraining matches or exceeds VideoLLM-online-7B (built on LLaMA-2-7B) on task recognition (94.4 vs. 92.1) and next-step forecasting (48.4 vs. 48.1), while trailing slightly on step recognition (57.4 vs. 59.8), all at 20× lower per-clip latency. Against the same-size GPT-2-medium generative baseline, VLog gains +12.8 points on step, +12.0 on task, and +16.3 on next-step accuracy. On EgoSchema, VLog as a narrator outperforms LLoVi (which uses the same LLaMA-3-8B answerer) by +3.4% subset and +4.3% full-set accuracy, and beats the generative GPT-2 narrator by +3.9% subset / +5.7% full-set, while processing videos ~21× faster (2.3s vs. 48.2s per 180s video). On Vidcab-Eval's causal retrieval track, VLog (87.3 CIDEr, 5.0 R@1) substantially outperforms the fine-tuned retrieval baseline (48.9 CIDEr, 2.1 R@1) and the generative model (53.7 CIDEr, 3.1 R@1), confirming that the generative retrieval architecture captures temporal reasoning that pure contrastive retrieval cannot. On HiREST, VLog with vocabulary upgrading (COIN+Upgrade, 1223 entries) achieves 12.6 CIDEr, surpassing LLaVA-OV-0.5B with Qwen2 vocabulary (1.0 CIDEr) by over 12×, validating the OOV expansion strategy.

## Relation to Prior Work

VLog departs from the dominant VideoLLM paradigm (Flamingo, VideoChat, MiniGPT-4) that relies on large subword vocabularies and autoregressive token-by-token generation, and from pure contrastive retrieval models (CLIP, SigLIP-based) that lack temporal reasoning. It builds on GPT-2's causal language modeling and SigLIP's vision-text alignment, but repurposes them through a novel 'generative retrieval' mechanism: a single retrieval token at the end of the LM sequence encodes both visual and query context, then performs a dot-product lookup over a narration-level vocabulary rather than generating subwords sequentially. The Narration Pair Encoding (NPE) is conceptually inspired by BPE subword tokenization but operates at the sentence/narration level, and the vocabulary upgrading via an LMM+LLM agentic workflow inverts the typical RAG direction into a 'Generative-Augmented Retrieval' approach.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 10–20× decoding speedup over generative VideoLLMs while matching or exceeding their accuracy on causal (before/after/next) retrieval makes real-time video narration feasible for edge and streaming applications (e.g., AR glasses assistants), a practical gap that large autoregressive models cannot close. The narration-vocabulary framing also opens a path toward interpretable, domain-specific video understanding where the model's 'knowledge' is an auditable set of events rather than distributed weights. Key limitations remain: the vocabulary is sourced primarily from Ego4D (egocentric), OOV detection relies on a fixed similarity threshold, and the causal-retrieval evaluation is on the authors' own 4.6K development set, leaving open whether the approach generalizes to truly open-ended or multi-domain narration without extensive vocabulary curation.
