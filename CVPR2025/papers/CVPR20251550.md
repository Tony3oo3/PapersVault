## Problem

Current video generation models and benchmarks are limited to short, single-scene clips (5–18 seconds) that lack coherent storylines, multi-scene progression, and character identity consistency across scenes. No publicly available dataset provides the hierarchical annotations—character banks with portraits and audio, scene-level narrative structure, and shot-level details—needed to train, evaluate, or analyze movie-level long video generation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251550/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

MovieBench comprises 91 movies (85 train / 6 test) averaging 45.6 minutes each at 1080p, sourced from LSMDC to ensure open licensing. For generation experiments, models consume text prompts (shot-level plot descriptions of ~66 words), character portrait images from the character bank, character audio samples, or a conditioning first frame; outputs are keyframes, short video clips (<10 s per shot), or talking-head videos. The dataset's unique structural element is the three-tier annotation hierarchy: movie-level (~43K-word synopsis + character bank), scene-level (~264-word shot sequences with scene categories), and shot-level (~66-word descriptions with time-aligned subtitles and per-character audio).

## Method

The dataset is built through a multi-stage pipeline: (1) a character bank is constructed per movie by scraping IMDB cast lists, detecting and isolating individuals in stills with GroundingDINO, and extracting per-character audio via face recognition (LightFace) plus speaker diarization (Pyannote) with cosine-similarity matching against reference audio; (2) scene boundaries are identified by iteratively prompting GPT-4-o with the current clip, the previous clip, and its scene label to decide continuity; (3) shot-level annotations (appearing characters, plot, background, camera motion, style) are generated by GPT-4-o using an interleaved multimodal sequence of video frames, character bank images/names, subtitles, and MAD audio descriptions. Quality is verified by human annotators (portrait quality 4.53/5, name relevance 4.92/5) and automated metrics (GPT-4-o achieves 93% F1_ID for character identification, 4.78/5 plot completeness). The paper also introduces three new evaluation metrics—Precision_ID, Recall_ID, and F1_ID—computed via DeepFace recognition on generated frames against ground-truth character sets, which existing metrics like CLIP Score or FID cannot capture.

## Results

On MovieBench, existing generation models show severe character consistency failures across all paradigms. For text-to-keyframe generation, AutoStory achieves the best F1_ID of 54.34%, while StoryDiffusion reaches 50.47% and StoryGen only 2.80%. For image-to-video generation, CogVideoX leads with F1_ID of 19.17% (vs. SVD 15.56% and I2VGen-XL 13.90%), and for text-to-video, Magic-Me achieves 10.17% F1_ID. Even the best image-to-video methods (SVD, CogVideoX) achieve FID scores of 1.25 and 1.23 respectively, yet their character identity recall remains below 16%, highlighting a fundamental gap between visual quality and identity preservation in movie-level generation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 8 generation models (not a single proposed method) on MovieBench to expose challenges in movie-level video generation. In text-to-keyframe generation, AutoStory (F1_ID 54.34%) and StoryDiffusion (50.47%) substantially outperform StoryGen (2.80%), but all three remain far from reliable multi-character consistency. In text-to-video generation, Magic-Me (F1_ID 10.17%) beats DreamVideo (3.74%) by a wide margin on character identity, though both are poor; Magic-Me also achieves better VBench Dynamic Degree (15.97% vs. 69.47% for DreamVideo, though DreamVideo's higher score reflects less dynamic content). In image-to-video generation, CogVideoX leads on F1_ID (19.17%) and FID (1.23), while SVD achieves the best FVD (190.48) and competitive F1_ID (15.56%), with I2VGen-XL trailing on both. Across all paradigms, no method exceeds 55% F1_ID for character consistency, and the gap between visual quality metrics (FID, FVD) and identity preservation is stark, confirming that current models cannot yet handle the multi-character, multi-scene demands of movie-level generation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by repurposing the movie-understanding literature (MAD, AutoAD, MovieNet) and short-video generation benchmarks (WebVid-10M, Panda-70M, MiraData) into a generation-oriented, hierarchical annotation framework. Rather than building on a single prior method, it assembles existing tools—GPT-4-o for scene/shot annotation, GroundingDINO for character detection, Pyannote for speaker diarization, and Whisper for transcription—into a pipeline that produces the first dataset structured around a 'script-to-movie' paradigm with explicit character banks (portraits + audio). The key conceptual departure from prior video-generation datasets is the three-level hierarchy (movie → scene → shot) and the inclusion of character identity as a first-class annotation, which no prior generation benchmark provided.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MovieBench exposes a stark gap: even SOTA image-to-video models (CogVideoX, SVD) achieve only ~15–19% F1 on character ID consistency, and text-to-video models drop below 10%, confirming that current architectures are fundamentally unprepared for multi-scene, multi-character narrative generation. The paper identifies four concrete open challenges—multi-character ID coherence, diverse-view consistency, character-based plot following, and synchronized audio-video generation—that remain unsolved and lack dedicated benchmarks. Limitations include the small scale (91 movies, ~69 hours), reliance on GPT-4-o for most annotations (with manual correction only on the 6-movie test set), and the fact that the dataset is drawn from a single source (LSMDC), which may limit diversity. The benchmark is likely to become a standard evaluation target as the field moves beyond 5–10 second clips.
