## Problem

Existing video-to-audio generation methods rely on contrastive learning for AV alignment and static global video guidance for generation, which capture overall semantic correspondence but fail to enforce local rhythmic synchronization. Contrastive learning treats each AV pair as a whole without differentiating temporal segments, and static video conditions cannot specify when and how sounds should appear within the audio timeline.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250525/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes 10-second video clips (frames resized to 224×224) as input and produces a corresponding 10-second audio waveform sampled at 8000 Hz. Internally, audio is represented as 128×128 log-mel spectrograms (STFT with 50 ms windows, 25 ms hop), and the flow model operates in the latent space of a pretrained AudioMAE encoder before decoding to waveform. Video features are extracted frame-by-frame via EVA-CLIP to provide temporally resolved conditioning signals.

## Method

FOLEY-FLOW comprises two stages. First, Video-Audio Masking Alignment (VAMA) trains the audio encoder to reconstruct temporally masked audio segments using the corresponding video segment features and unmasked audio context, forcing segment-level alignment that captures both semantics and rhythm (optimal masking ratio 0.8). Second, a Generalized Dynamic Conditional Flow (GVAF) built on the velocity flow framework generates audio step-by-step, where the condition at each temporal step is the video feature of the corresponding segment rather than a single global representation. This dynamic conditioning allows the flow to adapt its generation to evolving scene content and motion patterns, producing temporally synchronized audio. The video encoder (EVA-CLIP) and audio encoder (AudioMAE) are initialized from unimodal pretraining and jointly refined through the VAMA objective before the flow is trained.

## Results

On the VGGSound test set, FOLEY-FLOW achieves a KLD of 0.97, FAD of 0.52, and Alignment Accuracy of 98.97%, setting new state-of-the-art on all three metrics. The KLD is more than 2× lower than the next-best method (VATT, 2.25), the FAD is roughly half that of the runner-up (V2A-Mapper, 0.99), and the Align Acc exceeds the closest competitor (VATT, 82.81) by over 16 percentage points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FOLEY-FLOW outperforms all nine baselines on every reported metric (KLD, FAD, Align Acc) on VGGSound. The largest margins appear against diffusion-based and contrastive-alignment methods: Diff-Foley (KLD 3.15, FAD 6.40) and Im2Wav (KLD 2.54, FAD 6.32) show poor audio quality, while SpecVQGAN (Align Acc 48.79) and MaskVAT (Align Acc 63.87) struggle with temporal synchronization. Among the strongest recent baselines, VATT (NeurIPS 2024) achieves the best KLD (2.25) and Align Acc (82.81) among competitors, yet FOLEY-FLOW still leads by 1.28 in KLD, 1.83 in FAD, and 16.16 points in Align Acc. V2A-Mapper attains the second-best FAD (0.99) but lags substantially in KLD (2.78) and Align Acc (74.37), indicating that its lightweight foundation-model approach captures distributional quality but not fine-grained semantic or rhythmic alignment. The ablation (Table 2) confirms that both the Video-Audio Masking Alignment and Generalized Video-Audio Flow modules are necessary: removing either degrades performance, and removing both reverts to Diff-Foley-level results (KLD 3.15, FAD 6.40, Align Acc 82.47).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant two-stage paradigm in video-to-audio generation—contrastive AV alignment followed by video-conditioned diffusion—by replacing both stages with temporally explicit alternatives. The Video-Audio Masking Alignment (VAMA) module repurposes the masked autoencoder idea (originally for unimodal representation learning, e.g., MAE, AudioMAE) as a cross-modal alignment objective, forcing the audio encoder to reconstruct masked segments from corresponding video features rather than aligning global embeddings via contrastive loss. The generation stage replaces iterative diffusion with a velocity-flow framework whose conditioning is dynamically updated per video segment, drawing on normalizing-flow and flow-matching literature from image generation. Conceptually, the paper sits at the intersection of masked modeling for representation learning and flow-based generative modeling, but applies both to the specific problem of temporal audio-visual synchronization rather than global semantic matching.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The shift from contrastive to masked alignment for AV synchronization addresses a recognized limitation—contrastive learning treats AV pairs holistically and cannot enforce segment-level rhythmic correspondence—which could influence how future video-to-audio systems are designed. However, the evaluation is confined to 10-second VGGSound clips, and the paper does not report wall-clock generation times despite emphasizing efficiency, leaving open questions about real-time applicability and scalability to longer or more diverse video sequences. The near-perfect Align Acc (98.97%) raises the question of whether the metric saturates or whether the model is overfitting to the VGGSound distribution. Broader generalization to open-domain, longer-duration, or multi-source audio scenarios remains untested.
