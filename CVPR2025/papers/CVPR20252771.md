## Problem

While fMRI-based decoding of static visual stimuli has advanced substantially, the neural representation of motion in naturalistic scenes remains poorly understood. Prior work on motion decoding from brain activity has focused on artificial stimuli (e.g., random dot kinematograms) or entangles static and dynamic features in video reconstruction, making it difficult to isolate what the brain specifically encodes about motion. This paper asks whether fine-grained, object-level optical flow can be decoded from fMRI responses to naturalistic video, and whether such brain-derived motion signals can improve computer vision systems.

## Input / Output

The method takes fMRI voxel activations (5 consecutive TRs spanning 10 seconds, zero-mean/unit-variance normalized per session and averaged across repeated viewings) concatenated with DINOv2 features extracted from the initial video frame. It outputs dense optical flow fields at 32×32 spatial resolution, temporally downsampled to 3 frames per 2-second scan, quantized into 40 k-means clusters. These flow fields are then fed into DragNUWA (a motion-conditioned video diffusion model) together with the initial frame to produce an animated video. FlowSAM is used to mask salient objects so that flow is evaluated at object-level resolution rather than globally.

## Method

BrainNRDS architecturally disentangles static image representation from motion prediction: fMRI data passes through an MLP, is spatially broadcasted and concatenated with DINOv2 features of the initial frame, then processed by three residual 1×1 convolutional blocks with dropout and AdaptiveAvgPool2d global features, with a final 1×1 convolution classifying each spatial patch into a quantized motion codebook (trained with cross-entropy loss). This conditioning on the initial frame forces the network to extract only dynamic information from the neural signal, unlike entangled approaches such as MindVideo that simultaneously predict appearance and motion. For video generation, the predicted optical flow is passed to the pretrained DragNUWA diffusion model to animate the initial frame, producing temporally consistent video. In the complementary encoding direction, ridge regression is trained on features from diverse visual encoders (VideoMAE, CLIP ConvNeXt, VC-1, etc.) to predict voxel-wise fMRI responses, revealing that video-based encoders outperform static image encoders, particularly in visual and somatosensory cortex.

## Results

On the Dynamic Natural Vision dataset (3 participants), BrainNRDS achieves end-point error (EPE) of 0.543, 0.572, and 0.634 for subjects S1–S3 in motion decoding, significantly outperforming Stable Video Diffusion without brain data (EPE 1.192; all p < 0.001, Holm-Bonferroni corrected). For full video generation from fMRI (using MindVideo's decoded initial frame), BrainNRDS achieves VideoMAE cosine similarity of 0.769±0.006, CLIP cosine similarity of 0.896±0.003, and pixel SSIM of 0.214±0.01, surpassing MindVideo on all three metrics (p < 0.05). In the encoding direction, VideoMAE Large best predicts fMRI activity (Pearson r = 0.285–0.324 across subjects), outperforming the best static image model CLIP ConvNeXt (r = 0.219–0.272).

## Baselines & Comparisons

BrainNRDS is compared against two baselines: MindVideo (a brain-conditioned video diffusion model that jointly predicts appearance and motion) and Stable Video Diffusion (SVD, an image-conditioned video diffusion model with no brain input). In motion decoding, BrainNRDS achieves EPE of 0.543–0.634 across subjects, roughly halving the error of SVD (1.192) and also outperforming MindVideo's entangled motion prediction (Figure 3, paired t-tests p ≤ 1.2×10⁻⁸). When BrainNRDS is given MindVideo's decoded initial frame (Ours + MindVideo Best), it further improves over MindVideo alone on EPE, demonstrating that disentangling motion from appearance yields more accurate flow. In the video generation task (Table 1), BrainNRDS outperforms MindVideo on all three metrics—VideoMAE cosine similarity (0.769 vs. 0.742), CLIP cosine similarity (0.896 vs. 0.879), and pixel SSIM (0.214 vs. 0.171)—with all differences statistically significant (p < 0.05). SVD, which lacks any neural conditioning, produces plausible but misaligned flows (e.g., predicting wing-flapping when the eagle is actually gliding), confirming that brain data resolves action ambiguity that image-only models cannot.

## Relation to Prior Work

BrainNRDS extends the fMRI-to-video decoding line of work (MindVideo, NeuroClips) by explicitly disentangling static image representation from motion representation, predicting dense optical flow from brain activity rather than generating entangled video frames. It repurposes DragNUWA as a motion-conditioned animation engine, decoupling the 'what moves' question (solved by a small flow-prediction network trained on fMRI) from the 'how to render it' question (solved by an off-the-shelf video diffusion model). On the encoding side, the paper positions itself within the growing literature comparing vision model features against fMRI responses, extending prior static-image comparisons (CLIP, DINOv2) to video and embodied-AI encoders (VideoMAE, VC-1).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that brain-decoded motion signals resolve ambiguities (action type, camera-induced flow direction) that image-conditioned diffusion models trained on massive datasets cannot, suggesting latent information in neural data is complementary to large-scale visual pretraining. However, evidence is limited to 3 subjects on a single dataset with subject-specific training, leaving open whether the approach generalizes across subjects, datasets, or motion regimes. The disentanglement framework also opens a path toward interpretable, component-wise brain decoding (static vs. dynamic) that could inform both neuroscience and the design of biologically-inspired video understanding systems.
