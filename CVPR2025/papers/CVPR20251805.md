## Problem

Creating robot description files (URDF) for simulation and control typically demands significant manual effort, including CAD-to-URDF conversion or tedious XML editing. Existing data-driven approaches either require sensorimotor supervision (motor signals, IMU sensors), ground-truth annotations, or are limited to simple everyday objects with only a few degrees of freedom. No prior method constructs functional description files for complex robotic structures (up to 18 DoF with serially connected, multi-branch links) purely from unlabeled visual data.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a short time-series of whole-body point cloud frames (10 frames, 5,000 points each) captured while the robot executes randomly sampled motor commands, requiring no motor control signals, DoF labels, or ground-truth annotations. It outputs a complete URDF package: an XML file specifying links, joints, and their parameters, plus watertight mesh files for each link's geometry, all directly loadable in PyBullet. The pipeline assumes revolute joints and a tree-like kinematic structure without closed loops, and the motion sequences must be collision-free.

## Method

AutoURDF initializes S point clusters via K-means++ on the first frame, then tracks their 6-DoF poses (Cartesian center + quaternion) across all frames using two lightweight shared-MLP networks: a Step Model that registers clusters one frame ahead and an Anchor Model that refines registration from the initial frame to the target, both trained unsupervised with a bi-directional L1 Chamfer distance loss. A correlation matrix is computed over the tracked 6-DoF coordinate sequences using a weighted combination of Euclidean position distance and geodesic orientation distance; clusters with highly correlated motion are merged into links, with the optimal number of segments selected via Silhouette Score. Body topology is inferred by applying a minimum spanning tree to the accumulated positional distances between clusters and retaining only edges that cross segment boundaries. Joint parameters (rotation axis and center) are extracted from the relative SE(3) transformation between each child and parent link, constrained to 1-DoF revolute motion via Euler's rotation theorem, and link meshes are generated by integrating sparse point clouds across 10 frames into a dense local-frame cloud processed with marching cubes.

## Results

On the 8-robot validation set (5–18 DoF), AutoURDF achieves a mean L1 Chamfer Distance (CD) of 9.63±2.67 mm for point cloud registration, compared to 16.45±12.18 mm for Reart. For body topology inference, AutoURDF achieves a mean Tree Editing Distance (TED) of 2.26±2.16, substantially outperforming Reart (5.68±4.37) and MultiBodySync (6.64±4.07). The method also demonstrates strong speed: registration takes 50 s and URDF construction 12 s on an NVIDIA 3090, versus 35.5 min for Reart. With 5 sequences (50 frames) on the WX200 arm, joint angle error drops to 1.13° and joint axis normal distance to 1.16 mm.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AutoURDF is compared against two prior methods: Reart (Liu et al., CVPR 2023) and MultiBodySync/MBS (Huang et al., CVPR 2021). On point cloud registration (CD), AutoURDF reduces the mean error from 16.45 mm (Reart) to 9.63 mm, a 41% improvement, while also reducing the standard deviation from 12.18 to 2.67, indicating far more consistent performance across robots. On body topology inference (TED), AutoURDF achieves 2.26, beating Reart (5.68) by 60% and MBS (6.64) by 66%. The advantage is particularly pronounced on complex multi-branch robots: on PhantomX, AutoURDF scores 4.00 TED versus 13.00 (Reart) and 14.60 (MBS); on Solo, it achieves 0.00 TED (perfect topology) versus 4.00 (Reart) and 4.40 (MBS). MBS is only compared on TED since it does not produce registration outputs directly comparable to the CD metric. Qualitatively (Figure 7), AutoURDF produces cleaner part segmentation and tighter registration than all three baselines (NPP, MBS, Reart) on the same 10-frame sequences.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of robot self-modeling and articulated object modeling, but deliberately departs from both lines. Prior robot self-modeling work (Chen et al., Liu et al., Ledezma et al.) relies on motor commands, IMU sensors, or differentiable rendering for supervision, while articulated object modeling (Reart, MBS, Watch-It-Move) typically targets simple everyday objects with few DoF or requires ground-truth annotations. AutoURDF repurposes PointNet-style shared MLPs for 6-DoF cluster registration, K-means for part grouping, MST for topology inference, and marching cubes for meshing—combining these standard tools into a fully unsupervised pipeline that outputs simulator-compatible URDF files, a formulation the authors claim is the first for complex multi-DoF (up to 18) serially-connected robotic structures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Automated URDF generation removes a significant manual bottleneck in robotics simulation and control, and the demonstrated 40× speed advantage over Reart (62 s vs. 2,120 s) makes the approach practically deployable. However, the method is constrained to revolute joints and tree-like kinematic chains, produces no dynamic properties (mass, inertia), and struggles with structurally complex robots like the OP3 humanoid, requiring longer sequences for clean segmentation. Extending to prismatic/spherical joints, closed-loop mechanisms, and dynamic parameter estimation are the most immediate open questions, as is scaling to robots with more than 18 DoF or operating in cluttered environments.
