## Problem

Automating line art colorization in 2D animation production faces three critical gaps: existing methods assume pixel-alignment between the reference character design and input sketches, failing when poses and angles differ; they rely on non-binarized sketches that leak hidden color information, causing severe degradation on real binarized line drawings; and they require dense per-frame sketches for temporal consistency, which is labor-intensive in practice. AniDoc addresses all three simultaneously within a single video diffusion framework.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251796/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a reference character design image (background removed) and a sequence of binarized line art sketches (black lines on white, 256×256) as input, producing a temporally coherent sequence of 14 colorized frames. In sparse mode, only the start and end sketches are provided, with intermediate frames generated via keypoint trajectory interpolation. During inference, correspondence is established by extracting keypoints from the reference using X-Pose and matching them to the sketch via DIFT semantic features, since low-level descriptors like SIFT fail across the color-to-sketch domain gap.

## Method

AniDoc builds on Stable Video Diffusion's 3D U-Net, with the reference image latent duplicated across all frames and concatenated channel-wise for multi-frame self-attention. The core novelty is a correspondence-guided control branch: LightGlue+SIFT extracts matched keypoints between the reference and first frame during training, CoTracker propagates them across all frames to build integer-labeled point maps, and a ControlNet-style dual 3D U-Net encodes both the binarized sketches and these point maps as control signals injected into the main denoiser. To prevent color leakage, sketches are binarized during training and background augmentation (50% random removal via U2-Net) forces the model to learn foreground-background distinction. A two-stage training strategy first learns colorization with all per-frame sketches, then removes intermediate sketches and conditions on Gaussian heatmap trajectories of up to 5 keypoints (selected by motion magnitude) to learn sparse-sketch interpolation, enabling generation from only start and end keyframes.

## Results

On a test set of 200 anime clips spanning 10 different eras and styles, AniDoc achieves the best scores across all five metrics: PSNR 19.23, SSIM 0.7720, LPIPS 0.1704, FID 54.33, and FVD 230.18. The FID improvement is particularly striking, with AniDoc scoring 54.33 versus 110.48 for the next-best method (ToonCrafter+IP), representing roughly a 50% reduction. Similarly, FVD drops from 492.10 (ToonCrafter+IP) to 230.18, indicating substantially better temporal coherence. Reconstruction metrics (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS) also show consistent gains of 2–4 dB / 0.1–0.4 / 0.09–0.35 over the strongest baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AniDoc outperforms all three baselines on every reported metric. Against LVCD (and its IP-Adapter-augmented variant), AniDoc gains roughly 2.7 dB in PSNR, 0.13 in SSIM, and cuts FID by more than half (54.33 vs. 118.28), showing that explicit correspondence matching and binarized-sketch training yield far more accurate color transfer than relying on a pre-colorized first frame. Compared to ToonCrafter+IP-Adapter, AniDoc achieves a 4.26 dB PSNR advantage and a 53% lower FVD (230.18 vs. 492.10), indicating superior temporal consistency without needing colorized start and end frames. ID-Animator, a general-purpose identity-preserving video generation method, performs worst overall (FID 158.16, FVD 677.61), confirming that domain-specific colorization pipelines remain necessary for anime line art. Qualitatively, the paper notes that LVCD and ID-Animator fail to correctly colorize sketches when the reference character design differs substantially in pose or angle from the input, whereas AniDoc maintains fidelity thanks to its correspondence-guided mechanism.

## Relation to Prior Work

AniDoc builds on Stable Video Diffusion as its video generation backbone and adapts the ControlNet dual-branch architecture to inject sketch and correspondence signals into the denoising process. It departs from prior reference-based video colorization methods (LVCD, ToonCrafter, TCVC, TRE-Net) in two key ways: (1) it removes the requirement for pixel-aligned or pre-colorized keyframes by introducing an explicit correspondence mechanism (LightGlue/SIFT point maps during training, DIFT semantic matching during inference) that transfers color from an arbitrary character design to misaligned sketches, and (2) it extends the task from dense per-frame sketch conditioning to sparse start/end sketches with automatic in-betweening via a two-stage training strategy. The binarization-plus-background-augmentation training protocol directly addresses a practical flaw in prior methods that inadvertently learn to recover leaked color from non-binarized sketches rather than genuinely extracting color from the reference.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a concrete bottleneck in 2D anime production—manual colorization of in-between frames—by unifying colorization and interpolation into a single model that works from a single character design sheet, which is how the actual studio workflow operates. The correspondence-guided approach and sparse-sketch training are practical contributions that reduce artist labor without requiring dense line art or pre-colored keyframes. Limitations include short clip lengths (14 frames), modest resolutions (256×256 / 512×320), and dependence on keypoint matching quality; the authors note future work on interactive point control and longer, higher-resolution generation. An open question is how well the correspondence mechanism generalizes to characters with highly occluded or rapidly deforming parts where sparse keypoints may be insufficient.
