## Problem

All-in-one image restoration models waste parameters by keeping task-specific modules inactive for unrelated degradations, and existing Mixture-of-Experts approaches suffer from inconsistent expert behavior due to their uniform, rigid architecture. The core challenge is routing inputs to appropriate experts when degradation complexity is unknown a priori, without collapsing into single-expert dependency or uniform distribution that ignores task-specific processing needs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250821/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The model accepts a single degraded RGB image of unknown corruption type (haze, rain, Gaussian noise, motion blur, or low-light) and outputs a restored RGB image at the same resolution. Routing operates at the image level rather than token level, meaning each entire image is assigned to one expert per decoder layer. Training uses 128×128 crops with horizontal/vertical flips, and the model handles both isolated degradations and composite combinations of up to three degradation types simultaneously.

## Method

MoCE-IR employs a U-shaped asymmetric encoder-decoder where each decoder block contains a Mixture-of-Complexity-Experts (MoCE) layer with n nested experts of increasing window size and decreasing channel dimensionality (r = C/2^i), plus a shared expert using transposed self-attention in the channel dimension. Each expert performs FFT-based window self-attention in the spatial domain, and outputs are modulated via element-wise multiplication with the shared expert's holistic features before cross-attention merging. The key routing innovation is a complexity-aware bias term proportional to each expert's parameter count (normalized by the maximum), implemented as a spring-inspired force that preferentially directs inputs toward lower-complexity experts, with noisy top-1 selection and an auxiliary loss combining complexity-weighted importance and load balancing via coefficient of variation. A Sobel-filtered global feature vector provides high-frequency guidance to the gating functions, and a global residual connection from shallow features refines high-frequency details in the final output.

## Results

On the five-degradation all-in-one setting (AIO-5), MoCE-IR (25M params) achieves an average PSNR of 30.58 dB and SSIM of 0.919, outperforming the best prior heavy-scale method InstructIR-5D (17M, 29.55 dB) by 1.03 dB. The lightweight variant MoCE-IR-S (11M) reaches 30.08 dB average PSNR, surpassing AirNet (9M, 25.49 dB) by 4.59 dB. On the three-degradation setting (AIO-3), MoCE-IR achieves 32.73 dB average PSNR with 25M parameters, matching UniProcessor (1002M, 32.70 dB) while using 98% fewer parameters. In terms of efficiency, MoCE-IR requires only 80.59 GFLOPS and 5887 MB memory at 224×224 input, compared to 238 GFLOPS and 4829 MB for AirNet, with a runtime of 23.36 ms versus 42.17 ms.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MoCE-IR consistently outperforms all 21 baselines across both model scales and all degradation types. In the light scale (AIO-5), MoCE-IR-S (11M) surpasses the strongest prior method AirNet (9M) by 4.59 dB average PSNR, with particularly large gains on dehazing (+10.29 dB) and deblurring (+4.55 dB). In the heavy scale, MoCE-IR (25M) beats InstructIR-5D (17M) by 1.03 dB, Gridformer (34M) by 1.25 dB, and MambaIR (27M) by 1.61 dB, while using fewer or comparable parameters. On the three-degradation setting, MoCE-IR (25M) achieves 32.73 dB average PSNR, essentially matching UniProcessor (1002M, 32.70 dB) with 98% fewer parameters, and outperforming PromptIR (36M) by 0.67 dB and Art-PromptIR (33M) by 0.24 dB. The composited degradation results (CDD11) show MoCE-IR outperforming OneRestore by 0.64 dB average PSNR while being among the smallest models tested. No baseline is matched or beaten by MoCE-IR on any reported metric; the closest competitor is UniProcessor on AIO-3, where the gap is only 0.03 dB.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Restormer Transformer architecture [65] and FFT-based attention [21] into a Mixture-of-Experts framework, but departs fundamentally from uniform MoE designs [43, 46] by introducing non-uniform 'complexity experts' with progressively larger receptive fields and channel dimensions. Where prior all-in-one restoration methods (AirNet, PromptIR, IDR) rely on contrastive learning, tunable prompts, or meta-learning to handle multiple degradations, and where existing MoE-based restorers [29, 57, 62] use language or degradation priors for routing, this work replaces explicit task signals with a spring-inspired complexity bias that preferentially routes inputs to lower-complexity experts. The routing mechanism adapts the load-balancing loss from Riquelme et al. [43] by weighting expert importance by normalized parameter count, shifting from uniform utilization to complexity-proportional allocation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: MoCE-IR achieves state-of-the-art all-in-one restoration at 25M parameters (vs. 1002M for UniProcessor) with ~4× fewer FLOPS than AirNet, enabling selective expert bypass during inference. The emergent task-discriminative behavior—where a simple computational bias naturally assigns degradations to appropriately-complex experts without explicit task labels—offers a general principle for MoE design beyond image restoration. Key limitations include the image-level routing (which constrains spatial adaptivity), the absence of video or synthetic-to-real evaluation, and the reliance on a fixed number of experts. Open questions include whether SoftMoE or early-exiting strategies can further improve efficiency, and whether the complexity-bias principle transfers to other multi-task domains.
