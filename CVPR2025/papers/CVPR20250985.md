## Problem

Deep learning video denoisers achieve high quality on in-distribution noise but fail unpredictably on real-world footage where noise patterns vary wildly across codecs, encoders, and settings. They are also slow and offer no user control, making them impractical for professional editing workflows where editors need to balance noise removal against over-smoothing. Traditional denoisers handle diverse noise robustly and run fast, but require tedious manual parameter tuning that demands expertise.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250985/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a noisy video clip and first analyzes only the first frame (the anchor) to estimate a noise profile, then applies that single profile to denoise all subsequent frames, avoiding redundant per-frame noise estimation. The denoising operates independently on luma and chroma channels, with the small parameter-prediction network P(·;θ) producing spatially-varying maps for the Wiener filter (σ²) and bilateral Laplacian pyramid (σ_d, σ_r) at three pyramid levels. These six sets of parameters also serve as user-controllable knobs that can be uniformly scaled for real-time interactive adjustment.

## Method

The core idea is to make a traditional denoising pipeline differentiable so a hypernetwork can learn to predict its optimal parameters per input. A ConvNeXt backbone with an MLP head estimates a residual noise profile Δθ (stabilized via NPA: θ = θ⁰ + Δθ), which parameterizes a three-layer convolutional network P(·;θ) that outputs spatially-varying denoising parameters. The denoising itself is a classic two-stage pipeline: temporal denoising via Wiener filtering on four optically-flow-aligned neighbors (SpyNet at quarter resolution), followed by spatial denoising via a three-level bilateral Laplacian pyramid. Training uses a simple augmentation of AWGN (variance U(1,50)) followed by H.264 transcoding (CRF U(18,30)) on REDS videos, plus a consistency loss that encourages the hypernetwork to produce similar parameters regardless of which frame is chosen as anchor. This separation of noise analysis from denoising, combined with the small parameter space of classic filters, yields robustness to unseen noise patterns while running at 31.66 FPS on an RTX 3090.

## Results

On the CRVD (sRGB) benchmark, RFCVD achieves the best overall PSNR of 36.04 dB, outperforming the second-best NAFNet† (35.32 dB) by 0.72 dB, while running at 31.66 FPS — roughly 4× faster than the next-fastest method (BasicVSR++† at 7.41 FPS). The method ranks first across all five ISO levels (1600–25600) and also leads on SSIM and LPIPS (reported in supplementary). On a generalizability test with unseen degradation pipelines (film grain + AV1, spatially correlated noise + H.265), RFCVD again ranks first in PSNR and SSIM while maintaining the highest throughput.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RFCVD outperforms all nine baselines on overall PSNR across every ISO level on the CRVD benchmark. The closest competitor is NAFNet† (35.32 dB), a pure image denoiser, which RFCVD beats by 0.72 dB while being ~19× faster (31.66 vs. 1.69 FPS). Among video-specific methods, MF2F (35.25 dB, self-supervised) and FastDVDNet† (34.63 dB) trail by 0.79 and 1.41 dB respectively. The transformer-based VRT†, despite strong quality at lower ISOs (39.55 dB at ISO 1600), degrades sharply at high ISOs (26.01 dB at ISO 25600) and runs at only 0.05 FPS, making it impractical for interactive use. Real-ESRGAN and UDVD, which rely on sophisticated augmentation or self-supervision, lag significantly (28.29 and 29.77 dB overall), confirming the paper's hypothesis that classic denoising pipelines with learned parameters generalize better to unseen noise patterns. On the generalizability test (Table 3) with out-of-distribution degradations, RFCVD again ranks first in PSNR and SSIM, while VRT† and BasicVSR++† drop to last place, further supporting the robustness advantage of the classic-method-based approach.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant deep learning paradigm for video denoising—where a single end-to-end network simultaneously analyzes noise and produces clean frames—by instead training a hypernetwork to predict the parameters of a traditional denoising pipeline (Wiener temporal filtering à la Hasinoff et al. and bilateral Laplacian pyramid spatial filtering à la Zhang & Gunturk). The hypernetwork formulation draws on Ha et al.'s hypernetworks and is stabilized via NPA (Ortiz et al.), while the optical flow alignment uses off-the-shelf SpyNet. Conceptually, the paper repositions deep learning as a parameter-estimation front-end for well-understood signal processing, rather than as a replacement for it, and contrasts its simple AWGN + H.264 transcoding augmentation with the increasingly elaborate multi-stage degradation pipelines (e.g., Real-ESRGAN) that prior work has adopted to close the train-test gap.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for interactive video editing workflows where speed, robustness to unknown noise profiles, and user controllability are all essential—properties that most state-of-the-art deep denoisers sacrifice. The 31.66 FPS throughput (4× faster than the next-best method) combined with top CRVD quality and six user-tunable knobs makes this one of the few approaches viable for real-time creative use. Key open questions include closing the gap between predicted and oracle denoising parameters (Table 5 shows a ~0.7 dB headroom), and the deployment challenge that hypernetworks are not natively supported by mainstream inference runtimes (CoreML, WinML, OpenVINO), which limits immediate industrial adoption despite the method's conceptual simplicity.
