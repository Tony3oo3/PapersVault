## Problem

Despite strong performance on standard multimodal benchmarks, it remains unclear how Large Multimodal Models (LMMs) behave under out-of-distribution scenarios and domain-specific tasks such as medical imaging and molecular classification. No prior work has systematically identified the root cause of LMMs' failure on specialized domains or evaluated whether in-context learning can reliably bridge this generalization gap.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252680/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each LMM receives an RGB image (ranging from 28×28 MNIST-style digits to high-resolution chest X-rays, CT scans, and 2D molecular structures) paired with a text prompt specifying the classification task and candidate categories. In ICL experiments, the prompt additionally contains 0–8 in-context examples (image + label pairs) whose distribution can be controlled to match or deviate from the test data. The model outputs a single class label, and accuracy is computed over the full test set for each dataset.

## Method

This is an empirical study rather than a new architecture. The authors evaluate 15 LMMs (GPT-4, Gemini-1.5, LLaVA, CLIP, BLIP-2, etc.) across 20 datasets spanning synthetic, natural, medical, and molecular domains, then diagnose failure through three controlled experiments: (1) augmenting prompts with domain-specific context to test semantic misinterpretation, (2) linear probing on CLIP and LMM feature extractors to test visual feature sufficiency, and (3) a GPT-4o-scored semantic comprehension test. These experiments isolate mapping deficiency as the primary bottleneck. The authors then demonstrate that in-context learning with 2–8 examples from the target or shifted distribution substantially recovers accuracy, and systematically quantify ICL's vulnerability to label shifts (varying class proportions in ICE) and spurious correlation shifts (opposing domain–category associations between ICE and test data), while showing domain shifts alone cause only marginal degradation.

## Results

On 11 OOD generalization datasets, Gemini-1.5 achieves the highest average accuracy of 81.6%, followed by GPT-4 (70.9%) and LLaVA (64.4%), with several models surpassing previous SOTA (e.g., GPT-4 reaches 78.4% on DomainNet vs. 65.5% SOTA). On 9 domain-specific medical and molecular datasets, all 15 LMMs perform at or near random chance (average accuracy 26.8%–48.8% vs. 38.3% random), far below domain-specific SOTA (79.9% average). With in-context learning, GPT-4 improves from 45.3% to 84.3% on XCOVFour and from 64.2% to 92.7% on iWildCam using 8 examples from the target distribution, confirming mapping deficiency as the primary bottleneck.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On OOD generalization benchmarks, GPT-4 and Gemini-1.5 surpass previous SOTA on multiple datasets: GPT-4 reaches 78.4% on DomainNet (vs. 65.5% SOTA) and 51.7% on iWildCam (vs. 34.3% SOTA), while Gemini-1.5 achieves 81.0% on DomainNet and 74.8% on iWildCam. Among open-source LMMs, LLaVA leads with 64.4% average, but all open-source models fall well below GPT-4 and Gemini. On domain-specific medical and molecular datasets, every LMM—including GPT-4 (42.6% avg) and Gemini-1.5 (45.6% avg)—performs at or near random chance (38.3%), dramatically below domain-specific SOTA (79.9% avg), revealing a critical generalization gap. ICL with target-distribution examples substantially narrows this gap for GPT-4 (e.g., XCOVFour: 45.3%→84.3%; iWildCam: 64.2%→92.7%) and Gemini (HAM10000: 53.3%→75.6%), but ICL under label shifts and spurious correlation shifts causes significant performance degradation, indicating that example selection remains a critical practical concern.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the OOD generalization evaluation literature—previously focused on smaller vision models and single-domain benchmarks—to the LMM era, systematically probing 15 models across 20 datasets spanning synthetic, natural, and domain-specific imagery. It repurposes the in-context learning paradigm, well-studied in NLP, for vision LMMs and applies the classical distribution-shift taxonomy (domain, label, spurious correlation) to diagnose ICL's robustness. The structured failure analysis (semantic misinterpretation vs. visual feature extraction vs. mapping deficiency) is a new diagnostic framework that builds on linear probing and prompt-augmentation techniques from prior work but has not been applied to LMMs in this systematic way.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides practitioners with a clear warning that LMMs cannot be directly deployed on medical or molecular tasks without adaptation, and identifies mapping deficiency (rather than semantic or visual limitations) as the actionable bottleneck. The finding that ICL with domain-shifted examples nearly matches target-distribution examples opens a practical path for cross-domain adaptation, but the demonstrated vulnerability to label and spurious correlation shifts means ICE selection must be carefully designed. Open questions include whether ICL can be made robust to spurious correlations, how to extend the approach to tasks requiring deep domain expertise (where it failed on molecular data), and whether the OOD scaling-law reversal (larger models not generalizing better) holds as LMM architectures evolve.
