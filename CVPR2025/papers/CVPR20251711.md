## Problem

Current video generation models lack 4D controllability, making it impossible to produce multi-camera shooting, dolly zoom, or object motion editing effects. The most relevant prior work, GCD, requires expensive synchronized multi-view training data from simulators, which severely limits generalization to real-world scenes. This paper asks whether multi-camera shooting video generation can be learned directly from conventional monocular videos.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251711/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, the method takes a monocular dynamic video (49 frames at 320×512) and a target camera trajectory (intrinsics + extrinsics) as input, and produces a generated video that preserves the original dynamic content while following the new camera parameters. The intermediate dynamic 3D Gaussian field is constructed either via dense 3D point tracking or by lifting per-frame monocular depth maps into 3D space. During training, the conditional signal is a 13-frame rendered video from the Gaussian field (encoded to 16-channel latent features via a 3D causal VAE), which is channel-concatenated with the noise to form 32-channel input to the transformer.

## Method

The framework has two core components. First, D3D-PT performs dense 3D point tracking in two stages: sparse initialization via CoTracker with interpolation, followed by iterative RAFT-style refinement where a loosely coupled depth module (depth pyramid + ConvGRU + DepthHead) reads depth from the target frame's depth map rather than tightly binding depth to tracking features, enabling generalization across depth distributions. Second, the dynamic 3D Gaussian field is constructed without optimization by directly assigning Gaussian positions from the 3D point trajectories, with constant scale/opacity and pixel-derived colors. GS-DiT is obtained by finetuning CogVideoX with the rendered Gaussian-field video as a channel-concatenated condition, trained with standard DDPM loss. At inference, the Gaussian field is rendered with the desired camera parameters to produce the guidance video, and GS-DiT generates the final video conditioned on it, supporting camera extrinsic, intrinsic, and object motion editing.

## Results

On multi-camera shooting video generation (Table 4), GS-DiT achieves PSNR of 19.18 / 17.92 / 22.66 and SSIM of 0.60 / 0.60 / 0.73 on DAVIS, Sora, and Pixabay respectively, substantially outperforming GCD (PSNR 15.04 / 11.96 / 13.71) and MonST3R (PSNR 14.12 / 15.32 / 19.78). For dense 3D point tracking (Table 2), D3D-PT with ZoeDepth achieves average 3D-AJ of 9.0 and APD of 15.1, surpassing SpatialTracker (8.3 / 14.3) and BootsTAPIR+ZoeDepth (8.0 / 13.6). D3D-PT also accelerates inference by ~90×, reducing per-frame tracking time from 89.80 s (SpatialTracker) to 0.98 s.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GCD [49], GS-DiT wins decisively on all three video generation datasets: PSNR improves by 4.14 (DAVIS), 5.96 (Sora), and 8.95 (Pixabay), while LPIPS drops from 0.48/0.52/0.50 to 0.23/0.20/0.15, indicating GCD's simulator-trained model collapses on real-world videos. Against MonST3R [61], GS-DiT leads on PSNR across all datasets (e.g., 19.18 vs 14.12 on DAVIS) and on LPIPS, though MonST3R edges out GS-DiT on SSIM for Pixabay (0.74 vs 0.73) and Sora (0.59 vs 0.60 is a tie). For 3D point tracking, D3D-PT outperforms SpatialTracker [57] on average 3D-AJ (9.0 vs 8.3) and APD (15.1 vs 14.3) while matching its OA (83.9 vs 83.4), and also beats BootsTAPIR+ZoeDepth [12,3] on 3D-AJ (9.0 vs 8.0) and APD (15.1 vs 13.6). On 2D point tracking (Table 1), D3D-PT surpasses SpatialTracker on AJ across all three TAPVid sub-datasets (e.g., 51.9 vs 50.1 on Kinetics) and outperforms the dense tracker DOT [33] by 7.2% AJ on Kinetics, though it trails the bootstrapped BootsTAPIR [12] which benefits from a separate bootstrap training stage. The ~90× speedup over SpatialTracker (0.98 s vs 89.80 s per frame) makes dense 3D tracking practical for large-scale video training data preparation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of video diffusion transformers (building on CogVideoX), 3D Gaussian Splatting, and point tracking (CoTracker, DOT, RAFT). The most direct prior work is GCD, which generates multi-camera shooting videos but requires expensive simulator-rendered multi-view training data, limiting real-world generalization. GS-DiT departs from GCD by learning entirely from monocular videos: it constructs dynamic 3D Gaussian fields via a new dense 3D point tracking method (D3D-PT) without any optimization, then uses the artifact-laden rendered videos as conditional guidance for a finetuned DiT. The conceptual inspiration comes from classical Monocular Dynamic View Synthesis (MDVS), where 4D representations render novel views, but here the rendered output serves as a training signal rather than the final result.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GS-DiT makes 4D video control (camera extrinsics, intrinsics, and object motion editing) accessible from any monocular video, removing the need for synchronized multi-view captures or simulator data that plagued GCD. The D3D-PT method's ~90× speedup over SpatialTracker makes large-scale training data preparation practical. A key open question is whether the unoptimized Gaussian field (which inherently produces artifacts) can be replaced by an explicit 4D Gaussian field generated by the model itself, as the authors note as future work. The approach also raises the broader question of how much geometric fidelity the guidance signal needs for a diffusion prior to successfully 'hallucinate' the correct output.
