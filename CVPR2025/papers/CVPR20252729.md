## Problem

Traditional algebraic methods for proving polynomial inequalities (SOS, Lasserre hierarchy, Sherali-Adams) require fixing a truncation degree D in advance: too high makes the problem intractable, too low may yield no certificate. The paper addresses the gap of dynamically and adaptively selecting which basis elements to include in a Krivine-basis representation without exhaustively exploring all degrees up to a fixed bound.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252729/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a polynomial f(x) ≥ 0 to be certified non-negative over a hyperrectangle S = {x ∈ R^n | x_i ∈ [a_i, b_i]}, which is normalized to the unit hypercube [0,1]^n via an affine change of variables. It produces a Krivine-basis decomposition f(x) = Σ λ_{α,β} x^α(1−x)^β with all λ ≥ 0, where the sum is over a sparse subset of basis monomials selected incrementally by the agent. The resulting proof consists of a short sequence of non-negative intermediate lemmas with rational coefficients, making it fully verifiable.

## Method

The core idea is to cast basis selection as a sequential decision problem solved by a DQN agent. At each step, the agent picks a basis element x^α(1−x)^β from a growing action space (constructed by multiplying existing memory elements by x_i or 1−x_i), adds it to the memory, and solves an LP (via Gurobi) to obtain the current best lower bound γ. The state is (γ_t, κ) where κ tracks consecutive non-improving steps, and the reward is the normalized improvement in γ. To keep the action-space expansion tractable, the paper introduces a fast multivariate polynomial multiplication that encodes monomials into a univariate polynomial via base-D positional encoding, performs FFT-based convolution, and decodes back to multivariate form. The initial memory M_0 is seeded with all basis elements of degree ≤ deg(f), ensuring LP feasibility from the start.

## Results

On 10 polynomial inequality benchmarks (C1–C10), APPIRL requires an average of 75.4 proof steps versus 445.1 for Random Search (~6× fewer). The largest gap appears on C8 (5 variables, degree 2), where APPIRL proves the inequality in just 3 steps while Random Search needs at least 51 steps (17×). On the maximum stable set problem, APPIRL matches LDPP on G1 and G2 (6 steps each) and outperforms it on G3 (37 vs. 42 steps), while matching S2V-DQN exactly on all 8 graph instances (G1–G3, R1–R5).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Random Search, APPIRL wins on 8 of 10 benchmarks, requiring fewer steps than even the best (minimum) Random Search trial; the average improvement is roughly 6× (75.4 vs. 445.1 steps). Against LDPP [9] on the maximum stable set problem, APPIRL ties on G1 and G2 (both 6 steps) and wins on G3 (37 vs. 42 steps), despite LDPP exploiting problem-specific symmetry as inductive bias. Against S2V-DQN [30], APPIRL achieves identical step counts on all 8 graph instances (G1–G3, R1–R5), indicating equivalent performance on this specific task. Overall, APPIRL demonstrates that a general-purpose RL framework for Krivine-basis selection can match or exceed domain-specific RL methods on combinatorial verification while also handling arbitrary polynomial inequalities.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Positivstellensatz theorem (Krivine) to formulate polynomial inequality proving as a Krivine-basis representation problem, and on DQN (Mnih et al.) as the RL backbone for dynamic basis selection. It departs from traditional algebraic hierarchies (Lasserre, Sherali-Adams, Lovász-Schrijver) that are constrained by a fixed truncation degree D, and from prior learning-based dynamic proof systems (Fawzi et al.'s LDPP, S2V-DQN) that operate on SOS or graph-specific inference rules. The key conceptual shift is treating basis selection as a sequential decision problem solvable by DQN, augmented with an FFT-based multivariate-to-univariate polynomial multiplication trick to keep the action-space updates tractable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The approach offers a principled way to avoid the intractable degree-bound search that plagues SOS and moment-method proofs, and the Krivine-basis formulation naturally captures sparsity without coefficient inflation. However, the experimental scope is narrow (≤6 variables, degree ≤4, 10 benchmark inequalities), and the comparison with S2V-DQN on maximum stable set instances yields identical step counts across all eight graphs, leaving unclear whether the general RL framework offers any advantage over specialized methods. Open questions include scalability to higher-dimensional or higher-degree polynomials, whether the FFT acceleration provides measurable gains over naive multiplication at larger scales, and whether the DQN agent's learned policy generalizes across problem families without retraining.
