## Problem

Existing zero-shot skeleton-based action recognition (ZSSAR) methods rely on manually crafted semantic assumptions (e.g., verb+noun decomposition, GPT-generated local movement descriptions) to guide knowledge transfer, making them inflexible and noise-prone. Additionally, common prompt learning techniques applied to CLIP for ZSSAR exhibit unstable performance because prompts trained solely on seen classes disrupt the self-attention distributions that underpin CLIP's generalizability to unseen actions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250480/cropped_page3_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes 3D skeleton sequences of shape L×J×M×3 (frames × joints × actors × coordinates) and action class text labels as input. Skeleton features are extracted via a frozen Shift-GCN backbone into a J×256 joint-level representation, while text labels are prefixed with a learnable preamble prompt and encoded by a frozen CLIP-ViT-B/32 text encoder into 512-dimensional embeddings. The output is a predicted action class label; in the generalized setting, an entropy-based gating mechanism first determines whether the sample belongs to seen or unseen classes before final classification.

## Method

SCoPLe introduces two complementary prompting modules. The Dual-stream Language Prompting (DSLP) module runs two parallel streams through each of the first S CLIP text encoder layers: an original stream preserving authentic label semantics, and a prompted stream that injects layer-level learnable prompts and adaptively blends in the original stream's attention output via a learnable scalar α_s, preventing prompt-induced degradation of generalization. The Semantic-guided Skeleton Prompting module learns joint-shaped prompts (fake joint features matching the skeleton encoding format) and employs an Adaptive Visual Representation Sampler that uses the prompted text embedding as a cross-attention query over the concatenated [prompts, real joints] to extract class-relevant visual features for skeleton-to-text projection. Training uses a symmetric CLIP-style contrastive loss between projected visual and textual embeddings, while inference performs per-label cross-attention queries followed by nearest-neighbor classification in the shared embedding space.

## Results

SCoPLe achieves state-of-the-art ZSL accuracy across all four NTU-RGB+D splits: 84.10% (55/5), 52.96% (48/12) on NTU-60, and 74.53% (110/10), 52.17% (96/24) on NTU-120, surpassing the previous best (SA-DVAE) by up to +11.58% on the 48/12 split. For Generalized ZSSAR, SCoPLe attains harmonic-mean accuracies of 70.75%, 57.93%, 62.27%, and 52.23% on the four splits, improving over SA-DVAE by +4.48%, +15.37%, +1.85%, and +7.73% respectively. Under the random 3-split protocol (Table 3), SCoPLe leads on NTU-120 48/12 (53.34% vs 50.67%) and PKU-MMD 46/5 (71.41% vs 66.54%), while slightly trailing SA-DVAE on NTU-60 55/5 ZSL (83.72% vs 84.20%) but winning on harmonic mean (77.67% vs 75.27%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SCoPLe outperforms all 11 baselines on every reported split. Against the unmodified CLIP baseline (62.58% on NTU-60 55/5), SCoPLe gains +21.52% ZSL, and it also surpasses the prompting-only variants CLIP-CoOp (75.88%) and CLIP-MaPLe (65.59%) by +8.22% and +18.51% respectively, confirming that the dual-stream normalization and skeleton-side prompting are critical. Among prior ZSSAR methods, SCoPLe beats the previous SOTA SA-DVAE [22] by +1.73% ZSL on NTU-60 55/5, +11.58% on NTU-60 48/12, +5.76% on NTU-120 110/10, and +6.05% on NTU-120 96/24, with the largest GZSL harmonic-mean gains reaching +15.37% (48/12). It also exceeds PURLS [51] (79.23% / 71.95% / 52.01%) and STAR [9] (81.40% / 63.30% / 44.30%) on all three CLIP-based comparisons. On the random 3-split protocol, SCoPLe edges out SA-DVAE on NTU-120 (+2.67%) and PKU-MMD (+4.87%) but trails by 0.48% on NTU-60 55/5 ZSL, though it still wins on the harmonic mean. Older BERT-based baselines (ReViSE, JPoSE, CADA-VAE, SynSE, SMIE) are consistently outperformed by 10–35+ points, reflecting the advantage of CLIP's stronger pre-trained semantic space.

## Relation to Prior Work

SCoPLe builds directly on the CLIP pre-trained text encoder and adapts the prefix prompting paradigm from CoOp and the layer-level prompting from MaPLe, but introduces a dual-stream architecture that preserves the original attention distribution alongside prompted tuning to prevent the generalization degradation the authors identify in standard prompt learning for zero-shot settings. On the visual side, it departs from prior ZSSAR methods (SynSE, PURLS, STAR) that rely on manually crafted semantic decompositions or GPT-generated local movement descriptions, replacing them with data-driven joint-shaped prompts and a cross-attention sampler that uses text semantics to guide skeleton feature projection. The Shift-GCN backbone is used as a frozen feature extractor, consistent with prior baselines, so the novelty is entirely in the prompting and alignment mechanism rather than the skeleton encoder.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a clean, parameter-efficient alternative to the growing trend of labor-intensive semantic engineering in ZSSAR, showing that purely data-driven prompt tuning can match or exceed methods that require manual verb-noun decomposition or GPT-generated sub-motion descriptions. The dual-stream design addresses a practical concern for anyone adapting CLIP to zero-shot tasks: that prompts trained on seen classes can silently erode the model's generalization to unseen ones. A stated limitation is the loss of interpretability compared to methods like PURLS that explicitly model sub-motions, and the authors note future work on extending the prompting normalization concept to other modalities. The method's reliance on CLIP-ViT-B/32 and Shift-GCN as fixed backbones means its applicability is somewhat bounded by the quality of these pre-trained components.
