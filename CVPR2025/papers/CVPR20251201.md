## Problem

Ray-based Gaussian Splatting (RayGS) eliminates the linearization approximation of standard 3DGS, yielding superior rendering quality, but its CUDA-based software rasterization is too slow for high-frame-rate applications like VR and MR. Additionally, MIP-related aliasing artifacts in RayGS—arising when rendering at scales different from training—have not been addressed in the literature.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pre-trained 3D Gaussian Splatting scene model (a set of N primitives, each with a 3D center, covariance, opacity, and feature vector) and a camera pose, and produces a rendered novel-view image via per-pixel alpha blending. The key distinction from standard hardware-rasterized GS is that enclosing quads are placed in 3D space rather than projected onto the image plane, and the renderer requires near-plane clipping to be disabled to avoid visible discontinuities. Evaluation is conducted at 2× and 4× downsampling factors on MipNeRF360 and Tanks&Temples scenes using an RTX 2080.

## Method

The core contribution is a mathematically rigorous derivation showing that the set of maximum-density points along boundary rays of a RayGS primitive forms a 2D ellipse embedded in 3D space, which can be enclosed by a quad computed via an isomorphism to the unit circle. The vertex shader computes 3D quad vertices by solving a 2×2 eigendecomposition to align the quad with the ellipse axes, while the fragment shader evaluates the RayGS divergence through a simple scalar function of interpolated vertex quantities, avoiding per-pixel matrix inversions. A second contribution introduces a MIP formulation for RayGS that marginalizes the 3D Gaussian onto a plane orthogonal to the ray at the point of maximum density, yielding a 2D Gaussian smoothed by a pixel-area-dependent covariance; practical approximations (constant pixel size, pixel-invariant covariance) make this efficiently integrable into the rasterizer. The full pipeline is implemented in Vulkan (VKRayGS), achieving ~40× speedup over the CUDA-based GOF renderer while retaining state-of-the-art appearance quality.

## Results

VKRayGS achieves an average ~40× speedup over the CUDA-based GOF renderer, with FPS ranging from 172–351 (VKRayGS) versus 4–10 (GOF) across 14 scenes from MipNeRF360 and Tanks&Temples on an RTX 2080. Quality metrics remain closely matched: PSNR drops by at most 0.7 dB (e.g., 25.47→25.31 on bicycle), SSIM by at most 0.010, and LPIPS differences are within ±0.005, with VKRayGS actually outperforming GOF on several scenes (e.g., LPIPS 0.203 vs 0.206 on bicycle, 0.245 vs 0.281 on room). The paper attributes the small quality gaps to implementation misalignments (e.g., clipping) rather than the rendering formulation itself, noting that the rendering math is equivalent to GOF.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is GOF (Gaussian Opacity Field) [39], a CUDA-based software-rasterized RayGS renderer. VKRayGS wins decisively on speed, achieving 30–55× higher FPS across all 14 scenes (e.g., 177 vs 4 FPS on bicycle, 351 vs 7 on meetingroom), while quality metrics are nearly identical: PSNR differences are ≤0.7 dB, SSIM ≤0.010, and LPIPS is mixed (VKRayGS is better on 6 of 14 scenes, worse on 5, and tied on 3). The paper argues these small quality gaps stem from pipeline-level implementation differences (clipping, sorting) rather than the rendering formulation, since the per-pixel opacity computation is mathematically equivalent. A secondary comparison in Appendix B.1 shows VKGS (the GS counterpart) is ~2× faster than the INRIA CUDA GS implementation with comparable quality, further validating the hardware-rasterization approach. Overall, VKRayGS matches GOF's state-of-the-art RayGS quality while unlocking real-time frame rates suitable for VR/MR applications.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate lines of work in 3D Gaussian Splatting: the ray-based formulation (RayGS) that improves rendering quality by computing exact ray-Gaussian intersections, and hardware rasterization that enables fast, cross-platform rendering. While hardware-rasterized 3DGS (e.g., VKGS) was already established, extending it to RayGS is non-trivial because the primitive support in RayGS can span half-hyperbolas rather than simple 2D ellipses on the image plane. The paper's core mathematical contribution is showing that the support can be enclosed by quads placed in 3D space (rather than the image plane), with an isomorphism to the unit circle enabling efficient vertex computation. The MIP formulation for RayGS extends MIP-Splatting's anti-aliasing strategy to the ray-based setting, where the pixel-dependent covariance and modulation factor require new approximations for hardware integration.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ~40× speedup makes RayGS-based models viable for VR and MR applications where frame rates must be high and rendering quality must be artifact-free, a regime where prior CUDA-based RayGS implementations were infeasible. The MIP formulation addresses a practical gap—aliasing at diverging training/testing scales—that had not been explicitly solved for RayGS, and the qualitative results show it is essential for immersive viewing. A key limitation is that the contribution is purely at test time; the authors note that extending hardware rasterization to the training loop (where differentiability is needed) remains an open and non-trivial problem. The approach is demonstrated on Vulkan but the formulation is pipeline-agnostic, suggesting broad portability to OpenGL and other graphics APIs.
