## Problem

Existing video editing methods either adapt T2I diffusion models (lacking video priors for temporal coherence and motion edits) or apply video diffusion models in a naive manner (e.g., adding noise to the source video) without fully exploiting their rich spatiotemporal knowledge. Adapting proven T2I editing techniques like attention injection to modern T2V models is impractical due to the memory and compute demands of dozens of full-attention transformer blocks, leaving a gap in efficient, training-free video editing that handles both appearance and motion changes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251532/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a real-world video clip and a text prompt specifying the desired edit (e.g., 'swimming → flapping the wings' for motion, or 'cat → tiger' for appearance). The video is encoded into latent space via a 3D VAE, and the T2V model (CogVideoX with 48 DiT blocks) performs DDIM inversion and sampling over 50 steps in latent space. The output is an edited video decoded back to pixel space, with the guidance computed only over the first 60% of sampling steps and restricted to the first 4 sketching blocks, completing edits in roughly 3 minutes on a single 48 GB GPU.

## Method

FADE is a training-free framework built on two key insights. First, frequency-aware factorization: by visualizing attention maps and their Fourier spectra across all 48 transformer blocks, the authors identify that the first 4 blocks (sketching blocks) produce dense diagonal attention patterns encoding low-frequency spatial layout and temporal motion, while the remaining 44 blocks (sharpening blocks) handle high-frequency texture and detail refinement. Second, spectrum-guided modulation: rather than injecting or swapping attention maps, FADE collects the full-attention outputs from the sketching blocks for both the source trajectory (z*_t) and the target trajectory (z_t), applies a 3D discrete Fourier transform to each, isolates the low-frequency content via a low-pass filter, and computes the L2 difference as a guidance signal G_t. The gradient of G_t with respect to z_t is then subtracted from the standard DDIM update, steering the sampling trajectory to preserve the source video's foundational structure and motion while freeing high-frequency components for flexible edits. This avoids information leakage from the source, eliminates the need for null-text inversion or per-sample tuning, and reduces computation by using only 4 of 48 blocks for guidance.

## Results

On the DAVIS benchmark, FADE achieves the highest CLIP score for both appearance edits (0.3762) and motion edits (0.3683), indicating superior text-video alignment. It also attains the best temporal consistency (OSV) at 31.36 for appearance and 32.28 for motion edits, and the highest human preference score (0.35 and 0.43 respectively). Input fidelity remains competitive with 20.69 MaskPSNR and 0.3085 LPIPS on appearance edits. All edits complete in approximately 3 minutes on a single 48GB GPU, compared to over 15 minutes for prior methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FADE outperforms all four baselines on CLIP score and OSV (temporal consistency) for both appearance and motion edits. Against Tune-A-Video, FADE gains +0.024 CLIP and −3.65 OSV on appearance edits, and +0.040 CLIP and −3.57 OSV on motion edits, while also improving human preference from 0.12 to 0.35. Video-P2P and FateZero, which rely on attention feature injection from T2I models, show the weakest motion-editing performance (OSV 35.44 and 34.47 respectively), whereas FADE achieves 32.28, confirming that T2I-based priors struggle with temporal dynamics. CogVideoX-V2V, which uses the same underlying T2V model (CogVideoX) as FADE, is the closest competitor on CLIP for appearance edits (0.3754 vs 0.3762) but lags significantly on human preference (0.09 vs 0.35) and motion-edit OSV (35.52 vs 32.28), demonstrating that FADE's frequency-aware factorization and spectrum-guided modulation extract substantially more from the same video diffusion prior. FADE also matches or exceeds the best input-fidelity scores (M.PSNR 20.69, LPIPS 0.3085) while being training-free and ~5× faster than optimization-based methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

FADE extends the dual-branch DDIM inversion strategy from null-text inversion [18] and related T2I editing methods to the video domain, but departs fundamentally from the attention feature injection paradigm used by Video-P2P and FateZero, which swap or interleave attention maps to preserve content. Instead, it introduces a frequency-aware factorization of the T2V model's transformer blocks—identifying 'sketching' blocks (early, low-frequency, structural) versus 'sharpening' blocks (later, high-frequency, detail)—inspired by the timestep-level frequency progression observed in [9] but applied at the block level. The spectrum-guided modulation via 3D DFT and low-pass filtering replaces direct attention manipulation with a gradient-based trajectory correction, enabling more flexible edits including motion changes that prior attention-injection methods cannot handle.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FADE offers a practical, training-free video editing pipeline that handles both appearance and motion edits in ~3 minutes on a single GPU, compared to 15+ minutes for prior methods requiring per-sample tuning or null-text optimization. The finding that only 4 of 48 transformer blocks are needed for effective guidance has direct implications for efficient inference in large T2V models. However, the approach is validated on a single T2V architecture (CogVideoX) and a single benchmark (DAVIS, 20 videos), leaving open whether the block-level frequency progression generalizes across different DiT-based video models. The authors also note limitations with heavy occlusions requiring advanced temporal reasoning, suggesting the method's ceiling is bounded by the underlying video model's capabilities.
