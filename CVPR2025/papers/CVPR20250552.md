## Problem

Existing LMM benchmarks for multilingual and cultural evaluation are severely limited in scope: they cover at most 31 languages, restrict themselves to a single question type (typically MCQ), and underrepresent low-resource languages and non-Western cultures. This gap prevents meaningful assessment of whether state-of-the-art multimodal models can genuinely understand and reason about the full spectrum of global cultural and linguistic diversity.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250552/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each sample pairs an RGB image (cultural or generic scene) with a text question posed in one of 100 languages across 24 scripts. Questions come in four formats—two MCQs, two short-answer VQAs, one long-answer VQA, and one true/false per image—so the model must produce both closed-form selections and free-form text. Evaluation uses GPT-4o as a judge with type-specific criteria: accuracy for MCQ/T/F, correctness for short VQA, and consistency, fluency, and relevance for long VQA, with LLaMA-3.8.1B-Instruct as a cross-check for decision-making questions.

## Method

ALM-bench is constructed through a multi-stage pipeline: cultural images are sourced from the internet for 100 country-language pairs across 73 countries and 5 continents, while generic visual samples are drawn from LLaVA-Bench (In-the-Wild). GPT-4o generates four question types per image emphasizing cultural concepts and visual grounding, then native-speaker annotators (800+ hours, 80.3% native speakers) verify, correct, and filter both images (~10.7% discarded) and translations across all 99 non-English languages. The benchmark spans 19 domains—6 generic (indoor/outdoor scenes, memes, paintings, food, sketches) and 13 cultural (Architecture, Customs, Economy, Festivals, Food, Heritage, Lifestyle, Literature, Media, Music, Notable Figures, Religion, Sports)—yielding 22,763 QA pairs. Sixteen LMMs (both open- and closed-source, all released after 2023) are benchmarked, revealing a 27% performance gap between the best open-source model (GLM-4V-9B at 51.9%) and GPT-4o (78.8%), with pronounced degradation on low-resource scripts such as Ge'ez, Sinhalese, and Myanmar.

## Results

On ALM-bench (22,763 QA pairs across 100 languages), the best closed-source model GPT-4o achieves 78.8% overall accuracy, while the best open-source model GLM-4V-9B reaches 51.9%, revealing a 27% performance gap. GPT-4o's accuracy drops from 88.4% on English to 50.8% on Amharic, and GLM-4V-9B drops from 80.3% to 15.6% on the same language pair. Removing visual input causes GPT-4o's performance to fall by 27.3% absolute, confirming the benchmark's reliance on multimodal reasoning. Adding country-specific location information to prompts yields a 2.6–5% boost for closed-source models (GPT-4o: 83.57% vs 80.96%; Gemini-1.5-Pro: 81.52% vs 76.19%) but only marginal gains for open-source models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 16 LMMs on ALM-bench, with the headline comparison being GPT-4o (78.8%) versus GLM-4V-9B (51.9%), a 27-point gap that underscores the open-vs-closed divide in multilingual cultural understanding. Among open-source models, GLM-4V-9B, Qwen2-VL, Molmo, and LLaVA-OneVision achieve broadly comparable overall scores, all struggling severely on low-resource languages (e.g., Amharic, Kinyarwanda, Burmese). Gemini-1.5-Pro is a notable exception among closed-source models, showing minimal degradation on low-resource languages compared to GPT-4o's 6% high-to-low-resource drop. On cultural domain analysis, GPT-4o leads with 80.3% overall but drops to 72.7% on 'Notable Key Figures,' while open-source models perform worse on long VQA than short VQA (the reverse of closed-source behavior). The visual-context ablation shows GPT-4o loses 27.3% absolute accuracy without images, and Qwen2-7B loses 13% absolute / 24.8% relative, confirming that no model can substitute textual knowledge for visual grounding on this benchmark.

## Relation to Prior Work

ALM-bench extends the line of culturally-aware multilingual VQA benchmarks, most directly building on CVQA (31 languages, MCQ-only) and CulturalVQA (English-only, 5 domains), while sourcing its generic VQA component from LLaVA-Bench. It departs from prior work by scaling to 100 languages across 24 scripts and 73 countries, introducing four question types (MCQ, T/F, short and long VQA), and covering 13 cultural domains (including six novel ones: literature, music, festivals, economy, media, notable figures). Unlike earlier benchmarks that conflate linguistic diversity with cultural depth, ALM-bench explicitly pairs each language with a country-language cultural context and mandates native-speaker verification, addressing a gap the authors identify in both single-language cultural benchmarks (Henna, CulturalVQA) and multilingual ones (MMMB, MMBench, M4U).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a stark 27% accuracy gap between the best closed-source model (GPT-4o, 78.8%) and the best open-source model (GLM-4V, 51.9%), with open-source models collapsing to as low as 15.6% on low-resource languages like Amharic—highlighting a critical under-served population of ~5 billion speakers. The finding that adding country-specific context to prompts boosts closed-source models by 2.6–5% but barely moves open-source models suggests a fundamental architectural or training-data limitation rather than a simple prompting issue. Open questions include whether the cultural understanding gaps (e.g., confusing Mela Chiraghan with Eid Milad un Nabi) can be addressed through targeted fine-tuning or require fundamentally different cultural grounding mechanisms, and whether the 50/50 high/low-resource split adequately captures the long tail of the world's ~7,000 languages.
