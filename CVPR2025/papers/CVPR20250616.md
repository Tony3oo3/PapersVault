## Problem

Attribution-based explanation methods highlight broad object regions but fail to identify which specific fine-grained features (e.g., head vs. wings) drive a model's correct or incorrect prediction, making explanations indistinguishable between correctly and misclassified samples in fine-grained tasks. This paper addresses two questions: which fine-grained features contribute to misclassification, and where dominant local features should be adjusted to correct the prediction.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250616/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a single misclassified RGB image, a set of 20 correctly classified reference images from the target class, and a frozen pre-trained CNN (ResNet-50 or VGG-16), operating on the last convolutional layer's feature maps. It produces two contrastive saliency maps—an invariant region map (features responsible for the incorrect class) and a dominant region map (features driving the corrected prediction)—along with the modified counterfactual feature map. The approach is entirely non-generative: no new pixels are synthesized; instead, feature regions are swapped from existing reference samples.

## Method

FG-VCE comprises three stages: feature extraction, a Saliency Partition (SP) module, and iterative counterfactual generation. The SP module approximates per-point Shapley values by constructing spatially localized Gaussian kernels (with tunable σ) that create a 'dip' around each feature point, suppressing its contribution and measuring the prediction drop to isolate individual feature importance while disentangling neighboring entanglement. For counterfactual generation, the algorithm iteratively selects the highest-Shapley feature in the misclassified sample and replaces it with the most semantically similar candidate (cosine similarity) from a Top-m Shapley-filtered pool of correctly classified features, jointly optimizing similarity and classification loss until the prediction flips. This candidate-based, non-generative strategy avoids adversarial gradient attacks and preserves semantic consistency, requiring only forward passes through the frozen network.

## Results

On CUB-200-2011 with ResNet-50, FG-VCE achieves the best Insertion score of 43.02%, the lowest Deletion score of 10.27%, and the highest Compact Activation Score (ξ) of 9.53, outperforming the closest counterfactual baseline CCE (40.47% / 11.14% / 7.85) and the best attribution method FG-CAM (41.31% / 14.55% / 7.21). On Stanford Dogs with ResNet-50, FG-VCE reaches 42.15% Insertion, 12.08% Deletion, and 10.45 ξ, again leading all baselines. The method also demonstrates consistent gains with VGG-16, confirming generalizability across architectures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FG-VCE outperforms all 13 baselines across every metric and both datasets. Among attribution-based methods, FG-CAM is the strongest competitor (41.31% Ins., 14.55% Del., 7.21 ξ on CUB ResNet-50), but FG-VCE improves Insertion by +1.71, reduces Deletion by 4.28, and raises ξ by +2.32. Among counterfactual methods, CCE (ICCV 2023) is the closest baseline (40.47% / 11.14% / 7.85), with FG-VCE gaining +2.55 Insertion, −0.87 Deletion, and +1.68 ξ on CUB ResNet-50; the gap widens on the ξ metric for VGG-16 (8.96 vs. 6.32). SCOUT and CE trail further behind, with SCOUT achieving only 33.78% Insertion and CE 37.18% on CUB ResNet-50. Propagation-based (GBP, IG) and perturbation-based (RISE, EP) methods consistently underperform, with Deletion scores 3–8 points higher than FG-VCE. Notably, FG-VCE also achieves the lowest computational cost (5.2 s, 3.2 GB) compared to CCE (13.5 s, 5.4 GB) and RISE (16.9 s, 10.6 GB), making it both more accurate and more efficient.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the counterfactual explanation line of work (CCE [11], SCOUT [10], CE [43]) by introducing a fine-grained, non-generative variant that iteratively replaces the most Shapley-contributive feature in a misclassified sample with a semantically similar candidate from correctly classified references. Its key methodological departure is the Saliency Partition module, which uses spatially localized Gaussian kernels to disentangle individual feature-point contributions—a problem the authors argue traditional Shapley formulations and holistic saliency maps (GradCAM, Integrated Gradients) cannot address. Unlike PGD-based CCE [11], which perturbs features through imperceptible gradients, FG-VCE operates entirely in feature space using existing data samples, avoiding adversarial training while preserving semantic consistency.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The SP module is a plug-and-play component that consistently improves fine-grained localization across multiple contrastive explanation baselines (Table 3), suggesting it could be adopted more broadly in attribution research. However, the evaluation is limited to two fine-grained datasets (CUB-200-2011, Stanford Dogs) and two CNN backbones (ResNet-50, VGG-16), and the authors themselves flag transformer architectures as an open direction. The Compact Activation Score, while intuitive, has not been validated against human judgment or on larger-scale benchmarks, leaving its generalizability uncertain. A key open question is whether the iterative feature-replacement strategy scales to high-dimensional or multi-modal settings where the candidate pool becomes combinatorially large.
