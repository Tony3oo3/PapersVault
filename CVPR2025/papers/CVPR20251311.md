## Problem

Existing robotic manipulation policies struggle to generalize to novel objects and instructions because intermediate representations are either too coarse (language) to provide spatial precision or too resource-intensive (goal images, point flows) to scale. The paper identifies grounding masks as an underexplored intermediate representation that simultaneously conveys target object shape/size and placement location while benefiting from large-scale pretrained vision-language models. Additionally, existing simulation datasets lack instruction diversity and scene complexity, causing policies to overfit to simple instruction-scene mappings.

## Input / Output

The grounded VLM receives a single RGB image and a natural-language instruction, outputting two binary pixel-level segmentation masks (target object and placement area) via <SEG> tokens decoded through a SAM-based head. The policy network consumes N timesteps of multi-view RGB images (each channel-concatenated with the two masks and projected back to 3 channels), robot states, and the CLIP-encoded instruction, producing continuous arm actions (Smooth-L1 loss) and binary gripper actions (BCE loss). Masks are extracted once at episode start and held fixed throughout the manipulation, exploiting the fact that target and placement regions remain stable until the gripper interacts with them.

## Method

ROBOGROUND is a two-stage architecture: a GLaMM-based grounded VLM generates pixel-level masks for the target object and placement area from an image-instruction pair, and a GR-1-style transformer policy network consumes these masks as spatial guidance. The policy integrates masks through two complementary mechanisms: (1) channel-level concatenation of the image with both masks before ViT-MAE encoding, and (2) a Grounded Perceiver that extends the standard perceiver with three sets of learnable query tokens (global, object, placement) where the latter two attend to patch features via mask-fill operations, preserving spatial information in grounded regions while reducing token count to 3×k. The VLM is fine-tuned on simulated instruction-following data using autoregressive cross-entropy plus per-pixel BCE and DICE losses, while the policy is trained end-to-end with Smooth-L1 and BCE losses. The accompanying automated data pipeline generates 24K demonstrations with 112K diverse instructions across appearance, spatial, and commonsense categories, using 3,526 objects from 176 categories with up to 10 distractors per scene to enforce discriminative reasoning.

## Results

On the main simulated manipulation benchmark, ROBOGROUND achieves 89.0/43.3 (contact/success rate) on Easy pick-and-place, 78.5/30.5 on Appearance, 81.0/33.5 on Spatial, and 76.3/30.0 on Common-sense instructions, consistently outperforming all baselines. The largest gains appear on semantically challenging tasks: success rate more than doubles compared to GR-1 on Appearance (30.5% vs. 13.8%), Spatial (33.5% vs. 16.3%), and Common-sense (30.0% vs. 11.5%). On fundamental skills, ROBOGROUND reaches 72.0% (Open/Close), 69.3% (Press), and 54.5% (Turn/Twist), again leading all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ROBOGROUND outperforms all three baselines across every task category. Against the strongest baseline GR-1, the gains are most pronounced on semantically demanding instructions: success rate improves from 13.8% to 30.5% on Appearance (+16.7 pts), 16.3% to 33.5% on Spatial (+17.2 pts), and 11.5% to 30.0% on Common-sense (+18.5 pts), while the Easy task shows a smaller margin (42.8% → 43.3%). BC-Transformer, which encodes language via a single CLIP text feature with FiLM conditioning, lags GR-1 by roughly 10–15 points on success rate for hard tasks, confirming that global text encoding is insufficient for fine-grained instruction following. ACT, lacking any language input, performs worst overall (e.g., 3.8% success on Appearance), underscoring the necessity of language conditioning. The consistent gap between contact rate and success rate (e.g., 78.5% contact vs. 30.5% success on Appearance) suggests that object localization is well-solved by the mask guidance, while grasping pose estimation remains a bottleneck across all methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions grounding masks as a middle ground between coarse intermediate representations (language instructions, trajectory sketches) and fine-grained but resource-intensive ones (goal images, point flows) for guiding robot manipulation policies. It builds directly on GLaMM for pixel-level grounding and GR-1's transformer-based policy architecture, introducing a novel Grounded Perceiver that uses masks to direct attention at the patch level. The data generation pipeline extends RoboCasa by incorporating Objaverse objects and GPT-4-generated diverse instructions, departing from prior language-conditioned datasets that rely on fixed-format, low-complexity instructions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that grounding masks substantially improve generalization to unseen objects and instruction types (~100% relative improvement on unseen settings), offering a practical recipe for bridging high-level language understanding and low-level manipulation. The 24K-demo, 112K-instruction dataset with 3,526 objects across 176 categories is a useful resource for evaluating generalization in simulated manipulation. However, the work is confined to simulation and single-turn pick-and-place tasks; the persistent gap between contact rate and success rate (e.g., 89% vs. 43% on Easy) reveals that grasping remains a bottleneck, and real-world transfer, multi-turn tasks, and integration with pose-prediction modules are left open.
