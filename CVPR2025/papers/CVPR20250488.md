## Problem

Rectified flow transformers such as Flux achieve state-of-the-art image generation but lack disentangled editing capabilities, unlike GANs with their structured latent spaces. Existing editing methods developed for UNet-based diffusion models (bottleneck manipulation, noise-space guidance, LoRA training) do not transfer to the MM-DiT architecture of flow-matching transformers, where continuous text-image interaction in joint transformer blocks fundamentally changes how semantics are encoded.

## Input / Output

The method operates at inference time on FLUX.1-dev (30 denoising steps) and takes a noisy latent image, a base text prompt that generated the original image, and an edit text prompt describing the desired change. Two scale parameters (λ_fine for content edits, λ_coarse for style/appearance edits) and an optional attention mask threshold τ_m control the edit strength and spatial localization. For real images, the method integrates with RF-Inversion to map the image into the generator's latent distribution before applying edits.

## Method

FluxSpace identifies the outputs of Flux's joint attention layers as a linear representation space where semantic edits can be performed in a disentangled manner. For fine-grained editing, it computes three attention outputs (base condition, edit condition, null condition), applies orthogonal projection against the null-condition output to isolate the semantic direction from image content, then adds this direction to the base output scaled by λ_fine. For coarse-level edits (e.g., style transfer), it applies the linear representation hypothesis to pooled CLIP embeddings, projecting the edit embedding orthogonal to the base embedding and interpolating with λ_coarse. An optional self-supervised attention mask, derived from query-key attention between image features and the edit condition's first T5 token, spatially localizes the edit to improve disentanglement. The entire pipeline is training-free and requires no manually provided masks.

## Results

On a 60-image evaluation set, FluxSpace achieves the best content preservation scores across both edit types: CLIP-I of 0.9417 (eyeglasses) and 0.9038 (smile), and DINO of 0.9402 (eyeglasses) and 0.9347 (smile). In the user study (50 participants, 1–5 Likert scale), FluxSpace scores 4.19 overall user preference, substantially ahead of the next-best method (Sliders-FLUX at 3.34). On text alignment (CLIP-T), FluxSpace is competitive but not the top performer—LEDITS++ leads for eyeglasses (0.3723 vs. 0.3180) and TurboEdit for smile (0.3692 vs. 0.3503)—reflecting a deliberate trade-off favoring identity preservation over maximal prompt adherence.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FluxSpace outperforms all four baselines on content preservation (CLIP-I and DINO) for both edit types, with the largest margins over Sliders-FLUX (DINO gap of up to 0.155 on eyeglasses) and TurboEdit (CLIP-I gap of 0.110 on eyeglasses). On text alignment (CLIP-T), LEDITS++ leads for eyeglasses (0.3723 vs. 0.3180) and TurboEdit for smile (0.3692 vs. 0.3503), but the paper argues these gains come at the cost of identity alteration visible in qualitative comparisons. RF-Inversion is the closest competitor on content preservation (CLIP-I 0.9354 vs. 0.9417 for eyeglasses) but trails on smile edits (0.8834 vs. 0.9038) and user preference (2.91 vs. 4.19). Sliders-FLUX, despite being a flow-based method like FluxSpace, shows the weakest DINO scores (0.7858, 0.8683), indicating substantial identity drift during editing. In the user study, FluxSpace's 4.19 preference score exceeds the runner-up (Sliders-FLUX, 3.34) by 0.85 points, a margin the authors attribute to superior disentanglement—applying the target edit without disturbing unrelated facial features.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of disentangled editing research from UNet-based diffusion models (bottleneck features, noise space, text embeddings, weight space) to rectified flow transformers (MM-DiT/Flux), where no prior disentangled editing method existed. It builds on RF-Inversion for the inversion mechanism needed to handle real images, and draws on the linear representation hypothesis from NLP to justify orthogonal-projection-based semantic direction extraction. The key departure is identifying joint transformer attention layer outputs as a linearly-editable semantic space, combined with a dual-level scheme (fine-grained attention editing + coarse CLIP pooled-embedding modulation) that has no direct analogue in prior UNet-based editing work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Flux and similar rectified flow transformers are now the dominant text-to-image architecture, yet they lacked any training-free disentangled editing capability; this paper fills that gap with a practical inference-time method. However, the approach is somewhat narrow: real-image editing depends on RF-Inversion's imperfect inversion (the input is not fully mapped into the generator's latent distribution), and the linear representation assumption on attention outputs has not been validated across architectures or at scale. Open questions include whether the method generalizes to more complex multi-object edits, whether the attention-output linearity holds in other MM-DiT variants, and how to address the ethical risks of precise identity-preserving manipulation without consent.
