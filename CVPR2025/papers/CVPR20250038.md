## Problem

Normalization layers (LayerNorm, RMSNorm) are considered indispensable for stable training of deep Transformers, yet their actual mechanism has not been fully understood. This paper shows that the key effect of normalization—non-linearly squashing extreme activations while preserving the central linear range—can be replicated without computing any activation statistics, challenging the assumption that per-token mean/variance computation is necessary.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250038/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

DyT is a drop-in, element-wise replacement for each normalization layer in the original architecture, operating on tensors of shape (B, T, C) without any cross-dimension aggregation. It preserves the exact input/output interface of the replaced layer, so the surrounding architecture (attention, FFN, convolutions, diffusion denoising) remains unchanged. The only architectural modification is swapping each LN/RMSNorm module for a DyT module; no changes to activation functions (GELU, ReLU) or other components are made.

## Method

Dynamic Tanh (DyT) is defined as DyT(x) = γ · tanh(αx) + β, where α is a single learnable scalar per layer and γ, β are learnable per-channel vectors identical to those in standard normalization. The motivation comes from empirical analysis showing that LN layers produce S-shaped (tanh-like) input-output curves: each token is transformed linearly, but because tokens have different variances, the collective mapping across all tokens resembles a scaled tanh. The learnable α adapts to the input's scale (learning values close to 1/std of activations), while the bounded tanh function squashes extreme values non-linearly—replicating normalization's critical effect without computing per-token statistics. A default α₀ = 0.5 initialization works across most tasks, though LLMs benefit from task-specific tuning (smaller α₀ for larger models, higher α₀ in attention blocks vs. FFN blocks).

## Results

On ImageNet-1K supervised classification, DyT achieves 82.5% top-1 accuracy with ViT-B (vs. 82.3% with LN) and 83.6% with ViT-L (vs. 83.1%), while matching ConvNeXt-B (83.7%) and slightly improving ConvNeXt-L (84.4% vs. 84.3%). For LLaMA 7B–70B pretrained on 200B tokens, DyT matches RMSNorm with identical zero-shot lm-eval scores (e.g., 0.513 for 7B) and near-identical training loss (1.60 vs. 1.59 for 7B). On ImageNet generation with DiT, DyT improves FID for DiT-B (63.9 vs. 64.9) and DiT-L (45.7 vs. 45.9), though DiT-XL is slightly worse (20.8 vs. 19.9). Across self-supervised vision (MAE, DINO), speech (wav2vec 2.0), and DNA (HyenaDNA, Caduceus), DyT consistently matches or marginally improves upon LN.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Layer Normalization (Ba et al., 2016), DyT wins or ties on every benchmark: it gains 0.2–0.5% top-1 accuracy on ViT (ImageNet-1K), matches ConvNeXt, improves DiT-B FID by 1.0 point, and is within 0.1% on MAE/DINO self-supervised, wav2vec 2.0 speech, and HyenaDNA/Caduceus DNA tasks. The only slight regression is DiT-XL FID (20.8 vs. 19.9, +0.9). Against RMSNorm (Zhang et al., 2019), DyT is essentially tied across all four LLaMA scales: zero-shot lm-eval scores are identical (0.513, 0.529, 0.536, 0.549) and training loss differs by at most 0.01 (e.g., 1.60 vs. 1.59 for 7B). Overall, DyT never substantially underperforms either baseline, and in several settings (ViT-L, DiT-B, DINO patch-8) it provides small but consistent gains, supporting the paper's claim that normalization layers can be replaced without performance loss.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the long line of work attempting to train deep networks without normalization layers (Brock et al. for ConvNets, He and Hofmann for Transformers, Heimersheim for gradual LN removal), which typically required complex initialization schemes, weight normalization, adaptive gradient clipping, or extensive data augmentation. Instead, DyT is motivated by a direct empirical observation: LN layers in trained Transformers produce tanh-like S-shaped input-output mappings, suggesting that the essential effect of normalization is non-linear squashing of extreme values rather than per-token statistics. The approach also connects to Ni et al.'s finding that LN introduces strong non-linearity, but reframes it as a design principle: if the observable effect is a scaled tanh, then a scaled tanh suffices. Unlike prior 'no normalization' methods, DyT is a true drop-in replacement requiring no changes to the training recipe or hyperparameters.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work challenges a near-universal assumption in deep learning—that normalization layers are indispensable for training modern networks—and provides a mechanistic explanation for what they actually do (non-linearly squash extreme activations while approximately linearly transforming the bulk). The practical implication is significant: a single element-wise tanh with one learnable scalar replaces the entire normalization machinery, simplifying architectures and potentially reducing inference overhead. Key limitations include the inability to replace BN in classic ResNets and the need for careful α tuning in large LLMs. Open questions include a theoretical understanding of why this works, whether the approach extends to other normalization types (BN, GroupNorm), and whether the learned α can be further simplified or made architecture-agnostic.
