## Problem

Existing multi-object image editing methods face a fundamental trade-off: LLM-based approaches preserve global quantity consistency but suffer from aliasing of individual object attributes, while mask- or bounding-box-based methods extract distinct attributes but require one-to-one auxiliary tools per object, inflating training cost and reducing user convenience. No prior method simultaneously extracts separable object attributes and maintains consistent quantity perception across many objects without relying on external auxiliary tools.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250803/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a multi-object RGB image (containing 3–14 objects), a free-form editing instruction c_e, and a structured quantity caption c_q that enumerates object names and counts. During training, c_e is replaced with a null-text to isolate quantity-perception learning; at inference, c_e is user-specified to direct style transfer, object reinvention, or background regeneration. The output is a single edited image at SDXL resolution that preserves the original object count while applying the requested modifications. Training uses a dataset of over 1,200 multi-object images with 3–5 quantity captions per image, and evaluation spans ~3,000 generated results across 30 test images with 50–150 editing instructions each.

## Method

MoEdit introduces two lightweight modules on top of a frozen SDXL backbone. The Feature Compensation (FeCom) module performs cross-attention between the quantity caption c_q (as keys/values) and CLIP-encoded image features (as queries), producing compensation features that are added to the CLIP output with a tunable scale λ; this minimizes inter-object attribute interlacing and yields enhanced features I_g with distinct, separable object attributes. The Quantity Attention (QTTN) module then extracts global quantity information from I_g via a dedicated extraction network and injects it into the 4th U-Net block (B_4) through a second cross-attention with the block's noise input z_t^4, scaled by β, thereby imparting consistent quantity perception to the denoising process without any masks, bounding boxes, or LLM guidance. Only the FeCom and QTTN parameters are trained (all pretrained weights frozen) using MSE loss over 12,000 Adam steps at lr 2.5e-4, with λ and β both set to 1.0. The choice of B_4 as the sole injection point is ablated to balance quantity-preservation fidelity against editability, as injecting into multiple blocks simultaneously disrupts text-prompt guidance.

## Results

MoEdit achieves the best scores on 8 out of 9 objective metrics, including NIQE (2.749), HyperIQA (75.66), CLIP Score Whole (0.3254), CLIP Score Edit (0.2557), Q-Align Quality (4.8763), Q-Align Aesthetic (4.6172), AesBench (77.05), and MOS (75.44). TurboEdit achieves the best LPIPS (0.1684), indicating stronger perceptual similarity to the original image. On average, MoEdit improves over all baselines by 3.83%, with the largest gain in Q-Align Aesthetic (+5.82%) and the smallest in NIQE (+0.79%). In subjective Numerical Accuracy (quantity preservation), Emu2 slightly edges out MoEdit (avg ~84.9% vs ~83.3%), attributed to Emu2's clustering strategy that boosts numerical stability at the cost of editability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MoEdit outperforms all seven baselines on the majority of metrics. Against TurboEdit (the closest competitor), MoEdit leads on NIQE (2.749 vs 2.872), HyperIQA (75.66 vs 71.72), CLIP Score (0.3254/0.2557 vs 0.3101/0.2427), Q-Align (4.8763/4.6172 vs 4.6849/4.2181), AesBench (77.05 vs 72.13), and MOS (75.44 vs 11.35), but trails on LPIPS (0.2555 vs 0.1684), indicating TurboEdit preserves more of the original image's pixel-level structure. Against Emu2, MoEdit wins on all objective quality metrics (NIQE, HyperIQA, CLIP, Q-Align, AesBench) and MOS, while Emu2 retains a slight edge in average Numerical Accuracy (84.9% vs 83.3%), which the authors attribute to Emu2's clustering strategy sacrificing editability for numerical stability. The remaining baselines (SSR-Encoder, λ-Eclipse, IP-Adapter, Blip-diffusion, MS-diffusion) are substantially outperformed by MoEdit across nearly all metrics, with MOS scores below 8% compared to MoEdit's 75.44%, reflecting severe limitations in editability, quantity preservation, or both.

## Relation to Prior Work

MoEdit builds on SDXL as its diffusion backbone and extends the diffusion-based image editing literature (fine-tuning, internal-representation, and inversion-optimization approaches) to the multi-object setting. It departs from prior multi-object editing methods (LoMOE, MS-diffusion, SSR-Encoder) that rely on auxiliary tools such as masks, bounding boxes, or dedicated target queries to isolate individual objects, and from LLM-guided methods (Emu2, TurboEdit) that achieve global quantity consistency but sacrifice per-object attribute separability. The key conceptual departure is the auxiliary-free design: the FeCom module uses text-prompt-guided cross-attention to compensate for CLIP's known weakness in encoding distinct multi-object attributes, while the QTTN module injects quantity perception directly into a single U-Net block without any external guidance signals.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The auxiliary-free formulation removes the one-to-one association between auxiliary tools and objects, reducing training cost and user friction for multi-object editing pipelines. However, the evaluation is limited to a small dataset (1,200 training images, 30 test images) and a single base architecture (SDXL), leaving open questions about scalability to very large object counts, generalization to other diffusion backbones, and robustness in complex interaction scenarios that the authors themselves flag as future work. The moderate average improvement (~3.8%) over strong baselines like TurboEdit, combined with the fact that MoEdit actually underperforms on LPIPS, suggests the gains are real but not transformative for the broader diffusion-editing community.
