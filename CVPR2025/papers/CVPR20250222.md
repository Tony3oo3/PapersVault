## Problem

Existing versatile deep image watermarking methods face an inherent tradeoff: pixel-wise residual subtraction between a fixed localized watermark and its reconstruction forces a sacrifice of container image fidelity to achieve accurate tamper localization. Additionally, the fixed localized watermark limits embedding flexibility, and both localization and copyright extraction degrade sharply under severe noise or global AIGC editing (e.g., InstructPix2Pix), where the watermark can be entirely erased.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250222/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

At encoding, the method takes an original RGB image, a 100-bit copyright watermark, and a 2D localized watermark (which can be a natural image rather than a fixed pattern) to produce a single container image. At decoding, only the received (tampered and/or degraded) image is needed: the invertible network recovers an artifact map and copyright bits, and the passive tamper extractor fuses the artifact map with the received image (padded to 1024×1024, patch-embedded at window size 14) to output a pixel-level tamper mask. Unlike prior methods, no knowledge of the original localized watermark is required at the decoding stage.

## Method

OmniGuard replaces the fragile residual-subtraction mask generation with a hybrid forensic pipeline: a proactive invertible dual-watermark network (embedding both a 2D localized watermark and 1D copyright bits) feeds a passive Swin-ViT-based tamper extractor that classifies tampering artifacts directly from the reconstructed artifact map and the received image. A Q-Former-inspired degradation query, learned via supervised training on {artifact map, received image, ground-truth mask} triplets, adaptively modulates the artifact tokens to distinguish tampering-induced artifacts from degradation-induced ones. An adaptive localized watermark transform applies a reversible additive affine transformation that injects content-aware high-frequency information from the original image into the localized watermark, improving container fidelity by ~0.7 dB over a fixed natural-image watermark. A lightweight AIGC-editing simulator (Stable Diffusion VAE encode-decode for global edits and random partial watermark removal for local edits) is inserted during copyright-network training, reducing per-sample iteration time to 0.16 s versus 3.675 s for full diffusion-based approaches while matching or exceeding their bit-accuracy under instruction-driven editing.

## Results

On tamper localization (Table 2), OmniGuard achieves F1 scores of 0.895–0.961 and AUC ≥ 0.991 across Stable Diffusion Inpaint, ControlNet Inpaint, and splicing under both clean and degraded (JPEG, noise, color jitter) conditions. Under degraded conditions, OmniGuard maintains F1 ≥ 0.895 while EditGuard drops to 0.740–0.758, a gap of approximately 0.2 F1. On watermarking fidelity and robustness (Table 3), OmniGuard achieves 42.33 dB PSNR / 0.992 SSIM with 100-bit capacity, and with both watermarks embedded (OmniGuard†) reaches 41.78 dB PSNR—4.25 dB above EditGuard† (37.53 dB)—while attaining 99.6% bit accuracy on local SD Inpaint and 100% on JPEG (Q=70).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against all five passive localization baselines (MVSS-Net, CAT-Net, PSCC-Net, HiFi-Net, IML-ViT), OmniGuard dominates by a wide margin, achieving F1 > 0.89 versus their best of 0.355 (MVSS-Net on clean splicing), confirming that proactive watermarking is essential for reliable tamper localization. Against the primary proactive baseline EditGuard, OmniGuard is comparable under clean conditions (F1 0.961 vs 0.951 on SD Inpaint) but pulls ahead decisively under degradation: F1 0.947 vs 0.740 (SD Inpaint), 0.952 vs 0.752 (ControlNet), and 0.895 vs 0.758 (Splicing), a consistent ~0.2 F1 gap attributable to EditGuard's reliance on pixel-level residual subtraction. On watermarking fidelity, OmniGuard† (dual watermark) outperforms EditGuard† by 4.25 dB in PSNR (41.78 vs 37.53) and by 14.8% in average bit accuracy under AIGC editing (e.g., 98.1% vs 58.2% on InstructPix2Pix). Against Robust-Wide, which is specifically trained for instruction-driven editing, OmniGuard trails by only 0.9% on InstructPix2Pix bit accuracy (98.1% vs 99.0%) but trains 23× faster per sample (0.16 s vs 3.675 s) and fits on an 11 GB GPU. Against TrustMark, OmniGuard achieves comparable PSNR (42.33 vs 43.17 dB) but substantially better bit accuracy under local editing (99.6% vs 98.0%) and global AIGC editing (98.1% vs 92.8%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the versatile deep image watermarking framework introduced by EditGuard and V²AMark, which embed dual watermarks (localized + copyright) via invertible networks and extract tamper masks through pixel-wise residual subtraction. The key departure is replacing this fragile subtraction with a passive, degradation-aware Swin-ViT extractor that treats mask generation as a classification task, thereby decoupling encoding fidelity from decoding precision. It also draws on passive tamper localization architectures (IML-ViT, MVSS-Net) and robust watermarking techniques (Robust-Wide, TrustMark), unifying them into a single hybrid proactive-passive pipeline with an adaptive watermark transform and a lightweight AIGC-editing surrogate (VAE compression + partial removal) in place of full diffusion denoising.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As AIGC editing tools (InstructPix2Pix, SD Inpaint) become mainstream, the inability of prior versatile watermarks to survive global generative edits is a critical gap; OmniGuard's finding that VAE compression alone captures most of the watermark disruption (enabling 24× faster training than Robust-Wide) offers a practical recipe for the community. However, the method still flags entire images as tampered under global edits, and the AIGC simulator remains a surrogate rather than modeling actual multi-step diffusion, leaving open how well it generalizes to future editing architectures. The hybrid proactive-passive design also raises questions about deployment: the tamper extractor adds inference cost, and the adaptive watermark transform requires the original image at encoding time, limiting fully blind scenarios.
