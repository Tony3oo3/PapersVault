## Problem

Existing night-time scene segmentation methods—whether fully-supervised or domain-adaptation-based—assume that training and inference scenes follow the same distribution, leaving them unable to generalize to unseen nighttime scenes with varying illumination. No prior work specifically designs a generalizable paradigm for night-time segmentation that can transfer from a single source domain (day or night) to arbitrary unseen night conditions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252819/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes RGB driving scene images resized to 512×512 pixels and produces a per-pixel semantic segmentation mask over 19 common categories. It operates as a parameter-efficient adapter (11.5M trainable parameters) inserted between frozen DINOv2 ViT-L encoder layers, with a Mask2Former decoder for pixel-wise prediction. The adapter processes intermediate VFM features in the frequency domain, so the spatial input is never directly modified—only the internal representations are adapted.

## Method

NightAdapter projects frozen VFM features into the frequency domain via Discrete Sine Transform (DST), revealing that low-frequency bands carry illumination-insensitive scene content while mid- and high-frequency bands encode illumination-sensitive domain style. Two complementary components operate in a divide-and-conquer fashion: (1) Illumination-Insensitive Band Adaptation uses learnable tokens with attention-based similarity maps to enhance the two lowest-frequency bands, preserving domain-invariant semantic structure; (2) Illumination-Sensitive Band Adaptation first randomizes the per-channel responses of mid- and high-frequency bands within a modest range (U[0, 0.3]) to break domain-specific patterns while retaining edge information, then fine-tunes these randomized bands with additional token-based adapters. After adaptation, all modified bands are projected back to the spatial domain via inverse DST to feed the next frozen VFM layer. The entire scheme is trained end-to-end with AdamW for 40K iterations, following the REIN baseline's training protocol, and requires only 11.5M trainable parameters.

## Results

In the day-to-night generalization setting (CityScapes as source), NightAdapter achieves 84.0% mIoU on ACDC-Night, 74.1% on Dark Zurich, and 60.3% on Nighttime Driving, outperforming the REIN baseline by 13.4%, 9.8%, and 10.0% respectively. In the night-to-night generalization setting (NightCity as source), it gains 5.2% and 5.7% mIoU over REIN on ACDC-Night and BDD100K-Night. For in-domain evaluation, NightAdapter reaches 65.1% mIoU on NightCity and 57.8% on BDD100K-Night with only 11.5M trainable parameters, surpassing fully-supervised methods with 50–100M parameters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NightAdapter decisively outperforms all 43 compared baselines across every experimental setting. In day-to-night generalization, it surpasses the strongest VFM-based baseline REIN [71] by 13.4% mIoU on ACDC-Night and 9.8% on Dark Zurich, and beats the best Transformer-based UDA method CoDA [24] by 17.6% on ACDC-Night. Among PEFT methods (Table 2), NightAdapter leads the second-best SET [79] by 13.5% on ACDC-Night and 8.3% on Dark Zurich, while using only 11.5M trainable parameters versus SET's 6.1M. In night-to-night generalization (Table 4), it outperforms REIN by 5.2% on ACDC-Night and 5.7% on BDD100K-Night when trained on NightCity, and by 4.1% and 3.2% respectively when trained on NightCity-fine. For in-domain evaluation (Table 3), NightAdapter exceeds the best fully-supervised Swin-based method NightFormer [55] by 1.6% on NightCity and 2.4% on BDD100K-Night, while using roughly 10× fewer trainable parameters (11.5M vs. 111.9M). The method never loses to any baseline on any reported metric across all four experimental protocols.

## Relation to Prior Work

NightAdapter builds directly on REIN [71], which introduced a VFM-based (DINOv2 + low-rank adapter) framework for domain generalized semantic segmentation, and extends it by operating in the frequency domain via Discrete Sine Transform rather than in spatial space. Conceptually, it departs from prior frequency-based domain generalization methods (FSDR, Deep Frequency Filtering, Fourier-based frameworks) that treat low/high frequency as a generic content/style split, by specifically identifying illumination-sensitive (high + middle) and illumination-insensitive (low) bands through band-rejection analysis on VFM features. The band randomization mechanism—replacing sensitive-band values with uniform random draws before adaptation—is a novel intervention not seen in prior PEFT or frequency-domain DG literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 13.4% mIoU gain over the strongest VFM baseline (REIN) on day-to-night generalization, achieved with only 11.5M trainable parameters, demonstrates that frequency-domain adapters can substantially close the illumination domain gap without full fine-tuning. The clean separation of illumination-sensitive and insensitive bands via DST provides a reusable inductive bias that could transfer to other photometric shift scenarios (fog, rain, low-light). Open questions include whether the specific band boundaries (e.g., 768–1024 for insensitive bands) and randomization threshold (T=0.3) are universal or require per-domain tuning, and whether the approach generalizes beyond driving scenes to other night-time or low-contrast domains.
