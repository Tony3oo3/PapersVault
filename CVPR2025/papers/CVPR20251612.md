## Problem

Manually collected general annotated datasets like ImageNet contain hidden, non-transferable biases (e.g., consistent backgrounds, styles, or object positions per category) that cause pre-trained backbones to learn shortcut features and degrade cross-category/cross-domain generalization. Existing diffusion-based synthetic dataset generation methods replicate the distribution of ImageNet rather than reducing its bias, so they do not yield meaningful generalization gains. No prior work directly generates a low-biased annotated dataset for backbone pre-training.

## Input / Output

The method requires only the 1000 ImageNet category names as text prompts and a pre-trained Stable Diffusion 1.5 model—no image-text pairs or any external images are used during fine-tuning. The output is a fine-tuned diffusion generator (via LoRA) that produces synthetic RGB images annotated with their category labels, which are then used to pre-train backbone networks (ResNet50, ViT-S) for downstream transfer learning, detection, and segmentation tasks.

## Method

The core contribution is a bi-level semantic alignment loss that fine-tunes a pre-trained diffusion model via reinforcement learning (reward-based, following Deep Reward/CoMat) using only text prompts. The first level (entire dataset alignment) uses a Linear-ReLU-Linear discriminator in an adversarial scheme to push all generated images toward the CLIP text-feature distribution of all 1000 categories simultaneously, ensuring the synthetic dataset matches the global low-biased semantic space. The second level (individual image alignment) maximizes cosine similarity between each generated image's CLIP feature and the CLIP feature of its specific category description (e.g., 'photo of c_i'), preventing class ambiguity. A quality assurance loss based on Q-ALIGN (a fine-tuned mPLUG-Owl-2 MLLM scoring images 1–5) is added to counteract the image quality degradation caused by the semantic alignment objective. The final training objective combines both alignment losses with a weighted quality term, and gradients are enabled in only 5 of 50 denoising steps to save GPU memory.

## Results

On eight transfer learning datasets, lbGen-pretrained ResNet50 achieves 73.2% average top-1 accuracy, outperforming IN-Real (71.5%), IN-RealFake (71.8%), IN-SD1.5 (67.3%), and IN-GenRobust (64.5%). For ViT-S, lbGen reaches 74.4% average, beating IN-Real (72.3%) by 2.1% and the best synthetic baseline IN-RealFake (70.8%) by 3.6%. Notably, lbGen scores only 46.1%/46.3% on ImageNet validation (vs. 76.2%/78.7% for IN-Real), yet still generalizes better cross-category, demonstrating that in-domain accuracy does not correlate with transferability. On bias metrics, lbGen reduces texture inclination by 4.8–9.8% and achieves the lowest background gap (6.1–6.4%) among all methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

lbGen consistently outperforms all four baselines on average transfer learning accuracy for both ResNet50 and ViT-S. Against IN-Real (the manually collected ImageNet-1K), lbGen gains +1.7% (ResNet50) and +2.1% (ViT-S) on average, winning on 6–7 of 8 datasets despite scoring far lower on ImageNet validation (46.1% vs. 76.2%). Against IN-RealFake, the strongest synthetic baseline, lbGen leads by +1.4% (ResNet50) and +3.6% (ViT-S), with the largest margins on fine-grained datasets like Aircraft (+2.2/+5.3) and SUN397 (+8.4/+10.1). Against IN-SD1.5 (raw Stable Diffusion without fine-tuning), lbGen improves by +5.9% and +4.5%, confirming that the bi-level semantic alignment is critical. Against IN-GenRobust, lbGen leads by +8.7% and +8.1%, the largest gap, as GenRobust's distribution-matching approach does not address bias. On specific bias metrics (Table 3), lbGen achieves the lowest texture inclination (56.1/57.2 vs. 60.9/67.0 for IN-Real), highest context robustness (64.7/66.0 vs. 60.0/61.8), and smallest background gap (6.4/6.1 vs. 6.8/6.7), validating that the gains stem from reduced shortcut learning rather than memorization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on Stable Diffusion (Rombach et al., 2022) as its generation backbone and CLIP (Radford et al., 2021) as the semantic reference space, but departs fundamentally from prior synthetic dataset generation work (GenRobust, RealFake, FakeIt) that aimed to replicate ImageNet's empirical distribution. Instead of matching the real data distribution—which inherits its biases—the authors repurpose CLIP's language-aligned feature space as a 'low-biased' target, fine-tuning the diffusion model via RL-style reward gradients (following Deep Reward and CoMat) with a novel bi-level adversarial + cosine-similarity alignment loss. The key conceptual departure is treating a multimodal model's semantic space as a bias-free proxy for category semantics, requiring only class names rather than any image-text pairs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work offers a practical path to reducing the well-documented ImageNet bias problem without the cost of manual re-collection, and the finding that pre-training on generated low-biased data outperforms real ImageNet on cross-category transfer (especially in few-shot regimes) challenges the assumption that real photos are always the gold standard for pre-training. However, the core assumption—that CLIP's semantic space is genuinely 'low-biased' rather than merely differently biased—remains unvalidated, and the method is only demonstrated on ImageNet-1K with a single CLIP variant. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger or more diverse taxonomies, whether the quality ceiling of diffusion-generated images limits gains on pixel-level tasks, and whether the RL fine-tuning paradigm generalizes beyond the 50-step gradient window used here.
