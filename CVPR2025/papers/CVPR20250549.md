## Problem

Existing physics-based human reaching and grasping methods depend on extensive, task-specific MoCap datasets that are expensive to collect and limited in coverage, making them struggle with diverse scenes and unseen objects. This paper asks whether brief, easily accessible walking MoCap data—combined with kinematically generated grasping poses—can suffice to produce diverse, physically feasible full-body reaching and grasping motions, and how to effectively fuse these two qualitatively different data sources without inheriting artifacts from the generated data.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a scene specification (object position, table height ranging 0.05–1.65 m, table size 0.6–1.2 m, start/end positions 1.5–4 m from the table) and, during training, approximately 30 seconds of walking MoCap from AMASS/CMU plus actively generated SLERP-interpolated motions from standing to kinematically produced grasping poses (via VPoser body prior and GrabNet hand prior). The output is a sequence of physically simulated joint actions for a 3D humanoid with 15 body joints and a 23-joint dexterous hand (replacing the typical spherical hand), covering the full approach–reach–grasp–lift–return cycle. The character's dexterous hand introduces significantly more degrees of freedom than standard physics-based character rigs, and the policy must maintain balance throughout the entire multi-stage task.

## Method

The framework uses a hierarchical ASE-style architecture: a low-level policy π_L(a|s,z) trained with adversarial imitation (discriminator) and skill discovery (encoder) objectives maps a latent variable z to motions, while a high-level policy π_H(z|s) selects z under task-specific rewards (direction, pre-grasp, grasp, post-grasp) plus motion-prior rewards. The key novelty is a multi-iteration active training loop: after the first walking-only iteration, the system evaluates per-task success rate and discriminator prediction score, then actively generates minimal SLERP-interpolated grasping motions for the worst-performing task configurations (weighted by a combined score in Eq. 4) to augment the dataset. A local feature alignment mechanism, motivated by a pilot study showing that shallow critic layers capture cross-task motion patterns (walking and reaching cluster together in shallow layers but diverge in deeper layers), adds a Mahalanobis-distance reward (Eq. 5–6) that penalizes generated motions whose shallow-layer features deviate from the pre-computed walking feature distribution, thereby transferring natural balance and coordination patterns from walking to reaching and grasping while preserving motion diversity via per-feature thresholds.

## Results

On simple scenes, the method achieves 99.8% SR(Grasp) and 88.8% SR(Goal), matching or exceeding the Oracle Policy (97.4% / 59.0%) and outperforming all physics-based baselines. On complex scenes, it reaches 69.7% SR(Grasp) and 55.8% SR(Goal), surpassing the best baseline AMP* (66.7% / 55.3%) and the Oracle Policy (69.7% / 53.4%). Against concurrent SOTA methods (Table 3), the method achieves the highest SR(Grasp) at 69.7% (vs. 59.6% for Braun et al. and 54.4% for Omnigrasp) with the lowest failure score (12.0%) and competitive user-study naturalness scores (7.55 user, 7.38/7.25 GPT-4o/Kimi).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against ASE [24], the method wins decisively on both success rates (99.8% vs. 55.7% SR(Grasp) in simple scenes; 69.7% vs. 40.2% in complex), as ASE struggles to balance walking reproduction with task completion. AMP [23] is the strongest single-discriminator baseline, reaching 85.3% SR(Grasp) in simple scenes but still trailing the method by ~14 points; even the augmented AMP* (66.7% complex SR(Grasp)) falls short of the method's 69.7%. PMP [4] with part-wise priors achieves moderate success (73.6% simple, 68.7% complex SR(Grasp)) but suffers from balance issues as part count increases. Among concurrent SOTA methods, the method outperforms Braun et al. [5] (59.6% SR(Grasp), 22.2% SR(Goal)) and Omnigrasp [17] (54.4% SR(Grasp), 52.6% SR(Goal)) on both success rate and naturalness (user score 7.55 vs. 5.83 and 5.67). WANDR [11] reports higher user-study scores (8.33) and GPT-4o ratings (8.03), but it is a kinematic method with only 32% reach success and no physics-based grasping, making it not directly comparable on task completion.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the ASE hierarchical skill-embedding framework (adversarial imitation + skill discovery with a latent variable) and extends it with two novel components: an active data generation strategy that uses kinematic pose priors (FLEX, VPoser, GrabNet) to fill gaps in the motion space, and a local feature alignment mechanism that regularizes generated motions toward shallow-layer features of walking data. It departs from prior physics-based reaching/grasping methods (Braun et al., Omnigrasp) that require extensive task-specific MoCap datasets (GRAB, AMASS) by showing that ~30 seconds of walking data suffices. The pilot study's finding that shallow critic layers capture cross-semantic motion patterns (walking and reaching cluster together in early layers) provides the empirical motivation for the alignment mechanism, distinguishing this work from part-wise supervision approaches like PMP and PSE.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is substantial for humanoid robotics and animation: collecting task-specific full-body MoCap for diverse reaching and grasping scenarios is prohibitively expensive, and this work demonstrates that a small, easily-obtained walking dataset combined with active kinematic generation can produce physically feasible, natural motions across unseen objects and scenes. The feature alignment mechanism (Mahalanobis distance on shallow critic features) is a lightweight, generalizable regularization that could transfer to other motion synthesis tasks. Limitations include reliance on external kinematic pose priors for grasping pose generation, validation only in simulation with a simplified 15-joint character, and the iterative training pipeline requiring multiple rounds of data generation and retraining. Open questions include whether the shallow-layer alignment generalizes to more complex multi-object or bimanual interactions, and whether the approach transfers to real-robot deployment.
