## Problem

Current vision-language alignment methods like CLIP require full retraining of both vision and language encoders from scratch, demanding ~20k GPU hours and 400M image-text pairs, making development expensive and inflexible. Despite evidence that well-trained unimodal encoders (e.g., DINOv2, All-Roberta-Large) exhibit high semantic similarity in their embedding spaces, no practical framework existed to exploit this similarity for efficient multimodal alignment. The paper asks whether simple projection transformations between frozen, semantically similar unimodal encoders can achieve CLIP-level performance.

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images (processed by a frozen DINOv2 ViT-Large producing 256 patch tokens plus a CLS token) and text captions/prompts (processed by a frozen language encoder such as All-Roberta-Large or MpNet, supporting up to 200–300 tokens versus CLIP's 77-token limit). The output is a pair of aligned embeddings in a shared space; downstream, cosine similarity between image and text embeddings yields class labels for zero-shot classification, retrieval rankings for image-text matching, or per-patch class assignments for semantic segmentation. The spatial patch tokens from DINOv2 are preserved through the projector, enabling pixel-level localization without additional fine-grained alignment losses.

## Method

The framework has three components: (1) encoder pair selection via Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) computed on 5,000 COCO image-caption pairs, which empirically predicts alignment ease and downstream retrieval accuracy; (2) dataset curation combining a concept-coverage dataset (LAION-CLASS-Collected, 6M samples retrieved from LAION-400M using CLIP-based concept prototypes) with high-quality retrieval datasets (CC3M, CC12M, SBU) to balance semantic coverage and image-caption alignment; (3) training lightweight Token Projectors (11.5M of 670M total parameters, ~1.7%) with InfoNCE contrastive loss while keeping both encoders fully frozen. The projector architecture applies residual Token Projectors (linear + non-linear branches) to both local patch tokens and the CLS token on the vision side, and to text tokens plus a 2-layer MLP global projector on the text side, with adapted local tokens averaged and added to the adapted CLS token to form the final global embedding. This achieves 76.3% ImageNet zero-shot accuracy with 20× less data and 65× less compute than CLIP, while retaining unimodal strengths like multilingual capability, long-context handling, and superior localization.

## Results

The DINOv2-ARL projector model, trained on only 20M image-caption pairs with 11.5M trainable parameters (~1% of total), achieves 76.3% zero-shot ImageNet accuracy, surpassing OpenAI CLIP ViT-L (75.3%) and LAION-CLIP ViT-L (72.7%) trained on 400M pairs. On image-text retrieval, it scores 87.5%/74.1% (I2T/T2I) on Flickr30k and 60.1%/45.1% on COCO, matching or exceeding LAION-CLIP. For zero-shot semantic segmentation, DINOv2-ARL achieves 31.37 mIoU on Pascal VOC and 24.61 on Pascal Context, outperforming SPARC (27.36/21.65) and OpenAI CLIP (23.46/14.25). Alignment requires only 400 A100 GPU-hours, a 65-fold reduction over CLIP training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against OpenAI CLIP ViT-L (Radford et al., 2021), DINOv2-ARL wins on 10 of 12 classification datasets (e.g., ImageNet 76.3% vs 75.3%, CUB 66.1% vs 61.4%) and on image retrieval (Flickr T2I 74.1% vs 64.9%), while losing on Stanford Cars (73.9% vs 77.3%) and Food-101 (89.1% vs 92.9%). Against LAION-CLIP ViT-L (Schuhmann et al., 2022), DINOv2-ARL wins on ImageNet (76.3% vs 72.7%) and most datasets, though LAION-CLIP leads on Stanford Cars (89.6% vs 73.9%) and Oxford Flowers (73.0% vs 78.4% — actually DINOv2-ARL wins here). Against LiT (Zhai et al., 2022), DINOv2-ARL dominates on nearly all datasets, with the largest gaps on Stanford Cars (73.9% vs 24.3%) and Aircrafts (28.1% vs 15.2%). On segmentation, DINOv2-ARL outperforms SPARC (Bica et al., 2024) by 4.0 mIoU on Pascal VOC (31.37 vs 27.36) and 3.0 on Pascal Context (24.61 vs 21.65) without any fine-grained alignment loss. For multilingual tasks, DINOv2-MpNet (trained only on English data) surpasses M-CLIP/XLM-Roberta-Large-ViT-L-14 (Chen et al., 2023) by 8.5% average classification (57.1% vs 48.6%) and xlm-roberta-base@LAION-5B (Schuhmann et al., 2022) by 7.1% average classification (57.1% vs 50.0%), despite using 20M English pairs versus 2B+ multilingual pairs. It also outperforms NLLB-CLIP-large (Visheratin, 2023) by 21.7% average classification (57.1% vs 35.4%) and 3.6% average retrieval (58.0% vs 54.4%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the CLIP-style contrastive vision-language pretraining paradigm by replacing full encoder training with projector-only alignment between frozen unimodal encoders. It builds directly on recent CKA-based analyses showing high semantic similarity between well-trained vision and language embedding spaces (Maniparambil et al. 2024, Huh et al. 2024) and on the Token Projector architecture from SPARC (Mukhoti et al. 2023). Unlike LiT (Zhai et al. 2022), which still trains an entire text encoder, or LiLT (Khan & Fu 2023), which uses random encoder pairs and smaller datasets, this work introduces CKA-guided encoder pair selection and concept-balanced data curation as systematic prerequisites for projector training, positioning the contribution as a principled framework rather than a single model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 65-fold compute reduction and 20-fold data reduction for CLIP-level performance make multimodal model development dramatically more accessible, with direct implications for low-resource settings, domain-specific alignment, and environmental cost. The framework's flexibility—swapping in multilingual or long-context text encoders without retraining the vision side—opens a path to specialized multimodal systems that full CLIP retraining cannot easily accommodate. Key open questions include whether fine-grained alignment losses (e.g., SPARC-style) can further improve localization, how the approach integrates with LLM-based multimodal pipelines, and whether the assumption of pre-trained strong unimodal encoders (whose own training costs are substantial) limits the framework's applicability to domains lacking such encoders.
