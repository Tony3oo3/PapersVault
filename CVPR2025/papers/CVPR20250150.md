## Problem

Existing DFER methods focus on capturing spatial-temporal features but largely ignore the substantial noise in in-the-wild DFER datasets. Prior noisy-label research treats all noise as a single category, overlooking that DFER datasets contain two fundamentally different noise types: low-quality samples (occlusion, dim lighting, blurriness) that are objectively unusable, and mislabeled samples caused by subjective annotator biases (confirmation bias, low-intensity expressions labeled as Neutral) that are correctable.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes 16-frame facial expression video clips from in-the-wild DFER datasets (FERV39k, DFEW, MAFW) and produces a dynamically purified training subset each epoch, from which the backbone outputs emotion class labels. The purification is plug-and-play: an extra two-layer MLP branch is appended after the embedding layer of any existing DFER model (ResNet, VGG, ViT, Former-DFER) to compute per-sample weights, without altering the original architecture's other components.

## Method

D2SP operates in two sequential stages with dynamic re-evaluation each epoch. In the Coarse-Grained Pruning (CGP) stage, a two-layer MLP computes a per-sample weight w_i via sigmoid, which is multiplied by the predicted probability to yield a weighted score; the mean of these scores over the past t epochs is compared against threshold λ, and samples below it are pruned as low-quality noise. Training uses a Weighted Cross-Entropy Loss so that high-quality samples receive larger gradient contributions. In the Fine-Grained Correction (FGC) stage, the remaining samples are checked for prediction stability along two dimensions: (1) the predicted label must be consistent across all past t epochs, and (2) the average predicted probability for the model's predicted label must exceed the average probability for the ground-truth label by more than threshold τ; samples satisfying both conditions are relabeled. The entire purification pipeline is re-run every epoch (Dynamic Purification), allowing the model's improving representations to progressively refine which samples are pruned or corrected.

## Results

D2SP is evaluated as a plug-and-play framework on six DFER methods across three in-the-wild datasets (FERV39k, DFEW, MAFW), reporting Weighted Average Recall (WAR) and Unweighted Average Recall (UAR). The average improvement across all methods is 3.97% WAR and 2.45% UAR on FERV39k, 1.67% WAR and 0.84% UAR on DFEW, and 1.20% WAR and 0.56% UAR on MAFW. The largest single gain is ResNet50_LSTM on FERV39k, jumping from 40.75% to 48.57% WAR (+7.82) and 32.12% to 38.97% UAR (+6.85). Ablation confirms that the dynamic purification (re-purifying each epoch) is essential: static purification actually degrades performance by 1.08% WAR on FERV39k.

## Baselines & Comparisons

D2SP is applied as a plug-and-play module on top of four baseline architectures (ResNet, VGG, Former-DFER, ViT) combined with LSTM, and consistently improves every method on every dataset. ResNet-based models benefit the most: ResNet50_LSTM gains +7.82% WAR on FERV39k (40.75→48.57), the largest single improvement in the table, while ResNet18_LSTM gains +4.73% WAR. VGG-based models show moderate gains (+3.14 to +3.83% WAR on FERV39k), with VGG16_LSTM improving from 41.70% to 45.53% WAR. Former-DFER, already the strongest transformer-based baseline at 46.85% WAR on FERV39k, still improves by +2.49% to 49.34% WAR, and ViT_LSTM (the overall strongest at 48.67% WAR) gains +1.83% to 50.50%. On DFEW, where annotation noise is lower, improvements are smaller but still positive across all methods (e.g., VGG13_LSTM +2.56% WAR, Former-DFER +2.18% WAR). On MAFW, gains are the most modest (0.25–1.74% WAR), suggesting the dataset has relatively less of the two noise types D2SP targets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the small-loss principle from noisy-label learning (MentorNet, Co-teaching) into a learnable weight-computing module for pruning low-quality DFER samples, and adapts SSL pseudo-labeling ideas (MixMatch, DivideMix) into a prediction-stability-based label correction mechanism. It departs from prior noisy-label work, which uniformly treats all noise as a single category, by explicitly distinguishing objective data-quality noise (occlusion, blur) from subjective annotation noise (confirmation bias, low-intensity mislabeling as Neutral). The framework is positioned as a plug-and-play wrapper over existing DFER backbones (ResNet-LSTM, VGG-LSTM, ViT-LSTM, Former-DFER) rather than a new architecture, drawing on the full lineage of DFER methods from C3D through Transformer-based approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The two-type noise taxonomy and the corresponding dual-stage purification offer a practical, general recipe for DFER practitioners who work with in-the-wild datasets where annotation quality is a known bottleneck. However, the performance gains are modest on DFEW and MAFW (under 1% UAR), and the framework introduces additional hyperparameters (lambda, tau, t) that require per-dataset tuning, limiting its zero-config appeal. Open questions include whether the dynamic purification loop is truly necessary beyond a few epochs, whether the two-stage interaction (CGP reducing FGC correction count from 8271 to 1436) reflects a fundamental dependency or a thresholding artifact, and whether the approach generalizes to other noisy-label domains beyond facial expressions.
