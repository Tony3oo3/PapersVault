## Problem

Progress in machine understanding of sign languages has been slow, largely due to limited training data. For ASL fingerspelling specifically, prior datasets (e.g., ChicagoFSWild+) are over 10x smaller and sourced from low-resolution web video crops, making them ill-suited for the mobile text entry use case where Deaf signers could fingerspell as a faster alternative to on-screen keyboards. This paper addresses the data gap by introducing FSboard, a large-scale, domain-appropriate fingerspelling dataset collected from 147 Deaf signers via smartphone selfie cameras, and provides baseline models to establish performance benchmarks.

## Input / Output

The model consumes up to 256 frames of 85-dimensional 3D MediaPipe Holistic landmarks (covering hands, pose, and face) extracted at 30 Hz from 1944×2592 selfie camera video, with each frame mapped to one soft token in the encoder. It produces a greedy beam-search (beam size 5) transcription of up to 256 characters. The dataset spans five phrase domains—MacKenzie phrases, URLs, street addresses, phone numbers, and personal names—with an average sequence length of 21.2 characters, and the test set enforces 15 unique signers with zero phrase overlap to the training split.

## Method

The baseline finetunes ByT5-Small (300M), a pretrained character-level encoder-decoder, by linearly projecting the 85 MediaPipe Holistic landmarks into the encoder as one soft token per frame, trained with Adafactor (LR 0.001) on 32 TPUv3 for up to 200k steps. Pretraining is critical: training from scratch yields 33.8% CER versus 11.1% with the pretrained checkpoint, and scaling to ByT5-Base (580M) actually degrades performance due to overfitting. Ablations show that reducing frame rate to 15 Hz causes only mild degradation (11.8% CER) and removing face or pose landmarks adds ≤1.4% CER, indicating the model relies primarily on hand shape. The dataset itself was cleaned via a bootstrapping procedure: five ByT5 models were cross-validated over noisy clip boundaries, and clips where predictions agreed with ground truth were retained, iterated twice before manual correction. The best model achieves 11.1% CER and 52.9% top-1 accuracy, substantially outperforming ChicagoFSWild+ baselines (37.7% CER) but still trailing human raters at 2.2% CER with unlimited replays.

## Results

The ByT5-Small baseline finetuned on 30 Hz MediaPipe Holistic landmarks achieves 11.1% CER and 52.9% top-1 accuracy on the FSboard test set (15 unique signers, no train phrase overlap). Human performance with unlimited replays reaches 2.2% CER and 74% top-1 accuracy, while human one-pass performance is 22.4% CER and 19% top-1 accuracy—placing the model between the two human conditions. Reducing frame rate to 15 Hz causes only a small regression (11.8% CER), and removing face landmarks adds 0.9% CER, suggesting practical on-device optimizations are feasible.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The FSboard baseline (11.1% CER) substantially outperforms the ChicagoFSWild+ baselines at 37.7% CER, though the test sets differ in domain and source. It also beats the human reference scores reported for ChicagoFSWild+ (17.3% CER for Wild, 13.9% for Wild+), which the authors attribute to those datasets being mined from longer signing videos where annotators had broader context. On the same 100-sequence FSboard test subset, the model scores 13.5% CER versus 2.2% for a Deaf human rater with unlimited replays, but the model (13.5%) significantly outperforms human one-pass transcription (22.4% CER), suggesting a practical advantage for real-time use. The baseline also surpasses the best Kaggle competition entry (16.4% CER), which was constrained in model size and runtime. Pretraining from ByT5's checkpoint is critical: training the same architecture from scratch on FSboard alone yields 33.8% CER, a 3× degradation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the YouTube-ASL baseline architecture (linearly projecting 3D MediaPipe Holistic landmarks into a ByT5 encoder-decoder) and the ChicagoFS series of fingerspelling recognition datasets, but departs from both in key ways. Unlike ChicagoFS, which mines clips from web videos, FSboard is collected from 147 Deaf signers via smartphone selfie cameras in a controlled mobile text entry setting, making it 10x larger in character count. The community-centered data collection approach follows the participatory ML tradition established by PopSign and ASL Citizen, but targets continuous fingerspelling transcription rather than isolated sign classification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FSboard establishes a new scale benchmark for ASL fingerspelling recognition and provides a practical, community-driven dataset that could accelerate deployment of fingerspelling-based text entry for Deaf users. The significant gap between model performance (11.1% CER) and human performance (2.2% CER) highlights substantial room for improvement, while the finding that the model outperforms real-time human one-pass transcription (13.5% vs. 22.4% CER) suggests a near-term use case where technology genuinely aids rather than merely automates. Open questions include whether the synthetic phrase domains generalize to real-world queries, how to handle capitalization and special characters, and whether direct video modeling (beyond landmarks) can close the remaining gap for on-device deployment.
