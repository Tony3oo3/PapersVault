## Problem

Normal integration from pixel-level normal maps scales poorly to high resolutions because the underlying linear system grows quadratically with image size. Prior mesh-based approaches reduce vertex count via isotropic remeshing, but this design inherently struggles to represent anisotropic surface features such as ridges and furrows, leading to degraded accuracy at high compression ratios.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252380/cropped_page0_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a dense normal map (e.g., 2048² or up to 8192²) and camera intrinsics as input, with no 3D geometry available. It first converts the pixel grid into a triangle mesh (two triangles per pixel), then decimates it to a target vertex count or error threshold, producing a sparse anisotropic triangle mesh. This mesh is subsequently used for normal integration to recover a per-vertex depth map, which is bilinearly interpolated across faces to yield the final 3D surface.

## Method

The core contribution is a screen-space derivation of quadric error metrics (originally from Garland-Heckbert 3D mesh decimation) that operates solely on normal maps, using face Jacobians computed from surface tangents to project the quadric integral into 2D. The quadric combines a geometric term (deviation from the underlying surface) and an ODT term (even vertex distribution), unified under a single matrix M = n·nᵀ + λI. This matrix also serves as the metric for a localized generalized Delaunay criterion: for each edge, the two adjacent triangles are projected into their tangent plane and lifted by ‖·‖_{M_e} to form a tetrahedron, and the edge is flipped if it is not the lower diagonal. The full algorithm iterates three local operations—edge collapse (decimation via a priority queue on collapse cost), vertex position update (minimizing the screen-space quadric in the tangent space), and edge flip (generalized Delaunay alignment)—five times, achieving sub-millimetre accuracy at ~90% compression.

## Results

On the DiLiGenT-MV dataset (20 views, orthographic projection), the proposed anisotropic decimation achieves RMSE of 3.04 mm (BEAR), 6.61 mm (BUDDHA), 2.74 mm (COW), 5.29 mm (POT2), and 6.50 mm (READING) at the 'high' resolution setting, consistently outperforming isotropic meshing [14] by 0.3–0.8 mm across all objects. At this setting, the method uses roughly 10× fewer vertices than pixels yet reaches sub-millimetre accuracy relative to the pixel-based baseline [2]. On LUCES, the highest-resolution mesh setting stays within 0.1–0.3 mm of the pixel-based reference [13] for most objects, while a 95%+ compression ratio still preserves fine surface details.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against three baselines. Against isotropic screen-space meshing [14], the proposed anisotropic decimation wins on every object and every resolution setting in Table 1, with RMSE improvements of 0.2–0.8 mm at the 'high' setting (e.g., 3.04 vs. 3.37 mm on BEAR, 6.61 vs. 7.33 mm on BUDDHA). Against the pixel-based inverse plane fitting method [2] (Cao et al., CVPR 2021), the proposed method is competitive: it actually beats the pixel-based baseline on BUDDHA (6.61 vs. 6.74 mm) and is within 0.1–0.3 mm on the remaining objects at the 'high' setting, while using roughly 10× fewer depth variables. Against the ShadowPatch pixel-based method [13] on LUCES (Table 2), the highest-resolution mesh setting achieves RMSE within 0.1–0.3 mm of the pixel-based reference for most objects (e.g., 0.33 vs. 0.30 mm on BELL, 3.32 vs. 3.46 mm on BUDDHA), confirming sub-millimetre fidelity at high compression. The ablation (Fig. 7) further shows that both vertex alignment and edge alignment are necessary: decimation alone degrades significantly, and either alignment alone improves only one metric (RMSE or MAE) while the full algorithm improves both.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Garland and Heckbert's quadric error metrics (originally for 3D mesh simplification) into screen space by deriving quadrics directly from normal maps, since the 3D surface is unknown before integration. It also generalizes Chen's optimal Delaunay triangulation criterion—previously limited to 2D point sets with a fixed metric—to locally varying 3×3 matrices on 3D surfaces, enabling anisotropic edge alignment. The direct predecessor is Heep and Zell's isotropic screen-space meshing (ECCV 2025), which uses curvature-based vertex density but produces uniform triangles; this work departs by replacing isotropic remeshing with anisotropic decimation that explicitly aligns edges to ridges and furrows.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: at resolutions beyond 10 MP, mesh-based integration is up to 100× faster than pixel-based methods while maintaining sub-millimetre accuracy at ~90% compression, making high-resolution photometric stereo pipelines feasible on standard hardware. The method offers flexible control (error threshold or fixed vertex budget), fitting diverse deployment constraints. Open questions include extending to multi-view settings to resolve scale ambiguity, handling depth discontinuities (where edge alignment to boundaries becomes critical), and whether the anisotropic alignment strategy generalizes to other gradient-based reconstruction problems beyond photometric stereo.
