## Problem

When a suspect uses synthetic images from a defender's text-to-image model to train a model for a different task (e.g., image classification), existing protections like watermarking and training-data attribution fail because the suspicious model's task is not image generation and no synthetic images are available for inspection. This paper addresses this previously unexplored scenario (their 'Case 3') where the defender must verify, under a black-box setting, whether a suspicious model was illicitly trained on data from their generative model.

## Input / Output

The defender provides their own text-to-image model G_d and text prompts to generate a shadow dataset (e.g., 30,000 images at 32×32 for CIFAR-10) and a validation dataset (e.g., 5,000 images), while the suspicious model is accessed solely through an MLaaS API that returns prediction logits or labels in a fixed batch sequence. The output is a binary provenance verdict (illegal or legal) produced by a one-sided Grubbs' hypothesis test comparing batch-wise accuracies of the shadow model and the suspicious model on the validation set, requiring no knowledge of the suspicious model's architecture, loss function, or training hyperparameters.

## Method

TrainProVe is grounded in the generalization error bound from domain adaptation theory: for two models on the same task, smaller divergence between their training data distributions implies closer generalization performance. The method proceeds in three stages: (1) generate a shadow dataset D_sdw and a validation dataset D_v from G_d using distinct text prompt sets tailored to the suspicious model's label space; (2) train a shadow model M_sdw (e.g., ResNet-18) on D_sdw; (3) feed D_v to both M_sdw and the black-box M_sus batch-by-batch, compute per-batch accuracies, and apply a one-sided Grubbs' test to check whether M_sus's mean accuracy is an unusually low outlier relative to M_sdw's. Theorem 1 formally proves that the supremum of the generalization-error gap between two models trained on data from the same generative model is bounded above by the gap when one model is trained on data independent of that model, justifying the contrapositive logic: similar accuracies imply shared provenance. Unlike prior attribution methods that require multiple generative models and a trained attributor network, TrainProVe needs only the defender's single model, one shadow model, and statistical hypothesis testing.

## Results

TrainProVe achieves an average accuracy of 99.7% on CIFAR-10, 99.2% on CIFAR-100, and 78.6% on ImageNet-100 for verifying whether a suspicious model was trained on synthetic data from a specific text-to-image model. The corresponding average F1 scores are 99.2%, 97.9%, and 49.5%, and AUROC values are 99.8%, 98.1%, and 76.9% across the three datasets. Performance degrades on ImageNet-100 due to the higher class count (100 vs. 10/100) and larger image resolution (256×256 vs. 32×32), but TrainProVe still substantially outperforms all baselines in every setting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The closest external baseline is Han et al.'s work, which trains a learned attributor to classify suspicious models. TrainProVe outperforms it by a wide margin: on CIFAR-10, TrainProVe reaches 99.7% accuracy versus 76.8% for Han et al.; on CIFAR-100, 99.2% vs. 73.0%; and on ImageNet-100, 78.6% vs. 67.1%. A key structural advantage is that Han et al. requires the defender to have access to multiple generative models to train the attributor, whereas TrainProVe only needs the defender's own single model. The two internal ablation variants (TrainProVe-Ent using logit entropy and TrainProVe-Sim using pairwise cosine similarity) both underperform the full accuracy-based method, with TrainProVe-Ent dropping to 39.8% on CIFAR-100 and 35.6% on ImageNet-100, confirming that comparing predictions against ground-truth labels is critical. In the efficiency analysis on ImageNet-100, TrainProVe completes verification in 35.1 hours versus 146.9 hours for Han et al., while simultaneously achieving higher accuracy (0.78 vs. 0.48).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper grounds its method in the generalization error bound from Ben-David et al. (NeurIPS 2006), repurposing the H-divergence between source and target domains as a theoretical justification for comparing model accuracies to infer training data provenance. It explicitly positions itself as addressing 'Case 3' in text-to-image model protection, where prior work (watermarking for stolen images, training data attribution for knowledge distillation) is inapplicable because the suspicious model performs a different task (e.g., classification) and is only accessible as a black box. The closest prior method, Han et al. (ICASSP 2024), trains a separate attributor network to classify suspicious models but requires the defender to have access to multiple generative models; TrainProVe departs by using a one-sided Grubbs' hypothesis test on batch accuracies, requiring only the defender's own model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As open-source text-to-image models (Stable Diffusion, PixArt-α, etc.) become trivially accessible, the risk of unauthorized synthetic-data training for downstream tasks grows, yet no prior mechanism existed to detect such infringement in a black-box setting. The >99% verification accuracy on CIFAR-10/100 is compelling, but performance drops substantially on ImageNet-100 (78.6% accuracy) and under fine-tuning attacks (89–97%), suggesting the method's robustness is dataset- and scenario-dependent. Open questions include whether the approach extends to non-classification tasks (detection, segmentation), other modalities (text, audio), and whether the theoretical guarantee (Theorem 1) holds under the distribution shifts introduced by fine-tuning or mixed data sources.
