## Problem

3D Gaussian Splatting achieves fast, high-quality novel view synthesis but suffers from two inherent limitations: Gaussians lack defined physical boundaries, making them poor at representing flat surfaces and hard edges, and their smooth, ellipsoidal shape leads to inefficient space coverage (analogous to the sphere packing problem), requiring impractically large numbers of primitives for dense volumetric scenes. No prior method had leveraged differentiable smooth convex shapes as primitives for real-time novel view synthesis on realistic, large-scale scenes.

## Input / Output

The method consumes posed multi-view RGB images and a sparse point cloud from Structure-from-Motion as initialization. Each smooth convex primitive is parameterized by K=6 freely movable 3D points (defining the convex hull), a smoothness parameter δ, a sharpness parameter σ, an opacity scalar, and degree-3 spherical harmonic color coefficients, totaling 69 parameters per primitive versus 59 for a 3D Gaussian. The output is a differentiable 3D radiance field that renders novel views in real time via a custom CUDA tile-based rasterizer using α-blending, with the 2D indicator function of each convex computed per-pixel from the projected convex hull lines.

## Method

3DCS replaces 3D Gaussians with 3D smooth convexes (from CvxNet) as the rendering primitive. Each convex is defined as the convex hull of K freely optimizable 3D points; during rendering, these points are projected to 2D via the pinhole camera model, a 2D convex hull is extracted using Graham Scan, and a smooth indicator function is computed per pixel via a LogSumExp of signed distances to the hull lines, with δ controlling edge sharpness and σ controlling boundary transition steepness (both scaled by camera distance for perspective consistency). A custom differentiable CUDA rasterizer, following the 3DGS tile-based architecture, performs α-blending across all primitives sorted by depth. For adaptive densification, rather than splitting a convex into two, 3DCS splits it into K new smaller convexes centered at the original K points with increased sharpness, ensuring collective volume coverage while encouraging denser geometric structure. Training uses a combined L1 + D-SSIM + mask regularization loss with exponential learning-rate decay on point positions only.

## Results

On Tanks & Temples, 3DCS achieves the best LPIPS (0.157), PSNR (23.95), and SSIM (0.851) among all compared methods, improving over 3DGS by 0.81 PSNR and 0.026 LPIPS. On Deep Blending, 3DCS attains the highest PSNR (29.81) and lowest LPIPS (0.237), outperforming 3DGS (29.41 PSNR, 0.243 LPIPS). On Mip-NeRF360, 3DCS achieves the best LPIPS (0.207) and SSIM (0.802) but trails Mip-NeRF360 on PSNR (27.29 vs. 27.69). Across all datasets, 3DCS uses roughly 70% of the memory required by 3DGS (e.g., 282 MB vs. 411 MB on T&T) while maintaining real-time rendering (25–33 FPS).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against 3DGS (the primary baseline), 3DCS wins on all three quality metrics for Tanks & Temples (PSNR +0.81, LPIPS −0.026, SSIM +0.010) and Deep Blending (PSNR +0.40, LPIPS −0.006), and on LPIPS and SSIM for Mip-NeRF360 (LPIPS −0.007, SSIM +0.013), while trailing slightly on PSNR (27.29 vs. 27.21). 3DCS also outperforms GES on every metric across all three datasets (e.g., T&T PSNR 23.95 vs. 23.35; Mip-NeRF360 LPIPS 0.207 vs. 0.250) and 2DGS similarly (T&T PSNR 23.95 vs. 23.13; Mip-NeRF360 LPIPS 0.207 vs. 0.252). Compared to the NeRF-based Mip-NeRF360, 3DCS achieves substantially better LPIPS and SSIM on Mip-NeRF360 (0.207 vs. 0.237; 0.802 vs. 0.792) and a 1.73 PSNR gain on T&T, but requires 63 min of training versus 48 hours. The main trade-off is rendering speed: 3DCS runs at 25–33 FPS versus 134–154 FPS for 3DGS, though both remain in the real-time regime. ZipNeRF, evaluated only on Mip-NeRF360, still leads on PSNR (28.54 vs. 27.29) and LPIPS (0.189 vs. 0.207), but at the cost of 5 hours of training and 0.184 FPS rendering. Instant-NGP is dominated by 3DCS on all quality metrics across all three datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper merges two distinct research threads: the learnable convex decomposition framework of CvxNet (LogSumExp smooth signed distance functions and indicator functions) and the primitive-based splatting pipeline of 3D Gaussian Splatting (tile-based CUDA rasterizer, alpha blending, adaptive densification). The key conceptual departure is replacing inherently smooth, boundary-less Gaussian primitives with 3D smooth convexes that possess defined geometric boundaries, enabling hard edges and flat surfaces. It also positions itself against other alternative primitives (GES, 2DGS) that attempt to address Gaussian limitations through different functional forms, arguing that convex hull geometry is a more natural fit for structured scenes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that primitive geometry fundamentally affects reconstruction quality: convexes capture structured indoor scenes and hard edges with fewer primitives and less memory than Gaussians, while Gaussians retain an advantage on unstructured outdoor/vegetation content. This suggests that no single primitive is universally optimal, pointing toward hybrid or scene-adaptive primitive strategies as a promising direction. Open questions include whether the 2D convex hull projection (via Graham Scan) can be made faster to close the rendering speed gap with 3DGS (33 vs. 154 FPS), whether the approach extends to dynamic scenes, and whether the physically meaningful decomposition property can be exploited for downstream tasks like editing or segmentation.
