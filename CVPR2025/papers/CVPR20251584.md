## Problem

Deepfake detectors trained on third-party datasets (e.g., FF++, Celeb-DF) are vulnerable to backdoor injection by malicious data providers who can poison training samples with invisible triggers. Existing backdoor attacks on face forgery detection (e.g., PFF) rely on fixed, partially visible triggers that are easily reproduced once the generator is exposed, leaving a gap in understanding how passcode-controlled, adaptive, and imperceptible triggers could stealthily compromise detector trustworthiness.

## Input / Output

The trigger generator takes a 512×512 RGB face image and a 100-bit binary passcode string as input, producing an additive trigger pattern δ that is imperceptible to human observers. The poisoned sample x+δ is then inserted into the training set of a Deepfake detector. The passcode acts as a secret key: even if the generator architecture is exposed, valid triggers cannot be reproduced without the correct passcode, and similar passcodes (e.g., 124 vs. 123) fail to activate the backdoor.

## Method

The core contribution is a U-Net-based trigger generator trained with three jointly optimized objectives: a perceptual distance loss (LPIPS) to keep triggers invisible, a passcode recovery loss (cross-entropy via a decoder network) to bind the trigger to a specific passcode, and—uniquely for the clean-label scenario—a representation suppression loss that forces a pre-trained Deepfake detector F to misclassify the poisoned sample as the opposite class, thereby breaking the association between forgery artifacts and the true label. Two poisoning scenarios are studied: dirty-label (target label flipped) and clean-label (target label preserved), with the latter requiring the additional suppression objective to make the trigger-label association learnable. An auxiliary set D_aux with triggers generated from random passcodes is mixed into training to erase the generator's fingerprint, ensuring that only the correct passcode activates the backdoor. The backdoor is injected by simply training standard Deepfake detectors (ResNet, EfficientNet, DenseNet, MobileNet, F3Net, SRM, NPR, FG) on the poisoned dataset with their original configurations.

## Results

Under dirty-label poisoning, the method achieves ASR of 98.84–100% across all eight detectors (ResNet, EfficientNet, MobileNet, DenseNet, F3Net, SRM, NPR, FG) while preserving benign accuracy (BA) within ~1% of original accuracy (OA). Under the more challenging clean-label scenario, ASR ranges from 90.10% (SRM) to 97.68% (F3Net). In the head-to-head comparison against six backdoor baselines on FF++ (Table 5), the proposed method achieves an average ASR of 97.26% and average BA of 97.27%, outperforming the strongest baseline Blended (ASR 95.98%) by over 1.2 points while maintaining comparable benign accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all six baselines on average ASR. The closest competitor is Blended [4] (avg ASR 95.98%), which uses a visible blended trigger; the proposed invisible trigger surpasses it by 1.28 points while achieving comparable BA (97.27% vs 97.03%). PFF [29], the most recent Deepfake-specific backdoor method, achieves 92.05% avg ASR and 97.56% avg BA; the proposed method wins on ASR by 5.2 points but trails slightly on BA by 0.29 points. Among invisible-trigger methods, ISSBA [26] (avg ASR 81.24%) is substantially outperformed, with the gap reaching 17.9 points on ResNet. Visible-trigger methods (BadNet, Blended, SIG, LC) show highly inconsistent ASR across detectors—BadNet drops to 24.75% on SRM—whereas the proposed method maintains ASR above 96% on all four evaluated detectors. In terms of trigger visual quality (Table 6), the dirty-label variant achieves the best PSNR (35.71) and SSIM (0.9572) among all invisible-trigger methods, while the clean-label variant trades some PSNR (29.89) for the added stealth of label consistency.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the backdoor attack literature (BadNet, Blended, ISSBA, etc.) to the specific domain of deepfake detection, building most directly on PFF [29], which first demonstrated that face forgery detectors can be poisoned with translation-sensitive triggers. The key departures are threefold: (1) a passcode-controlled trigger generator (inspired by steganography/StegaStamp) that makes triggers non-reproducible without authorization, (2) a representation-suppression objective that enables clean-label poisoning by suppressing forgery-related features via an auxiliary detector, and (3) adaptive, sample-specific triggers generated by a U-Net rather than fixed patterns. The method repurposes standard backdoor attack machinery (encoder-decoder trigger generation, distance and recovery losses) but reconfigures them for the binary authenticity task where semantic categories are absent.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work raises a concrete supply-chain security concern: any deepfake detector trained on third-party data (FF++, Celeb-DF, DFDC) is potentially compromised, and the passcode mechanism makes the backdoor effectively undiscoverable through defensive trial-and-error. The failure of four standard backdoor defenses (FT, FP, NAD, ABL) to meaningfully reduce ASR suggests that the deepfake detection community lacks adequate countermeasures for this threat class. A notable limitation is fragility to Gaussian blur (ASR drops sharply at radius 11), though the authors argue heavy blurring is impractical in deployment. Open questions include whether trigger-aware data auditing or provenance verification can mitigate this risk, and whether the representation-suppression idea generalizes to other binary forensic tasks.
