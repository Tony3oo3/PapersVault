## Problem

Adapting large pretrained transformers to dynamic, unlabeled target distributions at test time on resource-constrained edge devices remains challenging. Existing parameter-efficient tuning (PET) methods are either single-source or, when combining multiple modules, require costly routing networks or extensive hyperparameter tuning, making them impractical for real-time, privacy-preserving edge deployment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252351/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

At deployment, AdMiT receives a small streaming batch of unlabeled target images (batch sizes from 1 to 256) and a pre-computed set of source KME embeddings (approximated via synthetic data to avoid raw data exchange). A public DenseNet201 feature extractor maps both source and target images into a shared representation space before KME computation. The output is a single integrated PET module (a weighted average of the top-M selected modules) that plugs into the frozen base transformer for classification; this module can be applied zero-shot or further refined via one step of sharpness-aware pseudo-label fine-tuning.

## Method

AdMiT pre-trains N separate PET modules (LoRA, adapters, or VPT) on distinct source distributions while keeping the base transformer frozen, and computes empirical Kernel Mean Embeddings for each source using a small synthetic dataset for privacy and efficiency. At test time, the target batch is mapped to its empirical KME, and a convex optimization (Eqn. 4) decomposes this target KME as a weighted combination of source KMEs, yielding mixture weights that serve as relevance scores without any routing network or forward inference. The top-M modules are selected, their weights rescaled to sum to one, and their parameters are averaged to form a new integrated module θ(t). This module is then optionally fine-tuned on the target batch using sharpness-aware pseudo-label entropy minimization (a SAM-style objective) to prevent model collapse during test-time adaptation. The entire matching-and-integration pipeline requires only a single inference step and no raw data exchange, with theoretical generalization bounds provided for both the KME approximation error and the zero-shot adaptation loss.

## Results

On ImageNet-C (static adaptation, averaged over 15 corruption domains), AdMiT achieves 63.8% top-1 accuracy at batch size 256, outperforming the best multi-source baseline CONTRAST (63.5%) and SESoM-PL (62.3%), while remaining close to the GT-Tuning upper bound (67.7%). On Digits-Five, AdMiT reaches 86.8% average accuracy across five target domains, surpassing CONTRAST (86.1%) and SESoM-PL (86.0%). In the dynamic CIFAR-100C forgetting evaluation, AdMiT retains 73.3% average accuracy on source domains after sequential adaptation across 15 target distributions, compared to 72.5% for CONTRAST and 64.5% for π-tuning-PL.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AdMiT outperforms all multi-source baselines on ImageNet-C at most batch sizes: it beats CONTRAST by 0.3–0.6 points (e.g., 63.8 vs 63.5 at BS=256; 62.3 vs 61.7 at BS=16) and SESoM-PL by 1.2–1.5 points. Against single-source TTA methods, AdMiT leads BECoTTA-Best by 3.4 points (63.8 vs 60.4) and SAR-Best by 5.7 points at BS=256, with the gap widening at smaller batch sizes (e.g., 58.7 vs 54.1 at BS=1). On Digits-Five, AdMiT (86.8% avg) edges out CONTRAST (86.1%) and SESoM-PL (86.0%) by roughly 0.7–0.8 points, while Model soup lags significantly at 69.7%. In the CIFAR-100C forgetting evaluation, AdMiT (73.3% avg) surpasses CONTRAST (72.5%) and dramatically outperforms π-tuning-PL (64.5%), SESoM-PL (60.5%), and Model soup (57.2%), indicating superior retention of source knowledge during sequential adaptation. The zero-shot variant (AdMiT-ZeroShot, 60.2% at BS=256) already exceeds all single-source TTA baselines, confirming that the KME-based module integration alone provides substantial multi-source benefit without any fine-tuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

AdMiT sits at the intersection of parameter-efficient tuning (LoRA, adapters, VPT) and multi-source test-time adaptation. It departs from prior multi-source methods like SESoM (routing networks), π-tuning (hyperparameter optimization), and CONTRAST (gradient-based weight search) by replacing learned or tuned combination mechanisms with Kernel Mean Embedding (KME)-based distribution matching in a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space. The weighted module averaging draws on the model-soup idea, while the sharpness-aware pseudo-label minimization for fine-tuning follows the SAR/CoTTA line of work. Conceptually, the key shift is from 'learn how to combine modules' to 'match target and source distributions in kernel space to determine combination weights,' eliminating the need for any additional trainable components at deployment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a practical path for edge deployment of transformer models where privacy constraints prevent data sharing and computational budgets preclude routing networks or iterative hyperparameter search. The theoretical generalization bounds (Theorems 3.1–3.2) provide rare guarantees for multi-source PET adaptation, and the finding that selecting only a handful of modules from a large pool (e.g., 3 out of 75) retains most of the performance is practically significant for storage-constrained settings. Open questions include whether KME-based matching degrades when source and target distributions are highly dissimilar (outside the linear-combination assumption), how the approach scales to hundreds or thousands of modules, and whether the DenseNet201 feature extractor for KME computation can be replaced by lighter alternatives without accuracy loss.
