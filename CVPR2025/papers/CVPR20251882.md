## Problem

Existing guided depth map super-resolution methods primarily address resolution downsampling but neglect two critical degradations introduced by practical depth acquisition and transmission: bit-depth compression, which flattens subtle depth variations into uniform representations and destroys fine geometric details, and densely distributed random noise, which corrupts the global geometric structure of the scene. This paper tackles both challenges simultaneously by decoupling fine-detail recovery from global-geometry estimation.

## Input / Output

The network takes a high-resolution RGB image and a compressed low-quality depth map (degraded by simultaneous downsampling, bit-depth reduction, and additive random noise) as inputs and produces a high-resolution depth map. The depth decoder predicts the output using depth bin centers rather than direct regression, and the compressed depth map is consumed at its native low resolution before being upsampled through the global geometry encoder.

## Method

GDNet splits reconstruction into two parallel encoders. The Fine Geometry Detail Encoder (FGDE) applies a Detail-aware Content Preservation Module (DCPM) at multiple scales: self-attention enhances low-resolution context-level features, then cross-attention uses high-resolution low-level features as queries and the enhanced context features as keys/values to preserve fine textures while injecting complementary semantic information. The Global Geometry Encoder (GGE) performs low-rank feature reconstruction: an MLP generates candidate basis vectors, QR decomposition yields a full-rank basis, and a projection matrix (with Neumann-series approximation for the matrix inverse) maps depth features into a compact low-rank space that suppresses noise while retaining global structure. A depth decoder fuses the two feature streams via MLPs and predicts the final depth map through bin-center regression, trained end-to-end with a Scale-Invariant Log (SILog) loss.

## Results

On the AIM 2024 Compressed Depth Upsampling Challenge dataset (TartanAir-based), GDNet achieves a MAE of 0.212 and RMSE of 0.375, outperforming the next-best method DAS-Depth (MAE 0.294, RMSE 0.432) by 27.9% in MAE and 13.2% in RMSE. On the synthesized Compressed-NYU dataset, GDNet achieves MAE 0.0322 and RMSE 0.0739, surpassing the runner-up SGNet (MAE 0.0426, RMSE 0.1051) by 24.4% in MAE and 29.7% in RMSE. The method won 1st place in the ECCV 2024 AIM Compressed Depth Upsampling Challenge.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GDNet outperforms all seven baselines on both evaluation datasets. On the AIM 2024 Challenge dataset, the closest competitor is DAS-Depth (MAE 0.294, RMSE 0.432), which GDNet beats by 27.9% in MAE; DINOv2-ControlNet (MAE 0.498) and RGA Inc. (MAE 0.512) trail by over 57% in MAE. The monocular depth estimation methods SGNet (MAE 1.337) and Depth AnythingV2 (MAE 2.193) are substantially worse, with GDNet achieving 84–90% lower MAE. On Compressed-NYU, GDNet (MAE 0.0322, RMSE 0.0739) beats SGNet (MAE 0.0426, RMSE 0.1051) by 24.4% in MAE, and outperforms UniDepthV2 (MAE 0.1414) by 77%. The gap widens dramatically against foundation-model-based approaches: Depth AnythingV2-Large (MAE 0.5247) and AdaBins (MAE 0.4233) are 13–16× worse in MAE on Compressed-NYU, indicating that general-purpose monocular depth estimators struggle with the specific degradation patterns of compressed depth maps.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the guided depth map super-resolution (GDSR) literature, which has primarily addressed resolution downsampling, by explicitly incorporating bit-depth compression and random noise as first-class degradation factors. The core conceptual departure is a decoupling strategy that separates fine geometry detail recovery (sourced from RGB via cross-attention in FGDE) from global geometric structure extraction (sourced from compressed depth via low-rank projection in GGE), rather than treating the compressed depth map as a single monolithic input. The low-rank feature reconstruction using QR decomposition and Neumann-series approximation for matrix inversion is a somewhat unusual borrowing from numerical linear algebra into a neural network pipeline, distinguishing it from the attention- and CNN-based approaches that dominate prior GDSR work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical motivation is strong: consumer-grade depth sensors and bandwidth-constrained transmission pipelines routinely produce depth maps degraded by simultaneous bit-depth reduction, downsampling, and noise, yet most prior GDSR methods ignore the latter two. The decoupling framework offers a clean, modular design that could be adapted to other multi-degradation restoration tasks. However, the evaluation is limited to two datasets (one challenge-specific, one synthetic), and the Compressed-NYU baselines include monocular depth estimators not designed for guided super-resolution, which inflates the apparent margin. Open questions include whether the low-rank GGE generalizes to other noise distributions, whether the approach scales to outdoor/long-range depth, and how it performs under real-world sensor noise rather than synthetic Gaussian noise.
