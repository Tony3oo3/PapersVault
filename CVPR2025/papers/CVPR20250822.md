## Problem

Existing omnimatte methods decompose video into object layers with associated effects (shadows, reflections) but rely on restrictive assumptions such as static backgrounds and accurate camera pose/depth estimation, and they lack a generative prior to complete occluded or dynamic regions. This paper asks whether a pretrained video diffusion model can be repurposed to perform object-and-effect removal without these assumptions, enabling clean layer decomposition on casually captured video.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250822/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB video (80 frames at 128px base resolution, e.g. 224×128), N binary object masks obtained via SAM 2, and a text prompt describing the scene. It produces N+1 layers: one clean-plate RGB background video and N RGBA omnimatte layers, each containing a fully-visible object plus its associated effects. The binary masks are converted into a three-valued trimask (remove=0, preserve=1, uncertain background=0.5) that guides the removal model; the diffusion model outputs RGB only, and alpha channels are recovered via a separate test-time optimization step. Outputs are upsampled from 128px to 640×384 using Lumiere's spatial super-resolution stage.

## Method

The core contribution is 'Casper,' a video object-effect-removal model finetuned from the Lumiere inpainting diffusion model using a small curated dataset (~1,600 video tuples from four categories: omnimatte results, tripod-captured video, Kubric synthetic multi-object scenes, and object-paste composites). Instead of a binary inpainting mask, Casper is conditioned on a three-valued trimask that explicitly marks regions to remove, preserve, and leave uncertain (for effects like shadows), and it retains RGB values in removal regions (following ObjectDrop) so the model can associate masked content with external effects. The framework operates in two stages: (1) Casper generates a clean-plate background and N solo single-object videos using different trimask configurations with shared noise initialization, and (2) a test-time optimization reconstructs each RGBA omnimatte layer from its (solo video, background) pair by jointly optimizing foreground RGB and a U-Net-predicted alpha under reconstruction, L0/L1 sparsity, and mask-supervision losses. The paper demonstrates via attention-map analysis that the pretrained video diffusion model already encodes object-effect spatial associations, explaining why a small finetuning dataset suffices.

## Results

On the 10-scene synthetic benchmark (5 Movie + 5 Kubric) following the OmnimatteRF protocol, the proposed method achieves the best average PSNR of 38.38 dB and LPIPS of 0.020, outperforming the strongest prior omnimatte method (OmnimatteRF: 37.38 dB / 0.023). The advantage is most pronounced on Kubric multi-object scenes, where the method reaches 44.07 dB PSNR and 0.010 LPIPS versus OmnimatteRF's 40.91 dB and 0.028. On the more challenging Movie scenes with real-world parallax, OmnimatteRF retains a slight edge (33.86 vs. 32.69 dB PSNR), while Omnimatte3D fails entirely on all evaluation scenes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the closest omnimatte competitor, OmnimatteRF [29], the proposed method wins on average (38.38 vs. 37.38 dB PSNR; 0.020 vs. 0.023 LPIPS) and by a large margin on Kubric multi-object scenes (44.07 vs. 40.91 dB PSNR; 0.010 vs. 0.028 LPIPS), but trails slightly on Movie scenes with real parallax (32.69 vs. 33.86 dB PSNR). Omnimatte3D [47] fails to produce valid outputs on all evaluation scenes, and the original Omnimatte [36] and LNA [23] are substantially weaker (24.29 and 23.10 dB PSNR respectively), confirming that 2D motion models struggle with parallax. Among inpainting-based baselines, ProPainter [66] (31.06 dB) and ObjectDrop [53] (31.14 dB) both lag the proposed method by roughly 7 dB in average PSNR, and Lumiere inpainting [7] (29.04 dB) is the weakest of the three, highlighting that standard inpainting cannot remove object-associated effects outside the mask boundary.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the geometric/motion-based lineage of omnimatte methods (Omnimatte, Omnimatte3D, OmnimatteRF) that rely on static-background assumptions, planar homographies, or 3D scene representations to associate objects with their effects. Instead, it repurposes a pretrained video diffusion model (Lumiere's inpainting variant) as a generative prior that already encodes object-effect associations, requiring only a small finetuning dataset and a novel trimask conditioning to perform object-and-effect removal. The approach also builds on ObjectDrop's strategy of preserving RGB values in removal regions to enable cross-region effect association, and adopts temporal MultiDiffusion for handling longer videos.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By replacing restrictive geometric priors with a generative video prior, this work removes the static-background and accurate-pose assumptions that limited all prior omnimatte methods, and uniquely enables completion of heavily occluded and dynamic regions. The practical impact is significant for video editing and VFX workflows, unlocking layer-based retiming, stylization, and object manipulation on casually captured footage. Key limitations include inability to handle physical deformations (bending poles, trampolines), difficulty disambiguating effects among very similar multi-object scenes, and high inference cost (~12 min for 80 frames at 128px base resolution). Open questions include whether instance-level supervision can resolve multi-object effect ambiguity and whether the approach scales to longer, higher-resolution videos.
