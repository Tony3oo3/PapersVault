## Problem

Standard 3D Gaussian Splatting models require millions of Gaussian primitives, consuming significant GPU memory that limits deployment on resource-constrained devices. Existing compression methods (e.g., pruning) require separate fine-tuning for each target compression ratio, making them impractical for adapting a single model to the diverse hardware constraints of different deployment environments (e.g., PC-connected vs. standalone VR headsets, various phone models). No prior method enables a single trained 3DGS model to elastically render at arbitrary compression ratios without retraining.

## Input / Output

The method takes a fully trained 3DGS model (all Gaussians with position, scaling, rotation, opacity, and SH color attributes), a continuous elastic ratio e (e.g., 0.01–0.15), and standard camera intrinsics/extrinsics. At inference, it produces a rendered novel-view RGB image using only ⌊eN⌋ Gaussians whose positions, scales, and rotations have been adaptively transformed. Notably, the elastic ratio is a free continuous input at test time, so the model generalizes to ratios (e.g., 2%, 4%, 6%, 8%) that were never explicitly seen during training.

## Method

FlexGS introduces two jointly trained modules for elastic 3DGS inference. First, an Adaptive Gaussian Selector uses a lightweight GsNet (two-layer MLPs on concatenated Gaussian attributes and the ratio) to produce logits, which are relaxed via Gumbel-Softmax into differentiable binary masks; a Global Importance (GI) score—combining volumetric extent, pixel-coverage frequency, opacity, and transmittance—provides a guidance mask that regularizes selection via L_GI, while a sparsity loss enforces the exact ratio constraint. Second, a Gaussian Transform Field queries a 4D Spatial-Ratio Neural Field (a multi-resolution volume over (x,y,z,e) factorized into 6 planes, following HexPlane-style decomposition) to retrieve ratio-dependent features, which three MLP heads map to displacement vectors for position, scaling, and rotation of the selected Gaussians. Training proceeds in two stages: 15k iterations of standard 3DGS optimization, followed by 20k iterations where the ratio is randomly sampled from {0.01, 0.05, 0.10, 0.15} each step, and the total loss combines rendering losses on both the selected subset and the full set against ground truth, plus the GI and sparsity regularizers.

## Results

On Mip-NeRF360, FlexGS achieves PSNR 22.730 / SSIM 0.6128 / LPIPS 0.4384 at the extreme 1% elastic ratio, outperforming LightGaussian* (14.455 / 0.3741 / 0.5780) by over 8 dB in PSNR and C3DGS (21.766 / 0.5440 / 0.5101) by ~1 dB. On Tanks & Temples at 1%, FlexGS reaches 23.090 PSNR, surpassing LightGaussian (22.795) and C3DGS (21.535). On ZipNeRF at 1%, FlexGS attains 20.963 PSNR versus LightGaussian (19.812) and C3DGS (19.955). Critically, all these results come from a single training run, whereas baselines require separate fine-tuning per ratio.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FlexGS is compared against three 3DGS compression baselines: LightGaussian (with and without post-pruning fine-tuning, denoted LightGS*), C3DGS, and EAGLES. FlexGS decisively outperforms LightGS* (the no-fine-tuning variant) across all datasets and ratios, with PSNR gains of 7–8 dB at 1% compression (e.g., 22.730 vs. 14.455 on Mip-NeRF360), highlighting the cost of skipping the fine-tuning stage. Against LightGaussian with fine-tuning, FlexGS wins at 1% and 5% on Mip-NeRF360 (22.730 vs. 22.265; 25.412 vs. 25.223 PSNR) and at all ratios on T&T and ZipNeRF, but LightGaussian narrowly edges FlexGS at 15% on Mip-NeRF360 (27.239 vs. 27.035 PSNR). Against C3DGS, FlexGS leads at every ratio on all three datasets, with the largest margins at 1% (e.g., +1.555 PSNR on T&T, +1.008 on ZipNeRF). EAGLES is consistently the weakest baseline, trailing FlexGS by 1–3 dB in PSNR across all settings. The key practical advantage is that FlexGS achieves these results from a single training session, while LightGaussian and C3DGS each require a separate ~16.7%-cost fine-tuning run per target ratio.

## Relation to Prior Work

FlexGS extends 3D Gaussian Splatting (Kerbl et al., 2023) by introducing an elastic inference framework that departs fundamentally from prior 3DGS compression methods (LightGaussian, C3DGS, EAGLES), which each require separate post-hoc pruning and fine-tuning per target ratio. Conceptually, it adapts the slimmable/elastic network paradigm (OFA, Flextron, Matryoshka) to the 3D Gaussian domain, using Gumbel-Softmax for differentiable discrete Gaussian selection and a Spatial-Ratio Neural Field (inspired by HexPlane and 4DGS) to predict attribute transformations. The Global Importance metric borrows from LightGaussian's opacity-and-scale heuristics but repurposes them as a training-time guidance signal rather than a fixed scoring rule.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 'train once, deploy at any ratio' paradigm directly addresses a practical bottleneck for 3DGS deployment across heterogeneous hardware (VR headsets, phones, PCs), eliminating the need for per-device retraining. However, all experiments are on relatively small scenes (MipNeRF360, ZipNeRF, T&T); scalability to city-scale scenes with tens of millions of Gaussians—where the memory pressure is most acute—remains untested. Open questions include whether the approach extends to dynamic scenes, whether the Transform Field's continuous interpolation degrades gracefully at extreme ratios (e.g., below 1%), and whether the 0.13s inference overhead is acceptable on actual edge GPUs rather than A100s.
