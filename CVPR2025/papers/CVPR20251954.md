## Problem

Automated segmentation of maxillofacial structures in CBCT volumes is critical for surgical planning and implant placement, yet existing datasets are limited in anatomical scope, often lack 3D voxel-level annotations, are privately stored, or rely on very small training sets (<50 scans) that limit generalizability. No publicly available dataset covers the full range of maxillofacial anatomy (jawbones, canals, sinuses, teeth, implants, etc.) with dense 3D labels, creating a bottleneck for developing and fairly benchmarking segmentation algorithms.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251954/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The input is a 3D CBCT volume at 0.3 mm isotropic voxel resolution (training scans from NewTom/NTVGiMK4 at 3 mA, 110 kV; test scans from i-CAT 3D resampled from 0.4 mm to 0.3 mm). The output is a voxel-level 3D segmentation mask with 42 distinct classes extending the FDI dental notation, covering upper/lower jawbones, left/right inferior alveolar canals, left/right maxillary sinuses, pharynx, all upper and lower teeth (including wisdom teeth), bridges, crowns, and dental implants. Volumes and labels are distributed in .mha format following the nnU-Net dataset structure, with 480 training and 50 held-out test scans.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is the ToothFairy2 dataset, annotated by 7 maxillofacial experts using a semi-automated protocol: five nnU-Net-based base models provide initial segmentations that clinicians refine on 2D slices across axial, sagittal, and coronal planes, with iterative model retraining as more labeled volumes accumulate. A concave α-shape post-processing step smooths jagged 2D-slice annotations into clean 3D polygonal meshes before voxelization. For benchmarking, the authors evaluate CNN (nnU-Net, nnU-Net ResEnc), Transformer (TransU-Net, nnFormer, UNETR++), and Mamba-based (UMamba, VMamba, Swin-UMamba) architectures under a customized nnU-Net plan that disables left/right mirroring (which degrades performance due to pronounced maxillofacial symmetry), adds an extra encoder layer for larger receptive fields, and applies per-class connected-component filtering as post-processing. UMamba achieves the best overall DSC (85.05%), while 2D slice-based models (VMamba, Swin-UMamba) surprisingly outperform 3D models on HD95 due to stronger local discriminative ability despite lacking full volumetric context.

## Results

On the ToothFairy2 test set (50 CBCT volumes, 42 classes), UMamba achieves the best average DSC of 85.05% (HD95 5.28 mm) under the default nnU-Net plan, while Swin-UMamba attains the lowest HD95 of 2.94 mm with a DSC of 79.64%. Jawbones and pharynx are the easiest structures to segment (DSC > 90% for most models), whereas artificial structures (implants, crowns, bridges) are the hardest, with DSC dropping to 14–44% depending on the model. With the authors' customized plan (disabling left/right mirroring, deeper bottleneck, connected-component post-processing), UMamba improves to 85.39% DSC and nnU-Net ResEnc reaches 84.99% DSC with HD95 of 8.57 mm.

## Baselines & Comparisons

UMamba clearly leads on average DSC (85.05%), outperforming the next-best model Swin-UMamba (79.64%) by over 5 points and the best CNN (nnU-Net ResEnc, 74.16%) by more than 10 points. However, Swin-UMamba achieves the best HD95 (2.94 mm), nearly 2× lower than UMamba's 5.28 mm, indicating tighter boundary precision despite lower overlap. Among transformers, nnFormer (76.79% DSC, 5.45 mm HD95) substantially outperforms TransU-Net (70.32%, 20.17 mm) and UNETR++ (71.43%, 17.23 mm), particularly on HD95 where it is competitive with Mamba models. Surprisingly, the 2D slice-by-slice models (VMamba, Swin-UMamba) achieve lower HD95 than most intrinsically 3D architectures, attributed to their stronger local discriminative ability, though they produce noisier, more fragmented predictions. On the hardest sub-task (artificial structures: implants, crowns, bridges), UMamba again leads at 43.89% DSC, while all other models fall below 37%, confirming that underrepresented classes remain a significant challenge regardless of architecture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the fragmented landscape of CBCT segmentation datasets—where prior work addressed either the inferior alveolar canal (Cipriano et al., Bolelli et al.) or teeth (Cui et al.) in isolation—into a unified 42-class maxillofacial segmentation benchmark. Methodologically, it builds directly on the nnU-Net self-configuring framework and benchmarks it against transformer (TransU-Net, UNETR++, nnFormer) and state-space model (UMamba, VMamba, Swin-UMamba) architectures, while introducing domain-specific adaptations (disabling L/R mirroring, deeper bottleneck, connected-component post-processing) and an α-shape-based annotation refinement pipeline. The dataset construction leverages semi-automated pre-annotation from five nnU-Net base models trained on previously public IAC and tooth datasets, iteratively refined by 7 maxillofacial experts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ToothFairy2 fills a critical gap by providing the largest publicly available 3D-annotated CBCT dataset covering the full maxillofacial anatomy, enabling reproducible benchmarking that was previously impossible due to private or structure-limited data. Key open questions remain: training data comes from a single acquisition center (potentially limiting cross-site generalizability), artificial structures (implants, crowns, bridges) are underrepresented, and common clinical objects like braces and bone plates are entirely absent. The finding that standard L/R mirroring augmentation degrades performance in this symmetric anatomical context, and that 2D slice-by-slice models can rival or exceed 3D models on local discriminative tasks, offers practical guidance for future maxillofacial segmentation pipelines.
