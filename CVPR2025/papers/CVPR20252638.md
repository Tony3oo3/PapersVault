## Problem

Recent fMRI vision decoding methods trained on the Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD) have only been evaluated on seen image reconstruction, leaving it unclear whether they generalize to mental imagery—a critical requirement for real-world applications in brain-computer interfaces and clinical communication. Mental images are encoded in brain activity with substantially lower signal-to-noise ratios and spatial resolution than seen images, and no prior work has demonstrated reconstruction of complex natural scenes from mental imagery.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252638/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes 7T fMRI brain activity patterns recorded during cued mental imagery tasks (participants imagine a memorized stimulus after seeing a single-letter cue for 3 seconds) and produces reconstructed images via diffusion-based generative models. The dataset contains 2,304 imagery test trials across 8 subjects spanning three stimulus complexity levels: simple geometric shapes, complex natural scenes, and conceptual single-word prompts. Reconstructions are sampled from the posterior distribution (10 samples per method per stimulus), and evaluation combines automated feature metrics (pixel correlation, SSIM, deep-network 2-way comparisons, brain correlation) with human identification experiments involving 500 raters.

## Method

The paper introduces NSD-Imagery, a benchmark dataset extending NSD with 7T fMRI responses from the same 8 participants performing a mental imagery task across three stimulus types (simple shapes, complex natural scenes, and conceptual words), enabling cross-decoding evaluation of vision-trained models on internally generated representations without any domain-specific retraining. Five state-of-the-art NSD-trained decoding methods (MindEye1, MindEye2, Brain Diffuser, iCNN, Takagi et al.) are benchmarked directly on the imagery trials. A key empirical finding is that vision and mental imagery decoding performance are largely decoupled across methods: MindEye2, the SOTA vision decoder using high-dimensional ViT-bigG embeddings to drive SDXL, generalizes poorly to imagery, while Brain Diffuser with its simpler ridge regression backbone and multimodal image-text features performs best. Human rating experiments further show that reconstruction quality for complex imagined stimuli can match or exceed that for out-of-distribution seen stimuli, suggesting training distribution alignment rather than stimulus complexity is the primary limiting factor for mental image reconstruction.

## Results

On the NSD-Imagery mental imagery benchmark, Brain Diffuser achieves the highest overall human identification accuracy at 73.95%, closely followed by MindEye1 at 73.00%, while the current SOTA vision decoder MindEye2 drops to 56.96% (near the 50% chance level). On complex stimuli specifically, Brain Diffuser reaches 82.70% and MindEye1 reaches 82.28%, compared to 64.83% for MindEye2. Quantitative feature metrics (Table 1) confirm the same pattern: MindEye1 leads on pixel correlation (0.086) and AlexNet-2 2WC (59.56%), while Brain Diffuser leads on SSIM (0.401) and Inception 2WC (52.73%), and all methods show a substantial drop from vision trials to imagery trials (e.g., MindEye1 brain correlation falls from 0.311 to 0.155).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates five NSD-trained fMRI-to-image decoders on the NSD-Imagery benchmark. Brain Diffuser [39] and MindEye1 [49] are the clear winners on mental imagery, achieving 73.95% and 73.00% human identification accuracy respectively, with Brain Diffuser also leading on conceptual stimuli (71.01% vs. 65.68%). MindEye2 [50], despite being the SOTA vision decoder (83.05% on vision trials), generalizes extremely poorly to mental imagery at 56.96%, barely above chance, suggesting its high-dimensional ViT-bigG embeddings break down under the lower SNR of imagery. iCNN [54] occupies the middle ground at 66.15% on imagery, while Takagi et al. [62,63] performs at chance level (52.89%) on both vision and imagery, and is excluded from further analyses. The critical finding is that vision performance does not predict imagery performance: MindEye2 ranks 2nd on vision (83.05%) but 4th on imagery (56.96%), while Brain Diffuser ranks 3rd on vision (80.13%) but 1st on imagery (73.95%), indicating that simpler architectures with multimodal features generalize better to mental imagery.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD) ecosystem by adding a mental imagery benchmark, building directly on the cross-decoding paradigm established by earlier mental imagery reconstruction studies (Thirion et al. 2019, Koide-Majima et al. 2024, Goebel et al. 2022) that used smaller, purpose-built datasets. Rather than proposing a new decoding architecture, it repurposes five existing NSD-trained vision decoding methods (MindEye1, MindEye2, Brain Diffuser, iCNN, Takagi et al.) as test subjects to probe whether improvements in seen-image reconstruction transfer to internally generated imagery. The conceptual lineage traces from the shared-representation evidence in visual cortex (Breedlove et al. 2020, Naselaris et al. 2015) through the recent diffusion-model-based reconstruction wave (Scotti et al. 2023, Ozcelik & VanRullen 2023) to this paper's empirical question of generalization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that vision decoding performance does not predict mental imagery decoding performance, and that the current SOTA vision method (MindEye2) generalizes near-chance while older, architecturally simpler methods (Brain Diffuser, MindEye1) perform well—challenges the field's implicit assumption that scaling vision decoders will automatically improve mental imagery applications. This has direct implications for brain-computer interface and clinical communication research, where the target signal is always internally generated. Key limitations include the small stimulus set (18 items), which precludes training on imagery data and forces the cross-decoding approach, and the narrow method pool (5 models), leaving the optimal architecture question open. The paper establishes that mental imagery benchmarks are essential for evaluating practical viability, not just vision reconstruction quality.
