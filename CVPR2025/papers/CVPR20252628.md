## Problem

In Composed Image Retrieval, the reference image and modified instruction often contain contradictory attributes (e.g., blue long-sleeve vs. grey short-sleeve), creating compositional conflicts that misrepresent user intent. Existing approaches mitigate these conflicts through coarse-grained learnable masks in feature space, which lack explicit awareness of which specific attributes are in conflict and produce uncontrollable results due to the complexity of high-dimensional feature spaces.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252628/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a reference RGB image and a free-form text instruction describing modifications (e.g., 'make it grey and short-sleeved') as input, and produces a ranked list of target images from a corpus. Internally, the reference image is first converted to a textual caption via BLIP-2, and GPT-4 is invoked twice: once to identify conflicting attributes and once to generate a 'kept instruction' retaining only non-conflicting attributes. The final query representation is a single vector obtained by adaptively fusing dual-instruction-guided visual features with text features, which is matched against pre-extracted target image embeddings via cosine similarity.

## Method

CCIN comprises two sequential modules. The Compositional Conflict Identification (CCI) module concatenates the BLIP-2 caption of the reference image with the modified instruction using a 'However' conjunction, then prompts GPT-4 with a custom template to output the specific conflicting attributes. The Compositional Conflict Neutralization (CCN) module first uses GPT-4 to strip those conflicting attributes from the caption, yielding a kept instruction; it then feeds both the kept instruction and the modified instruction in parallel into a dual-instruction Q-Former (extending InstructBLIP's single-instruction design) to extract two sets of instruction-aware visual tokens from the reference image. An adaptive MLP-Sigmoid fusion module combines the kept and modified visual tokens, then a second fusion step blends the result with text tokens to form the final query vector. Training employs a composite objective of image-text contrastive loss, a weighted regularization triplet loss, and a novel orthogonal projection regularization loss that enforces divergence between conflict information and target representations, with LoRA fine-tuning applied to the 38th and 39th ViT layers.

## Results

On FashionIQ, CCIN achieves a mean recall of 64.59 (R@10: 53.86, R@50: 74.75), outperforming the previous best SPRC (64.27) and the two-stage Re-ranking method (62.15) by 0.32 and 2.44 points respectively. On CIRR, CCIN attains a mean recall of 81.66 (R@1: 53.41, R@5: 84.05, R@10: 91.17, R@50: 98.00), surpassing SPRC (80.37) by 1.29 points. On Shoes, CCIN reaches a mean recall of 59.42 (R@1: 25.95, R@10: 65.76, R@50: 86.54), exceeding the prior best TG-CIR (58.05) by 1.37 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CCIN outperforms all 22 compared baselines across all three datasets. On FashionIQ, the closest competitor is SPRC [2] (64.27 mean), which CCIN surpasses by 0.32 points; the gap widens to 2.44 points over the two-stage Re-ranking method [29] (62.15) and 4.51 points over CaLa [19] (60.08). On CIRR, CCIN leads SPRC [2] by 1.29 points (81.66 vs. 80.37) and Re-ranking [29] by 1.84 points (81.66 vs. 79.82), while traditional methods like TIRG [39] and ARTEMIS [12] trail by over 27 points. On Shoes, CCIN (59.42) beats TG-CIR [45] (58.05) by 1.37 points and CAFF [41] (55.39) by 4.03 points. Notably, CCIN also surpasses TG-CIR [45] and SSN [51]—the two methods that explicitly address compositional conflicts via learnable masks—by 6.54 and 13.19 points on CIRR respectively, demonstrating that LLM-based conflict identification and dual-instruction neutralization is more effective than feature-space mask suppression.

## Relation to Prior Work

CCIN extends the InstructBLIP dual-instruction Q-Former paradigm from a single-instruction to a dual-instruction setting, where one instruction preserves non-conflicting visual attributes and the other encodes the user's modifications. It departs from prior conflict-handling approaches like TG-CIR and SSN, which rely on learnable masks for coarse-grained feature suppression, by replacing implicit mask prediction with explicit LLM-based (GPT-4) identification of specific conflicting attributes. The adaptive fusion module is adopted from CLIP4CIR, and the overall pipeline positions CCIN as a more interpretable and controllable alternative to feature-space manipulation for resolving multi-modal discrepancies in composed image retrieval.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean conceptual framing of compositional conflicts in CIR and demonstrates that explicit, attribute-level conflict identification via LLMs outperforms implicit mask-based suppression, offering a more interpretable and controllable retrieval pipeline. However, the practical impact is tempered by the heavy reliance on GPT-4 for both conflict identification and kept-instruction generation, raising questions about cost, latency, and reproducibility in deployment settings. The authors themselves note that multimodal LLMs (e.g., GPT-4o) and additional input modalities (e.g., sketches) are natural next steps, suggesting the current pipeline is a stepping stone rather than a final solution. Open questions include whether the LLM-based identification step can be distilled into a lightweight model and whether the dual-instruction Q-Former generalizes beyond the three evaluated datasets.
