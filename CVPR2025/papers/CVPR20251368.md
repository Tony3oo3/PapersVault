## Problem

Existing basis BRDF inverse rendering methods produce non-interpretable, spatially entangled basis BRDFs where each scene point blends many basis functions with high weights, and they fix the number of basis BRDFs regardless of scene complexity. This makes the reconstructed reflectance impractical for downstream tasks like material editing and selective relighting.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251368/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes co-located multi-view flash photography (point-light illumination from multiple camera positions) as input, with 200 training and 100 test views for synthetic scenes and 200/40 for real-world capture. It outputs a scene representation of 2D Gaussians (position, orientation, scale, opacity) paired with a dynamically-determined set of Disney BRDF basis functions and per-Gaussian sparse blending weights, from which surface normals, novel-view relit images, and extractable 3D meshes can be derived.

## Method

The method represents geometry as 2D Gaussian surfels whose reflectance is a weighted blend of N Disney BRDF basis functions, rendered via differentiable rasterization with alpha blending under a point-light model. A specular-weighted photometric loss (using a rendered half-way angle map to up-weight specular regions) stabilizes optimization of the inherently ill-posed inverse problem. To achieve interpretability, a low-temperature softmax and entropy-based sparsity regularizer force each Gaussian to depend on only a few basis BRDFs, while a dynamic control mechanism periodically merges radiometrically and geometrically similar basis BRDFs (via L2 radiometric difference and Chamfer distance on associated point clouds) and removes underutilized ones, so the final basis count adapts to scene complexity. The full pipeline runs in two phases: a warm-start optimization of geometry and fixed-count basis BRDFs, followed by a refinement phase that activates sparsity loss and merge/removal scheduling every 500 iterations.

## Results

On the synthetic multi-object flash-photography dataset, the proposed method achieves a mean angular error (MAE) of 9.81° for normal reconstruction, substantially outperforming IRON (15.71°), DPIR (17.04°), and GS³ (15.28°). Training time is also the fastest at 0.5 hours, compared to 12 h for IRON, 2 h for DPIR, and 1 h for GS³. Qualitatively, the method produces interpretable, spatially-separated basis BRDFs with adaptive counts, whereas all baselines yield non-interpretable, entangled basis BRDFs with a fixed number.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method wins on both accuracy and speed across all three baselines. Against IRON [45], which uses neural SDFs for geometry, the method reduces MAE from 15.71° to 9.81° (≈37% improvement) while cutting training time from 12 h to 0.5 h (24× faster); IRON's SDF-based normals are smoother and lose fine geometric detail. Against DPIR [8] (the authors' prior isotropic-point method), MAE drops from 17.04° to 9.81° (≈42% improvement) with 4× faster training, attributed to the switch from isotropic points to 2D Gaussians and the specular-weighted rendering loss. Against GS³ [2], which uses anisotropic 3D Gaussian splatting, MAE improves from 15.28° to 9.81° (≈36%) with 2× faster training; GS³'s neural-network-based relighting can mask geometric inaccuracies, but the proposed method recovers more accurate normals and, critically, interpretable basis BRDFs that GS³ and DPIR cannot provide. In qualitative comparisons (Figures 6–7), GS³ and DPIR produce spatially entangled, non-interpretable basis BRDFs, while the proposed method yields cleanly separated weight maps and physically meaningful BRDF lobes, enabling downstream tasks like selective material editing and object extraction that the baselines do not support.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the authors' own DPIR (CVPR 2024) by replacing isotropic points with 2D Gaussians (from Huang et al., SIGGRAPH 2024) for more accurate geometry, and introduces a basis BRDF control mechanism (merge and removal) inspired by Zhou et al. 2016's sparse SVBRDF acquisition, but embedded within a modern differentiable rasterization pipeline. It departs from concurrent methods like GS3 and IRON, which use fixed numbers of basis BRDFs and produce spatially entangled, non-interpretable results, by dynamically adjusting the basis count and enforcing sparsity via entropy regularization and low-temperature softmax. The specular-weighted rendering loss is a targeted addition to address the observation imbalance inherent in flash photography.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical contribution is that interpretable, spatially-separated basis BRDFs enable downstream tasks—material editing, selective object extraction, and environment relighting—that were impractical with the non-interpretable basis BRDFs produced by prior methods. The dynamic basis count also addresses a scalability concern: fixed basis counts either over-parameterize simple scenes or under-parameterize complex ones. Limitations include the assumption of opaque objects (no transparency or subsurface scattering) and evaluation on only four synthetic scenes plus one real scene, leaving open whether the interpretability guarantees hold for highly complex or multi-material scenes. The authors note that combining semantic information from large vision models is a promising direction for further improving basis BRDF interpretability.
