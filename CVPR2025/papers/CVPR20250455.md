## Problem

Existing diffusion-based adversarial purification methods apply uniform noise intensity across all pixels during the forward process, creating an irreconcilable tradeoff: sufficient noise to eliminate adversarial perturbations destroys semantic information, while mild noise preserves semantics but leaves perturbations intact. This paper identifies that the uniform operation is fundamentally flawed and proposes a spatially heterogeneous alternative guided by where the classifier actually attends.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250455/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an adversarial RGB image and the attention maps extracted from a single forward pass of the target classifier (e.g., WideResNet-28-10 or ResNet-50). The attention maps from each block are aggregated via a threshold τ to produce a binary spatial mask that partitions the image into high-attention and low-attention regions. The output is a purified image at the original resolution that the classifier can correctly identify, with the purification performed entirely at test time without any retraining of the classifier.

## Method

The core mechanism is a heterogeneous forward process that applies two different noise levels (t_l > t_s) to different spatial regions: high-intensity noise to pixels inside the attention mask (where the classifier focuses) and low-intensity noise outside it, combined via Hadamard product. The reverse process is a two-stage denoising: Stage 1 (t_s < t < t_l) formulates the problem as inpainting, where mask-interior pixels are predicted by the diffusion model while mask-exterior pixels are analytically computed via the forward process; Stage 2 (t < t_s) reverts to standard denoising since noise is now uniform. To enable evaluation against strong adaptive attacks, the multi-step resampling (U=20 iterations) from Repaint is replaced with a single DDIM-based step, reducing GPU memory to 2×T computation graphs and cutting time cost by ~90%, making full-gradient adaptive attacks feasible on 24 GB consumer GPUs.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with WideResNet-28-10 under ℓ∞ (ε=8/255), the method achieves 93.16% standard accuracy, 59.38% robust accuracy under PGD+EOT, and 80.45% under AutoAttack, outperforming all adversarial purification baselines. With WideResNet-70-16, it reaches 93.36% standard and 84.83% AutoAttack robust accuracy. On ImageNet (ResNet-50, ℓ∞), it achieves 74.51% standard and 60.06% robust accuracy, surpassing the best prior purification method (Geigh et al.) by 3.52% in robust accuracy. Under ℓ2 (ε=0.5) on CIFAR-10, the method attains 90.25% AutoAttack robust accuracy with WRN-28-10, leading all baselines by over 3.77%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against all adversarial purification (AP) baselines, the proposed method achieves the best standard accuracy (93.16%) and AutoAttack robust accuracy (80.45%) on CIFAR-10 WRN-28-10, outperforming the strongest AP competitors: Bai et al. [3] (91.41% / 77.08%), Lin et al. [24] (90.62% / 72.85%), and Lee et al. [20] (90.16% / 70.47%). Under PGD+EOT, the method leads Nie et al. [30] (DiffPure) by 2.54% (59.38% vs. 56.84%) and Lee et al. [20] by 3.56% (59.38% vs. 55.82%). However, adversarial training (AT) methods still surpass the purification approach on PGD robust accuracy (e.g., Gowal et al. [11] achieves 66.01% vs. 59.38%), reflecting the inherent advantage of training-specific defenses over test-time purification. On ImageNet, the method leads Geigh et al. [53] by 3.52% in robust accuracy (60.06% vs. 56.54%) and Song et al. [40] by 8.92% (60.06% vs. 51.14%), while also achieving the highest standard accuracy (74.51%). Under the ℓ2 threat model, the method dominates all AP baselines by 3.77% in AutoAttack robust accuracy (90.25% vs. 86.48% for Lee et al. [20]) and 9.33% over Nie et al. [30] (90.25% vs. 81.7%). Under BPDA+EOT, the method trails Song et al. [40] by 1.71% in robust accuracy (86.72% vs. 88.43%) but leads in standard accuracy by 3.16% (93.04% vs. 89.88%), yielding a net average advantage of 0.73%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends DiffPure (Nie et al., ICML 2022), which established the diffusion-based adversarial purification paradigm of adding uniform noise in a forward process and recovering clean images via reverse denoising. The key departure is replacing the uniform noise with heterogeneous, attention-guided noise: high-intensity noise is applied to regions the classifier attends to (where adversarial perturbations most influence predictions), while low-intensity noise preserves semantic information elsewhere. The two-stage denoising process adapts Repaint's (Lugmayr et al., CVPR 2022) inpainting formulation to handle the mixed-noise image, and the single-step resampling via DDIM (Song et al., ICLR 2021) replaces Repaint's multi-step resampling for a 90% reduction in compute. The overall framework remains rooted in the DDPM (Ho et al., NeurIPS 2020) forward/reverse diffusion structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a fundamental tension in diffusion-based purification—uniform noise intensity cannot simultaneously eliminate adversarial perturbations and preserve semantic information—and resolves it with a principled, interpretability-driven solution. The single-step resampling is a practical efficiency win that makes adaptive-attack evaluation feasible on consumer-grade GPUs (24 GB), lowering the barrier for rigorous robustness testing. However, the method requires white-box access to the classifier's internal activations to construct the attention mask, limiting applicability to black-box or API-only settings. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to transformer-based classifiers (where 'attention maps' have a different structure), whether the threshold τ can be learned rather than hand-tuned, and how the method performs under distribution shift or multi-modal adversarial attacks.
