## Problem

Self-supervised low-light RAW image denoising relies on sensor-specific noise synthesis, but existing pipelines require labor-intensive system gain calibration (days of controlled hardware setup) and signal-independent noise profiling (statistical fitting or training intermediate networks), preventing practical deployment at scale. This paper asks whether these preparation steps can be eliminated while retaining competitive denoising accuracy.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250188/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

During training, the pipeline takes clean RAW images and dark frames (captured automatically from the target sensor) to synthesize noisy-clean pairs at 512×512 patch resolution. At inference, a noisy RAW image has its dark shading subtracted before being fed to the U-Net, which outputs a denoised RAW image in the same Bayer-domain format. The only required preparation is collecting dark frames per analog gain setting, a fully automatic process finishable within one hour.

## Method

The core contribution is a hypothesizing-based shot noise synthesis that bypasses system gain calibration: since K = QE × AG and QE is physically constrained to 30–70%, the authors hypothesize a fixed QE (e.g., 50%) to compute K, then generate Poisson shot noise by sampling from P(I/K) and scaling by K. For signal-independent noise, they directly sample from captured dark frames rather than fitting parametric distributions or training proxy networks, and they omit high-bit-depth noise recovery (HBNR), arguing that low-bit quantization in dark frames realistically encodes quantization noise present at inference. Dark shading is computed by averaging dark frames and subtracted from both training and inference inputs. The denoising backbone is a standard U-Net trained with L1 loss for 1000 epochs, and the entire preparation reduces to dark frame collection, cutting setup from days to under one hour while achieving up to 0.54 dB PSNR improvement over the prior state-of-the-art self-supervised method PNNP.

## Results

On the SID and ELD Sony-A7S2 datasets, the proposed pipeline achieves average PSNR/SSIM of 40.85/0.9478 (SID) and 46.43/0.9834 (ELD), matching or slightly exceeding the concurrent self-supervised method PNNP (40.83/0.9479, 46.39/0.9834) while outperforming all other self-supervised baselines by 1–4 dB. On the LRID smartphone dataset, the method reaches 45.08/0.9779 overall PSNR/SSIM, a 0.54 dB PSNR improvement over PNNP (44.54/0.9757) and even surpassing the fully supervised PMN (44.97/0.9761). Ablation studies confirm that the denoising network is robust to system gain K variations (PSNR differences < 0.1 dB across calibrated vs. hypothesized K) and to dark shading accuracy (only 0.1 dB drop with just 10 dark frames at inference).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method is compared against 8 baselines spanning supervised (Paired, PMN), hybrid (LED, LLD), and self-supervised (P-G, ELD, SFRN, NoiseFlow, StarLight, PNNP) categories. Among self-supervised methods, the proposed pipeline achieves the best or near-best PSNR on all three datasets: it edges out PNNP by 0.02 dB on SID (40.85 vs. 40.83) and 0.04 dB on ELD (46.43 vs. 46.39), and leads by a substantial 0.54 dB on LRID (45.08 vs. 44.54). It outperforms SFRN by 0.71 dB on SID and 0.92 dB on ELD, and surpasses the flow-based NoiseFlow and StarLight by 3.7–4.5 dB on SID. Notably, the self-supervised method also beats the supervised PMN on LRID (45.08 vs. 44.97 PSNR), which the authors attribute to sensor-heating-induced noise variation that direct dark-frame sampling captures but paired training data does not. On SID, the method slightly trails PNNP in SSIM (0.9478 vs. 0.9479) but matches it in PSNR, while on ELD it leads in both metrics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the physics-based noise decomposition framework established by ELD, LLD, and PMN, which model RAW sensor noise as signal-dependent (Poisson shot noise) plus signal-independent components. It extends SFRN's idea of sampling dark frames directly as signal-independent noise, but departs sharply from the broader literature by eliminating three standard preparation steps: system gain calibration (replaced by hypothesizing quantum efficiency), parametric noise profiling (replaced by direct dark frame sampling), and high-bit-depth noise recovery (HBNR). The denoising backbone (U-Net from SID) is shared with all compared methods, isolating the contribution to the noise synthesis pipeline itself.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: reducing noise synthesis preparation from days (hardware setup, flat-field calibration, profiling network training) to under one hour (dark frame collection only) makes self-supervised RAW denoising deployable at scale across many sensor models. A key open limitation is the requirement for known analog gain (AG), which consumer camera users typically cannot access—only ISO values are exposed—making the hypothesizing step less straightforward for smartphone or DSLR end users. The authors identify sensor-agnostic denoising (pooling dark frames across models) and extension to other sensor types (e.g., EMCCD) as natural next steps, and the finding that direct sampling outperforms even a 100-component GMM on unseen dark frames suggests the field's investment in increasingly complex parametric noise models may be misdirected.
