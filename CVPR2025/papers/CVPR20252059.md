## Problem

Existing 3D scene reconstruction methods (NeRF, 3DGS) excel at novel view synthesis but lack the physical modeling needed for dynamic weather effects, while traditional physics-based rain simulations require manual scene setup and do not scale to in-the-wild environments. Prior attempts to bridge this gap—ClimateNeRF (static weather) and Gaussian Splashing (inefficient particle simulation)—fail to deliver both physical accuracy and real-time interactive rain synthesis in open-world scenes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252059/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes multi-view RGB images of a scene captured under clear or cloudy conditions as input and produces an interactive, free-viewpoint rainy scene rendered at over 30 fps. Users can dynamically adjust rain intensity (light drizzle to heavy downpour) in real time, controlling raindrop density, velocity, and direction. The output includes physically consistent rain streaks, ground water accumulation with ripples, splashes, and screen-space reflections/refractions that evolve over time.

## Method

RainyGS reconstructs the scene using PGSR (planar-based 3D Gaussian Splatting) to obtain high-precision geometry and appearance, then extracts a height map via top-down orthographic rendering. Rain dynamics are simulated on this 2D height field using shallow water equations with semi-Lagrangian advection for velocity and first-order upwind for the height field, where randomly generated raindrops fall, collide with ground or occlusion surfaces, and deposit volume into the height field. Rendering employs a two-pass rasterization: the first pass computes specular reflection via screen-space ray tracing, refraction via normal-based image distortion, and Blinn-Phong highlights, blended by a Fresnel term; the second pass splats raindrops as anisotropic Gaussian ellipsoids aligned with their velocity. The two passes are depth-composited per pixel, and the entire pipeline runs at 30+ fps with only ~0.025s additional cost over base PGSR rendering.

## Results

RainyGS achieves a rendering speed of 0.032 s per frame (over 30 fps) with 8.561 GB peak video memory on the Garden scene, adding only ~4.5× overhead over the base PGSR reconstruction (0.007 s, 7.989 GB). This is approximately 60× faster than PGSR augmented with full 3D ray tracing for water rendering (1.942 s, 14.161 GB) and ~12× faster than the Runway-V2V video generator (~0.4 s). The paper's evaluation is primarily qualitative, with visual comparisons across MipNeRF360, Tanks and Temples, and Waymo driving scenes demonstrating 3D consistency, physically accurate rain dynamics, and user-controllable rain intensity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Rain Motion [43], RainyGS produces physically consistent rain dynamics (proper ripple formation when droplets hit water, correct reflection distortion) whereas Rain Motion generates random, non-physical rain droplets and lacks 3D consistency across views. Against Runway Gen-3 Alpha [33], RainyGS maintains scene geometry and 3D view consistency, while Runway-V2V alters the original scene (e.g., transforming a vase into a glass one), produces nearly static rain content, and renders reflections/refractions that are not physically accurate (e.g., reflected desk legs remain straight through ripples). Against Instruct-GS2GS [41], RainyGS supports full dynamic rain effects (flying streaks, splashes, evolving puddles) while Instruct-GS2GS is limited to static editing and lacks advanced rain rendering. In the performance comparison (Table 1), RainyGS is ~60× faster than PGSR+RT (0.032 s vs. 1.942 s per frame) with only 0.57 GB additional memory over base PGSR, while Runway-V2V is ~12× slower than RainyGS. Overall, RainyGS wins on all qualitative and quantitative comparisons, with the primary trade-off being the reliance on accurate 3DGS scene reconstruction as a prerequisite.

## Relation to Prior Work

RainyGS sits at the intersection of 3D scene modeling (3DGS/PGSR for geometry and appearance) and physics-based fluid simulation (shallow water equations from Chentanez & Müller for water dynamics, screen-space ray tracing from McGuire & Mara for reflections). It departs from ClimateNeRF, which handles only static weather in neural radiance fields, and Gaussian Splashing, which uses computationally expensive particle-based fluid simulation within 3DGS. The key conceptual move is replacing full 3D fluid simulation with a 2D height-field shallow-water model that exploits the fact that rain accumulation is inherently a surface phenomenon, thereby bypassing the internal geometric detail that 3DGS cannot reliably represent.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first framework to deliver physically-accurate, dynamic rain synthesis in open-world 3D scenes at interactive rates (>30 fps), with user-controllable intensity from drizzle to downpour. The practical impact is significant for autonomous driving simulation (Waymo scenes), AR/VR, and gaming, where realistic weather augmentation is needed but manual artist setup is infeasible. Key limitations remain: shallow-water dynamics cannot model deep-wave or submerged-surface interactions, screen-space rendering cannot handle occluded reflections/refractions, and the method depends on accurate scene geometry from multi-view inputs. Open questions include whether the height-field approximation degrades in scenes with complex terrain or whether the approach can be extended to other precipitation types (snow, hail) with similar efficiency.
