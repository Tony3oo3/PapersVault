## Problem

Existing test-time adaptation (TTA) methods for CLIP treat each test sample independently, ignoring the rich cross-sample visual similarity structure present in real-world test batches. While ViT-based transductive TTA methods exploit batch-level statistics, they fail to adapt the text modality, which is critical for vision-language models. The fine-grained discriminative attributes shared among visually similar samples within a batch remain largely underutilized for CLIP adaptation.

## Input / Output

SCAP consumes a sequential stream of unlabeled test image batches (batch size 64, processed with CLIP ViT-B/16) along with a fixed set of class names. For each batch, the method first generates top-k (k=3) class predictions per image to identify candidate cliques, then produces a single class prediction per image by combining attribute prompts learned from the current batch with retained knowledge from prior batches. No ground-truth labels or training data are required at test time.

## Method

SCAP operates in three stages per batch. First, it mines Supportive Cliques: for each class, images whose top-k predictions include that class are grouped, and pairwise cosine similarity of CLIP image features is thresholded to form cliques of visually similar samples. Second, it learns per-clique visual and text attribute prompts by minimizing a joint entropy loss (pushing the clique's mean attribute feature toward the correct class text embedding) and a concentration loss (pulling individual image features toward the clique attribute center). Third, a retention module preserves learned knowledge across batches: a Text Retention Prompt is updated via exponential moving average, and a per-class Retention Cache stores (attribute feature, visual prompt) key-value pairs, merging the two most similar entries via graph propagation when the cache exceeds capacity L. At inference, each image's visual prompt is the concatenation of all associated clique prompts plus the matched retention prompt, and the text prompt is a weighted fusion of current-batch and retained text prompts, enabling CLIP to attend to fine-grained, attribute-specific image regions.

## Results

On the OOD benchmark, SCAP achieves an average top-1 accuracy of 65.63% across four ImageNet variants, outperforming the best prior TTA method TDA (63.89%) by 1.74%. The largest gains are on ImageNet-A (64.52% vs. 61.35% for Zero, the strongest single-dataset baseline) and ImageNet-R (81.68% vs. 80.24% for TDA). On the cross-domain benchmark, SCAP surpasses state-of-the-art methods by at least 0.18% (Caltech101) to 1.64% (Pets). The only marginal loss is on ImageNet-V2, where SCAP (64.65%) trails TDA (64.67%) by just 0.02%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SCAP outperforms all ten baselines on the OOD benchmark average (65.63% vs. 63.89% for TDA, the closest competitor). Against the strongest TTA baselines, SCAP gains +4.41% over Zero on ImageNet-A, +1.44% over TDA on ImageNet-R, and +1.11% over TDA on ImageNet-Sketch, while matching TDA within 0.02% on ImageNet-V2. Compared to the prompt-based TTA methods DART (63.48% avg) and TPT (60.81% avg), SCAP improves by 2.15% and 4.82% respectively, demonstrating the benefit of clique-based cross-sample information over per-sample prompt optimization. SCAP also surpasses training-time adaptation methods (CoOp, CoCoOp, Tip-Adapter) by 5.35%–6.35% in average accuracy, confirming that its test-time clique mining extracts more domain-specific knowledge than supervised prompt tuning. On the cross-domain benchmark, SCAP leads all compared methods on all four datasets (Aircraft, Caltech101, Flower102, Pets), with the largest margin of 1.64% on Pets.

## Relation to Prior Work

SCAP extends the prompt-learning-based TTA line initiated by TPT (entropy-minimized prompt tuning) and DART (dual-modal prompting with knowledge retention) by shifting from individual-sample adaptation to a transductive, batch-level setting. The key conceptual departure is the introduction of supportive clique mining: rather than treating each test image independently or applying coarse batch-level statistics (as in ViT-based transductive TTA or CLIPArTT), SCAP groups visually similar images into cliques and learns attribute-specific prompts per clique. The retention mechanism with graph-based aggregation of similar attribute prompts generalizes DART's simpler prompt retention to handle growing knowledge across many batches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SCAP addresses a practical gap in TTA: real-world test data arrives in batches, yet most CLIP-based TTA methods process samples individually, wasting cross-sample structure. The clique-based attribute mining offers a principled way to extract fine-grained discriminative features (e.g., 'head' vs. 'body') that individual-sample entropy minimization cannot capture. Limitations include reliance on CLIP's initial top-k predictions for clique formation (which may degrade under severe domain shift), sensitivity to batch size, and evaluation limited to ImageNet variants and four small cross-domain datasets. Open questions include how the approach behaves under extreme distribution shifts where initial predictions are unreliable, and whether clique-based grouping generalizes to non-classification tasks like retrieval or segmentation.
