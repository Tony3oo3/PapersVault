limitations: The evaluation is restricted to four standard image-based FAS benchmarks with a fixed 10-shot setting and a frozen ViT-B/16 CLIP backbone; the paper does not explore video-based anti-spoofing, adaptive prototype counts, or sensitivity to varying few-shot sample sizes beyond the default 10 per class, and the one-class scenario is only analyzed for the training-free variant.

## Problem

Face anti-spoofing models struggle to generalize to diverse client-specific test domains, yet privacy and security constraints prohibit sharing model parameters or source training data with clients. Existing domain adaptation requires both source and target data at training time, domain generalization relies on static parameters that underperform under large domain shifts, and few-shot learning methods assume target data is available during training. This paper tackles the under-explored setting of source-free, test-time few-shot adaptation where the client can only access a lightweight customizable layer and a handful of labeled target samples.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252001/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At test time, the method consumes a face image (processed by a frozen ViT-B/16 feature extractor whose parameters are inaccessible to the client), a small set of few-shot labeled target-domain images (10 per class by default), and pre-learned source prototypes (50 sub-centroids per class, stored as a lightweight D×K×2 tensor). The output is a binary bona fide/spoof label with a confidence score derived from mean cosine similarity to the (possibly transformed) prototypes. The key constraint is that the feature extractor acts as a black box: all adaptation occurs exclusively on the prototype layer or a 3.9 KB linear classifier, never on the backbone.

## Method

The core framework replaces a standard classifier with multi-centroid prototypes (K=50 sub-centroids per class) trained via a modified ArcFace margin loss, coarse-to-fine supervised contrastive loss, and orthogonal regularization, so that prototypes encode both classification boundaries and source-domain distribution geometry. For training-free adaptation, the method solves a regularized optimal transport problem (with Laplacian regularization to preserve structure) between source prototypes and few-shot target features, then applies a barycentric projection to shift each prototype into the target region while retaining its geometric role. For lightweight adaptation, the method introduces geodesic mixup: it computes discrete Wasserstein barycenters with free support along the geodesic path between source and target distributions (solved via the Sinkhorn algorithm with a randomly sampled mixing coefficient from Beta(0.4,0.4)), generating distribution-level synthetic training data that respects global geometry rather than performing instance-wise linear interpolation. A linear classifier is then trained for 100 iterations on this augmented feature data, adapting to target-specific characteristics while preserving source-domain knowledge.

## Results

On the cross-domain evaluation (leave-one-out over Idiap, OULU-NPU, CASIA-MFSD, MSU-MFSD), OTA's lightweight training variant (OTA‡) achieves an average HTER of 2.91% and average AUC of 99.39%, outperforming the best few-shot baseline ViTAF (3.93% HTER) and the best DG method CFPL (3.60% HTER). The training-free variant (OTA†) reaches 3.21% average HTER with zero additional parameters. On the cross-attack protocol, OTA‡ achieves 98.54% AUC on unseen 2D attacks and 99.63% AUC on unseen 3D attacks, surpassing the strongest baseline GAC-FAS (93.39% / 89.27%) by over 5 and 10 percentage points respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OTA‡ (lightweight) achieves the best average HTER (2.91%) across all four cross-domain splits, beating the strongest few-shot method ViTAF (3.93%) by 1.02 points and the best DG method CFPL (3.60%) by 0.69 points. The training-free variant OTA† (3.21% HTER) also outperforms all DG baselines without any learnable parameters, and surpasses Tip-Adaptor (4.84%) and NCM (4.41%) in the few-shot adaptation comparison (Table 3). Against Manifold Mixup (3.04% avg HTER), OTA‡ wins on TPR@FPR=1% (92.30% vs. 91.19% on ICM→O) while matching on HTER, demonstrating that distribution-level geodesic mixup is more effective than instance-level interpolation. On cross-attack evaluation, OTA‡ dominates all baselines by a wide margin: 99.63% AUC on unseen 3D attacks vs. GAC-FAS's 89.27%, a 10.36-point gap. The zero-shot prototype framework alone (OTA*) already matches or exceeds most DG methods (e.g., 3.43% vs. SA-FAS 7.82%), confirming that the prototype-based representation learning is a strong baseline independent of the adaptation modules.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the prototype-based representation framework of Tanwisuth et al. (NeurIPS 2021) and the ArcFace angular margin loss (Deng et al., CVPR 2019), extending both to a multi-centroid setting with supervised contrastive supervision. For adaptation, it repurposes regularized optimal transport (Courty et al., TPAMI 2016; Ferradans et al., 2014) not as a training-time alignment tool but as a test-time transformation of frozen prototypes, and introduces geodesic mixup by applying discrete Wasserstein barycenters (Alvarez-Esteban et al., 2016) as a distribution-level augmentation. The key departure from prior work is the strict source-free, no-model-access constraint: unlike DA methods that require source data at training time, DG methods that rely on static parameters, or SFDA methods that still expose the backbone, OTA adapts only a lightweight customizable layer (prototypes + optional linear classifier) using a handful of client-labeled samples at test time.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically critical deployment gap in face anti-spoofing: clients cannot share face data or receive model weights due to privacy and proprietary constraints, yet must adapt to their own domain. The finding that a parameter-free OT transformation of prototypes already matches or exceeds strong DG baselines, and that geodesic mixup further improves the decision boundary under extreme data scarcity, provides a lightweight (3.9 KB, ~23 min) and privacy-preserving adaptation recipe. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to video-based FAS, whether the one-class sensitivity (bona fide data proving more informative than spoof data) reflects a fundamental asymmetry, and whether the prototype count (K=50) and Laplacian regularization strength can be made adaptive rather than fixed. The framework is also potentially transferable to other biometric or security tasks with similar source-free constraints.
