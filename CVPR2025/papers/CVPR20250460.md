## Problem

Existing CLIP-based face forgery detection methods treat CLIP as a frozen feature extractor with a generic adapter trained on standard objectives, ignoring the unique blending-boundary traces that characterize face forgeries. This task-agnostic adaptation, combined with the absence of direct interaction between CLIP and the adapter, leaves CLIP's forgery-related knowledge entangled with unrelated knowledge and limits generalizability to unseen forgery types.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250460/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single face image (256×256 for the adapter, 224×224 for CLIP) extracted from video frames via RetinaFace, divided into 16×16 patches for the adapter and 14×14 patches for CLIP. It produces two outputs: a real/fake classification score derived from duplicated [CLS*] tokens in the final CLIP layer, and a spatial blending boundary map (h×w×1) predicted by the adapter's prediction head using 128 learnable query tokens. Training uses 32 frames per video from the FF++ c23 compression set, following the DeepfakeBench protocol.

## Method

A lightweight ViT-tiny adapter (first 8 layers, 5.7M trainable parameters) runs in parallel to a frozen CLIP ViT-L/14, guided by three task-specific objectives: masked blending boundary regression (L1), patch-wise contrastive learning distinguishing real vs. fake patches (L2), and sample-wise contrastive learning separating real from fake faces (L3). A bidirectional interaction strategy connects the two streams: CLIP visual tokens from layers {1, 8, 16} are fused into the adapter's shallow layers to support its learning, while the adapter generates an attention bias (via inner product of query tokens and projected visual tokens) that modulates self-attention on duplicated [CLS*] tokens within CLIP, steering its focus toward forgery traces without modifying any CLIP parameters. For contrastive losses applied to CLIP, a trainable 1×1 convolution produces enhanced tokens that are added back with a small factor α=0.05, preserving CLIP's pre-trained representations while injecting task-specific signal.

## Results

On the primary frame-level cross-dataset evaluation (trained on FF++ c23, tested on five datasets), the Forensics Adapter achieves an average AUC of 0.896, outperforming the previous best method LSDA (0.826) by approximately 7 percentage points. The largest single-dataset gain is on DFDC, where the method reaches 0.843 AUC versus LSDA's 0.736 (+10.7%). At the video level, the method achieves 0.957, 0.872, and 0.929 AUC on CDF-v2, DFDC, and DFDCP respectively, surpassing all prior methods. The entire trainable adapter contains only 5.7M parameters, compared to 19–36M for competing methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The Forensics Adapter outperforms all 14 frame-level baselines in Table 1, with the most notable margin over LSDA [71] (the prior state-of-the-art) by 7% average AUC and 10.7% on DFDC specifically. Against CLIP-based methods (Table 3), the method surpasses FCG [21] by 3.4% on CDF-v2, RepDFD [41] by 6.2% on DFDC, and VLFFD [59] by 2.4% on DFD, while also outperforming Vanilla CLIP [54] by 18–23% across datasets. At the video level (Table 2), the method beats the strongest video-based baselines LAA-NET [49] (0.954 on CDF-v2) and SBI [57] (0.862 on DFDCP) by 0.3% and 6.7% respectively, and surpasses LSDA [71] by 10.2% on DFDC. In the complexity comparison (Table 11), the method achieves higher video-level AUC than LipForensics [19], FTCN [73], DCL [58], and CFM [46] while using 3–6× fewer trainable parameters (5.7M vs. 19–36M). Alternative fine-tuning strategies (LoRA [24], subset fine-tuning [22]) yield substantially lower performance (0.770–0.807 AUC) compared to the proposed adapter (0.890–0.900), confirming that task-specific adaptation is critical rather than generic parameter-efficient tuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of CLIP-based face forgery detection (FFAA, GM-DF, FCG, VLFFD, RepDFD, CLIPping) by departing from the common paradigm of treating CLIP as a frozen feature extractor with a generic downstream head. Instead, it introduces a task-specific adapter (built on TinyViT) that learns blending boundaries through dedicated objectives (masked boundary regression, patch-wise and sample-wise contrastive losses), and a bidirectional interaction strategy that both absorbs CLIP features into the adapter and injects adapter knowledge back into CLIP via an attention bias mechanism inspired by the Side Adapter Network. The blending-boundary signal itself is not new (used in X-ray, SBI, LAA-NET), but its integration as the core inductive bias for adapting a frozen vision-language model is novel.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

With only 5.7M trainable parameters (3–6× fewer than competing methods), the Forensics Adapter achieves roughly 7% average AUC improvement over the prior best (LSDA) across five standard datasets, making it a practical and efficient baseline for CLIP-based face forgery detection. The paper explicitly positions itself as a reference point for future CLIP-adaptation work in this domain. Key open questions include whether the approach extends to whole-face synthesis detection (where performance drops notably), whether the text modality can be made useful rather than harmful, and whether the blending-boundary inductive bias generalizes to forgery types that do not involve local splicing.
