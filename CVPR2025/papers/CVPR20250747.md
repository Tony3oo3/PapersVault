## Problem

State-of-the-art 3D human pose estimation methods regress parameters of simplified body models (e.g., SMPL) that represent every joint as a ball-and-socket with three degrees of freedom, leading to predictions that violate biomechanical joint limits and are incompatible with biomechanical simulation. No end-to-end method existed to directly estimate the parameters of a biomechanically accurate skeleton model (SKEL) from a single image, and no training dataset with SKEL ground truth was available.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250747/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image of a person and outputs SKEL pose parameters (46-dim, one Euler angle per realistic degree of freedom, each with explicit joint limits), 10-dim shape parameters, and camera parameters, from which a 6890-vertex surface mesh and skeleton mesh are generated. Internally, the network regresses pose in a continuous rotation representation (converted via Gram-Schmidt to rotation matrices for loss computation, then to Euler angles for SKEL compatibility), rather than directly regressing the native Euler angles, which the ablation shows degrades performance.

## Method

HSMR uses a ViT backbone with a transformer head to regress SKEL parameters from a single image. Since no SKEL-annotated dataset exists, the authors convert SMPL pseudo-ground-truth from HMR2.0's large-scale training data to SKEL parameters via iterative optimization, then refine these pseudo-labels during training through a periodic SKELify procedure: the network's current estimate initializes an optimization that aligns the SKEL mesh with 2D keypoints, regularized by a shape prior and a joint-limit-aware pose prior (exponential penalty for exceeding per-joint rotation bounds). The pose regression target uses the continuous rotation representation rather than native Euler angles, and the parameter loss is computed in rotation-matrix space. This combination of biomechanical model constraints, pseudo-label refinement, and continuous rotation regression allows HSMR to match SMPL-based methods on standard benchmarks while substantially outperforming them on extreme poses (MOYO) and eliminating most joint-limit violations.

## Results

On standard 2D benchmarks (COCO, LSP-Extended, PoseTrack), HSMR matches HMR2.0 within 0.01–0.02 PCK, and on 3D benchmarks (3DPW, Human3.6M) it is within 0.5 mm MPJPE. The headline result is on MOYO (extreme yoga poses and viewpoints), where HSMR achieves 104.5 mm MPJPE and 79.6 mm PA-MPJPE, outperforming HMR2.0 by 18.8 mm and 10.8 mm respectively. HSMR also achieves the best per-vertex accuracy on MOYO (120.1 mm MPVPE vs. 136.7 mm for the next best method, PLIKS). Additionally, HSMR reduces joint-limit violations to near zero (0.0% for elbows, <5% for knees at 10° threshold) compared to 33–61% violations for all SMPL-based baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the primary baseline HMR2.0 [14], HSMR matches performance on all standard datasets (≤0.5 mm difference on 3DPW and Human3.6M, ≤0.02 PCK on 2D benchmarks) while achieving a large 18.8 mm MPJPE improvement on MOYO (104.5 vs. 123.3 mm), confirming the biomechanical skeleton's advantage on extreme poses. Compared to PARE [25], CLIFF [30], HybrIK [28], and PLIKS [48], HSMR achieves the best MOYO results by margins of 61.1, 50.1, 35.6, and 28.1 mm MPJPE respectively, while remaining competitive (within 0.01–0.05 PCK) on 2D benchmarks. Notably, HSMR also outperforms the two-stage HMR2.0+SKELfit baseline (Table 3) on most datasets and is orders of magnitude faster (feedforward vs. 3 minutes per frame). A key differentiator is biomechanical validity: all SMPL-based baselines exhibit 33–61% joint-limit violations (>10°) on MOYO, whereas HSMR reduces this to 0% for elbows and <5% for knees, since SKEL models only realistic degrees of freedom.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the established single-image 3D human pose estimation pipeline (ViT backbone + transformer head, following HMR2.0) to a new parametric model, SKEL, which replaces SMPL's ball-and-socket joints with a biomechanically accurate skeleton having only realistic degrees of freedom. The key methodological departure is the data pipeline: since no SKEL-annotated dataset exists, the authors convert SMPL pseudo ground truth via optimization (adapting SMPLify into 'SKELify') and then iteratively refine these pseudo labels during training, in the spirit of SPIN. The architecture itself is deliberately unremarkable, mirroring HMR2.0's design, so the contribution lies in the model choice, the data generation strategy, and the biomechanical pose prior (joint-limit penalties) rather than in network novelty.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work bridges a practical gap between computer vision pose estimation and biomechanics, where anatomical plausibility is non-negotiable. The finding that SMPL-based methods violate joint angle limits in up to ~58% of cases (HybrIK on MOYO) is a useful diagnostic for the field, suggesting that low MPJPE does not guarantee biomechanically valid poses. Open questions include whether the approach extends to video with temporal smoothness, whether integrating SKEL estimates into a physics simulation (e.g., OpenSim) yields further gains, and whether the pseudo-ground-truth ceiling limits ultimate accuracy. The reliance on pseudo labels and the observed temporal jitter are acknowledged limitations that future work must address.
