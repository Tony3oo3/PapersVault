## Problem

Event cameras capture scene changes at microsecond temporal resolution and 140 dB dynamic range but integrate radiance over all angles, discarding the angular information needed for light field operations like refocusing and depth estimation. Conversely, conventional light field cameras capture angular structure but at low frame rates and limited dynamic range. No prior system combined high-speed event sensing with angular (light field) capture, leaving a gap for post-capture manipulation of high-speed, high-dynamic-range scenes.

## Input / Output

The system takes asynchronous event camera data (brightness-change events with polarity and timestamp) acquired through either a kaleidoscope (yielding 3×3 spatially multiplexed views at reduced per-view resolution) or a galvanometer (yielding full spatial resolution but temporally multiplexed angular derivatives at 250 Hz scan rate). For the SloMoRF application, co-located RGB frames at 120 fps are fused with event data. Outputs include structured or unstructured event fields, post-capture refocused images at arbitrary focal planes, per-pixel depth maps at up to 100 Hz, HDR light fields, and interpolated high-speed video up to 10,000 fps (galvanometer) or 720 fps with color (kaleidoscope + RGB fusion).

## Method

The core insight is that event cameras measure temporal derivatives of log-radiance integrated over all angles, so angular information must be multiplexed into either the spatial or temporal dimension the sensor already captures. Spatial multiplexing uses a rectangular kaleidoscope (four first-surface mirrors) that maps n discrete angular views to spatial sub-regions via x_s = x mod(r/n), capturing temporal derivatives at multiple angles simultaneously without moving parts. Temporal multiplexing uses a galvanometer that steers incoming rays along a closed Lissajous curve C(t) at 250 Hz, so the event measurement becomes ∂B(x,ω)/∂ω · ∂C(t)/∂t, directly capturing angular derivatives at full spatial resolution. The two designs are complementary: the kaleidoscope handles dynamic scenes without motion blur but cannot capture static content, while the galvanometer captures both static and dynamic scenes but introduces motion blur for fast objects. Reconstruction leverages pre-trained E2VID for frame synthesis and TimeLens for RGB-event sensor fusion, with depth-from-focus applied to the structured galvanometer light field for instant true depth estimation.

## Results

The galvanometer design achieves post-capture refocusing at 250 Hz scanning frequency with 40 angular views, reconstructing a 10,000 fps (0.1 ms/frame) light field video at megapixel resolution—the fastest recorded at that resolution. The kaleidoscope-based SloMoRF pipeline interpolates 120 fps RGB video to 720 fps (5×) with dynamic refocusing, and extends to 960 fps when tracking a toy dart. Instant true depth estimation operates at 100 Hz, capturing a running person's depth in real time. The system successfully refocuses a rotating fan at 480 RPM and tracks nerf darts at 20 m/s, demonstrating capabilities beyond what standard high-speed cameras (limited to fixed focus) can achieve.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a hardware/computational imaging paper without formal algorithmic baselines; the primary comparison is between the two proposed multiplexing designs. The kaleidoscope (spatial multiplexing) excels at dynamic scenes with low bandwidth and color support but cannot capture static regions (no events generated without motion) and suffers reduced spatial resolution by a factor of n (3×3 views). The galvanometer (temporal multiplexing) captures both static and dynamic scenes at full spatial resolution with smooth refocusing, but introduces motion blur for fast objects and requires higher bandwidth. Compared to prior event-camera light field work (Habuchi et al. [16], limited to static scenes via LCOS at 22 ms response; Guo et al. [14], which trades spatial resolution for angular info via microlens array), the galvanometer design uniquely captures angular information for static scenes without sacrificing spatial resolution. The kaleidoscope design is functionally equivalent to the MLA approach of Guo et al. but adapted for macroscopic (not microscopic) applications. No head-to-head quantitative benchmark against a single competing method is provided; evaluation is qualitative and application-driven.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends light field imaging into the high-speed, high-dynamic-range regime by combining event cameras (which natively capture temporal brightness derivatives) with optical designs that recover the angular dimension lost through integration over all directions. The kaleidoscope design is directly adapted from Manakov et al.'s reconfigurable camera add-on, repurposed to spatially multiplex angular views onto an event sensor, while the galvanometer design draws on Fujii et al.'s rayspace acquisition but replaces discrete position selection with continuous Lissajous scanning to capture angular derivatives. For processing, the paper leverages TimeLens for RGB-event sensor fusion in the SloMoRF pipeline and E2VID for high-speed frame reconstruction, both originally designed for time-sequential event streams rather than the structured angular data produced here.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that post-capture refocusing, real-time true depth estimation, and HDR light field capture are all achievable at event-camera speeds (up to 250 fps at megapixel resolution with 40 angular views), opening applications in robotics, AR/VR, and high-speed photography that were previously infeasible. Key limitations include the kaleidoscope's inability to capture static scenes and its spatial resolution loss, the galvanometer's motion blur on fast objects, and a hard bandwidth ceiling around 250 Hz where random event loss degrades reconstruction. Open questions include adaptive scanning strategies (slower for static regions, faster for dynamic ones), importance-sampling-based simulation to replace the current brute-force approach, and training event-field-specific reconstruction models rather than repurposing time-sequential ones.
