## Problem

Color constancy methods struggle to generalize across different camera sensors due to varying spectral sensitivities, requiring retraining or calibration for each new device. While deep learning approaches have improved accuracy, they remain constrained to specific sensor characteristics, and statistical methods fail under complex or multi-illuminant lighting. This paper addresses the cross-camera generalization gap by leveraging the rich scene priors of pre-trained diffusion models to estimate illumination without sensor-specific training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250699/cropped_page0_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a 512×512 RGB image and a binary mask specifying where a neutral color checker is composited. Before encoding, the image is gamma-corrected (γ=2.2) into the sRGB domain to match the VAE's training distribution; after decoding, the result is inverse gamma-corrected back to the linear domain. The final output is the scene illuminant as an RGB vector, extracted by sampling the achromatic (gray) patches of the generated color checker using fixed spatial masks on a standardized grid.

## Method

GCC fine-tunes the stable-diffusion-2-inpainting U-Net at a single fixed timestep T with zero noise, enabling deterministic one-step inference (180 ms on an RTX 4090) instead of iterative denoising. Two key techniques ensure accurate checker generation: (1) Laplacian decomposition extracts only high-frequency components from the input latent, preserving the checker's geometric structure while suppressing low-frequency color that would bias the model toward reconstructing the neutral reference rather than harmonizing with scene illumination; (2) mask-based color jittering randomly perturbs the masked region during training, destroying sensor-specific color cues and forcing the model to infer illumination from surrounding context, which also circumvents the imprecise bounding-box annotations in existing datasets. At inference, a neutral color checker is pasted at the mask location, the pipeline runs a single U-Net forward pass, and the illuminant is read directly from the achromatic patches of the inpainted checker.

## Results

On the camera-agnostic cross-dataset evaluation (Table 1), GCC achieves a mean angular error of 2.35° (NUS-8→Gehler) and 2.38° (Gehler→NUS-8), outperforming the best prior learning-based method C4 (2.73°/2.28°) by 0.38°/0.10° in mean error. The worst-25% error drops to 4.57°/4.58°, substantially better than C4's 5.69°/4.60°, indicating stronger robustness on difficult scenes. On the leave-one-out protocol (Table 2), GCC achieves the best mean (2.03°) and worst-25% (3.69°) on NUS-8. Inference takes only 180 ms per 512×512 image on an RTX 4090, versus 17.98 s for a 25-step SDEdit baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GCC outperforms all learning-based baselines on mean, median, tri-mean, and worst-25% angular error in both cross-dataset directions. The closest competitor, C4 [76], achieves 2.73°/2.28° mean error versus GCC's 2.35°/2.38°, with GCC showing a particularly large advantage on worst-25% (4.57° vs 5.69° in NUS-8→Gehler), indicating superior handling of difficult illumination scenes. GCC slightly trails C4 on best-25% (0.78° vs 0.72° in NUS-8→Gehler; 0.80° vs 0.67° in Gehler→NUS-8), suggesting C4 is marginally better on the easiest subset. Against the best statistical method (GI [58], 3.07°/2.91° mean), GCC improves by roughly 0.7° in both directions. On the leave-one-out protocol (Table 2), GCC achieves the best mean (2.03°) and worst-25% (3.69°) on NUS-8, beating SIIE [1] (2.05°/4.48°) and C5 [2] (2.54°/5.61%). On the LSMI multi-illuminant benchmark (Table 6), GCC is competitive but not uniformly best: it leads on single-illuminant scenes (2.05° Galaxy, 2.10° Nikon) but trails LSMI-U [43] on multi-illuminant scenes (3.44° vs 2.35° Galaxy), reflecting the inherent difficulty of spatially varying illumination.

## Relation to Prior Work

GCC builds on the latent diffusion inpainting architecture of Stable Diffusion 2 (Rombach et al.) and adopts the deterministic single-step fine-tuning strategy from Garcia et al. (2024), which showed that image-conditional diffusion models can be repurposed for low-frequency estimation tasks without multi-step sampling. Conceptually, it extends the DiffusionLight paradigm (Phongthawee et al., 2024)—which inpaints a chrome ball for HDR lighting estimation—into the color constancy domain by inpainting a Macbeth color checker instead. This departs from the dominant line of cross-camera color constancy work (SIIE, C5, CLCC, C4) that learns sensor-invariant features through metric learning or contrastive objectives, replacing learned representations with the rich generative priors of a pre-trained diffusion model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The approach offers a practical path toward camera-agnostic color constancy without sensor-specific retraining, and the single-step 180 ms inference makes it viable for real-time applications. However, the performance gains over strong baselines like C4 and C5 are modest (e.g., 2.35° vs. 2.28° mean angular error on cross-dataset evaluation), and the method still requires training on color-checker-annotated datasets. An open question is how well the diffusion prior generalizes to truly novel camera sensors or extreme lighting conditions beyond the two benchmark datasets; the mixed results on the LSMI multi-illuminant benchmark (worse than LSMI-U on multi-source scenes) suggest the single-illuminant assumption embedded in the color checker formulation remains a limitation.
