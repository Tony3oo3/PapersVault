## Problem

CLIP and similar vision-language models align images and texts only at a global level, losing fine-grained visual details such as the distinction between a background region and a specific object. Prior attempts to improve this with detailed synthetic captions (e.g., DreamLIP) still fail to properly align local image tokens with semantically matching text, because their contrastive negative-pair selection creates an undesired shortcut that lets the model ignore image information.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251987/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes 224×224 RGB images and text sequences (≤77 tokens) as input. A key design choice is that each image is paired with K=8 diverse sub-captions sampled from a long MLLM-generated description, where each sub-caption consists of 1–3 sentences drawn either consecutively or from random positions. For each image–caption pair, the model produces a unique text-conditioned image embedding via attention pooling, alongside a shared global image embedding; downstream tasks consume these as similarity scores (retrieval), per-token similarity maps (segmentation), or class logits (classification).

## Method

FLAIR introduces a text-conditioned attention pooling layer that uses the global text embedding as a query to attend over local image patch tokens, yielding a caption-specific image representation rather than a single averaged global vector. To train this, the paper samples K=8 diverse sub-captions per image from long synthetic descriptions and defines two sigmoid-based contrastive losses: a text-conditioned loss (L_tcs) that aligns each text-conditioned image embedding with its corresponding caption, and a multi-positive global loss (L_mps) that aligns the global image embedding with all sub-captions. A critical design choice is the negative-pair definition: instead of DreamLIP's formulation (which allows the model to shortcut by comparing the text condition against the text embedding), FLAIR pairs the i-th image's text-conditioned embedding with the j-th image's caption, forcing meaningful cross-modal contrast. The full loss is the average of L_tcs and L_mps, and the model is trained with a standard ViT-B/16 vision encoder and CLIP text encoder on 30M re-captioned image-text pairs.

## Results

On standard retrieval, FLAIR-30M achieves 81.1% T2I and 94.7% I2T R@1 on Flickr30k, surpassing SigLIP-10B (75.6%/89.1%) by 5.5%/5.6% with 333× less training data. On the newly introduced fine-grained retrieval benchmark (IIW-FG), FLAIR-30M reaches 41.7% T2I R@1, outperforming SigLIP-10B (33.8%) by 7.9%. For zero-shot semantic segmentation, FLAIR-30M achieves 25.8% average mIoU across five datasets, a 14.4% absolute improvement over CLIP-400M (12.8%) and 25.1% over DreamLIP (0.7%). On zero-shot classification at 30M scale, FLAIR (56.8% avg) is on par with DreamLIP (57.0%) but trails large-scale models like Llip-2.5B (79.0%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FLAIR-30M outperforms all baselines on every retrieval benchmark. Against SigLIP-10B, FLAIR gains +5.5% T2I and +5.6% I2T on Flickr30k, +6.1% T2I on MSCOCO, and +7.9% T2I on IIW-FG fine-grained retrieval, despite using 333× fewer training samples. Against DreamLIP-30M (same data scale), FLAIR improves by +7.8% T2I on Flickr30k and +4.2% T2I on IIW-FG, confirming that the text-conditioned attention pooling and revised negative pair selection are the key architectural gains. On long-text retrieval (Table 2), FLAIR-30M surpasses LoTLIP-100M by +3.1% I2T on SV-1k and +8.9% I2T on SV-10k, and beats Long-CLIP-400M by +17.2% T2I on Urban-1k. On zero-shot segmentation, FLAIR-30M (25.8% avg mIoU) dramatically outperforms CLIP-400M (12.8%), OpenCLIP-2B (13.9%), and DreamLIP (0.7%), with the largest gap on VOC20 (73.0% vs. 47.2% for OpenCLIP). The one area where FLAIR does not lead is zero-shot classification at 30M scale, where it ties DreamLIP (56.8% vs. 57.0% avg) and trails Llip-2.5B (79.0%) by ~22%, indicating that image diversity at scale remains critical for global-level recognition.

## Relation to Prior Work

FLAIR builds directly on CLIP's dual-encoder architecture and SigLIP's sigmoid-based contrastive loss, while extending DreamLIP's local-token supervision with long MLLM-generated captions. The key architectural departure is text-conditioned attention pooling: instead of DreamLIP's learnable global image token or Llip's language-informed learnable tokens, FLAIR uses the global text embedding as a query to adaptively pool local image patch tokens, producing a unique image representation per image-text pair. A critical methodological fix over DreamLIP is the negative pair definition—using ⟨v_i,j^tc, t_j^g⟩ rather than ⟨v_i,j^tc, t_i^g⟩—which prevents the model from exploiting a text-conditioning shortcut. The diverse caption sampling strategy (randomly merging 1–3 sentences from long descriptions) is a data-side contribution that complements the architectural change.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The most practically significant finding is the data-efficiency gap: FLAIR trained on 30M re-captioned images outperforms SigLIP trained on 10B samples on retrieval (81.1% vs. 75.6% T2I R@1 on Flickr30k) and achieves a 14.4% average mIoU improvement on zero-shot segmentation across five datasets. This suggests that text-conditioned local alignment, rather than sheer data scale, is the bottleneck for fine-grained vision-language understanding. However, the gains are task-specific—FLAIR-30M remains on par with DreamLIP-30M on zero-shot classification and far behind 2.5B-parameter models—indicating that synthetic captions enhance fine-grained alignment but do not substitute for broad image coverage. Open questions include whether scaling the re-captioning pipeline to 1B+ images closes the classification gap, and whether the text-conditioned pooling mechanism transfers to higher-resolution or video settings.
