## Problem

Despite significant accuracy gains on standard benchmarks, there is no systematic analysis of how optical flow methods respond to semantic changes in scene content—specifically, whether matching relies more on shape or texture, and whether methods memorize objects from training data. This gap is critical for safety-critical applications like autonomous driving, where objects may change appearance or be entirely novel.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250347/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The evaluation pipeline takes pairs of RGB images from KITTI and produces dense optical flow fields, but the key distinction is that input images are semantically modified: textures are replaced via diffusion models while preserving geometry, objects are geometrically rescaled, or real-world objects (seen or unseen) are composited into the scene. Robustness is measured as the sensitivity of the predicted flow to these modifications, i.e., the difference between flow on the original pair and flow on the modified pair, rather than absolute error against ground truth alone.

## Method

The paper introduces Flow-R, a synthetic test set with three subsets (Flow-R-T for texture replacement, Flow-R-S for shape rescaling, Flow-R-O for adding seen/unseen objects), constructed by combining SAM-based segmentation, ControlNet-guided Stable Diffusion for texture generation, geometric scaling with flow recalculation for shape changes, and affine-transformed object compositing for new objects. The evaluation framework treats texture, shape, and object identity as controlled variables in a sensitivity analysis, comparing flow predictions on original versus modified image pairs. To diagnose why methods fail on unseen objects, the authors perform feature visualization across encoder levels and decoder GRU iterations, revealing that encoders retain information about new objects while the GRU block progressively suppresses unseen-object features across refinement iterations, effectively treating them as noise.

## Results

On the fine-tuned (C+T+S/K) setting, texture replacement (Flow-R-T) causes only marginal degradation: e.g., RAFT goes from 0.63 AEPE / 1.5% Fl-all on KITTI to 0.73 / 1.66%, while FlowFormer++ goes from 0.57 / 1.16% to 0.71 / 1.55%. In contrast, shape replacement (Flow-R-S) causes a dramatic increase: RAFT jumps to 1.96 AEPE / 10.2% Fl-all, and FlowFormer++ to 1.95 / 10.1%, roughly a 7–9× increase in outlier rate. For adding unseen objects (Table 3), foreground AEPE on the new object reaches 20–34 pixels across all methods, compared to 7–13 pixels for seen objects, confirming that SOTA methods systematically ignore motion of objects not present in training data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an evaluation study rather than a method paper, so the 'comparison' is across seven existing optical flow methods (RAFT, GMA, GMFlow, FlowFormer, FlowFormer++, SAMFlow, PWC-Net) on the proposed Flow-R robustness benchmarks. All methods exhibit the same qualitative pattern: they are robust to texture changes (Flow-R-T AEPE within ~0.1–0.9 px of KITTI baseline) but highly sensitive to shape changes (Flow-R-S Fl-all increases 5–9× for fine-tuned models). Among the fine-tuned models, FlowFormer achieves the lowest KITTI AEPE (0.53) and Fl-all (1.11%), while SAMFlow is best on the pre-trained setting (3.44 AEPE, 12.3% Fl-all). On the unseen-object test (Table 3), no method distinguishes itself: all produce foreground AEPE of 20–34 px for unseen objects versus 7–13 px for seen objects, with FlowFormer++ and SAMFlow marginally best (20.53 and 20.57 AEPE) but still failing to track the added object. The paper's key finding is that architectural differences (CNN vs. transformer vs. MAE pretraining) do not resolve the shape-sensitivity or memorization issues, as all tested families degrade similarly.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the adversarial-attack line of work on optical flow robustness (Ranjan et al., Schrodi et al., Schmalfuss et al.) by replacing learned perturbations with natural, semantically meaningful modifications—shape scaling, texture replacement via ControlNet, and insertion of real-world unseen objects. It draws conceptual inspiration from the shape-vs.-texture bias literature in classification (Geirhos et al., Islam et al.) and repurposes SAM and diffusion models as data-generation tools for constructing the Flow-R benchmark. Rather than proposing a new optical flow architecture, the paper positions itself as a diagnostic study of existing SOTA methods (RAFT, GMA, GMFlow, FlowFormer, FlowFormer++, SAMFlow), filling a gap the authors identify: no prior work systematically probes how these methods respond to controlled semantic changes in scene content.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that SOTA optical flow methods effectively 'ignore' unseen objects and even hallucinate the motion of a fully occluded original object raises concrete safety concerns for autonomous driving and other safety-critical deployments where novel objects routinely appear. The shape-over-texture sensitivity result provides actionable guidance: methods that rely heavily on 4D cost-volume matching are vulnerable to scale changes that are common in real driving (objects approaching the camera). Limitations include the primary reliance on KITTI (a single driving domain), a small set of 13 unseen objects, and the dataset-specific definition of 'seen' vs. 'unseen,' which may not generalize to truly open-world scenarios. Open questions include whether the GRU memorization behavior can be mitigated through architectural changes, whether the findings hold for non-driving domains, and how to construct robustness benchmarks that cover the full diversity of real-world object categories.
