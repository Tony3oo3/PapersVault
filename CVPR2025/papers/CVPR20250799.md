## Problem

Visual-linguistic face forgery detection relies on high-quality text annotations, but both human labeling and direct MLLM generation suffer from hallucination—incorrectly identifying unaltered regions as forged—especially for high-quality forgeries. This annotation inaccuracy undermines the interpretability and generalization benefits that multimodal models are supposed to provide.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250799/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The annotation pipeline consumes paired real-fake face images (224×224 RGB) to produce structured text captions describing which facial regions (mouth, nose, eyes, face) are manipulated and what type of forgery is present (color difference, blur, structure abnormal, texture abnormal, blend boundary). For detection, a single face image is fed to either the CLIP-based classifier (producing a binary real/fake score) or the fine-tuned LLaVA model (producing a binary verdict plus a natural-language explanation of the manipulation artifacts).

## Method

FFTG generates annotations in two stages: (1) Raw Annotation Generation computes a pixel-wise difference mask between real and fake images, extracts the forged region via landmark-based area averaging with a threshold, and classifies the forgery type using handcrafted criteria (Lab color distance, Laplacian variance, SSIM, GLCM contrast, and boundary gradient/edge/frequency analysis); (2) Annotation Refinement feeds the paired images plus the raw annotation and its derivation rationale into GPT-4o-mini via a four-component prompt (visual pair, guide, task description, and predefined format) to produce diverse, accurate captions. These annotations then drive a three-branch CLIP fine-tuning framework combining image-only classification, contrastive image-text alignment, and cross-attention fusion classification, or alternatively fine-tune LLaVA's projector and LLM for joint detection and explanation.

## Results

On cross-dataset face forgery detection (trained on FF++ HQ, tested on unseen domains), the method achieves 94.81% AUC on DFD, 83.21% AUC on DFDC-P, 85.10% AUC on Wild Deepfake, and 83.15% AUC on Celeb-DF. These results represent consistent gains of 4–14 percentage points in AUC over the strongest prior baselines across all four unseen datasets. The method also achieves 99.16% AUC / 3.11% EER on the intra-domain FF++ test set, matching the best unimodal baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The method outperforms every baseline on all four unseen datasets. The largest margins are against DCL on Wild Deepfake (+13.96 pp AUC, 85.10 vs 71.14) and against UIA-ViT on DFDC-P (+7.41 pp AUC, 83.21 vs 75.80). On Celeb-DF, the method edges out the closest competitors PCL+I2G (81.80) and DCL (82.30) by 1.35 and 0.85 pp respectively, while beating the raw CLIP baseline by 5.99 pp. On DFD, the gain over UIA-ViT (94.68) is modest at +0.13 pp, but the method still leads DCL by 3.15 pp. The consistent 4–7 pp advantage over the unimodal CLIP baseline across all datasets confirms that the three-branch multimodal training framework with FFTG text annotations is the primary source of the improvement.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of visual-language pretraining (CLIP, LLaVA) and face forgery detection, building directly on CLIP's dual-encoder contrastive framework and the pixel-difference mask generation strategy from LRL (Chen et al., AAAI 2021). It departs from prior visual-linguistic forgery detection work (DD-VQA, FFAA, X2DFD) by identifying annotation hallucination as the core bottleneck and proposing FFTG—a mask-guided, handcrafted-feature-informed annotation pipeline with structured MLLM prompting—rather than introducing a new detection architecture. The three-branch training framework (image classification + contrastive alignment + cross-attention fusion) is a practical extension of CLIP fine-tuning tailored to the forgery detection setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that the quality of text annotations is a first-order factor in visual-linguistic forgery detection, and that structured, evidence-grounded prompting can make automated annotations more accurate than human labels (89.48% vs 62.46% precision). This has practical implications for scaling forgery detection datasets without expensive manual labeling. However, the approach requires paired real-fake images for mask generation, limiting applicability to datasets without ground-truth pairs (only FF++ provides this). Open questions include whether the handcrafted forgery-type criteria generalize to novel manipulation methods, and whether the annotation pipeline can be adapted to video-level or multi-frame forgery scenarios.
