## Problem

Generative image compression methods like HiFiC achieve excellent visual quality but require orders of magnitude more decoding complexity than commercial codecs, making them impractical for real-world deployment. This paper demonstrates that good perceived image quality does not require precisely modeling the natural image distribution; instead, optimizing for a perceptual metric that captures human texture perception in the visual periphery is sufficient to close the quality gap while retaining low decoding cost.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251231/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The codec is overfitted per image: a single RGB image is encoded by jointly optimizing multi-resolution latent arrays, a small synthesis CNN, and an entropy network, producing a bitstream of quantized, entropy-coded latents plus quantized network parameters. Decoding additionally requires common randomness—i.i.d. standard Gaussian noise drawn from a pseudo-random generator with a fixed seed shared between encoder and decoder—so no extra bits are needed. Evaluation targets three bit rates (0.075, 0.15, 0.3 bits/pixel) on the CLIC2020 professional validation set of 41 images.

## Method

Starting from the C3 overfitted codec architecture, the paper replaces the MSE distortion loss with Wasserstein Distortion (WD), which compares local first- and second-order statistics of VGG feature maps using a spatially varying pooling kernel size σ. A saliency map from EML-net is converted into a σ-map so that salient regions (likely to be fixated) use small σ (demanding exact detail) while peripheral regions use large σ (permitting texture resampling). Common randomness arrays of fixed-seed Gaussian noise are concatenated with upsampled latents at multiple resolutions, enabling stochastic textures to be reproduced via noise shaping without encoding them. An efficient approximate WD implementation discretizes σ to powers of two, precomputing local mean and standard deviation maps via a 3×3 downsampling cascade, then interpolating between adjacent scales with linearly fading weights. A human rater study with 10 methods confirms that WD-optimized C3 with CR and saliency achieves a quality-rate trade-off comparable to HiFiC while requiring less than 1% of its decoder MACs, and that WD itself predicts human Elo scores with 94% Pearson correlation.

## Results

In a human rater study on the CLIC2020 professional validation dataset (41 images, 3 target bit rates: 0.075, 0.15, 0.3 bpp), C3 optimized for Wasserstein Distortion (WD) achieves a visual quality vs. bit rate trade-off comparable to the generative codec HiFiC, while requiring less than 1% of the decoder MACs (two orders of magnitude fewer). As an image quality assessment metric, WD achieves a Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) of 0.942 with Elo scores and correctly predicts 72.87% of individual binary ratings, substantially outperforming LPIPS (PCC 0.711), DISTS (PCC 0.726), and MS-SSIM (PCC 0.540). The paper also shows that adding common randomness (CR) and saliency-based σ-maps to WD-optimized C3 further improves perceived quality, particularly at low and high bit rates respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

C3/WD (with CR and saliency) achieves a quality-rate trade-off nearly matching HiFiC [31] in the human rater study, but requires over two orders of magnitude fewer decoder MACs, making it the first method to be simultaneously good, cheap, and fast. Against MSE-optimized baselines (C3/MSE [22], MLIC+ [21], VVC [1]), C3/WD shows a clear and consistent Elo advantage across all three bit rates, with the gap widening at lower bit rates where texture resampling matters most. The diffusion-based codec CDC [41] performs at a similar level to MSE-optimized methods, confirming that generative modeling alone does not guarantee perceptual quality. As an IQA metric, WD (PCC 0.936–0.942) dramatically outperforms LPIPS (0.711), DISTS (0.726), PIM-5 (0.764), MS-SSIM (0.540), and NLPD (0.539) in predicting Elo rankings, and also surpasses all of them in predicting individual binary ratings (72.87% vs. 69.96% for LPIPS). The paper notes that LPIPS required an equal-weight MSE term for training stability, whereas WD did not, suggesting WD's local-statistics formulation is inherently more robust as an optimization objective.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on C3 (Kim et al., 2024), a low-complexity overfitted neural codec from the COOL-CHIC family, and replaces its MSE distortion with Wasserstein Distortion (WD, Qiu et al., 2024), a perceptual metric that models foveal and peripheral vision through local statistical comparisons in VGG feature space. Conceptually, it departs from the dominant trend in learned image compression of leveraging generative models (HiFiC, CDC) to sample from the natural image distribution, arguing instead that modeling human texture perception is sufficient for high visual quality. The addition of common randomness (shared pseudo-random noise between encoder and decoder) and saliency-guided σ-maps (via EML-net) are novel contributions that enable texture resampling in non-salient regions while preserving detail where the eye is likely to focus.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that the 'good, cheap, and fast' trilemma in image compression can be resolved without generative models: C3+WD matches HiFiC's quality-rate trade-off while requiring less than 1% of the decoding MACs, making it deployable on mobile hardware where diffusion- or GAN-based codecs are infeasible. WD's 94% Pearson correlation with human Elo scores positions it as a strong candidate for both training loss and quality assessment, though the ad-hoc choices of VGG features, σ-map derivation, and single-dataset evaluation (41 CLIC2020 images) leave open questions about generalizability. Encoding complexity increases ~6× over MSE-optimized C3, and the authors acknowledge that better saliency models, feature selection, and larger-scale human studies are needed before the approach can be considered fully validated.
