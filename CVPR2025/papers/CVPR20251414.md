## Problem

Kinematic motion generation models produce physically implausible artifacts (foot sliding, floating, penetration) in interactive environments, while physics-based approaches are computationally expensive and difficult to train. A critical unresolved issue is the domain gap: when a physics simulator refines kinematic poses, the resulting simulated poses drift outside the learned motion prior, and in autoregressive generation this accumulated discrepancy causes progressive motion failure. POMP addresses this by semantically aligning kinematic and simulated poses within a shared phase manifold.

## Input / Output

At each frame, the model consumes a 541-dim motion variable vector comprising the previous phase encoding (5 channels over a 12-frame window), joint positions and velocities (31 joints), an 8-type gait one-hot vector, user-controlled 2D trajectory positions/directions, surrounding terrain heights, and full-body contact impulses (15 limb colliders). It outputs a 388-dim vector containing predicted phase variables, target joint positions and velocities, root transformation, and future trajectory. The physics simulator (Nvidia PhysX in Unity) operates as a fixed, non-differentiable plug-in outside the gradient loop, and the entire pipeline runs in real time with only 5 DDIM inference steps on a consumer GPU.

## Method

POMP is a frame-by-frame autoregressive architecture with three decoupled modules. The kinematic module uses an Ortho-MoE encoder (8 MLP expert branches with unsupervised orthogonality constraints on 1024-dim principle components) to produce a phase motion embedding, which conditions a diffusion decoder operating in the phase domain with 5 DDIM steps and an Analytic-DPM analytic variance estimate for fast, high-quality sampling. The dynamic module applies a PD servo controller to compute joint torques, then executes forward dynamics in PhysX to produce a physically plausible pose and contact impulses. The key contribution is the Aligned-PAE phase encoding module: it first learns a continuous 5-channel phase manifold (amplitude, frequency, offset, phase shift via differentiable FFT) from MoCap data alone, then in a second training stage aligns simulated poses to the same phase ellipse as their kinematic counterparts but at different phase positions, effectively projecting simulation results back onto the motion prior. Training is two-stage (phase encoder first, then kinematic module with frozen phase encoder), and only the kinematic and phase modules require gradient-based optimization.

## Results

On the human-terrain dataset, POMP achieves an FMD of 0.92 and foot skating of 0.01, substantially outperforming all kinematic baselines (best competitor: StyleERD at FMD 2.31). On 100STYLE, POMP reaches FMD 1.02 with 100 motion styles, compared to RTSM's 3.15 and Motion Puzzle's 9.44. Against physics-based methods on AMASS, POMP achieves 100% test success with 29.8 mm MPJPE, surpassing PHC (99.2%, 36.1 mm), MaskedMimic (99.2%, 35.1 mm), and PULSE (97.1%, 54.1 mm). The trade-off is runtime: POMP requires 42.42 ms per frame versus 7.55 ms for PFNN, though it still runs in real time on a 4060 GPU in ONNX format.

## Baselines & Comparisons

POMP dominates all kinematic baselines on motion quality: it reduces FMD by 11× over PFNN (0.92 vs 10.25), 7.5× over Mason et al. (0.92 vs 6.98), 2.5× over StyleERD (0.92 vs 2.31), 3.4× over RTSM (0.92 vs 3.15), and 10× over Motion Puzzle (0.92 vs 9.44), while also achieving the lowest foot skating (0.01). The primary cost is runtime (42.42 ms vs 7.55–22.85 ms for competitors) and parameter count (22.45M vs 0.48M–18.42M), though POMP remains real-time. Against physics-based RL methods on AMASS, POMP wins on both success rate (100% vs 97.1–99.2%) and MPJPE (29.8 mm vs 35.1–54.1 mm), while requiring only 17.2 hours on a single 3090 GPU compared to one week on A100 for PHC and two weeks on four A100s for MaskedMimic. The Ortho-MoE ablation (POMP-MoE, FMD 1.42) confirms that the orthogonality constraint contributes a 0.5 FMD improvement without extra parameters or compute.

## Relation to Prior Work

POMP sits at the intersection of three research threads: phase-based kinematic motion generation (PFNN, MANN, NSM), physics-based character control via reinforcement learning (PHC, MaskedMimic, PULSE), and plug-in dynamics refinement of kinematic models (DROP). Its key departure from DROP is the introduction of a phase-manifold alignment module (Aligned-PAE) that semantically projects simulated poses back onto the kinematic motion prior, rather than relying on projective-dynamics optimization that introduces a domain gap. The Ortho-MoE encoder extends the MoE gating idea from PFNN/MANN with an unsupervised orthogonality constraint to enforce disentangled phase-channel learning, while the diffusion decoder with Analytic-DPM variance estimation enables 5-step inference in the phase domain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

POMP offers a practical, real-time pipeline (ONNX-compatible, Unity/PhysX-integrated) that produces physically plausible motions without the multi-week RL training costs of PHC or MaskedMimic, making it attractive for game and VR/AR applications. The phase-manifold alignment concept—treating the phase ellipse as a shared 'song' where kinematic and simulated poses occupy different 'rhythms'—provides a principled mechanism for bridging the kinematic-dynamic domain gap that plagues prior plug-in approaches. Open questions include whether the autoregressive error accumulation is fundamentally bounded by the phase projection, how the method scales to non-humanoid or multi-character scenarios, and whether the Unity/PhysX dependency limits generalizability to other physics engines.
