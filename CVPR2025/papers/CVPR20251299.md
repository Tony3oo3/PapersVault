## Problem

Existing video understanding benchmarks are dominated by short clips (a few seconds to ~3 minutes), offer limited diversity in video genres and task types, and often include questions that can be answered from a single frame or from well-known narrative trivia without genuinely processing the long video. This makes them inadequate for evaluating whether MLLMs can truly comprehend and reason over extended visual sequences.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251299/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance pairs a long video (3 min to over 2 hours, spanning movies, surveillance, egocentric, cartoon, game, and documentary genres) with a text prompt. MLLMs consume the video via uniform frame sampling (e.g., 16–256 frames) or frame-rate sampling (e.g., 0.5–1 fps), and produce either a multiple-choice selection (4 candidates in the dev set, 6 in the test set) or a free-form text response (summary or caption). Videos are additionally segmented into incremental prefixes (first 3 min, first 6 min, full duration) so the same task can be probed at different lengths.

## Method

MLVU organizes 3,102 questions into 9 tasks grouped by three levels of temporal scope: holistic LVU (Topic Reasoning, Anomaly Recognition, Video Summarization) requiring global comprehension; single-detail LVU (Needle QA, Ego Reasoning, Plot QA, Sub-Scene Captioning) requiring localization of one relevant segment; and multi-detail LVU (Action Order, Action Count) requiring joint reasoning over multiple scattered segments. The Needle QA task is directly inspired by the text-domain Needle-In-the-Haystack-Search: a short 'needle' clip is randomly inserted into a long background video, and the question must be answerable only from that clip. Action Order and Action Count similarly insert probe clips from Kinetics or ActivityNet-Caption into background videos from a curated Universal Long Video Collection (ULVC), with manual verification that the target action does not already appear in the background. Generation tasks (Video Summarization, Sub-Scene Captioning) are scored by GPT-4 against human-annotated references, while all multiple-choice tasks use accuracy. The benchmark is evaluated zero-shot across 23 MLLMs (image, short-video, and long-video categories), and a derivative MLVU Time-ladder dataset enables controlled analysis of performance degradation as video length increases from 180 s to 600 s.

## Results

On the MLVU test set, GPT-4o achieves the best overall performance with an M-Avg of 54.5% (multiple-choice) and G-Avg of 5.87/10 (generation), outperforming all 22 other evaluated MLLMs. The best open-source long-video model, LLaVA-OneVision, reaches 51.7% M-Avg and 4.42 G-Avg, trailing GPT-4o by only 2.8% on close-ended tasks. However, even GPT-4o struggles with fine-grained tasks: it scores only 42.9% on Needle QA (NQA) and 35.0% on Action Count (AC), while most baselines perform near random (16.7%) on multi-detail tasks like Action Order and Action Count.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4o leads all 22 baselines on both M-Avg (54.5%) and G-Avg (5.87), with the largest margins on generation tasks (Video Summarization: 4.94 vs. next-best 3.75 for LLaVA-OneVision; Sub-Scene Captioning: 6.80 vs. 5.25 for InternVL-2). Among open-source long-video MLLMs, LLaVA-OneVision (51.7% M-Avg) narrows the gap to GPT-4o to just 2.8%, while Video-XL (46.3%) and VideoLLaMA2 (48.4%) trail by 8.2% and 6.1% respectively. On multi-detail tasks, only GPT-4o (AO: 46.2, AC: 35.0) and Video-XL (AO: 48.6, AC: 31.7) substantially exceed the 16.7% random baseline; all other 20 models score at or near chance on Action Order and Action Count. Short-video MLLMs (e.g., Video-LLaMA-2 at 18.8%, VideoChat at 17.7%) and early long-video models (MovieChat at 16.5%, LLaMA-Vid at 18.1%) perform close to random across most tasks, highlighting that dedicated long-video training is essential. Notably, even GPT-4o achieves only 42.9% on Needle QA, far below the near-perfect scores that long-context LLMs attain on analogous text-domain retrieval benchmarks, indicating that visual needle-in-haystack remains an open challenge.

## Relation to Prior Work

MLVU carves new ground in the video understanding benchmark landscape by addressing three specific gaps identified in prior work: insufficient video length (most benchmarks use clips under 1 minute), lack of genre diversity (prior long-video benchmarks focus on a single type like movies or egocentric footage), and task designs that can be shortcut without genuine video comprehension (e.g., questions about well-known movie plots answerable from text alone). The Needle Question-Answering task explicitly repurposes the text-domain NIHS (Needle-In-the-Haystack-Search) paradigm for video, while the multi-detail tasks (action ordering, action counting) draw on probe-insertion techniques from Kinetics and ActivityNet-Caption. Unlike concurrent benchmarks Video-MME and LongVideoBench, MLVU uniquely combines all three properties—substantial length extension (3 min to 2 hours), diverse genres (surveillance, cartoons, games, egocentric), and nine task types spanning holistic, single-detail, and multi-detail understanding—into a single unified evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that long video understanding remains a fundamentally unsolved problem: even GPT-4o achieves only 54.5% on multiple-choice tasks and a mere 42.9% on NQA, while analogous text-domain needle tasks are effectively solved by long-context LLMs. The near-total failure of most models on multi-detail tasks (action ordering, action counting) and the consistent performance degradation with increasing video length highlight that current MLLMs lack robust mechanisms for integrating and reasoning over distributed visual information. The ablation findings—that context length, image-understanding ability, and LLM backbone choice each independently contribute to LVU performance—provide a concrete roadmap for future model development. Open questions include whether the gap between text and video needle retrieval can be closed through better visual tokenization, and whether multi-detail reasoning requires fundamentally new architectural components beyond longer context windows.
