## Problem

Existing Large Reconstruction Models (LRMs) produce view-independent radiance fields that cannot model glossy or specular appearances, struggle to reconstruct unseen object parts without hallucinating, and cannot decompose geometry, materials, and lighting into relightable 3D content compatible with standard graphics pipelines. Optimization-based inverse rendering methods address these issues but require dense views and minutes-to-hours of computation, making them impractical for interactive 3D content creation.

## Input / Output

LIRM consumes sparse posed images (as few as 3–6 per update step, added progressively in sets) along with their camera intrinsics/extrinsics encoded as Plücker rays and background images that provide environmental lighting cues. Images are split into 8×8 patches and tokenized via a linear layer. The output is a hexa-plane neural SDF (48×48 tokens per plane, decoded to 384×384 resolution) plus four 128×128 NDE panoramas, from which small MLPs decode SDF values, normals, albedo, metallic, roughness, and view-dependent RGB color at any 3D query point, yielding a fully relightable representation usable in standard renderers.

## Method

LIRM is a 24-block, 16-head, 1024-dim self-attention transformer that jointly updates hexa-plane tokens and NDE panorama tokens from image tokens. Its key architectural innovation is an update module: after each forward pass, the predicted 3D tokens are fused with learned positional encodings via a two-layer MLP and fed back into the transformer alongside new image tokens, enabling progressive refinement with additional views at constant GPU memory. For 3D representation, a hexa-plane (6 planes dividing the bounding box into 8 sub-volumes) replaces the standard tri-plane, better separating textures on opposite sides of the object; SDF-based volume ray marching (Yariv et al.) renders geometry and material maps. View-dependent effects are modeled by predicting four NDE feature panoramas through the transformer, with a per-point MLP selecting which panorama to query via trilinear interpolation in angular space, capturing near-field reflections and occlusion without expensive multi-bounce computation. Training uses a three-stage coarse-to-fine schedule (increasing resolution, samples per ray, and decreasing SDF sharpness β) with L2 losses on rendered RGB, normals, and material maps plus LPIPS on RGB and albedo, taking ~2 weeks on 64 H100 GPUs.

## Results

On the Stanford-ORB real-world benchmark, LIRM-NDE (3rd update) achieves relighting PSNR-H of 24.60 / PSNR-L of 32.05, view synthesis PSNR-H of 27.03 / PSNR-L of 35.26, and the best shape Chamfer Distance of 0.31 among all compared methods. On synthetic ABO and GSO datasets under uniform lighting, LIRM-hexa (3rd update) reaches PSNR 32.98 and 30.65 respectively, outperforming MeshLRM (28.31 / 28.13) and GS-LRM (29.59 / 30.52). The model completes each inference step in ~0.3 s on an A100 GPU, compared to minutes-to-hours for optimization-based baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the concurrent LRM-based method MetaLRM [69], LIRM-NDE 3rd wins by a large margin on every metric (e.g., relighting PSNR-H 24.60 vs 21.46, view synthesis PSNR-L 35.26 vs 26.20). Versus the strongest optimization-based baseline Neural-PBIR [72], LIRM-NDE 3rd trails on relighting PSNR-H (24.60 vs 26.01) and view synthesis PSNR-H (27.03 vs 28.82) but achieves the best shape Chamfer Distance (0.31 vs 0.43) and the lowest view-synthesis LPIPS (0.018 vs 0.019), all in under a second versus hours of optimization. LIRM-diff 1st already reaches the second-best relighting PSNR-H (24.76) with only one set of 4 input views. On the synthetic GSO and ABO benchmarks, LIRM-hexa 3rd (PSNR 30.65 / 32.98) surpasses both MeshLRM [84] (28.13 / 28.31) and GS-LRM [99] (30.52 / 29.59) for radiance-field view synthesis, with the hexa-plane representation contributing a consistent 1–4 dB gain over the tri-plane variant. LIRM also outperforms NVDiffrecMc [24] and InvRender [103] on shape accuracy (CD 0.31 vs 0.51 and 0.44) while matching or exceeding their view-synthesis quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

LIRM extends the Large Reconstruction Model (LRM) lineage—specifically MeshLRM—by addressing three gaps that limit prior feed-forward 3D reconstruction: the inability to model view-dependent effects, the difficulty of reconstructing unseen object parts, and the absence of decomposed PBR materials compatible with standard graphics pipelines. It incorporates Neural Directional Encoding (NDE) for angular appearance modeling and SDF-based volume ray marching for geometry, but restructures both to operate within a single feed-forward transformer rather than per-scene optimization. The progressive update module, which feeds prior 3D tokens back through self-attention with new image tokens, departs from prior LRMs that treat all input views simultaneously and cannot scale beyond a fixed view count without quadratic memory growth.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LIRM demonstrates that sub-second, feed-forward inverse rendering with relightable PBR output is now practical, collapsing what previously required hours of per-scene optimization and dense multi-view capture into a single forward pass on 3–6 posed images. The progressive update mechanism offers a practical interaction model for content creators who can iteratively add views to fill in unseen regions without re-running the full pipeline. Open questions include how well the approach generalizes to non-convex or highly transparent objects, whether the NDE panorama workaround for near-field reflections scales to complex interreflection scenarios, and whether the 2-week/64-GPU training cost limits adoption outside well-resourced labs.
