## Problem

Most state-of-the-art inverse rendering methods require HDR images as input, which demands specialized capture hardware or exposure bracketing, limiting accessibility for casual users. LDR images lose critical lighting information through sensor saturation and non-linear camera response function (CRF) mapping, and existing LDR-based approaches rely on simplifying assumptions (distant lights, provided emitter masks, single-bounce transport) that fail for complex indoor scenes with spatially-varying illumination.

## Input / Output

The method takes multi-view 8-bit LDR images (PNG/JPEG) with known camera poses and a surface mesh (reconstructed via BakedSDF), supporting both constant and varying exposure levels across views. It produces a spatially-varying HDR emission field on the mesh, a neural-field material representation (albedo, roughness, metallicity via multiresolution hash encoding + MLP), and a shared CRF parameterized in the EMoR PCA basis. These decomposed components enable downstream relighting, object insertion, and novel-view synthesis under arbitrary illumination.

## Method

IRIS employs a multi-stage alternating optimization that resolves the ill-posedness of jointly recovering lighting, material, and CRF from LDR observations. The pipeline first initializes BRDF from monocular albedo predictions, then recovers HDR emitter radiance via single-bounce differentiable path tracing with a photometric loss that explicitly models the LDR formation (clipping + CRF), bakes diffuse/specular shading maps using multi-bounce path tracing, and finally jointly optimizes BRDF and CRF with albedo, CRF monotonicity, and material consistency regularizers. A key design choice is the EMoR-based CRF parameterization (PCA basis over 201 real-world camera curves), which keeps the non-linear tone-mapping differentiable and regularizable. Emitter geometry is automatically identified from cross-view saturation, and global illumination is approximated via a pre-computed surface light field to avoid expensive multi-bounce tracing during the emission recovery stage.

## Results

On the four synthetic FIPT scenes, IRIS achieves 22.33 PSNR for diffuse reflectance (k_d), 17.92 PSNR for material reflectance (a′), and 21.38 PSNR for roughness (σ), outperforming all LDR-input baselines by a wide margin. For emission recovery, IRIS attains 0.69 IoU and 0.12 L2 error on the emission map. In downstream relighting, IRIS reaches 22.86 PSNR / 0.882 SSIM / 0.173 LPIPS, surpassing the best LDR baseline (Li et al., 22.44 PSNR) and substantially outperforming FIPT* (11.63 PSNR). The gap to the privileged FIPT-with-HDR reference (29.95 PSNR for k_d) is the smallest among all LDR methods, indicating that IRIS recovers the most complete lighting information from LDR observations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

IRIS outperforms all three LDR baselines on every reported metric. Against Li et al. [25] (single-image data-driven), IRIS gains +5.6 PSNR on k_d, +3.9 on a′, +10.1 on σ, and reduces emission L2 error from 2.29 to 0.12, reflecting the advantage of multi-view physically-based optimization over single-image prediction. Against NeILF [45], IRIS improves k_d by +5.5 PSNR and σ by +4.4 PSNR; NeILF does not report emission metrics, but qualitatively fails to remove shadows from diffuse reflectance and misidentifies the emission mask. Against FIPT* (FIPT modified to accept LDR input with a provided emitter mask), IRIS leads by +6.8 PSNR on k_d, +8.2 on a′, and +16.4 on σ, while matching its emission IoU (0.69) and halving the emission L2 error (0.12 vs. 0.28). The only method that surpasses IRIS is the original FIPT with true HDR input, which serves as an upper-bound reference; IRIS narrows the gap to this reference more than any other LDR method, demonstrating that its HDR restoration pipeline recovers most of the lost lighting information.

## Relation to Prior Work

IRIS builds directly on the factorized light transport formulation from FIPT [43] for efficient BRDF-lighting decomposition, and adopts BakedSDF [46] and MonoSDF [49] for geometry reconstruction. The key departure is in the image formation model: whereas FIPT and NeILF++ assume HDR input or a known/learnable gamma correction, IRIS explicitly models the full LDR pipeline (sensor clipping, non-linear CRF via the EMoR parameterization from Grossberg & Nayar [11]) and recovers HDR emission through a dedicated single-bounce path-tracing stage. The multi-stage alternating optimization (HDR restoration → shading bake → joint BRDF/CRF update) is the paper's central methodological contribution, distinguishing it from prior single-loop differentiable path tracing approaches that struggle with the lighting-material-CRF ambiguity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By removing the HDR capture requirement, IRIS makes physically-based inverse rendering accessible to users with standard cameras or phones, broadening the practical applicability of relighting and object insertion. The ablation reveals that the CRF regularization and HDR restoration stages are individually critical (removing either drops roughness PSNR by >10 points), underscoring that the LDR-to-HDR inversion is not a minor preprocessing step but a core part of the inverse problem. Limitations include the assumption that primary emitters are visible in the input views, reliance on externally reconstructed geometry (BakedSDF), and evaluation on only four synthetic scenes for quantitative metrics, leaving open questions about scalability to larger or more complex indoor environments and to occluded or off-camera light sources.
