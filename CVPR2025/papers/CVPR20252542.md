## Problem

Real-time rendering super-resolution requires balancing reconstruction quality, temporal stability, and inference latency, but existing approaches either indiscriminately concatenate G-buffers with low-resolution frames (limiting useful information transfer), rely on radiance demodulation that fails on translucent and anisotropic materials, or use bidirectional video super-resolution that is too slow for real-time use. The paper asks how to selectively exploit different G-buffer components for their distinct roles—spatial detail versus temporal alignment—within a single lightweight network.

## Input / Output

The network takes a low-resolution rendered frame (270×480) together with four decoupled G-buffer channels—surface normal, BRDF, depth, and motion vector—and produces a 4× upsampled high-resolution frame (1080×1920) via PixelShuffle. The G-buffer components are not concatenated as a single input; instead, normal and BRDF are routed to the high-frequency detail pathway while depth and motion vector are routed to the temporal alignment pathway. Training uses 180 clips (100 frames each) from 20 Cycles-rendered scenes, and evaluation is on 8 held-out scenes of 200 frames.

## Method

RDG is an asymmetric UNet that decouples G-buffer guidance into two functional pathways. The encoder stacks Dynamic Feature Modulators (DFMs) that use asymmetric deformable sampling (1×7 and 7×1) to capture non-local spatial structure without the smoothing of pooling or the parameter cost of large-kernel depthwise convolutions. In the decoder, a High-Frequency Feature Booster (HFB) applies a cross-gating mechanism to fuse encoder features with projected normal/BRDF features, adaptively transferring high-frequency texture; a Cross-Frame Temporal Refiner (CTR) warps the previous hidden feature by the motion vector, then uses a partial cross-attention (splitting the query in half) with depth-map constraints to correct misalignment errors before fusing with the current frame. The model is trained end-to-end with a combined Charbonnier, temporal-consistency, and FFT frequency loss, and two channel-width variants (RDG-s at 16 channels / 0.304 M params / 7.93 ms and RDG at 36 channels / 1.474 M params / 18.78 ms) are provided for different latency budgets.

## Results

On the 8-scene rendering test set, RDG (1.474M params) achieves an average PSNR of 29.69 dB, SSIM of 0.8494, and VMAF of 88.96, outperforming all baselines. The lightweight RDG-s (0.304M params) reaches 29.37 dB PSNR and 87.22 VMAF at only 7.93 ms per-frame latency on an RTX 3090, producing 1080P output at 126 FPS. RDG uses 101.76 MB peak GPU memory and 18.78 ms latency, substantially lower than BasicVSR++ (404.60 MB, 67.12 ms) and NSRR (766.97 MB, 66.86 ms).

## Baselines & Comparisons

RDG outperforms the radiance-demodulation methods NSRD and FuseSR by a large margin: +3.40 dB PSNR over NSRD (29.69 vs. 26.29) and +1.95 dB over FuseSR (29.69 vs. 27.74), while also achieving far higher VMAF (88.96 vs. 67.47 and 71.83), indicating the decoupled G-buffer guidance avoids the severe re-modulation artifacts those methods produce on translucent/anisotropic materials. Against the direct-concatenation baseline NSRR, RDG gains +0.35 dB PSNR and +6.61 VMAF points while using 7.5× less GPU memory (101.76 MB vs. 766.97 MB) and 3.6× lower latency. Compared to the VSR baselines BasicVSR and BasicVSR++ (both adjusted for faster inference), RDG achieves +0.39 dB and +0.16 dB PSNR respectively, and a +4.57 VMAF advantage over BasicVSR++, while running at 18.78 ms versus 40.34 ms and 67.12 ms. The lightweight RDG-s matches or exceeds SAFMN and SMFANet in PSNR (29.37 vs. 29.33 and 29.12) with a 2.6× latency advantage (7.93 ms vs. ~21 ms) and a +5.6 VMAF gain over SAFMN, demonstrating that the decoupled G-buffer design yields superior temporal consistency even at very low model complexity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a specific niche at the intersection of video super-resolution, efficient super-resolution, and real-time rendering super-sampling. It departs from bidirectional VSR methods (BasicVSR, BasicVSR++) that are incompatible with causal rendering pipelines, from single-frame ESR methods (SAFMN, SMFANet) that lack temporal coherence, and from prior rendering SR approaches (NSRR, NSRD, FuseSR) that either indiscriminately concatenate G-buffer channels or rely on radiance demodulation. The key conceptual departure is the decoupled G-buffer guidance: rather than treating all G-buffer components as a monolithic input, the authors route normal and BRDF through a high-frequency feature booster for spatial detail, and depth and motion vectors through a cross-frame temporal refiner for temporal stability, within an asymmetric UNet backbone.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for real-time rendering pipelines (gaming, VR, film previsualization), where RDG-s achieves 126 FPS at 1080P on an RTX 3090 with only 0.3M parameters, offering a compelling quality-efficiency trade-off. The avoidance of radiance demodulation artifacts on translucent and anisotropic materials addresses a concrete failure mode of prior methods like NSRD and FuseSR. However, the evaluation is limited to Cycles-rendered synthetic scenes with a fixed 4× upscaling factor and a specific set of G-buffer channels, leaving open questions about generalization to other rendering engines, variable upscaling ratios, and real-world (non-synthetic) video. Whether the decoupled G-buffer strategy transfers to settings where G-buffer availability is partial or noisy remains unexplored.
