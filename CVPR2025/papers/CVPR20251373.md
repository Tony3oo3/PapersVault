## Problem

Image generation and editing tasks (instructive editing, customization, object insertion, controllable generation, etc.) are typically addressed by separate models with task-specific architectures and curated datasets, limiting knowledge transfer and increasing engineering overhead. The paper asks whether a single framework can unify these diverse tasks by exploiting their shared requirement of preserving input-output consistency while introducing controlled visual variation.

## Input / Output

UniReal accepts an arbitrary number of input images (categorized as canvas, asset, or control) alongside a free-form text prompt, and produces an arbitrary number of output images. Images are referenced in the prompt via special tokens (IMG1, IMG2, RES1, RES2) that are linked to visual tokens through learned index embeddings. The model handles variable aspect ratios and resolutions up to 1024×1024, and the hierarchical context/image prompts are auto-derived from the base prompt at inference, so users only need to write a natural-language instruction.

## Method

UniReal reformulates image generation and editing as discontinuous video generation: input and output images are treated as frames in a pseudo-video processed by a 5B-parameter diffusion transformer with full attention. Each image is VAE-encoded, patchified into visual tokens, and augmented with position embeddings, timestep embeddings (for noise tokens), learned image index embeddings, and category embeddings (canvas/asset/control) that serve as the image prompt. A T5 encoder processes the text prompt, and all tokens are concatenated into a single 1D sequence for the transformer. Training uses flow matching loss and leverages large-scale video frame pairs as universal supervision—the natural consistency and variation between discontinuous video frames implicitly cover add/remove/attribute-change/pose-change operations without task-specific data curation. A hierarchical prompting scheme (context prompt for task attributes like 'realistic style' or 'static scenario', plus image prompt for input roles) reduces ambiguity when mixing multiple tasks and data sources during joint training.

## Results

On the MagicBrush test set for instructive image editing, UniReal achieves the best CLIP_dir (0.151), CLIP_im (0.903), and CLIP_out (0.308), outperforming the strongest baseline EMU Edit (0.135, 0.897, 0.261) by 12%, 0.7%, and 18% respectively. On the EMU Edit test set, UniReal leads in CLIP_dir (0.127) and CLIP_out (0.285). For customized generation on DreamBench, UniReal achieves the best CLIP-T (0.326) while maintaining competitive CLIP-I (0.806) and DINO (0.702) scores, surpassing OmniGen (0.320/0.810/0.693) and SuTI (0.304/0.819/0.741) in instruction-following.

## Baselines & Comparisons

UniReal outperforms all task-specific editing baselines (InstructPix2Pix, MagicBrush, UltraEdit, EMU Edit, PixWizard) on the primary instruction-following metrics CLIP_dir and CLIP_out across both test sets, with the largest margin over EMU Edit on MagicBrush (CLIP_dir: 0.151 vs 0.135; CLIP_out: 0.308 vs 0.261). It also surpasses the universal models OmniGen and ACE on instruction-following, though ACE achieves higher CLIP_im (0.895) and DINO (0.862) on EMU Edit, reflecting its stronger preservation of unedited regions. On DreamBench for customized generation, UniReal leads all zero-shot methods in CLIP-T (0.326 vs OmniGen 0.320, SuTI 0.304) but trails SuTI in DINO (0.702 vs 0.741) and CLIP-I (0.806 vs 0.819), a trade-off the authors attribute to prompts requiring attribute edits. For object insertion, UniReal qualitatively outperforms AnyDoor by handling pose/status changes and preserving background without requiring input masks. In user studies, UniReal is preferred over SuTI, OmniGen, UltraEdit, and AnyDoor across detail preservation, alignment, and image quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

UniReal departs from prior universal image generation/editing models (OmniGen, PixWizard, ACE, InstructImagen) that typically use autoregressive or mixed causal/bidirectional attention over tokenized inputs. Instead, it borrows the architectural principles of video diffusion transformers (Sora-type models) and reformulates image tasks as 'discontinuous frame generation,' using full attention across a variable number of image 'frames.' The training loss follows flow matching, and the data strategy is the key conceptual departure: rather than curating task-specific editing pairs, the paper constructs supervision from natural frame-to-frame variations in large-scale video, leveraging off-the-shelf tools (Kosmos-2, SAM2, GPT-4o mini) to auto-generate instructions, masks, and object references.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The demonstration that video frame pairs alone can teach a model broad editing capabilities (add, remove, attribute change, pose change) and even generalize to multi-image composition without explicit multi-image training data is a scalable data strategy that reduces the bottleneck of hand-curated editing datasets. The hierarchical prompting scheme (context + image + base prompt) offers a practical mechanism for task disambiguation in multi-task training. However, stability degrades beyond ~5 input images, computation is intensive, and the quantitative margins over strong baselines (EMU Edit, UltraEdit) are moderate, leaving open whether the video-supervision advantage persists at higher resolutions or with more diverse task distributions.
