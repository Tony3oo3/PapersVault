## Problem

Large pre-trained vision models achieve strong monocular depth estimation without explicit depth supervision during pre-training, yet it remains unclear whether they develop the human-like monocular depth cues (elevation, light-shadow, occlusion, perspective, size, texture gradient) that underpin human depth perception. Existing benchmarks evaluate end-to-end depth estimation accuracy but do not probe whether models actually understand the individual visual cues that drive depth inference.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250070/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each of the six DepthCues tasks takes an RGB image (and, for object-level cues, one or two segmentation masks) as input. Task-specific features are constructed by masked spatial pooling of frozen model features for object/region cues, or by using the full image feature map for global cues (elevation, perspective). Outputs are binary classification accuracy for four cues and a threshold-based success rate for the two regression cues (horizon line and vanishing point estimation). The fine-tuning experiments additionally produce dense depth maps via linear probing on NYUv2 and DIW.

## Method

DepthCues comprises six probing tasks, each adapted from an existing dataset (HLW, SOBA, COCOA, NaturalScene, KITTI/SUN-RGBD, DTD) to isolate a single monocular depth cue. For each task, a lightweight probe (a 2-layer MLP with GELU for binary classification, or an attentive cross-attention probe for regression) is trained on frozen features extracted from 20 diverse pre-trained vision models, with the best layer selected via validation performance. The key insight is that probe learnability on a cue task serves as a proxy for the model's internal understanding of that cue, enabling systematic comparison across models with different pre-training objectives (self-supervised, generative, vision-language, multi-view reconstruction). The paper further demonstrates that multi-task fine-tuning on DepthCues via LoRA, even with sparse image-level annotations, improves downstream depth estimation when fine-tuned features are concatenated with original features.

## Results

Across 20 pre-trained vision models evaluated on six monocular depth cue tasks, DepthAnythingv2 achieves the highest overall performance, followed by DINOv2 and Stable Diffusion in the top five. On downstream depth estimation (linear probing), DINOv2 achieves 87.78% accuracy on NYUv2 and 11.99% WHDR on DIW, while CLIP lags significantly at 43.78% and 35.25% respectively. Fine-tuning DINOv2 on DepthCues and concatenating features with the original model yields the best depth estimation at 88.46% NYUv2 accuracy and 11.72% DIW WHDR, outperforming the unmodified DINOv2. Human evaluators achieve 95% ± 1.48% accuracy across tasks, confirming the benchmark is solvable.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DepthAnythingv2, a specialized depth estimation model, outperforms all other models on the overall DepthCues benchmark, while the dedicated depth model MiDaS is notably outperformed by several models pre-trained without any depth supervision (DeiT, SigLIP, DINO), suggesting that dense depth supervision is not the only path to strong depth cue understanding. Among vision-language models, CLIP achieves the lowest performance across all six cues, whereas SigLIP—trained on approximately 9× more data—shows significantly stronger depth cue awareness, highlighting the role of data scale. On the texture-gradient task specifically, all three multi-view pre-trained models (CroCo, LRM, DUSt3R) rank within the top four, outperforming strong single-view SSL competitors like DINOv2, indicating that cross-view pre-training uniquely captures surface-level geometric details. End-to-end trained baselines (ResNet18, ViT-B/16) and a trivial coordinate-map baseline establish floor performance, confirming that the depth cues are not trivially extractable from spatial position alone. The fine-tuning experiment shows that concatenating DepthCues-fine-tuned features with original features improves depth estimation for both DINOv2 (87.78% → 88.46% NYUv2) and CLIP (43.78% → 44.32%), while a noise-concatenation control confirms the gains are not merely from increased probe input dimensionality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the feature-probing methodology established by El Banani et al. (CVPR 2024) and Zhan et al. (NeurIPS 2024) for evaluating 3D awareness in large vision models, but shifts the focus from general 3D scene understanding to the specific monocular depth cues (elevation, light-shadow, occlusion, perspective, size, texture gradient) that human vision science identifies as essential for depth perception. Unlike prior depth estimation benchmarks that measure end-to-end depth accuracy, DepthCues decomposes depth perception into six interpretable sub-tasks, each probing a distinct visual cue. The paper also departs from concurrent work [76] by analyzing a broader range of 20 models across diverse pre-training objectives and by introducing a synthetic texture-gradient task that isolates surface-level geometric understanding.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark provides the first systematic evidence that human-like monocular depth cues emerge in large vision models trained without explicit depth supervision, and that this cue understanding correlates with downstream depth estimation performance. The practical finding that fine-tuning on sparse depth-cue annotations (fewer than 35k images) improves depth estimation via LoRA offers a data-efficient path to enhancing depth perception without expensive dense depth labels. Key limitations include the focus on static single images (excluding motion-based cues), the relatively small per-task datasets, and the fact that correlation between cue understanding and depth estimation does not establish causality. Open questions include whether these cues are truly mechanistically used by models or merely correlated, and whether the findings extend to video-based or embodied vision systems.
