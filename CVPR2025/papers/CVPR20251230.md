## Problem

Large vision-language models require hundreds to thousands of image tokens per input, making training and inference prohibitively expensive, especially at high resolutions. Prior token-reduction strategies either compress tokens before the LLM (causing irreversible information loss) or drop tokens at a single shallow layer (before the model has fully integrated visual information). The paper identifies the missing understanding of how token redundancy evolves across layers and uses this to design a lossless progressive reduction strategy.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251230/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes an RGB image (processed by a CLIP vision encoder into 576 tokens for LLaVA-1.5 or up to 2880 tokens for LLaVA-NeXT with multi-patch tiling) paired with a free-form text instruction, and produces a standard text answer. A key operational detail is that the image token count is not fixed throughout the forward pass: it starts at full resolution in the shallowest layers and is progressively pruned to near-zero by the deepest layers, so the effective sequence length seen by later transformer layers is dramatically shorter than the input.

## Method

PyramidDrop partitions the LLM's J-layer decoder into S stages (S=4 for 32-layer models) and, at the end of each stage, drops a fixed fraction (1−λ) of the remaining image tokens, yielding an exponential decay V_s = V_0·λ^(s−1). The dropping criterion is a lightweight similarity score computed by reusing the existing query matrix of the last instruction token and the key matrix of the image tokens from the final layer of each stage, so the overhead is O(V_s) per stage with no extra parameters. The core empirical insight driving the design is that all image tokens are necessary in shallow layers (where attention is uniform and global) but become progressively redundant in deeper layers (where attention concentrates on instruction-relevant regions). Because the same Q/K projections are already computed for self-attention, the ranking incurs negligible cost and is compatible with FlashAttention during both training and inference. The strategy is applied identically during training (reducing GPU-hours by 40–44%) and can be applied post-hoc as a plug-and-play inference accelerator without retraining.

## Results

PyramidDrop reduces LLaVA-NeXT-7B training time from 366 to 218 GPU hours (40.4% reduction) and inference FLOPs from 20.8T to 9.46T (54.5% reduction), while maintaining comparable average performance (67.5 vs 67.6) across 16 benchmarks. On LLaVA-1.5-7B, training time drops from 104 to 79 GPU hours (24% reduction) with inference FLOPs halved (3.82T → 1.78T). At higher resolution (p9), PyramidDrop achieves 44.3% training time reduction (483→269 GPU hours) and even slightly improves average benchmark score (67.4 vs 66.8), suggesting that removing redundant tokens helps the model focus on critical visual information.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole direct baseline is FastV [Chen et al., 2024], which drops 50% of visual tokens at layer 2 during inference. PyramidDrop outperforms FastV on nearly all benchmarks while using fewer FLOPs: on LLaVA-NeXT-7B, PDrop achieves 9.5T FLOPs vs. FastV's 10.6T, yet scores higher on MME (1533.0 vs. 1504.0), GQA (63.9 vs. 63.5), TextVQA (67.0 vs. 66.5), and SEED-Bench (70.0 vs. 69.3), with only a marginal 0.1-point loss on MMBench-CN (59.9 vs. 60.0). On LLaVA-1.5-7B, the advantage is even more pronounced: PDrop beats FastV on every single benchmark (e.g., MME 1500.8 vs. 1473.7, SQA 69.2 vs. 68.5, MMB-CN 58.5 vs. 57.3) while reducing FLOPs further (1.78T vs. 2.01T). The paper attributes FastV's inferior performance to its premature token dropping at layer 2, which discards visual information before the model has fully processed it, whereas PyramidDrop's progressive multi-stage strategy preserves all tokens in shallow layers where they are critical.

## Relation to Prior Work

PyramidDrop carves new ground by identifying a principled, layer-wise schedule for visual token reduction, departing from two dominant prior strategies: (1) pre-LLM compression methods (e.g., LLaVA-Prumerge, DECO) that lose information before the language model ever sees the full image, and (2) single-point early-layer dropping (FastV) that removes tokens at layer 2 regardless of depth-dependent redundancy. The method repurposes the self-attention Q/K matrices already computed in each transformer block for a lightweight ranking, avoiding any additional parameters. Conceptually, it draws an analogy to KV-cache compression techniques in pure LLMs (H2O, ScissorHands, StreamLLM) but applies the progressive-pruning idea specifically to the visual-token stream in LVLMs, grounded in a systematic empirical study of where redundancy actually emerges.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 40% training-time reduction and 55% inference-FLOPs savings on LLaVA-NeXT, with no measurable performance drop across 16 benchmarks, make this a practically attractive drop-in for any team training or deploying high-resolution LVLMs. The plug-and-play inference mode (no retraining) broadens its utility to frozen models. Open questions remain: the paper notes that video redundancy (temporal) differs fundamentally from spatial image redundancy, and the current λ=0.5, S=4 schedule is fixed rather than adaptive; whether the compactness effect observed in Figure 3 (PyramidDrop-trained models retain more information per token) generalizes to other architectures or training regimes is unexplored. The method also assumes a roughly uniform redundancy growth across layers, which may not hold for all task types or model families.
