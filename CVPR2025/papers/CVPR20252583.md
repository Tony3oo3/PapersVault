## Problem

General-purpose VLMs (GPT-4o, Gemini) and existing medical VLMs (Med-Gemini) fail to capture fine-grained visual details critical for medical diagnosis because they rely on memorized internet knowledge and coarse visual features rather than domain-specific expertise. Existing medical datasets are static and narrow, and no prior medical VLM incorporates on-demand feedback from clinically validated expert models (e.g., FDA-approved segmentation/classification tools) into its reasoning pipeline.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252583/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes 2D medical images at 384×384 (or 448×448 for the 40B variant) alongside a user text prompt and a system prompt listing available expert model cards. When the model decides to invoke an expert, it emits a structured keyword-argument token (e.g., <VISTA3D(hepatic tumor)>), and the expert's output is fed back as a conversational user message: segmentation masks are re-tokenized with overlaid textual descriptions on the original image, while classification results are returned as a list of yes/no disease-likelihood statements. Outputs span VQA answers, free-text radiology reports, multi-label disease classifications, and (via the expert) full 3D volumetric segmentation masks despite the VLM itself only processing 2D slices.

## Method

VILA-M3 extends the VILA auto-regressive VLM (CLIP vision encoder → linear projector → LLM backbone) with a novel fourth training stage: expert-guided instruction fine-tuning (IFT) that teaches the model when and how to trigger domain expert models and incorporate their outputs. During this stage, training conversations are synthesized by running expert models (VISTA3D for 127-class CT segmentation, BraTS for 4-modality brain MRI tumor segmentation, TorchXRayVision ensemble for CXR classification) on existing datasets and formatting their outputs as multi-turn dialogues with model-card context. At inference, the model predicts a trigger token with arguments to activate the appropriate expert, receives the result as a conversational feedback prompt, and integrates it into its final text generation—enabling hybrid 2D/3D reasoning where a 2D-input VLM leverages full volumetric 3D segmentation. All components (vision encoder, projector, LLM) are jointly fine-tuned in bf16 with a cosine LR schedule, and a small corpus of medical text data is mixed in to preserve general language capabilities.

## Results

VILA-M3-40B achieves BLEU-4 of 21.6, ROUGE of 32.2, and GREEN of 39.2 on MIMIC-CXR report generation, surpassing Med-Gemini (1.5T) by ~5.4% BLEU-4 and ~13.8% ROUGE. On ChestX-ray14 classification with expert feedback, VILA-M3-3B reaches an average F1 of 51.3, outperforming Med-Gemini (46.9) and GPT-4o (33.1). On CheXpert classification with expert feedback, VILA-M3-13B achieves an average F1 of 61.5, exceeding Med-Gemini (48.4) by ~13.1 points. Overall, the paper reports an average improvement of ~9% over Med-Gemini and ~6% over task-specific SOTA models across all benchmarks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VILA-M3 consistently outperforms Med-Gemini (1.5T) across all evaluated tasks: on MIMIC-CXR report generation, VILA-M3-40B achieves BLEU-4 21.6 vs. 20.5 and ROUGE 32.2 vs. 28.3; on ChestX-ray14 classification, VILA-M3-3B with expert reaches average F1 51.3 vs. Med-Gemini's 46.9; and on CheXpert, VILA-M3-13B with expert scores 61.5 vs. 48.4. Against GPT-4o, the gap is even wider: VILA-M3-3B achieves 51.3 vs. 33.1 on ChestX-ray14 and 61.5 vs. 33.9 on CheXpert, demonstrating that general-purpose VLMs remain unreliable for precision-critical medical tasks. Compared to task-specific SOTA baselines, VILA-M3 with expert feedback matches or slightly exceeds TorchXRayVision ensemble (51.3 vs. 50.0 on ChestX-ray14; 61.5 vs. 51.5 on CheXpert) and surpasses DCL (0.25B) on report generation (BLEU-4 21.6 vs. 10.9). VILA-M3 also significantly outperforms LLaVA-Med (7B) on report generation (BLEU-4 21.6 vs. 1.0) and LLaVA-Rad (7B) (21.6 vs. 15.4). On the general VILA benchmark (Table 3), VILA-M3 shows only modest degradation (7–11% for 3B–13B variants) after expert-guided IFT, confirming that medical specialization does not catastrophically erode general vision-language capabilities.

## Relation to Prior Work

VILA-M3 extends the VILA framework (Lin et al., 2024) by adding a fourth training stage—expert-guided instruction fine-tuning—that injects outputs from domain-specialized models (VISTA3D for CT segmentation, BraTS for MRI tumor segmentation, TorchXRayVision for CXR classification) into the VLM's conversational context via model cards. This departs from prior medical VLMs like Med-Gemini, LLaVA-Med, and Med-Flamingo, which rely solely on memorized internet knowledge and static training data without on-demand access to task-specific expert predictions. The on-demand expert triggering mechanism is conceptually related to agentic tool-use frameworks (e.g., MMedAgent, Voxelprompt) but is specifically designed for medical imaging where expert models are already clinically validated and FDA-approved.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

VILA-M3 is the first medical VLM to unify segmentation, classification, report generation, and VQA in a single framework with on-demand expert model consultation, offering a practical path toward clinically deployable multimodal AI that leverages already-approved narrow AI tools. Key limitations include the 40B variant's 23% degradation on general VLM benchmarks (attributed to the weaker Yi-34B backbone), the 2D-only input constraint despite 3D expert models, and reliance on a small set of expert models. Open questions include whether the approach scales to dozens of expert models without degrading language capabilities, how to handle conflicting expert outputs, and whether the multi-agent extension the authors envision can maintain the precision required for clinical decision-making.
