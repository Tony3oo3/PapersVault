## Problem

Sinusoidal MLPs (SIREN) are effective implicit neural representations but their initialization and training remain empirical, with no theoretical understanding of how layer composition generates new frequencies. This leads to overfitting with noisy high-frequency reconstructions, while existing bandlimit controls (e.g., BACON's box filter) introduce ringing artifacts and lack non-linear expressiveness. The paper seeks a principled framework that explains frequency generation and provides robust, differentiable control over the network's spectrum.

## Input / Output

The INR takes 2D spatial coordinates x ∈ [-1,1]² (resized image domain) and outputs a 3-channel RGB signal plus its analytical gradient ∇f. Training uses 90% of image pixels as supervision; the remaining 10% serve as an unsupervised test set for evaluating generalization. The input frequencies ω are frozen at integer multiples of 2π/p (where p is the signal period), so the network is strictly periodic and the learned parameters only adjust the amplitudes of a fixed harmonic basis.

## Method

TUNER introduces an amplitude-phase expansion (Thm 1) proving each hidden neuron decomposes into a sum of sines whose frequencies β_k are integer combinations of the frozen input frequencies ω, with amplitudes given by products of Bessel functions of the hidden weights W. A bounding theorem (Thm 2) then shows |α_k| ≤ ∏(|W_ij|/2)^{|k_j|}/|k_j|!, enabling bandlimit control by clamping W. Initialization uses spectral sampling: 70% of input frequencies are drawn uniformly from a low-frequency region near the origin (for flexible neighborhood generation) and 30% from a grid over the remaining range (to cover the full spectrum), with (1,0) and (0,1) always included to prevent sub-periods. During training, hidden weight columns are clamped with asymmetric bounds c_L > c_H (looser for low-frequency columns, tighter for high-frequency ones), or replaced by a learnable tanh(W)·c_j parameterization with an L1 regularization on the bounds to prevent unbounded growth.

## Results

On the Kodak dataset, TUNER achieves up to 41.2 dB signal PSNR (train) and 33.2 dB (test) with m=416, n=416 at θ=256, compared to SIREN's 24.7/22.3 dB and BACON's 27.7/22.7 dB. Gradient PSNR improvements are even more pronounced: TUNER reaches 23.0 dB (train) vs SIREN's 12.9 dB and BACON's 24.2 dB at the same setting. At the conservative θ=85 (B/3), TUNER matches SIREN on signal PSNR (34.3 vs 34.0 dB, m=416, n=416) while improving gradient PSNR by ~1.2 dB (28.9 vs 27.7 dB train). The bounding scheme alone yields an average 9.6 dB gradient PSNR improvement over unbounded training across the dataset.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SIREN, TUNER is comparable on signal PSNR at the conservative θ=85 (B/3) but pulls ahead on gradient PSNR by 1–3 dB. At higher bandlimits (θ=171, 256), TUNER's advantage becomes dramatic: at θ=256 with m=416, n=416, TUNER achieves 33.2 dB test signal PSNR versus SIREN's 22.3 dB (a 10.9 dB gap), because SIREN's unbounded hidden weights generate excessive high frequencies that cause overfitting. Against BACON, TUNER consistently wins on signal PSNR across all settings (e.g., 33.4 vs 25.4 dB at θ=85, m=416, n=416) and matches or exceeds it on gradient PSNR; qualitatively, BACON's hard box-filter truncation produces visible ringing artifacts that TUNER's soft amplitude bounding avoids. Against BANF (qualitative comparison only, Fig. 13), TUNER shows cleaner reconstructions without the edge distortions and ambiguous bandlimit that BANF exhibits at both θ=60 and θ=130.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on SIREN's sinusoidal MLP architecture for implicit neural representations, but departs from its empirical initialization by deriving a theoretical amplitude-phase expansion (Theorem 1) that reveals how layer composition generates frequencies as integer combinations of input frequencies, with amplitudes governed by Bessel functions. Where BACON controls the spectrum via hard-truncation with multiplicative filter networks (introducing ringing artifacts), TUNER instead uses a Bessel-function-based amplitude bound (Theorem 2) to softly limit the spectrum through simple weight clamping. The initialization scheme reframes input frequency selection as a form of spectral sampling grounded in Fourier series theory, rather than the uniform random sampling used in SIREN or the grid-based filtering in BANF.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The amplitude-phase expansion provides the first principled explanation for why sinusoidal MLPs achieve high representational capacity, shifting the design of INR initialization from trial-and-error to a theoretically guided procedure. The practical consequence—that clamping hidden weights to a small constant acts as an effective soft bandlimit filter without the ringing artifacts of hard truncation—is a simple, drop-in technique that could be adopted broadly. However, the analysis is restricted to 3-layer MLPs and image reconstruction on the Kodak dataset; extending the theory to deeper networks, 3D signals, or non-periodic domains remains open, as does understanding how the Bessel-function amplitude structure interacts with optimization dynamics in practice.
