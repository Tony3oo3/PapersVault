## Problem

Inferring a model's training data or functionality directly from its weights is hampered by nuisance variation introduced by the optimization process, particularly the initial weights. Existing weight-space learning methods either require complex permutation-invariant architectures or produce impractically large classifiers for modern models with hundreds of millions of parameters. The paper identifies that models sharing a common fine-tuning ancestor (a Model Tree) exhibit substantially reduced nuisance variation, yet no prior method exploits this structure to enable lightweight, scalable weight-space learning.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a single hidden layer's weight matrix X ∈ R^{d_W × d_H} (for LoRA models, the full matrix is reconstructed from the decomposed BA factors). For classification, it outputs a 100-dimensional binary logit vector indicating which CIFAR-100 classes were in the model's training set. For text alignment, it produces a weight embedding in a shared space with CLIP text embeddings, enabling zero-shot model classification via cosine similarity against a text prompt. The architecture is applied per-layer and can concatenate encodings across multiple layers before a final prediction head.

## Method

ProbeX is a probing-based architecture that operates on a single hidden model layer rather than full model I/O. It passes a set of learned probe vectors u_l through the weight matrix (z_l = X^T u_l), then applies a shared dimensionality-reduction matrix V, a ReLU non-linearity, and a small per-probe encoder matrix M_l, summing the resulting probe encodings before a final prediction head T. The factorization is theoretically grounded: Proposition 2 shows that linear ProbeX has identical expressivity to a dense linear expert when the weight tensor obeys a Tucker decomposition, yet uses roughly 30× fewer parameters (2.3M vs. 105M for ResNet). For multiple Model Trees, a Mixture-of-Experts scheme routes models to per-tree ProbeX experts via hierarchical clustering and nearest-centroid assignment. Training uses cross-entropy loss for class prediction and a CLIP-style contrastive loss for weight-text alignment, optimizing all parameters end-to-end in under 10 minutes on a single GPU.

## Results

On the discriminative split (predicting which 50 of 100 CIFAR-100 classes a model was trained on), ProbeX achieves 0.842 accuracy on ResNet, 0.705 on DINO, 0.765 on MAE, 0.885 on Supervised ViT, and 0.799 in the Mixture-of-Experts setting, using only 2.3M parameters per tree expert (9.2M total for MoE). In the generative split, ProbeX aligns Stable Diffusion weights with CLIP text embeddings, achieving 97.3% in-distribution and 89.8% zero-shot classification accuracy on SD200 (30 held-out classes). The method trains in under 10 minutes on a single 10GB GPU.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ProbeX substantially outperforms the StatNN baseline [Unterthiner et al.] across all Model Trees, with StatNN achieving near-random accuracy (0.50–0.63) while ProbeX reaches 0.70–0.89. Against the Dense Expert (a single linear layer on flattened weights), ProbeX improves accuracy by 10–22 percentage points per tree (e.g., 0.842 vs. 0.713 on ResNet, 0.885 vs. 0.663 on Sup. ViT) while using roughly 30× fewer parameters (2.3M vs. 105M per tree). In the zero-shot text-guided classification task on SD200, ProbeX achieves 89.8% accuracy versus 70.6% for Dense, and on SD1k it achieves 50.5% zero-shot versus 34.3% for Dense. The only setting where Dense slightly edges out ProbeX is in-distribution accuracy on SD1k (0.382 vs. 0.296), but ProbeX still dominates on the zero-shot split, confirming its superior generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Model Tree framework from Horwitz et al. [20] (originally for model attribution) and repurposes it as a structural prior that reduces nuisance variation in weight-space learning. It extends the probing paradigm from Kahana et al. [25] and Herrmann et al. [18], which previously operated only on model inputs/outputs, to probe individual hidden layers of a target model. The key departure from prior weight-space methods (Navon et al., Kofinas et al., Lim et al.) is the observation that within a single Model Tree, the complex permutation-invariant architectures those works require become unnecessary, and a lightweight factorized linear probe (ProbeX, derived via Tucker decomposition) suffices.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

With over one million largely undocumented models on Hugging Face, the ability to infer a model's training data from its weights alone is practically critical for model discovery and reuse. The finding that a few large Model Trees dominate the landscape makes the per-tree expert approach both effective and deployable. Key limitations remain: ProbeX cannot generalize to unseen Model Trees (requiring new expert training, though under 10 minutes), and the zero-shot text-alignment capability currently works only for Stable Diffusion models, not for discriminative architectures. Open questions include whether deeper probe encoders can improve generalization without overfitting, and whether the weight-text alignment can be extended beyond diffusion models.
