## Problem

Standard CLIP-style contrastive learning models treat images as bags of words, failing to capture compositional relationships such as object attributes, spatial relations, and ordering. Prior fixes required bespoke architectures with multiple losses or localization side data, raising scalability concerns. This paper asks whether simply enriching the semantic guidance—via better captions and a stronger text encoder—is sufficient to restore compositional understanding without any architectural modification.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251642/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes 256×256 RGB images (tokenized as 256 patches of 16×16) and text captions averaging 57 words (roughly 8× longer than the original alt-text) as input, producing 768-dimensional embeddings for each modality. The text encoder is a pretrained Gemini 1.5 Flash-8B (or Gemma2-2B) with its last 4 layers unfrozen and switched from left-to-right to bidirectional context, while the image encoder is a standard ViT-Base (86M parameters). Retrieval is performed via nearest-neighbor lookup in the shared 768-d embedding space, with no second-stage classification or cross-modal scoring.

## Method

The core contribution is a data-centric intervention on a standard two-tower CLIP trained with a single contrastive loss: (1) all 1B WebLI training images are recaptioned using Gemini 1.5 Flash, grounded on the original alt-text and web page title to minimize hallucinations, and (2) the text tower is replaced with a pretrained Gemini 1.5 Flash-8B whose last 4 layers are fine-tuned bidirectionally. Two data augmentations further sharpen the signal: random sentence sampling (using 1–10 randomly selected sentences from each long caption as the training target) and 64M synthetically generated hard-negative captions styled after ARO relation/attribute perturbations. Training runs 150K steps at global batch 65,536 on 256 accelerators, followed by 500 steps of fine-tuning at batch 4,096 on the hard-negative subset. The key finding is that these two semantic-guidance changes alone—without any architectural novelty—lift ARO relation accuracy from 59% (CLIP) to 92% and DOCCI retrieval recall@1 from 58.4% to 94.5%, surpassing bespoke architectures that employ cross-modal encoders and second-stage binary classification.

## Results

On the DOCCI image retrieval benchmark, the model achieves 94.5% recall@1 (test split) and 89.7% (full dataset), substantially outperforming the open-source CLIP baseline (58.4%) and the previous best bespoke architecture MATE (73.4%). On the ARO compositional benchmark, the model reaches 92% accuracy on relations and 94% on attributes, surpassing all prior methods including NegCLIP (71%/81%) and X-VLM (73%/87%). On SugarCrepe, the model achieves state-of-the-art or highly competitive results across all seven splits, surpassing GPT-4V on all but one. On zero-shot ImageNet classification, the model achieves 68.4% top-1 accuracy (79.1% with JFT data), below the SOTA 82.6% of CoCa, which the authors attribute to differences in training data distribution.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's method decisively outperforms all baselines on DOCCI retrieval (94.5% vs. MATE's 73.4%, CLIP's 58.4%, TIPS's 58.8%, and Long-CLIP's 45.2%), demonstrating that improved semantic guidance alone can surpass bespoke architectures with additional losses and side information. On ARO, the model (92% relations, 94% attributes) beats NegCLIP (71%/81%), X-VLM (73%/87%), BLIP (59%/88%), and CLIP (59%/63%), and even surpasses the text-only Gemini baseline (71%/82%), confirming the gains come from visual understanding rather than language priors. On SugarCrepe, the model matches or exceeds GPT-4V on 6 of 7 splits (e.g., 97% vs. 96% on replace-object, 94% vs. 90% on swap-attribute) while outperforming all other baselines by wide margins. On COCO retrieval, the model (56.3%) trails TIPS (59.4%) and X-VLM (56.1%) slightly, though the authors argue COCO is saturated and noisy (70.2% of 'failures' are acceptable per human annotation). On ImageNet zero-shot classification, the model (68.4%) underperforms CLIP (76.2%), ALIGN (76.4%), and CoCa (82.6%), which the authors attribute to the absence of JFT's fine-grained class hierarchy in their training data; adding JFT closes most of the gap to 79.1%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the CLIP/ALIGN two-tower contrastive learning architecture but deliberately departs from the dominant trend of introducing bespoke architectures (BLIP's multi-objective training, X-VLM's bounding-box grounding, MATE's LLM-based text encoder, Long-CLIP's modified positional embeddings) to address compositional failures. Instead, it isolates two data-side interventions—recaptioning 1B images with Gemini 1.5 Flash grounded on alt-text and page titles, and replacing the text tower with a pretrained Gemini 1.5 Flash-8B or Gemma2-2B encoder—while keeping the contrastive objective and ViT-Base image encoder unchanged. This positions the work alongside VeCLIP and CapPa on the data side but distinguishes itself by showing that visual grounding of captions plus a strong pretrained text encoder, without any architectural modification, suffices to match or exceed methods that tie image and text towers together with cross-modal encoders.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central practical implication is that the bottleneck for compositional understanding in CLIP-style models may be the quality of textual supervision rather than the architecture, a finding that redirects engineering effort toward scalable data pipelines (foundation-model recaptioning) rather than increasingly complex model designs. The paper also demonstrates that COCO retrieval is saturated and noisy (70.2% of 'failures' are acceptable per human raters), motivating the community to adopt more challenging benchmarks like DOCCI. Open questions include how to optimally balance data augmentations (sentence sampling vs. hard-negative fine-tuning show conflicting effects across benchmarks), whether these gains transfer to generative captioning models, and how to close the remaining gap on fine-grained classification without relying on JFT's class-hierarchy annotations.
