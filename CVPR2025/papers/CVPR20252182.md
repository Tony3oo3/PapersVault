## Problem

Existing 3D human motion prediction methods assume robots can react instantaneously, ignoring the tens-to-hundreds-of-milliseconds response latency inherent in data processing, analysis, and reaction planning. During this latency window, predictions are outdated and practically meaningless, yet simply discarding the latency period from the supervision target yields no improvement. The paper asks how to transform this seemingly useless latency data into a useful signal that enhances prediction accuracy for the valid (post-latency) target.

## Input / Output

The primary branch takes an observed 3D skeletal joint sequence of N poses (each pose is J×3 coordinates, with J=22 for Human3.6M/CMU-Mocap or J=23 for 3DPW) and outputs predicted joint positions for the valid target region of (T−T_L) frames, skipping the first T_L poses that fall within the latency window. The auxiliary branch additionally consumes the T_L latency-period poses as supplementary input, forming a reformed observation of (N+T_L) poses. At test time, only the primary branch runs on the original N-pose observation, so no extra inference cost is incurred; typical configurations use N=10, T=10 (short-term, 400 ms) or T=25 (long-term, 1000 ms) at 25 fps.

## Method

LAL is a two-branch auxiliary learning framework where the primary branch predicts the valid target from the original observation X, and the auxiliary branch predicts the same valid target from a reformed observation (X, Y^lat) that incorporates latency-period motion data. Rather than sharing encoder parameters as in standard auxiliary learning, LAL uses separate encoder-decoder pipelines for each branch and enforces a feature consistency loss L_con = ‖Enc_P(X) − Enc_A(X, Y^lat)‖₂ at the encoder output, which gradually injects latency insights into the primary branch during training. This design is motivated by the high task similarity (both branches perform motion prediction) and inconsistent input dimensionality, which make shared encoders with naive dimension-alignment layers ineffective. An optional refinement stage fixes the auxiliary branch and performs moment matching (first- and second-order statistics) between the primary and auxiliary encoded/decoded feature distributions to further align the primary branch toward the auxiliary latency feature distribution. The framework is model-agnostic and is demonstrated by retrofitting three GCN-based baselines (LTD, STS, SPGSN), with each branch mirroring the original model's architecture except for input/output layer dimensions.

## Results

On Human3.6M (average MPJPE, mm), LAL-enhanced models consistently outperform their non-LAL counterparts across all latency settings. At T_L=5 (≤200 ms latency), STS-LAL-5 achieves 49.2 mm at 400 ms and 106.6 mm at 1000 ms, improving over STS by 11.4 and 6.7 mm respectively; SPGSN-LAL-5 reaches 53.0/104.1 mm vs. SPGSN's 58.2/109.6 mm; LTD-LAL-5 reaches 58.7/108.5 mm vs. LTD's 63.5/114.3 mm. On CMU-Mocap (400 ms), SPGSN-LAL-5 achieves 33.9 mm vs. SPGSN's 37.0 mm; on 3DPW (1000 ms), SPGSN-LAL-5 achieves 106.3 mm vs. SPGSN's 111.1 mm. Critically, inference running time is essentially unchanged (e.g., STS: 28.20 ms → STS-LAL-5: 27.98 ms), confirming zero additional test-time cost.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares LAL-enhanced versions of three GCN-based baselines (LTD, STS, SPGSN) against their non-LAL counterparts and against three additional methods (Res. sup, DMGNN, PGBIG) in Table 1. LAL-enhanced models win across all activities and latency durations: for example, at 320 ms on the 'sitting' activity, SPGSN-LAL-3 achieves 40.4 mm vs. SPGSN's 42.3 mm, STS-LAL-3 achieves 32.8 mm vs. STS's 46.8 mm, and LTD-LAL-3 achieves 43.7 mm vs. LTD's 46.3 mm. The largest relative gains appear for STS, where LAL reduces 400 ms error by up to 11.4 mm (60.6→49.2), suggesting STS benefits most from the auxiliary latency signal. Against the non-GCN baselines (Res. sup, DMGNN, PGBIG), LAL-enhanced methods also dominate: at 320 ms on 'smoking', SPGSN-LAL-3 (26.6 mm) beats PGBIG (28.2 mm), DMGNN (32.1 mm), and Res. sup (70.1 mm). The one caveat is that at very short latency (T_L=1, 40 ms), gains are marginal (e.g., LTD-LAL-1: 63.0 vs. LTD: 63.5 mm at 400 ms), and some LAL-1 variants slightly underperform the baseline at 80 ms (STS-LAL-1: 15.8 vs. STS: 15.3 mm), indicating the auxiliary signal needs sufficient latency duration to be informative.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the GCN/GNN-based human motion prediction line (LTD, STS, SPGSN, DMGNN) by introducing a latency-aware reformulation of the prediction target, and draws on auxiliary learning paradigms from multi-task learning. It deliberately departs from the standard auxiliary learning pattern of shared encoders across tasks (as in [5, 37] for motion denoising/repairing), instead using separate encoder-decoder pipelines per branch with a tailored feature consistency loss, motivated by the high task similarity and inconsistent input dimensionality between the primary and auxiliary branches. The optional refinement stage borrows moment-matching ideas from domain adaptation [31] to further align primary-branch feature statistics with the auxiliary branch's latency-informed distribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The latency-aware formulation addresses a genuine practical gap: real robotic systems have tens-to-hundreds-of-milliseconds of processing delay during which predictions are stale, yet no prior motion prediction method accounts for this. The framework's model-agnostic design (drop-in for any encoder-decoder predictor) and zero additional inference cost make it immediately deployable, and the consistent gains across three datasets and five latency settings suggest broad applicability. Open questions include whether the approach extends to multi-agent prediction, how it behaves at very long latencies (>200 ms), and whether the consistency loss remains effective when the primary and auxiliary branches use architecturally dissimilar backbones.
