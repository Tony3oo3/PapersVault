## Problem

Synthesizing Lyapunov functions for nonlinear systems remains challenging: traditional SOS methods require expert-defined polynomial templates, while existing neural approaches (e.g., FOSSIL) rely on Square activation functions that only fit even-degree polynomials and depend on SMT solvers that struggle with high-dimensional polynomial verification. The paper addresses how to automatically learn expressive polynomial Lyapunov candidates that are both generalizable and amenable to efficient formal verification without manual template selection.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252412/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a continuous dynamical system defined by a polynomial vector field f: R^n → R^n with an equilibrium at the origin, along with a compact semi-algebraic interest state domain X (e.g., a ball of radius γ). It outputs a polynomial Lyapunov function V(x) of degree d (typically 2) with well-scaled coefficients, formally verified via SOS relaxation, together with the corresponding region of attraction X(c). The polynomial output is structured as a monomial expansion h(x) = Σ b_α x^α, making it directly compatible with SOS certificate checking rather than requiring interval-based SMT verification.

## Method

SynNLF is an iterative CEGIS loop between a Learner and a Verifier. The Learner trains a polynomial neural network using three cross-product expansion modes (Squ, Mul, Ski) that generate polynomial candidates of arbitrary degree through Hadamard products and skip connections, trained with a multi-task loss balancing positivity and negative Lie-derivative constraints via goal-oriented sphere sampling. The Verifier checks the candidate via Putinar's Positivstellensatz, reducing Lyapunov conditions to two independent LMI feasibility problems solved by MOSEK (rather than full SOS optimization, which avoids numerical sensitivity). When verification fails, a worst-case counterexample is found by polynomial optimization, and a minimum-volume ellipsoid is fitted around nearby Cex points to construct a high-accuracy counterexample set that guides the next training iteration, outperforming random δ-ball sampling.

## Results

SynNLF successfully synthesizes a valid polynomial Lyapunov function for all 20 benchmark examples, compared to only 10 for FOSSIL 1.0, 12 for FOSSIL 2.0, and 9 for SOSTOOLS. On the 6 examples solvable by all three neural methods, SynNLF achieves an average total time of 0.49 s, versus 2.32 s for FOSSIL 2.0 (≈4.7× faster) and 3.44 s for FOSSIL 1.0. In the Van der Pol oscillator example, SynNLF produces a degree-2 polynomial Lyapunov function in just 2 CEGIS iterations with a total runtime of under 1 s, and yields the largest estimated region of attraction among all compared tools.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against FOSSIL 1.0 (SMT-based Square NN with dReal), SynNLF wins on success rate (20/20 vs. 10/20) and on speed: on the 6 jointly solvable examples, SynNLF averages 0.49 s total time versus 3.44 s for FOSSIL 1.0 (≈7× faster). Against FOSSIL 2.0 (iterative-update variant), SynNLF again wins on success rate (20/20 vs. 12/20) and is ≈4.7× faster on the 6 common examples (0.49 s vs. 2.32 s). Against SOSTOOLS (classical SOS with manually defined polynomial templates), SynNLF achieves a higher success rate (20/20 vs. 9/20); SOSTOOLS is faster on the 9 examples it does solve (typically 0.7–10 s) but fails on 11 benchmarks due to floating-point coefficient degeneracy that triggers SMT verification timeouts. The key differentiator is that SynNLF's polynomial-expansion NN architecture (Squ/Mul/Ski modes) avoids the even-degree restriction of Square NNs and the template-selection burden of SOSTOOLS, while its SOS LMI feasibility check (rather than full SDP optimization) sidesteps numerical sensitivity issues.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the CEGIS (counterexample-guided inductive synthesis) framework introduced by Abate et al. for neural Lyapunov function synthesis, but makes two key architectural departures: it replaces SMT-based posterior verification with SOS/LMI feasibility testing, and it replaces the Square activation function with polynomial expansion modes (Squ, Mul, Ski) that produce polynomial outputs naturally compatible with SOS relaxation. The worst-case-driven minimum-volume ellipsoid counterexample construction builds on the authors' prior work on polynomial neural barrier certificates [46], upgrading from δ-ball random sampling to a geometrically informed Cex region. The overall design positions polynomial neural networks as a bridge between the expressiveness of learned functions and the tractability of algebraic verification, avoiding the scalability bottlenecks of interval-arithmetic SMT solvers on high-degree polynomials.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical bottleneck in formal Lyapunov synthesis: SMT solvers struggle with high-dimensional polynomial candidates, and traditional SOS methods require expert-chosen templates. By making the learned output inherently polynomial and verifying via LMI feasibility (rather than optimization), the approach achieves a 100% success rate on 20 benchmarks versus 50–60% for FOSSIL variants, while being ~4.7× faster than FOSSIL 2.0. However, the method is restricted to polynomial vector fields and compact semi-algebraic domains, and the SOS degree bound grows exponentially with dimension and polynomial degree, limiting scalability beyond the 6-dimensional systems tested. Open questions include whether the polynomial expansion modes can be extended to non-polynomial dynamics (e.g., via rational or piecewise approximations) and whether the ellipsoid Cex strategy generalizes to higher-dimensional state spaces where the minimum-volume computation itself becomes expensive.
