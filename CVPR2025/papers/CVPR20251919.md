## Problem

Existing inverse rendering methods rely on pre-defined, non-learnable importance samplers (e.g., cosine-weighted for diffuse, GGX for specular) that cannot adapt to the spatially and directionally varying integrand of the rendering equation. This mismatch produces high Monte Carlo sampling variance and suboptimal material and lighting estimation, particularly for scenes with complex, non-uniform reflectance properties.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20251919/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes posed multi-view RGB images of a scene and produces a 3D mesh (extracted from a tensorial signed distance field), per-surface-point material properties (albedo, metallic, roughness), and a lighting model comprising an environment map for direct illumination and an MLP for spatially-varying indirect lighting. The importance sampler itself is conditioned on the 3D surface position (via tensorial latent features) and the reflected direction, making it a spatially and directionally aware distribution over the incident hemisphere.

## Method

TensoFlow operates in two stages: first reconstructing geometry via a tensorial SDF (following TensoSDF), then estimating material and lighting using a learned importance sampler parameterized by a normalizing flow. The flow is an NICE-style autoregressive network with N=2 piecewise-quadratic coupling layers, where each coupling transform is conditioned on a tensorial latent feature V_f (spatial prior) and the reflected direction ω_r (directional prior), enabling the sampler to adapt its shape per surface point and viewing angle. The flow models the half-vector distribution rather than the incident direction directly, and is trained by minimizing cross-entropy between the rendering equation integrand and the flow's inferred PDF, using a frozen copy of the flow for sampling. Separate flows are maintained for diffuse and specular terms, and the diffuse evaluation additionally employs MIS combining the learned flow with a cosine-weighted distribution for robustness.

## Results

On the Stanford-ORB real-world dataset, TensoFlow achieves an average PSNR of 29.957, SSIM of 0.981, and LPIPS of 0.0441, outperforming TensoSDF (29.683 / 0.979 / 0.0463) across the three metrics. On the TensoSDF synthetic dataset, the paper reports consistent improvements over all four baselines (TensoIR, NeILF++, NeRO, and TensoSDF) across multiple scenes, though exact per-scene numbers are presented only in a figure. The gains are most pronounced on the synthetic benchmark, where the learnable importance sampler's ability to match spatially and directionally varying integrands provides a larger variance reduction than on the simpler real-world objects.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TensoFlow is compared against four NeRF-based inverse rendering methods: TensoIR and NeILF++ (both using stratified hemisphere sampling), and NeRO and TensoSDF (both using pre-defined importance samplers: cosine-weighted for diffuse, GGX for specular). On the TensoSDF synthetic dataset, TensoFlow consistently outperforms all four baselines across scenes and metrics, with the paper attributing the gap to the learnable sampler's ability to adapt to the integrand's spatial and directional complexity. On Stanford-ORB, TensoFlow beats TensoSDF on average PSNR (+0.27 dB), SSIM (+0.002), and LPIPS (−0.0022), though it loses on the Teapot scene's PSNR (29.342 vs. 29.971). The authors note that NeRO underperforms TensoSDF due to less accurate geometry reconstruction, and that the real-world gains are smaller than on synthetic data because Stanford-ORB objects have simpler geometry and lighting. The stratified-sampling baselines (TensoIR, NeILF++) are not directly compared on Stanford-ORB, limiting the cross-method comparison to the two importance-sampling methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

TensoFlow extends the inverse rendering pipeline established by TensoSDF [20] and NeRO [23], which rely on pre-defined importance samplers (cosine-weighted for diffuse, GGX for specular) to evaluate the rendering equation via Monte Carlo integration. The key departure is replacing these fixed distributions with a learnable normalizing flow (NICE-style coupling layers with piecewise-quadratic transforms from [25]) conditioned on a tensorial scene representation [6] and the reflected direction, yielding a spatially and directionally aware sampler. This positions the work at the intersection of neural importance sampling and tensorial scene representations, moving beyond the stratified-sampling approaches of TensoIR [15] and NeILF [35] that require many more samples for comparable accuracy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a fundamental variance bottleneck in inverse rendering: fixed samplers cannot adapt to the spatially and directionally varying integrand, leading to noisy gradients and suboptimal material/lighting recovery. By learning the sampler end-to-end, TensoFlow achieves meaningful variance reduction with the same ray budget, which should translate to more stable optimization and better relighting quality. The acknowledged limitation—that each sampled direction requires a forward pass through the flow, slowing sampling—suggests a tradeoff between variance reduction and throughput that remains open, particularly for real-time or large-scene applications. Whether the approach generalizes to more complex multi-object scenes with inter-reflections, or to 3D Gaussian splatting-based inverse rendering, is an interesting direction for future work.
