## Problem

It remains unclear whether vision-language models, trained on large-scale human data, exhibit the same perceptual biases as humans when confronted with color illusions. Prior evaluations relied on a small number of well-known illusion images sourced from the internet, making it difficult to rule out memorization and limiting the depth of analysis. This paper addresses the gap by constructing a large, diverse, photorealistic illusion dataset and systematically investigating the mechanisms behind VLM susceptibility.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20250691/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each input consists of a photorealistic color illusion image paired with a GPT-4o-generated natural language question that explicitly specifies the evaluation frame—either 'based on pixel values' or 'based on human perception.' For contrast and stripe illusions, the question is multiple-choice asking the model to compare the relative darkness or color of two specified regions; for filter illusions, the question is open-ended, asking the model to identify the perceived color of a specific object. The output is a single answer choice (e.g., 'A is darker,' 'They are exactly same') or a free-form color name.

## Method

The core contribution is an automated pipeline that generates 19,000 photorealistic color illusion images across three types (contrast, stripe, filter). For contrast and stripe illusions, procedurally generated simple shape-based images are fed into a ControlNet trained on MS COCO 2017 image-caption pairs, where the conditioning signal is a 10×10 quantized color grid of the simple image plus a random text prompt; the diffusion process progressively refines the noisy quantized input into a realistic scene while preserving the illusion structure. Filter illusions are created by applying contrasting HSV filters to MS COCO images to suppress a target hue while retaining perceptual color evocation. GPT-4o generates image-specific questions, and a Prolific-based human validation step (5 raters per image, 241 participants total) confirms that each 'illusion' image genuinely deceives at least 3 of 5 humans. For evaluation, the authors fine-tune open-source VLMs on non-illusion images to isolate illusion susceptibility, then propose a mixed-training baseline that pairs pixel-value questions with pixel-accurate answers and human-perception questions with human-like answers, enabling a single model to respond appropriately to both query types.

## Results

After fine-tuning on non-illusion images, all tested VLMs achieve >75% accuracy on non-illusion images but show significantly lower accuracy on illusion images. The proposed Mixed Training approach on LLaVA-1.5 (7B) dramatically improves illusion understanding: for pixel-based prompts, no-illusion response rates jump from 20–35% (base) to 68–84.5% (mixed training) across filter, contrast, and stripe illusions respectively; for human-perception prompts, human-like rates increase from 35–68% to 76.7–82.3%. Fine-tuning exclusively on no-illusion responses pushes no-illusion accuracy above 90% for contrast and stripe illusions, while few-shot examples with no-illusion answers boost no-illusion responses by approximately 25%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between the base LLaVA-1.5 (7B) model and the proposed Mixed Training approach, evaluated on the RCID dataset across three illusion types. Mixed Training decisively outperforms the base model on both prompt types: for pixel-based queries, no-illusion rates improve by 48.5 points on contrast (35.4→83.9), 48.0 points on filter (20.0→68.0), and 54.2 points on stripe (30.3→84.5). For human-perception queries, human-like rates improve by 41.6 points on contrast (37.4→79.0), 17.7 points on filter (64.6→82.3), and 36.6 points on stripe (40.1→76.7). The base model's weakness is that it produces many N/A responses (matching neither pixel values nor human perception), whereas Mixed Training sharply reduces this ambiguity. The paper also notes that external prompting strategies (CoT, few-shot, direct emphasis) yield only modest gains (~25% for few-shot no-illusion examples on contrast/stripe) compared to the substantial improvements from mixed training, underscoring that fine-tuning is the more effective intervention.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work examining whether VLMs perceive visual illusions like humans (IllusionVQA, GVIL) by replacing limited, internet-sourced illusion images with an automated generation pipeline built on ControlNet, producing 19,000 photorealistic illusion images. It also builds on the earlier finding that pure vision models (CNNs, ViTs) are susceptible to color illusions by isolating the visual-system contribution from the language/prior-knowledge contribution in VLMs. The dual-prompt framework (querying for pixel values vs. human perception) and the mixed-training baseline represent a methodological departure from prior work, which typically asked a single question per image without distinguishing these two ground truths.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that VLMs inherit human-like perceptual biases raises practical concerns for safety-critical applications (e.g., biomedicine) where pixel-accurate color judgment is essential, while simultaneously suggesting that understanding human perception could benefit human-centric tasks like image generation. The mixed-training approach that lets a single model answer both pixel-based and perception-based queries is a simple but useful recipe for practitioners. Open questions include whether these findings generalize beyond color illusions to other illusion types (geometric, motion), whether the synthetic-to-real gap in generated images limits ecological validity, and whether the observed model-size effect (larger models more susceptible) holds across architectures and scales.
