## Problem

Existing trajectory prediction models trained on complete observation sequences degrade significantly when deployed with shorter or incomplete observations (e.g., due to tracking failures or instantaneous pedestrians). Prior work addresses this by adding specialized architectural components—knowledge distillation, mixture-of-experts, or diffusion modules—which increase complexity and do not enable a single unified model to adapt to arbitrary lengths. The authors argue the core issue is not architectural insufficiency but rather the encoder's failure to extract length-invariant features from truncated trajectories.

## Input / Output

The model consumes an 8-frame observation sequence per agent (position, motion-angle cosine, temporal index) plus features from up to 20 nearest neighbors (distance, motion-angle cosine, azimuth-angle cosine, temporal index), and produces 12 future frames of 2D coordinates in k multimodal predictions. For shorter observations, unobservable frames are padded with the first observable frame to maintain fixed-length input. The contrastive learning component operates exclusively during training, so inference incurs no additional computational cost beyond the base model.

## Method

CLLS constructs positive sample pairs via random length masking (replacing the first t_unobs frames with the first observable frame), random rotation, and random scaling, then applies an NT-Xent-style contrastive loss over cosine similarities of features passed through a single-layer ReLU MLP projector. This encourages the encoder to produce consistent representations regardless of how many frames are genuinely observed. The proposed RNLS backbone pairs an MLP trajectory encoder with a cross-attention social module (target features as Query, flattened neighbor-time features as Key/Value) and a two-stage GRU predictor: the first GRU compresses the observation into a hidden state, and the second GRU autoregressively generates future feature frames that are decoded by an MLP into multimodal coordinates. The total loss combines the prediction L2 loss (with winner-takes-all over modes) and the contrastive loss weighted by ω=0.1, and the entire contrastive pipeline is discarded at inference, leaving a standard RNN forward pass.

## Results

Under the standard full 8-frame observation setting, RNLS & CLLS achieves 0.20/0.32 ADE/FDE on the ETH-UCY average and 7.2/11.0 on SDD, setting new state-of-the-art on ETH, ZARA1, ZARA2, and SDD-ADE. On the NBA dataset, the method reaches 0.65/1.20 (Rebounding) and 0.61/1.09 (Scoring), surpassing the second-best by 9.7%/12.4% and 4.7%/6.8% respectively. In the length-shift setting (2-frame observation), CLLS applied to SocialVAE yields 0.24/0.38 ADE/FDE on ETH-UCY average and 9.02/13.26 on SDD, outperforming BCDiff (0.26/0.44 and 9.05/14.86) without any additional inference cost.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the strongest full-observation baselines, RNLS & CLLS outperforms SocialVAE (0.21/0.33) and LED (0.21/0.33) on ETH-UCY average ADE/FDE, and beats LMTraj-SUP (7.8/10.1) on SDD ADE (7.2) though LMTraj-SUP retains a slight FDE edge (10.1 vs 11.0). On the NBA dataset, the method dominates all four baselines (Trajectron++, BiTraP, SGNet-ED, SocialVAE) in both Rebounding and Scoring splits. In the length-shift comparison (Table 1, 2-frame observation), CLLS applied to SocialVAE achieves 0.24/0.38 ETH-UCY average, beating BCDiff (0.26/0.44), DTO (0.29/0.50), and MOE (0.28/0.48), while on SDD it reaches 9.02/13.26 versus BCDiff's 9.05/14.86. CLLS on Trajectron++ yields 0.27/0.46 average, surpassing BCDiff (0.29/0.53) and DTO (0.33/0.61). The method underperforms BCDiff on PCCSNet (0.21/0.43 vs 0.19/0.39), which the authors attribute to PCCSNet's memory-bank structure and lack of explicit social interaction modeling. Across varying unobservable lengths (Table 2), RNLS & CLLS maintains near-constant performance (e.g., 0.20–0.22 ADE on ETH-UCY) while Trajectron++ & BCDiff degrades from 0.31 to 0.34, confirming the length-invariance benefit of contrastive training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the SimCLR contrastive learning framework (data augmentation, nonlinear projector, contrastive loss) from the image domain to trajectory prediction, introducing random length masking as a novel augmentation strategy to extract length-invariant features. It departs from prior length-shift methods (DTO, MOE, BCDiff, LaKD, FlexiLength) that all add architectural complexity—knowledge distillation, mixture-of-experts, diffusion, or specialized subnetworks—by arguing that existing architectures are already sufficient and the core issue is feature representation quality. The proposed RNLS network draws on the GRU-based iterative prediction paradigm from SocialVAE and cross-attention social modeling from AgentFormer, combining them into a lightweight RNN backbone specifically designed to benefit from the contrastive training signal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CLLS offers a practical, plug-and-play training-time-only method that can be attached to any existing trajectory prediction model without architectural modification or additional inference cost, which is a meaningful practical advantage over structural approaches. However, the paper acknowledges that rotation and scaling augmentations are ineffective for map-based data, and the contrastive objective may sacrifice fine-grained features, limiting applicability to certain scene types. An open question is whether the length-invariant feature extraction principle generalizes to other distribution shifts (e.g., sensor noise, occlusion patterns) or to longer-horizon prediction where the 'last two frames dominate' assumption may break down.
