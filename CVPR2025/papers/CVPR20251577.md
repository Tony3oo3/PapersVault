## Problem

Deep homography estimation methods are architecturally constrained to low-resolution inputs (typically 128×128), forcing downsampling that degrades accuracy on high-resolution images. Dense matching methods like RoMa offer resolution flexibility but are computationally prohibitive because they resolve every pixel correspondence, which is redundant for the 8-DOF homography task. The paper asks whether one can retain the accuracy of dense matching while exploiting homography's global smoothness to drastically reduce computation.

## Input / Output

The method takes a source-target image pair (RGB, satellite-map, or visible-infrared) and produces a grid-based displacement field (G×G×2) and a per-grid-point confidence map (G×G×1) at five scales ranging from H/14×W/14 to 4H/7×4W/7. From these, K=5000 correspondences are selected via confidence thresholding and uniform sampling, then RANSAC recovers the final 3×3 homography matrix. Training is fixed at 448×448, but an adaptive recurrence mechanism extends inference to arbitrary resolutions (tested up to 672×672) without retraining.

## Method

GFNet replaces pixel-level dense flow with a coarse grid flow, leveraging the fact that homography is globally smooth so a sparse set of correspondences suffices. Multi-scale features are built by fusing DINOv2 patch embeddings (projected to 64 channels, enriched with 2D positional encoding and cross-attention) into a lightweight FPN (channels [8,16,32,64]) yielding five scales from 1/14 to 1/1. At each scale, a decoder with local correlation windows (radius [7,6,4,2,0]) iteratively refines the grid flow and confidence map (N=2 iterations for multimodal cases), with DINOv2 patch matches providing the coarsest-scale initialization. An adaptive resolution recurrence at inference handles arbitrary input sizes by first running at training resolution then refining at the target resolution, and a symmetric forward-backward pass combines both directions' flows. Training uses composite homography data generation (two independent deformations) for input-sequence invariance and dynamic occlusion augmentation for robustness to moving objects.

## Results

On MSCOCO at 448×448, GFNet achieves 97.69 auc@3, outperforming the best deep homography method RHWF (93.02) and the SOTA dense matcher RoMa (96.01). On the multimodal GoogleMap dataset, GFNet reaches 51.44 auc@3, a +13.5% improvement over RoMa (45.29) and a 1.8× gain over RHWF (18.32). On VIS-IR, GFNet scores 21.28 auc@3, surpassing all homography estimation methods but trailing RoMa (25.74) by 17.3%. In terms of efficiency, GFNet reduces MACs by ~47% compared to RoMa while maintaining or exceeding its accuracy on most benchmarks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GFNet decisively outperforms all three deep homography estimation baselines (RHWF, MCNet, PRISE) across every dataset, with the largest margins on multimodal data: +17.1% auc@3 over RHWF on VIS-IR and +180.7% on GoogleMap. Among image matching methods, GFNet surpasses the sparse matcher SIFT+LightGlue and both semi-dense matchers (LoFTR, Efficient LoFTR) on all four datasets, with particularly large gaps on multimodal benchmarks where these methods struggle with photometric inconsistency. Against the SOTA dense matcher RoMa, GFNet wins on MSCOCO (+1.7% auc@3) and GoogleMap (+13.5% auc@3) while reducing MACs by ~47%, but loses on VIS-IR (−17.3% auc@3), likely due to RoMa's higher-dimensional features being more robust to low-light degradation. On VIRAT (dynamic occlusions), GFNet is within 1.2% of RoMa on auc@3 and slightly ahead of RHWF, benefiting from its explicit confidence-map-based occlusion handling. Overall, GFNet establishes a new state of the art for homography estimation at 448×448 resolution while matching or exceeding the accuracy of the best dense matcher at substantially lower computational cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

GFNet adapts the dense matching framework of RoMa for homography estimation, replacing pixel-level flow prediction with a coarser grid-based representation that exploits the global smoothness of homography transformations. It departs from the two dominant families of deep homography methods—4-point-based (RHWF, MCNet) and matrix-based (PRISE)—which are architecturally constrained to ~128×128 inputs, by inheriting the resolution flexibility of the matching paradigm. The integration of DINOv2 self-supervised features with a lightweight FPN for multi-scale representation is a notable departure from prior homography methods that rely on task-specific CNN encoders, and the composite-homography data generation strategy extends prior single-homography synthetic pipelines to enforce input-sequence invariance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By demonstrating that a grid of correspondences suffices for the 8-DOF homography problem, GFNet makes the accuracy of dense matching accessible at a fraction of the computational cost, opening the door to high-resolution and multimodal homography estimation that was previously infeasible with deep methods. The strong cross-domain performance (1.8× on GoogleMap) driven by DINOv2 suggests that foundation-model features can substitute for task-specific training in geometric estimation. Open questions include whether the grid-based strategy extends naturally to 3D pose or multi-view geometry, whether the iterative regression can be made adaptive per scene difficulty, and how the method behaves under extreme viewpoint changes or non-planar scenes where the homography assumption breaks down.
