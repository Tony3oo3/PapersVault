## Problem

Existing single-image reflection removal methods suffer from two structural limitations: hierarchical semantic features are progressively compressed or discarded during layer-by-layer propagation (Information Bottleneck), and dual-stream interaction networks enforce a fixed coupling pattern across all layers. These issues compound the inherent ill-posedness of the decomposition (infinitely many T, R pairs satisfy I = T + R), limiting the quality of the recovered transmission and reflection layers.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image captured through a transparent medium (e.g., glass) as input and produces two outputs: the transmission layer (scene behind the medium) and the reflection layer (reflected scene). A pretrained FocalNet extracts multi-scale hierarchical features from the input, while a separate ConvNeXt-based estimator predicts six per-channel parameters (α_{R,G,B}, β_{R,G,B}) that characterize the transmission rate. These parameters are converted into a spatial prompt tensor (64 × H × W) that modulates the column embedding features, allowing the network to adapt its decomposition to the specific reflection strength of each input.

## Method

RDNet comprises three modules: a Multi-Column Reversible Encoder (MCRE), a Transmission-rate-Aware Prompt Generator (TAPG), and a Hierarchy Decoder (HDec). The MCRE, inspired by GLOM and RevCol, processes multi-scale features through N parallel columns (4 in practice), where each column contains reversible units that exchange information between transmission and reflection streams via learnable channel-wise scaling (γ) rather than standard residual additions, guaranteeing lossless information propagation. Intra-level reversible connections link adjacent columns at the same scale, while inter-level bidirectional connections enable cross-scale interaction. The TAPG estimates per-channel transmission/reflection rates with a ConvNeXt backbone and converts them via a 3-layer MLP into a prompt that element-wise modulates the column embedding, providing an adaptive prior for the decomposition. Training proceeds in two stages—first the rate estimator, then the full network—with a combined loss of MSE on both layers, gradient-domain L1 on transmission, and VGG-19 perceptual loss.

## Results

RDNet achieves the best average PSNR of 26.65 dB and SSIM of 0.917 across four real-world benchmarks (Real20, Objects, Postcard, Wild) when trained with the Nature dataset, outperforming the strongest competitor DSRNet by 0.90 dB in PSNR. Without the Nature dataset, RDNet still leads with 25.95 dB average PSNR and 0.908 SSIM, surpassing DSRNet (25.40 dB) by 0.55 dB. The largest gains appear on the Wild dataset (27.70 dB vs. 26.49 dB for Zhu et al.) and Real20 (25.58 dB vs. 23.91 dB for DSRNet), indicating stronger robustness on challenging real-world conditions. A user study with 20 raters confirms a 77.2% win rate over Zhu et al. and 64.4% over DSRNet across 587 paired comparisons.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RDNet outperforms all seven baselines on average PSNR across both training settings. Against DSRNet (the strongest prior method), RDNet gains 0.55 dB (w/o Nat.) and 0.90 dB (w/ Nat.) in average PSNR, with the largest per-dataset margin on Real20 (+1.67 dB w/ Nat.) and Wild (+1.59 dB w/ Nat.). Against Zhu et al. (CVPR 2024), RDNet leads by 1.31 dB in average PSNR (w/ Nat.) but concedes SSIM on the Objects subset (0.921 vs. 0.931), the only metric where a competitor edges ahead. The gap over earlier methods is more pronounced: ERRNet and IBCLN trail by over 2 dB in average PSNR, while RAGNet and YTMT lag by roughly 1 dB. Dong et al. (ICCV 2021), which uses an iterative probabilistic confidence map, is surpassed by 2.44 dB in average PSNR (w/ Nat.), highlighting the advantage of the reversible multi-column architecture over iterative refinement. The user study further validates these quantitative gains, with RDNet preferred over DSRNet 64.4% of the time and over Zhu et al. 77.2% of the time.

## Relation to Prior Work

RDNet builds directly on the Reversible Column Network (RevCol, ICLR 2023) and GLOM's part-whole hierarchy, adapting their multi-column, bottom-up/top-down interaction structure to the reflection removal setting for the first time. It departs from the dominant dual-stream interaction paradigm (YTMT, DSRNet) by replacing fixed-pattern cross-stream gating with lossless reversible connections, arguing that multiplicative and linear reductions in prior interaction modules create an information bottleneck. The transmission-rate-aware prompt generator extends the idea of learned channel modulation (common in prompt-based vision models) to encode a physical prior (per-channel transmission rate) that guides the decomposition, a formulation not present in prior SIRR work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that information-preserving (invertible) feature propagation is not merely a theoretical nicety but yields a substantial 2.6 dB improvement over a standard U-Net-style skip connection in the SIRR setting, suggesting that reversible architectures may be broadly beneficial for decomposition tasks where no information is truly lost. The prompt generator addresses a practical gap—generalization to real-world reflection conditions with varying transmission rates—without requiring additional paired data. Open questions include whether the multi-column reversible design scales efficiently to higher-resolution or video reflection removal, and whether the learned transmission-rate prior can be extended to more complex physical models (e.g., angle-dependent or spatially varying coefficients).
