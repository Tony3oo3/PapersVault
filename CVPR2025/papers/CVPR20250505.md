## Problem

During multi-domain training, conflicting gradients from different source domains steer optimization toward local minima that encode domain-specific rather than domain-invariant features, causing poor generalization to unseen target domains. The paper identifies that the initial iterations of training are particularly critical, as gradient disagreement early on commits the model to a suboptimal basin, yet no prior method explicitly searches the parameter space for configurations where domain gradients align before resuming standard descent.

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images from multiple labeled source domains (e.g., 4 domains in PACS/VLCS/OfficeHome/TerraIncognita, 6 in DomainNet) and outputs a single class label per image. It requires domain labels during training, making it inapplicable to single-source DG. The backbone is a ResNet-50 pretrained on ImageNet, and the annealing step operates on mini-batches of 48 samples per domain, applying uniform perturbations of magnitude ρ=5e-5 to all model parameters.

## Method

GGA introduces a short annealing phase during early training (e.g., iterations 100–200 out of 5000) in which, at each step, the algorithm proposes n_a random parameter perturbations θ_a ← θ_t + U(−ρ, ρ) and accepts a perturbation only if it simultaneously increases the minimum pairwise cosine similarity of domain gradients and does not increase the mini-batch loss by more than 0.1. This Pareto-style search identifies a neighborhood where all source-domain gradients point in roughly the same direction, which the paper's generative-model analysis shows is a necessary condition for domain invariance. After the annealing window closes, training resumes with standard SGD/ADAM from the discovered parameter configuration. The procedure is model- and method-agnostic and can be layered on top of existing DG algorithms (SAM, Fish, SAGM, etc.) to further boost their OOD accuracy.

## Results

GGA achieves the best overall out-of-domain accuracy across five DG benchmarks, averaging 66.3% (total) and 71.7% (4-dataset average), surpassing the ERM baseline (63.9%) by 2.4% and the strongest prior method SAGM (66.1%) by 0.2%. GGA sets new state-of-the-art on PACS (87.3%), VLCS (79.9%), TerraIncognita (50.6%), and DomainNet (45.2%), while trailing SAGM (70.1%) and SAM (69.6%) on OfficeHome (68.5%). When combined with existing DG methods, GGA consistently boosts their performance by an average of ~1%, with the largest gain of +6.3% observed for MMD on TerraIncognita.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GGA outperforms all 17 compared baselines on the overall total accuracy (66.3% vs. SAGM's 66.1% and GSAM's 65.1%), and achieves the best single-dataset results on PACS (87.3% vs. SagNet's 86.3%), VLCS (79.9% vs. SAGM's 80.0%—a near-tie), TerraIncognita (50.6% vs. SAGM's 48.8%), and DomainNet (45.2% vs. SAGM's 45.0%). The only benchmark where GGA trails is OfficeHome (68.5%), where SAGM (70.1%), SAM (69.6%), GSAM (69.3%), and CORAL (68.7%) all perform better. Among gradient-based DG methods specifically, GGA surpasses Fish (63.9% total), RSC (62.7%), SAM (64.5%), GSAM (65.1%), and SAGM (66.1%). When GGA is applied on top of existing methods, it improves nearly all of them by ~1% on average (e.g., MMD +2.5%, GroupDRO +1.4%, MLDG +1.2%), with the exception of IRM which degrades by 2.1% due to its incompatible optimization objective. The ERM baseline is improved by a consistent 2.4% average across all five benchmarks.

## Relation to Prior Work

GGA carves out a distinct niche within gradient-aware domain generalization by repurposing the simulated annealing concept for a specific purpose: searching parameter space for points where domain gradients naturally align, rather than modifying gradients directly. It departs from gradient surgery methods (CAGrad, PCGrad, AND-mask) that mute or zero-out conflicting gradient components, and from Fish which explicitly optimizes the gradient inner product, by instead treating gradient alignment as a property of the parameter point itself and using random perturbations to discover such points. The connection to simulated annealing in deep learning (SA-GD, SEAL) is acknowledged but GGA's use is narrowly scoped to early training iterations and conditioned on a gradient-similarity acceptance criterion, making it fundamentally different from general-purpose SA optimization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GGA's practical value lies in its simplicity and composability: as a model-agnostic, method-agnostic plug-in applied during early training, it consistently boosts 17+ existing DG algorithms by ~1% on average without requiring architectural changes or additional data processing. Key limitations include its dependence on domain labels (ruling out single-source DG), added computational overhead from repeated gradient computations during annealing, and batch-size sensitivity. Open questions include whether adaptive (rather than fixed-magnitude) perturbations could further improve results, whether the approach extends to single-source settings, and whether the gradient-alignment objective can be formalized more rigorously beyond the empirical cosine-similarity metric.
