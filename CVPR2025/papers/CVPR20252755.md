## Problem

Supervised contrastive learning (SupCon) has been validated primarily on multi-class balanced benchmarks, yet real-world tasks such as medical diagnosis commonly involve binary imbalanced distributions. This paper is the first to systematically investigate SupCon on binary imbalanced datasets, revealing that its performance degrades sharply with increasing imbalance—falling below even weighted cross-entropy at moderate imbalance—and that the learned representation space collapses to a single point under heavy imbalance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20252755/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images (natural images from iNaturalist21 subsets and medical images from UK Biobank, MedMNIST, and FracAtlas) that are randomly augmented into positive pairs. It produces d-dimensional embeddings on the unit hypersphere S^{d-1} via a ResNet-50 encoder, from which binary class labels are obtained by fine-tuning a linear probe on a balanced 1% subset (natural datasets) or by direct AUC evaluation (medical datasets). The iNat21 subsets are artificially rebalanced to 50/50, 95/5, and 99/1 ratios while keeping total sample count constant.

## Method

The paper introduces two diagnostic metrics—Sample Alignment Accuracy (SAA) and Class Alignment Consistency (CAC)—that measure whether positive pairs are closer to each other than to all negatives, and whether local k-NN neighborhoods are class-pure, respectively, thereby detecting representation collapse invisible to canonical alignment/uniformity metrics. For the fix, Supervised Minority applies the full SupCon loss only to the minority class while using the unsupervised NT-Xent loss for the majority class, preventing the majority from dominating the gradient. Supervised Prototypes places two fixed prototypes at antipodal points on the hypersphere (the majority prototype positioned via gradient descent to minimize average distance to all training encodings) and conditionally attracts each sample toward its class prototype only when cosine similarity is below 0.5, otherwise relying solely on NT-Xent. A theoretical proof shows that the final-layer gradient is upper-bounded by the inverse of the number of positives, explaining why majority-class gradients saturate and cause collapse under high imbalance.

## Results

On three binary natural image datasets (iNaturalist21 subsets), standard SupCon drops from ~93% balanced accuracy at 50% class ratio to 54–56% at 1% minority representation, falling below weighted cross-entropy. Supervised Minority recovers 85.4% (Plants), 78.8% (Insects), and 75.3% (Animals) at 1% imbalance, outperforming SupCon by 20–35%. On four medical imaging datasets, Supervised Prototypes achieves the highest AUC on all four (77.9 UKBB, 90.7 BreastMNIST, 99.8 PneumoniaMNIST, 86.0 FracAtlas), surpassing the best long-tailed baseline by up to 2.6 AUC points on the most imbalanced dataset (UKBB, 4% minority).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SupCon [17] is the primary baseline being addressed: it outperforms weighted cross-entropy by >10% on balanced data but collapses to near-chance (54–56%) at 1% imbalance, underperforming even the simple weighted CE baseline by 5–7 points. Among the five long-tailed baselines, TSC (K=6) [21] and KCL (K=6) [15] are the strongest, maintaining 70–80% accuracy at 1% imbalance, but both are outperformed by Supervised Minority by 3–5 points. BCL [42] is competitive at 5% imbalance (80–85%) but degrades sharply at 1% (60–74%), while PaCo [7] and SBC [14] track SupCon's collapse closely, confirming that methods designed for many-class long-tail distributions do not transfer to the two-class setting. On medical datasets, Supervised Prototypes beats the best baseline (KCL K=3, 75.3 AUC on UKBB) by 2.6 points and outperforms all baselines on BreastMNIST (90.7 vs. 90.5 BCL) and FracAtlas (86.0 vs. 88.2 KCL K=3 — actually KCL K=3 leads here at 88.2). Overall, both proposed methods consistently outperform all six baselines at the highest imbalance levels, with the gap widening as imbalance increases.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the supervised contrastive learning (SupCon) framework of Khosla et al. to the specific and underexplored setting of binary imbalanced classification, where prior long-tailed contrastive learning work (PaCo, KCL, TSC, SBC, BCL) assumed many tail classes rather than a single rare minority. The two proposed losses are minimal modifications: Supervised Minority swaps the majority-class SupCon term for the NT-Xent loss of Chen et al., while Supervised Prototypes incorporates fixed hyperspherical prototypes from Kasarla et al., Mettes et al., and Yang et al. The diagnostic metrics SAA and CAC extend the alignment/uniformity framework of Wang & Isola by measuring inter-class neighborhood purity rather than only intra-class or intra-sample distances.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Binary imbalanced classification is the dominant formulation in medical imaging (78 of 114 studies in a recent survey), yet SupCon's failure mode on such data had not been characterized or addressed. The finding that standard alignment metrics (SAD, CAD) report near-perfect alignment even when the representation space has fully collapsed is a practically important diagnostic gap, and the proposed SAA/CAC metrics fill it with strong correlation to downstream accuracy (R² = 0.69). Open questions include whether the fixes generalize to multi-class imbalanced settings, whether the choice between Supervised Minority and Supervised Prototypes can be predicted from dataset properties, and whether the gradient-saturation mechanism identified in the proof applies to other contrastive objectives beyond SupCon.
