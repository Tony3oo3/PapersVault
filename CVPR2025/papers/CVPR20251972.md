## Problem

Reconstructing 3D geometry from sparse 2D cross-sections is critical for medical imaging (e.g., blood vessels in CT/MRI) but remains unsolved for thin, branching structures. Existing point-cloud methods fail due to inter-slice sparsity, bespoke algorithms scale poorly and require smoothing that erases thin features, and the closest neural approach (OReX) uses a discontinuous indicator field that produces artifacts and misses fine details. The paper targets a scalable method that faithfully reconstructs thin structures from arbitrary cross-sections without interpolation artifacts or over-smoothing.

## Input / Output

The method takes a collection of n 2D planes in R³ with arbitrary offsets and orientations, each carrying closed contours that mark the intersection of the plane with the target geometry boundary. From these contours, 2D signed distance fields are precomputed on each plane and used as supervision signals. The output is a neural 3D SDF f(x;θ) whose zero level set defines the reconstructed surface; evaluation uses 25–149 slices per object in both aligned (parallel) and non-aligned (arbitrary orientation) configurations.

## Method

CrossSDF learns a 3D SDF (rather than an indicator field) supervised by 2D SDFs computed from input contours, using a novel symmetric difference loss that only regresses on the region where predicted and target interior/exterior classifications disagree, thereby avoiding the 'laddering' artifacts caused by forcing the 3D field to match 2D SDF gradients within each plane. An adaptive per-contour sampling strategy directly fills the interior of every contour to ensure thin structures receive adequate supervision. A hybrid encoding combines a multi-resolution hash-grid (for fine detail) with random Fourier features (for smoothness), with the Fourier branch scaled by a small constant to suppress grid-interpolation discontinuities. Training is regularized with an Eikonal term to enforce SDF properties and a minimum surface loss to suppress spurious 'floater' surfaces in unsupervised regions.

## Results

On thin structures (the paper's primary focus), CrossSDF achieves Chamfer Distance (×100) of 0.21–0.50 across six anatomical meshes, substantially outperforming OReX (0.33–12), POCO (0.36–4.5), and Neural-IMLS (9–19). On thick structures, CrossSDF achieves CD (×100) of 0.10–0.80, again leading all baselines. On real CT scans (IRCADb01), CrossSDF attains 2D IoU of 0.63–0.88 on held-out slices, surpassing OReX (0.09–0.81) and Screened Poisson (0.21–0.84).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CrossSDF decisively outperforms OReX [33] on thin structures, where OReX's indicator-field representation and fixed-radius sampling cause it to miss thin branches entirely (CD up to 12× worse on Alveolis and Pulmonary). Against Screened Poisson [19], CrossSDF achieves lower CD on all six thin structures and avoids the severe ridging artifacts that Poisson produces between slices, though Poisson occasionally matches or slightly beats CrossSDF on Hausdorff Distance for denser slicings (e.g., Coronaries aligned: 6.1 vs 5.8). POCO [11], a data-driven point-cloud method, performs poorly as an out-of-distribution input, producing gaps and large CD values (up to 4.5×100 on Alveolis). Neural-IMLS [40] fails to converge on thin medical structures, collapsing to a single blob (CC=1) with CD values 30–80× higher than CrossSDF. On thick structures, CrossSDF maintains its lead over all baselines in CD, with the closest competitor being OReX on simple shapes like Eight (0.10 vs 0.18) and Neural-IMLS on Balloon Dog (0.25 vs 0.90).

## Relation to Prior Work

CrossSDF builds most directly on OReX [33], the prior neural-field approach for cross-section reconstruction, but departs from its indicator-field parametrization in favor of a 3D SDF supervised by 2D SDFs via a symmetric-difference loss. It also draws on the multi-resolution hash-encoding lineage (Instant NGP [29], Neuralangelo [22]) for capturing fine geometric detail, and on SDF-based reconstruction methods (DeepSDF [31], POCO [11], NeuralIMLS [40]) for the broader implicit-representation framework. The key conceptual departure is the insight that directly regressing 2D SDF labels causes 'laddering' artifacts, so the loss is restricted to the symmetric-difference region where interior/exterior classifications disagree, freeing the network to learn a true 3D SDF elsewhere.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Thin-structure reconstruction (blood vessels, neural tissue) is a critical bottleneck in medical imaging where current methods produce broken or over-smoothed geometry, and this paper provides both a method and a dedicated benchmark to address that gap. The symmetric-difference loss and adaptive per-contour sampling are clean, reusable ideas that could transfer to other sparse-supervision settings. However, the method remains optimization-based (slower than data-driven alternatives), the synthetic benchmark is small (6 shapes per category), and real CT results (Table 3) show Screened Poisson sometimes matching or exceeding CrossSDF on 2D IoU, leaving open whether the approach scales to clinically dense scans or benefits from learned priors.
