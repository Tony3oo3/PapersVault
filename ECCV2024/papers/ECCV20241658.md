## Problem

Attributing a generated image to its source model is critical for IP protection and accountability, yet existing approaches either require access to the target model (inverse engineering), modify the generation or training pipeline (watermarks, fingerprints), or demand large numbers of reference samples. This paper tackles the practical open-world setting where the source model is a closed commercial API (e.g., DALL-E-3) and only a handful of its outputs are available, with no model parameters accessible.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241658/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a query RGB image (resized to 224×224) and a few-shot reference set of 10–200 images known to originate from a specific generative model, plus a pool of non-target images sampled from open-domain datasets (COCO, ImageNet, Flickr, CC12M). It outputs a binary attribution score (AUC-evaluated) indicating whether the query shares the same source model as the reference set; in multi-source mode, it produces a class label over K candidate models via One-vs-Rest with a score threshold (default 0.5). Both CLIP encoders (ViT-B/16 image, text) remain frozen throughout.

## Method

OCC-CLIP formulates origin attribution as few-shot one-class classification on top of CLIP, using CoOp-style learnable context vectors (16 vectors) to define two contrasting text prompts for the target (source-model) and non-target (open-domain) classes. The key technical contribution is Adversarial Data Augmentation (ADA): during training, gradient-based perturbations (sign of the loss gradient scaled by ε) are applied to a subset of non-target images, effectively expanding their learned feature-space coverage and pushing the decision boundary closer to the target distribution. The overall optimization is a min-max objective—minimizing cross-entropy over the learnable prompts while maximizing it over the adversarial perturbations—implemented as alternating forward/backward passes in Algorithm 1. For multi-source attribution, K independently trained one-class classifiers are combined via argmax with a threshold, and the framework extends naturally to direct multi-class training.

## Results

On the primary origin attribution benchmark (target: Stable Diffusion, non-target: COCO, tested against 7 other generative datasets), OCC-CLIP achieves an overall AUC of 0.9548, outperforming the strongest baseline CoOp (0.9263) by ~2.9% and zero-shot CLIP (0.6426) by ~31%. The best traditional architecture (Swin-B-4) reaches only 0.7503 AUC, and the best patch-based deepfake detector (Feature-Patch) reaches 0.7231. On the DALL-E-3 commercial API experiment, OCC-CLIP achieves 0.9959 AUC with 50 shots, compared to CoOp's 0.9788.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OCC-CLIP outperforms all 12 baselines on the overall AUC metric. Against the closest competitor CoOp (0.9263), OCC-CLIP gains 2.85% overall, with the largest per-dataset margin on LDM (+4.28%) and ProGAN (+5.91%), while the smallest gap is on VQ-D (+2.00%). Among traditional CNNs and Transformers, Swin-B-4 is the strongest at 0.7503 AUC, still 20.45% below OCC-CLIP; the patch-based deepfake detectors (Image-Patch 0.6470, Feature-Patch 0.7231) perform poorly, confirming that existing deepfake detection methods do not transfer well to few-shot origin attribution. Zero-shot CLIP (0.6426) is outperformed by ~31%, highlighting the value of learned prompts plus ADA. On the DALL-E-3 commercial API (Table 8), OCC-CLIP leads CoOp at every shot count (e.g., 0.9754 vs. 0.9216 at 10 shots; 0.9959 vs. 0.9788 at 50 shots). In multi-source attribution (Table 9), OCC-CLIP consistently exceeds CoOp accuracy across 2-, 4-, and 6-class settings (e.g., 0.7723 vs. 0.7014 for 2 classes).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends CoOp's CLIP prompt-learning framework from multi-class few-shot classification to a few-shot one-class classification setting, where only a single target class (images from one generative model) is available. It departs from prior origin attribution approaches—watermarking, fingerprint injection, and inverse engineering—by requiring neither model access nor training modifications, and by operating with only a handful of reference images rather than large reference sets. The adversarial data augmentation (ADA) component, which applies gradient-based perturbations to non-target images during training, is a novel adaptation of adversarial training ideas to the one-class boundary-learning problem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical setting (inaccessible model parameters, few reference images) matches real-world constraints for commercial systems like DALL-E-3, and the API-based validation demonstrates that the approach works outside controlled benchmarks. However, multi-source attribution accuracy degrades substantially as the number of candidate models grows (dropping to ~0.65–0.77 for 6 classes), and performance on near-identical models (e.g., DeepFloyd vs. Stable Diffusion) remains challenging. Open questions include adversarial robustness of the attribution itself, scalability to rapidly evolving model families, and whether the one-class formulation generalizes beyond image generation to other AIGC modalities.
