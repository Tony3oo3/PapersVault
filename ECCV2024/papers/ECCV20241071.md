## Problem

Existing portrait relighting methods either depend on synthetic data that produces poor-quality results or on expensive light-stage captures that are inaccessible to most users. The prior at-home approach (Sengupta et al.) required fixed pose and expression, a dark room, and produced temporally unstable relit video with significant flickering, making it impractical for real video calls. This paper shows that casually captured monitor-lit recordings with free pose, expression, and ambient lighting are sufficient to train a high-quality, temporally consistent personalized relighting model.

## Input / Output

The method takes a source portrait image, a target monitor lighting image (an LDR image representing the 180° FoV portion of an environment map projected on a screen), and optionally a source monitor lighting image. When source lighting is unknown (e.g., for in-the-wild portraits), the network predicts a proxy source monitor image from the input portrait and uses it internally for de-lighting. The output is a relit portrait image under the target lighting plus the predicted source monitor image, operating at ~45 fps for real-time video relighting.

## Method

The architecture is a U-Net with a key modification: a Light-Conditioned Feature Normalization (LCFN) module inserted at every skip connection. LCFN uses AdaIN guided by source lighting features (encoded via an MLP to d=256) to normalize encoder features, effectively removing source illumination and propagating only intrinsic appearance (geometry and reflectance) to the decoder; target lighting is then re-applied via a second AdaIN pass during progressive upsampling. Source lighting is predicted from intermediate encoder features concatenated with a confidence-learning scheme, enabling relighting without known source illumination. Temporal consistency is enforced by exponential smoothing (α=0.7) of the de-lit skip-connected features across frames and weighted averaging (β=0.6, N=3) of the source monitor image to handle abrupt lighting changes. Training uses a GAN objective (L1 + perceptual + cycle-consistency generator loss, hinge discriminator loss) augmented with a monitor reconstruction loss that enforces accurate source-lighting prediction and thus tighter disentanglement.

## Results

On the LSYD dataset, the proposed method achieves LPIPS of 0.0832 (with known source lighting) and 0.0839 (without), outperforming Sun et al. (0.1712) and Sengupta et al. (0.1018) by 23.6% and 22.3% respectively. On the OLAT dataset, the method reaches LPIPS of 0.1809 (with known source lighting), beating Sun et al. (0.2273) and Sengupta et al. (0.2237) by 25.6% and 23.5%. For temporal consistency, the full pipeline (LCFN + source lighting smoothing) achieves an average adjacent-frame RMSE of 5.55 with only 0.65% of frames exceeding the high error threshold (0.4), compared to 5.86/1.06% for Sun et al. and 6.37/1.75% for Sengupta et al.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: Sun et al. [36] (single-image portrait relighting trained on OLAT light-stage data) and Sengupta et al. [30] (light stage on every desk, trained on casually captured data with fixed pose/expression). On LPIPS, the proposed method outperforms Sun et al. by 23.6% on LSYD and 25.6% on OLAT, and outperforms Sengupta et al. by 22.3% on LSYD and 23.5% on OLAT. Notably, even without requiring source lighting input (predicting it instead), the method still beats Sun et al. by 22.6% (LSYD) and 25.5% (OLAT) in LPIPS, a capability Sun et al. does not support. On temporal consistency, the full pipeline (LCFN + source lighting smoothing) achieves the lowest adjacent-frame RMSE (5.55) and the lowest high-threshold error rate (0.65%), beating both Sun et al. (5.86 / 1.06%) and Sengupta et al. (6.37 / 1.75%). The method also generalizes to in-the-wild portrait images with unknown lighting, a setting neither baseline handles.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the monitor-as-light-stage paradigm introduced by Sengupta et al. [30] by relaxing its strict constraints (fixed pose, dark room) and addressing its critical weakness of temporal instability. Architecturally, it departs from the standard U-Net skip-connection design used in Sun et al. [36] and Sengupta et al. [30], where the decoder must simultaneously remove source lighting and add target lighting, by inserting a Light-Conditioned Feature Normalization (LCFN) module that explicitly de-lights encoder features via AdaIN before they reach the decoder. The LSYD dataset it introduces positions casual desk recordings as a practical substitute for expensive spherical light stage rigs, while the OLAT experiments validate that the architecture generalizes to true light stage data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: high-quality, temporally stable video relighting at ~45 fps becomes accessible to anyone with a monitor and webcam, removing the barrier of light stage hardware for both research and consumer applications (e.g., video conferencing). The LCFN de-lighting-then-re-lighting decomposition is a clean architectural principle that could inform other appearance-transfer tasks. Key limitations remain: the 180° front-facing monitor constraint prevents modeling side or back lighting, and the monitor prediction capability raises privacy concerns that the authors acknowledge but do not resolve. Open questions include whether the approach extends to full-body relighting or multi-person scenes, and whether the deep-fake detection application is practically viable at scale.
