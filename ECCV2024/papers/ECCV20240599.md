## Problem

Existing multimodal LLM benchmarks (MMBench, MMMU, MME, Seed-Bench) conflate visual perception with language knowledge and reasoning, primarily testing recognition-level abilities that can be reduced to dense captioning. The paper demonstrates that a text-only GPT-4 given image captions performs comparably to or better than GPT-4V on MMBench, revealing that these benchmarks do not genuinely measure visual perception. BLINK addresses this gap by introducing tasks that require nuanced perceptual abilities—depth estimation, visual correspondence, reflectance comparison, multi-view reasoning—that resist mediation through natural language.

## Input / Output

Each question presents one or more RGB images (not resized during evaluation) annotated with visual prompts such as circles, boxes, or masks to highlight specific regions, paired with a text question and multiple-choice options that may themselves be images or text. The 14 tasks span pixel-level granularity (relative reflectance, visual correspondence) through patch-level (jigsaw) to full-image-level (forensic detection, art style), with 3,807 questions across 7,3K images split evenly between validation and test sets. Models output a single letter choice, extracted via predefined rules and GPT-3.5-turbo, and are scored by per-task and overall accuracy.

## Method

BLINK reformats 14 classic computer vision tasks into multiple-choice QA by curating data from existing datasets (HPatches, Depth in the Wild, Wild6D, TallyQA, DreamSim, SPair-71k, FunKPoint, LVIS, IIW, TARA) and generating new content (Stable Diffusion XL images for forensic detection, manually collected IQ test items). A key design principle is that each task must be unsolvable by replacing images with task-agnostic dense captions, verified empirically by showing Caption+LLM achieves random-guessing performance on BLINK while matching or exceeding GPT-4V on MMBench. The evaluation protocol uses temperature 0, 10 retries, and no image resizing, with 17 multimodal LLMs (7B–34B open-source and API-based) tested alongside six specialist CV models (DIFT, DepthAnything, LoFTR, DIRE, Ordinal Shading) as upper-bound references. The central finding is a stark perception gap: humans achieve 95.70% average accuracy while the best multimodal LLM (GPT-4V) reaches only 51.26%—just 13.17% above random—whereas specialist models outperform GPT-4V by up to 62.8% on individual tasks, suggesting these perceptual abilities have not yet emerged in current multimodal LLMs.

## Results

On the BLINK test set (1,906 questions across 14 visual perception tasks), humans achieve 95.70% average accuracy, while the best multimodal LLM, GPT-4V, reaches only 51.26%—just 13.17% above the 38.09% random-guessing baseline. Gemini Pro (45.72%) and Claude 3 OPUS (44.11%) perform even closer to chance, and the best open-source model, LLaVA-v1.6-34B, scores 45.05%. Specialist CV models substantially outperform all multimodal LLMs: DIFT achieves 96.51% on visual correspondence vs. GPT-4V's 37.21%, and DepthAnything reaches 97.58% on relative depth vs. GPT-4V's 58.87%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4V leads all multimodal LLMs at 51.26% overall, outperforming Gemini Pro (45.72%) by 5.54 points and Claude 3 OPUS (44.11%) by 7.15 points, yet all three remain 44–52 points below human performance (95.70%). GPT-4V shows a distinctive strength in visual similarity (83.09% vs. 55.88% for Gemini Pro) and art style (78.63% vs. 49.57%), suggesting a more nuanced aesthetic perception, while Gemini Pro and LLaVA-v1.6-34B share similar performance patterns across most tasks. Open-source 7B/13B models (MiniGPT-4-v2, InstructBLIP, LLaVA-v1.5) cluster around 35–42%, essentially at random-guessing level, with LLaVA-v1.6-34B (45.05%) as the clear open-source leader. On several tasks—jigsaw, semantic correspondence, multi-view reasoning, object localization, and relative reflectance—multiple models score below random chance, indicating systematic perceptual failures rather than mere difficulty. Specialist CV models (DIFT, DepthAnything, LoFTR, DIRE, Ordinal Shading) outperform GPT-4V by 18–63 percentage points on their respective tasks, demonstrating that the perceptual gap is not a fundamental limitation but a training/data gap that targeted specialist knowledge could help close.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of multimodal LLM benchmarks (MMBench, MMMU, MME, Seed-Bench) that the authors argue conflate visual perception with language knowledge and reasoning, effectively reducing evaluation to dense captioning. Rather than building on a prior method, BLINK repurposes 14 classic computer vision tasks—drawn from decades of CV literature on depth estimation, visual correspondence, multi-view geometry, and intrinsic image decomposition—into a unified multiple-choice format with visual prompting. The key conceptual departure is the explicit design criterion that tasks must resist reduction to text-only captioning, which the authors validate empirically by showing that a 'blind' GPT-4 with dense captions performs at chance on BLINK but well above chance on MMBench.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a stark and well-evidenced perception gap: humans achieve 95.70% average accuracy while the best multimodal LLM (GPT-4V) reaches only 51.26%, barely 13% above random, across 17 evaluated models. The finding that specialist CV models (DepthAnything, DIFT, LoFTR) outperform GPT-4V by 18–57% on individual tasks suggests a concrete pathway—distillation or integration of specialist perception modules—rather than waiting for these abilities to 'emerge' from scale alone. Open questions include whether visual prompting improvements can close the gap, whether the perception deficit is architectural or data-driven, and how to design training objectives that target sub-linguistic visual reasoning rather than recognition.
