## Problem

Radiance cache-based inverse rendering methods introduce bias into both renderings and optimization gradients: NeRF-based caches are evaluated at only a single point per camera ray (biased volume rendering), while cheap MLP caches fail to capture high-frequency and near-field illumination (biased reflection integral). This paper eliminates these approximations while keeping the pipeline computationally tractable on a single GPU.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240727/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes multi-view RGB images with known camera poses (100 training / 200 test views for synthetic scenes; 38 training / 10 test views for real scenes) and produces a volumetric NeRF geometry, spatially-varying Disney-GGX BRDF parameters (albedo, roughness, metalness), and an MLP-based environment map. The physically-based model can then synthesize novel views and relit renders under arbitrary lighting. Training runs on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU in two stages: first fitting the NeRF cache and initial geometry, then jointly optimizing geometry, materials, the fast cache, and the importance sampler.

## Method

The core contribution is an unbiased, low-variance rendering pipeline built on two variance-reduction techniques. First, a lightweight 'fast cache' (8 sample points through a low-resolution NGP decoded by a deferred-shading MLP) is trained to approximate the expensive NeRF-based radiance cache and used as a control variate: M' cheap fast-cache samples plus M expensive NeRF-cache samples yield an unbiased outgoing radiance estimate at a fraction of the cost of M' full NeRF evaluations. Second, a spatially-varying mixture of 128 von Mises-Fisher distributions, parameterized by an NGP at each 3D point, serves as an occlusion-aware importance sampler for incoming directions, allowing the model to 'turn off' lobes for occluded light sources. The volume rendering integral itself is estimated unbiasedly by drawing K indices from the categorical distribution over quadrature weights (K≪N), so the costly physically-based appearance is evaluated at only K points per ray rather than all N.

## Results

On the TensoIR-synthetic dataset, Flash Cache achieves the best albedo quality (MAE 3.355, PSNR 30.274, SSIM 0.955) and best relighting quality (PSNR 29.724, LPIPS 0.080) among all compared methods. For novel view synthesis, it achieves PSNR 34.908, slightly below TensoIR's 35.088 but well above NeRFactor (24.679) and InvRender (27.367). On the real Open Illumination dataset, Flash Cache outperforms TensoIR on specular scenes (relighting PSNR 27.810 vs. 26.955) while TensoIR retains an edge on diffuse scenes (relighting PSNR 30.932 vs. 29.679).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against NeRFactor [47], Flash Cache dominates across every metric: albedo MAE improves from 6.314 to 3.355, relighting PSNR from 23.383 to 29.724, and NVS PSNR from 24.679 to 34.908, reflecting the advantage of a full radiance cache over NeRFactor's direct-illumination-only model. Against InvRender [48], Flash Cache similarly wins on all metrics (albedo MAE 3.355 vs. 5.074; relighting PSNR 29.724 vs. 23.973), confirming that the low-capacity MLP cache in InvRender introduces significant bias. The closest comparison is TensoIR [15]: Flash Cache achieves better albedo (MAE 3.355 vs. 4.100; PSNR 30.274 vs. 29.275) and better relighting (PSNR 29.724 vs. 28.580; LPIPS 0.080 vs. 0.081), but slightly trails on NVS (PSNR 34.908 vs. 35.088; LPIPS 0.064 vs. 0.040). On real data (Open Illumination), the advantage shifts by material type: Flash Cache wins on specular scenes (relighting PSNR 27.810 vs. 26.955) where near-field interreflections matter, while TensoIR retains a lead on diffuse scenes (relighting PSNR 30.932 vs. 29.679), suggesting the bias-removal benefits are most pronounced for challenging light transport.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the radiance cache paradigm for inverse rendering established by TensoIR, InvRender, and NeRO, which replace expensive recursive path tracing with a learned representation of steady-state incoming radiance. It departs from these methods by identifying that both dominant cache designs—NeRF-based caches evaluated at a single point per ray (TensoIR) and low-capacity MLP caches (InvRender, NeRO)—introduce systematic bias into the rendering gradients. The paper's key technical move is to borrow control variates and occlusion-aware importance sampling (spatially-varying mixture of von Mises-Fisher distributions) from the forward Monte Carlo rendering literature, combining a high-quality NeRF cache with a lightweight 'fast cache' to achieve unbiased estimation at tractable cost. The volumetric representation itself follows Zip-NeRF and Ref-NeRF, and the material model uses the Disney-GGX BRDF as in prior neural inverse rendering work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By removing the bias that plagues existing radiance cache inverse rendering, this work enables more accurate recovery of material parameters (especially roughness and albedo) in regions affected by specular interreflections and near-field illumination, which are precisely the effects that low-capacity caches fail to capture. The control variate formulation (fast cache as a cheap proxy, NeRF cache for correction) is a practical and generalizable technique that could be adopted by other cache-based rendering pipelines. However, the authors note that the quadrature approximation to the volume rendering integral remains a source of bias, and the fast cache still struggles with very thin structures, increasing gradient variance. The paper explicitly does not claim efficiency gains over prior methods; its contribution is reduced bias at comparable cost, leaving open whether combining this approach with unbiased volume rendering (e.g., differential ratio tracking) or learned denoisers could further close the gap.
