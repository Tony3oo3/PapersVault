## Problem

During 3D Gaussian Splatting optimization, scene geometry progressively degrades in low-textured regions (walls, ceilings, flat furniture surfaces) because the photometric loss provides insufficient geometric constraints. This degradation causes significant rendering artifacts—blurring, incorrect boundaries, and floating ellipsoids—when synthesizing novel views that deviate substantially from training viewpoints, limiting the practical utility of Gaussian-based scene representations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240940/cropped_page1_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sparse point cloud (from SfM or SLAM preprocessing), initial camera poses, and monocular RGB images. The point cloud is used to extract per-point surface normals and partition points into smoothly connected regions (S_co) versus isolated points (S_ind), which determines whether each Gaussian is initialized as a thin ellipsoid or a standard sphere. The output is a 3D Gaussian scene model (a mix of regular and thin ellipsoidal Gaussians with position, scale, rotation, opacity, and spherical harmonics parameters) that supports real-time novel view rendering via differentiable rasterization and alpha-blending.

## Method

GeoGaussian introduces three geometry-aware mechanisms into the 3DGS pipeline. First, for points on smoothly connected surfaces, Gaussians are parameterized as thin ellipsoids: the third scale component s₃ is fixed to a small value, and the third column of the rotation matrix is initialized to the extracted surface normal, giving the covariance matrix explicit geometric meaning. Second, the densification strategy (clone and split) is constrained to the tangential space—cloned Gaussians are displaced only along the component of the accumulated position gradient that is perpendicular to the normal, and split Gaussians are placed on the plane defined by the parent's position and normal, preventing new Gaussians from drifting off the surface. Third, a geometric smoothness loss (E_geo) is added to the photometric loss, penalizing deviations of each thin Gaussian's point-plane distance from the local neighborhood average and penalizing normal misalignment among K-NN neighbors, thereby enforcing co-planarity during iterative optimization.

## Results

On the Replica dataset (Table 2), GeoGaussian achieves an average PSNR of 38.20, SSIM of 0.970, and LPIPS of 0.026 at 100% training data, outperforming 3DGS (37.84 / 0.964 / 0.032) and LightGS (38.18 / 0.964 / 0.032). The advantage grows substantially under sparse views: at 10% training data, GeoGaussian reaches 31.83 PSNR versus 30.62 for 3DGS and 30.63 for LightGS. On TUM RGB-D (Table 3), GeoGaussian achieves 26.31 PSNR and 0.111 LPIPS on average, surpassing 3DGS (24.73 / 0.144) and LightGS (24.75 / 0.145) by a clear margin.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against 3DGS [18], GeoGaussian wins on all three metrics across every sequence and data ratio; the PSNR gap widens from ~0.4 dB at 100% data to ~1.2 dB at 10% data (e.g., R1: 38.24 vs. 37.60 at 100%, 31.65 vs. 30.49 at 10%). Against LightGS [8], GeoGaussian similarly dominates, with the most notable gains in SSIM (0.970 vs. 0.964 average) and LPIPS (0.026 vs. 0.032), indicating better perceptual quality in low-texture regions. Against Gaussian-Splatting SLAM [23] (Table 1, different paradigm—online vs. offline), GeoGaussian achieves 38.78 PSNR and 0.027 LPIPS versus 37.50 and 0.070, but trails slightly in SSIM (0.969 vs. 0.960). Against Point-SLAM [32], GeoGaussian leads in PSNR (38.78 vs. 35.17) and LPIPS (0.027 vs. 0.124) but loses in SSIM (0.969 vs. 0.975). Against Vox-Fusion [43], GeoGaussian is substantially superior across all metrics (38.78 vs. 24.41 PSNR), though this comparison is less direct since Vox-Fusion operates online with RGB-D streams. On TUM RGB-D, GeoGaussian outperforms both 3DGS and LightGS by ~1.6 dB PSNR on average, with the largest gains in the f3/cabinet sequence (28.17 vs. 27.24/27.35 PSNR at 100% data), though it underperforms in the lag-cabinet sequence where the camera orbits a central object.

## Relation to Prior Work

GeoGaussian builds directly on 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [18], adopting its overall optimization framework, photometric loss, and splatting/blending pipeline, but departs from it in three key ways: (1) a geometry-aware parameterization that constrains Gaussians in smooth regions to be thin ellipsoids with an explicit surface normal encoded in the rotation matrix, (2) a densification strategy that keeps new Gaussians in the tangent plane of the original rather than placing them randomly within the ellipsoid, and (3) an additional geometric smoothness loss enforcing co-planarity among neighboring thin Gaussians. This positions the work between free-form 3DGS and mesh-extraction approaches like SuGaR [12], which also seek geometric structure but at the cost of significantly more GPU resources and a separate refinement stage.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical and well-recognized weakness of 3DGS—geometry degradation in low-textured regions that degrades novel-view rendering—by embedding geometric priors directly into the Gaussian parameterization and densification process. The approach is lightweight (no extra GPU modules, no mesh extraction) and integrates naturally into the existing 3DGS training loop, making it an attractive drop-in refinement for indoor scene reconstruction. However, its reliance on initial point-cloud normals limits robustness in scenarios where those normals are noisy or ambiguous (e.g., the lag-cabinet sequence where it underperforms 3DGS), and the offline optimization requirement precludes real-time SLAM use. Open questions include whether the geometry-aware constraints can be extended to unbounded outdoor scenes, whether depth or normal supervision can replace the point-cloud normal dependency, and whether the thin-Gaussian assumption generalizes to highly curved or non-planar surfaces.
