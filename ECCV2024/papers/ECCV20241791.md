## Problem

Directly applying the Segment Anything Model (SAM) to infrared small target detection (IRSTD) fails due to a significant domain gap: infrared images exhibit low signal-to-noise ratio, blurred target boundaries from gradual thermal radiation transitions, and heavy background clutter that natural-image-pretrained models are not designed to handle. The paper asks whether a foundation segmentation model can be effectively adapted to IRSTD by redesigning its encoder-decoder architecture rather than relying on prompt-based interaction.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241791/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a single infrared image resized to 512×512 as input and produces a binary segmentation mask (256×256) along with a corresponding edge prediction map. Unlike vanilla SAM, IRSAM operates without user prompts (no point or box inputs), making it suitable for fully automatic detection. The edge prediction is an intermediate output that refines the mask's shape and boundary precision, particularly for complex-structured or elongated targets.

## Method

IRSAM builds on Mobile-SAM (ViT-Tiny backbone) and introduces two modules. First, a Wavelet-based Perona-Malik Diffusion (WPMD) block replaces the gradient term in the classical PMD anisotropic diffusion equation with high-frequency wavelet components (LH, HL), producing a discrete diffusion step that smooths noise in flat regions while preserving edges; multiple WPMD blocks are inserted at different encoder levels. Second, a Granularity-Aware Decoder (GAD) performs two-way cross-attention between encoder image embeddings and a set of tokens (four mask tokens plus one learnable edge token), then fuses the resulting coarse features with multi-granularity encoder features (shallow 128×128×128 and deep 64×64×256) via transposed convolution and residual addition. Finally, MLPs generate dynamic convolution kernels from the updated tokens, applied via Hadamard product to produce the edge and mask predictions. Training uses AdamW (lr=1e-4, cosine decay) for 500 epochs with a combined Dice + 10×BCE loss on a single RTX 4090.

## Results

On the three public IRSTD benchmarks, IRSAM achieves the best performance across all metrics. On NUAA-SIRST, IRSAM reaches 80.78% IoU and 78.39% nIoU with a false-alarm rate of only 3.95×10⁻⁶, outperforming the closest competitor ISNet (80.02% IoU, 4.92×10⁻⁶ Fa). On NUDT-SIRST, IRSAM attains 92.59% IoU and 93.29% nIoU, surpassing UIUNet (88.91% IoU) by 3.68 points. On IRSTD-1k, IRSAM scores 73.69% IoU and 68.97% nIoU with 96.92% detection probability, leading UIUNet (72.91% IoU) and DNANet (69.80% IoU). The model uses only 12.33M parameters and runs at 103 FPS, offering a favorable accuracy–efficiency trade-off.

## Baselines & Comparisons

IRSAM outperforms all 18 baselines on every metric across all three datasets. Among deep-learning methods, the closest competitor is ISNet [47] on NUAA-SIRST (80.02% vs. 80.78% IoU, a 0.76-point gap) and UIUNet [30] on IRSTD-1k (72.91% vs. 73.69% IoU, a 0.78-point gap). On NUDT-SIRST, IRSAM leads UIUNet by 3.68 IoU points (92.59% vs. 88.91%) and DNANet [22] by 5.50 points. In terms of false-alarm rate, IRSAM achieves the lowest Fa on all three benchmarks (7.55, 6.94, and 3.95 ×10⁻⁶), beating ISNet's 4.92 on NUAA-SIRST and UIUNet's 4.97. Traditional hand-crafted methods (Top-Hat, IPI, PSTNN, etc.) are far behind, with IoU values below 31% on IRSTD-1k and Fa rates orders of magnitude higher. Compared to fine-tuned SAM variants (Table 2), IRSAM with its lightweight ViT-Tiny backbone (12.33M params, 71.63 GFLOPs) surpasses SAM-ViT-H with WPMD (680M params, 3341 GFLOPs) by 4.82 IoU points on NUAA-SIRST, demonstrating that the architectural modifications are more effective than simply scaling up the backbone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of foundation model adaptation and classical image processing. It builds directly on SAM/Mobile-SAM's encoder-decoder architecture but departs from prior SAM adaptations (which typically fine-tune the decoder or apply LoRA to the frozen encoder) by injecting a physics-inspired module—Wavelet-based Perona-Malik Diffusion—into the encoder at multiple levels. The WPMD module itself draws from the anisotropic diffusion literature (Perona & Malik) and wavelet-frame PDE approximations, repurposing a classical denoising/edge-preservation tool as a learnable neural block. On the IRSTD side, it contrasts with the long line of CNN-based (U-Net, GAN) and transformer-based methods that require large labeled infrared datasets, instead leveraging transfer learning from natural-image pretraining to mitigate data scarcity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a 12M-parameter ViT-Tiny backbone, augmented with domain-specific modules, can outperform SAM variants with ViT-H backbones (635M parameters) on IRSTD, suggesting that targeted architectural adaptation can be more effective than brute-force scaling for domain-shifted tasks. The WPMD module offers a principled bridge between classical PDE-based image processing and modern transformer architectures, a design pattern that could transfer to other low-SNR imaging modalities. Limitations include evaluation on only three relatively small datasets (427–1,327 images), reliance on Mobile-SAM rather than full SAM, and the fact that the SAM-comparison ablation (Table 2) is reported on a single dataset. Open questions include whether the wavelet-substituted diffusion formulation generalizes beyond infrared, and whether the GAD's edge-token mechanism can be extended to multi-class or instance-level segmentation.
