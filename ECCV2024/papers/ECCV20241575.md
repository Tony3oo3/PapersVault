## Problem

Matching pairs of irregular image fragments from a large mixed collection is a longstanding problem in cultural relic restoration, but existing rule-based methods are computationally expensive (O(N²) global search), difficult to tune, and fail to generalize across diverse fragment shapes. Learning-based jigsaw puzzle solvers do not transfer because they assume regular piece shapes and dense, non-lost fragment sets. No effective end-to-end learning-based network or suitable dataset existed for irregular image fragment pair-searching and matching.

## Input / Output

The network takes a set of N irregular RGB image fragments, each annotated with an ordered set of contour points. Contours are discretized into 7×7 binary patches at each contour point, and texture is sampled as image patches centered on those same points. For pair-searching, the model outputs a 128-dimensional global feature vector per fragment, from which cosine similarity scores rank candidate neighbors. For pair-matching, it outputs a similarity matrix of size M^(m)×M^(n) (one entry per contour-point pair), which after thresholding, morphological filtering, and RANSAC yields a rigid transformation matrix aligning the two fragments' contours.

## Method

PairingNet uses ResGCN as a shared backbone to extract per-contour-point features in two parallel branches: a contour branch (binary-encoded 7×7 patches → conv → pool → FC → ResGCN) and a texture branch (image patches → conv → pool → ResGCN), each producing an M×64 feature matrix. For pair-matching, a self-gated fusion module (adapted from attentional feature fusion) computes per-point sigmoid weights to dynamically blend contour and texture features, then a scaled dot-product with row-and-column softmax normalization produces the similarity matrix, trained with focal loss to handle extreme positive/negative imbalance. For pair-searching, a separate modified fusion pipeline feeds each modality through a Linformer encoder, followed by FC → average pooling → FC to yield the 128-dim global vector, trained with InfoNCE contrastive loss on paired vs. unpaired fragments. Inference applies a fixed threshold (ε=0.006), a single erosion and dilation step to clean the similarity matrix, and RANSAC to estimate the final transformation. The two modules are trained in a two-step strategy, and the authors additionally design a Fourier-based tearing algorithm to generate a large-scale irregular fragment dataset from 390 source images.

## Results

On the generated dataset (all difficulty), PairingNet achieves Recall@5 of 0.493 and NDCG@5 of 0.417 for pair-searching, and RR of 0.835, HD of 43.1, RE of 0.352, and NTE of 7.74×10⁻⁴ for pair-matching. On the real dataset (no retraining), it reaches Recall@5 of 0.415 and RR of 0.752. Inference time is 73 seconds for all fragment pairs, compared to 10 hours for Jigsawnet and 223 hours for the rule-based method.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PairingNet substantially outperforms both baselines on pair-matching across all difficulty levels: RR jumps from 0.194 (Jigsawnet) and 0.149 (rule-based) to 0.835, while HD drops from ~210 to 43.1 and NTE drops from ~33×10⁻⁴ to 7.74×10⁻⁴. On pair-searching, PairingNet leads on medium and low difficulty (e.g., Recall@5 of 0.501 vs. 0.383 for Jigsawnet on medium) but slightly trails Jigsawnet on the high-difficulty set (0.276 vs. 0.305 Recall@5), likely because Jigsawnet treats searching as a simpler binary classification. On the real dataset (zero-shot, no retraining), PairingNet achieves RR of 0.752 versus 0.153 (Jigsawnet) and 0.128 (rule-based), confirming strong generalization. Inference time is dramatically reduced: 73 seconds for all pairs versus 10 hours (Jigsawnet) and 223 hours (rule-based), a ~500× speedup over the rule-based method.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of rule-based fragment assembly (curvature/geometric-invariant matching) and deep learning approaches borrowed from jigsaw puzzle solving, but explicitly departs from both: rule-based methods are too slow and hard to generalize, while jigsaw solvers assume regular piece shapes and dense, closed-loop matching. The architecture draws on ResGCN for local feature extraction, a linear transformer encoder for global representation, and an attentional feature fusion mechanism (adapted from Dai et al., WACV 2021) to dynamically weight contour versus texture features. The key conceptual departure is formulating pair-searching as a contrastive learning problem (InfoNCE) over global fragment embeddings, decoupling the expensive O(N²) matching from the O(N) retrieval step.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a long-standing gap: no prior deep learning method or suitable dataset existed for irregular image fragment pair-searching and -matching, leaving the field reliant on slow, hand-tuned rule-based pipelines. The generated dataset (with a Fourier-based tearing algorithm) and the real scanned dataset provide a foundation for future learning-based work in cultural heritage restoration and document reassembly. However, the method's limitations are acknowledged—performance degrades on smooth-edged, texture-poor fragments (e.g., shredded white paper), and the evaluation relies on only two comparison methods, one of which is a partial rule-based pipeline. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to 3D fragments, handles larger-scale collections, and benefits from self-supervised pretraining as the authors suggest.
