## Problem

In federated continual learning on edge devices, existing approaches rely on centralized servers for knowledge aggregation, creating communication bottlenecks and privacy limitations. There is no mechanism for a device to selectively identify and transfer relevant knowledge from a specific neighboring device in a decentralized manner, especially when tasks are heterogeneous and some external knowledge may be harmful rather than helpful.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241625/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images from diverse datasets (32×32 for CIFAR-100 and digit datasets, 224×224 for ImageNet-classified and fine-grained datasets) along with neighboring model parameters fetched over the network. A key unusual input is a set of mutated (perturbed) image pairs used to compute decision distance vectors for backbone selection, avoiding raw data sharing. The output is a standard class label per image, with the model architecture dynamically expanding across sequential tasks via CPG's neuron expansion and masking.

## Method

Pick-a-back operates in two alternating modes per task: a continual learner (using CPG's expand-prune-mask strategy) trains locally, then a federated learner selects the best neighboring backbone model by computing Decision Distance Vectors (DDVs) inspired by ModelDiff. Specifically, mutated input pairs are injected into both the local model and each candidate neighbor, producing output distance vectors whose cosine similarity measures decision-pattern alignment—making it applicable across heterogeneous output dimensions. The most similar neighbor's model is fetched and used as the initialization backbone for the current task, after which continual training resumes on top of it. This selective, task-specific backbone retrieval is fully decentralized (no central server), privacy-preserving (only mutated data and model weights are exchanged), and communication-efficient (one model transfer per task rather than full data sharing).

## Results

On CIFAR-100 with LeNet-5 (20 tasks), Pick-a-back achieves an average accuracy of 71.8% and a top-task accuracy of 75.6%, improving over the no-backbone baseline (69.1% avg, 67.6% top) by +2.7% on average and +8.0% on the best task. Across architectures, improvements range from +1.3% avg (MobileNet-V1) to +4.0% avg (EfficientNet-B0), with top-task gains up to +9.8% (EfficientNet-B0) and +8.4% (VGG16-BN). On the most challenging fine-grained tasks (CUB, Stanford Cars, Flowers, WikiArt, Sketch) with EfficientNet-B0, Pick-a-back delivers an average improvement of +31.8% over the no-backbone baseline, peaking at +51.9%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Pick-a-back outperforms all six baselines on average accuracy. Against the federated learning baseline FedAVG (33.9%), the gap is dramatic (−37.9 points), as FedAVG simply averages parameters without accounting for task structure. Among continual learning baselines, ProgressiveNet (69.7%) and PackNet (69.7%) are the closest competitors, trailing Pick-a-back by 2.1 points on average; WSN (61.8%) and FedWeIT (61.0%) lag further behind by 10.0 and 10.8 points respectively. The most relevant federated continual learning baseline, FCCL (67.5%), is outperformed by 4.3 points on average. Notably, even simple selection strategies (Uniform +1.7, Internal +2.0, Random +1.6 over w/o Backbone) confirm that inter-device knowledge transfer itself is beneficial, but Pick-a-back's decision-pattern-based selection consistently extracts the most value, reaching 71.8% avg and 75.6% top accuracy—matching the oracle Upper bound on the top task while remaining within 1.9 points on average.

## Relation to Prior Work

Pick-a-back sits at the intersection of knowledge-reusable continual learning (CPG, ProgressiveNet, PackNet, WSN, Piggyback) and federated continual learning (FedWeIT, FCCL), but departs from both in a key way: it replaces the centralized server aggregation with direct, selective device-to-device knowledge transfer. The decision pattern similarity metric is borrowed from ModelDiff (a DNN testing tool) and repurposed here to handle heterogeneous output dimensions across clients, which prior federated similarity methods (e.g., cosine similarity on parameters) cannot address. The paper explicitly positions itself against class-incremental federated continual learning, instead targeting task-incremental learning where transfer from a relevant backbone is more natural.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The work offers a practical recipe for edge-AI practitioners who need continual learning without a central server: pick a neighbor whose decision patterns match yours, use it as a backbone, and resume local training. The consistent 2–8% gains across four architectures (LeNet-5 through VGG16-BN) and four dataset families, plus the 18.4% improvement under Dirichlet(0.5) heterogeneity, make the approach broadly applicable. However, the method still requires exchanging mutated input samples and full model weights between devices, raising unresolved privacy and communication-cost questions that the paper acknowledges but does not fully resolve. The large gap to the Upper bound on fine-grained datasets (up to 21% on Flowers) suggests the current similarity-based selection is far from optimal, leaving room for better matching strategies.
