## Problem

Standard video-text benchmarks are misleading because models can answer questions by exploiting shortcuts (object entities, spurious correlations) extractable from a single frame, rather than genuinely understanding video semantics through cross-frame reasoning. This paper asks whether current video-text foundation models truly grasp action semantics in natural videos or merely exploit dataset biases, and finds they fall far behind human-level performance when shortcuts are removed.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240338/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a video sequence and a list of six candidate captions, where the five negatives are counterfactually augmented versions of the positive caption—sharing identical object entities and text structure but with modified actions (e.g., 'kick' vs. 'catch' vs. 'throw' a football). The model outputs a ranking over the six captions, and the task is to retrieve the single caption whose action semantics match the video. Unlike standard video-to-text retrieval where negatives come from entirely different videos, here all candidates are plausible given the video's context, forcing the model to resolve ambiguity through temporal reasoning.

## Method

The paper introduces two contributions: (1) the RCAD evaluation task with the Feint6K dataset, built via a human-in-the-loop system where 40 annotators manually modify actions in existing captions while preserving objects, creating hard negatives that eliminate single-frame shortcuts; and (2) LLM-teacher, a knowledge distillation approach that generates synthetic negative captions by substituting action tokens via XLM-RoBERTa mask filling or LLM chatbot in-context learning, then uses a pretrained Sentence-BERT model to compute soft text-similarity logits as teacher signals. The video-text model is trained with a KL-divergence objective (Eq. 2) that matches its output distribution over the positive and k synthetic negatives to the LLM teacher's distribution, rather than using hard binary labels. This soft-logit formulation accounts for semantic similarity between different actions, preventing overfitting and yielding more discriminative action embeddings that improve RCAD R@1 by 7.2–7.4% on InternVideo and SimVTP with negligible drops on standard retrieval.

## Results

On the proposed Feint6K dataset (RCAD task), the best finetuned model InternVideo achieves 58.6% R@1 on MSR-VTT videos and 58.2% R@1 on VATEX videos, far below the 95.2% and 96.8% human baselines. Applying LLM-teacher with soft logits (LLM-teacher-lgt) improves InternVideo to 65.8% and 65.6% R@1 on Feint6K, while incurring only a 0.2% and 0.6% drop on standard video-to-text retrieval (48.9% and 87.3% R@1). On standard retrieval, InternVideo leads with 49.1% R@1 on MSR-VTT and 87.9% R@1 on VATEX, but these scores drop by over 30% when evaluated on the counterfactually augmented RCAD task, exposing the shortcut-learning limitation of contrastive video-text models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates seven baselines (CLIP, VideoCLIP, InternVideo, LanguageBind, CLIP4Clip, VindLU, SimVTP) on both standard video-to-text retrieval and the proposed RCAD task. On standard retrieval, InternVideo dominates with 87.9% R@1 on VATEX, but on Feint6K it drops to 58.2% R@1 — a 38.6% gap below the 96.8% human baseline. SimVTP, the second model tested with LLM-teacher, shows the largest relative improvement: its Feint6K R@1 rises from 35.7% to 43.5% (MSR-VTT) and 33.6% to 40.1% (VATEX) with LLM-teacher-lgt, a +7.8% and +6.5% gain respectively. InternVideo with LLM-teacher-lgt reaches 65.8% and 65.6% R@1 on Feint6K, the best model performance reported, yet still trails humans by ~30%. Notably, CLIP4Clip and VindLU achieve 50.8% and 53.4% R@1 on Feint6K(MSR-VTT) without LLM-teacher, outperforming SimVTP's 35.7% on the same split, suggesting that pretraining data scale and architecture matter independently of the contrastive shortcut issue. All models remain well above the 16.7% random baseline, confirming the task is solvable but substantially harder than standard retrieval.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the video-text contrastive pretraining lineage (CLIP, CLIP4Clip, SimVTP, InternVideo, LanguageBind) but departs from the standard evaluation paradigm by arguing that existing video-text retrieval and classification benchmarks are compromised by shortcut learning on object entities. It adapts the counterfactually augmented data framework from NLP (Kaushik et al., 2019) to the video domain, constructing hard negatives that preserve object entities while modifying actions, thereby forcing cross-frame reasoning. The LLM-teacher method extends knowledge distillation (Hinton et al., 2015) to the video-text contrastive setting, using a pretrained LLM to generate synthetic captions and provide soft pseudo-labels that counteract the shortcut saturation identified in contrastive video-text pretraining.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that SOTA video-text models (e.g., InternVideo) drop over 30% in accuracy on RCAD relative to standard retrieval, and trail human performance by ~39%, challenges the prevailing narrative that large-scale video-text pretraining yields genuine video understanding. The diagnostic that contrastive models learn substantially more discriminative object embeddings than action embeddings (Fig. 5a) points to a structural limitation of the contrastive objective on video-text data that likely affects downstream tasks beyond retrieval. Open questions include whether the shortcut problem is fundamental to all contrastive video-text pretraining or specific to the scale/object diversity of current datasets, whether LLM-teacher-style distillation can close the remaining ~30-point gap to human performance, and whether the RCAD evaluation generalizes to other video understanding tasks such as video question answering or temporal grounding.
