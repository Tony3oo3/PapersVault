## Problem

Dense supervision in 6-DoF grasp detection (billion-scale labels from GraspNet-1Billion) achieves strong performance but imposes extreme training overhead—slow convergence, ~35 GB memory, and ~55 GB storage—while sparse supervision is resource-friendly yet underperforms due to label ambiguity. No prior work has systematically explored how to select a minimal, unambiguous subset of dense labels that preserves performance while drastically cutting resource costs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240719/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single-view point cloud from a depth camera (Kinect or RealSense) and outputs 6-DoF grasp poses parameterized as [center, view (1–300), in-plane angle (1–12), depth (1–4), width, quality score]. Under economic supervision, each point retains all 300 views but only the single best grasp per view (300 labels per point instead of 14,400), and points without valid grasps (friction < 0.8 or collision) are pruned entirely, reducing the label set from 55 GB to 1.6 GB.

## Method

EconomicGrasp combines an economic supervision paradigm with a focal representation module on a 14-layer 3D UNet backbone (Minkowski Engine). The supervision strategy keeps all 300 grasp views per point to eliminate the ambiguity problem (where similar points would receive conflicting labels), prunes angles and depths to the best grasp per view, pre-computes scene-level labels, and removes non-graspable points—reducing storage to ~1/30 of dense supervision. The focal representation module introduces a global interactive attention over region points to build a discriminative feature for the specific grasp, a local interactive attention among the four grasp components (angle, depth, width, score) to capture their data-dependent coupling, and a composite score estimation that models quality as a 6-class classifier and linearly combines class probabilities. A selective match loss masks loss for input points lacking nearby valid labels, and training requires only 10 epochs with batch size 4, achieving ~1/4 training time and ~1/8 memory of the SOTA dense method GSNet while surpassing it by ~3 AP on average.

## Results

On GraspNet-1Billion (Kinect), EconomicGrasp achieves 62.59 / 51.73 / 19.54 AP on seen / similar / novel splits, surpassing the SOTA dense-supervision method GSNet (61.19 / 47.39 / 19.01) by 1.40, 4.32, and 0.53 AP respectively (~3 AP on average). On RealSense, the gains are even larger: 68.21 / 61.19 / 25.48 AP vs. GSNet's 65.70 / 53.75 / 23.98. In terms of resource cost, EconomicGrasp requires only 8.3 h training time (vs. 37.8 h for GSNet), 4.2 GB memory (vs. 35.4 GB), and 1.6 GB label storage (vs. 55 GB), corresponding to roughly 1/4 training time, 1/8 memory, and 1/30 storage.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EconomicGrasp outperforms all eight baselines across every split (seen, similar, novel) on both Kinect and RealSense cameras. Against the SOTA dense-supervision method GSNet [54], it gains +1.40 / +4.32 / +0.53 AP on seen / similar / novel (Kinect) and +2.51 / +7.42 / +1.50 AP (RealSense), with the largest margin on the similar-object split. Compared to the other dense method TSB [34], the improvement is even more pronounced: +13.17 / +10.24 / +4.19 AP on Kinect. Against sparse-supervision methods, EconomicGrasp leads GraNet [55] by +21.21 / +16.44 / +7.97 AP and TransGrasp [32] by +26.62 / +22.02 / +8.13 AP on Kinect. The sample-based baselines GPD [51] and PointNetGPD [29] trail by over 35 AP on seen, reflecting the fundamental limitation of the sample-then-evaluate pipeline. In real-world pick-and-place tests with a Franka Emika robot, EconomicGrasp achieves 92.3% success rate (39/36 objects) versus GSNet's 87.8% (41/36 attempts), and reduces failure cases (scenes with no successful grasp in top-50) from 109 to 36 out of 7680.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a middle ground between sparse-supervision grasp methods (S4G, TransGrasp, GraNet) and dense-supervision methods (GSNet, TSB) built on GraspNet-1Billion. It departs from the dense paradigm by identifying an 'ambiguity' problem—where reducing labels per point creates conflicting supervision signals—as the root cause of the sparse-dense performance gap, rather than architecture or loss design. The focal representation module repurposes ideas from object detection (multi-head prediction) and action quality assessment (score-as-classification) into a grasp-specific interactive attention design, while the backbone and region-based grouping follow the GSNet lineage.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical efficiency gains (1/4 training time, 1/8 memory, 1/30 storage) make 6-DoF grasp detection accessible in resource-constrained settings, and the ambiguity diagnosis offers a reusable lens for understanding why naive label reduction fails in other dense-supervision tasks. The authors acknowledge that building economic supervision from natural data (rather than pruning a dense dataset) remains an open and more challenging direction. Limitations include evaluation on a single dataset (GraspNet-1Billion) with a single backbone architecture, and testing only with a two-finger parallel gripper, leaving generalizability to other gripper types and datasets unverified.
