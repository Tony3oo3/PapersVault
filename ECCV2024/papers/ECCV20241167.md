## Problem

Multi-camera NeRF reconstruction suffers from color inconsistencies caused by differing camera settings (white balance, ISO, exposure) and lighting conditions, yet prior methods like NeRF-W and Ha-NeRF encode these differences as opaque latent codes, preventing fine-grained, physically interpretable color correction. No existing approach simultaneously models the nonlinear camera imaging pipeline and separates inherent view-dependent scene properties (specular, shading) from external camera-induced color shifts within a unified NeRF framework.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241167/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes 90 multi-view RGB images (1000×667) per scene captured by three cameras with deliberately varied settings and lighting, along with their camera poses; 60 views are used for training and 30 for testing. An optional reference image can be supplied at inference time to guide color translation of rendered novel views. The output is a unified-color NeRF (a single radiance field in a white-balanced scene color space) from which novel views can be rendered either in their native camera color space or translated to match the reference image's color distribution.

## Method

The core contribution is a per-camera 3×7 nonlinear color translation matrix M, predicted by an embedding network, that maps scene-space colors through a polynomial kernel Φ([R,G,B]→[R,G,B,RG,RB,GB,1]) into each camera's color space, explicitly simulating the physical imaging pipeline (white balance, tone mapping, gamma encoding). Scene color is decomposed into a view-independent diffuse component c_d and a view-dependent component c_s (specular/shading), both of which share the same camera matrix M before volume rendering. Training combines four tailored losses: pixel-level MSE reconstruction, L2 regularization on c_s to enforce clean diffuse/view-dependent separation, a gray-world L2 prior to anchor the scene color space, and a cross-camera differentiable histogram loss (computed in YUV space via sigmoid-approximated CDFs) that enforces global color-distribution consistency between rendered and ground-truth views as well as between a random novel view and the training view.

## Results

On the collected 6-scene multi-camera dataset, the proposed method achieves a mean PSNR of 29.69 dB, SSIM of 0.945, LPIPS of 0.180, and W2 (Wasserstein color distance) of 2.30×10⁻³, outperforming all baselines across every metric. The most dramatic gain is in color consistency: the W2 metric drops from 3.86 (Ha-NeRF) and 4.20 (NeRF-W) to 2.30, a ~40% reduction. On the challenging 'Room' scene with large reflective areas, the method achieves 31.10 dB PSNR versus 14.77 (NeRF-W) and 14.97 (Ha-NeRF), indicating robustness where latent-code-based methods fail.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The method is compared against three baselines: original NeRF, NeRF-W (latent-code appearance encoding), and Ha-NeRF (hallucinated NeRF with reference-image color translation). The proposed method wins on all four metrics (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, W2) across all six scenes, with the largest margins over NeRF-W and Ha-NeRF on the W2 color-consistency metric (2.30 vs. 3.86 and 4.20 mean). Notably, NeRF-W and Ha-NeRF actually perform *worse* than vanilla NeRF on the 'Room' scene (14.77/14.97 dB vs. 16.63 dB PSNR), which the authors attribute to large reflective areas that confuse latent-code-based appearance modeling. The W2 metric specifically measures the Wasserstein distance between the rendered image's color distribution and the training image's color distribution after applying the per-camera correction matrix, directly quantifying color consistency rather than pixel-level fidelity. The method's advantage is most pronounced in scenes with strong illumination variation (Pumpkin: W2 0.53 vs. 1.27/1.40) and complex geometry (Pipe: W2 0.42 vs. 1.50/0.60), confirming that the explicit nonlinear color translation matrix handles both external camera differences and inherent view-dependent effects more effectively than implicit latent codes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the NeRF volumetric rendering framework and the polynomial color-mapping formulation from Afifi & Brown (2020), repurposing the 3×7 nonlinear color translation matrix as a per-camera correction module embedded within NeRF's rendering pipeline. It departs from NeRF-W and Ha-NeRF, which encode appearance inconsistencies as view-dependent latent codes, by instead modeling the color differences as a physically interpretable simulation of the camera ISP pipeline (white balance, tone mapping, gamma encoding). It also extends URF's restrictive 3×3 white-balance matrix to a full nonlinear polynomial mapping and introduces a diffuse/specular decomposition so that the color correction matrix is shared across view-independent and view-dependent components.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The physically-motivated color correction module offers a more interpretable and controllable alternative to latent-code appearance encoding, enabling intuitive color translation to a reference image and potentially broader adoption in multi-camera NeRF pipelines (dynamic scenes, avatars). However, the evaluation is limited to a single self-collected dataset of six scenes, and the histogram loss adds ~10% training time while performing best only when rendered and reference views are spatially nearby. Open questions include whether a 3D histogram loss or a learned 3D color-distribution representation could generalize the cross-view consistency constraint, and whether the approach scales to larger, more diverse multi-camera rigs.
