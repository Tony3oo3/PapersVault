## Problem

Photorealistic virtual object insertion from a single image requires recovering scene lighting and tone-mapping, but inverse rendering is fundamentally ill-posed and existing methods—whether hand-crafted or data-driven priors—are tightly coupled to specific domains (indoor vs. outdoor, day vs. night). Large diffusion models possess broad scene understanding but fail out-of-the-box to produce physically consistent lighting effects (shadows, specular highlights) while preserving the identity of the inserted object.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single LDR RGB image captured by a consumer device, a posed 3D virtual object (geometry + PBR materials), a proxy ground plane acting as a shadow catcher, and the camera pose. It outputs an HDR environment map parameterized as N Spherical Gaussian lobes (7 parameters each), per-channel rational-quadratic spline tone-mapping curves (5 bins each), and the final alpha-composited image. The environment map is split into separate foreground and shadow components during optimization that are progressively fused, and the tone curve is applied independently to the rendered object and the shadow ratio to compensate for unknown camera sensor response.

## Method

DiPIR couples a differentiable path-tracing renderer (Mitsuba 3 with Path Replay Backpropagation) to a LoRA-personalized latent diffusion model, using the DM as a perceptual guidance signal rather than a direct generator. The lighting is represented as optimizable Spherical Gaussian lobes, and the shadow effect on the background is computed as a path-traced radiance ratio (with/without the object) using Multiple Importance Sampling. A key design choice is the personalization strategy: the DM is fine-tuned (rank-4 LoRA, <15 min) on the input scene image plus ~30–200 synthetic images of the object class to preserve concept identity while adapting to scene appearance. The optimization uses a novel LDS loss that replaces the standard SDS gradient with the delta between the personalized and non-personalized DM noise predictions, improving training stability. Two separate environment maps (foreground and shadow) are initialized and progressively fused via a luminance-consistency regularizer and a log-space Cauchy L2 penalty to recover high-intensity peaks early in optimization.

## Results

On the PolyHaven dataset, DiPIR achieves the best quantitative metrics across all measures: RMSE 0.048, SSIM 0.989, LPIPS 0.0147, and si-RMSE 0.027, outperforming the closest baseline (StyleLight) by 14% in RMSE and 27% in si-RMSE. On the Waymo outdoor driving dataset, DiPIR is preferred over all four baselines in user studies, with the strongest margin against StyleLight (77.8% preference) and the weakest against NLFE (67.4%), while maintaining consistent performance across sunny, cloudy, twilight, and night conditions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DiPIR outperforms all five baselines across both evaluation settings. Against Hold-Geoffroy et al. [22] (deep sky modeling for outdoor daytime), DiPIR is preferred 68.8% of the time overall, with the largest gap in night scenes (85.7%), where the sky-model baseline degrades severely. Against NLFE [62] (neural light field estimation), DiPIR wins 67.4% overall but is closest in twilight (44.4% for NLFE) where volumetric lighting generalizes better. Against StyleLight [61] (dual-branch StyleGAN for HDR panorama generation), DiPIR achieves the largest user-study margin (77.8% on Waymo, 75.8% on PolyHaven) and the largest quantitative gap (RMSE 0.048 vs. 0.056, si-RMSE 0.027 vs. 0.039), as StyleLight's indoor training distribution causes domain gap on outdoor scenes. Against DiffusionLight [44] (concurrent work inpainting a chrome ball), DiPIR wins 70.8% on Waymo and 66.7% on PolyHaven, with the advantage most pronounced in sunny daytime where DiffusionLight struggles to predict high-intensity peaks. Against Wang et al. [63] (indoor inverse rendering with spatially-varying lighting), DiPIR is preferred 84.8% of the time on PolyHaven, confirming its advantage even in controlled indoor settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

DiPIR positions itself at the intersection of two well-developed lines of work: diffusion-model-based generative priors (DreamFusion's SDS, LoRA personalization) and physically-based differentiable rendering (Mitsuba 3, Path Replay Backpropagation). Rather than using a diffusion model as a direct image generator for lighting (as in DiffusionLight's chrome-ball inpainting or StyleLight's GAN-based HDR synthesis), DiPIR repurposes a personalized LDM as a perceptual guidance signal that drives optimization of explicit Spherical Gaussian lighting and tone-mapping parameters through a differentiable PBR pipeline. The key methodological departure from DreamFusion is the LDS loss, which replaces the standard SDS gradient with the delta between a LoRA-personalized and the base diffusion model, and the addition of a concept-preservation fine-tuning strategy that prevents overfitting to the target scene.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By recovering physically-based lighting and tone-mapping from a single consumer-grade image, DiPIR enables photorealistic virtual object insertion without HDR capture rigs or multi-view data, with the added benefit that the recovered scene parameters support post-hoc material optimization, local emission control, and tone-mapping correction. The main limitations are the Spherical Gaussian representation's inability to handle highly specular materials realistically, the absence of scene-to-object reflections (color bleeding), and the per-scene LoRA fine-tuning overhead (~15 min). Open questions include whether more expressive lighting representations with generative priors can close the specular gap, and whether test-time-free personalization methods can eliminate the fine-tuning cost.
