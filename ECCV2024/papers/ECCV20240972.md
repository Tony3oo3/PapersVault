## Problem

Insect identification from automated camera traps faces a unique combination of challenges absent in prior species-recognition benchmarks: thousands of extremely similar species, severe long-tailed label distributions, and a large domain gap between high-quality human-captured photographs and low-resolution, variable-quality images from field-deployed webcams. No prior work has provided a large-scale benchmark for fine-grained insect classification that explicitly tests out-of-distribution generalization from curated training images to in-the-wild camera trap deployments across multiple geographies and hardware setups.

## Input / Output

The model consumes RGB insect image crops at a default 128×128 resolution; at inference, trap crops are black-padded to square to avoid shape distortion before resizing. The system performs two sequential tasks: a binary moth/non-moth filter (trained on 700K images) followed by fine-grained classification producing species, genus, and family labels, where higher-rank predictions are obtained by aggregating species-level logits. The training set (AMI-GBIF) contains ~2.5M images across 5,364 species with a pronounced long-tail (907 species have fewer than 20 images), while the evaluation set (AMI-Traps) comprises 52,948 insect crops from 2,893 trap images across five countries.

## Method

The paper trains five standard architectures (ResNet50, MobileNetV3-Large, ConvNeXt-T/B, ViT-B/16) with ImageNet-pretrained weights on AMI-GBIF and evaluates zero-shot transfer to AMI-Traps, framing insect monitoring as an out-of-distribution generalization problem. The key technical contribution is a Mixed Resolution (MixRes) data augmentation that randomly downscales training images to 25% or 50% of the model input size (each with probability 0.25, preserving original size with probability 0.5), forcing the network to learn features robust to the low-resolution crops typical of small insects in camera trap images. Combined with standard augmentations (random resize crop, horizontal flip, RandAugment N=2 M=9), MixRes yields a 4–7% absolute improvement on AMI-Traps species accuracy at the cost of a slight in-distribution drop. ConvNeXt-B emerges as the best-performing model, reaching 76.75% micro top-1 species accuracy on NE-America trap data, while the binary moth/non-moth classifier achieves 95% recall but only 68% precision on small crops, highlighting the difficulty of filtering non-target insects in the field.

## Results

On the fine-grained moth species classification task, ConvNeXt-B achieves the best out-of-distribution performance on AMI-Traps: 76.75% micro top-1 species accuracy in NE-America, 82.87% in W-Europe, and 51.92% genus accuracy in C-America (where species-level annotations are largely unavailable). In-distribution accuracy on AMI-GBIF is substantially higher (88.62–90.45% species), highlighting a 10–35 point domain gap. For the binary moth/non-moth task, ResNet50 achieves 86.48% accuracy on AMI-Traps with 95.03% recall but only 68.31% precision, with performance improving to 92.95% accuracy on crops >150px. The MixRes data augmentation technique yields a 4–7% improvement on AMI-Traps over the no-augmentation baseline while slightly reducing in-distribution accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ConvNeXt-B outperforms all other baselines on AMI-Traps in W-Europe (82.87% species vs. 79.39% ResNet50, 76.30% MobileNetV3-L) and C-America (51.92% genus vs. 47.44% ResNet50, 41.03% MobileNetV3-L), and leads on AMI-GBIF in-distribution accuracy across all three regions. ViT-B/16 is the closest competitor: it edges out ConvNeXt-B by 0.68 points on NE-America AMI-Traps species (77.43% vs. 76.75%) and by 0.41 points on W-Europe AMI-Traps family (91.68% vs. 91.27%), but trails ConvNeXt-B on W-Europe species (82.44% vs. 82.87%) and C-America genus (47.44% vs. 51.92%). ConvNeXt-T, the lighter variant, consistently lags ConvNeXt-B by 1.5–4 points on AMI-Traps species. MobileNetV3-L, the lightest model, shows the largest gap behind ConvNeXt-B (9.3 points on NE-America species, 6.6 points on W-Europe species), suggesting a meaningful accuracy cost for edge-deployment constraints. Overall, the paper positions ConvNeXt-B as the recommended baseline, with ViT-B/16 as a near-equivalent alternative that occasionally wins at higher taxonomic ranks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of biodiversity computer vision and insect ecology. Prior species-recognition benchmarks (iNaturalist, iWildCam, NABirds) have focused on birds, mammals, or plants, while the few insect-specific datasets (BIOSCAN-1M, Indian Butterflies) are either limited in taxonomic scope or lack the fine-grained, multi-region structure needed for real deployment. The insect camera trap literature (Sticky Pi, UV light traps) has addressed detection and coarse classification on small, single-site datasets, but no prior work has framed the problem as large-scale fine-grained classification with out-of-distribution generalization from curated human-captured images to automated trap imagery across continents. The MixRes augmentation is a novel, lightweight technique specifically designed to bridge the resolution gap between high-quality GBIF photographs and low-resolution trap crops.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

With insects comprising half of known species and facing a documented 'insect apocalypse,' scalable automated monitoring is urgent, yet expert entomologists are scarce and species vary dramatically across regions. The AMI benchmark provides the first realistic testbed for algorithms that must generalize across geographies, hardware, and the extreme long-tail of insect diversity, directly addressing the bottleneck that has kept insect data 'woefully inadequate.' Key limitations include the focus on nocturnal moths only (excluding diurnal pollinators with different visual challenges), coverage of just three regions, and the absence of object detection from the benchmark. Open questions include whether more sophisticated domain adaptation or foundation-model approaches can close the remaining OOD gap, and whether the MixRes-style resolution augmentation generalizes to other fine-grained, small-object domains.
