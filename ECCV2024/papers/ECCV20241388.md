## Problem

Existing dataset distillation methods either optimize directly in image space, ignoring the low-rank structure inherent in natural images, or rely on parameterization techniques (generators, shared memories) that incur additional storage, data, and training overhead. No prior method fully exploits the low-rankness across all dimensions of the entire dataset while enabling efficient cross-dimensional information sharing to maximize the informativeness of each synthetic sample.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241388/cropped_page0_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a real labeled RGB image dataset (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 64×64 for TinyImageNet, 128×128 for ImageNet subsets) and produces a condensed dataset parameterized as a set of 4D spectrum tensors (BCHW-like format) and separable kernel matrices, rather than raw pixel images. The total storage of tensors plus kernels is constrained to match the budget of IPC=1/10/50 synthetic images (Eq. 3), and individual images are reconstructed on-the-fly via matrix multiplication during training. The spectrum tensor spatial dimensions (t₃, t₄) are set to half or seven-eighths of the original resolution depending on IPC, while the channel dimension t₁ is chosen to satisfy the storage constraint with t₁ < u₁.

## Method

Each synthetic image is decomposed as x = T·K, where T is a 4D spectrum tensor and K is a kernel matrix that maps each dimension from length tₙ to uₙ (tₙ ≤ uₙ for compression). The kernel is further factorized into independent per-dimension sub-kernels (separable kernel), reducing parameter count from ∏tₙuₙ to ∏tₙuₙ. Multiple spectrum tensors and kernel matrices are combined pairwise (N_T × N_K images from N_T + N_K parameters), creating cross-dimensional information sharing that increases sample diversity and training efficiency. Training builds on MTT trajectory matching (minimizing ‖θ^s_{i+N} − θ^t_{i+M}‖²) and augments it with a real-data guided cross-entropy loss computed on the student model's final trajectory weights against real samples, compensating for limited teacher trajectory coverage. The decomposition acts as an implicit structural constraint that can be plugged into any optimization framework, and the kernel matrices are learnable (unlike fixed DCT/SVD/DWT bases), allowing the model to adapt frequency-band utilization during distillation.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with IPC=1, the proposed Neural Spectrum Decomposition achieves 68.5% top-1 accuracy, a 22.2 percentage-point (47.9% relative) improvement over the MTT baseline (46.3%). At IPC=10, it reaches 73.4% on CIFAR-10 and 46.1% on CIFAR-100, outperforming the strongest prior methods (Deng et al. at 71.2% and 42.9%, respectively). On TinyImageNet (IPC=1), the method attains 21.3% versus 16.0% for the best prior parametrization method (Deng et al.) and 8.8% for MTT. On ImageNet subsets (IPC=1), it achieves up to 68.6% on ImageNette, a 43% relative gain over MTT (47.7%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all 15 baselines across every dataset and IPC setting. Against the strongest trajectory-matching baseline MTT [2], it gains +22.2 pp on CIFAR-10 IPC=1 (68.5% vs 46.3%), +8.1 pp on CIFAR-10 IPC=10, and +12.5 pp on TinyImageNet IPC=1 (21.3% vs 8.8%). Compared to the best parametrization method (Deng et al. [6]), it leads by +2.1 pp on CIFAR-10 IPC=1, +2.2 pp on CIFAR-10 IPC=10, and +5.3 pp on TinyImageNet IPC=1. Among other original-space methods, FRePo [35] and FTD [7] are the closest competitors at higher IPC (e.g., FTD reaches 73.8% on CIFAR-10 IPC=50 vs. 75.2% for ours), but the gap widens dramatically at IPC=1 where FRePo/FTD score ~46.8% versus 68.5%. On ImageNet subsets (Table 3), the method surpasses GLaD [3] by 10–20 pp and MTT by 12–21 pp across all six subsets. In cross-architecture evaluation (Table 4), it achieves 71.4% on AlexNet (vs. 63.8% for Deng et al.) and 67.8% on VGG11 (vs. 60.6%), confirming superior generalization beyond the training ConvNet3 architecture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on MTT (Matching Training Trajectories) as its optimization backbone, augmenting it with a real-data distribution guidance loss. Conceptually, it departs from prior parameterization methods (IDC, HaBa, Addressable Memories) by identifying that their success rests on two shared principles—low-rankness and information sharing—and then generalizes these principles to the entire dataset rather than individual images. The spectral decomposition framework (spectrum tensors × separable kernel matrices) repurposes classical signal-processing tools (DCT, SVD, DWT) as a generic, learnable image representation, replacing the need for auxiliary generators or hallucination networks used by prior parametrization approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The decomposition is plug-and-play with any distillation optimizer and yields notably stronger cross-architecture generalization than prior methods (e.g., 71.4% on AlexNet vs. 63.8% for Addressable Memories), suggesting the spectral representation captures frequency-band information in a more architecture-agnostic way. However, the gap to full-dataset performance remains substantial (75.2% vs. 84.8% on CIFAR-10 at IPC=50), and experiments are limited to relatively small datasets and shallow ConvNets for most comparisons. Open questions include whether the low-rank-across-dimensions assumption holds for very high-resolution or multi-modal data, and whether the pairwise combination strategy scales efficiently when the number of spectrum tensors and kernel matrices grows.
