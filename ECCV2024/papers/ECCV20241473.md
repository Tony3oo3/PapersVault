## Problem

Real-time monocular SLAM requires simultaneously accurate pose estimation, dense 3D geometry, and computational efficiency, but no existing representation achieves all three. Sparse methods lack dense geometry, per-frame depth priors (codes, bases) produce inconsistent 3D surfaces across views, and volumetric representations (voxels, neural fields, Gaussian splatting) are too expensive for real-time joint optimization. The paper tackles this by asking whether a compact 3D representation can guarantee intrinsic 3D consistency while remaining tractable for second-order optimization.

## Input / Output

The system consumes monocular RGB video frames downsampled to 256×192, operating in a sliding window of 9 keyframes and 3 support frames (24 total). Each keyframe hosts a pre-trained depth covariance function (CNN feature map) and views at most 64 3D anchor points. Outputs include per-keyframe dense depth maps, a world-frame 3D point cloud, camera poses, and the compact anchor point set. Photometric residuals are computed on grayscale images using pixels with the highest gradient magnitude in 4×4 patches, and the depth covariance CNN is pre-trained on ScanNet and frozen at test time.

## Method

COMO encodes scene geometry as a compact set of 3D anchor points shared across keyframes and decodes dense per-keyframe depth maps via a zero-mean Gaussian Process whose covariance function is conditioned on per-pixel CNN features. The key mechanism is that GP conditioning (Eq. 3) guarantees every dense depth map passes exactly through its visible anchor points, so depth maps from different views intersect at shared 3D locations, enforcing 3D consistency by construction rather than through pairwise losses. The frontend leverages the same covariance function for visibility checks (solving a least-squares projection of dense depths onto sparse anchor coordinates), active initialization via conditional variance reduction to place new points in geometrically complex regions, and depth initialization for new anchors. The backend jointly optimizes camera poses (se(3) Lie-algebra updates) and anchor point log-depths via Gauss-Newton on photometric residuals with IRLS/Huber weighting, exploiting analytic Jacobians through the linear GP conditioning to keep the Hessian accumulation efficient. Additional priors (log-median depth, GP prior on correlated anchor points, pixel-location prior) regularize the ill-posed dense reconstruction without breaking the compact linear system structure.

## Results

On the Replica dataset, COMO achieves a mean ATE of 3.4 cm, the best among all dense methods (vs. DROID-VO at 6.9 cm and DeepFactors at 40.2 cm), though sparse methods DSO (1.0 cm) and ORB-SLAM3 (1.9 cm) remain superior in this favorable setting. On the more challenging TUM RGBD dataset, COMO achieves 10.8 cm mean ATE, outperforming all dense baselines including DROID-VO (11.4 cm), DepthCov (19.9 cm), and DeepFactors (24.9 cm), while the best sparse method DPVO reaches 8.4 cm. On 18 diverse ScanNet test sequences, COMO attains the lowest mean ATE and area-under-the-curve among all evaluated methods. For dense geometry, COMO achieves both the best depth accuracy and the best inter-frame consistency on Replica and ScanNet without explicit geometric or keypoint constraints.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among sparse baselines, ORB-SLAM3 and DSO both lose tracking on multiple TUM sequences (marked ✗), while DPVO achieves the best overall ATE (8.4 cm) but does not produce dense geometry. Among dense baselines, COMO clearly outperforms DeepFactors (10.8 vs. 24.9 cm on TUM; 3.4 vs. 40.2 cm on Replica), demonstrating that code-based per-frame depth priors suffer from significant drift despite photometric, geometric, and keypoint consistency losses. COMO also beats DROID-VO (10.8 vs. 11.4 cm on TUM; 3.4 vs. 6.9 cm on Replica), which reconstructs all pixels but lacks geometric correlation between depths. The ablation COMO-NC (no 3D correspondence tracking) scores 19.4 cm on TUM, confirming that sharing 3D anchor points across frames reduces error by nearly 50% over the per-frame 2D depth formulation of DepthCov (19.9 cm). TANDEM fails on most TUM sequences due to its reliance on DSO for odometry, and MonoGS (31.8 cm) is both less accurate and slower (412 s vs. 95 s on fr3/long) due to expensive volumetric rendering. On ScanNet, COMO achieves the lowest mean ATE and AUC, validating that 3D consistency from anchor points is particularly valuable under real-world noise and specular surfaces where photometric assumptions are violated.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends the depth covariance function introduced by the same authors in [9] (CVPR 2023), which modeled per-frame log-depth distributions via a learned Gaussian Process. The key conceptual departure is elevating the representation from per-frame 2D inducing depths (which share the same limitations as compact codes like DeepFactors) to a shared set of 3D anchor points across frames, so that the GP conditioning guarantee—that depth maps pass through specified 3D points—enforces intrinsic 3D consistency by construction rather than through pairwise losses. This positions COMO between sparse SLAM (efficient but no dense geometry), per-frame dense methods (dense but no 3D consistency), and volumetric methods (3D-consistent but too expensive for real-time joint optimization), occupying a previously unoccupied middle ground.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

COMO demonstrates that real-time joint optimization of camera poses and dense, 3D-consistent geometry is feasible with a compact representation (~240 anchor points), removing the long-standing trade-off between efficiency and geometric consistency in monocular SLAM. The 50% ATE reduction from 3D correspondence (COMO vs. COMO-NC) validates that sharing points across frames is the critical ingredient, not the GP itself. Open questions include whether the approach extends to full map-centric SLAM with loop closure and relocalization, whether replacing photometric error with learned correspondence (as in DROID-VO) would further improve robustness on challenging real-world data, and whether the off-the-shelf ScanNet-trained covariance function generalizes to outdoor or highly dynamic scenes.
