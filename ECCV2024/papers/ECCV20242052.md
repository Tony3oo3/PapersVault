## Problem

Cross-domain image-level regression in remote sensing remains understudied due to the absence of suited datasets and the difficulty of generalizing models across regions with different sensors, spectral ranges, and label distributions. In particular, source-free domain adaptation for regression—where no target-domain information is available during training and only a few labeled samples are accessible at test time—has received little attention, leaving a gap between the promise of universal models and their practical deployment in forest monitoring.

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB aerial or nanosatellite images at 0.2–0.5 m ground sampling distance (patch sizes 100–250 px) along with a small support set of N labeled samples per label group drawn from the target domain at test time. It outputs three continuous regression values per image: mean canopy height in meters, overstory tree count, and tree cover fraction as a percentage. The evaluation follows a universal source-free protocol where the model is trained exclusively on source-domain data (Denmark, France) and adapted to unseen target domains (Slovakia, Slovenia, Spain) using only 5–20 support samples, with no retraining on target data during the procurement stage.

## Method

The approach trains a ViT-B/16 feature extractor on the source domain using Geometric Order Learning (GOL), which enforces a well-ordered embedding space via M shared reference points and a composite loss (order, metric, and center losses) that aligns embedding directions and distances with label relations. At test time, embeddings are extracted on the target domain and a novel Manifold Diffusion for Regression (MDR) step refines predictions: an affinity matrix is built from k-NN cosine similarities in the embedding space, a symmetric normalized graph Laplacian is formed, and a linear system (I − αWₙ)⁻¹S is solved to propagate the few known support labels through local and global manifold structure. The final regression estimate is a weighted sum of the top-kᵥ support labels ranked by their diffused similarity scores. The key insight is that the geometric ordering enforced during training creates a clean, generalizable embedding structure that makes transductive diffusion meaningful for continuous-valued regression, outperforming inductive fine-tuning when the domain gap is large.

## Results

On the DRIFT dataset (5-shot, averaged across Denmark and France source domains and Slovakia, Slovenia, Spain targets), GOL+MDR achieves average R² of 0.59 for canopy height, 0.56 for tree count, and 0.71 for tree cover fraction. The combined GOL+FT+MDR variant reaches 0.62, 0.55, and 0.72 respectively. Off-the-shelf regression without adaptation performs poorly on height (R²=0.23) and is inconsistent across targets, while calibrated regression and fine-tuning reach 0.50–0.51 on height. The largest gains from MDR appear on the hardest domain gap (Denmark→Spain), where GOL+MDR scores 0.60 on height versus 0.34 for fine-tuning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against fine-tuning (FT [25]), GOL+MDR wins on all three tasks: height (0.59 vs 0.51 avg R²), count (0.56 vs 0.51), and cover (0.71 vs 0.65). The gap is most pronounced on the Denmark→Spain transfer, where FT scores only 0.34 on height while GOL+MDR reaches 0.60, indicating that transductive adaptation is more robust when the visual and semantic domain gap is large. Against GOL with kNN prediction [24], GOL+MDR improves height by 0.10 R² (0.59 vs 0.49) and count by 0.03 (0.56 vs 0.53), but slightly underperforms on cover (0.71 vs 0.72), where the task is easier and label shift is limited. The paper's key finding is that transductive methods (kNN, GOL, GOL+MDR) become increasingly advantageous as the implied domain gap grows, while inductive methods (FT, calibrated regression) suffer failure cases with R² below 0.3 in at least one setup.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper combines two previously separate threads: Geometric Order Learning (GOL) for enforcing structured embedding spaces in regression [24], and manifold diffusion / label propagation for transductive semi-supervised learning [51]. The key conceptual move is adapting the latter from classification to regression and using it as a test-time adaptation mechanism within a source-free domain adaptation framework, where the source-trained ordered embeddings are assumed to generalize across visual and label domains. The paper also positions itself within the 'universal source-free domain adaptation' paradigm [21], extending it from classification to regression, and introduces the DRIFT dataset to fill a gap the authors identify: no prior benchmark existed for cross-domain image-level regression with sub-meter remote sensing imagery.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The DRIFT dataset provides the first multi-country, multi-sensor benchmark for cross-domain forest regression, enabling systematic study of how visual and label shifts interact in Earth observation. The practical finding that transductive methods (GOL+MDR) outperform inductive fine-tuning when the domain gap is large gives deployment-oriented practitioners a clear decision rule: when you cannot estimate the domain gap in advance, a transductive approach is a safer default. Limitations include coverage of only five European countries and three tasks, and the method's reliance on the ordering structure surviving domain shift, which may break down for more extreme biome or sensor differences. Open questions include whether the approach extends to multi-task or multi-sensor fusion settings and whether the few-shot support set design (equally spaced groups) is optimal for skewed label distributions.
