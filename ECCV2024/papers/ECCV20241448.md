## Problem

Stable Diffusion models require billion-scale parameters and extensive compute for text-to-image generation, yet prior efficiency work focused almost exclusively on reducing sampling steps or applying quantization while retaining the original architecture. Architectural compression—removing blocks from the U-Net—remained largely unexplored due to the perceived prohibitive cost of retraining such massive models. This paper demonstrates that block pruning combined with feature-level knowledge distillation achieves 30–50% reductions in parameters, MACs, and latency while requiring only 13 A100 days and 0.22M training pairs, a tiny fraction of the 6,000+ A100 days and 2,000M pairs used for the original models.

## Input / Output

For zero-shot text-to-image generation, the model takes a text prompt and produces a 512×512 RGB image via 25 denoising steps on a 64×64 latent code, using classifier-free guidance at scale 7.5. For image-to-image translation (SDEdit), it additionally accepts an input image as content conditioning. For personalized generation (DreamBooth), it takes 4–6 subject images plus prompts for per-subject fine-tuning. On edge devices (Jetson AGX Orin, iPhone 14), inference completes in under 4 seconds with 10–20 denoising steps.

## Method

The method removes residual and cross-attention blocks from the U-Net of Stable Diffusion: in down stages, the second R-A pair is eliminated (first pairs are retained as they process changed spatial information); in up stages, the third R-A pair is kept to preserve skip-connection alignment; optionally, the entire mid-stage and innermost stages are pruned for further compression, yielding Base (0.76B), Small (0.66B), and Tiny (0.50B) variants. The pruned U-Net is retrained with a combined objective of the standard denoising task loss, output-level KD (matching the teacher's noise prediction), and feature-level KD applied at the end of each stage in both teacher and student. Because distillation is placed at stage boundaries where channel dimensions already match, no learnable regressors are needed. Training uses only 0.22M LAION-Aesthetics pairs on a single A100 for 13 days (v1) or 4 days (v2), and the teacher weights initialize the student, making the approach dramatically cheaper than training from scratch while achieving competitive zero-shot MS-COCO scores.

## Results

On zero-shot MS-COCO 256×256 (30K prompts), BK-SDM-Base (0.76B params) achieves FID 15.76, IS 33.79, and CLIP score 0.2878, trained with only 0.22M image-text pairs and 13 A100 days—roughly 1/48,000th of the data and 1/480th of the compute used to train the original SDM-v1.4 (6,250 A100 days, >2,000M pairs). BK-SDM-Tiny (0.50B) achieves a 51.9% parameter reduction with FID 17.12 and 43–47% latency reduction on both CPU and GPU. The v2 variants (0.72B–0.98B) reach FID 15.68–16.61 using just 4 A100 days, outperforming the 3.1B-parameter Würstchen-v2 (FID 22.40) and 12B-parameter DALL-E (FID 27.5) on all three metrics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BK-SDM-Base (0.76B) substantially outperforms all autoregressive baselines: DALL-E (12B, FID 27.5), CogView (4B, FID 27.1), CogView2 (6B, FID 24.0), and LAFITE (0.23B, FID 26.94) by 10+ FID points, while also surpassing the 3.1B-parameter Würstchen-v2 (FID 22.40) and the 0.32B GAN GALIP (FID 13.86) on IS (33.79 vs 32.87 and 25.16) and CLIP (0.2878 vs 0.2676 and 0.2817). However, BK-SDM-Base trails the original SDM-v1.4 (FID 13.05), Small Stable Diffusion (FID 12.76, trained on 229M pairs), GigaGAN (FID 9.09), DALL-E-2 (FID 10.39), and MobileDiffusion (FID 9.01) on FID, reflecting the inherent quality cost of architectural compression. Against the closest same-size competitor, Small Stable Diffusion (also 0.76B), BK-SDM-Base achieves higher IS (33.79 vs 32.33) and CLIP (0.2878 vs 0.2851) but worse FID (15.76 vs 12.76), though it uses 1,000× less training data (0.22M vs 229M pairs) and only 13 A100 days. The v2-Tiny variant (0.72B) achieves FID 15.68 in just 4 A100 days, beating SnapFusion (0.99B, FID ~13.6, >128 A100 days) on parameter count while remaining competitive on FID. In the A/B human preference study (2,501 comparisons), BK-SDM-Small wins 71.5% against GLIDE-filtered, 61.5% against GALIP, and 51.4% against Würstchen-v2, but loses 67.9% against the full SDM-v1.4, confirming the quality gap to the uncompressed original.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classical knowledge distillation and block-pruning literature (DistilBERT, FitNets, feature distillation) to the domain of billion-parameter latent diffusion models, specifically the U-Net in Stable Diffusion. Prior efficient-diffusion work focused on reducing sampling steps (progressive distillation, DPM-Solver) or applying quantization while retaining the full architecture; architectural compression of diffusion U-Nets was largely unexplored, partly due to the prohibitive cost of retraining. The block-removal strategy is directly inspired by DistilBERT's layer-halving with weight initialization, and the retraining relies on feature-level KD objectives from the GAN compression literature, but the combination and application to iterative denoising U-Nets with skip connections is new.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a 30–50% reduction in U-Net parameters and latency is achievable with only 13 A100 days and 0.22M image-text pairs, compared to the 6,000+ A100 days needed to train the original SDM from scratch, making architectural compression a remarkably cost-effective path to compact diffusion models. The finding that the entire mid-stage can be removed without noticeable quality degradation challenges the assumption that all U-Net stages are essential for T2I synthesis. Limitations include inheriting the source model's weaknesses (e.g., full-body human generation) and a residual quality gap versus the original (FID ~15.8 vs ~13.1). Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to SDXL, video diffusion, or other U-Net-based architectures, and whether further compression (e.g., combining with quantization or step reduction) can close the remaining quality gap.
