## Problem

Existing instruction-based NeRF editing methods (e.g., Instruct-NeRF2NeRF) suffer from blurry edited scenes due to multi-view inconsistency introduced by 2D diffusion model perturbations, and require full retraining for every new scene or style. This paper identifies that low-frequency image components are inherently more multi-view-consistent after editing and predominantly encode appearance style, while high-frequency components carry content details, and exploits this asymmetry for both fidelity and transferability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241081/cropped_page0_idx4.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-reconstructed NeRF (trained from posed RGB images) and a free-form text instruction as input. It outputs an edited NeRF capable of rendering novel views at the instructed style, with an additional continuous intensity parameter l ∈ [0,1] that interpolates between unedited and fully edited appearance at inference time. The feature field is rendered in VGG ReLU3-1 space, and the low-frequency filter operates at a 1/4 downsampling scale, which the ablation identifies as the optimal trade-off between editing effectiveness and artifact avoidance.

## Method

The framework decomposes NeRF rendering into a high-frequency branch (vanilla NeRF preserving scene geometry and texture) and a low-frequency branch that renders a VGG feature map, applies a learnable low-pass filter, passes it through a convolutional stylizer, and decodes back to RGB via a shared decoder. The final edited image is obtained by blending the high-frequency residual of the original scene onto the styled low-frequency component (Eq. 6). A formal theorem (Theorem 1) shows that editing in the low-frequency space reduces optical-flow-based multi-view inconsistency compared to editing in full resolution. Training is two-stage: first the feature renderer, low-pass filter, and decoder are trained to reconstruct low-frequency images and VGG features; then the stylizer is trained using Instruct-Pix2Pix-generated 2D edits (only their low-frequency components) as supervision, with all other modules frozen. Because the stylizer operates in a shared VGG feature domain, it transfers directly to novel scenes or even from 2D image collections to 3D NeRFs without retraining.

## Results

On multi-view consistency (Table 1), Freditor achieves the best short-term LPIPS of 0.092 and long-term LPIPS of 0.388, outperforming Instruct-NeRF2NeRF (0.104 / 0.414) and the 2D-only Instruct-Pix2Pix baseline (0.374 / 0.588). On image quality (Table 2), Freditor preserves near-original fidelity with BRISQUE 17.15 and Sharpness 255.03, whereas Instruct-NeRF2NeRF degrades severely to BRISQUE 38.99 and Sharpness 40.61 (original scene: 17.46 / 273.28). The low-frequency-only variant (Ours-Low) achieves the absolute best consistency scores (LPIPS 0.086 / 0.380) but at the cost of some detail, while the full pipeline (Ours) balances consistency and fidelity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the 2D editing baseline Instruct-Pix2Pix, Freditor wins decisively on multi-view consistency: short-term LPIPS drops from 0.374 to 0.092 and long-term LPIPS from 0.588 to 0.388, confirming that naively applying 2D diffusion edits to rendered views produces severe view inconsistency. Against the primary 3D baseline Instruct-NeRF2NeRF, Freditor achieves better consistency on all four metrics (e.g., short-term LPIPS 0.092 vs. 0.104; long-term RMSE 0.062 vs. 0.061 is a near-tie) while dramatically outperforming on image quality: BRISQUE improves from 38.99 to 17.15 (closer to the original's 17.46) and Sharpness recovers from 40.61 to 255.03 (original: 273.28). Instruct-NeRF2NeRF's blurring stems from 3D fusion of inconsistent 2D edits, whereas Freditor's frequency decomposition preserves high-frequency details while editing only the low-frequency feature space. Additionally, Freditor uniquely supports intensity control and cross-scene style transfer without retraining, capabilities absent in both baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Instruct-NeRF2NeRF's iterative dataset-update pipeline for instruction-based 3D editing and on NeRF's volumetric rendering as the underlying scene representation. Its key departure is moving the editing operation from pixel-space retraining (which causes multi-view inconsistency and blurring) into a VGG feature-space where a low-pass filter isolates the style-relevant low-frequency components, a stylizer modifies them, and a shared decoder reconstructs the edited appearance. The frequency-decomposition strategy is inspired by classical image processing and by prior observations that style resides in low frequencies while content details reside in high frequencies, but its application to NeRF feature fields for transferable and controllable editing is new.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses two practical bottlenecks in instruction-based 3D scene editing: the severe blurring caused by 2D diffusion-model perturbations and the per-scene retraining cost that limits scalability. By confining editing to the low-frequency feature space, it achieves near-original sharpness (255 vs. 40 for Instruct-NeRF2NeRF) while enabling zero-shot transfer of a trained stylizer to novel scenes and continuous intensity control via feature interpolation. The main open limitation is that naively blending high-frequency details back can conflict with the target style in certain regions, requiring an intelligent spatially-varying α that the authors leave as future work. Broader implications include the possibility of training stylizers entirely on 2D image collections and deploying them to arbitrary 3D scenes, which could significantly reduce the 3D editing workload.
