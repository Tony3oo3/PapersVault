## Problem

In hard parameter sharing multi-task learning, the shared encoder must produce representations useful for all tasks simultaneously, yet existing methods (loss weighting, gradient manipulation, multi-objective optimization) address negative transfer without directly targeting the quality or universality of the shared representations. The paper asks: how can we explicitly improve the universality of the shared encoder so that even an untrained or arbitrary task-specific predictor can achieve performance close to the optimal one?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20242141/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard RGB images (UTKFACE, ImageNet, PASCAL) or RGB-D images (NYUv2, CITYSCAPES) and simultaneously produces multiple task-specific outputs: class labels (age, gender, ethnicity), segmentation masks (semantic and part), depth maps, surface normal maps, and disparity maps. A key structural detail is that alongside each real task-specific predictor, the model maintains one or more architecturally identical dummy predictors with randomly initialized, fixed parameters that are used only during training for gradient regularization and discarded at inference.

## Method

DGR (Dummy Gradient norm Regularization) adds a penalty term to the standard MTL loss that minimizes the Frobenius norm of the gradient of each task's loss with respect to a randomly initialized, fixed dummy predictor sharing the same architecture as the real predictor. The theoretical foundation (Theorem 1) shows that the defined 'universality' of the shared encoder is inversely proportional to this gradient norm, so reducing it directly improves representation quality. In practice, three distinct fixed dummy predictors are used per task to prevent the encoder from over-adapting to a single fixed predictor, and the required Hessian is approximated via finite differences for computational efficiency. The method is a drop-in regularization term (controlled by hyperparameter λ) that can be seamlessly combined with existing MTL strategies such as DWA, PCGrad, CAGrad, IMTL, and Nash-MTL without modifying their core mechanisms.

## Results

On UTKFACE (3-task image classification), DGR applied to VANILLA improves Δ_MTL from +2.66% to +3.50%, and when combined with CAGRAD achieves the best overall Δ_MTL of +4.11% (age 0.5510, gender 0.9169, ethnicity 0.8094). On NYUv2 (3-task scene understanding), IMTL+DGR achieves the best Δ_MTL of +14.13% (mDist 22.37, mIoU 49.62, aErr 37.70) in the Split architecture, substantially outperforming VANILLA alone (+6.32%). On CITYSCAPES, NASH-MTL+DGR reaches Δ_MTL of +6.55% (mIoU 58.46, Part mIoU 55.33, aErr 0.75), the best among all combinations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DGR consistently improves most baselines when integrated: CAGRAD+DGR jumps from +3.17% to +4.11% Δ_MTL on UTKFACE, NASH-MTL+DGR goes from +3.64% to +3.77%, and AUTO-λ+DGR from +2.89% to +3.69%. On NYUv2, the gains are larger—IMTL+DGR reaches +14.13% Δ_MTL versus IMTL alone at +8.61%, and NASH-MTL+DGR achieves +11.65% vs. +9.85% for NASH-MTL alone. DWA is the one baseline where DGR does not consistently help (DWA+DGR drops from +8.26% to +6.02% on NYUv2 Split), and PCGRAD shows mixed results (slight improvement on NYUv2 but a small drop on UTKFACE). In the direct comparison against SAM and PTA (Figure 2), DGR achieves the highest average Δ_MTL improvement when integrated into AUTO-λ, CAGRAD, and NASH-MTL, outperforming both alternatives across all three base methods. The representation quality experiment (Fig. 3a) further confirms that DGR-learned features transfer better to simple classifiers (SVM, kNN, DT) than those from VANILLA or other MTL methods, particularly on the hardest task (age prediction).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing the concept of 'universality' for shared encoder representations in hard parameter sharing MTL, a property the authors explicitly note has never been formally defined or targeted in prior MTL work. While existing methods address negative transfer through loss weighting (Uncertainty, DWA, Auto-λ), gradient manipulation (PCGrad, CAGrad), or multi-objective optimization (Nash-MTL, IMTL), none directly regularize the quality of the shared representation itself. The approach draws a conceptual parallel to SAM (which minimizes loss discrepancy between nearby parameters) and to PTA (which uses multiple decoders), but repurposes the dummy-predictor mechanism specifically as a gradient-norm regularizer rather than an auxiliary task or pseudo-task generator.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DGR offers a simple, low-overhead plug-in that can be combined with virtually any existing MTL method, and the clean theoretical link between universality and gradient norm provides a principled justification for the regularization. However, the convexity assumption underlying Theorem 1 is questionable for deep networks, and the practical gains are modest (typically 1–2% Δ_MTL), with some configurations showing no improvement or slight degradation. Open questions include whether the universality concept generalizes beyond the hard parameter sharing setting, whether the finite-difference Hessian approximation remains tractable for very large predictors, and whether the benefits persist in truly large-scale MTL (e.g., dozens of tasks) where the current PASCAL experiment (5 tasks) still shows MTL underperforming single-task baselines.
