## Problem

Existing protections against unauthorized diffusion-model editing (e.g., PhotoGuard, Glaze) are designed for personalized models that rely on fixed, pre-defined prompts tied to specific subjects. Instruction-guided editing models such as InstructPix2Pix and MagicBrush use dynamic, user-specified instructions, rendering these prompt-specific defenses ineffective. This paper is the first to study and address the protection gap for instruction-guided image editing, where the protector has no knowledge of the editing instruction or type.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241677/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB source image and white-box access to the target instruction-guided editing model (InstructPix2Pix on SD v1.5 or its MagicBrush fine-tuned variant). The protector does not know the editing instruction, editing type, or any text prompt the malicious editor will use. The output is a perturbed image with an L2-norm budget of 4/255, visually indistinguishable from the original, which when fed into the editing model with any instruction yields unrealistic or subject-mismatched results. Perturbations are generated via projected gradient descent over 30 iterations with EOT over Gaussian smoothing, 5° rotation, and center cropping.

## Method

EditShield formulates protection as an optimization that maximizes the distance between the VAE latent encoding of the protected image and the original image (latent inconsistency loss), while simultaneously minimizing the pixel-level L2 difference to keep the perturbation imperceptible (perceptual consistency loss). The key insight is that shifting the VAE latent representation disrupts the image conditioning in the diffusion process, causing the edited output to lose subject identity regardless of the text instruction. Expectation Over Transformation (EOT) is integrated into the latent loss by averaging over a set of plausible image transformations (Gaussian kernel smoothing, rotation, center cropping), making the perturbation robust to pre-processing an editor might apply. The combined objective is solved with projected gradient descent under an ℓ2 perturbation budget of 4/255, initialized with small Gaussian noise, producing an untargeted, instruction-agnostic perturbation that generalizes across editing types (object addition, replacement, background change, style transfer) and synonymous instruction phrasings.

## Results

On both IPr2Pr (2,000 images) and MagicBrush (1,000 images), EditShield consistently produces lower CLIP image similarity and CLIP text-image direction similarity than PhotoGuard across both ip2p and ip2p-mb models, indicating that edited images from protected sources show greater mismatch with the source and the instruction. In terms of image quality (PSNR and SSIM), EditShield yields lower scores than PhotoGuard, confirming that the edited outputs are of poorer quality. GPT-4V evaluation on 100 source-edited pairs shows a substantial drop in both instruction-following consistency and content fidelity scores, with a notable increase in 'very poor' and 'poor' ratings. Human evaluation (50 participants, 20 image pairs) confirms that most EditShield-protected images fail to meet editing requirements.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole external baseline is PhotoGuard [Salman et al., 2023], which adds imperceptible perturbations to raise the computational cost of malicious text-prompt-based editing. EditShield outperforms PhotoGuard on all reported metrics: both CLIP image similarity and CLIP text-image direction similarity are lower (better protection) for EditShield across both ip2p and ip2p-mb models on both IPr2Pr and MagicBrush. EditShield also produces lower PSNR and SSIM scores than PhotoGuard, meaning the edited images are of worse quality and thus less usable by an unauthorized editor. The advantage is consistent across all four editing types (object addition, object replacement, background change, style transfer) and is robust to synonymous instruction phrases generated by ChatGPT. The paper also shows that removing EOT from EditShield degrades protection (higher similarity metrics), confirming that the transformation-robustness component contributes meaningfully over what PhotoGuard-style static perturbations can achieve.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of adversarial-perturbation defenses against diffusion-model misuse (PhotoGuard, Glaze, adversarial-example-based protections) from the personalized-editing setting (DreamBooth, Textual Inversion with fixed prompts) to the instruction-guided editing setting (InstructPix2Pix and its MagicBrush fine-tune), where the instruction is dynamic and unknown to the protector. The core mechanism—shifting the VAE-encoded latent representation via projected gradient descent with a perceptual-consistency constraint—repurposes standard adversarial-example tooling, while the Expectation-Over-Transformation (EOT) augmentation borrowed from robust adversarial training [1] addresses fragility to minor image transformations. The key conceptual departure from prior work is that the perturbation is instruction-agnostic: it targets the image's latent embedding rather than any specific text prompt, making it applicable regardless of what editing command an unauthorized user supplies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Instruction-guided editing models are increasingly accessible and require no fine-tuning or reference images, making them a more immediate threat for unauthorized manipulation of personal photos, celebrity images, and artwork than the personalized models that prior protections target. EditShield demonstrates that a modest, imperceptible perturbation (budget 4/255) can reliably degrade editing quality across diverse instruction types and synonymous phrasings, offering a practical pre-publication safeguard. However, the method requires white-box access to the editing model, limiting its applicability to closed-source or online platforms; the authors explicitly flag black-box protection as an open direction. Broader questions remain about whether such latent-space perturbations generalize to next-generation editing architectures and whether adaptive attackers could learn to invert or filter the perturbation.
