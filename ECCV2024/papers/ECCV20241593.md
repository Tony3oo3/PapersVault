## Problem

Existing Active Client Selection (ACS) methods in federated learning rely on client-reported loss values to prioritize informative clients, but this breaks down when clients have noisy labels: noisy clients exhibit high losses and get repeatedly selected, degrading convergence. Robust aggregation algorithms (Krum, Bucket, FixMix, Split) are designed to exclude outright outliers rather than partially noisy clients, and they require all clients to participate each round, negating communication savings. The paper addresses how to construct a communication-efficient client sampling distribution that identifies and down-weights noisy clients without trusting their reported losses or knowing the number of noisy clients in advance.

## Input / Output

The method consumes the full set of C client model update vectors (each of dimension D) collected in a single initial round, plus one additional 'probe' update generated by a known non-malicious client using randomly assigned labels to establish a worst-case baseline. The output is a fixed sampling distribution p_r over all C clients, computed once and reused across all subsequent rounds; each round then samples M clients from p_r for the FedAvg update. Notably, the distribution is static (not re-estimated per round), and the only external requirement is identifying one non-malicious client for the probe, which need not itself be noise-free.

## Method

The core mechanism is a global association score Q(h_c, G_c) that quantifies how well a client's model update correlates with the ensemble of all other clients' updates, using a Bayesian multiple-correlation framework. The score has two terms: a multiple-correlation term (how well h_c is predictable from other updates, regularized via a Gaussian probabilistic model optimized by conjugate gradient descent to remain well-defined when C ≥ D) and a contribution term (how much h_c helps predict other updates, derived from the diagonal of the optimized relevance matrix Σ_c). The sampling distribution is constructed by subtracting the probe's association score (from random labels) as a pseudo-minimum, clipping at zero, and normalizing, yielding a distribution inversely proportional to noise level without any hyperparameter tuning. For large C, the method subsamples B other updates to form G_c, reducing complexity from O(DC²+C³) to O(DB²+B³). The approach requires no client-reported losses, dataset sizes, or knowledge of the number of noisy clients.

## Results

Across six benchmark datasets (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, Food-101, CINIC-10, MINC, EMNIST) with 1,000 clients (900 noisy, 300 malicious) and M=50 clients selected per round, the proposed model-association-score method achieves significantly faster convergence than all baselines. The headline quantitative claim is that on CIFAR-100, the proposed method reaches the accuracy level that Random selection only attains by round 1,000 already at round 400 — a 2.5× speedup in convergence. Results are reported as mean accuracy over 10 repeated experiments with standard deviations, evaluated under both class-subset and Dirichlet (α=1.0) non-IID data distributions. The method is never worse than Random across all tested settings, including 10,000-client and 100-client scales, uniform label noise, and adversarial random-update attacks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against seven baselines: Random selection, AFL (Goetz et al.), FedCor (Tang et al.), InvLoss (inverse-loss sampling), RFA (Pillutla et al.), Bucket (Karimireddy et al.), FixMix (Allouah et al.), and Split (Liu et al.). The most striking finding is that the two purpose-built ACS methods — AFL and FedCor — perform significantly *worse* than Random when noisy clients are present, because their loss-based sampling distributions inadvertently up-weight noisy clients that exhibit high loss values. InvLoss (inverse loss weighting) partially addresses this and beats Random, but remains vulnerable when malicious clients transmit fabricated loss values. The four robust aggregation algorithms (RFA, Bucket, FixMix, Split) produce results comparable to or slightly below Random, as they are designed to exclude outliers entirely rather than leverage partially noisy clients. The proposed model-association-score method outperforms all seven baselines on every dataset and every experimental variant tested (varying client counts from 100 to 10,000, different noise injection strategies, and different data heterogeneity levels), with the largest gains observed on high-class-count datasets (CIFAR-100, Food-101, MINC) and under Dirichlet-distributed data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two established FL research threads: active client selection (AFL, Oort, FedCor) and Byzantine-robust aggregation (Krum, Bucket, FixMix, Split, RFA). It departs from loss-based ACS methods by arguing that loss values are unreliable or unavailable with noisy/malicious clients, and departs from robust aggregation methods by noting they are designed for outright outliers rather than partially noisy clients whose data still carries useful signal. The core technical contribution repurposes the classical statistics concept of multiple correlation, augmented with a Bayesian marginal-likelihood framework, to construct a client sampling distribution that quantifies how well each client's model update aligns with the collective, without requiring loss values, dataset sizes, or hyperparameter tuning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical finding that standard ACS methods (AFL, FedCor) perform worse than random selection under label noise is a useful warning for FL practitioners deploying in realistic, imperfectly-annotated settings. The method's elegance (no hyperparameters, no reliance on potentially manipulated loss values) makes it attractive, but two limitations constrain immediate adoption: it requires at least one identified non-malicious client to calibrate the sampling floor, and the sampling distribution is computed once in round 1 and held fixed, so it cannot adapt if client behavior evolves during training. Open questions include removing the known-client requirement, extending to dynamic or streaming client populations, and whether the cubic-in-C complexity (mitigated by subsampling) remains tractable at very large scale.
