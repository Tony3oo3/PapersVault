## Problem

Existing knowledge distillation methods for semantic segmentation rely on either more complex teacher models or additional sensor modalities (e.g., LiDAR, infrared) to provide privileged knowledge, both of which are costly or impractical in deployment. This paper asks whether the ground-truth label itself—always available in supervised settings—can be repurposed as privileged input to a lightweight teacher, eliminating the need for heavier architectures or extra sensors.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20242256/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The teacher model receives a 4-channel tensor: the standard 3-channel RGB image concatenated with a single-channel noised label (H×W), while the student model receives only the 3-channel RGB image at inference. The noised label is produced by one-hot encoding the ground-truth segmentation, scaling each class channel by an independently sampled Gaussian weight, summing across channels, and adding per-pixel Gaussian noise scaled by α. The output for both models is a pixel-wise class probability map of shape C×H×W, from which the argmax yields the segmentation prediction.

## Method

The core contribution is a Label Noising Module (LNM) that transforms the clean label into a single-channel privileged input via class-wise random scaling (distorting class identity) followed by pixel-wise Gaussian noise (disrupting intra-class consistency), preventing the teacher from taking a shortcut mapping from label to output while preserving useful spatial-semantic structure. To counteract output fluctuations caused by the random sampling in LNM, a dual-path consistency training strategy duplicates both the LNM and the teacher model, feeds each path independently sampled noise, and adds a KL-divergence consistency loss between the two outputs alongside standard segmentation losses. The student is then trained with standard knowledge distillation using the CWD channel-wise distillation loss on logits, requiring no architectural changes. This design allows the teacher to be the same architecture as, simpler than, or more complex than the student, and the noised label is discarded at inference time.

## Results

On Cityscapes, LAD with a lightweight label-assisted teacher (same architecture as student) achieves 77.7% val / 76.9% test mIoU for DeepLabV3-R18 and 76.2% val / 75.6% test mIoU for PSPNet-R18, outperforming all five compared distillation methods that rely on a heavier DeepLabV3-R101 teacher. The label-assisted lightweight teachers themselves reach 79.8% and 79.7% mIoU, surpassing the DeepLabV3-R101 teacher (78.1%). Distillation time per iteration is 259–270 ms, roughly 1.5–5× faster than competing methods (415–1295 ms). Across five datasets (Cityscapes, ADE20K, PASCAL-VOC, COCO-Stuff 10K/164K) and five model families, LAD consistently improves over the CWD baseline by 0.7–3.5% mIoU.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LAD outperforms all five compared distillation methods on both student architectures. For DeepLabV3-R18, LAD gains +0.7% val mIoU over the strongest baseline MasKD (77.7 vs 77.0) and +2.1% over CWD (75.6); for PSPNet-R18, the margins are +0.9% over MasKD (76.2 vs 75.3) and +1.8% over CWD (74.4). Notably, LAD achieves these gains while using a teacher of the same architecture as the student (13.6M params) rather than the 61.1M DeepLabV3-R101 required by all baselines, and with the lowest distillation time (259–270 ms vs 415–1295 ms). The paper further shows that even when a stronger external teacher (PSPNet-R101 at 79.8% or OCRNet-W48 at 80.7%) is used, LAD's label-assisted lightweight teacher still yields equal or better student performance (e.g., 77.7% vs 77.2% for DeepLabV3-R18), suggesting that structural similarity between teacher and student aids knowledge transfer. On PASCAL-VOC, LAD improves over CWD by +1.0% (DeepLabV3-R18) and +1.4% (PSPNet-R18), and on ADE20K it gains +1.5% and +1.7% mIoU over the CWD baseline for PSPNet-R50 and DeepLabV3-R50 respectively.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of knowledge distillation for dense prediction and Learning Using Privileged Information (LUPI). It departs from the two dominant distillation paradigms—using a heavier teacher (SKD, IFDV, CWD, CIRKD, MasKD) or injecting extra modalities (2DPASS, KD-Net)—by instead treating the ground-truth label, with intentional stochastic noise, as a privileged input channel to a lightweight teacher. The dual-path consistency loss is a novel training strategy to stabilize the teacher against the per-iteration randomness of the noising module, and the authors explicitly distinguish their intentional label noising from the annotation-noise literature. A denoising reinterpretation (Section 4.4) further connects the approach to score-matching / denoising-diffusion intuitions, though this is presented as a post-hoc explanation rather than a design principle.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical appeal is clear: practitioners no longer need to train or store a large teacher or acquire auxiliary sensors, and the teacher can be the same architecture as the student (self-enhancement) or even simpler, which broadens the design space for deployment-constrained settings. The consistent 1–3.5 mIoU gains across five datasets and five CNN backbones, plus the demonstration that a label-assisted PSPNet-R18 (79.7 mIoU) outperforms a DeepLabV3-R101 teacher (78.1 mIoU), suggest that architectural capacity is not the sole source of distillable knowledge. Open questions include whether the approach transfers cleanly to transformer-based segmenters (the DINOv2 + LoRA experiment is preliminary), how the noising distribution and magnitude should be tuned for very fine-grained or long-tail class distributions, and whether the denoising perspective can be formalized into a principled objective rather than an ad-hoc loss combination.
