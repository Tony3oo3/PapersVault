## Problem

Current open-source video generation models are limited to producing only 2–4 second clips due to poor training data annotations, prohibitive training costs (80 GB GPUs for a single 4-second video), and architectures that ignore temporal redundancy. This severely restricts creative applications and user freedom, as no affordable, training-free method exists to extend short video models to generate coherent animations of dozens of seconds.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241203/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

ZoLA takes a pre-trained short video diffusion or consistency model (trained on base length f frames) plus a text prompt, and optionally control sequences (e.g., pose), motion adapters with time-interval assignments, or multi-prompts with temporal intervals. It outputs a long animation video of up to 256 frames (~32 seconds) at 512×512p, requiring only 12 GB GPU memory and ~90 seconds of denoising time with consistency models. The method is entirely zero-shot: no retraining or fine-tuning of the base model is needed, and it can be combined with community adapters (ControlNet, LoRA, SDEdit) designed for short video or image models.

## Method

ZoLA extends generation length through two complementary expansions. First, spatiotemporal attention expansion (f → f') approximates global temporal attention by splitting features into overlapping windows of base length f, processing each window independently through the original temporal modules, and merging outputs with exponential-decay weights centered on each window. Second, denoising path expansion (f' → f'') formulates long-video denoising as a Set Cover Problem: at each denoising timestep, subsets of f' frames are selected via random starting indices and optional frame-skipping (analogous to varying fps), with overlapping frames averaged across sets. Two noise strategies further improve quality: a masked noise initialization that blends random noise with repeated base-length noise to constrain the noise manifold (balancing motion freedom vs. stability), and a noise-travel mechanism that reverses several denoising steps at early timesteps to resolve directional conflicts between overlapping frame sets, equivalent to score distillation sampling.

## Results

On 256-frame (32-second) video generation across 10 base models and 10 prompts (1,000 videos total), ZoLA achieves CLIP-SIM 0.9443, SSIM 0.6091, PSNR 15.44, LPIPS 0.3374, and FVD 387.1, closely approaching the short-video upper bound (CLIP-SIM 0.9693, SSIM 0.6255). In user studies, ZoLA is preferred for consistency 68.3% of the time and for visual quality 48.9% of the time. With consistency models, ZoLA generates a 32-second animation in 82.3 seconds using only 11.8 GB VRAM on a single A800.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ZoLA substantially outperforms both baselines on every reported metric. Against Conv-Attn (Blattmann et al., CVPR 2023), ZoLA improves CLIP-SIM from 0.8895 to 0.9443 (+0.055), SSIM from 0.4330 to 0.6091 (+0.176), and reduces FVD from 526.1 to 387.1 (−26%); in user studies, consistency preference jumps from 10.2% to 68.3%. Against Gen-L (Wang et al., 2023), which uses temporal co-denoising with fixed sliding windows, ZoLA improves CLIP-SIM from 0.8891 to 0.9443, SSIM from 0.4391 to 0.6091, and reduces FVD from 440.3 to 387.1; user-study consistency preference rises from 21.5% to 68.3%. The gap is most dramatic on the user-study consistency metric, where ZoLA is preferred roughly 3–7× more often than either baseline, indicating that the spatiotemporal attention expansion combined with noise init and noise travel addresses the long-range drift that both baselines suffer from. On computation, ZoLA uses 11.8 GB VRAM for 32-second generation versus 45.4 GB for Conv-Attn, while Gen-L uses less VRAM (5.1 GB) but takes 37% longer in wall-clock time (1284.4 s vs. 934.5 s).

## Relation to Prior Work

ZoLA builds directly on two threads: the local spatiotemporal attention mechanism from Align Your Latents (Blattmann et al., CVPR 2023), which it extends by splitting features into overlapping windows to approximate global temporal attention at longer lengths, and the set-based temporal co-denoising from Gen-L (Wang et al., 2023), which it generalizes by replacing fixed sliding-window selection with random starting indices and frame-skipping to form a more flexible Set Cover formulation. It deliberately departs from auto-regressive long-video approaches (NUWA-XL, MVD, etc.) that require retraining and suffer from error accumulation, instead proposing a purely zero-shot inference-time strategy that composes with any pretrained short-video diffusion or consistency model. The noise initialization and noise travel mechanisms are the paper's most original contributions, addressing denoising conflicts that arise when overlapping frame sets are processed independently.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: ZoLA enables 32-second animation generation on 12 GB GPUs (vs. 40+ GB for direct generation) and reduces inference to ~90 seconds with consistency models, making long video generation accessible to individual researchers and creators without retraining. Its zero-shot, model-agnostic design means it can be immediately applied as new short-video models emerge, and it composes naturally with community adapters (ControlNet, LoRA motion adapters, SDEdit) for creative control. Key limitations remain: generation quality is upper-bounded by the base short-video model, and the zero-shot nature makes very long animations prone to partial inconsistencies. Open questions include whether the approximation errors from local-attention substitution compound over extremely long sequences, and how the method interacts with future architectures that natively support longer temporal contexts.
