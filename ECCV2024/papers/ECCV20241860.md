## Problem

Multi-objective NAS based on weight-sharing supernets suffers from a 'small model trap' where the population rapidly converges to small architectures because they achieve lower validation error early in training, eliminating larger models before they can demonstrate their potential. Meanwhile, multi-tasking NAS methods that handle tasks from different datasets (e.g., EMT-NAS) optimize only accuracy, offering no control over model size or computational efficiency. No existing framework simultaneously addresses multi-objective optimization and cross-dataset architectural knowledge transfer.

## Input / Output

The method takes a 40-bit discrete encoding (NasNet-style search space with normal and reduction cells, each containing 5 blocks with 2 input bits and 2 operation bits selecting from depthwise-separable conv, dilated conv, max/avg pooling, and identity) and produces, per task, a set of four Pareto-optimal architectures grouped by model size (small to large), each characterized by its validation error and parameter count. Architectures are evaluated by sampling sub-networks from a task-specific weight-sharing supernet and training them for a fixed number of iterations on the respective dataset (CIFAR-10/100 at 32×32, ImageNet at 224×224, or MedMNIST variants at 28×28).

## Method

MO-EMT-NAS combines a multi-objective multi-factorial evolutionary algorithm (MO-MFEA) with per-task weight-sharing supernets to search for Pareto-optimal architectures across multiple tasks from different datasets. Each task maintains its own supernet and parameter set to avoid negative transfer, while cross-task offspring generation (controlled by a random mating probability) enables implicit architectural knowledge transfer via block-based crossover and bit-based mutation. To counteract the small model trap, a third auxiliary objective f_a = (1−params)·e^{−(1−params)(1−error)} is introduced, which generates size-dependent exponential selection pressure that preferentially retains larger models with comparable accuracy, preserving small, medium, and large architectures in the population. Environmental selection uses NSGA-II-style non-dominated sorting and crowding distance applied independently per task subpopulation. Computational efficiency is further improved by parallelizing the training and validation of each task's supernet via multiprocessing, reducing total runtime by 59.7%–77.7% compared to sequential single-task multi-objective baselines.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100, MO-EMT-NAS achieves hypervolume (HV) of 0.666±0.082 and 0.579±0.225 respectively, outperforming the MO-ST-NAS baseline (0.556±0.109 and 0.458±0.008). Across all MedMNIST task combinations (2–4 tasks), MO-EMT-NAS consistently achieves higher HV values than the single-tasking baseline, e.g., 0.77±0.13 on PathMNIST for the four-task setting vs. 0.47±0.04 for the single-task baseline. The best transferred architecture on ImageNet (Ours-C-100-D) reaches 75.47% Top-1 accuracy with 3.25M parameters. Runtime is reduced by 59.7%–77.7% (GPU days) compared to MO-ST-NAS, with savings increasing as more tasks are solved jointly.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is MO-ST-NAS, a multi-objective single-tasking variant of the same framework (i.e., MO-EMT-NAS with multi-tasking removed). MO-EMT-NAS achieves higher HV on every MedMNIST task combination, with the largest gains on PathMNIST (e.g., 0.77 vs. 0.47 HV in the four-task setting) and consistent 5–15% HV improvements on the OrganMNIST variants. On CIFAR-10/100, MO-EMT-NAS also dominates the baseline in HV (0.666 vs. 0.556; 0.579 vs. 0.458). Against the single-objective EMT-NAS [17], MO-EMT-NAS finds Pareto-optimal architecture sets that dominate the single models produced by EMT-NAS, while also providing flexible trade-offs between accuracy and model size. In terms of efficiency, MO-EMT-NAS saves 59.7%–77.7% GPU days over MO-ST-NAS, with the savings growing as the number of jointly solved tasks increases (capped at ~50% for two tasks due to parallelization, but reaching ~77% for three tasks). The ablation confirms the auxiliary objective f_a is critical: removing it drops HV from 0.571 to 0.443 on CIFAR-10 and from 0.532 to 0.356 on CIFAR-100, indicating the small-model trap severely degrades diversity without it.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends EMT-NAS, a single-objective multi-tasking NAS framework for tasks from different datasets, by incorporating multi-objective optimization (accuracy and model size) via NSGA-II-style non-dominated sorting, drawing on the MO-MFEA framework for cross-task knowledge transfer. It also builds on the small-model-trap analysis from CARS, which showed that two-objective MO-NAS with weight-sharing supernets loses medium-size models; here, the authors propose a novel auxiliary objective (an exponential function of normalized size and error) to retain the full spectrum of model sizes. The combination of multi-objective NAS with multi-tasking NAS across heterogeneous datasets is the key conceptual departure from both single-task MO-NAS and single-objective MT-NAS lines of work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical deployment need—obtaining a Pareto front of architectures spanning different model sizes for diverse hardware—while simultaneously handling multiple tasks from different datasets, a setting that prior MT-NAS work largely ignored. The auxiliary objective is a lightweight, interpretable fix for the small-model trap that outperforms CARS's dual-selection strategy in retaining medium-size models. Limitations include a small 40-bit search space, relatively small medical datasets, and the ImageNet evaluation being a retraining transfer rather than a full search, leaving open whether the approach scales to larger, more complex search spaces and whether the multi-tasking benefit persists for highly dissimilar tasks.
