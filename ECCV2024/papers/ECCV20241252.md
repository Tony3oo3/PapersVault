## Problem

Traditional score-based IQA methods reduce image quality to a single number, unable to capture the multi-factor reasoning humans use (identifying specific distortions, weighing their relative impacts, and making comparative judgments). This leads to failures in complex scenarios such as misaligned images or images with multiple competing distortions, where a scalar score cannot encode the necessary justification. The paper asks whether language-based, descriptive evaluation via MLLMs can better align with human perceptual reasoning than score regression.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241252/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives 2–3 RGB images (one reference plus one or two distorted versions) and a free-form text question, with each image wrapped in a unique tag (<Img-Reference>, <Img-A>, <Img-B>) to prevent cross-image confusion. Output is a natural-language paragraph that identifies texture types, enumerates distortion levels (brightness, color, noise, artifacts, blurriness), and states a comparative judgment with reasoning; for the comparison task, a short-answer prompt can elicit a single-phrase verdict ("Image A" or "Image B") suitable for accuracy computation.

## Method

DepictQA couples a frozen CLIP ViT-L/14 image encoder with a trainable linear projector and an LLM (Vicuna or LLaMA-2, 7B) fine-tuned via LoRA on only 0.25% of parameters. The key architectural choice is a hierarchical three-task framework—quality description, quality comparison, and comparison reasoning—that mirrors how humans first perceive distortions, then compare, then justify. To overcome limited high-quality annotations, training uses three data sources: 115k brief templated comparison pairs, ~5k detailed human-verified description/reasoning samples (duplicated 20×), and 48k IQA-unrelated content-description pairs as regularization. A unique per-image tag scheme (rather than a single shared <Img> tag) reduces multi-image confusion from 24% to 12%, and the full pipeline is trained for one epoch (~12 h on 8× A6000 GPUs) with next-token prediction loss on response tokens only.

## Results

On the BAPPS benchmark, DepictQA achieves 80.3%/84.2% comparison accuracy (Traditional/CNN distortion categories), approaching the human oracle of 80.8%/84.4% and surpassing the best score-based method (DISTS, 77.2%/82.2%) by over 3%. Across six additional IQA datasets (KADID, CSIQ, TID2013, LIVE, LIVE-MD, MDID2013), DepictQA consistently outperforms both FSIM and LPIPS in SRCC/PLCC, reaching 0.939/0.944 on KADID and 0.940/0.954 on CSIQ. On the description and reasoning tasks, DepictQA achieves a 64.0% reasonable rate and 76.2 GPT-4 score for description, and 53.0% reasonable rate and 76.4 GPT-4 score for reasoning, substantially outperforming general MLLMs (e.g., LLaVA-1.5: 18.0/65.8 description, 7.0/63.9 reasoning).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DepictQA outperforms all score-based IQA baselines on BAPPS comparison accuracy: it beats the best traditional method FSIM (62.7/79.4) by ~11% and the best deep-learning methods LPIPS (76.0/82.8) and DISTS (77.2/82.2) by 3–4%, while SSIM (60.3/79.1), VIF (55.6/74.4), DeepIQA (70.3/79.4), and PieAPP (72.7/77.0) trail by 8–25%. Against general MLLMs, DepictQA's 82.0% comparison accuracy dwarfs LLaVA-1.5 (43.0%), MiniGPT4 (38.0%), and LAMM (53.0%), while LLaVA and MiniGPT4 (LLaMA-2) simply fail to produce valid comparisons. On the reasoning task, DepictQA (53.0% reasonable rate, 76.4 GPT-4 score) substantially exceeds LLaVA-1.5 (7.0/63.9), MiniGPT4 (1.0/42.4), and LAMM (4.0/58.1). Against the proprietary GPT-4V, DepictQA achieves 90.0% vs. 65.0% comparison accuracy (a 25-point gap), while GPT-4V edges ahead on GPT-4 score for reasoning (106.4 vs. 77.6) due to linguistic fluency, though the human-evaluated reasonable rates are comparable (53.0% vs. 52.0%). On multi-dataset SRCC/PLCC evaluation, DepictQA surpasses both FSIM and LPIPS on all six benchmarks, with the largest margins on KADID (+0.08 SRCC over FSIM) and MDID2013 (+0.10 SRCC over FSIM).

## Relation to Prior Work

DepictQA builds on the LLaVA-style architecture (frozen CLIP ViT-L/14 encoder + trainable projector + LLM with LoRA fine-tuning) and the LAMM training framework, but departs fundamentally from both the score-based IQA literature (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, DISTS) and prior MLLM-based IQA efforts (Q-Bench, Q-Instruct, Q-Align) that still target score regression or single-image low-level perception. The key conceptual departure is replacing numerical quality scores with hierarchical language outputs—distortion identification, pairwise comparison, and multi-factor reasoning—mimicking how humans actually judge image quality. The multi-source training strategy (templated comparison data + high-quality reasoning data + IQA-unrelated content descriptions as regularization) and the unique per-image tags for multi-image disambiguation are methodological contributions not present in prior MLLM work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This paper demonstrates that a 7B MLLM fine-tuned on a modest dataset can match or exceed established score-based IQA methods on comparison tasks while providing interpretable, human-readable reasoning—a capability score-based methods fundamentally lack. However, the authors acknowledge significant limitations: only five predefined distortion types, modest gains over LPIPS/DISTS (~2-3%), time-consuming pairwise voting for score conversion, and unsatisfying description/reasoning quality. The open question of whether language-based IQA can fully replace score-based methods in practice remains unresolved, and the approach's generalization beyond the BAPPS distortion types is untested.
