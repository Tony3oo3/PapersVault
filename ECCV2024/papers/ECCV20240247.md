## Problem

Existing deep video desnowing methods trained exclusively on synthetic benchmarks suffer significant performance degradation on real-world snowy videos due to the distribution shift between synthetic and real snow. Collecting large-scale paired real-world training data is impractical because variable weather, object motion, and camera position make frame alignment extremely difficult. This paper is the first to explore semi-supervised learning for video desnowing, leveraging unlabeled real data to close the synthetic-to-real gap.

## Input / Output

The network consumes a 3-frame snowy video clip at 256×256 spatial resolution and outputs the corresponding desnowed RGB frames. During training, it jointly processes labeled synthetic pairs from RVSD and unlabeled real-world clips from the newly introduced Realsnow85 dataset (85 unpaired snowy videos spanning cities, parks, villages, and nature scenes with varied snowfall levels and lighting). At inference, only the student network is used to predict clean frames from the input sequence.

## Method

SemiVDN builds on a Mean-Teacher framework where a ConvNeXt encoder feeds into a Prior-guided Temporal Decoupling Experts (TDE) module—a physics-driven Mixture-of-Experts that replaces the standard FFN in a transformer block. TDE uses a Temporal Decomposition Router to compute continuous, differentiable temporal weights that adaptively combine tokens across frames, then routes them through three dedicated experts (snow, transmission, atmospheric light) to explicitly decompose the atmospheric scattering model components in a frame-correlated manner. A Prior-Guided Recovery Module then inverts Eq. 1 in feature space to reconstruct the clean background. To bridge the synthetic-real distribution gap, a Distribution-driven Contrastive Regularization (DCR) loss fits a Gaussian Mixture Model to real snow layers, selects the synthetic snow layer with minimum KL divergence as an ultra-positive sample, and constructs contrastive pairs that highlight snow-invariant background details while suppressing snow-specific artifacts. The overall training combines supervised pixel/perceptual/frequency losses on labeled data with unsupervised consistency, perceptual contrastive, dark-channel-prior, and total-variation losses on unlabeled data, with a time-dependent Gaussian warmup schedule for the unsupervised weight.

## Results

On the RVSD synthetic benchmark, SemiVDN achieves the best PSNR of 25.68 dB, SSIM of 0.9254, and LPIPS of 0.0785, outperforming the strongest video-level baseline SVDNet (25.06 dB, 0.9210, 0.0842) by 0.62 dB in PSNR. On the real-world Realsnow85 test set, SemiVDN also leads all 15 baselines on both no-reference quality metrics: NIMA 4.259 and MUSIQ 51.57, versus SVDNet's 4.220 and 50.78. The method achieves the best score across all five reported metrics (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, NIMA, MUSIQ) simultaneously, demonstrating consistent superiority on both synthetic and real data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SemiVDN outperforms all 15 baselines on every reported metric. The closest competitor is SVDNet [8] (ICCV 2023), the first dedicated video desnowing method; SemiVDN improves PSNR by 0.62 dB (25.68 vs. 25.06), SSIM by 0.0044, and reduces LPIPS by 0.0057 on RVSD, while also leading on real-world NIMA (+0.039) and MUSIQ (+0.79). Among image-level desnowing methods, Snowformer [10] and MPRN [79] are the strongest, yet both trail SemiVDN by over 1.3 dB in PSNR and ~0.03 in SSIM. Video super-resolution baselines (BasicVSR++ [6], IconVSR [5]) and general restoration models (Restormer [78], IR-SDE [38]) lag further behind, confirming that task-specific video desnowing with temporal modeling is critical. On the real-world Realsnow85 set, the gap widens for methods trained solely on synthetic data: ViWS-Net [70] (the best video adverse-weather baseline) scores 4.238 NIMA and 50.56 MUSIQ, still below SemiVDN, while diffusion-based WeatherDiffusion [41] drops to 4.106 NIMA, illustrating the domain-shift problem that the semi-supervised design addresses.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends SVDNet (Chen et al., ICCV 2023), which first formulated video desnowing via the atmospheric scattering model and introduced a temporal aggregation module, by replacing its convolutional-based component decomposition with a continuous Mixture-of-Experts (Temporal Decoupling Experts) that explicitly routes tokens to physics-specific experts (snow, transmission, atmospheric light) in a differentiable, frame-correlated manner. The semi-supervised training paradigm follows the Mean-Teacher framework (Tarvainen & Valpola, NeurIPS 2017) previously applied to image dehazing and deraining, but is here the first application to video snow removal. The Distribution-driven Contrastive Regularization departs from prior contrastive losses in restoration (which typically use VGG-projected pixel features) by operating in the decomposed feature space and using a GMM-based KL-divergence selection to identify synthetic snow layers that best approximate real snow distributions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical bottleneck: paired real-world snowy video data is infeasible to collect at scale, yet synthetic-to-real domain shift severely degrades desnowing performance. By introducing the first real-world video desnowing dataset (Realsnow85) and demonstrating that unlabeled real data meaningfully closes this gap, the work provides a template for semi-supervised video restoration under other adverse weather conditions. Limitations include the small dataset size (85 videos, 3-frame clips), reliance on a heuristic GMM-based ultra-positive sample selection, and evaluation limited to a single synthetic benchmark (RVSD). Open questions include whether the temporal decoupling experts scale to longer sequences, whether the approach transfers to rain or fog video removal, and whether larger unlabeled real datasets yield diminishing or accelerating returns.
