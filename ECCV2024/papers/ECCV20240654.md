## Problem

Behavioral cloning (BC) models for autonomous driving sim agents suffer from covariate shift during closed-loop deployment, where autoregressive rollouts drift from the training distribution, and cannot explicitly encode safety constraints like collision avoidance. This paper investigates whether the 'pre-training and RL fine-tuning' paradigm, widely successful for large language models, can be transferred to multi-agent driving behavior modeling to improve sim agent reliability.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a scene-centric representation comprising M map features, traffic light states (position and signal state), and T_prev history steps of N traffic participants (position, velocity, heading, object type), all encoded into a shared scene embedding by the transformer encoder. At each of T_pred autoregressive decoding steps, the model simultaneously outputs N discrete acceleration actions (one per agent) from a 13×13 uniformly spaced grid clipped to ±6 m/s², rather than a single token as in language models. These per-step Verlet-wrapped delta actions are integrated to reconstruct 2D positions for reward computation.

## Method

The method uses a two-stage training procedure on a modified MotionLM autoregressive encoder-decoder transformer: first, behavioral cloning pre-training maximizes the log-likelihood of ground-truth acceleration actions; second, on-policy RL fine-tuning with REINFORCE optimizes a simple reward combining L2 trajectory deviation from ground truth and a boolean collision penalty. The key architectural insight is that the multi-agent behavior model itself functions as the simulation environment for closed-loop rollouts, eliminating the need for an external dynamics model or back-propagation through time. During fine-tuning, the model autoregressively samples N actions per step over T_pred steps, computes per-agent normalized returns across the batch, and updates via a policy gradient on the surrogate objective. The paper also introduces a novel policy evaluation benchmark that measures a sim agent's ability to correctly rank a set of 64 random-shooting MPC planners, using Spearman rank correlation and absolute error against log-playback ground truth.

## Results

On the Waymo Open Sim Agents Challenge (WOSAC), the fine-tuned 10M model achieves a composite score of 0.608, up from 0.549 for the pre-trained 10M model, with the largest gains in collision (0.877 vs. 0.673) and offroad (0.825 vs. 0.655) metrics. The fine-tuned 10M model also substantially reduces ADE from 4.508 to 2.428 and MinADE from 2.274 to 1.706, indicating tighter alignment with ground-truth trajectories. On the proposed policy evaluation benchmark, the fine-tuned 10M model achieves a Spearman rank correlation of 0.866 and absolute error of 9.509 relative to log-playback, both better than the pre-trained 10M (0.845 and 10.014).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Wayformer [23], the fine-tuned 10M model wins on every reported metric: composite (0.608 vs. 0.575), collision (0.877 vs. 0.870), offroad (0.825 vs. 0.866 — Wayformer slightly ahead), ADE (2.428 vs. 2.498), and MinADE (1.706 vs. 2.498), with the most dramatic gap in MinADE. Against the state-of-the-art MVTE [34], the fine-tuned 10M model trails on composite (0.608 vs. 0.645) and collision (0.877 vs. 0.893), but substantially outperforms on ADE (2.428 vs. 3.859) and is marginally behind on MinADE (1.706 vs. 1.674). The authors attribute the composite gap to their re-implemented MotionLM encoder rather than the fine-tuning procedure itself, and note that fine-tuning MVTE with the same methodology is left as future work. On the policy evaluation benchmark, both fine-tuned models (1M and 10M) achieve higher rank correlation and lower absolute error relative to log-playback than their pre-trained counterparts, confirming that RL fine-tuning improves the sim agent's ability to discriminate planner quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the 'pre-training and RL fine-tuning' paradigm popularized for large language models (RLHF) to the autonomous driving sim-agent problem, building directly on the MotionLM autoregressive multi-agent motion prediction architecture. It departs from prior closed-loop training approaches (e.g., PredictionNet, SimNet) by eliminating the need for an external simulator or dynamics model—the multi-agent behavior model itself serves as the environment—and by replacing back-propagation through time with a minimalistic REINFORCE policy gradient that supports non-differentiable rewards. The policy evaluation benchmark draws from the RL off-policy evaluation literature but is the first to apply it specifically to assessing sim-agent quality for AD planner testing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The work demonstrates that a simple reward (trajectory fidelity + collision penalty) with on-policy RL can meaningfully improve safety-critical metrics (collision, offroad) in closed-loop simulation without sacrificing trajectory accuracy, addressing the covariate shift problem that plagues pure behavioral cloning. The proposed policy evaluation benchmark offers a more task-relevant way to assess sim agents than imitation metrics like ADE, directly measuring whether a simulator can correctly rank AD planners. Limitations include a simplified kinematic transition model, a single reward formulation, and the fact that the fine-tuned model still trails the SOTA MVTE on composite WOSAC score, leaving open whether the approach transfers to stronger base architectures or more complex reward structures (e.g., adversarial stress-testing).
