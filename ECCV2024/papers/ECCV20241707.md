## Problem

Vision-language models like CLIP achieve strong zero-shot classification but are black-box and fail on specialized, fine-grained concepts rarely discussed online. Existing interpretable concept-bottleneck approaches rely on hand-designed or externally retrieved attributes that require class names or expert knowledge, making them inapplicable to undocumented or novel visual categories. The paper asks whether discriminative, interpretable attributes can be discovered purely from visual groupings without any prior knowledge of what the classes are.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241707/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes RGB images partitioned into unlabeled groups (no class names provided) and produces, for each group, a set of discrete natural-language attributes that serve as a concept bottleneck. At inference, a new image is scored against each class's attribute set via CLIP ViT-B/32 similarity, and the highest-scoring class is predicted. The attribute sets are the primary learned artifact—human-readable descriptors that explain why an image belongs to a class—and the method explicitly avoids using class names or any textual prior during optimization.

## Method

The core mechanism replaces the mutation operator in evolutionary search with Llama-2-70B-Instruct, which uses in-context learning over a sorted history of past attribute sets and their classification losses to propose improved mutations. Each class's score is the mean CLIP similarity between the image and its attribute set (Eq. 1), and the evolutionary loop maintains a bank of attribute-set hypotheses, samples them biased by expected loss, and prompts the LLM per class to generate novel attributes that reduce cross-entropy. A binary pre-training phase (one class vs. all others, including unrelated classes) discovers common visual attributes before joint multi-class training, preventing the optimizer from converging on spurious noise. The key insight is that the LLM's in-context learning extracts patterns between attribute quality and performance, making the discrete search far more efficient than random word injection or crossover heuristics, while the vision-language model serves as a differentiable-free fitness function that scales to open-ended visual concepts.

## Results

On five fine-grained iNaturalist family datasets (Lichen, Wrasse, Wild Rye, Manzanita, Bulrush), the full method (10-prompt) achieves an average accuracy of 45.3%, outperforming the strongest baseline (Classification by Description) by 18.4% on average. On two KikiBouba datasets of novel, internet-undocumented concepts, the method reaches 78.9% and 59.2% accuracy, exceeding the best baseline by 22.2% on average. The 10-prompt variant consistently outperforms the 1-prompt variant, confirming that in-context learning over multiple past hypotheses is critical for effective attribute mutation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two primary baselines that have access to privileged information (class names) which the proposed method does not. Against CLIP zero-shot classification by class name [1], the 10-prompt method wins on all five iNaturalist families (e.g., 46.67% vs. 21.67% on Lichen, 52.0% vs. 32.0% on Wild Rye) and on KB1 (78.9% vs. 40.7%). Against Classification by Description [5], which prompts GPT-3 with the class name to generate attributes, the method wins on all iNaturalist datasets (e.g., 46.67% vs. 23.33% on Lichen, 50.0% vs. 26.0% on Manzanita) and on both KikiBouba datasets (78.9% vs. 28.9% on KB1, 59.2% vs. 41.7% on KB2). Notably, the gradient-based approach—which also lacks class names—achieves only 23.3% on Lichen and 16.7% on KB1, confirming that the LLM-driven evolutionary mutation is essential for effective discrete attribute search. The paper emphasizes that all baselines with class names would fail entirely on truly novel concepts, whereas the proposed method requires no prior knowledge of what the images depict.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of concept bottleneck models (Koh et al., Ferrari & Zisserman), vision-language models (CLIP), and LLMs-as-optimizers (Romera-Paredes et al. for code generation, Yu et al. for prompt discovery). It departs from prior concept bottleneck work by eliminating the need for hand-annotated or class-name-derived attributes, instead discovering them purely from visual data via evolutionary search. The key methodological departure from concurrent LLM-optimizer work (Jin et al. 2024, Han et al. 2023) is the absence of class names as input to the LLM, and the use of a VLM (CLIP) as the fitness function rather than a non-learning metric, enabling optimization over open-ended visual concepts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a path toward interpretable classifiers for specialized scientific domains where class names are unknown or concepts are too rare for web-scale pretraining, with the explicit bottleneck enabling direct auditing of dataset bias. However, the evaluation is limited to small-scale fine-grained datasets (5–6 classes per family) and two synthetic KikiBouba sets, leaving open whether the evolutionary search scales to hundreds or thousands of classes or to more complex visual concepts. The reliance on Llama-2-70B-Instruct for mutation and CLIP ViT-B/32 for scoring also inherits known limitations in LLM bias and VLM coverage, and the computational cost of iterative LLM calls per mutation step is a practical concern for broader adoption.
