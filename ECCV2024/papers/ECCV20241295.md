## Problem

SNCF observed that unsupervised contrastive features linearly separate in-distribution (ID) from out-of-distribution (OOD) samples, but this was demonstrated only on synthetically corrupted datasets. On real web-noisy data the separation is weaker, and even when an accurate OOD detector is obtained via direct linear-separator estimation, it fails to improve classification accuracy because it misses a small set of visually simple clean examples that are critical for supervised learning. The paper investigates this detection-accuracy gap and proposes a hybrid strategy that combines the linear separation with SOTA small-loss detection.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241295/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a web-noisy RGB image dataset with (potentially incorrect) class labels at resolutions ranging from 32×32 (CNWL) to 448×448 (Webly-fg), along with unsupervised SimCLR contrastive features extracted from intermediate ResNet blocks (specifically the second block for the linear separator). It produces a per-sample noise-detection score from the logistic-regression linear separator W, which is alternated with the PLS small-loss score Z during training, and ultimately a class prediction. The linear separator is trained on an approximate trusted subset constructed unsupervised from SOTA noise metrics rather than human annotations.

## Method

The core mechanism is Linear Separation Alternating (LSA): an unsupervised SimCLR pre-trained network provides contrastive features at an early ResNet block, on which a logistic regression is trained using an approximate ID/OOD subset derived from PLS or RRL noise scores, yielding a linear-separation detection W that is highly decorrelated (<0.5 Pearson) from the original small-loss or distance-based metrics. During supervised training with PLS, the noise-detection signal is alternated every epoch between W (even epochs) and the PLS small-loss score Z (odd epochs), so the classifier benefits from W's high specificity/sensitivity on OOD samples while retaining Z's ability to recover simple clean images with uniform backgrounds that W misses. PLS-LSA+ extends this with a two-network co-training and voting scheme where networks cross-validate each other's pseudo-labels and noise scores, reducing confirmation bias. The approach requires no human-labeled trusted subset and no clustering step, replacing SNCF's OPTICS-based retrieval with a direct linear-separator estimate.

## Results

On the CNWL dataset (32×32, PreActivation ResNet18), PLS-LSA achieves 64.43% top-1 accuracy at 20% web noise and 49.53% at 80% noise, consistently outperforming PLS (63.25% / 48.03%) and SNCF (61.56% / 45.62%). The co-training variant PLS-LSA+ further improves to 66.52% at 20% and 52.03% at 80% noise, setting new state-of-the-art across all noise levels. On mini-WebVision (InceptionResNetV2, 224×224), PLS-LSA+ reaches 82.08% top-1 and 94.16% top-5 on the mini-WebVision validation set, and 79.19% top-1 on ILSVRC12, surpassing all prior methods including FLY (80.96%) and SNCF+ (80.24%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PLS-LSA (single network) outperforms PLS by 1.2–1.5 points across all CNWL noise levels and surpasses SNCF by 2.9–3.9 points, demonstrating that the alternating linear-separation strategy adds value even without co-training. Against co-training baselines on CNWL, PLS-LSA+ beats MDM (64.40→66.52 at 20%), LRM (56.03→66.52), and ScanMix (59.06→66.52) by 2–10 points, establishing a clear advantage. On mini-WebVision, PLS-LSA+ achieves 82.08% top-1, exceeding FLY (80.96%), SNCF+ (80.24%), and PLS (79.01%) by 1.1–3.1 points; on ILSVRC12 it reaches 79.19% top-1 vs. RM (77.39%) and PLS (76.15%). On the fine-grained Webly-fg datasets, gains are more modest: PLS-LSA improves over PLS by only 0.1–0.3 points (e.g., 87.70 vs. 87.58 on Web-Aircraft), while PLS-LSA+ adds another 0.7–0.8 points (88.51 on Web-Aircraft with SimCLR init), still outperforming DivideMix (82.48) and Co-teaching (79.54) by 6–9 points. The paper notes that SimCLR pre-training is less effective on Webly-fg due to the fine-grained nature of the task, limiting the linear separation benefit.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends SNCF (Albert et al., ECCV 2022), which observed linear separability between in-distribution and out-of-distribution samples in unsupervised contrastive feature spaces but relied on OPTICS clustering and was validated only on synthetically corrupted data. Here, the authors move to real web-noisy datasets, replace clustering with a direct logistic-regression separator estimated from SOTA noise-detection scores, and discover that the separation is actually stronger in earlier network layers. The method is then integrated into PLS (Albert et al., WACV 2023), a semi-supervised two-stage noise-robust classifier, by alternating between the linear-separation detector and PLS's original small-loss detector each epoch (LSA), with an optional co-training variant (PLS-LSA+).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central empirical finding—that a substantially more accurate OOD detector (AUROC 85 vs. 66) actually *reduces* classification accuracy because it misses visually simple clean examples critical for learning—challenges the field's implicit assumption that better noise retrieval always helps. The demonstrated low correlation (<0.5 Pearson) between linear-separation and loss/distance-based detectors provides a principled justification for combining them, and the alternating strategy is a simple, drop-in improvement over PLS. Open questions remain: the linear separation is only observed empirically in intermediate layers with no theoretical account, it requires SimCLR pre-training (adding compute), and the authors note that fine-grained datasets (Webly-fg) yield only marginal gains, suggesting the phenomenon may be sensitive to class distinctiveness. Future work on enforcing separation from random initialization or developing adaptive alternating schedules could reduce the pre-training dependency.
