## Problem

Using 2D latent diffusion models to inpaint NeRF training images introduces two critical failures: the high stochasticity of diffusion outputs prevents the radiance field from converging to crisp geometry in uncovered regions, and autoencoding errors in the LDM produce a textural shift between inpainted and observed pixels that creates visible seams. Commonly used pixel-distance and perceptual losses further exacerbate both issues, yielding blurry, mist-like inpainting results.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240063/cropped_page0_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of posed RGB images (resized to 1008-pixel long edge) paired with binary inpainting masks that delineate regions to be filled (e.g., objects to remove), and produces a completed NeRF that renders photorealistic novel views with high-frequency detail in the inpainted regions. It supports both forward-facing and 360° scenes as well as single- and multi-object removal, and is evaluated against ground-truth test views where the object was physically absent during capture.

## Method

MALD-NeRF replaces pixel-level and perceptual losses in the inpainting region with a masked patch-based adversarial objective: a StyleGAN2 discriminator is trained on image patches where the reconstruction/inpainting boundary is hidden (pixels outside the mask are zeroed), preventing the discriminator from exploiting the LDM's textural shift, while a feature-matching loss on discriminator intermediate features provides additional geometric supervision. To tame the diffusion model's generation diversity, the LDM is per-scene finetuned via LoRA on both the text encoder and U-Net using a self-supervised inpainting loss with a scene-specific token, and an iterative dataset update protocol with HiFA-scheduled partial DDIM gradually propagates 3D-consistent information from NeRF renderings back into the inpainting process. Monocular depth (ZoeDepth) with a shift-scale alignment provides additional geometric regularization after 2,000 iterations. The paper further demonstrates through ablation that adding pixel-reconstruction or LPIPS losses to the adversarial scheme degrades quality, confirming that these objectives are fundamentally incompatible with diffusion-based NeRF inpainting.

## Results

On the SPIn-NeRF benchmark, MALD-NeRF achieves the best scores across all five metrics: LPIPS 0.4345, M-LPIPS 0.3344, FID 183.25, KID 0.0397, and FVD 89.01. The FID improvement is particularly notable, reducing from 219.80 (SPIn-NeRF) and 271.66 (Inpaint3D) to 183.25, indicating substantially better visual quality in the inpainted regions. On the LLFF dataset (where no physically-removed ground-truth views exist), MALD-NeRF also leads with C-FID 171.89 and C-KID 0.0388, narrowly beating InpaintNeRF360 (174.55 / 0.0397).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MALD-NeRF outperforms all three baselines on every reported metric. Against SPIn-NeRF (the original perceptual-inpainting NeRF method), MALD-NeRF reduces FID by 16.6% (219.80→183.25) and KID by 35.5% (0.0616→0.0397), while also improving LPIPS from 0.5356 to 0.4345. Against Inpaint3D, which trains a diffusion model on RealEstate10k, MALD-NeRF achieves a dramatically lower FID (183.25 vs. 271.66) and KID (0.0397 vs. 0.0964), though Inpaint3D reports a lower FVD (69.97 vs. 89.01)—a metric the authors note can favor blurry outputs with better temporal consistency. Against InpaintNeRF360, the closest competitor, MALD-NeRF leads on all SPIn-NeRF metrics (FID 183.25 vs. 222.12, KID 0.0397 vs. 0.0544) and on both LLFF metrics (C-FID 171.89 vs. 174.55, C-KID 0.0388 vs. 0.0397), with the LLFF gap being relatively small. The paper also includes a SPIn-NeRF(LDM) variant that swaps in the authors' latent diffusion model while keeping SPIn-NeRF's original losses, which performs worse than vanilla SPIn-NeRF on LPIPS and FID, confirming that the diffusion prior alone is insufficient without the masked adversarial training scheme.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the NeRF inpainting line of work (SPIn-NeRF, InpaintNeRF360, Inpaint3D) by replacing their reliance on pixel and perceptual losses with a masked adversarial training scheme that uses a StyleGAN2 discriminator and feature matching loss to supervise inpainting regions. It departs from prior approaches by arguing that pixel-level and LPIPS losses are actively harmful for 3D-consistent inpainting, and introduces per-scene LoRA finetuning of the latent diffusion model (building on DreamBooth and Instruct-NeRF2NeRF's iterative dataset update) to reduce the stochasticity that causes cross-view inconsistency. The masked adversarial design, inspired by AmbientGAN, specifically addresses the texture shift artifact introduced by the autoencoder in latent diffusion models—a problem prior methods did not explicitly target.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a useful empirical finding that pixel and perceptual losses, standard in NeRF training, degrade inpainting quality when combined with generative priors, redirecting the field toward adversarial supervision for 3D inpainting. However, the framework's reliance on adversarial training introduces stochasticity, and the authors acknowledge that inpainted textures remain blurrier than real-world textures due to the stochastic denoising process. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to few-shot settings or very large inpainting masks, and whether the per-scene LoRA finetuning cost is practical for large-scale deployment. The finding that LPIPS is an unreliable metric for generative NeRF inpainting (Figure 4) also has broader implications for how the community evaluates such tasks.
