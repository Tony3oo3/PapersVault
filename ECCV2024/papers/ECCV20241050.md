## Problem

Classical single-image calibration methods based on vanishing points and lines are highly accurate but catastrophically fail in environments without straight lines, while end-to-end deep networks are robust but less accurate because they must relearn projective geometry from scratch within finite model capacity. GeoCalib addresses this gap by hypothesizing that learned approaches lack the explicit 3D geometric constraints that make classical methods precise.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241050/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image of arbitrary resolution and produces gravity direction (roll and pitch angles), focal length (or vertical FoV), and distortion parameters, along with per-parameter uncertainty estimates from the LM covariance matrix. Internally, the DNN outputs a pixel-wise Perspective Field — a unit up-vector and latitude angle at every pixel — together with per-pixel confidence scores in [0,1] that weight the subsequent optimization. The perspective field is over-parameterized (one constraint per pixel) but only a sparse subset of high-confidence pixels is needed to fully constrain the camera parameters.

## Method

GeoCalib uses a SegNeXt encoder-decoder to predict a pixel-wise Perspective Field (up-vector and latitude) with learned confidences, then fits camera parameters to this field via a differentiable Levenberg-Marquardt optimization that treats gravity on the S² manifold and focal length in log-space. The key architectural insight is a division of labor: the network learns which visual cues are informative (via the perspective field and its confidences) while the LM solver enforces the hard constraints of projective geometry, so the network never needs to learn the estimation process itself. Training is end-to-end with a composite loss supervising both the optimized camera parameters and the intermediate perspective field, while confidences are shaped only by the optimization gradient (detached from the field loss) so they learn to down-weight uninformative regions. This design enables zero-shot transfer to arbitrary distortion models, partial calibration with known priors, multi-image joint optimization, and principled uncertainty estimation — none of which are available in black-box learned baselines.

## Results

GeoCalib achieves the lowest median roll error across all four datasets (0.28°–0.43°), outperforming the closest classical baseline UVP (0.38°–0.89°) and all deep methods (0.88°–3.50°). For pitch estimation, GeoCalib reaches 0.87°–1.94° median error, substantially better than UVP (1.34°–4.59°) and deep baselines (1.94°–5.73°). On focal length (FoV), GeoCalib achieves 3.03°–4.90° median error, roughly halving the error of UVP (5.57°–10.92°) and all deep methods (5.64°–18.65°). In the lens distortion experiment (Table 2), GeoCalib-pinhole (trained only on undistorted images) already achieves 4.55° FoV error and 0.07 px median distortion error, beating all baselines including those trained on distorted data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GeoCalib outperforms all five deep baselines (DeepCalib, Perceptual, CTRL-C, MSCC, ParamNet) on every metric across all four datasets, with the largest gaps in FoV estimation (e.g., 4.46° vs. 18.65° on MegaDepth for CTRL-C) and in roll AUC@1° (82.6% vs. 54.5% on MegaDepth). Against the classical Manhattan-world methods, GeoCalib is dramatically more robust: SVA catastrophically fails on TartanAir (9.48° roll error, 43.01° FoV error) and LaMAR (AUC@1° below 10%), while GeoCalib maintains sub-1° roll error everywhere. The closest competitor is UVP, which assumes three orthogonal vanishing points; GeoCalib beats UVP on median error for all three parameters on all four datasets (e.g., 0.36° vs. 0.51° roll on MegaDepth, 1.94° vs. 4.59° pitch), though UVP retains a slight edge on FoV AUC@1° for TartanAir (15.8 vs. 14.1) and Stanford2D3D (22.2 vs. 17.4), reflecting its stronger scene assumption on easy man-made samples. In the distortion experiment, GeoCalib-pinhole (zero-shot, no distorted training data) already beats all baselines including those trained on distorted images, and GeoCalib trained on distorted data further improves to 4.38° FoV and 0.06 px distortion error. For visual localization on InLoc, GeoCalib's gravity prior improves recall at 0.25 m/10° from 43.4% to 44.9% (DUC1) and 51.9% to 55.0% (DUC2), outperforming UVP's gravity prior (43.9% and 52.7%).

## Relation to Prior Work

GeoCalib builds directly on the Perspective Field representation from Jin et al. (CVPR 2023), which introduced a pixel-wise up-vector and latitude encoding of camera parameters. It departs from prior deep calibration methods (DeepCalib, CTRL-C, Perceptual, MSCC, ParamNet) that either regress parameters directly or use indirect cues without explicit geometric constraints, and from classical vanishing-point methods (SVA, UVP) that assume Manhattan-world structure. The key conceptual shift is replacing the black-box regression head with a differentiable Levenberg-Marquardt optimization, so the network learns to predict informative cues and confidences that steer a geometrically-grounded solver rather than relearning projective geometry from scratch. This follows the broader trend of end-to-end differentiable optimization in geometric vision (pose estimation, tracking, matching) but applies it to single-image calibration for the first time.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GeoCalib is the first deep method to consistently match or surpass classical geometry-based calibration in accuracy while retaining the robustness of learned approaches, closing a long-standing gap between the two paradigms. Its practical flexibility—supporting arbitrary distortion models without retraining, leveraging partial priors (known intrinsics or IMU gravity), multi-image joint optimization, and per-parameter uncertainty estimation—makes it significantly easier to integrate into real-world pipelines than prior black-box networks. Limitations include weaker FoV estimation compared to gravity (the latitude cue is inherently harder to predict) and reliance on a relatively small training dataset (OpenPano, ~42k images). Open questions include whether the approach extends to more complex camera models (e.g., full fisheye with multiple distortion terms) and whether the learned confidences can be further improved with self-supervised or contrastive signals.
