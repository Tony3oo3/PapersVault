## Problem

Existing multi-frame super-resolution methods rely on deformable convolution (DCN) for frame alignment, which suffers from small receptive fields and a fixed number of kernels, causing misalignment and poor high-frequency representation. Additionally, most MFSR architectures are locked to a single super-resolution scale factor (typically ×4) due to pixel-shuffle upsampling, requiring retraining or post-hoc downsampling for other scales.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241129/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes N=14 low-resolution burst frames in the 4-channel RGGB (Bayer) domain at H×W spatial resolution and produces a single high-resolution 3-channel sRGB image at 2sH×2sW, where s is the scale factor (×2, ×3, or ×4). The 2× spatial expansion relative to the scale factor s arises from the RGGB-to-sRGB domain conversion. A single unimodel directly predicts all three integer scale factors without architectural changes, and can even handle floating scales (e.g., ×3.5) at a modest PSNR cost.

## Method

BurstM replaces DCN-based alignment with an optical flow estimator (FNet from FRVSR, adapted from sRGB to RGGB input) that provides one-to-one pixel correspondences with a broader effective receptive field. The key architectural innovation is a Learnable Neural Warping module that operates in Fourier space: an EDSR-based encoder extracts latent vectors, which are then decomposed into amplitude, frequency, and phase components via lightweight convolutions; warping is performed by evaluating a continuous Fourier signal at offset coordinates using nearest interpolation, with relative (local grid) coordinates to address spectral bias. A Blender (same architecture as the encoder) merges the N warped Fourier features into a single representation, and an MLP decoder produces the sRGB output, which is combined with an upscaled skip connection from the reference frame before a final pixel-shuffle upsampling. Training uses L1 (or aligned L1 with PWCNet for real BurstSR) for the SR output plus an L2 photometric loss on the optical flow, with the model fine-tuned from SyntheticBurst to BurstSR.

## Results

On the real-world BurstSR dataset (×4), BurstM achieves 49.12 dB PSNR and 0.987 SSIM, outperforming the previous best (GMTNet, 48.95 dB) by +0.17 dB. On SyntheticBurst, BurstM leads at ×2 (46.01 dB) and ×3 (44.79 dB) with large margins over all baselines, while at ×4 it reaches 42.87 dB, placing second only to the much larger BSRT-Large (43.62 dB, 20.71M params vs. BurstM's 14.0M). BurstM also achieves the shortest inference time (11.6 ms) among compared methods on an RTX 3090.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On BurstSR ×4, BurstM (49.12 dB) beats all six baselines, with the closest competitor being GMTNet (48.95 dB, +0.17 dB gap) and Burstormer (48.82 dB, +0.30 dB gap). On SyntheticBurst ×2 and ×3, BurstM dominates with margins of over 5 dB (×2: 46.01 vs. next-best 41.25) and nearly 2 dB (×3: 44.79 vs. next-best 42.87), largely because it predicts each scale directly rather than downsampling from ×4. On SyntheticBurst ×4, BurstM (42.87 dB) trails the 20.71M-parameter BSRT-Large (43.62 dB) by 0.75 dB but surpasses the smaller BSRT-Small (42.72 dB) and Burstormer (42.83 dB) with fewer parameters. In terms of efficiency, BurstM (14.0M params, 11.6 ms inference) is faster than BIPNet, Burstormer, and BSRT variants despite having more parameters than most, thanks to its single optical-flow warping step replacing multi-layer DCN alignment.

## Relation to Prior Work

BurstM departs from the dominant DCN-based alignment paradigm in burst super-resolution (BIPNet, Burstormer, BSRT, GMTNet) by replacing deformable convolutions entirely with a lightweight optical flow estimator (FNet from FRVSR) operating in Fourier space. The Fourier-space warping draws on the implicit neural representation (INR) literature, specifically Fourier feature mapping to counteract spectral bias, as explored in recent SR work by Lee & Jin (2022) and Kim et al. (2023). The encoder backbone is EDSR, and the multi-scale unimodel design extends the fixed-scale formulation of prior MFSR methods by leveraging INR's continuous-domain flexibility to support ×2, ×3, and ×4 factors simultaneously without architectural changes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The multi-scale unimodel capability is practically significant: existing burst SR methods require retraining or post-hoc bicubic downsampling for non-×4 scales, and the paper shows this introduces measurable high-frequency loss. However, the large ×2 PSNR gap on SyntheticBurst (46.01 vs. ~41 dB) is partly methodological—comparing direct prediction against downsampled ×4 outputs—so the true architectural advantage at lower scales is less clear. Open questions include how the approach scales to larger frame counts, whether the FNet-based flow estimator generalizes to more severe motion or occlusion, and whether the Fourier-space warping can be extended to video SR or other multi-frame tasks. The occlusion corner case and the performance drop with floating scale factors (due to demosaic double-downsampling) remain unresolved limitations.
