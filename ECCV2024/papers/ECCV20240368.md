## Problem

State-of-the-art audio-visual methods rely on separate modality-specific backbones and expensive pretraining protocols, making them costly (e.g., MAViL requires 5,120 V100 GPU hours) and memory-intensive (>48 GB GPU memory), which limits scalability to larger datasets and models. Additionally, modality-specific architectures introduce handcrafted inductive biases, lack flexibility for handling missing modalities, and are not parameter-efficient. The paper asks whether a single shared vision transformer backbone can match or exceed these separate-encoder methods while being dramatically more efficient.

## Input / Output

The model takes an RGB video frame (H_v × W_v × 3) and a ~10-second audio spectrogram (H_a × W_a) as inputs. Because the spectrogram is single-channel while the frame is 3-channel, the pretrained ViT's 3-channel patch-embedding weights are averaged into single-channel weights for audio processing. During pretraining, the number of tokens fed to the encoder varies per iteration due to multi-ratio masking (0–50%), and during fine-tuning the model randomly receives audio-only, visual-only, or joint audio-visual inputs. Outputs are class labels (multi-label for AudioSet, single-label for VGGSound) or cosine-similarity retrieval scores between pooled audio and visual features.

## Method

AVSiam uses a single shared-weight ViT backbone to process both audio and visual patch embeddings, followed by average pooling to produce modality-specific features, a joint two-layer self-attention multimodal layer, and a six-layer MAE-style decoder for token reconstruction. The key training innovation is a multi-ratio random masking scheme: within each batch, samples are partitioned into groups, and each group is assigned a random masking ratio from 0% to 50% in 5% increments, so all samples in a group share the same number of unmasked tokens and can be stacked efficiently. Pretraining combines a contrastive audio-visual matching loss (cosine similarity with temperature τ=0.05) and a masked-token reconstruction loss over both modalities. This design reduces GPU memory by ~1.7–1.9× compared to separate-encoder baselines, enables larger contrastive batches, and projects both modalities into a common latent space that particularly benefits retrieval tasks.

## Results

On AudioSet-2M, AVSiam-Huge achieves 54.1 mAP, surpassing the previous best MAViL-Stage2 (53.3 mAP) while requiring only 800 V100 GPU hours vs. 5,120 (28.9× faster). On VGGSound, AVSiam-Huge reaches 68.0% top-1 accuracy, also exceeding MAViL-Stage2 (67.1%). The default AVSiam-Base (100M params, 177 V100 hours) matches AV-MAE on AudioSet-2M (50.1 vs. 50.0 mAP) and VGGSound (64.9% vs. 64.2%) with fewer parameters and significantly less pretraining time. For zero-shot audio-visual retrieval, AVSiam-Base outperforms CAV-MAE+ on all three datasets (e.g., VGGSound V→A: 19.0 vs. 14.8 R@1) while using 38% fewer parameters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MBT [52], AVSiam-Base matches or slightly exceeds on AudioSet-2M (50.1 vs. 49.6 mAP) and VGGSound (64.9% vs. 64.1%) while using 42% fewer parameters (100M vs. 172M). Against AV-MAE [20], AVSiam-Base is comparable on AudioSet-2M (50.1 vs. 50.0 mAP) and VGGSound (64.9% vs. 64.2%) but requires 16× less pretraining time (177 vs. 2,854 V100 hours). Against CAV-MAE [26], AVSiam-Base trails slightly on AudioSet-2M (50.1 vs. 51.2 mAP) and VGGSound (64.9% vs. 65.5%), but AVSiam-Base+ (trained on a larger dataset) reverses the gap (51.4 vs. 51.2 mAP, 66.7% vs. 65.5%) while still being 1.5× faster to pretrain. Against MAViL [28], the default AVSiam-Base underperforms MAViL-Stage2 on all classification metrics, but AVSiam-Huge surpasses it on AudioSet-2M (54.1 vs. 53.3 mAP) and VGGSound (68.0% vs. 67.1%) at only 15.6% of the pretraining cost. On zero-shot retrieval (Table 2), AVSiam-Base wins over both CAV-MAE variants on every dataset and direction (e.g., VGGSound A→V: 20.4 vs. 12.8 R@1), and in throughput AVSiam processes 75.4 samples/sec vs. 22.5 (CAV-MAE) and 3.84 (MAViL).

## Relation to Prior Work

AVSiam builds directly on the contrastive audio-visual matching formulation and multimodal decoder design of CAV-MAE [26], as well as the masked token reconstruction objective and decoder architecture of MAE [27]. Its key departure is replacing the standard modality-specific backbone pair (e.g., AST for audio + ViT for visual) used by CAV-MAE, MAViL, and AV-MAE with a single shared ViT that processes both spectrograms and RGB frames, inspired by evidence that ViTs generalize across modalities. The multi-ratio random masking scheme is a novel training strategy that departs from the fixed-ratio masking used in prior MAE-based audio-visual pretraining, enabling larger effective batch sizes for contrastive learning while maintaining representational robustness.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 28.9× reduction in pretraining cost relative to MAViL and the ability to handle audio-only, visual-only, and audio-visual inputs with one model make audio-visual pretraining accessible to labs without A100/H100 clusters, potentially accelerating the field. The finding that a shared latent space improves retrieval performance (e.g., 20.4 vs. 12.8 R@1 on VGGSound audio-to-video) suggests that modality-specific encoders may actively hinder cross-modal alignment, a hypothesis worth further investigation. Open questions include whether the shared-backbone approach extends to tasks requiring fine-grained temporal alignment (event localization, segmentation) and whether the efficiency gains persist at truly large scale (e.g., ViT-Giant or 100M+ video datasets).
