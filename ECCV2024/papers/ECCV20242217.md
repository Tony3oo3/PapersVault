## Problem

Remote photoplethysmography (rPPG) suffers from extremely low signal-to-noise ratio in facial videos, creating a fundamental trade-off: signal-processing methods generalize well but yield large heart-rate errors, while deep-learning methods achieve state-of-the-art in-distribution accuracy but hallucinate on out-of-distribution (OOD) samples. Prior implicit neural representations (INRs) are universal function fitters that indiscriminately fit both the plethysmograph signal and interfering factors, making them unsuitable for isolating the subtle blood-flow component without selectivity.

## Input / Output

The method consumes 10-second face video clips (300 RGB frames at 128×128) and produces a heart-rate estimate in bpm, a continuous plethysmograph waveform, a spatial neural signal-strength mask, and the explicit A-B decomposition (appearance + blood components). The SINR mask network receives a 6-channel input (original RGB concatenated with the B-function estimate) but only processes the first 64 frames to produce a single static mask per clip. Final rPPG extraction uses only the green channel of the B-function, followed by detrending and Butterworth band-pass filtering for heart-rate estimation via power-spectral density.

## Method

The core contribution is an Appearance-Blood (A-B) decomposition implemented via two sequentially trained INRs on a shared multiresolution hash encoding (MRHE) backbone, achieving a 50× speedup over traditional INRs. The appearance model is a cascaded offset-based network (translating phase-based motion representations into learned spatiotemporal offsets) that captures specular highlights and static appearance while provably unable to represent plethysmograph signals. The residual blood model is a shallow sinusoidal representation network (SRN) with MRHE, trained after freezing the appearance model on the video-minus-appearance residual, thereby isolating the blood-flow component. A 3D CNN then learns a spatial SINR-optimal mask from the 6-channel input (RGB + B-function), supervised indirectly by a 1D CNN that is discarded at inference. At test time, the rPPG signal is computed as a SINR-weighted spatial average of the B-function using the learned mask, followed by standard spectral heart-rate estimation.

## Results

On the self-collected optically challenging OOD dataset (104 videos, ~1 hour), the proposed method achieves a T-Test pass rate of 16.15% (vs. 31.54% for the best baseline Green), MAE of 4.61 bpm (vs. 7.80 bpm), and Pearson r of 0.65 (vs. 0.47), representing a 48.8% reduction in T-Test failures and 40.9% reduction in MAE over the best prior method. The r-consistency metric (harmonic mean of in-distribution and OOD Pearson r) reaches 0.78, the highest among all methods. On in-distribution evaluation (dataset [49]), the method achieves MAE of 1.66 bpm and r of 0.94, within 0.3 bpm of the best baselines (PhysNet: 1.19 bpm, PhysFormer: 1.06 bpm).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against four algorithmic baselines (Green, POS, CHROM, ICA) and five learning-based baselines (DeepPhys, TS-CAN, EfficientPhys, PhysNet, PhysFormer). On the OOD dataset, the proposed method decisively outperforms all nine baselines across every metric: it halves the T-Test failure rate compared to the best algorithmic method (Green, 16.15% vs. 31.54%) and nearly quadruples the Pearson correlation (0.65 vs. 0.47). Learning-based methods collapse on OOD samples—DeepPhys and TS-CAN achieve r near zero (0.07 and 0.01 respectively), while PhysFormer and PhysNet reach only r=0.32—confirming the overfitting-to-training-distribution problem the paper targets. On in-distribution evaluation (dataset [49]), the proposed method is competitive but not the absolute best: PhysFormer achieves MAE 1.06 bpm and r=0.96, versus 1.66 bpm and r=0.94 for the proposed method, a gap the authors note is not clinically significant (<0.3 bpm). In the cross-dataset setting (train on [49], test on [53]), the proposed method is the best performer (MAE 1.16, r=0.89), outperforming PhysNet (MAE 1.48, r=0.93) and PhysFormer (MAE 1.06, r=0.96) in consistency while matching their accuracy. The key trade-off is that algorithmic methods generalize well but are fragile to optical challenges, while learning methods are accurate in-distribution but fail catastrophically OOD; the proposed A-B decomposition via INRs achieves the best of both.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes implicit neural representations (INRs)—previously used as universal scene/video function fitters in NeRF, SIREN, and motion-adjustable video models—as *selective* decomposers for physiological signal extraction. It builds on multiresolution hash encodings (Müller et al., 2022) for speed, phase-based motion representations (Mai & Liu, 2022) as the appearance (A) function, and sinusoidal representation networks (Sitzmann et al., 2020) as the blood (B) function, exploiting the empirical observation that these architectures have complementary representational limitations. The key departure from prior rPPG work (DeepPhys, PhysNet, PhysFormer) is replacing end-to-end waveform regression with a principled A-B decomposition followed by SINR-optimal spatial combining, and the departure from prior INR work is using architectural inductive biases to *exclude* signal components rather than fit everything.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The A-B decomposition framework provides a theoretically grounded (SINR-optimal, uncertainty-minimizing) alternative to black-box rPPG regression, directly addressing the well-known in-distribution vs. OOD generalization trade-off that plagues learning-based plethysmography. The optically challenging OOD dataset (104 videos, ~1 hour) is a valuable resource for the rPPG community, and the 50× speedup from hash encodings makes INR-based video decomposition practical at dataset scale. Open questions remain: motion-robust rPPG is explicitly out of scope, the single spatial mask computed over 64 frames limits temporal adaptivity, and whether the decomposition framework extends to other vital signs (SpO2, blood pressure) or modalities is unexplored. Runtime, while improved, still lags real-time requirements for clinical deployment.
