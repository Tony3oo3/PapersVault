## Problem

Attribution-based explanation methods are typically evaluated under disparate settings—different datasets, model architectures, and ablation protocols—making it impossible to draw consistent conclusions about which methods are genuinely superior. Prior attempts at consistent evaluation (e.g., ROAD) address only protocol-level inconsistency and fail to generalize across heterogeneous models and datasets, leaving the field without a unified, comprehensive benchmark.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241870/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes RGB images from four datasets (NWPU-RESISC45, Food-101, ImageNet-1k, Places-365) paired with six pre-trained or fine-tuned CNNs (ResNet-18/50, Inception-v4, VGG-19, EfficientNet-b2, DenseNet-121) under two ablation protocols (MoRF and LeRF), yielding 26 test cases. Each attribution method produces a pixel-wise saliency map of the same spatial dimensions as the input; the evaluation then progressively removes the most or least salient pixels and measures the resulting accuracy drop, converting the full ablation curve into a single AUC score per method per case. The final output is a Meta-Rank score (a real-valued number, positive or negative) and a unified leaderboard ranking all methods.

## Method

Meta-Rank treats each (dataset, model, protocol) triplet as a test case and evaluates all attribution methods on every case via feature ablation, producing a per-case ranking. For each pair of methods, it computes the fraction of test cases in which one outperforms the other, converts this win-rate into a log-odds (logit) value to amplify small differences, and then solves a system of linear equations equating pairwise logit differences to differences in per-method Meta-Rank scores. This pairwise-aggregation design ensures that no single configuration dominates the final ranking, and the logit transformation makes the metric sensitive to marginal performance gaps. The framework is designed to be extensible: new attribution methods can be evaluated on existing cases and folded into the leaderboard by re-solving the equation system, with the ranking-fusion step completing in under 0.12 s even for 64 methods.

## Results

Across 26 test cases (4 datasets × 6 models × 2 protocols), the Meta-Rank leaderboard ranks InputGradient highest (score 2.3200), followed by Integrated Gradients (2.0736) and DeepLift (1.0780), while Deconvolution ranks last (−3.9734). Spearman correlation analysis reveals that only 2 out of 18 model-level case pairs and 5 out of 18 dataset-level pairs show strong correlation (>0.8), confirming that rankings are highly setting-dependent. However, rankings remain remarkably consistent across training checkpoints (e.g., Epoch-100 vs. Epoch-200 differ by only one rank swap in MoRF for ResNet-18 on NWPU-RESISC45). The Ranking Fusion module completes in ~0.12 s even for 64 methods on CPU.

## Baselines & Comparisons

InputGradient (Shrikumar et al., 2016) and Integrated Gradients (Sundararajan et al., 2017) emerge as the two strongest attribution methods, with Meta-Rank scores of 2.32 and 2.07 respectively, outperforming all other methods across the aggregate of 26 heterogeneous test cases. DeepLift (Shrikumar et al., 2017) ranks third (1.08), while Saliency (Simonyan et al., 2014), LRP (Bach et al., 2015), and Guided Backpropagation (Springenberg et al., 2014) cluster near zero (−0.05 to −0.26), indicating roughly average performance. Guided Grad-CAM (Selvaraju et al., 2017) and Deconvolution (Zeiler & Fergus, 2014) perform worst, with scores of −0.97 and −3.97 respectively. The state-of-the-art evaluation method ROAD (Rong et al., 2022) is shown to produce no stronger cross-case Spearman correlations than plain feature ablation when extended to heterogeneous models and datasets, undermining its claimed consistency advantage. Overall, the benchmark demonstrates that no single attribution method dominates universally, and that prior single-setting evaluations can be highly misleading.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the feature-ablation evaluation paradigm (MoRF/LeRF from Samek et al.) and the subsequent consistency-focused efforts of ROAR (Hooker et al.) and ROAD (Rong et al.), which attempted to resolve distribution-shift and information-leakage issues in attribution evaluation. It departs from this lineage by arguing that the core problem is not the ablation mechanism itself but the lack of cross-setting aggregation: prior works evaluate under narrow, inconsistent configurations (typically one dataset, one or two models), and even ROAD's consistency guarantee does not transfer across heterogeneous architectures and domains. Meta-Rank reframes the problem as a ranking-fusion task over diverse test cases rather than a single-protocol evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that attribution method rankings are highly inconsistent across models, datasets, and protocols—yet stable across training checkpoints—challenges the implicit assumption in the literature that a single 'best' attribution method can be identified from a limited set of experiments. The negative result that ROAD, the state-of-the-art consistent evaluation, performs no better than naive feature ablation when scaled to heterogeneous settings is a significant caveat for the field. Open questions include whether the missingness bias inherent to pixel removal in CNNs can be fully resolved, how to extend the benchmark to Vision Transformers, and whether the Meta-Rank aggregation principle generalizes to other XAI evaluation dimensions beyond faithfulness.
