## Problem

Standard fundamental matrix solvers (7-point, 8-point) require many point correspondences per RANSAC iteration, making them slow under high outlier ratios. While relative depth information is readily available from feature scales or monocular depth estimation, no efficient minimal solvers existed for uncalibrated cameras that exploit this information to reduce the sample size. Prior work on relative-depth-based pose estimation was limited to calibrated cameras or required expensive affine correspondences.

## Input / Output

The solvers consume 2D point correspondences paired with relative depth values σ_i = λ_i2/λ_i1 (the ratio of a 3D point's depth in the second camera to its depth in the first). Relative depths can be obtained from SIFT/SURF feature scales, learned scale estimators (Self-scale-ori), monocular depth maps (Depth Anything), or depth cameras; crucially, the solvers tolerate depths known only up to an unknown per-image scale factor by absorbing it into the translation vector. The output is the 3×3 fundamental matrix, the 3D translation vector, and (for partially calibrated cases) the focal length(s), with up to 3 or 2 discrete solutions depending on the solver variant.

## Method

The key geometric constraint is [T]_× σ_i m_i2 − F m_i1 = 0, which yields three linear equations per point-depth pair on the 12 unknowns (9 elements of F plus 3 of T). For general intrinsics (4p4d solver), four point-depth pairs produce a 12×12 system of rank 11, solved directly via null-space computation. For different unknown focal lengths (4p3d solver), three point-depth pairs give nine linear equations, and the remaining constraints are obtained by computing an elimination ideal (via Macaulay2) that eliminates rotation and focal-length parameters from F = [T]_× K_2 R K_1^{-1}, yielding a quartic polynomial in F and T; substituting the two-dimensional null-space parameterization reduces this to a cubic univariate polynomial with up to three solutions. For equal unknown focal lengths (3p3d solver), three points define a plane, and the method formulates a Euclidean homography H = R + (t/d) n^⊤ whose median singular value must equal 1, producing a quadratic in f² that is solved in closed form. All solvers are minimal (fewest correspondences needed) and run in milliseconds, making them suitable as the hypothesis generator inside GC-RANSAC.

## Results

With accurate relative depths and SP+LG features, the proposed 4p4d solver achieves rotation errors of 1.90° (Phototourism), 0.13° (KITTI), and 0.68° (ETH3D), outperforming the 7pt baseline (2.62°, 0.18°, 1.47°) and the 4SIFT solver (3.32°, 0.17°, 1.79°) across all three datasets. For the equal-focal-length case, the 3p3d solver reduces rotation error to 1.11° on Phototourism and 0.70° on ETH3D versus 1.22° and 0.81° for the 6pt solver. Run-times are also reduced: 4p4d with SIFT+NN runs in 1.59 ms on Phototourism versus 2.74 ms for 7pt. However, when relative depths are approximated from SIFT scales or monocular depth (Depth Anything), the advantage over 7pt narrows or reverses on the challenging Phototourism and ETH3D datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the standard 7pt solver [25], the proposed 4p4d and 4p3d solvers clearly win on rotation and translation error when accurate relative depths are available (e.g., 1.90° vs 2.62° rotation on Phototourism, 0.68° vs 1.47° on ETH3D with SP+LG), and also run faster (4.66 ms vs 7.05 ms). Against the closest related work, 4SIFT [3], the 4p4d solver with ground-truth relative depths is substantially better (1.90° vs 3.32° on Phototourism), but when relative depths are approximated from SIFT scales, 4p4d (3.31°) is essentially tied with 4SIFT (3.32°). The 5ORI solver [1] is consistently outperformed by 4p4d with accurate depths (1.90° vs 3.57° on Phototourism) but the gap closes with approximate depths. For the equal-focal-length setting, the 3p3d solver beats the 6pt solver [30] on all three datasets with accurate relative depths (e.g., 0.70° vs 0.81° rotation on ETH3D) and is faster (13.3 ms vs 21.6 ms), though with SIFT-scale or DepthAny approximations the advantage shrinks to near-parity. Overall, the paper's solvers dominate when precise relative depths are available but do not consistently beat 7pt when depths come from learned monocular estimation or feature scales, particularly on the challenging Phototourism and ETH3D benchmarks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on minimal solvers for relative pose estimation using additional feature information (Barath & Kukelova 2022 on SIFT scales/orientations, Barath 2018 on feature rotations) by reframing the problem around relative depth as the key geometric quantity. The closest prior work, Astermark et al. (2024), derived a closed-form relative pose solver using known relative depth but assumed calibrated cameras; this paper removes that assumption and works with general uncalibrated fundamental matrices. The algebraic machinery (elimination ideals via Macaulay2, Gröbner basis reduction) follows the standard toolkit for minimal camera geometry solvers, but the specific constraints linking F and T under relative depth for uncalibrated cameras are new.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Reducing the RANSAC sample size from 7 to 4 (or 3) points has exponential impact on iteration count, making these solvers attractive for high-outlier-ratio matching scenarios. However, the paper's honest finding that approximate relative depths (from SIFT scales or monocular depth estimation) can actually degrade performance below the standard 7pt solver—especially with learned matchers like LightGlue—limits immediate practical adoption. The open question is whether rapid progress in monocular depth estimation (e.g., Depth Anything) will close this gap, at which point these solvers would become a clear win. The derived algebraic constraints between F and T may also serve as useful inductive biases for learning-based depth estimation.
