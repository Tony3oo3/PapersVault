## Problem

Dynamic novel view synthesis remains challenging because per-point motion fields are inherently underconstrained, especially under monocular capture with non-rigid and overlapping motions. Existing methods either require dense temporal correspondences, suffer from slow training and rendering, or lack the interpretability to disentangle and independently control individual scene motions. DynMF addresses this by constraining the motion field through a shared low-rank trajectory decomposition that is both expressive and efficient.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20242115/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes monocular or multi-view video sequences (50–200 frames) with known camera poses, requiring only 2k randomly initialized Gaussians around the scene center—no depth maps or multi-camera first-frame reconstruction. It produces photorealistic novel views at over 120 FPS for 1K resolution (300+ FPS at 400×400), alongside an interpretable set of B learned basis trajectories and per-Gaussian motion coefficients that enable selective motion enable/disable for novel motion synthesis. Storage is roughly double that of a static 3D Gaussian Splatting scene.

## Method

DynMF factorizes the per-Gaussian displacement and rotation into a linear combination of B globally shared basis trajectories (B=10 for synthetic, 32 for real scenes), where each basis is a small 8-layer MLP (256-width) queried only on time with 32-frequency positional encoding. Each Gaussian stores a canonical position, canonical quaternion, and a vector of B motion coefficients; at render time, a single global forward pass of the time-MLP produces all B trajectories, which are then blended per-Gaussian via the coefficients to yield the deformed means and rotations. A custom sparsity loss (L1/L∞ ratio of each Gaussian's coefficient vector) forces each Gaussian to select only a few active trajectories, enabling motion decoupling and independent control, while an L1 penalty suppresses spurious background motion. The entire representation is bound to standard 3D Gaussian Splatting rasterization, preserving its real-time rendering speed while adding only the cost of one tiny MLP evaluation per frame.

## Results

On the D-NeRF synthetic monocular dataset, DynMF achieves a PSNR of 36.89 dB, SSIM of 0.983, and LPIPS of 0.02, surpassing the previous best (Gaufre at 34.80 dB PSNR) by over 2 dB while training in 20 minutes and rendering at 300 FPS. On the N3DV multi-view real-scene dataset, DynMF reaches 31.70 dB PSNR and 135 FPS in 40 minutes of training, matching or exceeding all baselines in speed while remaining competitive in quality. On HyperNeRF monocular real scenes, DynMF achieves competitive per-scene PSNR (e.g., 28.5 on Chicken, 28.1 on Peel Banana) comparable to the best Gaussian-splatting baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On D-NeRF, DynMF decisively outperforms all 8 baselines: it beats the closest competitor Gaufre [26] by 2.09 dB PSNR (36.89 vs 34.80) and 4DGS [12] by 2.63 dB, while also matching or exceeding their LPIPS (0.02) and rendering at 300 FPS versus 50–1257 FPS. On HyperNeRF, DynMF is competitive but not uniformly best: 4DGS [7] edges it out on Chicken (28.7 vs 28.5 PSNR) and Peel Banana (28.0 vs 28.1 is a tie), while DynMF leads on 3D Printer (22.4 vs 22.1) and Broom (22.1 vs 22.0). On N3DV, HexPlane [7] achieves slightly higher PSNR (32.22 vs 31.70) and SSIM (0.98 vs 0.946), but DynMF trains 18× faster (40 min vs 12 h) and renders 1500× faster (135 FPS vs 0.09 FPS). Against the original D-NeRF [34], DynMF improves PSNR by 7.22 dB on D-NeRF and 2.12 dB on N3DV while reducing training time from 15.9 h / 1344 h to 20 min / 40 min. The speed advantage over all Gaussian-splatting baselines (4DGS [12], Gaufre [26], 4DGS [51]) is consistent, with DynMF maintaining >120 FPS at 1K resolution.

## Relation to Prior Work

DynMF builds directly on 3D Gaussian Splatting as its rendering backbone, extending it from static to dynamic scenes by decomposing per-Gaussian motion into a small set of globally shared, time-queried MLP basis trajectories. This departs from the dominant deformation-field paradigm (D-NeRF, Nerfies, HexPlane) and from per-Gaussian deformation approaches (4DGS, Dynamic 3D Gaussians) by arguing that a scene's motion is inherently low-rank and can be captured by fewer than 10 shared trajectories rather than per-point or per-Gaussian deformation functions. The sparsity-driven disentanglement of motions connects to the classical trajectory-space factorization literature in non-rigid SfM (Akhter et al.) but applies it in a learned, differentiable rendering context for the first time.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The combination of real-time rendering (>120 FPS at 1K resolution), fast training (~5 min for photorealistic quality), and explicit motion controllability (enabling/disabling individual trajectories to synthesize novel motion combinations) addresses a practical gap that prior dynamic NVS methods have not simultaneously closed. The motion decomposition capability opens doors to video editing, interactive scene control, and efficient animation that are difficult with monolithic deformation fields. Open questions include scalability to very long sequences or topologically changing scenes, whether the learned basis can be made physically interpretable, and how the method handles scenes with many independent rigid bodies exceeding the basis capacity. The authors also note the representation is naturally suited for dense point tracking, suggesting a broader utility beyond rendering.
