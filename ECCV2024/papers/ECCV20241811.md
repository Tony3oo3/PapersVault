## Problem

Whether recently developed deep neural architectures (CNNs, Transformers, MLP-Mixers) are truly similar or fundamentally different remains unclear, and what architectural factors drive their (dis)similarities is unexplored at scale. Existing similarity measures are either non-quantitative (loss landscape visualization), computationally prohibitive (decision boundary comparison), or limited to specific architecture pairs, preventing a comprehensive, architecture-agnostic comparison across diverse model families.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241811/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes two trained image classifiers of any architecture (CNN, ViT, MLP-Mixer, etc.) and a set of input images (10% subsample of ImageNet validation, 5,000 images at 224×224 or 256×256). It produces a single log-scaled pairwise SAT score per model pair, which is then aggregated into a 69×69 similarity matrix used for spectral clustering into 10 groups, gradient-boosting-based feature importance ranking over 13 architectural components, and downstream predictions of ensemble error reduction and distillation performance. Models are constrained to 79–83% top-1 ImageNet accuracy to control for performance gaps.

## Method

SAT measures similarity by generating PGD adversarial perturbations (50 iterations, ε=8/255) for each model and computing the cross-misclassification rate: the fraction of inputs where model A's adversarial sample fools model B and vice versa, averaged over inputs both models classify correctly, then log-scaled. The key insight is that adversarial attack transferability simultaneously approximates differences in input gradients, decision boundaries, and loss landscapes—three widely used but individually noisy or intractable similarity proxies—while remaining quantitative, architecture-agnostic, and computationally cheap (no extra training, no ground-truth masks, no smoothing). The paper validates SAT's robustness to attack choice (PGD, AutoAttack, MIFGSM, low-frequency PGD, PatchPool, BIA) and applies it to 69 ImageNet classifiers, using a gradient boosting regressor on Hamming-distance feature vectors to rank 13 architectural components by importance and spectral clustering on the pairwise SAT graph to identify 10 coherent architecture groups.

## Results

On 69 ImageNet classifiers, SAT reveals a strong negative correlation (Pearson r = −0.32, p ≈ 0) between model similarity and ensemble error reduction rate, confirming that more diverse architectures yield better ensembles. In Table 3, ensembling 5 models from 5 different SAT clusters achieves 14.11% error reduction, outperforming random selection (14.01%) and single-cluster selection (12.58%). For knowledge distillation of ViT-Ti students, using a dissimilar CNN teacher yields the best performance, with a −0.51 Pearson correlation between teacher-student SAT and distillation accuracy for non-Transformer teachers, versus a +0.70 correlation for Transformer teachers. SAT also generalizes cross-domain: ImageNet-measured SAT correlates with Flowers-102 ensemble performance after fine-tuning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares SAT against two prior quantitative similarity metrics: Somepalli et al. [83] (decision-boundary distance on a 3-image on-manifold plane) and Tramèr et al. [95] (distance to nearest decision boundary). In the 2-ensemble scenario (Fig. 10, 8 of 69 models), SAT shows a strong positive correlation with ensemble performance, while both Somepalli et al. and Tramèr et al. show almost random or slightly negative correlations, meaning they fail to predict which model pairs will ensemble well. In Appendix A.1, SAT demonstrates lower variance than Somepalli et al. even with a small number of samples, whereas Somepalli's metric remains unstable even with large sample counts. In Appendix A.2, under the same N-ensemble protocol as Fig. 7, SAT-guided model selection consistently outperforms both Somepalli-based selection and naive architecture-feature-based clustering. Tramèr et al. [95] is additionally limited by computational instability in searching for ε, making it impractical for large-scale pairwise comparisons across 69 models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes adversarial attack transferability (AT), a concept studied primarily in the security/robustness literature, as a quantitative similarity metric between neural architectures. It departs from prior similarity approaches—prediction-based statistics (Meding et al.), layer-by-layer CKA (Raghu et al.), loss landscape visualization (Dinh et al.), input gradient comparison (Smilkov et al.), and decision boundary distance (Tramèr et al., Somepalli et al.)—by arguing that AT simultaneously approximates differences in input gradients, decision boundaries, and loss landscapes while remaining quantitative, low-variance, and architecture-agnostic (applicable to both CNNs and Transformers). The paper does not build on a single prior method but synthesizes insights from the adversarial robustness literature (e.g., architectural dependence of vulnerability) to justify AT as a unifying similarity signal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SAT provides a practical, scalable tool for practitioners who need to compare or select among architectures for ensemble construction or knowledge distillation, with the 69-model ImageNet analysis offering concrete guidance (e.g., base architecture and stem design dominate diversity; diverse architectures outperform diverse training regimes for ensembles). The architecture-dependent KD finding—similar teachers help within the same family (Transformer→Transformer) while dissimilar teachers help across families (CNN→Transformer)—challenges the one-size-fits-all teacher selection heuristic. Open questions include whether SAT generalizes beyond ImageNet-scale classification (e.g., video, multimodal), how it behaves under adversarially trained models where decision boundaries shift, and whether the metric can guide architecture search rather than merely post-hoc comparison.
