## Problem

Existing deepfake detection methods operate as binary classifiers that identify whether an image is fake but cannot articulate *why* in human-understandable terms. They fail to capture unnatural 'non-physical' semantic attributes—overlapped eyebrows, rigid pupils, inconsistent skin shading—that humans use via common sense to spot fakes, and their saliency-map explanations are difficult to interpret. The paper asks whether a model can be trained to reason over such common-sense knowledge and produce explicit textual justifications for its real/fake verdict.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20242363/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a 480×480 cropped face image and a natural-language question (either a general 'Is this face real or fake?' or a fine-grained query about a specific component such as eyebrows, skin, eyes, nose, or mouth) and auto-regressively generates a free-form answer following the template 'The X looks real/fake because X looks Y,' where Y is a common-sense reason drawn from a predefined vocabulary of ~30 descriptors. Detection accuracy is evaluated by parsing the real/fake token from the first generated sentence, while explanation quality is scored with BLEU-4, CIDEr, ROUGE-L, METEOR, and SPICE against up to three human-annotated answers per question.

## Method

The architecture builds on BLIP (ViT-B/16 image encoder + BERT text encoder/decoder) with two cross-attention layers: one injects visual context into the question tokens, and another conditions the causal answer decoder on the attended question representation. Training combines three objectives: (1) standard language-modeling cross-entropy over answer tokens, (2) a text InfoNCE contrastive loss that pulls the [CLS] representation of a correct answer toward a positive answer and away from a negative answer (same facial component, opposite real/fake label), and (3) an image InfoNCE contrastive loss that aligns the ViT [CLS] embedding of the anchor image with a positive image sharing the same textual reason and repels a negative image with a contradictory reason. The learned visual features are then fused additively (after shape-matching) into the vision encoder of any existing deepfake detector (XceptionNet, HiFi-Net, SBIs, RECCE), improving both intra-dataset accuracy and cross-dataset generalization without modifying the detector's classification head.

## Results

On the DD-VQA task, the full model (BLIP-TI) achieves 87.49% accuracy and 90.07% F1 for deepfake detection, with CIDEr 2.06 and SPICE 0.69 for explanation quality, improving ~3% F1 and ~6% accuracy over the BLIP baseline. When the learned multi-modal representations are injected into existing detectors, consistent gains are observed across all four baselines: e.g., HiFi-Net improves from 92.10 to 95.14 AUC on FF++(c40) and from 65.22 to 70.03 AUC on WildDeepfake (cross-testing), while RECCE gains 5.15 AUC points on WildDeepfake (64.31→69.46) and reduces EER by 4.63 points (40.53→35.90).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper enhances four existing deepfake detectors (XceptionNet, HiFi-Net, SBIs, RECCE) by injecting DD-VQA-trained visual representations, and wins on every reported metric for all four. The largest cross-testing gains appear on WildDeepfake: HiFi-Net improves AUC by 4.81 points (65.22→70.03) and EER by 3.44 points, while RECCE gains 5.15 AUC points (64.31→69.46) and 4.63 EER points. On intra-testing FF++(c40), HiFi-Net reaches 95.14 AUC (vs. 92.10 baseline) and RECCE reaches 95.36 AUC (vs. 95.02), with SBIs showing only a marginal 0.03 AUC gain due to its already near-saturated 99.64 AUC. A control experiment (RECCE-BLIP, row 8) using BLIP's initial pre-trained weights instead of DD-VQA fine-tuned weights shows that the text-modality training contributes an additional ~2% improvement over raw BLIP features. Finally, against pure ViT-based detectors (Efficient ViT, Convolutional Cross ViT), the DD-VQA model substantially outperforms on the DD-VQA general-question detection task, confirming that the textual explanation modality provides a meaningful advantage over vision-only architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on BLIP as its vision-language backbone, extending it from general image captioning to a domain-specific deepfake detection VQA task. It departs from prior interpretable deepfake detection work (Grad-CAM, saliency maps, DFGNN) by replacing visual-only explanations with natural-language rationales grounded in human common sense, drawing conceptual inspiration from Visual Commonsense Reasoning (VCR). Unlike VLFFD, which uses automatically generated text describing manipulation regions and methods, DD-VQA employs free-form human-annotated explanations of *why* a face looks fake, and introduces text and image contrastive losses (InfoNCE) to align multi-modal representations around these explanations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The DD-VQA dataset (2,968 images, 14,782 QA pairs) and the model-agnostic recipe for injecting VQA-trained visual features into existing binary detectors (XceptionNet, HiFi-Net, SBIs, RECCE) provide a practical path toward interpretable deepfake detection that goes beyond saliency maps. The ablation showing that textual explanations improve detection F1 by ~1.5% and that even BLIP's initial pre-training weights aid cross-dataset generalization suggests that language-grounded reasoning captures cues that purely visual features miss. Key open questions include whether the approach generalizes to high-quality diffusion-based or GAN-generated deepfakes (the authors acknowledge this limitation), whether other VL backbones (Flamingo, MiniGPT-4) yield similar gains, and whether the modest dataset size constrains the richness of learned common-sense knowledge.
