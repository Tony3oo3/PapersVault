## Problem

Existing multi-weather image restoration methods are jointly trained on specific synthetic weather conditions and fail to generalize to unseen weather types, including real-world weather footage and co-occurring combinations of trained conditions. This paper addresses the gap by formulating weather-degraded image restoration as a few-shot adaptation problem that can handle arbitrary, previously unseen weather conditions with a single unified model.

## Input / Output

The method takes a weather-degraded query image (resized to 224×224 RGB) and a few-shot support set of N clean–degraded image pairs sampled from the same weather condition (default N=1, up to 4). Rather than directly regressing the clean image, the model predicts the degradation pattern G(X) — decomposed into particles P and atmospheric effects — and subtracts it from the query to produce the restored output. During meta-test adaptation, only bias parameters are updated while the support set is split into alternating query and support roles across iterations.

## Method

MetaWeather extends the Visual Token Matching (VTM) meta-learning framework by replacing background prediction with degradation-pattern prediction, guided by the insight that weather effects are shared across images while backgrounds differ. The architecture uses a SimMIM-pretrained Swin Transformer encoder and a hierarchical U-Net-like decoder, with matching modules at each encoder level. The key novelty is spatial-channel matching: spatial matching (d=HW, l=C) extracts localized degradation particles like rain streaks and snow, while channel matching (d=C, l=HW) captures globally disseminated atmospheric effects like fog and haze, reflecting the decomposable structure of weather degradation. Each matching module employs a modified Multi-Dconv Head Transposed Attention (MDTA) with layer-normalized queries and keys and a learnable temperature. Training follows a two-phase episodic protocol: a meta-train phase on single-weather datasets (Rain13K, Snow100K, Foggy Cityscapes, Raindrop) with full end-to-end optimization, followed by a meta-test adaptation phase where only bias parameters are fine-tuned on the few-shot support set to preserve meta-trained knowledge.

## Results

On the BID Task II.A dataset (co-occurring unseen weather types), MetaWeather achieves an average PSNR of 24.28 dB and SSIM of 0.8244, outperforming the second-best method WeatherDiffusion (22.26 dB, 0.7917) by 2.02 dB in PSNR and 0.0327 in SSIM. On real-world datasets, MetaWeather reaches 34.81 dB / 0.9394 SSIM on SPA-Data (real rain) and 30.15 dB / 0.9222 SSIM on RealSnow (real snow), surpassing the best baselines (MAXIM at 32.21 dB on SPA-Data and Restormer at 29.65 dB on RealSnow). The method also achieves the second-lowest computational cost (16.1 GFLOPs) among all compared methods, well below WeatherDiffusion (6415.5 GFLOPs) and MPRNet (840.1 GFLOPs).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MetaWeather outperforms all 10 baselines across every weather combination on BID Task II.A, with the largest margins on complex multi-degradation cases (e.g., R+D+S+HM: 22.42 dB vs. WeatherDiffusion's 20.80 dB, a 1.62 dB gap). The closest competitor, WeatherDiffusion [21], achieves the second-best average PSNR (22.26 dB) but at 400× the computational cost (6415.5 vs. 16.1 GFLOPs). Among single-degradation-trained models fine-tuned for multi-weather, NAFNet [2] and MPRNet [42] are the strongest (20.76 and 20.80 dB average), yet still trail MetaWeather by over 3.4 dB. On real-world data, MetaWeather beats the best real-world-specific method (Patil et al. [22], 20.26 dB on SPA-Data) by 14.55 dB, demonstrating that adaptation via few-shot support pairs is far more effective than domain-translation-only approaches. FocalNet [3] and TransWeather [33] show moderate performance on co-occurring weather but degrade significantly on real-world datasets (29.03 and 26.62 dB on SPA-Data respectively), confirming their limited generalization to unseen conditions.

## Relation to Prior Work

MetaWeather extends Visual Token Matching (VTM), a universal few-shot meta-learning framework for dense prediction, to the weather-degraded image restoration domain. It departs from prior multi-weather restoration methods (All-in-One, TransWeather, WeatherDiffusion) that are confined to weather types seen during training, and from prior few-shot restoration work (MLDN, FLUID) that presupposes knowledge of the target degradation type. The key methodological departure is the spatial-channel matching algorithm, which decomposes weather effects into spatially-localized particles (rain streaks, snow) and channel-wise atmospheric effects (fog, haze), addressing each through a dedicated matching pathway within a Swin Transformer encoder–decoder architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first method to handle truly unseen weather conditions—both novel combinations of known degradations and real-world footage—via few-shot adaptation without assuming the degradation type in advance, which is practically important for deployed vision systems in autonomous driving and surveillance. The spatial-channel decomposition provides a clean, interpretable mechanism for why matching works: spatial attention captures particles while channel attention captures global atmospheric effects. Limitations include reliance on a parametric degradation model (Eq. 1), evaluation at 224×224 resolution, and testing with at most 4 support images; whether the approach generalizes to weather phenomena outside the transmission-map-plus-particles formulation (e.g., lens smearing, extreme turbulence) remains open.
