## Problem

Scene text removal (STR) requires expensive pixel-level annotations (5–10 minutes per image), yielding small training sets (~2.7k images) that underutilize modern architectures. Existing pretraining approaches like ViTEraser's SegMIM use indirect auxiliary tasks for implicit feature learning, but standard masked image modeling conflicts with STR because it encourages the model to reconstruct masked text rather than erase it. This paper asks whether low-cost text detection labels (bounding boxes) can directly enable end-to-end STR training without pixel-level annotations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241771/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

During pretraining, the method consumes RGB scene text images (resized to 512×512) paired with binary text region masks derived from bounding box annotations in 8 public STD datasets (111k images total). The BM stream receives a doubly-masked image (text regions plus random non-text regions masked), while the TE stream receives the original unmasked image. At inference, only the original RGB image is needed—no text detection or mask input—producing a single text-removed RGB image in one forward pass. The framework is architecture-agnostic, demonstrated on both CNN (Erasenet, PERT, CTRNet, FETNet) and Transformer (Uformer) backbones.

## Method

TMIM consists of two parallel streams sharing model weights via a Prompt-based Multi-task Learning (PML) strategy that injects task-specific 1D vector prompts into encoder feature maps. The Background Modeling (BM) stream masks all text regions and additionally masks random non-text regions using text masks sampled from other images, then supervises recovery only on non-text regions—forcing the model to learn background generation rules rather than text reconstruction. The Text Erasing (TE) stream feeds the original image through the same model and trains end-to-end STR using pseudo labels constructed by splicing BM predictions onto text regions of the input image. Training uses a combined loss of L1 + focal frequency loss, SSIM loss, and VGG feature loss, pretrained for 10 epochs on 111k STD images before 200-epoch finetuning on the target STR dataset. The key insight is that restricting BM supervision to non-text regions eliminates the MIM–STR conflict and simultaneously produces usable pseudo STR labels, enabling explicit weakly supervised text removal training from detection-only annotations.

## Results

On SCUT-EnsText, Uformer-B+TMIM achieves state-of-the-art 37.35 PSNR, 97.78 MSSIM, and 0.0470 MSE, surpassing the previous best ViTEraser‡ (37.11 PSNR) by 0.24 PSNR with fewer parameters (50.88M vs 191.9M) and a lighter pretraining schedule (10 epochs on 111k images vs 100 epochs on 98k images). The weakly supervised pretrained model without finetuning already reaches 36.62 PSNR, outperforming all fully supervised no-finetuning baselines by over 0.3 PSNR. On SCUT-Syn, Uformer-B+TMIM achieves 44.24 PSNR and 98.86 MSSIM, setting a new state-of-the-art across all Image-Eval metrics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Uformer-B+TMIM outperforms all 16 compared baselines on PSNR across both SCUT-EnsText and SCUT-Syn. Against the closest competitor ViTEraser‡ (which also leverages large-scale STD data for pretraining), TMIM wins on PSNR (37.35 vs 37.11), MSSIM (97.78 vs 97.61), and MSE (0.0470 vs 0.0474) while using 3.8× fewer parameters (50.88M vs 191.9M) and a 10× shorter pretraining schedule; however, ViTEraser‡ achieves a lower Detection-Eval F-score (0.768 vs 1.051), which the authors note is sensitive to the specific text detector used. Compared to the unpretrained Uformer-B reimplementation, TMIM yields a +2.18 PSNR gain (37.35 vs 35.17), and against other STR architectures (Erasenet +1.33, PERT +1.59, CTRNet +0.34, FETNet +0.63), TMIM consistently improves PSNR by 0.34–2.18 points, confirming its model-agnostic applicability. Against detection-based two-stage methods (DeepEraser† 35.84, CTRNet† 35.20, Tang et al.† 35.54), TMIM's single-stage inference achieves 37.35 PSNR without requiring a separate text detector at test time. Against inpainting methods given ground-truth text masks (LaMa† 35.25, SPL† 35.41, MAT† 34.22, CTSDG† 33.10), TMIM leads by 1.94–4.25 PSNR, demonstrating that task-specific pretraining is more effective than general inpainting for text removal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the masked image modeling (MIM) pretraining paradigm from MAE and ViTEraser's SegMIM by identifying and resolving a fundamental conflict: standard MIM reconstruction encourages the model to regenerate text on masked regions, which is the opposite of the STR objective. TMIM departs from prior pretraining methods (which use indirect auxiliary tasks for implicit feature learning) by restricting the Background Modeling stream's supervision to non-text regions only and leveraging the resulting text-region predictions as pseudo labels for a parallel Text Erasing stream, thereby enabling direct end-to-end STR training under weak supervision. The Prompt-based Multi-task Learning strategy further distinguishes the work by allowing both streams to share a single model without architectural modification, making the framework model-agnostic across CNN and Transformer backbones.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical impact is that STR models can now be pretrained on 111k images with only low-cost text detection labels (obtainable via OCR systems), dramatically reducing dependence on the expensive pixel-level STR annotations (5–10 min per image) that have limited dataset sizes to ~2.7k. The finding that a weakly supervised pretrained model (36.62 PSNR) outperforms fully supervised baselines trained on the actual STR dataset suggests that the bottleneck in STR has been data quantity rather than label quality, opening the door to much larger-scale pretraining. Open questions include whether the pseudo-label quality from the BM stream becomes a ceiling for more complex or densely packed text layouts, and whether the approach transfers to other content-removal tasks (e.g., watermark or logo removal) where similar detection labels are available.
