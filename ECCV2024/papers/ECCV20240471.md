## Problem

State-of-the-art scene flow methods consistently fail to describe the motion of small, safety-critical objects like pedestrians and bicyclists, yet standard evaluation metrics (e.g., Threeway EPE) mask this failure because such objects contribute less than 1% of total points in a scene. The paper asks whether current scene flow methods actually work well in practice and proposes both a class-aware, speed-normalized evaluation protocol and a simple baseline that exposes the gap.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240471/cropped_page0_idx5.webp)

## Input / Output

TrackFlow consumes two consecutive LiDAR point clouds (P_t and P_{t+1}) and outputs a per-point 3D flow vector for each point in P_t. Unlike direct flow regression networks, TrackFlow does not learn a point-to-flow mapping; instead, it assigns rigid-body motion from detected and tracked 3D bounding boxes to all points falling within each box, and assigns zero flow to unassigned points. The detector (LE3DE2E) is run at an unusually low confidence threshold of 0.2 to maximize recall on small objects, trading false positives for coverage.

## Method

TrackFlow generates scene flow by running a state-of-the-art 3D object detector (LE3DE2E, a PointPillars-based architecture trained with class-balanced sampling and copy-paste augmentation) followed by a Kalman filter-based 3D tracker (AB3DMOT) to produce object trajectories, then applying the rigid transformation between consecutive frames to every point inside each tracked bounding box. The key insight is that ground truth flow is itself generated via rigid transforms within annotated 3D tracks, so a high-recall detector and tracker naturally approximate the annotation process. The paper also introduces Bucket Normalized EPE, an evaluation metric that partitions points into class × speed buckets and reports speed-normalized error per bucket, enabling direct comparison of flow quality across objects moving at vastly different speeds (e.g., a 0.5 m/s error is negligible for a 20 m/s car but catastrophic for a 0.5 m/s pedestrian). On Argoverse 2, TrackFlow achieves state-of-the-art Threeway EPE and captures ~20% more pedestrian motion than the best prior method, a 1.5× improvement.

## Results

On the Argoverse 2 test split, TrackFlow achieves state-of-the-art Threeway EPE, improving by 0.0015 m (1.5 mm) over the next-best method (ZeroFlow XL 5x). On the proposed Bucket Normalized EPE metric, TrackFlow captures approximately 10% more total described motion across all meta-classes compared to DeFlow, and is the only method to describe more than 50% of pedestrian motion. TrackFlow's mean Dynamic Normalized EPE is 0.287, with per-class values ranging from 0.182 (CAR) to 0.397 (PEDESTRIAN). On the pedestrian category specifically, TrackFlow beats DeFlow by over 20% (a 1.5× improvement), while NSFP and ZeroFlow XL 5x describe less than 30% and 20% of pedestrian motion respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TrackFlow outperforms all four baselines (FastFlow3D, DeFlow, NSFP, ZeroFlow XL 5x) on both Threeway EPE and the proposed Bucket Normalized EPE. On Threeway EPE, the margin is small (1.5 mm over ZeroFlow XL 5x), but on Bucket Normalized EPE the gap is substantial: TrackFlow captures ~10% more total motion than DeFlow overall, and on pedestrians it beats DeFlow by >20% (1.5×), NSFP by >20%, and ZeroFlow XL 5x by >30%. However, the comparison is not uniformly in TrackFlow's favor per class: DeFlow achieves the lowest Dynamic Normalized EPE on CAR (Fig. 5a), and ZeroFlow XL 5x is best on OTHER VEHICLES (Fig. 5b). The paper's key finding is that standard Threeway EPE masks these per-class disparities because cars dominate the point count (>15% of all points vs. <1% for pedestrians), making all methods appear nearly equivalent on the aggregate metric while TrackFlow is the only one to reliably describe small-object motion.

## Relation to Prior Work

TrackFlow departs fundamentally from the learned scene flow paradigm (FastFlow3D, DeFlow, NSFP, ZeroFlow) by repurposing the tracking-by-detection pipeline: a class-rebalanced 3D detector (LE3DE2E) followed by a Kalman filter tracker (AB3DMOT) generates rigid per-object transforms that are assigned to constituent points. This mirrors how ground truth flow is actually annotated (rigid transforms within 3D bounding box tracks), making the approach a direct exploitation of the annotation process rather than a learned regression. The evaluation contribution, Bucket Normalized EPE, borrows the class-aware, speed-normalized structure of mAP from object detection and applies it to flow estimation, filling a gap left by Threeway EPE's point-averaging that masks failures on small, fast-moving objects.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central finding—that all state-of-the-art learned scene flow methods fail catastrophically on pedestrians and VRUs while a simple detector+tracker baseline succeeds—challenges the field's assumption that learned flow regression is necessary for high-quality motion estimation. Bucket Normalized EPE is likely to become a standard supplementary metric, as it exposes performance disparities invisible to Threeway EPE. Key limitations include TrackFlow's restriction to rigid motion within a closed taxonomy (no non-rigid deformation, no unknown object classes), and the strong dependence on detector recall at low confidence thresholds, which may not transfer to all sensor configurations. Open questions include whether learned scene flow architectures can incorporate class re-balancing to close the gap, and whether the tracking framework extends to open-world or non-rigid settings.
