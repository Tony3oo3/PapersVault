## Problem

Existing non-Lambertian benchmarks suffer from limited scene and object diversity (mostly indoor tabletop settings), rely on techniques like pre-scanned 3D models or Lambertian paint that are hard to scale, and critically, none provide multi-layer 3D ground truth for objects occluded by transparent surfaces. This paper addresses these gaps by introducing LayeredFlow, a real-world benchmark with 150k optical flow and stereo pairs across 185 scenes and 360 objects, and proposes multi-layer optical flow as a new task requiring flow prediction for all visible surfaces, including those behind transparent objects.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240054/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pair of RGB images (H×W×3) and a query pixel coordinate (x,y), producing a variable-length sequence of n ordered 2D flow vectors where each vector encodes the displacement of the i-th visible surface layer from the first to the second image. The number of predicted layers n can vary per pixel, and evaluation is performed at 540×960 resolution. Ground truth annotations are sparse (20–500 correspondences per image pair via AprilTag markers), and each annotated pixel carries a single layer index and material label rather than dense per-pixel multi-layer labels.

## Method

The baseline Multi-RAFT modifies RAFT by replacing its single context encoder with n=4 independent context encoders (same architecture, separate weights), each feeding into a shared ConvGRU-based update block to produce n per-layer flow predictions. Training uses a 60k-image synthetic dataset rendered in Blender from 30 artist-made scenes with randomized lighting, materials (glass/metal), and inserted non-Lambertian objects; samples with fewer than n ground truth layers are padded by duplicating the last layer. At inference, a pruning heuristic discards any layer's flow prediction that falls within δ=0.5 pixels of the previous layer's prediction to avoid redundant outputs. The real-world benchmark captures multi-layer ground truth by attaching AprilTag fiducial markers to scene objects (including behind transparent surfaces) on a calibrated stereo camera, then computing triangulated 3D positions and 2D correspondences from tagged and untagged image pairs.

## Results

On the first-layer subset of LayeredFlow, RAFT fine-tuned jointly on Sintel and the synthetic dataset (S+L) achieves the best overall EPE of 15.63 and bad-1px of 77.81%, outperforming the original RAFT (EPE 16.49, bad-1px 78.45%) and all other baselines. On the last-layer subset, fine-tuning on the synthetic dataset dramatically reduces the Behind Transparent EPE from 8.48 (RAFT) to 4.34 (RAFT-ft L), demonstrating improved see-through capability. For the multi-layer task, Multi-RAFT(S+L) achieves 76.38% bad-1px across all layers, beating RAFT (78.13%) and all other single-layer methods, with particularly strong gains on Layer 2 (88.85% vs. 100% for RAFT at bad-1px).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares Multi-RAFT and fine-tuned RAFT against 10 optical flow baselines (FlowNet-C, FlowNet2, PWC-Net, GMA, SKFlow, CRAFT, GMFlow, GMFlow+, FlowFormer, and original RAFT) on the LayeredFlow benchmark. On first-layer evaluation, RAFT-ft(S+L) achieves the best overall EPE (15.63) and bad-1px (77.81%), beating the strongest single-layer baselines GMA (EPE 16.58) and FlowFormer (EPE 18.49), and improving over original RAFT (EPE 16.49) by 0.86 EPE. On the last-layer 'Behind Transparent' category, RAFT-ft(L) nearly halves the EPE compared to RAFT (4.34 vs. 8.48), a gain no other baseline approaches. In the multi-layer evaluation (Table 5), Multi-RAFT(S+L) outperforms all 10 baselines on non-Lambertian surfaces (Transparent bad-1px: 81.41% vs. best baseline 83.03% for FlowFormer; Reflective: 60.44% vs. 61.68% for RAFT) while maintaining competitive diffuse performance (49.22% vs. 51.66% for RAFT). Notably, fine-tuning solely on Sintel (RAFT-ft S) degrades performance relative to original RAFT, confirming that the synthetic dataset is essential for non-Lambertian improvement.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on RAFT's recurrent all-pairs field transform architecture, extending it with multiple independent context encoders (one per layer) and a ConvGRU-based update block to produce multi-layer flow predictions. It departs from prior non-Lambertian benchmarks (ClearPose, TransCG, Booster, PhoCal, etc.) that rely on pre-scanned 3D models, Lambertian painting, or structured lighting to acquire ground truth—approaches that severely limit scene and object diversity. The AprilTag-based stereo data collection pipeline is a novel methodological contribution that sidesteps these constraints, and the Blender-based synthetic dataset with randomized materials, lighting, and object placement is specifically designed to fill the training gap for non-Lambertian surfaces that existing synthetic flow datasets (Sintel, FlyingThings3D) leave unaddressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LayeredFlow is the first real-world benchmark providing multi-layer optical flow ground truth for non-Lambertian objects, filling a critical gap that has hindered progress in 3D understanding of glass, metal, and other reflective/transparent surfaces. The multi-layer optical flow task itself is newly formulated, and the baseline results (Table 4) show that even the best method still achieves 100% error on layers 2 and 3 for RAFT, indicating the problem is far from solved and inviting substantial future research. A key open question is whether the synthetic-to-real transfer strategy (fine-tuning on Blender-rendered scenes) generalizes beyond the specific material categories tested, and whether the AprilTag-based annotation pipeline can be scaled to denser per-pixel ground truth. The finding that joint fine-tuning on Sintel plus the synthetic data (S+L) is necessary to avoid degrading diffuse-surface performance highlights a practical tension in domain-adaptive training for non-Lambertian objects.
