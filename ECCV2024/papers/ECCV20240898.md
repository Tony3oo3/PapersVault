## Problem

Existing typography generation methods produce only static single-letter images, and general-purpose video diffusion models lack the ability to accurately render legible multi-letter text with controlled per-letter motions. This paper addresses the gap by proposing the first dedicated model for generating kinetic typography videos—animatable, multi-letter text with aesthetic effects and readable glyphs—from natural language prompts.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240898/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes two types of text input: a static caption describing spatial appearance (colors, glyph style, background texture, letter arrangement) and a dynamic caption describing temporal motion (entrance order, rotation, fade-in effects), plus a word content string formatted with per-letter delimiters (e.g., 'A^|p|p|l|e') to enable letter-level conditioning. The output is a 24-frame kinetic typography video at 512×288 resolution and 8 fps (3 seconds), trained at 256×256 with 24 frames. The word content is processed through a CLIP text encoder with a prompt template and fed through a separate zero-convolution cross-attention branch distinct from the appearance/motion captions.

## Method

KineTy builds on a Latent Diffusion Model with AnimateDiff's motion module, but introduces three key modifications. First, it decouples caption guidance by routing the static caption through spatial cross-attention (per-frame) and the dynamic caption through temporal cross-attention (across frames), mirroring how professional designers separate appearance from motion. Second, inspired by ControlNet, it adds a zero-initialized convolution-weighted cross-attention branch for the word caption in both spatial and temporal paths, allowing the network to learn how much to attend to specific letter content without overwhelming the effect descriptions. Third, a glyph loss applies pixel-wise weighting via a blurred text-segmentation mask on the last frame to sharpen letter edges and enforce legibility. Training follows a two-stage strategy: 30 epochs of spatial pre-training (text-to-image with static captions only, temporal modules detached), followed by one epoch of full video training with frozen spatial attention and dynamic captions, using AdamW at lr=1e-4 on 8 A100 GPUs.

## Results

On the KineTy evaluation set (15,184 videos across 584 templates), KineTy achieves an average FVD of 125.90, substantially outperforming the next-best method Lavie (956.97) by a factor of ~7.6×. KineTy also leads on Inception Score (1.77 vs. 2.68 for Lavie) and CLIPScore (0.88 vs. 0.82 for Lavie). The only metric where KineTy trails is OCR F1-score (0.76 vs. 0.90 for TextDiffuser+SparseCtrl), which the authors attribute to two-stage methods algorithmically rendering glyphs before deforming them.

## Baselines & Comparisons

KineTy decisively outperforms all five baselines on FVD, IS, and CLIPScore across every motion category (Entrance, Emphasis, Motion). Against the strongest one-stage baseline Lavie, KineTy reduces FVD from 956.97 to 125.90 (7.6× improvement) and improves CLIPScore from 0.82 to 0.88. Against the two-stage baselines (DS-Fusion+SparseCtrl, GlyphControl+SparseCtrl, TextDiffuser+SparseCtrl), KineTy achieves 10–20× lower FVD, indicating far more temporally coherent and visually faithful kinetic typography. The sole exception is OCR F1-score, where TextDiffuser+SparseCtrl (0.90) and GlyphControl+SparseCtrl (0.69) surpass KineTy (0.76); the authors explain that two-stage pipelines algorithmically render a clean glyph image before applying style, giving them an inherent advantage in character recognizability. AnimateDiff performs worst on OCR (0.01 average), confirming that general-purpose video diffusion models cannot reliably render legible multi-letter text.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Latent Diffusion Model architecture (Rombach et al.) and AnimateDiff's motion module (Guo et al.) to extend text-to-video generation into the specific domain of kinetic typography. It departs from prior typography work (DynTypo, Shape-Matching GAN++) that handled only single letters with static effects, and from general-purpose video diffusion models (AnimateDiff, Lavie) that lack letter-level understanding. The key architectural departure is the separation of spatial (static caption) and temporal (dynamic caption) conditioning into distinct cross-attention branches, combined with a ControlNet-inspired zero-convolution pathway for word content and a mask-weighted glyph loss for legibility.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first dedicated model for generating kinetic typography videos from text prompts, and the 600K-video dataset with paired static/dynamic captions is a practical resource for the motion graphics community. The finding that separating appearance and motion guidance into spatial vs. temporal attention branches is critical (ablation FVD jumps from 126 to 730 without it) offers a transferable design principle for other structured video generation tasks. Limitations include English-only support, 3-second clips, and OCR accuracy still trailing two-stage glyph-explicit methods, leaving open questions about scaling to longer sequences, multilingual text, and real-time interactive design workflows.
