## Problem

Open-vocabulary segmentation methods either require expensive dense mask annotations for known classes or costly contrastive training at scale to align image regions with language. The paper asks whether existing frozen foundation models can be combined to synthesise a segmentation algorithm on-demand for arbitrary class sets, without any additional data, annotations, or fine-tuning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240125/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image and a free-form set of natural-language class names as input, and produces a pixel-wise semantic segmentation map that includes an explicit background class. Support images are generated on-demand via Stable Diffusion (N=32 per class), and target images are processed with a sliding-window scheme at two scales (448 and 336 px windows, 224 px stride) to accommodate the limited resolution of off-the-shelf feature extractors. Prototypes are precomputed per vocabulary and can be reused across an entire image collection, making per-image inference a simple nearest-neighbour lookup.

## Method

OVDiff operates in three training-free steps: (1) Stable Diffusion generates a support set of images per class using the prompt 'A good picture of a ⟨c_i⟩'; (2) cross-attention maps from the diffusion model identify foreground regions, optionally refined by CutLER unsupervised instance segmentation, and features from a pretrained visual encoder (SD, DINO, or CLIP) are aggregated into three tiers of prototypes—instance-level, class-level, and part-level (via K-Means)—for both foreground and background; (3) target image features are matched to prototypes via cosine similarity, with the highest-scoring prototype across all classes (including a unified background class formed from all negative prototypes) determining the pixel label. Two key design choices distinguish the approach: comparing same-modality visual features rather than cross-modal image-text embeddings, and leveraging the contextual priors encoded in diffusion-generated backgrounds to segment the background directly without thresholding. Additional refinements include CLIP-based multi-label category pre-filtering to suppress spurious prototype correlations and a 'stuff' filter that removes background prototypes of thing-classes that overlap with stuff-class foreground prototypes.

## Results

OVDiff achieves 66.3±0.2 mIoU on PASCAL VOC, 29.7±0.3 on PASCAL Context, and 34.6±0.3 on COCO-Object, outperforming all prior open-vocabulary segmentation methods without any training or mask annotations. With PAMR post-processing, results improve to 68.4±0.2 (VOC), 31.2±0.4 (Context), and 36.2±0.4 (Object). The method uses only pre-trained components (Stable Diffusion v1.5, DINO, CLIP, CutLER) and a support set of N=32 generated images per class. On VOC, OVDiff leads the previous best (CLIP-DIY, 59.9) by more than 6 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OVDiff outperforms all 10 compared baselines on PASCAL VOC, achieving 66.3 mIoU versus CLIP-DIY's 59.9 (the strongest prior training-free method) and OVSegmentor's 53.8 (the strongest trained method), a margin of 6.4 and 12.5 points respectively. On PASCAL Context, OVDiff (29.7) surpasses SegCLIP (24.7), MaskCLIP (23.6), and TCL (24.3), though TCL+PAMR (30.4) narrowly edges it out by 0.7 points. On COCO-Object, OVDiff (34.6) beats CLIPpy (32.0), CLIP-DIY (31.0), and TCL (30.4). The method also outperforms ReCo [51], the closest retrieval-based approach, by 41.2 points on VOC, demonstrating the advantage of synthesising support images on-demand over retrieving from a fixed database. Notably, OVDiff requires no further training or mask annotations, whereas most competing methods (TCL, SegCLIP, OVSegmentor, GroupViT, ViewCo) rely on expensive contrastive training at scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

OVDiff departs from both major lines of open-vocabulary segmentation: (1) zero-shot methods that fine-tune CLIP-style encoders with mask annotations, and (2) contrastive-training methods (TCL, SegCLIP, GroupViT, etc.) that learn grouping mechanisms from image-text pairs. Instead, it repurposes a frozen text-to-image diffusion model (Stable Diffusion) as both a data synthesizer and a feature extractor, using the model's cross-attention maps to identify foreground/background regions in generated support images and its latent features for prototype-based nearest-neighbor matching. The only additional components are an off-the-shelf unsupervised segmenter (CutLER) for mask refinement and CLIP for category pre-filtering—none of which are trained.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By eliminating all training, mask annotations, and curated datasets, OVDiff offers a plug-and-play 'segmenter factory' that can be instantiated for any user-specified vocabulary on demand, which is practically significant for deployment in open-world settings. The finding that background prototypes derived from generative contextual priors contribute the largest single ablation drop (−11.2 mIoU on VOC) validates a principled alternative to ad-hoc thresholding for background handling, a long-standing open issue. Limitations include upscaling artifacts (halos around objects), dependence on the quality of generated support images for fine-grained or rare categories, and the compute cost of generating and processing support sets, though this cost is amortized over many inference images.
