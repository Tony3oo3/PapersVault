## Problem

Existing low-bit fixed-point training methods set gradient quantization intervals using min-max ranges or by minimizing quantization error across all gradients. The paper shows that minimizing error for entire gradients narrows the interval excessively (since most gradients cluster near zero), causing large quantization errors for high-magnitude gradients that dominate the training dynamics and lead to unstable training and severe accuracy collapse at 4-bit.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241882/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard RGB images at task-specific resolutions (32×32 for CIFAR-100, 224×224 for ImageNet/COCO, variable for DIV2K/Set5) and produces class labels, bounding boxes with class scores, or upsampled images (2×, 3×, 4×) depending on the downstream task. All quantization is applied layer-wise with a single uniform quantizer per layer for weights, activations, and gradients, using 4–8 bit fixed-point values, with the first and last layers kept at full precision.

## Method

The paper derives an upper bound on the quantization error for large-magnitude gradients (ULG) as a function of the clipping factor γ, splitting large gradients into clip-in (inside the interval) and clip-out (clipped) subsets. By taking the derivative of ULG with respect to γ and approximating the clip-out ratio as locally constant, they obtain a closed-form optimal condition: the clip-in ratio should equal (2^b−2)/(2^b−1)·α and the clip-out ratio should equal α/(2^b−1), where α is the fraction of large gradients. Based on this condition, they design a lightweight per-layer, per-iteration update rule that nudges γ up or down by a fixed step β depending on whether the current clip-out ratio exceeds or falls below the target, requiring only a count of clipped gradients per iteration. This adaptive interval adjustment maintains a small quantization error specifically for large gradients across all layers and training stages, without learning the interval end-to-end (which would require Hessian computation) and without per-channel or piecewise quantizers, keeping the approach compatible with standard hardware integer arithmetic.

## Results

On ImageNet, the proposed method achieves 67.1% top-1 accuracy with ResNet-18 and 69.2% with ResNet-34 under the 4/4/4-bit setting, outperforming DSGC (29.8% and 31.8%) by over 37 points and IQB (59.9% and 60.1%) by 7.2 and 9.1 points respectively. On CIFAR-100, ResNet-20 reaches 65.0% top-1 at 4/4/4-bit, compared to 24.3% for DSGC and 61.1% for the min-max baseline. For object detection on COCO, the method achieves 34.2 mAP at 6/6/6-bit with Faster R-CNN (ResNet-50), surpassing the baseline (33.8 mAP). On super-resolution (Set5, 4/4/4-bit), EDSR achieves 37.61 dB PSNR at 2× scale, improving over the baseline (37.50 dB).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against three main FXP training baselines: DSGC [41], IQB [25], and WAGEUBN [36]. DSGC, which minimizes quantization error over entire gradients, suffers catastrophic accuracy drops at low bit-widths (e.g., 29.8% vs. 67.1% for ResNet-18 at 4/4/4 on ImageNet), confirming the paper's central claim that minimizing global error harms large-gradient quantization. IQB, which uses a piecewise FXP format, is the strongest competitor at 4/4/4 (59.9% for ResNet-18, 60.1% for ResNet-34) but still trails the proposed method by 7.2 and 9.1 points respectively, and requires specialized hardware. WAGEUBN is only reported at 8/8/8 and underperforms the proposed method (e.g., 66.9% vs. 70.4% for ResNet-18). The proposed method also outperforms the simple min-max baseline (γ=1.0) consistently, with the largest gains at 4/4/4 (e.g., +3.8 points for ResNet-18, +6.3 for ResNet-34 on ImageNet; +3.9 for ResNet-20 on CIFAR-100). On COCO object detection and Set5 super-resolution, the method also beats the baseline, though DSGC is not reported at 6/6/6 for COCO and no prior gradient-quantization work exists for super-resolution.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the low-bit fixed-point (FXP) training line initiated by DorefaNet [40], which first quantized gradients into FXP values, and builds directly on DSGC [41], which proposed adjusting the quantization interval to minimize the quantization error for entire gradients. The key departure is a conceptual re-framing: rather than minimizing error across all gradients (as DSGC does) or using a fixed min-max range (γ=1), the authors argue that the error for large-magnitude gradients is the critical quantity to control, and derive an upper bound and adaptive update rule targeting that quantity. The method also contrasts with IQB [25] (piecewise FXP, hardware-unfriendly) and DAIQ [38] (channel-wise quantization) by using a simple layer-wise uniform quantizer that is more amenable to hardware deployment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that minimizing total gradient quantization error is actively harmful—because it narrows the interval and inflates error on the few large gradients that dominate training dynamics—provides a clear diagnostic for why prior FXP methods degrade at low bit-widths. The proposed adaptive clipping-factor update is computationally negligible and hardware-friendly, making it a practical drop-in improvement for INT4/INT5 training pipelines. Open questions include whether the approximation dR(G_out)/dγ ≈ 0 holds for all gradient distributions (e.g., in transformers or very deep networks), whether the α hyperparameter can be learned rather than grid-searched, and whether the approach extends to sub-4-bit regimes where the quantization step size becomes very coarse.
