## Problem

Backdoor attacks on asynchronous event data from event cameras remain largely unexplored, despite the growing adoption of event-based vision in autonomous driving, surveillance, and object recognition. Existing image-level backdoor methods cannot directly poison the raw event stream because event data is asynchronous and sparse, and the event representation module is typically inaccessible to attackers during inference. This paper demonstrates that the event stream itself can be poisoned with highly stealthy triggers that are nearly imperceptible to human inspectors.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240179/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an asynchronous event stream (a sequence of discrete events, each with x-y spatial coordinates, a timestamp, and a binary polarity) and produces a poisoned event stream with injected malicious events. The trigger injector is a 5-layer MLP (64 channels per layer) that receives m=100 randomly sampled timestamps from the original event and outputs modified malicious timestamps. The poisoned event stream is then converted to an image-like representation (e.g., EST grid) and fed to a standard classifier, which outputs a class label. The poison ratio is 0.1, and the trigger occupies a 10×10 spatial region.

## Method

Event Trojan proposes two trigger types injected directly into the event stream: an immutable trigger with fixed spatial coordinates, timestamps, and polarities, and a mutable trigger whose timestamps are adaptively generated by a 5-layer MLP conditioned on sampled timestamps from the original event. The key novelty is a trigger optimization loss (Eq. 5) that simultaneously minimizes cosine similarity between poisoned and benign timestamps (to make the trigger distinguishable to the classifier) and matches the expectation and variance of poisoned timestamps to those of benign data (to preserve stealthiness and avoid degrading clean-data accuracy). The classifier and trigger generator are trained jointly via supervised learning, with the generator optimized only during training so that at inference the trigger can be applied independently. Extensive experiments across 22 victim architectures on N-Caltech101 and N-Cars show the mutable trigger achieves near-perfect ASR (>99%) while maintaining high CDA, and remains effective against the Neural Polarizer defense.

## Results

On N-Caltech101, the mutable trigger achieves ASR of 99.71% (ResNet-18), 97.65% (VGG-16), 99.94% (Swin-S), and 87.73% (ViT-S) while maintaining CDA of 86.21%, 85.26%, 88.99%, and 47.31% respectively. On N-Cars, the mutable trigger reaches 100% ASR across all four victim models with CDA of 92.72%–94.76%. The immutable trigger achieves 96.73% ASR on ResNet-18 (N-Caltech101) and 99.67–100% ASR on N-Cars, but underperforms the mutable trigger on several architectures (e.g., 18.12% vs. 97.65% ASR on VGG-16, N-Caltech101). Against the Neural Polarizer defense, the mutable trigger retains 64.11% ASR while representation triggers drop to 0–1.03%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to BadNets (representation-level trigger), the mutable trigger dominates on N-Caltech101: BadNets fails entirely on ResNet-18 (0% ASR) and severely degrades CDA (57.24% vs. 86.21%), while the mutable trigger achieves 99.71% ASR with higher CDA. On N-Cars, both methods reach near-100% ASR, but the mutable trigger maintains slightly better CDA (92.72% vs. 91.27% on ResNet-18). Against FIBA, the mutable trigger is substantially more effective on ResNet-18 (N-Caltech101): 99.71% vs. 43.39% ASR, and preserves CDA (86.21% vs. 82.47%). The immutable trigger, while competitive on N-Cars (99.67–100% ASR), suffers from poor generalization on N-Caltech101 for VGG-16 (18.12%), Swin-S (21.61%), and ViT-S (14.74%) due to its fixed spatial-temporal pattern. Against the Neural Polarizer defense, representation triggers are nearly neutralized (ASR drops to 0–1.03%), whereas the mutable trigger retains 64.11% ASR, demonstrating that event-level poisoning is significantly harder to defend against than representation-level poisoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the well-established backdoor attack paradigm (BadNets, FIBA) from the image domain to the fundamentally different setting of asynchronous event data, where the data is a sparse, multi-dimensional stream of (x, y, t, polarity) tuples rather than a dense pixel grid. It departs from prior image-level backdoor work by targeting the raw event stream itself rather than the derived image-like representation, arguing that the representation module is inaccessible to attackers in practice. The mutable trigger introduces a learned MLP-based timestamp generator with a novel loss combining cosine similarity (for effectiveness) and statistical matching (for stealthiness), which has no direct analogue in the image backdoor literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work opens a previously unstudied security threat for the rapidly growing field of event-based vision, demonstrating that even state-of-the-art defenses (Neural Polarizer) only partially mitigate the attack (ASR drops from ~99.7% to ~64.1% but remains dangerous). The finding that multi-dimensional poisoning is essential—poisoning timestamps or polarities alone yields near-zero ASR—highlights a structural vulnerability specific to event data that image-domain defenses do not address. Key limitations include evaluation on only two classification datasets and a single defense method; extending to segmentation, tracking, and other event-based tasks, as well as developing event-specific defenses, remain important open questions.
