## Problem

Determining whether a viewing graph is solvable (i.e., whether its fundamental matrices uniquely determine the cameras up to projective transformation) has been limited by computationally expensive algebraic geometry tools (Gröbner basis) and edge-based formulations whose unknowns scale with the number of edges. This paper addresses the gap by proposing a more direct, node-based polynomial formulation and introducing the new concept of infinitesimal solvability as a tractable, rank-based test that avoids solving polynomial systems entirely.

## Input / Output

The method takes an undirected viewing graph with n nodes (cameras) and m edges (fundamental matrices) as input, where each edge carries a known 3×3 fundamental matrix. It produces a binary verdict (infinitesimally solvable or not) and, for unsolvable graphs, a partition of edges into maximal infinitesimally solvable components. The key structural detail is that the Jacobian matrix has 13n−1 columns (one block of 12 per camera plus one auxiliary variable), making its size depend on nodes rather than edges, and it is sparse since each equation involves only two cameras.

## Method

The core formulation derives 10m quadratic equations from the condition that P_j^T F_ij P_i is skew-symmetric for every edge (i,j), augmented with 16 equations fixing the global projective ambiguity, n−1 scale-normalization equations, and n−1 full-rank constraints via auxiliary variables, yielding a system of 10m+2n+14 equations in 13n−1 unknowns. Infinitesimal solvability is then tested by evaluating the rank of the Jacobian of this system at a fabricated solution (random full-rank cameras with fundamental matrices computed in closed form): full rank implies a finite number of solutions locally, while rank deficiency certifies unsolvability. For unsolvable graphs, maximal components are extracted iteratively by computing the null space of the Jacobian, identifying nodes whose corresponding rows are zero (belonging to the component containing the reference edge), and repeating on the remaining subgraph. This node-based approach is significantly more efficient than the edge-based finite solvability method of Arrigoni et al. [4], taking less than 10% of the running time on the largest datasets.

## Results

On 29 real structure-from-motion datasets (ranging from 18 to 5460 nodes), the proposed infinitesimal solvability test produces identical component counts to the finite solvability method of Arrigoni et al. [4] in every case, with only 5 unsolvable graphs found (all yielding 4 components). The key advantage is computational: on the largest datasets, the proposed method takes less than 10% of the running time of [4] (e.g., 898 s vs. 10,979 s on Cornell Arts Quad; 2,361 s vs. 25,889 s on Piccadilly), and about 20% on medium-sized datasets. On synthetic exhaustive tests (all biconnected graphs up to 10 nodes) and 8,000 random 20-node graphs, both methods again agree perfectly on solvability classification.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the finite solvability method of Arrigoni et al. [4] (ICCV 2023), which uses an edge-based formulation where the number of unknowns scales with the number of edges. On correctness, the two methods are tied: they produce identical solvability verdicts and identical component partitions on all 29 real datasets and all synthetic graphs tested. On efficiency, the proposed node-based method wins decisively — the Jacobian matrix has dimensions proportional to the number of nodes (13n−1 unknowns) rather than edges, yielding a speedup of roughly 5–12× on large datasets (e.g., 898 s vs. 10,979 s on Cornell Arts Quad, a 12.2× speedup; 2,361 s vs. 25,889 s on Piccadilly, an 11× speedup). The paper notes that the Trager et al. [31] method was not included in comparisons because it was already subsumed by [4]. The open question is whether infinitesimal solvability (the proposed test) is provably equivalent to finite solvability; empirically no counterexample was found, but a formal proof remains outstanding.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the viewing graph solvability line initiated by Levi & Werman [17] and formalized algebraically by Trager et al. [31] and Arrigoni et al. [2,4]. The key conceptual departure is a node-based formulation that directly uses the skew-symmetry condition P_j^T F_ij P_i (Lemma 1 from Hartley & Zisserman) to link cameras to fundamental matrices, replacing the edge-based unknown transformations of prior work. The new notion of infinitesimal solvability—testing full rank of the Jacobian at a fabricated solution—generalizes the Jacobian check from algebraic vision to this setting, whereas prior work relied on the special algebraic group structure of the edge-based equations to reduce finite solvability to a linear rank test.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The node-based formulation yields a 10× speedup on large SfM datasets (e.g., 898 s vs. 10,979 s on Arts Quad), making solvability testing practical as a pre-processing step for real reconstruction pipelines. The open question of whether infinitesimal solvability implies finite solvability (and hence uniqueness) remains unresolved; the authors conjecture equivalence based on exhaustive synthetic tests and real-data agreement, but no proof exists. This gap matters because infinitesimal solvability only guarantees finitely many solutions locally, not globally, leaving a theoretical hole between the computable test and the desired uniqueness guarantee.
