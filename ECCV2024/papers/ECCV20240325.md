## Problem

Mobile spectral sensors are currently limited to illuminant estimation for auto white balance, despite their potential to improve image quality through richer spectral information. Directly integrating low-resolution multi-spectral images (Lr-MSI) into the mobile ISP pipeline is hindered by two challenges: the inherent complexity of spectral images (scene geometry, inter-reflections, complex illumination) and the severely constrained spatial resolution of on-device spectral sensors. This paper explores whether Lr-MSI can meaningfully contribute to tone enhancement for high-dynamic-range outdoor scenes.

## Input / Output

The method takes a 16-bit multi-exposure-fused RGB image (4096×3072, cropped to 512×512 during training) and a very low-resolution multi-spectral image (Lr-MSI) of only 16×16 spatial pixels with 10 spectral channels spanning 400–1000 nm, downsampled from a 1057×960×176 hyperspectral capture. The output is an 8-bit tone-enhanced image. The extreme spatial resolution mismatch (16×16 vs. 512×512) is a defining constraint: the spectral input carries no meaningful spatial detail, so the architecture must extract purely spectral and semantic guidance from it.

## Method

The framework has two phases. First, a joint RGB-Spectral decomposition model (dual FCN encoders with feature concatenation) predicts three priors from the complementary RGB and Lr-MSI inputs: shading S (as 8-level brightness classification leveraging near-infrared bands), reflectance R (via Retinex division), and material semantic segmentation M (5 outdoor categories). Second, JDM-HDRNet integrates these priors into the HDRNet bilateral-grid architecture through three mechanisms: (1) a localized brightness adaptation module that separates S and operates in reflectance space to reduce color-mapping difficulty; (2) a Spectral Perception Self-Attention (SPSA) module that uses R_msi to generate a cross-channel spectral perception map, reweighting features to guide bilateral grid coefficient prediction; and (3) a Mixture of Semantic Grid Experts where each material category owns a dedicated bilateral grid, dynamically fused via category-dependent weights. Training is two-stage: the decomposition model is first trained with cross-entropy loss, then JDM-HDRNet is trained end-to-end with MSE loss.

## Results

On the Mobile-Spec dataset, JDM-HDRNet achieves a PSNR of 29.83 dB, SSIM of 0.967, and ΔE* of 3.60, outperforming all eight compared methods on every metric. The closest competitor is UPE (PSNR 28.19, SSIM 0.946, ΔE* 4.79), which JDM-HDRNet surpasses by 1.64 dB in PSNR and 1.19 in ΔE*. The baseline HDRNet (PSNR 27.75, SSIM 0.939, ΔE* 5.12) is improved by 2.08 dB in PSNR and 1.52 in ΔE* through the integration of shading, reflectance, and material semantic priors.

## Baselines & Comparisons

JDM-HDRNet outperforms all eight baselines on all three metrics (PSNR, SSIM, ΔE*). The largest gap is against DPE [6] (unpaired GAN-based), which lags by 7.02 dB in PSNR and 7.46 in ΔE*, attributed to GAN-induced artifacts. Among color-transform-based methods, SepLUT-L [36] (PSNR 28.08) and UPE [34] (PSNR 28.19) are the strongest competitors; JDM-HDRNet exceeds them by 1.75 dB and 1.64 dB respectively. The 3D LUT family (3D LUT [39], CLUT [41], 4D LUT [24]) clusters tightly around 27.3–27.8 dB PSNR, all trailing JDM-HDRNet by over 2 dB. The direct baseline HDRNet [11] is improved by 2.08 dB in PSNR and 1.52 in ΔE*, confirming that the spectral priors (shading, reflectance, material semantics) provide meaningful guidance beyond the original bilateral grid design. CSRNet [14] (MLP-based) underperforms the LUT-based methods, suggesting that lookup-table representations are more effective for this tone-mapping task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the HDRNet bilateral-grid framework (Gharbi et al., 2017) by injecting spectral-image intrinsic decomposition priors—shading, reflectance, and material semantics—into the tone-mapping pipeline. The joint decomposition model adapts the FCN encoder-decoder architecture (Long et al., 2015) to a dual-branch RGB + low-resolution multi-spectral setting, drawing on the Retinex-based SIID literature (Chen et al., 2017; Cheng et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2022) but reframing it as a learnable, end-to-end classification problem rather than an optimization under hand-crafted priors. Conceptually, the paper departs from the dominant use of mobile spectral sensors (limited to auto white balance) by demonstrating that even a 16×16×10 spectral patch carries enough information to guide dynamic-range enhancement, color mapping, and per-material tone adaptation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Mobile-Spec dataset is the first to provide aligned 16-bit RGB and 176-band hyperspectral images for mobile photography, filling a clear data gap that will support future spectral-vision research. The finding that a mere 16×16 spatial resolution in the spectral stream suffices to yield ~2 dB PSNR gain over HDRNet suggests that the spectral channel dimensionality, not spatial detail, is the critical resource for tone enhancement—potentially easing hardware constraints for future phone sensors. However, the 16×16 resolution is not yet achievable on commercial smartphones, and all results are validated on a single 200-image outdoor dataset, leaving open whether the gains transfer to indoor scenes, larger datasets, or higher-resolution spectral sensors.
