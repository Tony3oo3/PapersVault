## Problem

Existing visual reasoning methods are heavily task-specific, trained on small datasets, and fail to generalize across domains. The paper asks whether the two fundamental stages of visual reasoning—symbolization (extracting symbolic representations from raw data) and logical reasoning—should be entangled in an end-to-end model or disentangled into separated components, and which component benefits more from sharing across tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241138/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework accepts heterogeneous multi-modal inputs: 2D puzzle images (RAVEN, CVR, SVRT, Bongard), 3D video clips of physical interactions (Filtered-CoPhy), and text questions paired with images (VQAv2). Each task-specific encoder converts its input into a high-dimensional feature vector (symbol representation), which is then fed to the shared reasoner. Outputs are task-specific: multiple-choice class labels for puzzles and VQA, and keypoint displacement predictions for 3D physics tasks.

## Method

The paper proposes a two-stage framework with separated task-specific symbol encoders (ResNet-18 at task-specific depths) and a single shared reasoner, demonstrating that sharing the reasoner across tasks substantially outperforms sharing the encoder or both. The optimal symbolization depth is identified per task by probing accuracy curves for an inflection point, revealing that different domains require different levels of feature abstraction. The reasoner is trained jointly on multiple cross-domain datasets following an 'approximation principle': as training diversity increases, the reasoner converges toward task-agnostic pure reasoning, improving out-of-domain generalization. Among tested architectures (MLP, CNN, Transformer, GCN, neuro-symbolic, MiniGPT-4), a lightweight MLP reasoner achieves the best or comparable performance across all nine benchmarks, challenging the assumption that more complex architectures are needed for reasoning.

## Results

The One-for-All model with a shared MLP reasoner achieves 55.7 on RAVEN, 72.4 on CVR, 86.6 on SVRT, 57.7 on Bongard-HOI, 69.6 on Bongard-LOGO, and 50.1 on VQAv2, using fewer than 100M parameters for the reasoner. When scaled up with MiniGPT-4 as the shared reasoner, the model reaches 97.8 on SVRT (beating the heavy ad-hoc SOTA of 96.9) and 63.6 on VQAv2. The framework also demonstrates higher reasoning consistency (e.g., 99.73 on SVRT vs. 99.42 for ad-hoc models), suggesting genuine cross-task generalization rather than task-specific fitting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the lite ad-hoc SOTA baselines, the One-for-All (MLP) model wins on RAVEN (55.7 vs. 53.4, Zhang et al.) and Bongard-LOGO (69.6 vs. 65.4, Nie et al.), ties on Bongard-HOI (57.7 vs. 57.7, Shu et al.), but trails on CVR (72.4 vs. 78.3, Zerroug et al.), SVRT (86.6 vs. 92.5, Messina et al.), and VQAv2 (50.1 vs. 54.1, Antol et al.). Against the heavy ad-hoc SOTA, the MiniGPT-4-based One-for-All surpasses the SVRT baseline (97.8 vs. 96.9, Messina et al.) but falls well short on RAVEN (21.6 vs. 94.1, Spratley et al.), Bongard-HOI (53.8 vs. 66.4, Raghuraman et al.), Bongard-LOGO (54.8 vs. 75.3, Raghuraman et al.), and VQAv2 (63.6 vs. 76.4, Jiang et al.). In the CLIP-as-general-encoder ablation (Table 8), the One-for-All model outperforms CLIP on RAVEN (55.7 vs. 23.6), SVRT (86.6 vs. 65.6), Bongard-HOI (57.7 vs. 51.1), and Bongard-LOGO (69.6 vs. 58.1), while CLIP edges ahead only on CVR (74.6 vs. 72.4), confirming that a shared foundation encoder cannot replace task-specific symbolization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant end-to-end paradigm in visual reasoning (where recognition and logical inference are entangled in a single network) by explicitly decomposing the problem into a task-specific symbolization stage and a shared reasoning stage. Conceptually, it extends the neuro-symbolic tradition of separating perception from reasoning, but goes further by empirically demonstrating that the reasoning component can be shared across heterogeneous domains while symbolization must remain domain-specific. The 'approximation principle'—that a generalizable reasoner emerges from training on diverse tasks and modalities—draws an analogy to how LLMs leverage pre-existing linguistic symbols to focus on high-level reasoning, but applies this insight to the visual domain where no such universal symbol layer exists.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides actionable design principles for building generalizable visual reasoning systems: use small, task-specific encoders at the right depth (identified via inflection-point probing) and a single shared reasoner trained across diverse domains. A key open question is whether the framework scales to truly open-ended, compositional reasoning beyond the curated benchmarks tested here, and whether the 'approximation principle' holds as the number of training domains grows further. The finding that large foundation models (CLIP, MiniGPT-4) do not automatically provide better symbolization or reasoning for visual tasks challenges the prevailing assumption that scale alone solves cross-domain generalization, suggesting that architectural decomposition may matter more than parameter count for reasoning-specific capabilities.
