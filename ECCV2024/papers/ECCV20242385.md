## Problem

Most meta-learning methods assume the context set is passively provided, but in practical settings (medical imaging, manufacturing) one can actively choose which points to label. The intersection of active learning and meta-learning has been poorly explored: prior work applied active selection at meta-training time (where it does not help) or conflated different active meta-learning settings, and standard active learning strategies (uncertainty-based, DPP, coreset) perform poorly in the very-low-budget regime that meta-learning demands.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pool of unlabeled data points, extracts features using the meta-learned network itself (penultimate-layer representations of f_θ({}) for optimization-based methods like MAML/ANIL/MetaOptNet, or the metric-based feature map for ProtoNet), and a label budget k equal to the context set size. It outputs k selected points (one per Gaussian component) that form the context set, which is then passed to the meta-learner to produce an adapted predictor evaluated on a disjoint target set. The key design choice is using the meta-learner's own features rather than raw pixels or self-supervised representations, ensuring the selection criterion aligns with the downstream classifier's geometry.

## Method

The paper proposes a simple GMM-based selection algorithm: fit a k-component Gaussian mixture with shared diagonal covariance to the meta-learned features of the unlabeled pool using EM with k-means initialization, then select the highest-density point (minimum Mahalanobis distance to its component mean) from each component. The theoretical motivation shows that under isotropic Gaussian class-conditional distributions with orthonormal means, labeling the cluster centers yields a Bayes-optimal max-margin separator, and more generally the selected points form an efficient set cover of the feature space. The paper further clarifies that active selection at meta-training time is mildly harmful, while active selection at meta-test/deployment time (where Pick_eval = Pick_deploy) is where gains are realized, and that in the very-low-budget regime, diversity (covering all classes) is far more important than uncertainty reduction.

## Results

On 5-way 1-shot MiniImageNet with MAML (unstratified), GMM achieves 37.68% accuracy vs. 34.85% for random selection, a 2.8-point gain; in the fully stratified setting the gap widens to 58.82% vs. 47.93% (10.9 points). On 5-way 5-shot MiniImageNet (unstratified), GMM reaches 59.05% vs. 58.84% for random, while uncertainty-based methods (Entropy 39.57%, Margin 53.92%) and Coreset (45.74%) fall far behind. Cross-domain evaluation on Places (1-shot) shows the largest gains: GMM achieves 60.01% vs. 44.28% for random, a 15.7-point improvement. On CUB with Baseline++ (5-shot, unstratified), GMM reaches 82.68% vs. 79.57% random and 65.71% ProbCover.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GMM consistently outperforms all nine baselines across every dataset and meta-learning algorithm tested. Uncertainty-based methods (Entropy, Margin) are the weakest, often performing worse than random selection in unstratified settings (e.g., Entropy 30.44% vs. random 34.85% on 1-shot unstratified MiniImageNet), confirming that model uncertainty is a poor signal in the very-low-budget regime. DPP and Coreset, while designed for diversity, only marginally beat random (DPP 36.44% vs. 34.85%) and Coreset is frequently worse than random (45.74% vs. 58.84% on 5-shot unstratified). Typiclust is competitive in stratified settings (55.65% on 1-shot stratified) but degrades in unstratified ones (35.46%). ProbCover is typically the strongest baseline (second-best in most settings) but suffers from its hard radius parameter, which is difficult to tune and causes large performance swings. Hybrid methods combining uncertainty and diversity (Weighted Entropy, BADGE, k-means Entropy) all underperform GMM by 3–15 points (e.g., BADGE 34.30% vs. GMM 37.68% on 1-shot unstratified MiniImageNet), indicating that in the extreme low-budget regime, pure diversity via GMM is more effective than any uncertainty-diversity combination.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a clearer conceptual space at the intersection of active learning and meta-learning, where prior work (Müller et al., Al-Shedivat et al., Boney et al.) had applied active selection at different stages (train vs. test) without a unified framework. Methodologically, the proposed GMM-based selection repurposes a classical clustering idea (fit k Gaussians, pick the mode of each) in a meta-learning-specific feature space, departing from the more complex DPP, Coreset, and ProbCover approaches that dominate low-budget active learning literature. The theoretical grounding connects to max-margin separator theory and Bayes-optimality under Gaussian class-conditional assumptions, linking the simple heuristic to a principled optimality result that prior active learning methods for meta-learning lacked.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway—that a trivially simple GMM consistently outperforms sophisticated active learning methods (DPP, Coreset, ProbCover, BADGE, hybrid uncertainty-diversity methods) across six meta-learning algorithms and multiple datasets—gives practitioners a low-complexity, well-motivated tool for context set selection. The finding that active selection at meta-train time is mildly harmful while test-time selection yields large gains redirects future effort toward deployment-stage strategies. Open questions include whether the GMM advantage persists in higher-budget regimes, whether it transfers to non-visual modalities, and whether the theoretical assumptions (isotropic Gaussians, near-orthonormal means) can be relaxed to cover more realistic feature geometries.
