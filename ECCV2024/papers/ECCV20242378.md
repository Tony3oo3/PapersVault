## Problem

The concept of a 'nasty teacher' (a DNN trained by self-undermining KD to resist distillation) was previously undefined in a rigorous sense, and it remained unclear whether any KD method could fully distill such a teacher. This paper formalizes the notions of distillable DNN and KD-resistant DNN using the label-smoothing student as benchmark, demonstrates that nasty teachers are KD-resistant to all state-of-the-art logit-based KD methods, and then proposes a method that makes them fully distillable.

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 64×64 for TinyImageNet) and produces a C-class probability distribution from the student. The teacher is accessed strictly as a black-box input-output oracle, so only its softmax output distribution is available—no logits or internal features. In the cross-domain setting, the teacher is pre-trained on one label set (e.g., 50 classes from CIFAR-100) while the student is trained on a completely disjoint label set, with all input samples drawn from the student's domain.

## Method

MKD is a generic distribution-based KD method that applies two sequential transforms to the teacher's output distribution before computing the student loss: a power transform (equivalent to temperature scaling) to increase conditional mutual information, followed by a label-specific Markov transform that multiplies the distribution by a C×C Markov matrix to separate and concentrate the per-label clusters in probability space. Each Markov matrix is parameterized as the row-wise softmax of a low-rank matrix (αI + AB) to keep the parameter count at C(1+2nC), and is co-learned jointly with the student via backpropagation. The student loss is identical to the underlying distribution-based KD method (e.g., standard KD or DKD) but operates on the Markov-transformed distribution rather than the raw teacher output. The key insight from information geometry is that nasty teachers produce interconnected, linearly-related distribution clusters that defeat existing KD methods, while the Markov transform decouples these clusters without modifying the frozen teacher, and the co-learning mechanism enables cross-domain transfer by adapting the transform to the student's domain.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with a nasty R18 teacher, MKD-KD achieves 87.82% (CNN), 92.91% (RC20), and 93.69% (RC32) top-1 accuracy, consistently exceeding the LS student benchmark (87.40%, 92.53%, 93.36%). On TinyImageNet with a nasty R50 teacher and ShuffleNetV2 student, MKD-KD reaches 61.48% versus 57.91% for LS student, a gain of +3.57%. Standard KD catastrophically fails on nasty teachers (e.g., 1.04% on TinyImageNet), while MKD-KD makes all nasty teachers fully distillable across CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and TinyImageNet.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MKD-KD outperforms all eight compared baselines on the nasty-teacher distillation task. Standard KD [5] catastrophically fails, dropping to 1.04% on TinyImageNet (vs. 55.79% LS), confirming that nasty teachers are KD-resistant to the original method. The three methods designed specifically for nasty teachers—Skep. [15], HTC [8], and Avg. [11]—recover partial accuracy but still fall below the LS student in most settings (e.g., Skep. achieves 86.71% vs. LS 87.40% on CIFAR-10/CNN; HTC reaches 92.48% vs. LS 92.53% on CIFAR-10/RC20). State-of-the-art logit-based methods DKD [38], DIST [7], NKD [33], and MLD [9] also fail to exceed LS student on nasty teachers (Table 1). MKD-KD is the only method that consistently surpasses LS student across all teacher-student pairs and all three datasets, with the largest margin of +3.57% on TinyImageNet (R50→SV2). On normal teachers (Table 3), MKD-KD and MKD-DKD also outperform their underlying KD and DKD methods by 0.1–3.5%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the self-undermining KD framework of Ma et al. [16] for constructing 'nasty teachers' and the information-geometry analysis of DNNs from Yang et al. [32], which characterizes cluster concentration via conditional mutual information (CMI) and inter-cluster separation. It departs from prior KD-resistance work (Skep. [15], HTC [8], Avg. [11]) by reframing the evaluation benchmark from CE student to LS student and introducing formal definitions of 'distillable DNN' and 'KD-resistant DNN.' The core methodological departure is the Markov transform: rather than designing new loss functions to counteract nasty teachers, MKD applies a learned per-class Markov matrix to the teacher's output distribution before feeding it into any standard distribution-based KD loss, effectively re-separating the probability clusters that self-undermining KD has deliberately entangled.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that a teacher trained on a completely non-overlapping label set can still yield a student outperforming LS via MKD-KD (while standard KD collapses to near-random accuracy) opens a genuinely new research direction in cross-domain knowledge transfer, though the current evidence is limited to two datasets and two student architectures. The open theoretical question of whether separation distance Γ provably increases under Markov transform (only CMI decrease is guaranteed by Theorem 1) remains unresolved. Practically, MKD's simplicity—adding a small co-learned matrix to any existing distribution-based KD pipeline—makes it easy to adopt, but the modest gains over LS (typically 0.3–3.5%) and the narrow cross-domain evaluation limit immediate impact on IP-protection strategies, which would require validation on larger, more diverse domain pairs.
