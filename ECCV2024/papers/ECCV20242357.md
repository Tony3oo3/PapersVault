## Problem

Existing topological comparison metrics for scalar functions, such as Wasserstein distance between persistence barcodes, are blind to the spatial localization of topological features: two functions can have identical barcodes yet place their cavities, holes, or connected components in entirely different regions. This limitation is particularly harmful in 3D shape reconstruction and segmentation, where a topologically correct but spatially misplaced feature is still an error. The paper asks whether a topological divergence can simultaneously capture multi-scale topology and the location of topological discrepancies.

## Input / Output

The method consumes two scalar functions f, g defined on a common domain — either vertices of an undirected graph or nodes of a regular n-dimensional lattice (e.g., a 32³ or 64×64 grid) — and produces three artifacts: (i) F-Cross-Barcodes, which are standard persistence barcodes computed on a constructed doubled graph or extended (n+1)-dimensional cubical lattice; (ii) a scalar SFTD value (sum of interval lengths to power p, typically p=1 or 2); and (iii) a point-cloud visualization mapping birth/death vertices back onto the original domain to highlight where topological differences are localized. In the CV experiments, f is a network prediction and g is ground truth; SFTD enters as an additional differentiable loss term alongside Dice or cross-entropy.

## Method

Given two scalar functions f, g on a graph G, the method constructs a doubled graph G̃ with 2n+1 vertices: n copies A_i carrying f-values, n copies A'_i carrying min(f,g)-values, and a global-minimum node O, with edges connecting A_i to A'_i and cross-edges (A_i, A'_j) for i<j. The k-th persistence barcode of the lower-star filtration induced by the vertex function f̃ on G̃ is defined as the F-Cross-Barcode k(f,g); each interval in this barcode corresponds to a topological feature present in f's sublevel sets but absent in g's (or vice versa), and its birth/death vertices localize the discrepancy in the original domain. For lattice domains, the construction is adapted to an extended (n+1)-dimensional cubical complex with one extra dimension of size 3, computed efficiently via GUDHI. SFTD_k(f,g) is then the sum of |d−b|^p over all F-Cross-Barcode intervals, and its gradient with respect to network parameters is obtained by a chain rule through the persistence barcode, following the differentiability framework of Carrière et al. The key theoretical guarantee is a stability bound: the bottleneck distance between F-Cross-Barcodes of (f,g) and (f′,g′) is at most the sup-norm perturbation of either function, ensuring robustness to noise.

## Results

On the 'Red blood cell' 3D reconstruction dataset, SHAPR augmented with SFTD (dimensions 0–3) achieves the best volume error (0.266 vs. 0.353 for SHAPR and 0.291 for SHAPR+W.D.), roughness error (0.276 vs. 0.361 and 0.294), and all topological Wasserstein-2 distances (H₀: 0.079, H₁: 0.217, H₂: 0.025 vs. 0.098/0.241/0.065 for SHAPR). On the 'Cell nuclei' dataset, SFTD (0–3) achieves the best 1-IoU (0.597) and H₂ error (0.011). The paper also reports that SFTD outperforms Betti matching loss in 2D segmentation while being approximately 35× faster (Appendix G).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares SFTD against two baselines: vanilla SHAPR [37] (no topological loss) and SHAPR+W.D. [36] (Wasserstein-2 distance between persistence barcodes as topological loss). On the 'Red blood cell' dataset, SFTD (0–3 dims) outperforms SHAPR+W.D. on volume error (0.266 vs. 0.291), roughness (0.276 vs. 0.294), and all three topological metrics (H₀ 0.079 vs. 0.098, H₁ 0.217 vs. 0.228, H₂ 0.025 vs. 0.032), while being slightly worse on 1-IoU (0.453 vs. 0.467) and surface error (0.189 vs. 0.184). Against vanilla SHAPR, SFTD wins on every metric except 1-IoU, with the largest gains in H₂ (0.025 vs. 0.065, a 62% reduction) and volume (0.266 vs. 0.353). On the 'Cell nuclei' dataset, improvements are smaller but SFTD (0–3) still achieves the best 1-IoU (0.597 vs. 0.612) and H₂ (0.011 vs. 0.027/0.038). The key qualitative advantage is that SFTD captures *localization* of topological features—two functions with identical persistence barcodes can still have non-zero SFTD if their topological features are in different spatial locations—whereas Wasserstein distance between barcodes is blind to this.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of differentiable topological losses for deep learning (Carrière et al., ICML 2021) by introducing a new construction — the F-Cross-Barcode — that encodes spatial localization of topological differences between two scalar functions, rather than merely comparing their persistence barcodes via Wasserstein distance. It departs from prior topological segmentation losses (e.g., Betti matching, which is restricted to 2D) and from the Wasserstein-distance topological loss used in SHAPR+W.D. (Waibel et al., MICCAI 2022), which the authors show fails to capture where topological features are located. The 3D reconstruction experiments are built directly on the SHAPR architecture, replacing its topological loss term with the proposed SFTD.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The F-Cross-Barcode / SFTD framework provides a differentiable, localization-aware topological metric that can be plugged into existing 3D reconstruction and segmentation pipelines, and its visualization capability offers a practical debugging tool for identifying where topological errors occur. However, the demonstrated improvements over Wasserstein-distance baselines are modest (e.g., 1-IoU improving from 0.467 to 0.449 on the Red blood cell dataset), and the method's computational cost on large 3D volumes remains a practical concern. Open questions include whether the approach scales to very high-resolution 3D medical imaging, how it behaves with multi-class segmentations beyond binary sublevel-set comparisons, and whether the localization signal provides meaningful gains in more complex topological settings (e.g., nested cavities or high-genus surfaces).
