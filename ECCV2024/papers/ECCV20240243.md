## Problem

While photorealistic 3D human body models have matured visually, spatial audio rendering for full-body avatars remains largely unsolved. The prior state-of-the-art (SoundingBodies) models the body soundfield as a single 17th-order ambisonic representation on a ~2m sphere, which cannot render near-field audio inside that sphere, requires expensive and unstable high-order coefficient estimation, and precludes real-time rendering.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240243/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a 3D body pose sequence (joint coordinates at 30 fps) and a head-mounted microphone audio signal (48 kHz) as input, and produces the spatial audio waveform at an arbitrary 3D listener position specified in spherical coordinates (r, θ, φ). Training data is only available on a sphere of 345 microphones surrounding the body, yet the model must generalize to render sound at any position in 3D space, including arbitrarily close to the body surface. The intermediate representation consists of K=12 low-order (2nd-order) ambisonic coefficient vectors, each attached to a body joint with a learned spatial offset (±20 cm) and a learned temporal weight.

## Method

The core contribution is replacing the single high-order ambisonic sphere with multiple low-order acoustic primitives (small spheres of 2nd-order spherical harmonics) attached to body joints, borrowing the volumetric-primitive paradigm from neural rendering (Gaussians, cuboids). A pose encoder (temporal convolutions + MLP) and an audio encoder (STFT → ResNet-LSTM blocks) produce features that are fused via a lightweight ResNet-attention module; a decoder with skip connections outputs the harmonic coefficients for all primitives, while separate MLP heads predict per-primitive spatial offsets (tanh-constrained to 20 cm) and temporal weights (softmax). Rendering is fully differentiable: each primitive's contribution at the target position is computed via spherical Hankel wave functions, and the final signal is the weighted sum over all primitives. Training is end-to-end supervised with a multiscale STFT loss (amplitude + real/imaginary), a shift-L1 loss for spatial alignment, and a cross-entropy loss on clip-level primitive activation labels, achieving 15× faster inference than the baseline while matching or exceeding its quality.

## Results

On the SoundingBodies dataset, the proposed method with 12 second-order acoustic primitives achieves an SDR of 3.597 dB on non-speech sounds and 8.448 dB on speech, compared to 3.052 dB and 9.635 dB for the SoundingBodies [44] baseline. The most striking advantage is inference speed: the proposed method renders 1 second of 48 kHz audio in 0.24 s, roughly 15× faster than the 3.56 s required by [44] (which does not even include the additional DSP overhead for high-order ambisonic encoding). Amplitude and phase errors are broadly comparable across both categories, with the proposed method slightly worse on non-speech amplitude (0.883 vs 0.832) but better on speech phase (0.417 vs 0.464).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is SoundingBodies [44] (Xu et al., NeurIPS 2023), which predicts 345 raw microphone signals and then computes a 17th-order ambisonic representation via traditional DSP. The proposed method wins on non-speech SDR (3.597 vs 3.052 dB, +0.55 dB) and speech phase error (0.417 vs 0.464), indicating better temporal alignment for speech. However, it trails on speech SDR (8.448 vs 9.635 dB, −1.19 dB) and speech amplitude error (0.943 vs 0.701), suggesting the low-order primitives capture less fine-grained spectral detail for speech than the high-order representation. On non-speech amplitude and phase the two methods are nearly tied (differences < 0.05). The decisive advantage is computational: the proposed method is ~15× faster at inference (0.24 s vs 3.56 s per second of audio), and the reported baseline time excludes the additional high-order ambisonic encoding/decoding step that [44] requires in practice, making the true speed gap even larger.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends SoundingBodies [44], the first system to render 3D spatial sound of humans from body pose and headset microphone audio, by replacing its single 17th-order ambisonic representation on a 2m sphere with multiple low-order (2nd-order) acoustic primitives attached to individual body joints. The core conceptual move is borrowing the volumetric-primitive paradigm from neural rendering (NeRF, 3D Gaussian Splatting, cuboid mixtures) and transferring it into the acoustic domain, where each primitive is a small local sound source modeled via spherical wave functions. The differentiable rendering pipeline follows the same end-to-end training philosophy as NeRF, with the wave propagation equation serving as the rendering equation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 15× inference speedup (0.24s vs. 3.56s per second of audio) and the ability to render sound in the near-field (close to the body) are practically significant for real-time AR/VR applications where the listener may be centimeters from a virtual character. However, the approach still requires training data from a 345-microphone spherical array in an anechoic chamber, limiting immediate deployment; the authors explicitly flag the need for learning from commodity hardware (e.g., smartphones) as a key open question. Generalization beyond human bodies and beyond 2nd-order harmonics (constrained by PyTorch's spherical wave function implementations) are further open directions.
