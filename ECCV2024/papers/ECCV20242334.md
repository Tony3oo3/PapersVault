## Problem

Long-range matching dataset distillation methods rely on a Fixed Trajectory Length (FTL) to align synthetic-data training trajectories with pre-collected expert trajectories. This fixed step size forces student trajectories to compress or stretch to match diverse expert patterns, causing persistent matching errors that accumulate over distillation iterations — a phenomenon the authors term the Accumulated Mismatching Problem (AMP). AMP leads the synthetic dataset to overfit seen expert trajectories while sacrificing generalization, particularly to unseen architectures.

## Input / Output

The method takes a real training dataset (32×32 CIFAR-10/100, 64×64 Tiny ImageNet, 128×128 ImageNet subsets) and a distillation network architecture (simple ConvNet) to generate expert training trajectories, which are pre-collected and stored in disk buffers (~60 MB per CIFAR expert, ~120 MB per ImageNet expert). The output is a synthetic dataset of a few images per class (IPC=1, 10, or 50) whose pixel values are learned parameters. Evaluation trains five randomly initialized networks of various architectures (ConvNet, ResNet-18, VGG-11, AlexNet) on the synthetic set and reports mean±std test accuracy on the original test split.

## Method

ATT replaces the fixed trajectory length with a dynamic, minimum-distance selection: at each distillation iteration, it unrolls a student trajectory on the synthetic data for up to N_S steps, computes the squared L2 distance between every intermediate prediction θ'_{i,t} and the expert target θ*_{i,N_T}, and selects the step N_opt that minimizes this distance (Eq. 6). Backpropagation is then performed only at the selected step, allowing early termination when a close match is found. This directly minimizes the per-iteration matching error ε_0 that feeds into the Accumulated Trajectory Error (ATE), breaking the error accumulation loop inherent in FTL methods. The approach requires no architectural changes to the LDD optimization framework and incurs negligible additional runtime (~0.8 s/iteration, comparable to vanilla LDD) because early-step selection avoids unnecessary tail-end computation.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, ATT achieves 48.3% (IPC=1), 67.7% (IPC=10), and 74.5% (IPC=50) test accuracy, outperforming the previous best LDD method FTD by 1.5%, 1.1%, and 0.7% respectively. On CIFAR-100, ATT reaches 26.1% (IPC=1), 44.2% (IPC=10), and 51.2% (IPC=50), improving over FTD by up to 0.9%. The most dramatic gains appear in cross-architecture generalization: ATT improves over FTD by 7.2% on VGG11 and 7.7% on AlexNet at IPC=10, and by 6.2% on AlexNet at IPC=50. On ImageNet subsets at IPC=10, ATT gains 1.0–2.2% over FTD across all four subsets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ATT outperforms all nine baselines across every dataset and IPC setting where comparisons are available. Against the closest LDD competitor FTD [16], ATT gains 0.5–1.5% on CIFAR-10/100, 0.6–1.3% on Tiny ImageNet, and 1.0–2.2% on ImageNet subsets. The advantage is most pronounced in cross-architecture evaluation (Table 1): ATT surpasses FTD by 7.2% on VGG11 and 7.7% on AlexNet at IPC=10, confirming that the Accumulated Mismatching Problem disproportionately harms generalization to unseen architectures. Against the original MTT [6], ATT improves by 1.5–2.9% on CIFAR-10 and 1.8–3.5% on CIFAR-100. Short-range methods (DC, DSA, DM, CAFE) and the kernel-based KIP [40] are consistently outperformed by 10–20+ points; KIP is competitive only at CIFAR-10 IPC=1 (49.9% vs. ATT's 48.3%) but falls behind at larger IPCs. Parameter Pruning [31] is marginally ahead of MTT on CIFAR-100 IPC=10 (43.1% vs. 39.7%) but still trails ATT by 1.1%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the long-range matching dataset distillation (LDD) lineage initiated by MTT (Cazenavette et al., CVPR 2022) and refined by FTD (Du et al., 2023), which identified Accumulated Trajectory Error (ATE) and adopted flat expert trajectories. The key conceptual departure is the identification of the Accumulated Mismatching Problem (AMP): the fixed trajectory length (FTL) strategy used by all prior LDD methods forces student trajectories to match at an inappropriate step, causing systematic overfitting to buffered expert trajectories. ATT addresses this with a minimal modification—dynamically selecting the step with minimum L2 distance to the expert target at each iteration—rather than always matching at a predetermined N_S.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The AMP diagnosis provides a clear mechanistic explanation for why LDD methods struggle with cross-architecture generalization, a critical but underexplored evaluation axis in dataset distillation. The 7%+ cross-architecture gains (e.g., VGG11, AlexNet) are substantially larger than the 1–2% same-architecture improvements, suggesting that FTL-induced overfitting is a primary bottleneck for deploying distilled datasets across diverse model families. Limitations include the continued need for large expert trajectory buffers (~60–120 MB per expert) and the computational cost of evaluating all intermediate steps, though the authors show runtime is comparable to vanilla LDD. Open questions include whether the minimum-distance selection generalizes to other matching objectives (e.g., gradient-based) and whether AMP-like effects appear in short-range matching methods.
