## Problem

Gradient-based explanation methods (e.g., SmoothGrad, Integrated Gradients) produce inconsistent attributions when their perturbation hyperparameters change, yet no prior work formally explains the source of this inconsistency. The paper identifies a pervasive spectral bias: the gradient operator amplifies high-frequency features while perturbation kernels attenuate them, and the interaction between the two creates a band-pass filter whose shape is entirely controlled by the perturbation scale, causing different hyperparameters to highlight different frequency bands of the decision boundary.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20242331/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image and a trained classifier (evaluated on ResNet-50 over ImageNet and Food101) and produces three complementary outputs: a standard pixel attribution map, an ArgLens map that assigns each pixel the perturbation scale (i.e., frequency band) at which its attribution is maximized, and a SpectralLens map that aggregates attributions across all frequency bands via an explanation prior. The Fourier analysis is performed 1D per pixel along the pixel-value axis rather than along spatial dimensions, and perturbations are applied independently per pixel, which constrains the framework to pixel-level attribution and limits its applicability to non-image modalities.

## Method

The paper introduces a probabilistic decomposition of any gradient-based explanation into a perturbation distribution, an explainer (gradient or squared gradient), and a summary statistic, then derives closed-form spectral representations for each combination. The key insight is that the gradient operator multiplies the Fourier spectrum by iω (a high-pass filter) while any perturbation kernel multiplies by its own Fourier transform (a low-pass filter), so their product is a band-pass filter whose center frequency shifts with the perturbation scale σ. To resolve the resulting inconsistencies, the paper proposes two remedies: (1) selecting an optimal σ per pixel by maximizing the cosine similarity between the classifier's power spectral density and the scaled perturbation kernel's PSD, and (2) SpectralLens (SL²), which integrates the squared-gradient attribution over a distribution p(σ) on perturbation scales (an 'explanation prior'), effectively averaging across frequency bands to produce a more stable, band-agnostic attribution. The squared-gradient variant is shown analytically to avoid the destructive interference and sign-flipping issues that plague the plain gradient variant.

## Results

Using single-pixel most-important-first (MoRF) insertion and deletion scores on ResNet-50, the proposed SpectralLens (SL²) achieves the best Insertion score on ImageNet (0.493 ± 0.016) and near-best on Food101 (0.395 ± 0.014), while the optimized-perturbation SmoothGrad (SG²_Opt) reaches 0.491 ± 0.015 and 0.394 ± 0.013 respectively. Squared-gradient variants consistently outperform their plain-gradient counterparts: SG² (0.465 Insertion, 0.157 Deletion on ImageNet) beats SG (0.212, 0.179) by a wide margin, and IG² (0.340, 0.168) beats IG (0.201, 0.182). On Food101, IG² achieves the best Deletion score (0.135 ± 0.005) among all gradient-based methods, while SL² attains the best Deletion among the proposed methods (0.157 ± 0.007).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against RISE (the strongest non-gradient baseline), SL² is essentially tied on ImageNet Insertion (0.493 vs 0.494) but trails on Food101 Insertion (0.395 vs 0.410); on Deletion, SL² is competitive on ImageNet (0.169 vs 0.174) and clearly better on Food101 (0.157 vs 0.198). Compared to Occlusion Sensitivity (OS), both SL² and SG²_Opt substantially outperform OS on Insertion (0.493/0.491 vs 0.384 on ImageNet) and Deletion (0.169/0.187 vs 0.237 on ImageNet), confirming that gradient-based methods with proper spectral design dominate the occlusion baseline. The squared-gradient design choice (Remark 5) is validated: SG² beats SG by 0.253 on ImageNet Insertion (0.465 vs 0.212) and IG² beats IG by 0.139 (0.340 vs 0.201), with similar gaps on Food101. The optimized perturbation scale (Remark 6) improves SG² to SG²_Opt by 0.026 on ImageNet Insertion (0.491 vs 0.465) and 0.023 on Food101 (0.394 vs 0.371), while the uniform explanation prior (Remark 7) further lifts SL² to the top Insertion score on both datasets. Notably, input multiplication (XIG vs IG) degrades performance on Food101 (XIG²: 0.204 Insertion vs IG²: 0.320), contradicting its common use for visual quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the probabilistic unification framework of Covert et al. (2022) for explanation methods and extends it with a spectral (Fourier-domain) analysis that had not previously been applied to pixel attribution. It derives closed-form spectral representations for SmoothGrad's Gaussian kernel and Integrated Gradients' rect kernel, revealing that the gradient operator acts as a high-pass filter while any perturbation acts as a low-pass filter, so their combination is inherently a band-pass filter. This spectral lens provides formal justification for empirical observations from Hooker et al. (2019) on squared gradients and from Bansal et al. (2020) on hyperparameter sensitivity, reframing the latter as a Rashomon effect caused by selecting different frequency bands.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The band-pass filter interpretation offers a principled, frequency-domain explanation for why gradient-based explanations are inconsistent across hyperparameters—a long-standing practical problem without a clear theoretical account. The two proposed remedies (cosine-similarity-based perturbation scale selection and SpectralLens aggregation) are straightforward and implementable, though empirical gains over standard SmoothGrad are modest. Key limitations include the restriction to 1D per-pixel Fourier analysis (not spatial), the Gaussian perturbation assumption, and the computational cost of multi-scale backpropagation. Open questions include extending the framework to function-space perturbations, other data modalities, and whether frequency attributions generalize across images.
