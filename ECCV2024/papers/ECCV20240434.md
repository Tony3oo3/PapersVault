## Problem

Existing deep blind video deflickering (BVD) methods suffer from high computational cost (e.g., ~10 min for an 8-second video), produce color artifacts and distortions, and cannot recover texture lost to local over-/under-exposure. No prior deep BVD method has explored a compact sub-pixel representation to capture illumination fluctuations, and no method simultaneously addresses both global illumination flicker and local exposure degradation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240434/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single flickering video sequence as input with no requirement for flicker type knowledge, reference frames, or additional guidance (i.e., fully blind). It produces a deflickered, temporally consistent video at the same resolution. The pipeline is designed to handle arbitrary flicker patterns including long- and short-term color/brightness flicker, old film artifacts, high-speed camera effects, and flicker introduced by generative video models, operating at 480p in experiments with inference ~10× faster than the prior state-of-the-art.

## Method

BlazeBVD is a three-stage histogram-assisted pipeline. Stage 1 extracts per-frame illumination (V-channel) histograms, applies Scale-Time Equalization (Gaussian temporal smoothing) to the histogram sequence, and derives three deflickering priors: filtered illumination maps (via histogram matching), a singular frames set (frames whose KL divergence from the smoothed histogram exceeds a local moving-average threshold), and binary exposure maps flagging over-/under-exposed pixels. Stage 2 uses a 2D-Unet (GFRM) conditioned on the filtered illumination map to correct global per-frame flicker, and a local module (LFRM) that warps neighboring frames via RAFT optical flow and fuses them into exposure-masked regions to restore lost texture. Stage 3 applies a lightweight 3D spatio-temporal network (TCM, based on RTN) trained with an adaptive mask-weighted warping loss that up-weights over-/under-exposed regions to refine temporal coherence without the blurring artifact seen in prior methods.

## Results

On DAVIS-2017-Test synthetic videos, BlazeBVD achieves PSNR 28.61 dB and SSIM 0.9638, substantially outperforming the strongest baseline Deflicker (23.93 dB / 0.9243) by over 4.7 dB in PSNR. The warping error E_warp of 0.0825 is slightly lower than Deflicker's 0.0840 and closer to the ground-truth value of 0.1097. On the Blind Deflickering Dataset synthetic videos, BlazeBVD averages PSNR 28.15 dB and SSIM 0.9538 versus Deflicker's 25.30 dB and 0.9349. Inference is approximately 10× faster than Deflicker (58.4 s vs. 614.2 s per 80-frame video) with 3.7× less memory (1389 MB vs. 5204 MB).

## Baselines & Comparisons

BlazeBVD outperforms all four baselines on PSNR and SSIM across both DAVIS-2017-Test and the Blind Deflickering Dataset. Against the primary baseline Deflicker [21] (neural atlas, CVPR 2023), BlazeBVD gains +4.68 dB PSNR and +0.0395 SSIM on DAVIS-2017-Test, while also achieving a marginally lower E_warp (0.0825 vs. 0.0840) that is closer to the GT value of 0.1097, suggesting Deflicker's slightly lower warping error is partly masked by image blurring. Against the classical STE method [8], BlazeBVD improves PSNR by 2.47 dB and SSIM by 0.0317, while reducing E_warp from 0.1117 to 0.0825, confirming that the learned 2D/3D networks recover texture details that pure histogram matching cannot. Against the temporal-consistency baselines ConvLSTM [20] and DVP [22], BlazeBVD leads by roughly 4.5 dB in PSNR and 0.038 in SSIM, with E_warp reductions of 0.054 and 0.059 respectively, indicating these methods fail to remove significant flicker. In user studies on real-world and generated videos (Table 3), BlazeBVD is preferred over Deflicker in 61–81% of pairwise comparisons across all seven video categories.

## Relation to Prior Work

BlazeBVD draws its core inspiration from the classic Scale-Time Equalization (STE) method of Delon (2006), repurposing it not as a standalone deflickering tool but as a prior-extraction stage that produces filtered illumination maps, singular frame indices, and exposure maps to guide subsequent neural network processing. It departs from the dominant deep BVD paradigm exemplified by Deflicker (Lei et al., CVPR 2023), which relies on a neural atlas to represent the entire video spatio-temporally, by instead using compact illumination histograms to decompose the problem into per-frame 2D corrections (GFRM) and local optical-flow-based texture transfer (LFRM). The temporal consistency refinement stage (TCM) builds on the RTN architecture (Wan et al., 2022) but introduces an adaptive mask-weighted warping loss to avoid the blurring artifacts observed in Deflicker's training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 10× inference speedup (58 s vs. 614 s at 80p) and reduced memory footprint (1.4 GB vs. 5.2 GB) make blind video deflickering practically deployable for long videos and real-time applications, a significant practical advance over atlas-based methods whose cost scales with video duration. BlazeBVD is also the first BVD method to explicitly address local over-/under-exposure texture loss alongside global illumination flicker, broadening the task's scope. However, the LFRM's reliance on RAFT optical flow introduces residual edge artifacts and color distortion in heavily exposed regions, and the method's effectiveness on very long-term or highly non-stationary flicker patterns remains untested. Open questions include whether the histogram-prior paradigm can be extended to handle more complex degradations (e.g., combined flicker + noise + compression) and whether learned flow estimation could further improve local texture transfer.
