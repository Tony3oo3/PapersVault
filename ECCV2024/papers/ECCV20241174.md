## Problem

Existing wildlife ReID methods are either tailored to a single species (e.g., whale flukes, seal pelage) or directly transplant person ReID techniques that ignore wildlife-specific discriminative cues. The paper identifies high-frequency image information (fur textures, spot patterns, edge contours) as a universal feature shared across species, but notes that natural environments introduce substantial high-frequency background noise (leaves, grass) that corrupts naive high-frequency extraction.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241174/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a 256×256 RGB wildlife image and simultaneously processes two streams: the original image and a frequency-domain-augmented high-frequency version derived via Fourier transform and Gaussian high-pass filtering. Both streams are patchified at 16×16 resolution and fed through a shared ViT encoder. At inference, only the original-stream class token embedding is used to compute distance matrices for gallery matching; the high-frequency stream is discarded after training.

## Method

Built on an ImageNet-pretrained ViT backbone, the Adaptive High-Frequency Transformer introduces three coupled mechanisms. First, Frequency-Domain Mixed Augmentation (FMA) blends the Gaussian high-pass-filtered spectrum with the original spectrum using a random binary mask (covering α∈[0,0.5] of the area), then inverse-transforms to produce a spatially augmented image that preserves stable features while simulating environmental detail variation. Second, Object-aware Dynamic Selection leverages the class-token attention scores from the final ViT layer to rank all patch tokens and retain only the top Z=μn (μ=0.5) high-frequency tokens, effectively suppressing background noise without requiring bounding boxes. Third, a Feature Equilibrium Loss applies a smooth-L1 penalty between the encoded high-frequency and original token embeddings (weighted by λ=0.1) to prevent the model from overfitting to high-frequency details at the expense of global visual information. Training combines ID and triplet losses on both the original and high-frequency class tokens, optimized with SGD (lr=0.001, cosine decay) over 150 epochs.

## Results

On the unified wildlife ReID benchmark spanning 8 species (terrestrial, aquatic, aerial), the proposed Adaptive High-Frequency Transformer achieves the best mAP across all datasets, with headline values of 44.5 on Panda, 30.4 on Elephant, 51.5 on Seal, 66.3 on Tiger, and 73.8 on Pigeon. It also attains the highest mINP (mean Inverse Negative Penalty) on every dataset, indicating superior performance on the most challenging matches. In the domain generalization setting (trained on Wildlife-71, tested on unseen species), the method reaches 72.3 mAP on Tiger and 78.9 R1 on Seal, outperforming the SOTA DG method UniReID by 5.6 mAP on Tiger.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all four supervised ReID baselines (RotTrans, AGW, TransReID, CLIP-ReID) on mAP across all 8 wildlife datasets, with the largest margins on Elephant (+1.3 over RotTrans) and Seal (+1.4 over TransReID). On mINP, the advantage is more pronounced, e.g., +2.2 over CLIP-ReID on Panda and +2.2 over TransReID on Seal, confirming stronger performance on difficult matches. In the multi-species setting (Table 2), the method beats TransReID by 4.8 mAP on Seal and 3.8 mAP on Elephant, and CLIP-ReID by 7.1 mAP on Elephant. In the domain generalization setting (Table 3), the method surpasses UniReID by 5.6 mAP on Tiger (72.3 vs 66.7) and by 16.3 R1 on Seal (78.9 vs 62.6), though it trails UniReID by 4.5 mAP on Seal (24.0 vs 28.5). The reproduced PHA baseline [51] from person ReID actually degrades performance on Shark (14.8 vs 20.2 baseline), underscoring that naive high-frequency amplification without object-aware selection is harmful in noisy wildlife scenes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the ViT-based ReID baseline (Dosovitskiy et al.) and extends the high-frequency augmentation idea from PHA (Zhang et al., CVPR 2023, originally for person ReID) to the wildlife domain. It departs from the dominant species-specific paradigm in wildlife ReID (panda, whale, seal, tiger methods) by proposing a unified multi-species framework that treats high-frequency information as a universal discriminative signal across all animals. The object-aware dynamic selection strategy is a key conceptual departure from PHA: rather than amplifying all local high-frequency patches, it uses the ViT's class-token attention as a soft mask to suppress background noise, addressing a limitation the authors identify in natural-environment wildlife imagery.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unified multi-species framework and standardized 70/30 identity-split protocol address a real fragmentation problem in wildlife ReID, where prior work used inconsistent partitions making cross-method comparison impossible. The ablation revealing that naive high-frequency augmentation can *degrade* performance (e.g., shark mAP drops from 20.2 to 14.8 with PHA) underscores that environmental noise in wild settings fundamentally changes the high-frequency learning problem compared to controlled person ReID. Open questions include whether the selection ratio μ can be made fully adaptive without per-dataset tuning, and whether the approach generalizes to species with minimal high-frequency texture (e.g., uniformly colored animals) beyond the tested set.
