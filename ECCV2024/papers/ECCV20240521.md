## Problem

Phase shifting profilometry (PSP) assumes a static object, but dynamic measurement violates this assumption, producing ripple-like errors in reconstructed point clouds. Existing motion-error compensation methods all depend on intermediate variables—estimated phase shifts, rigid transform matrices, or non-pixel-wise operators like Hilbert transforms—raising the question of whether the motion-affected phase sequence itself can compensate for error without any such auxiliary quantity.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240521/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a cyclically projected sequence of π/2 phase-shifted high-frequency fringe images captured at 90 fps (640×480) by a main camera, plus a synchronized auxiliary camera image for stereo phase unwrapping. Any K+N successive frames (where N=3 or 4 is the phase-shifting step and K is the binomial order) yield one depth map, making the output frame rate identical to the camera acquisition rate—a frame-wise loopable property that enables quasi-single-shot 3D reconstruction at 90 fps.

## Method

BSC computes motion-affected phase frames from successive captured images via standard 3- or 4-step phase shifting, then performs a pyramid-like weighted summation of K+1 successive phase frames using the (K+1)-th row of binomial coefficients (Yang Hui's triangle), with a custom ⊕ operator to handle wrapped-phase ambiguity at phase jumps. The theoretical analysis shows that the harmonic component of motion error (the source of ripple artifacts) is suppressed by both a 2^-(K+2) amplitude factor and higher-order finite-difference terms in the coefficients, causing error to diminish exponentially with K. The system employs a paraxial binocular structured light setup with a very short camera-camera baseline for unambiguous stereo phase unwrapping while keeping the camera-projector baseline long for accuracy, enabling a single set of high-frequency fringes. The entire pipeline is pixel-wise (preserving PSP's spatial independence at depth discontinuities) and requires no intermediate variables beyond the phase frames themselves, acting as a plug-and-play enhancement over standard four-step PSP.

## Results

On a periodically waving plate measured at 500 mm distance, BSC (8 frames) achieves the lowest RMSE across all motion speeds (−150 to 150 mm/s) compared to four competing methods, as shown in Fig. 5 (log-scale). At a specific speed of −88 mm/s, BSC reduces the motion error from 324.2 μm (traditional four-step PSP) to 54.78 μm, a ~6× improvement. In terms of temporal resolution, BSC achieves a depth-map frame rate of 90 fps (matching the camera acquisition rate), whereas μ-FTP operates at only 30 fps. The mean absolute phase error also decreases exponentially as the binomial order K increases (Fig. 3b), confirming the theoretical prediction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BSC outperforms all four baselines on absolute 3D reconstruction accuracy (RMSE) across the full speed range of −150 to 150 mm/s, with the gap widening at higher speeds (Fig. 5). Against the closest competitor PFS [4] (also pixel-wise, 5 frames), BSC achieves lower RMSE while using 8 frames; against PFD [10] (8 frames, non-pixel-wise), BSC matches the frame budget but wins on both accuracy and robustness at depth discontinuities. Against HTC [24] and μ-FTP [34] (both non-pixel-wise, 4 frames), BSC shows visibly less deformation at depth edges (Fig. 7) and lower RMSE, confirming the advantage of preserving PSP's pixel-wise property. On temporal resolution, BSC matches the camera's 90 fps rate, while μ-FTP is limited to 30 fps due to its non-loopable pattern group, meaning BSC can capture ~3× finer motion dynamics (Fig. 8, butterfly wing flapping). The only trade-off is that BSC requires 8 captured frames per depth map (vs. 4 for HTC/μ-FTP), though the binomial order K can be reduced to 3 (minimum 4 frames) for faster but slightly less accurate reconstruction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the cyclic projection strategy of Zhong et al. [31] for fast phase measurement profilometry, extending it with a paraxial binocular system that shifts the stereo phase unwrapping uniqueness constraint from the camera-projector baseline to the camera-camera baseline. It departs fundamentally from all three existing categories of motion error compensation (phase shifting estimation, object tracking, and automatic compensation via transforms) by eliminating the need for any intermediate variable: instead of estimating motion-induced phase shifts, tracking rigid transforms, or applying Hilbert/Fourier/differential operators, BSC directly sums successive motion-affected phase frames weighted by binomial coefficients. The connection to finite differences in kinematics (displacement, velocity, acceleration) provides a clean theoretical foundation that prior methods lacked.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

BSC achieves a depth map frame rate equal to the camera acquisition rate (90 fps) while maintaining pixel-wise robustness at depth discontinuities—a combination that prior methods could not simultaneously achieve, as non-pixel-wise methods (μ-FTP, HTC) sacrifice spatial independence and non-loopable methods reduce temporal resolution severalfold. The exponential error reduction with binomial order K offers a tunable accuracy-speed trade-off, making it a plug-and-play enhancement for existing four-step PSP pipelines. Open questions include applicability to high-contrast textured objects, robustness to complex surface geometries where block-matching phase unwrapping fails, and the limited speed range imposed by the small phase-shift error assumption.
