## Problem

Existing safety evaluations for Vision LLMs are fragmented, targeting either a single task (hallucination, ethics) or a single input modality (visual or language), leaving no unified assessment of how these models behave under out-of-distribution conditions and adversarial manipulation. This paper asks: how robust are current VLLMs when visual content is unfamiliar, when text instructions are counterfactual, and when either the vision encoder or the language backbone is actively attacked?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241348/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The Unicorn benchmark consumes four input types: (1) OOD images with unusual textures, weather, poses, or shapes paired with template-generated Yes/No or counting questions (4,244 instances in OODCV-VQA); (2) line-drawing sketch images with binary object-identification questions (4,000 instances in Sketchy-VQA); (3) adversarially perturbed images (200 NeurIPS17 images) with standard VQA prompts to measure misleading rate; and (4) toxic text instructions fed to the LLM component to measure jailbreak success. Outputs are scored as F1/accuracy for OOD tasks and attack success rate (ASR) for adversarial tasks, with toxicity of generated text assessed via the Perspective API (threshold 0.5) or GPT-3.5 as a classifier.

## Method

The benchmark comprises two evaluation axes. For OOD generalization, the authors construct OODCV-VQA from the OODCV image corpus using predefined question templates (Yes/No and digit-counting), then create a counterfactual variant that appends textual suffixes flipping the correct answer (e.g., 'if someone has added one X in the scene'); Sketchy-VQA uses 50 frequent object categories from a sketch dataset, with a challenging variant swapping to 50 least-frequent categories. For adversarial robustness, the key technical contribution is Mix.ATTACK: a transferable attack that optimizes a single perturbation ε (bounded by ε₀) on a clean image x to maximize CLIP image-text similarity with a *mixture* of multiple irrelevant target phrases simultaneously, rather than a single misleading object as in prior work (MF-IT). This is trained only on CLIP-ViT-L/14 and evaluated as a black-box transfer attack across 22 VLLMs with heterogeneous vision encoders. The jailbreaking evaluation additionally benchmarks GCG-style gradient-based attacks on the LLM component, comparing vanilla, white-box, and transfer settings between LLMs and their VLLM counterparts to isolate the safety impact of vision-language instruction tuning.

## Results

On the OODCV-VQA benchmark, the best open-source VLLMs (Qwen-VL-Chat, CogVLM) achieve ~76% overall accuracy, while GPT-4V scores 68.76% and Gemini Pro 75.03%. Counterfactual variants cause an average 18.3% drop across open-source models (62.0% → 43.7%), though GPT-4V and Gemini Pro degrade only 4.4% (71.9% → 67.5%). For the misleading attack, the proposed Mix.ATTACK achieves a 64.5% average attack success rate (ε=64/255), outperforming MF-IT (60.1%) by 4.4% and ATTACKBARD (54.4% at ε=32/255). In jailbreaking via language-side attacks, VLLMs show 17.3% higher ASR than their base LLMs under white-box attacks (94.8% vs 77.5% average), confirming that vision-language training weakens safety protocols.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed Mix.ATTACK is compared against two baselines: ATTACKBARD [16] (single-object CLIP-based adversarial perturbation) and MF-IT [59] (transferable VLLM attack). At ε=32/255, Mix.ATTACK achieves a 60.1% average misleading rate, surpassing MF-IT (48.8%) by ~11% and ATTACKBARD (57.8%) by ~2.3%. At the larger ε=64/255 budget, Mix.ATTACK+ reaches 64.5%, outperforming MF-IT by 4.4 percentage points as stated in the paper. Notably, Mix.ATTACK is trained solely on CLIP-ViT-L/14 yet transfers effectively to VLLMs using different vision encoders (EVA-CLIP-ViT-G, ImageBind-ViT, etc.), whereas MF-IT requires model-specific re-implementation. CogVLM is the most robust model against all three attacks (35.0% max ASR), while InternLM-X is the most vulnerable (86.0% under ATTACKBARD). GPT-4V and Gemini Pro largely refuse to answer adversarial inputs, making direct ASR comparison inapplicable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a unifying safety evaluation suite for VLLMs, departing from prior work that addressed safety in fragmented ways—adversarial robustness on the visual side [16, 32, 59], jailbreaking on the language side [51, 64], or hallucination/ethics in isolation [25, 46, 62]. Its adversarial component directly builds on MF-IT [59] by replacing single-object CLIP alignment with a mixed-textual-object strategy (Mix.ATTACK), and benchmarks against ATTACKBARD [16] under matched perturbation budgets. The OOD evaluation repurposes the OODCV image corpus [58] and the counterfactual reasoning paradigm [56] into a VQA format, while the sketch-based OOD setting draws on the Eitz et al. [18] sketch dataset. No prior benchmark simultaneously covers OOD generalization and multi-vector adversarial attacks across both vision and language inputs for VLLMs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that vision-language instruction tuning systematically weakens the safety protocols already present in base LLMs (VLLMs show 5.5–17.3% higher jailbreak ASR than their LLM counterparts) raises an urgent open question: how should safety be re-integrated during the visual instruction tuning stage, given that nearly all current VL training datasets lack safety-focused examples? The observation that a simple CLIP-ViT-L/14-trained perturbation transfers to VLLMs with entirely different vision encoders (EVA-CLIP, ImageBind, Fuyu's no-ViT design) suggests that the CLIP image-text alignment bottleneck is a shared vulnerability across architectures, pointing toward a potential unified defense. A key limitation is that the misleading attack evaluation uses only 200 images and three target objects, and the jailbreaking evaluation relies on a single toxicity classifier (Perspective API or GPT-3.5), leaving open whether conclusions generalize to more diverse attack distributions.
