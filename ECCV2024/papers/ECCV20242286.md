## Problem

Existing debiasing methods for deep learning models require heavy re-training or fine-tuning procedures to remove spurious correlations, raising the question of whether such complexity is truly necessary. This paper asks whether vanilla-trained models already contain 'unbiased sub-networks' that can be extracted in isolation without any additional training of the pruned network, and provides a theoretical framework showing that bias removal does not always improve target-task performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20242286/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a fully trained vanilla model (encoder + classifier head) with frozen parameters and produces a binary pruning mask over the encoder's weights (unstructured) or neurons (structured), yielding a sparser sub-network that no longer propagates bias information to the classifier. The pruned model then takes standard RGB images as input and outputs class labels, with the bias extraction head's accuracy serving as an upper bound on extractable bias information. No fine-tuning of the vanilla model is performed; only the gating parameters that define the mask are learned.

## Method

FFW (Finding Fantastic Weights) attaches a bias extraction head (Privacy Head) at the encoder's bottleneck output and introduces learnable gating parameters on the encoder's weights or neurons, initialized so the pruned model exactly matches the vanilla model at start. The gating parameters are optimized with a combined objective: a standard task loss to preserve classification accuracy plus an empirical mutual information loss between bias labels and the bias head's predictions, which drives the head's output toward a uniform distribution rather than simply minimizing CCE. A straight-through estimator maintains differentiability through the step function, and the temperature τ is iteratively halved until the soft mask converges to a hard binary mask. The key distinction from prior work is that the vanilla model's parameters remain entirely frozen throughout, and the approach provides guarantees on the amount of bias information extractable by the task classifier from the pruned sub-network.

## Results

On Corrupted-CIFAR-10, FFW achieves the highest task accuracy at the hardest bias level (ρ=0.995): 51.57% (unstructured) and 52.02% (structured), surpassing the best baseline uLA (34.39%) by ~17 percentage points. On Biased-MNIST at ρ=0.99, FFW reaches 97.79% task accuracy with only 15.74% bias extractability, matching or exceeding most baselines (BCon+BBal: 98.1%, EnD: 96.0%). On CelebA (BlondHair), FFW attains 87.08% task accuracy with 50.00% bias accuracy, competitive with BCon+BBal (91.4%) and DI (90.9%). On Multi-Color MNIST, FFW improves the worst subgroup (C_L/C_R) from 7.68% (vanilla) to 35.85%, an 11-point gain, though overall unbiased accuracy (36.37%) trails dedicated multi-bias methods like VCBA (73.1%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

On Corrupted-CIFAR-10, FFW clearly dominates at the strongest bias (ρ=0.995), beating uLA by 17 points (51.57% vs. 34.39%) and LfF by 23 points; at lower ρ values (0.99–0.95), uLA pulls ahead (e.g., 74.49% vs. 61.48% at ρ=0.95), likely because the weaker bias is easier for retraining-based methods to correct. On Biased-MNIST, FFW is competitive at ρ=0.99 (97.79% vs. BCon+BBal's 98.1%) but degrades sharply at ρ=0.999 (22.29% vs. BCon+BBal's 94.0%), which the authors attribute to insufficient unbiased training samples for the vanilla model to learn disentangled features. On CelebA, FFW (87.08%) trails BCon+BBal (91.4%) and DI (90.9%) by 3–4 points on BlondHair, but matches vanilla performance on Glasses (98.58% vs. 98.42%) where bias is naturally absent. On Multi-Color MNIST, FFW's overall unbiased accuracy (36.37%) is well below VCBA (73.1%) and DebiAN (72.0%), yet it uniquely improves the worst conflicting subgroup (C_L/C_R) from 7.68% to 35.85%, suggesting it equalizes performance across subgroups at the cost of overall accuracy. Across all datasets, FFW's key differentiator is that it requires no fine-tuning of the vanilla model—only optimization of lightweight gating parameters—while maintaining bias extractability near chance level (~10–17%).

## Relation to Prior Work

FFW builds on two key threads: Tartaglione's privacy head for estimating bias information at the encoder bottleneck [49], and Zayed et al.'s proposal to use structured pruning for fairness-aware model compression [57]. It departs from the dominant debiasing paradigm (supervised, bias-tailored, or unsupervised retraining) by treating the vanilla-trained model as a frozen object from which an unbiased sub-network can be surgically extracted via learned gating masks, drawing on the lottery ticket hypothesis [19] and Zhang et al.'s observation that sub-networks with reduced spurious-feature susceptibility exist [58]. The theoretical framework connecting model biasedness φ, task biasedness K_bia, and mutual information is novel and formalizes why bias removal is not always performance-neutral.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is significant in the era of foundation models: if unbiased sub-networks can be extracted from already-trained models without fine-tuning, the environmental and computational cost of debiasing drops dramatically. However, the results are mixed—FFW struggles at very high bias correlations (ρ=0.999) where the vanilla model has not learned disentangled features, and on Multi-Color MNIST it actually underperforms the vanilla model on average (36.4% vs. 58.9%), suggesting the approach is sensitive to how biases interact. Open questions include whether the method scales to larger architectures and foundation models, whether it generalizes to unknown or multiple interacting biases, and whether the structured sparsity (~45–50%) can be pushed further without task degradation.
