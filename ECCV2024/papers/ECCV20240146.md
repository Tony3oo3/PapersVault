## Problem

Existing adversarial attacks on 3D point clouds, even those employing geometric constraints like curvature or normal/tangential directions, still produce perceptible distortions because they neglect changes in local point distribution uniformity. The human eye readily detects divergence and convergence patterns in point spacing, yet no prior method explicitly measures or controls these uniformity alterations during the attack process.

## Input / Output

The method takes a 3D point cloud of 1,024 surface-sampled points rescaled to a unit cube and produces an adversarial point cloud that misclassifies a target DNN (PointNet, PointNet++, DGCNN, or PointConv). Perturbations are decomposed into per-point directions (unit vectors refined via flux suppression) and magnitudes (determined in a separate optimization step), with local regions of radius 0.1 centered at 20 FPS-selected key points serving as the spatial units for flux computation.

## Method

FLAT models the set of adversarial perturbation vectors as a discrete vector field and introduces a simplified flux metric (Eq. 5) that quantifies divergence/convergence within each local spherical region by summing the radial components of perturbation vectors at their boundary intersections. When a region's flux exceeds a threshold (set to the median across all regions), the method suppresses it via gradient descent on the angles θ_Q between each perturbation direction and the radial vector from the region center (Eq. 6), effectively rotating vectors to reduce net outward/inward flow. The full attack iterates three steps over 8 cycles: (1) generate initial perturbations with IFGM at a fixed magnitude, (2) refine perturbation directions within high-flux regions using the flux-suppression update, and (3) determine final perturbation magnitudes following the ITA protocol. This decoupling of direction and magnitude optimization avoids the collapse toward zero magnitude that simultaneous optimization would produce.

## Results

All methods achieve 100% attack success rate (ASR), so the comparison focuses on imperceptibility metrics (lower is better). On ModelNet40 against PointNet, FLAT requires the smallest perturbation across nearly all metrics: CD=1.539×10⁻⁴, HD=0.371×10⁻², l₂=0.426, GR=0.114, Curv=0.249×10⁻², EMD=0.460×10⁻², outperforming the best baseline ITA (CD=2.747, HD=0.414, l₂=0.534) by 44% on CD and 20% on l₂. Similar advantages hold across PointNet++, DGCNN, and PointConv, as well as on ShapeNet Part. FLAT also requires only 8 iterations versus 15 for the SDM-constrained variant, while maintaining competitive uniformity preservation (SDM=0.097 vs 0.084).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FLAT outperforms all five baselines on the majority of imperceptibility metrics across all four victim models and both datasets. Against the closest competitor ITA (Liu et al., TPAMI 2022), FLAT reduces CD by 44% (1.539 vs 2.747) and l₂ by 20% (0.426 vs 0.534) on PointNet/ModelNet40, while also achieving lower HD (0.371 vs 0.414) and EMD (0.460 vs 1.214). Compared to SI-Adv (Huang et al., CVPR 2022), FLAT cuts CD by 44% (1.539 vs 2.768) and l₂ by 42% (0.426 vs 0.731). The gradient-based baselines IFGM and PGD (Dong et al., CVPR 2020) require substantially larger perturbations—IFGM needs 2.6× the CD of FLAT on PointNet—confirming that unconstrained gradient steps produce visible distortions. GeoA³ (Wen et al., TPAMI 2020) and 3d-Adv (Liu et al., ICIP 2019) are also clearly outperformed, with 3d-Adv requiring 4× the CD of FLAT on PointNet. The only metric where FLAT does not dominate is GR on PointNet++ (0.110 vs GeoA³'s 0.114), a near-tie, and Curv on DGCNN/ShapeNet Part where ITA achieves a slightly lower value (0.112 vs FLAT's 0.135), but FLAT still leads on all other metrics in that setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

FLAT carves new ground by repurposing the physics concept of flux (from fluid mechanics) to address a perceptual gap in 3D point cloud adversarial attacks that prior geometric constraints (l2-norm, Chamfer/Hausdorff distance, curvature, tangential/normal direction guidance) leave unaddressed. Rather than building on a single prior method, it identifies that uniformity alterations—specifically local divergence and convergence of perturbed points—are the dominant perceptible artifact, and introduces a simplified flux measure over the perturbation vector field to detect and suppress these changes. The framework is deliberately modular: it uses IFGM for initial perturbation generation and borrows magnitude determination from ITA, but its core contribution is the flux-based direction refinement step that can be grafted onto any iterative attack.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The flux-based uniformity preservation is a practical, low-cost plug-in (2 seconds vs. 150 seconds for formal continuous-field flux) that consistently improves imperceptibility across four victim classifiers and two datasets, and it generalizes to other attack methods (PGD, IFGM, SI-Adv, ITA) without architectural changes. However, the evaluation is limited to classification on relatively small point clouds (1024 points), and the flux suppression operates as a post-hoc refinement rather than being integrated into the end-to-end optimization, which may limit its effectiveness under tighter perturbation budgets or for more complex 3D tasks like segmentation. Open questions include whether the simplified discrete flux remains adequate for denser point clouds or non-spherical local regions, and whether the approach transfers to video or dynamic point cloud settings.
