## Problem

Current video-language benchmarks cannot faithfully assess temporal understanding because static visual cues (objects, scenes, postures) are highly correlated with temporal information, allowing models to exploit shortcuts rather than genuinely comprehend how things change over time. Prior diagnostic datasets either lack a fine-grained taxonomy of temporal aspects or rely on synthetic/narrow scenarios, making it impossible to pinpoint which specific temporal abilities VidLMs lack.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241877/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each evaluation sample is a (video, correct caption, counterfactual caption) triple where the counterfactual retains identical static content but modifies exactly one of six temporal aspects (Direction, Intensity, Sequence, Localization, Compositionality, Type). The model computes a similarity score between the video and each of the two text descriptions; the prediction is correct if the true caption scores higher. The dataset contains 13k+ samples drawn from open-domain MSR-VTT and VATEX videos, with average caption lengths of ~12–15 words.

## Method

The core contribution is a human-in-the-loop annotation framework that semi-automatically generates high-quality counterfactual descriptions from existing video-text pairs using ChatGPT (gpt-3.5-turbo) with in-context learning. The framework iterates three stages: (1) generation via aspect-specific prompts with demonstrations sampled from positive, negative, and N/A exemplar sets; (2) automatic filtering combining an off-the-shelf NLI model (Sentence-BERT) for entailment checks and a fine-tuned 7-way classifier that requires consistent high-confidence predictions on both sentence orderings; (3) human revision of surviving candidates to correct mislabels. The exemplar sets and filter model are jointly refined across iterations until the filter achieves satisfactory precision on a held-out set, after which large-scale generation (20k per aspect) proceeds without human involvement. Starting from only 231 human-written counterfactuals and ~1500 labeling annotations, the framework yields a 13k+ sample benchmark with 94.8% manual quality-check pass rate.

## Results

On VITATECS, no evaluated model exceeds 70% accuracy on any temporal aspect except 'Type' (74–83%), and on the most temporally demanding aspects (Direction, Intensity, Sequence), all models perform barely above the 50% random baseline. The best-performing model, CLIP4Clip, achieves 65.3% average accuracy, while video LLMs Video-LLaMA (52.0%) and VideoChat (50.4%) are essentially at chance level. Human annotators achieve 94.3% average accuracy with high inter-annotator agreement (κ=0.86), highlighting a large gap between model and human temporal understanding.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CLIP-based temporally-adapted models (CLIP4Clip 65.3%, X-CLIP 65.2%, X-Pool 65.1%) marginally outperform video-text pre-training models (VIOLET 64.7%, Singularity 62.4%, ALPRO 58.2%), but the paper shows via ablation (Table 4) that removing temporal aggregation modules does not significantly hurt performance, indicating the advantage comes from better static-clue exploitation rather than genuine temporal understanding. Pure image-text model BLIP-large (64.1%) performs comparably to video-specific models, further confirming that video-text pre-training adds little temporal capability. Video LLMs (Video-LLaMA 52.0%, VideoChat 50.4%) perform at or below random chance on most aspects, suggesting their text encoders fail to capture temporal distinctions in natural language. Across all nine baselines, no model surpasses 70% on Direction, Intensity, or Sequence, while the 'Type' aspect (least temporally demanding) reaches 74–83%, confirming that models rely on static object/action cues rather than temporal reasoning. The 29–44 percentage-point gap between the best model (65.3%) and humans (94.3%) underscores the severity of the temporal understanding deficit.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the Winoground evaluation paradigm (distinguishing a correct description from a counterfactual one) to the video domain, but departs from prior temporal-understanding datasets (CATER, CLEVRER, SSv2) by operating on open-domain videos and introducing a six-aspect taxonomy (Direction, Intensity, Sequence, Localization, Compositionality, Type) that prior works lacked. For dataset construction, it builds on the growing line of LLM-assisted data generation (in-context learning with ChatGPT) and human-in-the-loop filtering, but applies this pipeline specifically to generate temporally-targeted counterfactuals rather than general NLI or VQA data. The paper positions itself as addressing a gap identified by multiple prior works (Sevilla-Lara et al., Lei et al., Park et al.) that noted models' insensitivity to temporal information, but argues those works either used synthetic scenes, focused on single actions, or failed to properly categorize temporal aspects.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that state-of-the-art video-language models perform barely above chance on most temporal aspects, and that the apparent superiority of CLIP-based models over video-text pre-trained models stems from static information exploitation rather than genuine temporal understanding. A key open question is how to actually train models to capture temporal concepts, since the paper shows that existing video-language training data is insufficient and that text encoders lose temporal sensitivity during image-text pre-training. Limitations include the relatively small dataset size (~13k samples), the binary evaluation format (correct vs. one counterfactual), and the reliance on LLM-generated counterfactuals whose quality, while validated, may not fully capture the diversity of real-world temporal expressions. The six-aspect taxonomy, while covering 98% of sampled temporal information, may not be exhaustive for all video domains.
