## Problem

Existing instance-level image retrieval methods assume objects are sharp and static, yet in real-world scenarios objects frequently appear motion-blurred due to fast movement, low frame rates, or long exposure times. No prior method or dataset addresses the task of retrieving objects when the query or database images contain motion blur, leaving a critical gap for applications like wildlife monitoring, sports analysis, and surveillance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20242249/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method accepts a single RGB image (3×H×W) containing a sharp or motion-blurred object against an arbitrary background and outputs a 128-dimensional L2-normalized descriptor vector used for nearest-neighbor search. The descriptor is designed to be invariant to the degree of motion blur (discretized into 6 blur levels), so a heavily blurred query should retrieve sharp matches and vice versa. During training, the BLISS mechanism additionally consumes the query's blur level to select contrastive samples within a specified blur-level range.

## Method

The BRIDGE module encodes images via a ResNet-50 backbone followed by GeM pooling, then routes the resulting feature vector through three parallel heads—blur estimation (predicting continuous blur severity from the alpha channel), localization (bounding-box regression), and classification (ArcFace margin loss)—whose outputs are concatenated and projected to the final 128-d descriptor. The key training innovation is the BLISS mechanism, which selects contrastive positive and negative samples whose blur levels fall within a range r of the query's blur level, thereby controlling the blur diversity seen during contrastive learning. Blur severity is formally defined from the image formation model (Eq. 3–5) using the eroded alpha mask, and the blur estimation loss forces the network to implicitly learn signal-to-noise separation between foreground object and background. The joint loss combines contrastive, ArcFace classification, blur estimation, and localization terms, and the method generalizes to real-world data without fine-tuning despite being trained entirely on synthetic Blender-rendered images.

## Results

On the synthetic dataset (no distractors), the proposed method achieves 91.78 mAP overall, outperforming Token (84.84), DOLG (83.03), and DELG (81.65). With 1M distractors, the gap widens: Ours reaches 84.09 mAP versus Token at 70.65, DOLG at 69.97, and DELG at 68.19. On the real-world dataset (trained only on synthetic data), Ours achieves 62.88 mAP, surpassing DELG (54.82), DOLG (54.64), and Token (43.33). The method also exhibits the smallest standard deviation across blur levels (15.65 vs. 16.11–16.93 for baselines), confirming superior robustness to varying motion blur severity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all three state-of-the-art baselines (DELG, DOLG, Token) across every evaluation setting. On the synthetic dataset with 1M distractors, Ours achieves 84.09 mAP, beating the strongest baseline Token by 13.44 points (70.65) and DELG by 15.90 points (68.19). On the real-world dataset, the advantage is 8.06 points over DELG (62.88 vs. 54.82) and a striking 19.55 points over Token (62.88 vs. 43.33), demonstrating strong cross-domain generalization from synthetic to real data. The ablation (Ours-sharp, trained only on sharp images) collapses to 27.57 mAP on synthetic and 40.24 on real data, confirming that blur-specific training data and losses are essential rather than optional. The method also achieves the lowest standard deviation across blur levels (15.65 vs. 16.11–16.93 for baselines in Table 4), indicating it degrades most gracefully as blur severity increases.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a new problem space by repurposing the standard global retrieval pipeline (ResNet-50 backbone + GeM pooling + contrastive/ArcFace training) for a task no prior work has addressed: instance-level retrieval of motion-blurred objects. Rather than building on a specific prior method, it augments the well-established retrieval architecture with two novel components — a multi-head BRIDGE module (blur estimation, localization, classification) and a BLISS mechanism for blur-level-aware contrastive sample selection — and introduces blur-specific loss terms (blur estimation loss, localization loss) alongside the standard contrastive and ArcFace losses. The paper explicitly positions itself against the gap in the literature where all existing retrieval methods (DELG, DOLG, Token) assume sharp, static objects, and against the broader blur-handling literature (deblurring, blur detection) which addresses different tasks entirely.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The primary practical contribution is the first benchmark for blur-robust retrieval, comprising 1.5M synthetic images across 1,138 object instances and 13K real-world images with manually annotated blur levels, which provides a foundation for future work in this area. The finding that standard SOTA retrieval methods degrade substantially (e.g., Token drops from 86.53 to 71.57 mAP between BL 1 and BL 6) while the proposed method maintains much tighter performance (93.05 to 82.86) validates that blur-specific training is necessary rather than optional. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to other blur types (camera shake, defocus), whether it extends to video retrieval, and how it scales to larger, more diverse real-world collections beyond the 35-object real dataset. The synthetic-to-real generalization (62.88 mAP on real data trained only on synthetic) is promising but tested on a relatively small and controlled real dataset.
