## Problem

Recent RIS models exhibit a large performance gap between easy and hard scenarios, struggling to distinguish among visually similar objects when the referring expression requires fine-grained understanding. The authors identify the training data as the bottleneck: existing benchmarks contain many over-simplified examples solvable by merely locating the referred class, providing insufficient exposure to the ambiguous, multi-object scenarios that define the true difficulty of the task.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241839/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, the model receives a single RGB image and a natural-language referring expression and outputs a binary segmentation mask for the target object. During training with NeMo, the input is instead a 2×2 mosaic (the original image plus three retrieved negatives) resized to half the original width and height; the ground-truth mask is placed only in the positive quadrant while the other three quadrants are labeled as background (all-negative). The augmentation is applied with probability γ, so the model also sees original single-image samples to match the inference-time format.

## Method

NeMo constructs each augmented sample by retrieving three negative images from the full training pool using CLIP cross-modal similarity between the referring expression and candidate images. Two hyperparameters control difficulty: an upper-bound threshold τ filters out images whose text-image similarity exceeds τ (preventing false negatives where the negative image contains an object perfectly matching the query), and a pool size K limits selection to the top-K most relevant remaining images (preventing the mosaic from becoming trivially easy). Three images are randomly drawn from this filtered pool and arranged with the positive image in a 2×2 grid. The key insight is that the difficulty must be calibrated to a 'Goldilocks' zone—neither too ambiguous (multiple valid targets) nor too trivial (irrelevant distractors)—and that this sweet spot differs across datasets (e.g., K=200 for G-Ref vs. K=800 for RefCOCO). The method is model-agnostic and requires no additional annotations, making it a drop-in augmentation for any supervised RIS pipeline.

## Results

NeMo consistently improves all five evaluated RIS models (LAVT, CRIS, ReLA, CGFormer, VPD) across four datasets, with average oIoU gains of +1.11 on RefCOCO, +1.21 on RefCOCO+, +1.55 on G-Ref, and +3.11 on GRES. On G-Ref with CRIS, NeMo achieves 58.47 oIoU (Val) and 59.07 oIoU (Test), compared to 55.91/58.50 without augmentation. The largest relative gains appear on more complex datasets (G-Ref, GRES), where longer queries and denser scenes benefit most from the curated visual ambiguity introduced by the mosaic.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NeMo decisively outperforms all three compared augmentation methods on G-Ref. Against YOLOv4's random mosaic, NeMo gains +2.25 oIoU (Val) and +3.07 P@0.5, while YOLOv4 itself only marginally improves oIoU (+0.31) but actually degrades Precision metrics. Against CutMix, NeMo leads by +1.97 oIoU (Val) and +3.49 P@0.7; CutMix similarly shows negligible oIoU gain (+0.59) but drops Precision. Against MixGen, the gap is largest: NeMo surpasses it by +4.85 oIoU (Val) and +5.64 P@0.5, whereas MixGen substantially degrades performance below the no-augmentation baseline (−2.29 oIoU Val). The paper attributes the failures of YOLOv4 and CutMix to referent loss or occlusion after cropping/overlaying, and MixGen's failure to difficulty in understanding the full scene during interpolation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant RIS research trajectory of architectural innovation (encoder-decoder designs, cross-modal fusion, transformer backbones) by identifying a data-side bottleneck: existing benchmarks under-represent visually ambiguous, multi-object scenarios that require fine-grained linguistic discrimination. Conceptually, NeMo repurposes the mosaic augmentation from YOLOv4 (object detection) and the retrieval-augmented paradigm from CLIP-based vision-language models, but introduces a difficulty-calibrated negative mining strategy specific to the referring segmentation setting. It sits at the intersection of data augmentation for segmentation and retrieval-augmented generation, yet is the first to systematically study how the *level* of visual ambiguity in augmented samples affects RIS learning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a simple, model-agnostic augmentation can consistently close part of the easy-hard performance gap across five architectures and four benchmarks, suggesting that data complexity—not just model capacity—is a limiting factor in RIS. The finding that other multi-modal augmentations (YOLOv4 mosaic, CutMix, MixGen) actually *degrade* RIS performance highlights that generic augmentation strategies are ill-suited to tasks requiring holistic scene understanding. Limitations include reduced effectiveness on domain-diverse image collections (e.g., mixing X-rays with natural photos), and the method's reliance on CLIP's retrieval quality for negative selection. Future work on object-level parsing for retrieval and adaptive difficulty scheduling could push gains further.
