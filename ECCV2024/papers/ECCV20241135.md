## Problem

Existing NLOS imaging methods require dense sampling, long acquisition times, and large scan apertures to produce high-quality reconstructions, making them impractical for real-world deployment. When samplings are reduced by 16× or 64× and apertures shrunk by 4×, prior signal-recovery and denoising approaches fail due to resolution limits, amplified Poisson noise, and over-smoothing caused by attempting to recover the entire frequency spectrum rather than the band-limited range containing informative signals.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241135/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The network receives noisy partial impulse responses H(x_p→x_c, t) subsampled from full 64×64 measurements (down to 16×16 or 8×8 points) and corrupted with Poisson noise following a SPAD sensor model. These are convolved in the frequency domain with 7 illumination phasor functions at different wavelengths to produce multi-band band-limited inputs (real and imaginary parts concatenated). The output is the enhanced phasor wavefront at the virtual aperture in the frequency domain, which is then propagated via the 2D FFT-based Rayleigh-Sommerfeld Diffraction algorithm to yield a 64×64×64 hidden volume.

## Method

LEAP couples a denoising autoencoder with a phasor-based frequency constraint: the network's inputs and outputs are phasor wavefronts at the aperture (band-limited by the Gaussian illumination function), which restricts learning to the frequency range where informative signals concentrate and discards spurious low- and high-frequency components. The architecture is a 3D residual CNN that extracts spatial-temporal features from the multi-wavelength phasor inputs, transforms them to the frequency domain, and predicts frequency-domain residuals added to upsampled inputs to recover clean full measurements. Training minimizes L1 loss only over the frequency band S = [Ω_C − ΔΩ, Ω_C + ΔΩ] defined by the target wavelength, explicitly confining the network's operations to the informative spectrum. This phasor-based supervision prevents the spectral bias toward low frequencies that causes over-smoothing in naive denoising autoencoders, while the denoising criterion provides noise robustness absent in prior signal-recovery networks like SSN.

## Results

On the synthetic ShapeNet dataset, LEAP achieves the best PSNR and RMSE across all four evaluation scenarios: 32.02 dB / 0.0892 RMSE (Conf-16), 28.07 dB / 0.0962 RMSE (Conf-8), 28.31 dB / 0.0969 RMSE (Conf-small), and 37.45 dB / 0.1414 RMSE (Non-16). The method outperforms the strongest baseline USM by 2.03 dB PSNR and reduces RMSE by 54% in the Conf-16 setting, and by 2.31 dB PSNR in the Non-16 setting. On real-world Stanford measurements, LEAP enables NLOS reconstruction with 16× or 64× fewer samplings and 4× smaller apertures, producing clean results with fine details (e.g., rear wheel of Bike, head of Dragon) where all baselines fail.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LEAP outperforms all seven baselines on PSNR and RMSE across all four scenarios. Against the closest competitor USM [19] (which adds a signal recovery network to LFE), LEAP gains +2.03 dB PSNR and reduces RMSE from 0.2699→0.0892 (Conf-16) and from 0.1798→0.1414 (Non-16), while also matching or exceeding USM's SSIM in 3 of 4 settings. SSN [42], which performs signal super-resolution without denoising, is particularly vulnerable to Poisson noise and produces severe artifacts, scoring 8.75 dB lower than LEAP in Conf-16. LFE [6] and RSD with interpolation (nearest/linear) fail to produce meaningful reconstructions under sparse sampling, with PSNR values 9–17 dB below LEAP. On real-world Stanford measurements (Bike, Dragon), LEAP is the only method that reconstructs fine details (rear wheel, head) under 16×16 and 8×8 sparse sampling, while SSCR [24] misses large portions of objects and FK [21]/LCT [28] yield only coarse, artifact-laden shapes. In the low-noise non-confocal Resolution dataset, all signal-recovery-based methods (USM, SSN, LEAP) perform comparably, confirming that LEAP's advantage is most pronounced under high-noise, short-exposure conditions.

## Relation to Prior Work

LEAP sits at the intersection of three research threads: phasor-field NLOS imaging (which formulates reconstruction as wave propagation through a virtual aperture), signal super-resolution networks like SSN that recover missing measurements, and denoising autoencoders for noise-robust representation learning. The key conceptual departure is using the aperture phasor field—naturally band-limited by the Gaussian illumination function—as the input/output space for the enhancement network, rather than operating on raw impulse responses. This implicitly constrains the network's frequency spectrum to the informative band, avoiding the over-smoothing that plagues naive denoising in the full measurement space. Compared to optimization-based few-shot methods (SSCR) and hardware-heavy SPAD array approaches, LEAP achieves practical NLOS imaging with negligible additional cost using a lightweight 3D residual CNN.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: enabling NLOS imaging with 16×–64× fewer samplings and 4× smaller apertures without additional hardware or optimization loops makes the technology more deployable in real-world settings like rescue operations or autonomous driving. The frequency-management insight—that the phasor field's natural band-limiting is the right inductive bias, and that naive denoising in the full spectrum is counterproductive due to spectral bias—offers a transferable principle for other inverse problems with known spectral structure. Limitations include reliance on ShapeNet synthetic data for training (constraining generalization to real-world object diversity) and the single-virtual-source assumption. Future directions noted by the authors include incorporating diffusion-based generative priors and more realistic NLOS renderers.
