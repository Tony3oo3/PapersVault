## Problem

Existing 3D panoptic segmentation methods are limited to closed-set categories and cannot predict labels for unseen objects in real-world driving scenes. While 2D open-vocabulary segmentation has made progress using frozen CLIP backbones, the authors show that simply extending these 2D approaches to 3D yields poor performance, especially for novel stuff classes, due to the camera-LiDAR domain gap and sparse pixel-point correspondences. No prior method addresses 3D open-vocabulary panoptic segmentation, which requires handling both novel things and novel stuff simultaneously.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a LiDAR point cloud and synchronized multi-camera images per scene, with LiDAR points voxelized and projected onto camera planes via intrinsic/extrinsic calibration to index per-pixel CLIP features (zero-padded where no valid pixel exists). At inference, the model also receives CLIP text embeddings for all category names (base + novel) and produces per-point panoptic predictions: a semantic class label and, for things classes, an instance ID. Classification is performed by a single head that regresses a class embedding in CLIP space, compared via cosine similarity against text embeddings rather than using a fixed per-class score vector.

## Method

The architecture fuses a learnable LiDAR encoder with a frozen OpenSeg CLIP vision encoder at the voxel level (concatenation), feeding the combined features into a P3Former-style transformer decoder with separate fixed queries for base stuff and learnable queries for things plus all novel classes. Two novel distillation losses drive open-vocabulary capability: an object-level loss (L_O) that forces predicted class embeddings of matched queries to align with mask-pooled CLIP vision features, and a voxel-level loss (L_V) that reconstructs per-voxel CLIP features as a weighted sum of all query embeddings and supervises them with L1 loss, providing gradient signal to regions lacking ground-truth labels. The voxel-level loss is particularly critical for novel stuff classes, where the baseline PFC collapses to near-zero PQ, while the object-level loss improves novel things recognition by removing the need for an ensemble of classifiers. Training uses focal classification loss, mask loss, and the two distillation losses with weights (1, 1, 1, 0.1) on nuScenes and SemanticKITTI.

## Results

On nuScenes (B12/N4 split), the proposed method achieves PQ of 62.0, PQ_N^Th of 49.6, PQ_N^St of 35.2, and mIoU of 60.1, substantially outperforming the PFC baseline (PQ 54.8, PQ_N^St 0.5, mIoU 55.5). On SemanticKITTI (B14/N5 split), the method reaches PQ of 42.2 and mIoU of 44.6, again significantly exceeding the PFC baseline (PQ 33.7, mIoU 33.4). The largest gains are on novel stuff classes, where PQ_N^St jumps from 0.5 to 35.2 on nuScenes and from 0.4 to 17.8 on SemanticKITTI, demonstrating the critical role of the voxel-level distillation loss.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the PFC baseline (P3Former + FC-CLIP fusion), the proposed method wins on every reported metric across both datasets. On nuScenes, PQ improves from 54.8 to 62.0 (+7.2), novel-things PQ from 37.3 to 49.6 (+12.3), and novel-stuff PQ from 0.5 to 35.2 (+34.7); on SemanticKITTI, PQ rises from 33.7 to 42.2 and mIoU from 33.4 to 44.6. The closed-set P3Former (full supervision) still leads on overall PQ (75.9 vs. 62.0 on nuScenes), but this comparison is intentionally unfair; the gap is concentrated in recognition quality (RQ) for novel classes where no ground-truth supervision exists, while segmentation quality (SQ) is comparable (87.0 vs. 89.8). Against the zero-shot OpenScene method, the proposed model achieves mIoU of 60.1 versus 42.1 on nuScenes, a 18-point advantage, though this comparison is also asymmetric since the proposed method uses partial labels. The ablation in Table 4 confirms that the voxel-level distillation loss L_V contributes the single largest gain (+5.7 PQ), particularly for novel stuff classes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on P3Former's transformer-based 3D panoptic segmentation architecture and FC-CLIP's frozen-CLIP single-embedding-head design for open-vocabulary classification, but departs from both by introducing multimodal LiDAR-CLIP feature fusion and two distillation losses (object-level and voxel-level) to bridge the camera-LiDAR domain gap. It extends the 3D open-vocabulary segmentation line (OpenScene, RegionPLC, PLA, CLIP2Scene), which had only addressed semantic or instance segmentation in isolation, to the full panoptic setting that handles novel things and novel stuff simultaneously. Unlike prior 3D open-vocabulary methods that rely on captioning models or contrastive learning, this work depends solely on pre-trained CLIP features and a learned LiDAR encoder, avoiding additional training data or auxiliary models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first method to tackle 3D open-vocabulary panoptic segmentation, filling a clear gap where prior work handled only semantic or instance segmentation separately. The dramatic failure of the naive 2D-to-3D extension for novel stuff classes (PQ near zero) and the subsequent recovery via voxel-level distillation (PQ from 0.2 to 35.2) highlight a fundamental challenge in transferring vision-language features to sparse 3D sensing. However, a substantial performance gap to closed-set methods remains (62.0 vs 75.9 PQ on nuScenes), and the authors note that current autonomous driving benchmarks have limited category diversity, making it difficult to fully assess open-vocabulary generalization. A large-scale benchmark with richer category annotations is needed to validate whether these techniques scale beyond the current evaluation setting.
