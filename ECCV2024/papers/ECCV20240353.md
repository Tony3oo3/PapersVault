## Problem

Existing compositionality benchmarks for vision-language models only probe whether models can reject hard negatives (semantically altered captions), but never test whether models remain invariant to hard positives (semantically equivalent captions). This omission means that reported improvements from hard negative finetuning are overstated: models become oversensitive to perturbations, incorrectly penalizing captions that preserve meaning, with performance drops up to 38.7% when hard positives are included in evaluation.

## Input / Output

The evaluation presents each image with three captions: the original caption c, a hard negative c_n (a single-word swap or replacement that changes meaning), and a hard positive c_p (a single-word swap or replacement that preserves meaning). The model outputs an image-text matching score for each caption, from which two metrics are derived: Augmented Test Accuracy (fraction where both c and c_p outscore c_n) and Brittleness (fraction where the model ranks one of {c, c_p} above c_n and the other below, indicating oversensitivity). The training set comprises 1,775,259 image-text pairs from 591,753 COCO-train captions, each augmented with one hard positive and one hard negative.

## Method

The paper introduces a two-part contribution: a new evaluation dataset of 56,191 images with 28,748 swap and 27,443 replace hard positives (sourced from VL-Checklist and the Attribute-Relation-Order benchmark), and a finetuning procedure that simultaneously trains CLIP ViT-B/32 on both hard negatives and hard positives. Hard positives are generated by prompting LLaMA-2 70B-Chat to make semantics-preserving modifications (synonym replacements or word-order swaps retaining meaning) to COCO-train captions, while hard negatives are generated via the CREPE process. The key insight is that hard-negative-only finetuning reduces the learning problem to perturbation detection — the model learns that any perturbation changes the label — whereas adding hard positives forces the model to learn when perturbations do and do not change meaning. The finetuning follows the SVLC procedure, and ablations show that the ratio of hard negative to hard positive loss weights critically affects the tradeoff between test accuracy and brittleness.

## Results

On the proposed hard-positive evaluation, CLIP ViT-B/32 drops from 61.6% to 46.8% Augmented Test Accuracy on REPLACE (−14.9 pts) and from 60.5% to 49.6% on SWAP (−10.9 pts) when hard positives are included. Hard-negative-finetuned models suffer even larger drops, up to −38.7 pts (DAC-LLM on REPLACE), while the authors' HP+HN model achieves 58.0% Aug. Test Acc. on REPLACE and 61.1% on SWAP with the lowest brittleness (16.9% REPLACE, 22.9% SWAP) among all finetuned models. Human annotators achieve 97–100% Aug. Test Accuracy with 0% brittleness, highlighting a large remaining gap.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the CLIP ViT-B/32 baseline, the HP+HN model improves Augmented Test Accuracy by 11.2 pts on REPLACE (58.0 vs 46.8) and 11.5 pts on SWAP (61.1 vs 49.6), while reducing REPLACE brittleness from 23.2% to 16.9%. Against hard-negative-only finetuned baselines, HP+HN wins on Aug. Test Accuracy across all six compared methods (e.g., 58.0 vs 48.9 for DAC-LLM on REPLACE, 58.0 vs 44.5 for SVLC) and achieves the lowest REPLACE brittleness (16.9%) among all finetuned models. On SWAP, HP+HN slightly trails SVLC in Aug. Test Accuracy (61.1 vs 61.6) but matches it in brittleness (22.9 vs 20.8) and outperforms NegCLIP, CREPE variants, and DAC-SAM. Notably, DAC-LLM achieves the highest Original Test Accuracy on REPLACE (87.6%) yet suffers the largest collapse when hard positives are added (−38.7 pts), demonstrating that hard-negative benchmark gains do not translate to true compositional understanding. The HP+HN model also preserves original-caption matching scores (mean 0.232 vs CLIP's 0.234), unlike DAC-LLM which drops them to 0.160, avoiding a critical failure mode for downstream retrieval and scoring applications.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the compositionality evaluation line initiated by CREPE, VL-Checklist, and the Attribute-Relation-Order benchmark, which all test whether CLIP-style models can distinguish an original caption from a hard negative (a semantic-changing perturbation). It departs from this lineage by introducing hard positives—semantic-preserving perturbations—as a necessary complement, arguing that a truly compositional model must be sensitive to meaning-changing edits while remaining invariant to meaning-preserving ones. The finetuning experiments build directly on NegCLIP, CREPE, SVLC, and DAC, which all use hard-negative-only augmentation, and the proposed training procedure follows the SVLC finetuning recipe while adding LLaMA-2-generated hard positives alongside CREPE-generated hard negatives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that hard negative finetuning—now a standard recipe for improving CLIP compositionality—actually degrades performance on hard positives by up to 38.7% challenges a widely held assumption in the field and calls into question whether current models possess a grounded sense of relational semantics at all. The released 1.77M-example training set and 112K-example evaluation set provide a concrete resource for future work, but the paper is limited to CLIP-style contrastive models and only two perturbation types (swap and replace), leaving open how the findings generalize to generative VL models (Flamingo, BLIP, GPT-4V) and to more diverse perturbation families. The gap between model performance (~58%) and human performance (99%) on the augmented evaluation suggests that compositionality in vision-language models remains far from solved.
