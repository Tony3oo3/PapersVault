## Problem

3D Gaussian Splatting optimises each Gaussian's outgoing radiance independently as spherical harmonics, which fails to capture complex view-dependent effects such as specular highlights and inter-object reflections. Because individual Gaussians are optimised in isolation without modelling shared scene illumination or material reflectance, rendered surfaces show inconsistent colour changes across viewing angles, particularly on reflective and glossy materials.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240359/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes multi-view RGB images with known camera poses and a sparse SfM point cloud for Gaussian initialisation, and produces photorealistic novel-view renderings at real-time rasterisation speed. The illumination field is stored as a coarse 150³ voxel factorised tensor grid (87.5% fewer voxels than TensoRF), and each Gaussian carries 16 BRDF feature channels (repurposed from 3DGS's 16×3 SH coefficients) plus 4 additional parameters for base colour and roughness-based IDE view-direction encoding.

## Method

3iGS decomposes each Gaussian's outgoing radiance into a constant diffused colour and a view-dependent specular colour, where the specular component is produced by a small neural network F that maps the Gaussian's BRDF features, a continuous incident illumination field, and the viewing direction (encoded via IDE) to a specular colour. The illumination field is represented as a vector-matrix factorised tensor grid (analogous to TensoRF's VM decomposition) that is jointly optimised end-to-end with the Gaussians; a Gaussian's mean coordinate is interpolated against this grid to retrieve its local illumination features. Rather than predicting physical material parameters (roughness, albedo, metallicity), BRDF features are treated as learned weights that modulate the incoming illumination, following a generalised spherical-harmonic convolution formulation. Training proceeds in two phases—first 3,000 iterations optimise only the diffused colour, then specular components are introduced—and the illumination grid is shrunk and resampled mid-training to tighten around the scene. The loss is a weighted combination of L1 and D-SSIM (λ=0.2) over 30,000 iterations, with the same adaptive Gaussian control as 3DGS.

## Results

On the NeRF Synthetic (Blender) dataset, 3iGS achieves an average PSNR of 33.64, SSIM of 0.970, and LPIPS of 0.029, surpassing 3DGS (33.30/0.969/0.030) and GaussianShader (33.38/0.968/0.029). On the Tanks and Temples real-world dataset, 3iGS reaches 30.20 PSNR, 0.953 SSIM, and 0.058 LPIPS, outperforming 3DGS (29.61/0.950/0.060) by a clear margin. On the Shiny Blender dataset, 3iGS (30.77 PSNR) trails GaussianShader (31.94 PSNR) but still beats 3DGS (30.37 PSNR). In terms of speed, 3iGS renders at 2.0× slower than 3DGS and trains at 3.2× slower, while GaussianShader is 6.3× slower at test and 12.1× slower at training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against 3DGS [17], 3iGS consistently wins across all three datasets: +0.34 PSNR on Blender, +0.40 PSNR on Tanks and Temples, and +0.40 PSNR on Shiny Blender, with matching or better SSIM and LPIPS in every case. Against GaussianShader [13] (reported numbers), 3iGS wins on the Blender dataset (33.64 vs 33.38 PSNR) and Tanks and Temples (30.20 vs 28.46 PSNR reproduced), but loses on Shiny Blender (30.77 vs 31.94 PSNR), which the authors attribute to Shiny Blender's simpler single-object geometry and direct environment-map lighting favouring GaussianShader's global cube-map approach. Against ray-tracing baselines (NeRF, Ref-NeRF, ENVIDR), 3iGS achieves substantially higher PSNR (33.64 vs 31.01–31.29 on Blender) while maintaining real-time rendering speed, whereas these methods require days of training and cannot render interactively. The reproduced GaussianShader results (31.48 PSNR on Blender) are notably lower than the originally reported numbers (33.38), suggesting sensitivity to implementation details. Overall, 3iGS is the best-performing real-time rasterisation method on complex multi-object scenes with indirect lighting, while GaussianShader retains an edge on simple single-object scenes dominated by direct illumination.

## Relation to Prior Work

3iGS extends 3D Gaussian Splatting by replacing per-Gaussian spherical harmonic radiance with a factorised tensorial illumination field (borrowed from TensoRF's vector-matrix decomposition) and learnable BRDF features mapped through a small neural renderer. It departs from physically-based inverse rendering approaches like GaussianShader and GIR, which predict interpretable material parameters (roughness, albedo, metallicity) via simplified BRDF models, instead treating BRDF as general neural features that modulate a shared continuous illumination field. The viewing-direction encoding via Integrated Directional Encoding (IDE) from Ref-NeRF is repurposed for the Gaussian splatting rasterisation pipeline, and the overall design draws conceptual inspiration from game-engine irradiance volumes and light probes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

3iGS addresses a well-known weakness of 3DGS—poor specular and view-dependent effects—while preserving real-time rasterisation speed (only 2× slower than vanilla 3DGS, versus 6.3× for GaussianShader), making it a practical upgrade for interactive applications. The use of a coarse 150³ factorised tensor grid (87.5% fewer voxels than TensoRF) for the illumination field is a compact and efficient design choice that keeps memory overhead low relative to the Gaussian count. Open questions include extending the bounded tensor grid to unbounded scenes, reducing the VRAM footprint inherited from 3DGS, and whether the approach generalises to dynamic or relightable settings. The modest but consistent gains across synthetic and real-world datasets suggest the illumination-field formulation is a sound direction, though the improvements remain incremental rather than transformative.
