## Problem

Zero-shot anomaly detection (ZSAD) must identify anomalies in images from entirely unseen categories without any training samples from those categories. Existing ZSAD methods either rely on hand-crafted textual prompts without adaptation (WinCLIP) or employ only static learnable prompts that struggle to capture diverse anomaly distributions across categories. The paper addresses how to maximally exploit annotated auxiliary anomaly-detection data to adapt a pre-trained vision-language model for robust, generalizable ZSAD.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240919/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image (resized to 518×518) and a category name embedded in textual captions (e.g., 'A photo of normal [CLS]' / 'A photo of damaged [CLS]') as input. It produces two outputs: a scalar image-level anomaly score in [0,1] and a pixel-level anomaly map of the same spatial resolution as the input. Patch embeddings are extracted from four intermediate layers (6th, 12th, 18th, 24th) of the ViT-L/14@336px backbone and aggregated in a multi-hierarchy manner for the final anomaly map.

## Method

AdaCLIP replaces the original transformer layers in CLIP's image and text encoders with prompting layers that concatenate learnable hybrid prompts (static + dynamic) to the vanilla input tokens. Static prompts are shared across all images and learned from auxiliary annotated data, while dynamic prompts are generated per test image by a Dynamic Prompt Generator (frozen CLIP backbone + learnable linear projection), enabling per-image adaptation. A projection layer aligns patch embedding dimensions with text embeddings, and pixel-level anomaly maps are computed via softmax-normalized cosine similarities between patch and text embeddings across multiple layers. For image-level scoring, a Hybrid Semantic Fusion (HSF) module clusters patch embeddings with KMeans, identifies the most anomalous cluster by averaging anomaly-map scores at corresponding positions, and aggregates its centroids into a semantic-rich embedding for robust image-level classification. Training uses only 4.6% additional parameters (40.7 MB) with dice and focal losses on auxiliary data for 5 epochs, preserving CLIP's pre-trained weights throughout.

## Results

On the industrial domain (7 datasets), AdaCLIP achieves average image-level AUROC/max-F1 of 90.2/89.6 and pixel-level AUROC/max-F1 of 94.2/50.2, outperforming the second-best method (APRIL-GAN) by 3.7% and 3.3% on max-F1 respectively. On the medical domain (7 datasets), AdaCLIP reaches 94.6/89.6 (image-level) and 85.3/57.4 (pixel-level), again ranking first on average. The method uses only 4.6% additional parameters (40.7 MB) on top of the 890.8 MB ViT-L/14@336px CLIP backbone, trained for just 5 epochs with a learning rate of 0.01.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AdaCLIP outperforms all five baselines on average across both industrial and medical domains. Against the closest competitor APRIL-GAN, AdaCLIP gains +3.7% image-level max-F1 (89.6 vs 85.9) and +3.3% pixel-level max-F1 (50.2 vs 46.9) in the industrial domain, and +0.6% / +2.8% respectively in the medical domain. The untrained baselines WinCLIP and SAA lag substantially behind, with WinCLIP achieving only 18.8% pixel-level max-F1 on average in the industrial domain versus AdaCLIP's 50.2%. Among trained VLM baselines, DINOV2 is competitive on pixel-level AUROC (93.7 vs 94.2) but trails significantly on image-level metrics (80.4 vs 90.2 AUROC), while SAM underperforms on both levels. Notably, AdaCLIP is not universally best per-dataset: APRIL-GAN edges it out on KSDD pixel-level max-F1 (56.2 vs 54.5) and DTD-Synthetic pixel-level max-F1 (72.7 vs 71.6), and DINOV2/SAM surpass AdaCLIP on ISIC pixel-level max-F1 (79.6/81.0 vs 71.4), indicating residual weakness on certain medical imaging tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

AdaCLIP extends the ZSAD line initiated by WinCLIP (hand-crafted CLIP prompts) and APRIL-GAN (learnable projection layers on auxiliary data) by introducing multimodal hybrid prompt learning. It draws directly from the prompt-learning literature—CoOp's static tokens, CoCoOp/IDPT's input-conditioned dynamic tokens, and MAPLE's multimodal prompting—but is the first to combine static and dynamic prompts simultaneously across both the image and text encoders of a VLM for anomaly detection. The HSF module departs from the standard max-pooling image-level scoring used by prior ZSAD methods, instead aggregating cluster-level semantics via KMeans to produce a more robust anomaly score.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that a pre-trained VLM can be adapted for zero-shot anomaly detection with only ~4.6% additional parameters, achieving SOTA across 14 industrial and medical datasets, and that cross-domain auxiliary data (e.g., industrial defects informing medical lesion detection) meaningfully improves generalization. The paper's analysis that diverse auxiliary data is more important than domain-matched data is a useful practical guideline for practitioners assembling training sets. Key limitations remain: performance degrades when test categories are far from auxiliary data, and the dynamic prompt generator is still a simple frozen-backbone + linear projection, leaving open the question of how to generate truly image-optimal prompts without ground-truth masks.
