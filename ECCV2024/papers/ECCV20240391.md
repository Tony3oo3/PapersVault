## Problem

Existing backdoor defenses in federated learning rely on data homogeneity and a minority malicious ratio to design client-wise selection rules, but under heterogeneous data both benign non-IID clients and malicious backdoor attackers deviate from the ideal global distribution, making it impossible to cleanly separate them at the client level. This paper argues that the discrimination should instead operate at the parameter level, where different neurons carry different importance for the target distribution.

## Input / Output

Each client receives local image-label pairs (28×28 MNIST/Fashion-MNIST, 32×32 CIFAR-10) and a small clean validation set (as few as 16 samples) held at the server. The method produces a globally aggregated CNN model and per-client aggregation weights derived from the Fisher importance discrepancy, rather than relying on data-size or uniform weighting. The validation set is used only to compute the target-distribution Fisher diagonal; it is never used for gradient updates.

## Method

SDFC computes a diagonal Fisher Information Matrix for each client's local model on both its private data D_k and the clean validation set U, yielding a per-parameter importance difference H_{k,v} = |M_k(D_k)[v] − M_k(U)[v]|. During local training, Fisher Difference Regularization (FDReg) adds a penalty λ·Σ_v H_{k,v}·(w_v^t − w_v^{t−1})² to the cross-entropy loss, stiffening parameters whose importance diverges between local and target distributions while leaving well-aligned parameters free to adapt. At aggregation, Fisher Discrepancy Aggregation (FDAgg) normalizes the per-client total importance discrepancy T_k = Σ_v H_{k,v} via max-min scaling and assigns weights α_k ∝ σ(−T̂_k), so clients whose local distribution closely matches the validation distribution contribute more. The entire pipeline is self-driven: no explicit client classification or thresholding is required, and the method handles both benign heterogeneity (Dirichlet β=0.5) and high malicious ratios (γ=50%) simultaneously.

## Results

On the V metric (average of benign accuracy A and backdoor failure rate R), SDFC achieves 92.98 on MNIST, 88.11 on Fashion-MNIST, and 75.39 on CIFAR-10 under β=0.5, γ=30%. The advantage becomes most pronounced at γ=50%: SDFC reaches V=76.85 on MNIST (vs. 50.43 for the best baseline CRFL), V=85.74 on Fashion-MNIST (vs. 43.85 for Sageflow), and V=64.92 on CIFAR-10 (vs. 48.36 for FLTrust). At β=1.0, SDFC similarly leads with V=94.25 (MNIST), 89.02 (Fashion-MNIST), and 74.48 (CIFAR-10) at γ=30%, and V=93.37, 87.85, and 59.79 at γ=50%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SDFC achieves the highest V metric across nearly all settings, with the most dramatic gains at γ=50% where most baselines collapse on backdoor removal (R drops below 50%). On MNIST at γ=50%, SDFC (V=76.85) outperforms the best baseline CRFL (V=50.43) by 26.4 points, while DnC, Sageflow, and RFA all fall below V=50. On CIFAR-10 at γ=50%, SDFC (V=64.92) leads FLTrust (V=48.36) and Finetuning (V=47.02) by roughly 17–18 points. At γ=30%, SDFC is competitive with DnC on MNIST (92.98 vs. 94.16) and CIFAR-10 (75.39 vs. 75.58) but clearly superior on Fashion-MNIST (88.11 vs. 87.58). Distance-based methods (FoolsGold, DnC) and statistics-based methods (Trim Median, Bulyan, RFA) maintain high benign accuracy but fail to remove backdoors under high malicious ratios, yielding V values near 50 (chance level for R). Model refinement methods (CRFL, Finetuning, RLR) show moderate backdoor removal but sacrifice benign accuracy, while SDFC preserves both simultaneously through its parameter-wise Fisher calibration.

## Relation to Prior Work

SDFC builds on the Fisher Information Matrix (FIM) from statistical learning theory, which has been applied to continual learning (EWC) and model pruning, but repurposes it for a fundamentally different purpose: measuring the *difference* in parameter importance between a client's local distribution and a clean validation distribution, rather than measuring importance for a single target. It also builds on the FedAvg aggregation protocol, augmenting it with a Fisher-based reweighting scheme. The key departure from prior backdoor defense work (FoolsGold, DnC, Bulyan, CRFL, etc.) is shifting the unit of analysis from client-wise discrimination—where heterogeneous and malicious clients are confounded—to parameter-wise calibration, where each weight element is individually regulated based on its importance discrepancy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine gap: existing backdoor defenses in federated learning rely on data homogeneity or minority-malicious-client assumptions that break down in realistic heterogeneous settings, and SDFC provides a self-driven mechanism that avoids these assumptions. However, practical adoption is constrained by the need for a small clean validation set at the server and the requirement that clients comply with the regularization term, leaving adaptive attacks (where malicious clients refuse to follow the protocol) unaddressed. Open questions include scalability to larger architectures and datasets beyond CIFAR-10, robustness to adaptive adversaries, and whether the FIM-based importance difference generalizes to non-CNN backbones such as transformers.
