## Problem

Existing autoregressive models for indoor scene synthesis generate objects in arbitrary or frequency-based order, ignoring the implicit spatial hierarchies and functional relationships that structure real scenes. This lack of semantic ordering leads to unrealistic placements with frequent object overlaps and poor spatial coherence, limiting the quality of synthesized scenes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240659/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a binary 2D floor plan layout (64×64 mask) as conditional input, encoded by a compact ViT into a single start token. It autoregressively outputs a sequence of oriented bounding boxes, each specifying a semantic class, 3D translation, bounding box size, and rotation angle, which are then matched to the closest-size 3D-FUTURE furniture instance within the predicted category. The sequential nature of the output means objects are placed one-by-one, with each new placement conditioned on all previously generated objects and the layout.

## Method

FOREST2SEQ mines the implicit hierarchy of a scene by clustering objects into functional zones via Modified Euclidean Distance Clustering (MEDC), which combines Euclidean center distance with a GIoU penalty to form a distance matrix processed by DBSCAN. Within each cluster, the largest object becomes the root of a subtree, and flexible objects (e.g., cabinets) that span multiple zones are attached to all candidate parents, yielding a scene forest rather than a single tree. Breadth-first traversal linearizes each tree into an ordered sequence, and during training a random tree is sampled from the forest to broaden the permutation space. A causal transformer decoder with absolute positional encodings generates objects sequentially, trained with a 5% mask/noise denoising strategy that randomly substitutes object embeddings and corrupts ground-truth categories to reduce error propagation. Continuous attributes (position, size, rotation) are modeled as 10-component logistic mixtures, while discrete class labels use softmax over logit vectors.

## Results

On the 3D-FRONT dataset, FOREST2SEQ achieves the best KL divergence across all four room types (Bedroom 4.2, Dining 5.5, Living 5.9, Library 5.2) and the best FID scores (Bedroom 67.9, Dining 40.2, Living 35.2, Library 69.1), outperforming the strongest baseline by an average margin of 2.58 in FID and 1.78 in KL. The method also attains the best CAS (closest to 50%) in every room type, indicating more realistic class distributions. In terms of efficiency, FOREST2SEQ uses only 9.99M parameters (48% fewer than the next smallest model) with an inference rate of 0.160 s per scene.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FOREST2SEQ outperforms all six baselines on KL and FID across every room type where comparisons are available. Against the closest competitor COFS [46], the gains are most pronounced in dining rooms (KL 5.5 vs. 9.3, FID 40.2 vs. 43.1) and living rooms (KL 5.9 vs. 8.1, FID 35.2 vs. 35.9). DiffuScene [59], the strongest diffusion-based baseline, is surpassed by 1.4–2.6 in KL and 2.8–5.6 in FID depending on room type. ATISS [47] and SceneFormer [64] trail by larger margins (e.g., KL 8.6 vs. 4.2 in bedrooms for ATISS; KL 31.3 vs. 5.9 in living rooms for SceneFormer). FastSynth [53] and LayoutGPT [16] are substantially weaker, with LayoutGPT reporting only partial results (bedroom and living room) and showing the worst KL among reported methods (17.5, 14.0). In the user study (Table 3a), FOREST2SEQ is preferred as most realistic in over 50% of living and dining room samples and dominates in bedrooms (43.3%) and libraries (48.5%), confirming the quantitative advantages perceptually.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the autoregressive transformer line of work for indoor scene synthesis (ATISS, COFS, SceneFormer) by introducing a structured, semantically-motivated ordering of objects rather than treating them as an unordered set or using arbitrary/frequency-based sequences. It draws on the set-to-sequence learning paradigm (Set2Seq, Tree2Seq) and the scene graph representation literature (DiffuScene, GRAINS), but departs from both by deriving a hierarchical tree/forest structure via clustering (MEDC + DBSCAN) and linearizing it with breadth-first search. The forest representation specifically addresses a gap in prior tree-based approaches: flexible furniture items (e.g., cabinets) that can plausibly belong to multiple functional zones, which a single tree cannot capture without ambiguity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides empirical evidence that incorporating an order prior significantly improves autoregressive scene synthesis, with the forest+BFS combination outperforming random, fixed, and DFS orderings across KL, FID, and CAS metrics. Practically, the method achieves competitive or superior quality with roughly 48% fewer parameters than the next-lightest baseline, making it attractive for deployment. Key limitations remain: the model does not condition on doors/windows (causing occlusion failures), lacks explicit spatial non-overlap constraints, and struggles with highly complex or non-standard layouts. The authors identify learning-to-ordering as an end-to-end module as a natural next step, and the forest representation could potentially generalize to other structured generation tasks beyond indoor scenes.
