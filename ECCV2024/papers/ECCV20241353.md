## Problem

All existing adversarial attacks on multiple object tracking (MOT) are digital, injecting pixel-level noise into video frames, making them infeasible in physical scenarios. No prior work has demonstrated a physically realizable attack that simultaneously disrupts both the detection and identity-association components of MOT systems. Additionally, existing evaluation metrics for MOT attacks ignore detection quality and assess only a few targeted objects rather than system-wide impact.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241353/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes clean video frames (from MOT15, MOT17, MOT20, or BDD100K) and optimizes a single printable adversarial patch (50 cm × 50 cm foam board, sized to ~30% of a 1.6 m pedestrian) that is embedded into frames digitally or physically placed in a scene. During training, the patch undergoes EOT augmentation (rotation, brightness, blurring, scaling) to simulate physical-world conditions, and is jointly optimized on both the full video dataset and a derived clip dataset of cropped pedestrian crops across time. The output is a smooth, printable patch that induces false detection boxes overlapping true ones, causing ID switches within a few frames.

## Method

PapMOT optimizes a printable patch via gradient descent with a composite adversarial loss of three terms: a novel bounding box restriction loss (L_bbr) that constrains the predicted detection box dimensions and minimizes the IoU gap between the patch-induced box and the true target box, a total variation loss (L_tv) for printability, and an average precision loss (L_ap) that suppresses the confidence score of the true detection so the false box wins association. The key insight is that disrupting tracking requires the false box to achieve high IoU with the Kalman-filter-predicted box from the previous frame, causing the Hungarian assignment to swap identities. A patch enhancement strategy crops pedestrian targets over time to build an additional clip dataset, and joint training on both the original and clip datasets increases attack success on specific targets. The overall loss is L_adv = β·L_bbr + γ·L_tv + δ·L_ap with β=1, γ=2.5, δ=2, and the trained patch is validated in both digital (YOLOX + ByteTrack/FairMOT) and physical (printed on foam boards under varying illumination, distance, and angle) settings.

## Results

On MOT17 with ByteTrack (YOLOX), PapMOT achieves TASR=94.6, SATSR=89.6, and IOR=79.5, substantially outperforming the strongest patch-based baseline ObjPatch (TASR=61.4, SATSR=76.7, IOR=7.4) and the digital attack Trasw (TASR=21.0, SATSR=26.8, IOR=42.3). On MOT17 with FairMOT, PapMOT reaches TASR=76.8 and SATSR=93.0, again leading all baselines. Across MOT20 and BDD100K, PapMOT maintains a 15–40 point TASR advantage over the next-best method, confirming consistent superiority in both pedestrian and autonomous-driving scenarios.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PapMOT outperforms all six baselines on TASR and SATSR across every dataset and victim model combination. Against the strongest patch-based competitor ObjPatch [28], PapMOT gains +33.2 TASR and +12.9 SATSR on MOT17/ByteTrack (94.6 vs 61.4; 89.6 vs 76.7), and the IOR gap is even more dramatic (79.5 vs 7.4), reflecting PapMOT's ability to disrupt identity association rather than merely fooling the detector. Against the digital attack Trasw [20], which focuses exclusively on data association, PapMOT leads by +73.6 TASR on MOT17/ByteTrack (94.6 vs 21.0) but trails slightly on IOR for MOT15/FairMOT (62.3 vs 66.2) and MOT20/FairMOT (56.6 vs 67.0), as Trasw's sole focus on ID-switching gives it a narrow edge on that single metric. The pixel-noise baselines RAnoise [21] and TOG [6] are consistently the weakest, with TASR values below 14 in most settings, confirming that imperceptible digital perturbations are far less effective than physical patches. The tracker-hijacking method Hijack [17] achieves moderate IOR (25–39) but very low TASR (8–18), indicating it disrupts identities without significantly degrading overall detection quality. Overall, PapMOT is the only method that simultaneously achieves high scores on all three metrics (TASR, SATSR, IOR) across all four datasets, validating its dual attack on both detection and tracking.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate lines of adversarial research: physical patch attacks on object detectors (Thys et al. 2019, Hu et al. 2022) and digital pixel-noise attacks on MOT systems (Jia et al. 2020, Lin et al. 2023). Prior patch attacks could only create new fake identities by fooling the detector but failed to disrupt the IoU-based data association that maintains identity consistency across frames; prior MOT attacks were purely digital and thus not physically deployable. PapMOT departs from both by introducing a bounding box restriction loss that forces the patch-induced false detection to geometrically overlap the true target box, thereby hijacking the Hungarian matching step in the tracker. The EOT augmentation framework from Athalye et al. (2018) is repurposed here to make the patch robust to real-world transformations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As the first physically realizable attack on MOT, PapMOT exposes a concrete vulnerability in tracking systems deployed in autonomous driving and surveillance where a printed 50 cm patch can cause ID switches within 30 frames. The new evaluation metrics (TASR, IOR, STASR) address a real gap: prior identity-based metrics ignored detection quality and system-wide cascading effects. However, the physical attack boundary is tightly constrained (effective only in relatively dark environments, within 0.8–4.1 m, and at angles under ~15°), and physical validation remains qualitative rather than quantitative. Open questions include whether more natural patch forms (clothing, textures) can widen the effective envelope, and whether MOT systems can be made robust to geometrically matched false detections without sacrificing tracking accuracy.
