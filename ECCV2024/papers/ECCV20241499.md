## Problem

Existing image coding for machines (ICM) methods require training separate codecs for each combination of bitrate, downstream task, and distortion-cognition trade-off, creating prohibitive deployment overhead. No prior work achieves variable-bitrate compression specifically optimized for machine vision tasks, and the distortion-cognition trade-off within a single codec has not been explored. This paper addresses the gap by proposing a single neural codec that simultaneously and controllably adjusts rate, reconstruction distortion, and machine task accuracy.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241499/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes standard RGB images as input (resized to 224×224 for ImageNet classification, scaled to 640–800 px for COCO detection, and native resolution for Cityscapes segmentation) and produces a two-part compressed bitstream: a primary latent code whose bitrate is set by a continuous parameter α ∈ [0,1], and a small auxiliary scalable bitstream encoding quantization error. The decoder reconstructs an image whose fidelity-task balance is controlled by a second parameter β ∈ [0,1], where β=1 yields the cognition-optimized image and β=0 yields the distortion-optimized image.

## Method

The architecture builds on the TIC transformer-based codec and introduces two key mechanisms. First, a cognition-oriented loss replaces the conventional MSE distortion term in the primary branch: it combines a MoCo contrastive loss (computed with a frozen pretrained backbone) to produce task-friendly representations, plus a local MSE penalty that constrains pixel values outside [0,1] to avoid the vanishing-gradient issues of hard clipping. Second, a channel-controllable latent gain unit (N gain matrices scaling C latent channels) enables variable-bitrate operation within a single model by adjusting quantization precision per channel. An auxiliary branch transmits the quantization error (y − ŷ) through a small scalable bitstream (only ~0.19M parameters) and a lightweight residual reconstruction network to recover distortion-oriented detail. The final output is a weighted interpolation x̄ = x̂₁ + (1−β)r, where x̂₁ is the cognition-oriented reconstruction and r is the residual, enabling continuous distortion-cognition trade-off. Training proceeds in two stages: Stage I optimizes the primary branch with the cognition-oriented loss across random bitrate points, then Stage II freezes the primary branch and trains the auxiliary branch with a standard MSE distortion loss.

## Results

On machine vision tasks (β=1), the proposed method achieves BD-Acc of 9.9876 on ImageNet classification, BD-AP of 1.8259 on COCO detection, and BD-mIoU of 0.1133 on Cityscapes segmentation, outperforming all ICM baselines. For image reconstruction (β=0), the method yields BD-PSNR of −0.4102 (ImageNet), −1.8293 (COCO), and −1.0240 (Cityscapes), which is inferior to the distortion-optimized TIC anchor but competitive with other learned compression methods. The key advantage is ultra-controllability: a single codec covers all bitrate points (α∈[0,1]) and all distortion-cognition trade-offs (β∈[0,1]) without retraining.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against TIC [48] (the base codec optimized purely for distortion), the proposed method (β=1) dramatically improves machine task performance: BD-Acc jumps from 0.69 to 9.99 on ImageNet, BD-AP from −0.44 to 1.83 on COCO, and BD-mIoU from −0.02 to 0.11 on Cityscapes, confirming that cognition-oriented training is essential for ICM. Compared to TransTIC [14], the closest ICM competitor, the proposed method wins on all three tasks (BD-Acc 9.99 vs 8.92, BD-AP 1.83 vs 1.81, BD-mIoU 0.1133 vs 0.1128), with the largest margin on classification (~1.07 BD-Acc). Against T-FT + TIC [11], the advantage is even more pronounced on detection (BD-AP 1.83 vs 0.71) and classification (BD-Acc 9.99 vs 8.35). For reconstruction (β=0), the method trades some PSNR relative to TIC (e.g., −0.41 vs +0.24 BD-PSNR on ImageNet) but still outperforms MBT-Mean [53] on ImageNet (−0.41 vs −0.85) and COCO (−1.83 vs −2.14), and outperforms Factorized [6] on all three datasets. The unique selling point is that all these operating points are accessible from a single trained model via α and β, whereas every baseline requires separate models per bitrate/task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of learned image compression (LIC) and Image Coding for Machines (ICM), building directly on TIC as its base codec architecture, G-VAE's channel-controllable latent gain units for variable-bitrate operation, and MoCo's contrastive loss formulation for the cognition-oriented objective. It departs from prior ICM work (TransTIC, T-FT, omni-feature learning) by introducing variable-bitrate capability and a distortion-cognition interpolation mechanism, neither of which existed in the ICM literature. It also departs from prior VBR methods (G-VAE, spatially-adaptive feature transform) by replacing distortion metrics with a contrastive cognition loss, addressing the assessment gap between human and machine perception that makes standard VBR codecs unsuitable for downstream tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that a single codec replaces the K×M×T separate models previously required for different bitrates, tasks, and trade-off points, substantially reducing deployment overhead in real-world ICM pipelines. The finding that a minimal auxiliary bitstream (~0.19M parameters) suffices to bridge the cognition-to-distortion gap suggests the two objectives share most of their information, with only a small residual encoding the difference. Limitations include evaluation on only three standard tasks with fixed downstream networks, a linear interpolation strategy that may not capture non-linear trade-offs, and reliance on a single base codec (TIC) without exploring whether the framework transfers to other architectures. Open questions include whether the cognition-oriented loss generalizes to novel or compositional tasks without retraining, and whether the approach extends to video or 3D data.
