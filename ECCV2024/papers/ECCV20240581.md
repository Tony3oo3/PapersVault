## Problem

Existing image editing models require clear, object-specific instructions (e.g., 'change the blue hat to red'), but responsible visual editing demands handling abstract, vaguely connected concepts like violence, discrimination, or privacy violations that are difficult to locate and modify. No prior work has explored how to automatically edit harmful images into responsible ones while minimizing content changes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240581/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a 512×512 risky RGB image, a text description of the risky concept (e.g., 'drug abuse', 'Marilyn Monroe'), and a task type (safety, fairness, or privacy) that determines the editing strategy—complete removal, concept diversification, or identity blurring. The output is a single responsible image where the risky concept is addressed while preserving overall scene structure. The entire pipeline is training-free, relying on off-the-shelf components (GPT-4V, Semantic-SAM, Stable Diffusion Inpainting) with fixed random seeds.

## Method

CoEditor decomposes responsible editing into a two-stage cognitive process powered by GPT-4V. In the Perceptual Cognitive Process (PCP), Semantic-SAM extracts object masks from the image, which are visually annotated with numbered tags (Set-of-Mark style); the risky concept is expanded via knowledge extraction into associated explanations; GPT-4V then reasons over both the annotated image and expanded concept text to identify the specific region(s) to edit. In the Behavioral Cognitive Process (BCP), the identified region is expanded and cropped, and GPT-4V generates a concrete editing target description that translates the abstract concept into specific visual instructions the inpainting model can follow. Finally, Stable Diffusion Inpainting synthesizes the edited region using the original image, the expanded mask, and the generated editing target. The key insight is that abstract concepts require explicit cognitive decomposition—first understanding *what* to edit through visual-language chain-of-thought, then planning *how* to edit—rather than feeding the raw concept directly to an editing model.

## Results

On the AltBear dataset under machine evaluation, CoEditor achieves an overall success rate of 65.56% and visual similarity of 0.5188, substantially outperforming InstructPix2pix (31.93% / 0.2924) and InstructDiffusion (44.14% / 0.3497). The largest gains appear in the safety subtask (70.13% success vs. 21.31% and 40.54%) and the fairness subtask (48.96% vs. 22.11% and 21.43%). Human evaluation (Table 2) confirms these trends, with CoEditor reaching 60.21% overall success and 0.4692 similarity, again leading both baselines by a wide margin.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CoEditor is compared against two instruction-based editing models: InstructPix2pix and InstructDiffusion, both of which rely on explicit, concrete editing instructions rather than abstract concept-level guidance. CoEditor wins on every subtask and both metrics (success rate and visual similarity) against both baselines. Against InstructPix2pix, CoEditor improves overall success rate by 33.6 percentage points (65.56% vs. 31.93%) and overall visual similarity by 0.226 (0.5188 vs. 0.2924). Against InstructDiffusion, the gains are 21.4 percentage points in success rate (65.56% vs. 44.14%) and 0.169 in similarity (0.5188 vs. 0.3497). The most dramatic gap is in the fairness subtask, where CoEditor nearly doubles InstructDiffusion's success rate (48.96% vs. 21.43%), highlighting the difficulty baselines face with abstract diversity-oriented instructions. InstructDiffusion generally outperforms InstructPix2pix (e.g., 44.14% vs. 31.93% overall success), but both struggle with the abstract, concept-level editing that CoEditor handles through its two-stage cognitive process.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of responsible visual synthesis (NSFW filtering, concept erasure, machine unlearning) and instruction-based image editing (InstructPix2pix, InstructDiffusion), but departs from both: prior responsible-synthesis work targets text-to-image generation rather than editing, and prior editing models require concrete, object-level instructions rather than abstract concept-level ones. The method borrows Set-of-Mark visual prompting for grounding and chain-of-thought reasoning for planning, combining them in a two-stage LMM pipeline (perceptual + behavioral cognition) that feeds a standard inpainting model. The AltBear dataset is a novel contribution that repurposes the teddy-bear proxy to make harmful-content research ethically tractable while preserving structural correspondence with real-world risky images.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper introduces a practically useful benchmark (AltBear) that the authors validate as consistent with real-world harmful content, lowering the ethical barrier for future responsible-editing research. The training-free, LMM-driven pipeline is computationally lighter than the diffusion-based editing baselines it outperforms, making it accessible. Key limitations include dependence on a single proprietary LMM (GPT-4V), a small 300-image test set, and only three subtask categories; open questions include whether the approach generalizes to more nuanced or compound risks, whether the teddy-bear proxy captures all dimensions of real-world harm, and whether the two-stage cognitive process can be distilled into a single end-to-end model.
