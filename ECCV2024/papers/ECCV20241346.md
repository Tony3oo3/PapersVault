## Problem

Diffusion models have shown strong performance in offline RL trajectory planning but require lengthy multi-step denoising processes at inference, making them impractical for time-sensitive applications like autonomous driving. Prior attempts to accelerate diffusion sampling typically sacrifice trajectory quality. The paper identifies that the denoising process naturally decomposes into a slow 'generate feasible trajectory' phase and a fast 'optimize trajectory' phase, and asks whether this decomposition can be exploited to decouple efficiency from quality.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a window of history states and corresponding returns-to-go (excluding actions from the trajectory representation, since actions are high-frequency and harder to model autoregressively) and produces a single action per timestep via an inverse dynamics model that maps consecutive predicted states to the action bridging them. The trajectory is represented as a 2×T matrix of states and cumulative returns-to-go, and the full planning loop operates in a receding-horizon fashion where only the first action is executed before re-planning.

## Method

Trajectory Diffuser decomposes diffusion-based planning into two decoupled subprocesses: (1) a GPT-style autoregressive transformer generates a feasible future trajectory by sequentially predicting next state and return-to-go tokens conditioned on history, and (2) a conditional diffusion model with classifier-free guidance performs trajectory optimization using only the final ~10% of denoising steps (e.g., 5 out of 50). The transformer is trained with standard next-token prediction on state-return pairs, while the diffusion model is trained with a noise-prediction objective conditioned on returns-to-go via classifier-free guidance to steer trajectories toward higher-reward regions. An inverse dynamics network converts the optimized state sequence into executable actions. The key insight is that the early denoising steps primarily produce a structurally valid trajectory (which the AR model replicates at 3–10× speed), while the late steps perform the actual reward-driven refinement, so retaining only the tail preserves quality while eliminating most of the computational cost.

## Results

On the D4RL locomotion benchmark, Trajectory Diffuser achieves an average normalized return of 84.2, outperforming all sequence-modeling baselines (AdaptDiffuser: 83.4, DD: 81.8, TT: 78.9, Diffuser: 77.5, DT: 74.7, BC: 51.9). In terms of inference speed, Trajectory Diffuser requires only 0.44s per action, achieving a 3–10× speedup over diffusion-based methods (Diffuser: 1.24s, AdaptDiffuser: 1.27s, DD: 4.61s) and a 12× speedup over Trajectory Transformer (5.43s). On the more challenging Adroit Pen task, Trajectory Diffuser reaches 96.7 average return versus 23.9 for Decision Diffuser, while on Kitchen it achieves 62.7 versus 61.0 for DD.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Trajectory Diffuser achieves the highest average return (84.2) on D4RL locomotion, edging out AdaptDiffuser (83.4) and Decision Diffuser (81.8), while being 3–10× faster in inference (0.44s vs. 1.24–4.61s per action). Against Trajectory Transformer (78.9 avg, 5.43s/action), Trajectory Diffuser wins on both performance and speed by a wide margin. On the Adroit Pen task, Trajectory Diffuser (96.7 avg) dramatically outperforms Decision Diffuser (23.9) and CQL (35.2 on Human data), suggesting the autoregressive initialization plus short diffusion optimization is particularly effective for high-dimensional manipulation. On Kitchen, Trajectory Diffuser (62.7 avg) slightly surpasses DD (61.0) and CQL (50.4), though the margin is smaller. The method is most competitive with AdaptDiffuser on locomotion (84.2 vs. 83.4) but pulls ahead decisively on Adroit, where the diffusion-only baselines struggle with high-variance, low-reward trajectories.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two established lines in offline RL: diffusion-based trajectory planners (Diffuser, Decision Diffuser, AdaptDiffuser) that achieve strong performance but require lengthy multi-step denoising at inference, and autoregressive sequence models (Decision Transformer, Trajectory Transformer) that are fast but lack an explicit optimization stage. The key conceptual departure is the observation that the diffusion denoising process can be decomposed into a slow 'generate feasible trajectory' phase and a fast 'optimize trajectory' phase, allowing the former to be replaced by a transformer-based autoregressive model while retaining only the final ~10% of diffusion steps for quality refinement. The trajectory representation (states + returns-to-go, no actions) follows Decision Transformer, and the conditional diffusion with classifier-free guidance follows Decision Diffuser, but the two-stage architecture is novel.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 3–10× inference speedup makes diffusion-based trajectory planning potentially viable for latency-sensitive applications (autonomous driving, real-time robotics) where full denoising is prohibitive. However, the performance gains over strong baselines are modest and inconsistent: Adroit results carry very high variance (±54–63), and the Kitchen Mixed task actually underperforms Decision Diffuser. Open questions include whether the decomposition generalizes beyond D4RL MuJoCo benchmarks to continuous control at scale or real-world deployment, whether the autoregressive component can be further compressed, and whether the optimal number of retained diffusion steps (found to be ~5 out of 100) is task-dependent or a universal property.
