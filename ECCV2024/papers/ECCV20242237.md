## Problem

Existing quantization backdoor attacks require the attacker to have full control over the victim's model training process, limiting them to scenarios where the attacker is the model provider. This paper addresses the more practical and pervasive threat where the attacker can only inject poisoned training data without any knowledge of the target model's architecture or training procedure, yet still causes the model to exhibit backdoor behavior exclusively after quantization.

## Input / Output

The attacker takes a clean RGB image training dataset (CIFAR-10 at 32×32, ImageNet-10 at 224×224, CelebA at 160×160) along with a target class label and a poisoning rate (default 5%), and produces a poisoned dataset where a small subset of images carries an imperceptible, input-dependent trigger (magnitude η=10/255) generated by a learned U-Net. The victim then trains a standard CNN classifier on this poisoned data and applies 8-bit post-training quantization; the resulting quantized model misclassifies triggered inputs to the target class while maintaining near-clean accuracy on untriggered inputs.

## Method

The core mechanism is an alternated training loop between a U-Net-based trigger generator b_ψ and a ResNet-18 surrogate classifier f_θ: the surrogate is trained on the poisoned data to mimic the victim's training, while the trigger generator is optimized to make the full-precision surrogate classify correctly but the quantized surrogate classify to the target class. The trigger is constructed as B(x) = (x + η·T(b_ψ(x))) * k, where T applies a DCT-based low-pass filter to suppress high-frequency artifacts (evading frequency-domain detection) and k is a Gaussian blur to preserve pixel correlations. A regularizer combines L2-norm minimization of the trigger magnitude with an adversarial-avoidance loss that constrains a separate clean classifier to remain correct on triggered inputs, preventing the trigger from degenerating into a universal adversarial perturbation. This design requires no access to the victim model, supports both dirty-label and clean-label poisoning, and transfers across different victim architectures (ResNet-18, VGG13, MobileNet) and quantization schemes (static, dynamic, weight-only).

## Results

On CIFAR-10, the proposed data-poisoning quantization backdoor achieves 92.32% attack success rate (ASR) under dirty-label and 91.52% under clean-label settings after 8-bit quantization, while maintaining benign accuracy (BA) of ~94% (vs. 94.38% for the clean model). On CelebA, ASR reaches 96.77% (dirty-label) and 96.89% (clean-label) with BA of ~79%. On ImageNet-10, ASR is 82.37% (dirty-label) and 81.27% (clean-label) with BA of ~86%, confirming effectiveness on larger images. The full-precision models show no backdoor behavior (poisoned-sample accuracy remains high), confirming the attack is only activated upon quantization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against prior quantization backdoor attacks on CIFAR-10 (Table 2), the proposed method achieves the highest quantized BA (94.05%) and the highest full-precision BA (94.20%), but its ASR of 92.32% trails [19] (96.73%) and [30] (99.25%) while substantially outperforming [38] (82.64%). The critical distinction is that all three baselines require full control of the victim's training process, whereas the proposed attack operates purely through data poisoning with zero access to the model. Against defenses, the attack bypasses STRIP (entropy distributions indistinguishable from clean models), evades Spectral Signatures (only 56/2500 poisoned samples detected, ASR remains 89.94%), and reduces frequency-based detection from 100% to 17–30% via DCT-based high-frequency removal. NAD is the most effective defense tested, reducing ASR from 91.52% to 79.62%, but the backdoor remains practically exploitable. Transfer experiments confirm the attack generalizes to VGG13 victims (ASR >90% on CIFAR-10/CelebA) and across static, dynamic, and weight-only quantization methods (ASR >89% in all cases).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the quantization backdoor attack line of work (Hong et al. 2021, Pan et al. 2021, Tian et al. 2022, Ma et al. 2023), which all require the attacker to fully control the victim's training process via modified loss functions or model optimization. The key departure is shifting the threat model from model-poisoning to data-poisoning: the attacker only supplies a small fraction of poisoned training data while the victim retains full control over architecture, training, and quantization. Methodologically, the paper introduces an alternated training loop between a U-Net-based trigger generator and a surrogate classifier, drawing on the learnable-trigger paradigm from input-aware backdoor attacks (Nguyen & Tran 2020) and the clean-label data poisoning framework (Turner et al. 2019), but repurposing them to exploit the full-precision/quantized behavioral gap rather than standard classification errors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is significant: even users who carefully audit their models at full precision and control their own training pipeline remain vulnerable if they source training data from third parties, since the backdoor is invisible until the quantization step. The attack's transferability across architectures (ResNet-18 surrogate → VGG13/MobileNet victim) and quantization schemes (static, dynamic, weight-only) broadens the attack surface considerably. Open questions include whether defenses can be designed specifically for the quantization-activation mechanism (e.g., differential testing between full-precision and quantized inference on held-out data), and whether the attack extends to other compression techniques like pruning or knowledge distillation. A limitation is that the attack only manifests after quantization, so users who never quantize are unaffected, and the 8-bit focus may not generalize to lower-bit or mixed-precision regimes.
