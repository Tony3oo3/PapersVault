## Problem

Prior work in continual representation learning showed that self-supervised learning (SSL) builds more robust representations than supervised learning (SL) under sequential task training, which is counter-intuitive since labels provide additional information. This paper challenges that conclusion by arguing the advantage is not due to the absence of supervision but rather to the MLP projector commonly used in SSL architectures, and asks whether equipping SL with the same projector can close or reverse the gap.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240145/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100 and SVHN, 224×224 for ImageNet-100) and produces 512-dimensional feature representations from a ResNet-18 backbone. During training, an MLP projector is appended to the backbone and used in the loss computation, but it is discarded at test time so all methods operate with identical inference-time parameters. Representations are evaluated via k-NN and Nearest Mean Classifiers on all previously seen tasks, and the training data is split into sequential non-overlapping class subsets (e.g., CIFAR-100/5 means 5 tasks of 20 classes each).

## Method

The core contribution is SL+MLP: standard supervised cross-entropy training with a multi-layer perceptron projector (linear layer + BatchNorm + ReLU blocks, following the MLPP architecture) inserted between the ResNet-18 backbone and the classification head, with the projector discarded after training. The paper shows this simple modification makes SL outperform both vanilla SL and SSL (BarlowTwins, SimCLR) in continual finetuning across in-distribution and transfer-learning settings. Spectral analysis reveals the MLP projector prevents neural collapse in SL, yielding flatter singular-value spectra and increasing feature diversity across tasks, while also reducing forgetting and improving forward transfer. The approach composes with existing continual learning strategies (LwF, CaSSLe, PFR), with SL+MLP+PFR achieving the best overall transfer performance. Ablations confirm that a single MLP block suffices and that BatchNorm is the most critical component, while projector depth and width have minimal impact beyond the first block.

## Results

On the main continual finetuning benchmark (Table 1), SL+MLP achieves k-NN accuracy of 65.9% on CIFAR-10/5, 61.9% on CIFAR-100/5, 47.1% on CIFAR-100/20, and 62.4% on ImageNet-100/5. These results surpass the strongest SSL baseline (BarlowTwins) on three of four splits (by +7.8, +7.1, and +5.4 percentage points respectively), while BarlowTwins retains an advantage on CIFAR-10/5 (76.2% vs 65.9%). Vanilla SL without the MLP projector lags far behind (e.g., 45.3% vs 61.9% on CIFAR-100/5), confirming that the projector is the critical component enabling supervised models to match or exceed SSL in continual representation learning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the primary SSL baseline BarlowTwins, SL+MLP wins on CIFAR-100/5 (61.9 vs 54.1, +7.8 pp), CIFAR-100/20 (47.1 vs 40.0, +7.1 pp), and ImageNet-100/5 (62.4 vs 57.0, +5.4 pp), but loses on CIFAR-10/5 (65.9 vs 76.2, −10.3 pp). Against SimCLR, SL+MLP wins on all four splits, with margins ranging from +1.5 pp (C10/5) to +17.7 pp (C100/20). Among supervised methods, t-ReX (cosine softmax + projector) edges out SL+MLP on CIFAR-10/5 (69.3 vs 65.9) and CIFAR-100/20 (50.8 vs 47.1), while SL+MLP leads on CIFAR-100/5 (61.9 vs 59.2) and ImageNet-100/5 (62.4 vs 59.2). SupCon (supervised contrastive loss + projector) is consistently outperformed by SL+MLP across all four splits, with the largest gap on CIFAR-100/20 (47.1 vs 30.0, +17.1 pp). The key takeaway is that the MLP projector, not the specific loss function, is the dominant factor: all supervised methods with a projector substantially outperform vanilla SL and most SSL baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research threads: (1) the recent discovery that an MLP projector is the key component responsible for the superior transferability of self-supervised representations over supervised ones (Wang et al., CVPR 2021; Sariyildiz et al., ICLR 2023), and (2) the continual representation learning literature, which had established that SSL methods (e.g., Barlow Twins, SimCLR) build more robust, less-forgetting representations than supervised learning under sequential training (Fini et al., CVPR 2022; Davari et al., CVPR 2022). The paper departs from the second thread by transplanting the MLP projector insight from the transfer learning literature into the continual learning setting, directly challenging the prevailing narrative that supervision is a liability for continual representation quality.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a simple MLP head flips the SL-vs-SSL ranking in continual representation learning has direct practical implications: practitioners with access to labels should prefer supervised training with a projector over the more computationally expensive SSL pipeline. However, the scope is limited to ResNet-18 on image classification, and the method still requires costly annotations, which constrains its applicability at scale where SSL's label-free advantage remains. Open questions include whether the result generalizes to larger backbones (ViTs, deeper ResNets), non-image modalities, and whether the MLP projector's benefit persists under more aggressive distribution shifts or longer task sequences.
