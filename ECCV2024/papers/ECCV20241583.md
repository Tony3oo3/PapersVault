## Problem

Existing contrastive-based anomaly detection methods (e.g., CSI) rely on traditional augmentations like rotation and cropping, which capture only coarse-grained feature differences and perform poorly on subtle, fine-grained medical anomalies such as small tumors or hemorrhages. The paper tackles the question of how to emphasize fine-grained discriminative features in a self-supervised manner without requiring prior knowledge of anomalous patterns.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241583/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes medical images of varying modalities (chest X-ray, endoscopy, retinal OCT, fundus) and resolutions (32×32 for MNIST-style datasets up to 256×256 for APTOS-2019) and produces a per-image anomaly score combining a nearest-neighbor contrastive similarity term and a shifting-classifier confidence term. For dissolving transformations, high-resolution inputs are downsampled to 32×32, processed by the diffusion model, then resized back to the original resolution before being fed to the feature extractor.

## Method

DIA introduces dissolving transformations that apply a single reverse diffusion step directly on a clean input image (rather than starting from noise), using a pretrained UNet-based diffusion model as a feature-aware denoiser that removes instance-specific fine-grained details while preserving coarse structure; the degree of feature removal is controlled by the diffusion time step t. The amplifying framework extends CSI's contrastive setup with a third transformation branch: each image undergoes 3K transformations (2K coarse-grained branches with shifting + non-shifting augmentations, and K fine-grained branches that additionally apply a random dissolving transformation). The key loss modification introduces new negative pairs between each image and its feature-dissolved counterpart, forcing the ResNet18 encoder to learn representations sensitive to the dissolved fine-grained features. An auxiliary classifier predicts which shifting transformation was applied, and the final anomaly score at inference combines the maximum cosine similarity to training samples (scaled by feature norm) with the classifier's confidence, both balanced per shifting transformation.

## Results

DIA achieves AUROC scores of 0.903 (Pneumonia MNIST), 0.750 (Breast MNIST), 0.851 (SARS-COV-2), 0.860 (Kvasir-Polyp), 0.944 (Retinal-OCT), and 0.934 (APTOS-2019), representing an average ~18.40% AUC boost over the CSI baseline across the six medical datasets. DIA attains the best AUROC on four out of six datasets, with the largest gains on Kvasir-Polyp (+25.1% over CSI) and Breast MNIST (+20.4% over CSI). The method requires no external training data or pretrained feature extractors, relying solely on a dataset-specific diffusion model for dissolving transformations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DIA outperforms the direct contrastive-learning baseline CSI on all six datasets, with the most dramatic gains on Kvasir-Polyp (0.860 vs. 0.609, +25.1%) and Breast MNIST (0.750 vs. 0.546, +20.4%), confirming that dissolving transformations effectively amplify fine-grained features that standard shifting transformations miss. Among all 13 compared methods, DIA achieves the best AUROC on four of six datasets (Pneumonia MNIST, SARS-COV-2, Kvasir-Polyp, APTOS-2019). The only method that surpasses DIA on any dataset is RD4AD, which leverages a pretrained model and achieves 0.996 on Retinal-OCT (vs. DIA's 0.944) and 0.759 on Breast MNIST (vs. DIA's 0.750). Reconstruction-based methods (GANomaly, UniAD) and normalizing-flow methods (CFlow, UFlow, FastFlow) consistently underperform, with several scoring below 0.60 on multiple datasets. Memory-bank methods (PatchCore, CFA) show high variance across datasets, and teacher-student methods (KDAD, Transformly, EfficientAD) are competitive on some datasets but unstable on others, whereas DIA maintains consistently strong results without requiring external pretrained weights.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the contrastive learning framework for anomaly detection established by CSI (Contrasting Shifted Instances) by introducing a third augmentation branch based on dissolving transformations derived from pretrained diffusion models. While prior work has used GANs for adversarial data generation (GANomaly, ALGAN, G2D) or diffusion models for reconstruction (AnoDDPM), DIA repurposes the single-step reverse diffusion process as a feature-aware denoiser that selectively removes instance-specific fine-grained features. The key conceptual departure is framing the contrastive negative pairs not as distributionally shifted instances (as in CSI) but as feature-dissolved counterparts, thereby forcing the encoder to encode the very fine-grained details that were removed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ~18.40% AUC improvement over CSI across six diverse medical datasets demonstrates that fine-grained feature learning is a critical bottleneck in medical anomaly detection that standard data augmentations (crop, rotation) cannot address. A key limitation is the requirement to train a dataset-specific diffusion model, and the paper shows that cross-domain diffusion models (e.g., CIFAR-trained) fail to dissolve the correct features, limiting generalizability. Open questions include whether the dissolving strategy can be adapted to 3D medical volumes, whether pre-trained large-scale diffusion models (e.g., Stable Diffusion) can be fine-tuned for this purpose, and whether the approach extends to supervised fine-grained classification tasks as the authors suggest.
