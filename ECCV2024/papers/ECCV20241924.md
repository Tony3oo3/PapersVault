## Problem

Existing test-time optimization methods for video motion estimation, such as OmniMotion, use a single unified neural field and transformation to represent all content, which cannot adequately capture the highly non-rigid deformations of dynamic objects while simultaneously modeling the rigid camera motion of static scenes. This unified approach leads to sub-optimal tracking accuracy, particularly on dynamic objects with complex inter-frame motion and appearance changes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241924/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on a single video clip (downsampled to 256×256) and is optimized per-video for 100k iterations using RAFT optical flow as pseudo-labels, 768-dimensional DINO features for feature rendering supervision, and motion segmentation masks for early-stage guidance. Beyond per-pixel trajectories and 2D correspondences, the method produces decomposed static and dynamic appearance renderings along with a per-3D-point motion confidence mask, enabling downstream tasks like object removal and video inpainting.

## Method

DecoMotion explicitly decomposes video content into two separate canonical 3D volumes: a static volume G_st with an affine transformation (augmented with 3 non-linear Real-NVP layers for robustness) and a dynamic volume G_dy with 6 Real-NVP invertible layers to model non-rigid deformation. The key architectural insight is that static scenes only need rigid camera-motion modeling while dynamic objects require complex non-linear warps, so each volume uses a tailored transformation and representation network (GaborNet with 512 channels). A novel feature rendering loss using pre-trained DINO features rectifies the dynamic transformation by forcing corresponding pixels to map to the same canonical 3D point in feature space, mitigating the color-similarity ambiguity that plagues photometric-only supervision. The two volumes are fused via an orthogonal approximation weighted by a learned per-point static confidence β, producing a unified representation from which motion trajectories and decomposed appearances are rendered through standard alpha compositing.

## Results

On TAP-Vid-DAVIS, DecoMotion achieves 74.4% tracking position accuracy (<δ_avg^x>) and 87.2% Occlusion Accuracy on DAVIS-Strided, and 69.9% and 84.2% on DAVIS-First. It outperforms the closest optimization-based competitor OmniMotion by 6.9% (Strided) and 7.2% (First) in <δ_avg^x>, and surpasses MFT by 3.6% and 3.1% respectively. Against dedicated point-tracking methods, DecoMotion leads TAPIR by 0.8% on DAVIS-Strided (74.4% vs 73.6%) while being within 0.1% on DAVIS-First (69.9% vs 70.0%), all while being supervised only by RAFT optical flow.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DecoMotion clearly outperforms the two most directly comparable optimization-based methods: OmniMotion (its direct predecessor) is beaten by 6.9% on DAVIS-Strided and 7.2% on DAVIS-First in <δ_avg^x>, while MFT(RAFT) is surpassed by 3.6% and 3.1% respectively. Against dedicated point-tracking methods, DecoMotion leads TAPIR by 0.8% on DAVIS-Strided (74.4% vs 73.6%) but trails by a marginal 0.1% on DAVIS-First (69.9% vs 70.0%), making it essentially on-par with the strongest dedicated tracker. It also exceeds PIPs++ (73.7% Strided, 69.1% First) and FGVC (66.4% Strided, 62.8% First) by comfortable margins. Notably, DecoMotion is supervised only by RAFT optical flow, which the authors note has limited capability for providing accurate correspondences, yet it still matches or exceeds methods that use richer supervision. On the Temporal Coherence metric, DecoMotion achieves the best TC of 0.69 on both splits, slightly better than OmniMotion's 0.74/0.73.

## Relation to Prior Work

DecoMotion directly extends OmniMotion's unified canonical 3D volume framework by splitting it into separate static and dynamic volumes, each with a tailored transformation (affine + few non-linear layers for static; full Real-NVP for dynamic). The volume rendering and alpha-compositing machinery is inherited from NeRF and OmniMotion, while the volume fusion step borrows the orthogonal approximation from NeRF-in-the-Wild. The key conceptual departure is the divide-and-conquer strategy: rather than forcing a single invertible network to handle both rigid camera motion and complex non-rigid object deformation, the paper assigns each regime its own representation and loss, and introduces a feature rendering loss (using DINO features) to combat color-space distractors that plague photometric-only supervision.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The decomposition strategy yields a ~7% absolute gain in tracking accuracy over OmniMotion and brings a test-time-optimization method to parity with dedicated learned trackers like TAPIR, suggesting that explicit structural priors (rigid vs. non-rigid) remain valuable even in the era of end-to-end learned correspondences. However, the method is per-video test-time optimization, limiting scalability to long or large-scale video collections, and it depends on multiple external components (RAFT flow, DINO features, motion segmentation masks) whose errors propagate. The authors acknowledge the brittleness of the motion segmentation guidance and outline future work on more elegant, segmenter-free decomposition (e.g., optimizing a rigid component first, then a non-rigid residual), which would be a meaningful step toward practical deployment.
