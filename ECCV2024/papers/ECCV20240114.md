## Problem

Spectral reflectance and broadband subsurface scattering have each been used for material classification, but their combination—spectral subsurface scattering (S⁴)—has not been exploited despite the strong wavelength dependence of scattering kernels offering highly discriminative features. Moreover, acquiring full S⁴ data via hyperspectral scanning is time-consuming and requires complex multi-shot instrumentation, limiting practical deployment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240114/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single monochrome image (1200×2048 pixels) captured through a grism of a point-illuminated sample surface, where the horizontal axis encodes spatial spread of the scattering kernel and the vertical axis encodes spectral dispersion. From this single S⁴ image, the authors can also decompose S² (spectral reflectance, 1×2048) and S³ (broadband scattering kernel, 1200×1) measurements for baseline comparison. The output is a material class label (25 white-material classes) or a continuous concentration value (10 coffee levels).

## Method

The core contribution is a single-shot S⁴ imaging setup that replaces sequential hyperspectral scanning with a grism (prism + 300 lines/mm grating) that spectrally disperses the backscattered point-illumination kernel along one spatial axis, yielding a 2D projection of the full S⁴ point spread function in a single exposure. The authors validate this projection's sufficiency by fitting the Farrell diffusion model to both multishot and simulated single-shot measurements, showing comparable reconstruction error across all materials. For classification, the raw S⁴ image (or its S²/S³ projections) is fed into SVM (RBF kernel), MLP, or a CNN, with PCA applied to match dimensionalities across measurement types. The approach outperforms both spectral-only (S²) and broadband-scattering-only (S³) baselines, achieving 58.80% accuracy on 25 white-material classes with CNN versus 54.13% and 41.33% respectively.

## Results

On the white materials classification task (25 classes, 3-fold cross-validation), S⁴ with a CNN achieves the highest accuracy of 58.80%, outperforming S² (35.47%) and S³ (54.13%) with the same classifier. With SVM, S⁴ reaches 52.27% versus 40.53% (S²) and 46.93% (S³). On the black coffee concentration regression task (10 levels, 2-fold cross-validation), S⁴ with MLP achieves the lowest MSE of 1.73, narrowly beating S³ (1.78) and substantially outperforming S² (5.15).

**Table 1: Averaged classification accuracy from 3-fold cross-validation on the white materials dataset (25 classes) for S², S³, and S⁴ with different classifiers.**

| Classifier | S² (Spectral Reflectance) | S³ (Broadband Scattering) | S⁴ (Spectral Scattering) |
|---|---|---|---|
| SVM | 40.53% | 46.93% | **52.27%** |
| MLP | 41.33% | 52.53% | **55.20%** |
| CNN | 35.47% | 54.13% | **58.80%** |

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's baselines are the two constituent measurement modalities: S² (surface spectral reflectance) and S³ (broadband subsurface scattering), both of which can be derived from the S⁴ measurement. On the white materials classification task, S⁴ consistently outperforms both baselines across all three classifiers, with the largest gap over S² (up to +23.33 percentage points with CNN) and a more modest but consistent gap over S³ (up to +4.67 points with CNN). On the coffee concentration regression task, S⁴ and S³ perform nearly identically (MLP: 1.73 vs. 1.78 MSE), while both substantially outperform S² (5.15 MSE), which is expected since the task involves varying particle concentration in a fixed medium where broadband scattering is more informative than spectral variation. The confusion matrix analysis reveals that S⁴ and S³ share similar error patterns, with S⁴ showing a more pronounced diagonal, while S² exhibits a distinctly different confusion structure—e.g., 'rubber' is classified at 100% accuracy with S⁴/S³ but only 53.33% with S².

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate measurement modalities for material classification—spectral reflectance (S²) and broadband subsurface scattering (S³)—into a single joint measurement (S⁴), building on the Farrell diffusion model for spectral subsurface light transport. It departs from prior spectral classification work (hyperspectral cameras, NIR/SWIR) and prior subsurface scattering work (dot-pattern projection, ToF cameras) by exploiting the wavelength-dependent scattering kernel as a combined feature. The single-shot acquisition via a grism draws on snapshot multispectral imaging designs (compressive, prism-mask, spatio-spectral scanning) but applies them specifically to the subsurface scattering kernel rather than general scene radiance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that the spectral dependence of subsurface scattering provides discriminative information beyond either modality alone, and that a single 2D projection suffices, making the measurement practical without hyperspectral scanning. However, significant limitations remain: the method requires controlled active illumination, smooth/planar surfaces, and is degraded by material opacity, restricting real-world deployment. Open questions include extending to multi-material scenes with overlapping kernels, handling rough or textured surfaces, and validating on larger, more diverse material libraries beyond the 25 white-material classes tested here.
