## Problem

Existing salient object ranking (SOR) methods rely on explicit visual saliency cues such as spatial frequency and semantic context, which fail in complex scenes involving human activities and interactions. The paper identifies that human observers' attention is reflexively guided by the poses and gestures of people in a scene (e.g., head orientation, walking direction), yet no prior SOR work has exploited human pose cues to model these high-level interactions.

## Input / Output

The model takes a single RGB image (resized to 768×768 at inference) and produces three outputs: per-instance salient object masks (via dot-product with FPN features), a discrete ranking order over detected salient instances (predicted as a classification problem), and 17-joint human pose coordinates for each detected person. Additionally, a mask head on the PDR module predicts the next fixated region, with the final map expected to correspond to background, encoding the end of the attention sequence.

## Method

PoseSOR is a query-based architecture built on a Swin Transformer + FPN backbone with a 6-layer Transformer decoder that first detects salient objects from N=100 queries. Two novel modules then exploit pose: (1) the Pose-Aware Interaction (PAI) module initializes pose queries by summing each salient object query with a latent joint representation, then uses cross-attention over instance-specific features and self-attention to model inter-joint relations, followed by an instance-level self-attention stage with scene tokens to capture person-environment interactions in a pose-aware manner; (2) the Pose-Driven Ranking (PDR) module transforms pose knowledge into directional queries (D=8 shifting directions) and coordinate tokens, computes shifting likelihood via softmax over their dot-product to predict the next attention target, and then models pairwise ranking relations among all queries via concatenated MLPs. The model is trained end-to-end with a combined loss of mask (BCE + Dice), ranking (cross-entropy), pose (L1 + heatmap), and auxiliary detection (Hungarian-matched) terms.

## Results

On the ASSR test set (2,418 images), PoseSOR achieves SA-SOR 0.673, SOR 0.871, and MAE 7.23, surpassing the previous best SA-SOR (0.650 by IRSR) by 3.5% and the best MAE (7.31 by Mask2Former) by 1.1%. On the IRSR test set (2,929 images), PoseSOR achieves SA-SOR 0.568, SOR 0.817, and MAE 6.29, outperforming the second-best SA-SOR (0.543 by IRSR) by 4.6% and the second-best MAE (7.13 by QueryInst) by 11.8%. The method achieves the best or near-best scores across all three metrics on both benchmarks, with the only slight deficit being SOR (0.871 vs. 0.873 on ASSR; 0.817 vs. 0.820 on IRSR) where OCOR edges ahead by a marginal 0.002–0.003.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PoseSOR outperforms all 11 baselines on SA-SOR (the primary ranking metric that penalizes missed objects) on both benchmarks: 0.673 vs. 0.650 (IRSR) on ASSR and 0.568 vs. 0.543 (IRSR) on IRSR. On MAE (segmentation quality), PoseSOR achieves the best scores on both benchmarks (7.23 on ASSR, 6.29 on IRSR), with a particularly large 11.8% margin over QueryInst (7.13) on IRSR. The only metric where PoseSOR does not lead is SOR (Spearman rank correlation without false-negative penalty), where OCOR [47] edges ahead by 0.002 on ASSR (0.873 vs. 0.871) and 0.003 on IRSR (0.820 vs. 0.817); the authors note this is because OCOR tends to miss salient objects, which SOR does not penalize but SA-SOR and MAE do. Among SOR-specific baselines, PoseSOR surpasses the strongest prior SOR methods (IRSR, OCOR, PSR, PPA) on SA-SOR and MAE while remaining competitive on SOR. Non-SOR baselines (SOD, SID, INS methods) are substantially weaker on ranking metrics, with the best instance segmentation method (Mask2Former) achieving only 0.635 SA-SOR on ASSR versus PoseSOR's 0.673.

## Relation to Prior Work

PoseSOR builds on the query-based transformer detection paradigm (DETR, Mask2Former) and the ranking-as-classification formulation from PPA, but departs from all prior SOR methods (ASRNet, PPA, IRSR, OCOR, PSR) by introducing human pose as an explicit cue for attention shift prediction. Previous SOR work has focused on explicit visual saliency cues—spatial frequency, semantic context, position embeddings, and graph-based instance competition—while this paper argues these cues are insufficient for scenes with complex human activities and interactions. The two novel modules (PAI and PDR) repurpose pose estimation within a detection-style architecture, treating skeletal joints as query tokens that interact with salient object queries and scene tokens.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to demonstrate that human pose knowledge provides a meaningful signal for salient object ranking, particularly in multi-person scenes where attention shifts are driven by body orientation and movement direction rather than low-level visual saliency. The ablation showing pose benefits scale with the number of humans present (largest gains at ≥3 people) suggests a clear regime where pose-aware methods will outperform purely visual-saliency approaches. A key limitation is that pose predictions remain coarse due to limited pose annotations, and the authors note that transferring knowledge from dedicated pose estimation models could unlock further gains—an open direction for future work.
