## Problem

Procedural video understanding suffers from a cold-start problem: existing methods are trained on web videos, making it difficult to query instructional content from raw, unedited video observations. Fixed-viewpoint (FV) video datasets, which are ideal for practical smartphone applications, have historically lacked environmental diversity due to expensive in-person camera setup. The paper revisits FV datasets using modern smartphone wide-angle lenses to capture diverse cooking environments without in-person assistance, and introduces two new benchmark tasks to expose the limitations of current SOTA methods on unedited overhead-view procedural videos.

## Input / Output

For OnRR, the input is a video segment up to a time point Z (25%, 50%, 75%, or 100% of the 16.6-minute average video) paired with a recipe text pool of ~1,828 candidates, and the output is a ranked list of feasible recipes plus the specific cooking stage (AP) within the retrieved recipe. For DVC-OV, the input is the full unedited overhead-view video (1920×1080, 30 fps, ultra-wide 13 mm-equivalent lens), and the output is a sequence of dense captions aligned to AP segments, each annotated with before/after/destination bounding boxes forming a visual action graph. The visual action graph structure—linking text instructions to spatial object movements via intra-action and inter-action edges—distinguishes this dataset from prior procedural video resources that use only coarse verb-noun instructions.

## Method

The core contribution is the COM Kitchens dataset with its visual action graph annotation, where each action-by-person (AP) is a tuple of word sequence, bounding boxes (before, after, destination), and start/finish frames, connected by intra-action edges (auto-generated from shared action indices) and inter-action edges (manually annotated to track ingredient flow across actions). For the OnRR benchmark, the authors evaluate contrastive video-text retrieval models (UniVL, CLIP4Clip, X-CLIP) by shortening recipe texts via POS tagging to fit token limits and decomposing the task into feasible recipe retrieval and recipe stage identification subtasks. For DVC-OV, they fine-tune Vid2Seq (SOTA dense video captioning) and explore two action-graph supervision strategies: relation labels (RL) using a TablERT-CNN decoder for edge-type prediction, and attention supervision (AS) minimizing KL divergence between self-attention and the graph alignment matrix. The key finding is that neither RL nor AS alone improves over fine-tuning without graph supervision, but their combination (RL+AS) yields the best performance (SODA_c 0.581 vs. 0.369), attributed to smoother temporal attention alignment that aids both caption generation and segment boundary detection.

## Results

On the novel Online Recipe Retrieval (OnRR) task, none of the three video-text retrieval baselines (UniVL, CLIP4Clip, X-CLIP) outperforms random selection in feasible recipe retrieval (e.g., UniVL R@1 = 3.4% vs. random 1.8% at early stage, but R@5 and R@10 are below random). In recipe stage identification, all models beat random (UniVL R@1 = 17.2%, X-CLIP R@1 = 10.3% vs. random 6.3% at early stage). On Dense Video Captioning (DVC-OV), zero-shot performance is near-zero (PDVC SODA_c = 0.022, Vid2Seq SODA_c = 0.017), while fine-tuned Vid2Seq with combined action-graph supervision (RL+AS) reaches SODA_c = 0.581, CIDEr = 6.195, and METEOR = 1.142.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates five SOTA methods as baselines on the COM Kitchens dataset to expose limitations of existing approaches. For OnRR feasible recipe retrieval, all three contrastive video-text models (UniVL, CLIP4Clip, X-CLIP) fail to beat random selection, with CLIP4Clip and X-CLIP scoring 0% R@1 at both early and middle stages, indicating that global contrastive alignment cannot identify which recipe is being followed from partial video. For recipe stage identification, UniVL leads at the early stage (R@1 = 17.2%) while X-CLIP leads at the middle stage (R@1 = 17.2%), both substantially above random (6.3%), but absolute performance remains low. For DVC-OV, zero-shot PDVC and Vid2Seq produce near-zero captions (SODA_c ≤ 0.022), confirming a severe domain gap between web/ego videos and unedited overhead-view cooking footage. Fine-tuning Vid2Seq on COM Kitchens improves SODA_c to 0.369, and adding combined action-graph supervision (RL+AS) further boosts it to 0.581 (CIDEr 6.195, METEOR 1.142), showing that structured graph annotations provide meaningful signal beyond plain caption supervision. Overall, the results demonstrate that current web-video-trained methods are insufficient for long, repetitive, overhead-view procedural videos, motivating new pre-training and fine-tuning strategies.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper revisits fixed-viewpoint (FV) procedural video datasets that dominated pre-deep-learning research (MMAC, MPII, Breakfast) but were largely abandoned due to the difficulty of collecting such videos at scale. It departs from the dominant web-video (YouCookII, COIN) and ego-centric (Epic Kitchens, EgoExo4D) paradigms by leveraging modern smartphone wide-angle lenses to collect unedited overhead-view cooking videos in diverse home environments without in-person technical support. The visual action graph annotation format draws on the VRF dataset's ingredient-action edge structure, but extends it to unedited long-form videos for the first time. For evaluation, the paper benchmarks against contrastive video-text retrieval models (UniVL, CLIP4Clip, X-CLIP) and transformer-based dense video captioning systems (Vid2Seq, PDVC), all of which were pre-trained on web or ego-centric video corpora.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dataset addresses a practical cold-start problem: building video-based recipe retrieval systems requires annotated unedited video data that web sources cannot provide. The benchmark results reveal a stark domain gap—SOTA retrieval models fail to beat random selection on feasible recipe retrieval, and DVC models collapse from ~5–8 SODA_c on YouCookII to near-zero on COM Kitchens—highlighting that current web-video-pretrained models cannot transfer to unedited, long, repetitive overhead-view content. Key open questions include how to bridge this domain gap (e.g., through new pre-training objectives or action-graph-aware architectures) and whether the visual action graph can serve as a richer supervision signal beyond the limited RL/AS ablations tested here. The dataset's modest size (145 videos, 40 hours) and single-domain focus (cooking) limit immediate scalability, but the low-cost smartphone collection pipeline suggests a path to expansion.
