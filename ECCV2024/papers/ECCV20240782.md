## Problem

Camera-based 3D occupancy prediction has advanced rapidly for outdoor driving but remains largely unexplored for indoor scenes, which present two distinct challenges: diverse room scales demanding higher depth precision, and dense, multi-scale objects with significant size variation. Additionally, the field lacks a scalable benchmark, as prior indoor work relies on the small NYUv2 dataset (795/654 samples), limiting generalizable evaluation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240782/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image as input and produces a 3D voxel occupancy grid with per-voxel semantic class labels, structured as Y^(X×Y×Z×C). On NYUv2 the grid is 240×144×240 (evaluated at 60×36×60) with 13 classes; on the newly introduced Occ-ScanNet it is 60×60×36 with 12 classes (11 semantic + free space). The depth branch internally produces a discrete depth distribution of shape N_bins×H×W per pixel, which is then projected into the 3D voxel space.

## Method

ISO combines a frozen pre-trained DepthAnything model for coarse metric depth estimation with a learnable DepthNet that refines it into a discrete depth distribution by concatenating the metric depth with 2D image features. The key architectural contribution is the D-FLoSP (Dual Feature Line of Sight Projection) module, which simultaneously projects both the depth distribution and multi-scale 2D features (from a 2D UNet with EfficientNet-B7 encoder) into 3D voxel space via perspective projection of voxel centroids. At each scale {1, 2, 4, 8}, the projected 2D features are element-wise multiplied with the projected depth distribution, and the results are summed to form the initial 3D voxel feature volume, which a 3D UNet then processes to predict occupancy. This dual projection design explicitly integrates depth uncertainty into voxel feature learning, addressing the depth ambiguity inherent in the original FLoSP approach, while the multi-scale weighting scheme accounts for the varied object sizes characteristic of indoor scenes.

## Results

On NYUv2, ISO achieves an overall IoU of 47.11 and mIoU of 31.25, surpassing the previous best method NDC-Scene (IoU 44.17, mIoU 29.03) by 2.94 IoU and 2.22 mIoU. On the newly introduced Occ-ScanNet benchmark, ISO reaches IoU 42.16 and mIoU 28.71, outperforming MonoScene retrained on the same data (IoU 41.60, mIoU 24.62) by 0.56 IoU and 4.09 mIoU. The largest per-class gains on NYUv2 come from furniture (34.08 vs. 31.00) and objects (17.67 vs. 14.96), confirming the benefit of depth-aware multi-scale feature fusion for small and irregular indoor objects.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ISO outperforms all five baselines on NYUv2 in both IoU and mIoU. Against the closest competitor NDC-Scene [45] (ICCV 2023), ISO gains +2.94 IoU and +2.22 mIoU, with the largest per-class improvements on furniture (+3.08) and objects (+2.71). Over MonoScene [5] (CVPR 2022), the gain is +4.60 IoU and +4.31 mIoU, indicating that the D-FLoSP depth integration and multi-scale fusion substantially close the gap left by the original FLoSP projection. The older multi-input methods (LMSCNet, AICNet, 3DSketch) trail by 8–17 IoU points, underscoring that monocular RGB-only approaches with learned depth now dominate. On the new Occ-ScanNet benchmark, ISO beats MonoScene retrained on the same data by +0.56 IoU and +4.09 mIoU, with the mIoU gap driven primarily by bed (+15.40) and sofa (+6.09), suggesting the depth branch is especially beneficial for large, flat indoor surfaces at varied scales.

## Relation to Prior Work

ISO directly extends Monoscene (Cao & de Charette, CVPR 2022), which introduced the FLoSP module for lifting 2D features to 3D voxels via ray projection. The key departure is the addition of a depth branch that leverages a frozen pretrained metric depth model (Depth-Anything) and a modified D-FLoSP module that fuses depth distributions with multi-scale 2D features before 3D aggregation, addressing the depth ambiguity inherent in the original FLoSP. On the data side, the paper positions itself against the long-standing NYUv2 benchmark by introducing Occ-ScanNet, a 40× larger indoor occupancy dataset built from ScanNet and CompleteScanNet annotations, filling a gap the authors identify in scalable indoor scene research.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Occ-ScanNet benchmark (45,755/19,764 frames) is the most practically significant contribution, as it removes the small-data bottleneck that has constrained indoor occupancy research and enables more meaningful training and evaluation. However, the paper acknowledges that semantic learning remains challenged by class imbalance, the 11-class taxonomy is limited relative to real-world diversity, and scene coverage is still narrower than outdoor driving benchmarks. Open questions include whether the pretrained-depth-branch strategy generalizes to unseen indoor environments and whether richer semantic taxonomies or instance-level reasoning can be incorporated without sacrificing the voxel-level occupancy formulation.
