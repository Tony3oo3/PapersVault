## Problem

Computer vision has almost exclusively relied on high-resolution cameras with human-intuitive placement, leaving the design of simple visual sensors largely unexplored. This paper asks whether extremely low-resolution sensors—down to a single photoreceptor (1×1 pixel)—can effectively solve active vision tasks, and how the strategic design of sensor placement, orientation, and field of view affects downstream performance.

## Input / Output

The agent receives per-photoreceptor signals that are 3-dimensional RGB averages (one scalar per color channel) computed by spatially averaging a rendered pinhole camera image over the sensor's field of view; for a B×B grid this yields B² such vectors. Navigation tasks additionally provide GPS+Compass readings, while DMC tasks use only visual input. The 7-dimensional design vector per sensor (position, yaw, pitch, roll, FoV) is fed as conditioning input to the policy network. The output is a continuous or discrete control action, and the design optimization procedure produces the optimized 7D parameter vector per sensor.

## Method

The core contribution is a joint design-control optimization framework that avoids the prohibitively expensive bi-level problem of retraining a policy for every candidate sensor design. A generalist control policy (3-layer Transformer + MLP) is conditioned on the design vector θ, and a separate Gaussian design policy π_φ(θ) samples sensor configurations at the start of each episode. Both policies are trained simultaneously with PPO: the control policy updates on task rewards from actions a₁:T, while the design policy updates on the same episode reward attributed to the design action a₀=θ. The PR sensor itself is implemented by spawning a virtual camera with the design's extrinsic/intrinsic parameters, rendering the image, and spatially averaging pixel values (or splitting into B² patches for grid sensors). This zero-order optimization approach is validated across Habitat navigation (PointGoalNav, TargetNav on Matterport3D scans) and DMC continuous control tasks, and a human survey confirms that computationally found designs outperform most intuitively designed alternatives.

## Results

On Habitat navigation tasks (Matterport3D), agents with just 32 photoreceptors (2 grids of 4×4) significantly outperform blind agents and achieve performance close to a 128×128 camera agent using the same 3-layer Transformer encoder, with visual signal bandwidth of only ~1% of the camera. On DeepMind Control Suite tasks, agents with as few as 2–8 single-pixel photoreceptors significantly outperform blind agents and perform on par with camera agents (e.g., Walker:Walk reaches reward 930/1000 with 4 PRs and longer training). The computational design optimization method improves upon initial random designs in 82.5% of cases, and the optimized camera design achieves 0.483 SPL on PointGoalNav and 0.448 success rate on TargetNav, surpassing the default intuitive camera design (0.447 SPL, 0.422 success). In a real-world deployment, an agent trained in simulation with only 64 PRs and no GPS/Compass successfully navigates to a target ball 2 out of 6 times in an unknown room.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three sensor configurations: photoreceptor (PR) sensors, a high-resolution camera, and a blind agent (no visual input). PR agents with as few as 32 photoreceptors significantly outperform the blind agent on both navigation tasks and most DMC tasks, while approaching the performance of the 128×128 camera agent when using the same shallow Transformer architecture. On DMC tasks, PR agents with 2–8 single-pixel sensors achieve rewards close to the camera baseline, though the camera agent benefits from more specialized RL algorithms and longer training in the literature. For sensor design, the computational optimization method outperforms both random designs (improving 82.5% of initializations) and human-intuitive designs collected via survey, which show high variance with some designs performing near-blind levels. The optimized camera design also beats the default Habitat simulator camera by ~8% SPL on PointGoalNav and ~6% success rate on TargetNav.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in computer vision of using high-resolution cameras with algorithmic improvements, instead exploring the extreme low end of visual sensing with photoreceptors as simple as 1×1 pixels. Methodologically, it builds most directly on the joint design-control optimization framework of Transform2Act [69], which applied bilevel-to-joint optimization to robot morphology, but here redirects that machinery to the under-explored problem of visual sensor design for active vision tasks with non-differentiable rewards. It also connects to the broader computational design literature (evolutionary morphology, differentiable simulators) and zero-order optimization, while drawing conceptual motivation from biological visual ecology (dragonfly acute zones, fish eye positioning).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges the near-universal assumption in robotics that high-resolution visual input is necessary for effective perception, showing that a handful of well-placed photoreceptors can approach camera-level performance on navigation and control tasks. The computational design optimization method provides a practical, generalizable tool for tailoring sensor placement to specific agents and tasks, and the human survey demonstrates that intuitive design is unreliable, reinforcing the need for automated approaches. Key limitations include reliance on local optimization (multiple local optima observed), simple robot morphologies, absence of practical cost/size constraints in the objective, and limited real-world validation (2/6 success in a single room). Open questions include scaling to complex morphologies, incorporating global search, and extending to tasks requiring fine-grained visual discrimination.
