## Problem

Dense neural SLAM systems based on implicit representations (e.g., NeRF) assume static scenes, so dynamic objects break the geometric and photometric consistency that underpins joint pose-map optimization, leading to system collapse. Prior work either removes dynamic objects via motion segmentation or relies on pre-defined semantic categories, but no method has deployed a NeRF-based SLAM framework in dynamic environments while adaptively deciding what to retain and what to discard from the map.

## Input / Output

The system consumes a streaming sequence of RGB-D images with known camera intrinsics, storing a keyframe every 5 frames. It produces per-frame camera poses, a continually updated SDF-based neural radiance field (128 samples per ray, 10 cm truncation distance), and an instance-level motion segmentation mask derived from a continually learned binary classifier. During tracking, 1024 static pixels are sampled per frame; during bundle adjustment, 2048 pixels from past keyframes and the current frame are used.

## Method

The framework jointly and continually optimizes three coupled variables—camera pose, a Co-SLAM-style SDF neural map, and a binary instance-level motion classifier—using photometric and geometric losses computed only over static regions. The classifier decomposes each frame into instances via FastSAM, encodes them with a visual context encoder (BLIP, CLIP, or DinoV2), and feeds the embeddings into a two-layer MLP that predicts static/dynamic status; it is updated via a bidirectional geometric inconsistency check (rendered vs. observed depth) with a replay buffer to prevent the classifier itself from catastrophically forgetting previously learned dynamic objects. The key insight is that forgetting is repurposed as a feature: by excluding dynamic-object pixels from the experience-replay buffer, the neural map naturally forgets moved or removed objects through continual distribution shift, while invariant static geometry is retained. An optional position-feature concatenation allows the classifier to track motion status per spatial location, so a relocated object is forgotten at its old position and memorized at its new one.

## Results

The paper evaluates camera tracking accuracy using ATE (RMS) in meters on 15 sequences across the Bonn and TUM RGB-D datasets. The proposed method achieves the best or near-best ATE on the majority of sequences, with particularly strong results on the challenging Bonn 'crowd' sequences (0.107–0.200 m) where Co-SLAM fails entirely and DynaSLAM degrades to 0.835–1.217 m. On the TUM fr3_sitting_static sequence, the method achieves the lowest error of 0.007 m, outperforming all baselines. Co-SLAM fails on 7 out of 15 sequences, while the proposed method completes all sequences successfully.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Co-SLAM (the static-scene baseline the method builds upon), the proposed approach wins decisively: Co-SLAM fails on 7 of 15 sequences, while the proposed method completes all of them, and on the 8 sequences where Co-SLAM succeeds, the proposed method achieves lower ATE in every case (e.g., 0.130 vs. 0.634 on Bonn/synchronous, 0.007 vs. 0.011 on TUM/fr3_sitting_static). Against DynaSLAM, the proposed method is clearly superior on the high-dynamics Bonn crowd sequences (0.107–0.200 m vs. 0.835–1.217 m) and on TUM walking sequences, but DynaSLAM retains an edge on simpler TUM sequences (e.g., 0.014 vs. 0.025 on fr3_walking_static, 0.009 vs. 0.018 on fr3_sitting_xyz) and on Bonn kidnapping sequences. Against ReFusion, the proposed method wins on 10 of 15 sequences, with the largest margins on Bonn/synchronous (0.130 vs. 0.441) and TUM/fr3_sitting_halfsphere (0.039 vs. 0.110). Against StaticFusion, the proposed method wins on 11 of 15 sequences, with the most dramatic improvement on Bonn/crowd (0.116 vs. 3.586). Overall, the method's key advantage is robustness under high dynamic content, where feature-based and residual-based baselines degrade or fail entirely.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Co-SLAM's SDF-based neural map with hash-grid encoding and volume rendering, and on the experience-replay continual learning paradigm established by iMAP and Continual Neural Mapping for test-time map optimization. It departs from the traditional dynamic SLAM line (DynaSLAM, ReFusion, StaticFusion) that relies on per-frame motion segmentation or predefined semantic categories, instead introducing a continually-learned instance-level classifier (FastSAM + visual context encoder) that implicitly tracks per-object motion status across time. The key conceptual departure is reframing catastrophic forgetting—a problem continual learning typically aims to avoid—as a desirable mechanism for naturally erasing dynamic objects from the neural map, rather than explicitly inpainting or deleting map regions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first dense neural-representation SLAM system demonstrated in dynamic environments, opening a path toward combining the expressive power of implicit maps with robustness to scene changes. However, the evaluation is limited to small indoor sequences (TUM, Bonn), and the pipeline's reliance on FastSAM for instance segmentation plus a visual encoder adds substantial per-frame computational overhead that limits real-time deployment. Open questions include scalability to large outdoor scenes, handling of non-rigid or partially occluded dynamics, and whether the geometric-inconsistency-based motion detection generalizes to photometric changes (lighting, weather). The bidirectional check and position-concatenation trick for maintaining instant object state are practical but ad hoc, suggesting the classifier design space is not yet fully explored.
