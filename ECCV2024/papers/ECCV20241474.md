## Problem

Prior scanpath prediction models operate at the pixel level using saliency maps, but psychophysical evidence shows that visual attention in goal-directed tasks is guided by objects rather than pixel intensities. This gap is especially problematic in cluttered scenes with many similar distractors (e.g., book covers on a shelf), where pixel-level models struggle to capture the object-centric nature of human search behavior.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241474/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a target object image (e.g., a single book cover) and a scene image containing the target among distractor objects arranged in a regular 2D grid (e.g., 6×14 or 3×9 patches). It outputs an autoregressive sequence of object-level fixations (not pixel coordinates), generated step-by-step until an end-of-sequence token is sampled. The object attention module is designed to handle scenes with varying numbers of objects without retraining, and the target is injected as an additional token into the encoder sequence.

## Method

OAT is an encoder-decoder transformer where each object is embedded via a CNN visual descriptor concatenated with a novel distance-based positional encoding (DPE) whose cosine similarity between locations decays following a Gaussian distribution, explicitly encoding spatial proximity. The target object is appended as an extra token in the encoder, enabling target-dependent encoding of all scene objects. The decoder processes past fixations autoregressively and uses cross-attention with encoder outputs, while a dedicated Object Attention (OA) module computes a probability distribution over all objects for the next fixation by multiplying encoder and decoder attention embeddings. Training is supervised with cross-entropy loss over the object sequence, with no pre-training or data augmentation. The DPE is pre-trained separately with a Gaussian-similarity loss before end-to-end fine-tuning, and the OA module's cross-attention mechanism is shown to be critical for modeling revisit and re-fixation behaviors.

## Results

On the Amazon book cover dataset, OAT achieves an Overall behavioural difference of only 7.4% from human scanpaths, compared to 40.4% for the best baseline (Gazeformer) and 146.6% for IRL. OAT also attains a Sequence Score of 0.950 and Fixation Edit Distance of 0.68, substantially outperforming all baselines (next best: Gazeformer SS=0.098, FED=8.65). On the authors' collected yogurt/wine dataset, the behavioural difference drops to just 3.1%, with OAT matching human search (62.9% vs 63.9%), revisit (4.0% vs 4.4%), and refixation (33.0% vs 31.7%) proportions almost exactly.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OAT outperforms all five baselines across every metric on both datasets. Against the pixel-level saliency baselines (Center [11] and WTA [9,13]), OAT achieves a 14× lower Overall behavioural difference (0.074 vs 1.053/1.186) and a 17× lower FED (0.68 vs 11.73/11.60), primarily because these methods cannot identify the target (accuracy ~1–1.3% vs OAT's 89.4%). Against IRL [34], which does model target relevance, OAT still wins by 20× on Overall (0.074 vs 1.466) and 20× on FED (0.68 vs 13.70); IRL over-predicts scanpath length (12.3 vs 8.4 fixations) and revisit rate (14.1% vs 2.3% human). Against Gazeformer [21], the closest competitor, OAT achieves a 5.5× lower Overall (0.074 vs 0.404) and 12.7× lower FED (0.68 vs 8.65); Gazeformer's key weakness is predicting all fixations simultaneously without conditioning on past gaze history, leading to under-predicted scanpath length (6.0 vs 8.4) and over-predicted refixations (15.6% vs 11.9% human). On the collected yogurt/wine dataset, OAT's advantage over Gazeformer narrows slightly on FED (7.19 vs 7.69) but remains decisive on Overall (0.031 vs 0.614) and SS (0.346 vs 0.177).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the goal-directed scanpath prediction line of work (Gazeformer, IRL-based methods) by shifting the unit of prediction from pixels to objects. It builds directly on the Gazeformer transformer architecture but introduces three key departures: a target-dependent encoder that treats the target as an extra token, a decoder that conditions on the full fixation history rather than predicting all fixations at once, and a distance-based positional encoding (DPE) that explicitly encodes spatial proximity via a Gaussian-decay loss. The conceptual motivation draws on psychophysical evidence that visual attention operates on objects rather than pixels, particularly in goal-directed search.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that object-level modeling captures human gaze behavior in cluttered, repetitive-object scenes (bookshelves, product arrays) far more accurately than pixel-level approaches, with a behavioral difference of only 3.1% on the collected dataset. However, the evaluation is limited to regular grid layouts with uniform object sizes; the authors acknowledge that extending to non-uniform shapes and natural scenes remains open. The novel behavioral metric (comparing search/revisit/refix distributions) is a useful addition to the scanpath evaluation toolkit, and the DPE component offers a reusable technique for any task where spatial proximity matters. Generalization to unseen layouts and targets is demonstrated but on a single held-out pair, leaving broader generalization claims somewhat under-tested.
