## Problem

Chin et al. (ACCV 2020) proposed computing Boolean influence via the Bernstein-Vazirani quantum circuit for robust fitting, but relied on an assumed quantum implementation of the ℓ∞ feasibility test that was never concretely provided, precluding any real quantum hardware demonstration. This paper closes that gap by designing an explicit quantum circuit for the ℓ∞ feasibility test in the 1D point-fitting case, enabling the first execution of quantum robust fitting on a real gate quantum computer (IonQ Aria).
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240007/cropped_page5_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes N noisy data points (1D values for point fitting, or linearized residuals from point correspondences for fundamental matrix estimation) and a user-supplied inlier threshold ε. The quantum circuit consumes 3N+2C+1 qubits, where C is the bit precision of the data, and the data must be pre-sorted in decreasing order and offset so the minimum is zero. The output is a per-point Boolean influence value (probability that toggling that point changes subset feasibility), which is thresholded to classify inliers/outliers and then used for least-squares model estimation.

## Method

The core contribution is a quantum subcircuit U_f that implements the ℓ∞ feasibility test for 1D point fitting within the Bernstein-Vazirani circuit. U_f uses ancillary qubits to (i) select the maximum and minimum data values in the subset encoded by |z⟩ via dedicated selector circuits A and B, (ii) encode the actual data values into quantum registers V1 and V2, (iii) compute their absolute difference using a QFT-based subtractor S1, and (iv) compare the result against 2ε via a comparator S2, with the final bit of |v2⟩ indicating feasibility. For higher-dimensional tasks like fundamental matrix estimation, the 1D point-fitting influences are accumulated across T RANSAC-style hypotheses by log-averaging the per-hypothesis influences, and the resulting composite influences are thresholded at multiple levels to select inlier subsets for least-squares fitting.

## Results

On the fundamental matrix estimation task (recall at NSGD ≤ 0.05), the influence-based method (Alg. 3) achieves 62.8% on TUM, 85.4% on KITTI, 85.1% on Tanks & Temples, and 50.3% on CPC. For inlier/outlier classification quality (ROC AUC), the accumulated influences from Alg. 3 score 0.83 (TUM), 0.94 (KITTI), 0.82 (T&T), and 0.87 (CPC), consistently outperforming both RANSAC residuals and the original influence method of [19]. On the quantum hardware feasibility test (IonQ Aria, M=50), approximate influences for small circuits (≤13 qubits) closely match true values, while larger circuits (19 qubits) show increased deviation due to NISQ-era noise.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against RANSAC [24], the influence-based method wins on 3 of 4 datasets for fundamental matrix recall (TUM: 62.8 vs 60.9; T&T: 85.1 vs 77.6; CPC: 50.3 vs 42.0) but narrowly loses on KITTI (85.4 vs 87.0). For ROC AUC inlier/outlier classification, the method dominates RANSAC on all four datasets (e.g., 0.94 vs 0.86 on KITTI, 0.83 vs 0.71 on TUM). Against USAC-openCV, results are mixed: the method wins on TUM (62.8 vs 54.0) and KITTI (85.4 vs 83.8) but loses on T&T (85.1 vs 88.1) and CPC (50.3 vs 52.5). Compared to the original quantum influence method of Chin et al. [19], the accumulated influence approach (Alg. 3) improves ROC AUC on TUM (0.83 vs 0.76), KITTI (0.94 vs 0.86), and CPC (0.87 vs 0.85), while tying on T&T (0.82). Overall, the method is competitive with but does not uniformly surpass the strongest classical baselines (CF-RSC from [11] leads on all four datasets), positioning it as a promising quantum-classical hybrid rather than a state-of-the-art classical replacement.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Chin et al. [19] (Quantum Robust Fitting, ACCV 2020), which proposed using the Bernstein-Vazirani circuit to compute Boolean influence for consensus maximization but left the critical ℓ∞ feasibility oracle U_f unspecified. The authors here supply that missing quantum subcircuit for the 1D point-fitting case, enabling the first execution of the full quantum influence pipeline on a real gate quantum computer (IonQ Aria). The influence-as-outlyingness paradigm itself is inherited from Suter et al. [52] and Tennakoon et al. [53], while the accumulation strategy for higher-dimensional models (fundamental matrix) is a new contribution that repurposes the 1D quantum circuit within a RANSAC-style sampling loop.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper closes a long-standing feasibility gap in quantum computer vision by demonstrating that the BV-based influence computation can actually run on hardware, not just in simulation. However, the NISQ-era constraints are severe: the quantum circuit is validated on only 4 data points (20 qubits), and the fundamental matrix experiments rely entirely on classical influence computation (Alg. 1) due to hardware limits. Open questions include whether the ℓ∞ feasibility circuit can be generalized beyond 1D, how error correction will affect circuit depth requirements, and whether the influence-accumulation strategy scales to problems where quantum speedup over classical sampling becomes meaningful. For the broader CV community, the practical takeaway is that influence-based robust fitting (even computed classically) is competitive with RANSAC on fundamental matrix estimation, but the quantum contribution remains a proof-of-concept.
