## Problem

Separating transmitted and reflected light from composite images through transparent surfaces is a highly ill-posed problem. Paired flash/no-flash subtraction is effective but requires the camera to be perfectly stationary between captures, making it impractical in the wild. Existing unsupervised 3D reflection separation methods (e.g., NeRFReN) avoid illumination control but fail under strong reflections, leaving a gap between the physical power of flash cues and the flexibility of unpaired multi-view 3D reconstruction.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20242188/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes two unpaired multi-view image sequences of the same scene—approximately 15 flash and 15 no-flash views at 1200×800 resolution—that must cover similar perspective ranges but need not be spatially aligned. It produces four independent 3D Gaussian Splat representations (transmitted-with-flash, transmitted-no-flash, reflected scene, and reflective fraction map), from which novel views and depth maps can be rendered for each separated component. A notable detail is that the physical flash/no-flash model operates in RAW space, while L1 and DSSIM losses are applied after gamma correction (γ=0.22) to avoid brightness bias in the learned representation.

## Method

Flash-Splat extends 3D Gaussian Splatting to be illumination-aware by maintaining four separate 3DGS representations (T_F, T_N, R, β) where R and β are shared across flash and no-flash views, while T_F and T_N are linked by a near-linear scaling relationship. The key mechanism is constructing two types of 'pseudo-pairs' from unpaired data: 2D pseudo-pairs, where novel view synthesis fills in the missing flash or no-flash counterpart at a captured viewpoint, and 3D pseudo-pairs, where separate 3D scenes are reconstructed from flash-only and no-flash-only views. A Pearson linearity loss between T_F and T_N rendered at 2D pseudo-pair views enforces that the flash/no-flash difference is reflection-free, directly reducing the ill-posedness of separation. The 3D pseudo-pair point clouds are used to initialize the four 3DGSs by classifying points as transmitted (intensity-increased) or reflected (intensity-unchanged), a strategy incompatible with implicit representations like NeRF. Optimization alternates single-view rendering: for a captured flash view, the method renders the composite from T_F, R, β and computes photometric loss against ground truth while simultaneously applying the Pearson linearity loss between T_F and T_N renders at that view.

## Results

The paper evaluates Flash-Splat primarily through qualitative visual comparisons on a custom real-world flash/no-flash dataset (30 views per scene: 15 flash, 15 no-flash, captured with a Canon Rebel R7). No quantitative metrics (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS) are reported in the main text. The authors state that baselines 'fail completely to produce a reasonable separation,' while Flash-Splat accurately reconstructs both transmitted and reflected 3D scenes. For novel view synthesis, Flash-Splat matches dedicated NVS methods (NeRF, 3DGS), and for depth estimation it outperforms NeRFReN on both transmitted and reflected components.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Flash-Splat is compared against five baselines, all evaluated on the same real-world scenes. Against NeRFReN [13] (the state-of-the-art unsupervised 3D reflection separation method), Flash-Splat achieves dramatically better separation; the paper states NeRFReN 'fails completely to produce a reasonable separation' under similar illumination conditions, while Flash-Splat cleanly isolates both transmitted and reflected components (Figures 5–8). Against Dong et al. [7] (a supervised single-image reflection removal method), Flash-Splat also substantially outperforms, producing coherent 3D scenes rather than 2D image-level results. For novel view synthesis (Figure 9), Flash-Splat matches the quality of dedicated NVS methods NeRF [36] and 3DGS [20], demonstrating that the reflection-separation task does not compromise rendering fidelity. For depth estimation (Figure 10), Flash-Splat produces depth maps that correctly differentiate transmitted and reflected geometry, whereas MiDaS [39,40] (a monocular depth baseline) cannot separate the two layers, and NeRFReN produces noticeably worse depth for both components.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two established lines of work: 2D flash/no-flash reflection removal (which requires tightly-aligned paired captures) and 3D inverse-rendering-based reflection separation (NeRFReN, ReflectionsIBR), which does not exploit illumination control. The key conceptual departure is using 3D Gaussian Splatting's novel-view-synthesis capability to construct 2D and 3D 'pseudo-pairs' from unpaired flash and no-flash views, thereby removing the paired-capture constraint while retaining the physical prior that flash illuminates transmission but not reflection. The 3D pseudo-pair initialization (classifying sparse points as transmitted vs. reflected by intensity change) is specific to point-cloud-compatible representations like 3DGS and has no direct analogue in NeRF-based methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By eliminating the need for paired captures, Flash-Splat makes flash-based 3D reflection separation practical for in-the-wild use, and as a byproduct provides separate novel-view synthesis and depth estimation for both transmitted and reflected scenes. Key limitations remain: the method requires flash and no-flash views to cover similar perspective ranges, cannot handle curved reflective surfaces or double reflections, and is restricted to static scenes. An open question is whether the 3D pseudo-pair initialization trick can be adapted to implicit representations, and whether the approach generalizes to more complex multi-layer or non-planar glass geometries.
