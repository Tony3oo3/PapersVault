## Problem

Stick-figure representations used in 2D-to-3D pose lifting discard occlusion information inherent in real images, creating mathematical ambiguities (e.g., indistinguishable left/right viewpoints when limbs overlap). Additionally, existing lifting methods generalize poorly across datasets because they depend on z-score normalization and camera viewing angles that shift between datasets. The paper tackles both issues by replacing stick figures with occlusion-preserving abstract images and training on synthetically generated viewpoints.

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes a single RGB image as input and produces a 14-joint 3D pose as output. Stage 1 converts the image into a flat abstract image via predicted 2D keypoint heatmaps and a 9×9 binary limb occlusion matrix; Stage 2 consumes this abstract image (or a cube-variant rendered from GT 3D keypoints during training) and outputs a 64×64 viewpoint heatmap and a 13×128×128 pose heatmap, from which the 3D pose is reconstructed using preset bone lengths. The reconstructed pose is related to ground truth by a rotation, so MPJPE is reported directly without Procrustes alignment under Protocol #1.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-stage framework that decomposes 3D pose estimation into two orthogonal, 1-to-1 CNN mappings: camera viewpoint in subject-centered coordinates and observed pose in camera coordinates. Abstract images—opaque, color-coded limb blocks (cube or flat variants)—preserve occlusion and part identity, and can be rendered from GT 3D keypoints at an unlimited number of synthetic spherical camera viewpoints, eliminating overfitting and enabling cross-dataset generalization without z-score normalization. Viewpoint is encoded as a wrapped Gaussian heatmap on a 64×64 cylindrical grid whose seam is always placed behind the subject (rotation-invariant), while pose is encoded as per-limb spherical-angle heatmaps on a 128×128 grid with wrapping in both axes. Both networks use an HRNet backbone pretrained on MPII/COCO with fully convolutional L2 supervision, and the image-to-abstract Stage 1 adds a small branch predicting the limb occlusion matrix alongside 2D keypoints.

## Results

On the cross-dataset benchmark (the paper's primary focus), the Cube variant achieves MPJPE of 92.31 mm on GPA, 95.83 mm on 3DPW, and 65.62 mm on SURREAL, outperforming Martinez et al. (117.37/135.53/108.63) and Zhao et al. (115.01/154.3/103.75) by 30–40%. In PA-MPJPE on 3DPW, the Cube variant reaches 64.28 mm versus 129.7–154.3 mm for prior methods, roughly a 2× improvement. On same-dataset Human3.6M, the Cube variant achieves 33.5 mm average MPJPE (competitive with the best baselines of 32.7–39.9 mm) and 29.1 mm PA-MPJPE, while the Hybrid variant (trained on mixed Flat+Cube inputs) scores 48.8 mm MPJPE and 39.7 mm PA-MPJPE.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The Cube variant outperforms Martinez et al. [23] and Zhao et al. [45] by 30–40% on all three cross-dataset benchmarks (e.g., 92.31 vs. 117.37 mm on GPA; 95.83 vs. 154.3 mm on 3DPW), even though those baselines benefit from test-set z-score normalization that is infeasible in practice. Against Wang et al. [39], the only prior work with extensive 4-dataset cross-dataset analysis, the Cube variant leads on GPA (92.31 vs. 98.3), 3DPW (95.83 vs. 124.2), and SURREAL (65.62 vs. 114.0). Goel et al. [9] achieves a lower 3DPW MPJPE (70.0 vs. 95.83), but was trained on two 3D pose datasets (H36M + 3DHP) rather than H36M alone, making the comparison less direct. On PA-MPJPE for 3DPW, the Cube variant (64.28 mm) roughly halves the error of the best prior single-dataset methods (129.7–154.3 mm). On same-dataset Human3.6M, the Cube variant (33.5 mm MPJPE) is competitive with but slightly behind the strongest GT-2D-keypoint baselines (Zhai et al. [43] at 32.7 mm; Zhou et al. [46] at 39.9 mm), while the Hybrid variant (48.8 mm) trails more noticeably, reflecting the cost of the image-to-abstract Stage 1 pipeline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant stick-figure paradigm in 2D-to-3D pose lifting (Martinez [23], Zhao [45], Li [16], Ci [7]) by replacing see-through limb segments with opaque, color-coded rectangular blocks that preserve occlusion ordering. While opaque 3D limbs have been used previously for ray-traced occlusion determination (Cheng [6]), this is the first work to use them as a direct CNN training input and to exploit them for unlimited synthetic viewpoint augmentation from 3D GT keypoints. The cylindrical heatmap encoding for both viewpoint and pose draws on the broader heatmap-regression tradition (Pavlakos [27], Zhou [46]) but introduces wrapping at the seam to avoid the 2π discontinuity that plagues rotation-matrix and quaternion encodings (Wang [39], Ghezelghieh [8]). The HRNet backbone [31] and the two-stage decomposition (image-to-abstract, then abstract-to-pose) are standard architectural choices repurposed for this new intermediate representation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Cross-dataset generalization remains a critical open problem in 3D pose estimation, and this paper demonstrates that the root cause is partly the loss of occlusion information in stick-figure representations combined with viewpoint bias from limited training cameras. The synthetic viewpoint augmentation strategy—rendering abstract images from arbitrary cameras given only 3D GT keypoints—offers a practical, scalable data augmentation technique that could be adopted by other lifting methods. Limitations include the added error from the Stage 1 image-to-abstract network, the reliance on 3D GT keypoints for Stage 2 training, and the relatively simple rectangular limb geometry that may not capture complex body shapes. Open questions include whether the approach extends to video (temporal consistency), whether richer limb geometry (e.g., tapered or articulated) would further improve same-dataset accuracy, and how the method handles occlusion by external objects rather than self-occlusion.
