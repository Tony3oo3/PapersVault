## Problem

Existing visual SLAM pipelines degrade significantly on casually captured videos because motion blur from camera movement and per-frame appearance changes (auto-exposure, white balance) corrupt the photometric signals used for tracking and mapping. These degradations accumulate over the incremental SLAM process, producing blurry maps and inaccurate trajectories. No prior SLAM system explicitly models the physical image formation process to jointly recover sharp geometry and correct camera poses from such degraded inputs.

## Input / Output

The method consumes casually captured RGB or RGBD video sequences (with inherent motion blur and varying exposure/white balance) and outputs a sharp HDR radiance field map in linear color space, a refined camera trajectory, and per-frame in-camera parameters (exposure time, three-channel white balance gains, and a 256-point camera response function grid). It operates as a drop-in module: the base SLAM pipeline (NeRF-SLAM or SplaTAM) provides initial poses and map structure, while I²-SLAM augments the rendering loss with the full image-formation chain. For RGBD inputs, a single depth pose is selected per frame from the exposure window to constrain the trajectory.

## Method

I²-SLAM inverts the physical imaging pipeline within the SLAM optimization loop. Motion blur is simulated by rendering N_cam=5 sharp HDR images from virtual poses linearly interpolated (Slerp for rotation, Lerp for translation) between start and end poses over the exposure window, then averaging them. Appearance variation is modeled by a differentiable tone-mapping chain: the HDR radiance is scaled by exposure time, multiplied by per-channel white balance gains, and passed through a monotonically increasing camera response function parameterized as a 256-point grid per channel with a leaky-clipping wrapper to preserve gradients outside [0,1]. The map representation (NeRF or 3DGS) is modified to output linear HDR radiance rather than LDR color, which linearizes the color space and simplifies both blur simulation and appearance modeling. A trajectory regularization loss constrains the blur displacement to align with the global SLAM trajectory and scale proportionally to exposure time and temporal velocity, with an extrapolation-based initialization for the start/end poses. The total objective combines an image rendering L1 loss, a depth rendering loss (applied at the best-matching pose within the exposure window), and the trajectory loss, all optimized jointly via gradient descent.

## Results

On TUM-RGBD (RGB-SLAM), I²-SLAM improves PSNR over NeRF-SLAM† by 1.26 dB (fr1/desk: 27.23 vs 25.97), 2.09 dB (fr2/xyz: 32.06 vs 29.97), and 4.19 dB (fr3/office: 28.91 vs 24.72), with corresponding SSIM and LPIPS gains. On the synthetic dataset, PSNR gains reach up to 4.07 dB (IF1: 30.16 vs 26.09). For tracking, ATE-RMSE drops from 7.13 cm to 1.95 cm on fr3/office and from 3.97 cm to 1.50 cm on SP. In the RGBD setting (SplaTAM baseline), I²-SLAM achieves up to 6.77 dB PSNR improvement on ScanNet 0785-00 (26.40 vs 19.63) and 4.80 dB on synthetic SP (26.18 vs 21.38).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against NeRF-SLAM† (RGB-SLAM), I²-SLAM wins on every rendering metric (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, Depth L1) across all 8 test scenes, with the largest PSNR gain of 4.19 dB on fr3/office and 4.07 dB on synthetic IF1. Tracking also improves substantially: ATE-RMSE drops from 7.13 cm to 1.95 cm on fr3/office and from 3.97 cm to 1.50 cm on SP, confirming that modeling the image formation process corrects pose errors that accumulate in the baseline. Against SplaTAM (RGBD-SLAM), I²-SLAM achieves clear rendering gains on most scenes, most dramatically on ScanNet 0785-00 (PSNR 26.40 vs 19.63, +6.77 dB) and synthetic SP (26.18 vs 21.38, +4.80 dB), where dynamic appearance changes are most severe. However, tracking improvements in the RGBD setting are modest (e.g., 1.41 vs 1.80 cm on 0024-01, but 3.25 vs 3.01 cm on 0031-00), since depth measurements largely constrain the camera pose. The paper also shows that I²-SLAM with only 20% of the optimization iterations (I²-SLAM-S) matches SplaTAM's runtime while still outperforming it, whereas SplaTAM-S degrades significantly under the same budget.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a plug-in module for existing dense visual SLAM pipelines (NeRF-SLAM, SplaTAM) rather than a new SLAM architecture. It draws on three distinct research threads: (1) motion deblurring in neural rendering (DeblurNeRF, BAD-NeRF), which handles blur but outside a SLAM tracking loop; (2) HDR radiance field reconstruction (HDR-NeRF, HDR-PleNoxels), which models exposure but not in an incremental SLAM context; and (3) classical HDR imaging (Debevec & Malik), which motivates the linear radiance map and per-frame tone-mapping parameters. The key departure is unifying motion-blur simulation (multi-pose integration over exposure time) and per-frame appearance variation (exposure, white balance, CRF) into a single differentiable image-formation loss that is jointly optimized with the SLAM trajectory, plus a trajectory regularization that anchors the blur motion to the globally estimated camera path.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Casually captured video is the standard input for deployed SLAM systems, yet most prior methods assume clean, consistently-exposed frames; this paper shows that explicitly inverting the imaging pipeline yields both sharper HDR maps and more accurate trajectories, with the largest tracking gains (e.g., 7.13→1.95 cm ATE on TUM fr3/office) on sequences with heavy motion blur. The modular design means the technique can be dropped into any dense SLAM method without architectural changes, which has broad practical appeal. However, the runtime cost is substantial (~5× slower mapping than SplaTAM), and the authors acknowledge that the multi-camera blur simulation is the bottleneck; an efficient approximation remains an open problem. The paper also introduces a synthetic dataset with realistic motion blur and auto-exposure, filling a gap in SLAM evaluation benchmarks that previously assumed ideal capture conditions.
