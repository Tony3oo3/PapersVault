## Problem

Existing biometric loss functions (triplet, softmax) are designed for closed-set identification and treat genuine and imposter scores symmetrically, ignoring the fact that in open-set evaluation (FNIR@FPIR) the detection threshold is set by the maximum imposter score distribution. This asymmetry means high-scoring non-mated probes disproportionately raise the threshold and cause false negatives, yet conventional losses give them no special gradient treatment. Additionally, gait recognition and person reID benchmarks lack official open-set evaluation protocols entirely.

## Input / Output

The method is modality-agnostic and operates on pre-extracted biometric features (face embeddings, gait sequence features, person reID features) paired with gallery templates. During training, each minibatch is partitioned into a gallery set and a probe set, with a random percentage of subjects designated as mated (their exemplars split between gallery and probe) and the rest as non-mated (all exemplars placed in probe only). The model outputs similarity scores between each probe and every gallery subject, from which both a detection decision (score above/below threshold) and an identification rank are derived.

## Method

The paper introduces two complementary loss functions applied on top of any existing biometric backbone. The Identification-Detection Loss (IDL) multiplies a detection score—sigmoid of (genuine similarity minus a threshold sampled from non-mated scores in the batch)—by an identification score based on a differentiable softrank that counts how many gallery subjects outscore the correct match; the product is maximized so the model must both exceed the threshold and rank the correct gallery subject first. The Relative Threshold Minimization (RTM) loss minimizes the softmax-weighted average of a non-mated probe's similarity scores across the gallery, which adaptively assigns larger gradients to non-mated probes whose scores are relatively high within the batch, directly suppressing the maximum-per-probe imposter scores that set the open-set threshold. The total loss is L = L_IDL + λ·L_RTM, and the approach requires no architectural changes or additional test-time computation.

## Results

On gait recognition, applying the proposed loss to SwinGait-3D improves rank-1 accuracy from 72.4% to 75.3% (+2.9%) on Gait3D and from 79.3% to 80.4% on GREW, while reducing FNIR@1%FPIR from 83.50% to 79.30% on Gait3D and from 58.32% to 57.29% on GREW. On face recognition (IJB-S), AdaFace+Ours boosts rank-1 from 68.44% to 69.92% on Surveillance-Single and cuts FNIR@0.1%FPIR from 45.98% to 43.48% on Surveillance-Booking. On person reID, CAL+Ours reaches 84.8% rank-1 and 30.81% FNIR@1%FPIR on CCVID, and 55.1% rank-1 and 64.80% FNIR@1%FPIR on MEVID, outperforming all prior methods by 2–3 points in both metrics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed loss is applied as a plug-in to existing SOTA backbones and consistently improves both closed-set and open-set metrics across all three modalities. In gait recognition, SwinGait-3D+Ours outperforms SwinGait-3D by +2.9% rank-1 and −4.20% FNIR@1%FPIR on Gait3D, and GaitBase+Ours reduces FNIR@1%FPIR from 86.43% to 82.99% on Gait3D while matching rank-1. All earlier gait baselines (GaitSet, GaitPart, GaitGL, SMPLGait) are substantially outperformed, with FNIR gaps exceeding 10 percentage points. In face recognition, AdaFace+Ours beats AdaFace on both IJB-S protocols, improving rank-1 by ~1.5% and reducing FNIR@0.1%FPIR by 2.5 points on Surveillance-Booking. In person reID, CAL+Ours surpasses CAL by +2.6% rank-1 and −2.15% FNIR on MEVID, and by +2.2% rank-1 and −3.08% FNIR on CCVID, while also outperforming AGRL, TCLNet, and PSTA by 7+ points in rank-1 on MEVID. The paper reports a two-sample t-test p-value of 0.052% for the CCVID improvement, confirming statistical significance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant closed-set paradigm in biometric recognition by identifying a specific structural mismatch: standard losses (triplet, softmax/arcface) treat genuine and imposter scores symmetrically, while the de facto open-set metric FNIR@FPIR depends asymmetrically on the maximum-per-probe non-mated score distribution. It builds on the open-set face recognition evaluation protocols (IJB-S, FRVT) and the EVM score-level approach [18], but shifts the intervention from post-hoc score calibration to training-time loss design, simulating open-set gallery/probe splits within mini-batches. The paper also extends the open-set evaluation framework to gait recognition and person reID, where no official open-set protocols previously existed, and distinguishes its problem from open-set image classification by noting that biometric test subjects are always disjoint from training subjects.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical relevance is high: real-world biometric deployments (surveillance, access control) are inherently open-set, yet the field has largely optimized for closed-set metrics. The finding that detection failures (genuine score below threshold) dominate over identification failures (wrong rank) across all three modalities suggests that future open-set improvements should prioritize threshold reduction over rank accuracy. The modality-agnostic loss design (IDL + RTM) is a drop-in addition to any existing biometric backbone with negligible FLOP overhead, making it broadly adoptable. However, the paper explicitly does not explore dedicated architectures for open-set settings, and the open-set benchmarks for gait/reID rely on a 50-trial median protocol that, while more stable than single-trial, still introduces variance (±5–8% std) that complicates fine-grained comparisons.
