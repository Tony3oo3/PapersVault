## Problem

Assessing the reliability of semantic segmentation models for safety-critical applications (e.g., autonomous driving) requires evaluating them under covariate shifts and on out-of-distribution objects, but collecting and annotating real OOD data is prohibitively expensive and limited in scale. Existing synthetic perturbation benchmarks (noise, blur) do not capture realistic distribution shifts, and no prior work has demonstrated that generative-model-produced data can faithfully proxy real OOD performance for segmentation reliability assessment.

## Input / Output

For covariate-shift generation, the pipeline consumes in-domain segmentation masks from Cityscapes validation plus a text prompt (CLIP-interrogator caption concatenated with a domain descriptor such as 'in snow' or 'in night') and outputs a synthetic RGB image aligned to the mask's layout in the target OOD domain. For OOD-object inpainting, it takes an in-domain Cityscapes RGB image, a sampled square region, and a text prompt naming the OOD object, then outputs a full-resolution inpainted image together with a pixel-accurate OOD mask extracted via Grounded Segment Anything. Downstream, these synthetic pairs yield per-model robustness scores (mIoU), a single temperature-scaling parameter for calibration, and OOD detection metrics (FPR95, AUROC, AUPR_IN/OUT) without ever requiring real OOD data.

## Method

The core mechanism is a fine-tuned Stable Diffusion 1.5 repurposed as a ControlNet, trained exclusively on Cityscapes images and masks with CLIP-interrogator captions as text prompts, enabling mask-conditioned, text-steerable scene generation. For covariate shifts, zero-shot prompting with a simple template [<caption>, in <domain>] generates OOD-domain images aligned to in-domain masks; for OOD objects, a RePaint-style partial inpainting strategy (inpainting only the inner half of a cropped box, then a light reverse-diffusion pass over the full image) inserts high-fidelity anomaly objects while preserving compositional consistency. The key empirical finding is that Pearson correlations between synthetic and real OOD mIoUs reach 0.85–0.94 across 40 pretrained segmenters, substantially outperforming in-domain Cityscapes scores as a proxy when domain gaps are large (e.g., night, snow). The same synthetic data is shown to support temperature-scaling calibration (72–90% of models improve ECE) and to augment RbA training for OOD detection, matching COCO-augmented baselines without any curation.

## Results

Across 40 pretrained segmenters, synthetic OOD data generated via fine-tuned Stable Diffusion + ControlNet achieves Pearson Correlation Coefficients (PCC) with real-shift mIoUs of 0.85 (Night), 0.86 (Rain), and 0.85 (Snow) using SD1.5, substantially outperforming the Cityscapes-validation baseline (PCC_CS) especially for large domain gaps (e.g., PCC_Syn exceeds PCC_CS by more than 2× on 'night'). For OOD object detection, entropy correlation between real and synthetic OOD regions reaches 0.94 PCC, and curated synthetic scores correlate at ~0.8 PCC across FPR95, AUROC, and AUPR metrics. For calibration, synthetic data yields ECE improvement in >90% of models on ACDC shifts and 72.5% on IDD. Training RbA on synthetic inpainted data achieves 97.2 AUROC / 84.9 AUPR / 8.1 FPR95 (Swin-B), competitive with COCO-augmented RbA (97.8 / 85.3 / 8.5).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the GAN-based TSIT [13], the proposed SD1.5 pipeline wins on all three weather domains (Night 0.85 vs. 0.83, Rain 0.86 vs. 0.84, Snow 0.85 vs. 0.81 PCC), and crucially requires no OOD training data whereas TSIT was trained on real OOD images. Against the physics-based fog simulator [32], SDXL achieves a higher PCC on Fog (0.89 vs. 0.82), while the default SD1.5 is slightly lower (0.77 vs. 0.82); however, the physics-based simulator is limited to a single domain and requires in-depth OOD domain knowledge, whereas the SD pipeline generalizes to arbitrary domains via prompting. For OOD detection training, RbA trained on the proposed synthetic data (curated) achieves 97.2 AUROC / 84.9 AUPR / 8.1 FPR95 with Swin-B, slightly below RbA+COCO (97.8 / 85.3 / 8.5) on AUROC and AUPR but better on FPR95; with Swin-L the gap narrows (97.2 / 88.0 / 7.9 vs. 98.2 / 88.7 / 8.2), and the 'all' (uncurated) set performs nearly identically to the curated set, indicating that training does not demand the same level of visual realism as benchmarking.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes Stable Diffusion (fine-tuned via ControlNet for mask conditioning) and RePaint-style partial inpainting to generate synthetic OOD driving scenes, positioning itself against two prior lines of work. On one hand, it departs from classical robustness benchmarks (ImageNet-C, ACDC, IDD) and the RELIS study by de Jorge et al., which all rely on expensive real OOD data collection; on the other, it explicitly counters Taori et al.'s argument that synthetic perturbations (noise, blur) fail to predict real-shift robustness, arguing that photorealistic diffusion-generated scenes constitute a qualitatively different and viable proxy. For OOD object detection, it extends the RbA framework and the SegmentMeIfYouCan benchmark by introducing an automatic inpainting pipeline (SD + Grounded SAM) to augment training and testing data with unseen object classes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for safety-critical deployment: practitioners can now screen and rank 40+ pretrained segmenters for arbitrary new OOD domains (flooded roads, burning buildings, autumn leaves) without any real data collection, with ~500 synthetic images sufficing for stable correlation. Key limitations include the restriction to driving scenes (Cityscapes), limited SDXL experiments due to memory constraints, and the fact that curation is still needed for the best OOD-detection benchmarking results. Open questions include whether this approach generalizes beyond driving to other perception tasks, whether future generative models will fully replace real-data validation, and how to handle OOD domains that are physically impossible or extremely rare in training data.
