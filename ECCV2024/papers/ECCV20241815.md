## Problem

Existing video understanding benchmarks either target narrow tasks (action recognition, captioning) or rely on multiple-choice questions that oversimplify tasks and fail to reflect real-world open-ended interactions. There is no comprehensive benchmark that simultaneously covers diverse video-specific skills (temporal perception, causal reasoning, description) in an open-ended format that can be reliably and automatically evaluated.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241815/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each instance pairs a YouTube video (720P+, averaging 14.6 s across 3.2 scenes) with a single open-ended question (~11 words). Models receive 8 or 16 uniformly sampled frames (concatenated into one image for ImageLLMs) plus the question, and produce a free-form text response. The evaluator (GPT-4) then consumes the response alongside a ~303-word instance-specific rule set and outputs a structured analysis followed by a binary correct/incorrect judgment.

## Method

AutoEval-Video constructs 327 instances across 9 skill dimensions (dynamic perception, state transitions, camera movement, explanatory/counterfactual/predictive/comparison reasoning, external-knowledge reasoning, and description) using three quality principles: no single-frame bias, no language bias (verified by blind annotators), and objective answerability. The key evaluation innovation is annotating instance-specific rules rather than a unified prompt or reference answer; these rules are hardened through an adversarial annotation loop where a dedicated red team iteratively crafts adversarial responses that break the current rules, and the annotation team refines them until no further attacks succeed (averaging 3 iterations). A reward/penalty mechanism incentivizes both teams across iterations. With these rules as prompt, GPT-4 achieves 97.0% evaluation accuracy on model/human responses and 96.6% on GPT-4V responses, matching or exceeding a single human expert (97.5%/94.9%), while ablations show that removing adversarial annotation drops accuracy to 91.5% and using a unified prompt drops it to 87.0%.

## Results

On AutoEval-Video, GPT-4V achieves the highest accuracy among all evaluated models at 32.2% (16 frames) and 22.2% (8 frames), substantially outperforming the next-best model Gemini-Ultra-Vision (20.9% / 17.9%). Among open-sourced models, VideoChat leads with 10.9% (16 frames) and 13.4% (8 frames). Human accuracy on the full videos is 72.8%, revealing a large gap (over 40 points) between the best model and human performance, underscoring the difficulty of the benchmark.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4V dominates all other models by a wide margin, achieving 32.2% accuracy (16 frames) versus 20.9% for Gemini-Ultra-Vision and 10.9% for the best open-sourced model (VideoChat). Among open-sourced VideoLLMs, VideoChat (10.9%) outperforms Video-LLaMA (8.5%), LLaMA-VID (8.0%), Video-ChatGPT (4.9%), and VideoChat2 (0.6%); among open-sourced ImageLLMs, InstructBLIP (14.3%) leads over LLaVA-1.5 (8.2%), Qwen-VL (10.0%), and BLIP-2 (1.2%). Notably, VideoLLMs generally outperform ImageLLMs among open-sourced models, and increasing input from 8 to 16 frames benefits ImageLLMs (e.g., GPT-4V: 22.2→32.2%) but not VideoLLMs, likely because the latter are trained on 8-frame inputs. Interestingly, GPT-4V correctly answers only 32.7% of instances where VideoChat is correct, indicating VideoChat has strengths in traffic-related tasks, dynamic object comparison, and video-specific (vs. general) responses. All models remain far below the 72.8% human accuracy, confirming the benchmark's difficulty.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from two lines of prior work. First, it moves beyond multiple-choice video QA benchmarks (Perception Test, Ego4d, AGQA) by adopting open-ended questions across nine skill dimensions, arguing that MC formats oversimplify tasks and fail to reflect real-world video dialogue. Second, it extends the LLM-as-judge paradigm (MMVet, LLM-Eval, MT-Bench) by replacing a single unified evaluation prompt with instance-specific rules, further hardened through a novel adversarial annotation mechanism where a red team iteratively attacks the rules. The benchmark construction also explicitly addresses single-frame bias and language bias, building on prior analyses of these failure modes in video-language datasets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 40-point gap between GPT-4V (32.2%) and humans (72.8%) quantifies how far current models are from reliable open-ended video understanding, and the case study pinpoints specific failure modes (temporal reasoning, cross-frame connection, overly general responses) that should guide future model development. The adversarial annotation mechanism for evaluation rules is a methodological contribution that other open-ended benchmarks could adopt to improve judge reliability. However, the benchmark's small size (327 instances) limits statistical power and generalizability, and the reliance on GPT-4 as evaluator introduces a single point of failure; whether the evaluation methodology scales to larger, more diverse benchmarks remains an open question.
