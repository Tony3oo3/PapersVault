## Problem

Explicit deconvolution methods like Richardson-Lucy require an accurate PSF model and degrade severely under noise, while generic deep learning deblurring models (U-Net, CARE, MIMO-U-Net, etc.) ignore the physical structure of optical convolution, leading to bloated parameter counts and unstable restoration on real microscopy data. No prior approach effectively encodes the inverse-problem structure of deconvolution into a compact, physics-informed neural network that handles the strong nonlinearity of noisy microscopy images.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20242014/cropped_page8_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single widefield microscopy image (3D volume in R³, blurred by the optical PSF convolution plus additive noise) and produces a deconvolved image approximating the ground-truth object. Training uses simulated widefield images generated with physically plausible PSFs (NA 0.4–1.0, excitation 450–490 nm, emission 450–600 nm, refractive index 1.33) augmented with Gaussian and Poisson noise; at test time the model performs blind deconvolution without access to the PSF, operating on both simulated (BioSR, ImageNet) and real (dSTORM, widefield-confocal) microscopy pairs.

## Method

The paper formulates deconvolution as solving the inverse of an integral operator, approximated by the pseudo-differential operator B = (KᵀK + εI)⁻¹, and realizes both Kᵀ and B as neural network modules grounded in the Beylkin-Coifman-Rokhlin (BCR) nonstandard wavelet decomposition. The forward operator Kᵀ is a Sequence Convolution Block that applies residual dense blocks at each wavelet decomposition level (from L down to truncation level L₀), while B is approximated by 1D convolutions followed by residual dense blocks. To address the accumulated loss from BCR truncation, the multi-stage variant (m-rBCR) reintegrates the input image as a posterior at each resolution level, concatenating it with intermediate features to regularize the ill-posed inversion. The residual dense structure provides the nonlinear robustness needed for noisy microscopy data, and the BCR band-matrix structure keeps the network at O(N) complexity, yielding ~30× fewer parameters than MIMO-U-Net and ~210× fewer than ESRGAN while matching or exceeding their PSNR/SSIM on real microscopy benchmarks.

## Results

On the simulated BioSR dataset, m-rBCR achieves the best PSNR of 24.89 dB and SSIM of 0.78, outperforming the next-best MIMO-U-Net (23.95 dB, 0.78). On the two real microscopy datasets, m-rBCR leads with PSNR 23.10 dB (widefield-confocal) and 20.13 dB (dSTORM), surpassing all 10 baselines. On the simulated ImageNet dataset, m-rBCR ranks second (PSNR 21.41, SSIM 0.86) behind MIMO-U-Net (22.35, 0.88). Despite these competitive results, m-rBCR uses only 0.237M parameters (~30× fewer than MIMO-U-Net's 6.807M and ~210× fewer than ESRGAN's 49.841M) with a runtime of 0.0036 s per image (~3× faster than MIMO-U-Net and ~300× faster than DDPM's 1.0968 s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

m-rBCR outperforms MIMO-U-Net (the stated deblurring benchmark) on three of four datasets: BioSR (24.89 vs 23.95 PSNR), widefield-confocal (23.10 vs 19.17), and dSTORM (20.13 vs 18.91), while trailing it on ImageNet (21.41 vs 22.35 PSNR). Against DDPM, m-rBCR wins on BioSR (24.89 vs 21.85), ImageNet (21.41 vs 20.22), and dSTORM (20.13 vs 17.46), but loses on widefield-confocal (23.10 vs 22.27 PSNR, though DDPM has higher SSIM 0.73 vs 0.70). m-rBCR also beats CARE, DnCNN, U-Net, RCAN, MPRNet, ESRGAN, and Noise2Noise on all four datasets where both are evaluated. The classical Richardson-Lucy method, despite having access to the explicit PSF, achieves the lowest scores (13.39 PSNR on BioSR), confirming that noise sensitivity undermines physics-based explicit deconvolution. The key efficiency advantage is that m-rBCR (0.237M params, 0.0036 s) is ~30× smaller and ~3× faster than MIMO-U-Net, ~210× smaller than ESRGAN, and ~300× faster than DDPM, while matching or exceeding their quality on real microscopy data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the BCR-net architecture (Fan et al., 2019), which mapped the Beylkin-Coifman-Rokhlin nonstandard wavelet decomposition onto a neural network to approximate linear forward operators in tomography. The key departure is adapting this linear-operator framework to the strongly nonlinear, noise-corrupted inverse problem of microscopy deconvolution by introducing residual dense blocks for regularization and a multi-stage posterior reintegration scheme to recover signal lost at the BCR truncation level. It also positions itself against general-purpose deblurring/denoising networks (MIMO-U-Net, CARE, ESRGAN, etc.) that ignore the underlying optical convolution physics, and against earlier physics-informed attempts (PCA-based blind deconvolution, PSF-embedded CNNs) that did not leverage the BCR wavelet structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central practical takeaway is that embedding the optical convolution model as an architectural prior allows a 0.24M-parameter network to match or exceed 6.8M–50M-parameter general-purpose deblurring models on real microscopy data, with 3–300× faster inference. This suggests that for domain-specific inverse problems, physics-informed inductive bias can substitute for brute-force model capacity, reducing both memory footprint and compute cost. The main limitation acknowledged is the naive pixel-level fusion across resolution stages; the authors note that wavelet-theoretic fusion strategies could further improve performance. An open question is whether the BCR-based pseudo-differential operator approximation generalizes beyond microscopy to other ill-posed imaging inverse problems (e.g., CT, MRI) where the forward operator has a similar integral structure.
