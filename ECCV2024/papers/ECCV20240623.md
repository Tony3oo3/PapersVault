## Problem

Channel attention modules (SE, ECA, SRM, etc.) are widely used to boost network performance but introduce non-trivial inference cost due to their multiplicative, input-dependent computation. Existing structural re-parameterization (SRP) methods cannot absorb attention modules into the backbone because the attention output depends on the input at inference time, making equivalent merging impossible. This paper identifies why this barrier exists and how to circumvent it.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240623/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard RGB images at task-appropriate resolutions (224×224 for ImageNet, 32×32 for CIFAR/STL10, variable for COCO) and produces class labels, segmentation masks, or bounding boxes depending on the downstream task. The key structural detail is that during training the attention module consumes a learnable vector ψ ∈ ℝ^{C×1×1} instead of the actual input features (no global average pooling), while at inference the attention module vanishes entirely — the backbone's convolutional kernels and BN parameters are pre-multiplied by the learned constant attention vector, so the deployed model has identical parameter count and FLOPs to the vanilla backbone.

## Method

ASR (Attention-alike Structural Re-parameterization) is motivated by the Stripe Observation: across diverse datasets, architectures, and attention modules, channel attention values converge to input-independent constant vectors during training. Exploiting this, ASR replaces the global average pooling in any channel attention module with a learnable vector ψ, so the attention output v_{ψ,θ} = σ(F_θ(ψ)) becomes a fixed constant after training. At inference, this constant is merged into the backbone via element-wise multiplication with convolutional kernels (K ⊙ v) and BN parameters (γ ⊙ v, β ⊙ v), yielding a single equivalent layer with zero extra parameters or FLOPs. The approach is plug-and-play: it works with any channel attention module (SE, ECA, SRM, SPA, IE) and composes with existing SRP methods (RepVGG, ACNet) and multiple ASR insertions, while also improving robustness to batch noise and constant noise attacks.

## Results

On ImageNet-1k, ASR consistently improves top-1 accuracy across all tested backbones with zero additional inference cost: ResNet50+ASR(ECA) reaches 76.87% (+0.74 over baseline), ResNet101+ASR(SRM) reaches 78.18% (+1.12), and ViT-B+ASR(SE) at 224×224 reaches 82.54% (+1.12). On CIFAR-100, ResNet164+ASR(IE) achieves 75.58% (+1.26) and MobileNet+ASR(IE) achieves 69.45% (+2.58). On COCO2017 with Mask R-CNN, ASR(SE) improves instance segmentation AP from 34.0 to 35.1 (ResNet50) and object detection AP from 37.3 to 38.1 (ResNet50), all while maintaining identical parameter count and inference speed to the vanilla backbone.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ASR is compared against standalone attention modules (SE, ECA, SRM, SPA, IE, CBAM, DIANet) and existing SRP methods (RepVGG, ACNet). Against attention modules, ASR achieves comparable or slightly lower accuracy than the standalone module (e.g., ASR(SRM) 75.23% vs. standalone SRM 75.38% on CIFAR-100) but with zero extra inference cost, whereas standalone SE slows inference by ~24–32%, CBAM by ~50–59%, and SPA by ~29–44% (Table 1). When combined (ASR(κ)+κ), the method consistently outperforms the standalone module: e.g., ASR(SE)+SE reaches 77.32% on CIFAR-100 vs. 75.29% for SE alone (+2.03). Against existing SRP methods, ASR provides additive gains: RepVGGB3+ASR(SE) improves from 87.05% to 88.29% on STL10 (+1.24), and ResNet110-ACNet+ASR(SE) improves from 83.54% to 86.13% on STL10 (+4.11). The key differentiator is that ASR matches or exceeds attention-module accuracy while preserving the exact parameter count and FPS of the vanilla backbone, making it a drop-in replacement with no deployment penalty.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two well-developed lines of work: structural re-parameterization (SRP) methods (RepVGG, ACNet, Do-Conv) that merge training-time branches into inference-time single layers, and channel attention modules (SE, ECA, SRM, SPA) that adaptively reweight features. The key departure is that prior SRP methods explicitly exclude attention modules because their multiplicative, input-dependent output cannot be absorbed into backbone parameters (Eq. 2). The authors resolve this incompatibility by empirically discovering the Stripe Observation—that channel attention values converge to input-independent constant vectors during training—and then replacing the GAP-derived input with a learnable vector, making the attention output a fixed constant that is trivially mergeable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical value is clear: any backbone can gain attention-like channel reweighting at zero inference cost, which is attractive for deployment-constrained settings where even small attention modules cause 20–60% speed drops (Table 1). However, the method is limited to channel attention and does not extend to spatial attention, which the authors acknowledge. The Stripe Observation, while empirically robust across architectures and datasets, lacks a theoretical explanation, and the modest accuracy gains (typically 0.5–1.5%) mean the technique is best viewed as a free lunch rather than a performance breakthrough. Open questions include whether the learned constant vectors capture meaningful dataset priors and whether the approach generalizes to attention variants with non-scalar per-channel outputs.
