## Problem

Self-supervised representation learning methods have struggled to match fully-supervised performance on fine-grained, long-tailed biodiversity classification tasks such as species recognition, particularly in data-sparse regions. The paper identifies that ground-level and aerial views of the same natural scene share rich mutual information that has not been systematically exploited through contrastive pre-training to improve downstream fine-grained classification.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20242137/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes paired 256×256 ground-level RGB images (from iNaturalist citizen science photos) and 256×256 four-channel RGB-Infrared aerial images (from 2018 NAIP at 60 cm/pixel, covering ~153×153 m). A key structural property is the many-to-one pairing: on average two ground-level images map to a single aerial image. The pre-trained encoders produce 512-dimensional embeddings that are fine-tuned with a classification head to output species labels, species presence predictions, crop type labels, or tree genus labels depending on the downstream task.

## Method

CRISP replaces CLIP's text encoder with a second image encoder, creating a dual-image contrastive framework where two ResNet50 encoders (ground-level initialized from ImageNet, aerial randomly initialized) learn a joint representation such that co-located image pairs are more similar than non-paired ones. The standard objective is a symmetric cross-entropy loss over cosine similarities with a learned temperature. Three variants address the many-to-one pairing: CRISP-Aug applies random crops, flips, and 90° rotations to aerial images; CRISP-M2o modifies the loss to treat all ground-level images within a 250 m Haversine radius as mutual positives; and CRISP-Par introduces a learned sigmoid-weighted parameter that dynamically balances the ground-level and aerial loss contributions. The parameterized variant consistently outperforms the others, suggesting the model learns to prioritize the more information-dense ground-level view for fine-grained species discrimination.

## Results

On the primary species recognition task (ground-level NMV imagery), CRISP-Par achieves 36.18% Top-1 accuracy and 11.25% Top-1 Macro accuracy at λ=0.25% training data, outperforming the strongest baseline (ImageNet-pretrained ResNet50) by 16.6 and 3.3 percentage points respectively. At λ=1%, CRISP-Par reaches 51.04% Top-1 and 21.38% Top-1 Macro, again leading all baselines. At the largest split (λ=20%), ImageNet-pretrained ResNet50 slightly edges out CRISP on Top-1 (74.90% vs. 70.96%) and Top-1 Macro (45.85% vs. 40.31%), indicating the multi-view advantage is most pronounced in data-sparse regimes. For species distribution mapping from aerial imagery, CRISP-Par achieves 15.51% Top-30 Macro accuracy at λ=0.25%, a 10.5% average improvement over baselines, while maintaining predictive power for rare species (6.8% Top-30-R vs. 0.27% for baselines).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the ImageNet-pretrained ResNet50 baseline, CRISP-Par wins decisively at low data: +16.6 pp Top-1 and +3.3 pp Top-1 Macro at λ=0.25%, and +10.7 pp Top-1 and +4.6 pp Top-1 Macro at λ=1%. At λ=5% and 20%, ImageNet slightly outperforms CRISP on Top-1 (by ~0.5 pp and ~4 pp respectively) and Top-1 Macro (by ~0.3 pp and ~5.5 pp), suggesting the multi-view signal is most valuable when labels are scarce. Against self-supervised single-view baselines (MAE-NMV, MoCo-v2), CRISP dominates across all λ values, with the gap largest at λ=0.25% (e.g., 36.18% vs. 20.28% Top-1 vs. MoCo-v2; 36.18% vs. 6.34% vs. MAE-NMV). For species distribution mapping from aerial imagery, CRISP-Par leads on class-balanced metrics (Top-5 Macro: 3.61% vs. 1.77% for SauMoCo; Top-30 Macro: 15.51% vs. 8.12% for SauMoCo at λ=0.25%) but trails on raw Top-5 accuracy at higher λ. For crop type mapping, CRISP outperforms Tile2Vec by 7.3 pp (MLP, 1,000 examples) and 9.9 pp (LR, 10,000 examples), and beats SauMoCo by 1.2 pp (MLP, 1,000 examples). On the continental-scale Auto Arborist dataset, CRISP improves Top-5 Macro accuracy by 4.6 pp (ground-level) and 3.25 pp (aerial) over the best single-view baselines across all three test cities.

## Relation to Prior Work

CRISP directly adapts the CLIP image-text contrastive framework by replacing the text encoder with a second image encoder, creating an image-image contrastive objective between ground-level and aerial views. It builds on BirdSAT's use of CLIP loss for ground-aerial bird pairs but extends to plants, introduces a parameterized loss that dynamically weights the two views, and modifies the loss to handle the many-to-one pairing inherent in citizen science data. For remote sensing, it departs from single-view self-supervised methods (Tile2Vec, SauMoCo, SatMAE) that exploit only spatial, temporal, or sensor relationships within aerial imagery, instead leveraging cross-view information from paired ground-level photos. The approach is conceptually related to wide-view image geolocalization but targets fine-grained classification in long-tailed natural world settings rather than scene identification in built environments.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The NMV dataset (3M+ ground-aerial pairs, 6,600+ plant taxa) provides a much-needed benchmark for multi-view natural world representation learning, and the finding that CRISP's largest gains occur in low-data settings and for the rarest species directly addresses the label-scarcity problem in biodiversity monitoring for the world's most biodiverse yet data-poor regions. However, species-level classification from aerial imagery alone remains extremely weak (Top-1 accuracy ~2-3%), and combining both views in a mixture-of-experts setting actually degrades performance compared to ground-level alone, suggesting the aerial view contributes limited discriminative information at species level. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to animals, to regions outside California, and whether the multi-view signal can be better exploited for the aerial view specifically.
