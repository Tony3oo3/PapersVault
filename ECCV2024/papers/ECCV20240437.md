## Problem

Existing spike camera reconstruction methods degrade significantly in low-light high-speed scenes because the sparse spike streams contain insufficient information to recover scene details. Additionally, reliable synthetic datasets for low-light high-speed reconstruction are lacking, as prior efforts either omit ground-truth image sequences or only cover static scenes, making it difficult to evaluate motion-sensitive reconstruction under realistic lighting.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240437/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes continuous binary spike streams of shape H x W x 41 (400x250x41 at full resolution, cropped to 64x64 during training) and produces a reconstructed light-intensity image at each temporal step. Temporal context is maintained across 21 consecutive spike streams in both forward and backward recurrent passes, so the network effectively aggregates information from a 21-frame window around the target timestamp. The output is a single-channel intensity map per time step, trained with an L1 reconstruction loss against ground-truth images.

## Method

The core contribution is a bidirectional recurrent reconstruction framework built around two key modules. First, a Light-Robust Representation (LR-Rep) computes a Global Inter-Spike Interval (GISI) map by tracking the release times of forward and backward spikes across the recurrent pass, then fuses GISI shallow features with raw spike-stream shallow features through a 3-layer attention block to produce a temporally enriched representation. Second, a fusion module extracts deep features via a 16-layer ResNet and combines them with adjacent forward/backward temporal features using Pyramid Cascading and Deformable convolution (PCD) to correct motion misalignment across timestamps. The forward and backward data flows are independent, and the final image is reconstructed by concatenating both directional temporal features through a 3-layer convolution. The approach is trained end-to-end with supervised L1 loss on the proposed RLLR dataset, which uses SPCS-simulated scenes with physically consistent light-source power settings.

## Results

On the synthetic LLR dataset, the proposed method achieves 45.075 PSNR and 0.98681 SSIM, surpassing the state-of-the-art WGSE (42.959 PSNR, 0.97066 SSIM) by approximately 2.1 dB in PSNR. The method also outperforms S2I (40.883 PSNR) by over 4 dB and traditional methods (TFI, STP, SNM) by more than 13 dB. On real datasets (PKU-Spike-High-Speed and low-light high-speed spike streams), the method produces the highest user-study scores and avoids the motion blur and dark-background artifacts observed in competing methods under low-light conditions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all seven baselines on the LLR synthetic benchmark. Against the closest competitor WGSE (AAAI 2023), it gains 2.1 dB PSNR (45.075 vs. 42.959) and 0.016 SSIM, demonstrating that the bidirectional recurrent framework with LR-Rep effectively compensates for sparse spike information in low-light scenes. S2I (CVPR 2021) trails by 4.2 dB PSNR, while SSML (IJCAI 2022) and RSIR (MM 2023) lag by 6.6 dB and 11.0 dB respectively; RSIR in particular suffers from severe motion blur because its AST representation compresses spike streams into static spike-count maps, discarding dynamic information. Traditional methods (TFI, STP, SNM) are far behind, with PSNR below 32, confirming that deep learning approaches are essential for this task. On real-world low-light spike streams, the method generalizes well and achieves the highest subjective user-study scores, whereas S2I and WGSE introduce visible artifacts in dark backgrounds and SSML/RSIR produce large motion blur.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the spike-camera reconstruction line (TFI → S2I → SSML → WGSE) by introducing a bidirectional recurrent architecture with a novel Global Inter-Spike Interval (GISI) representation that leverages both forward and backward spike release times, going beyond the standard Local Inter-Spike Interval (LISI) used in prior methods. The fusion module borrows the Pyramid Cascading and Deformable convolution (PCD) alignment mechanism from video restoration (EDVR) to handle motion misalignment across timestamps. It departs from the concurrent RSIR work, which compresses spike streams into a spike-count map (AST) and thus loses dynamic information, by preserving temporal ordering through the recurrent GISI update. The synthetic datasets build on the SPCS rendering engine and SpikingSim noise model, but add carefully controlled low-light sources to create a realistic evaluation setting absent from prior spike-camera benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Low-light degradation is a fundamental bottleneck for all neuromorphic sensors, and this paper provides both a practical method and the first dedicated synthetic benchmark (RLLR/LLR) for evaluating reconstruction under combined low-light and high-speed conditions. The generalization to real low-light spike streams (Dong et al. 2022) strengthens the practical relevance, though the reliance on synthetic ground truth for training remains a limitation. Open questions include whether the bidirectional recurrent mechanism scales to even lower illuminance where spike counts become extremely sparse, and whether the GISI representation can be extended to multi-channel (color) or higher-resolution spike cameras. The modest ablation gain of GISI over LISI (~0.08 dB) suggests the recurrent fusion and alignment components may be the primary drivers, leaving room for future work to isolate and refine the temporal-aggregation mechanism.
