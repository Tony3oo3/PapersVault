## Problem

Interactive vertebrae keypoint estimation methods still require users to manually identify and correct errors, which is especially costly when erroneous keypoints are densely clustered or overlapping in spinal X-rays. Existing approaches lack an autonomous mechanism to detect and fix the specific, recurring error types (vertex misidentification, bone misidentification, left-right inversion) that dominate vertebrae estimation failures before any user intervention occurs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20242340/cropped_page0_idx7.webp)

## Input / Output

KeyBot consumes an X-ray spinal radiograph together with the current keypoint prediction from an interactive backbone model. The detector evaluates keypoints in sliding windows of k consecutive points (k < K total) with a stride s < k, outputting a binary anomaly score per keypoint via sigmoid multi-label classification. The corrector produces K-channel Gaussian heatmaps at the original image resolution indicating reconstructed keypoint locations; only the channels corresponding to detector-flagged keypoints are extracted and injected into the interaction model as pseudo-clicks, while user-revised keypoints take priority and are excluded from KeyBot updates.

## Method

KeyBot comprises two independently trained CNN modules: a ResNet-18-based detector that performs multi-label binary classification (BCE loss) to flag erroneous keypoints, and a DeepLab-V3-based corrector that regresses corrected keypoint heatmaps (BCE loss against ground-truth heatmaps). Both are trained on synthetic errors that simulate three canonical failure modes—vertex misidentification (cyclic index displacement within ±r), bone misidentification (shifting an entire vertebra's keypoints up or down one level), and left-right inversion (swapping lateral keypoint pairs)—rather than on real, noisy model errors. At inference, the detector scans all keypoints in overlapping windows and flags any keypoint with an anomaly prediction; the corrector then generates pseudo-corrections exclusively for flagged keypoints, which are fed to the frozen interaction model alongside an accumulated history of all false predictions across iterations. This iterative loop (up to N KeyBot passes per user turn) runs autonomously before the user sees the result, reducing the user's role to verifying and fine-tuning rather than searching for major errors.

## Results

On the AASCE dataset, KeyBot-i1 reduces the mean radial error (MRE) from 51.58 (Kim et al.) to 44.18 without any user clicks, and further to 25.93 with one user click (UC1), outperforming BRS, f-BRS, RITM, and Click-Pose even at two user clicks. On BUU-AP and BUU-LA, KeyBot-i1 achieves MRE of 32.01 and 18.77 respectively, surpassing the best baselines (RITM at 36.43 and 23.27). In terms of annotation efficiency, KeyBot-i1 requires only 0.53 clicks to reach MRE ≤ 50 on AASCE (NoC₁₀@50), nearly halving the 1.03 clicks needed by Kim et al. and 1.38 by RITM, representing a 17% reduction in user clicks for target performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

KeyBot-i1 outperforms all five baselines across all three datasets in both MRE and NoC. Against the closest interactive keypoint estimation baseline (Kim et al. [10]), KeyBot-i1 reduces MRE by 14.3% on AASCE (44.18 vs 51.58), 24.3% on BUU-AP (32.01 vs 42.31), and 19.9% on BUU-LA (18.77 vs 23.43) without any user clicks. Against the strongest segmentation-based baseline RITM [22], KeyBot-i1 achieves 21.2% lower MRE on AASCE (44.18 vs 56.03) and 19.3% lower on BUU-LA (18.77 vs 23.27). The most striking result is that KeyBot-i1 with a single user click (MRE 25.93 on AASCE) surpasses BRS (29.93), f-BRS (52.53), RITM (31.03), and Click-Pose (44.08) at two user clicks. On NoC metrics, KeyBot-i1 requires 0.53 clicks to reach MRE ≤ 50 on AASCE versus 1.03 for Kim et al. and 1.38 for RITM, and only 0.05 clicks to reach MRE ≤ 30 on BUU-LA versus 0.12 for Kim et al. and 0.14 for RITM. Click-Pose [26] is the weakest baseline overall, with MRE values 10–15 points higher than KeyBot-i1 on AASCE and BUU-LA.

## Relation to Prior Work

KeyBot extends the interactive keypoint estimation framework of Kim et al. (MICCAI 2022) by inserting an autonomous error-correction stage between the model's prediction and user review. It draws conceptually from error-correction literature (PoseFix for human pose, PseudoClick for segmentation) but departs from PseudoClick's end-to-end training by independently training a detector and corrector on synthetic errors, decoupling them from the backbone interaction model to avoid inheriting its biases. The synthetic error simulation strategy (vertex misidentification, bone misidentification, left-right inversion) is specific to the repetitive, sequential anatomy of vertebrae and has no direct analogue in prior keypoint or segmentation error-correction work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

For clinical annotation pipelines, reducing the number of user clicks by ~17% while improving MRE by ~19% on AASCE translates to meaningful time savings when annotating thousands of spinal radiographs. The collaborative loop (KeyBot → user → interaction model) is a practical workflow pattern that could extend to other repetitive anatomical structures (ribs, phalanges) or other modalities. Open questions include whether the three predefined error types capture the full distribution of real-world failures, whether the approach generalizes to 3D vertebrae estimation, and whether the independent training overhead is justified over simpler end-to-end alternatives at scale.
