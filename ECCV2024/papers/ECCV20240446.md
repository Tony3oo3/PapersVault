## Problem

3D Gaussian Splatting projects 3D Gaussians onto a fixed image plane using a first-order Taylor (local affine) approximation, which introduces a projection error that has been largely overlooked in prior work. This error grows for Gaussians far from the projection center and becomes severe under wide-angle or short-focal-length settings, producing elongated Gaussians and cloud-like artifacts that degrade rendering quality.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240446/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a set of calibrated RGB images with SfM-derived camera poses and the corresponding sparse point cloud, identical to standard 3D-GS. It outputs a 3D Gaussian scene representation (means, covariances, opacities, spherical harmonics) and renders novel views in real time. A distinguishing detail is that the rasterizer operates on a unit sphere rather than the perspective image plane: each pixel's ray is mapped to a point on the sphere, and colors are retrieved by alpha-blending the 2D Gaussians living on per-Gaussian tangent planes. This design allows straightforward adaptation to fisheye and panoramic camera models by only changing the image-to-sphere mapping.

## Method

The paper first derives the Taylor remainder of the projective transformation, computes its expected squared Frobenius norm over the Gaussian distribution, and shows the resulting error function ε(θ_μ, φ_μ) attains its minimum when the Gaussian mean lies on the camera optical axis. From this extremum analysis, the authors propose Optimal Gaussian Splatting: instead of projecting every Gaussian onto the shared z=1 plane, each Gaussian is projected onto a tangent plane to the unit sphere at the point where the ray from the camera center through the Gaussian mean intersects the sphere, minimizing that Gaussian's individual projection error. A corresponding Jacobian J_p is derived for the new projection, and an invertible matrix Q is introduced to correctly invert the rank-2 projected covariance. A Unit Sphere Based Rasterizer then casts per-pixel rays to the sphere, queries the tangent-plane Gaussians, and performs alpha blending to produce the final image, preserving real-time performance while reducing artifacts—especially at short focal lengths where the original method degrades sharply.

## Results

Across 13 scenes from Mip-NeRF360, Tanks & Temples, and Deep Blending, Optimal Gaussian Splatting achieves an average PSNR of 27.17, SSIM of 0.836, and LPIPS of 0.210, outperforming the original 3D-GS (26.92 / 0.832 / 0.214) on all three metrics. The improvement over Mip-NeRF360 (27.11 / 0.803 / 0.241) is +0.06 PSNR and −0.031 LPIPS while maintaining real-time rendering speed. Under reduced focal lengths (×0.2 and ×0.3), the advantage over 3D-GS widens substantially: at ×0.2, Ours achieves 22.10 average PSNR versus 15.58 for 3D-GS, a 6.5 dB gap, demonstrating far greater robustness to wide-angle projection errors.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the direct predecessor 3D-GS [16], Optimal Gaussian Splatting wins on all three metrics (PSNR +0.25, SSIM +0.004, LPIPS −0.004) in the standard focal-length setting, with the gap widening dramatically under wide-angle conditions (e.g., +6.5 dB PSNR at ×0.2 focal length). Compared to Mip-NeRF360 [2], the method achieves a slightly higher average PSNR (+0.06) and lower LPIPS (−0.031) while retaining real-time rendering speed that Mip-NeRF360 cannot match. Against Instant-NGP (both Base and Big variants) [26], the improvement is substantial: +2.2 to +2.7 dB PSNR and roughly 0.10–0.14 lower LPIPS. Plenoxels [8] is the weakest baseline, trailing by over 4 dB in PSNR. The paper's key finding is that the advantage over 3D-GS is not uniform: it is modest at the original focal length but becomes the dominant factor as focal length decreases, where 3D-GS's fixed z=1 projection plane introduces severe elongation and cloud-like artifacts that the per-Gaussian tangent-plane projection avoids.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on 3D Gaussian Splatting (3D-GS) by Kerbl et al., which projects 3D Gaussians onto a fixed z=1 image plane using a first-order Taylor (local affine) approximation inherited from EWA splatting. While numerous follow-up works have improved 3D-GS's storage, sparse-view robustness, and mesh extraction, none had analyzed the fundamental projection error introduced by this approximation. The paper's departure is to treat the Taylor remainder as an optimizable error function of the Gaussian mean, revealing that the error is minimized when each Gaussian is projected onto its own tangent plane rather than a shared image plane — a per-Gaussian projection strategy absent from all prior 3D-GS variants.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The analysis provides a principled, closed-form explanation for why 3D-GS artifacts (elongated Gaussians, cloud-like structures) worsen with wider field of view, and offers a minimal-code fix that preserves real-time rendering while extending compatibility to fisheye and panoramic cameras that the original z=1 projection cannot handle. The improvement is modest under standard focal lengths (~0.25 dB PSNR) but becomes substantial at reduced focal lengths, making it practically relevant for AR/VR and wide-angle capture pipelines. A key open question, acknowledged by the authors, is how the analysis extends when Gaussian covariance is not held constant, and whether the tangent-plane formulation interacts with anisotropic scaling in ways that further alter the error landscape.
