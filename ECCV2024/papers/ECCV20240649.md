## Problem

Existing semi-supervised domain adaptation (SemiSDA) methods assume labeled target data is randomly sampled, but in real-world deployments users predominantly provide feedback on misclassified samples (Negatively Biased Feedback, NBF). This paper uncovers that NBF's biased distribution within class clusters causes SemiSDA methods to produce sub-optimal decision boundaries, leading to inferior adaptation performance compared to random feedback—a previously unexplored failure mode with practical safety implications in domains like medical imaging.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240649/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method requires a pre-trained source model (no source data access, following a source-free constraint), a small set of labeled target images obtained as user feedback (e.g., 3–15 per class, biased toward model errors), and a large pool of unlabeled target images. The adapted model outputs class predictions for image classification, pixel-level segmentation masks, or per-finding binary predictions for chest X-ray diagnosis. The labeled feedback is consumed as ground-truth annotations in a cross-entropy loss, while unlabeled data provides pseudo-labels for consistency regularization; the key distinction from standard SemiSDA is that the labeled subset is not a random sample but is concentrated in regions where the source model fails.

## Method

Retrieval Latent Defending (RLD) is a plug-and-play module that balances the supervised signal during SemiSDA adaptation. Before each epoch, the current model generates pseudo labels for all unlabeled target data, and a candidate bank is built by retaining only the top-p% most confident samples per class. At each training iteration, for every labeled sample in the mini-batch, k defending samples sharing the same pseudo label are randomly retrieved from the bank and appended to the batch. The total loss is the standard SemiSDA loss (supervised cross-entropy on labeled data plus consistency regularization on unlabeled data) augmented with an additional cross-entropy term on the defending samples, preventing the decision boundary from being dominated by the spatially biased NBF points. The approach requires no architectural changes, uses fixed hyperparameters (k=3, p=0.4) across all benchmarks, and can be combined with any existing SemiSDA or SemiSL baseline without modifying its core adaptation strategy.

## Results

On DomainNet-126 (ResNet, 378 feedback samples), Negatively Biased Feedback (NBF) degrades all SemiSDA baselines by 1.1–4.3% top-1 accuracy relative to Random Feedback (RF), e.g., FixMatch drops from 67.6% (RF) to 63.4% (NBF). Retrieval Latent Defending (RLD) reverses this, lifting every baseline above its RF counterpart by +2.4 to +9.8%, with the largest gains for CDAC (64.6→73.2) and FixMatch (63.4→73.2). On OfficeHome (195 feedback), RLD yields +2.1 to +5.3% improvements over NBF. In the medical MIMIC-CXR-V2 setting, RLD improves AUROC from 0.7639 (NBF-CE) to 0.7875, and on GTA5→Cityscapes segmentation it adds +3.3 to +3.5 mIoU over NBF.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RLD is not a standalone method but a plug-in that augments existing SemiSDA/SemiSL baselines; the comparison is therefore framed as each baseline under RF vs. NBF vs. NBF+RLD. Under NBF, every baseline suffers a drop: AdaMatch [6] loses 3.1%, CDAC [33] loses 3.7%, FixMatch [66] loses 4.2%, UDA [87] loses 4.3%, MME [58] loses 1.1%, FlexMatch [96] loses 1.9%, and FreeMatch [81] loses 1.8% on DomainNet-126. With RLD appended, all seven baselines surpass their respective RF scores, with the largest relative gains for CDAC (+8.6 over NBF) and FixMatch (+9.8), while the already-strong FreeMatch and FlexMatch see smaller but consistent gains (+2.8 and +3.3). The same pattern holds on OfficeHome (Table 4) and in the medical MIMIC-CXR-V2 experiment where Pseudo-labeling [1] under NBF-CE improves from 0.7639 to 0.7875 AUROC. For semantic segmentation, IAST [46] and RIPU [85] both drop 2.3–3.1 mIoU under NBF but recover and exceed RF by +3.3 and +3.5 mIoU respectively with RLD. The active-labeling strategy ENT [62], which produces feedback biased similarly to NBF, also benefits from RLD (+2.5 to +3.1 mIoU), confirming that the approach generalizes beyond strictly user-provided feedback to any negatively biased labeling protocol.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the standard SemiSDA setup, which assumes labeled target data is a random subset, by identifying that real-world user feedback is systematically biased toward misclassified samples (Negatively Biased Feedback). It builds on the source-free constraint from SFDA and the adaptation frameworks of methods like AdaMatch, CDAC, and FixMatch, but introduces a new plug-and-play loss component (Retrieval Latent Defending) that balances the mini-batch with pseudo-labeled defending samples. The work also contrasts with ActiveDA, where the machine selects samples for annotation, by addressing the fundamentally different scenario where users autonomously report errors. Conceptually, it repurposes the pseudo-labeling mechanism already present in SemiSDA methods but redirects it toward distributional balancing rather than unlabeled-data exploitation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that naively adapting with user feedback can degrade performance—particularly in medical imaging where this could affect patient outcomes—highlights a practical gap between SemiSDA theory and deployment reality. The solution is simple and architecture-agnostic, making it immediately adoptable by practitioners deploying ML products with feedback loops. Open questions include how the approach handles different feedback modalities (ratings, noisy labels, class-imbalanced feedback), whether it extends to test-time adaptation, and whether the NBF assumption generalizes beyond the simulated setup used in experiments. The paper also leaves open the question of whether more sophisticated defending-sample selection or adaptive k/p schedules could further improve results.
