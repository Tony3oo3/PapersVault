## Problem

When image stitching is naively extended to video, it introduces temporal instability (termed 'warping shake') in non-overlapping regions even when source videos are already stable due to modern hardware/software stabilization. Existing video stitching methods assume heavily shaky inputs, require offline processing of complete videos, and rely on fragile non-linear iterative optimization, making them impractical for real-time online use with today's typically stable handheld footage.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240102/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes two RGB video streams (reference and target) from hand-held cameras at arbitrary resolution (360×480 for training) without requiring camera poses, motion trajectories, or temporal synchronization. Only the target video is warped (unlike prior work that warps both), and the output is a stitched video with temporally smooth trajectories. In online mode, a sliding window of 7 frames is processed with just 1-frame latency, achieving ~28.2 ms per frame on a single RTX 4090Ti GPU.

## Method

StabStitch derives stitching trajectories by combining a temporal warp model (ResNet18 + local correlation layers predicting mesh control-point motions between consecutive frames) and a spatial warp model (same architecture with a global correlation layer for long-range matching plus a motion-consistency loss), then applies a TPS-based composition to obtain the stitching motion s(t) for the warped video. A lightweight 3D-CNN warp smoothing model takes sequences of stitching paths, spatial meshes, and binary overlap masks as input and outputs a smoothing increment Δ that simultaneously preserves alignment (via an overlap-aware data term), enforces trajectory smoothness (via a midpoint constraint), maintains spatial consistency (via a distortion term), and ensures cross-window coherence (via an online collaboration term). The entire framework is trained unsupervised with no ground-truth warps, and the online sliding-window design eliminates the need for future frames, enabling real-time stitching with one-frame latency.

## Results

On the StabStitch-D dataset, StabStitch achieves an average PSNR/SSIM of 29.89/0.890, marginally surpassing the re-trained UDIS++* (29.78/0.891) while significantly outperforming the original UDIS++ (27.19/0.859). LPC fails entirely on Low-Light and Low-Texture categories. In a user preference study against the best video stitching baseline (Nie et al. [47]), StabStitch is preferred 30.47% of the time versus 6.25% for the baseline (63.28% no preference). StabStitch also achieves real-time inference at 28.2 ms/frame, compared to over 40 minutes for Nie et al. [47] on a 26-second video.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against image stitching baselines, StabStitch achieves comparable or slightly better alignment (PSNR/SSIM) than UDIS++* on Regular, Low-Light, and Low-Texture categories, while sacrificing some alignment on Fast-Moving (23.36 vs 24.78 PSNR) in exchange for temporal stability. LPC, a traditional method, fails outright on Low-Light and Low-Texture scenes due to insufficient geometric features. Against the video stitching baseline Nie et al. [47], direct PSNR/SSIM comparison is not fair because that method warps both reference and target frames while StabStitch warps only the target; instead, a user study shows StabStitch is preferred 30.47% vs 6.25% (63.28% no preference) across 128 one-second clips rated by 20 participants. On inference speed, StabStitch processes one frame in 28.2 ms on an RTX 4090Ti, enabling real-time online stitching, whereas Nie et al. [47] requires over 40 minutes on a CPU for a 26-second video, making it impractical for online use.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of image stitching (UDIS++, LPC) and video stabilization (MeshFlow, DUT), but deliberately departs from prior video stitching work (Nie et al. 2017, Guo et al. 2016, Lin et al. 2016) that assumes freely moving hand-held cameras produce inherently unstable inputs requiring joint stabilization-and-stitching optimization. Instead, it reframes the problem around 'warping shake'—temporal instability in non-overlapping regions introduced by per-frame image stitching—under the assumption that modern hardware/software stabilization already yields stable source videos. The stitching trajectory formulation (Stitch-Meshflow) directly adapts MeshFlow's camera-path chaining to the stitching context, while the warp smoothing network borrows the unsupervised learning philosophy of DUT and the mesh-based warp representation of UDIS++.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: reducing stitching latency from over 40 minutes (Nie et al.) to ~28 ms/frame enables real-time online panoramic video, which is critical for applications like autonomous driving and VR. The StabStitch-D dataset (100+ video pairs, four scene categories) fills a gap in benchmarking for video stitching and could benefit the broader community. However, the scope remains limited to two hand-held video inputs at modest resolution (360×480 training), and the core assumption—that source videos are already stable—may not hold for all capture scenarios (e.g., action cameras, low-end devices without EIS). Open questions include generalization to more than two views, dynamic foreground handling, and whether the warping-shake formulation transfers to 360° or multi-camera array stitching.
