## Problem

Decomposing raster animated graphics (social media posts, ads) into editable sprites is critical for video editing, but existing video decomposition methods like LNA and Deformable Sprites are designed for natural scenes and suffer from slow convergence, unwanted deformation artifacts, and inability to handle the diverse element types (text, illustrations, backgrounds) typical of animated graphics. The paper addresses the need for a fast, artifact-free decomposition that produces a compact parametric representation suitable for interactive editing workflows.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241789/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a raster video (resized to 128px short side) and minimal user-provided bounding box annotations for visible objects in a single frame as input. It outputs K sprites, each consisting of a static 100×100 RGBA texture and a per-frame sequence of 6-parameter affine transformation matrices plus a scalar opacity value. The static texture assumption (no temporal dynamics in appearance) is the key simplification that reduces the search space compared to general video decomposition, and the affine+opacity parameterization is deliberately minimal to match the animation presets (zoom, fade, slide, shake) common in design tools.

## Method

The core approach formulates sprite decomposition as a gradient-based optimization minimizing reconstruction error between rendered sprites and the target video, with three key strategies for efficiency and quality. First, a U-Net image prior (Deep Image Prior) reparameterizes texture optimization as a search over latent codes and shared model parameters, whose convolutional inductive bias prevents pixel-level artifacts without explicit regularization. Second, a pre-trained video object segmentation model (TAM) is used for initialization: given the single-frame bounding box prompts, it produces segmentation masks across all frames, from which initial textures (temporal averaging within boxes) and affine parameters (box positions) are derived. Third, a warm-up phase searches over sprite rendering order to avoid local minima from incorrect layer stacking. The simplified affine+opacity representation itself acts as an implicit prior, eliminating the need for the regularization losses and optimization scheduling used by LNA and DS, and enabling convergence to competitive quality in roughly 10 minutes versus 40+ minutes for baselines.

## Results

On the Crello Animation test split (299 samples), at a fixed 10-minute optimization budget, the proposed method achieves Frame L1 of 0.0163, LPIPS of 0.0670, Sprite RGB L1 of 0.1179, and Sprite Alpha L1 of 0.0193, outperforming LNA (0.0339 / 0.2955 / 0.2654 / 0.0370) and DS (0.2446 / 0.3869 / 0.2965 / 0.0499) on all four metrics. After full convergence, the method achieves the best Sprite RGB L1 (0.0984) and Sprite Alpha L1 (0.0179), while DS attains the lowest Frame L1 (0.0054) and LPIPS (0.0224). Notably, the method's 10-minute sprite errors already surpass the converged sprite errors of both baselines, demonstrating a strong quality/efficiency trade-off.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two video decomposition baselines that output similar layered sprite representations: Layered Neural Atlases (LNA, Kasten et al., TOG 2021) and Deformable Sprites (DS, Ye et al., CVPR 2022). At the 10-minute budget, the proposed method decisively outperforms both on every metric, with sprite RGB L1 roughly 2.3× lower than LNA and 2.5× lower than DS. After convergence, the method wins on both sprite-level metrics (RGB L1: 0.0984 vs. 0.2332 for LNA and 0.1190 for DS; Alpha L1: 0.0179 vs. 0.0336 and 0.0294), while DS achieves lower frame-level L1 (0.0054) and LPIPS (0.0224), which the authors attribute to DS's higher representational flexibility fitting pixel-level error without producing a semantically correct decomposition. Qualitatively, LNA produces blurred reconstructions with rough sprite boundaries, DS sometimes merges objects into a single sprite, while the proposed method's constrained affine-plus-static-texture representation avoids these failure modes. The authors emphasize that sprite error is the more meaningful metric for editing applications, where per-object correctness matters more than aggregate pixel fidelity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of video decomposition (LNA, Deformable Sprites, Sprite-from-Sprite) and image vectorization, but deliberately narrows the representation space for the animated-graphics domain: static RGBA textures, per-frame affine transforms, and a scalar sprite-level opacity, rather than the dynamic textures, B-spline deformations, or MLP-based warping used by prior methods. It builds on the Deep Image Prior framework by using a U-Net as a shared texture prior across all sprites to regularize the optimization, and repurposes the pre-trained TAM video object segmentation model as an initialization oracle from a single-frame bounding-box prompt. The key conceptual departure from LNA and DS is the argument that their richer parameterizations are over-parameterized for animated graphics, where the simpler affine-plus-opacity model is both sufficient and more regularized.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is an interactive-quality decomposition pipeline (≈10 min on a single T4) that produces editable sprites suitable for designer workflows, validated on a new real-world dataset (Crello Animation) with ground-truth composition information that prior synthetic or natural-video datasets lack. The finding that a deliberately constrained representation outperforms more flexible ones on sprite-level quality (while DS achieves lower frame error) highlights a useful tension between reconstruction fidelity and decomposition correctness. Open questions include whether the static-texture assumption generalizes beyond preset animation types, whether keyframe-based parameterization (as suggested in the conclusion) can further reduce the per-frame parameter count, and whether the method extends to animation types beyond motion and opacity (e.g., blur, lighting).
