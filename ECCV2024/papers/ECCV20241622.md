## Problem

Existing FSCIL evaluation metrics (aAcc, lAcc) are dominated by base-class performance due to the large class-ratio between base and novel tasks, effectively masking poor novel-class generalization. Additionally, the potential of ViT intermediate features—which retain more generalizable, less class-specific information than final features—remains unexplored for improving novel-class accuracy in FSCIL.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241622/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes standard RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-100/miniImageNet, 224×224 for CUB-200) and outputs a class label via a cosine classifier over L2-normalized features. The ViT backbone is frozen after base-task training; only the lightweight FR module (~0.6M params) is trained during novel tasks, which follow a 5-way 5-shot (or 10-way 5-shot for CUB-200) protocol. The classifier prototypes for novel classes are initialized as mean features from the frozen backbone.

## Method

The core insight is that ViT intermediate layers (e.g., L8–L10) produce features that are more dispersed and generalizable for unseen classes, while final-layer features overfit to base classes. A Feature Rectification (FR) module—a 2-layer MLP operating on concatenated final and intermediate features—rectifies the final representation to be more transferable. Training uses two relation-transfer losses: an instance-level loss (smooth L1 on pairwise Euclidean distances) and a class-center loss (KL divergence between softmax distributions over class prototypes), plus a cosine constraint to preserve base-class discriminability and a cross-entropy loss for novel classes. Anti-forgetting is handled by sampling features from per-class Gaussian distributions fitted to old-task data. Multiple FR modules at layers 8, 9, and 10 are ensembled by averaging their classifier predictions, and the paper also introduces a generalized accuracy (gAcc) metric parameterized by α∈[0,1] with an AUC summary to provide balanced base-novel evaluation.

## Results

On miniImageNet, the proposed method (YourSelf) achieves 68.80% aAcc and 62.20% gAcc with only 11.57M parameters, outperforming the previous SOTA NC-FSCIL (67.90% aAcc, 60.93% gAcc) by 0.90% and 1.27% respectively. On CUB-200, it reaches 69.85% aAcc and 65.50% gAcc, surpassing SAVC (68.33% aAcc, 62.90% gAcc) by 1.52% and 2.60%. On CIFAR-100, it achieves 67.02% aAcc (comparable to NC-FSCIL's 67.06%) and 60.32% gAcc (beating NC-FSCIL's 59.73%). The method uses a lightweight Feature Rectification module (~0.6M params) on top of a ViT backbone, requiring no complex network redesign.

## Baselines & Comparisons

YourSelf outperforms all 18 compared baselines on both aAcc and gAcc across all three datasets. On miniImageNet, it beats the strongest aAcc competitor NC-FSCIL [59] by 0.90% (68.80 vs 67.90) and the strongest gAcc competitor ALICE [42] by 2.48% (62.20 vs 59.72), while using fewer parameters (11.57M vs 15.87M and 11.20M respectively). On CUB-200, the margin over SAVC [49] is larger: +1.52% aAcc and +2.60% gAcc, with SAVC using more than double the parameters (24.29M). On CIFAR-100, aAcc is essentially tied with NC-FSCIL (67.02 vs 67.06), but gAcc clearly favors YourSelf (60.32 vs 59.73), confirming the new metric reveals a more balanced base-novel performance. Notably, methods with high aAcc like CLOM [76] (57.23%) and C-FSCIL [20] (55.40%) show much lower gAcc (46.11% and 50.46%), exposing that their base-class dominance masks weak novel-class performance. The lightweight S3C [24] (0.34M params) achieves competitive gAcc (53.70%) but trails YourSelf by 8.50% on gAcc, highlighting the value of the FR module's intermediate-feature rectification.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a critical re-examination of the FSCIL benchmark rather than a direct extension of a single prior method. It departs from the standard aAcc/lAcc evaluation inherited from classical class-incremental learning by introducing the generalized accuracy (gAcc) metric, and it repurposes ViT intermediate features—previously exploited in dense prediction tasks (e.g., Path Aggregation Network, HRFormer)—for the first time in a classification setting. The Feature Rectification module draws conceptually from relational knowledge distillation (Park et al., Passalis et al.) but with a distinct objective: transferring hierarchical structural cues from shallower layers to rectify overfitted final features rather than matching a teacher's output. The anti-forgetting strategy via Gaussian feature sampling connects to prior work on distributional replay (Zhu et al., Tang et al.) but applies it to a frozen-backbone, few-shot regime.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The gAcc metric addresses a genuine blind spot in FSCIL evaluation: the large base-to-novel class ratio (10–12×) makes aAcc insensitive to novel-class performance, potentially rewarding methods that merely preserve base accuracy. The finding that intermediate ViT layers encode more transferable, less class-specific representations than the final layer challenges the default assumption that deeper features are uniformly superior, and the lightweight FR module (0.6M params) offers a practical, architecture-agnostic way to exploit this. Open questions include whether gAcc should become a standard reporting metric alongside aAcc, whether the intermediate-feature insight generalizes to non-ViT backbones (e.g., CNNs, MLP-Mixer), and whether the relation-transfer losses remain effective at larger scales or with more novel tasks. The method's reliance on a frozen ViT backbone and Gaussian feature sampling for replay also limits its applicability to settings where the backbone must be updated.
