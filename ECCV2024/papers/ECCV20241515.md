## Problem

Extracting high-quality iso-surfaces from neural implicit fields requires dense uniform grids, causing the number of network queries to grow cubically with resolution (e.g., >1 billion queries and >150 GB RAM at 1024³ resolution). Existing spatial decomposition methods waste the majority of queries in empty or geometrically flat regions, while surface-tracking and Delaunay-refinement approaches are either hard to parallelize or computationally expensive for high-resolution extraction.

## Input / Output

The method takes any queryable implicit field (neural SDF, density field, or analytical SDF) and a user-specified iso-value α, and produces an adaptive tetrahedron grid (McGrids) from which a 3D surface mesh is extracted via marching tetrahedra. Unlike uniform-grid methods that require a fixed resolution parameter, McGrids is controlled by a terminating threshold on midpoint approximation error, allowing it to allocate grid density adaptively—dense near surfaces and sharp features, sparse in voids—without a global resolution setting. The method is plug-and-play and requires no normal vectors or gradient information as input.

## Method

McGrids casts adaptive grid construction as a Monte Carlo sampling problem by defining a probability density function d(x) = κ / (γ + |f(x) − α|) that concentrates samples near the iso-surface and in high-curvature regions. Starting from a uniform distribution, the algorithm iteratively samples points from the current PDF, inserts them into a Delaunay tetrahedralization (with dual Voronoi diagram), updates the PDF using the new points' implicit values, and applies Lloyd's algorithm (stable CVT) to relax points toward their Voronoi centroids for grid regularity. A refinement phase then trims tetrahedra that do not intersect the surface and inserts midpoints into tetrahedra whose midpoint approximation error exceeds a user-defined threshold, iteratively subdividing until all cells meet the accuracy criterion. The final mesh is extracted from the converged adaptive tetrahedron grid using standard marching tetrahedra.

## Results

On a dataset of complex meshes (Stanford, Thingiverse, NIE), McGrids at a 1e-3 termination threshold achieves the best Chamfer Distance (CD = 0.54×10⁻⁵) with only 0.44M implicit field queries, 2.12 s runtime, and 0.91 GB memory — compared to 16.78M queries, 12.85 s, and 2.57 GB for Marching Cubes at 256³. At a tighter 5e-4 threshold, McGrids reaches CD = 0.51×10⁻⁵, F1 = 0.97, and EF1 = 0.77 with just 0.7M queries, outperforming Neural Dual Contouring (CD = 0.56, EF1 = 0.75) which requires 16.78M queries and 21.76 GB memory. In the differentiable multiview reconstruction application, McGrids achieves CD = 2.14×10⁻⁵ and NC = 0.96, beating DMT (CD = 2.51) and FlexiCubes (CD = 2.23).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Marching Cubes (256³ and 512³), McGrids achieves substantially lower CD (0.54 vs. 1.27 at 256³; 0.51 vs. 0.73 at 512³) while using 1–2 orders of magnitude fewer queries (0.44M–0.7M vs. 16.78M–134.22M) and far less memory (0.91–0.93 GB vs. 2.57–15.2 GB). Compared to Neural Dual Contouring, McGrids at 5e-4 matches or beats NDC on CD (0.51 vs. 0.56) and EF1 (0.77 vs. 0.75) with only 4% of the queries (0.7M vs. 16.78M) and 23× less memory (0.93 GB vs. 21.76 GB); at 1e-4, McGrids further improves ECD (0.21 vs. 0.23) and EF1 (0.81 vs. 0.75). Versus the Octree method, McGrids at 5e-4 wins on CD (0.51 vs. 0.56) and EF1 (0.77 vs. 0.71) with comparable query counts. In the differentiable reconstruction setting, McGrids outperforms both DMT and FlexiCubes on every metric (CD 2.14 vs. 2.51/2.23; NC 0.96 vs. 0.91/0.92; EF1 0.07 vs. 0.02/0.03), though at the cost of slower per-iteration runtime due to dynamic grid construction. Marching Tetrahedra at 256³ is the only baseline McGrids does not clearly dominate on all metrics, as MT256 has a lower ECD (2.56 vs. 0.47) but far worse CD (0.61 vs. 0.54) and EF1 (0.41 vs. 0.64).

## Relation to Prior Work

McGrids builds on Marching Tetrahedra as its final mesh-extraction step but fundamentally rethinks the grid-construction phase that all spatial-decomposition methods (Marching Cubes, Octree) treat as a fixed uniform or hierarchically-refined structure. The key conceptual departure is casting adaptive grid generation as a Monte Carlo probability-sampling problem, where a curvature-weighted PDF (Eq. 1) guides iterative point insertion into a Delaunay tetrahedralization, with CVT (Lloyd's algorithm) enforcing local regularity. This positions the work between the computationally expensive Delaunay-refinement literature and the query-wasteful uniform-grid methods, while the midpoint-insertion refinement step borrows ideas from adaptive meshing without requiring explicit curvature or normal computation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: McGrids reduces neural implicit field queries by 1–2 orders of magnitude (e.g., 1.07B → 62M in Fig. 1; 134M → 0.7M vs. MC512) while matching or exceeding mesh quality, directly addressing the cubic-scaling bottleneck that makes high-resolution extraction from learned SDFs prohibitively expensive. As a plug-and-play component, it integrates into differentiable pipelines (DMT, FlexiCubes) and could accelerate any application relying on neural implicit representations. Limitations include additional grid-construction overhead that makes it less attractive for low-resolution or analytically cheap fields, and the absence of normal-based vertex placement (as in Dual Contouring) for sharp features. Open questions include scalability to very large or dynamic scenes, and whether the Monte Carlo iteration count can be further reduced with variance-reduction techniques.
