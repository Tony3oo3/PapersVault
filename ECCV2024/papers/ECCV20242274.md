## Problem

Conventional deep learning approaches for camera-based respiratory measurement treat optical flow components and depth as independent scalar channels, ignoring the geometric relationships inherent in the underlying physical process. Respiratory motion is naturally described by a 2D vector field (apparent motion) coupled with a scalar field (depth), yet standard architectures lack the inductive bias to model these coupled geometric structures, limiting their ability to extract subtle respiratory signals from RGB video.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20242274/cropped_page4_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard RGB video, which is preprocessed into a 3D motion representation: optical flow (RAFT-Small) providing a 2D vector field and monocular depth estimation (MiDaS DPT-Large with SwinV2) providing a scalar field. Each pixel-frame is encoded as a Clifford algebra multivector (ν^(i), ν^(j), z) ∈ Cl_{p,q}, downsampled to 20 FPS at 36×36 resolution and windowed into 399-frame segments. The network outputs a per-frame respiratory waveform, from which respiratory rate (breaths/min) is extracted by maximizing the power spectral density of the bandpass-filtered signal.

## Method

CliffPhys replaces standard convolutional and linear layers with Clifford Neural Layers that operate entirely within a chosen Clifford algebra Cl_{p,q} (with p,q as hyperparameters over {Cl_{2,0}, Cl_{3,0}, Cl_{0,2}, Cl_{0,3}}). The architecture comprises two Clifford 3D-convolutional layers (T=15, K=3), two Clifford linear layers performing spatial downsampling from 36×36 to 128 to 1, and a final traditional linear layer projecting the multivector coefficients to a scalar. Crucially, the Clifford product (rather than dot-product projections) couples the motion and depth components through learned geometric transformations—rotations, reflections, and roto-translations—preserving their physical relationships throughout the network. No non-linear activations or channel expansion are used in the Clifford layers, keeping the architecture compact. Models are pre-trained on the synthetic SCAMPS dataset and fine-tuned on COHFACE with AdamW, cyclic learning rate, and negative Pearson loss.

## Results

On the COHFACE test set (intra-dataset), the best model CliffPhys_Cl_{0,2} with depth achieves RMSE of 1.97 breaths/min, MAE of 0.83, MAPE of 10.62%, PCC of 0.86, and CCC of 0.83. On the BP4D+ dataset (cross-dataset), CliffPhys_Cl_{3,0} with depth delivers the strongest performance with RMSE of 5.38, MAE of 3.50, MAPE of 19.36%, PCC of 0.41, and CCC of 0.36. All CliffPhys variants with depth information outperform every RGB-based neural method and motion-based baseline on both datasets, with the 3D Conv ablation (same architecture, standard layers) confirming the importance of the Clifford product (RMSE 4.64 vs 1.97 on COHFACE).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CliffPhys outperforms all five compared baselines on both COHFACE and BP4D+ across every metric. Against the motion-based Median baseline, the best CliffPhys model (Cl_{0,2}+depth) reduces RMSE from 3.35 to 1.97 on COHFACE and from 6.25 to 5.38 on BP4D+, while also improving PCC from 0.72→0.86 and 0.17→0.41 respectively. Against Profile1D, the gap is even wider (RMSE 1.97 vs 6.58 on COHFACE). Among RGB-based neural methods, MTTS-CAN is the strongest competitor (RMSE 4.84 on COHFACE, 6.86 on BP4D+), yet CliffPhys still leads by a substantial margin (1.97 and 5.38 respectively). The rPPG-based methods PhysFormer and ContrastPhys perform poorly on respiratory estimation (PCC near zero on COHFACE), confirming that rPPG signals are ill-suited for respiration. The 3D Conv ablation (identical architecture with standard layers) achieves RMSE 4.64 on COHFACE, demonstrating that the Clifford product itself—rather than the input representation alone—contributes roughly 2.7 RPM of RMSE reduction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes Clifford Neural Layers (Brandstetter et al., 2022), originally designed for PDE modeling, to the domain of camera-based respiratory measurement. It extends the standard camera-signal model (Eq. 1 from Wang et al.) by incorporating monocular depth estimation alongside optical flow, treating the resulting 2D vector field and scalar field as a unified element of a Clifford algebra rather than independent channels. Conceptually, the paper departs from the dominant dual-branch (motion + appearance) paradigm in rPPG and motion-based respiratory estimation by arguing that the geometric relationship between apparent motion and depth is best captured through hypercomplex algebraic operations rather than learned channel mixing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical takeaway is that a compact 5-layer architecture with strong geometric inductive bias can outperform substantially larger transformer and multi-task networks for subtle respiratory motion extraction, while using fewer parameters and FLOPs. However, cross-dataset generalization remains a significant limitation: BP4D+ results show a substantial performance drop compared to COHFACE, and even the best cross-dataset model (CliffPhys_Cl_{3,0}) achieves only PCC 0.41. Open questions include whether the Clifford algebraic structure generalizes to other physiological signals (heart rate, SpO2), whether the monocular depth estimation quality is a bottleneck, and whether the geometric bias advantage persists at larger scales or with noisier real-world footage.
