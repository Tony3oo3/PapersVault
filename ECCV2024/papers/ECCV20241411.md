## Problem

Negative prompting for diffusion models can improve prompt adherence and image quality, but finding effective negative prompts is difficult due to a semantic gap between human concept representations and those internal to the DM. What a human considers a natural negation of a prompt is often not what the DM treats as a negative, making good negative prompts unintuitive, seed-dependent, and requiring extensive trial and error.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241411/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single text prompt p as input and produces a final synthesized image by prompting the DM with the pair (p, n*), where n* is a diffusion-negative prompt. The intermediate step samples a diffusion-negative image via DNS, which is then translated into natural language either by a human user (DNP) or automatically by a captioning model such as BLIP-2 (auto-DNP). No retraining or fine-tuning of the diffusion model is required; the entire procedure operates at inference time.

## Method

The core contribution is Diffusion-Negative Sampling (DNS), derived from an energy-based model interpretation of diffusion models. The optimal negative prompt n* is defined as the one that maximizes the steepness of the log-odds ratio between the positive prompt p and the negative, which yields the closed-form solution ε_{n*} = −ε_p (equal-magnitude opposite noise). This translates to the DNS denoising equation ε̂ = ε̂_φ + s(ε̂_φ − ε̂_p), which is the exact 'flip' of classifier-free guidance and can be implemented by simply swapping the positive and negative prompt slots in any model that already supports negative prompting. The sampled diffusion-negative image is then captioned to produce n*, and the DM is re-prompted with (p, n*) to generate the final image. The approach is training-free, seed-agnostic, and composable with existing DM variants such as Attend-and-Excite.

## Results

On the A&E dataset, SD+auto-DNP achieves a Min. Object CLIP Score of 0.258 (+6.61% over SD) and Full Prompt CLIP Score of 0.346 (+3.28%), with human evaluators preferring it 3.4× more for correctness (64.22% vs 18.90%) and 2.5× more for quality (63.81% vs 25.73%). On the H&H dataset, SD+auto-DNP improves CLIP Score by 2.80% (human) and 3.88% (hand), with human evaluators preferring it ~3× more for correctness and ~2× more for quality over SD. When applied to the A&E model, auto-DNP boosts IS by 10.62% (11.86→13.12) and Min. Object CLIP by 4.54%, approaching the theoretical upper bound of 0.29.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Stable Diffusion (SD), auto-DNP consistently wins on every metric across both datasets: CLIP Score improves by 2.8–6.6%, IS by 1.4–2.9%, and human preference for correctness jumps from ~19–28% to ~58–64% (roughly 3×), while quality preference rises from ~23–29% to ~64–69% (roughly 2.5×). Against Attend-and-Excite (A&E), SD+auto-DNP actually matches or exceeds A&E on Min. Object CLIP (0.258 vs 0.264) and IS (13.35 vs 11.86) despite not being specifically designed for multi-object generation, and human evaluators strongly prefer SD+auto-DNP (64.22% correctness) over A&E (30.06%). When DNP is applied on top of A&E itself, it yields a 10.62% IS improvement and pushes Min. Object CLIP to 0.276, close to the 0.29 theoretical upper bound, confirming that DNP is composable with existing attention-based methods rather than competing with them.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the negative prompting framework introduced by Liu et al. [16] by providing a principled, training-free procedure (DNS) for automatically discovering effective negative prompts, rather than relying on human trial-and-error. Conceptually, DNS is derived as the exact 'opposite' of classifier-free guidance [11]: where CFG amplifies the conditional distribution p(z|p) over the unconditional p(z), DNS inverts this ratio to sample images least compliant with the prompt. The paper positions itself as complementary to attention-based correction methods (Attend-and-Excite [3], linguistic binding [21]) and structured conditioning approaches, arguing that DNP can be stacked on top of any of these without interfering with their intermediate steps.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of a 'semantic gap' between human and DM notions of negation provides a useful diagnostic for why negative prompting is so finicky in practice, and the DNS procedure (simply swapping positive and negative prompts) offers a trivially implementable fix that requires no retraining. However, the quantitative gains are modest (3–7% CLIP improvements, ~3× human preference), and the method adds an extra sampling pass per prompt-seed pair. Open questions include whether the semantic gap persists across architectures and scales, whether DNS generalizes to video or 3D generation, and whether the unintuitive negative prompts it produces can be further decoded to yield actionable insights about DM failure modes.
