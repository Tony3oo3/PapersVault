## Problem

As photorealistic talking-head avatar generators become ubiquitous in video conferencing and AR/VR, a critical safety question emerges: can we verify that a synthetic avatar is driven by an authorized identity, rather than someone else's likeness being used without consent? Existing deepfake detection methods classify real vs. synthetic media, but here all inputs are synthetic by design, making the relevant question one of identity attribution rather than authenticity. No prior dataset or method addresses this 'avatar fingerprinting' task.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240389/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a synthetic talking-head video clip of 71 frames as input and produces a dynamic identity embedding vector per clip. At evaluation, Euclidean distances between the query clip's embedding and a pool of self-reenactment embeddings are thresholded to classify the video as authorized (self-reenactment) or unauthorized (cross-reenactment). Crucially, no prior knowledge of the driving identity is required at test time; the model relies solely on the synthetic video's temporal facial dynamics, and the per-frame features are pairwise normalized landmark distances that are invariant to the target identity's facial shape.

## Method

The approach extracts 126 facial landmarks per frame and computes pairwise normalized Euclidean distances to obtain appearance-agnostic motion features, which are concatenated over 71-frame clips and fed into a temporal 3D CNN. A novel three-term contrastive loss shapes the embedding space: a pull term draws together clips driven by the same identity across different target appearances, a push term separates clips driven by different identities sharing the same target, and a time-shuffling term penalizes the model for relying on static expression snapshots rather than temporal progression. This design ensures the learned embedding captures idiosyncratic motion dynamics (e.g., how a person frowns or smiles) rather than generator-specific artifacts, enabling generalization to unseen talking-head generators (average AUC 0.83 vs. 0.85 on seen generators).

## Results

The proposed avatar fingerprinting method achieves an average AUC of 0.85 across three face-reenactment generators (face-vid2vid, LIA, TPS) on the NVFAIR test set of 35 held-out identities. On face-vid2vid specifically, the method reaches AUC=0.868, substantially outperforming both baselines. When trained on one generator and evaluated on the other two unseen generators, the method maintains an average AUC of 0.83, demonstrating strong cross-generator generalization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines adapted from deepfake detection to the avatar fingerprinting task. ID-Reveal [20], which learns an identity-aware embedding space for real-vs-synthetic detection, achieves AUC=0.720 on face-vid2vid, while Agarwal et al. [9], which trains a separate per-identity detector using person-specific facial expression patterns, reaches AUC=0.726. The proposed method (AUC=0.868) outperforms both by a wide margin of approximately 0.14–0.15 AUC. Notably, Agarwal et al. requires training 35 separate models (one per test identity), whereas the proposed method uses a single unified model. The ablation (Table 2) further shows that the landmark-distance features alone (with ID-Reveal's loss) yield AUC=0.754, and adding the proposed contrastive loss with the temporal-shuffling term pushes performance to 0.868, confirming that both the feature design and the loss formulation contribute substantially to the gap over baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a new task—avatar fingerprinting—that is conceptually distinct from both deepfake detection (real vs. synthetic) and active watermarking. It repurposes the cognitive-science finding that individuals have idiosyncratic 'dynamic facial identity signatures' (O'Toole et al., Hill & Johnston, Knappmeyer et al.) and the identity-specific feature ideas from deepfake detectors like Agarwal et al. and ID-Reveal, but reframes them as a verification problem over purely synthetic inputs. Methodologically, it combines a straightforward temporal CNN with a novel three-term contrastive loss (pull, push, time-shuffle) and uses pairwise normalized landmark distances as appearance-agnostic input features, departing from the 3DMM representations used by prior identity-aware detectors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The NVFAIR dataset (650K+ synthetic videos across 161 identities and three state-of-the-art generators) is the primary practical contribution, filling a gap no existing talking-head dataset addresses for this task. The demonstrated generalization to unseen generators (AUC 0.83) suggests the learned embeddings capture genuine identity dynamics rather than generator-specific artifacts, which is encouraging for real-world deployment. Key limitations remain: reduced discriminability for low-emotion subjects, degradation when generators fail to preserve critical expressions, and coverage of only one-on-one conversation styles. Open questions include whether micro-expression-level features can close the gap for neutral speakers, how the approach scales to group interactions, and whether it can be integrated into live video-conferencing pipelines with minimal latency.
