## Problem

Density-based OOD detection methods that explicitly model class-conditioned distributions face a scalability bottleneck: ResFlow requires training one flow model per class per layer (e.g., 400 models for CIFAR-100 with ResNet-18), while hybrid approaches demand retraining the entire classifier. Post-hoc score calibration methods, though simpler, degrade significantly under near-OOD (covariate shift) conditions. The paper asks whether a single flow model trained on a fixed classifier's penultimate features can learn class-aware densities sufficient for robust OOD detection across far-OOD, near-OOD, and mixed contexts.

## Input / Output

FlowCon consumes 32×32 RGB images, but the flow model itself operates on the penultimate feature vector of a frozen pretrained classifier (512-dim for ResNet-18, 128-dim for WideResNet), not on raw pixels. At inference, the method outputs a scalar OOD score defined as the maximum likelihood of the transformed latent vector across k per-class Gaussian distributions, and can additionally produce a class label via a Bayes' decision rule over those same distributions. The bijective constraint of normalizing flows forces the latent dimension to match the penultimate feature dimension, which limits performance on lower-dimensional backbones like WideResNet.

## Method

FlowCon trains a RealNVP flow (8 coupling blocks) on the frozen penultimate features of a pretrained classifier using two jointly optimized losses: the standard change-of-variables log-likelihood L_flow, and a novel contrastive loss L_con that replaces the conventional dot-product similarity in supervised contrastive learning with a flow-based similarity S_flow computed from per-sample likelihoods under learned class-conditioned Gaussians (a generalized Bhattacharyya coefficient when τ₁=0.5). This design lets the flow learn k class-specific multimodal distributions in latent space rather than collapsing to a single unimodal Gaussian, so ID samples occupy high-density regions while OOD samples fall into low-density regions. At test time, per-class distributions are aggregated by taking empirical means of per-sample μ and σ, and the OOD score is the maximum likelihood across all k class distributions. The total loss is L = L_con + λ·L_flow with λ=0.07, optimized via Adam at lr=1e-5 for 700 epochs, requiring no external OOD data and no modification to the original classifier.

## Results

On the far-OOD benchmark (Table 1), FlowCon achieves the best AUROC of 97.19 and AUPR-S of 99.43 on CIFAR-10/ResNet18, and 88.22 AUROC with 96.85 AUPR-S on CIFAR-100/ResNet18, outperforming all baselines. On CIFAR-10/WideResNet, FlowCon reports the highest AUPR-E (86.90) and competitive AUROC (96.42), while on CIFAR-100/WideResNet it achieves the best AUPR-S (96.60). Under the mixed near-/far-OOD setting (Table 2), FlowCon on CIFAR-10/ResNet18 attains 93.97 AUROC and 98.74 AUPR-S, substantially outperforming ResFlow (76.40 AUROC, 26.23 AUPR-S). For near-OOD (Table 3), FlowCon on CIFAR-100/ResNet18 achieves 82.80 AUROC and 67.6 FPR-95, the best among all methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FlowCon outperforms all post-hoc baselines (MSP, ODIN, Mahalanobis, Energy, ReAct) on AUROC and AUPR-S across every model/dataset combination in the far-OOD setting, with the largest margins over Mahalanobis (e.g., 88.22 vs. 73.46 AUROC on CIFAR-100/ResNet18). Against ResFlow [51], FlowCon wins on AUROC and AUPR-S in 3 of 4 settings (e.g., 97.19 vs. 95.60 AUROC on CIFAR-10/ResNet18; 88.22 vs. 85.12 on CIFAR-100/ResNet18), but ResFlow achieves a lower FPR-95 (13.22 vs. 16.26 on CIFAR-10/ResNet18) at the cost of requiring 400 separate flow models for CIFAR-100. Against Heatmap [17], which uses external OOD data during training, FlowCon matches or exceeds AUROC (97.19 vs. 96.47 on CIFAR-10/ResNet18) and AUPR-E (85.65 vs. 83.73) without any OOD training data, though Heatmap retains a slight FPR-95 advantage (15.37 vs. 16.26). In the mixed near-/far-OOD setting, FlowCon's advantage over ResFlow is dramatic: 93.97 vs. 76.40 AUROC and 98.74 vs. 26.23 AUPR-S on CIFAR-10/ResNet18, indicating ResFlow's regressor fails under covariate shift. For near-OOD (CIFAR-100 vs. CIFAR-10), FlowCon achieves the best AUROC (82.80) on ResNet18, while post-hoc methods like MSP and Energy remain competitive, reflecting the inherent difficulty of covariate-shift-only detection.

## Relation to Prior Work

FlowCon sits at the intersection of normalizing flows for density estimation (Papamakarios et al.) and supervised contrastive learning (Khosla et al.), but departs from both in a specific way: it replaces the standard dot-product similarity in SCL with a Bhattacharyya-coefficient-based similarity computed from flow likelihoods, enabling contrastive learning over probability distributions rather than feature vectors. Compared to ResFlow (Zisselman et al.), which trains one flow model per class per layer (yielding 400 models for CIFAR-100/ResNet-18), FlowCon consolidates this into a single flow model on the penultimate layer of a fixed classifier. Unlike hybrid approaches (Zhang et al.) that require retraining the classifier, FlowCon operates entirely post-hoc on frozen features, making it deployable without modifying the original network.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in eliminating two major deployment barriers: no external OOD dataset is needed during training, and the original classifier remains untouched. However, the dimensionality constraint of coupling-based flows (input dimension must equal output dimension) limits FlowCon's effectiveness on lower-dimensional features, as evidenced by its degraded performance on WideResNet's 128-dim penultimate layer versus ResNet-18's 512-dim layer. Near-OOD detection remains an open challenge where even FlowCon shows substantial overlap between ID and OOD likelihood distributions, and the paper acknowledges that addressing the dimensionality bottleneck is future work. The class-preserving property—where FlowCon's empirical distributions recover near-original classification accuracy—suggests a single branch can serve both OOD detection and ID classification, but this has not been validated at scale (e.g., ImageNet).
