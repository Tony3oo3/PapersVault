## Problem

Existing LLIE methods rely on features or semantic maps extracted from heavily degraded low-light inputs, which are unreliable under extreme illumination loss and noise. This ill-posed LL-to-NL mapping produces visually unsatisfactory results in real-world scenarios, particularly when the input is severely underexposed. The paper addresses this by introducing an external normal-light codebook prior that constrains the enhancement to a discrete space of known-good NL features, thereby reducing the ambiguity of the transformation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241267/cropped_page1_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single low-light RGB image (tested at 256×256 to 320×320 during training) and outputs an enhanced normal-light RGB image at the same resolution. A pre-trained VQGAN codebook (learned on DIV2K and Flickr2K NL images) is frozen and used as a lookup table during inference. A user-adjustable scalar β in the Adaptive Mix-up Block allows practitioners to trade off between codebook-driven naturalness and input-faithful detail when processing real-world images with unknown illumination conditions.

## Method

GLARE employs a three-stage training pipeline. Stage I pre-trains a VQGAN on thousands of NL images to build a discrete codebook of NL latent features. Stage II trains an Invertible Latent Normalizing Flow (I-LNF) conditioned on LL features to transform the complex NL feature distribution into a simple Gaussian whose mean is derived from the LL input, using negative log-likelihood as the training objective; at inference, sampling from this Gaussian and inverting the flow yields LL features aligned with NL representations for accurate nearest-neighbor code retrieval. Stage III introduces an Adaptive Feature Transformation (AFT) module with a dual-decoder architecture: a fixed NL Decoder processes matched codes for naturalness, while a trainable Multi-scale Fusion Decoder handles I-LNF-generated features for detail, with an Adaptive Mix-up Block using learnable sigmoid-gated coefficients to flexibly blend conditional encoder features into the decoding path.

## Results

On the five paired benchmarks, GLARE achieves the best PSNR across all datasets: 27.35 dB on LOL, 28.98 dB on LOL-v2-real, 29.84 dB on LOL-v2-synthetic, 30.10 dB on SDSD-indoor, and 30.85 dB on SDSD-outdoor. It also attains the lowest LPIPS (0.083 on LOL, 0.097 on LOL-v2-real), surpassing the second-best LL-SKF by 20.9% and 12.6% respectively. On unpaired real-world datasets (Table 2), GLARE records the best NIQE on MEF (3.66) and DICM (3.61) with the lowest mean (3.75). As a preprocessing step for object detection on ExDark, GLARE yields the highest mAP of 77.50, outperforming the previous best IAT (77.19) by 0.31 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GLARE outperforms the strongest prior method LL-SKF [65] by 0.55 dB PSNR on LOL (27.35 vs. 26.80), 0.53 dB on LOL-v2-real (28.98 vs. 28.45), and 0.73 dB on LOL-v2-synthetic (29.84 vs. 29.11), while also reducing LPIPS from 0.105 to 0.083 on LOL. On the SDSD benchmarks, GLARE surpasses Retformer [6] by 0.33 dB (SDSD-indoor) and 1.01 dB (SDSD-outdoor), and exceeds SNR [68] by 0.66 dB and 2.19 dB respectively. Among earlier Retinex-based and transformer-based methods (KinD [77], MIRNet [74], DRBN [70], URetinex-Net [64], Restormer [75]), GLARE leads by 6.5–10 dB in PSNR, indicating a substantial gap over these architectures. On the real-world unpaired evaluation (Table 2), GLARE achieves the best NIQE on MEF (3.66) and DICM (3.61) and the lowest mean (3.75), edging out RFR [18] (mean 3.81) and LL-SKF (mean 3.92). For the downstream ExDark object detection task, GLARE as a preprocessing step yields mAP 77.50, beating IAT [11] (77.19), KinD (76.69), LLFlow [58] (76.44), and LL-SKF (76.43) by at least 0.31 mAP points.

## Relation to Prior Work

GLARE builds on VQGAN (Esser et al., CVPR 2021) for learning a discrete NL codebook and on the conditional normalizing flow formulation of SRFlow (Lugmayr et al., ECCV 2020), but applies the flow at the feature level rather than image space and uses LL features as the prior mean rather than a standard Gaussian. It departs from prior codebook-based restoration methods (VQFR, RestoreFormer, RIDCP) that rely on Transformer-based code-index prediction, arguing instead that a generative invertible flow better bridges the LL-to-NL feature distribution gap. It also contrasts with semantic-prior, illumination-map, and edge-guided LLIE methods by sourcing its guidance from undegraded NL images rather than from the degraded input itself.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GLARE is the first LLIE method to exploit an external NL codebook as a structural prior, and the I-LNF module offers a principled, invertible mechanism for cross-domain feature alignment that could transfer to other degradation-to-clean mapping tasks (e.g., dehazing, denoising). The adjustable β in the AFT module provides a practical user-facing knob for real-world deployment where training and test illuminations diverge. Open questions include whether the codebook can be learned without paired NL data, how the approach scales to video or very high-resolution inputs, and whether the three-stage training pipeline can be unified into a single end-to-end objective.
