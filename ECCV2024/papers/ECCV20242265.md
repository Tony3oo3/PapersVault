## Problem

Existing aerial multi-object tracking datasets exclusively cover stationary-camera settings, leaving the performance of MOT algorithms in moving-camera scenarios—where the field of view shifts substantially between frames—entirely uncharacterized. This gap is critical because real-world aerial surveillance from airplanes and helicopters inherently involves camera motion, low frame rates (1–2 Hz), and large spatial coverage, conditions under which standard motion models and association strategies are known to degrade.

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes large aerial image sequences (5592×3728 px) acquired at 1–2 Hz with 55–100% inter-frame overlap, along with per-sequence metadata (GSD, frame rate, viewing angle). Detection is performed by patching images into 1024×1024 tiles with 20% overlap and multi-scale resampling, producing OBB detections that are converted to HBB for tracking. The output is a set of consistent track IDs per vehicle across the sequence, with the dataset itself providing polygon, OBB, and HBB annotations enriched with attributes (occluded, truncated, stopped, attached, etc.) for three aggregated vehicle classes (HDV, LDV, Motorcycle).

## Method

The paper's algorithmic contribution is Deep SR-SORT, an enhancement of Deep SORT that replaces the Kalman-filter-based motion model (set to zero influence) with two key mechanisms: (1) BRISK-based camera motion compensation to align detections across frames despite platform movement, and (2) an adaptive oriented-rectangle search area whose dimensions are computed from the estimated GSD, sequence frame rate, and a maximum vehicle speed of 60 m/s, then oriented and positioned ahead of each track using its OBB orientation. Objects falling outside this search radius receive infinite matching cost, preventing spurious long-range associations that plague IoU-based matching at low frame rates. Re-identification uses a ResNet-50 backbone trained on the VETRA training set with HBB crops (background included), and detection uses DINO with an OBB head pre-trained on EAGLE. The combination yields HOTA of 82.18 and MOTA of 88.45 on the VETRA test set, substantially outperforming ByteTrack (HOTA 36.37), BoT-SORT (50.84), Deep SORT (58.29), and Deep OC-SORT (46.84).

## Results

On the VETRA test set, the proposed Deep SR-SORT achieves a HOTA of 82.18%, MOTA of 88.45%, and IDF1 of 90.32%, with only 792 identity switches. This substantially outperforms the next-best baseline, Deep SORT (HOTA 58.29%, MOTA 76.42%, IDF1 58.05%, 3,077 IDSW), by 23.9 points in HOTA and 32.3 points in IDF1. MOTP remains consistently high (~88%) across all methods, indicating that detection quality is not the bottleneck; the primary differentiator is association accuracy under camera motion and low frame rates.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Deep SR-SORT decisively outperforms all four baselines on every tracking metric. Against ByteTrack (motion-only, HOTA 36.37%), the improvement is 45.8 HOTA points, as ByteTrack struggles to initialize tracks under the low frame rates (~1.3 FPS) and large inter-frame displacements in VETRA. BoT-SORT (HOTA 50.84%) benefits from camera motion compensation and Re-ID but still relies on IoU-based matching, which is ill-suited for large object displacements between frames. Deep SORT (HOTA 58.29%, the strongest baseline) uses Re-ID with the Kalman filter influence set to zero, avoiding motion-model errors but suffering from mismatches when similar vehicles appear at distance. Deep OC-SORT (HOTA 46.84%) actually underperforms Deep SORT despite adding CMC and adaptive appearance integration, because its motion model is poorly adapted to VETRA's fast camera and vehicle motion. Deep SR-SORT's key advantage—BRISK-based camera motion compensation combined with an adaptive oriented search radius—reduces IDSW from 3,077 (Deep SORT) to 792, a 74% reduction, by constraining the association search to a physically plausible displacement window.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Deep SORT tracking framework (Wojke et al., 2017) by adding BRISK-based camera motion compensation and an adaptive search radius, positioning it against the broader family of SORT variants (ByteTrack, BoT-SORT, Deep OC-SORT) that rely on Kalman-filter motion modeling and IoU-based association. Conceptually, it departs from the assumption that motion modeling is beneficial in MOT: the authors find that disabling the KF entirely in Deep SORT yields better results than incorporating it, suggesting that at low frame rates with high camera motion, appearance-only association is more reliable. The dataset itself extends the aerial MOT benchmarking landscape (UAVDT, VisDrone, KIT AIS, pNEUMA) by introducing moving-camera, low-frame-rate (1–2 Hz) sequences from airplane and helicopter platforms, a setting absent from all prior aerial tracking datasets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

VETRA fills a concrete gap: no prior aerial MOT dataset covers the combination of moving cameras, low frame rates, and large spatial coverage (up to 9.22 km²) that characterizes real-world high-altitude surveillance. The benchmark reveals that state-of-the-art online MOT algorithms degrade substantially under these conditions, with the best tested method (Deep SR-SORT) still achieving only ~82% HOTA, leaving clear room for improved motion modeling and association strategies. Open questions include how to better handle high-GSD sequences where appearance features are weak, how to resolve identity switches among visually similar HDVs in congested traffic, and whether geospatial metadata (provided but unused by current methods) can be effectively integrated. The application-driven vehicle classification scheme also opens a path toward seamless integration with transport-research data pipelines.
