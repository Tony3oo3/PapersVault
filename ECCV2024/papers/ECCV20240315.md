## Problem

Existing MRI analysis models are tightly coupled to specific contrasts, resolutions, and orientations, causing sharp performance drops when deployment conditions differ from training. While contrast-agnostic models have emerged, they remain task-specific, and no general-purpose, contrast-agnostic feature representation exists for brain imaging that can adapt to diverse downstream tasks across both MRI and CT modalities.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240315/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Brain-ID accepts 3D brain volumes in any MR contrast (T1w, T2w, FLAIR) or CT, at arbitrary resolutions (tested down to 7 mm isotropic) and with varying artifacts, producing a 64-channel anatomical feature map from the bottleneck of a 5-level 3D UNet. Downstream adaptation via a single linear layer yields task-specific outputs: reconstructed T1w volumes, 30-region segmentation masks, super-resolved 1 mm images, or bias field estimates. Notably, all pre-training is performed exclusively on synthetic images generated from segmentation labels, so the model never sees real acquired data during feature learning.

## Method

Brain-ID's core mechanism is an on-the-fly intra-subject data generator that, given a subject's brain segmentation labels, synthesizes a mini-batch of 4 images by applying a random deformation field (affine + stationary velocity field), painting per-region Gaussian intensities to simulate arbitrary contrasts, and progressively corrupting samples from mild to severe (resolution reduction, noise, artifacts). A 5-level 3D UNet backbone extracts features from these intra-subject samples, and a linear projection layer maps them to the subject's MP-RAGE anatomy, with training supervised by L1 reconstruction plus gradient L1 loss summed over all intra-subject samples. This anatomy-guided objective ensures features encode subject-specific morphology while remaining invariant to appearance. For downstream tasks, a single additional linear layer is appended: for contrast-independent tasks (segmentation, reconstruction) it operates on features alone, while for contrast-dependent tasks (super-resolution, bias field estimation) the original input image is channel-concatenated with the features before the linear layer, preserving contrast information the features intentionally discard.

## Results

On the largest evaluation dataset (ADNI, 1841 train / 204 test), Brain-ID achieves a reconstruction PSNR of 33.82 dB and SSIM of 0.993, substantially outperforming SynthSR (26.78 dB / 0.980) and CIFL (26.97 dB / 0.973). For brain segmentation, Brain-ID reaches a Dice of 0.837, surpassing FastSurfer (0.803) and SAMSEG (0.795). In feature robustness (Table 1), Brain-ID's intra-subject canonical features achieve SSIM 0.858 and MS-SSIM 0.921, compared to CIFL's 0.511 and 0.531, confirming far greater invariance to contrast, resolution, and corruption. Across all six datasets and four downstream tasks, Brain-ID attains the best or near-best score on every metric, with the largest margins over baselines on smaller datasets (e.g., ADNI3, AIBL-FLAIR).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Brain-ID outperforms CIFL [15] (the only prior contrast-agnostic brain feature method) by a wide margin on every metric: on ADNI reconstruction, PSNR improves from 26.97 to 33.82 dB (+6.85), and intra-subject feature SSIM jumps from 0.511 to 0.858. Against SynthSR [27] (state-of-the-art contrast-agnostic T1w synthesis), Brain-ID gains +7.04 dB PSNR on ADNI reconstruction and +1.24 dB on 3 mm super-resolution, while also handling segmentation and bias-field estimation that SynthSR cannot. For segmentation, Brain-ID (Dice 0.837 on ADNI) beats both FastSurfer (0.803) and SAMSEG (0.795), and extends to FLAIR and CT modalities where those T1w-only or contrast-specific methods are not applicable. Compared to the SCRATCH baseline (same architecture and data generator, trained without pre-training), Brain-ID's pre-trained features yield consistent gains that grow on smaller datasets: on ADNI3, Brain-ID achieves Dice 0.843 versus SCRATCH's 0.792, and on AIBL-FLAIR (272/30) the reconstruction PSNR gap widens to 28.10 vs 24.99 dB. The one area where Brain-ID's advantage is modest is bias-field estimation, where the task is largely anatomy-independent and all methods converge to similar norm-L2 scores.

## Relation to Prior Work

Brain-ID synthesizes two previously separate lines of work: the synthetic-data generation pipeline from the Synth* family (SynthSR, SynthMorph, SynthSeg) and the contrast-agnostic feature representation approach of CIFL. It departs from the Synth* models by learning a task-agnostic representation rather than a task-specific output, and departs from CIFL by replacing contrastive channel supervision with anatomy-guided (MP-RAGE) reconstruction and by introducing intra-subject mild-to-severe corruption generation. The one-layer adaptation strategy for both contrast-independent and contrast-dependent downstream tasks is a novel unification not present in either prior line.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Brain-ID addresses a core practical bottleneck in clinical MRI: the need to retrain or fine-tune models for every new contrast, resolution, or site protocol. By showing that a single synthetic-data-trained representation can be adapted to four distinct tasks (reconstruction, segmentation, super-resolution, bias field estimation) with one layer, it offers a path toward truly generalizable brain imaging pipelines, particularly for low-field and heterogeneous clinical settings. Key limitations remain: the method struggles with extensive pathology (since training data is generated from healthy anatomies), is not designed for non-voxel-wise tasks like functional mapping, and the features carry privacy risks equivalent to the original scans. Open questions include whether the anatomy-guided formulation generalizes beyond brain imaging and how to incorporate pathology into the synthetic generation loop.
