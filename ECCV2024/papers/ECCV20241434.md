## Problem

Inverse rendering of outdoor scenes from in-the-wild image collections is ill-posed due to illumination/albedo ambiguities and non-local, discontinuous cast shadows from sky occlusion. Prior methods either mask out sky pixels entirely or use non-differentiable visibility (e.g., mesh rasterisation or phased training), preventing shadow cues from informing geometry and illumination estimation. This paper exploits the fact that every sky pixel is a direct observation of distant lighting and introduces a differentiable outside-in visibility model to enable fully end-to-end training.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a multi-view outdoor photo collection with COLMAP-estimated poses and intrinsics, plus ViT-Adapter semantic segmentation maps (Cityscapes convention) used to identify sky, ground, foreground, and transient objects; rays are sampled only from non-transient pixels. The scene is gravity-aligned by fitting a plane to camera positions. Outputs include a neural SDF (hash-grid + 2-layer MLP), a per-point diffuse albedo field, per-image HDR illumination parameterised by a RENI++ latent code (K=100) and absolute scale, and a FiLM-conditioned SIREN spherical DDF on the radius-1 bounding sphere that encodes position-dependent sky visibility. Relit renderings are produced by swapping the learned illumination for novel environment maps.

## Method

NeuSky builds on NeuS-Facto (hash-grid SDF + volume rendering) and introduces two key mechanisms. First, a sky-pixel constrained illumination prior: sky pixels directly supervise the RENI++ conditional spherical neural field, acting as statistical outpainting of the full HDR environment from the partially observed sky, while a binary cross-entropy term on accumulated density along sky rays provides geometric supervision. Second, an outside-in differentiable sky visibility model: a Spherical Directional Distance Field (SDDF) on the radius-1 bounding sphere stores inward-looking depth from every point on that sphere; to test visibility of a scene point x in light direction d, the ray is extended to intersect the radius-1 sphere at s, and the DDF is queried at (s, −d). If the returned distance closely matches |s−x| the point is unoccluded; a learnable threshold ε and a sigmoid softening (steepness η) replace the binary test to make visibility differentiable. The DDF is tied to the SDF via four consistency losses (depth, zero-level-set, multi-view, and sky-ray) and is trained concurrently with the scene representation, so gradients from appearance losses propagate through visibility to jointly refine illumination, albedo, and geometry—including generating geometry outside all training view frustums to explain observed shadows.

## Results

On the NeRF-OSR relighting benchmark (three outdoor scenes), NeuSky achieves the best PSNR on Site 1 (22.50 dB) and Site 3 (18.31 dB), and the lowest MSE on both (0.005 and 0.016 respectively). On Site 2, SOL-NeRF leads with 18.18 dB PSNR while NeuSky scores 16.66 dB. Overall, NeuSky outperforms all three baselines on 5 of 6 scene-metric pairs (PSNR) and 5 of 6 MSE values, establishing state-of-the-art relighting quality for outdoor scenes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NeuSky outperforms NeRF-OSR [30] on all three sites by a wide margin (e.g., +3.16 dB PSNR on Site 1, +2.65 dB on Site 3), confirming that the sky-pixel illumination prior and differentiable visibility substantially improve over the SH-based lighting and shadow-network approach. Against FEGR [41], NeuSky wins on Site 1 (+0.97 dB) and Site 3 (+0.74 dB) but trails on Site 2 (−0.34 dB), yielding a net advantage of 2 out of 3 scenes. Compared to SOL-NeRF [36], NeuSky leads on Site 1 (+1.27 dB) and Site 3 (+0.73 dB) but loses on Site 2 (−1.52 dB), where SOL-NeRF's Spherical Gaussian sunlight model appears better suited to that particular illumination. The paper also reports that NeuSky produces visibly sharper albedo and surface normals than all three baselines while training end-to-end, and that its differentiable sky visibility enables geometry generation outside the training view frustum—a capability none of the compared methods offer.

## Relation to Prior Work

NeuSky builds on the NeuS-Facto neural SDF scene representation (itself combining NeuS, Mip-NeRF 360 scene contraction, and Instant-NGP hash grids) and the RENI++ rotation-equivariant spherical neural field illumination prior. It departs from prior outdoor inverse rendering methods (NeRF-OSR, FEGR, SOL-NeRF, NeuLighting) in two key ways: it is the first to exploit sky pixels as direct observations of the distant illumination environment, and it replaces non-differentiable or phased visibility models with a differentiable Spherical Directional Distance Field (SDDF) trained end-to-end. The DDF-for-visibility idea repurposes a representation previously used only for fast rendering (FiRE), and the sky-pixel constraint is framed as a form of statistical outpainting of the full environment from partial observations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By enabling end-to-end training where appearance-loss gradients flow through the visibility network into both illumination and geometry, NeuSky demonstrates that cast shadows can reconstruct geometry outside any training camera's view frustum and prevent shadow baking into albedo—capabilities unavailable in cascaded or non-differentiable visibility approaches. The sky-pixel constraint is a simple but previously unexploited observation that provides a strong, data-driven anchor for the illumination prior. Limitations include high GPU memory requirements and 5–8 hour training times, and the evaluation is confined to the small NeRF-OSR benchmark (8 sites, 3 relighting scenes). Open questions include extending to non-Lambertian reflectance, multi-bounce illumination, and whether the DDF visibility approach scales to larger or more complex outdoor scenes.
