## Problem

The Break-and-Make problem requires an agent to reverse-engineer a previously unseen LEGO assembly by interactively disassembling it and then rebuilding it from scratch. Prior approaches relying on implicit long-term memory (LSTMs, causal transformers over full observation history) fail to scale to assemblies requiring over 100 steps, as they must process the entire sequence at each decision point. This paper addresses the scalability bottleneck by introducing an explicit, self-curated memory structure that reduces each decision to a local two-image comparison.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20242384/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The policy consumes two 128×128 RGB images — the current simulator scene and the top image of the instruction stack — along with a binary phase token (Break or Make), tokenized into 16×16 patches yielding 64 tokens per image. The output is a hierarchical action: a discrete mode (Rotate, Translate, Pick, Assemble, Disassemble, Push Instruction, Pop Instruction), mode-specific parameters (e.g., rotation angle, brick shape/color, translation offset), and two 2D cursor locations (click and release) predicted as dense probability maps over the full 128×128 image via DPT decoders with conditional dot-product attention.

## Method

InstructioNet introduces an explicit instruction stack as memory: during the Break phase the agent periodically pushes the current scene image onto the stack, and during the Make phase it pops images one at a time, reducing each reassembly step to matching the current scene against a single target image. The architecture is a 12-block ViT (512 channels, 8 heads) that jointly encodes both images plus a readout token and phase embedding; DPT decoders attached to blocks 3, 6, 9, and 12 produce dense spatial features for cursor prediction, conditioned on the sampled high-level action to enable sequential action-head dependencies. Training uses an online DAgger-style imitation loop with a 75% expert / 25% model data mixture and a replay buffer to prevent catastrophic forgetting, supervised by a fast rule-based expert that also dictates when to push/pop the stack. A novel summed-probability cursor loss — summing softmax mass over all acceptable pixels and maximizing it via cross-entropy — outperforms both binary cross-entropy and MSE regression alternatives for the dense 2D action space.

## Results

On the RC-2 (2-brick) dataset, InstructioNet achieves F1a=0.93 and AED=0.18, substantially outperforming the best baseline Studnet-A (F1a=0.58, AED=1.11). The advantage grows with model complexity: on RC-8 (8-brick), InstructioNet reaches F1a=0.63 versus 0.12 for the best baseline (Studnet-B), and on RC-4 it achieves F1a=0.71 versus 0.25. On the new RC-Vehicles dataset (average 31 bricks, >100 steps), InstructioNet achieves F1b=0.59, F1e=0.51, F1a=0.53, and AED=43.36 — a setting where no prior baseline could be trained due to sequence-length constraints.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares InstructioNet against three baselines from the original LTRON paper [42]: an LSTM-based agent and two causal transformer variants (Studnet-A and Studnet-B). InstructioNet wins on every metric (F1b, F1e, F1a, AED) across all four datasets. The margin is modest on RC-2 (F1a 0.93 vs. 0.58 for Studnet-A) but becomes dramatic on larger assemblies: on RC-8, InstructioNet's F1a of 0.63 exceeds the best baseline (Studnet-B at 0.12) by over 5×, while LSTM and Studnet-A are essentially at chance (F1a ≈ 0.01–0.02). On the new RC-Vehicles dataset (avg. 31 bricks, >150 steps), no baseline comparison is possible because the LSTM and Studnet architectures require the full observation history, making them infeasible at that sequence length on available hardware. The authors note that the baselines were trained in a slightly different environment (with a hand viewport and different collision checking), so the comparison is approximate, but the performance gap is large enough to clearly demonstrate a new level of capability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Break-and-Make problem and LTRON simulator introduced by the same group in [42], where agents must reverse-engineer unseen LEGO assemblies through interactive disassembly. The key departure is replacing the implicit long-term memory used by prior baselines (LSTMs and causal transformers over the full observation history) with an explicit instruction-stack that stores self-curated images, reducing the policy's reasoning burden to just two images at a time. The training recipe draws on DAgger-style online imitation learning [32] with a replay buffer to mitigate distribution shift, and the conditional action heads follow the pattern established in complex game AI [41].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The instruction-stack approach demonstrates that task-structured explicit memory can dramatically outperform general-purpose implicit memory for long-horizon visual construction, a principle that may transfer to other sequential assembly or manipulation tasks. The RC-Vehicles dataset (avg. 31 bricks, 100+ steps) is a significant new benchmark that exposes the limits of prior methods, which completely fail at scale. However, the approach requires an online expert for training and assumes assembly is approximately the reverse of disassembly, limiting its applicability to open-world or non-reversible construction problems. The large performance gap (e.g., F1a 0.63 vs 0.12 on RC-8) is tempered by the authors' note that baselines were trained in a slightly different environment, making direct comparison approximate.
