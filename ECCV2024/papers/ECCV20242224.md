## Problem

When sensitive visual data (e.g., facial images) must be outsourced to a third party for DNN training, existing privacy techniques either destroy the latent features DNNs rely on (adversarial perturbation, privacy elimination), cannot handle original data at inference time (homomorphic encryption), or suffer significant accuracy drops (dataset distillation). The paper asks: how can one generate surrogate data that is visually unrecognizable to humans yet functionally equivalent for DNNs, including the ability to correctly classify the original data at test time?

## Input / Output

The framework takes original images of varying resolutions (28×28 MNIST, 32×32 CIFAR, 92×112 ORL, 250×250 LFW, up to 512×512 RSS), their class labels, and a local DNN model owned by the data owner as the feature extractor. It produces per-sample veiled surrogate images (SSIM < 0.05 relative to the original) that are visually unrecognizable, along with a third-party DNN model trained on the veiled data. Unlike dataset distillation, veiled data is generated independently per sample, so new images can be added without reprocessing the entire dataset, and the third-party model can differ architecturally from the data owner's local model.

## Method

Veil Privacy generates veiled data through two sequential steps. First, Random Pixel Flipping (RPF) normalizes pixel values to [−1, 1], constructs a random ±1 mask where each pixel is flipped with probability p (default 0.5), and applies element-wise multiplication, creating strong visual dissimilarity. Second, a Gradient Iteration Algorithm (GIA) uses SGD to minimize a composite loss: an L1 term aligning the local model's output on the veiled image with its output on the original (preserving DNN-perceptible latent features), and a negative L2 term penalizing pixel-level similarity (maintaining the visual veil). The local model's last fully-connected layer serves as the feature extractor F(·), and the iteration terminates after T=300 steps or when the loss drops below ξ=0.3. The key insight is the explicit separation of privacy into human-perceptible and DNN-perceptible dimensions, with RPF targeting the former and GIA targeting the latter, yielding data that is a 'learnable veil'—invisible to humans but transparent to DNNs.

## Results

Across 8 datasets (BioID, ORL, LFW, CelebA, MNIST, CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, RSS), the veiled model trained on veiled data achieves accuracy within 1–6% of the baseline model trained on original data. Notably, the veiled model also generalizes to the original data: e.g., 98.01% on BioID (vs. 99.34% baseline), 96.97% on ORL (matching baseline), and 81.73% on CIFAR-10 (vs. 85.99% baseline). SSIM between veiled and original images remains below 0.09 across all datasets, confirming strong visual obfuscation while preserving DNN-perceptible features.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares Veil Privacy against four dataset distillation methods (Cazenavette et al. [3], Nguyen et al. [25], Nguyen et al. [26], and Dong et al. [8]) on CIFAR-10 with AlexNet. Veil Privacy achieves 81.73% accuracy on the original data, substantially outperforming all four baselines: Cazenavette et al. (64.3±0.7%), Nguyen et al. [25] (66.3±0.2%), Nguyen et al. [26] (64.7±0.2%), and Dong et al. (57.5±0.5%). The margin over the strongest baseline (Nguyen et al. [25]) is approximately 15.4 percentage points. The authors note that dataset distillation inherently reduces predictive performance on original data and suffers from high similarity between synthetic and original samples, whereas Veil Privacy preserves utility while achieving near-complete visual obfuscation (SSIM < 0.09).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a new middle ground between two well-studied but incompatible paradigms: adversarial perturbation (which protects DNN-perceptible features at the cost of leaving human-visible privacy intact) and privacy elimination/encryption (which protects human-visible privacy but destroys or encrypts DNN-perceptible features). By explicitly decomposing image privacy into human-perceptible and DNN-perceptible dimensions, the authors repurpose gradient-based optimization (borrowed from adversarial example literature) in an inverted role—instead of fooling the model, the gradient iteration preserves model features while random pixel flipping handles the visual obfuscation. The closest prior work is dataset distillation, which also aims to preserve DNN utility in surrogate data, but Veil Privacy operates per-sample rather than at the dataset level and explicitly targets visual unrecognizability as a first-class objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a practically important gap: organizations that must outsource sensitive visual data (e.g., facial images for access control) to third-party ML developers currently lack a method that simultaneously hides identity from humans and preserves model utility. The finding that models trained on veiled data can recognize original data with minimal accuracy loss (e.g., 98.01% on BioID) suggests a viable path for privacy-preserving data sharing in regulated industries. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to generative models (diffusion, GANs) where feature preservation is less clearly defined, how it scales to very large datasets without per-sample gradient optimization, and whether the security guarantees hold against adaptive attackers who know the exact RPF+GIA pipeline and can perform targeted inversion beyond the tested MIAs and GPEN.
