## Problem

Existing Composed Video Retrieval benchmarks, particularly WebVid-CoVR, are dominated by object-centred modifications (color, shape, adding/removing objects) that can be solved with a single image frame, failing to test temporal video understanding. This paper identifies that 85% of WebVid-CoVR-Test samples require no action understanding, leaving a critical gap in evaluating whether models can handle fine-grained, action-focused video modifications.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240969/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a short egocentric query video clip (2–8 seconds, average 7.9s) and a concise textual modification instruction (average 4 words, e.g., 'Rinse it instead.') that describes only the semantic delta between query and target, not the full target description. The output is a ranked list of target videos from a gallery that is either global (10,661–12,526 clips) or local (up to 10 clips from the same source video). Each query has an average of 1.2 ground-truth targets, and up to 6 visually similar distractor clips per target are included to prevent trivial visual-similarity shortcuts.

## Method

TFR-CVR is a training-free, two-stage re-ranking framework. In Stage 1, a video encoder (e.g., LanguageBind) computes cosine similarity between the query video and all gallery clips, retaining the top n_c=15 visually similar candidates. In Stage 2, a video captioning model (LaViLa) generates a caption of the query video, which is then combined with the modification instruction via GPT-4 (using in-context examples) to produce a coherent target caption; this caption is matched against the filtered candidates using a text-video model (e.g., EgoVLPv2). The key design choice is that the two stages can use different models, exploiting complementary strengths: the visual encoder narrows the search to contextually relevant clips while the text-video model discriminates fine-grained action differences. This modular, training-free approach outperforms finetuned CVR models (BLIP_CoVR, BLIP_CoVR-ECDE) by a large margin on EgoCVR, achieving R@1 of 14.1% globally versus 5.4–6.0% for the finetuned baselines.

## Results

On the EgoCVR benchmark, the proposed TFR-CVR method achieves R@1 of 14.1% and R@5 of 39.5% in the global setting (gallery of ~10,661–12,526 clips), and R@1 of 44.2% and R@3 of 73.2% in the local setting (gallery of ~6.4 clips). This substantially outperforms the strongest baseline, EgoVLPv2 with average fusion (R@1 9.5% global, 30.7% local), and the purpose-built BLIP_CoVR model finetuned on 1.6M WebVid-CoVR triplets (R@1 5.4% global, 33.1% local). Text-only retrieval is nearly useless in the global setting (R@1 < 2% for all models), while visual-only retrieval reaches at most 7.6% R@1, confirming that both modalities are needed.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TFR-CVR outperforms all seven baselines on every metric in both the global and local settings. In the global setting, it beats the strongest naive baseline (EgoVLPv2 with average fusion, R@1 9.5%) by 4.6 points and the purpose-built BLIP_CoVR model (finetuned on 1.6M WebVid-CoVR triplets, R@1 5.4%) by 8.7 points, showing that explicit CVR finetuning on object-centric data does not transfer to action-centric egocentric retrieval. BLIP_CoVR-ECDE (R@1 6.0%) and CIReVL (R@1 2.0%) fare even worse, with CIReVL's image-domain captioning strategy failing to generalise to video. In the local setting, TFR-CVR's R@1 of 44.2% exceeds EgoVLPv2-avg (30.7%) by 13.5 points and BLIP_CoVR (33.1%) by 11.1 points, confirming that the two-stage visual-filtering-then-textual-reranking pipeline is critical when gallery clips are visually similar. Text-only variants of all models (CLIP, BLIP, EgoVLPv2, LanguageBind) achieve R@1 below 2% globally, while visual-only variants reach at most 7.6%, demonstrating that neither modality alone suffices for fine-grained composed video retrieval.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Composed Image Retrieval (CIR) paradigm—particularly the training-free CIReVL approach [28]—to the video domain, formulating Composed Video Retrieval (CVR) as a task requiring temporal understanding rather than single-frame matching. It departs from the sole prior CVR benchmark, WebVid-CoVR [51], by arguing that its automatically constructed evaluation set predominantly tests object-centric modifications solvable with image-level models, and instead curates a manually annotated egocentric benchmark (EgoCVR) from Ego4D that emphasizes subtle action-level changes. The proposed TFR-CVR method repurposes video captioning (LaViLa) and LLM-based caption reformulation from the CIR literature, adding a visual pre-filtering and re-ranking stage that leverages complementary video-language models (LanguageBind, EgoVLPv2).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

EgoCVR exposes a critical gap: models finetuned on 1.6M CVR triplets (BLIP_CoVR) fail to generalize when the evaluation demands genuine temporal action understanding, suggesting that current CVR training data and objectives do not capture the temporal reasoning the task actually requires. The benchmark provides a much-needed stress test for video-language models' fine-grained action discrimination, with 78.9% of queries requiring temporal understanding versus 85% object-centric in WebVid-CoVR. Open questions include whether dedicated CVR training data with temporal emphasis could close the gap, whether the egocentric-only scope limits generalizability, and how to cohesively fuse reference video and textual instruction rather than relying on captioning as an intermediary. The training-free TFR-CVR approach also suggests that modular composition of existing video-language models may be more effective than end-to-end finetuning for this task.
