## Problem

Transferring human dexterous manipulations to robot hand simulations is hindered by a fundamental tension: high-fidelity simulators produce non-smooth, discontinuous optimization landscapes due to abrupt contact changes, while relaxed simulators that ease optimization deviate too far from real physics for deployment. No single simulator simultaneously offers high fidelity and good optimizability for contact-rich manipulation tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20242083/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a kinematics-only human demonstration consisting of a hand mesh trajectory and an object pose trajectory, with no force or contact information available. It produces a dexterous Shadow hand control trajectory (joint motor torques and root velocities) that is ultimately deployed via model predictive control in a target black-box simulator (Bullet or Isaac Gym). Object properties and system parameters, which are unknown from the kinematic data, are jointly identified during optimization. The object is represented as a signed distance field while the hand is represented as a parameterized point set sampled from the ambient space around each body mesh.

## Method

The core contribution is a family of parameterized quasi-physical simulators that represent an articulated multi-body as a mass-point set (with parameter α controlling the degree of rigid-constraint relaxation), model contacts via an unconstrained spring-damper with tunable distance threshold and stiffness, and compensate for unmodeled effects through local and global neural residual physics networks. A physics curriculum inspired by homotopy methods progressively tightens these parameters: starting from a fully relaxed, soft-contact simulator where optimization is easy, through intermediate stages with gradually stiffened contacts, to a final stage where the analytical model is fixed and residual networks are trained to match a realistic black-box simulator. Within each curriculum stage, the control trajectory and simulator parameters (or residual network weights) are alternately optimized via gradient-based methods exploiting the differentiability of the semi-implicit time-stepping scheme. The final trajectory is refined through MPC in the target simulator, achieving over 11% success rate improvement over the best baseline on complex manipulations with changing contacts and non-trivial object motions.

## Results

On the Bullet simulator, the proposed method achieves a success rate of 27.45%/37.25%/58.82% (under three increasing thresholds of 10°-10cm-10cm, 15°-15cm-15cm, and a looser setting), substantially outperforming the best baseline (MPC with softened contact) at 0%/21.57%/37.25%. On Isaac Gym, the method reaches 21.57%/43.14%/56.86% success rate versus 0%/15.69%/31.37% for the best baseline. The method also achieves the lowest object rotation error (24.21° in Bullet, 25.97° in Isaac Gym) and translation error (1.97 cm, 2.08 cm) among all methods, representing an 11%+ absolute improvement in success rate over the previous best-performed method.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against model-free (DGrasp-Base, DGrasp-Tracking) and model-based (Control-VAE, MPC with base simulator, MPC with softened contact) baselines in both Bullet and Isaac Gym. The proposed method decisively outperforms all baselines on success rate: in Bullet, it achieves 58.82% at the loosest threshold versus 37.25% for the best baseline (MPC w/ soften), and 27.45% at the strictest threshold where all baselines score 0%. In Isaac Gym, the gap is even wider at the middle threshold (43.14% vs. 23.53% for DGrasp-Tracking w/ curriculum). On tracking accuracy metrics, the method achieves the lowest rotation error (24.21° Bullet, 25.97° Isaac Gym) and translation error (1.97 cm, 2.08 cm) across all methods, while MPC baselines achieve comparable MPJPE but far worse success rates due to their inability to handle contact-rich sequences. Notably, applying the physics curriculum to the model-free DGrasp-Tracking baseline improves its success rate from 0%/15.69%/15.69% to 7.84%/23.53%/37.25% in Bullet, demonstrating the curriculum's generality beyond the proposed method.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of differentiable simulation (building on Neuralsim's residual physics and ADD's analytically differentiable multi-body dynamics) and physics-based motion tracking (adopting the iterative trajectory-simulator co-optimization from SuperTrack and ControlVAE). Its key conceptual departure is the homotopy-inspired curriculum of parameterized simulators: rather than optimizing in a single black-box or a single relaxed simulator, it constructs a family of quasi-physical simulators whose constraints are progressively tightened, bridging the gap between optimizability and fidelity that prior work has treated as a binary trade-off. The point-set dynamics representation, which relaxes articulated rigid bodies into mass points with per-point actuation, is a novel analytical relaxation not seen in prior dexterous manipulation work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The physics curriculum is demonstrated to be a general optimization strategy beyond the authors' own method: applying it to a model-free baseline (DGrasp-Tracking) nearly rescues a failing approach, suggesting the curriculum idea could benefit a broader range of contact-rich manipulation pipelines. However, the work remains entirely simulation-to-simulation (no real-robot deployment), relies on a relatively simple spring-damper contact model that the authors acknowledge as a limitation, and is evaluated on a single hand morphology (Shadow hand) across 51 sequences. Open questions include whether the residual physics networks generalize across object categories and whether more sophisticated analytical contact models (e.g., IPC-style) can be incorporated into the parameterized framework.
