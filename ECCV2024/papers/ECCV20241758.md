## Problem

Few-shot NeRF overfits to high-frequency local details while ignoring low-frequency global structures. Prior work (FreeNeRF, SPARF) addresses this by gradually introducing higher-frequency Positional Encodings, but the paper reveals an inconsistency: the rendering loss still provides full-spectrum pixel supervision even when only low-frequency PEs are active, causing high-frequency pixel targets to interfere with global structure learning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241758/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes 3, 6, or 9 sparse RGB input views of a scene and produces a 3D color-and-density field (MLP) that can render novel views at arbitrary camera poses. During training, the input views are consumed in two phases: Gaussian-blurred versions serve as pixel supervision in the early phase, then the original unblurred views replace them. Evaluation is performed on DTU (object-level, white-table scenes) and LLFF (complex forward-facing scenes) using PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, and a geometric-mean Average score.

## Method

AR-NeRF aligns the frequency relationship between PE input and pixel supervision through two mechanisms. First, a two-phase rendering supervision applies Gaussian blur to ground-truth views during early training (reducing high-frequency pixel targets) and switches to raw views later. Second, an adaptive rendering loss weight learning strategy models the color field as a Gaussian with learned per-point variance; the rendered color variance is propagated via volume rendering, and the negative log-likelihood yields per-pixel adaptive weights that automatically down-weight pixels whose frequency content is not yet supported by the active PEs. A ray density regularization (Emptiness loss on opacity-normalized ray density) further suppresses floating artifacts. The total loss combines the two-phase rendering loss, the adaptive uncertainty-weighted loss, the ray density loss, and an occlusion penalty, all trained with a standard Adam optimizer and the FreeNeRF-style linear PE frequency schedule.

## Results

On the DTU dataset (3 input views), AR-NeRF achieves the best PSNR of 20.36 dB, SSIM of 0.788, and Average score of 0.095, outperforming FreeNeRF (19.92 dB, 0.787, 0.098) and MixNeRF (18.95 dB, 0.744, 0.118). On the more complex LLFF dataset (3 input views), AR-NeRF achieves PSNR 19.90 dB, SSIM 0.635, LPIPS 0.283, and Average 0.126, surpassing FreeNeRF (19.63 dB, 0.612, 0.308, 0.134) by a larger margin. For 6 and 9 input views on both datasets, AR-NeRF achieves comparable or state-of-the-art performance, with the gap to FreeNeRF narrowing as more views provide additional low-frequency supervision.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AR-NeRF outperforms FreeNeRF (the closest competitor using the same PE frequency regularization) on PSNR across all settings on both DTU and LLFF, with the largest gap on LLFF 3-view (19.90 vs. 19.63 dB) and DTU 3-view (20.36 vs. 19.92 dB). On DTU 6- and 9-view, FreeNeRF edges out AR-NeRF on SSIM and LPIPS by small margins (e.g., SSIM 0.844 vs. 0.841 at 6-view), while AR-NeRF still leads on PSNR and Average. Against MixNeRF, AR-NeRF wins on all metrics in the 3-view setting on both datasets (e.g., PSNR 20.36 vs. 18.95 on DTU, 19.90 vs. 19.27 on LLFF) and remains competitive at 6/9 views. RegNeRF and DietNeRF are consistently outperformed by 1–1.5 dB in PSNR at 3-view. Pre-training methods (SRF, PixelNeRF, MVSNeRF) underperform all per-scene methods even when trained on the target dataset, with MVSNeRF ft being the strongest pre-training baseline (PSNR 18.54 on DTU 3-view vs. AR-NeRF's 20.36). The paper attributes its larger improvement over FreeNeRF on LLFF to the more complex frequency composition in real-world scenes, where adaptive alignment between PE and pixel supervision is more critical.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on FreeNeRF and SPARF, which showed that gradually introducing high-frequency Positional Encoding (PE) components prevents overfitting in few-shot NeRF. The key departure is identifying an inconsistency that prior frequency-regularization methods overlooked: the rendering loss still provides full-bandwidth pixel supervision even when only low-frequency PEs are active, undermining the 'global first' learning objective. To resolve this, the authors repurpose the uncertainty learning framework of Kendall & Gal as an adaptive loss-weighting mechanism, and add a two-phase (blurred → raw) supervision schedule. The ray density regularization extends the emptiness loss of Score Jacobian Chaining and the ray entropy of InfNeRF with a relaxed geometry proxy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a lightweight, module-free solution to few-shot NeRF that practitioners can adopt without external depth estimators, diffusion models, or pre-training pipelines, making it attractive for real-time AR/VR and autonomous driving where data collection is limited. The frequency-alignment principle (matching PE bandwidth to supervision bandwidth) is a generalizable insight that could inform other implicit-representation training regimes. However, evaluation is confined to 3/6/9 views on DTU and LLFF; whether the approach extends to 1–2 views, 360° unbounded scenes (Mip-NeRF 360), or non-NeRF architectures (e.g., 3D Gaussian Splatting) remains open. The modest margins over FreeNeRF (≈0.3–0.5 dB PSNR) suggest the inconsistency, while real, is a secondary-order effect.
