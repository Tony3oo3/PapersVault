## Problem

Existing action recognition datasets classify distinct activities but none address the challenge of separating proper from improper execution of the same action, where the distinguishing feature (e.g., a safety hook being attached, a foul contact) appears for only a few seconds within otherwise nearly identical sequences. This gap is particularly acute in industrial safety contexts, where subtle procedural differences carry critical real-world consequences, and in sports analysis, where fouls are typically excluded from datasets due to their difficulty.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20242088/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes multi-view video clips (3840×2160, 30 FPS) captured simultaneously from fixed cameras, a drone, and a GoPro ego-view, with clips ranging from tens of seconds to several minutes. The output is a 10-class label combining five activity types (Electric Lift, Stepladder, Suspended Load, Basketball, Smartphone Zombie) each split into Proper and Improper. The critical difficulty is that class-distinguishing visual evidence is confined to a brief temporal window within each clip, unlike standard action recognition where distinguishing features persist throughout the video.

## Method

The proposed SARVLE framework uses a cascading two-stage pipeline: first, a UniFormer backbone performs coarse 5-class classification of the activity type; second, LLaVA (v1.6, Vicuna-7B) generates a natural-language caption of the video's central frame, which is then embedded via SetFit (all-MiniLM-L12-v2) and fed to a binary classifier that distinguishes Proper from Improper within each activity. The key insight is that linguistic description of the central frame captures contextual cues (e.g., 'harness not attached', 'excessive contact') that pure visual features at the image or video level struggle to isolate from the surrounding near-identical frames. All models are trained with Kinetics-400 pretrained weights via MMAction2, using 80/20 random splits, with MixUp and CutMix disabled to avoid corrupting the subtle temporal signals. The approach achieves 65.71% accuracy versus 57.52–61.06% for MViT V2, VideoMAE, and UniFormer baselines.

## Results

On the proposed Behavior Contrast dataset (10 classes: 5 industrial/everyday activities × proper/improper), the proposed SARVLE method achieves 65.71% top-1 accuracy, outperforming all pure video-based baselines. The strongest video-only baseline, UniFormer, reaches 61.06%, while MViT V2 and VideoMAE achieve 57.52% and 58.19% respectively. All methods operate under the same 80/20 train/validation split with pretrained Kinetics-400 weights, and the task is notably harder than standard action recognition because the distinguishing features appear for only a few seconds within clips lasting tens of seconds to several minutes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks three video-only action recognition models (MViT V2, VideoMAE, UniFormer) against the proposed SARVLE pipeline on the 10-class Behavior Contrast dataset. SARVLE wins on all three comparisons: it outperforms MViT V2 by 8.19 points (65.71% vs. 57.52%), VideoMAE by 7.52 points (65.71% vs. 58.19%), and UniFormer by 4.65 points (65.71% vs. 61.06%). The gap over UniFormer is the most informative, since UniFormer is also the coarse-classification backbone inside SARVLE itself; the additional LLaVA captioning + SetFit embedding stage contributes the remaining ~4.65 points by injecting semantic context that pure visual features miss. All baselines use Kinetics-400 pretrained weights and the same MMAction2 training protocol (10 epochs, two A6000 GPUs), so the comparison is controlled. The authors note that even the best method (65.71%) remains far from saturated, underscoring the inherent difficulty of detecting brief, fine-grained behavioral differences in long video clips.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs fundamentally from the standard action recognition paradigm, where datasets classify distinct action types (e.g., walking vs. running in UCF101, Kinetics-400). Instead, it introduces a 'behavior contrast' task: distinguishing proper from improper versions of the *same* action based on brief, subtle differences (e.g., whether a harness hook is attached before operating a lift). The method (SARVLE) builds on UniFormer for coarse 5-class classification, LLaVA for frame-level captioning, and SetFit for text embedding, forming a cascading vision-to-language pipeline. The dataset itself is positioned against simulated-action datasets like Assembly-101 and IKEA ASM in construction methodology, while its cross-view and multi-camera setup extends the Smarthome dataset's multi-perspective approach into the industrial domain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first dataset targeting the industrial safety domain with explicit proper/improper contrast, filling a gap the authors argue has been overlooked despite real-world urgency in fieldwork and sports analysis. The modest best accuracy (65.71%) underscores how challenging the task remains: distinguishing features appear for only a few seconds in multi-second clips, and no existing method is designed to localize and exploit such transient cues. Key open questions include whether temporal attention or event-detection-style architectures can substantially outperform the current video-level and frame-captioning approaches, and whether the dataset's limited scale (2.2K clips, single annotator, constrained equipment) limits generalizability. The t-SNE visualization showing the dataset occupies a far broader feature space than larger general-purpose datasets is an intriguing observation about domain-specific action diversity.
