## Problem

Existing discriminative loss functions for image classification and face recognition (ArcFace, CosFace, SphereFace) operate exclusively in the angular dimension of a unit hypersphere, causing inter-class feature overlap as the number of classes grows. The radial dimension of the embedding space remains largely unexploited for class separation, leaving no mechanism to distribute classes across different spherical shells and thereby increase the effective decision boundary volume.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20242342/cropped_page0_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework accepts standard RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR/MNIST, 64×64 for TinyImageNet, 224×224 for ImageNet-1K and face datasets) and produces either a class label (classification) or a verification decision (face recognition). Unusually, at inference time the prediction is not read from a softmax classification layer; instead, a radial-angular predictive measure computes resultant vector magnitudes between the test embedding and each scaled proxy via the cosine law, assigning the sample to the class with the smallest resultant vector.

## Method

HyperSpaceX introduces the DistArc loss, which arranges class features across multiple concentric hyperspheres by combining three terms: (1) cos(θ_{y_i}+m), an angular-margin penalty inherited from ArcFace that aligns embeddings with their class proxy direction; (2) cos(φ_{y_i}), the cosine of the angle between a scaled proxy ω_{r_{y_i}} = ω̂_{y_i}·r_{y_i} and the resultant vector R_{y_i} = −ω_{r_{y_i}}+x_i, which pulls embeddings radially inward toward their assigned spherical shell while maintaining angular compactness; and (3) squared Euclidean distance terms δ_{y_i} and δ_j that attract embeddings to their own scaled proxy and repel them from other classes' scaled proxies, enforcing inter-class radial separation. Each class is assigned a distinct radius r_{y_i}, so the latent space becomes a multi-hyperspherical manifold where superclasses are separated angularly and subclasses radially. The framework is trained with standard backbones (iResNet50, RN101, ViT-B/L from CLIP) under supervised classification, and the proposed predictive measure replaces the conventional softmax argmax at test time to exploit the radial-angular geometry learned during training.

## Results

On CIFAR-100 with 512-D embeddings, DistArc achieves 89.48% accuracy with ViT-L, outperforming Cross-Entropy (88.75%), ArcFace (87.27%), CosFace (85.40%), and OPL (84.83%). In the more challenging 2-D embedding setting, DistArc yields up to 19.57% improvement over Cross-Entropy on CIFAR-100 (79.56% vs. 67.89% with ViT-L) and over 22% on TinyImageNet (68.21% vs. 45.93%). For face recognition with iResNet50 (512-D), DistArc achieves SoTA on LFW (99.54%) and CP-LFW (91.10%), surpassing ArcFace (99.46%, 90.85%) and SphereFace2 (99.50%, 91.07%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DistArc consistently outperforms Cross-Entropy across all backbones and datasets, with the largest gaps in low-dimensional (2-D) settings where angular-only methods struggle with class overlap (e.g., +11.67% on CIFAR-100 ViT-L, +22.28% on TinyImageNet ViT-L). Against ArcFace, DistArc wins on CIFAR-100 (89.48% vs. 87.27% with ViT-L at 512-D) and TinyImageNet (86.79% vs. 85.55%), and the advantage widens dramatically in 2-D (79.56% vs. 66.44% on CIFAR-100). CosFace and OPL are generally weaker baselines; DistArc surpasses both by 2–6% at 512-D and by 10–20+ points at 2-D. In face recognition, DistArc achieves the best LFW score (99.54%) and CP-LFW score (91.10%), narrowly beating SphereFace2 (99.50%, 91.07%) and ArcFace (99.46%, 90.85%), while trailing ArcFace on AgeDB-30 (94.03% vs. 94.93%) and CA-LFW (93.32% vs. 92.47%—actually DistArc wins here). On CFP-FP, DistArc (95.41%) is marginally below ArcFace (95.47%) and CosFace (95.44%), indicating near-parity with the strongest angular-margin methods on that benchmark.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ArcFace lineage of angular-margin softmax losses (SphereFace → CosFace → ArcFace) by introducing a radial dimension alongside the angular one, moving from a single unit hypersphere to a multi-hyperspherical latent space where each class occupies a distinct radius. The DistArc loss retains ArcFace's cos(θ+m) angular term but augments it with a cos(φ) term (angle between a scaled proxy and a resultant vector) and squared-distance δ terms that pull embeddings toward their class radius and push them away from other class radii. It also departs from purely angular evaluation by proposing a radial-angular predictive measure based on shortest resultant vector magnitude, rather than the standard argmax over cosine similarities.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The core claim—that angular-only exploration becomes insufficient as class count grows and that radial separation on multiple hyperspheres restores discriminability—is conceptually appealing and supported by striking 2D results (e.g., 62% on CIFAR-100 vs. ~51% for ArcFace). However, the practical gains at standard embedding dimensions (512D, 2048D) shrink to 1–6%, and face-recognition improvements over ArcFace are marginal (≤0.5% on LFW), raising the question of whether the radial dimension adds meaningful value once angular space is already large. Open questions include whether the λ scheduling and radius assignment generalize beyond the tested backbones, whether the approach helps in truly high-class-count regimes (e.g., ImageNet-21K), and whether the 2D gains reflect a genuine representational advantage or simply the fact that 2D angular space is too small for many classes.
