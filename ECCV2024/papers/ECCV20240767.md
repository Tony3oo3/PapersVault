## Problem

Current LLM-based video understanding models are limited to videos of a few minutes and degrade on longer content due to noise and redundancy in extended contexts, as well as growing computational and memory costs. Additionally, existing long-video benchmarks are largely text-centric (generated from scripts/summaries) and do not require genuine visual comprehension, making it difficult to measure true long-video understanding.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240767/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system takes an arbitrarily long video (segmented into non-overlapping clips of at most 45 frames each, constrained by MiniGPT4-Video's 4K token context window) along with aligned per-frame subtitles and a free-form text query. It produces a natural-language answer to the query. Internally, the Video Descriptor also generates a detailed text description for every clip, which serves as the retrieval key. The retrieval operates purely in the text-embedding space (OpenAI text-embedding-3-small, 1536-dim), so no visual features are compared at retrieval time.

## Method

Goldfish is a three-stage retrieval-augmented pipeline: (1) a Video Descriptor (MiniGPT4-Video) encodes each clip's frames via EVA-CLIP, compresses every four adjacent visual tokens into one (256→64 per frame), projects them into LLM token space with a linear layer, and generates a natural-language clip description; (2) a Retrieval Module encodes each clip's description and subtitle with a shared text encoder and selects the top-k clips by cosine similarity to the query embedding, using an 'or' strategy that treats subtitle and summary embeddings as separate retrieval keys; (3) an Answer Module (Llama2-chat) receives the query plus the retrieved clip descriptions and subtitles to produce the final answer. MiniGPT4-Video itself is trained in three stages—image-text pretraining of the projection layer, video-text pretraining on up to 45 frames with subtitles (CMD + WebVid), and video-QA instruction fine-tuning (Video Instruction Dataset)—enabling it to serve both as the clip describer inside Goldfish and as a standalone short-video model. The key design choice over prior long-video methods (LLaMA-VID's 2-token-per-frame compression, MovieChat's memory consolidation) is to avoid processing the entire video at inference time, instead retrieving only the few clips relevant to the query, which simultaneously mitigates noise/redundancy and keeps compute bounded regardless of total video length.

## Results

On the proposed TVQA-Long benchmark (vision + subtitles), Goldfish achieves 41.78% accuracy, surpassing LLaMA-VID (26.81%) by 14.94 percentage points. In the vision-only setting, Goldfish attains 28.61% on TVQA-Long, 67.6% on MovieChat, 28.49% on Movie QA, and 23.09% on LLama-Vid, outperforming all baselines. For short-video understanding, MiniGPT4-Video reaches 72.93% on MSVD, 58.83% on MSRVTT, 67.9% on TGIF, and 46.94% on TVQA (with subtitles), setting new state-of-the-art across all five short-video benchmarks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Goldfish outperforms LLaMA-VID [20] on all four long-video benchmarks in the vision-only modality (e.g., 67.6% vs. 53.2% on MovieChat, 28.61% vs. 24.63% on TVQA-Long), and decisively wins on TVQA-Long with subtitles (41.78% vs. 26.81%), a fair comparison since LLaMA-VID was trained on the other three benchmarks. Against MovieChat [35], Goldfish achieves substantially higher accuracy across all long-video benchmarks (e.g., 67.6% vs. NA on MovieChat, 28.49% vs. 16.18% on Movie QA). On short-video benchmarks, MiniGPT4-Video surpasses LLaMA-VID-7B [20] on MSVD (72.93% vs. 69.7%), MSRVTT (58.83% vs. 57.7%), and TVQA (46.94% vs. N/A with subtitles), and outperforms Video-ChatGPT [26] by large margins on TGIF (67.9% vs. 51.4%) and TVQA (46.94% vs. 23.35%). Compared to BT-Adapter-7B [25], Goldfish leads on MSVD (72.93% vs. 67.7%), MSRVTT (58.83% vs. 57%), and TVQA (46.94% vs. N/A). Goldfish also dominates earlier baselines including Video-LLaMA [46], VideoChat [18], LLaMA-Adapter [48], and FrozenBiLM [44] across all short-video benchmarks, with the largest gaps on TGIF (67.9% vs. 51.4% for Video-ChatGPT) and TVQA (46.94% vs. 29.7% for FrozenBiLM).

## Relation to Prior Work

Goldfish extends the MiniGPT-v2/BLIP-2 architecture from single-image to multi-frame video encoding (MiniGPT4-Video) and positions itself against two lines of long-video work: memory-consolidation approaches (MovieChat) and token-compression approaches (LLaMA-VID), both of which sacrifice spatial-temporal detail to fit longer context windows. The key conceptual departure is borrowing the retrieval-augmented generation paradigm from NLP and image retrieval, applying it to video by selecting only top-k relevant clips rather than compressing the entire video into a fixed context. The paper also carves new ground in benchmarking by introducing TVQA-Long, which requires joint visual and textual understanding over full episodes, in contrast to prior long-video benchmarks (LLaMA-VID, MovieChat) that are largely text-answerable without visual access.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The retrieval-based framing offers a practical path to processing hours-long content (movies, TV series) without the quadratic memory/compute blowup of full-video encoding, and the TVQA-Long benchmark fills a genuine gap in evaluating long-video understanding with both modalities. However, several limitations temper the impact: the answer-module ablation reveals that feeding retrieved video clips back into the VLM causes hallucination and degrades accuracy, suggesting the system still relies heavily on text (subtitles) rather than true visual reasoning; open-ended evaluation depends on GPT-3.5 as judge; and the retrieval quality is bottlenecked by the quality of auto-generated clip summaries. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to temporal-reasoning questions spanning non-contiguous clips, and whether the hallucination problem in the answer module can be resolved without reverting to text-only context.
