## Problem

Small vision language models (SVLMs) are computationally efficient but lack the capacity to generate emotionally subjective explanations and often produce misaligned outputs where the image, emotion class, and text explanation disagree. Large models like LLaVA can be fine-tuned for this task but require billions of parameters and multi-GPU training. The paper asks whether a small model can match or exceed state-of-the-art small models and compete with large models on visual art emotion understanding while remaining trainable on a single consumer GPU.

## Input / Output

At inference, the model takes a single RGB art image and the fixed prompt 'the emotion is _' and autoregressively generates an emotion category (one of Ekman's 8 basic emotions) followed by a free-form natural language explanation. During training, the full input sentence concatenates the prompt with the ground-truth explanation (max length L=30 tokens), and the image is encoded by a frozen CLIP ViT-B/16 into 196 patch tokens of 768 dimensions. The GPT2 decoder (6 blocks, 12 heads, 768-dim embeddings) fuses image and text via cross-attention at each block, and generation uses nucleus sampling with probability 0.9.

## Method

The core architecture is a CLIP-GPT2 backbone augmented with three emotion-specific components. First, VAD emotion modeling: each word in the explanation is looked up in a psychology VAD dictionary to obtain a 3-dim (valence, arousal, dominance) vector; a 3-block transformer emotion encoder processes these vectors, which are then concatenated with standard text embeddings and projected back to 768-dim via a linear layer, enriching the input with affective semantics. Second, a VAD head (single linear layer) predicts per-word VAD vectors from the decoder hidden states, trained with MSE loss against ground-truth VAD vectors to enforce emotionally coherent generation. Third, a contrastive head (MLP) computes a ternary alignment score from mean-pooled image, emotion-label, and explanation features, trained with a contrastive loss that pushes matched triplets above two types of negatives (wrong emotion label, wrong image). The total loss is the sum of language cross-entropy, VAD MSE, and a weighted contrastive term (α=2), and the full 186M-parameter model trains in 18 hours on a single RTX 2080 Ti.

## Results

On the ArtEmis v1.0 test set, SEVLM achieves 65.6% emotion classification accuracy (ACC) and 62.1% emotion-alignment (EA), outperforming the best prior small model (NLX-GPT2) by +5.4% ACC and +7.7% EA. On the ArtEmis v2.0 Combined test set, SEVLM reaches 44.2% ACC and 42.6% EA, again leading all baselines. Compared to fine-tuned LLaVA 7B, SEVLM (186M params) achieves higher ACC (+3.8% on v1.0, +5.8% on v2.0) and EA (+2.1%, +3.3%) while being 37.5× smaller and trainable on a single RTX 2080 Ti. Against GPT4(V) on 100 sampled examples, SEVLM leads on ACC (63.2 vs 52.6), EA (56.3 vs 43.8), and all BLEU/METEOR/ROUGE scores.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the two-stage ArtEmis baselines (M2 and SAT, both from Achlioptas et al. [2]), SEVLM achieves +5.4% ACC and +7.7% EA on ArtEmis v1.0, and +0.9% ACC and +3.8% EA on v2.0, while also leading on B4, METEOR, ROUGE, and Unique. Compared to NLX-GPT2 [29] (the strongest prior CLIP-GPT2-based method), SEVLM gains +5.4% ACC and +7.7% EA on v1.0 and +0.9% ACC and +5.7% EA on v2.0, with the largest margin on the Unique diversity metric (+8.2% and +29.5%). Against fine-tuned LLaVA 7B [18], SEVLM wins on ACC (+3.8% v1.0, +5.8% v2.0) and EA (+2.1%, +3.3%) but trails on Unique (62.0 vs 84.3 on v1.0), which the authors attribute to LLaVA's stronger pre-trained language model producing more diverse (though less emotion-aligned) explanations. On a 100-sample evaluation against GPT4(V), SEVLM outperforms on every metric except Unique (96.0 vs 100.0), with particularly large gaps in BLEU-1 (54.8 vs 31.5) and emotion-alignment (56.3 vs 43.8). Notably, SEVLM achieves these results with only 186M parameters (vs 7B for LLaVA), trains in 18 hours on a single RTX 2080 Ti, and runs at 8.62 FPS inference—37.5× fewer parameters and 253× fewer FLOPs than LLaVA-FT.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the two-stage paradigm of ArtEmis (Achlioptas et al., 2021) and NLX-GPT2 (Sammani et al., 2022), which use separate emotion classifiers and captioning networks, by proposing an end-to-end small vision-language model. Its key conceptual departure is repurposing the VAD lexicon—a psychology tool previously applied only to discriminative tasks like sentiment classification and humor detection—into both the input embedding and the loss function of a generative VLM. The ternary contrastive loss (aligning image, emotion label, and explanation simultaneously) extends standard pairwise contrastive learning to a three-entity setting, which the authors argue is unique to the emotion-explanation domain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that domain-specific psychological knowledge (VAD vectors) plus a simple alignment mechanism can close most of the gap between a 186M-parameter model and a 7B-parameter LLaVA on a specialized task, all trainable on a single consumer GPU. This suggests a broader recipe: injecting expert-annotated structured knowledge into small models may be more effective than simply scaling up. Open questions include whether these techniques transfer to large models (the authors' LoRA attempt on LLaVA failed), whether the approach generalizes beyond the 8 Ekman categories and art imagery, and how to balance the accuracy-diversity trade-off that the Unique metric exposes.
