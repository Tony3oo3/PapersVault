## Problem

Blind image deblurring—recovering both a clear image and its blur kernel from a single degraded image—degrades severely under strong additive Gaussian noise, especially when the noise level is unknown. Existing MAP-based and deep-prior methods overfit to noise or produce unstable kernel estimates because they jointly optimize both the image and kernel in a highly ill-posed solution space, with no principled mechanism to suppress noise during kernel recovery.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single blurry, noisy image (e.g., 256×256 or 512×512) with no knowledge of the noise level, blur kernel, or original image. It outputs a deblurred clear image x₀ and a set of N_k estimated blur kernels h_i, each computed with a different noise suppression factor k_i to cover an unknown noise range. A single untrained DIP network produces both the clear image and intermediate images x_i (via group convolution filters on its last feature layer), which are then used analytically to derive the kernels—no separate kernel network is needed.

## Method

The core contribution is a noise-robust kernel estimation function derived by inverting the Wiener deconvolution: given a clear image x and blurry image y_m, the kernel is computed in closed form as Ĥ = X̄/(|X|²+k)·Y_m, where k is a noise suppression factor equal to the ratio of noise mPSD to kernel mPSD. The optimal k is analytically derived as k ≈ A(σ)², where A encodes kernel smoothness (A≈1 for sparse motion blur, A≈100 for Gaussian blur). Because the true noise level is unknown, a multiple kernel estimation scheme sets N_k distinct k values (e.g., 5², 15², 25²), each yielding a kernel estimate; the data-fidelity loss is averaged over all kernels, and the authors show that suboptimal k values still produce valid (if slightly noisy or blurry) kernels rather than incorrect solutions. A single untrained DIP network generates x₀ and intermediate images x_i, and the full optimization minimizes the averaged data-fidelity term plus a sparsity penalty on the inverse-filtered image and TV regularization on x₀, with non-negativity and sum-to-one constraints enforced on each kernel via absolute-value and normalization operations.

## Results

On the AFHQ-dog dataset with motion blur, the proposed method achieves 24.23 dB PSNR and 0.632 SSIM at σ=10, and 22.90 dB PSNR and 0.539 SSIM at σ=20, outperforming the next-best method (Pure-let) by 3.2 dB PSNR and 0.098 SSIM at σ=10. On the Sun et al. dataset, the method reaches 25.94 dB PSNR and 0.677 SSIM at σ=10, surpassing Pure-let (18.87 dB, 0.254 SSIM) by a margin of 7.1 dB PSNR. The advantage grows with noise level: at σ=20 on Sun et al., the gap over Pure-let widens to 7.5 dB PSNR (24.83 vs. 17.31).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all nine baselines on every metric (PSNR and SSIM) across both AFHQ and Sun et al. datasets at both noise levels. The closest competitor is Pure-let [24], which the proposed method surpasses by 3.2 dB PSNR (24.23 vs. 21.02) on AFHQ-dog at σ=10 and by 7.5 dB on Sun et al. at σ=20 (24.83 vs. 17.31). The MAP-based l₁/l₂ [18] is the second-best classical method but trails by 4.5 dB PSNR on AFHQ-dog at σ=10 and degrades severely at σ=20 (16.03 dB). The DIP-based methods (SelfDeblur [33] and WDIP [3]) perform worst under strong noise, achieving only 12–14 dB PSNR, indicating that their implicit priors are insufficient to handle the noise-robustness challenge. The dark-channel and l₀-prior methods (Pan-DCP [30], Pan-l₀ [29]) and the sparse-model method (Liang et al. [4]) collapse to near-noise-floor performance (7–11 dB PSNR) at σ=20. Notably, Sanghvi et al. [35] maintains relatively stable SSIM across noise levels (0.52 at both σ=10 and σ=20) but still lags the proposed method by 7+ dB PSNR.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Deep Image Prior (DIP) framework for natural image reconstruction and the classical Wiener deconvolution formulation, but departs from prior blind deblurring methods in a key conceptual way: it derives an analytical, closed-form kernel estimation function (Eq. 5) from the Wiener inverse filter, showing via MAP reformulation (Eq. 10) that only the clear image needs to be estimated rather than jointly optimizing image and kernel. This contrasts with edge-selection-based methods (Cho & Lee, Joshi et al.) and sparsity-regularized MAP approaches (Krishnan et al., Pan et al.) that rely on explicit kernel priors or sharp-edge assumptions that break down under strong noise. The multiple kernel estimation scheme, which sets multiple noise suppression factors to cover unknown noise levels, is a novel practical extension not present in prior noise-blind deblurring work (Jin et al., Bredell et al.).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method addresses a practically critical gap: most blind deblurring methods assume known or estimable noise levels, yet in real imaging systems (SEM, fluorescence microscopy) the noise level is rarely known a priori. The demonstrated ~3–4 dB PSNR gains over the strongest training-free baselines at σ=20, combined with successful application to real SEM and two-photon fluorescence images, suggest the approach could be directly useful in scientific imaging pipelines. Limitations include the per-image DIP optimization cost (no amortized inference), the reliance on Gaussian noise assumption for the analytical kernel function, and the need to pre-select the range of k values. Open questions include whether the multiple-k scheme can be made fully adaptive, how the method extends to non-Gaussian or signal-dependent noise, and whether the analytical kernel estimation generalizes to more complex PSF families beyond motion and Gaussian blur.
