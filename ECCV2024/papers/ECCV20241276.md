## Problem

Existing video shadow detection methods and datasets (ViSha, VISAD) are built around dominant, isolated shadow instances at low resolution, leaving complex real-world scenes with numerous small, overlapping, and multi-source shadows largely unexplored. This gap limits both the practical applicability of VSD models and the ability to evaluate them under conditions that reflect actual deployment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241276/cropped_page1_idx6.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a main frame at 512×512 resolution together with 4 neighboring reference frames (progressively scaled to match multi-level feature dimensions) and a set of high-resolution local patches (512×512×3) cropped directly from the original full-resolution image (e.g., 3840×2160) guided by a preliminary low-resolution mask. It produces two outputs: a low-resolution shadow mask M_lr from the intermediate feature F_4 (used internally to locate shadow regions for cropping) and a final high-quality shadow mask M_hq that integrates both temporal context and high-resolution local detail.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-stage training paradigm that formulates complex VSD as conditioned feature adaptation in both temporal and spatial domains. Stage 1 pre-trains a SegFormer image-level model on individual frames; Stage 2 transfers it to video-level by inserting a Temporal-Adaption Block and a Spatial-Adaption Block, both inspired by ControlNet's frozen-encoder + trainable-copy + zero-convolution design. The Temporal-Adaption Block progressively fuses reference frames at multiple resolutions, using modified self-attention with channel-wise attention to handle shadow deformation across time while preserving consistent context features. The Spatial-Adaption Block employs a mask-guided cropping strategy to extract high-resolution patches from the original frame, then fuses them into low-resolution global features via cross-attention with SAM-style position encoding, recovering fine shadow boundaries that would be lost in pure upsampling.

## Results

On the ViSha dataset, the proposed method achieves MAE 0.027, Fβ 0.801, IoU 0.684, and BER 8.96, outperforming the best prior VSD method (SCOTCH and SODA: MAE 0.029, Fβ 0.793, IoU 0.640, BER 9.07). On the newly introduced CVSD dataset, the method achieves MAE 0.046, Fβ 0.638, IoU 0.515, and BER 18.32, substantially surpassing the strongest baseline SC-Cor (MAE 0.070, Fβ 0.573, IoU 0.476, BER 19.94). The performance gap widens considerably on CVSD, highlighting the difficulty of complex real-world shadow patterns and the effectiveness of the temporal-spatial adaption design.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all 14 baselines across all four metrics (MAE, Fβ, IoU, BER) on both ViSha and CVSD. On ViSha, the closest competitor is SCOTCH and SODA [36] (MAE 0.029, IoU 0.640), which the method surpasses by 0.002 MAE and 0.044 IoU; among image-level shadow detection methods, DSD [76] and MTMT [5] are the strongest (MAE 0.043), trailing by roughly 0.016 MAE. On CVSD, the advantage is far more pronounced: the best VSD baseline SC-Cor [11] achieves MAE 0.070 and IoU 0.476, while the method reaches MAE 0.046 and IoU 0.515 — a 34% relative MAE reduction. Notably, all VSD methods substantially outperform their ISD counterparts on CVSD (e.g., SC-Cor MAE 0.070 vs. best ISD MTMT MAE 0.074), confirming the importance of temporal modeling, yet the proposed two-stage adaption design still yields the largest margin. The VOS baselines (STM, COSNet, FEELVOS) consistently underperform on both datasets, with FEELVOS [65] achieving the worst CVSD IoU (0.385) among all compared methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant video shadow detection paradigm of shared encoders with auxiliary fusion modules (optical flow, LSTM, attention) by reframing the problem as conditioned feature adaptation in both temporal and spatial domains. The architectural design directly inherits from ControlNet's frozen-encoder-plus-trainable-copy-with-zero-convolution pattern, applied here to fuse reference frames (temporal) and high-resolution local patches (spatial) into a pre-trained SegFormer backbone. The mask-guided cropping strategy borrows the prompt-encoding idea from SAM to localize where high-resolution details should be injected, while the two-stage image-to-video transfer paradigm contrasts with prior work that trains video models from scratch or uses implicit losses (contrastive, consistency regularization) to manage cross-frame information.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The CVSD dataset fills a critical gap: all prior video shadow datasets (ViSha, VISAD) contain only isolated, dominant shadows at low resolution, making them poor proxies for real-world scenes with dozens of small, overlapping shadows under diverse lighting. The paper's own results acknowledge that even the best methods still struggle substantially on CVSD (e.g., IoU drops from ~0.68 on ViSha to ~0.52 on CVSD), signaling that complex video shadow detection remains an open problem. The two-stage paradigm offers a practical recipe for transferring image-level segmentation to video without retraining from scratch, a pattern that could generalize beyond shadows. Open questions include whether the progressive reference-frame fusion and mask-guided cropping scale to longer sequences or even more cluttered scenes, and whether the approach can be extended to other fine-grained video segmentation tasks.
