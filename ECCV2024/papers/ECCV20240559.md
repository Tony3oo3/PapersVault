## Problem

Existing image-text alignment models (CLIP, BLIP, etc.) operate on a bag-of-words basis and fail at compositional reasoning (e.g., distinguishing 'horse eating grass' from 'grass eating horse'). A critical but overlooked cause is that standard negative-caption generation introduces distributional biases between positive and negative captions (e.g., GPT systematically substitutes 'giraffe' with 'elephant'), causing alignment models to exploit textual shortcuts rather than genuinely matching images to text.

## Input / Output

The model receives an RGB image and a free-form text caption, formatted into the prompt 'Does this image I match the following caption T. Answer Yes or No directly.' The output is a continuous alignment score in [0,1] computed as a softmax over the LLM's next-token logits for 'Yes' and 'No', which can be thresholded for binary classification or used directly for ranking multiple images against a single prompt.

## Method

The approach has three stages. First, GPT-4 generates two types of hard negative captions from positive ones: a 'replacing' strategy (substituting one linguistic element with a plausible alternative, e.g., 'cat'→'dog') to train perceptual discrimination, and a 'swapping' strategy (rearranging existing elements, e.g., 'apple left of banana'→'banana left of apple') to train relational reasoning. Second, a text-only BERT classifier is trained in a 5-fold cross-validation scheme to identify captions distinguishable purely by text; the top 30% most confidently predicted samples from both classes are removed, maximizing the entropy of the remaining text distribution so the model cannot shortcut via linguistic bias. Third, LLaVA-1.5 is fine-tuned on the filtered positive/negative pairs using the Yes/No prompt, with the alignment score extracted from the softmax of the two token logits. The same curated data also improves a lightweight BLIP2 Q-former with an ITM head, demonstrating generalizability across architectures.

## Results

LLaVA-score achieves a Winoground group score of 47.25 (image 68.00, text 53.75), substantially outperforming the next-best method LLaVA-1.5 zero-shot (34.25 group) and all other baselines. On SugarCrepe, it reaches 95.3/94.9/97.5 accuracy on replace/swap/add splits, and on MagicBrush it scores 87.28 group accuracy. On SeeTRUE (ROC-AUC), it achieves 88.8/77.7/84.9 on DrawBench/EditBench/COCO-T2I, and on the TIFA ranking task it attains the best Spearman ρ of 64.5 and Kendall τ of 49.8.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LLaVA-score outperforms all eight baselines on nearly every benchmark. The largest margin is on Winoground, where the group score jumps from 34.25 (LLaVA-1.5 zero-shot) to 47.25, and the image score from 49.75 to 68.00, indicating that fine-tuning with distribution-filtered data dramatically improves compositional reasoning. On SugarCrepe, the method leads all baselines by 1.8–13.9 points across the three splits (e.g., 94.9 vs. 87.1 for VisualGPTScore on swap). On MagicBrush, it achieves 87.28 vs. 82.61 for LLaVA-1.5 zero-shot and 78.31 for VisualGPTScore. On SeeTRUE, gains are more modest (e.g., 88.8 vs. 86.9 DrawBench ROC-AUC over LLaVA-1.5), suggesting the base model already performs well on synthetic-image alignment. On the TIFA ranking task, LLaVA-score achieves the best Spearman ρ (64.5) and Kendall τ (49.8), edging out LLaVA-1.5 zero-shot (61.5/45.8) and TIFA itself (59.7/47.2). Notably, CLIP, NegCLIP, and BLIP2-ITC perform at or below chance on Winoground, highlighting their inability to handle compositional structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on LLaVA-1.5 and BLIP2 as base vision-language architectures, fine-tuning them for image-text alignment scoring. It departs from prior negative-caption generation approaches (NegCLIP's random word shuffling, SugarCrepe's grammar/commonsense filtering) by identifying a distribution-level bias that per-instance coherence checking cannot address: even grammatically correct, logically coherent negatives generated by GPT carry systematic word-frequency and topic biases relative to the source dataset. The key conceptual contribution is using a text-only BERT classifier to iteratively remove the most confidently separable samples, thereby maximizing text entropy in the training set — a dataset-level intervention rather than a per-sample quality filter.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that alignment models can be trained to rely on textual distributional cues rather than actual image content is a practical warning for anyone curating contrastive or classification training data in multimodal settings. The filtering technique is simple, generalizable beyond image-text alignment to any multimodal scenario where one modality carries exploitable bias, and the resulting LLaVA-score model achieves strong gains on compositional reasoning benchmarks (Winoground, SugarCrepe) and T2I ranking. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger, more diverse training corpora where distributional overlap between positive and negative captions is naturally higher, and whether the 30% data discard rate is optimal across different base models and task formulations.
