## Problem

State-of-the-art optimization-based methods for neural implicit surface reconstruction (IGR, Neural-Pull, SIREN) minimize only the points-to-surface side of the Chamfer distance, leaving a blind spot that permits large spurious surface chunks in regions with no nearby input points. The widely adopted fix—SIREN's off-surface loss—has not been theoretically characterized, and this paper proves it is equivalent to a surface-area regularizer that trades spurious-surface removal for over-smoothing or even complete surface disappearance.

## Input / Output

The method takes an unoriented, sparse, and possibly noisy point cloud (no normals) contained in a known bounding region Ω, and produces a neural implicit surface defined as the zero-level set of an MLP f(θ,·): ℝ³→ℝ. The surface is constrained to approximate a signed distance field via the eikonal equation ‖∇ₓf‖=1, and a mesh is extracted at test time using marching cubes at 512 resolution for evaluation. The point density varies non-uniformly across shapes due to realistic scanning, and some shapes have missing coverage.

## Method

DiffCD extends the standard IGR loss by adding the missing surface-to-points Chamfer distance term, yielding a symmetric Chamfer distance between the point cloud and the implicit surface. The surface-to-points term requires sampling points xᵢ(θ) uniformly from S_θ and computing their derivatives ∇_θ xᵢ(θ); this is done via the level-set constraint from Atzmon et al., taking the minimum-norm solution that naturally yields linearly interpolating surfaces for sparse inputs. Surface sampling is made efficient by extracting a mesh via marching cubes every 1000 iterations and drawing candidate points from that mesh rather than the full bounding volume, followed by a 4-step SDF-descent to project candidates onto the zero-level set. The paper further proves (Theorem 1) that SIREN's off-surface loss L_SSA^α converges to the surface area |S_θ| as α→∞ when f is an SDF, explaining its over-smoothing behavior and motivating the geometric (rather than regularizing) nature of DiffCD's correction.

## Results

On the FAMOUS dataset (22 shapes, 3 noise levels), DiffCD achieves the best Chamfer Distance (CD) among all optimization-based methods in every setting: 0.518 (No Noise), 0.690 (Medium Noise), and 1.416 (Max Noise). On the larger Thingi10k test set (100 shapes, noise-free), DiffCD obtains CD=0.500, CD²=0.580, and CA=13.0°, yielding a 7.2% and 42.6% relative improvement in CD and CD² over the next-best method (SIREN with μ=0.33). In the noise-free FAMOUS setting, DiffCD also outperforms the supervised baselines Points2Surf (CD 0.589) and POCO (CD 0.539), while remaining competitive with NKSR† (CD 0.539).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among optimization-based baselines, DiffCD decisively outperforms IGR [14] and Neural-Pull [4] across all noise levels and all three metrics (CD, CD², CA); for example, on Max Noise FAMOUS, IGR scores CD=2.553 and CD²=27.912 versus DiffCD's 1.416 and 4.306. Against SIREN [27], DiffCD wins on CD and CD² in every setting while SIREN (μ=0.33) achieves a slightly lower CA (e.g., 41.1° vs 37.4° on Max Noise), reflecting SIREN's surface-area regularization that smooths details. On the noise-free FAMOUS setting, DiffCD (CD 0.518) beats both supervised baselines Points2Surf (0.589) and POCO (0.539), and is competitive with NKSR† (0.539), which benefits from a normal estimator trained on ScanNet. On Thingi10k, DiffCD (CD 0.500, CD² 0.580) matches NKSR† on CA (13.0°) and slightly trails it on CD² (0.463), while clearly outperforming all optimization-based methods. The paper notes that SIREN's advantage in CA comes at the cost of over-smoothing, as demonstrated by the surface-area regularization analysis in Sec. 5 and the shape-specific tuning required (Fig. 7–8).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the IGR [14] framework for optimization-based neural implicit surface fitting by identifying that its loss (and those of Neural-Pull [4] and SIREN [27]) only minimizes the points-to-surface side of the Chamfer distance, leaving a geometric blind spot that produces spurious surfaces. The key conceptual departure is adding the missing surface-to-points term to form a fully symmetric Chamfer distance (DiffCD), with gradient computation borrowed from Atzmon et al. [1] and surface sampling adapted from Yang et al. [31]. A second, purely theoretical contribution proves that SIREN's widely-used off-surface loss is mathematically equivalent to a surface area regularizer (Theorem 1), reframing a commonly cited 'non-manifold' penalty as an area-shrinking term.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The proof that SIREN's off-surface loss regularizes surface area provides a clean theoretical explanation for the over-smoothing artifact that practitioners have observed empirically, and suggests that tuning its weight is fundamentally a trade-off between removing spurious geometry and erasing true detail. DiffCD eliminates this trade-off by addressing spurious surfaces geometrically rather than through regularization, yielding consistent improvements across noise levels without shape-specific tuning. Open questions include whether the approach can be made to adapt automatically to unknown or spatially varying noise levels (currently requiring manual eikonal weight adjustment), and whether the ~6× computational overhead from surface sampling can be reduced. The authors also note that learned surface priors could complement the optimization-based framework.
