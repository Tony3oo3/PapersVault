## Problem

Naively applying CLIP with prompt learning to human-drawn sketches fails to generalise across both unseen categories and varying abstraction levels (from low-abstract edge maps to highly abstract QuickDraw doodles). The paper identifies that simply scaling training data across multiple abstraction levels yields only marginal gains, and that sketch abstraction is a continuous, hard-to-annotate spectrum that existing classifiers do not model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241493/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single raster sketch image (3×H×W, monochrome line drawing) as input and produces two outputs: a classification probability over an open-set of K categories, and a continuous abstraction score expressed as a softmax-normalised distribution over three coarse levels (low/medium/high). The abstraction score is not a single discrete label but a weighted mixture, reflecting that real sketches lie on a continuous spectrum between the three reference levels. An auxiliary output is a reconstructed vector sketch (sequence of 2D coordinates plus pen-state) used only during training.

## Method

The method adapts frozen CLIP (ViT-B/16) for sketches by injecting learnable visual prompts into the first 9 transformer layers of the vision encoder and learnable text prompts into the text encoder, while a lightweight Meta-Net predicts an instance-specific context vector π that shifts the text prompts to reduce overfitting to seen categories. To enforce sketch-specific traits, an auxiliary GRU-based raster-to-vector reconstruction loss trains the visual prompts to encode sparse stroke information. For abstraction generalisation, three codebook vectors (θ_l, θ_m, θ_h) are learned for low/medium/high abstraction; a codebook classifier predicts a softmax distribution over these levels, and the weighted sum of codebook vectors forms an abstraction prompt η that is added to the text prompts as a bias. An abstraction-mixup augmentation samples mixing coefficients from a 3-D Dirichlet distribution to synthesise intermediate abstraction features, training the codebook classifier on the continuous spectrum rather than discrete levels. The final loss combines cross-entropy classification, the raster-to-vector reconstruction, codebook classification, and mixup losses.

## Results

On the joint evaluation across Edgemaps (EM), TU-Berlin (TU), and QuickDraw (QD), the proposed method achieves 66.72%, 76.96%, and 45.20% top-1 few-shot accuracy on EM, TU, and QD respectively, beating the strongest baseline MaPLe by an average margin of 6.66%. In the zero-shot (unseen-category) setting, the method outperforms MaPLe by 5.80% and vanilla CLIP by 10.30% in average accuracy. When trained jointly on all three abstraction levels, the method reaches 62.96% average accuracy versus 45.59% for CLIP+CoOp, a gain of 17.4 percentage points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all 11 baselines across both few-shot (seen) and zero-shot (unseen) settings on all three datasets. Against the strongest CLIP-based competitor MaPLe [39], the method gains 6.66% average few-shot accuracy (62.96% vs 56.30%) and 5.80% zero-shot accuracy. Shallow prompt methods CoOp (45.59%) and CoCoOp (46.75%) lag the proposed method by roughly 17 and 16 percentage points respectively in the joint-training setting. Visual-prompt-only methods (VPT-A, VPT-P) underperform the combined vision+text prompting approach, with VPT-A achieving only 51.97% average few-shot accuracy. Traditional sketch classifiers (Sketch-A-Net, ResNet, Edge-Augment) are far behind, with Sketch-A-Net reaching only 3.14% zero-shot on TU and 0.68% on QD, highlighting the advantage of foundation-model-based generalisation. The method also surpasses Tip-Adapter by 6.91% and Linear Probe by 20.09% in few-shot accuracy, confirming that learned abstraction-aware prompts substantially outperform hand-crafted or non-parametric adaptation strategies.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the CLIP prompt-learning lineage (CoOp, CoCoOp, MaPLe) from photos to human-drawn sketches by introducing both visual and text prompts injected into the ViT and text encoders, respectively. It departs from prior sketch classifiers (Sketch-A-Net, ResNet-based methods) that are limited to closed-set recognition by leveraging CLIP's open-vocabulary capability, and from prior abstraction work (stroke-count, drawing-time proxies) by learning a continuous abstraction spectrum via codebook vectors with Dirichlet mixup. The raster-to-vector auxiliary loss is repurposed from the authors' own prior self-supervised sketch work [7] to enforce sketch-specific inductive bias on the visual prompts, while the Meta-Net for instance-specific context directly generalizes CoCoOp's adaptive prompt mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to frame sketch understanding through the lens of foundation-model generalisation, simultaneously addressing data scarcity (open-set categories) and abstraction variability — two bottlenecks the sketch community has struggled with independently. The 17.4% gap between naive joint training (45.6%) and the proposed method (62.9%) on QD+TU+EM is substantial, yet the paper explicitly acknowledges that generalisation across abstractions remains an open problem, as evidenced by the shaded region in Fig. 1. The human study (71–78% correlation, MOS 4.3/5) provides encouraging but not conclusive validation that the learned abstraction codes capture genuine perceptual abstraction rather than dataset identity. Future work will need to address finer-grained abstraction annotation, more diverse sketch styles, and whether the codebook approach scales beyond three coarse levels.
