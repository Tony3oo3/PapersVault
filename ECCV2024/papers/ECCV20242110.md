## Problem

Existing federated NAS methods (FedNAS, FedPNAS) search and train simultaneously, requiring O(N) repeated training cycles to satisfy N different deployment targets (hardware, latency, MACs), making them prohibitively expensive for diverse on-device inference. Additionally, these methods search over restricted architecture spaces—repeatedly stacking a single building block—which limits achievable accuracy under tight resource constraints. No prior federated NAS method decouples training from search while maintaining rich architectural diversity across depth and width.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20242110/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The training stage consumes a supernet (ResNet-10/26 for images, TCN for text) with elastic depth (1–3 blocks per stage across 4 stages) and width (expand ratios from 0.1 to 0.25), yielding ~5×10⁸ candidate subnets, along with non-iid client data partitions. The search stage takes a single deployment constraint θ (e.g., a MACs or latency budget) and outputs a specialized subnet configuration without any retraining. Because search is training-free, applying it to N different targets incurs O(1) total cost rather than O(N).

## Method

SuperFedNAS decouples federated training from architecture search: a single FL training stage co-trains all subnets inside one supernet, after which each client locally extracts a specialized subnet via predictor-guided evolutionary search (3-layer MLPs estimating accuracy and latency). The training algorithm, MaxNet, optimizes a novel min-max objective that explicitly minimizes the loss of the worst-performing subnet on each client's data partition, reducing interference between subnets of different sizes. Two key mechanisms make this tractable: (1) weight-shared sampling probability that prioritizes subnets overlapping more supernet parameters, mitigating staleness, and (2) a weighted aggregation scheme where the largest subnet's updates receive a decaying weight β (initialized at 0.9, cosine-decayed) to emulate the optimization dynamics of the min-max objective. This single-stage approach achieves up to 37.7% higher accuracy or 8.13× MACs reduction over prior federated NAS baselines with 11× lower training cost for 20 deployment targets.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, SuperFedNAS achieves 89.42–91.34% test accuracy across four MAC targets (0.45–3.75B), outperforming FedAvg (85.25–89.44%), FedNAS (77.33–89.43%), and FedPNAS (88.83–89.27%). On the harder CIFAR-100, SuperFedNAS reaches 56.35–58.25%, a 10.6–12.4 point improvement over FedAvg (43.19–45.00%) and up to 12.6 points over FedPNAS (45.77–47.80%). On CINIC-10, SuperFedNAS attains 73.12–75.38%, surpassing FedAvg by 11.4–9.4 points. The paper reports up to 37.7% higher accuracy at the same MACs or up to 8.13× MACs reduction at the same accuracy, with O(1) training cost for N deployment targets (vs. O(N) for baselines).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SuperFedNAS consistently outperforms all three baselines across every dataset and MAC target. Against FedAvg [28], the gap is largest on CIFAR-100 (up to 12.6 points at 0.45–0.95B MACs: 56.35% vs. 43.19%) and CINIC-10 (up to 11.4 points: 73.12% vs. 61.76%), while on CIFAR-10 the margin is 4.2–1.9 points. Against FedNAS [16], SuperFedNAS leads by up to 12.1 points on CIFAR-10 at the lowest MAC budget (89.42% vs. 77.33%) and by 12.6 points on CIFAR-100 (56.35% vs. 43.73%); FedNAS degrades severely under high non-iidness (e.g., 51.57% on CIFAR-10 at non-iid=0.1). Against FedPNAS [18], the closest competitor, SuperFedNAS still gains 0.6–3.5 points on CIFAR-10, 10.6–12.4 points on CIFAR-100, and 8.8–9.4 points on CINIC-10. The advantage is most pronounced at lower MAC budgets and on harder datasets, attributed to SuperFedNAS's richer architecture search space (variable depth and width per layer) versus the restricted block-stacking of FedNAS or the fixed base architecture of FedPNAS.

## Relation to Prior Work

SuperFedNAS transplants the centralized supernet paradigm from OFA [6]—where one large network co-trains many subnets with shared weights and search is decoupled from training—into the federated learning setting, where the key challenge becomes multi-objective optimization of shared weights across heterogeneous client data. It departs from existing federated NAS methods (FedNAS [16], FedPNAS [18], FedNASMobile [43]) that perform training and search simultaneously, incurring O(N) cost for N deployment targets, by introducing MaxNet: a single-stage FL training algorithm that optimizes a worst-subnet-loss objective to reduce interference. For the local search stage, it adopts predictor-guided evolutionary search from PNAS [27], making the extraction of specialized subnets training-free.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The O(1) training cost for arbitrary numbers of deployment targets is a practically significant efficiency gain for on-device inference scenarios where hardware, latency, and battery constraints vary widely. However, the evaluation is limited to small-scale datasets (CIFAR-10/100, CINIC-10, Shakespeare) and two architecture families (ResNet, TCN), leaving open whether the supernet approach and MaxNet's interference-reduction objective scale to ImageNet-scale data or more complex deployment constraints (memory, energy budgets). The paper also acknowledges that incorporating training-time system heterogeneity (bandwidth, compute limits) is orthogonal and left as future work, and the reliance on a single supernet per task limits cross-task generalization.
