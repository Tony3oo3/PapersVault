## Problem

Existing hallucination benchmarks for VLMs (e.g., POPE, CIEM, AMBER) only manipulate the text axis by varying questions, making it impossible to distinguish whether a model truly perceives scene content or merely guesses from prior knowledge. Without observing how answers change when the visual input itself is altered, high accuracy on these benchmarks can mask persistent hallucination.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240283/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes pairs of images (original and object-removed manipulated versions) from MS-COCO validation split, each paired with yes/no questions of the form 'Is there {object} in this image?' The dataset contains 2,227 images and 26,118 image-question pairs, with an average of 3.45 manipulated images per original. Outputs include four change-aware metrics (TU, IG, SB, ID) computed by comparing model answers before and after manipulation, an F1 harmonic mean of TU and (100−ID), object-wise error rates, and a two-axis (vision × text) correctness heatmap per scene.

## Method

The core mechanism is a three-stage image manipulation pipeline: (1) automatic object removal using SAM for mask extraction (guided by bounding boxes) and LaMa for inpainting with dilated masks, (2) rule-based filtering of artifacts like residual shadows and mask errors, and (3) human-guided refinement to eliminate ghost shadows and afterimages. On top of this dataset, four change-aware metrics are defined via a Filter operator over tuples (I_o, I_m, Q, A_o, A_m, R): TU measures correct answer flipping for removed objects, IG measures consistently wrong answers, SB (split into SB_p and SB_n) measures stubbornly unchanged answers, and ID measures spurious answer changes for unmanipulated objects. The key insight is that a truly understanding model must flip its answer when the queried object is removed while keeping answers stable for all other objects, enabling a two-axis (vision × text) disentanglement of hallucination sources that text-only benchmarks cannot provide.

## Results

On the BEAF benchmark, Shikra (7B) achieves the best True Understanding (TU) score of 52.7 and F1 of 67.6, substantially outperforming LLaVA-v1.5-7B (TU 32.6, F1 48.5), InstructBLIP-7B (TU 33.5, F1 49.5), and mPLUG-Owl2-7B (TU 24.6, F1 38.8). Among 13B models, LLaVA-v1.5 leads with TU 24.3 and F1 38.6, while InstructBLIP-13B lags significantly at TU 11.1 and F1 19.9 due to its extreme positive stubbornness (SBp = 84.4). Notably, TU values are dramatically lower than traditional accuracy on the same dataset (e.g., Shikra: TU 52.7 vs. Acc 84.5), revealing that high accuracy on standard benchmarks masks substantial hallucination.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Shikra (7B) dominates all other models on TU (52.7) and F1 (67.6), attributed to its location-aware training strategy that aids object existence judgment. InstructBLIP-13B is the weakest performer (TU 11.1, F1 19.9), primarily because it stubbornly answers 'Yes' regardless of scene manipulation (SBp = 84.4), whereas LLaVA-v1.5-13B achieves a 13-point TU advantage (24.3 vs. 11.1) by at least partially adapting its answers. Among 7B models, InstructBLIP-7B narrowly edges out LLaVA-v1.5-7B on TU (33.5 vs. 32.6) and F1 (49.5 vs. 48.5), while mPLUG-Owl2-7B trails both with TU 24.6. A critical finding is that traditional accuracy on the same BEAF dataset (Table 3: 69–84%) vastly overestimates true scene understanding (TU: 11–53%), indicating that models scoring well on POPE-style benchmarks may still hallucinate by guessing correct answers without grounding in the image.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the established text-axis-only hallucination evaluation paradigm (POPE, CIEM, AMBER) by introducing a vision-axis dimension: it manipulates images via object removal (using SAM for segmentation and LaMa for inpainting) and tracks how model answers change before and after the manipulation. Rather than building on a single prior method, it repurposes existing inpainting and segmentation tools for dataset construction and reframes hallucination evaluation as a change-detection problem. The four proposed metrics (TU, IG, SB, ID) are novel formulations that exploit the paired original/manipulated structure, going beyond the accuracy/precision/recall/F1 scores used in prior benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical implication is that high accuracy on existing hallucination benchmarks (e.g., 74.9% for LLaVA-13B) can mask substantial hallucination, as the corresponding TU score is only 24.3%, suggesting many 'correct' answers are guesses rather than grounded visual understanding. The stubbornness finding—that models persistently answer 'Yes' regardless of whether the object is present—highlights a systematic bias that prior metrics fail to capture. Limitations include reliance on MS-COCO (limited object diversity), manual intervention in the inpainting pipeline, and evaluation on only four VLMs; open questions include whether the vision-axis approach generalizes to other datasets, whether object insertion or resizing would yield complementary insights, and whether the findings hold for newer, larger VLMs.
