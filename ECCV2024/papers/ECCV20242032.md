## Problem

Obfuscation techniques for privacy-preserving ML inference have proliferated, but there is no standard benchmark, unified threat model, or consistent evaluation protocol to compare them. Existing works evaluate their defenses against ad-hoc attacks with unrealistic assumptions, making cross-method comparisons unreliable. This paper addresses the gap by proposing a modular benchmark platform, a taxonomy of attack models based on adversary side-knowledge, and standardized metrics for the privacy-utility-computation trade-off.

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes raw RGB images from four datasets (CIFAR-10 at 32×32, UTKFace, CelebA, and FairFace at face-crop resolution) and produces obfuscated representations as intermediate activations from split layers of a ResNet-18 backbone. The reconstruction attacks take these activations (optionally paired with obfuscator parameters θ₁ or labeled pairs {zⁱ, yₛⁱ}) and output reconstructed images or sensitive attribute predictions. Evaluation outputs are structured as Pareto-optimal curves over three weighted metrics: reconstruction quality (SSIM, PSNR, L1/L2, feature-space distance), task utility (cross-entropy), and weighted client/server wall-clock computation time.

## Method

The core contribution is a modular Python platform with four interchangeable modules—Models, Datasets, Algorithms, and Evaluation—governed by a benchmark specification that lets researchers plug in a single new technique without re-implementing the rest. The paper defines five attack models (A.1–A.5) parameterized by what the adversary knows: labeled pairs {zⁱ,yₛⁱ}, obfuscator weights θ₁, unlabeled representations {zⁱ}, or combinations thereof, with A.2 employing input-space optimization (SGD on the image) and model-space optimization (overfitting a decoder on a single z), and A.3/A.4 using GAN-style generators conditioned on leaked representations. Thirteen obfuscation techniques spanning noise injection, linear transforms, adversarial training, and learned embeddings are integrated and compared across split layers 0–7. Metrics combine input-space measures (L1, L2, PSNR, SSIM) with feature-space perceptual similarity from a pre-trained extractor, and a weighted computation cost that differentially prices client-side versus server-side wall-clock time to reflect mobile-device constraints.

## Results

The benchmark evaluates computational efficiency (runtime in ms) of 12 obfuscation techniques across 8 split layers of ResNet-18. Data-driven methods range from Shredder (18.6–26.1 ms) and NoPeek (29.5–36.2 ms) at the fast end to DeepObfuscator (83.5–118.6 ms) and DISCO (73.8–116.4 ms) at the slow end, while data-independent methods like Uniform Noise are dramatically slower (78.3–913.8 ms). For reconstruction quality (measured via SSIM), attack model A.2 (model-space optimization) generally outperforms A.1 (supervised training) across most split layers, except the last two layers where A.1 yields better reconstructions. The paper also shows that reconstruction quality degrades monotonically with increasing split depth, and that adversaries using a different training distribution (FairFace vs. UTKFace) still achieve surprisingly strong reconstruction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among data-driven obfuscation methods, Shredder [45] is the fastest (18.6–26.1 ms), followed by NoPeek [61] (29.5–36.2 ms) and Siamese Embedding [48] (16.6–38.1 ms), all of which are roughly 3–6× faster than DeepObfuscator [34] (83.5–118.6 ms) and DISCO [57] (73.8–116.4 ms). PAN [37] sits in the middle at 51.2–86.5 ms. Data-independent baselines are either extremely slow (Uniform Noise: 78–948 ms; PCA: 589 ms) or only applicable at the input layer (Gaussian Blur: 0.15 ms; Cloak: 7.8 ms; Linear Correlation: 11.9 ms), limiting their practicality for split-inference settings. In terms of reconstruction resistance, the paper finds that attack model A.2 (model-space optimization) generally produces higher-quality reconstructions than A.1 (supervised training) for most split layers, but A.1 surpasses A.2 at the deepest two layers where both methods lose significant perceptual similarity. The distribution-shift experiment shows that an adversary trained on FairFace can still reconstruct UTKFace activations effectively, indicating that obfuscation gains from domain-specific training are limited.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of obfuscation-based private ML inference and feature inversion attacks, drawing on GAN-based generative models (Goodfellow et al.) for its reconstruction attack model A.3 and on the adversarial representation learning literature (DeepObfuscator, DISCO, NoPeek, Shredder, PAN) for the defense side. It departs from the differential privacy tradition, which provides formal worst-case guarantees but is ill-suited to high-dimensional image data, by adopting an empirical evaluation framework with multiple adversary knowledge assumptions. Unlike prior obfuscation papers that each evaluate their own technique in isolation, SIMBA unifies them under a single modular platform with standardized metrics for privacy, utility, and computation, and introduces a taxonomy of attack models (A.1–A.5) that prior obfuscation works did not systematically consider.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark fills a clear gap: before SIMBA, obfuscation techniques were evaluated under inconsistent threat models and metrics, making cross-technique comparison unreliable. The released datasets of obfuscated representations and reconstructions, combined with the modular platform, lower the barrier for future work in private ML inference. However, the benchmark is limited to image classification with ResNet-18, relies entirely on empirical (non-formal) privacy evaluation, and does not account for communication costs, leaving open questions about scalability to other modalities, deeper architectures, and whether the observed privacy-utility trade-offs hold under stronger or more realistic adversary models.
