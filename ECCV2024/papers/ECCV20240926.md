## Problem

Existing object-centric NeRF methods decompose scenes into independent per-object neural models but ignore the semantic and physical relationships between objects, making them unsuitable for interactive scene editing where objects must be placed in physically plausible, semantically coherent configurations. This gap prevents flexible, user-friendly rearrangement of 3D scenes without manual fine-tuning or physically unrealistic results like object clipping.

## Input / Output

The method consumes multi-view RGB images (1280×720 from a handheld phone) with COLMAP-estimated camera poses and XMem-predicted instance masks to build the structured representation. For scene editing, it additionally accepts free-text instructions (e.g., 'mouse left to laptop') or a target reference image, which GPT-4/GPT-4V parses into spatial relationship triplets. The output is a hierarchical scene graph whose nodes carry per-object NeRF weights and whose edges encode support/relative-position relations, from which compositional volumetric rendering produces novel views with per-object shadows.

## Method

Structured-NeRF organizes per-object NeRFs into a hierarchical scene graph where support relationships determine a top-down optimization order (e.g., a cup holder must be placed before the cup on top of it). Training uses a five-term loss combining compositional photometric error, per-object accumulation and color supervision, and background inpainting (LaMa/LeftRefill) with Omnidata depth as an auxiliary signal to enforce multi-view consistency. For scene editing, GPT-4 or GPT-4V infers semantic spatial relationships, which are then optimized layer-by-layer: a semantic loss maps relationship triplets to potential-energy functions (Langevin-dynamics-inspired), followed by a physics loss using a Probabilistic Contact Model that computes gravity and Monte-Carlo-estimated overlap repulsion forces to drive objects to a stable equilibrium. A screen-space shadow map, adapted from classical shadow mapping, renders per-object shadows in the compositional volume rendering pass to enhance realism.

## Results

On ToyDesk2, Structured-NeRF achieves 26.55 PSNR, 0.8507 SSIM, and 0.186 LPIPS, outperforming Object-NeRF (24.82 PSNR) and UDC-NeRF (25.76 PSNR) by 1.79 and 0.80 dB respectively. On ScanNet, the method reaches 30.36 PSNR, 0.8394 SSIM, and 0.236 LPIPS, a 4.2 dB PSNR gain over Object-NeRF and a 4.2 dB gain over UDC-NeRF. For scene editing, Structured-NeRF achieves the best KID (0.14 on Kitchen), FID (197.22 on Kitchen), and user rating (3.96 on Dining Table) across all three test scenes compared to Stable Diffusion and DALL-E-NeRF.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Structured-NeRF consistently outperforms both Object-NeRF and UDC-NeRF on all three rendering metrics (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS) across both ToyDesk2 and ScanNet. The PSNR improvement over UDC-NeRF is particularly large on ScanNet (30.36 vs. 26.14, a 4.2 dB gain), while the LPIPS improvement is dramatic on ToyDesk2 (0.186 vs. 0.448), indicating substantially better perceptual quality. For scene editing, Structured-NeRF beats Stable Diffusion on all three scenes in KID, FID, and user rating, with the largest margin on Kitchen (FID 197.22 vs. 298.40; user rating 3.85 vs. 1.81). Against DALL-E-NeRF, the proposed method wins on KID and FID for Kitchen and Dining Table, and on user rating for all three scenes (3.96 vs. 3.64 on Dining Table, 3.85 vs. 3.47 on Kitchen, 3.88 vs. 3.17 on Office), though the KID gap on Office is negligible (0.04 vs. 0.04).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends object-centric NeRF decomposition (Object-NeRF, UDC-NeRF) by wrapping individual object models in a hierarchical scene graph that explicitly encodes semantic and physical relationships between objects, drawing on open-vocabulary 3D scene graph representations (ConceptGraphs, SayPlan). It departs from prior NeRF editing methods (Instruct-Nerf2Nerf, NeRF-In, SPIN-NeRF, InNeRF) that require manual pose adjustment or lack physical plausibility, by introducing a two-stage optimization—Langevin-dynamics-based semantic layout followed by a probabilistic contact model for gravity and repulsion—that automatically resolves object placement. The shadow rendering component repurposes classical shadow mapping from rasterized graphics into the volumetric NeRF rendering pipeline, and the editing interface leverages GPT-4/GPT-4V for relationship inference, a pattern emerging in concurrent robotics and scene-understanding work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The structured scene graph + physics optimization pipeline offers a practical path toward interactive, physically plausible 3D scene editing without requiring users to manually adjust object poses, which is a significant usability improvement over existing NeRF editing tools. However, the approach depends on GPT-4/GPT-4V for relationship inference (cost, latency, and availability concerns), uses a simplified homogeneous-material physics model with Monte Carlo mass estimation, and is evaluated on relatively small indoor scenes (ToyDesk, ScanNet, a few phone-captured scenes). Open questions include scalability to large or outdoor scenes, handling of deformable or articulated objects, real-time editing, and whether the physics model generalizes beyond simple gravity-and-repulsion interactions to more complex dynamics.
