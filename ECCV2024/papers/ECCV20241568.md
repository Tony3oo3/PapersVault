## Problem

Publicly available vision foundation models (e.g., DiNOv2) are highly vulnerable to white-box adversarial attacks because attackers can use the same public model as a surrogate. Traditional adversarial training requires modifying all model parameters at high computational cost, and no prior work has explored whether appending a small number of learnable tokens to the input sequence can serve as a lightweight, secret defense mechanism.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241568/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes standard RGB images patchified into token sequences and prepends a fixed set of 10 learnable Robustness Tokens (each of dimension embed_dim) to the front of the sequence before feeding it into the frozen transformer. The output is the feature representation from the patch and class tokens only; the Robustness Tokens and any register tokens are excluded from the extracted features. Downstream, these features support classification (ImageNet-1k) and semantic segmentation (ADE20K) via linear heads, and the Robustness Tokens remain secret from the attacker who crafts perturbations without knowledge of them.

## Method

Robustness Tokens are a small set of input-independent, learnable vectors prepended to the token sequence of a frozen Vision Transformer, optimized with two cosine-similarity objectives: an invariance term that keeps features on clean samples close to the original model's output, and an adversary term that pulls features extracted from PGD-attacked samples (with tokens) back toward the clean features (without tokens). Only the ~10 token vectors are trained (via Adam, lr=0.001, maximize=True) while all backbone parameters stay frozen, requiring roughly 200 gradient steps on 1600 ImageNet images to converge. Because the defense modifies the input rather than the parameters, it is orthogonal to and composable with existing adversarial training, and the tokens can be kept secret to deny the attacker full white-box knowledge. The paper further shows that the tokens implicitly counteract 'massive activations' in large transformers, which adversarial attacks exploit to distort feature geometry.

## Results

On ImageNet, Robustness Tokens dramatically improve adversarial robustness of DiNOv2 models: cosine similarity of features under white-box PGD attack jumps from near-zero (0.01–0.12) to 0.83–0.93, and classification accuracy under attack rises from 0.0–0.9% to 30.5–69.9% (e.g., DiNOv2-G+reg+rob reaches 69.9% vs. 0.9% for DiNOv2-G+reg). Segmentation mIoU on ADE20K under attack improves from 2.1–4.7% to 16.2–25.9%. Downstream clean performance is largely preserved: classification accuracy drops only ~0.5% on average while segmentation mIoU actually improves by ~0.2%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the original DiNOv2 baselines, Robustness Tokens yield orders-of-magnitude improvements in adversarial robustness: DiNOv2-G classification accuracy under PGD attack goes from 0.3% to 63.1%, and feature cosine similarity from 0.12 to 0.89. Compared to the register-token variant (DiNOv2+reg [14]), Robustness Tokens still provide substantial gains (e.g., DiNOv2-G+reg: 0.9% → 69.9% classification accuracy), showing that the two mechanisms are complementary. On DEIT-III [57], ROB tokens raise clean-vs-adversarial cosine similarity from 0.16–0.23 to 0.74–0.78 across base/large/huge sizes. On OpenCLIP [31], the improvement is even larger, from −0.02 to 0.13 up to 0.86–0.91, suggesting OpenCLIP is both more fragile and more amenable to token-based robustification. In all cases, the method wins decisively on robustness metrics while matching or slightly exceeding clean downstream performance of the unmodified baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the 'additional tokens' literature—register tokens (Darcet et al.), attention sinks (Xiao et al.), and prefix-tuning (Li & Liang)—for a fundamentally different objective: adversarial robustness rather than feature quality or generation performance. It departs from classical adversarial training (Madry et al.), which modifies model parameters, by instead appending a small set of secret learnable tokens to the input sequence while freezing all backbone weights. The mechanistic link to massive activations (Sun et al.) provides a new explanation for why larger ViTs are more vulnerable and how the tokens restore nominal activation magnitudes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical appeal is strong: 10 tokens trained in ~200 steps on 1600 images yield dramatic robustness gains (near-zero to 30–70% classification accuracy under white-box PGD) across DiNOv2, DEIT-III, and OpenCLIP, with negligible downstream degradation. The 'secret token' framing offers a lightweight, orthogonal defense layer that can be combined with existing methods. Key open questions include how well an attacker could infer or replicate the tokens given the same public backbone, whether the gains hold under task-specific (rather than task-agnostic) attacks, and whether the approach extends to non-vision sequence models. The paper itself acknowledges the inference vulnerability as a limitation requiring further study.
