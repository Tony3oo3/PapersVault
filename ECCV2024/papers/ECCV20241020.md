## Problem

Pixel-based normal integration scales quadratically with image resolution, making high-resolution reconstructions (e.g., 64 MP normal maps) prohibitively expensive—hours of computation for integration alone. Smooth, featureless regions contribute little geometric information yet consume the same computational budget as detailed areas. To the authors' knowledge, no prior work has formulated normal integration directly on general triangle meshes, leaving the opportunity to allocate resolution adaptively before solving the integration problem unexplored.

## Input / Output

The method takes a normal map (obtained from photometric stereo or shape from shading) and camera intrinsics as input. In the projective case, a weak-perspective approximation using the average camera-to-object distance is assumed unless the object is very close to the camera. The output is a feature-adaptive, isotropic triangle mesh in screen space with per-vertex depth values, controlled by a single user parameter ε (permitted surface approximation error in mm, e.g., 0.1 mm for high quality, 1 mm for low quality). The mesh typically requires 10–100× fewer vertices than foreground pixels, with compression increasing at higher input resolutions.

## Method

The pipeline has two stages. First, screen-space adaptive meshing: pixel-wise principal curvatures are computed from the normal map via the first and second fundamental forms (derived from normals alone, without depth), then lifted to vertices by taking the maximum over each vertex's star. These curvatures yield optimal edge lengths L = √(6ε/|κ| − ε²), and a uniform pixel-grid triangulation is iteratively refined through edge collapse/split, Delaunay flips, and foreshortening-compensated tangential smoothing to produce a sparse, isotropic mesh. Second, mesh-based normal integration: the normal-based Dirichlet energy (which avoids checkerboard artifacts and the Gibbs phenomenon) is discretized over the triangle mesh using discrete differential geometry, yielding a linear system with cotangent weights. Because the isotropic mesh keeps angles near 60°, the cotangent weights remain well-behaved, producing numerically stable, well-conditioned systems. The entire approach is non-learning and requires no training data.

## Results

On the DiLiGenT-MV dataset (5 objects × 20 views), the adaptive mesh at 'high' resolution (ε=0.1 mm) uses only 6–18% of the free variables of a pixel grid while achieving RMSE within ~0.3–0.7 mm of the best pixel-based method (e.g., Bear ortho: 3.37 mm vs. 2.97 mm for [7]). At 'low' resolution (ε=1 mm), the mesh requires as little as 1% of pixel variables. For 8192² normal maps, the meshing-first pipeline completes in ~5 minutes versus ~3 hours for pixel-based integration, with a compression factor of ~200×.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's adaptive mesh integration is consistently slightly worse in RMSE than the best pixel-based baseline [7] (Cao et al., CVPR 2021) across all 10 object-projection pairs, with gaps of roughly 0.3–0.7 mm (e.g., Cow perspective: 3.07 vs. 2.35 mm; Reading ortho: 6.83 vs. 6.34 mm). However, this comes at a 10–100× reduction in free variables, and the gap narrows at the 'high' quality setting. Compared to the 'Combined' pipeline (pixel-based integration [7] followed by remeshing [12]), the mesh-first approach achieves comparable or slightly better RMSE at high resolution while avoiding the full-resolution integration step entirely. Against the uniform-mesh baseline (same vertex count, no curvature adaptation), the adaptive mesh always achieves lower RMSE despite using equal or fewer vertices, confirming the value of curvature-driven refinement. The pixel-based method [20] (Heep et al.) is competitive with [7] on perspective views but is not available for orthographic evaluation in this paper. Overall, the paper trades a small accuracy penalty for dramatic compression and speed gains, positioning the method as complementary to rather than a replacement for pixel-based integration.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the adaptive remeshing framework of Dunyach et al. [12] from 3D surface deformation to screen-space triangulation driven by curvature extracted from normal maps, and combines it with the normal-based (rather than gradient-based) integration functional independently proposed by Cao et al. [6] and Heep [20]. The key departure from prior work is that all previous normal integration methods—whether variational Poisson solvers, inverse plane fitting [7], or mesh-deformation approaches [34]—operate on pixel grids or regular quad meshes, with mesh conversion only as a post-processing step. Here, the authors are the first to formulate and solve normal integration directly on an adaptive, curvature-driven triangle mesh, using a cotangent-weight discretization derived from discrete differential geometry that had not previously been published in this context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: for 64 MP normal maps, the meshing-first pipeline completes in minutes where pixel-based integration requires hours, and the sublinear vertex growth means compression improves at higher resolutions (up to ~200× at 8192²). This makes high-resolution photometric stereo reconstructions feasible on standard hardware without sacrificing geometric fidelity. Open questions remain around depth discontinuities (which would require topology-aware meshing), anisotropic meshes for sharp corners (where skinny triangles risk numerical instability from diverging cotangents), and whether the single-parameter control generalizes to multi-view or dynamic settings. The method is easily pluggable into existing photometric stereo pipelines, broadening its practical reach.
