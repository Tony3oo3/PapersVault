## Problem

Existing MLLM-based image generation methods (e.g., GILL) predict fixed-size visual embeddings to control Stable Diffusion, which introduces noise, lacks interpretability, and does not actively assist users in refining or editing images. There is no framework that leverages an LLM's world knowledge and reasoning to interactively guide generation and editing through explicit, detailed language prompts rather than implicit embeddings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241017/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system accepts user text instructions, optional input images (encoded via CLIP vision encoder + projection layer), and optional binary masks for inpainting/outpainting. The MLLM produces a two-part text output: a conversational response shown to the user and a generation prompt delimited by special tokens <gen_img> and </gen_img>. Because SD's CLIP text encoder handles only 75 tokens while LLMGA prompts often exceed 150 tokens, the encoder is applied iteratively to surplus tokens. The final outputs are generated, edited, or inpainted/outpainted images at SD1.5 resolution, plus the text response for interactive dialogue.

## Method

LLMGA employs a two-stage training scheme. In stage one, the MLLM (CLIP vision encoder frozen; projection layer and LLM optimized) is trained end-to-end with autoregressive cross-entropy loss on four curated datasets—prompt refinement, similar image generation, inpainting/outpainting, and instruction-based editing—alongside VQA and QA data, teaching it to produce detailed generation prompts wrapped in special tokens. In stage two, the MLLM is frozen and the SD UNet is fine-tuned (with all other SD components frozen) on LAION-Aesthetics and MSCOCO images using MLLM-generated detailed prompts, with iterative CLIP text encoding to handle prompts longer than 75 tokens. For instruction-based editing, the pre-trained T2I SD is used directly with Direct Inversion and prompt-to-prompt. A separate DiffRIR restoration network (built on DiffIR's architecture) takes the SD output and the masked original image as concatenated inputs, with random brightness/contrast perturbations during training, to correct texture, brightness, and contrast mismatches between generated and preserved regions.

## Results

On MSCOCO T2I generation, LLMGA-7B with fine-tuned SD1.5 achieves FID 18.52 and IS 41.04, improving over the original SD1.5 (FID 24.01, IS 35.99) by 5.48 FID and 5.05 IS. On the MagicBrush editing benchmark, LLMGA (zero-shot, no SD fine-tuning for editing) achieves L2 0.0218 and CLIP-T 0.3137, surpassing InstructPix2Pix (L2 0.0416, CLIP-T 0.2909) and approaching MagicBrush (L2 0.0224, CLIP-T 0.2979). For inpainting/outpainting on Places, LLMGA-7B (SD1.5-ft) reduces wide-mask outpainting FID from 5.01 (SD1.5) to 2.44, and wide-mask inpainting FID from 1.29 to 0.98.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SD1.5, LLMGA-7B (SD1.5-ft) achieves a 5.48 FID improvement (18.52 vs. 24.01) and 5.05 IS gain (41.04 vs. 35.99) on MSCOCO T2I, and reduces wide-mask outpainting FID by 2.57 (2.44 vs. 5.01) on Places. Against GILL, LLMGA-7B (SD1.5-ft) substantially outperforms on T2I (FID 18.52 vs. 25.11, IS 41.04 vs. 34.20), and the paper further shows that GILL's embedding-based control degrades rapidly with multi-turn conversation while LLMGA's language-prompt approach remains stable. On instruction-based editing (MagicBrush test set), LLMGA surpasses InstructPix2Pix on all five metrics (e.g., L2 0.0218 vs. 0.0416, DINO 0.8768 vs. 0.7252) without any editing-specific SD training, but trails MagicBrush on L1 (0.0814 vs. 0.0647), CLIP-I (0.8936 vs. 0.9293), and DINO (0.8768 vs. 0.8913) while matching or exceeding it on L2 and CLIP-T. For image restoration, DiffRIR (FID 2.2687) outperforms the original DiffIR (FID 2.3587) on Places outpainting, confirming the benefit of the reference-based scheme and color-degradation training.

## Relation to Prior Work

LLMGA extends the MLLM-for-generation line initiated by GILL and similar works, but deliberately departs from their core design choice of predicting fixed-size visual embeddings to control Stable Diffusion. Instead, it follows the LLaVA-style architecture (CLIP vision encoder + projection + LLM) and outputs detailed natural-language prompts, arguing that the language domain filters out embedding noise and better leverages the LLM's pre-trained textual knowledge. For the editing and restoration components, the paper composes existing tools—Direct Inversion, Prompt-to-Prompt, and the DiffIR restoration backbone—rather than introducing new generative or restoration architectures, positioning its contribution as a unified interactive orchestration layer on top of these established modules.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a practical, open-source recipe for making diffusion-based generation and editing more accessible through conversational interaction, and its two-stage training scheme (first teach the MLLM to write good prompts, then adapt the SD UNet to those prompts) is a reusable pattern for any MLLM+diffusion pipeline. However, the system is anchored to SD1.5 and its 75-token CLIP encoder, raising open questions about transfer to newer, higher-capacity diffusion backbones (SDXL, DiT-based models) and whether the iterative CLIP encoding trick scales. The zero-shot editing result (matching MagicBrush without dedicated editing training) is encouraging but evaluated on a single benchmark, and the restoration network remains a post-hoc patch rather than a solution integrated into the generation process.
