## Problem

While typographic attacks on CLIP-based VLMs were previously documented, the true mechanism behind LVLM susceptibility to such attacks remained unexplored. Prior work attributed the vulnerability solely to vision encoder attention shifts, but it was unclear whether LVLMs (which incorporate LLMs for richer multi-modal interaction) share the same failure mode or exhibit distinct cross-modal dynamics. This paper systematically investigates why LVLMs are distracted by typographic text and what factors govern the severity of the attack.

## Input / Output

The model receives an RGB image (optionally with typographic text overlaid at controlled font sizes of 3–15 px, opacities of 25–100%, 24 color variants, and 16 grid positions) paired with a text prompt containing a task-specific question and multiple-choice options. The output is a single letter corresponding to the correct option, or a free-form image description when using informative prompts. The TypoD dataset spans four sub-tasks—object recognition, visual attribute detection, enumeration, and commonsense reasoning—each with Base (1,570) and Large (20,000) scales, enabling evaluation across both perception-oriented and cognition-oriented tasks.

## Method

The paper introduces TypoD, a two-stage dataset: a Factor Exploring stage that varies font size, opacity, color, and spatial position to identify the most effective attack configuration, and a Factor Fixing stage that applies the optimal typography (15 px, 100% opacity, white, center-right position) to generate fixed-scale benchmarks. The core analytical contribution is three discoveries about the attack mechanism: (1) typographic text attracts vision encoder attention away from image content; (2) the text modality's information content modulates where the vision encoder focuses, meaning richer text options redirect attention back to true image content; and (3) LVLMs require cross-modal attention matching between the prompt and generated text descriptions to resist distraction. Based on these findings, the authors propose augmenting prompts with explicit image-description requirements (e.g., 'Provide a detailed visual description of the image to answer the following question'), which causes the LLM to generate intermediate visual descriptions that the question then queries, effectively shifting attention away from typographic text and reducing the average performance gap from 42.07% to 13.90%.

## Results

On TypoD-B (1,570 images), typographic attacks cause a 42.3% overall accuracy drop for LLaVA-v1.5 (87.3% → 45.2%) and a 27.1% drop for InstructBLIP (88.0% → 60.9%). On the larger TypoD-L (20,000 images), the gaps are 32.4% for LLaVA-v1.5 and 28.5% for InstructBLIP. The most vulnerable sub-task is Object Recognition (62.2% GAP for LLaVA-v1.5 on TypoD-B), while Visual Attribute Detection is the most robust (30.0% GAP). Through informative prompt engineering, the authors reduce LLaVA-v1.5's overall GAP from 42.3% to 18.2% (Prompt 3), and the abstract reports an average reduction from 42.07% to 13.90% across tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares two LVLMs (LLaVA-v1.5 and InstructBLIP) and one VLM (CLIP) under typographic attacks. InstructBLIP is consistently more robust than LLaVA-v1.5, with a 27.1% overall GAP versus 42.3% on TypoD-B, though this advantage narrows on TypoD-L (28.5% vs. 32.4%) and reverses on the Enumeration sub-task (46.3% vs. 26.5%). CLIP, using the same vision encoder as LLaVA, fails to distinguish image content from inserted typography in zero-shot classification, while LLaVA can partially discriminate the two, indicating the vulnerability is not purely in the vision encoder but also in the text-encoder's limited semantic options. The authors demonstrate that augmenting CLIP's text options with more informative prompts (Set 2 vs. Set 1) improves zero-shot classification accuracy by 30% (CLIP-B) and 58% (CLIP-L) under typographic attacks. For LLaVA-v1.5, progressive prompt enrichment (Prompt 1 → 2.1 → 2.2 → 2.3 → 3) reduces the overall GAP from 31.6% to 18.2%, with the largest single gain (12.5 points) coming from adding image-description requirements (Prompt 1 → 2.1).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of typographic attack research initiated on CLIP (Avrahami et al. 2022; Noever & Noever 2021; Azuma & Matsui 2023) to the LVLM setting, where the same vision encoders are coupled with LLMs like Vicuna. Prior work attributed typographic vulnerability to the vision encoder's attention being hijacked by inserted text; this paper departs from that framing by showing the text modality's information content is equally (or more) responsible for attention misallocation, and that augmenting prompt semantics can substantially mitigate the effect. The TypoD benchmark generalizes the single-task zero-shot classification evaluation used in prior CLIP studies to a multi-task VQA framework spanning object recognition, attribute detection, counting, and commonsense reasoning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The TypoD dataset (20,000 images across four task types) provides the first systematic platform for measuring typographic robustness in LVLMs, and the finding that prompt engineering alone can reduce the performance gap from 42% to ~14% offers a low-cost, deployment-friendly mitigation that practitioners can adopt immediately. However, the mitigation is prompt-based rather than architectural, its generalizability beyond LLaVA and InstructBLIP is untested, and it remains unclear whether the cross-modal attention-matching explanation holds for LVLMs with different projection mechanisms (e.g., Q-Former in BLIP-2) or for more sophisticated adversarial typography. Open questions include whether the attention-transfer mechanism identified here interacts with other multi-modal attack vectors (e.g., FigStep jailbreaks) and whether architectural changes to the vision-language interface could provide more robust protection than prompt-level fixes.
