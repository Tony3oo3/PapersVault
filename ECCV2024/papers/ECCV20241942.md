## Problem

Remote photoplethysmography (rPPG) is highly sensitive to environmental noise, yet its vulnerability to deliberate physical-domain attacks—where an attacker injects periodic signals via pulsating light or rhythmic movement, or blocks skin reflection with masks and makeup—remains largely unexplored. Prior security research in rPPG has been confined to the digital domain, and existing physical attack studies are limited in scope (e.g., a single LED, five short videos, or one mask type), leaving no comprehensive benchmark for evaluating rPPG robustness against a wide range of physical threats.

## Input / Output

The dataset provides 780 RGB facial videos (1920×1080, 30 fps, 70 s each) from 30 subjects across 26 attack scenarios, recorded with a Blackmagic Ursa Mini 4K camera under controlled studio lighting. Ground-truth physiological signals are captured simultaneously via a finger-oximeter PPG (120 Hz) and a single-lead ECG (1 kHz), with ECG used as the primary reference after bandpass filtering and 10-point averaging. Evaluation outputs include heart rate (highest PSD peak over 30-s non-overlapping segments), signal quality metrics (RMSE, MAE, Pearson R, SNR), and for active attacks, a success rate measuring how often the predicted HR is closer to the injected attack frequency than to the true HR.

## Method

ORPDAD introduces three physical attack categories—illumination (pulsating LED panels at 50/100 bpm with three intensity levels), movement (five body motions at two target frequencies plus natural talking), and concealment (opaque beanie/mask combinations and progressive translucent makeup application)—all recorded in a single controlled environment to isolate each attack variable. The empirical study benchmarks thirteen rPPG methods spanning hand-crafted (GREEN, CHROM, POS, LGI, ICA, PCA), supervised CNN (DeepPhys, TS-CAN, PhysNet, CVD), transformer (PhysFormer, PhySU-Net), and self-supervised (Contrast-Phys) approaches under two training protocols: Attack Unaware (trained only on still scenarios) and Attack Aware (trained with attack data, using attack-frequency-exclusive cross-validation to prevent memorization). Key findings show that hand-crafted and self-supervised methods are most susceptible, non-end-to-end methods using spatial-temporal maps offer the best innate robustness among supervised approaches, and incorporating attack data during training significantly improves supervised performance while paradoxically degrading self-supervised methods whose learned features lose physiological meaning.

## Results

On the still baseline (S1–S3), most supervised methods achieve ≤1 bpm RMSE (e.g., PhySU-Net 0.74, PhysNet 0.88, CHROM 0.91), confirming the controlled environment is not challenging. Under large illumination attacks (I3+I6), hand-crafted methods degrade to 13–28 bpm RMSE, while the most robust supervised non-end-to-end methods (PhySU-Net AU: 6.24, CVD AU: 7.17) remain substantially better than end-to-end counterparts (PhysNet AU: 16.1, DeepPhys AU: 26.8). Adding attack-aware training (AA) reduces PhysNet's large-illumination RMSE from 16.1 to 1.61 and PhySU-Net from 6.24 to 3.15, but paradoxically worsens self-supervised Contrast-Phys from 17.2 to 21.9 bpm RMSE. For movement attacks, large vertical motion (M2+M7) is the most damaging scenario, with hand-crafted methods reaching 11–27 bpm RMSE and even the best supervised AA method (PhysNet) still at 11.5 bpm RMSE.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among hand-crafted methods, CHROM (0.91 bpm RMSE on still) and POS (0.70 bpm) are the most robust to illumination attacks, retaining ≤14 bpm RMSE even under large attacks, while GREEN, ICA, and LGI degrade to 25–28 bpm. Among supervised methods, non-end-to-end architectures using spatial-temporal maps (PhySU-Net, CVD) consistently outperform end-to-end CNNs (DeepPhys, TS-CAN) and transformers (PhysFormer) under both AU and AA settings; for example, under large illumination AU, PhySU-Net achieves 6.24 bpm RMSE versus 26.8 for DeepPhys. PhysNet and PhysFormer are the strongest end-to-end methods, with PhysNet AA reaching 1.61 bpm RMSE under large illumination—nearly matching its still-baseline performance. Self-supervised Contrast-Phys is the most vulnerable method overall: it degrades to 17–22 bpm RMSE under attacks in AU, and paradoxically worsens in AA (21.9 bpm under large illumination, 20.1 bpm even on still scenarios), because attack-induced inter-data differences corrupt its contrastive learning objective. Attack-aware training (AA) dramatically improves all supervised methods (e.g., TS-CAN large illumination: 27.4→5.90 bpm; PhysNet: 16.1→1.61 bpm), confirming that exposure to attack data is the single most effective mitigation, though it does not fully restore still-baseline performance for the weakest architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of rPPG and physical security, departing from prior work that confined rPPG attack studies to the digital domain (PulseEdit, PrivacyPhys) or to a single, minimal physical scenario (a pulsating LED in [34] with only 5 videos from 4 subjects). It repurposes the well-established rPPG evaluation framework—spanning hand-crafted (GREEN, CHROM, POS, LGI, ICA, PCA), supervised CNN/transformer (DeepPhys, TS-CAN, PhysNet, CVD, PhysFormer, PhySU-Net), and self-supervised (Contrast-Phys) methods—under a new lens: systematic physical-domain attack scenarios. The novelty lies in introducing rhythmical movement as an attack vector (absent from all prior work) and in expanding illumination and concealment attacks from single-instance demonstrations to a controlled, multi-intensity, multi-scenario protocol (26 scenarios, 30 subjects, ~15.2 h of footage).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As rPPG moves toward deployment in clinical monitoring, driver drowsiness detection, and face liveness/anti-spoofing, the demonstrated vulnerability to low-cost physical attacks (a modulated LED panel, a metronome app, a face mask) is a critical security gap that must be addressed before widespread adoption. The finding that attack-aware training helps supervised methods but catastrophically degrades self-supervised Contrast-Phys (RMSE ~1 → ~20) reveals a fundamental tension in self-supervised learning: inter-data contrastive objectives amplify non-physiological attack signals rather than suppressing them. Open questions include whether inherently robust architectures (rather than data-dependent defenses) can be designed, whether these attacks transfer to liveness-detection pipelines, and how to extend the benchmark to more diverse environments and attack compositions.
