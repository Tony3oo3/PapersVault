## Problem

Existing LiDAR semantic segmentation models suffer significant performance degradation under adverse weather (fog, rain, snow), yet prior robustness methods either simulate specific weather conditions via physical models (impractical across all severities) or apply generic ML techniques without understanding the specific LiDAR corruption mechanisms. This paper identifies, through a data-centric toy experiment, that geometric perturbation and point drop are the two dominant distortion types driving segmentation failure, and proposes targeted augmentations for each.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240422/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes raw LiDAR point clouds (XYZ coordinates plus per-point intensity) and outputs per-point semantic class labels across 19 SemanticKITTI categories. During training, the augmentation pipeline modifies the input point cloud in-place: Selective Jittering perturbs XYZ and intensity within a randomly chosen depth or angular sector, while Learnable Point Drop removes a learned subset of points before they enter the segmentation backbone. The model is trained exclusively on clean data (SemanticKITTI or SynLiDAR) and evaluated on real adverse-weather scans (SemanticSTF) or synthetic corruptions (SemanticKITTI-C).

## Method

The core contribution is a two-part data augmentation strategy grounded in a toy experiment that isolates geometric perturbation and point drop as the primary causes of mIoU collapse. Selective Jittering (SJ) applies additive Gaussian noise to XYZ coordinates and intensity within a randomly selected depth range (DSJ) or azimuth range (ASJ), mimicking non-uniform refraction from droplets; a Range Jittering (RJ) variant restricts perturbation to the radial direction. Learnable Point Drop (LPD) employs a Deep Q-Network whose state is the sum of segmentation loss and output entropy on SJ-augmented points; the DQN predicts which point indices to remove, and its reward is the increase in loss-plus-entropy after dropping, thereby learning depth-aware erase patterns that replicate fog-induced occlusion. The two augmentations are applied sequentially during supervised training of standard backbones (MinkowskiNet, CENet, SPVCNN) with no architectural changes, requiring only a gradient-norm cap for DQN stability.

## Results

On the SemanticKITTI→SemanticSTF benchmark (the primary evaluation), the proposed method (Baseline+SJ+LPD) achieves 39.5 mIoU, a +8.1 mIoU improvement over the MinkowskiNet baseline (31.4) and +5.6 mIoU over the strongest prior method PointDR (33.9). Per-weather gains are +5.3 (dense fog), +7.4 (light fog), +7.9 (rain), and +7.8 (snow) mIoU. The method also generalizes across architectures, yielding +7.8 to +10.3 mIoU on SemanticSTF and +0.4 to +5.6 mIoU on SemanticKITTI-C for CENet, SPVCNN, and MinkowskiNet backbones.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against three data-augmentation baselines on the SemanticKITTI→SemanticSTF benchmark. The proposed method (39.5 mIoU) substantially outperforms both LaserMix (14.7 mIoU, −24.8) and PolarMix (15.3 mIoU, −24.2), which are general-purpose LiDAR mixing augmentations not designed for weather corruption. Against the most relevant prior method PointDR [33] (33.9 mIoU), which uses a task-agnostic teacher-student framework, the proposed method gains +5.6 mIoU, nearly tripling the +2.5 mIoU improvement PointDR achieved over the same baseline. The advantage is consistent across all four weather conditions (dense fog, light fog, rain, snow), with the largest per-weather gain in rain (+7.9 mIoU over baseline), attributed to Selective Jittering effectively modeling raindrop-induced geometric perturbation. The method also generalizes to other backbones (CENet, SPVCNN) and the SemanticKITTI-C corruption dataset, confirming that the gains are not architecture-specific.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from both simulation-based weather augmentation (LISA, fog/snow/rain physical models) and task-agnostic robustness methods (PointDR, Robo3D) by adopting a data-centric analysis that identifies which LiDAR distortions actually degrade segmentation performance. Rather than modeling each weather type explicitly, it reduces the problem to two universal distortion patterns—geometric perturbation and point drop—discovered through controlled toy experiments on SemanticKITTI. The Selective Jittering component repurposes the range-jittering concept from LISA but applies it selectively over depth or angle ranges, while the Learnable Point Drop introduces a novel use of Deep Q-Learning to discover which points to remove, a technique not previously applied to LiDAR data augmentation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that simple, targeted data augmentation (without any weather simulation) can nearly triple the robustness improvement over prior task-agnostic methods (+8.1 vs. +2.5 mIoU) suggests the field has been over-engineering the simulation side while under-investing in understanding which distortions matter. The two-distortion taxonomy provides a reusable diagnostic framework for future robustness work. However, LPD's inability to specifically target wet-ground point drops (noted in the paper's own qualitative analysis) and the added training complexity of a DQN module limit immediate adoption; whether the taxonomy generalizes beyond the four weather types tested (fog, rain, snow) remains an open question.
