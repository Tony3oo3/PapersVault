## Problem

Existing dexterous grasping policies are trained per-gripper and cannot be reused on a different hand, requiring expensive data collection and retraining for each new embodiment. While cross-object generalization has been studied, cross-gripper generalization for high-DOF dexterous hands remains largely unexplored, primarily because joint-space representations and geometry-dependent inputs cause policies to overfit to a specific gripper's morphology and shape.

## Input / Output

The policy consumes a scene point cloud (segmented into foreground object and background) and the current gripper joint configuration. From these, it derives two gripper-agnostic state representations: semantic key points (fingertip and middle-phalanx positions per finger plus palm root, computed via forward kinematics in the gripper's local frame, augmented with a 3D rotation vector) and a sampled Interaction Bisector Surface (4096 points with per-point features including distances to scene/gripper, foreground indicator, gripper-component one-hot, and side indicator). The policy outputs per-finger key-point displacements, a global translation/rotation change, and a binary stop signal; a separate lightweight adaptation MLP then converts the key-point displacements into concrete joint-angle changes for the specific gripper.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-stage hierarchical framework that decouples gripper-agnostic grasp planning from gripper-specific control. The unified policy model uses a transformer-based architecture with finger-wise encoders (MLP for semantic key points, local PointNet for IBS groups, plus a global PointNet), followed by N self-attention layers that fuse information across fingers and between the two representations, and finally finger-wise and global aggregation heads that predict per-finger displacements and global motion. The gripper-specific adaptation model is a small MLP trained with a self-supervised cycle loss (MSE between predicted and differentiable-forward-kinematics key-point positions) plus a self-collision penalty, converting key-point displacements into joint changes in ~0.4 ms. Training proceeds in two stages: joint training (SAC for the policy, self-supervised for the adaptation, 800k updates) on a single gripper, then transfer training where the frozen policy is paired with a newly initialized adaptation model (50k updates) for a target gripper. The key insight is that key-point displacements as actions (inspired by IK rigs) neutralize morphological differences, while the IBS as a state representation balances object and gripper geometry to prevent overfitting to either.

## Results

On the YCB + ContactPose object set, the proposed method achieves success rates of 61.8% (Schunk), 63.4% (Mano), and 52.5% (Rutgers), with generalized Q1 values of 0.167, 0.165, and 0.143 respectively. These results substantially exceed all four baseline methods (motion retargeting and policy transfer with joint or keypoint matching), which range from 6.4% to 48.9% success rate. The method also demonstrates strong transfer to the four-fingered Allegro Hand (55.0% SR, Q1 0.133) and the original Shadow Hand (71.3% SR, Q1 0.169) with minimal performance degradation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against four baselines that transfer a Shadow Hand policy to other grippers via motion retargeting (MR) or online policy transfer (PT), each combined with joint matching (JM) or keypoint matching (KM). The proposed method wins decisively on every gripper and metric: on Schunk it achieves 61.8% SR vs. the best baseline PT-JM at 40.1% (+21.7 pp), on Mano 63.4% vs. PT-JM at 48.9% (+14.5 pp), and on Rutgers 52.5% vs. MR-KM at 33.7% (+18.8 pp). Keypoint matching consistently outperforms joint matching in the retargeting setting (MR-KM > MR-JM), confirming that semantic keypoint correspondence is more robust than raw joint-space alignment. However, the expected improvement from offline MR-KM to online PT-KM does not materialize; the authors attribute this to morphological differences causing the target gripper to stick at fixed poses during keypoint optimization. The proposed method avoids this by directly predicting keypoint displacements on the target hand, yielding smoother and more adaptive control.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the IBS-based dexterous grasping framework of She et al. (SIGGRAPH 2022) from single-gripper to cross-gripper policy transfer, repurposing the Interaction Bisector Surface—which was previously leveraged for object-geometry robustness—as a gripper-geometry-agnostic state representation. It departs from prior cross-gripper grasp synthesis work (UniGrasp, AdaGrasp, GenDexGrasp) that predicts static final poses, instead formulating a dynamic reaching-and-grasping policy whose action is key-point displacements rather than joint angles. The hierarchical decomposition into a shared transformer-based policy and a lightweight per-gripper adaptation network draws on motion retargeting concepts (key-point correspondence) and locomotion policy transfer (morphology-agnostic controllers), but applies them to the contact-rich, geometry-sensitive domain of dexterous manipulation where prior locomotion-oriented approaches have not been tested.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first demonstrated transfer of a trained dynamic grasp policy across multiple high-DOF dexterous hands, eliminating the need for per-gripper data collection and training—a significant practical cost reduction for multi-robot deployments. The key-point-displacement action space and IBS state representation offer a clean, reusable formulation that could inform future cross-embodiment manipulation research. However, the method is limited to anthropomorphic hands with similar topology (it cannot transfer to non-anthropomorphic grippers like Robotiq 3F), all experiments are conducted in simulation with standardized physical parameters, and the baselines (motion retargeting, simple policy transfer) are relatively weak compared to other learned cross-embodiment approaches. Open questions include real-world deployment with diverse mass/friction parameters, scaling to more morphologically diverse hands, and whether the IBS representation generalizes to contact-rich tasks beyond grasping.
