## Problem

Entropy coding in distributed DNNs reduces communication overhead by adaptively encoding latent representations into variable-length bit streams, but its resilience to intentional (adversarial) and unintentional (corruption) interference has never been studied. Because the bit rate depends entirely on the estimated entropy of the latent representation, small input perturbations can inflate the encoded data size by up to 95%, saturating transmission bandwidth and degrading service for other users.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240560/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system consumes RGB images (clean, corrupted via ImageNet-C, or adversarially perturbed under l∞ constraints with ε up to 16/255) and produces two outputs: a variable-length encoded bit stream whose size (in KBytes) is the primary compression metric, and a class label from the tail network. The defense stage additionally outputs a denoised image. Unlike standard compression benchmarks that report bits-per-pixel, this work measures end-to-end data size of the full bit stream to directly quantify networking traffic between head and tail.

## Method

The paper first formulates a dedicated threat model (PGD-E) that maximizes the entropy of the latent representation rather than the classification loss, revealing that entropy-targeting and accuracy-targeting attacks exploit disjoint feature subspaces. Through total-variation and bit-rate map visualizations, the authors show the entropy model is disproportionately sensitive to high-frequency components and to perturbations in background (non-object) regions. The proposed defense is an object-aware total variation denoiser that solves a regularized minimization (fidelity + TV penalty) via sub-gradient descent, but applies the smoothing only in background regions by using the entropy model's own output P_Z(z) as a soft spatial mask—higher bit-rate (object) areas are preserved while low-bit-rate (background) areas are denoised. This standalone defense reduces attacked data size by ~9% below clean-image levels with only ~2% accuracy loss and remains effective against adaptive low-frequency and region-targeted attacks.

## Results

The proposed Object-Aware Total Variation Denoising defense reduces the transmission overhead of PGD-E attacked inputs by up to 95.32% (from 18.79 KB to 13.44 KB at ε=16/255) while recovering 7.74% accuracy (52.13% → 59.87%). At smaller perturbation budgets (ε=2/255, 4/255), the defended data size is actually 8.94% and 0.73% smaller than clean ImageNet (9.62 KB), with only 2.44% and 1.18% accuracy loss respectively. Against adaptive attacks (low-frequency and regional), the defense still reduces data size by ~67.7% with only ~1.2–1.4% accuracy degradation. The entropy attack (PGD-E) increases data size by up to 95% compared to clean inputs, while the standard accuracy-targeting attack (PGD-Acc) increases it by only ~7%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares two entropy model baselines: Factorized Prior (FP, Ballé et al. ICLR 2017) and Mean Scale Hyper Prior (MSHP, Minnen et al. NeurIPS 2018). MSHP achieves better clean compression (9.62 KB vs 11.65 KB average) but is significantly more vulnerable to entropy-targeting attacks, with data size increasing by 46.82% under PGD-E (ε=8/255) versus only 21.93% for FP. Conversely, FP suffers worse accuracy degradation under PGD-Acc (72.10% loss vs 57.10% for MSHP), confirming that the two entropy models encode different feature sets. The proposed defense is evaluated as a standalone module applied post-hoc to attacked inputs; it outperforms the no-defense baseline by reducing data size by 28–32% at high ε while simultaneously improving accuracy by 1.6–7.7%. Against both adaptive attacks (low-frequency and regional), the defense maintains its effectiveness, reducing data size by ~67.7% with minimal accuracy cost (~1.2–1.4%), demonstrating that neither attack strategy can bypass the object-aware masking.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the entropic student framework for distributed DNNs (Matsubara et al., 2022), which integrates entropy coding with knowledge distillation to compress latent representations for edge-to-server transmission, and on the entropy model designs of Ballé et al. (2017) and Minnen et al. (2018). It departs from the broader adversarial robustness and DNN efficiency-attack literature (Haque et al., 2020; Pan et al., 2022, 2023) by targeting a distinct vulnerability: the bit-rate channel of the entropy model rather than classification accuracy or compute savings. The defense mechanism repurposes classical total variation denoising (Rudin et al., 1992) with an object-aware soft mask derived from the entropy model's own output, creating a self-referential protection strategy not seen in prior robustness work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic characterization of how entropy models in distributed DNNs respond to both adversarial and natural interference, revealing that the compression channel is a distinct attack surface from the classification channel. The practical implication is significant for edge computing deployments where bandwidth saturation from a single compromised device can degrade service for all users. The defense is standalone and composable with adversarial training, but remains limited to image classification on ImageNet; open questions include whether the frequency-domain vulnerability generalizes to video, detection, or segmentation tasks, and whether more principled defenses (e.g., adversarial training of the entropy model itself) can close the remaining gap without the ~2% accuracy cost.
