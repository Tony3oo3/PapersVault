## Problem

Existing audio-driven 3D facial animation models are constrained by inconsistent annotation conventions across datasets (vertices vs. blendshapes vs. FLAME parameters), forcing each model to train on a single annotation format and limiting training data to under 1 hour. This prevents scaling up to the larger, more diverse datasets needed for robust, generalizable animation, particularly for multilingual speech and songs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241088/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a raw audio waveform (speech or song, multilingual) as input, processed through a TCN to 50 Hz features and a transformer encoder for contextualized representations. It simultaneously outputs multiple 3D facial annotation conventions via separate decoder heads: vertex displacements (up to 23,370×3 coordinates for BIWI), 52-dimensional ARKit blendshape weights, and 413-dimensional FLAME parameters. A frequency adaptor performs linear interpolation to align the 50 Hz audio features to each dataset's native frame rate (25, 30, or 60 fps), and vertex outputs are reconstructed from 512-dimensional PCA coefficients via a fixed projection matrix.

## Method

UniTalker is a multi-head encoder-decoder model where a pre-trained speech encoder (Wav2Vec2-XLSR-53 or WavLM-Base-Plus) feeds a non-autoregressive TCN motion decoder that branches into per-annotation decoder heads. Three training strategies ensure stability across heterogeneous annotations: (1) PCA reduces vertex-based outputs to 512 dimensions, equalizing trainable parameter counts across heads; (2) Decoder Warm-up freezes the audio encoder in a first stage before joint fine-tuning, aligning convergence rates; (3) Pivot Identity Embedding introduces a pseudo-identity label sampled with 10% probability during training (inspired by classifier-free guidance) to prevent dataset-specific bias from leaking into the identity conditioning. The model is trained on A2F-Bench (8 datasets, 934 speakers, 18.53 hours spanning multilingual speech and songs) with a composite MSE loss over reconstructed vertices, PCA values, and parameter weights, and can serve as a foundation model where fine-tuning on unseen annotations requires less than half the data of fine-tuning the audio encoder alone.

## Results

On BIWI, UniTalker-L-[D0-D7] achieves the best lip vertex error (LVE) of 3.86×10⁻⁴, a 9.2% reduction over the prior state-of-the-art SelfTalk (4.25×10⁻⁴), and the best mean vertex error (MVE) of 6.42×10⁻³ and upper-face vertex error (UFVE) of 6.15×10⁻³. On Vocaset, UniTalker-L-[D0-D7] achieves LVE of 8.30×10⁻⁶ m² (13.7% reduction over SelfTalk's 9.63×10⁻⁶ m²) and MVE of 5.52×10⁻³ m. Inference is dramatically faster: 0.054 s for 10 s of audio on a single V100, compared to 0.705 s (FaceFormer), 4.4 s (CodeTalker), and 16.5 s (FaceDiffuser).

## Baselines & Comparisons

UniTalker-L-[D0-D7] outperforms all four baselines on LVE, MVE, and UFVE on both BIWI and Vocaset. The closest competitor is SelfTalk, which UniTalker beats by 9.2% on BIWI LVE (3.86 vs 4.25 ×10⁻⁴) and 13.7% on Vocaset LVE (8.30 vs 9.63 ×10⁻⁶ m²). FaceFormer and CodeTalker, both autoregressive, are clearly outperformed on accuracy (e.g., FaceFormer LVE 4.98 vs 3.86 on BIWI) and are 13× and 81× slower at inference, respectively. FaceDiffuser, despite using a diffusion model, is the slowest (16.5 s) and does not beat SelfTalk on LVE. The one metric where UniTalker loses is FDD (upper-face dynamics deviation): SelfTalk achieves the best FDD on both datasets (3.59×10⁻⁵ on BIWI, 1.05×10⁻⁷ m² on Vocaset), suggesting UniTalker's diverse training data leads to more average upper-face motion statistics. In a user study (868 responses), UniTalker is preferred over FaceFormer, CodeTalker, and SelfTalk on realism (72–75%), lip sync (75–77%), and emotion (78–82%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the audio-driven 3D facial animation line (FaceFormer, CodeTalker, SelfTalk, FaceDiffuser) by introducing a unified multi-head architecture that simultaneously outputs multiple annotation conventions (vertices, ARKit blendshapes, FLAME parameters) from a single model, rather than training separate models per annotation. It departs from prior work's reliance on data preprocessing (retopology or 3DMM fitting) to unify annotations, instead using PCA dimension balancing, decoder warm-up, and Pivot Identity Embedding to handle heterogeneous annotation spaces natively. The foundation-model framing—fine-tuning the full UniTalker rather than just the pre-trained audio encoder—repurposes the transfer-learning paradigm established in NLP/vision for the audio-to-face domain, and the A2F-Bench benchmark scales training data from the typical <1 hour to 18.5 hours with multilingual and multi-vocal-type coverage.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

A2F-Bench (934 speakers, 8,654 sequences, 18.5 hours, multilingual speech + songs) fills a clear gap in the audio-to-face literature, where prior public datasets were English-only and under 1 hour. The finding that fine-tuning the full unified model with half the data surpasses prior SOTA trained on the full dataset suggests a practical path toward data-efficient adaptation to new annotation conventions. However, the 18.5-hour training scale remains tiny compared to the 56k hours used for the audio encoder, and the paper acknowledges that larger but noisier datasets (BEAT, Talkshow) were excluded due to annotation quality—scaling to such data remains an open challenge. Extending the unified framework to 2D facial animation and to truly large-scale, heterogeneous data is identified as future work.
