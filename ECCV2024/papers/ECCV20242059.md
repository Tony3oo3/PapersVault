## Problem

Standard segmentation losses (Dice, Cross-Entropy) optimize volumetric overlap and frequently break the connectivity of thin tubular structures, degrading downstream tasks like flow analysis and navigation. Existing topology-preserving losses such as clDice rely on GPU-based differentiable skeletonization of both prediction and ground truth, incurring >88% additional training time and >52% extra VRAM, which renders them infeasible for large 3D volumes and multi-class segmentation. No prior loss function simultaneously achieves topology preservation, resource efficiency, and multi-class compatibility.

## Input / Output

The method accepts 2D RGB images (roads, retinal fundus, concrete cracks) or 3D medical volumes (CBCT, CTA/MRA) and outputs a pixel-wise segmentation mask. A key preprocessing step converts the ground truth into a 'tubed skeleton' on the CPU (binarize → skeletonize → dilate with a radius-2 diamond kernel → multiply by class labels for multi-class), which is then fed alongside the raw prediction into the loss. This tubed skeleton can be precomputed offline or computed during data loading, so the network itself requires no architectural modification.

## Method

Skeleton Recall Loss replaces the expensive GPU-based differentiable skeletonization (used by clDice on both prediction and ground truth) with a single CPU-precomputed tubed skeleton of the ground truth. During training, a soft recall loss is computed between the network's predicted segmentation and this tubed skeleton, measuring how much of the skeleton the prediction covers, and is added to a standard generic loss (Cross-Entropy + Soft Dice) with a scalar weight w. The tubed skeleton is obtained by skeletonizing the binarized ground truth and dilating it with a radius-2 diamond kernel to create a wider target region, then multiplying by the class labels to assign skeleton segments to their respective classes in multi-class settings. Because no differentiable operation is applied to the prediction, the loss requires only ~8% additional training time and ~2% extra VRAM over the baseline, and scales near-constantly with the number of classes, enabling multi-class 3D segmentation that clDice cannot handle even on an A100 40 GB GPU.

## Results

On the nnUNet backbone, Skeleton Recall Loss achieves the best or near-best Dice and clDice scores across all five datasets. On the 3D TopCoW multi-class task (13 vessel classes), Skeleton Recall Loss reaches Dice 86.59 and clDice 94.35, while clDice Loss is infeasible (out of memory on an A100 40 GB GPU). On the 3D ToothFairy dataset, Skeleton Recall Loss improves Dice from 72.36 (clDice Loss) to 74.42 and clDice from 89.67 to 92.05. In terms of resource efficiency, Skeleton Recall Loss adds only ~8% training time and ~2% VRAM over the plain nnUNet baseline, compared to ~88% and ~52% for clDice Loss.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against clDice Loss [Shit et al., CVPR 2021], Skeleton Recall Loss wins on Dice and clDice across all five datasets (e.g., Dice 74.42 vs 72.36 on ToothFairy; Dice 93.72 vs 93.64 on TopCoW binary) and also achieves lower β₀ and β₁ errors on most datasets. Critically, on the 13-class TopCoW multi-class task, clDice Loss is infeasible (exceeds 40 GB VRAM on an A100), while Skeleton Recall Loss completes training with only ~2% additional VRAM. Against Topo-clDice Loss [Menten et al., ICCV 2023], which uses a topologically-correct differentiable skeleton, Skeleton Recall Loss again wins on Dice and clDice on all three 2D datasets (e.g., Dice 79.25 vs 78.94 on Roads; clDice 80.83 vs 80.19 on DRIVE) and on the 3D datasets. The only marginal losses are on β₀ error for Cracks (0.148 vs 0.142 for clDice Loss) and β₀ error for TopCoW multi-class (0.151 vs 0.137 for plain nnUNet), but these are offset by superior Dice and clDice scores. Overall, Skeleton Recall Loss dominates both baselines on the primary segmentation and connectivity metrics while reducing training overhead by more than 90%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of differentiable skeleton-based losses (clDice, Topo-clDice) for topology-preserving thin structure segmentation by eliminating the need for GPU-based skeletonization entirely. Instead of computing a differentiable skeleton on both prediction and ground truth, it precomputes a 'tubed skeleton' from the ground truth using inexpensive CPU operations (skeletonize + dilate via scikit-image) and applies a simple soft recall loss against the network prediction. Conceptually, this repurposes the skeleton from a differentiable intermediate in a bidirectional overlap calculation to a fixed target for a one-sided recall term, trading the bidirectional connectivity enforcement of clDice for a unidirectional 'don't miss the centerline' signal. The approach is positioned as a plug-and-play drop-in for any segmentation architecture (nnUNet, HRNet) and any number of classes, in contrast to prior methods that are architecture-agnostic but computationally intractable beyond binary 2D or small 3D volumes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: by reducing additional training overhead from ~88% time / ~52% VRAM (clDice) to ~8% time / ~2% VRAM, the paper makes topology-aware multi-class 3D segmentation (e.g., 13-vessel-class TopCoW) feasible on a single A100 40GB GPU where clDice simply runs out of memory. This opens the door to fine-grained anatomical vessel segmentation that was previously computationally infeasible. Limitations include the fixed tubed-skeleton radius (2 pixels), the one-sided nature of the recall (no penalty for spurious branches in the prediction that don't overlap the GT skeleton), and the reliance on a single weight parameter w whose sensitivity is only briefly explored. Open questions include whether the approach extends to open-set or unlabeled skeleton regions, whether adaptive tubed-skeleton radii could improve performance on structures of varying thickness, and whether the recall-only formulation can be augmented with a precision term without reintroducing differentiable skeleton computation.
