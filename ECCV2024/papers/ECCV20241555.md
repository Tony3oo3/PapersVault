## Problem

While aliasing during downsampling has been well-studied for classifier robustness, the impact of spectral artifacts introduced during upsampling on the stability of pixel-wise predictions (restoration, segmentation, disparity) remains unexplored. The paper highlights that upsampling challenges fundamentally differ from downsampling: the decoder must restore high frequencies that cannot be encoded at lower resolutions, so standard anti-aliasing prescriptions are necessary but insufficient. The central question is what architectural property of the upsampling operation enables stable, artifact-free high-resolution outputs.

## Input / Output

The method is a drop-in decoder modification applicable to multiple pixel-wise tasks: image restoration (degraded RGB image → restored image), semantic segmentation (RGB image → per-pixel class mask), and disparity estimation (stereo image pair → disparity map). The encoder is kept fixed in all experiments; only the transposed convolution kernels in the decoder's upsampling steps are changed. The approach is architecture-agnostic, demonstrated on both CNN-based (NAFNet, UNet+ConvNeXt) and Vision Transformer-based (Restormer, STTR-light) backbones, with no change to input resolution or output format.

## Method

The core contribution is Large Context Transposed Convolutions (LCTC): replacing the standard 2×2 or 3×3 transposed convolution kernels used for upsampling in decoder networks with larger 7×7 or 11×11 kernels, optionally paired with a parallel 3×3 kernel to retain local detail. The theoretical motivation derives from sampling theory: optimal interpolation approximates a sinc function whose spatial support is unbounded, so larger learnable kernels better approximate the ideal anti-aliasing interpolation and reduce the high-frequency replicas (aliases) that manifest as grid and ringing artifacts. Ablations confirm that the benefit is specific to the upsampling operation itself—increasing kernel size in the non-upsampling decoder convolutions (e.g., ConvNeXt-style blocks) does not improve stability and can even degrade performance, validating that spatial context must be available at the resolution-change step. The method is validated under adversarial attacks (PGD, CosPGD, FGSM, SegPGD) across three tasks, showing consistent robustness gains without requiring adversarial training, though it complements such training when applied.

## Results

On GoPro image restoration, LCTC (11×11+3×3) achieves PSNR 30.77 / SSIM 0.9392 on clean NAFNet images (vs. 32.87 / 0.9606 for Pixel Shuffle), but under 10-step PGD (ε≈8/255) it maintains PSNR 11.40 versus only 8.66 for Pixel Shuffle — a 31% relative improvement in robustness. On PASCAL VOC 2012 semantic segmentation, LCTC (11×11+3×3) improves clean mIoU from 78.34 to 79.33 and SegPGD-20-step mIoU from 5.54 to 7.08 over the 2×2 transposed convolution baseline. On FlyingThings3D disparity estimation, LCTC (7×7+3×3) reduces end-point error from 0.4927 to 0.4788 and 3px error from 1.54% to 1.50% compared to the 3×3 STTR-light baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against NAFNet [16] (Pixel Shuffle), LCTC 11×11+3×3 sacrifices ~2.1 dB clean PSNR (30.77 vs. 32.87) but gains +2.74 dB under 10-step PGD (11.40 vs. 8.66), a 31% relative robustness improvement. Against Restormer [89] (Pixel Shuffle), the clean trade-off is larger (29.44 vs. 31.99 PSNR) but the adversarial gain is even more pronounced: +3.80 dB at 10-step PGD (12.84 vs. 9.04). Against the STTR-light [50] 3×3 transposed convolution baseline on disparity estimation, LCTC 7×7+3×3 wins on both clean (epe 0.4788 vs. 0.4927) and attacked (epe 4.02 vs. 4.05) settings, confirming the benefit is task-agnostic. Against Pixel Shuffle [77] in the UNet segmentation setting (Table 5), LCTC 11×11+3×3 strictly outperforms on both clean mIoU (79.33 vs. 78.54) and SegPGD-20-step mIoU (7.08 vs. 6.69), demonstrating that the advantage is not limited to restoration. The small-kernel transposed convolution (3×3) consistently underperforms both Pixel Shuffle and LCTC, validating that the benefit requires sufficient spatial context rather than merely learnable upsampling weights.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the large-kernel idea from the encoding literature (ConvNeXt, Ding et al., Liu et al.) to the decoding side, but with a critical conceptual departure: prior anti-aliasing work (Grabinski et al., Zhang 2019) addressed spectral artifacts during downsampling in classifiers, while prior upsampling-artifact work (Durall et al., checkerboard artifacts) focused on generative models. Here, the authors are the first to systematically analyze aliasing during upsampling for pixel-wise prediction tasks and to show that the large kernel must be in the transposed convolution itself, not in subsequent decoder blocks. The method is a minimal architectural modification to existing SotA models (NAFNet, Restormer, STTR-light, UNet) rather than a new architecture from scratch.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that increasing decoder building-block kernels actively degrades performance (H2) is important because it contradicts the encoding-side intuition that more context is always beneficial, and it isolates the upsampling step as the critical locus for spectral artifact mitigation. Practically, LCTC offers a drop-in replacement for Pixel Shuffle or small transposed convolutions with consistent robustness gains across restoration, segmentation, and disparity estimation. Open questions include whether jointly optimizing encoder and decoder sampling (e.g., replacing pixel unshuffle in the encoder) eliminates the small clean-performance trade-off observed, whether other factors like spatial bias contribute to residual artifacts, and whether the kernel-size saturation observed around 11×11 reflects a fundamental limit or a training-difficulty artifact.
