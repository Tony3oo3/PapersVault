## Problem

Sign language video retrieval suffers from inherent uncertainty: the same gesture can convey different meanings across contexts (polysemy), and a single word may map to multiple sign expressions. Previous methods like CiCo enforce one-to-one point mappings between video frames and text words, which cannot capture this one-to-many semantic relationship, especially given the scarcity of fine-grained frame-word annotations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241179/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a sign language video (up to 64 frames) and a text query (up to 32 words) as input. The video is processed by a weighted combination of a domain-agnostic I3D encoder (pre-trained on BSL-1K) and a domain-aware fine-tuned encoder, followed by CLIP's image encoder; the text is encoded by CLIP's text encoder. Output is a ranked list for both text-to-video and video-to-text retrieval, scored by a combined similarity that blends token-wise max alignment with an optimal-transport distribution distance.

## Method

UPRet models both video and text features as multivariate Gaussian distributions by splitting each feature into mean and variance branches, each refined through multi-head attention and an MLP. K samples (default K=2) are drawn from each distribution via the reparameterization trick and pooled to fixed-length vectors. The core novelty is formulating cross-modal retrieval as an optimal transport problem: the token-wise alignment matrix serves as the cost matrix, and the Sinkhorn-Knopp algorithm computes the minimum-cost transport plan between the two sampled distributions. The final similarity combines token-wise max alignment with the OT-based distribution distance (weighted by λ_ot), and training uses a standard cross-modal contrastive loss over this composite score.

## Results

On the most challenging How2Sign benchmark, UPRet achieves 59.1% R@1 for text-to-video (T2V) and 53.4% R@1 for video-to-text (V2T) retrieval, improving over the re-implemented CiCo baseline by 2.7% and 3.1% respectively. On PHOENIX-2014T, UPRet reaches 72.0% R@1 in both directions, and on CSL-Daily it attains 78.4% R@1 (T2V) and 77.0% R@1 (V2T). The method also reduces mean rank (MnR) consistently, e.g., from 58.8 to 54.4 on How2Sign T2V, indicating more reliable ranking of relevant results.

## Baselines & Comparisons

UPRet outperforms all baselines across all three datasets and both retrieval directions. Against the strongest prior method CiCo (re-implemented as CiCo*), UPRet gains +2.7% R@1 on How2Sign T2V (59.1 vs. 56.4) and +3.1% on V2T (53.4 vs. 50.3), with a notable MnR reduction from 58.8 to 54.4. On PHOENIX-2014T, UPRet improves R@1 by 1.6% (T2V: 72.0 vs. 70.4) and 1.1% (V2T: 72.0 vs. 70.9) over CiCo*, while also reducing MnR from 4.8 to 4.4. On CSL-Daily, the gains are +2.1% R@1 (T2V: 78.4 vs. 76.3) and +3.1% (V2T: 77.0 vs. 73.9). The earlier SPOT-ALGN variants (SA-SR, SA-CM, SA-COMB) are substantially weaker, with R@1 scores 22–25 points below UPRet on How2Sign, confirming that the distributional modeling and optimal transport components provide meaningful improvements over point-based contrastive alignment.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the sign language video retrieval line established by SPOT-ALGN and CiCo, which frame the task as cross-modal contrastive learning with one-to-one point representations. The key departure is replacing point embeddings with multivariate Gaussian distributions (following the probabilistic embedding tradition of PCME and HBI) and introducing Sinkhorn-based Optimal Transport as the cross-modal distance metric, rather than relying on contrastive loss for distribution alignment. The sign encoder architecture (I3D pre-trained on BSL-1K combined with CLIP image encoder) is inherited directly from CiCo, so the novelty is concentrated in the distribution modeling and OT-based matching stages.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The work addresses a genuine gap in sign language retrieval: the polysemic and context-dependent nature of sign language means a single gesture can map to multiple textual interpretations, which point-based contrastive learning fundamentally cannot capture. The OT formulation provides a principled, differentiable mechanism for one-to-many alignment that could transfer to other retrieval tasks with inherent ambiguity. However, the paper acknowledges limitations in cross-lingual sign language retrieval and dependence on preprocessed features, leaving open whether the distributional approach generalizes across sign languages and cultures. The modest but consistent gains across three benchmarks suggest the method is sound but not yet transformative for the field.
