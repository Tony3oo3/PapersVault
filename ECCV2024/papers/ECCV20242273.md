## Problem

Unsupervised domain adaptation methods are typically trained and evaluated under inconsistent, method-specific hyperparameters (learning rates, batch sizes, augmentations, evaluation protocols), making cross-method comparisons unreliable and obscuring the true impact of adaptation algorithms. Beyond this evaluation gap, three fundamental factors—backbone architecture choice, the quantity of unlabeled target data actually exploited, and the nature of pre-training data—have been largely treated as fixed defaults (ResNet-50, full target set, ImageNet pre-training) without systematic empirical investigation.

## Input / Output

Each UDA method under study consumes labeled RGB images from a source domain and unlabeled RGB images from a target domain, producing a single class label per target image. The framework enforces a 90/10 train/test split on target data (unlike prior work that reuses the same images for both training and evaluation) to prevent overfitting to unlabeled target samples. Experiments span five benchmark datasets (DomainNet, OfficeHome, VisDA, CUB200, GeoPlaces) with domain pairs covering object, bird, and scene transfer, and the unlabeled-data study subsamples target images at 1%, 5%, 10%, 25%, 50%, and 100% fractions using stratified, random, or split-class sampling.

## Method

UDA-Bench is a PyTorch framework that re-implements eight representative UDA methods (DANN, CDAN, MDD, MCC, DALN, MemSAC, AdaMatch, ToAlign, PMTrans) under a unified training protocol, standardizing adaptation-independent factors such as optimizer, initialization, batch size, and evaluation while preserving each method's original loss coefficients and data loaders. The empirical study then varies three axes independently: (i) backbone architecture (ResNet-50, Swin-V2-tiny, ConvNext-tiny, DeiT-III-small, ResMLP-small), (ii) fraction of unlabeled target data (1–100%), and (iii) pre-training data and paradigm (supervised or self-supervised via SwAV, MoCo-V3, MAE on ImageNet-1M, Places-205-1M, or iNaturalist-1M). Key findings include that advanced ViT backbones reduce UDA gains by 50–70% relative to ResNet-50, that accuracy saturates within 25% of target data (contradicting Ben-David et al.'s theoretical scaling), and that in-task pre-training (e.g., iNaturalist for CUB200, Places for GeoPlaces) yields 7–17% absolute accuracy improvements over the default ImageNet pre-training across both supervised and self-supervised settings.

## Results

On DomainNet (Real→Clipart), Swin-V2-t achieves the best source-only target accuracy of 55.51% with a relative domain drop of 35.45%, compared to 44.85% and 45.21% for ResNet-50. Reducing unlabeled target data to 25% of the full set causes less than 1% accuracy drop across all UDA methods, indicating rapid saturation. In-task supervised pre-training (e.g., iNaturalist for CUB200) improves plain transfer accuracy by 8.5% (50.2%→58.7%) and boosts MemSAC adaptation by 17.1% over the standard ImageNet-pretrained baseline. UDA gains over source-only baselines shrink from ~20% relative improvement with ResNet-50 to ~7% with Swin on DomainNet R→C.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Under the standardized UDA-Bench framework, the relative ranking of UDA methods shifts dramatically with backbone choice: the SOTA method PMTrans [115] performs worse than the older DALN [14] on CUB200 and worse than CDAN [54] on DomainNet when all use the same DeiT backbone. CDAN [54], a simpler adversarial method from 2018, achieves the best accuracy with Swin, ConvNext, and DeiT backbones on DomainNet R→C, while MCC [38] outperforms all other methods with a ResMLP backbone. DANN [25] and MDD [111] show consistent but modest gains across backbones, with MDD benefiting from its non-adversarial formulation. MemSAC [41] and AdaMatch [7] (consistency-based) and ToAlign [103] (alignment-based) all exhibit the same pattern: their advantage over source-only baselines shrinks by 50–65% when moving from ResNet-50 to Swin-V2-t. The key takeaway is that no single UDA method dominates across all backbones and datasets, and the choice of backbone architecture has a larger impact on target accuracy than the choice of adaptation algorithm.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant UDA literature, which has focused almost exclusively on proposing new adaptation algorithms or loss functions, by instead conducting a controlled empirical study of method-agnostic factors (backbone architecture, unlabeled data volume, pre-training data) that have been confounded by inconsistent training protocols across prior codebases. It is closest in spirit to Kim et al. [44] (ECCV 2022) on pre-training for domain adaptation and Musgrave et al. [61,62] on fair UDA validation, but distinguishes itself by building UDA-Bench—a unified PyTorch framework that standardizes adaptation-independent hyperparameters—enabling the first systematic comparison of eight UDA methods across three axes of variation simultaneously. The theoretical grounding from Ben-David et al. [5] on domain adaptation provides the baseline against which the empirical findings are measured.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that UDA methods saturate with as little as 25% of unlabeled target data (only ~1% accuracy loss from 75% reduction) directly contradicts the inverse-relationship theory of Ben-David et al. that underpins the field, suggesting current adaptation objectives are fundamentally limited in their data utilization. Practically, the observation that a Swin-V2 backbone with source-only training already matches or exceeds SOTA UDA methods using ResNet-50 reframes where practitioners should invest effort—better backbones may be more impactful than better adaptation algorithms. Limitations include restriction to image classification (not segmentation), standard UDA setting only (no source-free, semi-supervised, or universal DA), and use of default hyperparameters rather than per-setting tuning. Open questions include whether redesigned adaptation objectives can actually exploit the abundant unlabeled data, and whether these findings generalize to other modalities and task formulations.
