## Problem

State-of-the-art text-to-image diffusion models (SDXL, DALL-E3, DeepFloyd-IF) achieve less than 25% word-level precision on visual text rendering, with prior specialized methods limited to single words or lines under ~20 characters. The paper identifies the root cause as text encoder deficiencies: CLIP encoders capture image concepts but not glyph-level detail, while T5/ByT5 encoders understand language but lack alignment with visual text signals. This work targets the ambitious goal of rendering text paragraphs of 100+ characters with near-90% accuracy, including automated multi-line layout planning.

## Input / Output

The model takes structured text prompts containing font specifications (e.g., '[font-color-127]', '[font-type-234]') alongside the target text content, where special tokens are replaced with global embeddings from an enriched codebook before encoding. Text boxes can be user-specified or automatically predicted via GPT-4, and the model handles paragraphs of tens to hundreds of characters with automatic multi-line layout planning within boxes of arbitrary size and aspect ratio. Outputs are full design images (text-rich posters, cards) or photorealistic scene-text images (road signs, billboards, T-shirts) at SDXL resolution.

## Method

Glyph-ByT5 is trained in a LiT-style framework where only the ByT5 text encoder is fine-tuned against a frozen DINOv2 vision encoder, using a scalable synthetic glyph-text dataset (~1M pairs) generated via graphic rendering. Training employs a novel box-level contrastive loss that treats each text box and its sub-text as an instance (rather than the whole image), combined with a hard-negative contrastive loss built from character/word-level glyph augmentation (replacement, repeat, drop, add). Glyph-ByT5 is then integrated into SDXL via a region-wise multi-head cross-attention mechanism: pixel embeddings inside target text boxes attend exclusively to Glyph-ByT5 glyph embeddings, while pixels outside attend to the original CLIP encoder embeddings, bridged by a lightweight 4-layer ByT5-to-SDXL mapper. The UNet is adapted with LoRA while all other SDXL weights remain frozen. A final design-to-scene alignment stage fine-tunes on a hybrid 12K-image dataset (scene text, synthetic, and design images) to extend capabilities to photorealistic scene-text generation.

## Results

On the VISUALPARAGRAPHY benchmark, Glyph-ByT5-S (292M params) achieves 92.58% word-level precision on ≤20-character prompts, 90.38% on 20–50 characters, 87.16% on 50–100 characters, and 83.17% on ≥100 characters, compared to 21.72% for the SDXL baseline. Scaling to 1M training pairs further improves these to 93.89%, 93.67%, 91.45%, and 89.17% respectively. On scene-text benchmarks, Glyph-SDXL achieves 93.56% Recall on SimpleBench and 92.00% on CreativeBench, while Glyph-SDXL-Scene reaches 95.88% Case-Recall on SimpleBench and 86.4 F-measure on MARIO-Eval.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Glyph-ByT5-S dramatically outperforms the SDXL baseline (21.72% → 92.58% on ≤20 chars) and the T5-L variant (48.46% → 92.58%), showing that glyph-alignment pre-training is the critical factor beyond merely using a larger or character-aware encoder. Against DeepFloyd-IF (4.3B params, T5-XXL) and DALL-E3, Glyph-ByT5-S achieves roughly 70–75 percentage-point gains across all character-length ranges, despite using only 292M encoder parameters. On scene-text benchmarks, Glyph-SDXL-Scene surpasses GlyphControl by ~51 points on SimpleBench Recall (92.69 vs. 42) and ~61 points on CreativeBench Recall (88.81 vs. 28). It also outperforms TextDiffuser (78.2 F-measure) and TextDiffuser-2 (75.1 F-measure) on MARIO-Eval by 8.2 and 11.3 points respectively (Glyph-SDXL: 90.4 F-measure). In a user study against DALL-E3, Glyph-SDXL is significantly preferred for typography accuracy but rated comparable or slightly lower on visual aesthetics and layout quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the character-aware ByT5 encoder and the LiT framework (freezing the vision encoder while fine-tuning the text encoder), extending them with a glyph-alignment pre-training objective using a box-level contrastive loss rather than the standard image-level CLIP loss. It departs from prior visual text rendering work (TextDiffuser, GlyphControl, GlyphDraw) that was limited to single words or short lines under ~20 characters, by targeting paragraph-level text of 100+ characters with automated multi-line layout planning. The integration into SDXL via a region-wise multi-head cross-attention mechanism distinguishes it from approaches that replace the original text encoder entirely, instead fusing glyph-specific and global semantic information spatially.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The jump from <20% to ~90% word-level precision, combined with the new paragraph-level rendering capability, makes accurate visual text generation practically viable for design and scene-text applications that were previously infeasible. The generalizable strategy—identifying a text encoder deficiency, constructing scalable synthetic paired data, and fine-tuning with a targeted loss—offers a template for addressing other fundamental limitations (spatial awareness, numeracy) in diffusion models. Open questions include whether the approach scales to multilingual settings, whether the 'language drift' phenomenon can be fully eliminated, and whether the 1M-pair training scale is sufficient or whether the authors' stated 100M target is necessary for robust open-domain performance.
