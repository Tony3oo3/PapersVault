## Problem

Conventional illuminant estimation in camera ISP pipelines relies on a single frame, even though HDR imaging pipelines inherently capture multiple frames of the same scene at different exposure times. This paper asks whether the color differences between long- and short-exposure frames—arising from the non-linear camera response function interacting with scene irradiance—can serve as a compact, illuminant-correlated signal for white balance, and whether lightweight models exploiting this signal can match or exceed methods requiring hundreds of thousands to millions of parameters.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pair of aligned raw images of the same scene: a long-exposure frame (I_l) and a short-exposure frame (I_s), captured at exposure times scaled by a factor e (e.g., ×e and 1/e relative to auto-exposure). From this pair, a 15-dimensional Dual-Exposure Feature (DEF) vector is computed, comprising a 3×3 color mapping matrix between rgb-chromaticity values and the upper-triangular entries of a 3×3 covariance matrix of the per-pixel ratio image. EMLP takes only this 15-dim DEF as input; ECCC additionally ingests 64×64 uv-chromaticity histograms from both I_l and I_s. All methods are evaluated at 384×256 resolution, and the output is a 3-element estimated illuminant RGB vector used for diagonal white-balance scaling.

## Method

The core contribution is the Dual-Exposure Feature (DEF), a 15-dimensional vector inspired by chromagenic color constancy theory that captures the illuminant-correlated color distortion differences between long- and short-exposure images. DEF is computed as the 3×3 least-squares color mapping matrix (C_c) between rgb-chromaticity values of I_s and I_l, concatenated with the upper-triangular entries of the 3×3 covariance matrix (C_v) of the per-pixel ratio I_s/(I_l+ε). Two lightweight estimators exploit DEF: (1) EMLP, a 354-parameter MLP (input fc→LReLU→two hidden fc layers→output fc) that maps DEF directly to the illuminant vector, and (2) ECCC, a 6,156-parameter modification of Convolutional Color Constancy where DEF is fed through a small MLP to produce blending weights that linearly interpolate over a bank of n downsampled (1/4 histogram size) learnable bias maps, which are then upsampled and added to the FFT-accelerated convolution of learned filters over the uv histograms of both exposures. Training uses angular error loss with Adam; ECCC additionally applies smoothness regularization on filters and biases, and biases are initialized from morphologically dilated histograms of ground-truth illuminants clustered by DEF via K-means.

## Results

On the test set, the proposed ECCC (with augmentation) achieves a mean angular error of 2.95° and median of 2.09°, while the ECCC + EMLP ensemble reaches 2.91° mean and 2.03° median. The lightweight EMLP (354 parameters) alone achieves 3.37° mean angular error, competitive with C5 (3.45°) and C5 model C/D (3.31°), despite using orders of magnitude fewer parameters (354 vs. 100K–32M). The best ECCC configuration reduces maximum angular error to 8.14° (vs. 14.78° for TLCC and 23.34° for PCC), and the ensemble achieves the lowest worst-5% error of 6.11°.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed ECCC (w/aug) outperforms all single-frame learning-based baselines on mean angular error: it beats TLCC (3.73°→2.95°, −0.78°), C4 (3.79°→2.95°), CWCC (3.79°→2.95°), FFCC (5.18°→2.95°), PCC (4.37°→2.95°), and BoCF (3.91°→2.95°). Against the strongest baseline C5 model C/D (3.31° mean, 2.01° median), ECCC wins on mean (2.95° vs 3.31°) and worst-5% (6.06° vs 7.67°) but slightly trails on median (2.09° vs 2.01°). The ECCC + EMLP ensemble achieves the best overall mean (2.91°) and median (2.03°), surpassing even C5 model C/D on both metrics. The EMLP alone (354 parameters) matches C5 (3.45°) and C5 model C/D (3.31°) on mean error while using 285×–618× fewer parameters. Notably, ECCC reduces the maximum angular error from 14.78° (TLCC) and 23.34° (PCC) to 8.14°, indicating substantially improved robustness on difficult scenes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper draws on the chromagenic color constancy theory (Finlayson et al.) and the two-camera illuminant estimation framework of Abdelhamed et al., but repurposes the core analogy: instead of requiring two physically distinct cameras with different spectral responses, it exploits the long- and short-exposure frames already present in HDR imaging pipelines. Methodologically, ECCC extends the Convolutional Color Constancy (CCC/FFCC) framework by replacing its single learned bias with a DEF-conditioned interpolated bias map, borrowing the dynamic-bias-generation idea from C5's hypernetwork but conditioning on exposure-derived features rather than additional camera images. EMLP departs from the conventional paradigm of feeding raw pixel values to a network, using only a 15-dimensional dual-exposure feature vector as input.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that HDR pipelines already capture the required dual-exposure frames, so the method adds zero extra hardware or capture cost while reducing model size by orders of magnitude (354–6,156 parameters vs. millions), making it attractive for embedded ISP deployment. However, all experiments are conducted on a single camera (Pixel 7 Pro) with a self-collected dataset, leaving cross-camera generalization untested—a critical gap given that prior work (C5, SHEF) specifically addresses this. Open questions include whether the DEF remains informative across different sensor response functions, whether a spatially varying version could enable local white balancing, and whether the chromagenic analogy holds under more extreme exposure ratios or sensor non-linearities.
