## Problem

Existing human preference models for text-to-image synthesis are trained with a naive cross-entropy paradigm that blindly minimizes the score of non-preferred images in every pair, causing overfitting on similar-quality pairs, and that ignores human robustness to small visual perturbations, making the model's choices unstable under minor distortions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240740/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes an RGB image and a text prompt as input and outputs a single scalar preference score per image, which is then used to rank images under the same textual condition. During training, each image is additionally passed through k weak augmentations (random horizontal flip and random resized crop with area ratio in [0.85, 1]) to produce extra views for the anti-interference loss. The model is initialized from CLIP-L or CLIP-H with shallow encoder layers frozen.

## Method

Stable Preference restructures training into two sequential phases. In Step 1, a sigmoid-gated loss (L1) applies the full preference + anti-interference loss only when the model's score difference ΔS is below a threshold b, and rapidly decays to near zero once the correct order is established, preventing over-differentiation of similar-quality pairs. In Step 2, a softmax-weighted loss (L2) reweights each pair in the mini-batch by exp(ΔS_i)/Σexp(ΔS_j), concentrating gradient updates on pairs with pronounced quality differences. The anti-interference loss (L_ai) computes a contrastive objective over k weakly augmented views per image: it minimizes the mean intra-image score spread (dist_intra) while maximizing the inter-image score gap (dist_inter), aligning the model's scoring stability with human insensitivity to small perturbations.

## Results

On the ImageReward test split, Stable Preference (CLIP-H†, initialized from HPS v2 checkpoint) achieves 68.0% selection accuracy, outperforming the previous best HPS v2 (65.7%) by 2.3 points. On the HPD v2 cross-domain test split (all models trained on ImageReward), SP (CLIP-H†) reaches 82.5%, surpassing the single-human-vs-averaged-human baseline of 85.0% by only 2.5 points while exceeding the raw CLIP-H baseline (74.8%) by 7.7 points. The anti-interference loss further improves robustness: under 0.90 center-crop perturbation on HPD v2, SP (CLIP-H) maintains 80.7% accuracy (unchanged from 80.7% original), while HPS v2 drops from 83.3% to 81.7%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Stable Preference (CLIP-H†) achieves 68.0% on ImageReward, beating HPS v2 (65.7%) by 2.3 points, ImageReward (65.1%) by 2.9 points, and PickScore (62.9%) by 5.1 points. Even without the HPS v2 initialization, SP (CLIP-H) at 66.8% still surpasses HPS v2 and ImageReward. In the controlled cross-domain experiment (Table 5, all models trained on ImageReward and tested on HPD v2), SP (CLIP-H†) reaches 82.5%, outperforming raw CLIP-H (74.8%) by 7.7 points and BLIP (74.2%) by 8.3 points, approaching the single-human-vs-averaged-human ceiling of 85.0%. The method also demonstrates superior robustness: under 0.90 center-crop perturbation, SP (CLIP-H) on HPD v2 stays at 80.7% (±0.0), while HPS v2 drops 1.6 points and ImageReward drops 0.2 points. On DrawBench, SP (CLIP-H†) ranks Realistic Vision (27.31) and Deliberate (27.33) at the top, consistent with human preference rankings in HPD v2.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the standard cross-entropy training paradigm used by all prior human preference models (HPS v1, HPS v2, ImageReward, PickScore), which uniformly maximize the preferred image score and minimize the non-preferred score in every pair. It redefines the training process into a two-step procedure—first correcting preference order with a gated loss, then selectively broadening margins only for pairs with pronounced quality differences—drawing conceptually from contrastive learning's inter/intra distance formulation. The anti-interference loss repurposes weak data augmentation (horizontal flip, center crop) in a preference-scoring context, an idea borrowed from contrastive representation learning but applied here to stabilize scalar preference scores rather than embedding geometry.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact lies in providing a more stable and less overfitting-prone training recipe for preference models, along with a ready-to-use benchmark (Table 6) for evaluating text-to-image generators. However, the paper explicitly limits itself to the visual side and leaves open important questions: handling diverse prompt formulations with consistent scoring criteria, designing finer-grained (per-attribute) preference models for interpretability, and integrating multiple preference models to better approximate average human judgment. The modest 1–2 point gains over strong baselines suggest the current paradigm is near a local optimum, and the text-side robustness gap remains unaddressed.
