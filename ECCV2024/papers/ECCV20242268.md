## Problem

Text-to-image diffusion models are vulnerable to backdoor attacks that manipulate outputs via hidden triggers, yet no comprehensive defense exists for T2I models specifically. Prior detection methods like DisDet only work on unconditional diffusion models and fail on text-conditional ones, while the complex T2I architecture (text encoder + UNet) allows attacks to exploit either component, and triggers can be embedded in any token position. The paper addresses the need for a lightweight, unified pipeline that detects, localizes, and mitigates backdoors on T2I diffusion models.

## Input / Output

The method takes a trained T2I diffusion model (Stable Diffusion v1.4) and a set of text prompts as input, extracting cross-attention maps from the UNet across 50 diffusion timesteps for each prompt. It produces three outputs: a binary benign/backdoor classification label per prompt (via FTT or CDA), the specific trigger token position within backdoored prompts (via binary search with CLIP or DINOv2 image similarity), and a detoxified model where the localized trigger no longer activates the backdoor. Detection runs in real-time (9.4 ms for FTT, 11.7 ms for CDA on an RTX 4090), while localization requires iterative image generation and is too slow for real-time use.

## Method

The core insight is the 'Assimilation Phenomenon': backdoor triggers cause the trigger token to suppress and assimilate the cross-attention maps of other tokens, producing consistent structural attention responses absent in benign samples. Two detection methods exploit this: FTT computes the Frobenius norm of the attention map set as a coarse-grained dispersion indicator with a fixed threshold (2.5), while CDA flattens attention maps, applies PCA (k=20), computes the covariance matrix as an SPD point on a Riemannian manifold, maps it to Euclidean space via Log-Euclidean Distance, and classifies with LDA. For localization, a binary-search algorithm recursively halves the prompt and checks which half still generates the target content using image similarity (CLIP at threshold 0.85 achieves 86.4% F1). For mitigation, the paper applies concept editing (Refact and UCE) to align the trigger token's representation with an unconditional prompt, with Refact reducing ASR from 0.97 to 0.01 and improving ASB from 0.52 to 0.85.

## Results

For backdoor detection, CDA achieves an average F1 score of 88.9% across both attack scenarios (Rickrolling and Villan Diffusion), outperforming FTT's 86.5%, with inference times of 11.7 ms and 9.4 ms respectively on an RTX 4090. For trigger localization, the binary-search method achieves an 86.4% F1 score using CLIP with a 0.85 similarity threshold. For mitigation, Refact reduces the Attack Success Rate (ASR) from 0.97 to 0.01 (99% detoxification) and improves Average Similarity to Benign (ASB) from 0.52 to 0.85, while UCE only achieves ASR 0.20 and ASB 0.53.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares two state-of-the-art concept editing methods—Refact [4] and UCE [14]—for backdoor mitigation. Refact dramatically outperforms UCE on both metrics: it achieves an average ASR of 0.01 versus UCE's 0.20, and an ASB of 0.85 versus UCE's 0.53. UCE fails on several triggers (e.g., 'coffee' and 'kitty' retain ASR of 1.00) and, even when it does suppress the attack, it corrupts the generated image quality (ASB barely improves from 0.52 to 0.53). The authors attribute Refact's superiority to the fact that backdoor triggers are often meaningless Unicode tokens that are easier to erase in the text encoder (where Refact operates) than in the cross-attention layers (where UCE operates). For detection, the paper's two proposed methods (FTT and CDA) are compared internally rather than against external baselines, with CDA showing an 8.9% F1 improvement over FTT on the harder Villan Diffusion attack.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the backdoor defense literature from unconditional diffusion models (DisDet) and classification networks to the text-conditional T2I diffusion setting, where prior detection methods fail because attacks do not perturb the noise input. The detection pipeline repurposes covariance discriminative learning on SPD manifolds (Wang et al., CVPR 2012) and Log-Euclidean mapping (Arsigny et al., 2007) to classify sets of cross-attention maps, while the mitigation stage repurposes concept editing tools (Refact, UCE) originally designed for erasing semantic concepts. The key conceptual departure is the 'Assimilation Phenomenon'—the observation that a backdoor trigger token structurally absorbs the cross-attention of surrounding tokens—which provides a detection signal absent from prior work on T2I backdoors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

With tens of millions of users downloading pretrained T2I models from open-source platforms, a lightweight (≈10 ms) detection-and-mitigation pipeline addresses a concrete deployment security gap that prior work has not filled. The finding that the state-of-the-art concept editor UCE fails to mitigate backdoors while the simpler Refact succeeds (99% ASR reduction) reveals that backdoor triggers—often meaningless Unicode tokens—occupy a different representational regime than the semantic concepts these editors were designed for, suggesting that dedicated backdoor-mitigation tools may be needed. Open questions include whether the Assimilation Phenomenon generalizes to future architectures (e.g., DiT-based models), whether detection robustness holds against adaptive or multi-trigger attacks, and whether the binary-search localization scales to longer prompts or multi-word triggers.
