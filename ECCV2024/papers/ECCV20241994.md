## Problem

Prior affective vision research has focused on the direct influence of visual stimuli on emotions, leaving a notable gap in understanding how language grounded in visuals—specifically multi-turn conversations about an image—constructs and conveys emotions. No existing dataset captures emotional reasoning that emerges from a dialog about a hidden visual stimulus, nor does it provide the explanatory language needed to understand why a particular emotion was constructed from the conversation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241994/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system takes a 10-turn QA dialog about a hidden image, two opposing opinion captions (one positive, one negative) that initiate the conversation, and optionally the image itself (as a BLIP text description for text-only models, or as a visual token for multimodal models). Outputs are structured as: (1) a free-form answer for the QA subtask, (2) one of 9 emotion class labels (8 Ekman categories plus 'something else'), and (3) a natural-language explanation of roughly 27–29 words. A key structural constraint is that the Questioner never sees the image during the dialog, so their emotion and explanation must be grounded purely in the conversation, while the Answerer has full visual access.

## Method

The core contribution is the AffectVisDial dataset (50K 10-turn dialogs over 50K WikiArt artworks, 27,180 working hours) and a three-subtask benchmark: dialog-based QA, dialog-based emotion classification, and affective explanation generation. For emotion prediction, a RoBERTa classifier is fine-tuned on the dialog plus the two initiating opinions, achieving 62.2% accuracy. For explanation generation, the authors jointly predict the emotion label and its textual explanation using a 'I feel EMOTION because EXPLANATION' prompt, training BART-Large, T5-Large, and the multimodal NLX-GPT (which encodes the image directly) from both Questioner and Answerer perspectives. They further evaluate zero-shot and instruction-fine-tuned LLMs (LLaMA2-7b, GPT-4, MiniGPT-4-v2) and introduce a G-eval-based reference-free evaluation protocol assessing coherence, groundedness, fluency, and emotional relevance, since standard reference-based metrics (BLEU, BERT-score) poorly capture the subjective, open-ended nature of affective explanations.

## Results

On the Affective Explanation Generation subtask (Questioner), BART-Large with full context (image + emotion labels + opinions + dialog) achieves the best Emo-F1 of 50.43 and BLEU of 0.28, outperforming T5-Large (Emo-F1 42.95) and NLX-GPT (Emo-F1 43.61). For the Dialog-based Question Answering subtask, LTMI-D achieves the highest R@1 of 0.25 and MRR of 5.62, surpassing VisDial-BERT (R@1 0.23, MRR 6.10) and generative LTMI-G (R@1 0.18). The RoBERTa-based emotion classifier reaches 62.2% accuracy on the 9-class emotion prediction task. Fine-tuned MiniGPT-4-v2 achieves Emo-F1 of 46.62, still trailing the dedicated BART baseline by ~4 points, while zero-shot GPT-4 reaches only 29.79 Emo-F1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the three main generative baselines, BART-Large consistently outperforms T5-Large and NLX-GPT on Emo-F1 (50.43 vs. 42.95 and 43.61 with full context), indicating it is the strongest architecture for joint emotion+explanation generation on this task. Dialog (D) is the single most impactful input: removing it drops BART's Emo-F1 from 50.43 to 45.52 and NLX-GPT's from 43.61 to 35.50, while T5-Large drops from 42.95 to 41.91. On the QA subtask, discriminative LTMI-D (R@1 0.25, MRR 5.62) beats generative LTMI-G (R@1 0.18, MRR 24.47) by a wide margin, and both outperform the nearest-neighbor baselines (NN-Q R@1 0.11; NN-QI R@1 0.07). Fine-tuned LLMs (LLaMA2-7b Emo-F1 37.02; MiniGPT-4-v2 Emo-F1 46.62) improve substantially over their zero-shot counterparts (14.45 and 25.28 respectively) but still fall short of the dedicated BART baseline, while zero-shot GPT-4 (Emo-F1 29.79) and MiniGPT-4-v2 (25.28) lag further behind, suggesting task-specific training on affective dialog data remains necessary even for large foundation models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of visual dialog (VisDial, CLEVR Dialog) and affective computing (ArtEmis, Artelingo), but departs from both by introducing emotion as a *constructed* outcome of a multi-turn conversation about a hidden visual stimulus rather than a direct response to the image. Prior visual dialog datasets lack any affective labels or explanations, while prior emotion datasets (ArtEmis, Artelingo) capture single-turn reactions to visible images without the dialog-mediated reasoning process. The paper also draws on Ekman's eight-category emotion taxonomy and the Mephisto crowdsourcing framework, but the specific three-subtask formulation (dialog QA + emotion classification + affective explanation generation) is presented as absent from prior literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 50K-dialog AffectVisDial dataset fills a genuine gap: no prior resource captures how emotions are *constructed* through conversation about a visual stimulus, with both a questioner (no image access) and answerer (image access) providing emotion attributions and explanations. The finding that even GPT-4 in zero-shot (Emo-F1 ≈ 30) substantially underperforms fine-tuned BART (Emo-F1 ≈ 52) suggests that emotional alignment in visually grounded dialog requires task-specific training rather than general language capability, an open question for future LLM alignment work. Key limitations include the subjective nature of emotion labels, a single dialog per image, and the reliance on reference-based metrics (BLEU, BERT-score) that may poorly capture creative or diverse explanations—issues the authors partially address with G-eval but do not fully resolve.
