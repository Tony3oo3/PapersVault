## Problem

Computing accurate gradients at visibility discontinuities (triangle edges, occlusion boundaries, and geometry intersections) in rasterization-based differentiable renderers remains challenging. Existing approaches either modify the forward pass (e.g., analytical antialiasing in nvdiffrast, soft rasterization), are computationally expensive (ray tracing with boundary integrals), or fail entirely at self-intersecting geometry, which is common during inverse rendering when the current estimate is far from the solution.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a standard rasterized image (RGB, depth, normals, or segmentation mask) produced by an unmodified z-buffer rasterizer, along with per-pixel triangle IDs and fragment positions from the forward pass. It outputs per-vertex gradients (and texture gradients) by scattering edge-level gradients to fragments and back-propagating through the barycentric interpolator. Because the forward pass is untouched, the method is directly applicable to discrete outputs like binary segmentation masks or integer depth values where any filtering or smoothing would corrupt the signal.

## Method

EdgeGrad introduces a micro-edge formulation that reinterprets the rasterized image as the output of a continuous process in which all edges are axis-aligned (strictly vertical or horizontal) and positioned exactly between two adjacent pixels, making the antialiasing filter an identity operation. For each neighboring pixel pair separated by an edge, the gradient is computed in closed form as (1/2)(∂L/∂I_A + ∂L/∂I_B)(I_A − I_B) + Ω, where Ω captures smooth-region contributions from automatic differentiation. A lightweight edge classification step (no edge, adjacent, overhanging, or intersecting) determines how the edge gradient scatters to fragments via ∂p/∂r, with overhanging cases assigning the full gradient to the foreground fragment only. For geometry intersections, a 2D derivation in the x-z or y-z plane yields an explicit formula for ∂p/∂r using the normals of the two intersecting primitives, requiring no additional data structures or geometry preprocessing. A theoretical analysis via the 2D divergence theorem confirms that the micro-edge approximation converges to the exact boundary integral as pixel size diminishes.

## Results

On the Blender synthetic dataset (8 scenes), EdgeGrad achieves a mean PSNR of 28.82 dB, SSIM of 0.936, and LPIPS of 0.081, outperforming Nvdiffrast (27.41 dB, 0.919, 0.102) and the continuous-only baseline (20.11 dB, 0.828, 0.220). The largest gains appear on scenes with complex geometry: Ficus improves from 24.96 dB (Nvdiffrast) to 26.56 dB, and Drums from 23.15 dB to 24.01 dB. In runtime, EdgeGrad is significantly faster than both Mitsuba and Nvdiffrast across all tested image sizes and triangle counts, while matching or exceeding Mitsuba's gradient accuracy at large resolutions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Nvdiffrast [20], EdgeGrad consistently wins on all three metrics across all 8 Blender scenes, with the largest PSNR gains on geometry-heavy scenes (Ficus: +1.6 dB, Drums: +0.9 dB, Chair: +3.2 dB). The continuous-only baseline (ignoring discontinuity gradients) is dramatically worse, confirming that edge gradients are essential for mesh reconstruction quality. Compared to the ray-tracing baselines Mitsuba [36] and Redner [21], EdgeGrad achieves comparable gradient accuracy at a fraction of the computational cost, and uniquely matches finite-difference ground truth at geometry intersections where both ray tracers fail. The ablation without intersection handling (EdgeGrad -intersect.) is nearly identical to the full method, suggesting that while intersection handling is theoretically important, its practical impact on final reconstruction quality is marginal in the tested scenes. In the dynamic head application, EdgeGrad produces visibly cleaner inner-mouth geometry (teeth, tongue, lips) compared to Nvdiffrast, which suffers from interpenetration artifacts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the nvdiffrast [20] codebase, replacing its analytical antialiasing module with the proposed EdgeGrad module, and draws mathematical inspiration from Li et al.'s [21] edge-sampling framework for differentiable ray tracing. The key conceptual departure is the micro-edge formulation: rather than modifying the forward pass (as nvdiffrast's antialiasing, Soft Rasterizer's blurring, or Rhodin et al.'s fuzzy edges do), the authors reinterpret the discrete rasterized image as the output of a continuous process whose boundaries happen to align with pixel edges. This reframing eliminates the need for rendering approximations while enabling a local, per-pixel-pair gradient computation that also handles geometry intersections—a case no prior rasterization-based method addresses without expensive preprocessing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ability to compute accurate gradients at self-intersecting geometry without complex intersection detection and face-splitting at every optimization step is a practical capability gap that prior rasterization methods could not fill, and it unlocks applications like fully automatic mouth-interior fitting that previously required volumetric simulation or artist-created priors. The method's preservation of the forward pass (no filtering or smoothing) makes it applicable to discrete outputs like segmentation masks and depth maps where prior soft-rasterization approaches would corrupt the signal. Limitations include inheriting all rasterization constraints (no transparency, no PBR/GI), potential misinterpretation of adjacent primitives in finely tessellated meshes, and modest quantitative gains over nvdiffrast on standard benchmarks, suggesting the primary value is in robustness and applicability rather than peak accuracy.
