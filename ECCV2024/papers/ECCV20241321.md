## Problem

State-of-the-art learned image compression (LIC) methods reduce spatial redundancy through autoencoder networks and entropy coding but do not explicitly remove frequency-domain correlation of latent representations, unlike traditional codecs that rely on DCT or DWT. Prior attempts to embed wavelet-domain processing into LIC (e.g., [3, 19]) performed poorly because they operated the entire CNN in the wavelet domain, disrupting the signal distributions that convolutional layers expect.

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image of arbitrary resolution (pixel values normalized to [-1,1]) and produces an arithmetic-coded bitstream plus a reconstructed image. The entropy coding is structured in two sequential steps: low-frequency DWT subband coefficients are coded first, then the three high-frequency subbands are coded using the decoded LF coefficients as a prior. Training uses 480×480 patches extracted from CLIC, LIU4K, and COCO images resized to 2000×2000, with rotation and scaling augmentation.

## Method

The core contribution is a plug-and-play WeConv module that performs a DWT on the input, applies separate convolutions to the low-frequency and concatenated high-frequency subbands, and then applies an inverse DWT to return to the spatial domain, allowing it to function as a single layer within a standard CNN without disrupting downstream signal statistics. A second contribution, WeChARM, applies DWT to the encoder's latent representations before quantization and entropy coding, splitting the coding into a low-frequency pass followed by a high-frequency pass that conditions on the decoded LF coefficients, with channel-wise autoregressive (ChARM) modeling using 5 slices in each pass. The WeConv module is inserted at selected downsampling layers in both the core and hyperprior autoencoders, while an inverse WeConv (IWeConv) with transposed convolution is used in the decoders. The system is trained end-to-end with a Lagrangian rate-distortion objective using MSE or MS-SSIM distortion, Adam optimizer, and 1.5M iterations. The key insight is that by sandwiching DWT/IDWT around a small convolutional block rather than operating the entire network in the wavelet domain, the module preserves CNN compatibility while explicitly sparsifying the signal in the frequency domain.

## Results

On the Kodak dataset, WeConvene with the 9/7 wavelet achieves a BD-Rate reduction of −9.8% over H.266/VVC, compared to −3.9% for the best prior LIC method (Zhu2022) and −3.1% for Fu2023. The simpler Haar-based WeConvene already reaches −8.2%, and adding only the WeConv module (without WeChARM) to the baseline yields −4.7% with negligible increase in encoding/decoding time (0.110s/0.147s vs. 0.109s/0.142s for baseline). On Tecnick 100, the method achieves −9.46% BD-Rate over VVC, and on CLIC 2021 it reaches −9.20%, consistently outperforming all compared LIC methods by 0.2–0.5 dB in PSNR.

## Baselines & Comparisons

WeConvene (9/7) outperforms every compared LIC method on BD-Rate over VVC on Kodak: it beats Zhu2022 [38] (−3.9%) by 5.9 percentage points, Fu2023 [13] (−3.1%) by 6.7 pp, Xie2021 [36] (−0.8%) by 9.0 pp, and Zou2022 [39] (−2.2%) by 7.6 pp. Methods that are worse than VVC itself—Cheng2020 [9] (+2.6%), He2021 [17] (+8.9%), Qian2022 [32] (+3.2%), and Hu2021 [18] (+11.1%)—are all surpassed by a wide margin. In PSNR terms, WeConvene with Haar exceeds the best of Zhu2022 and Fu2023 by ~0.2 dB at all rates and gains >0.5 dB over VVC, especially at low rates. On Tecnick 100, it outperforms Xie2021 [36] by ~0.2 dB at most rates, and on CLIC 2021 it surpasses Xie2021, Zou2022, and VVC by up to 0.5 dB at high rates. The speed–performance trade-off is favorable: encoding/decoding times (0.386 s / 0.445 s) are roughly 2× those of Zou2022 [39] but far faster than sequential-context methods like Fu2023 [13] (420 s) or Qian2022 [32] (86 s), while model size (113 MB) is only 8–14% larger than Zou2022.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the ChARM channel-wise autoregressive entropy model (Minnen & Singh, ICIP 2020) and its 5-slice configuration from Liu et al. (CVPR 2023), adapting both to operate in the wavelet domain. It departs from prior frequency-domain LIC attempts (Akyazi 2019, Iliopoulou 2023, octave convolutions) that performed entire networks in the wavelet domain and achieved poor results, instead embedding DWT as a local transform within individual CNN layers (WeConv) while preserving spatial-domain signal flow. Conceptually, it bridges the traditional transform-coding paradigm (DCT/DWT → quantization → entropy coding) with the learned autoencoder framework, arguing that explicit frequency-domain decorrelation complements rather than replaces learned spatial redundancy removal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that the simplest Haar transform yields a 4.3-point BD-Rate improvement over VVC with negligible computational overhead challenges the implicit assumption in LIC that learned convolutions have fully subsumed the role of explicit linear transforms. The WeConv module is modular and plug-and-play, potentially applicable to other CV tasks beyond compression, and the framework opens a path for incorporating richer wavelet theory (multi-level transforms, adaptive wavelet selection per layer) into learned coding. Limitations include the sequential two-step entropy coding that increases latency, the CNN-only architecture (no transformer), and the moderate model-size increase when WeChARM is enabled. Open questions include whether multi-level or learned wavelet bases can push gains further, and whether the approach transfers to video compression.
