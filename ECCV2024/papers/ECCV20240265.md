## Problem

Existing animatable 3D reconstruction methods from casual videos (e.g., BANMo) use unstructured Gaussian ellipsoid bones scattered across object surfaces, lacking any hierarchy or correlation between bones with similar motions. This makes manipulation cumbersome (requiring dozens of independent bone adjustments for a single pose), reduces interpretability, and often demands many input videos for plausible results. The paper addresses this by imposing a tree-structured hierarchy on the bones so that motions are decomposed in a coarse-to-fine manner.

## Input / Output

The method takes multiple casually captured monocular RGB videos of a single object, along with preprocessed 2D object masks (PointRend), optical flow (VCN-robust), and dense CSE features extracted per frame. It produces an animatable canonical NeRF (VolSDF-based) coupled with a tree-structured set of Gaussian ellipsoid bones organized across multiple depths, from which a 3D mesh can be extracted via marching cubes. A distinctive property is that users can interactively add or delete child bones at any node of the hierarchy after optimization to refine control over specific body parts.

## Method

The core contribution is a hierarchical neural deformation model where Gaussian ellipsoid bones form a tree: parent bones at depth 1 capture coarse motions over broad regions, and child bones at deeper levels capture finer motions over sub-parts, with each bone's world transformation computed by left-multiplying its ancestors' local transforms. Optimization proceeds in a coarse-to-fine schedule, starting with 5–6 depth-1 bones and progressively appending two child bones per parent at each stage (20k iterations per depth). A bone occupancy function, built from the Mahalanobis distance to each ellipsoid, drives three regularization losses: a bone mask loss that aligns bone interiors with the object's foreground mask, an overlap loss limiting how many bones can occupy a surface point, and a coverage loss ensuring each bone covers a meaningful portion of nearby surface. Deformation is applied via linear blend skinning with weights computed from Mahalanobis distance plus MLP-predicted deltas, and the canonical shape is a VolSDF-based NeRF rendered through differentiable volume rendering.

## Results

On the Eagle dataset, the proposed method achieves CD=4.64 and F2=81.59, outperforming all template-free baselines (BANMo: CD=4.66, F2=81.44) while using only 10 leaf bones versus 25+ for competitors. On the AMA human sequences, the method achieves CD=7.11/F2=65.88 on Swing and CD=6.15/F2=72.07 on Samba, surpassing BANMo (CD=7.33/F2=64.88 and CD=7.22/F2=64.99 respectively) and CAMM (CD=9.02/F2=56.00 and CD=7.50/F2=62.17). For neural rendering, the method achieves the best PSNR across all four test sequences (e.g., 32.63 on Eagle, 31.74 on Samba) and highest SSIM (0.924–0.942), outperforming BANMo by 1.0–1.6 dB in PSNR.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the template-free baselines, the proposed method consistently outperforms both ViSER and BANMo across all datasets and metrics. On Eagle, Ours (CD=4.64, F2=81.59) edges out reproduced BANMo (CD=4.66, F2=81.44) while using only 10 leaf bones versus BANMo's 25. On Samba, the gap widens substantially: Ours achieves CD=6.15/F2=72.07 versus BANMo's CD=7.22/F2=64.99, a 14.7% improvement in F2. Against the skeleton-based CAMM, Ours wins decisively on both human sequences (Swing: F2 65.88 vs 56.00; Samba: F2 72.07 vs 62.17) and is competitive on Eagle (F2 81.59 vs 81.21). Against RAC, the results are mixed: RAC achieves lower CD on Swing (6.10 vs 7.11) and higher F2 (70.33 vs 65.88), but Ours outperforms RAC on Samba (CD 6.15 vs 6.63, F2 72.07 vs 67.71) and does so without any predefined skeleton, whereas RAC requires category-specific skeletal templates.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends BANMo's framework of Gaussian ellipsoid bones with linear blend skinning for animatable 3D reconstruction from casual videos, introducing two key departures: a tree-structured hierarchical bone system that decomposes motions in a coarse-to-fine manner, and a bone occupancy function that regularizes bone placement to align with object geometry. It draws conceptual inspiration from part-based generative methods (e.g., Local Deep Implicit Functions, Neural Parts) for the occupancy-based regularization, repurposing their part-alignment ideas into a reconstruction pipeline. Unlike skeleton-based approaches (CAMM, RAC) that require predefined structural templates, the hierarchy is learned fully unsupervised, while unlike flat-bone methods (BANMo, ViSER), it explicitly encodes parent-child motion correlations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The structured bone hierarchy makes animatable 3D models significantly more accessible to non-expert users by enabling coarse-to-fine manipulation with far fewer control points (10 vs. 25+), and the bone occupancy regularization improves both reconstruction quality and interpretability without requiring category-specific priors. Open questions remain around dynamic discovery of joints and conjunctions (the current hierarchy is fixed after optimization), extension to multi-object scenes, and whether the coarse-to-fine optimization scheme generalizes to more complex or topologically changing deformations. The practical impact is notable for content creation pipelines where rapid, intuitive manipulation of reconstructed models is needed.
