## Problem

Traditional shape recovery methods (photometric stereo, structured light, SfM) rely on visible-surface reflectance and fail for dark, transparent, or translucent objects. While thermal cameras can image such objects, no prior work exploits the shape-dependent heat conduction within an object to recover its 3D geometry from thermal videos. This paper asks whether the intrinsic surface Laplace operator—and hence shape—can be recovered from noisy surface temperature measurements induced by simple uncalibrated light sources.

## Input / Output

The method consumes two 15-second thermal videos (FLIR Boson, 512×640, 6 FPS) captured with uncalibrated incandescent lights placed on opposite sides of the camera, a 2D object mask, and known emissivity and surrounding temperature. It produces a 3D depth map (mesh), the scaled surface Laplace operator Δs, per-vertex absorbed heat flux, a global convection coefficient, and scaled heat capacity. Notably, the shape optimization starts from a flat triangular mesh (no 3D prior), and the Laplacian is recovered up to an unknown scale factor α, so the output mesh is determined up to global scale.

## Method

The core contribution is a surface approximation of the volumetric heat conduction PDE that replaces the 3D tetrahedral Laplacian with a 2D cotangent-weighted surface Laplacian scaled by a uniform thickness δ, validated to <5% error for thicknesses up to 1 cm. The linearized heat equation (radiation term first-order approximated) is cast as a linear system in the unknowns {scaled Laplacian Δs, absorbed heat flux βφq, convection coefficient hc, scaled heat capacity C}, solved jointly over all video frames via gradient descent with a temperature-regularization term to handle NETD noise. Shape is then recovered from Δs by optimizing vertex depths along camera rays, resolving the locally binary convex/concave ambiguity using a hinge loss derived from pixel-wise comparison of normalized absorbed heat flux images from the two opposite-side lights (exploiting the insight that heat flux resembles Lambertian shading). A coarse-to-fine strategy first reconstructs low-frequency shape at downsampled resolution, then refines at full resolution to capture high-frequency details.

## Results

The method achieves an average absolute distance error of less than 1.8 mm compared to dense SFM scans for real-world objects up to 13.5 cm in height, and 1.4 mm for a 3D-printed Stanford Bunny. In volumetric simulations, shape recovery errors range from 0.6 mm (1 cm thickness) to 0.9 mm (20 cm thickness) for the Stanford Bunny, validating the surface approximation. The method successfully reconstructs objects with diverse visible BRDFs (black, textured, transparent, translucent) where standard visible-light 3D scanning methods fail entirely.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not perform a direct quantitative head-to-head comparison with prior shape-from-thermal methods (Nagase et al. [33], Tanaka et al. [49]), instead noting qualitatively that its reconstructions are 'significantly higher quality' than those approaches. The primary quantitative comparison is against Kinect and SFM scans used as ground truth references: the paper's method achieves sub-1.8 mm errors, comparable to or better than SFM's own 0.8–1.7 mm errors on textured objects. Critically, the paper demonstrates that for transparent, translucent, and black-painted objects, both Kinect and SFM 'fail terribly' at shape recovery, while the thermal-based method still produces usable reconstructions. This positions the contribution as enabling shape recovery in regimes where all visible-light baselines are inapplicable, rather than outperforming them on a shared benchmark.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the shape-from-intrinsic-operator line of work (Boscaini et al. [4], Chern et al. [8]) by recovering the surface Laplacian not from clean geometric measurements but from noisy thermal video via the heat conduction PDE. It departs from prior thermal shape-recovery efforts (Nagase et al. [33] using multi-spectral attenuation, Tanaka et al. [49] using steady-state photometric stereo) by exploiting transient heat conduction with uncalibrated light sources in under 15 seconds rather than calibrated distant lighting over minutes. The key conceptual departure from [4] is starting from a flat-plane initialization with noisy intrinsic quantities rather than a good initial shape estimate, and resolving the resulting ambiguities using absorbed heat flux as a shading-like cue from two uncalibrated lights.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work opens heat conduction as a viable shape cue, enabling 3D reconstruction of objects that are transparent or translucent to visible light (where SFM and structured light fail) with 1–2 mm accuracy. Key limitations include the assumption of known emissivity, homogeneous material properties, and static objects, all of which restrict practical deployment. Open questions include whether the surface approximation holds for highly anisotropic or layered materials, whether emissivity can be jointly estimated, and whether the approach extends to dynamic or non-rigid objects. As thermal sensors become cheaper and more sensitive, this physics-based pipeline could complement visible-light methods in robotics, manufacturing inspection, and medical thermography.
