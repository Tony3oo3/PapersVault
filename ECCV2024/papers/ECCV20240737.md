## Problem

Existing long-tailed object detection methods focus almost exclusively on mitigating classification bias (e.g., re-weighting, re-sampling, balanced grouping) while ignoring the regression branch. This paper reveals that a significant regression bias exists: rare classes suffer substantially higher RCNN regression losses than frequent and common classes, directly degrading bounding box quality and detection accuracy for tail categories. The root cause is identified as the use of class-specific regression heads, which are particularly ill-suited for rare classes that have few training samples and large scale shifts between train and validation sets.

## Input / Output

The method operates within standard two-stage detectors (Faster R-CNN, Mask R-CNN, Cascade R-CNN) on RGB images, producing bounding boxes, class labels, and optionally segmentation masks. The key architectural modification is at the RCNN regression head: instead of a single class-specific linear layer per class, the output is a weighted combination of a shared class-agnostic head and the original class-specific head, parameterized by a scalar α. The regression output remains the standard 4-tuple (δx, δy, δw, δh) per class, but is now informed by cross-class object priors learned from the shared branch.

## Method

The paper proposes three remedies for the regression bias, all modifying the RCNN regression head while leaving the classification head unchanged. The primary method (CAB) adds a shared class-agnostic regression head W₀ and combines it with each class-specific head via W'_i = αW₀ + (1−α)Wᵢ, with α=0.5 empirically optimal; this requires no dataset statistics and is trivially implementable. Two alternatives are also explored: clustering heads (sorting classes by instance count or mean box scale, grouping into K clusters that share one head) and merging heads (directly assigning all rare, common, or frequent classes to a single shared head). The core insight is that rare classes benefit from class-agnostic regression (analogous to the RPN, which shows balanced losses across frequency groups) while frequent classes slightly prefer class-specific regression, so a weighted blend optimizes across all three frequency tiers. The method is validated on LVIS, COCO-LT, and COCO with ResNet and Swin-Transformer backbones, achieving state-of-the-art AP and AP^b while also generalizing to stricter metrics (AP_boundary, AP_bbox^fixed) and the mask prediction branch.

## Results

On LVIS1.0 with Mask-RCNN R50-FPN, the proposed method (SeeSaw + CAB) achieves 28.9 AP^b and 27.7 AP for object detection and instance segmentation, respectively, surpassing the previous best (ECM) by 1.2 AP^b. With the stronger Cascade R-CNN Swin-B backbone, the method reaches 44.2 AP^b and 39.9 AP, setting a new state-of-the-art. The improvement is most pronounced for rare classes, where AP^b_r increases by 4.6 points over RFS and 4.3 over EQLv2. The method also generalizes to COCO-LT (up to +2.1 AP^b_1 on the rarest subset) and balanced COCO2017 (+0.5 to +0.7 AP across backbones).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed CAB regression method, combined with SeeSaw loss, consistently outperforms all seven baselines across every backbone and architecture tested. On Mask-RCNN R50-FPN, it beats the strongest competitor ECM [7] by 1.2 AP^b (28.9 vs. 27.7) and 0.5 AP (27.7 vs. 27.2), while surpassing SeeSaw [35] by 1.6 AP^b and ROG [42] by 1.7 AP^b. The margin over weaker baselines is larger: RFS [12] is outperformed by 4.2 AP^b, EQLv2 [33] by 2.9 AP^b, and BSCE [29] by 3.4 AP^b. On the stronger R101-FPN backbone, the advantage over ECM narrows to 1.1 AP^b (30.7 vs. 29.3), and on Cascade R-CNN with Swin-B, it is 0.6 AP^b (44.2 vs. 43.6), suggesting diminishing returns as the backbone capacity increases. The gains are most concentrated in rare-class AP^b_r (e.g., 20.2 vs. 19.6 for ECM on R50-FPN), confirming that rectifying regression bias primarily benefits tail categories.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of long-tailed object detection work (RFS, EQLv2, SeeSaw, ECM, BAGS, ROG), which has focused almost exclusively on the classification branch—adjusting loss functions, re-weighting, or re-sampling to counter class imbalance. The authors identify a previously overlooked issue: the class-specific regression heads in the RCNN head produce significantly higher losses for rare classes, a 'regression bias' that no prior method has addressed. Their remedy draws an analogy to the RPN's class-agnostic regression, which is naturally immune to this bias, and proposes three architectural modifications (adding a shared class-agnostic branch, clustering heads by scale/instance count, or merging heads by frequency group). The paper explicitly distinguishes its contribution from Wang et al. [36], who also experimented with class-agnostic heads but attributed performance gaps to classification rather than regression.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that the regression branch is a meaningful, previously unaddressed bottleneck in long-tailed detection shifts the field's attention beyond loss-function engineering on the classification head. The proposed class-agnostic branch is a simple, plug-and-play modification that consistently improves across four baselines, three datasets, multiple backbones (ResNet, Swin-Transformer), and even the mask branch, making it a low-cost addition to existing pipelines. However, the practical gains are modest (roughly 1–2 AP), and the authors note that improvements diminish with stronger baselines, suggesting a ceiling effect tied to backbone capacity. Open questions include whether the benefit persists with much larger vision models or self-supervised pretraining, and why merging 'common' classes outperforms merging 'rare' classes—a phenomenon the authors attribute to train/validation partition shifts in LVIS.
