## Problem

Pre-trained vision-language models like CLIP provide generic visual understanding but lack the ability to produce application-specific, fine-grained, and structured (tabular) outputs. For museum exhibits, where documentation is naturally organized as attribute-value tables (material, origin, production date, etc.), no existing VLM-based method can predict missing tabular entries from an image while exploiting the contextual dependencies among known attributes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20242041/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a museum exhibit image (typically ~224×224 pixels or slightly larger), a set of query attributes whose values must be predicted, and a set of known attribute-value pairs serving as context. The image is encoded by CLIP's ViT-B-32 image encoder, while all attribute names and values are encoded by CLIP's frozen text encoder. Output is a ranked list of predicted values per query attribute, obtained by computing cosine similarity between the predicted embedding and embeddings of all candidate values (200–10,000 for classifiable attributes), making the task a multi-class multi-label classification for structured attributes and a retrieval task for free-text attributes.

## Method

MUZE builds on CLIP's ViT-B-32 image encoder and frozen text encoder, adding a lightweight parseNet — a 2-layer, 512-wide, 8-head Multi-Head Attention Transformer (matching CLIP's text encoder architecture) — that maps the image embedding to tabular structure. The parseNet input is a concatenated sequence of the image embedding, query attribute embeddings, and context attribute-value embeddings, with [MASK] tokens inserted after each query attribute; predicted value embeddings are read from the corresponding token positions. Training uses a cosine loss between predicted and target value embeddings, jointly optimizing parseNet and the unlocked layers of the image encoder, with a separate model trained per attribute. The key insight is that known attribute-value pairs provide contextual signal that significantly improves prediction of missing values, and the tabular structure (shared attributes across images) is exploited by embedding attributes separately rather than using a joint image-text encoder like BLIP.

## Results

On A-MUZE, MUZE-CFA achieves a Mean Average Precision of 4.87, Mean Average Accuracy of 23.51, and Mean Acc@1 of 22.81, compared to CLIP-FA (the strongest baseline) at 3.20, 15.60, and 14.19 respectively. On B-MUZE, MUZE-CFA reaches Mean Average Accuracy of 30.36, Mean Acc@1 of 30.47, and Hit Rate@5 of 34.63, versus CLIP-FA's 22.66, 20.29, and 28.14. The paper reports that MUZE achieves roughly 1.5–2× better average results than the best fine-tuned CLIP variant and more than 3× better than pretrained CLIP. Gains are largest on classifiable attributes (e.g., categories: 74.95% Acc@1 vs. 40.13% for CLIP-FA; materials: 84.46% vs. 34.94%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares MUZE-CFA against three CLIP-based baselines: pretrained CLIP, CLIP-FC (fine-tuned on full concatenated captions), and CLIP-FA (fine-tuned per-attribute on attribute values). MUZE-CFA consistently outperforms all three baselines across nearly every attribute on both A-MUZE and B-MUZE. Against the strongest baseline CLIP-FA, MUZE-CFA improves Mean Average Accuracy from 15.60 to 23.51 on A-MUZE (+51%) and from 22.66 to 30.36 on B-MUZE (+34%), and Mean Acc@1 from 14.19 to 22.81 on A-MUZE (+61%) and from 20.29 to 30.47 on B-MUZE (+50%). Pretrained CLIP and CLIP-FC perform poorly (Mean Acc@1 below 2 on A-MUZE), confirming that the tabular structure and context integration in parseNet are critical. The few attributes where MUZE does not improve (e.g., artistMakerPerson, historicalContext) show only marginal degradation, indicating the method does not harm performance on inherently harder or more open-ended attributes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on CLIP's separate image and text encoders, deliberately choosing this architecture over joint-embedding VLMs (e.g., BLIP) because the tabular query-answer setting requires attributes to be embedded independently and shared across images. It departs from prior structured-data-generation work (document parsing, chart-to-table) where structured elements correspond to localized text regions, instead formulating prediction of global image properties with joint cross-attribute dependencies. Among cultural heritage datasets (iMet, AQUA, VISCOUNTH), MUZE is the first to combine large-scale tabular structure with VLM-based prediction, and the parseNet is a lightweight 2-layer transformer that repurposes CLIP's text-encoder architecture for sequence-level tabular completion.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 200K+ image-table dataset is the first public resource of its kind for structured understanding of museum exhibits, enabling reproducible research in cultural heritage AI. The finding that a minimal parseNet on frozen CLIP embeddings yields 1.5–2× gains over fine-tuned CLIP suggests the bottleneck for structured prediction is output formatting and context integration rather than representation quality. However, performance on free-text attributes (historicalContext, briefDescription) remains very low, and the method is validated only on a single domain with a small ViT-B-32 backbone; generalizability to other structured-prediction settings and scaling to larger VLMs remain open.
