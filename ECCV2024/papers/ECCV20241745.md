## Problem

Despite face pareidolia being a well-documented human perceptual phenomenon, no large-scale annotated dataset existed to study it computationally, and it was unknown whether modern face detectors exhibit pareidolia or under what image conditions it arises. The paper addresses three gaps: the absence of a dedicated pareidolia dataset, the unexplored behavioral gap between human and machine face detection on pareidolic stimuli, and the lack of a mathematical model predicting when pareidolia is most likely to occur.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241745/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The dataset consists of 5,000 RGB images downscaled to 512×512 with white-space padding, each annotated with pareidolic face bounding boxes plus attributes (perceived emotion, gender, intentionality). The detection models (RetinaFace with MobileNet or ResNet-50 backbones) consume standard RGB images and output face bounding boxes with confidence scores; evaluation uses Average Precision at the same thresholds as the original RetinaFace protocol. The psychophysics and model-validation experiments additionally use synthetic noise images generated by modulating random Fourier coefficients with a Gaussian envelope of variable width.

## Method

The paper introduces the 'Faces in Things' dataset (sourced from LAION-5B via CLIP text retrieval and manually annotated with VIA), then uses it to benchmark RetinaFace and show that fine-tuning on animal faces (AnimalWeb) roughly doubles pareidolic detection AP, closing about half the gap to a pareidolia-trained model. To explain this effect, the authors propose two simple statistical models: a Gaussian process model where each spatial-frequency mode contributes independently to face-likeness, and a Poisson feature-based model where required features (eyes, nose, mouth) must appear in correct spatial regions. Both models predict a 'peak pareidolia' effect—detection probability peaks at medium image complexity and drops for both too-simple and too-complex inputs—which the authors confirm through a human psychophysics experiment (subjects count faces in noise images of varying frequency bandwidth) and by running fine-tuned detectors on the same synthetic stimuli.

## Results

On the Faces in Things dataset, pre-trained RetinaFace (MobileNet) achieves only 7.9% AP for pareidolic face detection, and fine-tuning on WIDER FACE (human faces) as a control raises this to just 9.8%. Fine-tuning on animal faces (AnimalWeb) approximately doubles performance to 16.7% AP, closing roughly half the gap to the pareidolia-trained model (33.9% AP). The combined Animal+Pareidolia fine-tuning (30%/70% split) achieves the best result at 36.4% AP (MobileNet) and 31.7% AP (ResNet50).

**Table 1: Effect of Fine-Tuning on Pareidolic Face Detection (AP on Faces in Things).**

| Fine-tuning Data | MobileNet AP | ResNet50 AP |
|---|---|---|
| None (pre-trained) | 7.9% | 2.8% |
| Human (Control) | 9.8% | 3.6% |
| Animal | 16.7% | 15.4% |
| Pareidolia | 33.9% | 27.1% |
| Animal + Pareidolia | **36.4%** | **31.7%** |

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against other face detector architectures; instead, it uses RetinaFace (MobileNet and ResNet50 backbones) as a fixed model and varies the fine-tuning data to probe what induces pareidolia. The key comparison is between training interventions: adding animal faces to the training stream roughly doubles pareidolic AP (16.7% vs. 9.8% control for MobileNet), while direct pareidolia fine-tuning more than triples it (33.9%). Notably, combining animal and pareidolia data (30%/70%) outperforms pareidolia alone by 2.5 AP points, suggesting the two face categories are complementary. On the ResNet50 backbone, the animal fine-tuning effect is even more dramatic (15.4% vs. 3.6% control), indicating the benefit of animal diversity is architecture-independent. The reciprocal experiment shows that pareidolia fine-tuning also most improves animal face detection, reinforcing the representational overlap between animal and pareidolic faces.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of face detection and the neuroscience of pareidolia, a phenomenon well-studied in psychology but largely overlooked in computer vision. While prior work (Abbas & Chalup, 2019) showed that mid-level CNN features from face detection could generalize to a handful of pareidolic images, and datasets like Totally-Looks-Like explored human-machine perceptual similarity, no large-scale annotated face pareidolia dataset or computational model of the phenomenon existed. The paper repurposes the well-established RetinaFace detector and WIDER FACE/AnimalWeb benchmarks as controlled testbeds, and introduces the first formal mathematical models (Gaussian and Poisson-based) predicting when pareidolia should occur.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Faces in Things dataset (5k annotated images) and the two simple mathematical models provide the first tools for the CV community to systematically study pareidolia, with potential applications in reducing false positives in face detectors, understanding human visual attention, and designing systems that better model human perception. The finding that animal face training bridges roughly half the human-machine pareidolia gap suggests an evolutionary mechanism that could inform more natural face detection architectures. Open questions include whether the effect generalizes beyond RetinaFace, whether the peak-pareidolia prediction holds for more complex natural scenes, and how to induce pareidolia in detectors without explicit pareidolic training data.
