## Problem

Existing super-resolution methods are predominantly designed for visible-light images and fail to account for the unique spectral characteristics of infrared imaging, such as fewer high-frequency components, longer wavelengths, and sensor-induced noise. The forward propagation in standard neural networks further diminishes already-sparse high-frequency details, and infrared optical components focus light less effectively than visible-light counterparts, resulting in suboptimal reconstruction quality when generic SR models are applied to infrared images.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240070/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single low-resolution grayscale infrared image (downsampled via bicubic interpolation at ×2 or ×4) as input and produces a corresponding high-resolution infrared image. During training, the network also consumes paired positive and negative text prompts (e.g., describing sharp vs. blurry infrared characteristics) through a frozen CLIP text encoder to compute a semantic degradation loss. Training uses 64×64 patches with a batch size of 32, and the full model is compact at 6.28M parameters and 52.5 GFLOPS.

## Method

The architecture combines a Restormer-style deep feature extraction backbone (spatial window self-attention + channel-wise self-attention + SFNN) with a novel Contourlet Residual block that decomposes deep features via a Laplacian Pyramid and Directional Filter Bank to explicitly capture multi-scale, multi-directional high-pass subbands characteristic of infrared spectra, then fuses these spectral coefficients back with spatial features through a residual connection. For optimization, the paper introduces a two-stage CLIP-based prompt learning strategy: in stage 1, learnable positive and negative text prompts are fine-tuned (with an unlocked text encoder) to maximize semantic separation between HR and LR images in CLIP latent space; in stage 2, the text encoder is frozen and a degradation loss pushes SR outputs toward positive-prompt semantics while repelling them from negative-prompt semantics. The total training objective combines this degradation loss with standard MSE pixel loss and VGG-based perceptual loss, and the training alternates between prompt refinement and network fine-tuning.

## Results

On the M3FD benchmark at ×2 scale, the proposed method achieves PSNR of 48.581/49.233/49.614 dB and SSIM of 0.9930/0.9932/0.9930 on Set5/Set15/Set20, attaining the best SSIM across all three sets and the best PSNR on Set15 and Set20. At ×4 scale, it reaches 40.779/40.765/41.949 dB PSNR and 0.9626/0.9600/0.9717 SSIM, securing the top PSNR on Set5 and the top SSIM on Set20. The method achieves these results with only 6.28M parameters and 52.5G FLOPs, substantially less than larger competitors like HAT-L or EDT-B†.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against six SOTA transformer-based SR methods (SwinIR, DAT, CAT, ART, HAT, EDT) in their light, standard, and large variants. At ×2 scale, the proposed method clearly outperforms SwinIR by ~1.7 dB PSNR and surpasses all mid-size models (DAT, CAT, ART, HAT, EDT) on SSIM across all three test sets, while matching or exceeding the largest variants (HAT-L, EDT-B†) on PSNR for Set15 and Set20. At ×4 scale, the method achieves the best PSNR on Set5 (40.779 vs. DAT's 40.764) and the best SSIM on Set20 (0.9717), though DAT edges it out by ~0.05 dB PSNR on Set15 and Set20. Notably, the method achieves these results with 6.28M parameters and 52.5G FLOPs, compared to substantially larger footprints for HAT-L and EDT-B†, indicating a favorable accuracy-to-complexity trade-off. On the TNO and RoadScene datasets (qualitative evaluation), the method demonstrates superior preservation of high-frequency infrared details and edge fidelity relative to all baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the transformer-based SR lineage (SwinIR, HAT, Restormer) by retaining spatial window and channel-wise self-attention blocks, but departs from them by inserting a Contourlet transform (Laplacian Pyramid + Directional Filter Bank) as a learnable residual module to explicitly recover multi-scale, multi-directional high-frequency subbands that infrared images inherently lack. It also extends the CLIP-based prompt learning paradigm (previously used in StyleCLIP, DiffusionCLIP, and Prompt-to-Prompt for image editing) into the SR setting for the first time, formulating a two-stage degradation loss that aligns generated images with positive textual descriptors while repelling negative ones. The work is positioned against visible-light SR methods that the authors argue are ill-suited to infrared's longer wavelengths, lower spatial frequency content, and sensor-specific noise characteristics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Infrared SR is practically important for military reconnaissance, remote sensing, and home-care applications, yet has received comparatively little methodological attention relative to visible-light SR; this paper offers a targeted architectural answer (Contourlet residual + CLIP prompts) that achieves competitive SOTA with fewer parameters than the largest baselines. However, the performance margins over strong transformer baselines (HAT-L, EDT-B†, DAT) are thin (≤0.3 dB PSNR), and evaluation is confined to three small infrared datasets (M3FD, TNO, RoadScene) at only ×2 and ×4 scales, leaving open whether the prompt-learning and Contourlet components generalize to other modalities, larger upscaling factors, or real-world sensor degradations beyond bicubic. A key open question is whether the CLIP prompt mechanism provides meaningful gains over simpler perceptual or adversarial losses once dataset size and diversity increase.
