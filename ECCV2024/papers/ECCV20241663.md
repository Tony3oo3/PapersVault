## Problem

Continuous-wave time-of-flight (C-ToF) cameras derive depth by integrating four raw frames captured at different emission phases, but this assumes a static scene. Any motion between captures invalidates the phase-to-depth mapping, producing ghosting and large depth errors on dynamic objects. Existing corrections either discard moving pixels, assume small motion, or rely on 2D optical flow that cannot resolve axial (depth-direction) motion, leaving no principled way to reconstruct a 4D scene that explains the raw C-ToF measurements under asynchronous capture.

## Input / Output

The method takes sequential quartets of raw C-ToF frames (four phase-offset images per integer timestep at 320×240) plus pre-computed RAFT optical flow between consecutive integer timesteps as weak motion supervision. It outputs a 4D neural scene representation—density σ(x,τ), view-dependent amplitude L_a(x,ω,τ), and bidirectional 3D velocity v(x,τ)—from which depth is extracted either by volume-rendering the density field (Eq. 7) or by rendering the raw quartet and applying standard C-ToF phase extraction (Eq. 1), the latter preserving sharper edges.

## Method

F-ToRF replaces direct phase-to-depth computation with a differentiable C-ToF image formation model (Eq. 8–10) that renders complex-valued phasor or real-valued raw-frame intensities from a 4D neural field, so depth is an emergent property rather than a supervised target. The key mechanism is a phase-invariant reprojection loss: because the C-ToF rendering model inherently accounts for illumination phase, the scene at an integer timestep can be reprojected via the learned velocity field to a fractional timestep and rendered to match the corresponding raw frame, automatically accommodating the large intensity differences between phase offsets that would break brightness-constancy assumptions. Training is two-stage: first optimizing under a (incorrect) synchronous-capture assumption to initialize geometry, then switching to the asynchronous reprojection loss plus a small-weight optical flow loss that matches the projected 3D velocity to 2D RAFT flow at integer timesteps. This decouples the underconstrained joint estimation of density and velocity, letting the reprojection constraints refine both while the flow provides only weak directional guidance.

## Results

On seven synthetic scenes, F-ToRF (Ours d_ToF) achieves an average 10.8× reduction in depth MSE on dynamic regions and 14.6× on all regions compared to raw C-ToF depth, outperforming the 2D flow baseline (7.3× / 5.8×) and TöRF (1.8× / 1.8×). The volume-rendered depth (Ours d) achieves 6.6× / 5.8× reduction, slightly lower due to softer density. Gains are largest for axial motions (e.g., Arcing Cube: 6.824 vs. 15.570 MSE for 2D flow on dynamic regions) and diminish for purely axial-only motion (Orthogonal Speed Test) and occluded scenes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

F-ToRF (Ours d_ToF) outperforms the 2D Flowed Raw Frames baseline (RAFT + Softmax Splatting) on average by 10.8× vs. 7.3× dynamic-region MSE reduction, with the largest margins on axial-motion scenes (Arcing Cube: 6.824 vs. 15.570; Axial Speed Test: 1.999 vs. 5.912). However, 2D Flow wins on purely lateral or axial-only scenes (Sliding Cube, 3 Chairs Speed Test, Occluded Cube, Orthogonal Speed Test), where the 3D velocity field adds little over 2D warping. TöRF, which assumes synchronous capture, is consistently the weakest baseline (1.8× reduction), confirming that ignoring asynchronous raw-frame timing is a major source of error. The volume-rendered depth (Ours d) trails Ours d_ToF on dynamic regions (6.6× vs. 10.8×) because density is only indirectly optimized, producing softer depth edges; the paper notes TöRF actually beats Ours d on low-disparity scenes (Sliding Cube, Occluded Cube), likely because the shared MLP must also predict the velocity field. On real-world sequences (Pillow, Baseball, Fan, Target, StudyBook), F-ToRF qualitatively resolves ghosting and false texture artifacts that persist in both baselines, though the fast-rotating Fan scene violates the linear-motion assumption and produces incorrect depth within the swept blade volume.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends TöRF [Attal et al., NeurIPS 2021], which first modeled C-ToF image formation via a neural radiance field but incorrectly assumed the four raw frames are captured synchronously. The key departure is introducing asynchronous capture through a 4D scene representation with forward/backward velocity fields (borrowed from Neural Scene Flow Fields [Li et al., CVPR 2021] and HyperReel [Attal et al., CVPR 2023]), supervised by a novel phase-invariant reprojection loss that exploits the physically-based model's natural handling of sinusoidal phase. This contrasts with prior motion-correction approaches (Lindner & Kolb, Schmidt, Hussmann) that rely on 2D optical flow or temporal derivatives and cannot resolve axial motion, and with the 2D Flowed Raw Frames baseline that warps frames via Softmax Splatting before integration.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

C-ToF sensors are ubiquitous in consumer devices, and motion artifacts remain a fundamental limitation that degrades depth in any dynamic scene; this work provides a principled 4D reconstruction framework that generalizes beyond C-ToF to other multi-exposure imaging (structured light, HDR, low-light). Key limitations include the slow NeRF-based optimization, the linear-motion assumption (which fails on the Fan sequence), reliance on potentially erroneous optical flow supervision, and the co-located emitter assumption. Open questions include whether static/dynamic scene decomposition, higher-order motion models, or non-co-located emitter modeling would further improve results, and whether the approach can be accelerated for practical use.
