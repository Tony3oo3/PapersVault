## Problem

Existing dynamic 3D Gaussian Splatting methods can render novel views and track 3D points in space and time, but they cannot track 3D semantic entities specified through intuitive interfaces like text prompts or clicks. Concurrent static 3D semantic Gaussian methods handle segmentation but fail on dynamic scenes. The paper addresses the gap of a unified representation that jointly captures appearance, geometry, and semantics for dynamic scenes from a single monocular video.

## Input / Output

The method takes a monocular video with calibrated camera intrinsics/extrinsics (from SfM) and produces per-frame 2D feature maps from a pretrained extractor (CLIP, DINOv2, or SAM) upsampled to match RGB resolution. At inference, the user specifies an object via a text prompt (compared in CLIP space) or a 3D click (compared in the distilled feature space), and the system outputs dense per-point semantic segmentation masks, tracked 3D point trajectories over time, and novel view renderings at arbitrary timesteps. A secondary editing mode accepts a text prompt and modifies the texture of selected Gaussians via SDS with Stable Diffusion v2.1.

## Method

DGD extends Deformable 3D Gaussians by attaching a learnable high-dimensional semantic feature vector to each Gaussian alongside its color, position, rotation, scale, and density. A deformation MLP takes Fourier-encoded (position, time) pairs and outputs offsets to the Gaussian's spatial parameters, while the Gaussians' colors and features are rasterized via volumetric rendering and supervised against ground-truth RGB and 2D foundation-model feature maps simultaneously. The key design choice is joint optimization: spatial parameters are influenced by both color and semantic losses, so geometry adapts to both appearance and semantic supervision signals. Training runs 40K iterations (first 3K freeze the deformation field to establish the static canonical Gaussians), using Adam with exponential LR decay and the AST annealing mechanism. For tracking, Gaussians are selected by cosine similarity of their feature vectors to a query, then deformed to any timestep; for editing, an SDS-like loss from Stable Diffusion v2.1 is backpropagated through 500 randomly rendered views at random timesteps to modify only the colors of selected Gaussians.

## Results

On the HyperNeRF and D-NeRF datasets, DGD achieves mean IoU scores ranging from 68.35 (Jumpingjack/Hands) to 93.80 (Standup/Helmet), consistently outperforming the 2D LSeg baseline across all seven evaluated scenes. The most dramatic gains occur on scenes where LSeg fails to localize the target object (e.g., Standup/Helmet: 93.80 vs. 1.08 mIoU; Jumpingjack/Hands: 68.35 vs. 0.89 mIoU). In a perceptual user study (50 users, 1–5 scale), DGD scores 4.63/5 on both segmentation quality and cross-view consistency, compared to 2.71 and 3.4 for the static 3D Gaussian baseline (Feature 3DGS).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DGD is compared against two baselines. Against the 2D LSeg baseline, DGD wins on all seven scenes in Table 1, with margins ranging from a modest +2.56 mIoU on Split-cookie (78.65 vs. 76.09) to a massive +92.72 mIoU on Standup/Helmet (93.80 vs. 1.08). LSeg struggles particularly on dynamic scenes where the target object occupies only a small or moving region, often segmenting the entire object rather than the locally specified part. Against the concurrent static 3D Gaussian baseline (Feature 3DGS, [54]), DGD achieves higher perceptual quality in a 50-user study, scoring 4.63/5 on both segmentation quality (Q1) and cross-view consistency (Q2) versus 2.71 and 3.4 for the baseline. Visually (Fig. 5), DGD produces sharper segmentation boundaries at the canonical frame. The key advantage of DGD over both baselines is that it operates on a single monocular video and supports dynamic tracking over time, whereas LSeg is frame-by-frame 2D and Feature 3DGS requires a static multi-view collection.

## Relation to Prior Work

DGD extends the dynamic 3D Gaussian framework of Deformable 3D Gaussians [49] by augmenting each Gaussian with a learnable semantic feature vector, jointly optimized with color and spatial parameters through a shared deformation MLP. This bridges two previously separate lines of work: static 3D semantic distillation (Feature 3DGS [54], LangSplat [33], LERF [14]) and dynamic 3D appearance reconstruction (3DGS [13], Gaussian-Flow [20]). The semantic editing component repurposes the SDS-like loss from DreamFusion [31] to modify only the color attributes of user-selected Gaussian subsets, leaving geometry and deformation untouched.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DGD provides a practical, real-time (30+ FPS) pipeline for dense 3D semantic tracking and editing from a single monocular video, lowering the barrier for non-expert interaction with dynamic 3D scenes. Key limitations include failure on low-frame-rate videos and transparent objects, reduced 3D feature resolution due to upsampling of 2D features, and the absence of a true dynamic 3D semantic baseline for fair comparison. Open questions include whether video-based feature extractors can improve temporal consistency, whether geometric and deformation editing can be made as intuitive as texture editing, and whether the joint optimization of appearance and semantics is truly necessary or whether decoupled optimization would suffice.
