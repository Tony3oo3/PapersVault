## Problem

Existing 3DGS-based SLAM methods track the camera by minimizing 2D photometric error between rendered and observed images, failing to directly exploit the 3D explicit representation that 3DGS already provides. Decoupled approaches that separate tracking and mapping into independent pipelines require additional computational resources and store redundant feature information. This paper asks whether a single 3D Gaussian map can serve both tracking and mapping simultaneously, with each process providing useful information to the other.

## Input / Output

The system consumes RGBD frames where the depth image is downsampled and reprojected into a 3D point cloud for G-ICP alignment, while the RGB image provides color and opacity supervision for the Gaussian map. Outputs include per-frame camera poses estimated via G-ICP scan matching, a shared 3D Gaussian map (positions, covariances, colors, opacities) that is continuously optimized, and rendered RGB/depth images for evaluation. The entire pipeline runs at up to 107 FPS on an RTX 4090, with a 30 FPS mode matching typical sensor rates.

## Method

The core mechanism is a coupled SLAM loop where G-ICP tracking and 3DGS mapping share a single Gaussian map: G-ICP aligns the current frame's point cloud (with computed covariances) against a subset of the 3DGS map's Gaussians to estimate pose, and when a keyframe is selected, those same covariances directly initialize new 3DGS primitives without densifying or opacity reset. Two scale-alignment techniques bridge the tracking-mapping gap: an ellipsoid regularization (dividing the SVD scale matrix by its median) preserves the geometric character of map Gaussians during tracking, while a depth-dependent normalization (dividing scale by z^1.5) prevents newly added far-range Gaussians from having excessively large covariances. Dynamic keyframe selection uses the G-ICP correspondence proportion to trigger tracking keyframes, while additional mapping-only keyframes are inserted at fixed intervals to enrich the map without accumulating scan-matching drift. During mapping optimization, randomly selected keyframes (rather than only recent or covisible ones) are used for rendering loss to avoid local minima, and elongated Gaussians are pruned but never densified since G-ICP already supplies appropriately positioned primitives.

## Results

On the Replica dataset, the proposed method achieves state-of-the-art tracking accuracy with an average ATE RMSE of 0.16 cm (limited to 30 FPS), more than halving the error of the previous best coupled method (Gaussian Splatting SLAM at 0.32 cm). At 30 FPS, it also achieves the best map quality with PSNR 38.83 dB, SSIM 0.98, and LPIPS 0.04. Without a tracking speed limit, the system reaches up to 107 FPS (98.11 FPS average across scenes) while still outperforming all baselines on rendering quality (PSNR 35.93 dB). On TUM RGB-D, the method achieves 2.4 cm ATE RMSE and 20.72 dB PSNR at 30 FPS, with system speeds up to 74 FPS.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The method decisively outperforms all 3DGS-based coupled baselines on both tracking and mapping. On Replica, it reduces ATE RMSE by 50% over Gaussian Splatting SLAM (0.16 vs. 0.32 cm) and SplaTAM (0.16 vs. 0.36 cm), and by ~70% over GS-SLAM (0.16 vs. 0.50 cm) and Point-SLAM (0.16 vs. 0.54 cm). In terms of map quality at 30 FPS, it achieves PSNR 38.83 dB, surpassing Gaussian Splatting SLAM (37.50), Point-SLAM (35.62), GS-SLAM (34.27), and SplaTAM (33.89), while running 27× faster than Gaussian Splatting SLAM and ~130× faster than SplaTAM/Point-SLAM. Against the decoupled Photo-SLAM on TUM, the method trades a small PSNR gap (20.72 vs. 21.90 dB) for a massive speed advantage (30 FPS vs. unreported system FPS), and is competitive on tracking (2.4 vs. 1.3 cm ATE). Against ORB-SLAM3 (decoupled), it loses on TUM tracking (2.4 vs. 1.3 cm) but avoids the need for a separate feature-based map. On TUM, SplaTAM retains a PSNR advantage (23.46 vs. 20.72 dB) due to its depth-loss handling strategy, but the proposed method is ~92× faster (30 FPS vs. 0.32 FPS).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper fuses two previously independent lines of work—G-ICP scan matching (Segal et al., 2009) for pose estimation and 3D Gaussian Splatting (Kerbl et al., 2023) for scene representation—into a single coupled SLAM system. It departs from prior 3DGS-based SLAM methods (GS-SLAM, SplaTAM, Gaussian Splatting SLAM) that track via 2D photometric error, and from decoupled approaches (Orbeez-SLAM, vMAP, Photo-SLAM) that maintain a separate ORB feature map for tracking. The key conceptual departure is recognizing that both G-ICP and 3DGS operate on the same primitive (3D points with covariance), enabling direct sharing of Gaussians between tracking and mapping without redundant computation or separate feature extraction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 107 FPS full-system speed (tracking + mapping) represents a dramatic practical improvement over photometric-based 3DGS SLAM (~0.3 FPS) and even decoupled ORB-based systems (~24 FPS), making dense-representation SLAM viable for latency-sensitive robotics and AR applications. The SOTA tracking accuracy on Replica (0.16 cm ATE, >50% improvement over prior best) validates that active 3D geometric tracking outperforms indirect 2D photometric tracking. A key limitation is reliance on depth quality: on noisy TUM data, map quality degrades noticeably, and the authors note that trading some speed for RGB-based depth denoising is a natural future direction. Open questions include whether the covariance-sharing mechanism generalizes to LiDAR-only or multi-sensor settings, and whether the ellipsoid regularization and z^1.5 scale alignment transfer to other 3DGS variants.
