## Problem

Per-scene optimization methods (NeRF, 3DGS) achieve photorealistic large-scene reconstruction but require hours of optimization and overfit to source views, producing artifacts under large viewpoint changes. Generalizable feed-forward approaches and large reconstruction models are fast but are limited to small objects or scenes and cannot handle the complexity of urban-scale environments (>10,000 m²) with hundreds of source images. G3R addresses this gap by combining data-driven priors from learned networks with iterative gradient feedback from differentiable rendering.

## Input / Output

G3R takes all available multi-view camera images (1080×1920, typically >100 frames) along with an approximate geometry scaffold (downsampled LiDAR points or MVS mesh faces) and camera intrinsics/extrinsics. The output is a set of 3D Neural Gaussians — a latent representation that can be exported to standard 3D Gaussians for real-time rasterization at >90 FPS. Unlike feed-forward methods that select only a few nearby source views, G3R consumes all source images simultaneously through accumulated 3D gradients, and the resulting representation is editable (dynamic actors can be repositioned, cameras can be moved) to produce multi-camera simulation videos.

## Method

G3R introduces a learned optimizer (G3R-Net, a 3D UNet with sparse convolution) that iteratively refines a 3D Neural Gaussian representation over 24 steps. At each step, the current representation is differentiably rendered to all source views, the reconstruction loss is backpropagated to produce 3D gradients w.r.t. the representation, and these gradients — which encode all source images in a unified, occlusion-aware 3D form — are fed alongside the current representation into G3R-Net to predict a residual update scaled by a cosine-decaying schedule γ(t). The scene is decomposed into static background, rigid dynamic actors (moved via 3D bounding boxes), and a distant region, each handled by a separate network. Training is supervised across many large outdoor scenes with both source-view and novel-view rendering losses (MSE + LPIPS + flatness regularization), enabling the network to learn reconstruction priors that generalize to unseen scenes and improve robustness to large view extrapolation.

## Results

On PandaSet, G3R achieves PSNR 25.22, SSIM 0.742, and LPIPS 0.371 in 123 seconds of reconstruction time, matching or slightly exceeding per-scene 3DGS (PSNR 25.14) while being ~25× faster (123 s vs. 50 min 14 s). The faster G3R(turbo) variant reaches PSNR 24.76 in just 31 s. On the more challenging BlendedMVS dataset (drone-captured, >10,000 m²), G3R attains PSNR 25.22 and LPIPS 0.390, outperforming 3DGS (PSNR 25.12, LPIPS 0.462) in perceptual quality while reducing reconstruction time from 39.5 min to 210 s. Both variants render at 121 FPS (PandaSet) and 97 FPS (BlendedMVS), enabling real-time camera simulation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against per-scene 3DGS, G3R achieves a marginal PSNR gain (25.22 vs. 25.14 on PandaSet; 25.22 vs. 25.12 on BlendedMVS) and a clear LPIPS advantage (0.371 vs. 0.372; 0.390 vs. 0.462), while cutting reconstruction time by roughly 25× (123 s vs. 50 min; 210 s vs. 39.5 min). Against Instant-NGP, G3R wins on all three metrics (PSNR +0.88, SSIM +0.013, LPIPS −0.065 on PandaSet) and is ~17× faster. Against generalizable NVS baselines (MVSNeRF, ENeRF, GNT, PixelSplat), G3R leads on PSNR by 0.8–2.0 dB and on SSIM by 0.01–0.09 on PandaSet; the gap widens dramatically on BlendedMVS where generalizable methods collapse to 15–19 PSNR while G3R holds at 25.22. The sole exception is ENeRF's LPIPS on PandaSet (0.306 vs. 0.371), which the authors attribute to image-warping artifacts that inflate perceptual scores despite poor geometry. On BlendedMVS, even the adapted PixelSplat++ (19.60 PSNR) trails G3R by 5.6 dB, confirming that one-step feed-forward prediction cannot match iterative gradient-guided refinement for large, complex scenes.

## Relation to Prior Work

G3R builds directly on 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as its differentiable rendering backbone, extending it with a latent feature vector (3D Neural Gaussians) and a static/dynamic/distant scene decomposition to handle large unbounded dynamic scenes. Conceptually, it departs from both per-scene optimization (NeRF, 3DGS) and feed-forward generalizable methods (MVSNeRF, ENeRF, GNT, PixelSplat) by framing reconstruction as a learned iterative optimizer: rather than selecting a few source views for unprojection or running thousands of gradient steps, it uses rendering gradients as a unified 3D signal that a 3D UNet processes over just 24 iterations. This places it in the 'learned optimizer' lineage (Andrychowicz et al., Wichrowska et al.) and the iterative-network family (DeepView), but is the first to scale this paradigm to city-scale scenes with >100 input images.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

G3R is the first method to achieve generalizable, high-fidelity reconstruction of large scenes (>10,000 m²) in under two minutes with real-time rendering (>90 FPS), making digital twin creation for autonomous driving simulation and VR practically feasible at scale. The cross-dataset generalization (PandaSet → BlendedMVS → Waymo) and robustness to meter-scale view extrapolation suggest the learned prior genuinely captures 3D structure rather than memorizing source views. Open questions remain around large extrapolation artifacts (requiring scene completion), dependence on LiDAR/MVS scaffolds for initialization, and the absence of non-rigid deformation and emissive lighting modeling, which limit applicability to fully dynamic urban environments.
