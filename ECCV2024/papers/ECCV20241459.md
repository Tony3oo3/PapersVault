## Problem

Existing line segment detectors are specialized for line-like structures in man-made scenes and discard segments that would approximate curved contours, limiting their utility for vectorization and shape approximation tasks. Capturing both straight and curved shapes requires reformulating detection as an approximation problem where complexity (number of segments) becomes as important as accuracy, a tradeoff that prior methods do not explicitly address.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241459/cropped_page3_idx6.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a predicted image gradient map (magnitude and orientation) from a deep model such as DeepLSD or SAM, converts it into a sparse 2D point cloud retaining only high-magnitude pixels weighted by their gradient value, with point normals aligned to gradient direction. Output is a configuration of line segments, each defined by a supporting line (weighted least-squares fit) and its inlier points, with extremities set by the furthest inlier projections. Two user-specified parameters (ε, σ) — fitting tolerance and minimum inlier count — control the level of approximation detail, and when combined with ASIP the line segments are further connected into closed, intersection-free polygons.

## Method

LineFit formulates line segment fitting as a point-to-line problem inspired by 3D planar primitive detection, using a fitting tolerance ε that absorbs gradient noise and controls approximation granularity on curves. Configuration quality is measured by an energy combining three normalized terms: fidelity (mean weighted point-to-line distance), completeness (weighted inlier ratio), and regularity (degrees of freedom penalizing extra segments and rewarding co-linearity, parallelism, orthogonality, or vanishing-point concurrence). Optimization proceeds via an exploration mechanism that alternates local operators (merge/split, insert/exclude) scheduled in a priority queue by ascending energy change, with global operators (EM-style transfer for precision, and a regularity operator enforcing geometric constraints) applied after each local sequence. The algorithm is purely geometric with no learned parameters, initialized from LSD or CGAL region-growing, and converges when energy stabilizes over two cycles or after 10 iterations.

## Results

On YorkUrban (line-like structures), LineFit achieves the best AP of 52.0% and best AF of 45.7%, slightly ahead of DeepLSD (51.0% AP, 44.6% AF). On RoofSat (satellite orthographic views), DeepLSD leads in AP (48.9% vs. 42.1%), but LineFit achieves the best recall (39.0% AR vs. 34.4% for DeepLSD) and best AF (37.9%). On BSDS500 (curved objects), LineFit outperforms all competitors by a significant margin on precision, recall, and F-score, and is the only method that can vary the number of line segments over a wide range via the fitting tolerance parameter. Activating the regularity operator (Ours*) reduces the Degree of Freedom score by ~60% (81.1→47.1 on YorkUrban, 96.2→57.8 on RoofSat) at a small cost in AP/AR.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against ELSED (traditional region-growing), LineFit wins on both YorkUrban and RoofSat in AP and AF, with the gap being largest on RoofSat (42.1 vs. 39.1 AP). Against HAWP (learned wireframe parsing), LineFit dominates on YorkUrban (52.0 vs. 39.2 AP; 41.5 vs. 17.6 AR) but HAWP edges out on RoofSat AP (45.4 vs. 42.1), though LineFit still leads in recall and F-score. Against LETR (transformer-based), LineFit wins decisively on both datasets, with LETR performing particularly poorly on RoofSat (21.3% AP). Against DeepLSD (hybrid deep gradient + LSD fitting), the comparison is closest: LineFit wins on YorkUrban (52.0 vs. 51.0 AP, 45.7 vs. 44.6 AF) while DeepLSD wins on RoofSat AP (48.9 vs. 42.1), though LineFit recovers in recall (39.0 vs. 34.4 AR) and F-score (37.9 vs. 36.3 AF). On BSDS500 with curved objects, LineFit outclasses all four baselines by a significant margin on all three metrics (precision, recall, F-score), and uniquely supports a wide range of output complexities via the fitting tolerance ε. The regularity operator (Ours*) trades ~1 point of AP/AR for a ~60% reduction in degrees of freedom, making it the preferred setting for structured scenes.

## Relation to Prior Work

LineFit follows the hybrid strategy pioneered by DeepLSD and LSDNet—predicting a deep image gradient map and then fitting line segments geometrically—but deliberately departs from their line-like-structure-only objective by reframing detection as a point-to-line approximation problem inspired by 3D planar primitive detection in point clouds. The energy-minimization framework with local (merge/split/insert/exclude) and global (transfer/regularity) operators draws on geometry processing and variational shape approximation techniques, while the downstream polygonalization pipeline builds directly on ASIP's kinetic polygonal partitioning. Unlike wireframe-parsing methods (HAWP, LGNN) that enforce connectivity, LineFit enforces geometric regularities (co-linearity, vanishing-point concurrence, orthogonality) during the fitting process itself rather than as a post-processing step.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The fitting tolerance parameter provides a principled complexity-distortion tradeoff for approximating curved shapes, which is essential for vectorization and polygonalization tasks that existing line detectors cannot address. The introduction of the RoofSat dataset for satellite roof wireframes fills a gap in orthographic-projection line detection benchmarks. Key limitations include the non-real-time nature of the optimization, the dependence on a good initial configuration, and the inability to embed the method in end-to-end architectures. The authors identify generalization to parametric curves (e.g., Bézier) as a natural next step, which would further bridge the gap between line-segment fitting and full vector graphics representation.
