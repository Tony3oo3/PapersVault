## Problem

Pre-trained text-to-image models consistently fail to generate images faithful to spatial relations when entities are composed in uncommon ways, yet the field lacks a formal account of *why* generalization fails. The paper hypothesizes that the root cause is not insufficient data quantity but a skew in the underlying phenomenon space: the training distribution does not proportionally cover all role-filler bindings, leaving certain concept-position pairings underrepresented or absent.

## Input / Output

The model receives a text prompt specifying two named objects and a binary spatial relation (e.g., 'a cat is on top of a mouse') and produces a single image placing those objects in the requested arrangement. In synthetic experiments, inputs are template-generated captions over unicode icons rendered at 32×64 resolution; in natural-image experiments, inputs are What'sUp benchmark captions rendered at 64×32. Critically, the model is evaluated on *unseen* concept pairs to isolate relational generalization, and outputs are scored by automated classifiers (convolutional kernel matching for synthetic icons, a fine-tuned ViT-B/16 for natural objects) that check whether each object appears in the correct spatial half.

## Method

The paper formalizes each scene as a role-filler binding ψ(F, R) and introduces two distributional metrics—Completeness (fraction of concepts observed in each position) and Balance (entropy of the position distribution per concept)—computed separately under linguistic roles (subject/object) and visual roles (top/bottom). Using these metrics, the authors systematically construct training subsets of the phenomenon space with controlled levels of completeness and balance while holding coverage roughly constant, then train pixel-space diffusion models (350M UNet + T5-small for synthetic; 470M UNet for natural) and test on held-out concept pairs. The key finding is that visual incompleteness or imbalance severely caps generalization accuracy, linguistic skew primarily delays rather than prevents generalization, and vanilla data coverage is a poor predictor of performance. The framework is further validated on latent diffusion with a frozen Stable Diffusion 2.1 VAE, confirming that the metrics transfer across pixel and latent generation.

## Results

On the What'sUp benchmark (15 concepts, 64×32 pixel-space diffusion), the fully complete and balanced training set (CPL=100/100, BLC=100) achieves 48.64% final accuracy with a 33.67 train-test gap, compared to 10.17% accuracy and 80.49 gap for a complete-but-imbalanced set (BLC=73) and 18.75% for an incomplete set (CPL(r2)=50). On synthetic images (up to 90 concepts), visual incompleteness causes testing accuracy to plateau below 50% for small concept sets, while the fully complete and balanced configuration achieves 100% testing accuracy for all N. Linguistic skew has a milder effect: it delays generalization (larger train-test gaps) but rarely prevents eventual convergence to near-perfect accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing generation methods; instead, it ablates training-set distributional properties to isolate their causal effect on generalization. The most striking comparison is between the fully complete and balanced configuration (48.64% acc, 33.67 gap) and the complete-but-imbalanced one (10.17% acc, 80.49 gap) in Table 1, showing that balance alone accounts for a ~38-point accuracy swing at comparable coverage (~44–48%). Incompleteness is even more damaging: dropping CPL(r2) from 100 to 50 reduces accuracy to 18.75% despite similar coverage. On the linguistic side (Table 2), the effect is milder—accuracy ranges from 57.14% to 67.29%—but the train-test gap still widens from 30.21 to 40.72 as completeness drops. Across both perspectives, vanilla coverage (44–50%) is nearly constant, confirming that raw coverage is a poor predictor of generalization while completeness and balance are strongly correlated with performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the denoising diffusion architecture (Ho et al., 2020) and text-conditioned diffusion (Zhang et al., 2023) as its experimental platform, but its conceptual contribution departs from the dominant 'scale up data' paradigm in text-to-image generation. It draws on the role-filler binding framework from cognitive science and prior compositional generalization work in vision-language understanding, while extending the phenomenon-space analysis of Okawa et al. (2024) and the compositional support notions of prior work to a formal, two-perspective (linguistic and visual) metric system. The key departure is from prior work that attributes generalization failures to architectural limitations or insufficient data quantity, instead arguing that the *structural* properties of data coverage—completeness and balance of role-filler bindings—are the critical, under-studied axis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first formal metrics (completeness and balance) for quantifying how training data distribution affects relational generalization in text-to-image models, offering a principled alternative to the arms race of scaling raw data volume. Practically, it reveals that most open-source diffusion implementations omit image positional encodings and that CLIP's text encoder fails to distinguish spatial roles—both actionable findings for practitioners building spatially-aware generators. Open questions remain around evaluating entity-relation compositions in cluttered scenes, extending beyond binary relations, and developing architectures that explicitly model role-filler bindings rather than relying on cross-attention to implicitly learn them.
