## Problem

State-of-the-art image manipulation detection methods rely on high-quality training datasets with pixel-level annotations that are costly and impractical to acquire, and they generalize poorly to unseen manipulation types. This paper addresses the gap by proposing a unified framework that operates under both weakly supervised (image-level labels only) and fully unsupervised (no labels) settings, and is the first to exploit Implicit Neural Representation as a pre-processing mechanism for manipulation detection.

## Input / Output

The method accepts a standard RGB image and internally generates a 4-channel input by concatenating the original RGB channels with a single-channel reconstruction error map produced by a pre-trained INR. The output is dual-granularity: a pixel-level manipulation mask obtained via upsampling and sigmoid on the final feature map, and an image-level authenticity score produced by adaptive global average pooling over high-confidence tampered regions. The framework is trained exclusively on CASIAv2 (7,491 authentic + 5,063 tampered images) and evaluated across seven datasets including novel manipulation types from IEdit and MagicBrush.

## Method

The core insight is that an INR trained solely on authentic images learns an average representation and fails to faithfully reconstruct tampered pixels, producing a reconstruction error map that acts as a manipulation prior. This NRR error map is concatenated with the RGB input and fed into a shared-weight ResNet50 two-branch (Siamese) architecture: the main branch processes the 4-channel input while the complementary branch processes the INR-reconstructed image, and their features are matched via dot-product similarity. Selective pixel-level contrastive learning (InfoNCE) is applied only to pixels where unsupervised K-means clustering of both feature maps and matching scores agree, mitigating uncertainty from absent pixel labels. For image-level prediction, an Adaptive Global Average Pooling combines Otsu's thresholding with clustering to pool exclusively over high-confidence tampered regions. In unsupervised mode, a self-distillation strategy uses pseudo-labels selected from predictions consistent across both Otsu and clustering to supervise shallower classification heads.

## Results

In the weakly supervised setting (Table 2), the proposed method achieves the best C-F1 across all five standard benchmarks, reaching 0.310 on CASIAv1, 0.479 on Columbia, and 0.332 on Coverage, outperforming WSCL by 4.7–18.6% in C-F1. On the novel manipulation task (Table 3), the weakly supervised method (trained on only 12.5K CASIAv2 samples with image-level labels) achieves 0.535 ACC and 0.664 I-F1 on IEdit, matching or exceeding most fully supervised methods trained on up to 1.7M synthetic samples. On MagicBrush, the method achieves 0.122 P-F1 and 0.201 C-F1, remaining competitive with fully supervised baselines that use 64K–1.7M training images. In the unsupervised setting (Table 1), the method leads all five baselines on P-F1 across all five datasets, e.g., 0.166 vs. 0.134 (CFAI) on CASIAv1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the five unsupervised baselines (NOI, CFAI, MCA, NoisePrint, IVC), the proposed method wins on both IOU and P-F1 across all five standard datasets, with the largest margin over MCA (e.g., P-F1 0.166 vs. 0.089 on CASIAv1). In the weakly supervised comparison, the method outperforms FCN [46] on every metric across all datasets and beats WSCL [65] on all metrics except Columbia I-F1 (0.695 vs. 0.720), where WSCL retains a narrow edge due to Columbia's lack of post-processing artifacts. On the novel manipulation task (IEdit and MagicBrush), the weakly supervised method (12.5K training images, image-level labels only) achieves 0.535 ACC on IEdit, surpassing all fully supervised baselines except TruFor (0.505 ACC) and Hifi-Net (0.531 ACC), while matching TruFor's I-F1 (0.664 vs. 0.665). On MagicBrush, the method trails the best fully supervised baseline TruFor (C-F1 0.201 vs. 0.418) but substantially outperforms SPAN (0.004), CAT-Net (0.063), CR-CNN (0.078), and ObjectFormer (0.085) in C-F1, demonstrating that limited supervision with INR-based pre-processing generalizes far better than pixel-level supervised training on unseen manipulation types.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in IMD that relies on high-pass noise filters (SRM, Bayar) or fully supervised pixel-level annotations by introducing Implicit Neural Representation (INR) as a pre-processing stage whose reconstruction error serves as a manipulation prior. It builds on the self-distillation training strategy [69] for its unsupervised mode and adapts the selective-supervised concept from noisy-label learning [31] to pseudo-label selection. The selective contrastive learning component extends InfoNCE-based contrastive learning [22] to the IMD setting by restricting supervision to high-confidence regions identified through the intersection of Otsu thresholding and K-means clustering, addressing the absence of pixel-level ground truth.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in enabling IMD with only image-level labels (or no labels at all), which aligns with how real-world tampered images are typically encountered. The finding that an INR trained exclusively on authentic images systematically fails to reconstruct tampered regions provides a new, filter-free mechanism for highlighting manipulation that is complementary to noise-based approaches. However, the method still produces imprecise edge localization (prediction masks larger than ground truth), and absolute pixel-level F1 scores remain modest (e.g., ~0.20 on CASIAv1), leaving open whether INR-based priors generalize to all manipulation types, particularly those where tampered content is visually consistent with authentic content. The competitive performance against fully supervised methods on novel manipulation datasets (IEdit, MagicBrush) suggests the approach may be particularly valuable as tampering techniques evolve beyond training distributions.
