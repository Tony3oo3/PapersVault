## Problem

Current human trajectory predictors assume that behavioral trends in a deployment scene remain constant over time, making them unable to adapt to transient context-specific changes such as new infrastructure, weather, time-of-day shifts, or social events. This paper formalizes the adaptive trajectory prediction problem and asks how a pre-trained predictor can be efficiently re-tuned to a new deployment scene using extremely small amounts of observed data (e.g., 2 humans for 30 seconds).
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241012/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The predictor consumes H=8 frames of agent position history rendered as trajectory heatmaps (each h×w, with h=288, w=480) concatenated with a 12-class semantic segmentation map S, plus a per-scene latent corridor prompt p_k that is summed element-wise into every heatmap frame. The output is T=12 frames of predicted future trajectory heatmaps, from which 2D pixel-space positions are extracted via softargmax for ADE/FDE evaluation. The latent corridor itself is parameterized as a rank-1 outer product of two vectors of dimensions h and w, adding fewer than 0.1% parameters to the ~900K-parameter base model.

## Method

The core mechanism augments a frozen YNet-based encoder-decoder-predictor-head pipeline with a per-scene learnable latent corridor: a spatially-aligned prompt of size h×w, compactly represented as the outer product of two Kaiming-initialized vectors (h and w dimensional), which is added element-wise to each input trajectory heatmap frame. During adaptation, the encoder and decoder remain frozen while only the prompt (and optionally the predictor head) is trained with the same binary cross-entropy loss on trajectory heatmaps used for pre-training. Three prompting configurations are studied: latent corridor only (LC), latent corridor with a jointly tuned shared predictor head (LC+Joint FT), and latent corridor with per-scene finetuned heads (LC+Per-Scene FT), the last yielding the best in-scene performance. The approach is data-efficient, requiring as little as 30 human-seconds of new observations, and trains in minutes on a single GPU, enabling rapid or real-time adaptation to transient scene changes.

## Results

On MOTSynth (437 scenes), the best variant (LC + Joint Finetune) achieves 42.6 ADE / 85.3 FDE, a 9.9% ADE improvement over the scene-aware YNet baseline (47.3 ADE), with per-scene gains reaching up to 23.9%. On real MOT and WildTrack data, LC + Per-Scene Finetune achieves 37.4 ADE (MOT) and 27.4 ADE (WildTrack), yielding a 16.4% average ADE improvement over the non-adaptive scene-aware baseline. On in-the-wild EarthCam webcam data, the same configuration reaches 17.5 ADE / 30.8 FDE, a 26.8% ADE improvement over the scene-aware baseline. The method adds less than 0.1% parameters to the base predictor and trains in minutes on a single GPU.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the constant-velocity baseline, all learned methods improve substantially (e.g., 47.3 vs. 78.5 ADE on MOTSynth), confirming that scene-aware learning is essential. The scene-aware YNet baseline (47.3 ADE on MOTSynth, 44.2 on MOT, 33.6 on WildTrack, 23.9 on EarthCam) is the strongest non-adaptive method and serves as the primary comparison. The paper's LC + Per-Scene Finetune variant outperforms YNet by 16.4% average ADE on MOT/WildTrack and 26.8% on EarthCam, and also beats the direct finetuning baseline (ATP Finetune) by 11.2% on real data, showing that the latent corridor prompt provides complementary scene-specific information beyond what head finetuning alone captures. LC-only adaptation (no finetuning) is competitive with finetuning alone on real data (e.g., 43.0 vs. 41.8 ADE on MOT) while being faster to train and enabling instant recovery of the base model. On MOTSynth, LC + Joint Finetune (42.6 ADE) outperforms both LC-only (44.6) and the scene-aware baseline (47.3), demonstrating that jointly tuning the predictor head across scenes further amplifies the benefit of per-scene prompts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on YNet (Mangalam et al., ICCV 2021) as its frozen base predictor, which already incorporates scene context via semantic segmentation and trajectory heatmaps, and on PECNet for the no-scene baseline. Conceptually, it transplants the prompt-tuning paradigm from large language models (Lester et al., 2021) and vision transformers (Jia et al., ECCV 2022) into the trajectory prediction domain, where prior adaptation work has relied on fine-tuning, memory banks of stored trajectories, or meta-learning. The key departure is that instead of modifying the predictor's weights or augmenting it with a growing memory, the method injects a single low-rank image-space prompt (the 'latent corridor') that is element-wise added to input heatmaps, keeping the base model frozen and requiring less than 0.1% additional parameters.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper formalizes adaptive trajectory prediction as a distinct problem and demonstrates that extremely small amounts of deployment data (as few as 60 human-seconds) can yield substantial accuracy gains, which is practically important for deploying predictors in new or changing environments. The rank-1 prompt representation achieving comparable performance to a full-rank prompt is a useful efficiency result, and the qualitative evidence of learned ground-plane awareness and scene-specific behavioral trends (e.g., diagonal plaza crossing) suggests the prompts capture meaningful spatial structure. Open questions include whether the approach extends to dynamic cameras, indoor environments, or longer time horizons, and whether real-time adaptation (e.g., to a sudden crowd event) is achievable with the current training speed. The baselines are self-implemented simplified versions of YNet and PECNet, so absolute comparisons to the full published models remain to be verified.
