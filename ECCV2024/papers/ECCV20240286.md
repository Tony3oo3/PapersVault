## Problem

Diffusion-based image super-resolution methods struggle to perceive semantic content and degradation information from heavily degraded low-resolution images, producing restorations with incorrect content or unrealistic artifacts. Existing approaches either rely on empty prompts or simple object-tagging models that lack spatial/scene understanding and fail to capture low-level distortion cues critical for modeling the degradation process.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240286/cropped_page3_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a 128×128 center-cropped LR image and two textual prompts generated by LLaVA (a multimodal LLM): a high-level prompt describing objects, spatial positions, and scene context, and a low-level prompt describing quality factors such as blur, noise, color, and lighting. Both prompts are encoded via the CLIP text encoder into embedding vectors. The output is a 512×512 HR image, generated by a 20-step DDPM sampling process with classifier-free guidance (scale 5.5) using negative prompts like 'blurry, dotted, noise, unclear, lowres, over-smoothed'.

## Method

XPSR is a two-stage framework: first, LLaVA generates high-level and low-level semantic prompts for the LR image, which are encoded via CLIP into two embedding vectors; second, a frozen Stable Diffusion v1.5 backbone with a trainable ControlNet (cloned from SD's U-Net encoder) performs conditional denoising. The key architectural contribution is the Semantic-Fusion Attention (SFA), which processes the two prompt embeddings through parallel cross-attention branches rather than serially, then fuses them via a fusion attention that takes queries from the high-level branch and keys/values from the low-level branch, enabling adaptive selection of semantic features without information overwrite. A Degradation-Free Constraint (DFC) applies L1 losses in both pixel space (at three pyramid scales) and latent space (at three U-Net encoder scales) between ControlNet encoder outputs and downsampled HR counterparts, forcing the encoder to extract semantic-preserved features while suppressing degradation artifacts. During training, only ControlNet and an added Conditional Attention module in the U-Net are updated (SD parameters frozen), optimizing a weighted sum of the standard diffusion noise-prediction loss and the DFC loss (λ=0.05).

## Results

XPSR achieves the best no-reference perceptual quality scores across all three evaluation datasets: MANIQA 0.6080, CLIPIQA 0.7816, and MUSIQ 69.99 on DIV2K-Val; MANIQA 0.6059, CLIPIQA 0.7354, and MUSIQ 70.23 on RealSR; and MANIQA 0.5713, CLIPIQA 0.7360, and MUSIQ 67.84 on DRealSR. On DIV2K-Val, XPSR surpasses the second-best method (SeeSR) by 10.34% in MANIQA, 8.57% in CLIPIQA, and 1.64% in MUSIQ. However, XPSR consistently trails GAN-based methods (BSRGAN, Real-ESRGAN, SwinIR) on reference-based fidelity metrics (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, DISTS, FID), which the authors attribute to the inherent trade-off between generative realism and pixel-level fidelity in diffusion-based restoration.

## Baselines & Comparisons

XPSR decisively outperforms all seven diffusion-based baselines (StableSR, DiffBIR, PASD, SeeSR, LDM) and three GAN-based baselines (BSRGAN, Real-ESRGAN, SwinIR) on the three no-reference perceptual quality metrics (MANIQA, CLIPIQA, MUSIQ) across all three datasets, with margins of 5–10% over the nearest competitor SeeSR. Conversely, XPSR consistently underperforms on all reference-based fidelity metrics: on DIV2K-Val it scores 22.80 PSNR vs. BSRGAN's 24.42, 0.5627 SSIM vs. Real-ESRGAN's 0.6324, and 33.38 FID vs. StableSR's 28.32; similar gaps appear on RealSR and DRealSR. Among diffusion-based methods specifically, XPSR trails StableSR on FID (33.38 vs. 28.32 on DIV2K-Val) and SeeSR on LPIPS (0.3761 vs. 0.3283), but leads all diffusion methods on perceptual quality by wide margins. The authors argue this fidelity–realism trade-off is inherent to diffusion-based generation and that current full-reference metrics (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS) poorly reflect human perception, as illustrated by their Fig. 4 where XPSR outputs are visually superior yet score lower. Overall, the comparison positions XPSR as the strongest method for perceptual quality but not for pixel-level fidelity, a distinction that is central to the paper's contribution.

## Relation to Prior Work

XPSR extends the diffusion-based ISR line initiated by StableSR and DiffBIR, which relied on empty prompts or simple feature warping, and PASD/SeeSR, which used off-the-shelf object tagging models for high-level prompts. The key departure is the introduction of MLLM-derived cross-modal semantic priors (via LLaVA) that provide both high-level scene/object descriptions and low-level quality/distortion assessments, fused through a novel parallel Semantic-Fusion Attention module rather than the serial cross-attention used in prior T2I conditioning. The Degradation-Free Constraint, which enforces pixel- and latent-space alignment between ControlNet encoder outputs and the HR reference, is a new training objective not present in prior ControlNet-based SR methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that MLLM-generated textual priors can substantially close the semantic gap that degraded LR images create for diffusion-based SR, yielding large gains on perceptual quality metrics (MANIQA, CLIPIQA, MUSIQ) across synthetic and real-world benchmarks. However, the persistent fidelity gap relative to GAN-based methods (PSNR, SSIM) remains unresolved, and the authors themselves note that current full-reference metrics may not reflect human perception. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to video SR, whether stronger base diffusion models (e.g., SD-v2.1 at matching resolution) would narrow the fidelity gap, and whether the MLLM inference cost is acceptable for real-time applications.
