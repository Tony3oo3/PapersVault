## Problem

There is no comprehensive benchmark for pixel correspondence estimation (optical flow, scene flow, point tracking) that simultaneously provides both event data and RGB frames with dense per-pixel annotations. Existing benchmarks are either scene-biased, lack event modality, offer only sparse annotations, or cover a limited object vocabulary, hindering the development of algorithms that fuse information from both modalities and evaluate generalization across diverse objects.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241779/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark provides three input modalities per sequence: final RGB images (simulating motion blur and limited dynamic range via random exposure, clipping, sampled CRFs, and 8-bit quantization), clean RGB images (artifact-free), and asynchronous event streams synthesized with DVS-Voltmeter at 640×480 resolution. Ground truth consists of dense per-pixel optical flow and scene flow between arbitrary frame pairs, plus dense point tracking trajectories (grid-sampled at size 20 for evaluation) across 865 test sub-sequences. Evaluation is conducted at strides 1, 2, 4, and 8 from a reference frame, using 12,804 test frame pairs for flow/scene flow and EPE, δ_avg, MTE, and survival rate as metrics.

## Method

The dataset is constructed in Blender with 143 sequences (80 indoor, 63 outdoor) assembled from purchased photorealistic scenes, 29 scanned human bodies, 88 artist-designed animals, and 104 Mixamo characters re-targeted to over 100 motion models, plus 50 custom outdoor scenes with camera trajectories mimicking handheld, car, and drone shots. Ground truth for dynamic and deformable objects is obtained by baking face indices and barycentric coordinates into textures, enabling exact 3D position recovery via P = λ₁V₁ + λ₂V₂ + λ₃V₃, while static objects use standard back-projection from camera poses and depth maps. Semantic labels for 410 categories are assigned automatically using CLIP image-text matching against LVIS-derived category descriptions. The evaluation protocol benchmarks 17 existing methods (image-based, event-based, and fusion) across three tasks, analyzing robustness under large frame intervals and extreme illumination, cross-dataset generalization after fine-tuning on BlinkVision, and per-category performance distributions to identify hard vs. easy object classes.

## Results

On the BlinkVision optical flow benchmark (EPE, lower is better), the best RGB-based method FlowFormer++ achieves 7.20 average EPE on clean images and 8.90 on final (realistic) images across strides 1–8, outperforming RAFT (9.27/10.54) and GMA (8.60/10.21). After fine-tuning on the BlinkVision training split, FlowFormer+++ reduces average EPE to 4.73 (clean) and 6.04 (final), while RAFT† reaches 5.85/7.08. Event-based methods underperform RGB methods substantially: E-FlowFormer scores 12.76 and E-RAFT 14.07 (clean, avg), and the event+RGB fusion method DCEIFlow is worst at 28.43. Fine-tuning event methods on BlinkVision narrows the gap (E-FlowFormer†: 6.02, E-RAFT†: 6.75), but they still trail the best RGB methods at larger strides.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among RGB-based optical flow methods, FlowFormer++ (7.20 avg EPE-clean) marginally beats FlowFormer (7.24) and clearly outperforms RAFT (9.27) and GMA (8.60); after fine-tuning on BlinkVision, FlowFormer+++ (4.73) surpasses RAFT† (5.85) by over 1.1 EPE. Event-based methods are substantially weaker: E-FlowFormer (12.76) and E-RAFT (14.07) trail the best RGB method by ~5–7 EPE, and the event+RGB fusion DCEIFlow is worst at 28.43, suggesting current fusion strategies are ineffective. After fine-tuning on BlinkVision, E-FlowFormer† (6.02) and E-RAFT† (6.75) close the gap to FlowFormer+++ (4.73) at strides 1–4 but still degrade more sharply at stride 8 (13.60 vs. 10.58), indicating event methods lack robustness to large temporal gaps. Cross-dataset evaluation (Table 8) shows that fine-tuning on BlinkVision improves generalization: RAFT drops from 2.08 to 1.69 EPE on Sintel-clean, and E-FlowFormer improves from 2.38 to 1.75 on E-Blender, confirming the dataset's diversity. For point tracking, PIPs++ achieves the best δ_avg (31.34 clean, 28.12 final) among RGB methods, while event-based trackers (AMH, HASTE, DET) score 18–33 δ_avg, confirming a significant gap. For scene flow, CamLiFlow leads RGB methods (1.45 EPE-3d at stride 1, clean) over RAFT-3D (1.91), while the event-based RPEFlow (5.08 EPE-3d) lags considerably.

## Relation to Prior Work

BlinkVision carves new ground by unifying three previously fragmented benchmark lineages: image-based correspondence benchmarks (Sintel, FlyingThings, Kubric, PointOdyssey), event-based benchmarks (BlinkFlow, DSEC, MVSEC), and multi-modal fusion benchmarks (EKubric). No prior dataset simultaneously provides dense per-pixel annotations for optical flow, scene flow, and point tracking across both RGB and event modalities with a large semantic vocabulary. The construction pipeline repurposes Blender's rendering (as in Kubric), DVS-Voltmeter for realistic event simulation, and CLIP-based open-vocabulary labeling to automate semantic annotation at scale, but the key departure is the deliberate assembly of 410 diverse categories with deformable characters, animals, and naturalistic camera motion that prior synthetic benchmarks lacked.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The most impactful finding is that existing event-based methods perform worse than image-based methods on this benchmark, challenging the working assumption that continuous, high-dynamic-range event data should inherently advantage correspondence estimation. The paper attributes this to limited event training data and information loss from voxel-grid quantization, pointing to a clear research opportunity for better event representations and long-range flow algorithms. As a synthetic benchmark, BlinkVision's photorealism and category breadth (33.4% of LVIS) make it a strong proxy for real-world generalization, but its ultimate value depends on whether methods trained here transfer to real event-camera hardware. Open questions include whether the event-voxel quantization bottleneck can be overcome with native event-native architectures and whether the 410-category vocabulary is sufficient to capture long-tail real-world objects.
