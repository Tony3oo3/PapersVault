## Problem

Existing methods for 3D articulation reconstruction rely on category-specific priors, explicit motion parameters, or decoupling objects into static and moving parts, limiting them to single-joint or single-motion-type objects. This paper asks whether multiview images of an object in a few articulation states are sufficient to learn a generalizable, view-invariant representation that can synthesize unseen intermediate states—without 3D supervision, articulation codes, or assumptions about the number or type of moving parts.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240416/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes 100 multiview RGB images per state (dome-like camera arrangement covering the upper hemisphere) across 4 discretized articulation states, along with corresponding camera intrinsics and extrinsics; no 3D ground truth, point clouds, or motion annotations are provided. At inference, the system outputs a view-invariant latent embedding per state and, by linearly interpolating between two state latents, generates the weights of a NeRF that renders novel views and 3D geometry of an intermediate articulation configuration never observed during training.

## Method

LEIA uses a learnable latent dictionary (one embedding per state, stored via torch.nn.Embedding) that conditions a set of hypernetworks to predict low-rank modulation matrices (P^l × Q^l, rank r ≪ K) applied element-wise to the weights of an Instant-NGP-based NeRF, avoiding the cost of directly predicting full network parameters. Training jointly optimizes the latents, hypernets, and base NeRF with a Smooth L1 photometric loss, a BCE foreground mask loss, a latent manifold loss that minimizes the average L2 distance to K nearest neighbors in latent space (enforcing a smooth, interpolable manifold), and depth/occlusion regularizers for rendering quality. At test time, linear interpolation between two state latents is passed through the hypernetwork to produce a new set of low-rank modulations, yielding a NeRF that reconstructs a previously unseen intermediate articulation. This design eliminates the need for part disentanglement or explicit motion priors, allowing a single universal architecture to handle arbitrary combinations of revolute and prismatic joints across multiple object parts.

## Results

On the PartNet-Mobility dataset, LEIA achieves an average PSNR of 29.55 dB, SSIM of 0.96, LPIPS of 0.06, and Chamfer Distance of 0.36 for interpolated state reconstruction, outperforming the PARIS baseline (PSNR 27.81, CD 0.45) by ~1.7 dB in PSNR and ~20% in Chamfer Distance. For multi-part articulation objects specifically, LEIA reaches 30.85 dB average PSNR versus PARIS's 27.39 dB, a gap of over 3 dB. The VanillaInt baseline (simple latent interpolation without manifold loss) achieves 27.81 dB PSNR, confirming that the latent manifold loss and depth/occlusion regularizers contribute meaningfully to reconstruction quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LEIA is compared against PARIS [Liu et al., ICCV 2023], the closest prior method that also uses multi-view images of articulated states without 3D supervision. For single-part articulation objects, LEIA and PARIS perform comparably (28.24 vs. 28.23 dB average PSNR), with PARIS holding a slight edge on a few individual objects (e.g., Storage1: 28.66 vs. 26.69 dB). However, for multi-part articulation objects where multiple parts move independently, LEIA significantly outperforms PARIS by 3.46 dB in average PSNR (30.85 vs. 27.39) and by 0.08 in Chamfer Distance (0.54 vs. 0.62), because PARIS's part-disentanglement pipeline and learned motion prior break down when more than one part articulates. LEIA also matches or beats PARIS on LPIPS across all multi-part objects (e.g., Sunglasses: 0.09 vs. 0.06 for PARIS, but Oven: 0.05 vs. 0.05). The VanillaInt ablation (no manifold loss, no regularizers) shows that the structured latent space is critical: it drops 1.7 dB in PSNR on single-part objects and 0.8 dB on multi-part objects relative to full LEIA.

## Relation to Prior Work

LEIA builds on the NeRF/Instant-NGP architecture for 3D representation and employs a hypernetwork with low-rank modulation (following Schwarz et al. and Skorokhodov et al.) to generate state-specific NeRF weights from a learnable latent dictionary. Its closest conceptual predecessor is PARIS, which also reconstructs articulated objects from multi-view images but explicitly decouples static and moving parts and learns a motion prior, restricting it to single-part articulation. LEIA departs from this lineage by forgoing part disentanglement and motion-type specification entirely, instead relying on a latent manifold loss to impose structure on the embedding space and enable interpolation across states. The autoencoder-style latent dictionary draws from prior work on implicit neural representations for video and 3D shapes (Sen et al.), repurposed here for articulation state encoding.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LEIA demonstrates that multi-part object articulation can be captured without explicit motion modeling or part segmentation, opening a path toward a unified, motion-agnostic framework for articulated 3D reconstruction. However, the method lacks physical consistency guarantees in interpolated states and struggles with severe occlusion, both acknowledged limitations. The experimental scope—12 synthetic objects from PartNet-Mobility and a single real-world scene—leaves open whether the approach generalizes to the long tail of real-world articulations or scales to objects with many joints. Future work could explore whether the latent manifold structure can be combined with physics-based constraints to recover physically plausible intermediate motions.
