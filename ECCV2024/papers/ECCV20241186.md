## Problem

Existing event camera simulators either convert 3-channel videos into events (losing the full optical path and spectral information) or rely on learned event representations with limited generalization. No prior simulator directly interfaces with a 3D scene while modeling the complete light-to-sensor-chip process, including lens optics and quantum efficiency, making it impossible to generate physically faithful asynchronous event streams.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241186/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

PECS consumes a 3D scene (modeled in Blender, exported to PBRT), a physically calibrated lens model (e.g., Prophesee Gen4), and a manufacturer-provided quantum efficiency curve. It produces asynchronous 4D event tuples {x, y, polarity, timestamp} in the original signal domain, as well as multi-task synthetic datasets (RGB frames, event streams, bounding boxes, depth maps) when deployed in Unreal Engine. Unlike video-to-event simulators, the input is never a pre-rendered frame sequence; rays are traced directly from the 3D scene through the lens geometry.

## Method

PECS comprises two core modules. The realistic lens simulation block uses the PBRT renderer with an optimized pupil-sampling strategy: for each sensor pixel, feasible ray directions are pre-computed and the exit pupil is approximated as an ellipse (valid for centrally symmetric event-camera lenses), drastically reducing ray-tracing cost within the lens. The multispectral rendering module computes photocurrent via i_p = C·∫λ·QE(λ)·L(λ)dλ, where L(λ) is the spectral radiance obtained from ray tracing; when the spectrum is represented implicitly (e.g., via neural radiance fields), Monte Carlo integration with a piecewise-constant importance-sampling PDF (Eq. 10–11) is used to minimize variance, while simple numerical integration suffices for discrete-channel spectra. Events are then generated from the logarithmic difference of consecutive photocurrents. Two novel asynchronous spatiotemporal metrics—Chamfer distance and a robust Gaussian distance over normalized {x,y,p,t} tuples—are introduced to quantify fidelity against ground-truth events from a Prophesee Gen4 camera.

## Results

On six real-world recording sequences (rotating disks and translating checkerboards at various speeds and lighting), PECS achieves an average Gaussian distance of 1.545 and Chamfer distance of 3.631, outperforming all four baselines on both metrics. The largest gap is against ESIM, where PECS reduces Chamfer distance from 52.380 to 3.631 (a 93% reduction). In the downstream event-based video reconstruction task on the HS-ERGB dataset, the E2VID network trained on PECS synthetic events achieves 9.678 PSNR, 0.420 SSIM, and 0.447 LPIPS, surpassing V2E (8.997 PSNR) and ESIM (8.889 PSNR).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PECS outperforms all four baselines on both Gaussian and Chamfer distance across all six sequences. Against ESIM (CoRL 2018), the gap is the most dramatic: PECS reduces average Chamfer distance from 52.380 to 3.631, a 93% improvement, because ESIM concentrates all events at the input frame timestamp, lacking temporal continuity. Against VOLT (ECCV 2022), PECS achieves a 0.281 lower Gaussian distance and 13.038 lower Chamfer distance; VOLT provides good temporal continuity but converts three-channel video to grayscale without modeling quantum efficiency or spectrum. Against V2E (CVPRW 2021), PECS reduces Chamfer distance from 11.843 to 3.631 (69% reduction); V2E's hand-crafted pipeline loses optical information by operating on RGB frames. Against ICNS (Frontiers in Neuroscience 2021), the closest competitor, PECS still achieves a 0.142 lower Gaussian distance and 3.104 lower Chamfer distance, attributed to ICNS's lack of multispectral rendering despite its advanced sensor model. In the downstream video reconstruction task (Table 4), PECS-trained E2VID beats V2E by 0.681 PSNR and 1.7% LPIPS on HS-ERGB, confirming that higher simulation fidelity translates to better generalization on real data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant video-to-event paradigm (V2E, ESIM, VOLT, ICNS) by directly interfacing with 3D scenes through physically-based rendering, and from learning-based simulators (EventGAN, domain-adaptive simulators) by operating in the raw signal domain rather than learned representations. It repurposes computer graphics techniques—PBRT ray tracing, pupil sampling, and Monte Carlo integration with importance sampling—for the specific problem of photocurrent estimation in event sensors, incorporating quantum efficiency curves that prior video-based simulators cannot access. The closest prior work (Mou et al. 2022, Tsuji et al. 2023) acknowledges the need for spectral information but still relies on lossy video-to-spectrum conversion, which PECS avoids by rendering multispectrally from the scene.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PECS provides the first physically-grounded pipeline for generating synthetic event data at scale, with the UE integration enabling multi-task dataset generation (detection, depth, reconstruction) that could reduce reliance on expensive real-world event camera recordings. However, the downstream video reconstruction gains are modest (~0.7 dB PSNR over V2E), and evaluation is limited to six controlled laboratory sequences with a single camera model (Prophesee Gen4), leaving open whether the physical modeling advantage persists across diverse real-world scenes, sensor types, and higher-level tasks. A key open question is whether the added computational cost of full ray-traced lens simulation and multispectral rendering is justified over simpler approximations for most practical training scenarios.
