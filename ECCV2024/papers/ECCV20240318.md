## Problem

Annotating multi-object tracking datasets from video is extremely costly due to the temporal dimension, yet existing efficient-labeling approaches either ignore the dense temporal structure of videos or are restricted to single-object settings. No comprehensive solution exists that handles multiple objects across all frames while exploiting the spatio-temporal dependencies inherent in track annotations to minimize human effort.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240318/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes video sequences through a YOLOX detector run at a deliberately low confidence threshold to produce an over-complete set of detection candidates, which are then organized into a graph where nodes are detections and edges are association hypotheses. The output is a complete set of tracking annotations (bounding boxes with identity assignments across time) where the vast majority of nodes and edges are resolved as pseudo-labels by the model, and only a small subset of high-uncertainty nodes/edges are flagged for manual annotation. Annotators operate at the track level rather than the frame level, making decisions such as accepting/rejecting a detection (1 click), refining a box (2 clicks), or associating a node across time (1 click).

## Method

SPAM chains four components: (i) synthetic pre-training on MOTSynth, (ii) pseudo-labeling of the target dataset with the pre-trained model followed by self-training on those pseudo-labels, (iii) uncertainty-based active learning to select which nodes/edges require human annotation, and (iv) a hierarchical GNN model that unifies detection filtering and identity association. The architecture extends SUSHI's graph hierarchy by adding a GNN_node layer that leverages spatio-temporal consistency to filter false positives from the over-complete detection set, enabling high-recall detection without manual box annotation. Active learning computes per-node uncertainty as the maximum entropy over neighboring edge predictions and distributes the annotation budget across hierarchy levels, so a single annotation at a deeper level resolves multiple uncertainties simultaneously. The key insight is that most association and detection decisions are trivially solvable by a strong pre-trained model, so human effort is reserved only for the small fraction of genuinely ambiguous cases, achieving near-ground-truth tracking performance with 3–20% of the manual annotation cost.

## Results

As a tracker, SPAM achieves 67.5 HOTA / 80.7 MOTA / 84.6 IDF1 on MOT17, 65.8 / 76.5 / 81.9 on MOT20, and 64.0 / 89.2 / 63.4 on DanceTrack, outperforming the previous best (SUSHI) by 1.0, 1.5, and 0.7 HOTA points respectively. As a labeling engine, SPAM labels trained into ByteTrack or GHOST match or exceed ground-truth-trained performance using only 3.3% of the annotation effort on MOT17, 10% on MOT20, and 20% on DanceTrack — saving 96.7%, 90%, and 80% of manual labeling cost respectively. Retraining the SPAM model on its own pseudo-labels (with zero human annotation) yields a 4–6 HOTA, 4–6 MOTA, and 4–7 IDF1 improvement over the synthetic-only model across all three datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SPAM outperforms all ten tracker baselines on every reported metric across all three datasets. The closest competitor is SUSHI [12], the graph-hierarchy tracker that SPAM extends; SPAM improves over it by 1.0 HOTA (67.5 vs 66.5) and 1.5 IDF1 (84.6 vs 83.1) on MOT17, 1.5 HOTA and 2.1 IDF1 on MOT20, and 0.7 HOTA on DanceTrack. Among transformer-based trackers, MOTR [72] leads on IDF1 for MOT17 (73.4) but trails SPAM by 11.2 IDF1 points; TrackFormer [44] and MeMOT [10] are 10+ HOTA points behind. Among detection-based trackers, MotionTrack [49] and UTM [70] are the strongest on MOT17/MOT20 but still fall 1.4–2.4 HOTA short of SPAM. For the active-learning comparison (Fig. 5), SPAM's graph-hierarchy uncertainty selection surpasses Coreset [55] (which prioritizes diversity over uncertainty and performs worst), Ensemble [5], and Consistency [21] for both detection and association label selection, staying within 1.2 HOTA of the oracle upper bound. In the labeling-efficiency experiments (Table 5), SPAM labels at 3.3% annotation effort match or slightly exceed GT-trained ByteTrack/GHOST on MOT17, while on the harder MOT20 and DanceTrack, 10% and 20% effort respectively close the remaining gap to GT performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

SPAM extends SUSHI's graph hierarchy formulation for multi-object tracking by adding a GNN_node layer for detection filtering and repurposing the entire hierarchy as a unified labeling framework that handles both detection validation and identity association. The synthetic pre-training strategy builds on MOTSynth, while the pseudo-labeling and uncertainty-based active learning components adapt ideas from the image domain (where they are well-established) to the temporal tracking setting. The paper explicitly positions itself against prior efficient-labeling works for video that either ignore dense temporal dependencies, operate on single objects, or only produce sparse frame-level annotations, arguing that a track-centric, graph-based approach is necessary for comprehensive multi-object labeling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The demonstration that trackers trained on SPAM labels match or exceed GT-trained performance with only 3–20% of the annotation effort is a concrete, well-evidenced recipe (validated across three datasets and two tracker architectures) that could dramatically reduce the cost of building large-scale tracking datasets. The finding that re-identification features transfer well from synthetic to real data while detection does not provides actionable guidance for where to focus annotation budgets. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond pedestrian tracking to other object categories or segmentation tracking, how it scales to very long sequences, and whether the strong dependence on a high-quality synthetic pre-training source limits applicability to domains where realistic simulation is difficult.
