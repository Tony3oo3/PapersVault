## Problem

Video transformer models like ViViT's factorized encoder achieve strong action recognition accuracy but require prohibitive training time and GPU/TPU memory, especially at larger model scales and higher frame counts. This creates a significant barrier for researchers at universities and smaller organizations who lack access to large-scale compute clusters. The paper asks whether training costs can be roughly halved without sacrificing accuracy by strategically freezing part of the model during training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240268/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes a video clip as a sequence of image patches (e.g., 8 to 128 frames, each split into 16×16 patches) and outputs a single action class label via a classification head. The spatial transformer processes each frame independently while the temporal transformer operates across the frame sequence, and the adapter module sits between them as a two-layer MLP that transforms frozen spatial features before temporal processing. At inference time, the adapter adds negligible overhead, so inference time matches standard ViViT.

## Method

SFA-ViViT (Spatial Frozen and Adapter Initialized) is a two-stage training recipe. In Stage 1, the full ViViT factorized encoder is trained on only 8 frames with the spatial transformer initialized from a JFT image checkpoint and the temporal transformer from scratch, serving to initialize the temporal component with short-term temporal context. In Stage 2, the model is fine-tuned on the full frame count (32–128 frames) with the spatial transformer frozen and a compact two-layer MLP adapter inserted between the frozen spatial output and the temporal transformer, so gradients flow only through the adapter and temporal transformer. This cuts total training time to ~56–62% of standard ViViT while matching or slightly exceeding baseline accuracy (up to +1.79% on Moments in Time), and the freed memory enables processing up to 80 frames on a single RTX 2080 Ti or scaling the spatial backbone to ViT-g (1.5B parameters) with 48 frames.

## Results

On Kinetics-400, SFA-ViViT-H achieves 84.42% top-1 accuracy (vs. 84.21% for standard ViViT-H) at only 0.57× the training time (32.3h vs. 56.71h). On Moments in Time, the improvement is most pronounced: SFA-ViViT-H reaches 39.96% top-1 (vs. 38.17%), a +1.79% gain, at 0.59× training time. Across all six benchmarks, the SFA strategy matches or slightly exceeds baseline accuracy while reducing total training time to 56–63% of the original, and enables processing up to 128 frames (ViViT-H) or 48 frames (ViViT-g) on the same hardware where standard training is memory-limited to 96 and 8 frames respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against standard ViViT training (the primary baseline), SFA-ViViT matches or slightly outperforms on all six benchmarks: +0.19% top-1 on K400 (ViViT-H), +0.21% on K600, +1.79% on Moments in Time, and +0.46% on Something-Else, while cutting training time to 56–63% of the original. Against Dualformer, VideoSwin, and Video-FocalNets (Table 7), the SFA variant achieves equal or marginally higher top-1 accuracy (e.g., 81.12 vs. 81.07 for Dualformer-B; 80.52 vs. 80.45 for VideoSwin-B; 84.25 vs. 83.55 for Video-FocalNet-B) at roughly half the training time (0.51×–0.59×). In the efficiency comparison (Table 8), SFA-ViViT-B achieves 82.8% top-1 on Kinetics-400 at 0.58× training time, outperforming ST-Adapter (82.0% at 1×), STTS (80.7% at 1.07×), and SBP (82.2% at 0.92×) on both accuracy and training time. The only method with higher throughput is STTS (129 vs. 88 samples/s), but it requires 1.07× training time and lower accuracy. Overall, SFA-ViViT wins or ties on accuracy in every head-to-head comparison while consistently halving training time.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on ViViT's factorized encoder architecture (Arnab et al., ICCV 2021) and the broader family of late-fusion video transformers (Dualformer, VideoSwin, Video-FocalNets), proposing a two-stage training recipe rather than a new architecture. It departs from prior efficiency-focused methods (ST-Adapter, STTS, TokShift) that reduce FLOPs or parameter count but leave training time largely unchanged, by specifically targeting training time and memory through spatial transformer freezing combined with a lightweight spatial-only adapter. The critical conceptual departure is the explicit two-stage initialization of the temporal transformer from a short-frame pretraining run, a step the authors show is essential and often overlooked in standard video transformer training pipelines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial for resource-constrained researchers: the method enables processing up to 80 frames on university-grade GPUs (vs. 16) and allows a 1.5B-parameter ViT-g spatial backbone to be used with 48 frames (vs. only 8 with standard training), democratizing access to large video transformer models. A key limitation is that inference time is unchanged relative to standard ViViT, so the savings are purely in training cost. Open questions include whether the approach extends to non-factorized architectures (e.g., all-to-all spatiotemporal attention), whether the 8-frame Stage 1 sweet spot generalizes across all datasets, and whether the adapter can be further compressed or replaced by other parameter-efficient fine-tuning mechanisms.
