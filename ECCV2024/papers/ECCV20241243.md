## Problem

Existing backdoor detection methods suffer from two critical limitations: they are highly sensitive to trigger size and fail on stealthy attacks (blending and defense-adaptive attacks) that use large, dynamic triggers, and they rely heavily on benign training examples for trigger reverse engineering, making them infeasible in practical settings like Model Zoo where training data is unavailable. The paper identifies that trojaned behaviors across diverse attack types can be uniformly attributed to a small set of critical neurons forming a 'trojan path,' which prior methods do not exploit.

## Input / Output

The method requires white-box access to the target DNN and a benign seed, which can be a real image from the validation set or simply random noise (pixel values in [0,255]) when no training data is available. It outputs a binary trojaned/benign determination based on whether the Label Change Rate (LCR) exceeds a threshold of 50%, along with the identified trojaned label and the reversed trigger perturbation. The reversed trigger is computed as the partial derivative of the critical trojan path with respect to the input, scaled by a transparency factor μ (default 0.5) and clipped to [0,255].

## Method

CatchBackdoor introduces the concept of a neural path—a sequence of top-k neurons (by gradient-based contribution to prediction changes) connected by data flow that dominates the model's decision. Starting from a benign path constructed from a seed, the method performs differential fuzzing that iteratively maximizes neuron contributions within the path, causing it to converge to a unique critical trojan path regardless of the initial seed. Triggers are then reversed by taking the partial derivative of this converged path with respect to the input, generating testing examples that trigger the trojaned label. Detection is determined by computing the LCR over a batch of reversed examples: if more than 50% of predictions flip to a single label, the model is flagged as trojaned. The convergence property (different benign seeds yield the same critical trojan path) is what makes the method robust to trigger size and independent of benign training data.

## Results

CatchBackdoor is evaluated using Label Change Rate (LCR) with a 50% detection threshold across 3 datasets (MNIST, CIFAR-10, a-ImageNet) and 7 models (LeNet-1/4/5, AlexNet, ResNet20, VGG16/19) under 11 trojan attacks. On stealthy attacks (blending and defense-adaptive), CatchBackdoor achieves roughly 2× higher LCR than most baselines; e.g., on ABE (MNIST/LeNet-1) it reaches 86.0% LCR versus 57.0% for K-Arm and 64.8% for ANP. On Poison Frogs (CIFAR-10/AlexNet), CatchBackdoor attains 91.0% LCR compared to 79.0% (ABS) and 82.6% (K-Arm). When no training data is available (random-noise seeds), LCR remains around 73% on average, still well above the 50% threshold.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CatchBackdoor outperforms all 8 baselines on defense-adaptive attacks: on ABE (MNIST/LeNet-1) it reaches 86.0% LCR vs. 57.0% (K-Arm), 64.8% (ANP), and 81.2% (UNICORN); on DFST (CIFAR-10/LeNet-4) it achieves 61.4% vs. 51.2% (K-Arm) and 55.8% (UNICORN). On blending attacks, CatchBackdoor leads on Poison Frogs (94.0% on MNIST/LeNet-1 vs. 92.1% UNICORN, 90.8% TopoTrigger) and Hidden Trigger (72.6% vs. 70.4% TopoTrigger). However, on clean-label attacks (BP, Sleeper Agent), K-Arm and TopoTrigger slightly edge out CatchBackdoor (e.g., BP: K-Arm 78.2% vs. CatchBackdoor 77.8% on MNIST/LeNet-1; Sleeper Agent: K-Arm 74.8% vs. 78.0% on MNIST but K-Arm leads on CIFAR-10/AlexNet at 86.8% vs. 82.0%). On modification attacks (BadNets, Dynamic Backdoor), all methods perform comparably (82–94% LCR), with CatchBackdoor competitive but not always the top performer. NC and TABOR fail entirely (LCR < 50%) on most blending and defense-adaptive attacks, while ABS and TND also miss several stealthy attacks, highlighting CatchBackdoor's robustness advantage on the hardest attack categories.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant trigger reverse-engineering paradigm (Neural Cleanse, TABOR, ABS, K-Arm) by reframing backdoor detection as a neuron-level path-finding problem rather than a pixel-level trigger search. Prior methods assume triggers are static and small, and require benign examples to guide the search; CatchBackdoor instead identifies a 'neural path' of top-k critical neurons whose contributions drive prediction changes, then uses differential fuzzing to converge from a benign path to a trojan path. The concept of neuron contribution (gradient-based importance) borrows from interpretability, but its application as a convergence target for fuzzing to reverse triggers is novel. The method also repurposes the fuzzing paradigm from software testing into a structured search over neuron activation patterns.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in addressing two real deployment gaps: detection under stealthy attacks with large/dynamic triggers (where prior methods fail entirely) and detection without access to training data (relevant to Model Zoo scenarios). However, the method shows clear limitations on clean-label attacks (BP, Sleeper Agent) where the trojaned label is not the original class, and LCR degrades on deeper architectures (VGG19 on a-ImageNet drops to ~76%). Open questions include whether the convergence property holds for multi-trigger or multi-label backdoors, how the method scales to very large transformer models, and whether the 50% LCR threshold generalizes across domains beyond image classification.
