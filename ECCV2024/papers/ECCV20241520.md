## Problem

3D object detectors trained on one sensor type (e.g., 128-beam mechanical LiDAR) suffer substantial accuracy degradation when transferred to a different sensor mechanism (e.g., solid-state LiDAR or 4D radar), due to domain gaps in point density, intensity, and geometry. Existing domain adaptation datasets conflate sensor differences with geographic and weather variations, making it impossible to isolate and evaluate cross-mechanism domain gaps. No public dataset simultaneously contains multiple sensor mechanisms with synchronized, calibrated, and annotated data for 3D detection.

## Input / Output

The DIG method consumes labeled point clouds from a source sensor and unlabeled point clouds from a target sensor, both cropped to a 120° HFOV and 70.4 m range, then voxelized at 0.1×0.1×0.15 m resolution. The source and target can be any pair among OS128 (128-beam MS LiDAR), XT32 (32-beam MS LiDAR), and M1 (solid-state LiDAR), with 4D radar and camera data also available in the dataset. Output is 9-DoF 3D bounding boxes (center, dimensions, three rotation angles) across 13 categories, evaluated with orientation-aware mAP at 50 precision steps and a proposed mean Closed Gap (mCG) metric aggregating performance over all six cross-sensor transfer directions.

## Method

DIG (Density-Intensity-Geometry) is a three-component adaptation pipeline built on a Voxel-RCNN backbone trained via OpenPCDet. BCN applies a log transform followed by a Box-Cox transformation to each sensor's intensity channel, mapping both source and target intensities into near-normal distributions before rescaling to [0,1], thereby aligning the intensity statistics that differ by up to 2× across sensors (e.g., OS128 uses [0,512] while others use [0,256]). BDS assigns per-point retention probabilities that depend on both the point's beam index (matched to the nearest source-domain beam) and its radial distance, enforcing a similar density-vs-range profile in the target as in the source. GAM generates pseudo-labels on the target domain using the source-trained detector, then in a second training phase progressively replaces source ground-truth labels with high-confidence target pseudo-labels (inspired by GBA), gradually shifting the model's geometric priors toward the target sensor's point-cloud structure. The three modules are applied jointly during the adaptation phase, and ablation shows BCN contributes the largest single gain (up to 52.5 CG), followed by BDS and GAM.

## Results

On the CMD dataset, the proposed DIG method achieves a mean closed gap (mCG) of 42.89 across six cross-mechanism domain adaptation tasks (OS128, XT32, M1), substantially outperforming ST3D (−16.72), ST3D++ (9.23), and DTS (29.22). The best individual task performance is 55.09 CG for XT32→OS128, where DIG reaches 17.39 mAP versus 26.44 mAP for the oracle (target-domain-trained) model. In the ablation study, the BCN (intensity normalization) module contributes the most, adding 52.53 CG on M1→OS128 and 31.71 CG on OS128→XT32, followed by BDS (11.78 and 19.70 CG respectively).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DIG outperforms all three baselines by a large margin on the mCG metric: +59.61 over ST3D, +33.66 over ST3D++, and +13.67 over DTS. ST3D and ST3D++ (teacher-student pseudo-label methods designed for cross-geographical adaptation) perform poorly on cross-mechanism tasks, with ST3D actually yielding negative mCG (−16.72), meaning it degrades performance below direct transfer on average. DTS, which explicitly addresses beam-density differences via Random Beam Re-Sampling, is the strongest baseline and excels when beam counts differ substantially (e.g., 53.51 CG on M1→XT32), but it lacks intensity and geometry handling. DIG's advantage is most pronounced on tasks involving solid-state LiDAR (M1), where its BCN module aligns intensity distributions that other methods either discard or naively normalize. The hardest task for all methods is XT32→M1 (best CG: 25.22 by DIG), suggesting a larger inherent domain gap between low-beam mechanical and solid-state scanning patterns.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by isolating sensor-mechanism differences (beam count, scanning pattern, sensing modality) as the sole source of domain shift, a confound that prior cross-dataset domain adaptation work (ST3D, ST3D++, DTS) could not disentangle from geographic and climatic variation. Existing multi-sensor datasets (PandaSet, K-Radar, VoD, LiDAR-CS) each cover only a subset of sensor types, preventing systematic cross-mechanism evaluation. The DIG baseline repurposes well-known tools—Box-Cox transformation, distance-aware down-sampling, and pseudo-label mixing—into a unified pipeline targeting the three identified gaps (density, intensity, geometry), rather than introducing a new architectural paradigm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CMD is the first dataset to simultaneously provide synchronized, calibrated data from five sensor mechanisms (32-beam, 128-beam, solid-state LiDAR, 4D radar, cameras) with unified 3D annotations, making it a critical resource for the growing automotive-grade sensor ecosystem. The mCG metric offers a more comprehensive evaluation than single-task CG, and the finding that ST3D/ST3D++ can yield negative CG on cross-mechanism tasks highlights that pseudo-label-based methods designed for geographic shifts are ill-suited to sensor-mechanism gaps. Limitations include the modest scale (50 sequences, 10K frames), the exclusion of 4D radar from the main benchmark, and the unresolved XT32→M1 gap, leaving open questions about whether multi-source adaptation or richer geometric alignment can close the remaining domain disparities.
