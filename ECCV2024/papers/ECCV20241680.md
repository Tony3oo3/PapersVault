## Problem

Visual interestingness has been studied predominantly as a single scalar or binary property, yet interest is inherently subjective and varies across observers. No prior work has made this subjectivity tangible or provided a principled way to distinguish images that are broadly appealing (commonly interesting) from those that resonate only with niche communities (subjectively interesting). This paper argues that every image can be interesting to some observer and seeks a data-driven continuum that captures this spectrum.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image, extracts its 768-dimensional CLIP ViT-L/14@336px embedding, and outputs a continuous common interestingness (CI) score in [0,1] via a linear regression head. The coarse version assigns the image to one of 119 semantic partitions and returns the partition's CI score directly; the fine-grained model interpolates within partitions. The training corpus consists of 504,241 liked images from 2,337 Flickr users, with the target label derived from the fraction of unique users who liked images in the same semantic partition.

## Method

The core pipeline partitions the CLIP embedding space into semantic clusters: UMAP reduces 768 dimensions to 7, k-means produces initial partitions, and bottom-up hierarchical clustering (Ward distance threshold θ_image=3 combined with IoU of user-sets threshold θ_CI=0.25) recursively merges similar partitions until convergence, yielding 119 final clusters. The CI score for each partition is the normalized count of unique users who have at least one liked image in that partition, providing a data-driven measure of how broadly a semantic image type is appreciated. For fine-grained scoring, a simple linear regression is trained on the original 768-dim CLIP embeddings with the [0,1]-normalized partition CI scores as targets, enabling per-image prediction without discrete partition assignment. The authors further characterize what drives common versus subjective interestingness by analyzing perceptual features (VILA aesthetic scores, HDR, shallow DoF), denotative features (Google Open Images point labels), and connotative features (CLIP emotion prompts) across three CI-ordered groups.

## Results

The paper introduces a data-driven Common Interestingness (CI) score computed over 119 semantic partitions of 504,241 Flickr images from 2,337 users. The median CI score across partitions is 0.32, with the top partition (aesthetic landscapes/sunsets) reaching CI = 0.65 and niche topics like Star Wars figurines at CI = 0.14. When 149,457 images from Flickr's own 'Interestingness' category are mapped onto the CI partitions, 77.05% fall in the common-interest group, 16.60% in the intermediate, and 6.35% in the subjective group. The trained linear-regression model on 768-d CLIP embeddings reproduces CI scores qualitatively (e.g., Eiffel Tower at sunset scores higher than a LEGO or selfie version; a burger-in-motion scores higher than a static burger), though no standard regression metric (MAE, R²) is reported.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not run a head-to-head quantitative comparison against prior interestingness predictors (e.g., Gygli et al. [26], Constantin et al. [19]); instead it positions its contribution as a new data-driven *definition* rather than a new scoring model. The closest empirical comparison is against Flickr's own 'Interestingness' ranking: 77.05% of Flickr's curated interesting images fall into the authors' common-interest partitions, confirming substantial overlap while also revealing that Flickr's curation includes 6.35% of images the authors classify as subjectively interesting (niche or provocative content). The authors note that their CI definition is independent of absolute view/like counts, which they argue are skewed by recommendation systems, whereas prior work (Gygli et al.) relies on predefined feature cues and human-labeled ratings. The computational model (linear regression on CLIP embeddings) is presented as a proof-of-concept for the CI definition rather than a competitive predictor, and its limitations are acknowledged qualitatively (e.g., failure on context-dependent images like TIME's Top 100 war photos).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the feature-driven approach to visual interestingness exemplified by Gygli et al., who hand-selected cues (complexity, novelty, etc.) and validated them against human labels. Instead, it defines interestingness directly from user behavior data (Flickr likes across 2.5k users), using CLIP embeddings, UAP dimensionality reduction, and hierarchical clustering to discover semantic partitions without predefined features. It also extends the cognitive-psychology framing of bottom-up vs. top-down processing (Berlyne, Shiffrin & Schneider) by operationalizing the 'common' (bottom-up, broadly shared) versus 'subjective' (top-down, individually resonant) axis as a measurable continuum rather than a binary label.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The FlickrUser dataset (500k images, 2.5k users) and the common-interest scoring framework provide a new empirical foundation for studying visual interestingness that is independent of skewed social metrics like views and likes. The clear limitation that the model fails on top-down contextual information (e.g., TIME's Top 100 war or memorial photos) highlights an open problem: incorporating viewer context, cultural knowledge, and situational meaning into interestingness prediction. Expanding beyond a single platform and accounting for negative-stimulus interest (images that provoke but are not 'liked') are identified as important next steps.
