## Problem

CLIP's image-level alignment training causes certain patches in deeper ViT layers to become 'global patches' that aggregate information from the entire image, suppressing the local semantic correlations between neighboring patches. This makes CLIP's dense features poorly suited for pixel-level tasks like open-vocabulary semantic segmentation, where local context discrimination is essential. Existing remedies either add complex trainable decoders or fine-tune CLIP, both of which compromise the zero-shot generalization that makes CLIP attractive.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image (resized to 224×224 for analysis or 336×336 for main results) and a set of text class prompts, processing them through a frozen CLIP ViT-B/16 encoder. The output is a pixel-level segmentation mask where each pixel is assigned a class label, determined by collaborative voting of patch logits within DBSCAN-clustered regions. All model parameters remain frozen throughout; the only learned components are the text embeddings from CLIP's pretraining.

## Method

CLIPtrase is a training-free pipeline with three stages applied to the final layer of CLIP's frozen ViT. First, Semantic Correlation Recovery computes cosine-similarity self-correlation matrices on the query, key, and value streams across all attention heads, averaging them into a single correlation matrix that restores local patch-to-patch semantic relationships disrupted by global patches. Second, Patch Clustering applies DBSCAN (ε=0.7, min_samples=3) to the L2-normalized correlation matrix to group semantically coherent patches, then computes per-cluster prototype attention weights to assign masks via argmax. Third, Denoising identifies residual global patches by flagging any patch whose average inter-patch correlation exceeds its self-correlation, removes them, and re-clusters. The bounded range of cosine similarity (vs. unbounded inner product) is critical for stable DBSCAN behavior, and the pipeline can optionally swap the clustering module for SAM point-prompt segmentation to obtain sharper object boundaries.

## Results

CLIPtrase achieves an average mIoU of 33.53% across 9 segmentation benchmarks, a 22.3% absolute improvement over vanilla CLIP (11.04%). On individual datasets, it reaches 81.20% mIoU on VOC20, 34.92% on PC59, 17.04% on ADE150, and 5.89% on ADEfull. When combined with SAM for boundary refinement, the average mIoU further improves to 34.49%, with gains most visible on VOC20 (82.25%) and PC59 (36.44%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against vanilla CLIP, CLIPtrase improves average mIoU from 11.04% to 33.53% (+22.3%), with the largest relative gains on datasets with many classes (PC459: 1.88→9.95, ADEfull: 0.79→5.89). Among training-free methods, CLIPtrase outperforms SCLIP by 2.03% on average (33.53 vs 31.50) and beats CLIP-Surgery on both reported datasets (COCO-stuff: 24.06 vs 21.9; PC59: 34.92 vs 29.3). Against training-based SOTA, CLIPtrase trails SAN by a notable margin on most benchmarks (e.g., VOC20: 81.20 vs 94; PC59: 34.92 vs 53.8; ADE150: 17.04 vs 27.5; ADEfull: 5.89 vs 10.1), but narrows the gap on ADEfull to within 4.2 points. It also underperforms CAT-Seg and OVSeg on VOC20 (81.20 vs 93.7/92.6) and PC59 (34.92 vs 57.5/53.3), confirming that the training-free constraint still imposes a performance ceiling relative to fine-tuned approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against two dominant paradigms for CLIP-based open-vocabulary semantic segmentation: decoder-based methods (SAN, GroupViT, MaskCLIP) that attach trainable decoders to frozen CLIP features, and fine-tuning-based methods (OVSeg, MAFT) that adapt CLIP through additional training. It departs from both by arguing the root cause of CLIP's poor dense-prediction performance lies in an internal attention pathology — a small set of 'global' patches that absorb attention from the [CLS] token and suppress local patch-to-patch semantic correlation. The diagnosis connects to the concurrent 'attention sinks' and 'registers' literature, but applies it specifically to explain and fix CLIP's segmentation failure through a training-free self-correlation recovery, DBSCAN clustering, and denoising pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete mechanistic explanation for why CLIP underperforms on dense prediction tasks, which is valuable for anyone building on CLIP features beyond image-level classification. The training-free nature (no additional data, no gradient updates) makes the approach immediately deployable and composable with other systems like SAM. However, a significant gap remains versus trainable SOTA (e.g., 17.04 vs. 27.5 mIoU on ADE150), and the method is validated only on ViT-B/16 at two resolutions, leaving open whether the global-patch diagnosis and fix generalize to larger or differently-architected vision encoders. Extending the analysis to detection and panoptic segmentation is identified as future work.
