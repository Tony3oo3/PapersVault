## Problem

Underwater image restoration is severely ill-posed because wavelength-dependent attenuation and backscatter increase exponentially with depth, and no ground-truth clean underwater images exist for supervised training. Existing diffusion-based restoration priors require training data of the desired output distribution, which is unavailable here. Additionally, the paper identifies that a color-only prior is insufficient to guide restoration, since the degradation is depth-dependent and the depth must be jointly inferred.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241659/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single white-balanced linear underwater RGB image at 256×256 resolution and produces both a restored clean RGB image and a per-pixel depth map. Sampling takes approximately 3 minutes per image on an Nvidia A100 GPU. The depth is not a separate pre-estimation step but is jointly refined with the image at every denoising iteration, and the water parameters (attenuation, backscatter, and backscatter color, 9 values total) are also estimated from the single input.

## Method

The core contribution is a joint RGBD diffusion prior trained on in-air outdoor scenes (DIODE, RedWeb-S, HR-WSI, KITTI) by fine-tuning a pretrained unconditional ImageNet diffusion model, replacing the first and last U-Net layers to handle 4 channels (3 RGB + 1 depth). During inference, posterior sampling (DPS-style) is performed where the revised underwater image formation model I = J·exp(−φ_a·D) + φ^∞·(1−exp(−φ_b·D)) serves as the likelihood term, with the 9 water parameters optimized in parallel via gradient descent on the reconstruction loss. A depth-weighted reconstruction loss (multiplying per-pixel loss by estimated depth without backpropagating through it) stabilizes estimation in far regions dominated by backscatter, and two auxiliary losses prevent color saturation and enforce a gray-world assumption. The depth channel uses a smaller guidance scale than the RGB channels, reflecting its different role inside the exponential in the forward model.

## Results

On the NYU-v2 simulation (1449 images with random water parameters), Osmosis achieves PSNR 22.74, SSIM 0.89, and LPIPS 0.06, substantially outperforming all baselines by 4–10 dB in PSNR. On 50 real-world underwater images, Osmosis attains the highest no-reference MUSIQ score of 56.62, surpassing the next-best methods MMLE (56.26) and GDCP (56.25). Notably, the prior was trained exclusively on in-air RGBD data (DIODE, RedWeb-S, HR-WSI, KITTI) and never saw underwater or indoor images, yet it outperforms methods like semi-UIR and the synthetic-dataset approach that were explicitly trained on the NYU-v2 simulation data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On the NYU-v2 simulation, Osmosis (PSNR 22.74) beats every baseline by a wide margin; the closest competitors are UW-Net (18.04), Ucolor (17.92), and semi-UIR (17.82), all of which lag by roughly 4–10 dB. Two of these baselines (semi-UIR [26] and the synthetic-dataset method [34]) were explicitly trained on the same NYU-v2 data, yet Osmosis—trained only on in-air outdoor RGBD—still outperforms them. On real-world images (MUSIQ, 50 images), Osmosis (56.62) edges out MMLE (56.26) and GDCP (56.25), while methods like CWR (39.63), FUnIE-GAN (42.70), and UW-Net (46.02) fall well behind. For depth estimation, Osmosis produces smoother, more detailed maps than GDCP, IBLA, unveiling, UW-Net, monoUWnet, and Depth Anything. A key ablation variant, DA-Osmosis (which replaces the jointly estimated depth with a fixed Depth Anything [58] estimate), drops to MUSIQ 51.23 versus Osmosis's 56.62, confirming that the joint RGBD prior's iterative depth refinement is critical for restoration quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the diffusion posterior sampling framework (DPS) and the parameter-optimization approach of GDP to the underwater restoration setting, but introduces a key departure: rather than using a color-only image prior, it trains a joint RGBD diffusion prior on in-air outdoor datasets (DIODE, RedWeb-S, HR-WSI, KITTI) and couples it with the physical underwater image formation model as a guidance signal. The RGBD prior is initialized from a pretrained ImageNet diffusion model (Dhariwal & Nichol) with input/output layers expanded to four channels, following the modality-extension strategy of DepthGen. Unlike prior supervised or semi-supervised underwater methods that require paired clean/degraded data, Osmosis is fully unsupervised at inference, relying solely on the physical model and the in-air prior to generate posterior samples.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central insight—that a prior trained entirely on in-air RGBD scenes can outperform methods trained on underwater data—addresses the fundamental data-scarcity bottleneck in underwater restoration and suggests a general recipe for inverse problems where clean in-domain data is unavailable. The joint color-depth prior also yields a byproduct depth estimate that surpasses dedicated monocular depth methods, demonstrating that the correlation structure in natural scenes is more informative than domain-specific supervision. Limitations include the ~3-minute per-image sampling time on an A100 GPU, fixed resolution, and the acknowledged domain gap between in-air and underwater appearances, which constrains immediate deployment in real-time applications. Open questions include whether the approach can be accelerated (fewer steps, distilled models), whether it generalizes to other wavelength-dependent degradations (e.g., atmospheric haze), and whether the in-air prior advantage persists at greater depths where the physical model's assumptions break down.
