## Problem

Defending against data-free model extraction (DFME) is challenging because the attacker's query distribution and strategy are unknown to the defender. Existing defenses are either computationally and memory inefficient (requiring multiple backpropagation steps at test time or ensembles of models) or can only detect/verify theft after it has already occurred, leaving no proactive mechanism to prevent extraction.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20242119/cropped_page6_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a standard RGB image x as input and produces a class prediction (soft-label probabilities or hard-label top-1) as output. Unusually, the model weights are not fixed: a small per-layer CNN generates input-dependent perturbation magnitudes that are multiplied with Gaussian noise and added to each layer's weights, so the same victim model instantiates a different function for every query. This means the deployed model has an uncountably infinite function space rather than a single fixed function.

## Method

AAUG introduces learnable, data-specific random weight perturbations to the victim model: for each layer i, a small CNN g_θi(x) produces a perturbation scale that modulates Gaussian noise added to the weights (Ŵ_i = W_i + g_θi(x)·ε). Training combines three objectives: (1) standard cross-entropy on ID data to preserve utility; (2) an uncertainty-guided loss that maximizes perturbation magnitude on simulated OOD data (attack queries) while minimizing it on ID data; and (3) an attack-aware bi-level (max-min) objective where the lower level simulates an attacker training a clone on OOD data, and the upper level maximizes the clone's loss on ID data to ensure poor generalization. The bi-level optimization follows a FOMAML-style approximation for efficiency. At deployment, a fresh random perturbation is sampled per query, making the model a form of random smoothing over weight space that requires an impractically large query budget to fit.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, AAUG reduces the clone model accuracy by up to 40% under the DFME soft-label attack (from 87.36% undefended to 46.82% with ResNet18-8X clone), surpassing the best baseline (P-poison at 78.06%) by ~31 percentage points. On CIFAR-100, AAUG reduces clone accuracy by up to 37% (58.72% → 21.31%), outperforming the best baseline by ~17 points. On ImageNet-100, AAUG reduces clone accuracy by more than 20% (35.89% → 15.58%), exceeding the best baseline by over 14 points. AAUG also maintains victim model utility at 94.21% on CIFAR-10 and 75.73% on CIFAR-100, slightly better than all baselines, while achieving 25–103× test-time speedup over P-poison and GRAD.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AAUG decisively outperforms both P-poison [41] and GRAD [36] across all three DFME attack settings and all datasets. Under the DFME soft-label attack on CIFAR-10, AAUG achieves 46.82% clone accuracy (ResNet18-8X) versus 78.06% for P-poison and 79.33% for GRAD — a gap of roughly 31–32 percentage points. On CIFAR-100 under DFME, AAUG reaches 21.31% versus 38.72% (P-poison) and 39.07% (GRAD), a ~17-point margin. For the hard-label DFMS-HL attack, the advantage is smaller but still substantial: on CIFAR-100, AAUG drops clone accuracy to 45.68% while RandP/P-poison/GRAD all remain above 71%, a gap of ~26 points. The baselines struggle most under hard-label attacks because they preserve the top-1 label for most queries, whereas AAUG's random weight perturbation directly disrupts label consistency. In terms of victim model utility, AAUG matches or slightly exceeds all baselines (e.g., 94.21% vs. 94.65% for GRAD on CIFAR-10), while being 25–103× faster at test time since it requires no per-query optimization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the bi-level optimization framework of MAML/FOMAML, repurposing the inner-outer loop structure from meta-learning into a max-min defensive training objective where the inner loop simulates an attacker fitting a clone model on OOD data and the outer loop degrades the clone's generalization on ID data. It departs from existing model-extraction defenses (output perturbation methods like P-poison and GRAD, ensemble-based methods like EDM) by shifting the defense from test-time output manipulation or multi-model storage to a training-time weight-space perturbation that is data-dependent and stochastic. The conceptual link to random smoothing for adversarial robustness is drawn explicitly, but the application to model-extraction defense with the uncertainty-guided ID/OOD gap objective is new.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 25–103× test-time speedup over output-perturbation defenses makes proactive model-extraction prevention practically deployable in API-serving settings where per-query optimization is infeasible, and the extension to self-supervised models and data-based extraction broadens the method's relevance beyond the narrow DFME setting. The authors acknowledge that clone-model accuracy is not driven to chance level, leaving open whether the defense can be tightened further (e.g., via more expressive perturbation networks or stronger bi-level objectives). A key open question is how the defense holds against adaptive attackers who themselves learn to model the weight-perturbation distribution, a scenario the paper only partially addresses.
