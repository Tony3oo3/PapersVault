## Problem

Standard knowledge distillation assumes the teacher and student operate on identically distributed data, but practical deployment often requires the student to perform in a new target domain that differs significantly from the teacher's source domain. Existing two-stage workarounds (adapt-then-distill or distill-then-adapt) accumulate errors across stages, incur high computational costs from repeated full-network training, and typically still require access to source data. This paper asks whether one can directly distill a compact student in the target domain from a source-domain teacher in a single end-to-end stage without any source data.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a frozen pre-trained teacher network (e.g., ResNet34, ResNeXt101, VGGNet16) trained on a source domain, plus labeled target-domain RGB images; no source-domain data is accessed at any point. The teacher network is augmented with lightweight learnable adapters (two 1×1 conv layers per block, totaling ~2% of teacher parameters) that are the only teacher components updated during training. The output is a compact student network (e.g., ResNet18, ResNeXt50, VGGNet13) that produces class labels on target-domain images, with the student trained via SGD for 120 epochs while adapters use Adam.

## Method

The core mechanism is a Fourier-transform-based knowledge adapter inserted after each block of the teacher network: the spatial feature is adapted by two learnable 1×1 convolutions, then decomposed into phase (domain-invariant semantics) and amplitude (domain-specific statistics) via the Fourier transform. The amplitude is refurbished by blending the target-domain-adapted amplitude with the original source-domain amplitude using a learnable mixing coefficient λ, while the phase is kept immutable to preserve domain-invariant knowledge; the recombined frequency feature is inverse-Fourier-transformed back to spatial form for downstream blocks. A fusion-activation mechanism then transfers the decoupled domain-invariant phase features from all teacher blocks to the student: features are average-pooled to a common size, concatenated, and passed through a two-layer FC bottleneck with sigmoid to produce channel-wise attention weights that gate the feature distillation loss. Training is interactive and one-stage: the teacher adapters are updated on target data with a cross-entropy loss plus a KL-divergence loss that makes the teacher student-friendly, while the student is trained with cross-entropy, a KL-divergence distillation loss from the teacher's soft targets, and the domain-invariant feature transfer loss.

## Results

On four domain adaptation benchmarks (Office-Home, Office-Caltech, PACS, DomainNet), 4Ds achieves an average classification accuracy of 87.80% with the ResNet34→ResNet18 pair, outperforming the standard back-propagation student baseline (84.60%) by 3.20 points and even slightly exceeding the full teacher network trained on target data (87.66%). The method consistently surpasses all two-stage approaches (best: AAD-BAIT at 85.90%), all domain-adaptation knowledge-transfer methods (best: AAD-BAIT at 85.90%), and all 13 traditional KD baselines (best: USKD at 84.98%). Across all four teacher-student pairs tested in Table 1, 4Ds student networks achieve accuracies within 0.1–0.5 points of their corresponding teacher networks trained directly on target data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

4Ds outperforms all 20 baselines across all four datasets. Against the strongest two-stage method (AAD-BAIT, 85.90% avg), 4Ds gains +1.90 points while requiring only target data (no source data). Against the best domain-adaptation knowledge-transfer method (HRKD, 84.69%), 4Ds leads by +3.11 points. Among traditional KD methods, the closest competitor is USKD (84.98%), which 4Ds surpasses by +2.82 points; notably, 4Ds also beats DKD (84.81%) and DIST (83.19%) by 2.99 and 4.61 points respectively. The most striking result is that the compact ResNet18 student trained by 4Ds (87.80%) slightly exceeds the full ResNet34 teacher trained directly on target data (87.66%), demonstrating that cross-domain distillation can produce a smaller model that matches or beats the larger source-domain model. On the hardest dataset (DomainNet), 4Ds achieves 72.46% vs. 69.82% for the standard BP student (+2.64) and 72.82% for the teacher, while the weakest baseline (SP) drops to 55.81%, highlighting the severity of the domain gap that 4Ds effectively bridges.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of knowledge distillation (Hinton et al., 2015) and Fourier-based domain generalization (Lee et al., 2023), repurposing the decompose-adjust-compose paradigm to decouple domain-invariant phase from domain-specific amplitude within a frozen teacher network. It departs from standard KD, which assumes identical data distributions for teacher and student, and from two-stage pipelines (distill-then-adapt or adapt-then-distill) that the authors argue accumulate errors and require source data. The key conceptual move is treating the teacher's Fourier-decomposed features as a knowledge interface: the immutable phase is distilled to the student via a fusion-activation mechanism, while the learnable amplitude adapter (only 2% of teacher parameters) absorbs target-domain statistics, enabling a single end-to-end training loop without any source data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical motivation is strong: deploying compact perception models on edge devices (autonomous driving, smartphones) where the teacher was trained in simulation or a different visual domain, and source data is unavailable due to privacy. By matching or exceeding the accuracy of the full teacher trained directly on target data with a smaller student, 4Ds offers a viable one-stage compression path that avoids the error accumulation and compute overhead of two-stage alternatives. Limitations include evaluation restricted to image classification with ResNet/VGG backbones on four DA benchmarks; whether the Fourier phase-as-semantics assumption holds for denser tasks (segmentation, detection) or for domains with more complex style shifts remains open. The method also assumes the teacher and student share the same label space, which limits applicability to open-set or novel-class scenarios.
