## Problem

Untrained network priors (DIP) for image reconstruction suffer from three intertwined issues: strong sensitivity to architecture choices, overfitting to noisy or partial measurements, and slow per-image optimization. Existing approaches address these separately—through architecture search, early stopping, or parameter reduction—leaving practitioners to laboriously tune architectures per task. This paper asks whether the spectral bias underlying DIP's implicit regularization can be directly modulated in an architecture-agnostic way to simultaneously mitigate all three problems.

## Input / Output

For MRI, the method consumes multi-coil under-sampled k-space measurements (4× or 8× acceleration) along with the forward operator (undersampling mask, 2D DFT, and coil sensitivity maps) and a fixed random noise vector z ~ U(0,1) as the network input. The noise input is preprocessed by either Gaussian blurring (filter size 5, σ ∈ [0.5, 2.0]) or replacement with sinusoidal Fourier features of limited frequency range (L = 4 or 8), which is the key architectural-agnostic modification. The output is a complex-valued reconstructed image in image space; for natural image experiments, the same framework handles RGB inpainting and Gaussian denoising (σ = 25).

## Method

The paper proposes three plug-and-play frequency regularization techniques that operate on the core components driving spectral bias in DIP, without altering the network topology. First, the input bandwidth is constrained by either Gaussian-blurring the noise vector or substituting it with Fourier features of a narrowed frequency range, preventing the network from rapidly fitting high-frequency noise. Second, a Kaiser-Bessel window-based upsampler with adjustable spatial extent M and shape parameter β is inserted at decoder upsampling stages, providing controllable low-pass attenuation beyond what nearest-neighbor or bilinear interpolation offers. Third, each convolutional layer's Lipschitz constant is made learnable via a SoftPlus parameter, and the layer weights are normalized when their ℓ∞ operator norm exceeds the learned bound, with a λ-weighted penalty on the Lipschitz magnitudes in the training loss. Together, these three mechanisms decouple the learning of low and high frequencies, delay overfitting, and improve extrapolation in inpainting-like tasks, enabling compact underperforming architectures (e.g., A2-64) to match or surpass larger counterparts (e.g., A8-64) with up to 90× faster runtime than self-supervised baselines.

## Results

On fastMRI brain (4× undersampling), the regularized A8-64 architecture achieves 33.85 dB PSNR and 0.885 SSIM, surpassing the supervised UNet (33.35 dB, 0.889 SSIM) and approaching ZS-SSL (34.39 dB, 0.878 SSIM) while running ~2× faster (6.6 vs 14.0 min/slice). On fastMRI knee, A2-64 (Ours) reaches 32.07 dB PSNR and 0.781 SSIM, exceeding both the supervised UNet (31.15 dB, 0.776 SSIM) and ZS-SSL (32.00 dB, 0.773 SSIM). On out-of-domain Stanford 3D FSE data, A2-64 (Ours) achieves 31.43 dB PSNR vs 29.16 dB for the trained UNet, demonstrating superior generalization. Combining the method with self-validation early stopping further reduces runtime from ~6 min to 0.3 min/slice with negligible quality loss.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against ZS-SSL (the strongest self-supervised baseline), the proposed A8-64 (Ours) trails by 0.54 dB PSNR on brain (33.85 vs 34.39) but matches or exceeds it on knee (32.07 vs 32.00 for A2-64 Ours), while running roughly 2× faster (6.6 vs 14.0 min/slice). Against the supervised UNet, the regularized architectures win on both datasets: A8-64 (Ours) beats UNet by 0.50 dB on brain and A2-64 (Ours) by 0.92 dB on knee, and the advantage widens on out-of-domain Stanford FSE data (31.43 vs 29.16 dB PSNR). Against other untrained baselines (DIP, Deep Decoder, ConvDecoder), the gains are substantial—A2-64 (Ours) improves over vanilla A2-64 by 2.65–4.45 dB PSNR and over ConvDecoder by 0.26–2.48 dB across datasets. The vanilla (unregularized) versions of the same architectures underperform all baselines except CS-ℓ₁, confirming that the frequency regularization is the source of the improvement rather than the architecture itself. On out-of-domain data, A2-64 (Ours) + early stopping achieves 31.30 dB PSNR in just 0.3 min/slice, outperforming the trained UNet (29.16 dB) at ~100× faster inference.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Deep Image Prior (DIP) framework and the growing body of work explaining DIP's implicit regularization through spectral bias (Chakrabarty & Maji, Rahaman et al., Shi et al.). It departs from prior untrained-network MRI methods (ConvDecoder, Deep Decoder) that address overfitting via architectural underparameterization, and from ZS-SSL which relies on self-validation early stopping, by instead proposing architecture-agnostic frequency-domain interventions: bandwidth-constrained inputs (drawing on Fourier features from INRs/NeRF), controllable Kaiser-window upsampling (extending Liu et al.'s finding that unlearnt upsampling drives spectral bias), and learnable per-layer Lipschitz bounds (adapting Gouk et al.'s regularization). The key conceptual departure is unifying three previously separate challenges—architecture sensitivity, overfitting, and runtime—under a single frequency-control perspective, rather than treating them as independent problems requiring distinct solutions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a few lines of code (Gaussian blur on the noise input) can close the performance gap between underperforming and well-performing architectures, eliminating the need for laborious architecture search in untrained MRI reconstruction. The 90× runtime reduction over ZS-SSL while matching or exceeding its accuracy makes zero-shot self-supervised MRI reconstruction substantially more deployable in clinical settings. Open questions include whether the hyperparameters (blur sigma, Fourier feature order L, Kaiser window M and β) can be automated or learned per-slice, whether the approach extends to 3D volumetric reconstruction or non-inpainting inverse problems, and whether the spectral-bias modulation generalizes to generative tasks where high-frequency detail is desirable rather than an artifact to suppress.
