## Problem

Range-based near neighbor search in high dimensions is critical for plagiarism detection, where missing even one similar item can lead to unfair evaluation. Existing approximate methods (LSH, graph-based, KD-tree) sacrifice recall or require extensive parameter tuning, while prior group-testing approaches for NN search assume a large gap between the least-related neighbor and the most-related non-neighbor, or are computationally inefficient due to repeated pool reconstruction. This paper addresses the need for an exact, parameter-free, streaming-capable range-based NN search that exploits the natural sparsity of high-dimensional similarity distributions.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a unit-normalized, non-negative query feature vector (1000-dim softmax output from VGGNet) and a database of N similarly formatted vectors, along with a pre-specified cosine similarity threshold ρ. It outputs a binary flag array of length N marking each database vector as a neighbor (dot product ≥ ρ) or non-neighbor, guaranteeing 100% precision and recall. Unlike KNN-based competitors, the output is a complete set of all qualifying indices rather than a fixed-size list, and the method requires no choice of K or other hyperparameters.

## Method

The core mechanism is an adaptive binary splitting group testing procedure where pools are formed by summing (or taking element-wise max of) database vectors, with all pool vectors precomputed offline via cumulative sums. Starting from the full-database pool, the algorithm recursively splits positive pools (dot product with query ≥ ρ) into two halves, computing the right-half dot product directly and deriving the left-half by subtraction, until single vectors remain. Pools testing negative are pruned entirely, exploiting the empirical fact that softmax-based features yield a sharply decaying similarity distribution where most vectors are dissimilar to any query. A theoretical analysis models individual dot products as truncated normalized exponential (TNE) random variables and applies the CLT to derive closed-form expressions for pool pruning probability and expected number of dot product computations per query, showing the algorithm is agnostic to the TNE parameter λ while its performance is governed by it.

## Results

On four large-scale image datasets (MIRFLICKR, ImageNet, IMDB-Wiki, InstaCities; 500K–1M vectors, d=1000), Our-Sum achieves 100% precision and recall at ρ=0.8 while reducing mean query time by 4×–120× over exhaustive search (e.g., 54 ms vs. 1222 ms on MIRFLICKR; 4.9 ms vs. 1276 ms on ImageNet). In the streaming setting, Our-Sum's index build time is 3.8 s for 80% of a 1M-vector database (vs. 15 s for IVF, 700 s for HNSW, 2000 s for FLANN-R), and its mean insertion time is 0.28 ms per new vector (vs. 2.2 ms for IVF, 236 ms for HNSW). The method requires no hyperparameter tuning and all data structures are built purely offline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Our-Sum dominates on the accuracy axis: it is the only method (besides exhaustive search and FALCONN++) that achieves 100% precision and recall across all four datasets, while being 9.4× faster than FALCONN++ on MIRFLICKR (54 ms vs. 509 ms) and 104× faster on InstaCities (58 ms vs. 6015 ms). Against SCANN—the closest competitor in raw query time (23 ms vs. 54 ms on MIRFLICKR)—Our-Sum trades a modest speed penalty for guaranteed exact results, whereas SCANN's accuracy depends on a tunable bit-rate parameter. FLINNG [21], the other GT-based method, matches Our-Sum's recall but suffers catastrophic precision (0.268–0.372), meaning most retrieved items are false positives. FALCONN [15] is 5.6× slower than Our-Sum on MIRFLICKR (302 ms vs. 54 ms) with lower recall (0.954). HNSW [32] is competitive on ImageNet (90 ms) but degrades severely on IMDB-Wiki (1076 ms) where the neighbor count is large, and it loses 1.5–4.8% in both precision and recall. In the streaming setting, Our-Sum's 0.28 ms insertion time is 8× faster than IVF, 840× faster than HNSW, and 93× faster than FLANN-R, while maintaining 100% P/R (FLANN-R drops to 0.31–0.59 recall).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the classical group testing (GT) framework—specifically Hwang's generalized binary splitting—to the range-based near neighbor search problem, departing from prior GT-based NN methods (FLINNG, Local Orthogonal-Group Testing, and the ACMMM 2014 framework) in three key ways: it guarantees exact (100% precision and recall) results rather than approximate ones, it eliminates the requirement that true neighbors be significantly more similar than all non-neighbors, and it uses precomputed cumulative sums to create pools in O(1) per-vector updates rather than rebuilding indices. Conceptually, it also draws on the compressed sensing literature for its theoretical analysis (TNE distribution + CLT), while the practical implementation is a simple iterative binary splitting over a stack of pool intervals. The method is positioned against the full spectrum of approximate NN search techniques (LSH, graph-based, KD-tree, inverted multi-index, vector quantization) as an exact alternative that avoids their parameter-tuning burden and recall losses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

For applications demanding perfect recall—plagiarism detection in image or music competitions, forensic retrieval—this paper provides a parameter-free, streaming-capable method that achieves 4–120× speedup over exhaustive search with zero accuracy loss, a combination no prior approximate method offers. The main limitations are that it currently supports only cosine distance, requires a sharp decay in the similarity distribution (satisfied by softmax features but not all descriptors), and demands O(Nd) memory for cumulative sums. Open questions include extending to billion-scale databases, reducing the frequency of 'false positive' pools (where a pool sums to a high dot product despite all members being dissimilar), and generalizing beyond cosine similarity to other distance metrics.
