## Problem

Existing uncertainty quantification methods for imaging inverse problems produce pixel-wise uncertainty maps that are difficult to interpret and poorly connected to the actual downstream imaging goal (e.g., detecting a pathology). There is no principled way to quantify how much additional uncertainty the measurement-and-reconstruction process contributes to a specific task output, nor a mechanism to adaptively decide when enough measurements have been collected.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241599/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes accelerated MRI k-space measurements (at nested acceleration rates R ∈ {16, 8, 4, 2}), a calibration set of (recovered-image, true-task-output) pairs constructed by applying the measurement model and reconstruction network to ground-truth images, and a trained soft-output classifier (ResNet-50 for meniscus tear detection). It produces a conformal prediction interval [b_l, b_h] ⊂ ℝ in the task-output space (e.g., [0,1] for binary classification) guaranteed to contain the true task output f(x) with probability ≥ 1−α; the interval width serves as the task-uncertainty metric, and in multi-round mode the output is additionally a stopping decision when the width falls below a user-set threshold τ.

## Method

The core contribution is applying split conformal prediction in the task-output space rather than the image space: a nonconformity score is defined between the true task output z = f(x) and the recovered task output f(x̂), and the empirical quantile of calibration scores yields an interval with a marginal coverage guarantee. Three conformal schemes are proposed: (1) Absolute Residuals (AR), which gives a fixed-width interval around f(x̂); (2) Locally Weighted Residuals (LWR), which scales the interval by the standard deviation of task outputs across p posterior samples from a conditional normalizing flow, making the width adapt to per-image difficulty; and (3) Conformalized Quantile Regression (CQR), which uses empirical α/2 and 1−α/2 quantiles of the posterior-sample task outputs as adaptive bounds. A multi-round acquisition protocol exploits local adaptivity by collecting nested k-space measurements and stopping as soon as the interval width drops below τ, achieving higher average acceleration rates than a fixed-round strategy.

## Results

In the multi-round accelerated MRI protocol (α=0.01, τ=0.1), the locally adaptive CQR method achieves the highest average acceleration rate R̄=6.762, followed by LWR at R̄=5.157, compared to R̄=2.000 for the non-adaptive AR method, while all three maintain empirical coverage near the target 0.99. LWR achieves the lowest average maximum center error (0.020±0.002) versus AR (0.032±0.017) and CQR (0.044±0.009). Across acceleration rates R∈{16,8,4,2}, the average mean interval length decreases monotonically with more measurements, and both LWR and CQR consistently produce shorter intervals than AR at every R. Empirical coverage across 10,000 Monte-Carlo trials matches the theoretical Beta-Binomial distribution, confirming the marginal coverage guarantee holds in practice.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three conformal prediction schemes—AR (absolute residuals, non-adaptive, point-estimate based), LWR (locally weighted residuals, adaptive, posterior-sampling based), and CQR (conformalized quantile regression, adaptive, posterior-sampling based)—on the same accelerated MRI pipeline. Both adaptive methods (LWR and CQR) substantially outperform the non-adaptive AR baseline in average acceleration: LWR achieves R̄=5.157 and CQR achieves R̄=6.762, versus R̄=2.000 for AR, meaning they stop collecting measurements after fewer rounds on average. LWR produces the tightest center error (0.020) but is more sensitive to small posterior sample sizes p, while CQR is more robust to small p and achieves the highest acceleration at the cost of slightly larger center error (0.044). All three methods achieve empirical coverage within 0.003 of the target 1−α=0.99, confirming that the adaptive methods do not sacrifice statistical guarantees for shorter intervals. The AR method is inherently limited because its interval width is x̂-invariant, so it cannot adapt to easier or harder reconstructions and must always collect the maximum number of measurements to meet the threshold.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes split conformal prediction (Lei et al., 2018) and conformalized quantile regression (Romano et al., 2019) from their standard role in pixel-wise or discrete-label uncertainty to a new target space: the continuous output of a downstream task (e.g., a soft-output classifier). Prior UQ work in imaging inverse problems—Bayesian neural networks, posterior sampling, and pixel-wise conformal prediction—produces per-pixel uncertainty maps that the authors argue are poorly aligned with clinical decision-making. The multi-round acquisition protocol, which uses the conformal interval width as a stopping criterion for data collection, has no direct precedent in the conformal prediction or accelerated MRI literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By shifting UQ from pixel space to task-output space, the paper offers a more clinically interpretable uncertainty measure with distribution-free statistical guarantees, and the multi-round protocol demonstrates that locally adaptive conformal intervals can nearly triple the effective MRI acceleration rate (R̄≈6.8 vs. 2.0) while maintaining coverage. Key limitations include reliance on a pre-defined downstream task, validation on a single pathology (meniscus tear) and single modality (knee MRI), and the use of only aleatoric uncertainty from the posterior sampler. Open questions include extension to multi-class or 3D tasks, incorporation of epistemic uncertainty, and rigorous clinical validation of the threshold τ and error-rate α choices.
