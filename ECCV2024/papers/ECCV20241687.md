## Problem

Pseudo-labelling methods are typically evaluated on benchmark datasets where labelled and unlabelled data share the same distribution, masking a critical vulnerability: when the two sets come from different distributions, certain feature channels exhibit dramatically different activation levels due to set-private information. This paper identifies these as 'disguising channel activations' and shows that even state-of-the-art pseudo-labelling models (e.g., MIC) overfit to them, causing the model to make decisions on features that collapse on the unlabelled set and thereby exacerbating the confirmation bias loop.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241687/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a labelled image set (with ground-truth class labels) and an unlabelled image set drawn from a different distribution, and produces task-specific outputs: class labels for image classification, pixel-level segmentation masks for semantic segmentation, and bounding boxes for object detection. The component modeller is injected as a plug-in after specific backbone layers (e.g., after each residual block in ResNet, inside the ASPP module in MiT, or before the R-CNN head), modifying the forward feature tensor without changing the input resolution or output format of the base model.

## Method

The core mechanism models each channel's activation as a mixture of two independent components — a set-private component F_s and a task-related component F_t — parameterised by a learnable per-channel mixture proportion α produced by a small proportion estimator network. A joint adversarial training objective (a set discriminator on F_s with BCE loss and a task classifier on F_t with CE loss) forces α to reflect how much of each channel's activation is driven by set-private versus task-related information. Channels whose α is closest to 0.5 are identified as disguising activations (containing substantial amounts of both components), and a channel masking regularisation zeros out the top-k such channels before they propagate to subsequent layers. This masking is guided by the learned α rather than being random or based on a single component, and it introduces no additional cost at inference time, making it a drop-in regularisation for any existing pseudo-labelling pipeline.

## Results

On Office-Home (12 cross-domain settings, ResNet-50), the proposed method (Ours(MIC)) achieves 72.8% average top-1 accuracy, outperforming the MIC baseline (71.5%) by 1.3% and the best non-pseudo-labelling method SDAT (71.9%) by 0.9%. On VisDA-2017 (ResNet-101), it reaches 87.4% average accuracy (+0.9% over MIC), and on DomainNet it achieves 46.2% (+1.3% over MIC). For semantic segmentation (GTA→Cityscapes, MiT backbone), the method attains 75.9 mIoU (+1.1% over MIC), and for object detection (Cityscapes→Foggy Cityscapes, Faster R-CNN), it reaches 48.4 mAP (+0.8% over MIC).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all 17 compared baselines across every dataset and task. Among pseudo-labelling methods, it surpasses MIC (the primary baseline) by 1.3% on Office-Home, 0.9% on VisDA-2017, 1.3% on DomainNet, 1.1% mIoU on GTA→Cityscapes segmentation, 0.8% mAP on Foggy Cityscapes detection, and 0.9% mIoU on Synthia→Cityscapes. It also beats other pseudo-labelling approaches: FixMatch (67.7%), CKP (68.7%), IA (69.5%), and CAPLS (70.6%) on Office-Home by 3.1–5.1%. Among non-pseudo-labelling domain adaptation methods, it exceeds SDAT (71.9% on Office-Home, 84.3% on VisDA, 42.8% on DomainNet) and MCC (71.3%, 78.8%) by 0.9–3.4% depending on the dataset. For semantic segmentation, it outperforms DAFormer (68.3/60.9 mIoU) and HRDA (73.8/65.8 mIoU) on GTA and Synthia settings respectively. For object detection, it surpasses SIGMA (44.2 mAP), SADA (44.0), SWDA (35.3), MTOR (35.1), and DAFaster (32.0) by 4.2–16.4 mAP.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on constructing 1-Lipschitz neural networks through layer-wise constraints. Previous approaches have focused on individual techniques (spectral normalization, orthogonal parameterization), but no prior work systematically compared them under identical training protocols. The unified algebraic perspective from recent work [15] informs the analysis, showing that several methods share common mathematical foundations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This systematic comparison provides practitioners with clear guidance on which 1-Lipschitz construction method to choose based on their constraints (training budget vs. certified accuracy). The finding that several methods are mathematically equivalent suggests future work should focus on truly novel approaches rather than incremental variations. Open questions include whether these methods scale to deeper networks and larger datasets like ImageNet.
