## Problem

Existing 3D reconstruction methods (3D Gaussians, NeRF) capture appearance and geometry but cannot estimate physical properties or simulate elastic object dynamics. MPM-based approaches like PAC-NeRF assume known material models with a single global parameter, limiting adaptability to real heterogeneous objects, while per-particle MPM is computationally prohibitive. The core gap is an expressive yet efficient physical model that supports gradient-based inverse parameter estimation from video.

## Input / Output

The method consumes sparse multi-view RGB videos (synthetic: 10 viewpoints × 30 frames at 512×512; real-world: 3 viewpoints at 1920×1080 for dynamics plus 50–70 static images) along with camera calibration parameters and SAM-derived object masks. For real-world data, static and dynamic captures are in different coordinate systems and are aligned via a learned scale-rotation-translation registration. The output is a simulatable 3D object: 3D Gaussian kernels whose positions are driven by a spring-mass system with per-anchor stiffness, initial velocities, and boundary-condition parameters, enabling future-frame prediction and simulation under arbitrary initial states or environmental edits (gravity, ground stickiness, object softness).

## Method

Spring-Gaus embeds a 3D Spring-Mass model directly into 3D Gaussian kernels: volume sampling extracts anchor points from Gaussian centers, which are connected by k-nearest-neighbor springs with a nonlinear force law (Hooke's law generalized by exponent p_k) and a learnable soft vector η that modulates effective connectivity. Gaussian kernel positions are updated each timestep via Inverse Distance Weighting interpolation from anchor positions, and the entire simulation (semi-implicit Euler integration + differentiable rendering) is end-to-end differentiable for gradient-based parameter identification. A key simplification assigns one stiffness scalar per anchor (rather than per spring) and fixes mass and damping globally, reducing the optimization to N_A stiffness values plus a shared κ and boundary parameters. Optimization is decoupled into three stages—static 3D Gaussian reconstruction, appearance refinement to compensate for IDW-induced drift, and dynamic reconstruction—so that physical parameter learning is not confounded by geometry fitting.

## Results

On synthetic dynamic reconstruction (Table 2), Spring-Gaus achieves a mean Chamfer Distance of 0.85×10³ mm² and EMD of 0.040, substantially outperforming PAC-NeRF (CD 2.11, EMD 0.052), Dynamic 3D Gaussians (CD 1305, EMD 1.116), and 4D Gaussian Splatting (CD 4.43, EMD 0.095). On future prediction (Table 1), Spring-Gaus achieves mean CD 5.16, EMD 0.095, PSNR 17.06 dB, and SSIM 0.897, surpassing PAC-NeRF on all four metrics (CD 17.94, EMD 0.134, PSNR 15.77, SSIM 0.870). The method also generalizes to real-world multi-view videos and supports simulation under edited boundary conditions, initial velocities, and gravity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against PAC-NeRF [18], Spring-Gaus wins on every reported metric: in dynamic reconstruction, mean CD drops from 2.11 to 0.85 (2.5× improvement) and EMD from 0.052 to 0.040; in future prediction, mean CD drops from 17.94 to 5.16 (3.5×), EMD from 0.134 to 0.095, PSNR rises from 15.77 to 17.06 dB, and SSIM from 0.870 to 0.897. Against Dynamic 3D Gaussians [26], Spring-Gaus is dramatically superior in geometric accuracy (mean CD 0.85 vs. 1305, EMD 0.040 vs. 1.116), reflecting that Dy-Gaus optimizes per-frame Gaussian motion without any physical model. Against 4D Gaussian Splatting [44], Spring-Gaus again leads on both CD (0.85 vs. 4.43) and EMD (0.040 vs. 0.095), confirming that a physics-based spring-mass prior yields more accurate geometry than purely data-driven 4D representations. The only per-object exception is the Chess sample in dynamic reconstruction, where PAC-NeRF's CD (0.96) slightly beats Spring-Gaus (1.80), though Spring-Gaus still wins on the overall mean.

## Relation to Prior Work

Spring-Gaus builds directly on 3D Gaussian Splatting (Kerbl et al., 2023) for its appearance and geometry representation, replacing the implicit NeRF-based representations used by PAC-NeRF (Li et al., 2023) and DANO (Le Cleac'h et al., 2023). Its key conceptual departure is substituting the Material Point Method (used by PAC-NeRF) with a learnable 3D spring-mass system, motivated by MPM's high computational cost (tens of thousands of dense particles) and its assumption of a known global material model. The paper also extends the dynamic scene reconstruction line (D-NeRF, Dynamic 3D Gaussians, 4D Gaussian Splatting) by coupling the 3DGS representation with a differentiable physics model, and extends DANO's rigid-object differentiable simulation to the elastic regime.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The work addresses a practical gap: existing 3D reconstruction methods (3DGS, NeRF) produce visually faithful but physically inert assets, while MPM-based physics-informed methods are computationally expensive and assume homogeneous materials. The spring-mass formulation offers a lightweight, fully differentiable alternative that supports per-anchor stiffness, enabling heterogeneous elastic behavior without material-model assumptions. Limitations include evaluation on only a handful of real-world samples (qualitative only), restriction to elastic objects (no rigid, fluid, or viscoelastic regimes), and reliance on multi-view video. Open questions include whether the spring-mass model can capture large-deformation or topological-change behaviors, how the method scales to scenes with multiple interacting objects, and whether the decoupled optimization pipeline generalizes to longer sequences or noisier captures.
