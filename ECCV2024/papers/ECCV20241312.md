## Problem

Narrative videos (movies, TV series) contain rich contextual elements—character identities, dialogues, storylines, and temporal event dependencies—that existing video understanding models handle only through task-specific architectures and tailored modules. While LLM-based video models have emerged, they rely on pre-defined visual-textual templates that are inflexible when multiple heterogeneous contexts (images, subtitles, plots, history clips) must be jointly associated. The paper asks whether a single generative model can flexibly accommodate diverse video contexts and tasks through a unified instruction-following framework.

## Input / Output

The model consumes an interleaved sequence of video frames (sampled at 8–64 frames depending on dataset), character photos, Whisper-transcribed subtitles, IMDb plot paragraphs, and prior video clips with their narrations, all arranged in a free-form instruction template. Visual inputs are encoded as frame-level [CLS] tokens (not patch tokens) to keep sequence length manageable, with subtitles and plots capped at 512 tokens. The model produces a single autoregressive text response—ranging from a class label or character name to a full audio description or multiple-choice answer—terminated by an EOS token, with a default maximum response length of 64 tokens.

## Method

MovieSeq builds on the LLaVA framework, using LLaMA-2 as the language backbone with LoRA (rank 16, alpha 16) applied to all linear layers (only 0.12% additional trainable parameters) and CLIP ViT-B/16 as the visual encoder. The key architectural modification is extracting frame-wise [CLS] tokens rather than patch tokens and projecting them through a single-layer transformer with temporal position encoding, enabling efficient handling of multiple variable-length video clips and images within one sequence. The core design insight is a generic instruction format (context X_ctx + question X_q → answer X_a) where the context field can be any combination of video, character images, subtitles, retrieved plot paragraphs, or history clips, allowing the same question to yield different answers under different contextual arrangements. Plot sampling uses retrieval-augmented sentence-paragraph matching for specific queries and a temporal-ratio-based windowing strategy for general captioning. Training uses standard autoregressive loss on the answer tokens only, with multi-task co-training across six datasets to improve generalization.

## Results

On the MAD audio description benchmark, MovieSeq with all contexts (video, character images, plot, history) achieves CIDEr 24.4 and R@5/16 of 51.6, surpassing the strongest baseline AutoAD-II (CIDEr 19.5, R@5/16 50.8) by 4.9 CIDEr. On CMD video-text retrieval, adding subtitles to video input boosts geometric mean from 9.2 to 38.9, outperforming TESTA (R@1 21.5) with R@1 of 25.8. On TVC video captioning, MovieSeq with video+subtitles+character images achieves CIDEr 64.8, exceeding SWINBERT (55.4) and All-in-one (56.3). On MovieQA, MovieSeq with video+plot reaches 66.41% accuracy, surpassing SSCB with plot (56.70%) and HMMN (46.28%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MovieSeq consistently outperforms task-specific baselines across all six benchmarks. On MAD audio description, MovieSeq (CIDEr 24.4) beats AutoAD-II (19.5) by 4.9 CIDEr without any additional pretraining data, while AutoAD-II relies on a dedicated character recognition module trained on external databases. On Movienet actor identification, MovieSeq achieves 81.4 F1-score, surpassing the purpose-built TFM Decoder (79.1) and CLIP cosine similarity (51.0). On CMD retrieval, MovieSeq(vid,sub) achieves geometric mean 38.9 and R@1 25.8, outperforming TESTA (R@1 21.5) and LF-VILA (geo. mean 26.4), though TESTA edges out slightly on R@10 (50.7 vs. 50.3). On TVC captioning, MovieSeq with all contexts (CIDEr 64.8) exceeds SWINBERT (55.4) and All-in-one (56.3) by 9–13 CIDEr points. On MovieQA, MovieSeq(vid,plot) reaches 66.41% accuracy, beating SSCB with plot (56.70%) by nearly 10 points. On LVU classification, MovieSeq(vid,sub) achieves 68.1% average accuracy, marginally surpassing S5 (67.0%) but underperforming on the Scene sub-task (65.9 vs. 73.5), indicating subtitles help relational/speech understanding but can distract from purely visual scene recognition.

## Relation to Prior Work

MovieSeq builds directly on the LLaVA visual instruction tuning framework [49], extending it from single visual inputs to flexible interleaved multimodal sequences (images, subtitles, plots, video history). It departs from the task-specific paradigm of prior movie understanding work (e.g., AutoAD-II [23] training a dedicated character recognition module, or LF-VILA [70] for retrieval) by unifying diverse tasks under one generative LLM with a shared instruction format. The interleaved input mode draws conceptual inspiration from Flamingo [2] and Otter [37], but repurposes it for narrative video contexts rather than few-shot demonstrations, and introduces practical data-construction pipelines (Whisper for subtitles, RAG for plots, temporal ratio sampling) that prior LMMs for video did not address.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a single generative model with LoRA-tuned LLaMA-2 can handle five distinct video tasks across six benchmarks without task-specific architectures, offering a practical and extensible recipe for video context understanding. The key limitation is the architectural compromise of using CLS tokens instead of patch tokens to manage variable-length multimodal inputs, which the authors acknowledge constrains visual fidelity. Open questions include how to scale to truly long contexts (full movies) without degrading visual representation, and whether the interleaved instruction format generalizes beyond narrative video to other domains (e.g., social media with avatars, ASR transcripts, and metadata). The finding that multi-task co-training yields only modest gains (+1.5 avg) suggests that data quality and context design matter more than task diversity for this regime.
