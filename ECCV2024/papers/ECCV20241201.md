## Problem

Existing weakly-supervised video temporal grounding methods rely on proposal-based frameworks that only model coarse-grained, proposal-level cross-modal alignment, failing to capture fine-grained frame-word correspondence needed for accurate boundary localization. Additionally, their performance is severely constrained by the quality of pre-defined moment proposals, which are generated uniformly regardless of video content. This paper addresses both limitations by recasting the grounding problem as a cooperative game between individual frames and words.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241201/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an untrimmed video encoded by a pre-trained 3D CNN (C3D for ActivityNet Caption, I3D for Charades-STA) downsampled to T=200 or T=64 frame features, and a text query embedded via GloVe with M=20 word tokens. Instead of generating moment proposals via sliding windows, the model directly produces query-guided frame-wise relevance scores from which start and end timestamps are inferred by thresholding and expanding around the highest-scoring frame. Only video-level matched pairs are provided as supervision—no frame-wise or boundary annotations are used.

## Method

The core mechanism models each video frame and query word as a player in a multivariate cooperative game, using the Banzhaf (or Shapley) value to measure each player's marginal contribution to self-modal semantics and the Banzhaf interaction index I_B to quantify the additional benefit of a frame-word coalition over their independent contributions. The cross-modal game is extended to three levels—word, phrase (via SQAN), and sentence—capturing multi-granularity alignment. Learnable headers (convolution + self-attention) are trained with Kullback-Leibler divergence soft supervision to approximate the expensive game-theoretic computations, enabling fast inference without enumerating coalitions. Training combines video-level contrastive loss, masked-query reconstruction loss, and game-guided self-relevance/alignment losses, with the final moment predicted by expanding from the highest-scoring frame using a score-ratio threshold.

## Results

On Charades-STA, Ours(Banzhaf) achieves 54.17% R@1 (IoU=0.5) and 88.93% R@5 (IoU=0.5), outperforming the best weakly-supervised baseline CPL by 4.53% on R@5 IoU=0.5. On ActivityNet Caption, Ours(Banzhaf) reaches 35.65% R@1 (IoU=0.5) and 70.26% R@5 (IoU=0.5), surpassing all compared WS methods. The Shapley variant performs comparably, achieving 54.31% R@1 (IoU=0.5) on Charades-STA and 35.80% R@1 (IoU=0.5) on ActivityNet Caption. Cross-dataset evaluation further shows Ours(Banzhaf) outperforms LCNet by 4.42–11.21 points across both transfer directions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed game-theoretic framework outperforms all 14 baselines on both datasets across every reported metric. Among weakly-supervised methods, the closest competitors are CPL [125] (49.24% R@1 IoU=0.5 on Charades-STA) and DM2 [70] (51.39%), which the Banzhaf variant surpasses by 4.93 and 2.78 points respectively. On ActivityNet Caption, Ours(Banzhaf) leads CPL by 4.28 points on R@1 IoU=0.5 (35.65 vs. 31.37) and by 27.13 points on R@5 IoU=0.5 (70.26 vs. 43.13), indicating a particularly large advantage in multi-moment retrieval. Earlier WS baselines such as SCN [49], WSTAN [104], ICVC [7], MARN [87], CRM [38], CNM [124], ACN [109], VCA [105], and LCNet [112] trail by 15–31 points on R@1 IoU=0.5 (Charades-STA), reflecting the gap between proposal-based and proposal-free frame-wise scoring. Even the strongest fully-supervised method DRN [115] (53.09% R@1 IoU=0.5) is surpassed by the weakly-supervised Ours(Banzhaf) (54.17%), demonstrating that the game-theoretic fine-grained alignment compensates for the absence of frame-level annotations. Cross-dataset transfer (Table 2) further confirms the advantage: Ours(Banzhaf) achieves 18.74% R@1 IoU=0.7 (A→C) and 20.37% R@1 IoU=0.5 (C→A), exceeding LCNet [112] by 6.68 and 9.72 points respectively, attributed to the proposal-free design's dataset-agnostic flexibility.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant proposal-based paradigm in weakly-supervised video temporal grounding (WS-VTG), where methods like CPL, LCNet, and SCN generate moment candidates via sliding windows and score them against queries. Instead, it recasts the frame-word alignment problem through multivariate cooperative game theory, drawing on the Banzhaf interaction index (Grabisch & Roubens, 1999) and prior applications of Shapley/Banzhaf values to vision-language pre-training (Li et al., 2022; Jin et al., 2023). The self-modal game for contextual enhancement and the multi-level cross-modal game (word, phrase, sentence) are novel architectural choices that replace proposal selection with direct frame-wise scoring, while the reconstruction loss and contrastive training follow established WS-VTG practice (CNM, SCN).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By eliminating the proposal-based framework, this work addresses a practical bottleneck: proposal quality is dataset- and video-dependent, and the proposal-free design yields notably better cross-dataset generalization (e.g., 18.74 vs. 14.32 R@1 IoU=0.7 on A→C). The game-theoretic interaction index also provides an interpretable signal for which frame-word pairs cooperate, which could inform debugging and analysis of grounding failures. However, the training cost is higher (0.94s/sample vs. 0.27s for CPL) due to coalition sampling, and the method's reliance on 5,500 Monte Carlo samples for game-value estimation raises scalability questions for longer videos or real-time deployment. Whether the game-theoretic framing generalizes beyond WS-VTG to fully-supervised or unsupervised grounding settings remains open.
