## Problem

Existing flow-based VFI algorithms train on randomly cropped patches where motion complexity is uniformly distributed, causing different complexity levels to interfere with each other during optimization. This results in suboptimal interpolation for regions with fast motion, occlusion, or complex local motion, particularly when test data falls outside the training distribution. The paper identifies through SI/TI analysis that PSNR degrades sharply with increasing temporal complexity, confirming that a single flow network cannot simultaneously optimize for all motion regimes.

## Input / Output

The method takes two consecutive video frames (I_0, I_1) as input and produces an interpolated intermediate frame I_t along with a 3-channel Motion Complexity Map (MCM) of size H×W, where each channel encodes the per-pixel probability of easy, medium, or hard motion complexity. Internally, three separate flow networks (trained on complexity-classified data) each produce an optimized warped intermediate frame, which are concatenated into a 3-channel tensor fed to MCENet. The method is designed as a plug-in for existing flow-based VFI pipelines (demonstrated on RIFE and EMA-VFI) and operates at resolutions from 720p to 4K.

## Method

IAM-VFI uses a two-stage training procedure. In Stage 1, the training dataset (Vimeo90K and its 2× EDSR-upscaled version) is overlap-cropped into 256×256 patches, each classified into easy/medium/hard by a Temporal Information threshold, and three separate flow networks are trained on each subset to specialize for that motion regime. In Stage 2, a U-Net-based Motion Complexity Estimation Network (MCENet) with a proposed Motion Attention Block takes the three optimized warped frames as input and outputs the MCM, which is used to linearly combine the three frames into the final intermediate frame. A novel complexity loss replaces standard GT supervision: the MCM-weighted frame is compared via L1 to a frame weighted by a ground-truth MCM (constructed by assigning each pixel to the complexity class with minimum reconstruction error), encouraging the network to learn spatially adaptive complexity routing. The MCM is additionally fed as extra input to the synthesis network to guide residual estimation.

## Results

Applying IAM-VFI to RIFE achieves the best PSNR on UVG (32.17 dB, +1.04 dB over RIFE†) and Xiph 4K (34.20 dB, +0.95 dB over RIFE†), outperforming all 11 baselines including EBME, IFRNet, and VFIformer. When applied to EMA-VFI, the method achieves the best PSNR on HD (32.89 dB), all four SNU-FILM subsets, Xiph 2K (37.01 dB), and Xiph 4K (34.29 dB), with a +0.93 dB gain on UVG over the original EMA-VFI. The runtime increases from 48.3 ms (original RIFE) to 87.5 ms (full IAM-VFI) at 720p on a single RTX A6000.

## Baselines & Comparisons

IAM-VFI applied to RIFE outperforms all 11 baselines on UVG (32.17 dB vs. EBME's 31.79 dB, the closest competitor) and Xiph 4K (34.20 dB vs. M2M-PWC's 33.77 dB), with gains of 1.04 dB and 0.95 dB over the original RIFE respectively. EMA-VFI+Ours achieves the best PSNR on HD (32.89 dB, beating EBME's 32.44 dB by 0.45 dB), all four SNU-FILM subsets (e.g., Extreme: 25.59 vs. ABME's 25.23), and Xiph 2K (37.01 dB, beating M2M-PWC's 36.40 dB). Kernel-based methods (SepConv, AdaCoF, CDFI) and transformer-based VFIformer are consistently outperformed by 1–2 dB on most benchmarks, with VFIformer and several others unable to run on UVG or Xiph due to OOM. The closest flow-based competitors are EBME and IFRNet, which trail EMA-VFI+Ours by 0.45–0.60 dB on HD and SNU-FILM Hard/Extreme. The data classification ablation (Table 2) confirms that training on complexity-stratified patches alone improves PSNR by up to 0.5 dB on the matching complexity class, validating the core premise that motion complexity matters for optimal flow estimation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on RIFE (ECCV 2022) and EMA-VFI (CVPR 2023) as base flow-based VFI architectures, repurposing their flow and synthesis networks as plug-in components. Conceptually, it draws from region-aware restoration work (SROBB, ClassSR) that uses per-region complexity labels to guide processing, but transplants this idea into the VFI setting where the relevant complexity axis is temporal (motion) rather than spatial. The key methodological departure from prior VFI training is the use of Temporal Information (TI) to partition training patches into easy/medium/hard subsets, training separate flow networks on each, then introducing MCENet (a U-Net with Motion Attention Blocks) to produce a per-pixel Motion Complexity Map that adaptively blends the three specialized outputs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical and well-motivated limitation: a single flow network trained on randomly cropped patches cannot simultaneously optimize for static, moderate, and fast motion regions within the same frame. The modular design (drop-in for any flow-based VFI) makes the approach broadly applicable, and the consistent 0.4–1.0 dB gains across HD, SNU-FILM, UVG, and Xiph benchmarks demonstrate robustness. However, the practical cost is significant: three separate flow networks must be trained and run at inference, increasing runtime from ~48 ms to ~87 ms at 720p, and the authors acknowledge that a single unified flow network handling all complexities remains an open problem. The approach also inherits the limitation that TI-based classification is a coarse, global proxy for local motion difficulty, and the complexity map is learned only on Vimeo90K, raising questions about generalization to out-of-distribution content.
