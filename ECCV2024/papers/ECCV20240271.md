## Problem

Existing image forgery detection and localization (IFDL) methods suffer from poor out-of-distribution generalization and high false alarm rates on authentic images, largely because they derive detection scores as a byproduct of pixel-level localization predictions. While CLIP demonstrates strong zero-shot discrimination between authentic and forged images, it lacks forgery-specific prompts and its image encoder is insensitive to local tampering regions, making direct application to IFDL non-trivial.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240271/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image (H×W×3) as input and produces two outputs: a pixel-level forgery localization mask (H×W×1) from a U-Net decoder, and an image-level detection score computed as the softmax-normalized cosine similarity between the CLS token and a pair of learned text embeddings in the CLIP latent space. Notably, the detection score is computed independently of the localization map, breaking the sequential dependency that causes false alarms in prior methods. Noise features are additionally extracted via BayarConv and processed through a separate ViT-small backbone before being injected into the frozen CLIP encoder.

## Method

CLIP-IFDL freezes the entire CLIP model and introduces two trainable modules. First, Instance-aware Dual-stream Prompt Learning (IDPL) initializes a pair of continuous prompts from the word embeddings of 'real' and 'fake', then refines them in two stages: a Prompt Adjustment Network (PANet, a 2-layer bottleneck) modulates prompts by the image's CLS token, and an Embedding Adjustment Network (EANet, a Transformer decoder) further adjusts the text embeddings using image features as context, with a small learnable α (initialized to 10⁻³) preventing image features from overwhelming the prompt signal. Second, a Forgery-enhanced Noise Adapter (FENA) extracts noise features via BayarConv + ViT-small, fuses them with CLIP image features at layers {0,3,6,9,12} through cross-attention, maintains a persistent memory across injection points, and connects to the frozen backbone via zero-initialized linear layers to avoid disrupting CLIP priors. A lightweight U-Net decoder (1.12M parameters) with FiLM modulation from the learned text features produces the final localization mask, and training uses a combined Dice loss for localization and binary cross-entropy on the CLIP-space similarity for detection.

## Results

On the pre-trained localization setting (Table 1a), CLIP-IFDL achieves the best pixel-level AUC on four of five benchmarks: Coverage (94.3%), CASIA (92.5%), NIST16 (89.7%), and IMD20 (97.8%), trailing only PSCC-Net by 0.6% on Columbia (97.6% vs. 98.2%). In the fine-tuned setting (Table 1b), it attains the highest AUC and F1 on all three datasets (e.g., 91.3%/77.9% on CASIA, 99.8%/89.3% on NIST16). For image-level detection on CASIA-D, CLIP-IFDL reaches 99.83% AUC and 98.75% F1, outperforming all baselines. On the challenging real-world IMD20 dataset, it achieves 0.678 AUC with 73.9% specificity and 68.7% sensitivity, the best balance among all methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CLIP-IFDL outperforms all six pre-trained baselines on Coverage, CASIA, NIST16, and IMD20, with the largest margins over ManTraNet (e.g., +17.8% AUC on IMD20) and SPAN (e.g., +12.8% on CASIA). The closest competitor is SAFL-Net, which trails by 0.7–1.3% AUC on most datasets but is notably weaker on IMD20 (96.5% vs. 97.8%). PSCC-Net is the only method that beats CLIP-IFDL on Columbia (98.2% vs. 97.6%), likely due to distributional similarity between its synthetic training data and Columbia. In the fine-tuned setting, CLIP-IFDL surpasses all baselines including RGB-N, SPAN, PSCC-Net, ObjectFormer, HiFi-Net, and SAFL-Net on both AUC and F1 across Coverage, CASIA, and NIST16. On the detection task (CASIA-D), it leads SAFL-Net (99.50% AUC) by 0.33% and ObjectFormer (99.70%) by 0.13%, while on the challenging COVER and IMD20 detection benchmarks it achieves the highest AUC (0.732 and 0.678) with substantially better specificity–sensitivity balance than all competitors.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research threads: noise-based forgery localization (RGB-N, ManTraNet, ObjectFormer) and CLIP-based prompt learning (CoOp, prefix-tuning). It departs from prior IFDL methods in a key architectural decision: rather than deriving detection scores from localization predictions (the standard pipeline in ManTraNet, SPAN, ObjectFormer, etc.), it decouples detection into a CLIP text-image similarity computation and localization into a separate U-Net decoder. The noise adapter (FENA) repurposes the BayarConv noise extraction and zero-initialized adapter pattern from T2I-Adapter and side-adapter networks, but applies them to a frozen CLIP ViT backbone rather than a diffusion model, bridging the information gap between noise and RGB domains via cross-attention fusion.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The decoupling of detection from localization directly addresses a practical pain point in forensic deployment: high false-alarm rates on authentic images that can disrupt media pipelines. By leveraging frozen CLIP priors, the method gains open-world generalization to unseen forgery types (demonstrated on Faceshifter and CocoGlide), which is critical as diffusion-based forgeries proliferate. However, the approach still depends on BayarConv noise features, which may be less informative for certain forgery types (e.g., subtle semantic edits or high-quality diffusion outputs), and the prompt learning is limited to a binary real/fake distinction rather than fine-grained forgery taxonomy. Open questions include whether the CLIP-based detection signal remains robust under heavy compression or adversarial perturbations, and whether the framework extends naturally to video or multi-modal forgery scenarios.
