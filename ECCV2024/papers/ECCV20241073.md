## Problem

Existing zero-bit watermarking methods (DNN0B, SSLWM) rely on known or pre-trained embedding functions, making them vulnerable to signature theft (an attacker can run the same DNN to recover and reuse a watermark) and to watermark erasure via recursive overwriting. Additionally, these methods lack calibrated statistical guarantees on their detection performance, offering no principled control over the false positive rate.

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image (MSCOCO, 118k train / 5k test) and a 32-dimensional unit vector (the secret key signature, SKS) generated in the same semi-hemispherical domain as the SKN's natural response to that image. It produces a watermarked image with an L2-norm-bounded adversarial perturbation (ε = 6.3×10⁻⁴, yielding ~32 dB PSNR) and, at detection time, three p-values: a length p-value (χ² test), an angle p-value (hypersphere uniformity test), and a combined p-value. The SKN is a ResNet18 with its final layer replaced by 32 linear neurons, trained so its output on clean images follows a standard multivariate normal distribution.

## Method

The framework has three stages. First, a secret key network (SKN) is trained via a Wasserstein loss plus a variance-matching loss so that its output on natural images follows a standard multivariate normal (SMVN) distribution, establishing the statistical foundation for detection. Second, watermark embedding is performed by a modified PGD attack (using the gradient value rather than its sign, with an L2 perturbation bound) that minimizes an adversarial loss with two terms: one driving the SKN output length to a target value (t_l = 63, corresponding to p = 10⁻⁴) and another driving the output direction to align with the SKS (target cosine = 1). Third, detection uses two complementary hypothesis tests — a χ² test on the recovered vector's squared norm (HT4L) and a uniformity test on the unit hypersphere for the angle between the recovered vector and the SKS (HT4A) — combined via Theiler's p-value multiplication, yielding a detector with a user-specified false positive rate. The key security insight is that the SKN itself (a ~100M-parameter network trained with a loose distributional objective) serves as a non-duplicable secret key: watermarks embedded by one SKN are undetectable by a differently initialized SKN of the same architecture, preventing both signature theft and recursive overwriting.

## Results

On MSCOCO, the proposed SKN framework achieves 99.48% detection rate (combined p-value < 0.05) on watermarked images with a false positive rate of 4.96% on clean images, closely matching the target significance level. In terms of imperceptibility at target PSNR 32, the method attains SSIM of 0.9768, substantially outperforming DNN0B (0.9103) and SSLWM (0.9026). For robustness, average detection rates reach 99.02% (Gaussian noise), 99.40% (Gaussian blur), and 99.22% (JPEG), while rotation (85.89%) and cropping (90.58%) show more moderate performance. The method also runs fastest among compared approaches at 0.67 s/image for watermarking and 0.005 s/image for detection.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DNN0B [36] (Vukotić et al., Entropy 2020), the proposed method dramatically outperforms on model-fake security (4% vs. 100% false rate) and watermark-removal resistance (>5 vs. 2 overlapping watermarks), while also achieving higher SSIM (0.9768 vs. 0.9103) and better robustness to Gaussian noise (99.02% vs. 92.20%), Gaussian blur (99.40% vs. 81.88%), and JPEG (99.22% vs. 95.65%). DNN0B retains a slight edge on random-fake security (0.08% vs. 1.94%) and cropping robustness (97.90% vs. 90.58%). Against SSLWM [10] (Fernandez et al., ICASSP 2022), the proposed method wins on nearly every metric: model-fake security (4% vs. 100%), SSIM (0.9768 vs. 0.9026), Gaussian noise (99.02% vs. 40.88%), Gaussian blur (99.40% vs. 88.33%), JPEG (99.22% vs. 78.12%), and runtime (0.67 s vs. 7.13 s). Against HiDDeN [45] (Zhu et al., ECCV 2018), the comparison is limited to imperceptibility at PSNR 32, where the proposed method achieves higher SSIM (0.9768 vs. 0.9531) and lower MAE (0.0134 vs. 0.0204). The only notable weakness is rotation robustness (85.89%), where SSLWM (86.43%) and DNN0B (84.36%) are comparable, attributed to the spatially concentrated perturbation pattern of the adversarial attack.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the zero-bit watermarking line (DNN0B, SSLWM) that uses pretrained CNNs as embedding spaces and adversarial attacks for signature injection, but departs fundamentally by treating the DNN itself as a secret key rather than a publicly known function. It also bridges traditional statistical watermarking (which offers calibrated hypothesis tests but relies on known linear transforms) with deep learning watermarking (which offers non-linear capacity but lacks detection guarantees) by training a ResNet18 to produce outputs following a standard multivariate normal distribution, enabling chi-squared and angle-based hypothesis tests. The Wasserstein-distance training objective for enforcing the SMVN output distribution is a new application, and the modified PGD with L2-norm perturbation and gradient-value updates is tailored specifically to simultaneously control output length and direction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a real security gap in zero-bit watermarking: existing methods using publicly available pretrained CNNs are vulnerable to signature theft and watermark overwriting, whereas the secret SKN makes both infeasible without access to the network weights. The calibrated statistical guarantee (controllable false positive rate via significance level α) is a meaningful addition that deep-learning watermarking methods lack, giving practitioners a principled detection threshold. Limitations include weaker robustness to rotation and cropping compared to baselines (attributed to spatially concentrated perturbations), evaluation on a single dataset (MSCOCO) and architecture (ResNet18), and the practical challenge of securely storing and deploying a 100M-parameter secret network. Open questions include whether the SMVN training objective generalizes to larger backbones, whether multi-bit watermarking can be incorporated, and how the framework performs under generative-model-based image editing at scale.
