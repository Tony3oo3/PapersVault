## Problem

Current physics-based humanoid controllers trained from MoCap demonstrations cannot simultaneously handle sparse, under-specified multi-modal directives, recover from failure states, and blend motions from different sources into novel combinations. The paper identifies three jointly-necessary capabilities—Catch-up (resynchronizing from out-of-sync or fallen states), Combine (blending upper/lower body segments from distinct motions), and Complete (filling in unspecified joint details)—and notes that no existing method achieves all three under a single framework.

## Input / Output

The controller receives the current humanoid pose q_t and a masked motion directive (q̂_{t+1:t+H}, I_{t+1:t+H}) over a sliding window of H future steps, where the binary mask I selects which pose dimensions (root position/orientation/velocity, joint Euler angles, local or global joint positions) and which specific joints are constrained. This unified masking scheme encodes diverse modalities: full MoCap (all channels unmasked), video keypoints (only global positions, possibly with occluded joints masked), VR controllers (limited keypoints), joystick (root velocity and orientation only), or text-to-motion output. The output is a vector of joint-level set points a_t fed to a fixed-gain PD controller that produces actuator torques for the physics simulator.

## Method

MHC is trained via reinforcement learning on an augmented dataset M⁺ that includes all upper/lower-body combinations of the 87 base MoCap clips, with each episode using a randomly concatenated directive of 2–4 sub-sequences and a randomly sampled initial pose (10% fallen poses) to force catch-up behavior. The key training mechanism is selective masking: each episode applies a random channel-level mask (choosing which pose channels to expose) and optionally a joint-level mask (randomly hiding 0–100% of position joints), so the controller learns to generate natural motion from arbitrarily sparse directives. The reward combines a hierarchical tracking term (root height → orientation → velocity → joint angles, where each finer term activates only if the coarser one exceeds 0.9), a style term from an ensemble of five part-wise GAN discriminators (upper-right, upper-left, root, lower-body, full-body), and an energy cost penalizing action changes and torques. This multi-objective formulation with masked demonstrations is the core insight: the masking regularizes the policy to be robust to missing information, while the adversarial style reward ensures natural motion quality that pure tracking rewards cannot provide.

## Results

On the Reallusion MoCap dataset (MReal), MHC achieves 51.05 mm MPJPE and 92% success rate on imitation, 59.24 mm MPJPE and 89% success on catchup, and 95.09 mm MPJPE and 48% success on combine. On the ASE rollout dataset (MASE), MHC achieves 56.23 mm MPJPE and 97% success on imitation, 63.46 mm MPJPE and 98% success on catchup, and 60.95 mm MPJPE and 78% success on combine. The most dramatic gains appear in the combine task, where MHC more than doubles the success rate of the ASE baseline (48–78% vs. 17–25%). Ablations confirm that the style reward, random pose initialization, and motion augmentation are each individually critical for the respective CCC capabilities.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole quantitative baseline is ASE [20] (Won et al., ACM TOG 2020), a state-of-the-art multi-clip physics-based controller trained with adversarial skill embeddings. MHC outperforms ASE on every task and dataset combination: on imitation, MHC reduces MPJPE by roughly 58% on MReal (51.05 vs. 123.51 mm) and 44% on MASE (56.23 vs. 100.28 mm), while raising success rates from 60→92% and 77→97% respectively. On catchup, MHC again halves MPJPE (59.24 vs. 125.96 on MReal; 63.46 vs. 102.50 on MASE) and lifts success from 55→89% and 70→98%. The largest relative gap is in the combine task, where ASE's success rate is only 17–25% (it was never trained on upper/lower-body combinations), whereas MHC reaches 48–78% thanks to its augmented training set M⁺. Notably, ASE does not support under-specified directives at all, so the complete-capability experiments (channel-level and joint-level masking) have no direct ASE comparison; MHC is evaluated standalone and retains stable performance even when up to 75% of joints are masked.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the line of multi-clip physics-based humanoid controllers (ASE, PMP, C-ASE) that distill MoCap data into reusable skills, but departs from them by introducing a unified masked-directive formulation that simultaneously addresses catch-up, combination, and completion—capabilities that prior works handle in isolation or not at all. The selective channel- and joint-level masking of training directives is a novel training augmentation that repurposes the standard tracking-reward + adversarial-style-reward recipe into a multi-modal controller without architecture changes. The downstream integration with DAC-MDPs for zero-shot high-level planning extends the data-driven planning literature, which had not previously been coupled with a reusable low-level motion controller.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MHC provides a single, versatile low-level controller that can be directed by VR, joystick, video, or text inputs without retraining, which is practically important for game engines, VR applications, and humanoid robotics where motion capture is incomplete or noisy. The zero-shot DAC-MDP integration demonstrates that high-level task specification (e.g., 'go to goal while crouch-walking and taunting') can be achieved without any fine-tuning, opening a path toward composable, goal-driven character behavior. Limitations include evaluation on only 87 skills and a single humanoid model, qualitative (not quantitative) multi-modal results, and untested generalization to truly novel motions outside the training distribution. Open questions include scaling to larger skill libraries, real-time deployment on physical robots, and whether the masking strategy generalizes to non-humanoid or multi-agent settings.
