## Problem

Increasing CNN kernel sizes to approximate the global receptive field of Vision Transformers quickly saturates: ConvNeXt plateaus at 7×7, and even sophisticated large-kernel methods like RepLK and SLaK become over-parameterized well before reaching a global field. The paper asks whether signal-processing tools can scale the receptive field of convolutions without the quadratic parameter growth that plagues naive kernel enlargement.

## Input / Output

WTConv operates on standard RGB images at task-appropriate resolutions (224×224 for ImageNet classification, 2048×512 for ADE20K segmentation, 1280×800 for COCO detection) and produces the corresponding task outputs: class labels, pixel-wise segmentation masks, or bounding boxes with instance masks. The layer is a drop-in replacement for depth-wise convolutions, so it inherits the spatial dimensions and channel counts of the surrounding architecture; the only configuration knobs are the number of wavelet levels per stage and the small kernel size (e.g., 5×5) applied at each level.

## Method

WTConv replaces a single large depth-wise convolution with a cascade of small-kernel convolutions performed in the wavelet domain. At each of ℓ levels, the Haar wavelet transform decomposes the low-frequency component into four sub-bands (LL, LH, HL, HH) at half spatial resolution; a small k×k depth-wise convolution is then applied to all four sub-bands, and the inverse wavelet transform reconstructs the output. Because the IWT is linear, outputs from successive levels are simply summed, yielding a multi-frequency response. The key scaling property is that the receptive field grows as 2^ℓ·k while trainable parameters grow only linearly in ℓ (ℓ·4·c·k²), so a global receptive field on 224×224 input requires only ~2M depth-wise parameters versus ~4M for GFNet. The repeated decomposition of low frequencies inherently emphasizes them, which the paper shows translates to improved shape bias, corruption robustness, and effective receptive field coverage.

## Results

On ImageNet-1K (120-epoch, ConvNeXt-T), WTConv achieves 81.7% top-1 accuracy with only 2.04M depth-wise parameters and a [160,80,40,20] receptive field, outperforming all large-kernel baselines. Under the 300-epoch schedule, WTConvNeXt-T/S/B reach 82.5/83.6/84.1% top-1, surpassing both ConvNeXt and Swin at every scale with minimal parameter overhead (e.g., +4M params, +0.1 GFLOPs for the Small variant). On ADE20K segmentation, WTConvNeXt-S achieves 49.0 mIoU (vs. 48.7 for ConvNeXt-S), and on COCO detection, WTConvNeXt-S reaches 52.6 AP^box / 45.6 AP^mask (vs. 51.9 / 45.0 for ConvNeXt-S).

## Baselines & Comparisons

WTConv outperforms all seven baselines on ImageNet-1K top-1 accuracy. Against ConvNeXt (the base architecture), WTConv adds 0.7% top-1 (81.7 vs. 81.0) with only 2.04M additional depth-wise parameters. Against RepLK [10], WTConv achieves +0.2% accuracy (81.7 vs. 81.5) while using roughly half the depth-wise parameters (2.04M vs. 3.84M) and a much larger receptive field (160 vs. 31 at the first stage). Against SLaK [34], WTConv again gains +0.2% with fewer parameters (2.04M vs. 2.52M). Against GFNet [43], WTConv achieves +0.5% accuracy with only ~47% of the depth-wise parameters (2.04M vs. 4.29M), and GFNet's over-parameterization causes it to underperform even the vanilla ConvNeXt baseline on the scalability study (Table 5). Against VAN [20], WTConv gains +0.6% with a far larger effective receptive field. Against FFC [4] (evaluated on ImageNet-50/100/200 with MobileNetV2), WTConv 1-level achieves 86.24% on IN-50 vs. FFC's 84.92%, and WTConv 3-levels reaches 83.84% on IN-200 vs. FFC's 82.96%. In the 300-epoch comparison, WTConvNeXt also surpasses Swin Transformer at all three scales (T/S/B), e.g., 84.1% vs. 83.5% for the Base variant.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the large-kernel convolution line (RepLK [10], SLaK [34]) by replacing the quadratic parameter growth of larger kernels with a logarithmic one, achieved through cascaded Haar wavelet decomposition. It also builds on the multi-frequency decomposition idea from wavelet-based architectures [1, 14, 30, 33], but departs from those by proposing a lightweight, drop-in layer for depthwise convolutions rather than a fully customized network. The conceptual link to RepLK's output-summing strategy is explicit: WTConv sums convolutions across wavelet levels rather than across kernel sizes, and the shape-bias observation from [10] motivates the emphasis on low-frequency components.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

WTConv offers a practical, parameter-efficient path to global receptive fields in pure CNNs, which is valuable for practitioners who want transformer-like global mixing without the architectural overhaul. The demonstrated improvements in shape bias, corruption robustness, and downstream tasks (segmentation, detection) suggest broader utility beyond classification. However, the runtime overhead from sequential WT-conv-IWT operations limits immediate deployment, and the modest accuracy gains (0.2–0.5% on ImageNet) mean the technique is most compelling in parameter-constrained or robustness-sensitive settings. Open questions include whether richer wavelet bases or parallelized implementations can close the runtime gap and whether the approach transfers to non-depthwise or 3D convolutional settings.
