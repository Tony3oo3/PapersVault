## Problem

Traditional cameras capture orders of magnitude more visual information than needed for lightweight tasks such as counting people, monitoring occupancy, or estimating traffic speed, raising privacy concerns and making self-powered deployment impractical. The paper asks: what is the smallest number of visual measurements sufficient for a given task, and how can a camera be physically constructed to produce exactly those measurements?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241715/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The camera does not produce an image; instead, each freeform pixel (a 16×16 mm optical mask paired with a photodiode, 70°×70° FOV) yields a single scalar measurement representing a linear projection of the scene. As few as 2–8 such measurements are fed into a small inference network that outputs task-specific values (e.g., integer people count, binary zone-occupancy flags, continuous traffic speed in mph). The entire 24-pixel prototype can read out and BLE-transmit all measurements at 30 fps using only harvested ambient light (~600 lux), with no battery or external supply.

## Method

The core insight is that a freeform pixel—implemented as an arbitrary transmittance mask M(x,y) in front of a photodetector—performs a linear projection of the scene, so a set of N such pixels is equivalent to the first fully-connected layer of a neural network. The full system (optical first layer + inference network with leaky-ReLU hidden layers) is trained end-to-end on video from an auxiliary RGB camera, with the learned first-layer weights directly specifying the printed mask patterns. A physically realistic sensor model is embedded in the forward pass: the mask transmittance is parameterized through a sigmoid to enforce 0≤M≤1, the detector's directional response and finite active area are modeled as multiplicative and convolutional terms, and gain, read/quantization noise, and a soft-clipping saturation function complete the pipeline. After training, the masks are inkjet-printed on transparency film and mounted above a custom 24-channel photodiode array with transimpedance amplifiers, a multiplexer, and a BLE-enabled microcontroller, yielding a camera that matches the performance of a 64×64 or 32×32 conventional camera while using 2–8 pixels.

## Results

The prototype minimalist camera with 8 freeform pixels monitors a workspace (people count, zone occupancy, door status) with performance on par to a 64×64 traditional camera, representing a 2048× reduction in pixel count. Specifically, 2 freeform pixels achieve 1.16 RMSE in people counting, 95.4% door detection, 99.2% desk occupancy, and 84.1% couch occupancy. For room lighting estimation, 8 freeform pixels achieve 94.0% accuracy across five lights, and for traffic speed estimation, 8 freeform pixels achieve 2.30 RMSE in mph. In a privacy evaluation, 16 freeform pixels designed for people counting achieve only 2.0% face recognition accuracy on CelebA, versus >98% for traditional cameras.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares minimalist cameras (freeform pixels) against baseline cameras (traditional square-pixel grids) at matched performance levels. For people counting in a workspace, 2 freeform pixels match a 64×64 baseline (4096 pixels), a 2048× reduction; for synthetic patch counting, 4 freeform pixels match a 32×32 baseline (1024 pixels), a 256× reduction. For room lighting estimation, 16 freeform pixels match a 12×12 baseline (144 pixels), and for traffic speed, 16 freeform pixels match an 8×14 baseline (112 pixels). The freeform pixels consistently achieve equivalent task performance with orders of magnitude fewer measurements. In the face recognition privacy test, the minimalist camera (2.0% accuracy on CelebA) is dramatically inferior to state-of-the-art traditional-camera systems (>98%), confirming that the reduced measurements are insufficient for biometric identification.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Pooj et al.'s minimalist camera concept (handcrafted optical masks paired with photodetectors) by making the mask shapes automatically learnable as the first layer of a neural network, trained end-to-end on task-specific data. It draws methodological inspiration from deep optics (Sitzmann et al., Chang et al.), which jointly optimizes optics and software, but departs in motivation: rather than enhancing image quality or improving a downstream task's accuracy, the goal is to minimize the number of measurements to the theoretical floor. It also distinguishes itself from single-pixel and lensless cameras (FlatCam, PhlatCam, DiffuserCam), which capture linear projections to reconstruct full images, by bypassing reconstruction entirely and solving the task directly from a handful of measurements.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The demonstrated 2048× reduction in pixel count (2 freeform pixels matching a 64×64 baseline for people counting) and the fully self-powered operation (30 fps wireless transmission from ambient light alone) make a concrete case for deploying vision sensors in privacy-sensitive or power-constrained settings where traditional cameras are impractical. Key limitations include the narrow scope to 'lightweight' tasks (the authors acknowledge that fine-grained tasks like face identification would require pixel counts approaching a traditional camera), the reliance on printed transparencies rather than electronically reconfigurable masks, and the privacy claim being demonstrated but not formally guaranteed. Open questions include whether spatio-temporal mask control via spatial light modulators can extend the solvable task range, and whether the approach generalizes beyond the specific scenes and tasks evaluated.
