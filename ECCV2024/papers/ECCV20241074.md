## Problem

Existing text-driven diffusion models target high-level semantic editing (inpainting, stylization) but have not been adapted to fine-level restoration tasks like denoising, deblurring, and super-resolution. Blind restoration methods cannot resolve semantic ambiguities (e.g., distinguishing a horse from a zebra in a blurry image) or degradation-type ambiguities (e.g., preserving intentional bokeh while removing unwanted Gaussian blur), and prior text-guided approaches address only one of these ambiguities in isolation.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a degraded RGB image, a semantic text prompt describing the desired scene content (e.g., 'a panda sitting by the bamboo'), and a restoration text prompt specifying degradation type and numeric strength (e.g., 'deblur with sigma 3.0'). Both prompts can be empty, reducing the system to a blind restoration model. The output is a single restored image decoded from the latent space via a VAE decoder, with the restoration prompt enabling continuous control over enhancement granularity rather than a binary task selection.

## Method

SPIRE decouples the restoration problem into a frozen text-to-image prior (semantic prompt processed by the pretrained LDM backbone) and a learnable restoration-aware branch (a ControlNet adaptor that ingests the degraded image and restoration prompt). The ControlNet encoder takes the downsampled degraded image concatenated with noisy latents, with cross-attention to CLIP token embeddings of the restoration prompt to propagate degradation-specific information. A novel modulation fusion layer adaptively rescales and shifts the frozen backbone's skip-connection features using γ and β parameters produced by a lightweight 1×1 zero-initialized convolution on the ControlNet output, enabling better alignment between the generative prior and restoration conditioning. Training uses ~100M text-image pairs with a mixed degradation pipeline (50% parameterized degradations with numeric prompts, 50% full Real-ESRGAN degradation with a generic 'Remove all degradation' prompt), and the semantic prompt is randomly dropped 10% of the time to support prompt-agnostic restoration.

## Results

On the MS-COCO parameterized degradation benchmark, SPIRE achieves the best FID of 11.34 and LPIPS of 0.219, outperforming ControlNet-SR (FID 13.65) and DiffBIR (FID 17.26) by significant margins. On the Real-ESRGAN degradation benchmark, SPIRE attains the best FID (14.42) and CLIP-I (0.935), while SwinIR retains the highest PSNR (24.40) but produces visibly blurry outputs (FID 43.22). On real-world images (RealPhoto60), SPIRE with full semantic prompts achieves the highest CLIP-IQA (0.6610), MUSIQ (67.82), and MANIQA (0.4474) scores among all tested configurations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SPIRE outperforms all baselines on FID and LPIPS across both degradation settings. On parameterized degradation, SPIRE (FID 11.34) beats ControlNet-SR (13.65) by 1.31 and DiffBIR (17.26) by 5.92; on Real-ESRGAN degradation, it achieves FID 14.42 versus ControlNet-SR's 16.99 and DiffBIR's 19.17. SwinIR achieves the highest PSNR (24.40) and SSIM (0.717) but produces the worst perceptual quality (FID 43.22, LPIPS 0.423), confirming its blurry regression-based outputs. Zero-shot prompt combinations (DiffBIR+SDEdit, DiffBIR+CLIP) actually degrade performance relative to their base models in FID and LPIPS, indicating that naively injecting semantic prompts without joint training is counterproductive. ControlNet-SR+CLIP is the closest competitor (FID 13.00 vs. 11.34), but SPIRE's joint training with both semantic and restoration prompts yields a further 1.66 FID improvement. On real-world images, SPIRE with full LLaVA-generated semantic prompts achieves the best no-reference quality scores (CLIP-IQA 0.6610, MUSIQ 67.82, MANIQA 0.4474), surpassing DiffBIR (0.6983 CLIP-IQA but lower MUSIQ 69.69) and SwinIR (0.6190 CLIP-IQA).

## Relation to Prior Work

SPIRE extends the text-driven diffusion paradigm—previously applied to high-level semantic editing (inpainting, stylization)—to low-level image restoration tasks like denoising, deblurring, and super-resolution. Architecturally, it builds on ControlNet's parameter-efficient adaptor design atop frozen Latent Diffusion Models, but introduces a modulation fusion layer to adaptively rescale the generative prior, addressing distribution shift between the frozen backbone and the learned restoration branch. Conceptually, it departs from blind restoration methods (StableSR, DiffBIR) that treat all degradations uniformly, and from task-specific models (SwinIR, Real-ESRGAN) that require separate architectures per degradation type, by unifying both semantic content control and quantitative degradation-strength control under a single text-prompted framework. The decoupling of semantic prompts (routed through the frozen LDM backbone) from restoration prompts (routed through the ControlNet branch) is a key design choice that preserves the pretrained text-to-image prior while learning restoration-specific conditioning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SPIRE establishes a new paradigm of instruction-based image restoration where users can flexibly control both what to restore (semantic identity) and how much to restore (degradation type and strength) via natural language, eliminating the need for task-specific model selection. The finding that the model learns a continuous latent space of restoration strength from discrete language tokens—without explicit CLIP fine-tuning for numerics—suggests that diffusion models can internalize quantitative degradation parameters in a generalizable way. Limitations include reliance on synthetic degradation pipelines for training (only four parameterized degradation types), evaluation primarily on MS-COCO and DIV2K, and the fact that real-world performance gains from semantic prompting depend on prompt quality (e.g., LLaVA-generated descriptions). Open questions include whether this approach scales to more complex, multi-stage degradation combinations and how well it generalizes to truly unknown or compound degradations not seen during training.
