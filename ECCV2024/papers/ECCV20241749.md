## Problem

Most Vision Transformer (ViT) deployments ignore fairness, and it remains unclear whether CNN-oriented debiasing algorithms transfer to ViTs given their fundamentally different patch-based attention architecture. Existing fair-ViT methods either sacrifice accuracy for fairness (e.g., TADeT manipulates query matrices directly) or incur prohibitive computational cost (e.g., DSA requires two-stage adversarial training, Dr-Fairness uses bilevel optimization). The paper asks whether one can simultaneously improve accuracy and fairness in ViTs without heavy computational overhead.

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB face images (CelebA, 200k+ images) and outputs a class-label score vector (e.g., Attraction, Expression). The sensitive attribute (Gender or Hair color) is accessible only during training; at inference the model treats it as unavailable and computes a single weighted sum of all group-specific masks. The training data is partitioned into G parts (G=10 in experiments), each part containing samples from exactly one sensitive group, with equal image counts within a group but potentially unequal counts across groups.

## Method

FairViT introduces two complementary mechanisms. First, adaptive masking inserts a group-specific mask M_{l,h,i} and scalar weight ζ_i into every attention head at every layer: the forward pass element-wise multiplies the attention output by the weighted sum of all masks, while the backward pass assigns non-zero gradients only to the mask and weight corresponding to the sample's group (Eqs. 7–8), making them trainable parameters that are iteratively refined. Second, a distance loss is constructed by fitting a logistic-regression hyperplane in the (ŷ, ŷ_k) plane during validation (where ŷ is the target-class score and ŷ_k is the sum of top-k non-target scores); during training, the loss penalizes the geometric distance of each sample's score pair from this hyperplane, pushing all points to the correct side. The total loss is L = L_ce + α·L_dist, with the first epoch trained on cross-entropy alone before the hyperplane becomes meaningful. The adaptive masking adds only O(p·d) overhead per layer, keeping total training time comparable to vanilla ViT.

## Results

On the CelebA dataset across three task settings (Attraction/Gender, Expression/Gender, Attraction/Hair color), FairViT achieves the highest accuracy in all settings: 84.01%, 94.27%, and 82.52% respectively, surpassing the strongest baseline FSCL+ by 4.5–6.8 percentage points. FairViT also attains the best Balanced Accuracy (79.96%, 94.12%, 81.56%) and the lowest Demographic Parity in two of three settings (0.701 for Attraction/Hair color, 1.205 for Expression/Gender). On Equalized Opportunity, FairViT is competitive (1.15, 1.52, 2.10) though slightly behind FSCL+ (0.79, 1.20, 1.79) in all three settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FairViT outperforms the vanilla ViT baseline by a wide margin on all metrics across all three settings (e.g., +10.0% ACC and +7.6% BA on Attraction/Gender), confirming that the adaptive masking and distance loss add substantial value over unmodified ViT. Against TADeT and TADeT-MMD, FairViT achieves higher accuracy (by 3.5–5.5%) and balanced accuracy while maintaining competitive EO and DP, whereas TADeT's direct manipulation of the query matrix sacrifices accuracy for fairness. Compared to FSCL and FSCL+ (the strongest fairness-oriented baselines), FairViT gains 4.5–6.8% in accuracy and 5–6% in balanced accuracy, but concedes slightly on Equalized Opportunity (e.g., 1.15 vs. 0.79 on Attraction/Gender). On Demographic Parity, FairViT achieves the best value in two of three settings (0.701 vs. 0.834 for Attraction/Hair color; 1.205 vs. 1.263 for Expression/Gender) but is marginally worse than FSCL+ on Attraction/Gender (2.837 vs. 2.604). In terms of computational cost, FairViT runs in ~5.1 min per epoch versus ~35 min for FSCL/FSCL+, making it roughly 6× faster while delivering superior accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

FairViT extends the ViT architecture (Dosovitskiy et al.) by inserting group-specific adaptive masks into the self-attention output, drawing conceptual inspiration from multi-channel convolution. It departs from prior ViT fairness methods—TADeT (which directly manipulates query matrices at the cost of accuracy) and DSA (which requires costly two-stage adversarial training)—by treating masks and their weights as trainable parameters updated via gradient descent, and by adding a distance loss regularizer derived from a logistic-regression decision boundary. The approach also contrasts with CNN-oriented debiasing methods like FSCL/FSCL+, which the authors argue cannot be directly transferred to ViT's patch-based attention structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FairViT offers a practical, low-overhead recipe for fair ViT training that avoids the accuracy sacrifice common in prior fairness-aware ViT methods, making it relevant for real-world deployment where both accuracy and fairness are required. However, the evaluation is confined to a single dataset (CelebA) with binary sensitive attributes and a single task (attribute classification), leaving open whether the adaptive masking and distance loss generalize to multi-class sensitive groups, other architectures, or tasks like detection and generation. The authors themselves note future work on extending to text-to-image and graph generation, and on understanding the intrinsic mechanisms behind the distance loss.
