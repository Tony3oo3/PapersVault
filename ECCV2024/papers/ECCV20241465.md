## Problem

The impact of labeled data selection on category discovery performance has been entirely overlooked in the literature, which has focused on methodological improvements using fixed labeled/unlabeled splits. This paper reveals a counterintuitive finding: unlike supervised transfer learning where the most similar source data is optimal, category discovery is hurt when labeled data is either too similar (causing confusion between novel and seen categories) or too dissimilar (providing no useful visual context), creating a 'sweet spot' of intermediate similarity that has not been exploited.

## Input / Output

The method takes a large labeled source dataset (potentially spanning multiple unrelated domains, e.g., birds, cars, aircraft, dogs, and insects combined) and an unlabeled target dataset, and produces per-instance weights ω_i ∈ [0, ∞) for each labeled sample that modulate their contribution to the training loss. The similarity score for each labeled category is computed as the minimum cosine similarity between its centroid and all unlabeled data points, requiring no knowledge of the number of novel categories or their ground-truth labels. The weighted labeled data is then fed into a standard discovery model (e.g., SimGCD) without any architectural modification.

## Method

The paper proposes two unsupervised data selection strategies that assign weights to labeled data based on its similarity to the unlabeled target. The 'Binning' method first runs k-means on the unlabeled data to estimate within-target similarity, uses this as a threshold to discard overly dissimilar labeled samples, then ranks remaining labeled categories by similarity and keeps the most dissimilar ones (removing the too-similar tail). The 'Soft weighting' method is more lightweight: it computes the cosine similarity between each labeled category centroid and the farthest unlabeled data point, then maps this similarity through the beta distribution PDF b(x; α, β) to generate a continuous weight, where α=β=5 (default) produces a peaked curve that upweights intermediate-similarity data and downweights both extremes. These weights are injected directly into the supervised contrastive loss (Eq. 2) and the labeled classifier cross-entropy loss (Eq. 4) of SimGCD, so that too-similar or too-dissimilar labeled examples have reduced influence on the learned representation. The approach is plug-and-play, requiring no changes to the underlying discovery algorithm, and generalizes across GCD, XCon, SimGCD, and μGCD.

## Results

On the standard Semantic Shifts Benchmark setting (Table 4), the proposed Beta soft-weighting selection applied to μGCD achieves state-of-the-art 'New' category accuracy: 66.6% on CUB, 52.2% on Stanford Cars, 59.8% on FGVC-Aircraft, and 65.4% on Stanford Dogs. The same selection applied to SimGCD yields 64.0% (CUB), 46.3% (Cars), 57.1% (Aircraft), and 59.6% (Dogs) on 'New' categories. Notably, using only the 'Medium' similarity split of labeled data (one-third of the full set) already matches or exceeds using all labeled data, confirming that less is more for discovery. The supervised selection method of Cui et al. [9], which selects the most similar source data, consistently degrades performance across all four discovery methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The Beta soft-weighting selection consistently outperforms all four discovery baselines (GCD, XCon, SimGCD, μGCD) on 'New' category accuracy across all four SSB datasets, with the largest gains on CUB (+2.0 over μGCD, +6.3 over SimGCD) and FGVC-Aircraft (+3.0 over μGCD, +5.3 over SimGCD). The k-means clustering baseline is far below all supervised discovery methods, confirming that labeled data provides essential guidance. The supervised selection method of Cui et al. [9], which greedily picks the most source-target similar categories, consistently hurts discovery performance (e.g., μGCD drops from 64.6 to 60.4 on CUB New, SimGCD drops from 57.7 to 55.4), validating the paper's core claim that the most similar labeled data is counterproductive for discovery. The hard Binning selection also improves over baselines but is slightly less effective than Beta soft-weighting in most settings. On the larger NABirds and Herbarium-19 benchmarks (Table 3), the Beta method still yields consistent gains (e.g., +1.8 on NABirds New, +1.3 on Herb-19 New over SimGCD), though the Binning method edges out Beta on the very fine-grained NABirds dataset.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the generalized category discovery (GCD) literature (GCD, SimGCD, XCon, μGCD) but deliberately takes an orthogonal angle: rather than proposing a new discovery algorithm, it investigates how the choice of labeled source data affects downstream discovery performance. It departs from the supervised transfer learning data selection paradigm of Cui et al. [9], which selects the most similar source categories to the target via Earth Mover's Distance, by showing that this 'most similar is best' principle is actively harmful for discovery. The paper repurposes the beta distribution as a soft weighting mechanism to encode the 'neither too similar nor too dissimilar' sweet spot, and introduces a k-means-based hard binning alternative that avoids requiring cluster count estimates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that less labeled data can outperform more, and that the optimal source sits in a similarity 'sweet spot' rather than at the maximum-similarity end, opens a new research direction for data curation in discovery and challenges the conventional wisdom inherited from supervised transfer. Practically, the beta-weighting approach is a lightweight, plug-and-play modification that improves multiple state-of-the-art discovery methods without architectural changes. Open questions include how to automatically calibrate the similarity range without ground-truth hierarchy annotations, whether the sweet-spot phenomenon persists in coarser-grained or non-fine-grained discovery settings, and how to extend this to online or open-world discovery where the target distribution shifts over time.
