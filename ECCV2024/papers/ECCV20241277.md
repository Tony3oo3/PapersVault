## Problem

Existing adversarial attacks on skeleton action recognition (SAR) are confined to digital scenarios where an attacker can precisely modify joint coordinates at inference time, requiring knowledge of the target model's architecture and parameters. This paper identifies that such digital manipulations are impractical in the physical world, where human movement is imprecise and model internals are inaccessible, and introduces the first physical backdoor attack framework for SAR that operates under these realistic constraints.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241277/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on 25-joint 3D skeleton sequences (N×T×3) from datasets like NTU RGB+D and PKU-MMD, producing a single action class label. The key distinction is that the 'trigger' is not a pixel-level perturbation but a physically performable action (e.g., bending sideways, nodding, crossing hands) that the attacker executes concurrently with their intended action. At inference, any skeleton sequence containing the trigger action is misclassified to a fixed target class regardless of the actual performed action, while clean sequences retain normal classification accuracy.

## Method

The core mechanism embeds trigger actions into skeleton sequences via a kinematic chain simulation: the attacker selects a root joint (unchanged) and a key joint (targeted), uses inverse kinematics (Jacobian transpose method) to compute joint orientations along the chain, applies linear interpolation across frames for smooth temporal transitions, and propagates positions to all downstream joints via forward kinematics using quaternion-based rotation matrices. Two attack variants are proposed: P-PSBA (poison-label, where poisoned samples are relabeled to the target class) and C-PSBA (clean-label, where only target-class samples are poisoned and an adversarial perturbation is added to suppress original class features and amplify trigger learning). Trigger robustness to human variability is ensured by randomly sampling action duration and spatial hyperparameters (e.g., bend angle) from uniform distributions. The approach is validated across Hyperformer, CTR-GCN, and INFO-GCN on three datasets, and tested against CLP, D-BR, and STRIP defenses, with a real-world Kinect V2 dataset confirming physical feasibility.

## Results

Under the poison-label setting (P-PSBA), the 'bending sideways' trigger achieves ASR of 98.55% (Hyperformer), 95.00% (CTR-GCN), and 95.07% (INFO-GCN) on NTU RGB+D at just 1% poisoning rate, while maintaining clean accuracy above 90%. On NTU RGB+D 120 and PKU-MMD, the same trigger at 1% poisoning reaches ASR above 99% for Hyperformer and CTR-GCN. In the clean-label setting (C-PSBA), ASR plateaus around 70–80% even at 90% poisoning of the target class. Real-world validation with a Kinect V2 camera confirms the attack's physical feasibility, with 'bending sideways' at 2% poisoning yielding ASR of 91.72% (NTU RGB+D) and 95.05% (PKU-MMD) for Hyperformer.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates P-PSBA's resilience against three backdoor defenses: CLP (channel Lipschitz pruning), D-BR (fine-tuning-based removal), and STRIP (input perturbation filtering). Against CLP, the ASR drops by less than 0.5 percentage points across all models and poisoning rates (e.g., Hyperformer at 2%: 92.88% → 92.81%), rendering the defense effectively useless. D-BR similarly fails to reduce ASR meaningfully (e.g., INFO-GCN at 2%: 99.20% → 99.15%), confirming that the physical trigger's distributed nature across multiple joints resists sample-distinguishment-based removal. STRIP, which relies on entropy differences between clean and poisoned inputs, also cannot detect the attack because the entropy distributions of poisoned and clean samples remain nearly identical (Fig. 5). In summary, PSBA is essentially immune to all three tested defenses, with ASR reductions below 1% in every configuration, highlighting a significant gap in current backdoor defense strategies for skeleton-based systems.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the well-established backdoor attack literature (originally developed for image classification and later adapted to point clouds and NLP) to the skeleton action recognition domain, which the authors identify as previously unexplored. It departs from prior adversarial attacks on SAR, which assumed digital access to modify joint coordinates at inference time, by proposing physically executable triggers embedded via kinematic transformations (quaternion-based rotations, inverse and forward kinematics). The trigger design draws on robotics-style kinematic chain modeling rather than the pixel-level or point-level perturbations used in image and point cloud backdoor attacks, addressing the unique constraint that skeleton data has far fewer degrees of freedom and must remain physically plausible.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SAR systems are deployed in surveillance, healthcare monitoring, and interactive applications where misclassification has real consequences, yet this work demonstrates that a small fraction of poisoned training data (as low as 1–2%) can reliably redirect predictions when a natural-looking trigger action is performed, and that three widely-used backdoor defenses (CLP, D-BR, STRIP) reduce ASR by less than 1 percentage point. The clean-label variant, while achieving lower ASR (~70–80%), shows that even label-inspection-based detection is insufficient. Open questions include whether defenses can be designed specifically for kinematically-plausible triggers, how the attacks generalize to other skeletal modalities (e.g., 2D pose estimation), and whether the physical-world validation (Kinect V2) holds under more diverse real-world conditions such as occlusion or varying body types.
