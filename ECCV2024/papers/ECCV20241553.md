## Problem

Current motion-language models achieve strong retrieval scores on benchmarks like HumanML3D but largely ignore the temporal ordering of events within compound actions, treating descriptions as bag-of-words rather than sequences. No existing evaluation explicitly tests whether a model can distinguish a correct event chronology from a shuffled one, leaving a critical gap in how cross-modal alignment is assessed.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241553/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes 3D skeletal motion sequences (represented as relative joint positions and accelerations, following the HumanML3D/AMASS format) paired with natural-language captions. For the CAR evaluation, captions are decomposed into discrete events via GPT-3.5 and then randomly shuffled to produce chronologically incorrect counterparts; the model must select the correct ordering from the two candidates. For generation, the fine-tuned text encoder feeds a single initial token into autoregressive or diffusion-based motion generators (T2M-GPT, MotionDiffuse, ReMoDiffuse) to produce full 3D motion sequences.

## Method

The paper introduces Chronologically Accurate Retrieval (CAR), a binary-choice test where a model scores a motion against both the ground-truth caption and its event-shuffled version, reporting the fraction of correct choices. To improve temporal alignment, the authors extend TMR's contrastive loss by appending shuffled-event descriptions as hard negative text samples: the similarity matrix grows from N×N to N×(N+K), and the motion-to-text cross-entropy term now includes these negatives while the text-to-motion term excludes them (since shuffled texts have no unique motion counterpart). This forces the encoder to encode event order rather than just vocabulary overlap. Ablations show that removing the VAE stochastic bottleneck on the text side is particularly important, as sampling from a distribution blurs the fine-grained ordering signal that distinguishes chronologically correct from incorrect descriptions.

## Results

On the HumanML3D test set, the proposed method (TMR + shuffled-event negative samples) achieves CAR accuracy of 91.4–99.7% across language encoders, compared to 62.9–66.8% for standard TMR (chance level = 50%). In motion-to-text retrieval with chronologically corrupted texts as candidates, the proposed method improves R@1 from 6.2–7.9% (TMR) to 8.2–9.7% and R@10 from 24.5–33.8% to 24.0–34.1%. For text-to-motion generation, fine-tuning the CLIP encoder with negative chronological samples improves T2M-GPT's R-Precision Top-1 from 0.489 to 0.528 and FID from 0.116 to 0.070, and MotionDiffuse's FID from 0.651 to 0.367.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against TMR [27] (the primary retrieval baseline), the proposed method achieves a dramatic improvement in CAR accuracy: from 62.9–66.8% (near chance) to 91.4–99.7%, confirming that standard contrastive training fails to encode event chronology. On motion-to-text retrieval with shuffled texts as candidates, the proposed method improves R@1 by approximately 1.5–2.5 points (e.g., 9.65% vs. 7.76% with t5-large in orig→event). For motion generation, the fine-tuned CLIP encoder with negative chronological samples consistently outperforms the original encoders: T2M-GPT improves from R-Precision Top-1 0.489 to 0.528 and FID 0.116 to 0.070; MotionDiffuse improves FID from 0.651 to 0.367; ReMoDiffuse improves R-Precision Top-1 from 0.489 to 0.525 and FID from 0.140 to 0.116. All three generation baselines (MotionDiffuse [47], T2M-GPT [46], ReMoDiffuse [48]) benefit from the proposed fine-tuning, with T2M-GPT showing the largest FID reduction (0.116→0.070), suggesting the autoregressive architecture is particularly sensitive to temporal ordering in the text embedding.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on TMR (Petrovich et al., ICCV 2023), adopting its VAE-based contrastive learning framework for text-motion retrieval, and extends it by incorporating chronologically shuffled event descriptions as hard negative samples during training. Conceptually, it draws on the CLIP-style contrastive paradigm and the observation from Yuksekgonul et al. [44] that vision-language models exhibit bag-of-words behavior when text elements are permuted, applying this diagnostic lens to the motion-language domain. It also connects to video moment retrieval and audio generation literature where temporal correspondence between text and sequential data is explicitly modeled, positioning the motion-language gap as an underexplored parallel.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The CAR diagnostic reveals that SOTA motion-language models achieve only ~60–67% chronological discrimination (barely above chance), exposing a fundamental blind spot: strong retrieval performance does not imply temporal understanding of compound actions. The simple remedy—treating shuffled-event texts as hard negatives—closes this gap to >90% CAR and yields modest but consistent gains in generation metrics, suggesting temporal alignment is an under-addressed axis in cross-modal motion modeling. Key open questions remain: whether the model relies on superficial linguistic cues (pronouns, articles) rather than genuine temporal reasoning, how to achieve frame-level word-to-motion grounding, and whether motion-side augmentation (reordering action segments) can complement the text-only approach.
