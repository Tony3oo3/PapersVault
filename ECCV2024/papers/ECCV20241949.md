## Problem

While LLMs are known to be vulnerable to jailbreak attacks, it remains unclear how the visual modality specifically undermines the harmlessness alignment of multimodal large language models (MLLMs). This paper systematically investigates whether and how image inputs act as alignment backdoors, and how cross-modal fine-tuning disrupts the safety properties inherited from backbone LLMs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241949/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single harmful text instruction (containing one harmful keyword or key phrase) as input and produces two outputs: a modified text instruction where the harmful keyword is replaced by a text-to-image pointer (e.g., 'the object in the image'), and a vertically concatenated adversarial image composed of three stacked components — an adversarial noise image on top, a diffusion-generated harmful image in the middle, and a typography rendering of the original keyword at the bottom. The adversarial noise image is shared across all instructions within the same harmful category, while the diffusion-generated image is uniquely optimized per instruction.

## Method

HADES is a three-stage jailbreak that transfers harmfulness from the well-aligned text channel to the less-aligned image channel. First, it hides harmfulness by replacing the harmful keyword in the text with a text-to-image pointer and rendering the keyword as a typography image, so the text no longer explicitly contains harmful content. Second, it amplifies image harmfulness semantically by iteratively optimizing a diffusion model (PixArt-α) prompt using GPT-4 as both attacker and judge over 5 turns, scoring generated images on a 1–10 harmfulness scale and refining the prompt based on captions and explanations. Third, it amplifies harmfulness adversarially by gradient-optimizing a single noise image (constrained to [0,1]^{w×h×c}) to maximize the log-likelihood of affirmative responses (e.g., 'Sure!') across 10 target outputs, treating the adversarial image analogously to a text jailbreak prompt. The final image stacks all three components vertically, and the modified text directs the model's attention to the image, exploiting the OCR and captioning pathways to recover the hidden harmful intent.

## Results

HADES achieves an average Attack Success Rate (ASR) of 90.26% on LLaVA-1.5, 88.93% on LLaVA-1.5L, and 82.53% on LLaVA (full version with adversarial image). For closed-source models, the +Opt image setting (without gradient-based adversarial image) yields 71.60% ASR on Gemini Pro Vision and 15.07% on GPT-4V. The full HADES pipeline (typography + text-to-image pointer + optimized image + adversarial image) increases ASR by 24.66–51.46 percentage points over the typography-only baseline across the three LLaVA variants.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark HADES against other named jailbreak methods; instead, it ablates its own three components (text-to-image pointer, optimized image, adversarial image) and compares across six MLLMs. The optimized image (i_opt) is the single most impactful component, boosting ASR by 12.93–37.20 pp over the typography-only baseline across all models. The gradient-based adversarial image (i_adv) adds a further 11.73–17.60 pp on open-source models, pushing LLaVA-1.5 to 90.26% and LLaVA to 82.53%. Among closed-source models, Gemini Pro Vision is far more vulnerable (71.60% ASR) than GPT-4V (15.07%), suggesting GPT-4V retains stronger image-side alignment. The text-to-image pointer alone has mixed effects: it increases ASR on models with strong OCR (Gemini ProV +16.93%, LLaVA +6.53%) but decreases it on LLaVA-1.5 (−7.47%) and LLaVA-1.5L (−14.13%), likely due to OCR misreadings and already-weak text-side alignment in those models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM jailbreaking literature (e.g., GCG, PAIR, FigStep) to the multimodal setting, but its primary conceptual departure is a systematic empirical analysis of how the visual modality specifically undermines harmlessness alignment—a question prior MLLM safety benchmarks and adversarial image attacks addressed only implicitly. HADES repurposes existing components—typography-based visual prompts (à la FigStep), diffusion-model image generation, and gradient-based adversarial noise—into a unified three-stage pipeline that hides harmful intent in text and amplifies it through crafted images. The paper positions itself as bridging white-box and black-box MLLM attacks, whereas prior work typically treated these as separate paradigms.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that even blank images substantially increase attack success rates, and that cross-modal fine-tuning imposes an 'inverse alignment tax' (improving multimodal capability while degrading safety), provides concrete evidence that current MLLM training pipelines systematically erode the safety alignment of their backbone LLMs. This has direct practical implications: developers must incorporate image-side adversarial samples during cross-modal fine-tuning rather than relying on text-side alignment alone. Open questions include whether these vulnerabilities persist as MLLMs adopt more sophisticated safety training, how to properly co-align text and image modalities without sacrificing multimodal utility, and whether the white-box adversarial noise component generalizes to future architectures.
