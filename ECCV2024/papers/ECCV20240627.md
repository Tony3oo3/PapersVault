## Problem

Current data parameterization methods for dataset condensation encode shared features among images using flat factorization, overlooking the fact that images share common features in a hierarchical manner due to the inherent structure of classification systems (e.g., cats and dogs share animal-level features, while two cats share cat-level features). This paper asks whether explicitly modeling this hierarchy within a data container can yield more efficient information sharing and better condensation performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240627/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a standard RGB image classification dataset (32×32×3 for CIFAR-10/100 and SVHN, 64×64×3 for Tiny-ImageNet) and produces a parameterized Hierarchical Memory Network whose learnable weights (three-tier memories, per-class feature extractors, and a shared decoder) constitute the entire storage budget, measured in IPC. The HMN does not store images; instead, it generates synthetic training images on demand by indexing into its instance-level memories, so the number of generated images per class is a tunable hyperparameter independent of the stored parameter count.

## Method

HMN is a three-tier hierarchical data container: a single dataset-level memory shared across all classes, one class-level memory per class, and one instance-level memory per generated image. To produce an image for class c, a class-specific feature extractor processes the dataset-level memory, and its output is concatenated with the class-level and instance-level memories before being decoded by a single shared decoder into a synthetic image. Training uses gradient matching (a low-memory batch-based loss) rather than the trajectory-based losses typical of prior data parameterization methods. A key additional contribution is over-budget condensation followed by double-end pruning: the HMN is first condensed with 10% more instance-level memories than the budget allows, then both the easiest (highest AUM) and hardest (lowest AUM) examples are pruned per class via grid search over a hard-pruning rate, exploiting the fact that each generated image has its own independent instance-level memory that can be removed without affecting others.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, HMN achieves 65.7% / 73.7% / 76.9% test accuracy at 1/10/50 IPC, outperforming all data-parameterization baselines (IDC, HaBa, LinBa) at 10 and 50 IPC and matching LinBa (66.4%) at 1 IPC while using a low-memory batch-based loss instead of trajectory-based loss. On CIFAR-100, HMN reaches 36.3% / 45.4% / 48.5% at 1/10/50 IPC, and on SVHN it reaches 87.4% / 90.0% / 91.2%. On Tiny-ImageNet (1/10 IPC) and ImageNet-10 (1 IPC), HMN achieves 19.4% / 24.4% and 64.6% respectively, surpassing all baselines. When compared under the same gradient-matching loss (Table 3), HMN outperforms the next-best data-parameterization method by at least 3.7% (1 IPC), 5.9% (10 IPC), and 2.4% (50 IPC) on CIFAR-10.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HMN outperforms all image-container baselines (DC, DSA, DM, CAFE+DSA, MTT, IDM) across every dataset and IPC budget, with the largest gaps on CIFAR-10 (e.g., 73.7% vs. MTT's 65.3% at 10 IPC) and Tiny-ImageNet (24.4% vs. MTT's 23.2% at 10 IPC). Among data-parameterization methods, HMN beats IDC on all reported settings (e.g., 76.9% vs. 74.5% on CIFAR-10 50 IPC; 64.6% vs. 60.4% on ImageNet-10 1 IPC) and HaBa on all settings (e.g., 73.7% vs. 69.9% on CIFAR-10 10 IPC). Against LinBa, HMN wins at 10 and 50 IPC on CIFAR-10 (73.7% vs. 71.2%; 76.9% vs. 73.6%) and on all SVHN budgets, but LinBa edges HMN by 0.7% on CIFAR-10 1 IPC (66.4% vs. 65.7%)—though LinBa uses a memory-intensive trajectory-based loss while HMN uses batch-based gradient matching. Under the same gradient-matching loss (Table 3), HMN's advantage over LinBa widens to 3.7% (1 IPC), 5.9% (10 IPC), and 6.2% (50 IPC) on CIFAR-10, confirming the architectural benefit is not an artifact of loss choice. The only notable loss is on CIFAR-100 50 IPC where IDM (50.0%) slightly exceeds HMN (48.5%), and on CIFAR-100 10 IPC where IDM (45.1%) is within 0.3% of HMN (45.4%). In the continual-learning setting (Figure 4), HMN outperforms DSA, DM, and IDC by up to 16% at 1 IPC, demonstrating that the condensed data quality translates to downstream applications.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the data parameterization line of work for dataset condensation (IDC, HaBa, LinBa) by introducing a three-tier hierarchical memory structure (dataset-level, class-level, instance-level) that explicitly mirrors the hierarchical nature of classification taxonomies. Unlike prior factorization-based containers (HaBa, LinBa) that share features through flat basis vectors, HMN separates shared information into distinct tiers, enabling natural instance-level pruning. The post-condensation pruning algorithm additionally borrows the Area Under the Margin (AUM) metric from coreset selection literature, applying it to identify redundant synthetic images for removal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in demonstrating that a low-memory batch-based loss (gradient matching) combined with an effective data container architecture can match or exceed trajectory-based methods that consume orders of magnitude more GPU memory (15 hours vs. 14 days for CIFAR10 1 IPC). This addresses a key scalability bottleneck in dataset condensation. Open questions include whether combining HMN with trajectory-based losses would yield further gains, whether the hierarchical structure generalizes to non-hierarchical or open-vocabulary classification settings, and whether the pruning strategy transfers to other data container architectures. The paper also leaves the exploration of larger over-budget rates and more complex pruning schedules as future work.
