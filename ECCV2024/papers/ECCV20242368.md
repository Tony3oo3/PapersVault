## Problem

Estimating the physical forces driving human motion (ground reaction forces, joint torques) from inexpensive sensors remains unsolved because no large-scale dataset exists with synchronized high-quality pose and force plate measurements across diverse subjects and activities. Prior force estimation methods are either too slow for real-time use or have never been validated against measured ground truth, making their accuracy unknown relative to clinically established thresholds.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20242368/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark task takes a noisy time series of joint angles (from any off-the-shelf motion capture system) and a scaled Rajagopal Full Body Model (22 segments, 37 DOF) as input, and must produce four mutually consistent outputs: joint accelerations, 3D ground reaction forces at each foot, 3D ground reaction moments at each foot, and full-body joint torques. The dataset provides over 24 million frames at 100 fps with optical marker positions, force plate measurements, and pre-computed dynamics, spanning 53.4% single support, 21.2% double support, and 25.4% flight phase across nine activity types.

## Method

The key algorithmic contribution extends the Werling et al. [71] sequential optimization pipeline to handle long captures where subjects step off force plates, creating frames with unobserved external forces. Rather than discarding those frames or solving separate sub-problems (which permits unrealistic boundary velocities), the method explicitly introduces center-of-mass acceleration variables for each unobserved frame, constructs a linear system via semi-explicit Euler integration, and solves for initial conditions and mass via pseudoinverse while regularizing the unobserved accelerations. The final non-convex optimization over joint angles, bone scales, and inertial properties then includes inverse dynamics loss only on frames with observed forces, preserving kinematic continuity across the full capture. For the benchmark, baselines include an analytical F=ma method with even force splitting, a 2-layer MLP (512 hidden units) predicting forces from a window of joint angles and accelerations, and GroundLinkNet (a convolution-based architecture from prior work retrained on this dataset).

## Results

On the AddBiomechanics Dataset test set, the MLP baseline achieves 1.29 N/kg GRF error, 1.33 Nm/kg joint torque error, and 0.03 Nm/kg GRM error, substantially outperforming the analytical F=ma baseline (2.79 N/kg GRF, 2.77 Nm/kg joint torque, 6.15 Nm/kg GRM). The GroundLinkNet architecture retrained on AddBiomechanics achieves slightly better GRF error (1.17 N/kg) with comparable joint torque (1.34 Nm/kg) and GRM (0.07 Nm/kg) errors. These data-driven baselines approach the ~1 N/kg GRF accuracy of offline trajectory optimization [29], but at real-time speed rather than 0.001× real time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's MLP baseline (1.29 N/kg GRF) and retrained GroundLinkNet (1.17 N/kg GRF) both substantially outperform the original GroundLinkNet self-reported on its own dataset (>3.06 N/kg) and UnderPressure (>5.74 N/kg), though these comparisons are not perfectly apples-to-apples due to different test sets and metrics. Against the offline trajectory optimization method of Karatsidis et al. [29] (~1 N/kg GRF), the data-driven baselines are within ~0.2 N/kg in GRF accuracy while running at real-time speed versus 0.001× real time. The analytical F=ma baseline is the weakest at 2.79 N/kg GRF, confirming that learning-based methods are necessary for competitive force estimation. The MLP and GroundLinkNet baselines are nearly tied on all four metrics, with GroundLinkNet having a marginal edge on GRF (1.17 vs. 1.29 N/kg) but the MLP slightly better on GRM (0.03 vs. 0.07 Nm/kg). Overall, the paper establishes that simple architectures can already achieve near-optimization-level GRF accuracy on this benchmark, setting a high bar for future work.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the AddBiomechanics automated dynamics reconstruction pipeline of Werling et al. [71] by introducing a method to handle long capture sessions where subjects step off force plates, enabling the use of optical motion data from unobserved-force frames to constrain dynamics on observed-force frames. It positions itself against existing human dynamics datasets (UnderPressure, GroundLink, and several smaller collections) that are limited in scale, use insufficient force measurement quality (e.g., pressure insoles), or lack standardized processing. On the estimation side, it builds on the GroundLinkNet architecture [19] and trajectory optimization approaches [29] but reframes the evaluation around a unified benchmark with multiple metrics (GRF, GRM, joint torques, CoM acceleration) rather than the single-metric, task-specific evaluations common in prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first large-scale, standardized dataset (273 subjects, 70+ hours, 24M+ frames) that pairs high-quality force plate measurements with motion capture across diverse activities, directly addressing the bottleneck that has prevented fair comparison of force-estimation methods. The proposed benchmark with clinically grounded metrics and a standard train/test split enables the field to move beyond self-reported, non-comparable evaluations. Key limitations include a bias toward walking and running, only 21.2% of data reaching clinical-grade quality thresholds, and the requirement for manual annotation of force plate transitions. Open questions include whether fast, generalized models can achieve clinically acceptable accuracy across all activity types, and whether the dataset can support real-time dynamics estimation from inexpensive sensors like phone cameras.
