## Problem

Traditional projectors display 2D content at a single depth, and existing multi-depth approaches (coded apertures, light-field projectors, focus-tunable lenses, multi-projector arrays) suffer from limited programmability, reduced brightness, high cost, or framerate penalties. Holographic projection could form arbitrary depth-varying content with a single SLM pattern, but naively its etendue constrains depth variation to meter-scale separations, making practical applications infeasible.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241628/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes target intensity patterns specified at 2–5 discrete depth planes (e.g., 0.4–0.7 m from the system) and produces a single 1920×1200 SLM phase pattern that, when illuminated by a 530 nm laser, projects the desired depth-varying content into the scene. For depth estimation and light-curtain applications, a co-located NIR camera captures the projected scene, and the system outputs per-pixel depth maps or binary intersection detections. Simulation runs at 9600×6000 (1.6 μm pitch) to avoid aliasing the 1.0 mm×1.4 mm microlens array, and the projected pattern spans roughly 0.1 m×0.15 m at 0.7 m.

## Method

The key insight is that etendue (area × solid angle) fundamentally couples depth variation to field-of-view in a holographic projector; the paper places a static microlens array at the front focal plane of the projection lens to spatially multiplex the SLM and expand etendue by ~3.2×4.5, increasing usable FOV from ~3.8° to ~15° at a fixed 15 cm depth spread. The forward model chains angular-spectrum propagation through the SLM-to-array gap, an optimizable lens-array modulation M (modeled as multiply–propagate–multiply, inspired by ABCD thick-lens matrices), and propagation to each target depth. Phase retrieval solves a multi-plane intensity-matching optimization with Adam over the SLM phase. A novel calibration procedure jointly recovers M, SLM distortion, camera modulation, and propagation kernels from 17,340 SLM-pattern/camera-capture pairs, handling sub-millimeter misalignments and optical aberrations that prior etendue-expansion work ignores.

## Results

The etendue-expanding lens array (1.0 mm × 1.4 mm pitch, 4.7 mm focal length) expands the projector field-of-view from ~3.8° (naive holographic setup) to ~15°, corresponding to a 3.2×4.5 etendue increase. The system requires a minimum 5 mm separation between target planes (before the projector lens) to reliably project unique content, and achieves an effective stereo baseline of ~9 mm for a target at 0.5 m. Depth estimation uses a two-stage coarse-to-fine refinement across 5 planes, and the system simultaneously forms two programmable light curtains without stereo calibration or a baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four depth-varying projection approaches in simulation (Fig. 2). The coded aperture setup with two SLMs [20] struggles to form the desired content at two planes, producing significant artifacts. The time-multiplexed focus-tunable lens with a high-speed projector [55] performs better visually but exhibits low-frequency crosstalk errors and requires multiple SLMs with reduced framerate. A holographic system using a custom random binary phase mask [28] suffers from loss of contrast. The proposed lens-array holographic system matches the quality of the time-multiplexed approach while using only a single SLM pattern and no temporal multiplexing. For depth sensing, the authors argue their holographic depth cue is complementary to structured light and CWTOF: unlike structured light, its resolution increases with larger aperture (avoiding the depth-of-field/brightness tradeoff), and unlike CWTOF, it provides unambiguous coarse depth that can resolve CWTOF's range ambiguity without sacrificing resolution or framerate.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the etendue-expansion lens array concept from holographic near-eye displays (where it resolves the FOV–eyebox tradeoff) to holographic projectors, where it resolves an analogous depth-variation–FOV tradeoff. It builds on prior holographic structured-light and CWTOF work by the same group [13,14] but shifts the goal from single-plane illumination to multi-plane programmable content. The optimization-driven calibration of the lens array departs from prior etendue-expansion work [2,28,36] that assumed ideal optics or required custom-fabricated phase masks, instead learning the full modulation from in-setup captures with off-the-shelf microlens arrays. For depth recovery, it adapts graph-cuts-based depth-from-texture [43] to the specific structure of holographic depth-varying patterns.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes a practical single-SLM system that simultaneously projects unique content at multiple depths, opening applications in screenless 3D interfaces, privacy-preserving projection, and baseline-free light curtains that were previously infeasible with a single projector. However, the depth variation remains severely limited (target planes must be ≥5 mm apart for reliable separation), speckle artifacts degrade contrast, and the 9600×6000 simulation requirement makes real-time operation impractical. Key open questions include whether neural wave-propagation models can close the sim-to-real gap, whether temporal multiplexing or custom scattering masks can push etendue further, and whether the depth-cue approach can be fused with CWTOF or structured light in a single capture to yield practical sensing improvements.
