## Problem

Existing lip-syncing methods either require clip-specific training on long videos or produce visible artifacts when editing high-quality footage, and their reliance on low-dimensional audio features to directly modify high-dimensional visual data leads to instability. Meanwhile, 3D facial models as spatial guidance for efficient, generalized editing remain underexplored, and no prior work unifies high-fidelity lip-syncing with face-swapping within a single model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241096/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes Wav2Vec2-extracted audio features, a 256×256 target frame (25 FPS) with the lower face masked, a reference frame sampled from the nearest 30 frames, and a canonical 3D template mesh (Hifi3DFace or BFM topology) as inputs. It produces high-fidelity lip-synced video frames, and when configured for face-swapping, simultaneously outputs identity-swapped frames driven by the same audio. Inference processes video in 100-frame chunks with 20-frame overlap, and the 3D mesh is rendered as a white-colored overlay rather than a textured mesh, keeping the pipeline simple.

## Method

ReSyncer is a two-stage framework: (1) a Style-SyncFormer built from a single Transformer encoder-decoder pair predicts stylized 3D mesh displacements from audio, injecting speaking style by adding a style feature (encoded from 90 consecutive reference frames) to mesh tokens, and (2) a rewired StyleGAN2 generator that overlays the predicted white-colored mesh onto the masked target frame as strong spatial guidance while a reference frame supplies texture and identity. The key reconfiguration inserts the encoded 6-channel input [mesh-overlay, reference] into both the noise space (multi-scale feature maps) and the W space (style vector), eliminating the need for audio conditioning in W and decoupling driving dynamics from identity. This design naturally extends to face-swapping by enlarging the mask to the full face, substituting a different identity's mesh and frame, and adding ArcFace identity cosine distance and feature-matching losses, all within the same unified weights.

## Results

On lip-sync (primary task), ReSyncer achieves the best scores across all four metrics on both HDTF (SSIM 0.84, PSNR 31.76, LMD 4.34, ΔSync 0.66) and VoxCeleb2 (SSIM 0.85, PSNR 32.88, LMD 3.19, ΔSync 0.88), outperforming the closest competitor StyleSync by a notable margin on ΔSync (0.88 vs 1.57 on VoxCeleb2). On face-swapping (FF++), ReSyncer achieves the lowest pose error (1.31°) and expression error (5.13°) among all methods, with ID retrieval (96.70%) and ID similarity (0.665) slightly below StyleSwap (97.05%, 0.677). In the user study, ReSyncer scores 4.57/5 on lip-sync quality, 4.67/5 on generation quality, and 4.54/5 on video realness, substantially ahead of all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ReSyncer outperforms all four lip-sync baselines (Wav2Lip, ReTalking, SadTalker, StyleSync) on every metric across both HDTF and VoxCeleb2. The largest gap is against StyleSync (the closest competitor and the authors' prior work): ReSyncer reduces ΔSync from 1.57 to 0.88 on VoxCeleb2 and LMD from 3.28 to 3.19, while also improving PSNR by 0.36 dB. SadTalker, though a talking-head generator rather than a lip-sync editor, is the weakest baseline on sync quality (ΔSync 3.03 on VoxCeleb2). For face-swapping on FF++, ReSyncer achieves the best pose error (1.31°) and expression error (5.13°), beating StyleSwap (1.56°, 5.28°) and SimSwap (1.36°, 5.07°), but trails StyleSwap slightly on ID retrieval (96.70% vs 97.05%) and ID similarity (0.665 vs 0.677). E4S is the weakest face-swap baseline, with the lowest ID similarity (0.495) and highest expression error (11.52°). The paper also shows that combining separate SOTA lip-sync and face-swap methods (e.g., StyleSwap + StyleSync) produces inferior results to ReSyncer's unified single-model approach, particularly in temporal consistency and detail preservation.

## Relation to Prior Work

ReSyncer extends the StyleSync line of work (same StyleGAN2-based encoder and W-space insertion scheme) by replacing the audio-driven W-space animation with explicit 3D mesh overlay as spatial guidance, and by adopting FaceFormer's periodic positional encoding and biased causal attention for a lightweight single-block Transformer (Style-SyncFormer) that predicts stylized mesh displacements. The key conceptual departure from prior lip-sync methods is the use of roughly fitted, white-colored 3D meshes—rather than 3DMM coefficients or textured rendering—as the intermediate representation bridging audio and image domains, which the authors show provides sufficient spatial structure for the generator to recover high-fidelity details. The framework also unifies face-swapping training within the same generator by reconfiguring the masking region and injecting identity from a second source into the W space, a combination not previously achieved in a single model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for virtual performer creation: a single model handles generalized lip-sync, personalized fine-tuning, speaking-style transfer, video-driven animation, and face-swapping, reducing the need for separate pipelines. The finding that a simple white-colored mesh overlay (no texture fitting, no precise 3DMM fitting) suffices as spatial guidance lowers the implementation barrier compared to textured-rendering approaches. Open questions include real-time inference speed (the autoregressive mesh prediction and StyleGAN2 generation may be slow), robustness to large pose changes or occlusions, and the ethical risks of combining high-fidelity lip-sync with face-swapping in one accessible tool. The paper acknowledges these concerns but restricts distribution to research use.
