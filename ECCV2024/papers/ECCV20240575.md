## Problem

Current text-to-image diffusion models fail to faithfully adhere to spatial relationships specified in prompts (e.g., 'a cat to the left of a dog'). The paper identifies a root cause: spatial words like 'left/right', 'above/below', 'front/behind' are severely under-represented in existing vision-language datasets, with ground-truth COCO captions capturing only 21% of spatial relationships and LAION captions only 6%. This data bottleneck, rather than model architecture, is posited as a fundamental reason for poor spatial consistency.

## Input / Output

The method takes natural language prompts containing spatial prepositions and object descriptions as input and generates 512×512 RGB images via Stable Diffusion v2.1. During training, each image is paired with both a standard caption and a spatially-focused SPRIGHT caption (2 sentences describing left/right, above/below, front/behind, relative sizes), with one randomly selected per training step at a 50:50 ratio. The SOTA configuration uses only 444 image-caption pairs where each image contains more than 18 objects, as identified by the Recognize Anything tagging model.

## Method

The paper introduces SPRIGHT, a dataset of ~6 million synthetically re-captioned images from CC-12M, Segment Anything, COCO, and LAION-Aesthetics, generated by prompting LLaVA-1.5-13B with a spatial-focused instruction. Fine-tuning proceeds by jointly optimizing the U-Net and CLIP text encoder of SD 2.1 (lr=5e-6, AdamW, batch size 128, 15k steps; CLIP frozen for first 10k steps) on <15k image-caption pairs. The key efficiency insight is that partitioning training data by object count and training exclusively on images with >18 objects yields state-of-the-art T2I-CompBench spatial scores (0.2133) with only 444 images, because multi-object images inherently encode more spatial relationships, teaching the model to generate all mentioned objects in correct spatial arrangement. Ablations show that a 50% spatial-to-general caption ratio is optimal, longer captions outperform shorter ones, and CLIP fine-tuning specifically benefits long-caption generalization.

## Results

Fine-tuning Stable Diffusion 2.1 on just 444 image-caption pairs from SPRIGHT (images with >18 objects) achieves a state-of-the-art T2I-CompBench Spatial Score of 0.2133, a 41% improvement over the SD 2.1 baseline (0.1507). On the VISOR benchmark, the model reaches 60.68% Object Accuracy and 16.15% on VISOR-4 (4-object spatial accuracy), compared to 47.83% and 4.70% for the baseline. Image fidelity also improves, with ZS-FID dropping from 21.646 to 16.149 and CMMD from 0.703 to 0.512. Using only ~0.25% of the full SPRIGHT dataset yields a 22% improvement in spatial accuracy while simultaneously improving FID and CMMD.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On the VISOR benchmark, the proposed method (fine-tuned on <500 SPRIGHT images) achieves the best scores on 5 of 7 spatial metrics, surpassing DALL-E 2 [47] on uncond (43.23% vs 37.89%), cond (71.24% vs 59.27%), VISOR-2 (51.88% vs 47.23%), VISOR-3 (33.09% vs 23.26%), and VISOR-4 (16.15% vs 7.49%), while DALL-E 2 retains a slight edge on OA (63.93% vs 60.68%) and VISOR-1 (73.59% vs 71.78%). The method dramatically outperforms all other baselines (GLIDE, CogView2, DALL-E Mini, Structured Diffusion, Attend-and-Excite) across every VISOR metric, with the largest gap on VISOR-4 where it scores 16.15% versus the next-best 7.49%. On GenEval, the model achieves 0.51 overall, beating SD 2.1 (0.50) and PixArt-Alpha (0.48) but trailing SDXL (0.55), though it improves on position (0.11 vs 0.07) and two-object generation (0.59 vs 0.51) over SD 2.1. On T2I-CompBench Spatial Score, the method sets a new state-of-the-art at 0.2133, exceeding all previously reported values. The key finding is that these gains are achieved with fewer than 500 training images, making the approach substantially more data-efficient than prior spatial-control methods like GLIGEN [30] or SpaText [1] which require additional conditioning architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Stable Diffusion v2.1 as its base architecture, fine-tuning both the U-Net and CLIP text encoder. Conceptually, it departs from prior spatial-control methods (GLIGEN, SpaText, ReCo) that introduce additional architectural conditioning signals, instead arguing that the root cause of spatial inconsistency is a data bottleneck: existing VL datasets simply lack spatial language. It aligns with the synthetic-captioning trend initiated by DALL-E 3, RECAP, and PixArt-Alpha, but redirects that effort toward explicitly encoding spatial relationships rather than general descriptiveness, and adds the novel finding that object density in training images is a critical lever for spatial consistency.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SPRIGHT is the first large-scale (~6M image) spatially-focused vision-language dataset, filling a clear gap that the authors quantify (ground-truth COCO captions capture only ~21% of spatial relationships vs. ~305% in SPRIGHT). The practical finding that fewer than 500 images with >18 objects suffice to reach SOTA spatial scores offers a highly data-efficient recipe for practitioners. Open questions remain: whether the object-density finding generalizes beyond SD 2.1 and CLIP-based encoders, how to handle negation robustly (the paper reports a significant performance drop on negation-only prompts), and whether scaling SPRIGHT to full training (rather than fine-tuning) yields further gains. The CKA-based layer analysis is a first step toward interpretability but remains preliminary.
