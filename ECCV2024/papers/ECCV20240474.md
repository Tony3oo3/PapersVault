## Problem

Existing group dance generation methods are architecturally constrained to a fixed, dataset-determined number of dancers and require processing all dancers simultaneously through cross-entity attention or group global attention mechanisms, causing memory and compute costs to grow with dancer count. This makes it infeasible to generate choreography for large or arbitrary groups while preserving motion naturalness and inter-dancer synchronization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240474/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a music sequence (a set of T audio frames) as the sole conditioning signal and produces 3D group dance motion for an arbitrary number N of dancers, each represented as a sequence of T SMPL 24-joint poses using 6D continuous rotations, 3D joint positions and velocities, plus 3D root translations. Crucially, N at inference is decoupled from the group sizes seen during training: the prior network is run once on the music to obtain a shared latent distribution, and each dancer's motion is generated by independently sampling from that distribution and decoding, so memory remains constant regardless of N.

## Method

PDVAE replaces the standard Gaussian latent vector of a conditional VAE with a variational phase manifold: the encoder (a Transformer decoder with cross-attention between motion and music) outputs D latent curves, which are parameterized by sinusoidal functions whose frequency-domain coefficients (amplitude, frequency, offset, phase shift) are extracted via differentiable FFT and treated as the variational latent variables. A group consistency loss (KL divergence between per-dancer posteriors plus L2 distance between their phase manifold vectors) forces all dancers in a group to share the same latent manifold given the same music. At inference, only the music-conditioned prior and decoder are needed; new motions are generated by sampling phase parameters from the learned distribution and reconstructing periodic latent curves that are decoded back to SMPL poses, yielding O(1) memory per dancer. Siren activations are used throughout to better model the periodic structure of motion.

## Results

On AIOZ-GDance, PDVAE achieves the best scores across all seven metrics: FID 31.01, MMC 0.271, GenDiv 10.98, PFC 2.33, GMR 30.08, GMC 84.52, and TIF 0.163. On AIST-M, it similarly leads with FID 31.49, MMC 0.257, GenDiv 11.81, PFC 1.42, GMR 41.24, GMC 78.64, and TIF 0.076. Critically, in the scalability analysis (Table 2), PDVAE maintains stable performance (FID ~31) even at 100 dancers, while GCD runs out of memory at 10 dancers and GDanceR at 100 dancers, with memory consumption remaining constant regardless of dancer count.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PDVAE outperforms all six baselines on both datasets across every reported metric. Against the closest competitor GCD (contrastive diffusion), PDVAE improves FID from 31.16→31.01 and GMR from 31.47→30.08 on AIOZ-GDance, and from 35.36→31.49 and 42.52→41.24 on AIST-M, while also achieving higher GMC (84.52 vs. 80.97) and lower TIF (0.163 vs. 0.167). The diffusion-based EDGE achieves competitive single-dancer FID (31.40) but suffers severe group-level degradation (GMR 63.35, GMC 61.72), indicating it cannot maintain synchronization across dancers. GDanceR, the prior group choreography method, shows the largest gap in group metrics (GMR 51.27 vs. 30.08 on AIOZ-GDance) and degrades sharply at 10 dancers (FID jumps to 57.93). The most decisive advantage is scalability: at 10 dancers, GCD exhausts 4 GB GPU memory entirely, and at 100 dancers both GCD and GDanceR fail, while PDVAE maintains FID 30.97 with constant memory usage. On AIST-M, DanY (a diffusion-based single-dancer method adapted for groups) trails PDVAE by 8.76 in FID and 15.11 in GMC, confirming that single-dancer architectures do not transfer well to group settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the conditional VAE framework (Sohn et al., 2015) and the sinusoidal phase parameterization from DeepPhase (Starke et al., 2022), repurposing frequency-domain phase variables (amplitude, frequency, offset, phase shift) as the variational latent space rather than a standard Gaussian vector. It departs from prior group dance methods (GDanceR, GCD, DanY) that rely on cross-entity attention or group global attention to process all dancers simultaneously, replacing that architecture with a shared phase manifold from which individual dancer motions are sampled independently. The group consistency loss is a new contribution that enforces manifold unification across dancers in the same group, addressing a gap not addressed by prior single-dancer VAE or diffusion approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The constant-memory scalability to 100+ dancers is a practical breakthrough for real-world applications like virtual metaverses and large-scale animation, where prior methods simply run out of memory beyond 5–10 dancers. The phase manifold representation offers a compact, interpretable frequency-domain encoding of motion that could transfer to other periodic motion tasks beyond dance. However, the VAE framework introduces acknowledged limitations (posterior collapse, unstable out-of-distribution sampling), and it remains open whether diffusion-based or flow-based generative models could be adapted to this manifold formulation to improve sample quality while retaining the scalability advantage.
