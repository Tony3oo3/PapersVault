## Problem

Existing generalized few-shot 3D segmentation methods (CAPL, Xu et al.) treat all unannotated background points as a single redundant class or use base classes as 'fake novel' samples during episodic training, ignoring the diverse semantic structure that novel-class points actually inhabit. This conflation causes ambiguous base/novel prototype differentiation and discards the very background context where novel-class points reside, degrading few-shot adaptation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240963/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes 3D point clouds (2048 points per 1m×1m block with xyz, RGB, and normalized coordinates) plus K-shot novel-class support point clouds at inference. During training, it additionally consumes rendered multi-view images (synthetic cameras at 8 azimuth regions) and a list of indoor-object text prompts encoded by CLIP. Output is a per-point semantic label over both base and novel classes, produced by cosine-similarity matching against a unified prototype matrix of size (N_b + N_n + 1) × C.

## Method

The core mechanism replaces CAPL's 'fake novel' prototypes with pseudo-prototypes derived from actual background points. A background feature extraction pipeline renders the point cloud from multiple synthetic viewpoints, projects each unannotated point to pixel coordinates via a pinhole model (with optional sphere rendering for sparse clouds), and aggregates multi-view pixel features from the OpenSeg foundation model into a per-point embedding. These background point features are then clustered by cosine similarity against CLIP text embeddings of indoor object names, yielding K pseudo-masks and corresponding pseudo-prototypes via masked average pooling over DGCNN point features. During episodic training, a subset of pseudo-prototypes is sampled alongside learnable base prototypes to form the classifier, forcing the model to learn distinctive background semantics rather than collapsing them into one class. At inference, pseudo-prototypes are swapped for true novel-class prototypes generated from support data, while the learned base prototypes remain fixed.

## Results

On S3DIS, the proposed method achieves 50.23% mIoU-N and 60.06% HM in the 5-shot setting, and 39.78% mIoU-N and 51.34% HM in the 1-shot setting, outperforming the previous state-of-the-art (Xu et al.) by approximately 7% and 10% in mIoU-N respectively. On ScanNet, the method reaches 23.34% mIoU-N (5-shot) and 15.57% mIoU-N (1-shot), improving over Xu et al. by ~5% and ~1% respectively. The method also achieves the smallest standard deviation across all four metrics (e.g., 2.00% HM std vs. 2.75–2.94% for baselines), indicating more stable performance across different support samples.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all four baselines on every metric (mIoU-B, mIoU-N, mIoU-A, HM) across both S3DIS and ScanNet in both 1-shot and 5-shot settings. Against the closest competitor Xu et al. [43], the gains are most pronounced in novel-class segmentation: +10.1% mIoU-N on S3DIS 1-shot (39.78 vs. 29.66) and +4.8% on ScanNet 5-shot (23.34 vs. 18.58). CAPL [38], designed for 2D generalized few-shot, lags significantly on novel classes (e.g., 23.87% mIoU-N on S3DIS 1-shot vs. 39.78% for the proposed method) because it relies on 'fake novel' prototypes from base classes rather than actual background semantics. attMPTI [47] and PIFS [1], which were not designed for the generalized setting, perform worst overall (e.g., attMPTI achieves only 11.39% mIoU-N on S3DIS 1-shot), confirming that standard few-shot methods struggle when base-class recognition is also required. The method also shows the lowest standard deviation (2.00% HM on S3DIS 5-shot), indicating more robust generalization across different support sample selections compared to CAPL (2.94%) and Xu et al. (2.75%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the CAPL [38] and Xu et al. [43] lineage of generalized few-shot 3D segmentation, which either generates 'fake novel' prototypes from base classes or supplements predictions with low-level geometric features. Rather than treating unannotated background points as a single redundant class, the authors repurpose 2D vision-language foundation models (OpenSeg, CLIP) as semantic feature extractors: they render multi-view images from point clouds, aggregate pixel-level features back to 3D points, and cluster them against text-embedding centroids to form pseudo-classes. This positions the work at the intersection of foundation-model-assisted 3D perception and prototype-based few-shot learning, using the DGCNN encoder and episodic training framework inherited from Xu et al. [43] and attMPTI [47].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The core insight—that background points during training are a superset of future novel-class points and thus carry transferable semantic structure—offers a principled alternative to the 'fake novel' heuristic, and the multi-view rendering + foundation-model pipeline is a reusable recipe for injecting 2D semantic knowledge into 3D point clouds without requiring RGB-D inputs. However, the gains are dataset-dependent (10% mIoU-N on S3DIS 1-shot but only ~1% on ScanNet 1-shot), pseudo-class assignment still contains substantial false positives/negatives, and the method's reliance on hand-crafted text prompts and a fixed number of pseudo-classes limits scalability to open-vocabulary or very large class sets. Open questions include whether the rendering-based feature transfer generalizes to outdoor or large-scale scenes, and whether learned (rather than text-anchored) cluster centers could reduce the prompt-engineering burden.
