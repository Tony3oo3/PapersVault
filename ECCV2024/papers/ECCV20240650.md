## Problem

NeRF-based dense SLAM systems are bottlenecked by their ray-tracing rendering pipeline, forcing them to sample only a limited number of camera rays and requiring many optimization steps, which prevents real-time operation. While 3D Gaussian Splatting offers a fast rasterization-based alternative, naively applying it to SLAM introduces overfitting from strong anisotropy, poor surface alignment that degrades tracking, and uncontrolled growth of Gaussians that slows the system down.

## Input / Output

The system consumes an online RGB-D image sequence (color + depth per frame) and incrementally builds a 3D Gaussian field while simultaneously estimating camera poses. Outputs include the camera pose trajectory, the Gaussian field itself (renderable for color, depth, opacity, and uncertainty maps), and a 3D mesh extracted via TSDF fusion. A lightweight variant operates at half resolution to roughly double tracking speed at a small accuracy cost, and the full system achieves ~15 Hz on an RTX 4090.

## Method

CG-SLAM builds a consistent, uncertainty-aware 3D Gaussian field optimized with a composite loss combining color, SSIM, alpha-blending depth, median-vs-alpha-blending depth alignment, a soft isotropy (scale) regularization, and a geometry variance term. A novel depth uncertainty model assigns each Gaussian primitive a scalar uncertainty computed from depth residuals at its dominated pixels across a keyframe window; primitives exceeding a threshold are suppressed to low opacity and re-optimized, filtering outliers without hard deletion. For tracking, the authors derive closed-form pose derivatives through the EWA splatting pipeline and implement a CUDA rasterizer that decouples tracking from mapping, using Lie-algebraic rotation parameters for sequential pose refinement and a sliding-window bundle adjustment (with NetVLAD-based co-visibility detection) to correct drift. Gaussian management spawns new primitives at under-observed pixels (opacity < 0.5) and inherits the original splitting/cloning/removing densification strategy.

## Results

On the Replica dataset, CG-SLAM achieves an average ATE RMSE of 0.27 cm, outperforming the best Gaussian-based baseline SplaTAM (0.36 cm) by ~25% and the best NeRF-based baselines Point-SLAM/Vox-Fusion (0.54 cm) by 50%. On TUM-RGBD, the method reaches 4.0 cm average ATE RMSE versus 5.48 cm for SplaTAM and 8.38 cm for Co-SLAM. For reconstruction on Replica, CG-SLAM attains 1.01 cm mapping accuracy, surpassing Point-SLAM (1.26 cm) and Co-SLAM (2.10 cm). The system operates at ~8.5 Hz (full) or ~16.8 Hz (lightweight), substantially faster than NeRF-based methods (0.6–1.3 Hz) and competitive with other Gaussian-based approaches.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CG-SLAM consistently outperforms all six baselines across all three datasets. Against the closest Gaussian-based competitor SplaTAM, CG-SLAM reduces average ATE RMSE from 0.36 cm to 0.27 cm on Replica (25% improvement), from 5.48 cm to 4.0 cm on TUM-RGBD (27% improvement), and from 11.88 cm to 8.08 cm on ScanNet (32% improvement). Against the strongest NeRF-based baselines (Point-SLAM and Vox-Fusion at 0.54 cm on Replica), CG-SLAM achieves a 50% reduction in tracking error. In reconstruction, CG-SLAM's 1.01 cm accuracy on Replica beats Point-SLAM (1.26 cm) and Co-SLAM (2.10 cm), though it trails slightly in completion (2.84 cm vs. Co-SLAM's 2.08 cm) due to the inherent hole-filling limitation of Gaussian representations without a global MLP. In runtime, CG-SLAM (8.47 FPS) is ~10× faster than NICE-SLAM (0.86 FPS) and Point-SLAM (0.63 FPS), and slightly faster than GS-SLAM (8.34 FPS), while the lightweight variant reaches 16.83 FPS at only a minor accuracy cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

CG-SLAM sits at the intersection of two research threads: NeRF-based dense SLAM (NICE-SLAM, Co-SLAM, Point-SLAM, Vox-Fusion), which established the paradigm of jointly optimizing scene representation and camera poses via differentiable rendering, and 3D Gaussian Splatting (Kerbl et al., 2023), which replaced the expensive ray-tracing pipeline with a fast, fully differentiable rasterizer. The paper departs from concurrent Gaussian-SLAM efforts (GS-SLAM, SplaTAM) by introducing task-specific designs absent in those systems: a soft anisotropy regularization to counter the overfitting tendency of free-form Gaussian ellipsoids, a depth-uncertainty model that scores and prunes uninformative primitives, and a thorough derivative analysis of camera poses through the EWA splatting Jacobian that enables a decoupled GPU tracking/mapping pipeline. The EWA splatting formulation from Zwicker et al. (2001) provides the mathematical backbone for the pose-derivation theory.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By achieving ~15 Hz tracking (16.8 Hz in the lightweight variant) versus 0.6–14 Hz for NeRF-based baselines, CG-SLAM demonstrates that the rasterization paradigm can finally close the long-standing efficiency gap in rendering-based SLAM, making dense neural-representation SLAM viable for real-time applications like AR/VR and robotics. The depth-uncertainty model and the finding that a *soft* anisotropy constraint (rather than full isotropy or no constraint) is critical for stable tracking offer transferable design principles for future Gaussian-based reconstruction systems. Key open questions remain: the substantial memory footprint of the Gaussian field limits scalability to large scenes, the system cannot handle dynamic objects, and the weak extrapolation to unobserved regions (evident in the Completion metric) suggests that a hybrid representation or learned prior may be needed for truly complete mapping.
