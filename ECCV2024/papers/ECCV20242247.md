## Problem

Underwater images suffer from caustic light patterns and water-column backscatter that severely degrade computer vision tasks like keypoint matching and SfM. Existing caustics removal methods require ground-truth annotations or prior 3D scene alignment, and backscatter removal methods demand raw image format or calibrated stereo rigs—making them impractical for real-world single-image use on standard RGB camera output.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20242247/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Both networks take a single RGB image (not raw) as input and operate in a single forward pass at 352×576 or 352×608 resolution. CausticsNet outputs a predicted caustics map and a caustics-free image; BackscatterNet outputs a descattered image, the estimated per-channel backscatter (B^∞ and β_B), and relies on a monocular depth map whose resolution bounds the effective backscatter resolution. Training consumes overlapping video frame pairs from diver-collected underwater footage, with temporal offsets of ~0.1 s for caustics and ~1 s for backscatter to ensure sufficient scene change.

## Method

The core insight is to use a pre-trained self-supervised monocular SLAM system (SC-SfMLearner) as a differentiable teacher signal for both restoration networks. CausticsNet is trained to predict the reprojection residual between two overlapping frames from a single image; since the residual in caustic scenes is dominated by the caustic pattern, the network learns caustics identification without any labels, regularized by total-variation and L2 terms. BackscatterNet estimates the six Akkaynak-Treibitz backscatter parameters per image and removes backscatter using the estimated monocular depth; its loss enforces photometric consistency between descattered frame pairs (via the SLAM reprojection), style invariance under color jitter, and backscatter color matching at far-field pixels. Both networks are trained end-to-end on 145 hours of unlabeled diver video from 35 coral reef sites, requiring no ground-truth colors, caustics masks, or raw images.

## Results

On the R-Caustics caustics segmentation benchmark, CausticsNet achieves 72.4% F1 and 93.6% accuracy in a zero-shot, domain-transfer setting (per-split threshold), matching the accuracy of a supervised DeepLabV3+ baseline (93.8%) while running three orders of magnitude faster. On the downstream image matching task (geometrically verified COLMAP keypoints across 6 held-out reef videos), CausticsNet alone yields 6,085 total matched keypoints (1.98× improvement over unmodified RGB), and the combined CausticsNet+BackscatterNet pipeline achieves 5,696 (1.85×). All 16 compared underwater image enhancement/restoration baselines produce fewer matched keypoints than the unmodified RGB input on most scenes, with the best baseline (LowComplexityDCP) reaching only 4,954 (1.14×).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CausticsNet decisively outperforms all 16 underwater image enhancement/restoration baselines on the downstream keypoint matching task, achieving 6,085 total verified keypoints versus 4,954 for the best baseline (LowComplexityDCP, 1.14×) and 4,512 for unmodified RGB. Notably, 14 of the 16 baselines produce *fewer* matched keypoints than the unmodified RGB input on most scenes, indicating that generic enhancement methods actively degrade scene-consistent feature matching. On the R-Caustics caustics segmentation task, CausticsNet (zero-shot, no training on R-Caustics) achieves 93.6% accuracy, essentially matching the supervised DeepLabV3+ baseline (93.8% accuracy, 81.25% F1) while requiring a single forward pass instead of 10,890 patch-based passes. BackscatterNet alone provides a modest improvement (1.07×) over RGB, confirming that caustics removal is the dominant factor for matching quality. The combined pipeline (CausticsNet+BackscatterNet) at 1.85× is slightly below CausticsNet alone (1.98×), suggesting that descattering can occasionally introduce minor inconsistencies that slightly reduce matching, though it still substantially outperforms all baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the self-supervised monocular SLAM framework (SC-SfMLearner, Bian et al. 2021) as a training signal generator for underwater image restoration, rather than as an end goal. The key conceptual departure from prior caustics removal (Forbes et al., Agrafiotis et al.) and underwater color restoration (Sea-Thru, Sea-Thru-NeRF) is that the SLAM reprojection residual naturally isolates temporally-inconsistent caustics and depth-dependent backscatter, providing free per-pixel supervision without ground-truth labels, raw images, or 3D scene alignment. BackscatterNet additionally inverts the Akkaynak-Treibitz physical model (Eq. 1) but learns its parameters end-to-end via the SLAM photometric loss rather than per-image optimization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first single-image caustics removal method that requires no ground-truth annotations, no in-domain training data, and no prior SfM alignment, making it applicable to arbitrary underwater RGB video in real time. The practical impact is significant for underwater photogrammetry and ecological surveying, where caustics and backscatter are the primary obstacles to reliable keypoint matching. Key limitations include: output resolution is bounded by the monocular depth map resolution, only additive backscatter is modeled (not attenuation, which dominates in deep water), and the SLAM system is frozen during restoration training. Open questions include whether joint training of SLAM and restoration networks, multi-frame temporal modeling, and learned attenuation could further close the gap to full SfM reconstruction.
