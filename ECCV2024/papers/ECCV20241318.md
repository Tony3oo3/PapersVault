## Problem

Existing neural rendering methods for 3D human avatars condition solely on a single static pose, creating an inherent ambiguity when identical poses correspond to different appearances due to motion inertia (e.g., a dress hem still settling after an abrupt stop). This limitation is compounded by current multi-view datasets that deliberately minimize inertia effects through tight-fitting garments and controlled, gradual motions, leaving the problem unaddressed in both method and evaluation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241318/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes multi-view RGB video (10 synchronized cameras at 100 fps on I3D-Human; 23 cameras on ZJU-MoCap) together with pre-extracted per-frame SMPL pose parameters. From these poses it derives a delta pose sequence—a window of per-joint rotation differences (axis-angle) and global translation deltas over a configurable temporal span (length L, step s, delta step s_d)—which is fed alongside the current pose as conditioning. The output is a canonical triplane radiance field that, when queried with a target pose and its delta sequence, yields novel-view and novel-pose renderings via standard volume rendering.

## Method

Dyco resolves pose-conditioning ambiguity by encoding a delta pose sequence (temporal pose residuals) as additional input to both the non-rigid deformation MLP and the canonical triplane volume, so that inertia-induced appearance and geometry variations become disentangled from static pose. To prevent overfitting from the high-dimensional sequence input, a localized dynamic context encoder first applies a kinematic-chain mask—retaining only joints in the same chain as the query point's nearest joint—then compresses the masked sequence via two MLPs (spatial reduction to 16-dim, temporal flattening to 32-dim) before concatenation with positional embeddings. The canonical space is represented by a triplane low-rank grid rather than a deep MLP, allowing a shallow 2-layer deformation network and fast convergence. The authors also introduce the I3D-Human dataset (6 subjects, ~10k frames, loose clothing with abrupt motions) and a motion-based evaluation metric (DME) computed via RAFT optical-flow end-point error between rendered and ground-truth frame pairs.

## Results

On the I3D-Human dataset, Dyco achieves 31.22 PSNR, 0.9738 SSIM, 34.54 LPIPS×10³, and 4.52 DME on novel-view rendering, and 30.12 PSNR, 0.9691 SSIM, 39.55 LPIPS×10³, and 3.98 DME on novel-pose rendering, outperforming all baselines across every metric. The most notable gain is in DME (dynamic motion error), where Dyco reduces the error by 0.41–1.21 pixels compared to baselines, confirming superior modeling of inertia-induced appearance changes. On ZJU-MoCap (tightly-clothed, slow motion), Dyco achieves 32.80 PSNR and 20.32 LPIPS×10³ on novel view, matching or exceeding all baselines, while remaining competitive on novel pose (29.71 PSNR vs. 30.01 for 3DGS-Avatar).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Dyco outperforms all four baselines on every metric for both novel-view and novel-pose rendering on I3D-Human. Against the strongest baseline 3DGS-Avatar (Gaussian splatting-based), Dyco gains +0.60 PSNR and −5.20 LPIPS×10³ on novel view, and +0.91 PSNR and −5.06 LPIPS×10³ on novel pose, with a DME improvement of 0.41 and 0.28 respectively. Against HumanNeRF (the closest NeRF-based baseline), the gap is even larger: +1.69 PSNR and −7.50 LPIPS×10³ on novel view. On ZJU-MoCap, where inertia effects are minimal, Dyco achieves the best LPIPS (20.32) on novel view but is marginally behind 3DGS-Avatar on PSNR (32.80 vs. 32.81) and novel-pose PSNR (29.71 vs. 30.01), confirming that the pose-sequence conditioning does not degrade performance in low-dynamics settings. The DME metric, introduced by the authors, shows Dyco's advantage is most pronounced on I3D-Human (up to 1.21 px lower than AniNeRF), validating that the delta-pose sequence specifically captures inertia-driven motion that per-frame metrics miss.

## Relation to Prior Work

Dyco extends the HumanNeRF/NeuralBody architecture (LBS rigid transform + non-rigid deformation + canonical radiance field) by adding a delta-pose sequence as an additional conditional input to both the deformation module and the canonical volume. Conceptually, it draws on DMPL and Dyna, which parameterized velocity and acceleration for mesh-based naked-body animation, and on DRAPE, which used motion history for clothing, but is the first to bring this inertia-aware conditioning into the implicit neural rendering setting. The triplane canonical representation (from EG3D) replaces the MLP-based volume to reduce network depth and accelerate convergence, while the localized dynamic context encoder is a novel design that masks kinematically irrelevant joints to prevent overfitting to the high-dimensional sequence input.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies and empirically validates a concrete failure mode of existing implicit avatars—ambiguity when identical poses map to different appearances due to inertia—which prior datasets (ZJU-MoCap, PeopleSnapshot) deliberately avoided through tight clothing and slow motion. The I3D-Human dataset and DME metric provide the field with tools to evaluate this previously unmeasured dimension of dynamic fidelity. Limitations include the small dataset scale (6 subjects, 10k frames), reliance on multi-view capture, and the fact that the pose-sequence window is fixed at training time, raising open questions about generalization to arbitrary motion lengths and monocular settings. Whether the delta-pose formulation can be extended to handle more complex cloth physics (e.g., wind, multi-layer garments) remains unexplored.
