## Problem

Fine-tuning CLIP via prompt learning or adapter tuning on base classes alone causes novel-class features to become entangled with base-class features, producing a strong prediction bias toward base classes in GZSL. Existing calibration or OOD-detection approaches treat domain separation as a post-hoc step, while fine-tuning methods ignore the feature confusion entirely, leaving no unified mechanism to both preserve base-class discrimination and recover novel-class transferability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240528/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, the method consumes a single RGB image and a set of class-name text prompts (each prepended with 4 learnable tokens of 512-dim vectors) processed by frozen CLIP ViT-B/16 encoders. The output is a two-stage decision: first a domain label (base vs. novel) assigned by comparing the cosine similarity between the 16-dim visual latent feature and the concatenated base/novel text latent features against an automatically calibrated threshold τ, then a class label produced by a separate classifier within the assigned domain. The refinement module blends adapter outputs with original CLIP features via a weighted residual (ε·t̃ + (1−ε)·t) before final classification.

## Method

PVA introduces two Spherical VAE adapters (visual and text) that map frozen CLIP 512-dim features into a shared 16-dim latent space, where alignment is enforced by an Earth Mover's Distance loss between the two latent distributions, cross-domain reconstruction losses (each decoder reconstructs the other modality's input), and a dual classification loss operating at both instance level (CLIP-style softmax over prompts) and latent level (softmax over latent features). The text adapter input is a learnable prompt of length M=4 rather than hand-crafted attributes, providing task-specific discriminative tokens. At test time, base and novel samples are separated by a cosine-similarity threshold automatically calibrated from training-set statistics (parameter τ), converting GZSL into an independent supervised classification and a zero-shot task. A residual refinement module then blends the adapter output with the original CLIP encoder output (controlled by ε) to inject general knowledge from the pre-trained model and improve novel-class transferability.

## Results

On the GZSC task, PVA+R achieves harmonic means of 90.7% (AwA2), 72.0% (CUB), and 65.8% (SUN), setting new state-of-the-art on AwA2 and SUN. The novel-class accuracy (N) reaches 88.0% on AwA2, a 15.3-point improvement over CoOP (72.7%), directly addressing the prediction bias problem. On cross-dataset transfer (trained on AwA2, tested without fine-tuning), PVA+R outperforms CoOP by 4.0%, 0.4%, and 2.6% harmonic mean on CUB, SUN, and FLO respectively. The data separation module achieves AUC > 96% on all four benchmarks for distinguishing base from novel samples.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PVA+R achieves the best harmonic mean on AwA2 (90.7%) and SUN (65.8%), surpassing the strongest embedding-based method PSVMA (75.4% and 52.3%) by 15.3 and 13.5 points respectively. On CUB, PVA+R (72.0%) trails PSVMA (73.8%) by 1.8 points, the only dataset where it does not lead. Compared to the prompt-learning baseline CoOP, PVA+R gains 15.3 points on AwA2 novel accuracy (88.0% vs 72.7%) and 16.1 points on CUB novel accuracy (65.7% vs 49.2%), confirming that the variational adapter effectively mitigates the feature confusion that degrades CoOP's transferability. Against the calibration-based GatingAE, PVA+R improves harmonic mean by 21.4, 15.3, and 24.4 points on AwA2, CUB, and SUN, showing that explicit domain separation via latent similarity outperforms learned gating. In cross-dataset transfer, PVA+R beats CoOP on all three target datasets (CUB +4.0%, SUN +0.4%, FLO +2.6% H), while CoOP actually underperforms the untrained CLIP on CUB (52.1% vs 55.0%), highlighting the overfitting problem in standard prompt fine-tuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

PVA sits at the intersection of two fine-tuning paradigms for CLIP—prompt learning (CoOP, CoCoOP) and adapter-style tuning (CLIP-Adapter, Tip-Adapter)—and is, to the authors' knowledge, the first to unify them in a single framework for GZSL. The variational adapter structure draws directly from Spherical VAEs (Davidson et al. 2018; Schonfeld et al. 2019), repurposing the vMF-distribution latent space not for feature generation but for creating a shared alignment space where base/novel separation becomes a simple cosine-similarity threshold. Conceptually, the paper departs from calibration-based GZSL methods (ESZSL, COSMO, OOD) that learn an explicit domain detector, instead embedding the separation logic into the adapter's latent geometry so that the same trained model handles both domain assignment and classification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The divide-and-conquer reframing—converting a single biased GZSL problem into an independent base-classification task plus a zero-shot novel-class task—offers a clean conceptual solution to the prediction-bias problem that has plagued VLM-based GZSL. The residual refinement connecting adapter outputs back to frozen CLIP features is a practical recipe for preserving general knowledge while injecting task-specific discrimination, and the cross-dataset transfer results suggest the learned latent geometry generalizes beyond the training domain. However, the approach incurs additional training cost (two SVAE adapters plus prompts), the cross-dataset gains are modest (0.4% on SUN), and it remains unclear whether the variational structure is strictly necessary or whether simpler alignment losses could achieve comparable separation quality at lower cost.
