## Problem

Existing explanations of LoRA frame adaptation matrices as approximations of full-tuning weight differences, yet this view cannot explain why very low ranks suffice, why only certain layers need adaptation, or why linear updates replicate nonlinear ViT behavior. The paper addresses this gap by reinterpreting adaptation matrices as control variables that perturb a pre-trained ViT system, enabling a principled optimal-control analysis grounded in the Pontryagin Maximum Principle.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241408/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes standard RGB images (32×32 CIFAR-100, SVHN, and Food-101) as input, which are patch-embedded into token sequences by a frozen pre-trained ViT. The output is a class label produced by the final classification head. Unusually, the only trainable parameters are binary-valued control matrices (entries restricted to ±1) with per-layer trainable scalar amplifiers, rather than conventional floating-point weights.

## Method

The paper formulates ViT fine-tuning as a continuous-time optimal control problem where the residual ViT blocks define the system dynamics and the adaptation matrices serve as controls. Applying the Pontryagin Maximum Principle, it shows that for bounded control sets (hypercubes), the Hamiltonian-maximizing controls take extreme boundary values (Bang-Bang principle), yielding binary ±1 matrices. A key theorem proves that in the continuous-time limit, binary controls achieve the same reachable state set as full-precision controls, since any matrix is a convex combination of binary matrices that can be unfolded over time. The resulting BiC algorithm optimizes binary matrices via Hamiltonian maximization with momentum-based sign updates to prevent unstable flipping, and introduces trainable per-layer scaling factors to modulate perturbation magnitude. Empirically, BiC matches or exceeds full-precision AdaptFormer at a fraction of the parameter and communication cost, and the paper shows that high-rank low-precision controls outperform low-rank full-precision ones for vision tasks.

## Results

On CIFAR-100, SVHN, and Food-101, BiC-768 achieves 86.61%, 97.08%, and 88.31% one-time accuracy using only 0.35% of total parameters, matching the full-precision AdaptFormer-768 (86.73%, 97.04%, 88.39%) which requires 8.40% of parameters. At matched computation cost, BiC-32 outperforms rank-1 AdaptFormer by +1.85%, +3.99%, and +2.56% on CIFAR-100, SVHN, and Food-101 respectively. In a distributed setting on SVHN, BiC requires only 3.5% of the communication cost of its full-precision counterpart to reach the same terminal test accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against AdaptFormer (the vision-specific LoRA variant), BiC-64 uses 11x fewer bytes (0.13% vs 1.46%) while achieving nearly identical accuracy: 85.74% vs 85.90% on CIFAR-100, 97.01% vs 96.89% on SVHN, and 87.46% vs 87.61% on Food-101. At the higher rank, BiC-768 (0.35% bytes) matches AdaptFormer-768 (8.40% bytes) within 0.12% on CIFAR-100 and actually edges it out on SVHN (97.08% vs 97.04%). When computation cost is held equal (Table 2), BiC-32 decisively beats rank-1 AdaptFormer by +1.85%, +3.99%, and +2.56% across the three datasets, demonstrating that high-rank low-precision controls are more effective than low-rank full-precision ones for vision tasks. Against Visual Prompt Tuning (VPT), BiC-64 outperforms by a wide margin: +3.30% on CIFAR-100, +2.99% on SVHN, and +4.48% on Food-101, while using only 1.4x the parameter budget.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reinterprets LoRA and AdaptFormer through the lens of classical optimal control theory, departing from the prevailing 'weight difference' explanation by recasting adaptation matrices as control variables that perturb a pre-trained ViT system. It builds on the line of work treating neural networks as discretizations of optimal control problems (Haber & Ruthotto, Li et al.) but applies the Pontryagin Maximum Principle specifically to the PEFT setting, showing that LoRA's gradient-descent updates are a special case of Hamiltonian maximization. The binary controller (BiC) design draws on Bang-Bang control theory from classical control, and the soft-bounding optimization scheme is related to prior binary neural network training methods, but the paper's focus on reducing controller precision (rather than model precision) distinguishes it from quantized fine-tuning work like QLoRA.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The control-theoretic framing offers a principled explanation for why low-rank adaptation works without requiring the 'low intrinsic rank' assumption, and the Bang-Bang result provides a theoretical justification for binary controllers that could reduce communication costs by ~30× in federated settings. However, the continuous-time reachability proof (Theorem 2) is an idealization that does not directly transfer to discrete ViT blocks, and all empirical validation is limited to CIFAR-scale datasets with a single ViT architecture, leaving open whether the rank-over-precision finding and binary-control equivalence hold at ImageNet scale or for LLMs. A key open question is whether the PMP-derived optimal controls can be computed efficiently in practice without the iterative MSA procedure, and whether the framework extends to non-linear control forms beyond the linear assumption.
