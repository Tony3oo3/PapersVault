## Problem

Pretrained CLIP-like models entangle latent content and style information in their representations, making them sensitive to prompt choice, distribution shifts, and adversarial perturbations. While causal representation learning theory shows that data augmentation can provably isolate content from style, it remains unclear how to practically implement this within frozen CLIP architectures, and image augmentation alone struggles to alter all style factors simultaneously due to the high dimensionality of visual style.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240544/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, the method takes an RGB image and a set of class-name text prompts, passing each through CLIP's frozen image/text encoder followed by the trained disentangled MLP to produce content feature vectors for cosine-similarity classification. During training, the text-augmentation variant (CLAP) consumes structured template prompts of the form 'a [art style] [image type] of a [object size] [object color] [class]' (480 per class), while the image-augmentation variant (Im.Aug) uses Stable Diffusion–synthesized images generated from those same prompts, augmented with random cropping and color distortion.

## Method

CLAP trains a lightweight residual MLP (with a zero-initialized, bias-free linear layer inspired by ControlNet's zero-conv) on top of CLIP's frozen text encoder using an InfoNCE contrastive loss over pairs of original and augmented prompts. The key insight is that text augmentation—deleting style attributes (art style, image type, object color, object size) or swapping prompt order—more reliably perturbs all latent style variables than pixel-level image augmentation, because text's logical structure allows targeted, property-wise manipulation. A secondary loss term anchors each augmented prompt to its bare class name to increase intra-pair variation. After training on text features, the same disentangled network is transferred to CLIP's image encoder, so both modalities share a single content-extraction head, preserving zero-shot inference while yielding features that are more robust to prompt perturbation, domain shift, and adversarial attacks.

## Results

On zero-shot classification, CLAP achieves 79.9% average top-1 accuracy (ZS(C) prompt) across PACS, VLCS, Office-Home, and DomainNet, surpassing CLIP (77.4%) and Im.Aug (76.1%). The advantage is most pronounced under the noisy dynamic prompt ZS(NC), where CLAP reaches 76.1% versus 70.4% for CLIP and 69.9% for Im.Aug. In the 1-shot linear-probe setting, CLAP improves over CLIP by up to +10% on PACS, and under FGSM adversarial attacks, CLAP's zero-shot classifier gains +7.6% over CLIP on average. CLAP also reduces the zero-shot performance variance across prompts (range R) from 2.7% (CLIP) to 1.1%, indicating substantially better prompt robustness.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares CLAP against two baselines: the original CLIP (ViT-B/16) and Im.Aug (the image-augmentation variant of the same disentangled-network framework). CLAP wins on every dataset and every prompt setting in the zero-shot evaluation, with the largest margin appearing under the noisy ZS(NC) prompt where CLAP gains +5.7% over CLIP and +6.2% over Im.Aug on average. Im.Aug, by contrast, shows inconsistent results: it improves over CLIP on PACS but degrades on DomainNet (51.5% vs. 57.8% under ZS(C)), suggesting that image augmentation alone fails to alter all style factors. In the 1-shot linear-probe setting, CLAP outperforms CLIP by +10% on PACS, +3.5% on VLCS, +2.5% on Office-Home, and +1.5% on DomainNet, while Im.Aug yields smaller and less consistent gains. Under adversarial attacks (FGSM, PGD-20, CW-20), CLAP's zero-shot and 1-shot classifiers surpass CLIP by +7.6%/+8.5% (FGSM), +1.0%/+11.7% (PGD-20), and +1.1%/+2.3% (CW-20), respectively, all exceeding the corresponding Im.Aug improvements.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper operationalizes the theoretical disentanglement framework of Von Kugelgen et al. (2021), which proved that contrastive learning with data augmentation isolates content from style, by applying it to frozen CLIP encoders via a lightweight residual MLP. It departs from prior disentanglement work that relied on image augmentation (crops, color jitter) by introducing text/prompt augmentation as a more effective style-intervention mechanism, motivated by the higher semanticity and logical structure of language. The disentangled network architecture adapts ControlNet's zero-conv trick to a zero-linear residual block, and the prompt augmentation techniques are customized from EDA's random deletion and swap operations. The causal generative model unifying image and text under a shared latent space (content c, style s) provides the theoretical justification for cross-modal transfer: a network trained to disentangle text features can be applied to image features.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CLAP offers a practical, low-cost recipe (two-layer MLP, 11–47 min training, frozen CLIP) to reduce CLIP's well-known prompt sensitivity and adversarial vulnerability, with consistent gains across four domain-generalization benchmarks and three model scales. The key open question is whether the causal assumptions (content exclusively determines label; style is fully intervened upon by the chosen augmentations) hold beyond the controlled template-prompt setting, and whether the approach transfers to open-vocabulary or generative tasks. A limitation is that the image-augmentation variant requires synthetic data generation via Stable Diffusion, and the text-augmentation variant's gains may depend on the structured template format rather than generalizing to arbitrary prompts.
