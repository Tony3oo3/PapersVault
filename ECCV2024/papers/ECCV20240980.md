## Problem

Existing localization methods rely on visual sensors, point clouds, or GPS signals, but natural language offers a more natural and lightweight interface for humans to communicate their location to embodied agents. Prior language-based localization work (e.g., Text2Pos) targets outdoor point-cloud maps and requires a fixed vocabulary mapping, limiting generalization to open-set indoor descriptions. This paper introduces 'language-based scene-retrieval': identifying which scene from a collection of disjoint 3D scene graphs an open-set natural language query describes, without requiring any geometric or metric information.

## Input / Output

The method takes an open-set natural language text query (no fixed template) and a database of 3D semantic scene graphs that contain only word2vec-encoded object labels, attributes, and spatial-relationship edges (no 3D positions or point clouds; edges are pruned to a 1.5 m bounding-box distance threshold). The text query is first parsed by GPT-4 into a 'text-graph' of objects, attributes, and stated relationships, which is then vectorized identically to the scene graphs. The output is a per-scene matching score (cosine similarity or MLP-predicted probability) from which the highest-scoring scene graph is selected as the retrieval result.

## Method

Text2SceneGraphMatcher (Text2SGM) learns a joint embedding space for text-graphs and scene-graphs using a single Graph Transformer module that performs self-attention within each graph and cross-attention between the text-graph and scene-graph node sets, followed by additive node aggregation and average pooling to produce fixed-length graph embeddings. The two embeddings are concatenated and fed to a 3-layer MLP that outputs a matching probability, while the raw embeddings also support cosine-similarity scoring. Training uses a combined contrastive objective: a softmax cosine-similarity loss over the batch plus a softmax matching-probability loss, both supervised by whether the text and scene graph belong to the same scene. A retrieval-based inference variant precomputes scene-graph embeddings with a fixed dummy text input, enabling nearest-neighbor search without re-running cross-attention at query time. The authors use only one attention module (N=1) because additional layers cause over-smoothing that collapses small graph representations.

## Results

On the ScanScribe test set (top-k out of 10 candidates), Text2SGM with cosine similarity scoring achieves 68.27% top-1 recall, 84.95% top-2, 91.65% top-3, and 97.14% top-5, substantially outperforming CLIP2CLIP (33.09% top-1) and Text2Pos (11.63% top-1). On the more challenging Human-Annotated dataset, Text2SGM(cos-sim) reaches 53.90% top-1 and 94.87% top-5 recall, still ahead of CLIP2CLIP (49.87% top-1) and Text2Pos (9.57% top-1). The retrieval-based variant (precomputing scene graph embeddings) matches the joint-embedding variant within 0.1–0.5% recall, confirming the learned embeddings are meaningful. Runtime is ~12–36 ms per query and the full scene-graph embedding database requires only 0.13–0.34 MB, an order of magnitude less storage than CLIP2CLIP (5.63–14.54 MB).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Text2SGM decisively outperforms Text2Pos on both datasets across all top-k settings, with a gap of roughly 57 percentage points in top-1 recall on ScanScribe (68.27% vs. 11.63%) and 44 points on Human (53.90% vs. 9.57%). The large margin over Text2Pos is attributed to its reliance on a fixed vocabulary mapping that fails to generalize to open-set human-written queries, as well as its inability to handle indoor scenes. Against CLIP2CLIP, Text2SGM wins by a wide margin on ScanScribe (68.27% vs. 33.09% top-1) but the gap narrows considerably on the Human dataset, where CLIP2CLIP's large-scale pre-training gives it an edge on the hardest retrieval setting (top-75 out of 142: 98.64% vs. 95.87%). On the Human dataset top-5 out of 10, CLIP2CLIP edges out Text2SGM by a marginal 0.24 points (95.11% vs. 94.87%), suggesting CLIP's broader visual pre-training helps with out-of-distribution human language. Overall, Text2SGM is the strongest method in 11 of 12 top-k configurations across both datasets, with the sole exception being CLIP2CLIP's narrow top-5 advantage on Human.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of language-based localization (Text2Pos, Text2Loc, RET) from point-cloud maps to lightweight 3D semantic scene graphs, and extends image-based scene retrieval (3DSSG) to the language modality. It repurposes the Graph Transformer architecture for cross-modal matching between LLM-extracted text-graphs and scene graphs, using a joint embedding objective inspired by CLIP-style contrastive learning. The key departure from prior coarse-localization work is the elimination of geometric/metric information and the use of open-set natural language without a predefined vocabulary mapping, enabled by GPT-4-based text-to-graph extraction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides a practical, low-bandwidth channel for humans to communicate location to embodied agents using only natural language, with real-time inference and minimal storage (hundreds of KB vs. MBs for CLIP-based alternatives). The human-annotated dataset addresses a gap in open-set evaluation, though it is small (147 descriptions). Open questions include whether the approach scales to hierarchical, building-level scene graphs, whether adding metric information would enable fine-grained 6DoF localization, and how performance degrades as the scene database grows beyond a few hundred scenes.
