## Problem

Transformer-based representation learning models like CLIP and DINO lack an architectural prior for selective attention: their encodings do not separately attend to individual concepts, causing them to struggle with robustness to distractions and compositional generalization. Unlike human perception, which constrains attention to one concept at a time, standard transformer readouts (CLS token, global average pooling) produce monolithic encodings with no mechanism to partition task-relevant aspects of the input.

## Input / Output

SPARO takes the sequence of hidden states H ∈ R^{n×d} from a ViT or text transformer backbone and produces a slot-structured encoding y ∈ R^{L×V} that is the concatenation of L independently-attended slots, each of dimension V (typically 64). Each slot y_l is l2-normalized separately before the concatenation is divided by √L to yield a globally normalized vector. This structure means the output is not a single flat vector but a collection of low-dimensional concept encodings, enabling post-hoc selection or intervention on individual slots for downstream tasks.

## Method

SPARO replaces the final transformer block with L single-head attention mechanisms, each with a learned query embedding q_l and key projection K_l, producing one slot per head via y_l = W K_l H^T softmax(H K_l^T q_l / √D). The value projection W is shared across all slots to reduce parameters, and the low slot dimensionality (V=D=64) combined with the single-head bottleneck forces each slot to selectively attend to a limited set of features. In CLIP, the cosine similarity between image and text encodings decomposes into the expected cosine similarity between corresponding slots, imposing a cross-modal alignment prior that both modalities encode the same L concepts. This design differs from attentional poolers (which use multi-head attention and mix head outputs) and slot attention (where slots compete for positions rather than positions competing within a single head), and ablations confirm that both the separate-head bottleneck and slot-wise normalization/projection are critical for the observed gains.

## Results

On CC15M-trained CLIP, SPARO achieves 43.7% zero-shot ImageNet-V1 accuracy (vs. 38.4% for standard CLIP and 39.9% for CLIP+GAP), a +14% relative improvement over CLIP. On SugarCrepe compositionality, SPARO reaches 0.730 (vs. 0.699 CLIP, 0.701 CLIP+GAP), and on ObjectNet robustness it reaches 0.279 (vs. 0.238 CLIP). At scale (LAION-400M, ViT-B/32), SPARO improves ImageNet-V1 to 63.5% (vs. 61.7% CLIP) and SugarCrepe to 0.770 (vs. 0.748 CLIP). For DINO (ViT-S/16, self-supervised), SPARO improves both 20-nearest-neighbor (70.6% vs. 68.5%) and linear probe (75.7% vs. 73.5%) ImageNet accuracy by +3% each.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SPARO consistently outperforms standard CLIP across all four training scales and all seven evaluation metrics (ImageNet-V1/V2/Sketch/A/R, ObjectNet, SugarCrepe), with the largest gains on robustness benchmarks (e.g., +15% relative on ImageNet-R at CC15M) and compositionality (+4.4% on SugarCrepe at CC15M). Against CLIP+GAP, which has access to all final-layer token outputs, SPARO still wins on every metric in every setting, confirming that the separate-head attention bottleneck provides benefits beyond simply using more input positions. For self-supervised DINO, SPARO improves both 20-NN (70.6% vs. 68.5%) and linear probe (75.7% vs. 73.5%) ImageNet accuracy by +3% each without changing any other hyperparameter. In the ablation against the attentional pooler (AttPool) [Lee et al., 2019], SPARO achieves 0.372 ImageNet zero-shot accuracy with 161M parameters versus AttPool's 0.344 with 306M parameters, demonstrating that the separate-head constraint is both more accurate and more parameter-efficient. Notably, SPARO also outperforms CLIP+GAP on retrieval (e.g., +10.2% relative on COCO image R@5 at CC3M), indicating the slot structure benefits both modalities independently.

## Relation to Prior Work

SPARO builds directly on CLIP and DINO as pretraining frameworks, replacing their standard pooling readout (CLS/EOS token extraction or global average pooling) with a slot-structured attention mechanism. Conceptually, it departs from the attentional pooler (Set Transformer) by enforcing a critical structural constraint: each slot is produced by an independent single-head attention operation rather than a shared multi-head attention, preventing information mixing between concepts. It also differs from slot attention, where slots compete for input positions, by having input positions compete for each slot through a single attention head. The paper frames this as introducing a missing cognitive prior—selective attention over individual concepts—into transformer encoders that previously lacked any mechanism to separately represent attended concepts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The slot-structured encoding enables a practically useful capability absent in standard CLIP/DINO: post-hoc selection of task-relevant concepts (e.g., keeping only top-32 slots by ImageNet accuracy improves SugarCrepe compositionality from +4% to +9%). This opens a path toward interpretable, task-adaptive use of pretrained encoders without retraining. However, the paper does not impose explicit independence or disentanglement constraints on slots, leaving open whether stronger distributional objectives could further improve concept separation. The moderate but consistent gains across recognition, robustness, retrieval, and compositionality benchmarks (2–4% typically, up to 14% on small-data ImageNet) suggest the architectural prior is beneficial but not transformative at scale, and the technique's simplicity (replacing one transformer block) makes it an easy drop-in for existing pipelines.
