## Problem

Neural implicit surface reconstruction methods struggle with textureless and specular objects because their image-reconstruction losses tightly couple shape and appearance, leading to the shape-radiance ambiguity where surfaces distort to fit reflection colors. Polarization images provide direct geometric constraints on surface normal azimuth angles independent of appearance, but prior polarimetric reconstruction methods either rely on mesh representations that cannot correct topology errors or use orthographic polarimetric constraints that neglect the perspective effect of the camera lens, introducing surface cracks and swelling.

## Input / Output

The method takes 40–60 synchronized polarization images per object (four polarizer angles: 0°, 45°, 90°, 135°) captured with a Sony color polarization camera, along with camera poses estimated via COLMAP from derived RGB images. The output is a neural SDF represented as a 16-level multi-resolution hash grid (resolutions 32–2700) decoded by two shallow MLPs, which is converted to a watertight 3D mesh via Marching Cubes. The color images used for photometric supervision are obtained by averaging the four polarization-angle images and demosaicing.

## Method

PISR represents shape as a multi-resolution hash grid with separate SDF and color MLPs, enabling 4–30× faster convergence than purely MLP-based methods while allowing topology changes during optimization. The core contribution is a perspective polarimetric loss that incorporates camera ray directions into the normal constraint (rather than the standard orthographic formulation), reducing L1 Chamfer distance by 30% over the orthographic variant. To resolve the π/2 ambiguity between diffuse and specular reflections, the loss is segmented by degree of polarization: below a DoP threshold of 0.3, both disambiguation branches are multiplied (only one needs to be minimized); above it, only the specular branch is used. A normal-smoothing loss with criss-cross pattern sampling in image space regularizes the discrete hash-grid representation, preventing local minima and surface cracks. Optimization proceeds in three stages: photometric-only initialization, joint polarimetric + normal refinement, and final photometric + polarimetric polishing with the normal loss annealed to zero.

## Results

On the PISR polarization dataset of four textureless and specular objects, PISR achieves an average L1 Chamfer distance of 0.5 mm and an F-score of 99.5% at 1 mm threshold, substantially outperforming all five baselines. The second-best method, PMVIR, achieves 1.0 mm CD and 90.5% F-score, roughly twice the error and 9 percentage points lower in F-score. PISR also converges 4–41× faster than the other methods, completing optimization in 0.5 hours versus 10.8–20.5 hours for the neural SDF baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PISR outperforms all five baselines on every object and both metrics. Against the closest competitor PMVIR (mesh-based polarimetric inverse rendering), PISR halves the average Chamfer distance (0.5 mm vs. 1.0 mm) and improves F-score by 9 percentage points (99.5% vs. 90.5%), attributed to the perspective polarimetric constraint and the ability of neural SDF to rectify shape topology during optimization. Among RGB-only methods, NeuS is the strongest (1.3 mm CD, 83.2% FS), while Ref-NeuS and NeRO—designed specifically for specular objects—actually underperform NeuS on this dataset, confirming the severity of the shape-radiance ambiguity. PANDORA, which also uses polarization images but through polarimetric volume rendering, performs worst overall (1.8 mm CD, 68.8% FS), suggesting that implicit polarization cues via rendering are less effective than explicit geometric constraints for textureless and specular surfaces. In terms of speed, PISR converges in 0.5 hours (20k iterations) compared to 20+ hours for Ref-NeuS and NeRO, 10.8 hours for NeuS, and 15 hours for PANDORA, a 4–41× speedup enabled by the multi-resolution hash grid representation.

## Relation to Prior Work

PISR sits at the intersection of neural implicit surface reconstruction (NeuS, NeRO, Ref-NeuS) and polarimetric multi-view 3D reconstruction (PolMVS, PMVIR, PANDORA). It departs from prior polarimetric methods in two key ways: (1) it uses the perspective polarimetric constraint [4] rather than the orthographic one adopted by PolMVS, PMVIR, and others, and (2) it applies polarization as a direct geometric regularizer on the SDF rather than through polarimetric volume rendering (PANDORA, NeISF) or mesh-based inverse rendering (PMVIR). The hash-grid-based neural SDF representation follows the Instant-NGP/NeuS2 lineage for efficiency, while the image-space normal smoothing with criss-cross sampling is a novel regularization strategy inspired by belief propagation in classical MVS.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PISR provides the first public dataset of real textureless and specular objects with ground-truth meshes for polarimetric 3D reconstruction, filling a notable gap that has limited quantitative evaluation in this subfield. The perspective polarimetric constraint is a practical, drop-in improvement over the orthographic formulation used in all prior polarimetric reconstruction work, yielding a 30% Chamfer distance reduction. However, the method's applicability is bounded: it degrades on rough, well-textured, or mirror-like surfaces where AoP estimates become noisy, and it requires dense multi-view input (40–60 views). Open questions include whether integrating learned normal or depth priors could reduce the view requirement, and whether the polarimetric loss can be combined with the polarimetric inverse rendering methods (NeISF, PANDORA) for joint geometry-and-material recovery.
