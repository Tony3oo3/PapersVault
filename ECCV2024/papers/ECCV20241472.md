## Problem

Existing NeRV methods suffer from spectral bias, where neural networks learn low-frequency components faster than high-frequency ones, causing poor reconstruction of fine spatial details and motion patterns in video. No prior NeRV approach has explicitly addressed this frequency-domain imbalance, leaving a gap between the model's capacity to represent LF structure and its ability to capture HF textures.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241472/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes an RGB video sequence (tested at 480×960, 960×1920, 720×1280, and 1080×1920 resolutions) and produces reconstructed frames, temporally interpolated frames, compressed bitstreams, or in-painted frames depending on the task. Unusually, the encoder does not embed the full frame: it applies 2D DWT and embeds only the low-pass sub-band C_LL (reduced to H/2×W/2), while the three high-frequency sub-bands (C_LH, C_HL, C_HH) are never stored but synthesized entirely by the decoder. Model capacity is held at 3M parameters across all comparisons.

## Method

SNeRV decomposes each frame via 2D Haar DWT into LF and HF wavelet sub-bands, then embeds only the LF component through down-sampling blocks to form a content-adaptive embedding e_t. The decoder progressively up-samples e_t using five NeRV blocks, refines the LF features with a Multi-resolution Fusion Unit (MFU) that concatenates multi-scale features through transposed convolutions and residual blocks, and synthesizes the three HF sub-bands via a High-Frequency Restorer (HFR) with three small convolution blocks; the final frame is recovered by inverse DWT. A temporal extension applies an additional 1D DWT along the time axis to adjacent frame pairs, producing backward and forward temporal embeddings that are jointly up-sampled with e_t through Temporally Extended Up-sampling Blocks (TUBs) using 3D convolutions. Training uses a combined loss of L1+SSIM in the pixel domain plus an L1 loss on the four wavelet coefficient sub-bands, encouraging accurate frequency-domain reconstruction.

## Results

On video regression at 960×1920 resolution, SNeRV backbone (Ours(B)) achieves 35.40 dB average PSNR on UVG and 31.22 dB on DAVIS, outperforming HNeRV (34.08 / 29.12 dB) and DNeRV (33.91 / 29.23 dB) by approximately 1.3–1.5 dB and 2.0–2.3 dB respectively. The temporal extension (Ours(T)) achieves 31.96 dB on UVG and 21.28 dB on DAVIS for video interpolation, surpassing DNeRV (30.34 / 20.14 dB) by ~1.6 dB and ~1.1 dB. On the Bunny dataset at 3M model size, Ours(B) reaches 39.64 dB PSNR after 300 epochs, exceeding HNeRV (38.06 dB) and DNeRV (37.55 dB) by 1.58 dB and 2.09 dB respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SNeRV backbone (Ours(B)) consistently outperforms all NeRV-based baselines on video regression: it beats NeRV [8] by ~3.3 dB (UVG 960) and ~5.8 dB (DAVIS 960), E-NeRV [24] by ~3.0 dB and ~4.3 dB, HNeRV [7] by ~1.3 dB and ~2.1 dB, and DNeRV [48] by ~1.5 dB and ~2.0 dB. For video interpolation, the temporal extension Ours(T) surpasses DNeRV [48] by ~1.6 dB (UVG) and ~1.1 dB (DAVIS), and HNeRV [7] by ~2.6 dB and ~2.1 dB, while Ours(B) without temporal modeling is slightly behind DNeRV on interpolation (29.37 vs. 30.34 dB on UVG), confirming a regression-vs-interpolation trade-off. In video compression, Ours(B) improves coding efficiency over H.264 [45] and all NeRV-based codecs, achieving comparable BD-PSNR gains with H.265 [40], DVC [25], and FVC [15] at low bit-rates, while DCVC [23] requires 2,268G MACs versus Ours(B)'s 168G. When integrated into HiNeRV's [20] coding framework, Ours(B)+HiNeRV improves BD-PSNR by 0.3 dB over HiNeRV alone. On video in-painting, Ours(B) achieves 30.30 dB average, outperforming HNeRV (29.59 dB) and DNeRV (29.92 dB) by 0.71 dB and 0.38 dB respectively.

## Relation to Prior Work

SNeRV builds directly on the HNeRV framework [7], which introduced content-adaptive frame embeddings jointly optimized with a learned decoder, and on DNeRV [48], which modeled temporal dynamics via a dynamical system. The key conceptual departure is the explicit use of 2D DWT to separate low-frequency and high-frequency components, embedding only the LF content while synthesizing HF details through specialized modules (MFU and HFR), thereby circumventing the spectral bias identified in NTK theory [6,36] that prior NeRV methods had not addressed. The temporal extension further reframes inter-frame correlation as an additional 1D frequency decomposition along the temporal axis, a framing not present in prior NeRV work that typically handled motion via flow fields or residual dynamics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By explicitly addressing spectral bias in NeRV through frequency-domain decomposition, SNeRV provides a principled architectural solution to a known but under-addressed limitation, yielding consistent 1–2 dB gains across regression, interpolation, compression, and inpainting tasks. The observed trade-off between the backbone (superior regression) and temporal extension (superior interpolation) highlights a fundamental tension between spatial spectral fidelity and temporal generalization under fixed model budgets, offering a useful lens for task-specific NeRV design. Open questions include whether more compact HF synthesis architectures can reduce the computational overhead of the temporal extension (181.8G vs. 90.5G MACs), and whether the DWT-based decomposition generalizes to higher-frequency content or longer sequences without further parameter growth.
