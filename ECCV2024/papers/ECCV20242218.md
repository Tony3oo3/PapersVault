## Problem

Fast-NeRF models achieve rapid training and inference by relying on explicit data structures (voxel grids, hash grids) with small or no neural components, but their reconstruction quality plateaus quickly. Naive quality-improvement strategies—increasing grid resolution, adding more sampled points per ray, or enlarging the MLP—either hit overfitting plateaus, deviate from the fast-model paradigm, or incur disproportionate computational costs. The paper asks whether rendering quality can be substantially improved without escalating these costs.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes a set of M independently pre-trained Fast-NeRF models at increasing resolutions (each roughly doubling parameters from the previous) as black-box experts, plus a set of 2D images with known camera poses for joint fine-tuning. At inference, rays are cast through pixels, N points are sampled along each ray, and a density-based filtering step discards empty-space points before routing. The output is a single rendered image where each pixel color is computed via the standard volume rendering equation over gate-weighted expert predictions of radiance and density.

## Method

The core mechanism is a Sparse Mixture of Experts over M resolution-diverse Fast-NeRF models, treated entirely as black boxes. A lightweight gate—comprising an explicit feature grid (matching the expert's data structure type) and a 2-layer MLP—maps each filtered 3D point to a probability distribution over experts, and Top-K routing dispatches the point to the K highest-probability experts whose outputs are weighted-summed. A novel resolution-weighted auxiliary loss (L_rw-aux) applies geometrically increasing penalties to higher-resolution experts, inducing sparsity in high-resolution models and encouraging frequency-based scene decomposition (low-res experts handle low-frequency content, high-res experts handle fine details). The gate is inserted at the earliest stage of the pipeline and its output is multiplied with expert predictions as late as possible, which is what makes the framework model-agnostic. After independent pre-training of each expert, the gate and all experts are jointly optimized for 20k iterations with a total loss combining the photometric NeRF loss and the resolution-weighted auxiliary term.

## Results

Across all four datasets and three Fast-NeRF architectures (DVGO, TensoRF, Instant-NGP), the Top-2 MoE configuration consistently improves PSNR over the single-model baseline by 0.2–2.4 dB. The largest gains appear on NSVF (e.g., DVGO: 35.21 → 37.59 dB; TensoRF: 36.70 → 37.98 dB) and Synthetic-NeRF (e.g., TensoRF: 32.98 → 34.09 dB), while more challenging real-world datasets (TanksAndTemple, LLFF) see ~0.3–0.5 dB improvements. Top-2 matches the full-ensemble (Ens) quality (which uses all M experts) while requiring roughly half the GFLOPs per rendered image and significantly fewer active parameters (e.g., DVGO on Blender: 39M vs. 97M non-zero params). Training time remains competitive: Top-1 can even be faster than the baseline (DVGO on Blender: 21 min vs. 26 min), and the worst-case Top-2 training is ~1 hour.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against three Fast-NeRF baselines (DVGO, TensoRF, Instant-NGP) at comparable resolutions, as well as a full-ensemble (Ens) that jointly fine-tunes all M models and averages predictions. Against each single-model baseline, the proposed Top-2 MoE wins on PSNR across every dataset and architecture: gains range from +0.2 dB (Instant-NGP on LLFF: 24.97→25.17) to +2.4 dB (DVGO on NSVF: 35.21→37.59). The TensoRF baseline shows the largest absolute improvements (up to +1.4 dB on Blender), while Instant-NGP shows the smallest (≤0.7 dB), likely because its native multi-resolution hash grid already captures some frequency decomposition. Compared to the full ensemble, Top-2 matches or nearly matches PSNR (within 0.05–0.1 dB) while using roughly half the GFLOPs and 40–60% fewer non-zero parameters (e.g., DVGO on Blender: 39M vs. 97M params; 940 vs. 2206 GFLOPs). Notably, Top-1 can be faster than the baseline in some settings (DVGO on Blender: 21 min vs. 26 min training) due to the resolution-weighted routing that favors low-resolution experts, though it sacrifices ~0.3 dB relative to Top-2.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research threads: Fast-NeRFs (DVGO, TensoRF, Instant-NGP), which replace the heavy implicit MLP with explicit data structures for speed, and Sparse Mixture of Experts (Shazeer et al., 2017), which routes tokens to a subset of specialized sub-networks. The closest prior work, Switch-NeRF, applies MoE to NeRFs but uses a heavy 4-layer MLP gate and a full-implicit architecture, making it neither model-agnostic nor fast. This paper departs by treating each expert as a black box (enabling model-agnosticism), replacing the heavy gate with a lightweight grid-plus-shallow-MLP formulation inspired by Fast-NeRFs, and introducing a resolution-weighted auxiliary loss that explicitly biases routing toward lower-resolution experts to decompose scenes by frequency.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The model-agnostic framing makes this a practical plug-and-play upgrade for any Fast-NeRF pipeline, and the resolution-based routing offers a principled way to decompose scenes into frequency bands without manual segmentation. However, the method still requires an independent pre-training phase for each expert, is sensitive to the auxiliary loss weight λ, and remains slower than its baselines in both training and inference, limiting its appeal for real-time applications. Open questions include whether end-to-end joint training (without pre-training) can close the quality gap, whether the approach extends to dynamic or 4D scenes, and whether the frequency-based decomposition generalizes beyond the tested architectures.
