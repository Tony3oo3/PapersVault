## Problem

Active depth sensors (ToF, LiDAR) are constrained by low frame rates, high energy consumption, and spatial sparsity, while coupled RGB cameras can operate at much higher frame rates. Existing depth completion methods process a single frame in isolation and cannot exploit multi-view geometry to recover dense depth at the RGB camera's frame rate. This paper introduces the task of temporal depth stream densification: producing dense depth maps at the high RGB frame rate using only sparse depth measurements from a lower-rate active sensor.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241631/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a target RGB frame at time t, a single source RGB frame from a previous time instant, sparse depth points projected from the active sensor onto the target view (with sub-pixel coordinates), and the relative camera pose. The temporal sparsification ratio τ = f_D/f_RGB controls how frequently sparse depth frames are available (e.g., τ=0.2 means one depth frame per five RGB frames). The output is a dense depth map at full resolution aligned with the target RGB frame, computed internally at 1/8 resolution and upsampled via learned convex upsampling.

## Method

DoD comprises three stages: (1) multi-modal encoding extracts geometry features via a shared ResNet18 encoder producing epipolar correlation features (41 depth hypotheses × 3×3 patches over 2 m), monocular multi-scale features from ResNet34 at 1/2, 1/4, 1/8 resolution, and projected sparse depth with sub-pixel coordinates; (2) iterative multi-modal integration fuses these cues over N=10 GRU iterations, where a Visual Cues Integration branch embeds monocular and geometric features in the hidden state, and a Depth Cues Integration branch computes a sparse depth update in the residual space (ΔD_d = D̃ − D) that is fused with a visual depth update, enabling outlier rejection and graceful handling of missing values; (3) depth decoding performs learned 2× convex upsampling three times using monocular context and a softmax-weighted 3×3 neighborhood. Training uses an exponentially decayed L1 loss (ν=0.8) over the N intermediate depth maps, with random source-frame selection from a buffer of N previous frames to augment pose diversity.

## Results

On ScanNetV2, DoD achieves MAE 0.041, RMSE 0.103, and σ<1.05 of 0.899 in 2D depth metrics, outperforming the best depth completion baseline NLSPN (MAE 0.067, σ<1.05 0.847) and the best MVS+Depth baseline Guided CAS-MVSNet with 8 views (MAE 0.124, σ<1.05 0.635). In 3D reconstruction, DoD attains an F-Score of 0.871 and Chamfer distance of 0.032, surpassing NLSPN (F-Score 0.822, Chamfer 0.041) and all MVS-based methods (best F-Score 0.671). On KITTI with LiDAR at 1 Hz, DoD achieves MAE 0.347 and σ<1.05 of 0.944, marginally outperforming CompletionFormer (MAE 0.348, σ<1.05 0.939) and substantially beating the best MVS+Depth method (MAE 0.575). The framework also generalizes well to 7Scenes (MAE 0.043) and TartanAir (MAE 0.648) without fine-tuning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DoD consistently outperforms all 8 baselines across every dataset and metric. Against depth completion methods (SpAgNet, NLSPN, CompletionFormer), which use only 1 view and projected sparse depth, DoD improves MAE by 39–42% on ScanNetV2 (0.041 vs. 0.067–0.075) and by 23–33% on KITTI (0.347 vs. 0.426–0.532 at 1 Hz), while also achieving higher σ<1.05 (0.899 vs. 0.824–0.847). Against MVS+Depth methods (Guided CAS-MVSNet, Guided UCS-Net, Guided PatchMatch-Net with [39]), DoD wins by a large margin even when those methods use 8 views: on ScanNetV2, DoD's MAE of 0.041 is 3× lower than Guided CAS-MVSNet's 0.124 (8 views) and 6× lower than the 2-view variant (0.286). Pure MVS methods (SimpleRecon, CAS-MVSNet, UCS-Net, PatchMatch-Net) without depth guidance perform worst, with MAE 0.093–0.184 on ScanNetV2 and F-Scores below 0.671 in 3D. On KITTI at 0.5 Hz LiDAR, DoD's advantage over depth completion narrows (MAE 0.492 vs. 0.555 for CompletionFormer) but it still leads all methods, while MVS+Depth baselines degrade significantly (MAE 0.828–0.837 for 8-view methods). The ablation confirms that both multi-view and depth cues contribute: removing MVS cues raises MAE from 0.041 to 0.049, while removing depth cues raises it to 0.187.

## Relation to Prior Work

DoD carves new ground by framing 'temporal depth stream densification' as a distinct task, sitting at the intersection of depth completion (which assumes temporally aligned sparse depth) and multi-view stereo (which typically requires many views and ignores sparse depth). The architecture repurposes well-known components—ResNet encoders, GRU-based iterative refinement, epipolar correlation sampling, and convex upsampling—but their specific combination for the two-view, temporally-misaligned setting is novel. The closest prior work is the authors' own Guided MVS [39], which injects sparse depth into MVS cost volumes but requires many views and struggles with non-object-centric scenes; DoD deliberately departs from this by operating with only two views and an iterative update mechanism that filters depth outliers via multi-view cues.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses deployment constraints (energy consumption, frame-rate ceilings) that the authors argue have been 'often ignored in the literature' but are critical for ToF-based AR, LiDAR-based autonomous driving, and video-based 3D reconstruction. A key open question is handling moving objects, which inherently corrupts projected sparse depth and degrades multi-view matching; the authors acknowledge this as a limitation without a full solution. The KITTI results also reveal a scenario where DoD is structurally disadvantaged (long-range, low multi-view overlap), suggesting the approach's benefits are most pronounced in closer-range or higher-overlap settings. Broader impact includes enabling lower-power active sensing in battery-constrained devices and unlocking higher effective depth frame rates for safety-critical perception.
