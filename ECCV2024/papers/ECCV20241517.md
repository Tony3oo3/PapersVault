## Problem

Human video-language datasets used to train generalizable reward functions rarely contain failure videos, making it difficult for models to distinguish successful from unsuccessful robot executions. Additionally, a significant domain shift exists between diverse human videos and the limited, controlled robot videos available for training, and fine-tuning on small robot datasets risks overfitting and catastrophic forgetting of the VLM's generalization ability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241517/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a short video clip (L frames of H×W×C resolution) and a natural language task instruction as input, producing a scalar reward score via the cosine similarity between the encoded video and text features. For failure-conditioned scoring, the text input is augmented with a learnable failure prompt (a sequence of L_p^f tokens) concatenated before the task description embedding, and the model outputs K distinct failure-context scores per task. The reward is used downstream by VMPC or DDPG+CEM planners to select actions.

## Method

Adapt2Reward builds on the Singularity VLM and introduces three key mechanisms. First, cross-domain contrastive learning (CDC) aligns robot and human video features sharing the same task label while separating different tasks, bridging the embodiment gap. Second, a pool of K learnable failure prompts per task is created: failure videos are clustered via spherical K-means at each epoch, and each cluster is assigned a unique prompt that is concatenated with the task text embedding to form a failure context. Third, a modified video-language contrastive loss (fVLC) pulls each failure video toward its cluster's failure prompt while pushing it away from the success text and other failure prompts, and the standard VLC loss is extended to include failure text features as additional negatives for success videos. A BCE loss on robot success/failure samples provides the binary supervision signal, and all losses are combined with equal weighting during training.

## Results

On the MetaWorld task generalization benchmark (Table 1), Adapt2Reward achieves an average success rate of 52.73% across 4 target tasks, substantially outperforming all baselines: Concept2Robot (33.75%), DVD (29.81%), and LIV (35.44%). In environment generalization (Fig. 3b), Adapt2Reward exceeds the best-performing baseline by over 28% on average across four progressively challenging visual variants. In the Concept2Robot Environments (68 tasks, Fig. 5), Adapt2Reward-based RL policies match or exceed manually crafted reward strategies on the majority of tasks, and outperform Concept2Robot across most tasks. Under viewpoint variation and distractor objects (Table 2), Adapt2Reward degrades by less than 12% versus 5–37% for Concept2Robot, and outperforms it by 20–46% under distractor conditions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Adapt2Reward consistently outperforms all three baselines across both environment and task generalization settings. Against Concept2Robot (33.75% avg), Adapt2Reward gains approximately 19 percentage points (52.73%), with the gap widening to over 28% in environment generalization where Concept2Robot relies on a fixed 174-way classifier. Against DVD (29.81% avg), the improvement is roughly 23 points, largely attributable to Adapt2Reward's explicit modeling of failure modes via learnable failure prompts, which DVD entirely omits. Against LIV (35.44% avg), Adapt2Reward leads by about 17 points, benefiting from cross-domain contrastive learning that aligns human and robot video representations. In the Concept2Robot Environments (68 tasks), Adapt2Reward's dense reward scores enable RL policies to match or exceed manually crafted binary rewards on most tasks, while Concept2Robot's generic object templates ('[Something]') limit its ability to recognize task-specific object interactions. Under visual perturbations (viewpoint changes, distractor objects), Adapt2Reward degrades far less than Concept2Robot, confirming superior visual robustness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the DVD (Chen et al., 2021) framework for learning generalizable robotic reward functions from human videos, but explicitly addresses DVD's critical oversight: the absence of failure data in the training pipeline. It builds on the Singularity VLM as its backbone and departs from prior reward-learning approaches (Concept2Robot's classifier-based reward, LIV's language-image embeddings) by introducing learnable failure prompts—K-means-clustered failure modes encoded as text-encoder tokens—combined with cross-domain contrastive learning to bridge the human-robot embodiment gap. The method also contrasts with inverse reinforcement learning and LORel-style approaches by leveraging large-scale human video-language data (SS-V2) rather than requiring extensive robot demonstrations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete and underappreciated gap: human video-language datasets contain almost no failure videos, yet reward functions must distinguish success from failure, creating a fundamental asymmetry that prior methods (DVD, Concept2Robot) sidestepped. The failure-prompt mechanism offers a principled way to extract structure from limited robot failure data without overfitting, and the ~30% improvement over prior methods in both environment and task generalization is practically meaningful for reducing the data burden in robotic reward learning. However, all experiments are conducted in simulation (MetaWorld, C2R-Envs) with simple manipulation tasks, leaving open whether the approach transfers to real-world robots, longer-horizon tasks, or more diverse failure taxonomies. The reliance on a fixed number of clusters (K=3) per task also raises scalability questions for tasks with many distinct failure modes.
