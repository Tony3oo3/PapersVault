## Problem

Large vision-language models (LVLMs) frequently generate hallucinated, harmful, or deceptive content when faced with unanswerable questions, jailbreaking attacks, or misleading prompts, yet it remains unclear whether the model internally 'knows' it is about to produce such content. Prior work demonstrated that hidden states of the first generated token in text-only LLMs encode answerability signals, but this finding was limited to NLP settings and required access to internal model representations. This paper asks whether the more accessible and model-agnostic logit distributions of the first token in multimodal LVLMs carry comparable hidden knowledge, and whether this knowledge can be exploited for practical safety improvements.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an RGB image paired with a text prompt, processes them through a frozen LVLM (e.g., LLaVA-7B with a 32,000-token vocabulary), and extracts only the logit vector of the very first generated token (a 32,000-dim real-valued vector). This vector is fed into a lightweight linear classifier—logistic regression for binary tasks (answerable/unanswerable, safe/harmful, normal/deceptive) or Linear Discriminant Analysis for multi-class tasks (ImageNet 1000-way classification). The output is either a classification label used to gate or modify generation, or a template string (e.g., 'Sorry, this question is unanswerable because') that replaces the first token and is fed back into the autoregressive model to elicit a reasoned refusal. The approach is prompt-agnostic, working consistently across original open-ended, hint-augmented, and meta-question prompt formats.

## Method

The core technique is linear probing on the logit distribution of the first autoregressively generated token: a frozen LVLM produces the first-token logits for each training image-question pair, and a simple linear classifier (logistic regression or LDA) is trained on these logits to predict task-specific labels. The key empirical finding is that this first-token logit vector is the most discriminative representation—performance degrades monotonically for subsequent tokens as the model becomes increasingly committed to its output, while hidden states from intermediate layers (~10+ layers) achieve comparable but not superior performance. A practical decoding strategy leverages the classifier at inference: if the first-token logits predict an inappropriate instruction, the first token is replaced with a task-specific template ending in 'because', and the LVLM's autoregressive architecture naturally completes a reasoned explanation. The approach is model-agnostic (validated across seven LVLMs including LLaVA, InstructBLIP, mPLUG-Owl, LLaMA-Adapter, MMGPT, and MiniGPT-4), requires no modification to the LVLM weights, and outperforms both fine-tuning and retraining with LoRA on most tasks while using orders of magnitude fewer learnable parameters.

## Results

Linear probing on the first-token logit distribution consistently outperforms the original LVLM across all seven evaluated models and three prompt types. On LLaVA-7B with the original open-ended prompt, linear probing raises VizWiz unanswerability detection from 71.5% to 84.4% ACC (AUC 62.0→91.0), reduces jailbreaking attack success rate from 70.3% to 6.4% (AUC 64.0→97.8), and improves deceptive-question identification from 55.2% to 93.4% ACC (AUC 55.2→98.3). The gains are robust: across all 21 model–prompt combinations, linear probing achieves ≥80% ACC on every task, while the unmodified LVLMs frequently fall below 60%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the unmodified LVLMs (the primary baseline), linear probing wins decisively on every task and model: e.g., LLaVA-7B's jailbreaking ASR drops from 70.3% to 6.4%, and MAD-Bench ACC jumps from 55.2% to 93.4%. Against CLIP embeddings (image, text, or both), linear probing on LLaVA's first logits achieves slightly higher AUC on all three tasks (e.g., 90.05 vs. 86.19 on VizWiz), but the close performance of CLIP reveals significant dataset bias—jailbreaking images and prompts are visually and lexically distinct from normal inputs. Against finetuning and retraining LLaVA with LoRA on the same training data, linear probing matches or exceeds the finetuned model on five of six tasks (unanswerability, jailbreaking, deception, hallucination, image classification) while using orders of magnitude fewer learnable parameters and substantially less training time; the finetuned model only edges out LP on Math-Vista correctness prediction. The paper also shows that after finetuning, the linear probing performance on each task is weakened, suggesting the hidden knowledge is partially destroyed by parameter updates.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the linear probing paradigm for trustworthiness detection from Slobodkin et al. [25] and Qian et al. [21], which used hidden states of the first generated token in NLP-only settings, to the multimodal vision-language domain. The key methodological departure is using logit distributions over the vocabulary rather than internal hidden states, requiring less architectural knowledge and empirically outperforming hidden-state probing. The paper also positions itself against the broader LVLM safety literature (RLHF alignment, prompt tuning, dedicated safety classifiers) by offering a lightweight, model-agnostic alternative that operates at inference time without retraining.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical decoding strategy—inserting a trained linear classifier on first-token logits into the autoregressive loop—offers a cheap, plug-and-play safety mechanism that outperforms LoRA finetuning on the same data, which is a useful finding for practitioners seeking to harden LVLMs without expensive retraining. However, the discovery that CLIP alone (the vision encoder) already achieves strong performance on all three safety tasks reveals significant distribution bias in current benchmarks, undermining confidence in reported LVLM safety metrics and calling for more carefully controlled evaluation datasets. Open questions include whether non-linear or unsupervised probing could extract richer hidden knowledge, and whether the first-token signal persists in larger or architecturally different models (e.g., Mamba-based LVLMs).
