## Problem

Existing audio-visual segmentation (AVS) methods exhibit a detrimental segmentation preference: they rely on shortcut learning of which objects are 'likely to make sound' during training rather than establishing reliable audio-visual correlation. This is especially problematic in multi-source scenes where audio is inherently ambiguous, causing models to segment objects even when the audio is completely silent. The paper asks whether incorporating textual semantics can mitigate this preference by providing the robust, abstract semantic information that audio alone lacks.

## Input / Output

The method consumes video frames sampled at 1-second intervals, corresponding audio clips split at the same rate, and text cues generated at inference time by an off-the-shelf image captioner (LLaVA-1.5) followed by a frozen LLM (LLaMA2) that reasons via Chain-of-Thought about which objects in the scene are likely sounding. Audio features are extracted with a frozen VGGish backbone, visual features with a Swin-Base Transformer, and text features with ImageBind. The output is a pixel-level segmentation mask per frame identifying sounding objects, decoded by a Mask2Former-style transformer decoder with bottleneck adapters.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-stage text cue pipeline combined with a Semantics-Driven Audio Modeling (SeDAM) module. First, LLaVA-1.5 produces dense scene descriptions, and a frozen LLaMA2 with a task-specific few-shot CoT prompt template reasons about potential sounding objects, yielding text cues that capture object interactions (e.g., a person not playing a guitar). Second, SeDAM projects audio features into distinct latent components (enforced by an InfoNCE loss) and uses the text cues as queries in crossmodal multi-head attention to form 'sounding object features' that carry both audio information and semantic meaning. A dynamic mask computed from attention scores filters noisy text cues. These enriched features are then injected into pre-trained Mask2Former mask queries via the Prompting Mask Queries with Semantics (PMQS) module, and lightweight bottleneck adapters fine-tune the visual encoder and decoder. The key design choice is that text enhances rather than replaces audio guidance, and the LLM is entirely frozen and excluded from training to ensure fairness.

## Results

On the AVS-Benchmarks, TeSO achieves 83.27% mIoU and 0.933 F-score on V1S, 66.02% mIoU and 0.801 F-score on V1M, and 68.53% mIoU and 0.813 F-score on AVSS-binary, setting new state-of-the-art on all three subsets. On the AVSS semantic segmentation task, TeSO reaches 38.96% mIoU, surpassing the previous best (BAVS, 33.59%) by 5.37 points. In audio-control experiments, TeSO exhibits the largest performance drop under muted audio (−42.6% mIoU) and 40 dB white Gaussian noise (−47.4% mIoU), confirming that its segmentation is genuinely driven by audio rather than learned object priors.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TeSO outperforms all 12 baselines on every subset where they are directly comparable. On V1S, it leads BAVS (82.68%) by 0.59 mIoU and AVSegFormer (82.06%) by 1.21 mIoU, while also achieving the highest F-score (0.933 vs. 0.904 for AVS-BG). On the more challenging multi-source V1M, TeSO's 66.02% mIoU surpasses the previous best MUTR (64.00%) by 2.02 points and GAVS (63.70%) by 2.32 points, with a particularly large F-score margin over GAVS (0.801 vs. 0.774). On AVSS-binary, TeSO (68.53%) beats GAVS (67.70%) by 0.83 mIoU and BAVS (55.45%) by a substantial 13.08 points, indicating that text-enhanced audio guidance is especially effective in complex multi-source scenes. On the AVSS semantic task (Table 2), TeSO (38.96% mIoU) exceeds BAVS (33.59%) by 5.37 points and CATR (32.80%) by 6.16 points. In the audio-control ablation (Table 4), TeSO shows the greatest sensitivity to audio removal (−42.6% mIoU under mute, −47.4% under 40 dB noise), whereas baselines like AVS-Bench drop only 6.6–11.1%, confirming that prior methods rely more on segmentation preferences than true audio-visual grounding.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the prompt-based AVS paradigm established by GAVS, which uses audio to prompt a visual foundation model (Mask2Former), and departs from both fusion-based methods (AVSegFormer, AuTR, CATR) and the text-replacement approach of BAVS. Rather than directly fusing audio with visual features or substituting audio with text tags, TeSO introduces text semantics as an intermediary that *enhances* audio guidance: an off-the-shelf captioner (LLaVA-1.5) generates scene descriptions, a frozen LLM (LLaMA2) reasons about potential sounding objects via chain-of-thought prompts, and the resulting text cues are cross-attended with latent audio features in the SeDAM module before prompting mask queries. The key conceptual departure is framing the problem as one of weak audio-visual correlation caused by audio's inherent ambiguity, and proposing textual semantics as a bridge to restore reliable audio control over the visual space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's most valuable contribution is the diagnostic observation that existing AVS models exhibit 'segmentation preference'—producing plausible masks even under completely silent audio—suggesting they rely on visual shortcuts learned during training rather than genuine audio-visual grounding. The audio-control experiment (Table 4) provides a useful evaluation protocol for measuring true audio sensitivity, which the field lacks. Limitations include reliance on off-the-shelf captioners and LLMs that add inference latency and are not jointly trained, the modest performance gains on single-source (V1S) scenarios, and the fact that the segmentation-preference diagnosis is supported by only a few baselines and one perturbation type. Open questions include whether the text-enhancement strategy generalizes to unseen sound categories, whether end-to-end training of the text-cue pipeline could further improve results, and whether the approach scales to real-time applications given the LLM inference cost.
