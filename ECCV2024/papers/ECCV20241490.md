## Problem

Existing early-exit (EE) methods attach small prediction branches to intermediate backbone layers, but these exits underperform because early-stage features lack the global context and semantic depth needed for reliable predictions. Moreover, EE designs are tightly coupled to specific architectures and require joint training, limiting their generalizability across the rapidly evolving landscape of modern vision models. The paper asks whether completely independent, pre-trained tiny models can replace a substantial portion of a large model's workload with negligible performance loss, without any additional training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241490/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The system takes a standard RGB image and produces either a class label (ImageNet-1k classification) or bounding boxes with class labels (COCO object detection). The key operational detail is that the inference path is dynamic and input-dependent: simple samples are resolved entirely by the tiny saver model, while complex samples are forwarded to the large base model after the saver's confidence falls below a user-configurable threshold. This threshold can be adjusted at runtime to trade off latency against accuracy, and for detection the confidence is derived from the average score of detected boxes above a 0.05 filter.

## Method

TinySaver cascades a pre-trained efficient (tiny) model before a pre-trained large model, using the tiny model's output confidence as both the early-exit decision and the routing signal—eliminating the need for a separate trained router. No additional training is required; both models retain their original weights, and the only tunable parameter is the confidence threshold. The paper introduces a ΔC_tr metric computed on the training set to systematically select the optimal saver for a given base model by maximizing the fraction of samples the saver can handle without degrading system accuracy. An extension called the Exit Sequence Network (ESN) feeds the saver's high-level features into backbone-attached exits, combining TinySaver's performance floor with traditional EE flexibility. The authors further show that in classification tasks, the tiny model's inherent confidence is a competitive router compared to trained MoE routers, since the router's learning objective aligns with the classifier's, making a separate encoder-based router harder to make cheaper.

## Results

On ImageNet-1k classification, TinySaver achieves up to 91.9% FLOPs reduction (MaxViT_large: 42.80 → 3.46 GFLOPs) with ≤1% accuracy drop, and up to 88.5% reduction (ConvNeXtV2_huge: 114.92 → 13.21 GFLOPs) at the same threshold. At zero accuracy loss, savings range from 41.4% (ConvNeXtV2_huge) to 78.9% (MaxViT_large). On COCO object detection, TinySaver (Co-DINO-Swin-L + YOLOv8n) reaches 53.1 Box AP at 645 GFLOPs, outperforming Cascade Mask R-CNN (50.4 AP at 745 GFLOPs) and approaching HTC++ (56.4 AP at 1043 GFLOPs) with substantially lower compute.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against EE-based methods (CF-ViT, Dynamic Perceiver, MSNet, Hong et al., Wang et al.), TinySaver significantly outperforms on the accuracy-vs-FLOPs Pareto frontier (Fig. 5a), attributed to its model-agnostic design that leverages independently trained efficient models rather than co-designed exit branches. Compared to MoE-based approaches (Mobile V-MoEs, Tutel, V-MoE), TinySaver achieves greater complexity reduction because the tiny model simultaneously serves as both expert and router, eliminating the overhead of a separate trained router (Eq. 5 analysis). Versus static models (ResNet, ViT, DaViT, EfficientNet, EfficientViT, EfficientFormer, MaxViT, ConvNeXt V2, MobileNetV3), TinySaver-enabled models surpass smaller versions of the same architecture and typically exceed all static models in a similar FLOPs range (Fig. 5b). On COCO detection, TinySaver (Co-DINO-Swin-L + YOLOv8n) beats Cascade Mask R-CNN (50.4 AP at 745 GFLOPs) with 53.1 AP at 645 GFLOPs, and approaches HTC++ (56.4 AP at 1043 GFLOPs) with 56.5 AP at 671 GFLOPs, though it does not uniformly dominate all static detectors as it does in classification, consistent with the paper's analysis that bounding-box quality is less correlated with confidence scores.

## Relation to Prior Work

TinySaver departs from the dominant early-exit paradigm, which attaches auxiliary prediction heads to intermediate backbone layers, by instead cascading a fully independent, pre-trained tiny model before the base model and using its output confidence as the routing signal. Conceptually, it also reframes the system as a top-1 Mixture of Experts where the smaller expert doubles as the router, eliminating the need for a separately trained gating network. The approach builds on the rich ecosystem of efficiency-focused architectures (EfficientFormer, MobileNetV3, EfficientViT, etc.) as plug-and-play savers, and leverages the authors' prior DyCE work for threshold tuning, but requires no joint training or architectural modification of either model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: up to 90% FLOPs reduction with negligible accuracy loss across diverse modern architectures, achieved with zero additional training—just cascade two off-the-shelf models and tune a threshold. This makes dynamic compression accessible to practitioners who cannot afford retraining or architectural redesign. Key limitations remain: the confidence-based routing works best for classification, and the COCO detection experiments show degraded performance because bounding-box quality is poorly correlated with detection scores, pointing to an open question about external evaluators for non-classification tasks. The finding that the ESN extension (adding traditional EE heads on top of the saver) yields no meaningful improvement suggests the simple cascade is already near-optimal for classification, but also limits the method's flexibility for multi-task or domain-specific exits.
