## Problem

Nearly all existing GNN explainers identify important subgraphs based on association (predictability, gradients, perturbation), making them vulnerable to spurious correlations in training data. The paper argues that a truly explainable GNN should uncover the intrinsic causal relation between the explanatory subgraph and the label, but no prior method performs genuine causal inference (do-calculus) to identify the causal explanatory subgraph.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241638/cropped_page11_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a graph G with its predicted (or ground-truth) label and an expressivity threshold δ. It iterates over every node v in the graph as a candidate reference node, builds a k-hop causal structure centered on v, and trains a separate GNN-NCM for each. The output is the explanatory subgraph Γ induced by the causal structure centered on the node v* that achieves the highest node expressivity, effectively selecting the subgraph that most causally determines the graph label.

## Method

CXGNN operates in three steps. First, it defines a causal structure 𝒢 centered on a reference node v that partitions variables into observable node labels and latent node/edge effects, proving (Theorem 1) that this structure admits a structural causal model (GNN-SCM) supporting do-calculus interventions. Second, because do-calculus is computationally intractable on real graphs, it constructs a GNN-NCM—a trainable neural causal model where each node's causal mechanism is a feedforward network parameterized by θ, trained by minimizing a cross-entropy loss over interventional distributions p(y_v | do(v_i)) approximated via Monte Carlo sampling of latent effects. Third, it defines node expressivity as the expected label probability under the trained GNN-NCM, iterates all nodes as reference centers, and returns the subgraph of the GNN-NCM achieving maximum expressivity as the causal explanatory subgraph.

## Results

On six synthetic graph datasets, CXGNN achieves 100% groundtruth match accuracy on BA+House and Tree+House, 67.6% on BA+Cycle, 61.2% on Tree+Cycle, 46% on Tree+Grid, and 44% on BA+Grid, dramatically outperforming all baselines (best baseline OrphicX reaches at most 43%). On real-world molecular datasets, CXGNN achieves 66.67% and 75.00% groundtruth match accuracy on Benzene and Fluoride carbonyl respectively, while all six baselines score 0%. In terms of explanation accuracy, CXGNN reaches 100% on BA+House, BA+Grid, Tree+House, and Tree+Grid, and 73.46% on Benzene, comparable to or slightly below the best causality-inspired baselines (RCExplainer achieves 100% on most synthetic sets).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CXGNN decisively outperforms all six baselines on the headline groundtruth match accuracy metric: on synthetic datasets it achieves 100% on BA+House and Tree+House versus at most 100% (RCExplainer, BA+House only) and 43% (OrphicX, BA+Grid) for the best baselines, and on real-world datasets it reaches 66.67% (Benzene) and 75% (Fluoride carbonyl) while every baseline scores 0%. Among association-based explainers, GNNExplainer [46] is the strongest, reaching 75–77% explanation accuracy but only 0.2–2.2% GT match; PGMExplainer [33] and Guidedbp [12] perform substantially worse, with Guidedbp scoring 0% accuracy on three of six synthetic sets. Among causality-inspired explainers, RCExplainer [38] achieves 100% explanation accuracy on most synthetic sets but collapses to 0% GT match on five of six, indicating it over-identifies subgraphs; OrphicX [20] is the closest competitor on GT match (39–43% on BA+House/BA+Grid) yet still trails CXGNN by 57–61 percentage points on those datasets. GEM [19] achieves high accuracy (88–98%) but only 1–6% GT match, confirming that high coverage does not translate to exact identification. Overall, CXGNN trades a small amount of explanation accuracy (e.g., 83.33% vs. 100% on BA+Cycle) for a large gain in exact groundtruth identification, validating that causal inference captures the true explanatory subgraph more precisely than correlation- or causality-inspired heuristics.

## Relation to Prior Work

CXGNN builds directly on the Neural Causal Model (NCM) framework of Xia et al. [43], which established that structural causal models can be parameterized as trainable neural networks, and adapts this to the graph domain by proving the existence of a G-constrained GNN-NCM for every GNN-SCM. It departs from the dominant association-based GNN explainer lineage (GNNExplainer, PGMExplainer, GuidedBP) and from the causality-inspired but non-causal methods (GEM, RCExplainer, OrphicX) by performing actual do-calculus interventions rather than relying on correlation, attention, or information-theoretic proxies. The shared observation that graphs decompose into causal and non-causal subgraphs is inherited from prior work, but CXGNN is the first to operationalize this via explicit causal effect computation on a trained NCM.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first GNN explainer grounded in formal causal inference (do-calculus), offering a principled alternative to the association-based and causality-inspired methods that dominate the field. The striking groundtruth-match gap on synthetic datasets (100% vs. 0–39% for baselines) provides strong evidence that spurious correlations fundamentally limit existing explainers. However, performance drops substantially on real-world molecular graphs (66–75% match), partly because groundtruth annotations there are arguably noisy, and the method's reliance on k-hop neighborhoods and per-node NCM training raises scalability questions for large graphs. The authors flag robustness under adversarial perturbation as a key open direction, and the practical applicability to larger, noisier real-world graphs remains to be demonstrated.
