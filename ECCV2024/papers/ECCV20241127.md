## Problem

Existing action quality assessment methods rely on coarse-grained video or clip-level representations and use expensive frame-wise annotations only for stage boundary prediction, without truly understanding the semantic meaning of different action sub-stages. The paper asks whether cheaper textual prior knowledge—professional terminology describing action types—can substitute for or complement costly frame-wise annotations to enable finer-grained action understanding and more accurate quality scoring.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241127/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an action video (sampled into 16-frame clips, e.g., 96 frames → 9 clips for FineDiving) and multi-level textual annotations derived from professional terminology (e.g., dive number '407B' decomposed into 'inward take-off, 3.5 somersaults pike flight, entry into the water'). It produces a regressed quality score and, in the frame-wise annotated setting, per-frame stage boundary confidence scores. The framework is designed as a plug-in module: when feature dimensions of an existing backbone do not match CLIP's 512-d space, a simple linear projection layer bridges the gap, allowing integration into USDL, CoRe, TSA, TPT, and HGCN without architectural redesign.

## Method

The core contribution is a multi-grained vision-language alignment framework (MVLA) with two components. First, Vision-Language Knowledge Learning (VLKL) encodes video-level, stage-level, and (for frame-wise annotated data) frame-level textual semantics via CLIP's text encoder, then aligns them with corresponding visual embeddings through a multi-grained cross-entropy similarity loss with temperature scaling, pulling visual features toward their semantically matching text at each granularity. Second, a Semantic-Aware Collaborative Attention (SCA) module performs two-stage cross-attention: CLIP visual features (as query) attend to text embeddings (as key/value) to produce action-knowledge-enriched representations, which then serve as key/value for I3D features (as query), thereby transferring textual semantics into the CBP branch without direct cross-modal confusion. Two operational schemes are provided—FAM (learned frame-level segmentation with BCE loss) and FUM (fixed stage boundaries with KL-divergence clip-level alignment)—making the approach applicable regardless of whether frame-wise annotations exist. The CLIP image encoder is frozen to save computation, with lightweight temporal transformer layers added to compensate for the lack of temporal prior in image-level pre-training.

## Results

On FineDiving (w/ DN), TSA-MVLA achieves AIoU@0.5 of 98.66, AIoU@0.75 of 67.16, Spearman ρ of 0.9419, and R-ℓ2 of 0.2840, improving over the prior SOTA TSA by 16.15%, 32.85%, 2.16%, and 0.0580 respectively. On MTL-AQA (w/ DD), the best plug-in result (TPT-MVLA) reaches ρ = 0.9655 and R-ℓ2 = 0.224. On JIGSAWS, HGCN+MVLA achieves average Spearman correlation of 0.90 and average R-ℓ2 of 3.471. On the long-term Fis-V dataset, MLP-Mixer+MVLA attains the best average Spearman correlation of 0.783 and average MSE of 13.06.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's MVLA framework is plugged into six existing AQA methods (USDL, MUSDL, CoRe, TSA, TPT, HGCN) and consistently improves all of them across four benchmarks. On FineDiving, TSA-MVLA outperforms the prior SOTA TSA [46] by 16.15% in AIoU@0.5 and 32.85% in AIoU@0.75 (w/ DN), while also improving assessment (ρ: 0.9419 vs 0.9203; R-ℓ2: 0.2840 vs 0.3420). On MTL-AQA, every plugged-in method improves over its unmodified counterpart; for example, TPT-MVLA beats TPT by 0.98% in ρ and 0.067 in R-ℓ2 (w/o DD), and CoRe-MVLA beats CoRe by 0.58% in ρ and 0.023 in R-ℓ2 (w/ DD). On JIGSAWS, HGCN+MVLA achieves the best average Spearman (0.90) and TPT+MVLA the best average R-ℓ2 (2.964), both surpassing their respective baselines by 3.40% and 1.381 on average. On Fis-V, MLP-Mixer+MVLA achieves the best average Spearman (0.783) and average MSE (13.06), outperforming GDLT+MVLA (0.779 / 13.85) and CoRe+MVLA (0.770 / 14.58). The method never degrades any baseline, confirming its plug-and-play effectiveness across short-term and long-term AQA with or without frame-wise annotations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the AQA line of work initiated by TSA/FineDiving [46], which introduced frame-wise annotations for stage-aware action segmentation, by replacing the expensive frame-wise supervision with cheaper multi-level textual annotations derived from professional terminology. It departs from prior AQA methods (USDL, CoRe, TPT, HGCN) that operate on coarse video- or clip-level representations by introducing a multi-grained vision-language alignment framework that injects CLIP-based textual semantics at frame, stage, and video levels. The semantic-aware collaborative attention (SCA) module is a novel architectural contribution that uses a two-step cross-attention design—first aligning text into CLIP visual features, then using those as intermediaries to guide I3D features—explicitly to avoid the confusing cross-modal, cross-semantic interactions that would arise from direct text-to-CBP attention.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The plug-and-play design, validated across six different base methods and four datasets (short-term and long-term, with and without frame-wise annotations), makes this a practically adoptable module for the AQA community rather than a monolithic new pipeline. The finding that cheap professional-terminology text annotations can substantially outperform expensive frame-wise annotations for action segmentation (AIoU@0.5: 98.66 vs. 82.51) challenges the assumption that fine-grained pixel-level labels are necessary for accurate stage boundary detection. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to domains lacking clear professional terminology (e.g., general human activity), how it scales to longer videos beyond the tested figure skating clips, and whether the frozen-CLIP approximation loses meaningful information compared to full fine-tuning in more diverse action spaces.
