## Problem

Existing transform-based low-rank tensor factorization (t-LRTF) methods rely on discrete transforms (e.g., DFT, DCT, or learned discrete mappings) along the third temporal/spectral dimension, which only capture sparse samples of the spectral or temporal signature. This discreteness limits their effectiveness in temporal/spectral degeneration scenarios such as video frame interpolation/extrapolation and multispectral image spectral super-resolution, where the underlying signal is inherently continuous along that axis.

## Input / Output

The method takes a partially observed third-order tensor as input: for video frame synthesis, a set of observed frames with missing frames (e.g., every other frame); for MSI band interpolation, an MSI with randomly missing spectral bands; and for MSI spectral super-resolution, a 3-band RGB image with a known spectral response matrix. The output is the fully recovered tensor at the original resolution (e.g., 256×256×31 for MSIs, 240×176×3×31 for video). Notably, the model is a single-instance supervised optimization that requires only the observed data and a task-specific weight matrix—no external training dataset is needed.

## Method

FLRTF replaces the discrete transform in the t-SVD-based low-rank tensor factorization with a continuous functional transform f_θ(·): (0,1] → R^{n̂₃}, parameterized by a 5-layer MLP with sinusoidal activation and positional encodings. The k-th frontal slice of the recovered tensor is computed as X(:,:,k) = (A △ B) ×₃ f_θ(z_(k)), where z_(k) = k/n₃ is a continuous sampling coordinate, A ∈ R^{n₁×r×n̂₃} and B ∈ R^{r×n₂×n̂₃} are latent factor tensors, and △ denotes the face-wise product. A key theoretical result (Theorem 2) proves that the ℓ₁-difference between adjacent slices is bounded by γ₁γ₂γ₃ˡδˡ⁻¹/n₃, showing the functional transform inherently encodes local smoothness in the third dimension while preserving global low-rankness. The entire model (factor tensors and MLP weights) is optimized end-to-end via Adam for 10,000 iterations on a squared-error data-fidelity loss, with the task-specific weight matrix W encoding the observation pattern (diagonal mask for interpolation, spectral response matrix for super-resolution).

## Results

On the MSI spectral super-resolution task, FLRTF achieves 40.60 dB PSNR and 0.9760 SSIM on ARAD_1K, surpassing the best deep-learning baseline (HSACS, 33.27 dB) by over 7 dB, and 35.33 dB PSNR on CAVE versus 26.96 dB for the best baseline (SSDCN). For video frame interpolation, FLRTF attains 32.83 dB PSNR on Apply Eye Make-up and 31.94 dB on Typing, outperforming the strongest baseline (TNN-3DTV) by 1.4–3.5 dB. On MSI band interpolation, FLRTF yields an average PSNR gain of approximately 6 dB over the second-best method (HLRTF) across all four CAVE scenes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FLRTF outperforms all nine baselines across all three tasks. In video frame interpolation (Table 2), FLRTF beats TNN-3DTV (the strongest discrete-transform baseline) by 1.4–3.5 dB PSNR and surpasses HLRTF by 2.9–3.7 dB; in extrapolation, it leads HLRTF by 1.3–1.8 dB on three of four clips. For MSI band interpolation (Table 3), FLRTF achieves roughly 6 dB PSNR improvement over HLRTF (e.g., 43.22 vs. 38.24 on Toy) and 11+ dB over DCTNN, while also reducing SAM by 30–50% relative to the best baseline. In MSI spectral super-resolution (Table 4), FLRTF dominates all five deep-learning baselines (HRNet, GDNet, HSACS, HSRnet, SSDCN) by 7.3 dB on ARAD_1K and 8.4 dB on CAVE in PSNR, though HSACS achieves a marginally lower SAM (6.05° vs. 6.91°) on ARAD_1K. The comparison with LRTFR (Table 5) confirms FLRTF's advantage over functional-representation approaches applied to Tucker decomposition, with consistent 1–1.5 dB PSNR gains on video interpolation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the low-tubal-rank tensor factorization (LRTF) lineage—rooted in t-SVD (Braman et al.) and the LRTF framework of Zhou et al.—by replacing the discrete transform along the third dimension with a continuous functional transform parameterized by an MLP with positional encodings. The most direct predecessor is HLRTF (Luo et al., CVPR 2022), which already uses an MLP as a learned (but still discrete) transform in the t-SVD framework; FLRTF's key departure is making that transform a continuous implicit neural representation, so that the third-dimension coordinate becomes a real-valued input rather than an index. This connects the tensor-factorization literature to the broader implicit neural representation (INR) paradigm, which has been applied to image/video representation but not previously to the transform step of tensor decomposition.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The continuity of the functional transform provides a principled mechanism for capturing local smoothness in the temporal/spectral dimension, which is precisely what discrete transforms (DFT, DCT, or learned discrete maps) cannot express; Theorem 2 formalizes this as a bounded-difference guarantee between adjacent slices. The per-instance, training-free nature of the optimization (only observed data required, no external training set) makes FLRTF attractive for deployment in settings where a dedicated training dataset is unavailable. Open questions include scalability to much larger tensors (the experiments use at most 256×256×31), whether the continuous-transform advantage persists at higher resolutions or longer temporal sequences, and how the method compares to amortized learned approaches when a training set is available.
