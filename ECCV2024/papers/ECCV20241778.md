## Problem

Long-tail recognition suffers from head-class bias because one-hot supervision ignores inter-class feature correlations, and tail-class predictions exhibit high uncertainty across training epochs. Existing long-tail methods do not exploit the consistent knowledge embedded in past predictions, nor do they model the feature-level relationships between classes that one-hot labels discard. This paper asks how a model can learn in a 'reflective' manner—reviewing past predictions, summarizing cross-class structure, and correcting gradient conflicts—to close the head-tail performance gap.

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR, 224×224 for ImageNet/Places/iNaturalist) and outputs a class label via a softmax classifier, identical to the base long-tail method it augments. A key operational requirement is that the model must retain the full prediction distribution and backbone features from epoch t−1 in memory to serve as soft labels and class-center estimates for epoch t, which the authors note adds memory overhead for very large category counts. The plug-and-play design means no architectural change is needed; the three modules operate on logits and features already produced by the existing backbone and classifier.

## Method

Reflective Learning adds three lightweight modules to any existing long-tail loss. (1) Knowledge Review (KR) minimizes a temperature-scaled KL divergence between the previous epoch's prediction and the current epoch's prediction, but only over the Correctly Classified Instances (CCI) set from epoch t−1, preventing transfer of erroneous knowledge. (2) Knowledge Summary (KS) computes per-class feature centers as the median of backbone features from epoch t−1, builds a C×C cosine-similarity matrix, and blends it with the one-hot label (weighted by α) to form a soft supervisory target that encodes inter-class feature correlation. (3) Knowledge Correction (KC) detects gradient conflicts between the base LTR gradient and the combined KR+KS gradient (cosine < 0) and projects the LTR gradient onto the orthonormal complement of the KR+KS direction before summing, eliminating negative transfer. The total loss is L_RL = L_LTR + L_KR + L_KS, with KC applied at the gradient-update level, making the approach compatible with re-balancing, augmentation, and ensemble long-tail methods across CNN and Transformer backbones.

## Results

On CIFAR-100-LT (IF=100), the best configuration MDCS+RL† achieves 57.3% top-1 accuracy, surpassing MDCS† (56.1%) by 1.2 points, while SADE+RL† reaches 54.7% versus SADE†'s 53.2%. On iNaturalist 2018, NCL+RL† attains 76.5% overall accuracy (77.8% on few-shot), outperforming NCL† (74.9%) by 1.6 points. On ImageNet-LT, MDCS+RL† achieves 62.7% overall accuracy, a 0.9-point gain over MDCS†. Across all five benchmarks, RL consistently improves few-shot class accuracy by 1–4.3% while maintaining or slightly improving many-shot performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RL is a plug-and-play module that consistently improves every baseline it is combined with. On CIFAR-100-LT (IF=100), SADE+RL† (54.7%) outperforms SADE† (53.2%) by 1.5 points, and MDCS+RL† (57.3%) beats MDCS† (56.1%) by 1.2 points. The gains are most pronounced for weaker baselines: LDAM+RL (49.2%) surpasses LDAM (42.0%) by 7.2 points, and Decouple+RL (50.9%) exceeds Decouple (43.8%) by 7.1 points. On iNaturalist 2018, NCL+RL† (76.5%) improves over NCL† (74.9%) by 1.6 points, with the few-shot accuracy jumping from 74.5% to 77.8%. On ImageNet-LT, RIDE+RL (59.0%) edges out RIDE (56.3%) by 2.7 points, and SADE+RL† (62.0%) surpasses SADE† (61.2%) by 0.8 points. Among regularization-based methods, MiSLAS&RL (53.1%) outperforms MiSLAS alone (47.0%) by 6.1 points on CIFAR-100-LT, and the combined WD&RL+WD&Max&RL (56.8%) beats WD+WD&Max (53.6%) by 3.2 points, confirming orthogonality with existing regularization strategies.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the knowledge distillation framework of Hinton et al. [14] (temperature-scaled KL divergence) and consistency regularization from semi-supervised learning [3,22,36], but repurposes both for long-tail recognition in a temporal self-distillation setting: the model distills from its own predictions in the previous epoch rather than from a separate teacher or expert ensemble. It departs from prior long-tail distillation methods (e.g., NCL [23], LFME [43]) that transfer knowledge between head and tail classes or between multiple experts, instead exploiting temporal prediction consistency filtered by correctly-classified instances. The gradient projection mechanism (KC) draws on gradient surgery literature to resolve conflicts between the base LTR loss and the auxiliary KR/KS losses, a component not previously applied in the long-tail setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The plug-and-play design means practitioners can add LTRL to any existing long-tail method (BSCE, RIDE, SADE, MDCS, NCL) without architectural changes, and the consistent 1–5% gains across four datasets and both CNN and Transformer backbones make it a practical drop-in module. The main limitation is the memory overhead of storing previous-epoch predictions, which becomes non-trivial at very large scale (tens of thousands of categories). The authors note the reflective-learning paradigm could extend to other domains (LLMs, detection, generation), but no such experiments are provided, leaving the generality claim open. The KC module's contribution is small (~0.4%), suggesting the gradient-conflict problem, while real, is not the dominant bottleneck.
