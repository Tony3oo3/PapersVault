## Problem

CLIP models exhibit a strong text spotting bias, but the source of this bias in the training data pipeline was not systematically identified. This paper asks whether the high proportion of 'parrot captions'—captions that merely repeat text visible in the image pixels—planted in LAION-2B is the root cause, and whether this shortcut undermines genuine vision-language alignment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241124/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis consumes 2 billion image-text pairs from LAION-2B, OCR polygon predictions from DeepSolo (ViTAEv2-S backbone), and pre-trained CLIP/OpenCLIP ViT-B-32 weights. Text removal is performed via fast-marching inpainting over OCR-predicted polygons, and synthetic benchmark images are rendered from N-gram vocabularies extracted from captions and co-embedded text. Outputs include per-cluster parrot caption statistics (CoTR, embedded text ratios), relative CLIP scores (RSA/RSC) quantifying text dominance, and trained CLIP/BLIP checkpoints evaluated on 38 DataComp zero-shot tasks plus text-oriented benchmarks (TextVQA, TextCaps, ST-VQA).

## Method

The paper conducts a three-pronged empirical investigation. First, it profiles LAION-2B by K-Means clustering 2B images on CLIP ViT-B-32 features (reduced to 256-d via PCA), running DeepSolo OCR on all images, and computing the Co-Emb. Text Rate (word-set IoU between caption and spotted text) to quantify parrot caption prevalence. Second, it inspects released CLIP and OpenCLIP models by inpainting embedded text (All-Emb. and Co-Emb. variants plus random-inpainting baselines) to measure score drops (RSA/RSC), and by generating synthetic images with rendered N-gram text from caption and co-embedded vocabularies to probe text spotting preferences in a controlled, overfitting-free setting. Third, it trains CLIP (ViT-B, RN50) and BLIP on 3M/12M LAION subsets curated by escalating parrot-caption criteria—embedded text ratio, CoTR thresholds, and RSA/RSC thresholds—demonstrating that higher parrot caption density yields stronger text spotting capacity but progressively degrades zero-shot classification, retrieval, VQA, and captioning performance, confirming that parrot captions teach CLIP to spot text at the expense of true visual-language semantics.

## Results

Profiling LAION-2B reveals that 54.60% of images contain embedded visual text, and the Co-Emb. Text Rate reaches 28.24% (fuzzy: 55.79%) among text-containing images, meaning roughly 30% of caption words are literally printed in the image. Removing embedded text via inpainting drops mean CLIP scores from 0.3358 (raw, w/ text) to 0.2974 (All-Emb. removal) and 0.2993 (Co-Emb. removal), while random inpainting baselines stay near 0.326–0.334, confirming text content—not distribution shift—drives the score. Training CLIP (RN50, 3M) exclusively on text-embedded images collapses DataComp Avg. from 0.256 (random) to 0.164, whereas filtering to text-free images improves it to 0.282. On BLIP downstream tasks, parrot-caption training (CoTR≥0.3) boosts TextVQA from 15.36 to 19.28 but degrades VQAv2 from 71.07 to 67.40 and COCO captioning CIDEr from 115.6 to 104.9.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares OpenAI CLIP (trained on WIT-400M) against OpenCLIP (trained on LAION-2B) on synthetic N-gram text-spotting benchmarks. OpenCLIP consistently shows stronger text-spotting scores across all frequency and length groupings, confirming that LAION's parrot-caption-rich data amplifies the bias relative to WIT-400M. Both models are more sensitive to the Co-Emb. text vocabulary than the general caption vocabulary, and both prefer longer words, suggesting word-level rather than character-level spotting. On the data-curation ablations, the 'w/o Emb. Text' subset outperforms the random baseline on DataComp Avg. by +0.026 (RN50, 3M) and +0.013 (ViT-B, 12M), while the 'w/ Emb. Text Only' subset underperforms random by −0.092 (RN50, 3M) and −0.126 (ViT-B, 12M). For BLIP, parrot-caption training (CoTR≥0.3) gains +3.92 on TextVQA over random but loses −3.67 on VQAv2 and −10.7 on COCO CIDEr, illustrating a clear see-vs-read trade-off. The RSA/RSC ablations show a monotonic degradation: at RSA≥0.3, DataComp Avg. drops to 0.097 (RN50) from 0.239 at RSA<0.0, while text-spotting scores on the synthetic benchmark keep rising, confirming that higher parrot dominance in training data trades general vision-language alignment for narrow OCR-like behavior.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the CLIP/OpenCLIP contrastive vision-language pre-training framework and the LAION-2B dataset, extending prior bias-study work (e.g., [13, 19, 25] on multimodal neurons and text-dominant classification) by identifying the specific data-level cause—parrot captions—rather than merely documenting the bias. It departs from earlier data-curation efforts [7, 24, 28] that filter text-dominant images by showing that the problem is not simply the presence of embedded text but the *concurrent* repetition of that text in captions, and by providing causal training experiments that prior profiling-only studies lacked. The use of BLIP [21] for downstream text-oriented task evaluation extends the analysis beyond zero-shot classification to generation and retrieval settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that ~50% of LAION-2B images contain visual text and ~30% of caption words parrot that text fundamentally undermines the assumption that CLIP score filtering yields semantically aligned image-text pairs, with direct practical consequences for anyone building on LAION-style pipelines. The causal demonstration that parrot captions (not merely embedded text) drive the text spotting bias challenges the 'emergent ability' narrative and suggests that the contrastive objective itself may be insufficient to prevent shortcut learning at scale. Open questions include whether bias-aware curation (e.g., penalizing Co-Emb. Text Rate) can recover the lost generalization without sacrificing legitimate text-reading capability, and whether the same parrot-caption phenomenon pervades other large-scale datasets like COYO or DataComp.
