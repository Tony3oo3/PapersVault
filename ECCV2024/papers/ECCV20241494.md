## Problem

Direct regression from audio to full 3D facial mesh sequences is ill-posed, causing existing data-driven methods to produce over-smoothed animations with poor lip-speech synchronization. These methods ignore both linguistic priors (phoneme-to-viseme articulation) and the structural role of key motion frames, missing detailed facial dynamics such as mouth opening or puckering.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241494/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes raw speech audio as its sole user-facing input and produces a sequence of 3D facial mesh deformations (N frames × V vertices × 3 coordinates) on a neutral template. Internally, it also consumes ASR-transcribed text and phoneme boundary timestamps (via Montreal Forced Aligner) to determine key motion indices, and wav2vec 2.0 audio features for both key motion decoding and full-sequence completion. Experiments use 25 fps on BIWI (23,370 vertices) and 30 fps on VOCASET (5,023 vertices).

## Method

KMTalk introduces a progressive coarse-to-fine learning mechanism that decomposes the ill-posed audio-to-mesh mapping into two stages. First, the Linguistic-based Key Motion Acquisition (LKMA) module uses phoneme-boundary timestamps to identify key motion indices, then a transformer-based Key Motion-focused Decoder synthesizes 3D facial expressions at those positions, trained with a pseudo-complete-sequence loss that interleaves generated key frames with ground-truth non-key frames. Second, the Cross-modal Motion Completion (CMC) module encodes key motions into motion flow features via cross-attention with sinusoidal positional encodings of non-key frames (leveraging CITL's implicit motion manifold), then a Multimodal-Guided Decoder fuses these motion flow features with wav2vec 2.0 audio features through a learned gated mechanism to decode the full N-frame mesh sequence. This separation reduces cross-modal uncertainty by anchoring the generation to linguistically meaningful articulatory events before interpolating the remaining frames.

## Results

On BIWI-Test-A, KMTalk achieves the lowest Lip Vertex Error (LVE) of 3.97×10⁻⁴ mm and the lowest Upper-Face Dynamics Deviation (FDD) of 2.54×10⁻⁵ mm, outperforming the strongest baseline SelfTalk (LVE 4.25, FDD 3.58) by 6.7% and 29% respectively. On VOCA-Test, KMTalk achieves LVE of 2.26×10⁻⁵ mm (30% lower than SelfTalk's 3.22) and FDD of 4.06×10⁻⁷ mm (marginally better than SelfTalk's 4.09). In user studies, 60.0% of participants preferred KMTalk's lip synchronization over SelfTalk on BIWI-Test-B, and 75.6% preferred it over FaceFormer.

## Baselines & Comparisons

KMTalk outperforms all five baselines on every metric across both datasets. The largest gains are over SelfTalk, the strongest prior method: 30% lower LVE on VOCA-Test (2.26 vs 3.22) and 29% lower FDD on BIWI-Test-A (2.54 vs 3.58), indicating substantially better lip synchronization and facial dynamics. Against CodeTalker, KMTalk reduces LVE by 17% on BIWI-Test-A (3.97 vs 4.79) and by 42% on VOCA-Test (2.26 vs 3.94). The margin over FaceFormer is even wider, with 25% lower LVE and 45% lower FDD on BIWI-Test-A. On VOCA-Test FDD, KMTalk (4.06) edges out SelfTalk (4.09) by only ~0.8%, suggesting upper-face dynamics are already well-handled by SelfTalk's self-supervised lip-reading component. The paper further shows that integrating KMTalk's progressive learning scheme into FaceFormer, CodeTalker, and SelfTalk (without retraining) consistently improves their results (e.g., SelfTalk FDD drops from 3.58 to 2.87 on BIWI-Test-A), confirming the orthogonality and general applicability of the key-motion-embedding strategy.

## Relation to Prior Work

KMTalk departs from the dominant paradigm in data-driven speech-to-3D-face methods (VOCA, MeshTalk, FaceFormer, CodeTalker, SelfTalk) that directly regress the full facial motion sequence from audio, which the authors argue leads to over-smoothing due to the ill-posed cross-modal mapping. Instead, it borrows the coarse-to-fine philosophy from keyframe-based video generation and combines it with linguistic priors (phoneme-boundary localization via ASR + Montreal Forced Aligner) to first identify and synthesize key motions, then completes the sequence via a data-driven cross-modal motion completion module. The Motion Flow Encoder draws on the implicit motion manifold from CITL [32], and the LKMA loss design follows SelfTalk [36], but the progressive two-stage decomposition with phoneme-anchored keyframes is the paper's core architectural departure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The progressive key-motion-then-completion strategy offers a principled way to reduce cross-modal mapping uncertainty without requiring new data or architectures, and the integration experiments (Table 5) demonstrate that the scheme can be grafted onto existing methods (FaceFormer, CodeTalker, SelfTalk) without fine-tuning to yield consistent gains, suggesting it is a broadly applicable training strategy rather than a monolithic system. Open questions include sensitivity to dialect or noisy ASR outputs (acknowledged by the authors), whether the phoneme-boundary assumption holds for non-English or highly expressive speech, and whether the approach extends to longer sequences or multi-speaker settings. The reliance on an external ASR + forced-alignment pipeline also introduces a preprocessing dependency that may limit real-time deployment.
