## Problem

Human-labelling errors in vision datasets (typically ~5% noise) have distinct characteristics from synthetic label errors and pose unique challenges to Supervised Contrastive Learning (SCL) that differ from their effects on cross-entropy training. Existing noise-mitigation methods target unrealistic synthetic noise rates (40–80%) and overfit on real datasets, while the specific mechanism by which mislabelled samples corrupt SCL learning signals—particularly through false positives—remains unexplored.

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard RGB images in mini-batches, where positive and negative pairs are determined by assigned class labels within each batch. It produces a learned embedding f(x) for each image, which is then used for classification via a frozen linear probe trained with cross-entropy. The key operational detail is that the loss reweights positive and negative pairs based on their cosine similarity to the anchor embedding, requiring no additional model components or data beyond the standard SCL training loop.

## Method

SCL-RHE modifies the standard supervised contrastive loss by reweighting positive and negative pairs according to their embedding similarity to the anchor, motivated by the finding that ~99% of incorrect SCL learning signals arise from 'easy positives' (mislabeled samples that are visually similar to the anchor). The derivation uses a Positive-Unlabeled (PU) learning decomposition to express the ideal positive sampling distribution in terms of observable quantities, then introduces a von Mises-Fisher density term with concentration parameter β that down-weights easy positives and up-weights hard positives. Monte Carlo importance sampling approximates the corrected expectations over the true latent-class distribution, yielding a closed-form loss (Eq. 14) that corrects for the mislabeling rate τ without requiring pseudo-labels, k-NN graphs, or additional confidence modules. A symmetric correction is applied to negatives to emphasize hard negatives, and the entire procedure adds negligible computational overhead compared to standard SCL.

## Results

SCL-RHE achieves 84.21% top-1 accuracy on ImageNet-1K with BEiT-3 (trained from scratch), outperforming SupCon (82.57%), Sel-CL (81.87%), TCL (81.74%), and cross-entropy (77.91%). On CIFAR-10, SCL-RHE reaches 90.16% with BEiT-3 and 96.39% with ResNet-50, surpassing the next-best SupCon by 1.2% and 0.4% respectively. In the transfer learning setting (Table 4), SCL-RHE achieves the best accuracy across all 8 fine-tuning datasets, with particularly large gains on iNaturalist 2017 (+3.4% over SupCon) and Places365 (+2.2%). SCL-RHE also reduces training time by ~30% compared to Sel-CL and ~38% compared to TCL.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SCL-RHE consistently outperforms all four baselines across every dataset and both model architectures. Against SupCon [27], the closest competitor, SCL-RHE gains 1.64% on ImageNet-1K (BEiT-3), 3.70% on CIFAR-100 (BEiT-3), and 0.45% on CIFAR-10 (ResNet-50). Against the synthetic-noise-mitigating methods Sel-CL [29] and TCL [24], SCL-RHE leads by 2.34% and 2.47% on ImageNet-1K (BEiT-3), and by 4.08% and 5.25% on CIFAR-100 (BEiT-3); the paper attributes Sel-CL/TCL's underperformance to overfitting on a small subset of confident pairs when noise rates are realistically low (~5%). Against cross-entropy [52], SCL-RHE achieves the largest margins on transformer models (e.g., +16.3% on CIFAR-10 with BEiT-3), highlighting that contrastive objectives are especially beneficial for ViT-based architectures on limited data. In the transfer learning setting, SCL-RHE also beats all baselines on all 8 fine-tuning datasets, with the gap widening on fine-grained or large-scale benchmarks like iNaturalist 2017 and Places365.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the supervised contrastive learning (SCL) framework of Khosla et al. (SupCon) by incorporating a PU-learning-based importance sampling mechanism to handle human-labelling errors, drawing on the hard-negative weighting ideas from Robinson et al. and the PU learning literature. It departs from prior SCL noise-mitigation methods (Sel-CL, TCL) that target synthetic label errors at 40–80% rates by instead addressing the realistic regime of low-rate (~5%) human errors, which the authors show are visually similar to their assigned class and thus indistinguishable from true positives in embedding space. The key conceptual shift is from excluding 'confident' samples (which risks overfitting at low noise rates) to down-weighting easy positives via a von Mises-Fisher-inspired reweighting, implemented through Monte Carlo importance sampling over the batch.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that ~99% of incorrect learning signals in SCL arise from false positives (not false negatives) provides a clear diagnostic that simplifies the noise-mitigation problem to a single direction, and the empirical demonstration that synthetic-noise methods (Sel-CL, TCL) actually underperform plain SupCon on realistic datasets is a useful caution for practitioners. The method's zero computational overhead and consistent gains across ten datasets and two architectures (ViT/BEiT-3 and ResNet) make it a practical drop-in replacement for standard SCL. Open questions include whether the constant mislabeling rate assumption (τ) can be estimated automatically, whether the approach transfers to non-visual modalities or multi-label settings, and whether the PU-learning framework extends to instance-dependent noise patterns.
