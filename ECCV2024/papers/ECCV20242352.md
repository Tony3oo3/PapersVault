## Problem

Existing NLoS reconstruction methods—both filtering-based (e.g., Light Cone Transform) and data-driven—assume the hidden scene is static during acquisition. At the fastest available scan rate of 4 Hz (250 ms per frame), a moving human body changes pose and location substantially, so a single transient measurement encodes multiple configurations simultaneously, producing severe motion-skew artifacts that render reconstructions unrecognizable. No prior work explicitly models this motion/scan interplay for articulated human pose estimation.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a single dynamic NLoS transient measurement (32×32 spatial × 512 temporal bins at 64 ps resolution) acquired by horizontally sweeping a 2 m × 2 m relay wall at 4 Hz, where each vertical scan slice corresponds to a slightly different pose configuration. It outputs the central 22-joint pose and a per-joint velocity vector for that scan interval, which are then combined with adjacent frames via 4-point cubic Bézier interpolation to produce a smooth 32 fps pose sequence.

## Method

The core insight is that a dynamic transient is a concatenation of partial static slices I_d = [I_0 | I_1 | … | I_n], and the spatial skew between successive slices encodes first-order pose change (velocity). The authors formalize this as a motion-aware image formation model where the inverse transport operator T⁻¹ jointly recovers the central pose X and velocity V from the full dynamic measurement. They parameterize T⁻¹ with a neural network: an encoder compresses the transient into a code vector, which is decoded by two parallel ResNet-13 branches—one predicting pose, the other velocity—trained with a combined L₂ loss L = L_X + βL_V. Training data are synthetic NLoS transients rendered from 8,000 AMASS motion-capture sequences (32 fps, SMPL body model) through a custom transient renderer, with Poisson noise calibrated to match the Lindell et al. experimental hardware. For high-framerate output, consecutive (pose, velocity) pairs define 4-point Bézier control polygons that are evaluated at 32 fps to produce temporally coherent sequences.

## Results

On the overall AMASS test partition, the motion-aware (Dynamic) network achieves MPJPE of 0.042 m, V_err of 0.129 m/s, PCP of 87.77%, and PCK of 86.91%, compared to the static-trained network's 0.059 m, 0.136 m/s, 82.36%, and 80.02% respectively. The gap widens dramatically on fast-motion failure cases: on a Dance sequence, the static model's PCP collapses to 17.85% (PCK 2.27%) while the dynamic model maintains 80.95% (PCK 84.09%). On a Run&Turn sequence, the static model scores PCP 30.47% versus 79.04% for the dynamic model. On a slower Jog sequence, both models perform well (PCP 95.83% vs 98.41%), confirming that motion skew is the primary failure mode.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Light Cone Transform (LCT, O'Toole et al. [45]), the proposed method wins decisively on all dynamic sequences: LCT produces severe motion blur and unrecognizable reconstructions on fast motion (e.g., the jumping subject in Fig. 7 bottom row), while the dynamic network recovers most poses within recognition margins. Against HiddenPose (Liu et al. [32]), the proposed method also wins: the authors report that HiddenPose 'did not perform competitively at low resolutions' and is limited to front-facing poses in a small relay region, whereas the dynamic network estimates poses anywhere in the 3D scene even under strong self-occlusion (Fig. 8). The internal static-network baseline is competitive on slow motions (Jog: MPJPE 0.017 vs 0.015 m) but degrades catastrophically on fast motions (Dance PCP drops from 82.36% overall to 17.85% on the specific sequence), confirming that motion skew is the critical failure mode the dynamic model addresses. The authors also attempted to replicate Isogawa et al. [23] but were unable to reproduce results due to missing configuration files, so no quantitative comparison is provided for that method.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the NLoS imaging literature (Light Cone Transform, fast F-K migration) and the nascent NLoS human pose estimation line (Isogawa et al.'s sequence-to-sequence model, Liu et al.'s HiddenPose) by explicitly modeling the temporal skew introduced by mechanical scanning as a first-class component of the image formation model. Rather than treating the transient as a static snapshot or relying on sequence-level priors, the authors decompose each scanned frame into partial slices and recover both a central pose and a velocity vector via a lightweight ResNet-13 encoder-decoder, then reconstruct smooth motion with cubic Bezier splines. This departs from prior NLoS dynamic work that either requires strong motion priors (Isogawa) or operates only at the native 4 Hz without addressing the intra-frame temporal inconsistency.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By identifying motion skew as a systematic, modelable error source rather than an irreducible artifact, the paper provides a principled baseline for dynamic NLoS pose tracking and demonstrates that velocity information is recoverable even at the slowest available scan rates (4 Hz). Key open questions remain: the method is trained entirely on synthetic data with simplified noise, exhibits bone-length inconsistencies on fast real-world motion, and does not yet account for interreflections, varying BRDFs, or floor/ceiling bounces. The work also highlights a fundamental hardware trade-off (scan speed vs. photon budget) that will constrain all future dynamic NLoS systems, making motion-aware reconstruction methods essential rather than optional.
