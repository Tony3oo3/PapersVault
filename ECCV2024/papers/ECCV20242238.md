## Problem

Event-based action recognition research is constrained by the absence of comprehensive, large-scale real-world datasets; existing event datasets are either synthetic (converted from RGB frames), limited to few categories and subjects, or lack the scenario diversity needed to reflect practical deployment challenges. This paper addresses these gaps by introducing a new benchmark that captures the full spectrum of real-world conditions—varying lighting, camera motion, distances, and action scales—under which event cameras must operate.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20242238/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The dataset comprises 22,046 asynchronous event sequences from a DVXplorer Lite sensor at 320×240 resolution, stored as aedat4 files containing event tuples (t, x, y, polarity), IMU streams, and trigger signals, with a total of 18 billion events. Each sequence carries 14 attribute annotations (background complexity, duration, illumination direction, distance, height, location, camera motion, person number, perspective, props, action range, shadow, posture, diurnality) that enable fine-grained group-level analysis beyond simple class labels. The recognition task outputs one of 200 action class labels, evaluated under a cross-subject protocol partitioning 47 participants into 31 training, 8 validation, and 9 test subjects.

## Method

The core contribution is a dataset construction pipeline and a multi-dimensional benchmark evaluation framework rather than a novel architecture. Data was collected from 47 subjects performing 200 daily actions across diverse real-world settings, with each action captured from front, side, and moving-camera views at varying distances (up to 30 m) and heights (up to 3 m), spanning morning, afternoon, and evening sessions. The benchmark evaluates 12 methods across four input representations—frame-based (C3D, I3D, R2+1D, SlowFast, TSM), learnable-based (EST), token-based (TimeSformer, Swin-T, ESTF, GET), and spike-based (Spikformer, SDT)—under both cross-subject and 14-attribute multi-group protocols. A key empirical finding is that token-based models (Swin-T at 48.06% top-1) outperform all other families, and that camera motion causes dramatic accuracy drops (Swin-T: 58.05% static vs. 27.84% moving) because high event rates from background motion overwhelm action signals. Frame-setting ablations further show that shorter time steps (0.125 s) with zero frame gap maximize accuracy, while larger gaps introduce information loss that degrades recognition across all frame-based models.

## Results

On the DailyDVS-200 dataset (22,046 samples, 200 action classes, 47 subjects), the best-performing method is Swin-T (token-based, Transformer backbone) achieving 48.06% top-1 and 74.47% top-5 accuracy under cross-subject evaluation. Among frame-based methods, SlowFast leads with 41.49% top-1, while TSM achieves the highest top-5 (71.46%) among frame-based approaches. Spike-based methods (Spikformer: 36.94%, SDT: 35.43%) and the learnable-based EST (32.23%) all underperform the best token-based and frame-based models, highlighting the continued challenge of event-based action recognition in diverse real-world conditions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Swin-T [36] clearly outperforms all 11 other baselines, achieving 48.06% top-1 accuracy, a 6.57-point margin over the next-best token-based method TimeSformer (44.25%) and a 6.57-point margin over the best frame-based method SlowFast (41.49%). Within the frame-based family, SlowFast (41.49%) edges out TSM (40.87%) on top-1, though TSM leads on top-5 (71.46% vs. 68.19%); both substantially surpass older 3D CNNs like C3D (21.99%) and I3D (32.30%). Among token-based event-specific methods, GET [43] (37.28%) and ESTF [58] (24.68%) trail Swin-T by 10.78 and 23.38 points respectively, suggesting that general-purpose video Transformers still dominate over event-specific token designs on this challenging benchmark. Spike-based methods Spikformer (36.94%) and SDT (35.43%) remain competitive with mid-tier frame-based models but fall well short of the best token-based approaches. The learnable-based EST [19] (32.23%) performs comparably to I3D (32.30%), indicating that end-to-end event representation learning has not yet closed the gap with strong frame-based backbones on this dataset.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in the event-based action recognition dataset landscape by addressing a clear gap: prior real-world DVS action datasets are either small-scale (DvsGesture: 11 classes/29 subjects, PAF: 10 classes/10 subjects, DailyAction: 12 classes/15 subjects) or lack diversity in subjects and conditions (HARDVS: 300 classes but only 5 subjects; THU^E-ACT-50: fixed scenes). Rather than building on a specific methodological lineage, DailyDVS-200 repurposes the standard cross-subject evaluation protocol from frame-based action recognition and extends it with 14 fine-grained attribute annotations (camera motion, lighting direction, action range, diurnality, etc.) that no prior event dataset has provided. The empirical evaluation draws on the full spectrum of existing event representation paradigms—frame-based (C3D, I3D, SlowFast, TSM), learnable (EST), token-based (TimeSformer, Swin-T, GET), and spike-based (Spikformer, SDT)—positioning the dataset as a unified testbed across these families.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dataset exposes a significant and persistent gap: the best event-based method (Swin-T, 48.06% top-1) still lags well behind what frame-based models achieve on comparable benchmarks, and performance degrades sharply under realistic conditions (camera motion drops Swin-T from 58% to 28%; micro-actions score only 34.6%). A notable open question is why mixing dynamic-camera training data with static data *decreases* accuracy on both test splits, suggesting current architectures cannot effectively disentangle ego-motion from action semantics. The 14-attribute annotation scheme opens new research directions—attribute-conditioned recognition, robustness to specific nuisance factors, and targeted data augmentation—that go beyond simple accuracy benchmarks. However, the evaluation is limited to a single sensor (DVXplorer Lite, 320×240) and a single evaluation protocol, and the dynamic-camera finding rests on one model (TSM) with a specific data-mixing experiment, so broader generalizability remains to be established.
