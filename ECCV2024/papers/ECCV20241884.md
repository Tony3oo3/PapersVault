## Problem

Existing Novel Class Discovery methods build a shared representation space for known and novel classes, but the imbalanced number of samples between the two groups pushes the model toward dominant classes. When known samples dominate, novel class pseudo-supervision is overlooked; when novel samples dominate, known class knowledge is progressively marginalized. This creates a difficult trade-off between reviewing known classes and discovering novel classes that prior methods have not adequately addressed.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a mini-batch containing labeled RGB images from C^l known classes and unlabeled RGB images from C^u novel classes (with C^u known a priori), processed through a two-stage pipeline: supervised pre-training on labeled data followed by joint discovery training on all data. The output is a single concatenated logit vector of dimension C^l + C^u, yielding both a known-class label and a novel-class cluster assignment per test sample. For fine-grained datasets, the backbone is a DINO-pretrained ViT-B-16 using the [CLS] token (768-d), while generic datasets use ResNet-18.

## Method

SCKD maintains two disjoint representation spaces (known and novel) rather than a shared one, and introduces a frozen replica encoder E^r (deep-copied after supervised pre-training) to preserve unbiased known-class features. A cosine-similarity score matrix S is computed between replica-encoder features of labeled samples and target-encoder features of unlabeled samples, then normalized. This matrix drives bidirectional pseudo-label synthesis: known-space logits are mapped to novel pseudo-labels (Eq. 3) and novel-space logits are mapped to known pseudo-labels (Eq. 4), each scaled by a smoothing coefficient α. Two KL-divergence distillation losses—L_{k→n} (known guides novel discovery) and L_{n→k} (novel guides known review)—are summed into the SCKD loss, which is added to a standard cross-entropy term (with Sinkhorn-Knopp pseudo-labels for unlabeled data) weighted by β. The key insight is that every sample, regardless of label status, contributes to both reviewing known classes and discovering novel classes, eliminating the dominance bias inherent in shared-space approaches.

## Results

On the unlabeled training subset (task-aware protocol), SCKD achieves clustering accuracy of 92.6% on CIFAR100-20, 68.2% on CIFAR100-50, 82.18% on ImageNet-100, 56.8% on Stanford Cars, 73.1% on CUB, and 56.5% on FGVC-Aircraft, outperforming the strongest baselines (IIC, rKD, SimGCD) on 6 of 7 datasets. On the testing subset (task-agnostic protocol), SCKD achieves the best overall accuracy across all five evaluated splits, e.g., 70.3% All on CIFAR100-50 and 76.8% All on CUB. When the number of novel classes increases from 20 to 80 on CIFAR100, SCKD's known-class accuracy drops only from 76.1% to 74.7%, whereas UNO drops from 73.2% to 62.5% and IIC from 75.9% to 64.7%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SCKD outperforms the strongest recent baselines IIC (CVPR 2023) and rKD (ICCV 2023) on 6 of 7 datasets in the training-subset clustering accuracy, with the largest margins on CIFAR100-50 (+2.4 over IIC, +2.9 over rKD), CUB (+1.8 over IIC, +7.4 over rKD), and ImageNet-100 (+1.94 over IIC, +1.24 over rKD). The sole exception is CIFAR10, where IIC reaches 99.1% versus SCKD's 95.6%, likely due to the simpler data distribution limiting the benefit of the similarity-based module. Against SimGCD (ICCV 2023), SCKD leads on all four overlapping datasets (e.g., +7.9 on CUB, +6.6 on Stanford Cars). On the task-agnostic testing protocol (Table 3), SCKD achieves the best All accuracy on every split, with notable gains on novel-class clustering (e.g., 72.3% on CUB vs. 69.4% for IIC and 67.5% for rKD). In the varying-cluster experiment (Table 4), SCKD's known-class accuracy degrades far less than UNO, IIC, or rKD as novel classes increase from 20 to 80, confirming its robustness to class imbalance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant NCD paradigm of building a single shared representation space for known and novel classes (as in KCL, NCL, IIC, rKD) by instead maintaining two disjoint representation spaces and bridging them through a similarity-weighted self-cooperation distillation loss. It builds directly on UNO's two-stage training framework and multi-head architecture, and on DTC's class-number estimation procedure, while drawing conceptual inspiration from the self-knowledge distillation literature (BAN, FRSKD, DLB) but repurposing it for cross-group information transfer rather than intra-model layer guidance. The key conceptual departure is treating the known/novel sample imbalance as a first-class problem and using every sample for both review and discovery simultaneously, rather than assigning labeled samples exclusively to known-class learning and unlabeled samples exclusively to novel-class discovery.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper highlights a practical and previously under-examined failure mode in NCD: as the ratio of known to novel samples shifts, existing methods degrade asymmetrically, with known-class accuracy dropping sharply when novel classes dominate. The self-cooperation distillation is a lightweight, plug-in loss that requires no additional forward passes beyond the standard model, making it easy to adopt. However, the improvements over strong baselines (IIC, rKD) are modest (1–3 points), the method underperforms on CIFAR10 (attributed to limited semantic richness), and the frozen replica encoder adds a small memory overhead. Open questions include whether the disjoint-space formulation is truly necessary or whether the gains could be captured by simpler regularization, and how the method behaves in fully open-world settings where the number of novel classes is genuinely unknown rather than estimated.
