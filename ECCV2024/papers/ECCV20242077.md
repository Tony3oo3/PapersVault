## Problem

While crowdsourcing platforms like MTurk have been extensively studied for annotation quality, the internal quality assurance (QA) processes employed by annotation companies remain largely unexamined. This paper investigates whether internal QA actually improves annotation quality, how its benefit compares to investing in better labeling instructions, and whether QA efficacy depends on specific real-world image characteristics.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20242077/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The study operates on 57,648 instance segmentation masks of surgical instruments from 4,050 unique laparoscopic video frames (HeiCo dataset), annotated by 924 annotators and 34 QA workers across five providers. Each annotation task consumes raw RGB frames paired with one of three progressively detailed labeling instruction sets (168, 446, or 961 words across 7, 10, or 16 slides), and produces per-instance segmentation masks scored by Dice Similarity Coefficient and Normalized Surface Distance. Additionally, 57,636 metadata annotations capturing real-world image characteristics (underexposure, occlusion, motion blur, object intersection, etc.) are used to stratify QA benefit.

## Method

The paper conducts a controlled multi-provider experiment where all five annotation providers (four companies and MTurk) annotate identical images under three instruction conditions, with annotation companies performing their standard internal QA pipeline (annotator produces masks → QA worker reviews and modifies before delivery). Annotation quality is quantified via DSC, NSD, severe error rate, and spam detection (combining duration-based heuristics with manual inspection by an experienced engineer), while per-image costs are tracked to enable budget-normalized comparisons. The key analytical contribution for RQ3 is a logistic mixed model with random effects for image, annotator, and company that tests whether specific real-world image characteristics modulate the probability of QA yielding a positive quality change. The central finding is that internal QA provides at most marginal DSC gains (below 2% relative improvement for two of four companies, none for the other two), whereas upgrading from minimal to picture-augmented labeling instructions produces substantially larger quality improvements, suggesting that resource allocation toward instruction design rather than QA yields better annotation outcomes per dollar spent.

## Results

Across 57,648 instance segmentation masks from 924 annotators and 34 QA workers, annotation companies produced zero spam annotations (vs. ~20% on MTurk) and more than 2× fewer images with severe errors, while being 61.1% cheaper in the median (range: 24.5%–81.5% of MTurk cost per image). Internal QA by annotation companies yielded at most a <2% relative improvement in median DSC for two of four companies, with no measurable change for the other two. Improving labeling instructions from minimal text (168 words, 7 slides) to extended text with pictures (961 words, 16 slides) produced substantially larger DSC gains than adding QA on top of minimal instructions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between four annotation companies and Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk) on identical laparoscopic images from the HeiCo dataset. All four companies outperform MTurk on every quality metric: they eliminate spam entirely (~20% on MTurk), reduce severe errors by a factor of >2, and achieve higher median DSC and NSD with smaller interquartile ranges. On cost, companies are 61.1% cheaper in the median despite including a QA step. The second key comparison is internal QA versus no-QA within annotation companies: QA provides at most a <2% relative DSC improvement for two companies and no measurable change for the other two, making it far less impactful than upgrading labeling instructions from minimal text to extended text with pictures. A third comparison shows that QA's benefit is not uniform across images: it significantly increases the odds of improvement for images with underexposure, object intersection, and background occlusion, but not for motion artifacts or overexposure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the annotation-quality evaluation literature that has primarily compared crowdsourcing platforms (especially MTurk) against domain experts or examined external QA as a post-processing step. It departs from prior work by focusing specifically on the *internal* QA processes within annotation companies—a mechanism that has received little systematic study—and by jointly analyzing QA effectiveness alongside labeling-instruction quality and real-world image characteristics. The closest prior work (Rädsch et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2023; Lu et al. 2023) either omitted QA entirely, focused on external QA by requesters, or involved substantially smaller annotator cohorts, leaving a gap this paper fills with 57,648 masks from 924 annotators across five providers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

In a data-annotation market projected to reach $8.2B by 2028, the finding that internal QA yields at most ~2% DSC improvement while richer labeling instructions produce far larger gains directly challenges how researchers and companies allocate annotation budgets. The practical implication is that requesters should invest in instruction design before paying for QA, and that QA resources should be targeted at images with specific challenging characteristics (underexposure, occlusion) rather than applied uniformly. Key limitations include the single safety-critical domain (laparoscopic surgery), inability to disclose company identities, and the question of whether findings generalize to other annotation tasks or to the emerging landscape of foundation-model-assisted annotation.
