## Problem

State-of-the-art end-to-end autonomous driving planners are evaluated exclusively in open-loop settings on nominal driving scenarios, leaving their behavior in closed-loop, safety-critical situations entirely unknown. Prior work has shown that open-loop displacement metrics do not correlate with actual driving quality, but no photorealistic closed-loop simulator existed that could test end-to-end planners in rare, collision-prone scenarios. This paper exposes that current SotA planners crash in the vast majority of safety-critical scenarios despite accurately perceiving the environment.

## Input / Output

The closed-loop simulator consumes 360° rendered camera images (from NeuRAD), ego-vehicle state (position, heading, speed), a high-level command (left/right/straight), and CAN-bus signals, and produces a sequence of future waypoints up to 3 seconds ahead. An LQR controller converts these waypoints into steering angle and acceleration commands, which are fed into a discrete kinematic bicycle model (acceleration limited to ±6 m/s²) to propagate the ego state. The evaluation outputs a NeuroNCAP score (0–5 stars) per scenario and a binary collision flag, with scenarios run 100 times each under fixed random seeds for statistical robustness.

## Method

The core contribution is a four-step closed-loop simulation loop: (1) a NeRF-based renderer (NeuRAD-L, trained 100k steps per sequence) generates photorealistic 360° camera views from the current ego-vehicle state, (2) an end-to-end planner predicts a future trajectory, (3) an LQR controller maps waypoints to steering and acceleration signals, and (4) a kinematic bicycle model advances the ego state one timestep. Safety-critical scenarios are created by editing dynamic actors within the NeRF representation—removing all non-stationary actors, selecting one as a target, and jittering its position, rotation, and velocity to produce stationary, frontal, or side-collision configurations inspired by Euro NCAP protocols. The NeuroNCAP score awards 5 stars for full collision avoidance and linearly decreases from 4 to 0 stars based on the ratio of actual impact velocity to the reference (no-action) impact velocity. A real-to-sim gap study confirms that the NeRF rendering introduces negligible degradation in perception, prediction, and open-loop planning metrics, validating that observed failures stem from the planners themselves rather than simulation artifacts.

## Results

On the NeuroNCAP safety-critical benchmark, both SotA end-to-end planners (UniAD and VAD) without trajectory post-processing achieve average NeuroNCAP scores of only 0.73 and 0.66 respectively, with collision rates of 88.6% and 92.5%. With post-processing, VAD improves to 2.75 average NNS and 50.7% collision rate, while UniAD reaches 1.84 and 68.7%. Notably, a simple rule-based baseline (brake if TTC < 2s with an object ahead) using the same perception outputs achieves 2.65–2.67 average NNS and 68.7–69.9% collision rate, outperforming the end-to-end planners without post-processing. In frontal scenarios, collision rates remain extremely high (73.6–100%) across all configurations, indicating a fundamental limitation in handling oncoming-traffic criticality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates two SotA end-to-end planners—UniAD (CVPR 2023) and VAD (ICCV 2023)—against each other and against a simple rule-based baseline on the NeuroNCAP safety-critical benchmark. Without trajectory post-processing, both planners perform catastrophically: UniAD scores 0.73 average NNS (88.6% collision rate) and VAD scores 0.66 (92.5%), both worse than the naive baseline (2.65–2.67 NNS, ~69% collision rate). With post-processing, VAD outperforms UniAD across all scenario types, achieving 2.75 vs. 1.84 average NNS and 50.7% vs. 68.7% collision rate. However, even the best configuration (VAD + post-processing) still collides in 73.6% of frontal scenarios and 49.8% of side scenarios, revealing that neither planner generalizes to safety-critical situations despite strong perception (recall >80% at 5–25m). The real-to-sim gap study confirms that the failures are not due to rendering artifacts but rather to the planners' inability to handle the covariate shift between nominal training data and critical scenarios.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on NeuRAD (Tonderski et al., CVPR 2024) for photorealistic neural rendering of driving scenes, extending its actor-editing capability to construct safety-critical scenarios. It departs from the dominant open-loop evaluation paradigm used by UniAD and VAD, where predicted trajectories are compared to recorded ground truth without being executed, by introducing a full closed-loop pipeline (render → plan → control → propagate) that mirrors deployment conditions. The scenario design draws from the Euro NCAP collision-avoidance protocol, repurposing an industry safety standard into a computational benchmark, and the collision-focused NeuroNCAP score replaces the displacement-based metrics (L2, ADE) that prior open-loop benchmarks rely on.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that state-of-the-art end-to-end planners crash in 88–92% of safety-critical scenarios despite near-perfect perception—exposes a dangerous disconnect between open-loop benchmark scores and real-world safety, with direct implications for the deployment readiness of learned planners. The publicly released simulator and scenario suite give the community a reproducible, sensor-realistic stress test that was previously unavailable. Key limitations remain: the kinematic bicycle model omits tire dynamics and delays, the renderer cannot handle deformable actors (pedestrians), and the target actor follows a fixed trajectory without reacting to the ego-vehicle, all of which constrain the benchmark's coverage of real-world complexity. Open questions include whether the planning failures stem from training-data distribution shift, insufficient multi-modal reasoning, or the absence of explicit safety constraints in the loss landscape.
