## Problem

Current text-to-image inversion methods (Textual Inversion, DreamBooth, Custom-Diffusion) can only invert appearance-based concepts tied to nouns or styles, but fail to faithfully invert adjectives and verbs that are entangled with the subject they modify. This failure stems from two inherent properties: such concepts change the subject's appearance (causing subject features to leak into the concept embedding) and often require multi-word descriptions that exceed a single text embedding's capacity.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240384/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Lego requires exactly 4 exemplar images per concept—2 showing the subject with the concept (I_C) and 2 showing the same subject without it (I_C̄)—plus user-specified sets of positive (synonym) and negative (antonym) words for each semantic token in the concept. The output is a set of optimized text embeddings: one <subj> embedding for the exemplar subject and n <cpt_i> embeddings for the concept, which are injected into any frozen T2I diffusion model's text encoder to generate new images applying the personalized concept to arbitrary target subjects.

## Method

Lego augments the Textual Inversion framework with two mechanisms operating in parallel on the same frozen diffusion model. First, Subject Separation dedicates an explicit <subj> embedding that is optimized via the standard inversion loss on both the with-concept and without-concept image sets, forcing the concept embeddings to dissociate from subject appearance features. Second, a Context Loss (a modified InfoNCE objective) steers each of the n concept embeddings individually toward the embeddings of its semantic word's synonyms and away from its antonyms, enabling faithful multi-word concept representation. The subject embedding is recovered by minimizing the inversion loss alone, while each concept embedding minimizes a weighted sum of the inversion loss and the context loss (λ·L_context), allowing the method to handle concepts described by multiple tokens without conflating their semantic roles in the text-embedding space.

## Results

Lego is evaluated on 10 concepts (cardinality 3/4/5, frozen in ice, burnt and melted, closed eyes, smiley emoji face, crumpled and squeezed, walking on a rope, arms raised) using 4 exemplar images (2 with, 2 without the concept). On the LLM (Flan-T5 XL) metric, Lego outperforms natural-language-guided LDM on all 7 non-numerical concepts, with the largest gap on 'walking on a rope' (133/200 vs 4/200) and 'burnt and melted' (136/200 vs 61/200). On the human preference metric (Amazon Mechanical Turk, 14,000 total votes), Lego is preferred over LDM in over 70% of cases for every concept, peaking at 84% for 'walking on a rope' and 77% for 'burnt and melted'. For cardinality control, Lego produces correct subject counts in 107/200 (3 subjects), 63/200 (4), and 36/200 (5) images versus 85, 18, and 18 for LDM respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary quantitative baseline is LDM with natural-language prompts (Rombach et al., CVPR 2022); Lego wins on every concept in both the LLM VQA metric and the human preference study, with the most dramatic advantage on action concepts like 'walking on a rope' (133 vs 4 LLM confirmations; 84% vs 16% human preference). Qualitatively, DALL.E 2 (Ramesh et al., 2022) and Attend-and-Excite (Chefer et al., ACM TOG 2023) can generate the general concept but fail to faithfully reproduce the specific visual characteristics of the reference image (e.g., particular crack patterns in ice), whereas Lego transfers those personalized details. Custom-Diffusion (Kumari et al., CVPR 2023) and Textual Inversion (Gal et al., 2022) both suffer from appearance leakage when inverting subject-entangled concepts: Custom-Diffusion's 'melting' embedding inserts Rubik's-cube features into unrelated subjects, and TI's 'closed eyes' embedding leaks the source cat's appearance into Batman and doll images. Lego's Subject Separation step explicitly prevents this leakage, and its Context Loss guides multi-word concept embeddings to semantically appropriate regions of the text-embedding space, giving it a clear advantage over all five baselines for personalized adjective/verb concept inversion.

## Relation to Prior Work

Lego builds directly on Textual Inversion (TI) [16], augmenting its frozen-model, single-embedding framework with two targeted modifications: a Subject Separation step that dedicates an explicit embedding to the exemplar subject, and a contrastive Context Loss that steers multiple concept embeddings toward semantically meaningful regions of the text-embedding space. It departs from the dominant line of appearance/style inversion (TI, DreamBooth, Custom-Diffusion, ELITE) and from relation inversion (ReVersion [24]) by targeting a distinct class of concepts—adjectives and verbs—that are inherently entangled with the subject's appearance, making prior disentanglement strategies (e.g., steering away from subject words) insufficient. The paper positions itself as the first systematic study of this subject-entangled concept inversion problem, filling a gap between appearance-based and relation-based personalization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that even state-of-the-art T2I models (DALL.E 2, Stable Diffusion 2.1) cannot faithfully reproduce the specific characteristics of a personalized concept from a reference image, highlighting a fundamental limitation of text-only conditioning for fine-grained concept control. The simplicity of the proposed solution—just one additional embedding and a contrastive loss—makes it broadly applicable to any text-conditioned diffusion model without architectural changes. Open questions include whether the manual selection of positive/negative words for the Context Loss can be automated, how the method scales to longer multi-concept compositions, and whether the 4-image requirement can be further reduced. The reliance on a less-capable LDM backbone for most quantitative evaluations also leaves room for validation on stronger models.
