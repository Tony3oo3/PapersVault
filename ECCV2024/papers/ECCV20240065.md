## Problem

Diffusion-based generative image editing (inpainting and outpainting) suffers from four persistent issues: inferior visual quality, poor consistency with the surrounding image, weak instruction adherence, and slow inference. While human feedback learning (RLHF) has been successfully applied to text-to-image generation, it has not been explored for the editing setting where the model must simultaneously respect the unmasked input region and follow a text prompt. ByteEdit is the first work to bring multi-reward feedback learning into generative image editing.

## Input / Output

The model receives an RGB input image, a binary region-of-interest mask (one of four types: global, irregular, square, or outward-expansion), and a free-form text prompt describing the desired content. It outputs a full-resolution edited image where the unmasked region is preserved and the masked region is filled with generated content. For outpainting, the prompt is optional (prompt-free expansion is supported); for inpainting-erasing, the prompt specifies the object to remove and the model must reconstruct the background coherently.

## Method

ByteEdit trains three complementary reward models—a BLIP-based aesthetic scorer, a CLIP-based image-text alignment scorer, and a ViT-based pixel-level coherence scorer—and uses them to fine-tune a diffusion U-Net via Perceptual Feedback Learning (PeFL). The key mechanism is a stagewise optimization: heavy-noise steps are skipped by conditioning on the masked input at a fixed intermediate timestep, gradient-free denoising proceeds to a randomly sampled step t′, and the model is then optimized to directly predict the clean image x₀′ using the reward score as a differentiable signal, augmented with L1 and VGG perceptual losses for detail fidelity. For acceleration, the pixel-level coherence reward model is repurposed as a trainable GAN discriminator, enabling adversarial training that allows progressive reduction of inference steps from 20 to 8 without any distillation, while the adversarial signal also extends PeFL supervision to high-noise stages for improved stability.

## Results

On expert-evaluated scores (1–5 scale), ByteEdit achieves the best coherence across all three tasks: 3.54 (Outpainting), 3.73 (Inpainting-Editing), and 3.99 (Inpainting-Erasing), surpassing Adobe (3.52, 3.46, 3.85) and Canva (2.72, 3.42, 2.92). On quantitative alignment metrics, ByteEdit attains CLIPScore 0.329 and BLIPScore 0.691 on EditBench, outperforming EMILIE (0.311, 0.620), BLD (0.280, 0.596), and DiffEdit (0.272, 0.582). In large-scale user preference studies (>12,000 samples per task), ByteEdit's GSB superiority over Adobe reaches 105%, 163%, and 112% on Outpainting, Inpainting-Editing, and Inpainting-Erasing respectively. The paper reports a 388% quality and 135% consistency improvement for Outpainting over its own baseline model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ByteEdit is compared against three industry products (Adobe Firefly, Canva, MeiTu) and three academic inpainting methods (DiffEdit, BLD, EMILIE). Against Adobe, ByteEdit wins on coherence in all three tasks (3.54 vs 3.52, 3.73 vs 3.46, 3.99 vs 3.85) and on structure/aesthetics for Outpainting (3.25 vs 3.07, 3.26 vs 3.14), but loses on Inpainting-Editing structure (3.39 vs 3.60) and Inpainting-Erasing structure (4.03 vs 4.28). Against Canva and MeiTu, ByteEdit dominates across nearly all metrics, with the largest gaps in Outpainting coherence (3.54 vs 2.72/3.01) and Inpainting-Editing coherence (3.73 vs 3.42/2.77). On EditBench quantitative metrics, ByteEdit leads EMILIE by 5.8% CLIPScore (0.329 vs 0.311) and 11.6% BLIPScore (0.691 vs 0.620), and outperforms BLD and DiffEdit by wider margins. In user preference (GSB) studies, ByteEdit is preferred over Adobe in 105–163% of comparisons depending on task, indicating a clear perceptual advantage despite the narrow expert-score gaps on some structure sub-metrics.

## Relation to Prior Work

ByteEdit adapts the reward-model-based feedback learning paradigm from text-to-image generation (ImageReward/ReFL [39]) to the distinct setting of generative image editing, where the model must preserve unmasked regions while generating masked content. It repurposes the coherent reward model as an online discriminator for adversarial acceleration, drawing on the adversarial diffusion distillation line (SDXL Turbo [30], UFOGen [41]) but eliminating the need for a separately trained discriminator. Unlike prior editing methods (Imagen Editor, SmartBrush, RePaint) that each addressed a single challenge—mask extraction, quality, or consistency—ByteEdit unifies aesthetic, alignment, and pixel-level coherence rewards into one training pipeline, positioning itself as the first to bring human feedback learning into the editing domain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By demonstrating that feedback learning can simultaneously improve quality, instruction adherence, and consistency in editing tasks while also enabling step reduction without distillation, ByteEdit offers a practical recipe for production-grade editing systems that outperform major commercial products (Adobe, Canva, MeiTu). The finding that progressive step reduction under reward supervision does not degrade—and in some tasks even improves—output quality challenges the standard assumption that fewer diffusion steps necessarily sacrifice fidelity. Open questions include whether task-specific reward models can push performance further, how the approach integrates with emerging few-step samplers (LCM, SDXL-Turbo), and whether the framework extends to video editing or more complex instruction-based manipulation.
