## Problem

Existing automated shoeprint matching methods rely on handcrafted features or ImageNet-pretrained CNNs trained on small shoeprint datasets, and they match crime-scene prints to clean reference impressions rather than more informative tread depth maps. Additionally, prior methods do not enforce spatial correspondence, so a stripe pattern on a heel could incorrectly match a stripe on a toe. The lack of large-scale training data and the absence of spatially-aware matching limit retrieval accuracy for noisy, partially visible crime-scene shoeprints.

## Input / Output

The method takes a globally aligned crime-scene shoeprint image (noisy, partially visible, from surfaces such as blood, dust, or sand) as a query and retrieves a ranked list of shoe models from a reference database of 56,847 tread depth map instances spanning 24,766 shoe models. The database stores depth maps (384×192 resolution) predicted from online retailer tread images, and evaluation uses top-100 retrievals scored by hit@100 and mAP@100, where a single query may have multiple positive shoe models sharing the same tread pattern.

## Method

CriSp comprises three components: (1) a data augmentation module that simulates crime-scene degradation (occlusion via overlapping prints or random shapes, erasure for grainy texture, and background noise) applied to clean training prints; (2) a modified ResNet50 encoder that removes the final global pooling to produce spatial feature maps of shape [128, H, W], enabling location-aware comparison between print regions and tread regions; and (3) a spatial feature masking module that zeros out encoder features outside the visible region of a partially visible query print, so only visible portions influence retrieval. Training uses supervised contrastive learning with a temperature-scaled cosine similarity loss, and data is sampled in pairs per shoe model to guarantee positive pairs within each batch. The key design choice is matching to tread depth maps rather than RGB tread images or clean prints, as depth removes distracting albedo and lighting information while preserving the geometric tread pattern.

## Results

On val-FID (real crime-scene shoeprints), CriSp achieves hit@100 = 0.5472 and mAP@100 = 0.2071, substantially outperforming the strongest baseline ZSE-SBIR (hit@100 = 0.4528, mAP@100 = 0.1412) by ~9.4 and ~46% respectively. On val-ShoeCase (simulated blood and dust prints), CriSp matches ZSE-SBIR on hit@100 (0.8052 blood, 0.9444 dust) but nearly doubles its mAP@100 (0.4355 vs. 0.1849 for blood; 0.6792 vs. 0.4063 for dust). All prior methods benefit significantly from the paper's data augmentation module, with ZSE-SBIR improving from hit@100 = 0.0943 to 0.4528 on val-FID when augmentation is applied.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CriSp outperforms all five baselines on both validation sets. Against the closest competitor ZSE-SBIR [34], CriSp gains +0.094 hit@100 and +0.066 mAP@100 on val-FID, and nearly doubles mAP@100 on val-ShoeCase (0.4355 vs. 0.1849 blood; 0.6792 vs. 0.4063 dust) while tying on hit@100. The shoeprint-specific baseline MCNCC [29] performs poorly (hit@100 = 0.0849 on val-FID, 0.0000 on val-ShoeCase) because it relies on off-the-shelf ImageNet features and was designed for a much smaller database (1,175 images vs. 56,847). General image-retrieval methods SupCon [28] and FIRe [55] struggle without careful data sampling and augmentation, achieving hit@100 below 0.21 on val-FID. SketchLVM [46], which leverages CLIP, reaches hit@100 = 0.1981 on val-FID but still trails CriSp by a wide margin. The paper notes that ZSE-SBIR requires recomputing all database features per query (non-scalable), whereas CriSp precomputes spatial features once and applies masking at inference time.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of automated shoeprint matching and cross-domain image retrieval (CDIR), but departs from prior shoeprint matching work in a key way: instead of matching crime-scene prints to clean reference impressions (as in MCNCC [29] and earlier handcrafted-feature methods), it formulates the problem as retrieval against tread depth maps predicted from online retailer images. The training framework draws on supervised contrastive learning [28] and the data augmentation strategy is inspired by FG-SBIR literature that bridges domain gaps between query and database modalities. The spatial feature masking module is a novel technical contribution not present in prior shoeprint or general retrieval methods, enabling region-correspondence-aware matching that holistic feature approaches cannot achieve.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first large-scale benchmark (ref-db with 56,847 instances, 24,766 models) and two validation sets for shoeprint-to-tread retrieval, filling a critical gap that has limited deep learning progress in this forensic domain. The finding that depth maps substantially outperform both RGB tread images and clean prints as reference representations (Table 3) challenges the natural assumption that clean impressions are the best matching target. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to unaligned prints (the paper assumes manual alignment), whether transformer architectures can incorporate spatial masking more naturally, and the broader ethical implications of deploying AI-based footwear matching in criminal justice without rigorous fairness guarantees.
