## Problem

SR training datasets have been constructed under the assumption that high-resolution, uncompressed images are essential, yet the relative importance of image resolution versus quality (low compression artifacts) and within-image diversity (number of distinct object regions) has remained unexplored. This paper asks whether low-resolution but high-quality, diverse images from large web-scale datasets can match or exceed the performance of carefully curated high-resolution datasets like DF2K and LSDIR.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240433/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The SR models take ×4 downsampled low-resolution RGB images and reconstruct high-resolution versions, evaluated via PSNR and SSIM on the Y channel in YCbCr space across five standard benchmarks (Set5, Set14, BSD100, Urban100, Manga109). The key distinction is in the training data: DiverSeg variants contain only low-resolution images (average ~178k–233k pixels per image) filtered for quality and diversity, rather than the 1.1M–2.8M pixel high-resolution images used in DF2K or LSDIR.

## Method

The DiverSeg pipeline has two automated steps. First, source selection estimates each candidate dataset's JPEG quality by computing blockiness values (subband DCT coefficient variations) for all images, fitting a kernel density estimate over those values, and comparing it via KL divergence against basis distributions from DF2K re-compressed at known qualities {0.5, 0.75, 0.85, 0.95, 1.0}; datasets with estimated quality below 90% are discarded (eliminating Places365 at 75%). Second, object-based filtering uses SAM (ViT-H) to count segmentation masks per image and retains only those with ≥100 segments, explicitly selecting images with diverse object regions. The resulting DiverSeg-I (259k from ImageNet-1k), DiverSeg-P (267k from PASS), and DiverSeg-IP (527k union) datasets are then used to train five SR architectures (MSRResNet, EDSR, RCAN, SwinIR, HAT) under their original training protocols, demonstrating that low-resolution diverse data outperforms high-resolution curated data across all models and benchmarks.

## Results

The HAT-L model trained on DiverSeg-IP (526k low-resolution images) achieves 33.20/0.9080 PSNR/SSIM on Set5, 29.59/0.8019 on Set14, 28.11/0.7556 on BSD100, 28.81/0.8547 on Urban100, and 33.19/0.9342 on Manga109, outperforming the same model trained with the standard ImageNet→DF2K pre-training/fine-tuning pipeline on four of five benchmarks. Notably, DiverSeg-I filters out 77.5% of ImageNet-1k images, meaning the DiverSeg-trained model uses far fewer training images while still surpassing the full ImageNet→DF2K approach. Across all five SR architectures tested (MSRResNet, RCAN, EDSR, SwinIR, HAT), models trained on DiverSeg-I consistently match or exceed those trained on high-resolution DF2K and LSDIR datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The strongest comparison is HAT-L trained on DiverSeg-IP versus HAT-L trained with the standard ImageNet→DF2K pre-training/fine-tuning protocol: DiverSeg-IP wins on Set14 (29.59 vs 29.47 PSNR), BSD100 (28.11 vs 28.09), Urban100 (28.81 vs 28.60), and Manga109 (33.19 vs 33.09), while losing marginally on Set5 (33.20 vs 33.30). HAT trained on DiverSeg-I similarly outperforms HAT with ImageNet→DF2K on four of five datasets (e.g., Urban100: 28.51 vs 28.37 PSNR; Manga109: 32.90 vs 32.87). Against earlier CNN-based SOTA methods (SAN, HAN, NLSN, RRDB, RCAN-IT), the DiverSeg-trained HAT models lead by 0.4–0.5 dB PSNR on Urban100 and 0.4–0.5 dB on Manga109. SwinIR trained on DiverSeg-I surpasses SwinIR trained on DF2K on all five benchmarks (e.g., Set14: 29.23 vs 29.09 PSNR; Urban100: 27.83 vs 27.45). The key efficiency advantage is that DiverSeg-I uses only 260k images (22.5% of ImageNet-1k) without any pre-training stage, yet matches or exceeds the full ImageNet→DF2K pipeline that requires training on all 1.28M ImageNet images first.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the established SR dataset construction paradigm (DIV2K, DF2K, LSDIR) that mandates high-resolution, uncompressed images, by repurposing large-scale image recognition datasets (ImageNet-1k, PASS) through an automated quality-and-diversity filtering pipeline. The quality estimation step builds directly on the JPEG blockiness measure from Bhardwaj & Pankajakshan [4], applying it via kernel density estimation and KL divergence to estimate dataset-level compression quality without ground-truth labels. The diversity filtering repurposes SAM and Detic—models designed for segmentation and open-vocabulary detection—into a dataset curation tool, a use case not explored in prior SR dataset literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By demonstrating that low-resolution but high-quality, object-diverse images outperform high-resolution datasets across five architectures and five benchmarks, this work challenges a foundational assumption in SR research and offers a practical, fully automated pipeline that eliminates the costly manual curation of uncompressed HR images. The finding that low-quality images actively degrade training (PSNR decreases mid-training) rather than merely failing to help is a useful diagnostic for practitioners. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to other degradation types (motion blur, noise), whether hybrid low- and high-resolution training could push performance further, and whether the object-diversity benefit persists under blind SR settings where the degradation is unknown.
