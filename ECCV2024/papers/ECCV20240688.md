## Problem

Existing autonomous driving VLM benchmarks reduce complex driving decisions to simple QA with boolean or multiple-choice answers, lacking the multi-step perception–prediction–reasoning chains that reflect real decision-making. Additionally, standard captioning metrics (BLEU, CIDEr) cannot distinguish whether a model's intermediate reasoning steps actually support its final conclusion, creating reasoning ambiguities in evaluation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240688/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes 5 cropped video frames at 224×224 pixels, a natural-language query, and structured perceptual priors (object bounding boxes, categories, motion states) that are tokenized into region-level features via RoIAlign and positional embeddings rather than fed as raw text. Outputs are structured reasoning chains that interleave textual explanations with special placeholder tokens (<LOC> for bounding boxes, <MOT> for trajectory coordinate sequences), which are decoded into precise geometric predictions by a dedicated vision decoder head.

## Method

The framework augments an InstructBLIP-based VLM (BLIP-2 vision encoder + Q-former + LLM) with two new modules: a prior tokenizer (two-layer MLP) that independently encodes region-level image features and positional/motion embeddings from perceptual priors, and an instructed vision decoder (transformer decoder + task-specific heads) that decodes object locations and trajectories from special tokens in the LLM's output sequence. Training is two-stage: pre-training aligns the prior tokenizer and Q-former with frozen LLM and vision encoder, then fine-tuning uses LoRA on the LLM while fully training the vision decoder, optimizing a combined autoregressive text loss and detection-style perception loss (BCE + MSE). The accompanying Reason2Drive dataset (632K video-text pairs from nuScenes, Waymo, and ONCE) is auto-constructed via an object-centric database and GPT-4-augmented templates spanning perception, prediction, and reasoning at both object and scenario levels, and is evaluated with the proposed ADRScore metric that aggregates reasoning alignment, redundancy, and missing-step scores.

## Results

On the Reason2Drive validation set, the proposed method (with Vicuna-7B) achieves an ADRScore of 0.463 and ADRScore-S of 0.432, substantially outperforming the strongest baseline (InstructBLIP + Vicuna-7B) at 0.351 and 0.214 respectively. The gap is most pronounced on ADRScore-S (a 101% relative improvement), confirming that traditional captioning metrics (B@4, METEOR, ROUGE, CIDEr) understate the reasoning gap between models. On downstream control signal prediction (nuScenes), pre-training on Reason2Drive reduces speed RMSE from 3.743 to 2.842 and steering RMSE from 5.926 to 4.866.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method (prior tokenizer + instructed vision decoder) wins across all metrics against every baseline. Compared to InstructBLIP + Vicuna-7B (the strongest baseline), it improves ADRScore by 0.112 (0.463 vs 0.351) and ADRScore-S by 0.218 (0.432 vs 0.214), with the latter gap being more than 2× larger than the former, demonstrating that traditional captioning metrics mask the true reasoning disparity. Against BLIP-2 variants, the improvement in ADRScore-S is even more dramatic (0.432 vs 0.162–0.171). For perceptual prediction quality (Table 5), the method achieves 0.806 bounding box accuracy and 1.875 trajectory ADE, surpassing MiniGPT-4 (0.723, 2.334) and Kosmos-2 (0.745, 2.563). On downstream planning (Table 8), pre-training on Reason2Drive reduces speed RMSE by 17% and steering RMSE by 18% relative to direct fine-tuning of InstructBLIP, confirming the dataset's transferability to control signal generation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the InstructBLIP/BLIP-2 architecture (frozen vision encoder, Q-former alignment, LLM decoder) and extends it with two purpose-built modules—a prior tokenizer for ingesting object-level perceptual priors and an instructed vision decoder for generating spatial predictions—addressing what the authors identify as a fundamental weakness of standard VLM pipelines in driving contexts. Conceptually, it departs from prior driving VLM datasets (Talk2Car, NuScenes-QA, DriveLM, DRAMA) that reduce driving to single-step QA with boolean or multiple-choice answers, instead framing the task as a structured perception→prediction→reasoning chain. The ADRScore evaluation metric also breaks from the NLP community's reliance on holistic captioning metrics (BLEU, CIDEr) by explicitly measuring step-wise reasoning alignment, redundancy, and missing steps.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As the largest language-based driving dataset to date (632K+ pairs across three source datasets), Reason2Drive provides a much-needed resource for studying interpretable, multi-step reasoning in autonomous driving, and the ADRScore protocol offers a more discriminative evaluation that traditional metrics demonstrably fail to provide. The finding that most existing VLMs struggle to leverage perceptual priors and produce accurate spatial predictions highlights a concrete gap between current VLM capabilities and the requirements of reliable driving systems. Open questions include whether the chain-based reasoning formulation generalizes to unseen driving scenarios beyond the three source datasets, whether GPT-4-augmented annotations introduce systematic biases, and whether the architectural additions (prior tokenizer, instructed vision decoder) transfer effectively to larger or differently-structured VLMs.
