## Problem

Model performance evaluation in computer vision is expensive due to annotation costs, yet practitioners default to simple random sampling with naive averaging, which is statistically suboptimal. No comprehensive study has compared sampling and estimation strategies on CV data, and there is no trusted 'backpocket method' that practitioners can apply out-of-the-box to minimize the number of annotated test samples needed for precise accuracy estimation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20242350/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a test dataset of N instances with model predictions (e.g., CLIP ViT-B/32 confidence scores), an annotation budget n ≪ N, and optionally a surrogate model's predictions (e.g., CLIP ViT-L/14) as a proxy for model performance, which can be calibrated via isotonic regression on a held-out subset. The output is a stratified sample of n instances to annotate and a design-unbiased estimate of model accuracy (or other performance metric) with a known variance, achieving up to 10× precision gains over naive random sampling on datasets like CIFAR-10.

## Method

The framework co-designs three components: stratification via k-means clustering on a proxy Ẑ of model performance (e.g., predicted classification error), stratified simple random sampling with proportional or Neyman allocation, and estimation via either the Horvitz-Thompson (design-based) or difference (model-assisted) estimator. A key theoretical result (Proposition 2, Corollary 1) shows that minimizing the MSE of the HT estimator under proportional stratification is equivalent to solving a k-means criterion on E_P[Z|X], justifying the use of k-means on the proxy rather than on raw feature embeddings. The difference estimator leverages model predictions on the unlabeled portion of the dataset to correct bias, and Proposition 3 proves it always achieves lower MSE than HT under SRS when the proxy is well-specified. Empirically, stratified sampling with proportional allocation on k-means clusters of the proxy consistently outperforms simple random sampling, with up to 10× efficiency gains on CIFAR-10, while the DF estimator provides additional gains when data has already been collected via SRS.

## Results

The paper evaluates the efficiency (MSE) of different sampling designs and estimators for estimating zero-shot classification accuracy of CLIP ViT-B/32 across the LAION CLIP-Benchmark. Stratified sampling (SSRS) with proportional allocation and k-means on model performance predictions achieves up to ~10× efficiency gains over simple random sampling (SRS) with the Horvitz-Thompson (HT) estimator on CIFAR-10, ~5× on Stanford Cars, and no gains on Dmlab Frames. The model-assisted difference estimator (DF) with a calibrated proxy from a stronger surrogate model (CLIP ViT-L/14) consistently achieves equal or lower MSE than HT under SRS, with the largest gains when the model's accuracy is high. Neyman allocation provides additional gains over proportional allocation only when stratum variances are well-estimated, and can underperform SRS when plug-in variance estimates are inaccurate.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is naive averaging (HT estimator) under simple random sampling (SRS), which is the standard practice in CV. SSRS with proportional allocation and k-means stratification on the proxy Ẑ consistently achieves lower MSE than SRS+HT across all tasks, with relative efficiency gains ranging from ~10× (CIFAR-10, some LAION tasks) to no gain (Dmlab Frames). The DF estimator under SRS with a calibrated proxy from a stronger surrogate model (CLIP ViT-L/14) always matches or beats HT under SRS, whereas uncalibrated DF can perform substantially worse than HT on some tasks. SSRS with Neyman allocation can further reduce MSE beyond proportional allocation (e.g., on Stanford Cars), but when stratum standard deviations are poorly estimated (e.g., Pascal VOC 2007), Neyman allocation allocates too little budget to high-variance strata and can underperform even SRS. Under SSRS with fine-grained strata, HT and DF yield virtually identical precision, so the choice of estimator matters less than the stratification quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper systematically applies classical survey sampling techniques—Horvitz-Thompson estimation, model-assisted difference estimation, stratified random sampling with proportional and Neyman allocation—to the problem of CV model evaluation, a domain where practitioners have defaulted to simple random sampling and naive averaging. The key theoretical contribution is showing that optimal stratification for the HT estimator under proportional allocation is equivalent to a k-means clustering criterion on predicted model performance (Proposition 2, Corollary 1), derived from decompositions of proper scoring rules [58]. While individual components (HT, DF, Neyman allocation) are well-established in the statistics literature, no prior work had provided a unified, CV-specific framework connecting stratification design, sampling allocation, and estimator choice, nor had it empirically validated these methods across a broad palette of CV benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a 10x reduction in annotation cost on CIFAR-10 (and ~5x on Stanford Cars) means practitioners can achieve the same estimation precision with far fewer labeled samples, directly addressing the cost bottleneck in building test sets. However, the paper honestly notes that gains vary substantially across datasets (no savings on DMLab Frames) and that Neyman allocation can backfire when the plug-in variance estimator is inaccurate. Open questions include how these methods behave under distribution shift (where the proxy may be poorly calibrated), whether sequential sampling can further improve efficiency, and whether the framework extends cleanly to multi-metric or multi-domain evaluation settings common in production CV systems.
