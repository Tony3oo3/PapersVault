## Problem

High-resolution monocular metric depth estimation in the real domain faces a fundamental dilemma: training on synthetic data yields sharp boundary details but poor scale accuracy due to the domain gap, while training on real data provides correct scale but blurred boundaries because real depth annotations (LiDAR, stereo, Kinect) are sparse or missing near object edges. Additionally, existing tile-based frameworks like PatchFusion rely on a multi-stage training pipeline and a fusion mechanism that limits end-to-end optimization and generalization.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single high-resolution RGB image (up to 4K at 2160×3840) and produces a pixel-wise metric depth map at the same resolution. Processing is tile-based: the image is first downsampled for a global coarse depth prediction, then split into overlapping patches (e.g., 540×960 for 4K, 256×512 for CityScapes) that are individually refined. The final depth is computed as the sum of the coarse prediction (cropped to the patch region) and a learned residual depth map, with consistency-aware inference to handle patch overlaps.

## Method

PatchRefiner replaces the fusion-based high-resolution pipeline with a residual refinement architecture: a frozen coarse depth network (e.g., ZoeDepth) provides a global scale-aware prediction, and a refiner network—containing a base depth model of the same architecture plus a lightweight multi-scale decoder—predicts a residual depth map by aggregating L-level intermediate features from both networks via concatenation and convolutional blocks. For real-domain training, a teacher-student strategy is employed: a teacher pretrained on synthetic data generates pseudo labels for real images, and the student is trained with a Detail and Scale Disentangling (DSD) loss that combines L_silog on real ground truth (preserving scale accuracy) with a ranking loss and a scale-shift invariant loss on the pseudo labels (injecting boundary detail without propagating scale errors). The ranking loss provides ordinal supervision on sampled point pairs using a tolerance threshold, while the scale-shift invariant loss aligns predictions to pseudo labels via a closed-form least-squares scale and shift, making both losses insensitive to the absolute metric scale of the synthetic-trained teacher.

## Results

On UnrealStereo4K, PatchRefiner (ZoeDepth+PR, P=16) achieves 98.82% δ₁, 0.033 REL, 0.892 RMS, 5.417 SiLog, and 0.750 SEE, reducing RMSE by 18.1% and REL by 15.7% over the best PatchFusion variant (ZoeDepth+PF, P=16: RMS 1.088, REL 0.040). On the real-domain CityScapes dataset, the DSD loss strategy yields a 19.2% increase in boundary recall (48.78 vs. 40.92 for fine-tuning) while maintaining scale accuracy (δ₁ = 95.36% vs. 95.42% for fine-tuning). The method also achieves the lowest SEE (0.750) among all compared approaches on UnrealStereo4K, confirming superior boundary delineation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PatchRefiner outperforms all seven baselines on every reported metric. Against the direct tile-based predecessor PatchFusion [27], the residual refinement formulation yields an 18.1% RMSE reduction (0.892 vs. 1.088) and 15.7% REL reduction (0.033 vs. 0.040) with the ZoeDepth backbone, and similar gains with Depth-Anything (0.860 vs. 0.984 RMS). Compared to the single-pass ZoeDepth [2] base model, PatchRefiner improves δ₁ by over 1.1 percentage points and reduces RMS by 31%. Against Depth-Anything [56] (the strongest recent monocular depth model), PatchRefiner with the Depth-Anything backbone achieves 98.878% δ₁ vs. 97.773% and 0.860 vs. 1.235 RMS. Older super-resolution and fusion methods (iDisc [38], SMD-Net [51], Graph-GDSR [10], BoostingDepth [34]) are all surpassed by a wide margin, with RMS gaps of 0.37–0.51. On the real-domain CityScapes evaluation, the DSD loss strategy dominates both fine-tuning (19.2% higher boundary recall) and mixed-data training (6.3% higher δ₁, 16% lower RMS) while preserving scale accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

PatchRefiner directly extends the tile-based high-resolution depth estimation paradigm established by PatchFusion (Li et al., CVPR 2024), replacing its three-step coarse-fine-fusion pipeline with a simpler two-step residual refinement process where a frozen coarse model provides scale-consistent baselines and a refiner network predicts residual depth. The pseudo-labeling strategy draws on teacher-student frameworks from semi-supervised depth estimation, while the proposed Detail and Scale Disentangling (DSD) loss repurposes the ranking loss from structure-guided depth prediction (Xian et al., CVPR 2020) and the scale-shift invariant loss from robust monocular depth estimation (Ranftl et al., TPAMI 2022) into a novel dual-supervision mechanism for transferring detail from synthetic pseudo labels without corrupting real-domain scale. This positions the work at the intersection of high-resolution tile-based methods, synthetic-to-real domain adaptation, and relative depth estimation techniques.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a fundamental and persistent dilemma in high-resolution depth estimation: synthetic data provides sharp boundary details but incorrect metric scale, while real data provides correct scale but lacks boundary ground truth due to sensor limitations. The DSD loss offers a practical, principled solution to this tradeoff, and the 19.2% boundary recall improvement on CityScapes while maintaining scale accuracy demonstrates its effectiveness across diverse sensor modalities (stereo, LiDAR, reconstruction). However, the framework still depends on a frozen coarse model for scale anchoring, and the teacher-student pipeline adds training complexity; whether the DSD loss generalizes to other pseudo-labeling scenarios beyond depth estimation remains an open question. The finding that naive mixed training (simply combining synthetic and real data) actually degrades scale accuracy substantially is a useful caution for practitioners attempting synthetic data augmentation.
