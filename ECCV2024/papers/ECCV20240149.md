## Problem

Existing exposure correction methods rely on labor-intensive paired annotations from expert photographers, which introduces individual stylistic biases and limits dataset scale, resulting in poor generalizability. Additionally, these methods prioritize aesthetic quality over low-level feature preservation, causing degradation in downstream tasks like edge detection and segmentation. This paper asks whether exposure correction can be learned without any manual ground truth while simultaneously preserving pixel-level details.

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image and a single reference image (used to calibrate the target exposure level) as input, producing a pixel-wise exposure-corrected RGB image at the same resolution. During training, multi-exposure sequences are freely generated from RAW data via an emulated ISP pipeline, with images in the same sequence serving as mutual ground truth—no manual annotation is required. The correction is implemented as a lightweight pixel-wise transformation in sRGB space (19,388 parameters total), making it resolution-independent and capable of real-time 4K processing.

## Method

UEC decomposes exposure correction into three modules: an Exposure Feature Encoder (two conv layers + global pooling producing a 96D vector), a Parameter Predictor that computes a scalar exposure difference ΔE in latent space between input and reference, and an Exposure Corrector that applies the transformation I_out = λ·I_in + (1−λ)·h(I_in), where h is a 1×1 convolution iterated three times. Training is fully unsupervised via two principles: Restoration Supervision, where images from the same multi-exposure sequence act as mutual ground truth (L2 loss), and the Monopoly Principle, where two references of different EVs from a different scene enforce relative brightness ordering in the outputs (ReLU loss). A total variation loss preserves spatial coherence. The key insight is that exposure variation is a one-dimensional quantity capturable by a single scalar in latent space, enabling a minimal pixel-wise correction that avoids the detail-destroying artifacts of heavier image-to-image translation approaches.

## Results

On the standard MSEC benchmark, UEC achieves PSNR 18.756 and SSIM 0.812, trailing the supervised SOTA ECM (PSNR 20.874, SSIM 0.877) but surpassing all other baselines including Afifi et al. (PSNR 19.483, SSIM 0.739). On the authors' Radiometry Correction Dataset, UEC outperforms ECM on both metrics (PSNR 20.548 vs. 20.445; SSIM 0.868 vs. 0.744). For downstream edge detection, UEC achieves PSNR 22.665 and F1 0.969 versus ECM's PSNR 16.312 and F1 0.922, demonstrating substantially better low-level feature preservation. On the generalizability test (LOL dataset), UEC pretrained on MSEC (PSNR 18.571) significantly outperforms ECM pretrained on MSEC (PSNR 15.439).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the supervised SOTA ECM [12], UEC loses on the MSEC benchmark (PSNR 18.756 vs. 20.874; SSIM 0.812 vs. 0.877) but wins decisively on the Radiometry Correction Dataset (PSNR 20.548 vs. 20.445; SSIM 0.868 vs. 0.744) and on edge detection (PSNR 22.665 vs. 16.312; F1 0.969 vs. 0.922). On the generalizability test using the LOL dataset, UEC pretrained on MSEC (PSNR 18.571, SSIM 0.728) substantially outperforms ECM pretrained on MSEC (PSNR 15.439, SSIM 0.650), indicating that the unsupervised radiometry-only training transfers better to unseen scenes. Compared to Afifi et al. [2], UEC achieves lower PSNR (18.756 vs. 19.483) but higher SSIM (0.812 vs. 0.739) on MSEC, and on LOL UEC (18.571) far exceeds Afifi et al. (14.268). All other baselines (HDRCNN, DPED, DPE, Zero-DCE) are clearly outperformed by UEC on both PSNR and SSIM. Notably, UEC achieves these results with only 19,388 parameters (0.079 MB) versus ECM's 182,443,267 parameters (695 MB), running 4.85× faster on GPU and 23.41× faster on CPU.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes Afifi et al.'s [2] emulated ISP pipeline for generating multi-exposure sequences from RAW data, but fundamentally reinterprets the learning paradigm: instead of supervised mapping from a poorly exposed image to a manually corrected ground truth, images within the same sequence mutually serve as each other's supervision signal. Conceptually, it borrows the style-transfer framing (style encoder + transformation function) but replaces the one-hot or triplet-loss priors with a scalar exposure difference computed in latent space, guided by a novel 'Monopoly Principle' that enforces relative brightness ordering across scenes. The method family aligns with lightweight color-transformation approaches (curve-based, LUT-based) rather than heavy image-to-image translation networks, and the explicit separation of radiometry from colorimetry departs from prior work that conflates exposure correction with stylistic retouching.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 0.01% parameter count (19K vs. 182M) and real-time 4K capability make exposure correction viable on embedded/mobile hardware where prior SOTA models are impractical, and the radiometry-only focus reduces stylistic bias that limits generalizability across datasets. The finding that supervised ECM actually degrades edge-detection performance below unmodified inputs is a useful caution for practitioners applying enhancement pipelines before downstream CV tasks. Open questions include whether the approach extends beyond radiometry to more complex degradations (noise, blur), whether the 'unsupervised' framing holds when paired data from an ISP pipeline is still required, and whether the Monopoly Principle generalizes to non-monotonic exposure relationships in HDR or multi-sensor capture.
