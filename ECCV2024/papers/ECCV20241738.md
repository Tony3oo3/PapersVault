## Problem

Prior visual relationship detection methods operate exclusively on visible view images, leaving unexplored the question of how inter-object relationships transform when the scene is observed from an unseen viewpoint. This transformation is view-dependent and non-deterministic—relationships may be preserved, altered, or become entirely invisible—making it impossible to simply apply a standard VRD model to a synthesized novel view without losing structural correspondence.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241738/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single observed source-view RGB image and a known cross-view transformation matrix T_st (encoding rotation and translation between source and target cameras). It outputs two quantities per object pair: a continuous relationship representation in the target view and a binary presentness mask indicating whether that relationship is visible (as opposed to merely absent). During training, a pre-trained depth estimator provides per-pixel depth to project source bounding boxes into the target view for correspondence matching, but depth is not required at inference.

## Method

The core architecture is an equivariant graph neural network built on Vector Neurons, where detected objects form nodes and inter-object relationships form edges in a scene graph. An Equivariant Message Passing (EMP) module propagates information across nodes and edges while enforcing the constraint φ(T_g(x)) = T_g(φ(x)), so that relationship representations transform correctly under the cross-view transformation T_st. The predicted target-view relationships are obtained by applying T_st to the source relationship features through an MLP, and a separate sigmoid head predicts presentness from the final node and edge features. Training uses three losses: an L2 relationship transformation loss against features extracted by a pre-trained Graph-RCNN on the target view (mitigating the lack of multi-view annotations), an L2 presentness loss, and a KL-divergence structure loss that preserves the local neighbor topology of each edge in the predicted scene graph.

## Results

On the hand-labeled Realestate10K test set, VRT (using only the original view) achieves R@50 of 30.3 and R@100 of 34.7 for scene graph classification, and R@50 of 33.6 and R@100 of 37.8 for relationship prediction, nearly matching Graph-RCNN with direct access to the target view (31.0/34.9 and 34.0/38.2). On VidVRD, VRT outperforms VRDFormer (which uses the target view) by 2.76 points in R@50 (21.35 vs. 18.59). As a plug-in module, VRT improves Synsin NVS by +0.47 dB PSNR on Realestate10K, PixelNeRF by +0.95 dB PSNR (3-view) on DTU, pedestrian intention accuracy by +10.5% on PIE, and visual grounding accuracy by +9.9% on Nr3D.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VRT nearly matches Graph-RCNN [86] on Realestate10K (30.3 vs. 31.0 R@50 SGCls) despite not having access to the target view, a 0.7-point gap. VRT substantially outperforms all Synsin+VRD pipelines (Synsin+GRCNN, Synsin+TDE, Synsin+BGNN) by 6–7 points in R@50, demonstrating that synthesizing a view first and then detecting relationships is far less effective than directly predicting transformed relationships. On VidVRD, VRT beats VRDFormer [97] (which uses the target view) by 2.76 R@50 points, confirming generalizability. As a plug-in, VRT improves Synsin [83] by +0.47 dB PSNR and +0.070 SSIM on Realestate10K, PixelNeRF [90] by +0.95 dB PSNR (3-view) on DTU, PIE [61] by +10.5% intention accuracy and +6.99% F1, and ReferIt3D [2] by +9.9% overall grounding accuracy (with the largest gain of +14.2% on view-dependent queries), showing consistent benefits across all downstream tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the standard visual relationship detection (VRD) paradigm, which operates on visible view images, by formulating the problem of predicting relationships in unseen novel views from a single observed source view. Architecturally, it builds on Vector Neurons [16] to construct an equivariant graph neural network that enforces transformation equivariance on relationship representations during message passing, a design not previously applied to edge-level relationship prediction in scene graphs. Conceptually, it contrasts with the vanilla NVS-then-VRD pipeline by directly predicting transformed relationships and their presentness, avoiding the quality bottleneck of synthesized views. The training strategy borrows from knowledge distillation (using a pre-trained Graph-RCNN as a teacher on target views) and from graph structure preservation losses [87].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

VRT introduces a principled way to reason about how spatial relationships change across viewpoints without requiring expensive multi-view annotations, which is relevant for robotics, AR/VR, and autonomous driving where multi-view understanding is needed but acquisition is costly. The plug-in demonstration across NVS, visual grounding, and pedestrian intention estimation suggests VRT features are broadly useful, though the improvements are moderate (e.g., +0.47 dB PSNR on Synsin, +10.5% Acc on PIE). Key limitations include reliance on a pre-trained VRD model for supervision, 2D bounding-box-based scene graphs, and evaluation on relatively small hand-labeled test sets (500 pairs). Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to 3D relationships, larger scene graphs, and whether the equivariant inductive bias provides meaningful advantages over simpler transformation-conditioned architectures at scale.
