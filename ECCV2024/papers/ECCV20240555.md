## Problem

Existing novel view synthesis methods operate at timescales where the finite speed of light is negligible, while transient videography systems are limited to single-viewpoint capture. No multi-viewpoint transient video dataset existed, and no rendering framework could synthesize videos of propagating light from arbitrary, moving camera viewpoints where propagation delays and relativistic effects become significant.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240555/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes multi-viewpoint transient videos captured by a scanning single-pixel SPAD (512×512 spatial resolution, 4096 time bins of 4 ps each for captured data; 300–900 bins for simulated data) along with calibrated camera intrinsics and extrinsics. It produces a continuous transient video from any novel static or dynamic viewpoint, where each pixel is a high-dimensional time-varying radiance signal. The output also supports peak-time visualizations (hue = peak time, brightness = peak intensity, with isochrone bands) and separate direct and global illumination components.

## Method

The core contribution is a transient field: a neural mapping from a 3D point and 2D ray direction to an N-dimensional discrete-time signal representing time-varying radiance, paired with a conventional density field. A modified volume rendering equation composites samples along each ray, applying a Kronecker-delta time shift (implemented via linear interpolation) that accounts for the speed-of-light propagation delay from each scene point to the camera center of projection—without this shift, the mapping from scene points to transients is ambiguous across viewpoints. The representation is parameterized with Instant-NGP multiresolution hash encoding and trained with a gamma-compressed L2 loss against Poisson-distributed SPAD photon counts. After training, dynamic viewpoint rendering is achieved by defining time-varying camera extrinsics per time bin, and the framework extends to relativistic effects (Lorentz contraction, aberration, searchlight) and direct-global separation via Gaussian mixture decomposition of the transients.

## Results

On the simulated dataset, the proposed method achieves a PSNR of 32.97 dB, LPIPS of 0.247, SSIM of 0.965, and transient IoU (T-IOU) of 0.830, substantially outperforming T-NeRF (PSNR 26.35, T-IOU 0.729) and K-Planes (PSNR 20.55, T-IOU 0.358). On the captured dataset, the proposed method reaches PSNR 24.95 dB and T-IOU 0.468, surpassing K-Planes (PSNR 24.12, T-IOU 0.395) while rendering a transient video in 28 s versus K-Planes' 37 minutes. An ablation removing the propagation-delay term drops T-IOU from 0.830 to 0.334 (simulated) and from 0.468 to 0.174 (captured), confirming that explicit speed-of-light delay modeling is critical for accurate multi-view transient synthesis.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: TransientNeRF (T-NeRF) [37] and K-Planes [12]. On simulated data, the proposed method outperforms T-NeRF by 6.6 dB in PSNR and 0.10 in T-IOU, and outperforms K-Planes by 12.4 dB in PSNR and 0.47 in T-IOU; T-NeRF cannot model indirect light transport and K-Planes does not account for viewpoint-dependent propagation delays. On captured data, the proposed method edges out K-Planes by 0.83 dB in PSNR and 0.07 in T-IOU while being roughly 80× faster at inference (28 s vs. 37 min). T-NeRF is excluded from the captured-data evaluation because it requires a known coaxial light-source geometry that the hardware setup does not provide. The ablation without propagation-delay modeling shows that removing the Kronecker-delta time shift causes a dramatic T-IOU collapse (0.830→0.334 simulated; 0.468→0.174 captured), confirming that the speed-of-light delay term is the key differentiator over both baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the NeRF/Instant-NGP volumetric rendering framework to the ultrafast timescale by introducing a 'transient field'—a mapping from a 3D point and ray direction to a high-dimensional discrete-time signal—combined with an explicit propagation-delay term in the volume rendering equation. It builds directly on Velten et al.'s femto-photography time-warping procedure and peak-time visualization, generalizing them from single-viewpoint to multi-viewpoint volume rendering. The closest prior method, TransientNeRF, handles only the direct (lidar) component from a coaxial sensor-source setup; this paper departs by modeling non-coaxial illumination, global light transport (scattering, refraction, diffraction), and dynamic camera trajectories, while also introducing the first real multi-viewpoint transient video dataset.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The first captured multi-viewpoint transient video dataset and the accompanying rendering framework open a previously inaccessible modality for 3D scene understanding at picosecond timescales, with applications in education, art, biomedical imaging, and material property recovery. A key limitation is that long SPAD acquisition times restrict the method to static scenes; the authors note that emerging SPAD arrays could enable dynamic capture. Open questions include whether the learned transient fields can be inverted to recover per-material properties (reflectance, scattering albedo) without additional supervision, and whether the approach scales to larger or more complex scenes beyond the small tabletop setups demonstrated here.
