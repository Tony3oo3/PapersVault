## Problem

Previous privacy-preserving cameras (PPCs) rely on a static point spread function (PSF) optimized to blur facial identity while preserving task-relevant information. However, an attacker with physical access to the camera can recover this static PSF by simply photographing a point light source, after which powerful deconvolution algorithms (e.g., total variation denoising) can reconstruct the original scene and defeat the privacy guarantee. This paper addresses the gap by designing a camera whose PSF changes dynamically from image to image, making PSF inversion attacks infeasible.

## Input / Output

The camera captures a real-world scene through a conventional imaging lens (f/1.8, 640×640 sensor, 1 μm pixels) with a spatial light modulator (SLM) in the pupil plane that imposes a programmable phase. Before each exposure, a random latent code ε is drawn uniformly from [-1,1]^350 and mapped through a learned embedding to a 350-dimensional Zernike coefficient vector, which determines the PSF for that single image. The output is a blurred 640×640 RGB measurement that hides subject identity (face recognition accuracy < 10%) while retaining sufficient structure for downstream tasks such as object detection, pose estimation, and crowd counting.

## Method

The DyPP camera learns a nonlinear embedding g (a 4-layer MLP, 512 nodes/layer) from a uniform latent space to the Zernike-coefficient space, constrained to a 'privacy manifold' defined by simultaneous bounds on face-recognition accuracy (privacy) and task performance (utility). The differentiable camera model computes the PSF via Fourier optics: the pupil phase is a linear combination of 350 Zernike polynomials, the complex pupil transmittance is exponentiated, and the PSF is the squared magnitude of its 2D Fourier transform evaluated at three wavelengths (460, 550, 640 nm). Training jointly optimizes four losses: a noninvertibility loss that maximizes the MMSE reconstruction error in the Fourier domain (encouraging smooth, low-frequency PSFs), a diversity loss that maximizes the log-determinant of the sample covariance of Zernike vectors (ensuring PSF-to-PSF variability), a privacy loss that penalizes face-recognition accuracy above a threshold p via a sigmoid-approximated 0/1 margin on an ArcFace recognizer, and a utility loss that penalizes task performance below a threshold τ. The key mechanism is that because a different PSF h_t is sampled before each exposure, an attacker who measures the PSF with a point light source obtains h_t but the image was formed with h_t′, so deconvolution with the wrong PSF fails to recover private details.

## Results

On privacy, DyPP achieves 9% PubFig face recognition accuracy (matching PP-HPE at 9% and beating Defocus Lens at 14%), with comparable LFW AUROC (0.72) and AgeDB-30 AUROC (0.63). On utility, DyPP outperforms PP-HPE on object detection (AP 36.0 vs 33.9) and crowd counting (ShanghaiTech B MAE 19.3 vs 21.3), while slightly underperforming on pose estimation (AP 42.2 vs 42.8). The key robustness result: under white-box PSF inversion (TVD), all static-PSF cameras see PSNR jump from ~15 to ~20–22 and PubFig accuracy from 9–14% to 54–72%; under the only practical black-box attack on DyPP, PSNR remains at 17.3 and PubFig accuracy stays at 14%, demonstrating that the dynamic PSF effectively neutralizes inversion attacks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Defocus Lens [51], DyPP wins on every utility metric: object detection AP 36.0 vs 24.9, pose estimation AP 42.2 vs 30.8, and crowd counting MAE 19.3 vs 25.3 (ShanghaiTech B), while also matching or exceeding its privacy (PubFig 9% vs 14%). Against PP-HPE Lens [29], DyPP is essentially tied on privacy (PubFig 9% vs 9%, LFW 0.72 vs 0.73) and pose estimation (AP 42.2 vs 42.8), but clearly wins on object detection (AP 36.0 vs 33.9) and crowd counting (MAE 19.3 vs 21.3, Beijing BRT MAE 2.35 vs 2.93). Against the low-resolution baselines [57,58], DyPP dominates on utility (e.g., object detection AP 36.0 vs 8.2) while achieving comparable privacy (PubFig 9% vs 7%). The critical differentiator is attack robustness: under white-box PSF inversion, Defocus and PP-HPE see PubFig accuracy surge to 72% and 65% respectively, whereas DyPP under the only feasible black-box attack retains 14% accuracy—a 40+ point gap that no static-PSF camera can match.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the differentiable deep-optics framework for privacy-preserving cameras (PPCs) established by Hinojosa et al. (ICCV 2021) and the Zernike-parameterized SLM pupil model from Sitzmann et al. (ACM TOG 2018). The key departure is identifying that all prior PPCs use a static PSF, which is trivially recoverable via a point-light-source measurement (PSF inversion attack), and proposing a dynamic PSF sampled from a learned privacy manifold to defeat this attack. The manifold embedding is trained adversarially with four coupled losses (noninvertibility, diversity, privacy, utility), going beyond the single-objective PSF optimization of prior PPC designs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper exposes a fundamental vulnerability in every existing static-PSF PPC: a motivated attacker with physical camera access can fully break the privacy guarantee with a single measurement, a threat model prior work had not considered. The dynamic-PSF solution is validated both in simulation across three utility tasks and with a physical LCOS-SLM prototype, making it one of the first hardware-feasible designs robust to this attack class. Open questions include the cost and speed of SLM-based dynamic optics for real-time deployment, whether the learned manifold remains diverse enough under distribution shift, and whether the black-box attack (trying 64 PSFs) scales to larger PSF sets in practice.
