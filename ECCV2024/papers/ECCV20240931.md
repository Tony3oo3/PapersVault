## Problem

Conventional multi-object tracking (MOT) predicts only object trajectories ('where'), which is insufficient for applications like visual surveillance and robotics that also require understanding what objects are doing, how they interact, and what the overall scene depicts. No prior benchmark or end-to-end method jointly addresses trajectory estimation with trajectory-associated semantic understanding (instance captions, interactions, and video-level captions).
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20240931/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a video sequence (average ~23 s, up to 116 s) and produces four outputs: per-frame axis-aligned bounding boxes forming object trajectories, a natural-language caption per tracked instance describing its behavior, directed interaction triplets (<subject, predicate, object>) between trajectory pairs drawn from 335 WordNet-derived verb types, and a single summary caption per video. Detection operates frame-by-frame with a proposal threshold τ_p, but trajectory association and all semantic predictions require the complete sequence, creating a structural mismatch the training procedure must resolve.

## Method

SMOTer is a three-stage end-to-end tracker built on ByteTrack: (1) a DLA backbone extracts per-frame features, CenterNet generates proposals, BYTE associates them into trajectories, and RoI Pooling yields per-target features; (2) a Video Fusion Module sequentially applies cross-attention to merge frame features into a global video representation, while a Trajectory Fusion Module concatenates per-frame target features along time and refines them with self-attention; (3) separate heads predict video captions (cross-attention between video and trajectory features + text decoder), instance captions (linear projection of trajectory feature + text decoder), and interaction labels (cross-attention between two trajectory features + MLP). The key training insight is resolving the conflict between frame-level detection loss and video-level semantic loss by computing detection losses per frame but deferring trajectory association until the full sequence is processed, enabling true end-to-end optimization where semantic tasks reciprocally improve tracking accuracy (+3.15% HOTA over ByteTrack).

## Results

On BenSMOT, SMOTer achieves 71.98% HOTA, 77.71% MOTA, and 80.65% IDF1 for trajectory estimation, outperforming the strongest baseline TransTrack (71.31% HOTA, 74.08% MOTA) and its own base ByteTrack (68.84% HOTA, 73.87% MOTA) by +3.15% and +3.74% respectively. For semantic understanding, SMOTer attains the best CIDEr scores on both video captioning (0.343) and instance captioning (0.087), and achieves 0.368 F1 on interaction recognition. The end-to-end training strategy ensures that adding semantic tasks does not degrade tracking performance, with SMOTer surpassing ByteTrack on nearly all tracking metrics despite the additional objectives.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SMOTer is compared against seven two-stage MOT baselines (SORT, DeepSORT, OC-SORT, ByteTrack, TransTrack, MOTR, MOTRv2) that are augmented with the same feature fusion and semantic generation modules for fair comparison. On tracking, SMOTer achieves the best HOTA (71.98%), MOTA (77.71%), and IDF1 (80.65%), surpassing the strongest baseline TransTrack by +0.67% HOTA and +3.63% MOTA, though it trails TransTrack on LocA (87.11 vs. 91.31) and FP (6388 vs. 4420). Compared to its architectural base ByteTrack, SMOTer gains +3.15% HOTA and +3.74% MOTA, demonstrating that joint semantic training does not harm tracking. On semantic understanding (Table 3), SMOTer leads on video captioning CIDEr (0.343 vs. 0.309 for DeepSORT) and instance captioning CIDEr (0.087 vs. 0.074 for TransTrack), but slightly trails TransTrack on interaction F1 (0.368 vs. 0.376). The end-to-end SMOTer consistently outperforms all offline two-stage combinations, validating that joint training is more effective than sequential task training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the MOT paradigm by coupling trajectory estimation with three trajectory-associated semantic tasks (instance captioning, interaction recognition, and video captioning), framing the result as a new unified task called SMOT. Architecturally, SMOTer is built on ByteTrack's detection-and-association pipeline, augmented with a cross-attention Video Fusion Module and a concatenation-plus-self-attention Trajectory Fusion Module that feed separate text decoders and an MLP interaction head. The end-to-end training strategy, inspired by Global Tracking Transformers, resolves the frame-level vs. sequence-level loss conflict that would otherwise force offline assembly of independent trackers and captioners. The paper explicitly differentiates itself from concurrent dense video object captioning work [58] by adding interaction and video-level captioning, providing a dedicated dataset, and supporting joint training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

BenSMOT is the first dedicated benchmark for SMOT, and the task formulation—jointly predicting trajectories and their semantic descriptions—opens a concrete path toward video understanding that goes beyond localization. The observation that semantic auxiliary tasks improve tracking accuracy (e.g., +3.15% HOTA over ByteTrack) suggests a mutual-benefit relationship between perception and language that could inform future multi-task tracking designs. Key limitations include the human-only focus, restriction to pairwise interactions, short average clip length (~23 s), and the fact that SMOTer is explicitly a baseline rather than a state-of-the-art method, leaving substantial room for algorithmic improvement. Open questions include scaling to non-human objects, handling multi-entity interactions, and extending to long-term tracking scenarios.
