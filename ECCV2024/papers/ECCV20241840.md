## Problem

Fine-tuning pre-trained vision-language models like CLIP on downstream tasks improves in-distribution accuracy but degrades out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization due to overfitting. Existing parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods either rely on pre-defined structural rules (e.g., low-rank adaptation, bias-only updates) that ignore task-specific data, or are tied to specific architectures such as transformers, making them difficult to apply broadly across VLP backbones.

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images and text prompts (e.g., 'a photo of a [CLASS]') which are encoded by a pre-trained image encoder and text encoder respectively, producing normalized embeddings whose cosine similarity yields class prediction probabilities via a softmax over L classes. The sparsity level α (default 0.1%) controls the fraction of parameters that remain learnable, and the approach is applied to both vision backbones (ViT-B/16, ResNet-50/101, ViT-B/32) and NLP backbones (T5-large/3B, DeBERTa-large) across classification, NER, and question-answering tasks.

## Method

SAFT operates in two phases: first, it computes the average gradient of the cross-entropy loss over the downstream training set and selects the top-α fraction of parameters by gradient magnitude as the learnable subset, forming a fixed binary mask; second, it fine-tunes only the masked parameters via SGD while resetting all unmasked parameters to their pre-trained values after every update step. The key insight is that parameters with large gradient magnitudes are most influential for the downstream task, so updating only them yields fast convergence while the frozen majority preserves the pre-trained model's OOD inductive bias. The approach is architecture-agnostic, introduces no extra parameters, and the paper derives a compression-based generalization bound that scales with the (much smaller) number of learnable parameters rather than total model size.

## Results

On the ImageNet distribution-shift benchmark (16-shot), SAFT achieves 61.32% average OOD accuracy using only 0.15M learnable parameters (0.1% of the model), outperforming conventional fine-tuning (FT) by 5.15% on OOD average while matching FT on ID accuracy (72.71% vs 71.55%). SAFT also achieves the best harmonic mean of 79.11% in the base-to-new class generalization setting across 11 datasets, and the highest cross-dataset transfer average of 66.67% when fine-tuned on ImageNet and evaluated on 10 unseen datasets. On the BOSS NLP benchmark, SAFT outperforms LoRA in 4 out of 5 tasks in terms of OOD performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SAFT achieves the best OOD average accuracy (61.32%) across all nine baselines in the distribution-shift setting, using only 0.15M parameters. Against the most direct competitor, conventional full fine-tuning (FT, 149.62M params), SAFT gains 5.15% on OOD average (61.32% vs 56.17%) while maintaining comparable ID accuracy (72.71% vs 71.55%), demonstrating that sparse adaptation effectively mitigates overfitting. Among parameter-efficient methods, SAFT outperforms LoRA (60.47%) by 0.85% and MaPLe (60.28%) by 1.04%, despite using a comparable or smaller number of parameters. Against ensemble-based methods WiSE-FT (60.62%) and CLIPood (60.40%), SAFT leads by 0.70% and 0.92% respectively, without requiring model averaging at inference. In the NLP extension (BOSS benchmark), SAFT surpasses LoRA in 4 out of 5 tasks on OOD performance (e.g., +0.58 on NER, +0.58 on SA, +1.89 on TD, +1.41 on NLI, +0.42 on EQA), confirming the method's model-agnostic applicability beyond vision-language models.

## Relation to Prior Work

SAFT sits at the intersection of parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) and OOD generalization research. Conceptually, it draws on the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis (sparse subnetworks carry better OOD inductive bias) and compression-based generalization bounds, but departs from prior PEFT methods like LoRA (which exploits low-rank structure in transformers) and adapter modules (which add new parameters) by instead selecting a sparse subset of existing parameters via gradient magnitude. Unlike ensemble-based approaches (WiSE-FT, CLIPood) that blend pre-trained and fine-tuned weights, SAFT operates as a single-model masking strategy, and unlike prompt-tuning methods (CoOp, CoCoOp, MaPLe) it introduces no extra parameters. The gradient-averaging selection criterion is related in spirit to Elastic Weight Consolidation but is applied to parameter selection rather than regularization, and the architecture-agnostic design contrasts with transformer-specific methods like LoRA.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SAFT offers a practically attractive recipe: update only 0.1% of parameters with a simple top-k gradient-magnitude mask, and gain ~5% OOD accuracy over full fine-tuning while preserving ID performance. Its architecture-agnostic nature (validated on ViT, ResNet, T5, and DeBERTa) makes it broadly applicable beyond CLIP. However, the unstructured sparsity of the selected parameters poses a real hardware acceleration challenge on current GPUs, and the paper acknowledges this as a key limitation. Open questions include whether the sparsity pattern can be made more structured (e.g., block-wise or layer-wise) without sacrificing performance, and whether the optimal sparsity level transfers across tasks or must be tuned per dataset.
