## Problem

Existing human motion synthesis methods depend on motion capture data, which is expensive to collect and inherently limited in scope, confining them to pre-recorded motion categories and flat environments. This data dependency prevents open-set generalization to novel behaviors, styles, and terrains. The paper asks whether multimodal large language models, trained on internet-scale image-text data, can replace mocap entirely for open-set humanoid motion synthesis.

## Input / Output

The system takes a natural language motion instruction (e.g., 'walk', 'jump shot in basketball') and a 3D scene as input. The MLLM agents consume rendered RGB images of the humanoid, current joint coordinates, and text descriptions iteratively; the motion tracking policy additionally ingests a flattened height-map vector around the pelvis for terrain awareness. The output is a continuous, physically simulated motion sequence at 20 fps produced by the ODE physics engine, with no motion data required at any stage.

## Method

FreeMotion splits motion synthesis into two stages. In stage 1, two specialized GPT-4V agents collaborate sequentially: a keyframe designer translates the instruction into a series of full-body and body-part descriptions with inter-keyframe time intervals, while a keyframe animator adjusts the humanoid's pose to match each description using a fixed set of kinematic commands (IK chains, pelvis rotation, single-joint roll) with up to 5 visual-feedback iterations per body part. In stage 2, linear interpolation bridges keyframes into a 20 fps trajectory, which is then corrected by a CVAE-based motion tracking policy paired with an MLP world model (adapted from ControlVAE) that enforces physical plausibility. A key design choice is integrating height maps as visual signals into both the policy encoder and world model, enabling environment-aware tracking over irregular terrain without task-specific reward engineering.

## Results

On HumanAct12 motion synthesis (12 action categories), FreeMotion achieves 46.50% average user preference, outperforming MDM (22.67%) and MLD (30.83%) without any motion capture data. On zero-shot Olympic sports, FreeMotion dominates with 66–94% user preference per sport versus 2–18% for MotionCLIP and 2–20% for AvatarCLIP. On human-scene interaction, FreeMotion reaches 95% success rate for sitting and reaching (comparable to UniHSI's 94.3% and 97.5%) and 60% for lying down, with the lowest contact error on reaching (0.012 m). On stepping stones, FreeMotion matches or slightly exceeds ALLSTEPS on most terrain configurations without requiring task-specific reward engineering.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against data-driven methods MDM and MLD on HumanAct12, FreeMotion wins on 9 of 12 action categories (Walk, Run, Jump, Lift_dumbbell, Eat, Turn_steering_wheel, Phone, Boxing, Throw) but loses on Warm up, Drink, and Sit, where the baselines benefit from in-distribution training data. On zero-shot Olympic sports, FreeMotion achieves a decisive margin over both CLIP-based baselines (MotionCLIP and AvatarCLIP), scoring 66–94% versus 2–20%, primarily because MLLMs can reason about sequential motion composition (e.g., lifting a ball before jumping in a basketball shot) that CLIP similarity cannot capture. On style transfer, FreeMotion (58.67% average) more than doubles the performance of MotionCLIP (19.08%) and AvatarCLIP (22.25%), excelling especially on 'Drunk' (79.33%) and 'Depressed' (68.00%) styles that require world-knowledge reasoning. For human-scene interaction, FreeMotion matches or exceeds UniHSI on sitting (95% vs 94.3%) and reaching (95% vs 97.5% with lower contact error 0.012 vs 0.016), but underperforms on lying down (60% vs 81.5%) due to contact-rich dynamics. On stepping stones, FreeMotion is competitive with ALLSTEPS, winning on flat yaw=20°, single-step Θ=−50°, continuous-step Θ=−50°, and spiral Θ=−30° configurations while trailing slightly on flat yaw=0° and single-step Θ=50°.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on ControlVAE [45] for its CVAE-based motion tracking policy and MLP-based world model, extending it with height-map environmental signals for terrain-aware synthesis. It departs from prior MLLM-for-motion work (Eureka, LIV, Rocamonde et al.), which used MLLMs to design reward functions or compute CLIP similarity for only a handful of simple actions, by instead leveraging GPT-4V's world knowledge in a two-stage keyframe-decomposition pipeline. The key conceptual departure from data-driven methods (MDM, MLD, MotionCLIP, AvatarCLIP) is the complete elimination of motion capture data, replacing learned motion manifolds with MLLM-driven semantic reasoning over keyframes followed by physics-based motion filling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work demonstrates that internet-scale multimodal foundation models can serve as a practical substitute for expensive motion capture in open-set human animation, potentially democratizing motion data collection for scenes, interactions, and styles that are impractical to mocap. However, the method currently cannot handle complex motions (dancing), long instructions, or contact-rich scenarios, and its reliance on GPT-4V API calls introduces cost and reproducibility concerns. Open questions include whether finetuning an MLLM with expert motion knowledge could close the gap on complex sequences, and whether the keyframe-decomposition paradigm generalizes to multi-agent or human-human interaction settings.
