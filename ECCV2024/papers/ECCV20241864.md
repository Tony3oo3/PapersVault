## Problem

Multimodal foundation models (e.g., GPT-4V, CLIP) fail to accurately ground high-level manipulation concepts to low-level physical states due to the scarcity of annotated robot data, and existing top-down approaches require costly human labeling of key states. This paper asks whether manipulation concepts can instead be discovered bottom-up from the physical regularities inherent in unannotated demonstrations, without relying on human semantics or heuristic rules.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241864/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes unannotated demonstration trajectories as sequences of state vectors (s_t ∈ R^Q) normalized to a fixed length T by interpolation; actions are omitted since key states should be determined by states alone. A learnable concept embedding e_k ∈ R^P is concatenated to every state along the trajectory, and the network outputs a probability distribution over T time steps per concept, from which key state indices are extracted via a differentiable soft-argmax (dot product with a fixed temporal coordinate vector). Downstream, the localized key states feed into a CoTPC policy that jointly predicts actions and key state estimates.

## Method

The core insight is that key states (manipulation concepts) are committed to more physical constraints than non-key states, which manifests as maximal mutual information between a key state and its preceding state Δt steps earlier (the MaxMI criterion). The Key State Localization Network (KSL-Net) is a fully convolutional encoder followed by an MLP that maps augmented state-concept pairs to a per-timestep probability distribution. Training is self-supervised: a frozen, pretrained InfoNet mutual information estimator (attention-based) provides a differentiable MI estimate at the predicted key state locations, and the loss minimizes the negative sum of these estimates across K concepts. A pairwise-distance softplus regularization enforces diversity among discovered concepts to prevent clustering at a single global MI maximum, and non-maximum suppression prunes redundant concepts post-training. The discovered key states are then plugged into the CoTPC framework for concept-guided behavior cloning, replacing manually annotated subtask boundaries.

## Results

On four ManiSkill2 manipulation tasks (Pick & Place Cube, Stack Cube, Turn Faucet, Peg Insertion), the proposed MaxMI-discovered concepts achieve a mean task success rate of 78.9% on seen configurations, outperforming the closest baseline CoTPC (60.8%) by 18.1 points. On unseen configurations, the method maintains 76.0% on Pick & Place and 63.0% on Stack Cube, while CoTPC drops to 70.0% and 46.0% respectively. In the Franka Kitchen long-horizon benchmark, the concept-guided policy completes a mean of 3.46 subtasks per episode versus 3.17 for BeT and 3.11 for DT.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The method outperforms all 11 baselines on the primary ManiSkill2 benchmark. The closest competitor, CoTPC [24] (which uses manually annotated key states), achieves 60.8% mean success rate versus 78.9% for MaxMI, with the largest gap on Stack Cube (88.6% vs. 58.8%) and Peg Insertion (77.6% vs. 52.8%). Decision Transformer [7] (30.9%) and MaskDP [32] (22.8%) struggle with multi-step tasks, while BeT [42] (10.5%) and Decision Diffuser [1] (16.7%) perform poorly overall. In the key-state-selection ablation (Table 4), multimodal foundation models (CLIP, BLIP, BLIP2, FLAVA) all underperform MaxMI, with FLAVA closest at 63.6% on Peg Insertion seen vs. 77.6% for MaxMI. AWE [44] is the only baseline that beats MaxMI on a single task (Pick & Place seen: 96.2% vs. 91.0%), but it collapses on other tasks (e.g., 4.3% on Peg Insertion unseen). ComPILE [25] achieves 70.2% on Peg Insertion seen but only 11.8% unseen, whereas MaxMI maintains 21.3% unseen, demonstrating superior generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the CoTPC framework [24], which trains concept-guided manipulation policies using manually annotated key states, and on InfoNet [20], a differentiable neural mutual information estimator. It departs from the top-down paradigm (human annotation or multimodal foundation models like CLIP/GPT-4V for concept grounding) by proposing a bottom-up, information-theoretic criterion (MaxMI) that identifies key states purely from their physical significance. The approach also connects to unsupervised skill discovery (Option-Critic, ComPILE, AWE), but distinguishes itself by using mutual information between a state and its preceding state as the discovery signal rather than trajectory segmentation or waypoint extraction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The MaxMI criterion provides a principled, annotation-free mechanism for discovering manipulation concepts, potentially reducing the heavy labeling burden that limits current robot learning systems. The empirical observation that mutual information peaks at key states across diverse tasks offers a new lens on what makes a physical state 'significant,' and the fact that discovered concepts align with human semantics without explicit semantic supervision is encouraging for interpretability. However, all validation is in simulation (ManiSkill2, Franka Kitchen), and the authors acknowledge that the local modeling nature of MaxMI may not guarantee concept diversity, suggesting a multi-scale extension as future work. Real-robot validation and scaling to more complex, open-ended manipulation remain open.
