## Problem

Text-to-image diffusion models are known to memorize and replicate training images, raising copyright and privacy concerns, but prior work has largely focused on identifying which data causes memorization without explaining the internal model mechanism. This paper asks: what does the model's cross-attention look like when it is memorizing versus generating novel content, and can that signal be exploited for lightweight detection and mitigation?

## Input / Output

The method consumes a standard text prompt fed into a Stable Diffusion model and reads the cross-attention score matrices (A) produced at each U-Net layer, attention head, and hidden dimension during the reverse diffusion process. For detection, it outputs a scalar memorization score computed from attention entropy over the last T/5 steps (metric D) or from a single U-Net layer at the first diffusion step (metric E_{t=T}^l). For mitigation, it outputs a modified logit vector that is fed back into the softmax of cross-attention, yielding a generated image with reduced similarity to training data while preserving FID and CLIP alignment scores.

## Method

The core insight is that memorized prompts cause cross-attention to concentrate disproportionately on a small set of 'trigger tokens' (specific prompt tokens and summary tokens like <end>/<padding>), whereas non-memorized prompts disperse attention toward the semantically empty beginning token. Detection leverages this via attention entropy: metric D averages entropy over the last T/5 steps plus the change in summary-token entropy, while metric E_{t=T}^l uses entropy at a single U-Net layer (l=4) at the first diffusion step, requiring only one forward pass. Inference-time mitigation combines two logit modifications—masking summary tokens to −∞ and re-scaling the beginning-token logit by a factor C>1—which simultaneously suppresses trigger-token attention and restores the dispersed pattern. Training-time mitigation simply discards mini-batch samples whose attention entropy exceeds a threshold, avoiding the extra empty-prompt inference required by prior work. All components add negligible computation, preserving both training and inference speed.

## Results

For memorization detection on SD v1.4, the proposed Ours-D metric achieves near-perfect AUROC of 0.9998 and TPR@3%FPR of 0.997 in 1.745 s (using 50 steps, 1 generation), while the single-step Ours-E metric reaches AUROC 0.9933 and TPR 0.980 in just 0.116 s. On SD v2.0, Ours-E is the only method exceeding 0.99 AUROC (0.9968) while remaining the fastest (0.281 s). For inference-time mitigation, the method reduces the Similarity Score from 0.7 to 0.25–0.3 with negligible FID change and 20% lower runtime than Wen et al.; for training-time mitigation, it reduces similarity to ~0.2 with no CLIP score degradation and zero additional training overhead.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Carlini et al. [7], the proposed methods dominate on both accuracy and speed: Ours-D achieves AUROC 0.9998 vs. Carlini's best 0.9983 (at 50 steps, 16 seeds) while being 16× faster (1.745 s vs. 27.983 s); Ours-E is 241× faster (0.116 s vs. 27.983 s) with only a 0.65% AUROC drop. Against Wen et al. [40], Ours-D matches or exceeds AUROC (0.9998 vs. 0.9992) at 2.4× lower cost (1.745 s vs. 4.133 s) on SD v1.4, and Ours-E achieves a substantially higher AUROC on SD v2.0 (0.9968 vs. 0.9215 for the comparable 1-step, 1-generation setting) in 0.281 s. For mitigation, the inference-time method is 20% faster than Wen et al. while achieving comparable similarity reduction (0.25–0.3 vs. similar range), and the training-time method incurs zero additional training time versus a 10% overhead for Wen et al., with equivalent similarity reduction (~0.2) and no CLIP score degradation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes cross-attention analysis—previously employed for interpretability [33] and controlled generation [11,18,19]—to diagnose memorization in T2I diffusion models, a task previously addressed through data-level observations (deduplication [7,37], caption replication [32]) or output-level heuristics (empty-prompt comparison [40], multi-seed consistency [7]). Rather than building on a specific prior detection or mitigation pipeline, it carves new ground by identifying a mechanistic link: memorization corresponds to overfitting of cross-attention onto a small set of 'trigger tokens,' particularly summary tokens. This reframes memorization from a data-property problem to an attention-distribution problem, enabling detection and mitigation that operate directly on the attention scores without additional inference passes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers the first mechanistic explanation of *why* specific prompts trigger memorization (concentrated attention on trigger/summary tokens) rather than merely cataloging *which* data causes it, and translates this into a single-step detection metric (AUROC > 0.993 in ~0.1 s) and a zero-overhead inference-time mitigation that preserves FID and CLIP score. Open questions include whether the trigger-token phenomenon generalizes to newer architectures (e.g., DiT-based models like PixArt-α, SDXL) where cross-attention is structured differently, and whether the heuristic mitigation (masking summary tokens, re-scaling the beginning-token logit) can be replaced by a principled regularizer. The reliance on a fixed set of memorized prompts from Webster [36] and a single fine-tuning setup limits confidence in the training-time results.
