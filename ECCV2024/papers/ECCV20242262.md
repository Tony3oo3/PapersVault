## Problem

Vanilla CLIP performs poorly on ordinal regression tasks (e.g., 26% accuracy on historical image dating, 6.09 MAE on age estimation) due to two root causes: insufficient exact-number captions in pre-training data and a contrastive objective that does not explicitly discriminate numerical values within sentences. Existing CLIP-based ordinal regression methods (OrdinalCLIP, L2RCLIP) learn ordering prompts from scratch, overlooking the rich pre-trained number-related linguistic alignment already present in CLIP.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image (resized to 256×256, randomly cropped to 224×224) and produces a continuous ordinal numerical value. Rather than directly predicting a number, the model first performs coarse classification over linguistically-described bins (e.g., 'teenager', 'middle adult', 'older adult'), then refines the prediction via a lightweight MLP regressor that outputs a learnable shift per bin. The final prediction is a weighted sum of bin centres modulated by the regressor's shift estimates.

## Method

NumCLIP introduces a coarse-to-fine ordinal regression paradigm built on CLIP's ViT-B/16 backbone. In the coarse stage, numerical labels are discretized into bins and each bin is mapped to a common linguistic description (initialized via LLM queries and made learnable in CoOp style), converting a dense regression problem into a simpler image-to-language matching task that leverages pre-trained alignment. In the fine stage, a novel Fine-grained Cross-modal Ranking-based Contrastive (FCRC) loss replaces standard InfoNCE by weighting each negative sample according to its ordinal label distance from the anchor, derived from a mutual information analysis that shows indiscriminate minimization of anchor-negative MI destroys ordinal structure. The FCRC loss assigns higher weights to farther negatives and lower weights to near neighbors, preserving both semantic and ordinal alignment in CLIP's feature space. A lightweight MLP regressor concatenated to the classification logits provides the final continuous prediction, and the entire pipeline is trained end-to-end with the text encoder frozen.

## Results

On MORPH II age estimation, NumCLIP achieves an MAE of 2.08, outperforming the strongest CLIP-based baseline L2RCLIP (2.13) and the best dedicated ordinal regression method MWR (2.13). On Historical Image Dating, NumCLIP reaches 69.61% accuracy and 0.35 MAE, surpassing L2RCLIP (67.22%, 0.43) by 2.39% in accuracy. On Image Aesthetics Assessment, NumCLIP attains 76.53% overall accuracy and 0.236 MAE, exceeding L2RCLIP (76.07%, 0.245). Under the 1-shot setting on MORPH II, NumCLIP reduces MAE to 3.61 versus 4.54 for L2RCLIP and 5.09 for CoOp.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NumCLIP outperforms all 13 compared baselines across all three ordinal regression benchmarks. Against the strongest CLIP-based competitor L2RCLIP [38], NumCLIP improves MAE from 2.13 to 2.08 on MORPH II, accuracy from 67.22% to 69.61% on Historical Image Dating, and overall accuracy from 76.07% to 76.53% on Image Aesthetics. Compared to OrdinalCLIP [17], the gains are larger: 0.24 MAE reduction on age estimation, 3.17% accuracy gain on dating, and 3.48% accuracy gain on aesthetics. Against general prompt-learning CoOp [44], NumCLIP achieves 0.31 MAE lower on MORPH II and 7.71% higher accuracy on dating. Among dedicated ordinal regression methods, NumCLIP beats MWR [36] (2.13 MAE, 57.8% dating accuracy) and POE [16] (2.35 MAE, 54.68% dating accuracy) by clear margins, and surpasses the best image-domain methods (AVDL [39], DRC-ORID [13], PML [5]) on age estimation. The most dramatic gap is against zero-shot CLIP [31], where NumCLIP reduces MAE from 6.09 to 2.08 on age estimation and lifts dating accuracy from 26.08% to 69.61%, confirming that the linguistic-concept mapping and FCRC regularizer are essential for numerical reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends CLIP-based ordinal regression beyond the rank-prompt learning paradigm of OrdinalCLIP and L2RCLIP by reframing the problem through a cognitive-science lens: rather than learning ordering from scratch, it leverages pre-trained language alignments by mapping numerical bins to common linguistic descriptions (e.g., 'teenager', 'older adult'). The training objective builds on InfoNCE's mutual information lower bound, extending it with a ranking-based weighting scheme (FCRC loss) that accounts for ordinal proximity among negative samples—a departure from standard contrastive learning that treats all negatives equally. The coarse-to-fine architecture draws on CoOp-style learnable prompts but repurposes them as intermediate linguistic classifiers rather than direct number tokens.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a documented and practically important weakness of VLMs—poor numerical/ordinal reasoning—by showing that a simple linguistic re-encoding of labels plus an ordinal-aware contrastive loss can close much of the gap to task-specific SOTA methods. The few-shot results (1.33 MAE reduction at 1-shot) suggest the approach is label-efficient, which matters for domains where annotated ordinal data is scarce. Open questions include whether the task-specific LLM-generated linguistic mappings can be made fully automatic or generalized across domains without manual prompt design, and whether the FCRC loss's mutual information extension holds under larger batch sizes or more extreme ordinal ranges. The method remains tied to CLIP's ViT-B/16 backbone and three relatively small benchmarks, leaving scalability to larger VLMs and longer ordinal scales untested.
