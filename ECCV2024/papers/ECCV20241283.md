## Problem

Pre-trained large language and multi-modal models achieve strong performance but offer limited insight into how their latent spaces encode compositional relationships between concepts. Existing Compositional Zero-Shot Learning (CZSL) methods are task-specific and lack a general theoretical framework that specifies what structural properties a model's embedding space should satisfy to support composition. The paper asks: can we define axiomatic criteria for compositional structure in latent spaces, and to what extent do contemporary LLMs and vision-language models actually satisfy them?

## Input / Output

For the CZSL task, the method takes RGB images (encoded via a ResNet backbone) and attribute-primitive text labels (encoded via GloVe embeddings + MLP), producing attribute-primitive pair predictions with cosine-similarity-based contrastive scoring. For the LLM analysis, the method constructs text prompts of the form 'an image of a(n) <object>' and 'an image of a(n) <attr_1>, ..., and <attr_n>', computes the difference between the mean-pooled token embeddings of these two prompts to obtain morphism vectors, and derives compositionality scores (additivity of multi-attribute morphisms), homogeneity scores (cosine similarity of a single attribute's morphism across objects), and Yoneda embedding vectors (magnitude profiles across all attribute-object pairs).

## Method

The core contribution is a Category Theory reformulation of CZSL in which attributes are Morphisms (directed arrows) acting on primitive Objects, rather than both being Objects in a product category with no interactions. Multi-attribute compositions (e.g., 'red sports car') are then simply compositions of morphisms, eliminating the need for a separate multi-product category. The practical instantiation, CatCom, uses a Cross-Attention module where the Query is derived from the attribute embedding and the Key/Value come from the primitive embedding, effectively learning the morphism functions end-to-end with a contrastive cosine-similarity loss on top of an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet. This same categorical machinery is then applied to analyze six text encoders (BERT, ALBERT, RoBERTa, DeBERTa, GPT-2, CLIP) and three LLMs (Phi-2, Zephyr, Mistral) by checking whether their embedding difference vectors satisfy the compositionality constraint (τ_{a1&a2} ≈ τ_{a1} + τ_{a2}), whether attributes act homogeneously across primitives, and whether Yoneda embeddings reveal isomorphic structure across models.

## Results

On single-attribute CZSL (Closed-World), CatCom_t achieves HM of 19.5 on MIT-States, 48.8 on UT-Zappos, and 15.8 on C-GQA, matching or exceeding the state-of-the-art CGE_t (21.4, 47.3, 14.5) on two of three datasets. In the more challenging Open-World setting, CatCom_t reaches HM 10.6, 37.4, and 4.6, substantially outperforming CGE_t (10.6, 30.3, 3.2) on UT-Zappos and C-GQA. For multi-attribute composition, CatCom achieves mAUC of 84.7 on aPY and 87.9 on SUN, surpassing all baselines except SymNet_m on SUN (88.4). The frozen CatCom variant already matches or exceeds trainable-backbone baselines in the Open-World setting, highlighting the efficiency of the categorical formulation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CGE_t (the strongest single-attribute baseline), CatCom_t wins on UT-Zappos (HM 48.8 vs 47.3) and C-GQA (HM 15.8 vs 14.5) in Closed-World, and on UT-Zappos (37.4 vs 30.3) and C-GQA (4.6 vs 3.2) in Open-World, while tying on MIT-States. The frozen CatCom variant already surpasses all frozen-backbone baselines (CompCos, CGE, SymNet) in Open-World, e.g., achieving HM 9.5 vs 8.4 for CompCos on MIT-States. OADis_t is competitive in Closed-World (HM 18.9 vs 19.5 on MIT-States) but falls behind CatCom_t in Open-World (10.3 vs 10.6 on MIT-States, 37.4 vs 37.4 on UT-Zappos). SymNet is consistently the weakest baseline across all settings. For multi-attribute composition, CatCom (mAUC 84.7/87.9) outperforms AMT (84.5/82.5), FMT (70.5/75.5), GALM (84.2/86.5), and SymNet_s (79.9/86.7) on both aPY and SUN, but trails SymNet_m (83.4/88.4) by 0.5 points on SUN while leading by 1.3 on aPY.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes Category Theory (functors, morphisms, product categories, Yoneda embeddings) as a formal lens on Compositional Zero-Shot Learning, a task well-studied through graph-based [46], symmetry-based [38], and disentanglement-based [55] methods. The key conceptual departure is the 'morphism formulation,' where attributes are treated as directed transformations (morphisms) acting on primitive objects rather than as co-equal objects in a product category, enabling multi-attribute composition via morphism composition without defining new categories. The practical CatCom architecture is a straightforward cross-attention instantiation of this view, while the Yoneda-embedding-based diagnostics for LLM latent spaces extend the categorical toolkit beyond CZSL into model interpretability, a direction only nascently explored in prior work [45].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a principled 'compositionality index' and attribute-homogeneity metric that can be computed cheaply (linearly, via homogeneity, rather than quadratically via full compositions) to diagnose whether a model's latent space respects compositional structure, providing a structured alternative to visual inspection. The finding that DeBERTa's latent space is more suitable for vision-language text encoding than Mistral's, despite being orders of magnitude smaller, offers practical guidance for model selection in multi-modal pipelines. Limitations include the exploratory nature of the LLM analysis (no downstream task validation of the compositionality score), the CZSL results being competitive rather than dominant, and the categorical framework not yet being incorporated into training objectives. Open questions include whether enforcing functoriality or morphism-composition constraints during pre-training would improve compositional generalization, and whether the Yoneda-based model comparison generalizes beyond the four attribute types tested.
