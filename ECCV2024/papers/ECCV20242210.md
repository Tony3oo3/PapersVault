## Problem

Standard contrastive learning suffers from feature suppression, where the model captures only a limited subset of input features while ignoring others, leading to indistinguishable representations for semantically different inputs. This issue affects both unimodal (SimCLR, MoCo-v2) and multimodal (CLIP) settings, causing downstream failures such as orientation-based hallucinations in multimodal LLMs. Existing remedies either sacrifice previously well-learned features through adversarial training or require additional reconstruction losses that are computationally impractical at scale.

## Input / Output

In the unimodal setting, the method takes RGB images (e.g., 32×32 CIFAR-MNIST, 64×64 Trifeature, 64×64 STL-10) and produces image embeddings; in the multimodal setting, it takes 224×224 images paired with text and produces image-text similarity scores for matching. The final output representation is not a single encoder's output but an element-wise concatenation of embeddings from all N trained stages, increasing the embedding dimensionality proportionally to the number of stages. The framework is model-agnostic and compatible with any NCE-based contrastive learning backbone (ResNet-18, ResNet-50, ViT-L-14).

## Method

MCL trains N sequential encoder stages, each using a modified InfoNCE loss with feature-aware negative sampling: at stage j, negative samples for an anchor are restricted to those sharing the same pseudo label (the concatenation of K-means cluster assignments from all preceding stages), forcing the model to discover features beyond those already dominant. After each stage, K-means clustering is applied to the new representations to define cluster assignments for the next stage's negative sampling. A mathematical constraint K^N ≤ M/b ensures clusters remain large enough to form valid training batches. After all stages complete, cross-stage representation integration concatenates the embeddings from every stage into a single final representation, preserving all previously learned features without requiring any modification to the base model architecture or loss function.

## Results

On the unimodal linear evaluation protocol, MCL achieves an average accuracy of 0.93 across Trifeature, CelebA, and CIFAR-MNIST, outperforming vanilla SimCLR (0.83), IFM (0.82), FD (0.82), and TS (0.77). The most dramatic gain is on CIFAR-MNIST, where the suppressed CIFAR feature accuracy jumps from 0.29 (SimCLR) to 0.87 (MCL) while MNIST accuracy is preserved at 0.99. In the multimodal setting, MCL-tuned CLIP (ViT-L-14) raises the average MMVP benchmark accuracy from 20.0 (original CLIP) to 32.6, with individual attribute improvements up to 3× (e.g., S&C from 26.7 to 73.3, C&A from 40.0 to 80.0).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MCL outperforms all four unimodal baselines on the overall average (0.93 vs. 0.83 SimCLR, 0.82 IFM, 0.82 FD, 0.77 TS). The advantage is most pronounced on the severely suppressed CIFAR feature in CIFAR-MNIST: MCL reaches 0.87, compared to 0.29 for SimCLR, 0.11 for IFM, 0.09 for TS, and 0.78 for FD. Notably, IFM and TS actually underperform vanilla SimCLR on average (0.82 and 0.77 vs. 0.83), indicating that their adversarial or scheduling strategies can degrade previously well-learned features. FD is the closest competitor (0.82 average) but still trails MCL by 0.11, particularly on Trifeature Shape (0.75 vs. 1.00) and CIFAR (0.78 vs. 0.87). In the multimodal setting, MCL-tuned CLIP (ViT-L-14) achieves 32.6 average MMVP accuracy versus 20.0 for the original OpenAI CLIP (STAGE0), a 63% relative improvement, with the ResNet variant improving from 19.3 to 24.4.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the SimCLR and MoCo-v2 contrastive learning frameworks as backbones, and extends the CLIP multimodal contrastive learning paradigm. It departs from prior feature suppression mitigations (adversarial training in feature space by Robinson et al., input-space perturbation by Tamkin et al., and reconstruction-loss augmentation by Bleeker et al.) by introducing a training-procedure-only modification: multistage feature-aware negative sampling combined with cross-stage representation concatenation. Unlike those methods, MCL requires no loss-function changes, no adversarial training, and no architectural modifications, and is the first to address feature suppression uniformly across both unimodal and multimodal contrastive learning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Feature suppression in CLIP directly causes hallucination failures in multimodal large language models that use CLIP as their vision encoder, making this a practically urgent problem. The model-agnostic, training-procedure-only nature of MCL makes it easy to retrofit onto existing contrastive learning pipelines without retraining from scratch. However, the absolute MMVP scores remain low (32.6 average for ViT), the cross-stage integration is a simple concatenation that the authors acknowledge is suboptimal (e.g., O&D attribute does not improve), and the unimodal experiments rely on relatively toy datasets (Trifeature, CIFAR-MNIST). Open questions include whether more sophisticated integration strategies, parameter-efficient tuning (e.g., LoRA), and scaling to larger datasets can close the remaining gap.
