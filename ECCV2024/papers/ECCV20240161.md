## Problem

Existing open-set recognition (OSR) evaluation metrics like AUROC and FPR95 average over all possible thresholds and provide no guidance for selecting an operational decision threshold, while recent evaluations have shifted to small-scale datasets that do not reflect real-world deployment. The paper addresses both gaps by proposing Operational Open-Set Accuracy (OOSA), which requires predicting a threshold from a validation set with knowns and a surrogate unknown set before testing, and by introducing PostMax, a post-processing algorithm that advances state-of-the-art OSR performance on large-scale ImageNet-scale data.

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard 224×224 RGB image crops and processes them through a frozen pre-trained network (Hiera-H or ConvNeXtV2-H trained on ILSVRC2012-1K) to extract a deep feature vector and a logit vector. The output is a binary known/unknown decision plus, for known samples, a class label; the decision is made by comparing the GPD cumulative probability of the normalized maximum logit against an operational threshold θ* selected on a separate validation set. No additional training, fine-tuning, or negative/unknown samples are required at any stage.

## Method

PostMax operates in two stages. First, for each correctly classified training sample, it computes a normalized logit ℓ = max(L(x)) / ‖FV(x)‖, dividing the maximum logit by the L2 norm of the deep feature vector; this normalization is motivated by the finding that on large-scale ILSVRC2012 networks, unknown samples exhibit *larger* feature magnitudes than known samples (the opposite of small-scale observations), so dividing by magnitude enhances known/unknown separation. Second, it fits a Generalized Pareto Distribution (GPD) via Peak-over-Threshold Extreme Value Theory to the collection of normalized logits from correctly classified training samples, yielding parameters (μ, σ, ξ). At inference, the normalized max logit of a test sample is passed through the GPD CDF Ψ(μ,σ,ξ) to produce a calibrated probability of class inclusion, and the operational threshold θ* is selected by maximizing Open-Set Accuracy (OSA) on a validation set of knowns plus a surrogate unknown set, then frozen for test-time deployment. The approach requires no negative samples, no additional training, and no explicit unknown-class modeling.

## Results

PostMax achieves the best OOSA across all seven unknown datasets on both Hiera-H and ConvNeXtV2-H, with headline values of 0.864 (OpenImage-O, Hiera-H) and 0.849 (OpenImage-O, ConvNeXtV2-H). On the standard AUROC/FPR95 metrics, PostMax reaches 0.96/0.21 on iNaturalist (Hiera-H) and 0.95/0.26 (ConvNeXtV2-H). Statistical testing (paired two-tailed t-tests with Bonferroni correction) confirms all improvements are significant: OOSA gains of 2.3–12.4%, AUROC gains of 4.7–17.9%, and FPR95 reductions of 9.6–28.1% over every baseline, with all p-values below 1.0×10⁻⁷.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PostMax outperforms all four baselines (MSP, MaxLogit, NNGuide, SCALE) on OOSA, AUROC, and FPR95 across every unknown dataset and both architectures. The strongest baseline is NNGuide (leader on the OpenOOD benchmark), which PostMax surpasses by 2.5–12.4% OOSA on Hiera-H and 2.3–10.4% on ConvNeXtV2-H; the gap is largest on 21K-P Easy (0.818 vs. 0.744 OOSA, Hiera-H). SCALE is consistently the weakest method, trailing PostMax by up to 24.6% OOSA (0.818 vs. 0.568 on 21K-P Easy, Hiera-H). MSP and MaxLogit, the simple thresholding baselines, are beaten by 1–16% OOSA depending on dataset and architecture. One notable exception: when 21K-P Easy is used as the surrogate and 21K-P Hard as the test unknowns (Table 3), PostMax (0.634) does not beat MSP (0.673) on Hiera-H, suggesting that fine-grained unknowns close to known classes remain challenging for all methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

PostMax sits within the post-processing lineage of OSR methods that adapt pre-trained closed-set classifiers without retraining, building directly on the logit/softmax thresholding baselines of Hendrycks et al. and the OSCR evaluation framework of Dhamija et al. Its core scoring mechanism replaces ad-hoc confidence scores with a Generalized Pareto Distribution fitted via the Peak-over-Threshold approach from Pickands' extreme value theory, a departure from the Fisher-Tippett-Gnedenko/Weibull formulations used in earlier EVT-based OSR work (OpenMax, EVM). The paper also deliberately departs from the small-scale, AUROC-centric evaluation paradigm that dominated recent OSR research, instead adopting large-scale ImageNet-1K protocols with proper train/validation/test splits and a surrogate unknown set for operational threshold selection.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The OOSA metric fills a practical gap by forcing a single operational threshold to be predicted on validation data and then evaluated on unseen test data, which is how engineers actually deploy OSR systems. The finding that large-scale networks produce *larger* feature magnitudes for unknowns (opposite to small-scale observations) is a conceptual correction that changes how normalization should be applied. Open questions remain about how representative a surrogate unknown set must be of truly open-world unknowns, and whether the GPD-based scoring generalizes beyond ILSVRC2012-scale training to foundation models or multi-modal settings. The 21K-P Hard results, where PostMax loses its advantage, suggest that fine-grained visual similarity challenges may require fundamentally different approaches.
