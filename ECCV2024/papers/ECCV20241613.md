## Problem

Traffic speed data collected from fixed sensors or rideshare platforms is inherently sparse—many road segments lack empirical observations at any given time—making it difficult to build dense, city-scale traffic models. Prior image-driven traffic modeling methods either assume a known road network or regress speeds directly without accounting for the varying number of underlying observations, limiting their ability to capture temporal variation and generalize to unseen locations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20241613/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes 1024×1024 overhead RGB images at ~0.3 m/pixel resolution, a geolocation (easting/northing in Web Mercator, normalized to [-1,1]), and a time stamp (day of week and hour of day, cyclically encoded). It produces three dense per-pixel outputs: a traffic speed map in km/h (derived from the shift parameter of a per-pixel Student's t-distribution), a binary road segmentation mask, and a road orientation map over 16 angular bins. Because ground-truth speeds are provided as road-segment averages, a region aggregation layer averages per-pixel predictions along each segment before metric computation.

## Method

The architecture is a multi-stage encoder (convolutional stem → two EfficientNet-style inverted-residual stages → two SwinV2-inspired transformer stages with MHSA) paired with SegFormer-style MLP decoders for each of the three tasks. A novel Geo-Temporal Positional Encoding (GTPE) module encodes location, time, and their interaction through three SIREN-based pathways, producing a 64-dim embedding that is added to visual tokens at each transformer stage as a positional encoding. For traffic speed estimation, the network outputs per-pixel shift (μ) and scale (σ) parameters of a Student's t-distribution, with degrees of freedom ν set to the actual count of traffic observations per road segment; training minimizes the negative log-likelihood of observed speeds under this distribution, naturally weighting segments with fewer observations more conservatively. The three tasks (speed, road segmentation, orientation) are trained jointly with equally weighted losses (NLL, BCE+Dice, and cross-entropy respectively), and the auxiliary tasks provide synergistic gradients that improve speed estimation while enabling generalization to locations with unknown or changed road networks.

## Results

On the DTS benchmark (micro evaluation), the proposed method with the Student's t-distribution objective achieves RMSE 8.84, MAE 6.64, and R² 0.63, substantially outperforming the prior state-of-the-art (Workman et al. [45]) which reports RMSE 10.64, MAE 8.09, and R² 0.46. Under the macro evaluation protocol (averaging across specific times), the method achieves overall RMSE 9.15, MAE 6.76, and R² 0.65 versus 11.12, 8.34, and 0.49 for the baseline. The probabilistic formulation alone (switching from Pseudo-Huber to Student's t) yields a 1.18 RMSE improvement over the same architecture with the regression loss.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against a single prior state-of-the-art baseline, Workman et al. [45] (CVPR 2023), which introduced the DTS benchmark and a Pseudo-Huber regression approach for dynamic traffic speed estimation from overhead imagery. The proposed method wins on all three metrics (RMSE, MAE, R²) under both micro and macro evaluation protocols. In the micro setting, the full method (Student's t + GTPE + multi-task) reduces RMSE by 1.80 (10.64 → 8.84) and improves R² by 0.17 (0.46 → 0.63) over the baseline. In the macro setting, the overall RMSE drops from 11.12 to 9.15 and R² rises from 0.49 to 0.65. Ablations confirm that the gains come from both the probabilistic objective (≈1.18 RMSE reduction) and the geo-temporal positional encoding (≈0.44 RMSE reduction over image-only), with the architecture itself also contributing (≈0.62 RMSE reduction over the baseline architecture with the same loss).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Workman et al. (CVPR 2023), which introduced the Dynamic Traffic Speeds (DTS) benchmark and the task of estimating time-varying traffic speeds from overhead imagery. The key departures are methodological: replacing direct speed regression (Pseudo-Huber loss) with a probabilistic Student's t-distribution formulation that models uncertainty via observation counts, introducing a SIREN-based geo-temporal positional encoding (GTPE) module for dense per-pixel location and cyclic time context, and adding auxiliary road segmentation and orientation tasks for multi-task synergy. The architecture itself composes well-known components (CoAtNet-style encoder, SwinV2 transformer stages, SegFormer-style MLP decoder) in a configuration tailored to this geo-referenced, multi-modal setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ability to generate dense, city-scale traffic speed models from overhead imagery addresses a practical gap: empirical speed data is sparse and incomplete, limiting downstream applications in urban planning and autonomous driving. The GTPE module is a reusable component for any geo-temporal prediction task, and the finding that fine-tuning only ~65k GTPE parameters enables efficient location adaptation (NYC → Cincinnati) suggests a lightweight path to multi-city deployment. Open questions include whether the approach scales to cities with fundamentally different road geometries, how well it performs in real-time or near-real-time settings, and whether the probabilistic formulation's uncertainty estimates are well-calibrated for downstream decision-making.
