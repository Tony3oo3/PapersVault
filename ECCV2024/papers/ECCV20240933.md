## Problem

Weakly-supervised camouflaged object detection (WSCOD) has been largely unexplored, with only two prior works supporting limited label types (scribbles or points) and exhibiting large performance gaps versus fully-supervised methods. Even when leveraging SAM as a foundation model, four specific challenges arise in COD: SAM cannot natively accept scribble prompts, it produces extreme or semantically erroneous masks for camouflaged objects, and direct knowledge distillation from SAM to a small student yields unstable feature representations under limited supervision.

## Input / Output

The framework accepts 512×512 RGB images paired with one of three weakly-supervised prompt types (point, bounding box, or scribble) and outputs a binary pixel-level segmentation mask. Scribble prompts are preprocessed into discrete point sets via skeleton extraction and uniform grid sampling before being fed to SAM. The student model uses PVT-B4 as encoder with a four-branch convolutional decoder, while SAM (ViT-H) operates offline as a frozen teacher, requiring only a single forward pass per image during training.

## Method

SAM-COD addresses SAM's COD-specific failures through three complementary modules: a Prompt Adapter that converts scribbles to discrete points via Zhang-Suen skeletonization and grid-based sampling; a Response Filter that discards masks whose area-to-image ratio falls outside [τ_s, τ_b]; and a Semantic Matcher that scores SAM's candidate masks by semantic entropy computed against a COD-trained model, selecting the mask maximizing the ratio of segmentation confidence to semantic entropy. The selected optimal mask is then distilled into the student via Prompt-Adaptive Knowledge Distillation (PKD), which applies a binary mask M^F to weight the distillation loss differently for key regions defined by the prompt type (foreground for scribbles, inscribed circle for points, inner quarter-box for boxes). A Self-Knowledge Distillation (SKD) loss with visual augmentations (scale, crop, translate, flip, Gaussian blur) further stabilizes the student's feature representations. Training proceeds in two stages: first training the semantic matcher encoder-decoder, then performing offline distillation with only PKD and SKD losses.

## Results

On the three COD benchmarks (CAMO, COD10K, NC4K), SAM-COD with scribble supervision achieves MAE of 0.060/0.029/0.039 and S_m of 0.836/0.833/0.859, outperforming the best weakly-supervised method WS-SAM by an average of 26.8% in MAE, 6.1% in S_m, and 5.5% in E_m. With bounding-box supervision, SAM-COD reaches MAE 0.028 on COD10K and F_β^w 0.813 on NC4K, surpassing the state-of-the-art fully-supervised ZoomNet (MAE 0.029, F_β^w 0.784 on NC4K). The mixed-label setting (randomly assigning one of three label types per image) yields the best overall MAE of 0.058 on CAMO, demonstrating that label diversity improves feature learning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SAM-COD substantially outperforms the strongest prior weakly-supervised method WS-SAM [14] across all three datasets and all label types, with an average MAE reduction of 26.8% (e.g., 0.029 vs. 0.038 on COD10K with scribble). It also surpasses the best fully-supervised COD method ZoomNet [25] on most metrics: with box supervision, SAM-COD achieves MAE 0.028 vs. 0.029 and F_β^w 0.745 vs. 0.729 on COD10K, and MAE 0.037 vs. 0.043 and F_β^w 0.813 vs. 0.784 on NC4K. Against the earlier scribble-based CRNet [15], SAM-COD improves MAE from 0.092 to 0.060 on CAMO and from 0.049 to 0.029 on COD10K. Direct use of SAM (zero-shot or fine-tuned as SAM-S/SAM-P) remains far behind: SAM-S achieves MAE 0.105 on CAMO vs. 0.060 for SAM-COD, confirming that the prompt-adaptive knowledge distillation and response filtering are critical. On the SOD extension (Table 8), SAM-COD with box supervision achieves MAE 0.031 on ECSSD and 0.033 on DUTS-TE, outperforming both fully-supervised AFNet [11] and BASNet [26] as well as point-supervised PSOD [13].

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on SAM as a frozen teacher model for segmentation, departing from prior COD work that relied on fully-supervised pixel-level annotations or limited weak supervision (scribble-only in CRNet, scribble+point in WS-SAM). Rather than fine-tuning SAM for COD (as SAM-Adapter does), it repurposes SAM as an offline knowledge source and distills its predictions into a compact PVT-based student, introducing prompt-adaptive distillation that weights the loss differently depending on whether the prompt is a point, box, or scribble. The response filter and semantic matcher modules are novel engineering contributions specifically designed to correct SAM's known failure modes in camouflage scenes (extreme-area masks and semantically wrong local segments), filling a gap that prior SAM-in-COD work did not address.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: reducing annotation cost from ~60 minutes per image (pixel masks) to 2–10 seconds (point/box/scribble) while matching or exceeding fully-supervised performance makes large-scale COD dataset construction feasible. The data-efficiency finding—where one training image per category outperforms CRNet trained on the full dataset—suggests SAM's prior knowledge transfers effectively even under extreme label scarcity, though this result is demonstrated on a single dataset and deserves replication. Open questions include whether the offline SAM dependency (7-hour forward pass, ViT-H) is practical for iterative development, whether the approach extends to video COD or other weakly-supervised dense prediction tasks, and whether the prompt-adaptive distillation principle generalizes beyond the three prompt types tested.
