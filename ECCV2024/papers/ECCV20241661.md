## Problem

First-order iterative algorithms (ISTA, FISTA, TwIST) for CASSI hyperspectral reconstruction require the Lipschitz constant of the loss gradient, which equals the largest eigenvalue of the operator H*H. For practical scene sizes this is a matrix of dimension (NL)×(NL) — e.g., 64K×64K for 256×256 scenes — making its direct computation a bottleneck in an otherwise optimized pipeline. The paper eliminates this bottleneck by showing the constant can be extracted from a matrix roughly three orders of magnitude smaller.

## Input / Output

The method takes S binary aperture masks, each a vector of length N (spatial pixels), and produces the exact Lipschitz constant L(F) as the largest eigenvalue of the S×S matrix P^T P, where P is the N×S matrix with the masks as columns. The result requires L ≥ N (spectral channels at least as many as spatial pixels), which is the typical CASSI configuration. The output is a single scalar that sets the step-size constraint τ < 1/L(F) in the reconstruction algorithm.

## Method

The core insight exploits the diagonal-sum structure of the basic CASSI snapshot map H_o, which sums array values along anti-diagonals. By decomposing the state space X into orthogonal diagonal subspaces X_k and analyzing the restricted operators, the paper proves that H_p H_p* is explicitly diagonal in the standard basis of Y_0 (Theorem 1), with eigenvalues given by the per-diagonal counts of unblocked cells. For the full multi-snapshot map, H*H decomposes as a direct sum over these diagonal subspaces, and under L ≥ N the largest eigenvalue is always achieved in the 'middle' diagonals where all restricted S×S matrices hh* are identical and equal to P^T P (Theorem 3). This reduces the eigenvalue computation from an (NL)×(NL) problem to an S×S problem. A supplementary probabilistic analysis shows the exact constant is typically 53–63% of the naive triangle-inequality upper bound, with the gap shrinking as N grows.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 3) shows that the Lipschitz constant L(F) = ‖H*H‖ can be computed as the largest eigenvalue of an N×S matrix P^T P (where P stacks the S aperture masks as columns), rather than the largest eigenvalue of the full D×D operator H*H with D = N·L. For typical CASSI parameters (N ≈ L ≈ 256, S ≈ 16), this reduces the eigenvalue problem from a 65,536×65,536 matrix to a 256×16 matrix — roughly three orders of magnitude smaller. The numerical experiment (Table 1) shows that the exact Lipschitz constant is approximately (1/2 + 1/(2S)) times the simpler upper bound from Theorem 2, with the ratio ranging from 0.625 (S=4) to 0.531 (S=16) across N = 128–512 pixels.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing reconstruction algorithms; instead it compares two strategies for computing the Lipschitz constant needed by first-order solvers (ISTA, FISTA, TwIST). The direct approach computes the largest eigenvalue of H*H, a D×D matrix with D = N·L (e.g., 65,536×65,536 for N = L = 256), which the authors identify as a computational bottleneck. The proposed method (Theorem 3) replaces this with the largest eigenvalue of P^T P, an N×S matrix (e.g., 256×16), a reduction of roughly three orders of magnitude in matrix dimension. The paper also provides a simpler but looser upper bound (Theorem 2) via the triangle inequality, L(F) ≤ Σ_s Σ_i p_s(i) = SN/2 for half-transparent masks. The numerical experiment quantifies the gap: the exact value is only about 53–63% of this upper bound, meaning the triangle-inequality bound overestimates the Lipschitz constant by a factor of 1.6–1.9, which would force unnecessarily small step sizes in iterative solvers. The theoretical analysis (Section 4.2) further shows the ratio converges to 1/2 + 1/(2S) as N → ∞, with a provable upper bound on the deviation of order 1/√N.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the standard CASSI measurement model of Wagadarikar et al. (2008), exploiting the specific algebraic structure of the basic snapshot map H_o (which sums along diagonals) and the coded aperture projection to derive an explicit spectral decomposition. The Lipschitz constant computation itself is a standard prerequisite for first-order methods like ISTA, FISTA, and TwIST, but prior CASSI reconstruction work has typically computed it by finding the largest eigenvalue of the full D×D Gram matrix H*H. The key departure here is recognizing that the diagonal-summing structure allows H_p H_p* to be diagonalized in the standard basis (Theorem 1), which cascades into the compact N×S formula in Theorem 3.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The three-orders-of-magnitude reduction in matrix size (from ~64K×64K to 256×8 in a typical setup) removes a genuine computational bottleneck in otherwise well-optimized CASSI reconstruction pipelines, and the result is exact rather than an upper bound. The probabilistic analysis in Section 4.2, showing the exact Lipschitz constant is approximately half the naive upper bound, provides useful guidance for practitioners who may not want to compute the eigenvalue at all. A key limitation is the assumption L ≥ N (more spectral channels than spatial pixels), which covers most practical CASSI systems but leaves the L < N case open; the 2D extension, while straightforward, assumes row-wise independence that may not hold under imperfect optical alignment.
