## Problem

Existing multimodal emotion recognition datasets (MELD, CAER, M3ED) are small (<14k samples), heavily skewed toward Neutral with near-zero representation of minority emotions like Fear and Disgust, and reuse characters across train/test splits, making test sets not truly unseen. These limitations hinder reliable cross-dataset generalization and minority-emotion modeling, and no large-scale, class-balanced, speaking-segment-aligned benchmark with strict content-level partitioning has been available.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261369/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each sample is a 3–6 second speaking-segment clip with three temporally synchronized modalities: a video segment (face present in ≥90% of frames, verified by YOLOv8), an audio waveform cropped to the exact utterance interval, and a Whisper-generated text transcript containing at least 12 words ending in terminal punctuation. The output is a single dominant emotion label from seven Ekman-based categories (Anger, Disgust, Fear, Joy, Sadness, Surprise, Neutral). Splits are enforced at the movie/franchise level (70/10/20), so no actor, scene, or dialogue appears in more than one split, guaranteeing a fully unseen test set.

## Method

The pipeline segments 3,100 movies/TV series into 306,544 dialogue clips via Whisper ASR with word-level timestamps, extracts aligned video/audio/text streams, and verifies synchronization within 0.1 s. Emotion labels are generated by a logit-based fusion of DistilRoBERTa (text) and Wav2Vec 2.0 (audio) outputs, where softmax-normalized logits from both modalities are combined with a KL-divergence penalty (λ=0.5) to reward cross-modal agreement and penalize disagreement. A two-stage refinement—threshold filtering on neutral-class scores (sigmoid < 0.05) and face-presence (≥90% frames) followed by human validation by 20 expert annotators (≥3 per clip, Fleiss κ=0.62)—reduces the pool to 30,000 balanced clips. Benchmarking evaluates five SOTA multimodal models (MulT, MISA, EmotionCLIP, TCL-MAP, EMOE) under in-domain, cross-dataset transfer, low-data (10/30/50%), neutral-class removal, and unimodal-vs-multimodal protocols, reporting per-class F1, W-F1, Macro-F1, and an imbalance gap Δ to quantify class-bias.

## Results

On SpEmoC, TCL-MAP achieves the best in-domain performance with W-F1 of 73.67 and Macro-F1 of 73.34, while EMOE follows closely at W-F1 70.30 and Macro-F1 70.25. The imbalance gap (Δ = W-F1 − Macro-F1) drops to ≤0.65 on SpEmoC for all models, compared to 15–25 points on MELD and CAER, confirming that the balanced class distribution yields near-uniform per-class performance. Minority emotions (Fear, Disgust) that score near-zero F1 on MELD/CAER reach 36–71 F1 on SpEmoC, demonstrating the dataset's value for underrepresented emotion modeling.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks five SOTA multimodal emotion recognition models (TCL-MAP, EMOE, MulT, MISA, EmotionCLIP) under identical training protocols across SpEmoC, MELD, and CAER. TCL-MAP leads on SpEmoC with W-F1 73.67 and Macro-F1 73.34 (Δ=0.33), outperforming EMOE (70.30/70.25, Δ=0.05) by ~3.4 W-F1 points, while MulT (53.37/52.72), MISA (50.78/47.50), and EmotionCLIP (51.30/49.50) trail by 15–20 points. On MELD, TCL-MAP still leads (W-F1 62.68) but with a large imbalance gap of 17.22, whereas on CAER all models degrade sharply (TCL-MAP W-F1 28.26, EMOE 36.52, MulT 36.03). The critical finding is that SpEmoC's balanced design compresses the Δ gap to ≤0.65 for every model, versus 15–25 on MELD/CAER, and lifts minority-class F1 (Fear, Disgust) from near-zero to 36–71, a gain no single model architecture can replicate on the imbalanced baselines. Cross-dataset transfer (Tables 5–8) further shows SpEmoC-trained models degrade more gracefully and retain non-trivial minority-class F1 where MELD/CAER-trained models collapse to 0.0.

## Relation to Prior Work

SpEmoC positions itself as a direct response to the scale, balance, and splitting limitations of existing multimodal emotion datasets (MELD, CAER, IEMOCAP, CMU-MOSEI, M3ED, EmoWOZ), all of which are under 23K clips, heavily neutral-dominated, and prone to content leakage across splits. Rather than proposing a new model architecture, the paper repurposes a hybrid annotation pipeline (DistilRoBERTa + Wav2Vec 2.0 logit fusion with KL-divergence regularization, YOLOv8 face verification, Whisper segmentation) and a multi-stage filtering strategy to curate a balanced 30K-clip benchmark from 306K raw segments. The evaluation framework deliberately uses five established multimodal models (MulT, MISA, EmotionCLIP, TCL-MAP, EMOE) as fixed probes, isolating dataset-design effects from architectural contributions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The near-balanced seven-emotion distribution and strict movie/series-level splitting address two persistent confounds in MER benchmarking—class imbalance masking minority-emotion failures and actor/scene overlap inflating test performance—making SpEmoC a more reliable yardstick for cross-dataset generalization. However, the dataset remains English-only, sourced from scripted movies/TV rather than spontaneous conversation, assigns a single dominant emotion per clip, and relies on approximate demographic inference from visual content, all of which limit ecological validity. Open questions include whether the balanced-supervision advantage persists under fine-grained or continuous emotion labels, whether multilingual and culturally diverse extensions preserve the balance property, and whether the logit-fusion annotation pipeline generalizes to non-English or low-resource speech.
