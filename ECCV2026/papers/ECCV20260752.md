## Problem

Optical flow models are trained on synthetic data (FlyingChairs, FlyingThings3D) and evaluated on narrow or synthetic benchmarks (Sintel, KITTI, Spring), yet real-world deployment requires generalisation to diverse, uncontrolled video. This paper asks whether accuracy on these standard benchmarks actually predicts real-world optical flow quality, and finds that it does not: real-world accuracy has stagnated or even degraded for models that outperform RAFT on Sintel, KITTI, and Spring.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20260752/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The evaluated models take two consecutive RGB frames (1280×720 for FlowFactor; variable resolution for Slow Flow and TAP-Flow) and produce a dense 2D optical flow field. Evaluation uses the Fl-all metric (a pixel is correct if its Euclidean displacement error is <3 px or <5% of ground-truth magnitude), applied to sparse annotations of 5 manually selected points per frame pair for FlowFactor and TAP-Flow, and to dense flow maps for Slow Flow. The FlowFactor dataset isolates one confounding factor per category—large displacements (≥25 px), repetitive textures, occlusions, or lighting variation—so that each 250-pair subset tests a single axis of difficulty.

## Method

The paper constructs an 8,204-sample real-world evaluation benchmark by combining three datasets: FlowFactor (1,000 sparsely annotated HD frame pairs factorised into four isolated challenge categories), TAP-Flow (3,757 frames repurposed from TAP-Vid-DAVIS point-tracking annotations), and Slow Flow (3,447 dense flow frames from high-speed camera reconstruction). A broad set of recent optical flow models (CNN- and transformer-based, from PWC-Net through SEA-RAFT and MegaFlow) is evaluated using their standard C+T 'Things' checkpoints via the PTLFlow framework, with implementation correctness verified against reported Sintel and KITTI scores. The key analytical contribution is a factorised diagnostic: Spearman rank correlations between per-category FlowFactor accuracy and aggregate real-world accuracy reveal that lighting variation (ρ=0.75) and large displacements (ρ=0.74) are the strongest predictors of real-world performance, while in the top-performing regime a trade-off emerges between displacement-heavy and stationary/distortion-based factors. Finally, the paper shows that augmenting C+T pre-training with additional synthetic data (e.g., TartanAir) does not systematically close the real-world generalisation gap.

## Results

The paper evaluates a broad set of optical flow models (via PTLFlow) on 8,204 real-world frame pairs spanning FlowFactor (1,000 pairs), TAP-Flow (3,757 frames), and Slow Flow (3,447 frames). The headline finding is that for models outperforming RAFT on KITTI, Sintel, and Spring, real-world F1-all accuracy stagnates or degrades, indicating that current synthetic benchmarks no longer track real-world progress. Among FlowFactor's four isolated factors, varying lighting (Spearman ρ=0.75) and large displacements (ρ=0.74) correlate most strongly with Slow Flow accuracy, while in top-performing models, displacement-heavy categories correlate *negatively* with lighting and occlusion categories, revealing a robustness–accuracy trade-off. The 5-point sparse annotation protocol is validated with a Spearman correlation of 0.95 (p=3.51×10⁻²²) against a second independent point set.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks numerous models from the PTLFlow framework using their standard C+T (Things) checkpoints, with RAFT serving as the reference threshold. GMA [19] demonstrates superior real-world robustness over SEA-RAFT [40] under extreme lighting changes and occlusions—maintaining near-zero motion fields where SEA-RAFT erroneously predicts motion—yet both achieve comparable F1-all on KITTI, masking this gap. FlowFormer++ [32], which scores well on Sintel, shows noticeable errors around legs, arms, and hair in real-world scenes (Fig. 1). Models that surpass RAFT on Sintel/KITTI/Spring (e.g., SEA-RAFT, FlowFormer++) do not translate that advantage to real-world data, with real-world F1-all stagnating or worsening. Adding TartanAir (>1M pairs) to C+T pre-training does not consistently improve FlowFactor accuracy; the best FlowFactor performers use either plain C+T or C+T plus a small amount of more realistic data (C+T+S+K+H). Overall, no single model dominates all four FlowFactor categories, confirming that current architectures face an inherent tension between large-motion accuracy and photometric/occlusion robustness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in optical flow evaluation by repurposing the TAP-Vid-DAVIS point-tracking benchmark into a flow evaluation suite (TAP-Flow) and introducing FlowFactor, a factorized real-world dataset that isolates four confounding factors (large displacements, repetitive textures, occlusions, lighting variation) in controlled settings. Conceptually, it departs from the dominant paradigm of aggregate-metric evaluation on domain-narrow or synthetic benchmarks (Sintel, KITTI, Spring) by arguing that confounder-specific, real-world evaluation is necessary to diagnose where models actually fail. The evaluation protocol builds on the PTLFlow framework and the Fl-all metric from KITTI, while the sparse annotation strategy follows Doersch et al.'s TAP-Vid protocol, making it a synthesis of existing tools applied to a new diagnostic purpose rather than a methodological departure from a specific prior method.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central finding—that models surpassing RAFT on Sintel, KITTI, and Spring stagnate or degrade on real-world data—challenges the field's implicit assumption that synthetic benchmark progress translates to deployment readiness, and the factorized analysis reveals a concrete trade-off between large-motion accuracy and robustness in stationary, photometrically varying scenes. The demonstration that adding TartanAir-scale synthetic data does not close the gap redirects the research agenda away from pure data/compute scaling toward new architectural or training strategies. Limitations include the relatively small FlowFactor dataset (1,000 pairs, 5 sparse points each), reliance on a single checkpoint regime (C+T Things), and the fact that the model pool is constrained to those available in PTLFlow, which may underrepresent the latest architectures. Open questions include whether the observed trade-offs are architectural or data-driven, and whether denser real-world annotations could enable targeted fine-tuning that breaks the stagnation.
