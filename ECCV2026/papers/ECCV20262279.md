## Problem

Standard vision-language models like CLIP learn a single shared embedding space, collapsing the multi-relational structure of art-historical reasoning (style, iconography, historical context) into one homogeneous alignment. While graph-based approaches can encode relational structure, they operate transductively, requiring the full graph at test time and thus failing on unseen artworks. The paper asks whether one can learn relation-aware multimodal representations that capture multiple valid image-text relationships while remaining fully inductive at inference.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262279/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an artwork image (RGB), a text description, and a relation type label (e.g., 'style', 'artist', 'date', 'genre') as input; the relation type can be inferred from the text itself at inference, so no external annotation is needed. It produces multiple relation-conditioned embeddings per item—the same image yields a different vector for each relation it participates in—enabling cross-modal retrieval where queries are scored in relation-specific subspaces, as well as art metadata classification. At inference, no graph connectivity is required; only the individual image or text and its relation type are needed.

## Method

CANVAS builds on a frozen OpenCLIP ViT-B/32 backbone, fine-tuning only the last three transformer blocks and projection heads. Its core mechanism is a sheaf-inspired relation-conditioned layer: for each image-text edge, the node embedding is concatenated with the relation-type embedding and passed through a three-layer MLP that outputs FiLM-style scale (γ) and shift (β) parameters, affinely modulating the node into a relation-specific subspace; N=3 such layers are stacked and averaged. To regularize training, the method constructs a line graph from the bipartite image-text graph and computes a heat-kernel diffusion matrix on its Laplacian, yielding a soft target that down-weights negatives sharing a common image or text. The final loss combines symmetric InfoNCE with a graph-regularized KL divergence (following SoftCLIP), weighted by a mixing coefficient η, so that structurally proximate pairs are penalized less harshly than truly unrelated ones.

## Results

CANVAS achieves the best image-to-text (I2T) retrieval across all three datasets, with Recall@10 reaching 0.514 on HERTZIANADP, 0.714 on SEMART+, and 0.781 on WikiArt+. In text-to-image (T2I) retrieval, CANVAS leads on HERTZIANADP (R@10 = 0.158) and SEMART+ (R@10 = 0.489), while remaining competitive on WikiArt+ (R@10 = 0.217 vs. SigLIP-ft's 0.307). For relation-aware classification, CANVAS achieves the best or second-best accuracy on every metadata task (Artist, Period, Genre, Style, Date, Material) across all three datasets, with particularly strong gains on relationally complex attributes like Style (0.637 on SEMART+) and Period (0.794 on HERTZIANADP).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CANVAS decisively outperforms all baselines in image-to-text retrieval: on HERTZIANADP (unseen to CLIP pretraining), it reaches I2T R@10 of 0.514 versus 0.084 for CLIP-ft and 0.022 for SigLIP; on SEMART+ it achieves 0.714 versus 0.150 for SigLIP-ft; and on WikiArt+ it reaches 0.781 versus 0.209 for SigLIP-ft. In text-to-image retrieval, CANVAS wins on HERTZIANADP (0.158 vs. 0.083 for SigLIP) and SEMART+ (0.489 vs. 0.446 for SigLIP-ft), but trails SigLIP-ft on WikiArt+ (0.217 vs. 0.307). Graph-based baselines (ArtSAGENet, GraphCLIP) perform poorly in retrieval (R@10 < 0.01 for ArtSAGENet) and are constrained by transductive requirements, while multi-vector document models (ColPali, ColQwen) and contrastive variants (MSC, RCML) also fall well short. For classification, CANVAS achieves the best or second-best accuracy on every relation type across all datasets, with no single baseline matching its consistency; it particularly excels on Style (0.637 on SEMART+) and Period (0.794 on HERTZIANADP), where single-embedding VLMs struggle most.

## Relation to Prior Work

CANVAS sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) contrastive vision-language pretraining (CLIP, SigLIP), which it fine-tunes with a lightweight sheaf-inspired module; (2) cellular sheaf theory and Neural Sheaf Diffusion (Bodnar et al., 2022), whose restriction maps are repurposed as relation-conditioned FiLM modulations that deform a shared embedding into per-relation subspaces; and (3) graph-regularized contrastive objectives (SoftCLIP, Gao et al., 2024), adapted here via a heat-kernel soft target computed on the line graph of the image-text bipartite graph. The key departure from prior graph-augmented art methods (ArtSAGENet, GraphCLIP) is inductivity: CANVAS requires no graph connectivity at inference, processing individual image-text-relation triples in isolation. It also extends beyond the purely semantic, within-image relations targeted by RCML and MSC to model heterogeneous, non-semantic relations (historical context, artist, date) between an artwork and its descriptions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that multi-relational alignment is not merely a theoretical nicety but yields large, consistent retrieval and classification gains, particularly on HERTZIANADP—a dataset absent from CLIP's pretraining corpus—suggesting the sheaf mechanism provides genuine generalization rather than memorization. The three newly released benchmarks (HERTZIANADP, SEMART+, WikiArt+) fill a gap in multi-relational art understanding evaluation. Open questions include whether the approach scales to dozens of relation types, how relation-type inference at inference time (mentioned but deferred to an appendix) performs in practice, and whether the sheaf-inspired modulation transfers to non-art domains (medical imaging, cultural heritage) as the authors speculate. The near-zero performance in the no-relation-modulation ablation underscores that the FiLM-based restriction maps, not the graph regularization alone, carry the representational load.
