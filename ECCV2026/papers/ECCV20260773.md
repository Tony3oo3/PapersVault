## Problem

Local 3D object editing remains challenging because existing methods require fully edited 2D views, precise 3D masks, or computationally expensive multi-stage pipelines (3D→2D→3D), none of which match how humans naturally specify edits—by pointing to a coarse region and describing the desired change. No prior approach handles this coarse, practical input setting end-to-end while preserving unedited regions and maintaining 3D consistency.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20260773/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a 3D object (voxelized to a 64³ grid and encoded into 16×16×16 structure latents and per-voxel texture latents via TRELLIS), a deliberately coarse 3D bounding box that overestimates the target region, and a single 2D image prompt showing the desired modification (not a fully edited multi-view rendering). It outputs an edited 3D object decodable as mesh, 3D Gaussian Splatting, or NeRF. During training, 24 views of the target part are rendered and one is randomly sampled per step as the conditioning image; at inference, the user supplies just one view, and the model is robust to viewing-angle mismatches.

## Method

EditVerse3D builds on the TRELLIS rectified-flow backbone, concatenating the input latent, a binary mask derived from the coarse bounding box, and random noise along the feature channel dimension for separate structure and texture flow-matching models. The key training innovation is a region-aware adaptive loss that (1) normalizes masked and unmasked region losses independently so neither dominates, (2) applies hard-example mining by up-weighting the top-τ% highest per-index losses, and (3) uses joint normalization of the object and mask to preserve spatial alignment. Data augmentation includes training with randomly scaled and shifted bounding boxes (rather than exact part shapes) and filtering out editing pairs with unrealistically small target volumes. The authors construct a large-scale dataset of ~85k meshes and 500k editing pairs by removing parts from objects in Partverse and Objaverse, treating restoration as an 'add' operation that generalizes to 'replace' edits.

## Results

On the Replace task, EditVerse3D achieves the best Chamfer Distance (0.005), PSNR (36.32 dB), SSIM (0.995), LPIPS (0.008), DINO similarity (0.981), and FID (28.34) across all six metrics. On the Add task, it similarly leads with CD 0.005, PSNR 28.67 dB, SSIM 0.962, LPIPS 0.029, DINO 0.984, and FID 2.960. The method also preserves unedited regions better than all baselines (e.g., CD 0.002 vs. 0.006 for Repaint on the unedited region), and runs in ~20 s—comparable to vanilla TRELLIS—while requiring only a coarse 3D bounding box and a single 2D image prompt.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EditVerse3D outperforms all four baselines on every metric for both Replace and Add tasks. The closest competitor is Repaint (a mask-based inpainting variant applied to TRELLIS), which EditVerse3D edges out by small but consistent margins—e.g., CD 0.005 vs. 0.007 and FID 28.34 vs. 29.32 on Replace, and PSNR 28.67 vs. 27.44 dB on Add. VoxHammer, which extends Repaint with intermediate-layer feature blending, ranks second-worst among the 3D-native methods, trailing EditVerse3D by roughly 4× in CD on Replace (0.022 vs. 0.005) and 2.8× in FID on Add (8.28 vs. 2.96). FlowEdit, which computes a vector-field difference between source and target prompts without any mask, performs poorly (CD 0.071 on Replace), confirming that unguided editing trajectories are hard to control in 3D. Instant3dit, which relies on editing four sparse 2D views and reconstructing 3D, is the weakest baseline by a large margin (CD 0.297 on Replace, PSNR 8.08 dB on Add), illustrating the cumulative error of the 3D→2D→3D pipeline. Notably, EditVerse3D achieves these gains while requiring only a coarse bounding box and a single 2D image prompt, whereas Repaint and VoxHammer need precise 3D masks and pre-edited 2D views, and Instant3dit demands exact 3D masks plus four edited renderings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on TRELLIS, a structured 3D latent generation framework, repurposing its encoder-decoder and rectified flow components for local editing rather than unconditional generation. It departs from the three dominant 3D editing paradigms—2D-render-edit-lift-back pipelines (e.g., Instant3dit), SDS-based optimization, and training-free latent-space editing (e.g., VoxHammer, Repaint, FlowEdit)—by eliminating the need for fully edited 2D views, precise 3D masks, or multi-stage reconstruction. The key conceptual shift is treating editing as a supervised flow-matching problem conditioned on a coarse 3D bounding box and a single reference image, trained end-to-end with a region-aware loss that balances masked and unmasked regions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical framing—accepting a coarse bounding box rather than a precise mask—aligns with how users actually interact with 3D content, lowering the barrier to 3D editing. The constructed dataset (~85k meshes, 500k editing pairs) addresses a long-standing data scarcity that has hindered supervised 3D editing research. However, the training data consists almost entirely of 'add' edits derived from part removal, and generalization to 'replace' edits, while demonstrated, remains untested for more complex operations like deletion or structural modification. Open questions include whether the approach scales to multi-region simultaneous editing, whether the coarse-box formulation can be replaced by more natural user interactions (e.g., drag or brush), and whether the TRELLIS backbone is essential or whether the region-aware loss transfers to other 3D generative architectures.
