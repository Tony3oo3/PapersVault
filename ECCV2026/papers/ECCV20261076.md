## Problem

Prior work on LLMs and early LVLM studies reported that test-time scaling methods (e.g., Chain-of-Thought prompting, self-consistency) are ineffective or even harmful for small models, leading to the assumption that LLM test-time scaling techniques do not transfer to vision-language models. This paper asks whether that assumption holds: can conventional LLM test-time scaling methods be directly applied to LVLMs, and if so, for which models, tasks, and compute budgets are they effective?

## Input / Output

Each LVLM receives an RGB image (encoded into visual tokens, compressed by a factor of 4 in the Qwen series) concatenated with a text prompt of the form {question};{think-prompt};{post-prompt}, where the think-prompt varies by method (e.g., 'think step-by-step' for CoT, the question itself for Prompt Repetition). The model generates a reasoning chain followed by a structured final answer (a single letter for multiple-choice, or a word/phrase for open-ended questions), with a maximum output length of 1,024 tokens. A reliable answer-extraction fallback re-prompts the model with the generated chain if the initial output does not comply with the required format.

## Method

This is a comprehensive empirical study rather than a new architecture: the authors systematically evaluate nine zero-shot test-time scaling methods (CoT, S-CoT, Plan-and-Solve, Self-Consistency, Self-Aggregation, Self-Refinement, Describe-Answer, Compositional CoT, and Prompt Repetition) across 13 instruction-tuned models spanning 2B–72B parameters (Qwen-2.5-VL, Qwen-3-VL, InternVL-3.5, Molmo2) on six benchmarks covering perception, mathematical reasoning, logical reasoning, and hallucination. Three key findings emerge: (1) small models (2B–8B) benefit most from test-time scaling, with gains up to ~30% that close or exceed the gap to 32B baselines; (2) on perception-only tasks, extra compute causes models to overthink and hallucinate, degrading accuracy; (3) attention analysis and causal image-token-dropping experiments show that visual information is encoded in a brief early window of generation, after which reasoning is dominated by text-only self-attention. The study further quantifies reasoning-chain quality via an LLM-as-a-judge protocol measuring rationale sufficiency and fine-grained sentence-level dynamics, validated against human evaluation.

## Results

Across 13 models and 6 benchmarks, test-time scaling yields the largest gains for small models: Qwen3-VL-2B with Self-Consistency improves WeMath accuracy from 35.46% to 64.25% (+28.8%), and Qwen3-VL-4B with simple CoT surpasses the Qwen3-VL-32B baseline on HallusionBench (75.50 vs. 74.76), WeMath (71.55 vs. 66.03), and LogicVista (57.59 vs. 44.42, +13.2%). On perception-focused benchmarks like RealWorldQA, most TTS methods degrade performance (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL-7B Self-Refinement drops from 70.20% to 47.97%). CoT prompting offers the best accuracy–runtime tradeoff, achieving strong reasoning gains at ~6 s per sample versus ~45 s for Self-Consistency.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Self-Consistency [36] delivers the largest absolute gains, boosting Qwen3-VL-2B WeMath from 35.46% to 64.25% and Qwen3-VL-4B LogicVista from 40.85% to 62.95%, but at the highest runtime cost (~45 s). Self-Aggregation [15] is a close second on reasoning benchmarks (e.g., Qwen3-VL-4B WeMath 75.40%) with moderate runtime (~31 s). Standard CoT [37] and Plan-and-Solve [34] provide strong reasoning improvements at low cost (~6–8 s), with CoT generally outperforming PaS on WeMath and LogicVista. Self-Refinement [19] yields moderate reasoning gains but causes severe degradation on perception tasks (Qwen2.5-VL-7B RealWorldQA drops from 70.20% to 47.97%). Describe-Answer [7] and CCoT [22], both designed specifically for VLMs, underperform standard LLM-derived methods: CCoT barely improves over baseline on most benchmarks (e.g., Qwen3-VL-4B WeMath 57.59% vs. CoT's 71.55%), and Describe-Answer often degrades reasoning scores. Prompt Repetition [12] is the cheapest method (same runtime as baseline) and provides a small but consistent boost on WeMath (~+4% for Qwen3-VL-2B) by enabling bidirectional attention over the prompt.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the full suite of zero-shot test-time scaling methods developed for LLMs (CoT, Self-Consistency, Self-Refinement, Self-Aggregation, Plan-and-Solve, Prompt Repetition) and applies them directly to LVLMs, challenging the prior consensus that these techniques are ineffective for small models. It departs from VLM-specific test-time scaling approaches (CCoT, DCoT, DD-CoT) by showing that the simpler, model-agnostic LLM methods outperform them. The work also builds on the mechanistic interpretability literature (attention analysis, causal interventions) to explain *why* test-time scaling helps or hurts, going beyond the purely empirical comparisons in prior studies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that small (2B–8B) instruction-tuned LVLMs benefit most from test-time scaling — reaching or exceeding 32B/72B baselines — has direct cost and latency implications for deploying VLMs in production, suggesting a practical accuracy-compute trade-off that practitioners can exploit. The complementary finding that extra compute degrades perception-only tasks, supported by attention decay analysis and causal image-token-dropping experiments, highlights an open question: how to automatically route queries to the appropriate compute budget (reasoning vs. perception) without a task classifier. The paper's scope is limited to image-text VQA; whether these findings extend to video understanding, multi-image reasoning, or agentic settings remains unexplored.
