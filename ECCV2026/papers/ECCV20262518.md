## Problem

Deploying AI-generated video detectors in real-world services requires ultra-low false positive rates (≤0.1%) on authentic content, a regime where standard metrics like AUROC fail to reflect actual operating behavior. Existing supervised and training-free detectors rely on clip-level aggregation that dilutes sparse, localized manipulation evidence, causing severe performance drops on partial edits where most of the video remains real. The paper addresses both the detection gap for partially edited videos and the evaluation gap for service-aligned operating points.

## Input / Output

SPLIT consumes a video clip as RGB frames, extracting segments of up to 2 seconds sampled at 8 fps, cropped 10% from the longer side and resized to 224×224 pixels. A frozen vision encoder (default XCLIP-B/16, but any transformer or CNN backbone works) produces per-frame patch tokens of shape T×N×D. The output is a single scalar manipulation score per clip, thresholded against a real-only calibrated value τ_α to yield a binary real vs. AI-generated/edited decision at a chosen FPR target (0.1%, 1%, or 5%).

## Method

SPLIT is entirely training-free and operates on patch tokens from a frozen pretrained vision encoder, computing two complementary signals without any learned parameters. Two-step Temporal Roughness (TTR) measures per-patch deviations from smooth temporal evolution by comparing accumulated 1-step path length against a normalized 2-step chord length, taking a log-ratio that amplifies flicker and local temporal misalignment. Local Spatial Motion Incoherence (LSMI) constructs a feature-space motion field (frame-to-frame patch differences) and computes forward-difference spatial gradients, capturing spatially inconsistent temporal changes typical of localized edits. The two signals are fused multiplicatively with a fixed gamma exponent (γ=8) to sharpen real-fake separation at strict thresholds. A threshold is calibrated on a real-only set to achieve a target FPR, and cross-real threshold transfer evaluates calibration stability when the real-data source shifts.

## Results

SPLIT achieves 74.45% Fake Recall at FPR=0.1% on FakeParts (partial edits), 85.16% on GenVideo (earlier generators), and 84.52% on ViF-Bench (2024–2025 generators), all under cross-real threshold transfer. At the looser FPR=5% operating point, these rise to 89.70%, 97.35%, and 98.46% respectively. The method is entirely training-free, requiring only a frozen XCLIP-B/16 forward pass plus lightweight patch-level TTR and LSMI computations, with inference time matching D3.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SPLIT outperforms all 11 baselines by a wide margin at FPR=0.1% across all three benchmarks. Against the strongest supervised baseline ReStraV (35.67% on FakeParts, 45.52% on GenVideo, 33.93% on ViF-Bench), SPLIT leads by +38.8, +39.6, and +50.6 points respectively. The training-free D3 baseline, which uses clip-level second-order temporal volatility, is dramatically outperformed: 2.28% vs. 74.45% on FakeParts and 0.97% vs. 84.52% on ViF-Bench, confirming that patch-level granularity is critical for partial edits. Among supervised video-level methods, AIGVDet (9.13% FakeParts, 45.38% GenVideo, 25.69% ViF-Bench) and FTCN (5.44%, 32.80%, 15.46%) are the next strongest but remain far below SPLIT. Image-level methods (NPR, STIL, FID) and face-centric detectors (MINTIME, TALL) perform poorly on partial edits, with most scoring below 10% at FPR=0.1% on FakeParts. SPLIT also demonstrates superior cross-real threshold transfer stability (Table 4), maintaining actual FPR close to the nominal target in both calibration directions, whereas ReStraV and AIGVDet exhibit severe domain-dependent drift (e.g., ReStraV jumps from 0.00% to 15.30% actual FPR when switching calibration source).

## Relation to Prior Work

SPLIT extends the training-free frozen-encoder line initiated by D3 (clip-level second-order temporal volatility) and ReStraV (frame-level trajectory straightness in DINOv2 space) by shifting the unit of analysis from global clip/frame summaries to individual patch tokens. Rather than pooling spatial information before measuring temporal dynamics, SPLIT preserves the 2D spatial grid of patch features and introduces two new local signals—Two-step Temporal Roughness (comparing 1-step vs. 2-step feature path lengths) and Local Spatial Motion Incoherence (spatial gradients of a feature-space motion field)—that are specifically sensitive to region-limited manipulations. The multiplicative gamma-corrected fusion and the service-aligned evaluation protocol (Fake Recall at fixed FPR with real-only calibration and cross-real threshold transfer) are novel contributions not present in prior training-free or supervised detectors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a training-free detector that generalizes to unseen 2024–2025 generators (Sora-2, Wan2.2, Kling) without retraining, while operating at the ultra-low FPR (≤0.1%) regime that real deployment services require. The service-aligned evaluation protocol and cross-real threshold transfer metric address a genuine gap in how the field reports detection performance, and the patch-level formulation offers a clear recipe for handling partial edits that defeat global approaches. Open questions include the severe degradation on inpainting (2.48% at FPR=0.1%), the sensitivity to JPEG compression (the reported numbers in Table 5 appear inconsistent with the text's claim of 'substantially less harmful'), and whether the approach extends to longer videos or higher-resolution content where patch count grows quadratically.
