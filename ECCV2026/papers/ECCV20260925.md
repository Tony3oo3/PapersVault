## Problem

Existing K-12 benchmarks fail to simulate authentic human examinations in three critical ways: they are static and susceptible to data contamination, they evaluate only final-answer accuracy while ignoring reasoning process and time constraints, and they require human intervention (manual OCR, image cropping) that makes the AI task input fundamentally different from what a student sees. This paper asks whether large multimodal models can truly navigate real high-school exam papers end-to-end under realistic grading criteria.

## Input / Output

The benchmark supports three input modalities of increasing difficulty: Text-Only (parsed question stem and options), Text-Image (question text plus cropped figure images), and Image-Only (a raw, uncropped snapshot of a full exam page plus a target question index, with no preprocessing). The model must output a step-by-step reasoning chain followed by a final answer enclosed in a \boxed{} command. Evaluation then produces four distinct scores per question: binary accuracy, a process score penalizing three defined reasoning-error types (condition interpretation, logical assumption, deductive reasoning), an efficiency-weighted accuracy (ARL) that discounts verbose generations, and a weighted Overall Exam Score (OES) on a 100-point scale that combines process and outcome components with educator-assigned point values.

## Method

The framework has two core technical pillars. First, a dynamic data construction pipeline uses the MinerU structural OCR framework (fine-tuned on Chinese exam papers) to convert raw PDFs into Markdown and cropped images, then applies an LLM parser with variable templates—injecting question-type definitions, target JSON schemas, and layout descriptors into the prompt—to decompose content into structured fields; a human-in-the-loop verification step and text-similarity de-duplication ensure quality, enabling periodic ingestion of fresh 2026 exam papers to mitigate contamination. Second, the Mock-Exam evaluation protocol scores each response across four dimensions: outcome accuracy via automated answer matching, a process score P_i = V_i − τ·Σx_{i,k} that deducts a fixed penalty τ=3 per detected reasoning error (CIE, LAE, DRE), an efficiency metric ARL that multiplies correctness by a log-ratio of average-to-actual generation length, and a weighted OES that combines process and outcome with w_p=0.5 and sub-question-level partial credit. All LLM-based judgments (process errors, answer verification) are aggregated across a panel of three non-self evaluators (drawn from GPT-4o, Gemini-3-flash, DeepSeek-V3, Qwen3-30B) to reduce single-judge bias, achieving Cohen's κ of 0.87 versus 0.92 for human-human agreement.

## Results

On LiveK12Bench (2,114 questions across Math, Physics, Chemistry, Biology), Gemini-3-pro achieves the highest Accuracy and Overall Exam Score (OES) across most subjects: 88.3% Acc / 90.3 OES in Math, 86.2% / 87.9 in Physics, 79.4% / 76.7 in Chemistry, and 90.7% / 78.6 in Biology. Claude-opus-4.6 leads in reasoning efficiency (ARL) across all four disciplines, indicating it achieves comparable accuracy with fewer generated tokens. Under the full Mock-Exam protocol (jointly evaluating process rigor and efficiency), GPT-5's score drops from 79 to 53 out of 100, exposing a substantial gap between idealized reasoning and exam-realistic performance. All models show the largest performance degradation in Chemistry and Biology relative to Mathematics and Physics, and the Image-Only (full-page) modality causes an average 33.7% accuracy drop for the GPT series.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Gemini-3-pro outperforms all other models on Accuracy and OES in Mathematics (88.3/90.3) and Physics (86.2/87.9), and leads in Process Exam Score for Chemistry (+2.6) and Biology (+3.1) over the second-best. Claude-opus-4.6 is the efficiency leader, achieving the highest ARL in every subject (e.g., 94.6 in Math vs. 82.9 for Gemini-3-pro), meaning it reaches similar accuracy with substantially fewer reasoning tokens. GPT-5 trails Gemini-3-pro by 5.6 Acc points in Math and 15.1 in Chemistry, and suffers the largest degradation under the full Mock-Exam protocol (OES drops to 53 in Biology). Among open-source models, Kimi-k2.5 is the strongest competitor (85.0 Math Acc, 85.4 Physics OES), approaching proprietary models while also showing superior visual robustness on the Complex Layout Set. Gemini-3-flash closely mirrors its pro counterpart (within 1–2 points on most metrics), making it a highly cost-effective alternative. GPT-4o, lacking explicit thinking tokens, is an outlier far below all other models (35.0 Math Acc, 24.2 OES), confirming that chain-of-thought reasoning is essential for K-12 exam performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of K-12 multimodal evaluation benchmarks (K12Vista, MMSciBench, M3Exam, GAOKAO-MM) by addressing three gaps those works leave open: static data vulnerable to contamination, one-dimensional accuracy-only scoring, and human-assisted input preparation. It repurposes structured document extraction (MinerU) and LLM-based parsing not as the contribution itself but as infrastructure for a dynamic ingestion pipeline, and borrows the LLM-as-judge paradigm from general evaluation literature while extending it to a multi-dimensional 'Mock Exam' protocol that scores process correctness, reasoning efficiency (ARL), and weighted exam performance simultaneously. The Image-Only full-page modality has no direct precedent in prior K-12 benchmarks, which all operate on pre-cropped or text-parsed inputs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a substantial and consistent gap (GPT-5: 79→53 OES) between idealized per-question accuracy and exam-realistic performance, suggesting that current LMMs are not yet reliable as autonomous tutors in unconstrained settings. The 'lucky guess' finding—models achieving high outcome scores while committing systematic reasoning errors—challenges the common assumption that correct final answers validate a model's reasoning quality. Open questions include whether the Chinese-exam-specific knowledge taxonomy generalizes to other curricula, whether the LLM-judge process scoring (87% F1 vs. 93% human-human ceiling) is sufficient for high-stakes deployment decisions, and whether the dynamic pipeline can maintain quality at scale as question volume grows.
