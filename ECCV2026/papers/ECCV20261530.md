## Problem

Multimodal LLMs produce reasoning chains with subtle step-level errors that cascade into incorrect answers, yet existing verification approaches either require expensive labeled supervision with inconsistent cross-task performance (domain-specific critics) or aggregate multiple scores via simple averaging that treats fundamentally different disagreement patterns as equivalent. The paper identifies that the *structure* of cross-modal disagreement—how and between whom agents disagree—carries diagnostic information about step validity that no existing training-free method exploits.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261530/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At each reasoning step, the base model (Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct) generates n=3 candidate continuations via temperature sampling (T=0.8, top-p=0.6) given an image, question, and partial reasoning trace. Three frozen agents (the same 7B model with modality-specific prompts) independently score each candidate with a scalar in [0,1] in complete isolation—no agent sees another's score. The output is a single verified continuation selected via a dual acceptance criterion (mean consensus confidence > 0.6 AND consensus dispersion < 0.1), with a fallback continuous ranking by s̄*−Δ* when no candidate passes both thresholds.

## Method

VERDICT frames step verification as a coupled scoring problem among three frozen, modality-specialized judges (Visual, Logical, Contextual), where each agent's reported score balances agreement with the group mean against fidelity to its own raw judgment, governed by a per-agent stubbornness parameter λ_i. This interaction is formalized as a coordination game whose unique Nash equilibrium (guaranteed by Rosen's concavity theorem) admits a closed-form linear-system solution, requiring no iterative optimization or training. The consensus preserves the raw mean while redistributing scores asymmetrically—high-λ agents resist consensus pressure more—so the resulting dispersion Δ* captures cross-modal conflict in a way no separable or variance-based measure can replicate (formalized in Proposition 1). A dual acceptance criterion filters by both mean confidence and dispersion, and among accepted candidates the highest mean consensus score is selected, making the framework a plug-in verifier with no base-model modification or task-specific tuning.

## Results

VERDICT achieves 70.15% average accuracy across six multimodal reasoning benchmarks, improving over the unverified base model (66.31%) by +3.84 points on average. Gains range from +1.62% on AI2D to +5.95% on CV-Bench-3D, with VERDICT improving on every single benchmark without any degradation. Among domain-agnostic baselines using the same three agents, VERDICT outperforms the strongest (Mean, 68.70%) by +1.45 points on average, and surpasses all five domain-specific critics (which range from 57.98% to 66.15% average) by 4.00–12.17 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VERDICT outperforms all five domain-specific critics on average accuracy: LLaVA-Critic (66.15%), Critic-V (64.74%), DreamPRM (64.06%), Sherlock (60.69%), and VisionSR (57.98%), with margins of +4.00 to +12.17 points. Critically, every domain-specific critic degrades below the base model on at least two benchmarks (e.g., Sherlock drops 18.26 points on CV-Bench-3D; VisionSR collapses 22.21 points on CV-Bench-3D), whereas VERDICT improves on all six. Among domain-agnostic baselines that share the same three frozen agents, Mean is the strongest simple aggregator (68.70% avg), and VERDICT beats it by +1.45 points on average and by +0.68 to +2.57 points on every individual benchmark. The Variance baseline (67.65%), which explicitly rejects high-variance candidates, still trails VERDICT by 2.50 points on average, confirming that treating all disagreement symmetrically is insufficient. The paper's key structural claim is that VERDICT is the only method that simultaneously achieves training-free operation, disagreement-aware scoring, and plug-in compatibility, and the only one that never degrades below the unverified base model on any tested benchmark.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from two established lines of step-level verification: domain-specific process reward models (PRM800K, VisualPRM, Sherlock, LLaVA-Critic) that require expensive labeled supervision, and training-free aggregation methods (Weaver, reward model ensembles, variance filtering) that combine scores via averaging or majority voting. Its conceptual departure is framing verification as a coupled scoring problem among frozen, modality-specialized judges, formalized as a coordination game whose unique Nash equilibrium (via Rosen's concave n-person game theorem) yields a closed-form consensus. Unlike prior multi-agent formulations that rely on adversarial zero-sum interactions (e.g., GANs), VERDICT employs cooperative coupling where each agent's adjusted score depends on all others, making the structure of cross-modal disagreement explicit rather than averaging it away.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in providing a training-free, domain-agnostic verifier that never degrades below the base model on any tested benchmark—a property no evaluated domain-specific critic achieves—making it attractive for data-scarce or rapidly shifting deployment settings. The finding that the consensus formulation's advantage over simple averaging widens as judge quality decreases (from +0.68 at 7B to +1.21 at 2B) suggests the algorithmic structure can partially compensate for weaker verifiers, a useful property for resource-constrained settings. Key limitations include the failure mode where all agents are confidently wrong (consensus converges on incorrect assessment) and a 3.8× wall-clock overhead from multi-agent scoring. Open questions include characterizing how frequently the 'all agents wrong' regime occurs in practice, whether adaptive verification at high-uncertainty steps can reduce overhead, and whether the coordination-game formulation generalizes to non-multimodal settings such as compositional code verification or reward model ensembles.
