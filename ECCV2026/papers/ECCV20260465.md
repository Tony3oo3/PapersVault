## Problem

Existing event-based feature detection and tracking methods fail to sustain throughput above the incoming event rate, making them unable to process live event streams without lossy sampling. Most state-of-the-art approaches rely on GPU acceleration or fixed temporal windows that cannot adapt to variable motion, rendering them unsuitable for power- and compute-constrained embedded edge devices.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20260465/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a live asynchronous event stream where each event encodes pixel coordinates (x, y), a timestamp, and a binary polarity. Rather than processing at a fixed cadence, the pipeline is motion-defined: it activates only when the per-active-pixel event density exceeds a threshold, so static scenes produce no computation. Outputs are corner detections with spatio-temporal stability, short intra-slice tracklets (linear spatio-temporal segments with fitted velocity), and long-lived feature tracks maintained by per-feature Kalman filters, all produced without any frame-based input or learned components.

## Method

SPEEDTrack's core contribution is the Motion-Defined Concurrent Slices (MDCS) architecture, which co-optimizes algorithmic structure with embedded CPU characteristics. An activity slicer monitors the ratio of events to active pixels and spawns self-contained slices only when motion density exceeds a threshold, eliminating wasted computation during static periods. Within each slice, a slice-local ordinal surface (storing the index of the most recent event per pixel) replaces global per-pixel queues, enabling independent eHarris corner detection with an ignore mask that skips redundant re-computation. Detected corners are fit to linear spatio-temporal models to produce tracklets, which are then associated across slices via a constant-velocity Kalman filter with KD-tree candidate retrieval. The entire pipeline is expressed as a task graph where a runtime scheduler dynamically assigns ready tasks to available cores, achieving near-linear scaling across CPU cores with minimal inter-thread synchronization because each slice is processed independently.

## Results

On the EDS dataset, SPEEDTrack achieves a Stable Feature Age (SFA) of 0.558 and Expected Feature Age (EFA) of 0.421, surpassing all event-only baselines (AEB-Tracker: 0.300/0.290, HASTE: 0.070/0.050, EM-ICP: 0.115/0.090) and approaching the learning-based DeepEvT* (0.515/0.430). On the EC dataset, it attains SFA 0.822 and EFA 0.713, second only to ETAP (0.870/0.851). For pose estimation, SPEEDTrack achieves AUC of 14.4%/20.8%/27.2% (5°/10°/20°) on EC and 10.9%/14.0%/17.5% on EDS, ranking second overall behind SuperEvent. In terms of runtime, SPEEDTrack sustains a throughput of 4.92 M events/s with 49.7 μs latency on an embedded CPU, exceeding the mean event rate of the EDS dataset and outperforming all compared methods in throughput.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SPEEDTrack outperforms all traditional event-only trackers by a wide margin: on EDS it beats AEB-Tracker (0.300→0.558 SFA), EM-ICP (0.115→0.558), and HASTE (0.070→0.558); on EC it surpasses AEB-Tracker (0.540→0.822), EM-ICP (0.320→0.822), and HASTE (0.425→0.822). Against the strongest event-only learning-based method ETAP [14], SPEEDTrack trails by 0.184 SFA on EDS (0.558 vs 0.742) and by 0.048 SFA on EC (0.822 vs 0.870), but does so without any GPU or neural network. Compared to the hybrid DeepEvT* [29] (events-only, learning-based), SPEEDTrack edges ahead on EDS SFA (0.558 vs 0.515) but falls slightly on EFA (0.421 vs 0.430), while on EC it is behind on both metrics (0.822/0.713 vs 0.785/0.770 for SFA/EFA respectively—actually ahead on SFA). For pose estimation (Table 3), SPEEDTrack is second only to SuperEvent [3] on both datasets (EC: 14.4% vs 22.7% at 5°; EDS: 10.9% vs 15.2% at 5°), while substantially outperforming RATE, EventPoint, and LLAK. In throughput, SPEEDTrack (4.92 Mev/s) exceeds the mean EDS event rate and all other methods shown in Fig. 1R, whereas ETAP and DeepEvT require GPU acceleration and cannot run on the embedded CPU.

## Relation to Prior Work

SPEEDTrack adapts the classical eHarris/eFAST corner detector (Mueggler et al., BMVC 2017) into a slice-parallel formulation with an ordinal-surface representation and an ignore mask, but its central contribution is architectural rather than algorithmic: the Motion-Defined Concurrent Slices (MDCS) framework couples motion-adaptive event slicing with task-graph scheduling to maximize multi-core CPU utilization. This departs from the dominant trajectory in event-based feature tracking—where prior work (HASTE, RATE, DeepEvT, ETAP) focuses on algorithmic innovations such as template matching, ICP alignment, or deep feature networks—by treating the hardware-aware co-optimization of established components as the primary problem. The paper explicitly positions itself as a systems-oriented contribution that makes existing primitives viable on embedded CPUs without GPU acceleration.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in closing a deployment gap: most SOTA event-based trackers require GPU acceleration and cannot sustain throughput above the mean event rate, making them unsuitable for power-constrained edge devices. SPEEDTrack demonstrates that a learning-free pipeline on an 8-core Arm CPU can exceed the mean event rate of the EDS dataset while achieving tracking accuracy (SFA 0.558 on EDS) competitive with deep methods like DeepEvT*, at only 12 W average power. Key limitations remain: the method cannot handle extreme event rates (~100 M events/s), does not recover lost tracks after occlusion, and is sensitive to non-egomotion events (e.g., AC-frequency light flicker). The MDCS architecture is task-agnostic and could be extended to other event-based perception tasks, and the authors plan to apply it to 3D vision.
