## Problem

Video VLMs rely on spatiotemporal self-attention, causing video-prefill compute to grow quadratically with the number of frames, which rules out real-time streaming and makes long videos prohibitively expensive. Existing efficiency methods (frame subsampling, token compression, sliding-window recency) either degrade accuracy substantially or merely redistribute quadratic cost over a shorter sequence without changing the scaling law. The paper asks whether streaming video prefill on a frozen backbone can approximate full self-attention with a small, principled set of carried tokens rather than an ad-hoc recency window.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a video sampled at 1 FPS (capped at 512 frames per example) and a text question, and produces a standard autoregressive text answer. It operates entirely at inference time on a frozen pretrained VLM backbone (no fine-tuning or architectural changes), processing frames sequentially in a streaming fashion. The video KV cache is built incrementally frame-by-frame, and the final text generation conditions on the full per-frame detailed cache, so the output quality depends on all video tokens being retained for decoding even though cross-frame attention during prefill is restricted.

## Method

StateKV maintains two per-layer KV caches during streaming video prefill: a fixed-capacity compressed state (cstate, budget B tokens) that carries cross-frame context, and a full detailed state (dstate) that accumulates all per-frame tokens for final decoding. When processing frame n, the model attends only to the concatenation of the previous frame's cstate and the current frame's tokens, yielding O(1) marginal cost per frame. After each frame, the cstate is refreshed by scoring all candidate tokens (previous cstate entries plus current-frame tokens) with video-only attention mass and retaining the top-B, implementing an importance-based eviction/admission rule motivated by the observation that inter-frame attention concentrates on a small set of slowly-evolving 'temporal sink' tokens. After all N frames are processed, standard autoregressive decoding runs over the full dstate, so the compressed state is used only to approximate cross-frame interactions during encoding. RoPE positions are tracked via a virtual sequence length independent of the physical cstate size, and a single RoPE scaling factor is fixed end-to-end to keep cached keys compatible with generated tokens.

## Results

Across seven model backbones (InternVL3 1B/2B/8B, Qwen3-VL 2B/4B/8B, Eagle2.5-8B) and three long-video benchmarks (VideoMME, MLVU, OVOBench Real-Time), StateKV (B=4096) stays within roughly 1 point of full self-attention on average while reducing video-prefill complexity from O(N²) to O(N). On VideoMME, StateKV-8B (InternVL3) achieves 62.52% vs. 64.19% for Full SA and 54.56% for ReKV; on the largest model (Qwen3-VL-8B), StateKV reaches 68.11% vs. 70.52% Full SA. A key practical finding is that StateKV-8B at B=4096 matches or exceeds Full SA-1B (46.2%) at comparable FLOPs, demonstrating that compute savings can be reinvested in model scale.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole external baseline is ReKV (Di et al., ICLR 2025), a sliding-window streaming-prefill method that retains only the most recent R frames for cross-frame attention. StateKV outperforms ReKV on every model–benchmark combination in Table 1, with average gaps of roughly 10 points on VideoMME, 12 points on MLVU, and 13 points on OVOBench. The advantage is most dramatic on MLVU, where ReKV suffers severe instability (e.g., InternVL3-2B drops to 5.49% vs. 57.31% for StateKV), suggesting that strict recency windows are a poor match to the attention patterns learned by these backbones. StateKV also tracks Full SA much more closely: the average gap to Full SA is under 1.5 points across all settings, whereas ReKV lags Full SA by 5–20 points depending on model and benchmark. The paper further shows that at matched FLOPs, StateKV's compute-accuracy frontier is strictly above ReKV's, and that the FLOPs savings enable running a model two scales larger (e.g., InternVL3-8B + StateKV) at a similar per-frame cost to a much smaller Full SA baseline (InternVL3-1B).

## Relation to Prior Work

StateKV builds directly on the streaming-prefill paradigm introduced by ReKV, which processes video frames sequentially with a sliding-window mechanism to construct the video KV cache. The key conceptual departure is replacing ReKV's strict recency prior with an importance-based token selection that carries a fixed-capacity set of 'temporal sink' tokens between frames, motivated by the attention-sink literature from language transformers. This positions StateKV between fixed-budget KV compression methods (InfiniPot-V, StreamMem, MovieChat) and streaming approximations (ReKV, HERMES, LiveVLM, StreamKV): it compresses the running state used during prefill while preserving full per-frame detail for final decoding, rather than either discarding information or retaining all past frames.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: reducing video-prefill from O(N²) to O(N) enables real-time streaming and makes hour-long video understanding computationally feasible, and the FLOPs savings can be reinvested in larger backbones so that a bigger StateKV model outperforms a smaller Full SA model at the same compute budget. A key limitation is that the mechanistic validation of the attention-sink assumptions is limited to the tested models and inputs; untested or future backbones may not exhibit the same concentrated cross-frame attention structure. Open questions include whether the importance-based selection generalizes to architectures with fundamentally different attention patterns (e.g., state-space hybrids like VAMBA) and whether the slow-evolution assumption holds for highly dynamic video content.
