## Problem

Cross-entropy loss, the de-facto standard for training classifiers, encourages over-confident predictions, continues updating weights after correct classification, and can decrease even as predictions worsen. Specialized losses (LogitNorm, logit-adjusted, DICE) improve specific tasks but perform poorly outside their domain. The paper seeks a single general-purpose loss that performs competitively across image classification, OOD rejection, adversarial robustness, imbalanced learning, continual learning, and semantic segmentation.

## Input / Output

The loss operates on the raw logits (pre-softmax) from the final layer of any classifier, paired with the ground-truth class index. For image classification it produces a class label; for semantic segmentation it produces a per-pixel class map; for OOD/adversarial evaluation it produces a rejection decision based on maximum softmax probability or maximum logit score. The only task-specific configuration is the margin: HEM sets per-class margins inversely proportional to class frequency (μ_i = √(M/ns_i)), while HEM- uses a single shared margin.

## Method

HEM defines a per-logit error e_i = max(0, y_i − y_l + μ_i) for all incorrect classes, where y_l is the correct-class logit and μ_i is the margin. The key innovation is the adaptive combination of these errors: first, all errors below the (detached) mean are zeroed out, then the mean is computed over only the remaining above-zero values. This two-step thresholding keeps gradient magnitude stable throughout training—early on it focuses on the largest errors, and late in training it prevents the loss from collapsing to zero when only a few errors remain. Two variants are provided: HEM (class-specific margins scaled by inverse class frequency to handle imbalance) and HEM- (a single shared margin for fair comparison with CE and MM). The loss has a single hyperparameter M (set to 2000), and produces exactly zero loss for any sample whose correct logit exceeds all others by at least the margin, enabling computational-graph pruning during training.

## Results

Across 10 evaluation metrics spanning 13 datasets and 19 architectures, HEM loss outperforms CE on 8 of 10 tasks. The headline gains are: +4.52% on unknown class rejection (OOD detection), +17.17% on AutoAttack adversarial rejection, +5.31% on semantic segmentation (mIoU), and +1.27% on continual learning. HEM's only losses are on clean and corrupted image classification with balanced data, where CE retains a small 1.19% and 1.07% advantage respectively. On imbalanced data, HEM reverses the clean-accuracy gap (HEM +0.70% over CE) and extends its OOD advantage to +9.33%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CE loss, HEM trades a small ~1.2% drop in clean/corrupt accuracy (balanced data) for large gains in robustness: +4.52% OOD rejection, +17.17% adversarial rejection, +1.27% continual learning, and +5.31% semantic segmentation. Against LogitNorm (LN), a specialised OOD-rejection loss, HEM matches or slightly exceeds LN on OOD rejection but decisively outperforms it on continual learning and semantic segmentation, where LN performs very poorly. Against Logit-adjusted (LA), designed for imbalanced data, LA achieves higher clean/corrupt accuracy on long-tailed data, but HEM surpasses LA on OOD rejection and semantic segmentation. Against DICE, the standard segmentation loss, HEM outperforms it on every task including semantic segmentation itself, where DICE performs no better than CE. Against the original multi-class margin (MM) loss, HEM's adaptive error-combining (mean-of-above-zero + thresholding) yields consistent improvements across all 10 metrics, with the gap widening as the number of classes increases.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the multi-class margin (MM) loss of Crammer and Singer (2002), identifying that its naive averaging of per-logit errors causes unstable gradient magnitudes during training. It departs from the field's default reliance on cross-entropy by arguing that margin-based losses, properly modified, can serve as a general-purpose classification loss for DNNs. The paper positions HEM against a suite of task-specific losses—LogitNorm for OOD rejection, logit-adjusted loss for imbalanced data, and DICE for segmentation—showing that each excels in its target domain but degrades sharply outside it, motivating a single loss that performs competitively across all of them.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

HEM offers practitioners a drop-in replacement for CE that simultaneously improves OOD rejection, adversarial robustness, continual learning, and semantic segmentation without architectural changes, which is practically valuable given that real-world deployments often require multiple robustness properties at once. The key open question is whether the heuristic for setting class-specific margins (inversely proportional to class frequency) can be improved or learned, as it underperforms on segmentation where imbalance patterns differ from classification. The ~1.2% clean-accuracy cost on balanced data is small but non-zero, and the paper acknowledges that hyper-parameters were tuned for CE, so a fully re-optimised HEM training recipe might close that gap. Extending HEM to non-visual domains (e.g., language) and exploring synergies with existing regularisation or augmentation strategies are natural next steps.
