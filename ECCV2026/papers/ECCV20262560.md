## Problem

Standard 3D printing pipelines require a watertight mesh as input, making the most common 3D representation—unoriented point clouds from LiDAR, RGB-D cameras, and generative models—incompatible with fabrication. The typical workaround of reconstructing meshes from point clouds is ill-posed and introduces geometric artifacts (incorrect faces, holes, topological inconsistencies) that propagate into invalid toolpaths and printing failures. No prior method generates executable G-code directly from raw point clouds without this error-prone mesh reconstruction step.

## Input / Output

The method takes an unoriented 3D point cloud (30,000 surface points normalized to [-1,1]) and a set of slice indices as input. For each slice, points within a vertical window (Multi-Slice Conditioning) are projected onto a 256×256 XY grid, producing a multi-channel tensor (occupancy mask, density map, height-offset map) that serves as the per-slice image input. The output is a complete, executable FDM G-code file specifying nozzle XY motion, Z-height, and extrusion length for every segment, generated without any intermediate mesh representation.

## Method

PrintAnything introduces a Geometric Plan (G-plan) map—a compact per-slice 2D representation with three channels: an occupancy map (where material is deposited), a region map (structural categories: wall, infill, support, skirt, gap), and a flow map (spatially varying extrusion density). A Point Transformer V3 encoder produces a global shape feature, which is combined with an MLP-embedded slice height via FiLM conditioning and injected into a U-Net (GPNet) that predicts the three G-plan maps from the slice-projected 2D input. A lightweight infill recommender then selects a pattern and scale from a template library by optimizing a Pareto frontier of strength and cost proxies, and a deterministic converter rasterizes the final maps into ordered toolpaths with physical coordinate mapping and per-segment extrusion computation. Training is fully supervised on the Slice-100K dataset using a multi-task loss combining BCE+Dice (occupancy), weighted cross-entropy (region), and masked Huber (flow) terms.

## Results

On Slice-100K, PrintAnything achieves a Chamfer Distance of 0.047, F1_3D of 0.741, and F1_2D of 0.677, outperforming all three mesh-based baselines across every metric. The method's advantage is most pronounced at the slice level (F1_2D), where it surpasses the next-best baseline (Poisson, 0.587) by 9.0 points, indicating more faithful per-layer toolpath generation. All evaluations are conducted on a 9:1 train/test split of the Slice-100K dataset with 30k sampled surface points per shape.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PrintAnything outperforms all three mesh-reconstruction-then-slice baselines on every reported metric. Against the classical Poisson surface reconstruction baseline, it reduces CD by 46.6% (0.047 vs. 0.088) and improves F1_2D by 9.0 points (0.677 vs. 0.587). Against the learning-based DWG method, which is the closest competitor in 3D geometry (CD 0.062, F1_3D 0.712), PrintAnything still leads on all three metrics, with the largest gap in slice-level fidelity (F1_2D: 0.677 vs. 0.496, a 18.1-point margin), suggesting that mesh artifacts from DWG degrade toolpath quality disproportionately. Against MeshAnything, the gap is the widest: PrintAnything achieves 70% lower CD (0.047 vs. 0.157) and 32.1 points higher F1_2D (0.677 vs. 0.356), indicating that autoregressive mesh generation introduces substantial geometric errors that propagate into invalid or fragmented toolpaths. The consistent pattern across baselines is that mesh-based pipelines accumulate reconstruction errors that are amplified during slicing, whereas the direct G-plan map prediction avoids this failure mode entirely.

## Relation to Prior Work

PrintAnything departs from the dominant point-cloud-to-fabrication paradigm, which first reconstructs a mesh (via Poisson, DWG, or MeshAnything) and then relies on a conventional slicer to produce G-code. Rather than building on any single prior G-code generation method, the paper repurposes standard components—Point Transformer V3 for global encoding, a U-Net with FiLM conditioning for slice-wise prediction—and introduces the G-plan map (occupancy, region, flow) as a novel intermediate representation that directly encodes the geometric and extrusion properties a slicer would otherwise derive from a mesh. The slice-wise point projection with Multi-Slice Conditioning is a new input formulation tailored to the layer-by-layer nature of FDM, and the lightweight infill recommender with Pareto-frontier selection is a practical addition not seen in prior G-code generation work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By eliminating the mesh-reconstruction bottleneck, PrintAnything opens a direct path from raw sensor data (LiDAR, RGB-D, 3D scans) to printable toolpaths, which is practically significant for rapid prototyping and reverse-engineering workflows where mesh repair is costly or infeasible. The G-plan map provides a compact, interpretable bridge between point-cloud geometry and G-code that could be extended to other printing technologies or more complex material constraints. However, the evaluation is limited to a single dataset (Slice-100K), a single FDM printer (Bambu Lab X1-Carbon), and a fixed set of printing parameters; generalization to diverse printer settings, multi-material printing, and non-FDM processes remains an open question the authors acknowledge as future work.
