## Problem

Existing streaming video diffusion models that support interactive prompt switching suffer from two failure modes: (i) re-cache at each prompt boundary discards prior KV semantic states, weakening cross-segment continuity and degrading perceptual quality; and (ii) unbounded RoPE indices during long rollouts drift outside the pretrained backbone's bounded positional range, eroding motion priors and long-horizon dynamics.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20260061/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes a sequence of six 10-second text prompts (switched at interaction boundaries) and Gaussian noise, producing a 60-second video at 832×480 resolution built from sequential 5-second clips. Generation is fully causal and streaming: each new clip conditions only on a bounded KV cache (sink + local + junction tokens) and the current prompt embedding, with no access to the full frame history. The base architecture is Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B with causal attention masking inherited from CausVid.

## Method

Anchor Forcing introduces two cache-centric mechanisms. First, an anchor-guided re-cache maintains an anchor memory of three parts—sink cache (fixed KV from the earliest N_S frames), junction cache (KV from the first N_J frames after each prompt switch), and rolling local cache (most recent W frames)—and warm-starts re-cache at each prompt boundary from this anchor memory rather than discarding prior KV states, thereby preserving boundary-adjacent semantic evidence. Second, a tri-region RoPE assigns region-specific, range-bounded positional indices to the sink, local, and junction caches separately, capping all effective RoPE positions at P_max=21 (matching the pretrained CausVid range) instead of using a monotonically growing global index, which keeps attention geometry within the regime the backbone was trained on. The full model is trained via distribution-matching distillation (DMD) followed by LoRA-based streaming long-tuning up to 60 seconds, allowing the generator to learn adaptive cross-region retrieval during distillation.

## Results

On the 60-second interactive benchmark (six 10-second prompt switches), Anchor Forcing achieves a dynamic degree of 73.00 and overall quality score of 82.25, substantially outperforming the strongest streaming baselines (MemFlow: 45.47/79.35; Infinity-RoPE: 35.17/79.98; LongLive: 26.37/78.92). Segment-wise ViCLIP scores show that while early-segment semantic adherence is comparable across methods (~24.4–24.9), Anchor Forcing degrades far less over time, maintaining 23.5–23.6 in the final 30 seconds versus 21.6–22.2 for baselines. On the 5-second single-prompt VBench, Anchor Forcing attains the highest total (83.99) and quality (84.84) among streaming AR models, approaching the non-streaming Wan2.1 teacher (84.27/85.24). On 30-second generation, it leads in total (83.25), quality (84.40), and dynamic degree (79.17) while trailing LongLive and MemFlow on semantic score (78.67 vs. 80.64/79.31).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the three interactive streaming baselines, Anchor Forcing wins decisively on dynamic degree (73.00 vs. 45.47 for MemFlow, 35.17 for Infinity-RoPE, 26.37 for LongLive) and overall quality (82.25 vs. 79.35/79.98/78.92). On segment-wise CLIP, Anchor Forcing is marginally lower in the first two segments (24.48/24.35 vs. 24.87/24.82 for MemFlow/LongLive) but pulls ahead from 20 s onward, with the gap widening to ~1.0–2.0 points by 50–60 s, indicating substantially less temporal degradation. In the 5-second non-interactive setting (Table 2), Anchor Forcing achieves the best total (83.99) and quality (84.84) among streaming AR models, edging out DeepForcing (83.85/84.61) and Self-Forcing (83.83/84.60), though it trails them slightly on semantic score (80.57 vs. 80.84/80.79). On 30-second generation (Table 3), Anchor Forcing leads in total (83.25), quality (84.40), dynamic degree (79.17), imaging quality (69.37), and temporal style (24.17), but concedes the semantic score to LongLive (80.64) and MemFlow (79.31). In the human preference study, Anchor Forcing is preferred over all baselines by a wide margin (64.55% temporal coherence, 67.73% prompt following, 63.18% perceptual quality), with the next-best method (LongLive) receiving only 15.45%/15.00%/14.55%.

## Relation to Prior Work

Anchor Forcing sits squarely in the line of distilled streaming autoregressive video diffusion models (CausVid, Self-Forcing, LongLive, MemFlow, Infinity-RoPE) that reuse rolling KV caches with sink tokens for efficient long-horizon generation. It departs from prior re-cache strategies (LongLive, MemFlow) that discard all prior KV states at prompt switches by introducing an anchor memory that integrates sink, junction, and local caches to preserve boundary-adjacent semantic evidence. On the positional-encoding side, it extends RoPE from the unbounded global indexing used in Infinity-RoPE and LongLive to a tri-region, range-bounded scheme that keeps effective indices within the 21-frame regime seen during CausVid/Wan pretraining, directly targeting the positional distribution shift that weakens pretrained motion priors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies two concrete, well-isolated failure modes in interactive streaming video generation (evidence loss at prompt switches and RoPE distribution shift) and provides targeted, relatively lightweight fixes that yield large gains in motion dynamics without architectural changes. The tri-region RoPE in particular is a simple, architecture-agnostic indexing trick that could be adopted by other streaming video models to better retain pretrained motion priors. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to larger backbones (the 1.3B Wan model is modest), to interaction types beyond discrete prompt switching (e.g., continuous camera control or object-level edits), and whether the 60-second evaluation horizon is sufficient to stress-test the bounded-indexing strategy at truly long horizons.
