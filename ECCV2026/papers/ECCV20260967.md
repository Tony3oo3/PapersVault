## Problem

Mainstream OOD detection research pursues unconstrained SOTA accuracy, producing methods (CLIP-based, large supervised models) whose inference complexity exceeds TinyML hardware budgets by orders of magnitude (e.g., ~35,000,000 MFLOPs vs. the ~300 MFLOPs ceiling). This paper argues the meaningful benchmark is maximizing accuracy within a fixed, TinyML-appropriate inference budget, and shows that existing efficient methods (e.g., VAE-based) still sacrifice detection accuracy to meet that budget.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image (32×32 for CIFAR-100, 224×224 for ImageNet-1k) and produces an OOD score computed as the KL divergence between the test image's encoder posterior and the stored in-distribution mean/variance in latent space; a binary ID/OOD decision is made by thresholding this score. Training requires only unlabeled in-distribution samples (no class labels), and inference is a single encoder forward pass followed by a closed-form KL computation—no importance sampling, no decoder call, no post-hoc de-biasing.

## Method

The framework is a VAE trained in three stages. Stage 1 introduces a latent-space de-biasing loss that forces pixel-intensity perturbed variants of each ID image to converge toward a calibrated reference point in the latent space via a KL-divergence regularizer, internalizing the de-biasing process during training so that inference requires only one encoder pass (≈35 MFLOPs) instead of hundreds of importance-sampling iterations. Stage 2 generates hard negative samples through a three-step pipeline: Mosaic augmentation initializes candidates, iterative optimization maximizes their KL divergence from ID statistics in both the latent space and at every BatchNorm layer of the frozen encoder, and a screening step retains only samples whose latent-space likelihood falls between the 5th and 95th percentiles (near the ID boundary but outside it). Stage 3 fine-tunes the Stage-1 model on ID samples plus the screened hard negatives, using a hinge-style reconstruction loss that penalizes the decoder for reconstructing negatives too well. At inference, the OOD score is simply D_KL(g_φ(z|x_test) ‖ N(μ_ID, σ²_ID)), yielding a single scalar per image with no additional sampling or post-processing.

## Results

On CIFAR-100, the proposed method achieves AUROC scores of 67.3 (Tiny-ImageNet), 63.4 (CIFAR-10), 73.1 (Texture), 75.5 (SVHN), and 73.2 (Places365), outperforming the best unsupervised baseline (DP) by 1.2–1.5 AUROC points across all test cases. On ImageNet-1k, it reaches 62.0 (NINCO), 59.6 (SSB-hard), 65.2 (iNaturalist), 66.5 (Texture), and 66.9 (OpenImage-O), again surpassing all baselines. The method operates at only ~35 MFLOPs inference complexity with 0.12 ms latency on an NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano, compared to 292.53 ms for NNGuide and 29.21 ms for DP, while also having the smallest inference model size (1.0× relative).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all 16 baselines across every ID/OOD test case on both CIFAR-100 and ImageNet-1k, under a matched TinyML inference latency on a real Jetson Orin Nano device. Among unsupervised VAE baselines, DP (DisCoPatch) is the closest competitor, but the proposed method exceeds it by 1.2–1.5 AUROC (e.g., 75.5 vs. 74.0 on CIFAR-100/SVHN; 66.9 vs. 65.6 on ImageNet-1k/OpenImage-O). The de-biasing VAE baseline (DB [8]) is the weakest VAE method, trailing the proposed approach by 9–12 AUROC points, highlighting the critical role of latent-space de-biasing. Among supervised methods, NNGuide achieves the highest AUROC (e.g., 73.4 on CIFAR-100/SVHN) but requires 292.53 ms latency and a 1.5× larger model, whereas the proposed method reaches 75.5 AUROC at 0.12 ms with the smallest footprint. For negative-sample generation, the paper's three-step strategy (Mosaic init → KL-driven update → percentile screening) surpasses CSI, CnC, VOS, NPOS, and HamOS by 1.3–8.6 AUROC across all test cases (Table 2), with the largest gap on ImageNet-1k/NINCO (62.0 vs. 53.4 for CSI). Inference efficiency is also a decisive advantage: the method runs at 0.12 ms with 1.0× relative model size, versus 1.27 ms / 3.5× for DB and 29.21 ms / 5.1× for DP.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on VAE-based OOD detection, particularly the de-biasing VAE (DB) method of Chauhan et al. [8], which corrects pixel-intensity bias via importance sampling at inference. The key departure is relocating this de-biasing from inference-time (hundreds of sampling operations) to training-time through a latent-space KL regularization inspired by CR-VAE [33], eliminating the need for repeated sampling entirely. The negative sample generation pipeline repurposes the BatchNorm feature-statistics idea from DeepInversion [41] in an inverted manner (maximizing rather than minimizing KL divergence) and combines it with Mosaic augmentation and a probabilistic screening step, positioning it against existing outlier-synthesis methods (CSI, CnC, VOS, NPOS, HamOS). The paper carves new ground by explicitly framing OOD detection under a fixed TinyML inference budget (~300 MFLOPs) as the primary evaluation criterion, rather than unconstrained accuracy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work addresses a genuine deployment gap: mainstream OOD detection methods (CLIP-based at ~35M MFLOPs, supervised at hundreds of MFLOPs) are orders of magnitude too expensive for battery-powered edge devices, rendering their SOTA accuracy practically irrelevant. The 35 MFLOPs inference cost and 0.12 ms latency on a Jetson Orin Nano make real-time OOD detection feasible in safety-critical TinyML applications (health monitoring, wildfire detection, search-and-rescue). Open questions include whether the three-stage training pipeline generalizes to non-image modalities (audio, sensor data), whether the 300 MFLOPs ceiling remains the right target as edge hardware evolves, and whether the latent-space de-biasing strategy transfers to other generative architectures beyond VAEs. The method's reliance on a frozen encoder during Stage 2 and the need for stored ID statistics also raise questions about adaptability in non-stationary deployment environments.
