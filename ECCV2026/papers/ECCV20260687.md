## Problem

Robot manipulation policies benefit from egocentric (first-person) demonstrations, yet the vast majority of human videos are recorded exocentrically (third-person), creating a fundamental viewpoint mismatch. Prior exo-to-ego approaches rely on pixel-space diffusion synthesis, which hallucinates unobserved regions, introduces high-frequency spatial artifacts, and produces temporally inconsistent trajectories that corrupt the kinematic signals needed for robot control. The paper argues this reflects a deeper misalignment: manipulation rewards depend on relative hand-object geometry, not photorealistic rendering, so pixel-level synthesis is both unnecessary and harmful for downstream policy learning.

## Input / Output

The method takes a sequence of monocular exocentric RGB frames from an uncalibrated, potentially moving camera, along with corresponding exocentric hand-object poses (extracted via HAMER for hands and FoundationPose for objects when not provided). It produces an egocentric hand-object pose trajectory on the joint manifold M_pose = (SE(3)×R^K) × SE(3), expressed in an object-relative coordinate frame so that the trajectory is identifiable up to a single global rigid transformation. These translated poses are then projected into a unified state-action space alongside robot teleoperation data to co-train an Action Transformer policy that outputs 6-DoF end-effector displacement and finger articulation commands.

## Method

Exo2EgoPolicy formulates exo-to-ego translation as a continuous-time score-matching diffusion model (VP-SDE) operating directly on the SE(3) pose manifold rather than pixel space. A 12-layer Transformer score network is conditioned on a geometric latent z_t (concatenation of DINOv2 image features and MLP-encoded exocentric pose) and the previously inferred pose p_{t-1}, making generation autoregressive and propagating temporal context across frames. A temporal consistency loss L_temp, built on a GRU-based motion prior Φ, acts as a geodesic low-pass filter in pose space, and the paper derives a non-divergence bound showing cumulative trajectory error is uniformly bounded when the transition operator is contractive (L < 1). The object-relative pose formulation (predicting (p_obj)^{-1}·p_hand) marginalizes out unknown camera extrinsics, and Proposition 1 establishes identifiability up to a global SE(3) transformation under bounded inter-frame camera motion. For policy learning, an MDP-based sufficiency argument shows that for quasi-static tasks whose reward depends on relative hand-object geometry, pose is a sufficient statistic, enabling joint behavioral cloning on translated human demonstrations and limited robot teleoperation data in a shared state-action space.

## Results

On the H2O pose translation benchmark (Unseen Objects split), Exo2EgoPolicy achieves PA-MPJPE of 11.4 mm and F@5mm of 0.685, reducing PA-MPJPE by 27% over the strongest pixel-space baseline EgoX (15.7 mm) and improving F@5mm by over 30%. On the Ego Humanoid Manipulation Benchmark, the method achieves 81.9% mean Success Rate on short-horizon Seen tasks and 73.7% on Unseen tasks, surpassing EgoVLA (77.8%/69.1%) by 4.1 and 4.6 points respectively. On long-horizon tasks, the gap widens to +7.4% SR (Seen) and +7.3% SR (Unseen) over EgoVLA, demonstrating that temporally consistent pose sequences reduce compounding errors across subtask boundaries.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Exo2EgoPolicy outperforms all seven baseline categories across every evaluation setting. Against pixel-space view translation methods (EgoWorld, 4Diff, EgoX), the pose-space formulation yields 6.4–11.4% higher mean SR on short-horizon Seen tasks, with the gap driven by superior pose accuracy (PA-MPJPE 11.4 vs. 15.7–20.6 mm) that avoids structural artifacts from hallucinated background regions. Against EgoVLA (the strongest prior method using egocentric human pretraining), Exo2EgoPolicy gains +4.1% SR (Seen) and +4.6% SR (Unseen) on short-horizon tasks, and +7.4%/+7.3% on long-horizon tasks, confirming that exo-to-ego pose translation extracts higher-quality and more spatially diverse training signal than egocentric video alone. The Exo-only baseline (injecting raw exocentric poses without translation) provides only +1.1% over robot-only, confirming that viewpoint alignment is essential and that additional data alone does not close the domain gap. On the pose translation benchmark (H2O, Unseen Objects), the method reduces PA-MPJPE from 15.7 (EgoX) to 11.4 mm and improves F@15mm from 0.844 to 0.912, with the largest relative gains on the most challenging Unseen Actions split where F@5mm improves by over 30%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant exo-to-ego view generation paradigm (4Diff, EgoWorld, EgoX, Exo2Ego-V), which formulates the problem as pixel-space synthesis via diffusion or GANs, by instead operating directly on the SE(3) pose manifold. It repurposes continuous-time score-matching diffusion (VP-SDE) from image generation to structured pose inference, conditioning on geometric latents from DINOv2 and pose encoders rather than raw pixels. On the policy-learning side, it extends the EgoVLA co-training protocol by injecting pose-translated exocentric demonstrations into the same state-action space as robot teleoperation data, rather than requiring matched egocentric human video or specialized hardware (DexCap, EgoMimic). The MDP sufficiency argument and the SE(3) identifiability proposition are novel theoretical contributions not present in prior exo-to-ego or imitation-learning work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled justification for why pose-level abstraction is not merely a practical shortcut but a representation aligned with the information structure of quasi-static manipulation rewards, potentially redirecting future exo-to-ego research away from pixel fidelity toward geometric consistency. The finding that pose-space translation outperforms EgoVLA (which uses actual egocentric human video) suggests that geometric alignment quality can outweigh viewpoint correctness, an insight with implications for how human video is leveraged in robot learning. Key limitations include the restriction to quasi-static tasks where rewards depend on relative hand-object geometry, reliance on external pose extractors (HAMER, FoundationPose) whose errors propagate, and evaluation on a single robot platform (Unitree H1) and benchmark. Open questions include whether the approach extends to dynamic or visually-rewarded tasks, how it behaves under severe occlusion or multi-object interactions, and whether the SE(3) formulation generalizes to non-rigid or deformable objects.
