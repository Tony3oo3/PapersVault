## Problem

Post-hoc OOD detection methods that exploit loss-landscape curvature (e.g., Laplace approximations) incur substantial computational cost and rely on an unverified assumption that ID samples occupy flatter regions than OOD samples. This paper provides the first systematic empirical and theoretical investigation of the ID-OOD curvature discrepancy, showing that Hessian statistics are consistently smaller for ID data and that the gap widens with distributional shift severity, then leverages this finding for an efficient detector.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image and a pre-trained CNN (ResNet-50 for ImageNet, ResNet-18 for CIFAR), extracting the penultimate-layer feature vector h ∈ R^d and the linear classifier weights. It produces a single scalar OOD score S_FOLD(x) = tr(∇²_h̃ L), where higher values indicate greater OOD likelihood. The score is computed via Hutchinson's trace estimator using Hessian-vector products, requiring only a forward pass plus a small number of backward passes—no parameter-space Hessian construction or auxiliary OOD data.

## Method

FOLD computes a feature-space Hessian by transporting the logit-space curvature of the energy function L(z) = log Σ exp(z_i) through the linear classifier: ∇²_h L = W_g (∇²_z L) W_g^T, which captures directional sharpness aligned with class-discriminative subspaces. To prevent magnitude-induced degeneracy (where large feature norms flatten the landscape and collapse the Hessian to zero), FOLD applies partial feature normalization h̃ = h / ||h||^α with 0 < α ≤ 1, balancing directional and magnitude information. The detection score is the trace of the normalized feature Hessian. AUTOFOLD automatically selects α by generating pseudo-OOD samples via ID logit masking (setting the true-class logit to −∞) and maximizing AUROC between ID and masked scores on a small ID validation set, eliminating the need for external OOD data.

## Results

On the standard OOD detection benchmarks (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, ImageNet-200, ImageNet-1K), FOLD-R achieves the best average AUROC of 87.91% and FPR95 of 40.12%, improving over the strongest prior baselines (KNN at 86.23% AUROC, VIM at 42.42% FPR95) by 1.63% and 2.30% respectively. The base FOLD variant attains 87.20% average AUROC and 42.42% FPR95, already surpassing all 13 baselines on average AUROC. AUTOFOLD, which auto-calibrates the normalization exponent without external OOD data, achieves 86.24% AUROC and 43.67% FPR95, remaining competitive while eliminating the need for OOD validation sets. All FOLD variants maintain inference latency comparable to a single forward pass, unlike KNN, VIM, or RMDS which incur substantial setup or per-sample overhead.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FOLD-R dominates all 13 baselines on average AUROC (87.91% vs. the next-best KNN at 86.23% and ReAct at 86.28%) and on average FPR95 (40.12% vs. VIM at 42.42% and KNN at 43.24%). On individual benchmarks, FOLD-R leads on CIFAR-100 (82.22% AUROC), ImageNet-200 (89.07% AUROC, 38.92% FPR95), and ImageNet-1K (88.04% AUROC, 40.36% FPR95), while KNN retains a slight edge on CIFAR-10 AUROC (92.19% vs. 92.30% for FOLD-R, essentially tied). The base FOLD variant matches VIM exactly on average FPR95 (42.42%) and beats KNN by 0.97% on average AUROC. FOLD-A excels specifically on ImageNet benchmarks (88.28% AUROC on IN-1K, 89.68% on IN-200) but underperforms on CIFAR-10 (89.43% AUROC), reflecting its sensitivity to dataset complexity. Notably, FOLD achieves these gains with near-zero setup time and inference cost comparable to a single forward pass, whereas KNN, VIM, and RMDS require lengthy feature collection or distribution-fitting procedures that add significant latency.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself within the post-hoc OOD detection paradigm, specifically extending the line of curvature-based uncertainty estimation that previously relied on expensive parameter-space Hessian approximations (e.g., Laplace-based methods). It builds on the key insight from Lee et al. [33] that logit-space Hessian spectrally corresponds to the full parameter Hessian, but departs by transporting this curvature through the linear classifier into feature space, yielding a geometry-aware 'feature Hessian' that captures class-discriminative structure. The partial feature normalization mechanism also connects to prior work on feature norm effects in OOD detection (Mahalanobis++ [39], Park et al. [45]), but the paper's finding that full normalization is harmful on complex datasets while partial normalization is beneficial represents a conceptual departure from those approaches. AUTOFOLD's logit-masking strategy for self-supervised calibration is distinct from leave-one-class-out training, operating purely at inference time without retraining.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The first systematic quantification of the ID-OOD curvature gap provides empirical grounding for a previously implicit assumption in curvature-based OOD methods, and the feature Hessian offers a practical, near-zero-overhead alternative to parameter-space curvature estimation. The finding that partial (not full) normalization is optimal on complex datasets like ImageNet is a useful practical correction to prior normalization-based approaches. However, the theoretical analysis is limited to a simplified binary Gaussian setting, and the performance gains over strong baselines (1.63% AUROC, 2.30% FPR95) are modest, leaving open whether the curvature signal remains discriminative under more extreme distribution shifts or in non-convex, multiclass regimes. The method's reliance on a fixed pretrained classifier and its sensitivity to the normalization exponent α (requiring AUTOFOLD for robust deployment) are practical considerations for real-world adoption.
