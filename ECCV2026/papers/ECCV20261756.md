## Problem

High-dimensional self-supervised features like DINOv3 encode rich but entangled information about style, lighting, and semantics, making them excellent for reconstruction yet difficult to use as generative conditioning signals for video diffusion. While these features have been explored for subject-specific editing and alignment, their use as a dense, general-purpose conditioning interface for pretrained video diffusion models—enabling tasks like video domain transfer and video-from-3D generation—remains unexplored.

## Input / Output

The method consumes per-frame DINOv3-ViT-S/16 patch features at 384×60×90 (low spatial resolution, high channel dimensionality), optionally rendered from 3D geometry (untextured meshes, colored voxel grids, or Concerto point-cloud descriptors), along with a first frame (which may be stylized or relit) and a text prompt. For 3D-derived conditioning, a binary visibility mask encoded through the video VAE is provided as auxiliary input to handle holes in sparse geometry. The output is a 49-frame video that follows the structural and semantic guidance of the conditioning features while adopting the appearance of the first frame, with the temporal adapter compressing the 49-frame sequence to 13 latent frames matching the VAE's temporal resolution.

## Method

Control-DINO injects DINOv3 features into a frozen CogVideoX-5B-I2V backbone via a ControlNet-style residual adapter: a lightweight transformer branch (hidden dim 256) concatenates the noisy latent with conditioning features along the channel dimension, processes them through spatially mixing transformer blocks, and injects zero-initialized additive residuals into the first 16 layers of the base model. The key training strategy is appearance-decoupled supervision: DINO features are extracted from the original video while the denoising target is an appearance-augmented version (photometric shifts, GAN-based style transfer, or blur), forcing the adapter to rely on spatial and semantic structure rather than reproducing appearance cues. A temporal adapter of two causal Conv3D layers aligns the conditioning sequence length to the VAE latent grid. The same interface extends naturally to 3D by rendering geometry-derived DINO-like features (from mesh, voxel, or Concerto descriptors) into 2D conditioning maps at the same low-resolution, high-dimensionality format, enabling video-from-3D generation without pixel-level detail.

## Results

On the out-of-domain Tanks and Temples transfer benchmark (Table 1), Control-DINO achieves the best subject consistency (0.8913 at stride 8, 0.8461 at stride 16) and background consistency (0.9060, 0.8862) among all compared methods, along with the highest COLMAP registration scores (0.9530, 0.9932), indicating superior 3D structural coherence under large camera motion. It also leads in VBench aesthetic quality (0.5610, 0.5508). On the in-domain DL3DV140 reconstruction task (Table 3), Control-DINO reduces FID from 47.98 (Wan2.2 Depth) and 37.65 (Wan2.2 Canny) to 20.23, while improving mIOU from 0.036 to 0.299, demonstrating that dense DINO features constrain the output distribution more tightly than geometric signals.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Wan 2.2 Fun Depth and Canny (the primary geometric-signal baselines), Control-DINO wins on subject and background consistency at both strides, and on aesthetic quality; however, Canny retains an edge on motion smoothness and imaging quality at stride 8, and on CLIP similarity at both strides, reflecting its stronger first-frame appearance fidelity. Against AnyV2V, Control-DINO dominates across nearly all metrics, with the most dramatic gap in COLMAP registration (0.9530 vs. 0.5470 at stride 8; 0.9932 vs. 0.1875 at stride 16), indicating that DINO feature conditioning preserves 3D structure far more reliably than DDIM-inverted latent transfer. Against the larger Cosmos-Segmentation ControlNet (Table 2), Control-DINO trades some subject/background consistency (0.8913 vs. 0.9128 at stride 8) and CLIP similarity (0.4717 vs. 0.7306) for substantially better aesthetic (0.5610 vs. 0.4987), imaging (0.7146 vs. 0.6666), and registration rate (0.9530 vs. 0.9374 at stride 8; 0.9932 vs. 0.7755 at stride 16), despite using a smaller backbone and less training data. On the video-from-3D task (Table 4), the 2D Control-DINO reference achieves PSNR 27.93 and FID 38.6, while the 3D-derived variants (Voxel: PSNR 22.80, FID 65.4; Concerto: PSNR 21.19, FID 68.0) remain competitive with the 2D baselines (Wan Canny: PSNR 19.82, FID 72.2) despite operating from sparse geometry without full RGB coverage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ControlNet paradigm for video diffusion by replacing traditional low-level geometric signals (edges, depth, pose) with dense DINOv3 self-supervised features as the conditioning input. It departs from prior DINO-for-video work (DIVE's per-video MLP optimization, CAGE's sparse patches, DwD's PCA-pruned sim-to-real pipeline) by introducing a general-purpose, spatially dense ControlNet branch trained with an appearance-augmentation strategy that decouples structure from style. The 3D conditioning extension builds on Concerto/Sonata-style geometry descriptors, repurposing them as drop-in feature maps for the same adapter, which no prior video diffusion work has done in a unified manner.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a single conditioning interface that spans 2D domain transfer (stylization, relighting) and 3D-guided generation (mesh, voxel, point-cloud), reducing the fragmentation of control signals in video diffusion. The practical finding that low spatial resolution (60×90) compensated by high channel dimensionality (384) enables robust control from coarse 3D representations is useful for settings where only LiDAR or occupancy data is available. Limitations include residual appearance leakage for out-of-distribution styles and an inherent trade-off between control strength and generative diversity; the authors identify learned spatial weighting and extension to other foundation feature spaces as natural next steps.
