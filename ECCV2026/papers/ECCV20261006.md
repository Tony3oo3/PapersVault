## Problem

Existing adversarial patch attacks on Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models optimize for action-space divergence, which overfits to the specific action head of the target architecture and yields poor cross-architecture transferability. The paper identifies a more fundamental, shared vulnerability: before planning any motion, a VLA model must visually localize its own robotic arm (a 'visual proprioception loop'), and this self-localization step is universally present across heterogeneous VLA architectures regardless of their action encoding.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB observation image (with a 5%-area adversarial patch composited via a binary mask) and a natural-language instruction as input to the VLA model, and produces a universal adversarial patch P ∈ [0,1]^{H×W} that, when placed in the camera's field of view, causes the model to output erroneous action trajectories across diverse tasks. The patch is optimized in a white-box setting on one surrogate model (e.g., OpenVLA-Spatial) and then evaluated in a black-box manner against 12 unseen victim models spanning three architectures (OpenVLA, UniVLA, CronusVLA) and four task suites, as well as across a real-to-sim domain gap (BridgeData V2 → LIBERO).

## Method

VLA-Hijack executes a 'Proprioceptive Identity Rewiring' via two concurrent objectives. First, Attention-Guided Proprioceptive Suppression extracts multi-head self-attention maps from the visual encoder's final layer, normalizes them into a saliency weight map W, and minimizes the W-weighted cosine similarity between clean and adversarial feature maps, creating a 'perceptual vacuum' that blinds the model to its true arm. Second, Multimodal Proprioceptive Injection fills this vacuum by alternating (with period τ) between Semantic Concept Anchoring—aligning patch token features to a Top-k set of text embeddings for 'robot', 'arm', 'hand', etc.—and Visual Prototype Projection—cloning the real arm's visual features via a SAM-3-segmented, ℓ₂-normalized global average pooling vector. The total loss L_total = L_suppress + λ·L_inject^(t) is optimized over 500 steps with inner-loop refinement, and the alternating schedule prevents gradient conflicts between the two injection modalities while the suppression term remains active throughout.

## Results

On the LIBERO simulation benchmark (Table 1), VLA-Hijack achieves a 100% Failure Rate (FR) in the white-box setting on the OpenVLA-Spatial surrogate and a 61.64% average Transfer FR across 11 black-box victim models, compared to 37.53% for the strongest baseline (UADA₁₋₃). In the more challenging Real-to-Sim cross-domain setting (Table 2), where patches are trained on BridgeData V2 and tested on LIBERO, VLA-Hijack reaches a 63.68% Transfer Avg. FR—roughly 3× the best baseline (TMA₁₋₇ at 21.02%). Physical-world evaluation on OpenVLA and π₀ confirms 100% and 92.5% average FR on two manipulation tasks, validating the attack's practical threat.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All eight optimization-based baselines (UADA₁, UADA₁₋₃, UPA₁, UPA₁₋₃, TMA₁, TMA₁₋₃, TMA₇, TMA₁₋₇) originate from Wang et al. [25], which targets the action output space via action-error backpropagation. VLA-Hijack decisively outperforms every baseline on both the in-domain (Table 1: 61.64% vs. 37.53% best) and cross-domain Real→Sim (Table 2: 63.68% vs. 21.02% best) transfer settings, with absolute margins of 24.11 and 42.66 percentage points respectively. Notably, in the cross-domain setting all optimization-based baselines collapse to 18–21% Transfer FR—barely above the naive 'Arm Image' baseline (23.45%)—indicating their patches overfit to domain-specific action tokens and fail to generalize across the real-to-sim visual gap. VLA-Hijack's advantage is most pronounced on OpenVLA and UniVLA victims (93–100% FR), while CronusVLA remains the hardest target (11–26% FR), likely due to its multi-frame continuous action head. The paper also notes that baseline attacks are architecturally incompatible with UniVLA (they rely on OpenVLA's discrete action tokens), whereas VLA-Hijack operates purely on visual features and transfers seamlessly across all three architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in VLA adversarial attacks (Wang et al. [25], Lu et al. [19], Xu et al. [30]) that targets the model's action output space, arguing that such action-space optimization overfits to architecture-specific action heads and suffers from poor cross-architecture transferability. It repurposes an observation from prior work—that adversarial patches spontaneously evolve into arm-like patterns—as evidence of a deeper, universal vulnerability: the visual proprioception loop through which VLA models must localize their own embodiment before planning motion. The proposed framework introduces two new loss components (attention-guided proprioceptive suppression and alternating multimodal injection) that have no direct analogue in prior VLA attack literature, effectively shifting the attack target from divergent action encodings to a shared perceptual mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of visual proprioception as a universal, architecture-agnostic vulnerability in VLA models has broad implications for how these systems are designed and deployed in safety-critical settings; if the model's self-localization can be hijacked by a small visible patch, then robustness must be built into the perception pipeline rather than the action head. The physical-world validation (100% and 92.5% failure rates on real robots) elevates this from a simulation curiosity to a tangible deployment risk. Open questions include whether the attack generalizes to non-manipulation tasks (navigation, locomotion), whether multi-camera or proprioceptive-sensor fusion can mitigate the vulnerability, and whether the 'phantom embodiment' concept extends to other embodied AI paradigms beyond VLA manipulation.
