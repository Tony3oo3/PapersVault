## Problem

Existing fine-grained perception benchmarks rely on explicit textual cues (e.g., 'Where is the red cup?') and low-resolution inputs, evaluating only passive text-to-image grounding rather than a model's ability to autonomously discover implicit visual cues. No prior benchmark systematically tests whether MLLMs can perceive micro-scale differences or commonalities across high-resolution image pairs without any textual guidance, leaving a critical gap in assessing autonomous, high-resolution multi-image perception.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261157/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each sample presents a pair of high-resolution RGB images (average resolution ~1895 px, approaching 2K) alongside a multiple-choice question that deliberately contains no explicit textual cue pointing to the relevant visual detail. The model must output a single letter (A–E), where options A–D describe candidate visual cues and option E is always 'No visible difference' or 'No visible commonality,' ensuring logical completeness and preventing spurious successes through random guessing. The MCQ format with exact letter matching eliminates the n-gram matching bias (ROUGE-L, CIDEr) that plagues prior image-difference-captioning evaluations.

## Method

DiCoBench is constructed via a multi-stage semi-automated pipeline: base high-resolution images are sourced from V*, micro-target masks are extracted and expanded with a 2× context margin, GPT-5.1 generates diverse task-aware modification instructions, and FLUX.2 Klein performs the actual micro-scale edits while preserving the complex background. The 765 samples span 8 task categories organized into two progressive tracks—Differential Visual Cues (Attribute, Entity, Spatial Relationship, Reasoning) and Commonality Visual Cues (Instance, Category, Spatial Grounding, Reasoning)—each with a customized generation and filtering protocol. All synthesized pairs undergo strict four-criteria human verification (textual fidelity, visual naturalness, mutual distinguishability across options, and a <5% area scale constraint), with a human fallback mechanism for failed generations. The key design insight is formulating cross-image fine-grained perception as an MCQ task to eliminate evaluation metric bias, while the two-track taxonomy systematically covers both the detection of visual differences and the equally neglected cognitive dimension of visual commonalities.

## Results

On DiCoBench (765 high-resolution multi-image MCQ samples), the best closed-source model Gemini-3-Pro achieves only 58.1% average accuracy, while the best open-source model TreeVGR-7B reaches 48.8%, compared to 98.3% human accuracy — a gap of over 40 percentage points. Performance is most severely degraded on Reasoning sub-tasks in both tracks, where most models score near or below 30% (e.g., Gemini-3-Pro: 28.0% on Difference-Reasoning, 36.4% on Commonality-Reasoning). Category tasks within Commonality are the relative strength for models (most scoring above 50%), while Attribute tasks in the Difference track remain the weakest for most models (ranging 20–49.4%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among open-source models, TreeVGR-7B (48.8% avg) outperforms all other open-source baselines, surpassing DeepEyes-7B (42.9%), Qwen2.5-VL-7B (41.0%), and Qwen3-VL-8B (40.3%) by 6–9 points, and dramatically exceeding the much larger InternVL3.5-241B-A28B (29.7%) and Gemma3-27B (31.9%). TreeVGR-7B also beats several closed-source models including GPT-4o (29.0%) and GPT-4.1 (25.0%), though it still trails Gemini-3-Pro (58.1%) by nearly 10 points. Within the Qwen family, the 7B Qwen2.5-VL (41.0%) outperforms the 32B Qwen2.5-VL (31.4%) and both Qwen3-VL variants (40.3%, 30.9%), suggesting that scaling alone does not improve fine-grained cross-image perception. Gemma3-12B and Gemma3-27B perform nearly identically (31.4% vs 31.9%), and both are outperformed by the smaller Qwen2.5-VL-7B. A striking finding is that nearly all open-source models converge to ~20% on Commonality-Reasoning (essentially random among 5 options), indicating a systematic failure mode in detecting functional relationships across disparate images.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from three established lines: (1) text-guided single-image fine-grained perception benchmarks (V*, HR-Bench, TreeBench, Visual Probe) that rely on explicit textual cues to direct attention; (2) multi-image perception benchmarks (MuirBench, BLINK, MLLM-CompBench) that operate at low resolution and focus on macro-level reasoning; and (3) Image Difference Captioning (IDC) datasets (CLEVR-Change, DiffTell, OmniDiff, OneDiff) that frame perception as open-ended text generation evaluated by n-gram metrics and address only visual differences. DiCoBench carves new ground by combining all three dimensions—multi-image, high-resolution (~2K), and implicit visual cues without textual guidance—while introducing the previously unaddressed 'commonality' track and eliminating evaluation metric bias through an MCQ formulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a 40.2% accuracy gap between humans (98.3%) and the best model (Gemini-3-Pro at 58.1%), revealing that cross-image fine-grained perception has been substantially overestimated in the field. The systematic failure of nearly all open-source models to identify any commonality in reasoning tasks (defaulting to 'no visible commonality') points to a fundamental architectural limitation in cross-image associative reasoning at micro-scale. The human time-budget experiment suggests that increased computational investment (analogous to more inference steps) could narrow the gap, opening a concrete research direction. Open questions include whether task-specific training on high-resolution multi-image comparison data can close the gap, and whether the commonality dimension requires fundamentally different inductive biases than difference detection.
