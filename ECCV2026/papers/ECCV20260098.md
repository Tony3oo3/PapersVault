## Problem

Existing Adversarial Robustness Distillation methods assume the teacher's decision boundary is a perfect geometric oracle, forcing the student to blindly inherit the teacher's local sensitivity. The paper identifies that when a robust teacher exhibits local instability (e.g., from robust overfitting), exact logit or gradient matching actually degrades the student's own stability — a phenomenon termed 'Stability Punishment.' Prior rectification strategies only correct static prediction errors (wrong labels) but fail to filter fine-grained dynamic sensitivity noise at the gradient level.

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 64×64 for Tiny-ImageNet) and produces K-class probability distributions via softmax. Unusually, the distillation pipeline requires computing both clean and adversarially-perturbed outputs from both teacher and student, since the gating mechanisms compare dynamic probability variations Δp = p(x+δ) − p(x) rather than static logits alone. The adversarial perturbation δ is generated in an inner loop by maximizing KL divergence between student and teacher outputs within an L∞ ball of radius ε=8/255.

## Method

AEGIS replaces uniform logit/gradient matching with two selective gates that conditionally filter teacher supervision. The Semantic Logit Gate (SLG) checks whether the teacher's adversarial prediction matches the ground truth; if correct, the student aligns with the teacher's adversarial logits, but if the teacher is fooled, the target pivots to the teacher's stable clean logits to prevent error propagation. The Sensitivity Calibration Gate (SCG) operates on probability variations and splits into two sub-losses: a Target-class Robust Distillation (TRD) term that only suppresses the student's target-class confidence drop when it exceeds the teacher's tolerance bound (the teacher's maximum non-target probability rise), and a Non-Target Robust Distillation (NTRD) term that aligns structural variations only for classes where the teacher remains more confident in the ground truth. The overall training is a min-max problem where the inner loop generates adversarial examples via KL-divergence maximization (following AdaAD) and the outer loop minimizes a weighted combination of SLG and SCG losses, with the SCG gate closing automatically once the student's local stability matches or exceeds the teacher's.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with a ResNet-18 student and WideResNet-34-10 teacher, AEGIS achieves 53.22% robust accuracy under AutoAttack (AA), a 3.12% absolute gain over the original ARD baseline (50.10%) and 0.63% over the strongest prior method DGAD (52.59%). The method also attains 58.68% PGD-10 and 57.89% PGD-20 robust accuracy, the highest among all compared methods. Under a stress test with ε=16/255, AEGIS preserves nearly 50% accuracy while ARD and IGDM drop below 40%, demonstrating superior resilience to large perturbations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AEGIS outperforms all nine baselines on AutoAttack, PGD-10, PGD-20, and FGSM on CIFAR-10 (ResNet-18), with the largest margin being +3.12% AA over ARD and +1.81% AA over IAD. The closest competitor is DGAD, which edges out AEGIS on clean accuracy (85.46% vs. 84.69%) and CW₂ (81.54% vs. 81.16%) due to its error-corrective label swapping, but AEGIS surpasses it by 0.63% on AA and 1.03% on PGD-10, confirming that semantic correctness alone does not guarantee geometric stability. Against the gradient-matching method IGDM, AEGIS gains +1.42% AA and +1.52% PGD-20, validating that selective sensitivity gating avoids the stability punishment inherent in blind gradient alignment. On CIFAR-100, AEGIS leads all methods on AA (28.22% vs. 28.16% for AdaAD) and PGD-10 (34.76% vs. 34.47%), while on Tiny-ImageNet it achieves the best AA (17.78%) and average (27.31%) among all distillation baselines. The consistent pattern across datasets is that AEGIS's advantage grows under stronger attacks (AA, PGD-20) relative to weaker ones (CW₂), aligning with the paper's thesis that filtering teacher instability matters most under aggressive perturbations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the adversarial robustness distillation (ARD) lineage—ARD [8], AdaAD [14], DGAD [29], IGDM [18]—by importing a key skepticism from standard knowledge distillation (Stanton et al. [34], Nagarajan et al. [25]) into the adversarial setting: the teacher's local geometry is not a perfect oracle. Prior ARD methods correct static errors (wrong labels) but assume the teacher's gradient sensitivity is always a valid target; AEGIS departs by introducing a 'stability punishment' phenomenon where blind alignment actively degrades a student that is locally more robust than its teacher, and addresses it with two selective gates (SLG for logit-level correctness, SCG for gradient-level sensitivity) that dynamically detach the student from unreliable supervision.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of stability punishment formalizes a practical failure mode in ARD that practitioners likely encountered but had no principled remedy for, and the gated framework offers a drop-in modification to existing ARD pipelines. The practical impact is notable—preserving ~50% accuracy at ε=16/255 where baselines collapse below 40%—but the evaluation is confined to CIFAR-10/100 and Tiny-ImageNet with relatively small student architectures, leaving open whether the gains persist at ImageNet scale or with deeper transformer-based students. An open question is whether the hand-designed gating criteria (indicator functions on argmax correctness and probability-variation comparisons) can be replaced by learned, differentiable gates that adapt per-sample without discrete switching.
