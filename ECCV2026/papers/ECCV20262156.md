## Problem

Adversarial vulnerabilities in digital pathology models have been documented but treated as a monolithic challenge: existing defenses apply uniform regularization or input denoising without identifying *which* neural circuits are causally responsible for adversarial failures. This lack of mechanistic understanding makes it impossible to diagnose why a model is vulnerable, to distinguish clinically meaningful morphological features from spurious artifacts (staining irregularities, scanner noise), or to satisfy emerging regulatory requirements for explainability and risk documentation in AI-based medical devices.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes 256×256 H&E-stained pathology patches at 20× magnification, tiled from whole-slide images (TCGA-Prostate for Gleason grading, BreastPathQ for Nottingham grading), and optionally an adversarial perturbation (PGD with ε=8/255, CW, or DeepFool). Beyond the standard cancer-grade classification output, the method produces a structured set of vulnerable circuits—specific attention heads in ViT middle layers (6–9) or convolutional filters in ResNet-50 later blocks (3–4)—along with a sparse feature decomposition that labels each learned direction as morphological or spurious (staining, scanner noise, or compression artifact).

## Method

MIAT (Mechanism-Informed Adversarial Training) operates in three stages. First, sparse autoencoders (k=4096, 4× overcomplete, λ=0.1) decompose penultimate-layer activations into monosemantic feature directions, revealing that ~40% of learned features correspond to non-morphological artifacts. Second, activation patching (Algorithm 1) performs causal intervention by swapping clean and adversarial activations layer-by-layer, identifying circuits that are *sufficient for robustness* (restoring clean activations corrects the prediction) or *necessary for vulnerability* (corrupting clean activations induces misclassification). Third, a circuit-specific regularization term L_reg = Σ_{a∈C}‖a(x′)−a(x)‖² is added to the cross-entropy loss (weight α=0.01), penalizing activation drift only in the identified vulnerable circuits rather than uniformly across the network. Circuits are periodically re-discovered during training to track evolving vulnerabilities, and targeted adversarial examples are generated to specifically amplify the identified circuits, yielding 7–12% absolute gains in adversarial accuracy over standard adversarial training while preserving clean accuracy within 1–2%.

## Results

On TCGA-Prostate (Gleason grading), MIAT achieves 75.3% PGD accuracy and 62.8% CW accuracy with ViT-B/16, while maintaining 86.8% clean accuracy — outperforming the strongest baseline LAT (70.1% PGD, 57.3% CW, 85.0% clean) by 5.2 and 5.5 percentage points respectively. On BreastPathQ (Nottingham grading), MIAT reaches 73.7% PGD and 60.4% CW with ViT, again leading all baselines. For ResNet-50, MIAT achieves 70.5% PGD (TCGA-Prostate) and 69.2% PGD (BreastPathQ), consistently exceeding the next-best method by 2–7 percentage points across both datasets and all three attack types (PGD, CW, DeepFool).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MIAT outperforms all three baselines (TRADES, MART, LAT) on every metric across both datasets and both architectures. Against TRADES, MIAT gains 5.2–10.1 percentage points on PGD accuracy and 5.5–8.1 points on CW accuracy, while also achieving 1.8–3.7 points higher clean accuracy — indicating that TRADES's uniform regularization over-smooths activations at the cost of both robustness and clean performance. Against MART, MIAT leads by 5.2–9.6 points on PGD and 5.5–8.1 points on CW, with a 2.0–2.6 point clean accuracy advantage. Against LAT (the closest competitor, which also uses a layer-wise approach), MIAT still achieves a 5.2-point PGD gain and 5.5-point CW gain on TCGA-Prostate ViT, while maintaining 1.8 points higher clean accuracy. The paper attributes MIAT's advantage to its circuit-specific targeting: unlike TRADES and MART, which apply indiscriminate regularization, MIAT surgically penalizes only the activations of identified vulnerable circuits, preserving diagnostic signal while suppressing reliance on spurious artifacts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes two tools from the mechanistic interpretability literature—sparse autoencoders (Gao et al., 2024) for feature disentanglement and activation patching (Zhang & Nanda, 2023) for causal circuit identification—and applies them to the adversarial robustness problem in digital pathology, a domain where prior work (Ghaffari Laleh et al., 2022; Foote et al., 2021) characterized vulnerabilities at the input or feature-map level without isolating specific neural circuits. The resulting MIAT training procedure extends standard PGD adversarial training (Madry et al., 2017) with a circuit-specific L2 regularization term, departing from uniform-regularization baselines like TRADES, MART, and LAT that treat the network as a monolithic object. Conceptually, the paper bridges the shortcut-learning literature (Geirhos et al., 2020), which identified spurious-feature reliance behaviorally, with mechanistic dissection that localizes those shortcuts to specific attention heads or convolutional filters.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a concrete, auditable pathway for satisfying emerging FDA SaMD and EU MDR requirements that demand transparency and risk documentation for AI-based diagnostic devices, moving beyond post-hoc saliency maps to circuit-level vulnerability reports. However, the one-time SAE training and activation-patching overhead, the absence of adaptive-attack evaluation, and validation on only two single-institution datasets limit immediate clinical translation. Open questions include whether the identified circuits remain stable under distribution shift across laboratories, whether the approach scales to foundation models and multimodal pathology (H&E + genomics), and whether the 7–12% robustness gain over standard AT persists at larger scale or with stronger attacks.
