## Problem

Existing roadside cooperative perception datasets are collected under a single fixed LiDAR configuration, making it impossible to systematically study how unimodal and camera-LiDAR fusion architectures behave under variations in point sparsity induced by sensor resolution, sensing distance, and training-inference resolution mismatches. This gap prevents fair cross-architecture comparisons under point cloud distribution shifts that are inevitable in real-world deployments where LiDAR hardware is incrementally upgraded or constrained by budget.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261412/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The dataset provides synchronized LiDAR point clouds at three resolution levels (16, 64, 128 beams) and 1080p RGB images from four traffic cameras at a real urban intersection, with point clouds restricted to x,y ∈ [−60 m, 88.8 m] and z ∈ [−8 m, 0 m] at 0.3 m voxel size, and fusion-model images resized to 256×704. Outputs are 220k expert-annotated 3D bounding boxes across 10 traffic-participant classes with unique tracking IDs per sequence, evaluated under the nuScenes protocol (mAP, NDS for detection; AMOTA, AMOTP for tracking). A 3D box is deemed 'valid' only if it contains at least five LiDAR points, a criterion that reduces usable supervision at lower resolutions without altering the ground-truth set.

## Method

RESOLVE deploys six LiDARs (two symmetric groups of 16-, 64-, and 128-beam units) and four cameras at a single intersection with PTP/NTP time synchronization and ICP-based spatial calibration, enabling controlled resolution-isolated experiments where all other sensing and environmental factors remain fixed. The benchmark evaluates unimodal LiDAR detectors spanning sparse convolution (PointPillars, SECOND, CenterPoint), transformer (VoxSeT, DSVT), and state-space (Voxel Mamba, LION) backbones, multimodal LiDAR-camera fusion (BEVFusion, UniTR), tracking-by-detection methods (AB3DMOT, SimpleTrack, Poly-MOT, MCTrack), and cooperative perception paradigms (early, late, and intermediate fusion via AttFuse, F-Cooper, CoBEVT, V2X-ViT). Key empirical findings include: multimodal fusion at low resolution matches or exceeds unimodal performance at high resolution, indicating camera features compensate for LiDAR sparsity; gradient flow from the multimodal loss reshapes early LiDAR backbone features to rely less on point-density cues; and resolution mismatch between training and inference causes up to 40% mAP degradation, with intermediate fusion proving most robust across resolution shifts.

## Results

On the RESOLVE dataset, the best unimodal LiDAR detector (LION, Mamba backbone) achieves 95.9 mAP / 91.1 NDS at high resolution, while the best multimodal detector (UniTR, Transformer backbone) reaches 94.9 mAP / 87.4 NDS at high resolution. Increasing LiDAR resolution from low to medium boosts mean mAP across all models by ~7.4%, but the additional gain from medium to high is only ~1.0%, indicating diminishing returns. Notably, BEVFusion (multimodal, low-res LiDAR) achieves 86.3 mAP, outperforming the unimodal TransFusion-L (82.5 mAP) at the same low resolution, demonstrating that camera features partially compensate for LiDAR sparsity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among unimodal LiDAR detectors, Mamba-based architectures (LION: 95.9 mAP, Voxel Mamba: 95.4 mAP) outperform Transformer-based (DSVT: 94.6, VoxSeT: 90.1) and SparseConv-based (CenterPoint: 87.4, PointPillars: 80.6) methods at high resolution, with the gap widening at low resolution where LION retains 88.1 mAP versus PointPillars' 75.1. For multimodal fusion, UniTR (Transformer) leads at 94.9 mAP (high-res), while BEVFusion (SparseConv) trails at 93.1 mAP, suggesting Transformer backbones better exploit cross-modal complementarity. The key finding is that BEVFusion at low resolution (86.3 mAP) surpasses the unimodal TransFusion-L at the same resolution (82.5 mAP) by 3.8 points, and even approaches TransFusion-L at medium resolution (86.6 mAP), confirming camera features compensate for LiDAR sparsity. In cooperative perception (Fig. 7), intermediate fusion methods (AttFuse, F-Cooper, CoBEVT, V2X-ViT) consistently outperform early and late fusion, but all suffer significant degradation at low resolution, indicating cooperation alone cannot recover severely missing geometry. Resolution mismatch experiments show a model trained at low resolution drops 40% mAP when evaluated at high resolution, while a high-resolution model drops only 4.6% at medium but still 40% at low resolution, highlighting asymmetric domain shift.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in the roadside cooperative perception (I2I) dataset space by introducing the first real-world benchmark with synchronized multi-resolution LiDAR (16/64/128-beam) alongside camera data, whereas all prior roadside and V2X datasets (DAIR-V2X, V2X-Real, RCooper, UrbanIng-V2X) are collected under a single fixed LiDAR configuration. Rather than proposing a new architecture, it repurposes a broad set of existing unimodal (PointPillars, CenterPoint, DSVT, LION, Voxel Mamba) and multimodal (BEVFusion, UniTR) detectors along with standard TBD trackers and cooperative fusion paradigms (early, late, intermediate) as evaluation subjects under a controlled resolution-shift protocol. The conceptual departure is methodological: by holding all other sensing and environmental factors constant while varying only LiDAR beam count, it isolates point-cloud distribution shifts in a way no prior dataset permits.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The controlled multi-resolution protocol enables the first systematic quantification of how LiDAR sparsity affects detection, tracking, and cooperative fusion, revealing that the low→medium resolution jump yields ~7.4% mAP gain while medium→high adds only ~1%, suggesting diminishing returns that inform cost-efficient sensor procurement. The finding that multimodal fusion at low resolution can match or exceed unimodal performance at high resolution, combined with the severe 40% degradation under training-inference resolution mismatch, provides actionable guidance for deploying heterogeneous roadside sensor fleets. A key limitation is the single-intersection scope; whether these resolution-sensitivity patterns generalize to multi-intersection corridors, different traffic densities, or longer-range sensing remains open.
