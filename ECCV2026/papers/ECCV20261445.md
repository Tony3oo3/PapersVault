## Problem

Existing visual navigation and interactive navigation methods assume either a static, traversable environment or a single pre-arranged task with full observability. In real cluttered settings, a mobile manipulator must handle sequential, unknown tasks where every decision to move or detour around an obstacle has persistent consequences on future navigability, yet no prior framework jointly reasons over exploration, selective obstacle relocation, and long-horizon task completion under partial observability.

## Input / Output

The agent receives RGB-D observations from onboard fisheye cameras, a known floor plan (room topology and adjacencies, but no room contents or geometric routes), and one text task instruction at a time in a sequential stream. Rather than outputting a full action sequence, the LLM produces a single high-level constraint resolution per timestep—e.g., 'move object X to drop zone Y' or 'explore room Z'—which a low-level Dijkstra-based navigator and pick-place primitive then expand into concrete collision-free navigation and manipulation actions. The scene graph is serialized as structured text (object nodes with attributes like betweenness centrality, path costs, and blocking relations) rather than being fed as images to the LLM.

## Method

CoReLIN recasts the LLM's role from action-sequence generator to constraint reasoner over a structured scene graph. A perception module incrementally builds a directed graph where nodes are discovered objects/rooms and edges encode blocking relations; each object node is annotated with its betweenness centrality in the traversable grid graph, shortest-path cost, set of blockers, and alternative-path cost. At each step, the frozen LLM reads the textual serialization of this graph plus the current task and selects which blocking object to relocate (or whether to detour or explore), implicitly optimizing a cost-benefit trade-off between manipulation effort (navigation-to-obstacle + 2× pick-place time + navigation-to-drop-zone) and the connectivity gain measured by betweenness centrality. A standard motion planner (Dijkstra over the partial grid graph for navigation, a reliable pick-place primitive for manipulation) executes the chosen constraint resolution, and the resulting observations update the scene graph in a closed-loop perceive-plan-act cycle. The entire pipeline is zero-shot: no task-specific fine-tuning of the LLM, and the low-level planner is filtered to expose only reachable, collision-free options to the LLM's decision space.

## Results

On ProcTHOR-10k with 20 sequential tasks per episode, CoReLIN(known) achieves the highest Long-term Efficiency Score (LES) in medium (4–6 rooms: 88.37) and large (7–10 rooms: 86.56) environments, outperforming the strongest heuristic baseline (Always Interact, 74.73 LES in 7–10 rooms) by ~16%. In the unknown-environment setting, CoReLIN(unk) still reaches 49.99 LES in 7–10 rooms, far exceeding all learning-based baselines (ADIN: 12.21, InterNav: 0.20). Success rate for CoReLIN(known) reaches 100% in 7–10 rooms at g=20, while Price of Clutter (PoC) stays near 1.23, indicating the agent preserves long-term navigability far better than baselines that accumulate clutter (PoC 2.9–3.4).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CoReLIN decisively outperforms both learning-based baselines: ADIN [38] achieves only 2.86–13.81% SR and InterNav [45] only 3.00–17.75% SR across all settings, because both were designed for single-goal interactive navigation and fail to account for persistent environment modifications that degrade future tasks. FastDownward [13], a classical PDDL planner with full environment knowledge, also underperforms (SR 4–38%), struggling with the combinatorial explosion of movable obstacles in cluttered multi-room scenes. Against the strongest heuristic baselines, CoReLIN(known) wins on LES in 4–6 rooms (88.37 vs. 87.19 for Always Interact) and 7–10 rooms (86.56 vs. 74.73), with the margin growing as complexity increases; in small 1–3 room environments, Always Interact and Clean+S/P marginally edge out CoReLIN on LES (~94 vs. ~89) because short navigation distances limit the long-term impact of clutter. The unknown-environment variant CoReLIN(unk) still surpasses all learning-based baselines by 3–6× in LES in large environments, confirming that the gains come from constraint-based reasoning rather than privileged ground-truth access.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of interactive navigation (InterNav, ADIN, CaMP, SPIN) and LLM-based embodied planning (Inner Monologue, VoxPoser), but departs from both in a key architectural choice: rather than using the LLM as an action-sequence generator or a learned policy for single-task obstacle avoidance, CoReLIN recasts the LLM as a frozen constraint reasoner over a structured scene graph, deciding which environmental constraints to resolve (which obstacle to move, where to place it, which room to explore) rather than how to move step-by-step. It extends the NAMO literature (which assumes full geometry knowledge, small obstacle sets, and single goals) and prior interactive navigation methods (which are episodic, pre-arranged, and assume full observability) into a lifelong, sequential, partially-observed setting where environment modifications persist and compound. The betweenness-centrality-based scene graph representation and the cost-benefit abstraction over obstacle-drop-zone pairs are novel formalizations that bridge graph-theoretic connectivity reasoning with LLM semantic judgment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The introduction of Lifelong Interactive Navigation as a formal problem, along with the LES and Price of Clutter metrics, gives the field a principled way to evaluate whether a robot's environment modifications help or hurt future tasks — a dimension largely ignored by prior single-task interactive navigation benchmarks. The finding that purpose-built learned interactive navigation policies (ADIN, InterNav) collapse to near-zero success in the lifelong setting while simple heuristics remain competitive suggests the problem structure fundamentally shifts what capabilities are needed, motivating new research directions beyond incremental policy improvements. Open questions include whether the fixed manipulation cost and single-agent pick-and-place assumptions limit real-world applicability, how the approach scales to extreme clutter or multi-agent coordination, and whether tighter perception-planning coupling (beyond the current grid-graph abstraction) would close the gap between the unknown-environment and known-environment variants.
