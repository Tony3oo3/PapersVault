## Problem

Existing optical flow methods rely on the brightness constancy assumption and heuristic motion representations that degrade under geometric zoom, illumination variation, and structural changes, while deep OF networks are computationally expensive and lack physical interpretability. Prior decomposition-based approaches (e.g., Hodge decomposition) remain confined to single-frame spatial analysis without integrating temporal dynamics, leaving a gap in principled, spatiotemporal motion modeling for video understanding.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262461/cropped_page8_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes consecutive grayscale video frames (or RGB converted to grayscale) and operates on local 3×3 pixel patches with a uniform time increment Δt. It outputs a decomposed velocity field v_R = v_c + v_d and, for downstream use, a three-channel representation F_R = [|v_d|, |v_c|, f] where red and green channels encode DF and CF magnitudes respectively and blue preserves the current frame intensity. This representation replaces conventional HSV flow visualization, avoiding nonlinear color-space artifacts in low-texture or high-motion regions.

## Method

ReynoldsFlow grounds motion estimation in the Reynolds transport theorem (RTT) and Helmholtz-Hodge decomposition (HHD), decomposing the velocity field into a curl-free (CF) component capturing expansion, nonrigid deformation, and illumination change, and a divergence-free (DF) component capturing incompressible transport. The CF component is derived from a variational form of the RTT (Eq. 10) solved on 3×3 patches using Simpson's-rule boundary integrals and a Gaussian-smoothed residual, while the DF component is obtained by solving a Gaussian-weighted least-squares (Lucas-Kanade-style) system on a divergence-compensated residual map D that subtracts CF advection and CF dilation terms from the temporal intensity difference. The entire pipeline is training-free and unsupervised, requiring no learned parameters or task-specific optimization. The resulting three-channel magnitude-plus-intensity representation is designed as a plug-and-play input for downstream networks (e.g., YOLOv11n, SwingNet, C3D) without architectural modification.

## Results

On the six downstream benchmarks, ReynoldsFlow achieves the best or near-best performance across the board: GolfDB PCE 0.812, Anti-UAV AP₅₀ 0.792 / AP₅₀₋₉₅ 0.446, ARD100 AP₅₀ 0.602 / AP₅₀₋₉₅ 0.326, and UAVDB AP₅₀ 0.895 / AP₅₀₋₉₅ 0.547. On action recognition, ReynoldsFlow reaches 0.402 on HMDB51 and 0.714 on UCF101, slightly below the best DL-based baselines (SEA-RAFT 0.419, RPKNet 0.734) but well above all classical OF methods. In the controlled flow-estimation experiments (Table 2), ReynoldsFlow attains the lowest AEPE (0.058 px) and AAE (3.10°) on Geometric Divergence and the best R_sp (0.9015) and ACS (0.9993) on Pure Rotation, outperforming all 11 baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ReynoldsFlow decisively outperforms all classical OF baselines (Horn-Schunck, Lucas-Kanade, Farneback, Brox, TV-L1, DeepFlow, PCAFlow, DIS) on every detection benchmark: on Anti-UAV it reaches AP₅₀ 0.792 vs. the best classical method TV-L1 at 0.600, and on UAVDB it achieves AP₅₀ 0.895 vs. TV-L1's 0.779. Against DL-based flow methods (RPKNet, SEA-RAFT, DPFlow), ReynoldsFlow wins on all three UAV detection datasets by large margins (e.g., ARD100 AP₅₀ 0.602 vs. RPKNet 0.088) and on GolfDB PCE (0.812 vs. RPKNet 0.801). The only losses are on action recognition: SEA-RAFT edges ReynoldsFlow on HMDB51 (0.419 vs. 0.402) and RPKNet leads on UCF101 (0.734 vs. 0.714), though ReynoldsFlow still surpasses all classical methods by 10+ points on both. In the controlled flow-estimation experiments, ReynoldsFlow achieves the best AEPE and AAE on Geometric Divergence (0.058 px, 3.10°) and the best R_sp on Pure Rotation (0.9015), confirming its theoretical advantage in handling divergence and curl separately.

## Relation to Prior Work

ReynoldsFlow sits at the intersection of two previously separate lines of work: the Helmholtz-Hodge decomposition, which has been applied to single-frame image processing and spatial flow estimation (e.g., HDNet [40]), and the Reynolds transport theorem, which was previously used only for shot-change detection [47]. The paper's key departure is combining RTT with HHD in a spatiotemporal setting to decompose video motion into curl-free and divergence-free components, going beyond the brightness-constancy assumption that underpins classical Lucas-Kanade [31] and Horn-Schunck [20] formulations. For the DF component, the solver directly follows the LK Gaussian-weighted least-squares framework, while the CF component is derived from a variational form of the transport equation — a formulation not present in prior OF literature. The resulting three-channel representation (|v_d|, |v_c|, intensity) is positioned as an alternative to HSV-based flow visualization, drawing on the observation that nonlinear color-space transformations degrade downstream task performance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The main practical contribution is a training-free, plug-and-play motion representation that can be dropped into existing detection, recognition, or pose-estimation pipelines without task-specific retraining, offering a lightweight alternative to 3D-convolution or transformer-based spatiotemporal models. The physics grounding (RTT + HHD) provides interpretability that purely data-driven OF networks lack, and the controlled checkerboard experiments convincingly validate the theoretical zero-curl/zero-divergence constraints. However, the controlled evaluation relies on a simple textured target, and the downstream gains, while consistent, are modest on action recognition (e.g., 0.402 vs. 0.419 for SEA-RAFT on HMDB51), leaving open whether the advantage persists on more complex, high-occlusion, or large-displacement real-world scenes. The 3×3 window assumption and the local-constancy approximation also limit applicability to very fast or highly non-rigid motion.
