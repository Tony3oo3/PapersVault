## Problem

Vision-Language-Action models require targeted finetuning for each deployment environment, but existing methods like LoRA broadly adapt all parameters regardless of task specifics, risking overfitting to narrow training distributions and eroding the pretrained representations that enable generalization. The paper asks whether few-shot expert demonstrations can identify which specific attention heads encode task-relevant physical reasoning, enabling more precise and generalizable adaptation of VLAs to new robotic tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262667/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The VLA consumes RGB images from two cameras (left arm and wrist), a language instruction, and robot proprioceptive state (joint angles) to produce a continuous action vector of joint velocities and gripper commands. For adaptation, the method ingests 20 teleoperated demonstrations recorded at 6 Hz, from which it extracts per-timestep attention head activations across all 18 layers and 8 heads of the PaliGemma backbone. The final output is a standard model checkpoint with LoRA adapters on a sparse subset of heads, deployable in existing VLA pipelines without additional inference overhead.

## Method

Robotic Steering is a three-step mechanistic finetuning approach grounded in the hypothesis that specific attention heads specialize in encoding task-relevant physical reasoning. In step one (semantic attribution), it extracts activations from every attention head over the few-shot demonstrations and scores each head via k-NN regression—predicting ground-truth actions from distance-weighted nearest-neighbor timesteps in that head's representation space—selecting the top m=20 heads with lowest mean squared error. In step two (selective finetuning), it applies head-selective LoRA, training low-rank adapters (rank 8) only on the query and output projections of the selected heads while freezing the visual encoder, action expert, LLM backbone, and all other attention heads. In step three, inference proceeds as a standard forward pass with the finetuned weights. This selective adaptation uses 96% fewer trainable parameters than full-head LoRA while matching or exceeding its success rate and showing superior robustness to lighting, object, and scene variations.

## Results

On five real-robot manipulation tasks with a Franka Emika Panda arm, Robotic Steering (KNN) matches or outperforms full-head LoRA across both π₀ and π₀.5 VLA backbones using only 20 demonstrations. For π₀, Robotic Steering achieves 80%, 75%, 90%, 85%, and 17.5% success rates versus LoRA's 75%, 45%, 75%, 60%, and 10%; for π₀.5, it reaches 72.5%, 85%, 77.5%, 80%, and 27.5% versus LoRA's 62.5%, 90%, 70%, 65%, and 20%. Robotic Steering also reduces trainable parameters by 96% (15.8M vs. 29M) and finetuning time by ~21% (185–189 min vs. 214–239 min). Zero-shot baselines (π₀-DROID, π₀.5-DROID) achieve near 0% on most tasks, confirming the necessity of task-specific adaptation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against full-head LoRA [35], Robotic Steering wins on 4 of 5 tasks for π₀ (gains of +5 to +30 percentage points) and on 4 of 5 tasks for π₀.5 (gains of +7.5 to +12.5 pp), with the sole exception being π₀.5 Press Button Hard where LoRA edges ahead (90% vs. 85%). Zero-shot π₀ [12] and π₀.5 [59] baselines confirm that without finetuning, success rates drop to 0–35%, underscoring that the gains come from the selective adaptation rather than the base model. In the head-selection ablation, CMA [70] (causal ablation) achieves only 15% on Place Marker in Mug versus 80% for K-NN regression, while REINFORCE [37] matches the 80% success rate but requires 93 minutes of head-selection time compared to 0.2 minutes for K-NN. Across all comparisons, Robotic Steering consistently uses 96% fewer trainable parameters and ~21% less training time than full-head LoRA, making it the most parameter- and compute-efficient method tested.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends LoRA-based finetuning for Vision-Language-Action models by introducing a mechanistic interpretability step that identifies which specific attention heads encode task-relevant physical reasoning before adaptation. It draws on the task-vector and activation-steering literature from NLP and vision (induction heads, causal mediation analysis, REINFORCE-based head selection) but is the first to apply such selective, head-level finetuning to VLAs for learning new robotic manipulation tasks. Conceptually, it bridges functional specificity from neuroscience with the structured attention architecture of transformers, departing from prior VLA finetuning work (uniform LoRA, VLA-specific adapters) that updates parameters without considering which components carry task-specific physical information.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: 96% fewer trainable parameters, 21% less training time, and a standard deployable checkpoint with zero inference overhead make mechanistic finetuning accessible for real-world robotic iteration. The counterintuitive finding that sparse, task-specific adaptation is *more* robust to environmental variation (lighting, object shape, distractors) than broad LoRA challenges the working assumption that wider parameter updates yield better generalization. Key limitations include evaluation on a single Franka Emika embodiment, restriction to LLM attention heads (ignoring visual encoder and action expert features), and dependence on open-source model weights. Open questions include whether the approach scales to long-horizon tasks, multi-embodiment settings, and whether parameter-level (rather than head-level) feature selection could further refine adaptation.
