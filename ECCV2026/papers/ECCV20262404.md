## Problem

Existing visual privacy benchmarks treat privacy as a binary property, assigning risk based on the single most severe detected attribute while ignoring how individually benign cues (e.g., a wristband, room number, and treatment device) can compose to uniquely identify a person. No prior framework provides a composition-aware, graded severity assessment aligned with regulatory distinctions between inherently identifying data and data that becomes sensitive only through linkage.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262404/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image and produces a continuous privacy severity score in [0,1] derived from the counts of detected attributes across four CPRT levels, alongside 22 binary attribute-presence annotations. Evaluation uses three prompting strategies (zero-shot, intuition-based, taxonomy-guided) where the model is asked to assign a score without being given the scoring function or boundary values, preventing reverse-engineering. The SFT variant consumes taxonomy-guided instruction-response pairs and outputs a structured severity assessment with rationale.

## Method

The core contribution is the Compositional Privacy Risk Taxonomy (CPRT), a four-level hierarchy partitioning 22 attributes by atomic sensitivity and compositional identification potential, paired with a continuous scoring function that enforces lexicographic dominance (any L_i attribute strictly outweighs all lower-level combinations) via weights w=(330,30,5,1), followed by ratio stretching and square-root interpolation to empirically-derived boundaries extracted from 16D ordinal-triplet-loss embeddings with IDW smoothing. A 6,736-image dataset is constructed from VISPR with dual-annotator (GPT-5.1 + Gemini 3 Flash) attribute labels validated against human raters (87.4% inter-annotator agreement, κ=0.652). Eight VLMs are benchmarked under three prompting conditions, revealing that frontier models align well under taxonomy guidance but systematically underestimate intermediate composition-driven risks. To close the deployability gap, Qwen3-VL 8B is fine-tuned with LoRA (rank 64, batch 128, 5 epochs) on taxonomy-guided instruction pairs, approaching frontier-level Spearman correlation (0.762) and matching frontier MAE (0.140).

## Results

Under taxonomy-guided prompting, Gemini 3 Flash achieves the strongest alignment with compositional severity (Spearman ρ=0.872, Pearson r=0.884, MAE=0.140, Bias=+0.009), followed closely by GPT-5.2 (ρ=0.844, r=0.850, MAE=0.158, Bias=−0.046). The SFT-tuned Qwen3-VL 8B model reaches ρ=0.762, r=0.799, MAE=0.140, approaching frontier-level performance while remaining locally deployable. In zero-shot settings, all models exhibit strong negative bias (−0.17 to −0.37), systematically underestimating privacy severity, with smaller open-weight models (Llama 3.2-VL 11B, MiniCPM-V 8B) showing correlations below 0.60.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Gemini 3 Flash leads all comparisons under taxonomy prompting with the highest Spearman (0.872) and near-zero bias (+0.009), while GPT-5.2 trails slightly (ρ=0.844) but shows a small negative bias (−0.046). The SFT-tuned Qwen3-VL 8B closes much of the gap to frontier models, matching Gemini 3 Flash on MAE (0.140) and achieving 87% of its Spearman correlation, though it lags on inter-level accuracy (0.849 vs. 0.938). Llama 4 Maverick and Qwen3-VL 32B occupy a mid-tier band (ρ≈0.75–0.76) but retain substantial negative bias (−0.18 to −0.20), indicating persistent underestimation of intermediate risks. Pixtral 12B and MiniCPM-V 8B show weaker alignment (ρ=0.53–0.72), while Llama 3.2-VL 11B performs worst overall (ρ=0.354), suggesting that model scale alone is insufficient without compositional reasoning capability. Notably, taxonomy guidance yields the largest relative gains for Qwen3-VL 8B (Spearman nearly doubles from 0.383 to 0.751), whereas frontier models benefit more modestly, confirming that structural scaffolding is most critical for smaller models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant binary or max-attribute framing of visual privacy benchmarks (PicAlert, VISPR, PrivBench) by importing the k-anonymity principle from structured-data privacy into the visual domain, formalizing privacy as a compositional, graded severity problem. It builds on VISPR's structured attribute taxonomy but reorganizes it around two axes—atomic sensitivity and compositional identification potential—rather than treating the most severe detected attribute as the image's risk. The regulatory alignment (GDPR Art. 4/9, HIPAA, EU AI Act) and the continuous lexicographic scoring function are novel contributions not present in prior visual privacy frameworks, which either collapse risk into binary labels or rely on subjective preference scores.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a regulation-aligned, interpretable scoring mechanism that could serve as a standard for privacy-aware VLM deployment, and the publicly released 6.7K-image benchmark with compositional ground-truth scores enables systematic evaluation of future models. The finding that even frontier VLMs systematically underestimate composition-driven intermediate risk—and that an 8B LoRA-fine-tuned model can close most of the gap—suggests practical on-device privacy assessment is feasible without proprietary APIs. Key limitations include the uniform-contribution assumption within severity levels (synergistic attribute combinations are not modeled), reliance on LLM-based annotation, and evaluation restricted to single-image settings, leaving multi-image and cross-source compositional risks unaddressed.
