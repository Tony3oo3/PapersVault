## Problem

Existing controllable video generation methods entangle camera and object motion into a single pixel-space tracking signal, making joint control ill-posed when viewpoints change. They also treat user-specified motion as mere kinematic displacement, ignoring causal action-consequence relationships between objects (e.g., a hand pushing a cup should make it slide). MoRight addresses both gaps by disentangling camera from object motion and learning motion causality directly from data.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single reference image, sparse motion trajectories drawn as simple strokes or curves on the first frame (reprojected into a canonical static view rather than per-frame pixel tracks), target camera poses, and an optional text prompt, and produces a T-frame video at 480p resolution. Notably, the model requires only first-frame reprojected trajectories—no future-frame pixel trajectories or 3D supervision—and supports two inference modes: forward reasoning (specify active motion, predict passive consequences) and inverse reasoning (specify passive outcomes, recover plausible driving actions).

## Method

MoRight uses a shared-weight dual-stream DiT architecture built on Wan2.1-14B with flow matching: a canonical stream generates object motion under a fixed static camera, while a target stream generates the video with both camera and object motion. The two streams exchange information via cross-view self-attention at every transformer block, transferring canonical-view motion cues to arbitrary camera poses without explicit 3D geometry. For motion causality, training decomposes object trajectories into active (user-driven) and passive (consequence) components and randomly drops one via motion dropout, forcing the model to infer the missing component and learn action-response dynamics. Camera conditions are injected via Gen3C-style depth-warped image encoding, and motion conditions via per-pixel trajectory maps with temporal-correspondence embeddings, both added as residual features at each block. Only the camera encoder, trajectory encoder, and self-attention layers are fine-tuned from the pretrained backbone.

## Results

On the Cooking benchmark, MoRight achieves the best overall performance across all metrics: PSNR 16.44, SSIM 0.594, rotation error 2.16°, translation error 10.11, and EPE 4.27, outperforming all baselines including WanMove (PSNR 16.42, EPE 5.47) and ATI (PSNR 15.93, EPE 5.87). On DynPose-100K, MoRight is slightly behind WanMove on photometric quality (PSNR 12.30 vs. 13.91) but achieves competitive motion accuracy (EPE 7.64 vs. 8.05) despite using only first-frame reprojected trajectories rather than privileged per-frame tracks. For motion causality on WISA, MoRight achieves the best FID (52.95), FVD (876.03), and Physical Commonsense score (0.76) among all methods, while on Cooking it leads in FID (39.94), FVD (730.46), and PC (0.88).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MoRight is compared against five baselines: the uncontrolled Wan2.1 base model, Gen3C (camera-only control), and three motion-conditioned methods (MP, ATI, WanMove) that all receive privileged per-frame foreground/background pixel tracks. On the Cooking benchmark, MoRight wins on every metric—PSNR (16.44 vs. 16.42 WanMove), SSIM (0.594 vs. 0.589), rotation error (2.16° vs. 2.93°), translation error (10.11 vs. 13.27), and EPE (4.27 vs. 5.47)—despite using only first-frame reprojected trajectories rather than full per-frame tracks. On DynPose-100K, WanMove edges out MoRight on photometric quality (PSNR 13.91 vs. 12.30) and camera accuracy (Rot 4.12° vs. 4.55°), but MoRight achieves the best EPE among non-privileged methods (7.64) and is competitive with WanMove (8.05). For motion causality (Table 2), MoRight achieves the best FID (52.95 on WISA, 39.94 on Cooking), FVD, and Physical Commonsense scores, while its Semantic Adherence is slightly lower than baselines that receive full interaction descriptions in their prompts. In human evaluation, MoRight is preferred over ATI and WanMove in 53.5% (controllability), 54.6% (motion realism), and 55.9% (photorealism) of cases, confirming that disentangled control and causal reasoning yield perceptually superior results even with weaker input signals.

## Relation to Prior Work

MoRight builds on the Wan2.1-14B latent video diffusion backbone, adopting Gen3C's camera-warping strategy for viewpoint encoding and Motion Prompting's per-pixel trajectory embeddings for motion conditioning. It departs from the prevailing 'renderer' paradigm—where all pixel displacements are specified jointly—by introducing a dual-stream architecture that separates object motion (in a canonical static view) from camera motion (in the target view), linked via cross-view self-attention. The active/passive motion decomposition with dropout-based training is a novel mechanism for learning action-consequence dependencies, contrasting with prior approaches that rely on external physics engines or two-stage VLM-to-video pipelines for causal reasoning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MoRight establishes a practical path toward interactive video generation where users specify actions and observe physically plausible consequences, supporting both forward prediction and inverse action recovery—a capability directly relevant to embodied AI agents, world models, and immersive content creation. The finding that first-frame reprojected trajectories suffice to match methods requiring privileged future-frame pixel tracks suggests that disentangled representation, not input richness, is the key to controllability. However, evaluation is limited to 50-video subsets of three benchmarks, and the authors acknowledge failures in complex interactions, fast camera motion, and long-horizon physical consistency, leaving open whether the causal reasoning generalizes beyond the curated interaction categories tested.
