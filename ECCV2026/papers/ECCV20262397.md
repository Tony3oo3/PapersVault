## Problem

Existing text-driven human motion generation methods produce fixed-length clips under static agent configurations, making them brittle to realistic solo-social transitions where individuals act alone, enter interactions, and later disengage without fixed temporal boundaries. Most approaches model solo motion and human-human interaction with separate frameworks, so they cannot support temporally incremental streaming generation that seamlessly switches between one- and two-person social coupling without resetting or reinitializing the generative process.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262397/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes an incremental stream of natural language instructions (one active at a time, replaced by a new one at change points) encoded via a frozen DistilBERT, together with a variable-length causal latent prefix of previously generated motion. It outputs a continuous 3D motion stream for 1–2 agents in a 272-dimensional per-frame representation (root yaw, world-frame velocity, partner-relative displacement, root-local joint positions/velocities, 6D joint rotations, and foot-contact indicators), generated segment-by-segment in a 64-dimensional causal latent space with 4× temporal downsampling. The number of active agents and the social mode (solo vs. interaction) can change dynamically mid-stream without any architectural reset.

## Method

ARMS introduces a dynamics-asymmetric Anchor-Relational representation in which one agent (Anchor) models canonical temporal dynamics with a zeroed relational displacement term, while the other (Relational) encodes the partner-relative root offset, so that global integration is restricted to a single branch and inter-person geometry is preserved without drift. A shared causal temporal VAE (1D causal convolutions, 4× downsampling) encodes each agent's motion identically whether in solo or interaction mode, yielding a unified latent space. On top of this, a causal relational diffusion transformer (8 layers, 512-dim, 4 heads) denoises a concatenated two-agent latent stream using segment-wise causal attention with a mode-aware relational gating mask that either enables cross-agent visibility (interaction mode) or restricts attention to Anchor tokens only (solo mode). Training uses flow matching with a temporally structured noise schedule (diffusion forcing) where contiguous latent segments of size S=5 share a common noise level, and inference employs a temporally offset refinement schedule (K=50 steps, δ=5 offset) so that earlier segments become deterministic before later ones finish denoising, enabling smooth streaming without hard segment boundaries.

## Results

On the InterHuman benchmark, ARMS (full-window) achieves the best R-Precision across all ranks (0.529/0.690/0.764 for Top-1/2/3) and the highest Diversity (8.089), while the streaming variant maintains competitive FID (4.444) and MM Dist (3.778) with slightly lower R-Precision (0.492/0.647/0.723). On InterX, the streaming model leads all baselines on R-Precision (0.479/0.679/0.780), MM Dist (3.405), and Diversity (9.399). For streaming transition quality, ARMS reduces Peak Jerk to 0.077 (solo↔interaction) and 0.071 (interaction↔interaction), compared to 3.131 and 0.265 for the adapted InterMask baseline, with AUJ of 1.070 and 2.925 versus 7.444 and 22.440.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On InterHuman, ARMS (full-window) outperforms all 12 baselines on R-Precision at every rank, with the closest competitor TIMotion trailing by 2.8 points on Top-1 (0.501 vs. 0.529) and 3.0 points on Top-3 (0.734 vs. 0.764). HINT achieves the lowest FID (3.100) among all methods, beating ARMS's 4.436, but lags substantially on R-Precision (0.432 vs. 0.529 Top-1). On InterX, the streaming variant of ARMS leads all baselines on R-Precision (0.479/0.679/0.780) and MM Dist (3.405), while Interact2Ar retains a slight FID advantage (0.148 vs. 0.279). For streaming transition quality, ARMS reduces Peak Jerk by 40× (0.077 vs. 3.131) and AUJ by 7× (1.070 vs. 7.444) compared to the adapted InterMask baseline on solo↔interaction transitions, and by 3.7× on interaction↔interaction transitions (0.071 vs. 0.265 PJ). The streaming variant sacrifices ~3–4 points of R-Precision relative to full-window generation but maintains near-identical FID and MM Dist, confirming that incremental rollout preserves realism while enabling unbounded-length synthesis.

## Relation to Prior Work

ARMS sits at the intersection of two previously separate research threads: causal autoregressive motion generation (MotionStreamer, CLoSD, DART) and human-human interaction diffusion (InterGen, InterMask, TIMotion, HINT). It builds directly on the causal latent-space compression and diffusion-forcing segment-wise noising paradigm from MotionStreamer and the causal relational diffusion backbone from Yu et al., but departs from both by introducing a dynamics-asymmetric Anchor-Relational representation that decouples per-agent temporal integration from inter-agent spatial alignment. The key conceptual departure from interaction-centric methods (which assume a fixed two-person configuration) and from long-horizon single-person methods (which ignore relational geometry) is the mode-aware relational gating mask that lets a single model switch between solo and interaction regimes without reinitialization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first framework to handle seamless solo-to-interaction-to-solo transitions within a single streaming generative process, addressing a gap the authors identify as 'largely unexplored' in the literature. The Anchor-Relational representation offers a clean, generalizable solution to the long-standing tension between canonicalized incremental motion (drift-prone) and global-coordinate interaction modeling (distribution-limited), and could be adopted by other interaction or multi-agent motion systems. Key open questions include scaling beyond two agents (the paper acknowledges pairwise extension is possible but higher-order social reasoning is needed), replacing hard mode gating with gradual blending for smoother transitions, and incorporating scene-level or crowd-level context for collision avoidance.
