## Problem

Existing text-guided image editing benchmarks are limited in scale (typically <20K edited images), cover few editing models and tasks, and lack both large-scale human preference pairs and fine-grained scores simultaneously. No scalable MLLM-based evaluation model has demonstrated strong alignment with human preferences across diverse editing scenarios while also serving as a reward signal for model refinement.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20260906/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

EditHF jointly processes an edited image I_e, its corresponding source image I_s, and a text editing prompt T (both instruction-based and description-based formulations). Images are presented at 1024×1024 resolution. The model outputs three continuous quality scores (visual quality, editing alignment, attribute preservation) on a 0–100 scale via an MLP head, a human-interpretable textual evaluation via the language decoder, and pairwise preference judgments derived from score differences. Human annotations use a 5-point continuous scale converted to Z-scores and linearly mapped to [0,100].

## Method

EditHF adopts InternVL3.5 (InternViT vision encoder + LLM backbone) with a trainable projector and an adaptive decoding strategy: the last hidden states are first passed through a text decoder for interpretable responses, then fed into an MLP head for fine-grained score regression. Training proceeds in three stages—(1) textual learning with cross-entropy loss on five discrete quality levels, (2) pointwise learning with MSE loss for absolute score prediction, and (3) pairwise learning with a log-sigmoid ranking loss prioritizing larger rank differences first for coarse-to-fine relative quality understanding. This joint pointwise+pairwise training enables both cross-task absolute scoring and within-group relative comparison. For EditHF-Reward, DPO is applied to the editing model's Transformer (with frozen VAE), using EditHF-computed overall scores (geometric mean with weights 0.3/0.4/0.3) to construct chosen/rejected pairs, with a flow-prediction-error-based loss that references the pre-tuning model for stability.

## Results

On EditHF-1M, EditHF achieves SRCC_global of 0.8548 (Visual Quality), 0.8488 (Editing Alignment), and 0.8475 (Attribute Preservation), with pairwise comparison accuracy of 91.5%, 93.0%, and 92.0% respectively, outperforming all 38 baselines. On cross-benchmark generalization (Table 5), EditHF achieves the best score on all six external benchmarks, including 0.6792 on GenAI-Bench, 0.6608 on AUORA-Bench, and 0.7750 on EditScore-Bench. When used as a reward model (EditHF-Reward), fine-tuning Qwen-Image-Edit with Global-DPO yields an 11.77% improvement in human-rated overall score on EditHF-1M and 10.36% on the EditHF metric.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EditHF outperforms all 38 baselines across every metric and dimension on EditHF-1M. The strongest competitors are fine-tuned MLLMs: Qwen3-VL(8B)* achieves 0.7751 SRCC_global on Visual Quality and 0.8246 on Editing Alignment, while EditHF reaches 0.8548 and 0.8488 respectively (gaps of ~7–8 points). Traditional IQA methods (BRISQUE, MS-SSIM) and vision-language metrics (CLIPScore, HPSv2) are nearly useless for editing-specific dimensions, with SRCC_global below 0.15 on Editing Alignment, confirming they cannot capture instruction-following semantics. Editing-specific reward models EditScore and EditReward(MiMo) also trail EditHF by 40+ points on SRCC_group, attributed to their much smaller training data (3K and 200K pairs vs. 29.1M pairs). On cross-benchmark generalization (Table 5), EditHF achieves the best score on all six external benchmarks, beating GPT-5 (0.5961 on GenAI-Bench) and LMM4Edit (0.6521 on AUORA-Bench) by 8 and 1 points respectively, demonstrating that the million-scale training data translates to robust generalization beyond the training distribution.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of image editing benchmarks (TedBench, EditVal, EditEval, EBench-18K, EditScore, EditReward) by scaling to over 1M edited images from 23 models across 43 tasks, and by collecting both preference pairs and absolute scores—whereas prior benchmarks typically provided one or the other. Methodologically, it builds on MLLM-based evaluators (EditScore, EditReward, LMM4Edit) but departs from them by jointly training on score regression and pairwise comparison in a single model, rather than specializing in one paradigm. The EditHF-Reward component repurposes the evaluation model as a DPO reward signal for editing model refinement, following the broader trend of using learned reward models in RLHF pipelines but applying it specifically to text-guided image editing with flow-matching backbones.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

EditHF-1M fills a critical gap by providing the first million-scale, multi-dimensional human-annotated benchmark for image editing, making it a likely standard reference for the field. The demonstration that a single MLLM can serve as both an evaluation metric and an RL reward signal (with Global-DPO yielding ~12% improvement over the base model) opens a practical pathway for iterative refinement of open-source editing models using closed-source model outputs as supervision. Open questions include whether the reward signal generalizes to editing models with substantially different architectures, whether the 43-task taxonomy captures emerging editing paradigms, and whether the annotation protocol's reliance on 3–15 annotators per sample limits statistical robustness at this scale.
