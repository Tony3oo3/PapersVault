## Problem

State-of-the-art SR transformers rely on 16–30+ blocks of small shifted-window attention (8×8 or 16×16), forcing information between distant positions to propagate indirectly through many layers. This paper asks whether direct wide attention over large position-fixed windows (64×64 at training, up to 128×128 at inference) with only 12 blocks can match or surpass these deep narrow-window designs, and identifies the memory cost of additive relative position biases as the key barrier to large windows.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261676/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a low-resolution RGB image and produces a high-resolution RGB image at ×2, ×3, or ×4 scale. At inference, the feature map is partitioned into overlapping tiles (128/112/96 pixels for ×2/×3/×4 respectively, with 16-pixel overlap) for the attention computation, while the QKV projections, depthwise convolutions, and FFN operate on the full feature map to propagate information globally. A bilinear-upsampling residual is added to the PixelShuffle reconstruction, and the final 3×3 conv is zero-initialized so the network starts as a pure bilinear upsampler.

## Method

WARP replaces the standard linear QKV projection with a shared nonlinear module (1×1 conv → GELU → 3×3 depthwise conv with residual → GELU → 1×1 conv, expansion ratio 12) that produces a 6C-dimensional output split into spatial and channel QKV triplets; sharing this single module across all 12 blocks keeps the model at 20M parameters versus 115M for independent projections. Spatial attention uses 2D Rotary Position Embeddings (splitting each head in half for x and y coordinates) to encode relative positions via query-key rotations, achieving O(N) memory and compatibility with FlashAttention over 4,096–16,384 tokens without materializing an N×N bias matrix. Each block computes both spatial and channel attention from the shared projection, with per-block QK-normalization and output projection for block-specific adaptation. For resolution-adaptive inference beyond the 64×64 training patch, the paper adapts YaRN's NTK-aware frequency scaling to 2D RoPE and introduces an inverted temperature (τ < 1) that sharpens rather than flattens attention, since larger SR windows add spatially redundant tokens; a 50K-iteration fine-tuning phase adapts the model to each scale's tile size and temperature.

## Results

WARP (20M params, 12 blocks) achieves the best or tied-best PSNR on all five benchmarks at ×3 and ×4, and on four of five at ×2. At ×3, it leads every benchmark, with the largest margin on Urban100 (30.75 dB, +0.19 dB over PFT) and Manga109 (35.79 dB, +0.12 dB over PFT). At ×4, it reaches 28.26 dB on Urban100 (+0.06 dB) and 32.73 dB on Manga109 (+0.10 dB) over the previous best. At ×2, WARP wins on Set5 (38.71 dB), Urban100 (34.99 dB), and Manga109 (40.52 dB), ties BSD100 (32.67 dB), and trails PFT by only 0.01 dB on Set14.

## Baselines & Comparisons

WARP (20M params, 12 blocks) is compared against 11 baselines spanning CNN (EDSR, RCAN, HAN) and transformer (SwinIR, CAT-A, ART, DAT, HAT, IPG, ATD, PFT) methods. At ×3, WARP wins on all five benchmarks, surpassing the previous best PFT by +0.09 dB (Set5), +0.19 dB (Urban100), and +0.12 dB (Manga109). At ×4, it achieves the best or tied-best PSNR on all five, with the largest gains on Urban100 (+0.06 dB over PFT) and Manga109 (+0.10 dB). At ×2, WARP leads on Set5 (+0.03 dB), Urban100 (+0.09 dB), and Manga109 (+0.03 dB), ties BSD100 with PFT, and trails PFT by a marginal 0.01 dB on Set14. The largest margins consistently appear on Urban100 and Manga109—benchmarks with repetitive, long-range structures—while gains on smoother benchmarks (Set5, BSD100) are smaller, confirming that wide attention's advantage is most pronounced where self-similar patterns span distances beyond local windows. Notably, WARP achieves these results with only 12 blocks versus 16–30+ blocks in competing transformer methods, and with a standard self-attention mechanism (no shifted windows, cross-attention, or auxiliary losses).

## Relation to Prior Work

WARP departs from the dominant SR transformer paradigm of deep narrow-window stacking (SwinIR's 8×8 shifted windows, extended by HAT, PFT, and others to 16–30+ blocks) by using only 12 blocks of wide position-fixed attention (64×64 at training, up to 128×128 at inference). The key enabler is 2D Rotary Position Embeddings (building on RoPE [26] and its 2D vision adaptation [12]), which replace the additive relative position biases that force O(N²) memory and make large windows impractical. The paper further adapts YaRN [22], an LLM context-extension technique, to 2D vision transformers, and introduces a shared nonlinear QKV projection that replaces the standard linear projection inherited unchanged from the original transformer across all prior SR methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that 12 blocks of wide attention with a shared nonlinear projection can match or surpass 30+ block narrow-window methods at 20M parameters suggests the depth-width tradeoff in SR transformers has been underexplored, offering a structurally distinct design point. The finding that SR requires inverted temperature (sharper, not flatter, attention) when extrapolating window size—opposite to the LLM intuition behind YaRN—is a useful empirical correction for anyone adapting context-extension to vision. Open questions include the high ×2 inference latency (3.7 s at 512²) from quadratic attention at large tiles, whether τ can be learned end-to-end rather than swept per scale, and whether the shared-projection idea transfers to other dense prediction tasks like denoising or deblurring.
