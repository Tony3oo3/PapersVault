## Problem

Existing generative-prior-based task-driven image restoration methods (e.g., EDTR) uniformly update all latent tokens, which is computationally expensive and risks overwriting reliable semantic cues already preserved in the degraded input, causing harmful semantic drift. The paper identifies that task-relevant visual information is unevenly distributed across 1D token sequences, with only a small subset of tokens driving downstream performance, yet no prior method exploits this sparsity for selective restoration.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20260013/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a degraded RGB image (512×512) suffering from composite degradations (downsampling, JPEG compression, Gaussian blur, and noise) and produces a task-specific restored RGB image. A frozen SwinIR pre-restoration module is applied before tokenization to suppress artifacts, and the TiTok encoder (frozen) maps the pre-restored image into a fixed-length 1D sequence of K tokens (K=64 or 256). The output is task-conditional: the token switch produces a different binary mask for classification, segmentation, or detection, so the same degraded input yields different restored images depending on the target task.

## Method

TaskTok operates in TiTok's 1D latent token space and introduces two key components: a lightweight token refiner (6 transformer layers) that restores degraded token representations using global token-to-token context, and a learnable token switch that produces a task-specific binary mask selecting which tokens to refine versus preserve from the LQ sequence. The switch is initialized via a greedy token-importance ordering (derived by iteratively replacing HQ→LQ tokens and ranking by accuracy drop) and trained with a straight-through estimator for the non-differentiable binarization, plus a mask regularizer that penalizes selecting too many tokens. Training follows EDTR's two-stage protocol: Stage 1 optimizes TaskTok with high-level feature distillation loss, token-level L1 supervision on selected tokens only, and the mask penalty; Stage 2 freezes TaskTok and fine-tunes downstream task networks. Notably, the method bypasses TiTok's codebook quantization and feeds continuous refined features directly to the decoder to avoid quantization-induced semantic shift.

## Results

Under the complex Mix-B degradation setting, TaskTok-256 achieves 56.2% Top-1 accuracy on ImageNet classification, 62.7% mIoU on PASCAL VOC2012 segmentation, and 28.6% mAP on object detection, while restoring only 34, 79, and 78 tokens respectively. This represents a +4.6% accuracy gain over EDTR (51.6%) and +3.7% over TiTok-256 (52.5%) for classification, with EDTR requiring 4096 tokens. In terms of efficiency, TaskTok-256 runs at 59.9 img/s, approximately 8.3× faster than EDTR (7.2 img/s), demonstrating that selective token restoration simultaneously improves task performance and reduces computation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TaskTok-256 outperforms all four baselines on all three tasks under Mix-B. Against EDTR (the strongest prior method using diffusion priors), TaskTok-256 gains +4.6% Top-1 accuracy (56.2% vs. 51.6%), +1.8% mIoU (62.7% vs. 60.9%), and +0.5% mAP (28.6% vs. 28.1%), while restoring only 34–79 tokens versus EDTR's 4096. Against TiTok-256 (full-token 1D restoration), TaskTok-256 improves classification by +3.7% and detection by +2.9%, again with far fewer tokens updated. Against pixel-space restorers SwinIR and SR4IR, TaskTok-256 leads by +9.8% and +9.4% in classification accuracy respectively, though SwinIR achieves higher PSNR (23.46 vs. 20.45), confirming that perceptual fidelity and task performance are misaligned. On generalization to unseen datasets (CUB200, Oxford-IIIT Pet) and unseen backbones (ViT-B/16, ConvNeXt-B), TaskTok-256 also achieves the best results, e.g., 54.5% on CUB200 vs. EDTR's 50.3% and 66.5% with ConvNeXt-B vs. EDTR's 62.6%.

## Relation to Prior Work

TaskTok builds directly on the 1D tokenization paradigm introduced by TiTok, which compresses images into a fixed-length sequence of latent tokens without fixed spatial correspondence, and on EDTR's two-stage training protocol that couples a generative restorer with downstream task networks via feature distillation. The key conceptual departure from prior TDIR methods (SR4IR, EDTR, TiTok) is the shift from uniform, full-token restoration to selective, task-dependent token refinement: a learnable token switch determines which subset of tokens to update, while a lightweight refiner restores only those. This is enabled by the index-wise specialization property of 1D tokens, which the paper probes empirically to show that different token positions encode distinct visual attributes (fine texture vs. global color), making selective manipulation feasible in a way that 2D grid-based tokenizers do not naturally support.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a very small fraction of latent tokens (12–34 out of 64–256) drives most downstream task performance, and that indiscriminate restoration of all tokens can actually degrade task accuracy by introducing spurious high-frequency cues or overwriting reliable information already present in the degraded input. This reframes TDIR from a 'restore everything' problem to a 'restore the right things' problem, with practical implications for efficiency (8.3× faster than EDTR) and for understanding the gap between perceptual fidelity and machine recognizability. Open questions include whether the learned token switch generalizes to entirely new task types without retraining, how the approach behaves under more severe or structured degradations (e.g., occlusion, sensor-specific artifacts), and whether the selective-update principle transfers to other 1D tokenized generation or editing pipelines beyond restoration.
