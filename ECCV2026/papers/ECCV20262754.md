## Problem

Existing facial privacy protection methods face an unresolved tradeoff: traditional approaches (blurring, pixelation) irreversibly degrade visual quality, while generative methods synthesize entirely new identities that bear no resemblance to the original subject. No prior method simultaneously fools automated face recognition systems while preserving the natural visual appearance needed for everyday social utility.

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB face image and a randomly sampled 512-dimensional target identity embedding (from a frozen InceptionResNetV1) as input, and produces a single protected face image of the same spatial resolution. The output is spatially blended with the original via a learned face mask so that only facial regions are modified while the background remains untouched. Two deployment modes are supported: proactive (protecting the gallery set before upload) and reactive (protecting the probe set of already-public images).

## Method

MIRAGE decomposes the input face via a single-level Haar DWT into one low-frequency (LL) and three high-frequency (LH, HL, HH) subbands, exploiting the observation that identity-discriminative information concentrates in LL while visual appearance resides in the high-frequency bands. An AdaIN-based cross-attention module perturbs the LL subband by conditioning it on the random target identity embedding, causing recognition encoders to extract a different identity, while CBAM self-attention modules independently refine each high-frequency subband to preserve fine-grained texture without any identity conditioning. The manipulated subbands are recombined via IDWT, passed through a U-Net refinement network (concatenated with the original as a 6-channel input) to remove wavelet artifacts, and finally blended with the original using a learned spatial face mask. Training is governed by a composite loss centered on the proposed FAC Loss, which combines a band-specific frequency-aware margin loss (higher margin and weight on LL) with an SSIM-weighted focal loss on high-frequency subbands, supplemented by attribute, L1+L2 reconstruction, VGG-19 perceptual, and PatchGAN adversarial losses.

## Results

Under proactive (gallery) protection, MIRAGE reduces AdaFace recognition accuracy from a clean baseline of 96.16% to 70.24% (−25.92%) on CelebA-HQ (gender-agnostic), with consistent drops across seen-gender (69.72%) and unseen-gender (71.54%) settings. On LFW, MIRAGE achieves the strongest protection among all compared methods under MagFace, reducing accuracy to 44.13% (proactive) and 44.89% (reactive). Protected images retain high visual fidelity with an average PSNR of 32.85 and SSIM of 0.89, and protection is further reinforced under JPEG compression (e.g., ~6% additional drop for AdaFace at QF=50).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FPP [38] and DiffAM [41] provide the weakest protection, with AdaFace accuracy remaining near the clean baseline (~96%) across all datasets, indicating they fail to meaningfully disrupt identity features. FALCO [2] achieves the strongest numerical privacy, consistently yielding the lowest recognition accuracy (e.g., 56.77% vs. MIRAGE's 70.24% for AdaFace on CelebA-HQ proactive), but at the cost of fundamentally altering the subject's facial appearance, rendering outputs impractical for social use. FAS [24] is competitive on CelebA-HQ and 105-Pins but is outperformed by MIRAGE on LFW under both models (e.g., 65.42% vs. 71.70% for AdaFace; 44.13% vs. 56.37% for MagFace, proactive). MIRAGE's decisive advantage is the privacy–utility balance: it matches or exceeds FALCO on LFW while preserving natural visual appearance (PSNR 32.85, SSIM 0.89), whereas FALCO produces faces that bear no resemblance to the original subject. Against adaptive white-box retraining, MIRAGE-protected images yield only a marginal +1.84% accuracy recovery for AdaFace, confirming robustness.

## Relation to Prior Work

MIRAGE carves out a new niche at the intersection of frequency-domain analysis and attention-based image manipulation for facial privacy. Prior generative approaches (FALCO, FAS, DiffAM) achieve strong identity disruption but fundamentally alter the subject's appearance, while adversarial perturbation methods preserve appearance yet remain vulnerable to purification attacks. This paper repurposes the well-established observation that face recognition models are differentially sensitive to frequency components [14,15] into a constructive privacy framework: DWT decomposes identity (LL) from appearance (LH/HL/HH), cross-attention (AdaIN) perturbs the identity-bearing subband, and self-attention (CBAM) preserves high-frequency texture. The FAC loss, combining a band-specific margin loss with an SSIM-weighted focal loss, is a novel training objective not seen in prior facial privacy work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine gap: no prior method simultaneously fools recognition systems and retains natural visual resemblance, which is essential for social-media utility. The dual-mode (proactive gallery / reactive probe) pipeline and demonstrated robustness to JPEG compression and adaptive fine-tuning make the approach practically deployable. However, limitations remain: protection is evaluated only on single-face 2D images with a single-level Haar DWT, and the visual-fidelity claim rests on SSIM/LPIPS/PSNR metrics rather than human studies. Open questions include whether the frequency-selective strategy extends to video, 3D face models, multi-person scenes, or more aggressive purification attacks, and whether the approach generalizes beyond the nine tested DFR architectures.
