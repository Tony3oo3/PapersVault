## Problem

Existing fall detection and activity monitoring datasets are typically clip-based with coarse labels, limiting their use for online frame-level action recognition as events unfold. They also suffer from small numbers of fall instances, lack of wheelchair user scenarios, and absence of out-of-distribution evaluation splits, making it difficult to assess whether models generalize beyond controlled lab settings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262600/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Models consume 48-frame sliding windows of 2D or 3D skeleton sequences (3D obtained via MotionAGFormer lifting from monocular video) or 16-frame RGB clips extracted from person-centric crops resized to 640×480; fusion models concatenate both streams. Labels are assigned by majority vote over five frames centered on the window midpoint, producing frame-level predictions across 30 action classes. The dataset provides untrimmed multi-camera videos (up to 3072×2048 at 25 fps) with dense temporal annotations, enabling online recognition where adjacent actions within a window inform the current prediction.

## Method

SAFER-Activities is a 66+ hour, 46-participant dataset with 85,310 frame-level annotated action instances across 30 classes, including 5,406 falls, a dedicated wheelchair subset (~15 hours), and a non-lab home-environment OOD test set. The benchmark evaluates a spectrum of architectures: a lightweight CNN1D baseline, GCN-based skeleton models (ST-GCN++, MS-G3D, DG-STGCN), heatmap-based PoseC3D, frozen RGB backbones (CLIP, DINOv3, VideoMAE) with linear classifiers, and five multimodal fusion strategies (concatenation, ModDrop, QMF, OGM-GE, MMCL). Skeleton models are trained via the PYSKL framework with SGD and cosine annealing, while RGB and fusion models use AdamW. Key empirical findings: 2D skeleton models achieve the best in-lab scores (MS-G3D at 87.2%) and strongest OOD generalization (PoseC3D at 79.3%), frozen RGB features collapse under domain shift (DINOv3 drops from 72.1% to 23.8%), and fusion improves in-domain wheelchair recognition (78.6%) but degrades OOD performance below skeleton-only baselines.

## Results

On the in-lab non-wheelchair test set, 2D skeleton models lead with MS-G3D achieving 87.2% macro F1, followed by ST-GCN++ (86.9%) and PoseC3D (86.6%). Under out-of-distribution (non-lab) evaluation, PoseC3D is the most robust at 79.3% F1, while frozen RGB backbones collapse severely (DINOv3: 72.1%→23.8%, CLIP: 70.6%→35.6%, VideoMAE: 80.3%→48.7%). On the wheelchair subset, multimodal fusion with DINOv3 or VideoMAE achieves the best score of 78.6%, surpassing the best 2D skeleton model (MS-G3D, 77.5%). Cross-dataset evaluation on ImViA confirms skeleton generalization: PoseC3D reaches 96.8% F1 and CNN1D 93.0%, while CLIP and DINOv3 predict zero fall events (0% F1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among 2D skeleton baselines, MS-G3D [28] achieves the best in-lab score (87.2%) but PoseC3D [16] is the most robust under domain shift (79.3% OOD) and cross-dataset transfer (96.8% F1 on ImViA), outperforming ST-GCN++ [15] (76.7% OOD) by 2.6 points. The 3D skeleton models (MS-G3D 3D, DG-STGCN [14]) trail their 2D counterparts by 1–5 points in-lab and do not close the gap OOD, suggesting monocular 3D lifting via MotionAGFormer introduces noise that hurts generalization. Frozen RGB backbones are clearly inferior: VideoMAE [45] is the strongest at 80.3% in-lab but drops to 48.7% OOD, while CLIP [39] (35.6%) and DINOv3 [43] (23.8%) nearly fail entirely under domain shift and predict zero falls on ImViA. For fusion, ModDrop [34] is the most effective strategy, recovering VideoMAE+CNN1D from 54.5% to 67.3% OOD, but no fusion method surpasses skeleton-only CNN1D (73.8%) on the non-lab set. QMF [51] and OGM-GE [37] do not consistently improve over simple concatenation, and MMCL [26] (skeleton-only at inference) matches CNN1D on OOD (66.5–68.0% vs. 73.8%) while slightly improving wheelchair recognition (69.7% vs. 68.2%). Overall, skeleton-based methods dominate OOD and cross-dataset settings, while fusion provides a 1–3 point in-domain boost most visible on the wheelchair subset.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in the fall detection and activity monitoring dataset space rather than extending a specific methodological lineage. Prior fall datasets (Guerrero et al., ImViA, UP-Fall, Auvinet et al., Baldewijns et al.) are limited by small scale, clip-level or binary annotations, absence of wheelchair scenarios, and lack of out-of-distribution evaluation splits. SAFER-Activities departs from these by providing frame-level annotations across 30 classes in untrimmed multi-camera video, a dedicated wheelchair subset, and a non-lab home-environment test set, while benchmarking with the standard toolkit of skeleton GCNs (ST-GCN++, MS-G3D, PoseC3D, DG-STGCN), frozen RGB backbones (VideoMAE, CLIP, DINOv3), and multimodal fusion strategies (ModDrop, QMF, OGM-GE, MMCL).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dataset fills a critical gap for smart healthcare monitoring research by enabling online (frame-level) action recognition and providing the first large-scale wheelchair activity benchmark with pose estimation annotations. The empirical finding that skeleton models retain 70–80% F1 under domain shift while frozen RGB features collapse (DINOv3: 72.1%→23.8%; CLIP and DINOv3 achieve 0% recall on cross-dataset ImViA) has direct practical implications for deployment: appearance-based features are unreliable outside the training environment, and no tested fusion strategy closes this gap. Key limitations include the use of adult actors rather than elderly or regular wheelchair users, in-lab-only wheelchair recordings, and the absence of depth or inertial modalities, leaving open questions about ecological validity and whether adaptive fusion or multi-view architectures can recover the visual context that skeleton-only models discard.
