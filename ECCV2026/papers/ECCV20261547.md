## Problem

Existing defenses against data reconstruction attacks in split inference (e.g., DISCO, NoPeek) assume privacy leakage is proportional to retained information and attempt to suppress or decorrelate features, yet strong reconstructions persist. This paper argues that reconstruction vulnerability is not solely determined by mutual information between inputs and representations, but also by the local geometric organization of feature space that enables inversion models to learn stable inverse mappings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261547/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an intermediate representation h ∈ ℝ^d from a frozen client encoder (e.g., CLIP-ViTB/16 at layers 3/6/9/12) and a pre-selected class-level prototype x_target (the training instance with highest cosine similarity to the class centroid in the client encoder's feature space). It produces a transformed representation h̃ = T_θ(h) of the same dimensionality, which is transmitted to the server. At inference time, the adversarial reconstruction decoder is discarded, so only the lightweight residual MLP transformation is deployed on the client side.

## Method

ReTarget instantiates T_θ as a residual MLP (h + MLP(LN(h)) with two linear layers and GELU activation) trained jointly with the server under three objectives: (1) an adversarial reconstruction loss where a lightweight transformer-based patch decoder g is trained to minimize reconstruction error while T_θ is trained via gradient reversal to maximize it, explicitly pushing representations toward regions where the decoder fails; (2) a semantic consistency loss that aligns the decoded image's client-encoder features with a class-level prototype rather than the original instance, preserving discriminative structure while reducing instance-level identifiability; (3) a standard cross-entropy task preservation loss. The key design choice is guiding representations toward class-level prototypes rather than suppressing information, which preserves task-relevant decision boundaries while disrupting the local neighborhood structure that inversion models exploit. The adversarial decoder is used only during training and removed at deployment.

## Results

On MS COCO with CLIP-ViT-B/16, ReTarget achieves the strongest reconstruction degradation across all four split layers: at Layer 3, MS-SSIM drops to 0.0209 (vs. DISCO 0.7818, NoPeek 0.3760) and LPIPS rises to 0.6337 (vs. DISCO 0.1025, NoPeek 0.4690), a 35% LPIPS improvement over NoPeek. At Layer 12, MS-SSIM reaches 0.0134 and LPIPS 0.8389. Task accuracy is preserved at 74.6% (Layer 3) through 78.6% (Layer 12), competitive with or exceeding both baselines. Under adaptive DRAG++ attacks, reconstruction quality remains substantially degraded (e.g., Layer 3 MS-SSIM 0.0481, LPIPS 0.6435).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ReTarget outperforms DISCO on all three reconstruction metrics at every split layer; the gap is most dramatic at Layer 3 where MS-SSIM drops from 0.7818 (DISCO) to 0.0209 (ReTarget), a 97% reduction, while LPIPS increases from 0.1025 to 0.6337. Against NoPeek, ReTarget achieves up to 35% higher LPIPS (0.6337 vs. 0.4690 at Layer 3) and substantially lower DINO feature similarity (0.1695 vs. 0.4263), indicating that NoPeek's decorrelation leaves more semantically recognizable content. Compared to Patch Token Dropping from DRAG, ReTarget induces larger semantic displacement (flip@1 0.2465 vs. 0.1954, logit L2 43.39 vs. 24.64 at Layer 3) while maintaining higher classification accuracy (74.6% vs. 71.0%). Notably, DISCO achieves a larger reduction in instance-level mutual information ΔI(X;H) at Layer 9 (−0.771 vs. −0.352 for ReTarget) yet provides weaker reconstruction protection, supporting the paper's claim that information suppression alone does not predict inversion robustness. Under adaptive DRAG++ attacks, ReTarget still degrades reconstruction substantially (Layer 3: MS-SSIM 0.0481, LPIPS 0.6435), confirming the defense is not merely exploiting a fixed decoder mismatch.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant information-suppression paradigm in split inference defense (DISCO's channel pruning, NoPeek's decorrelation) by arguing that reconstruction vulnerability is governed not only by mutual information between inputs and representations but also by the local geometric organization of feature space. It repurposes adversarial training via gradient reversal—previously used for robustness—toward destabilizing inversion decoders, and introduces a class-prototype alignment regularizer that guides reconstructions toward class-level semantics rather than instance-level detail. The motivation draws on DRAG's empirical observation that patch shuffling (which preserves most information) can drastically reduce DRA quality, and on model-inversion literature showing that inversion performance depends on the compatibility between the inversion procedure and the representation geometry.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical takeaway is that a lightweight residual MLP transformation, trained with adversarial reconstruction and prototype-guided regularization, can substantially degrade reconstruction fidelity (up to 35% LPIPS improvement at shallow layers) while maintaining competitive classification accuracy, offering a low-overhead defense for split inference deployments. However, the geometric interpretation is explicitly framed as a motivating hypothesis rather than a complete theory: the authors show that local-neighborhood disruption alone does not predict reconstruction difficulty at the sample level, and that mutual-information reductions do not fully explain inversion robustness across methods and layers. Open questions include whether the geometric account can be formalized into a predictive framework, whether the defense generalizes to non-CLIP backbones and non-ImageNet domains, and how it interacts with newer, stronger attacks beyond those evaluated.
