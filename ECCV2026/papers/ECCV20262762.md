## Problem

Existing synthetic image attribution methods are confined to closed-set classification over known generators or collapse all unknown sources into a single rejection class, requiring full retraining whenever a new generative model emerges. This inflexibility is unsustainable given the rapid pace of new image generation architectures. The paper introduces few-shot attribution, a paradigm that identifies unseen generators from only a handful of support samples at test time without any retraining.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262762/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB query image and a support set of K labeled samples from N unseen generator classes (N-way K-shot, evaluated at 5-way and 15-way with K=10). Each image is processed through two complementary views: an aspect-ratio-preserving resize with center crop for global features and a direct high-resolution crop for local texture details. The model outputs a 128-dimensional embedding used for both tasks: a binary real/fake decision via a learned angular boundary threshold, and a generator attribution label via nearest-prototype classification against the support set.

## Method

OmniDFA employs a dual-path ConvNeXt-Small backbone that extracts features from both a globally-resized and a locally-cropped view of the input, channel-wise concatenates them, and projects through an MLP to a 128-dim embedding. Training uses supervised contrastive learning to maximize angular separation between different generator classes, combined with a sphere center loss that pulls real-image features toward a learnable centroid to counteract the feature dispersion caused by the large volume of authentic samples. At inference, attribution is performed by computing prototype centers from the support set and assigning each query to its nearest prototype, while detection uses a boundary threshold γ (computed via Tukey's fences and updated with momentum) that defines the maximum angular distance from the real center. The approach is trained with RandAugment, Gaussian blur, and JPEG compression for robustness, and requires no fine-tuning or retraining when new generators appear at test time.

## Results

On the proposed OmniFake dataset, OmniDFA achieves 75.34% accuracy / 74.45% Macro-F1 in 5-way 10-shot attribution and 53.54% / 51.74% in 15-way 10-shot, outperforming the strongest baseline FSD by 2.03% and 2.47% respectively. For zero-shot AIGI detection on OmniFake, OmniDFA reaches 95.58% accuracy and 96.97% AP, surpassing the next-best method ComFor by 5.83% in accuracy. On the cross-dataset GenImage benchmark, OmniDFA attains 95.86% average accuracy (vs. 90.53% for AIDE), and on the real-world Chameleon benchmark it achieves 83.48% accuracy and 80.09% F1, exceeding the second-best method by 17.71% in accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OmniDFA outperforms all 15 baselines across every evaluation setting. In few-shot attribution (Table 2), the closest competitor is FSD [56], which also uses metric learning, trailing OmniDFA by 2.03% (5-way) and ~2.5% (15-way); all dedicated attribution methods (DNA-Det, CPL, SiameseNet, POSE, OOC-CLIP, UnivAttr) fall 15–26 percentage points behind, confirming that closed-set or open-set-rejection training does not transfer to the fully open few-shot setting. For zero-shot detection on OmniFake (Table 3), OmniDFA leads ComFor [40] by 5.83% in accuracy and AIDE [58] by 7.57%, while UnivFD [38] and NPR [47]—both single-generator-trained—degrade severely (57.24% and 79.28% accuracy respectively). On the cross-dataset GenImage benchmark (Table 4), OmniDFA (95.86%) surpasses PatchCraft [62] (90.32%) and AIDE [58] (90.53%) by roughly 5–6 points, and dwarfs older methods like CNNSpot [52] (56.20%) and DIRE [53] (60.42%). The most striking margin appears on the imbalanced Chameleon benchmark, where OmniDFA's 83.48% accuracy and 80.09% F1 exceed AIDE (65.77% / 40.19%) by 17.71% in accuracy, while most baselines exhibit extreme real/fake bias (e.g., PatchCraft: 1.39% F-Acc vs. 96.52% R-Acc). ComFor [40], trained on thousands of Stable-Diffusion-based generators, is the strongest detection baseline overall but still lags OmniDFA by 5.83% on OmniFake and 5.54% on GenImage, underscoring the benefit of architecturally diverse multi-class training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from both closed-set attribution (DNA-Det, CPL, POSE), which requires retraining for every new generator, and open-set rejection methods that collapse all unknown sources into a single class. It repurposes supervised contrastive learning (Khosla et al., NeurIPS 2020) and sphere center loss (Wen et al., ECCV 2016) within a dual-path ConvNeXt backbone to frame attribution as an N-way K-shot prototypical classification problem over unseen generators. The dual sampling strategy (aspect-ratio-preserving global crop + high-resolution local crop) addresses a practical limitation of prior detection work that resizes to fixed scales, while the center loss specifically regularizes the large real-image class to counteract feature dispersion.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The few-shot attribution paradigm directly addresses the practical bottleneck that new generative models emerge faster than detectors can be retrained, offering a training-free adaptation path at test time. The OmniFake dataset (45 architecturally distinct generators, 1.17M synthetic images) fills a critical gap: prior attribution datasets had at most 25 generators and often included fine-tuned variants that obscure true architectural differences. Open questions remain around the slightly lower real-image accuracy caused by the rigid boundary update rule, the performance drop under Gaussian blur beyond the training augmentation range, and whether the paradigm extends to video or 3D generation. The 17.71% accuracy gap on Chameleon suggests current detectors are far from robust in truly in-the-wild settings.
