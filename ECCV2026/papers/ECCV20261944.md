## Problem

Video-LLMs incur prohibitive inference cost due to the quadratic attention complexity over massive visual token sequences from long videos. Existing token compression methods either rely solely on visual cues and ignore text queries, apply uniform compression ratios across frames regardless of content density, or require costly pre-training and intrusive LLM modifications to incorporate text guidance. The paper targets the gap of a lightweight, plug-and-play mechanism that injects query relevance before the LLM while adapting compression to the dynamic information density of individual frames.

## Input / Output

The method consumes I video frames, each encoded into N spatial-semantic tokens (dimension D) by a pre-trained visual encoder (e.g., SigLIP-ViT), along with a text instruction encoded into a single aggregated embedding T ∈ R^D by a matched text encoder. It outputs a compressed visual token sequence F_retained formed by concatenating per-frame selected token subsets in temporal order, where each frame i retains K_i = ⌈N·r_i⌉ tokens under a dynamically allocated ratio r_i ∈ [10%, 40%], yielding an average retention of 15% or 25% across the video. The entire pipeline operates strictly before the LLM, leaving its weights, attention layers, and KV-cache unmodified.

## Method

GTR is a two-stage, training-free token compression framework. In Stage 1 (Guide), each token receives a global uniqueness score (1 minus cosine similarity to its cross-frame position average) and a text relevance score (cosine similarity to the aggregated instruction embedding); these are fused with fixed weights (α=0.375, β=0.625) and summed per frame to produce a frame-level significance score that is min-max normalized into a dynamic retention ratio between 10% and 40%. In Stage 2 (Refine), a local distinctiveness score is computed as 1 minus the mean cosine similarity to spatially adjacent tokens (Chebyshev-distance neighborhood, boundary-adaptive), and the final per-token importance linearly combines all three scores (λ_g=0.3, λ_t=0.5, λ_l=0.2) to rank and select the top-K_i tokens per frame. The design is entirely pre-LLM and architecture-agnostic, requiring no retraining, no LLM-guided signals, and no modifications to attention or cross-attention layers, making it directly compatible with FlashAttention-style kernels and heterogeneous backbones (LLaVA-OneVision, LLaVA-Video, Qwen2-VL).

## Results

On LLaVA-OneVision-7B across four benchmarks (MVBench, LongVideoBench, MLVU, VideoMME), GTR retains 99.8% of full-token average performance at 25% token retention and 95.8% at 15% retention. On the LLaVA-Video-7B backbone, the corresponding figures are 94.0% and 89.1%. At 15% retention, GTR reduces total inference latency from 26 min 03 s to 18 min 15 s (~1.4× speedup) while using only 15.8 GB GPU memory, compared to 24.5–27.1 GB for FastV, SparseVLM, and PDrop.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GTR outperforms all five baselines on both LLaVA backbones at both compression ratios. Against the closest competitor VidCom², GTR leads by 0.2 pp at 25% retention (99.8% vs 99.6%) and by 0.7 pp at 15% (95.8% vs 95.1%) on LLaVA-OV-7B, with similar margins on LLaVA-Video-7B (94.0% vs 93.6% and 89.1% vs 88.5%). The gap widens substantially against visual-only or in-LLM methods: at 15% retention, GTR exceeds SparseVLM by 4.6 pp (95.8% vs 91.2%), PDrop by 8.4 pp, and FastV by 10.8 pp. DyCoke, which relies on LLM-guided keyframe selection, underperforms GTR by 12.8 pp at 25% retention (87.0% vs 99.8%), confirming that GTR's lightweight pre-LLM text scoring is more effective than costly in-LLM guidance. On Qwen2-VL (VideoMME, 25%), GTR also achieves the best overall score (94.5%) versus VidCom² (94.0%) and SparseVLM (93.7%), with the largest margin on long videos (99.8% retention). In efficiency terms, GTR matches VidCom² in latency and GPU memory while delivering higher accuracy, and avoids the 24–27 GB memory overhead of FastV, SparseVLM, and PDrop by remaining compatible with FlashAttention-style kernels.

## Relation to Prior Work

GTR sits at the intersection of two threads in Video-LLM efficiency: unconditional token pruning (STTM, DynTok, FLOC) that relies solely on visual redundancy, and conditional methods (HICom, DyCoke, DyToK, HoliTom) that inject text or instruction signals but typically require expensive pre-training, LLM-guided keyframe priors, or in-LLM attention modifications. GTR departs from both by performing all compression entirely before the LLM using only a lightweight text encoder (SigLIP) and three cosine-similarity-based scores, avoiding any backbone modification while still achieving query-aware, frame-adaptive token budgets. Its two-stage Guide-Then-Refine design repurposes the cross-frame averaging idea from temporal redundancy detection and the local neighborhood scoring from spatial saliency, but unifies them under a single dynamic-allocation framework that prior methods handle with fixed ratios or segment-level thresholds.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for deployment: retaining only 15% of visual tokens while preserving 95.8% of full-token performance, with GPU memory around 16 GB (vs. 24–27 GB for in-LLM pruning baselines), makes long-video QA feasible on consumer-grade hardware without retraining. The plug-and-play compatibility demonstrated with SparseVLM and FastV (adding GTR yields consistent +0.5–0.9% gains) suggests it can serve as an orthogonal enhancement layer in existing pipelines. Open questions include whether the fixed hyperparameters (α, β, λ weights, r_base/r_max) transfer robustly to unseen domains or ultra-long videos (>10 min), and whether the text-encoder dependency introduces a ceiling when the visual encoder and text encoder are not natively aligned (as with Qwen2-VL). The authors themselves flag that current benchmarks may not fully isolate temporal reasoning, leaving the true benefit of frame-adaptive allocation under complex temporal queries partially unvalidated.
