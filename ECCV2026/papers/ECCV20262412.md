## Problem

Gradient manipulation methods for multi-task learning (e.g., PCGrad, CAGrad, Nash-MTL) produce more balanced solutions than scalarization-based approaches but incur O(K) time and memory costs by computing and storing all K task gradients at each iteration. This scalability bottleneck becomes prohibitive in large-scale MTL with many tasks and deep architectures, while O(1) scalarization methods fail to explicitly control the loss discrepancy that causes task imbalance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262412/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method is task-agnostic and operates on any set of K supervised loss functions sharing a common backbone. In practice, inputs range from RGB images (Cityscapes, NYU-v2, CelebA) to molecular SMILES structures (QM9), and outputs are task-specific: segmentation masks, depth maps, surface normals, 40 binary attribute labels, or 11 molecular property regressions. The only additional parameter beyond the shared model x is a small routing network W with a softmax layer that outputs K task weights, keeping the overhead negligible relative to the backbone.

## Method

LDC-MTL formulates loss discrepancy control as a bilevel problem: the lower level minimizes a weighted sum of K task losses w.r.t. shared parameters x, while the upper level minimizes the accumulated absolute gaps between adjacent weighted losses w.r.t. router parameters W. A penalty-based reformulation converts this into a single constrained objective, and the key empirical observation—that the gradient norm ∇_W g(W, z_N^t) from the inner-loop approximation is over 100× smaller than ∇_W g(W, x^t)—allows dropping the expensive inner loop entirely. The resulting Algorithm 1 is a fully single-loop, first-order method requiring only one gradient pass per epoch for both W and x, achieving O(1) time and memory. Under Lipschitz-smoothness and a Polyak-Łojasiewicz condition on the lower-level function, the method is proven to converge to an ε-accurate Pareto stationary point for all K loss functions in O(ε^{-2}) iterations.

## Results

On the 40-task CelebA dataset, LDC-MTL achieves the lowest performance drop of Δm% = −1.31 and mean rank (MR) of 2.62, substantially outperforming the best gradient-manipulation baseline FairGrad (Δm% = 0.37, MR = 5.67) and the best scalarization baseline GO4Align (Δm% = 0.88). On the 11-task QM9 dataset, LDC-MTL attains Δm% = 49.5 and MR = 4.73, again the best among all 14 baselines. On Cityscapes (2-task), LDC-MTL achieves Δm% = −0.57 (best), and on NYU-v2 (3-task) it reaches Δm% = −4.40, closely matching FairGrad (−4.66). All results are obtained with O(1) time and memory cost, compared to O(K) for gradient-manipulation methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LDC-MTL outperforms all 14 baselines on CelebA (40-task) and QM9 (11-task), achieving Δm% = −1.31 vs. the next-best FairGrad (0.37) and GO4Align (0.88) on CelebA, and Δm% = 49.5 vs. GO4Align (52.7) and STCH (56.9) on QM9. On Cityscapes (2-task), LDC-MTL attains the best Δm% = −0.57, beating the closest scalarization baseline UW (5.89) by over 6 points and the best gradient-manipulation method FairGrad (5.18) by 5.75 points. On NYU-v2 (3-task), LDC-MTL (Δm% = −4.40) is within 0.26 of FairGrad (−4.66) and clearly ahead of all other methods, including the reproduced GO4Align* (1.94) and STCH (−1.35). In terms of efficiency, LDC-MTL incurs at most a 1.11× overhead over linear scalarization, while gradient-manipulation methods like Nash-MTL require ~12× and ConsMTL ~11× more training time on CelebA. The paper also shows that LDC-MTL solutions lie on the Pareto frontier and dominate weight-swept LS, FAMO, and GO4Align solutions on Cityscapes, confirming that the dynamic loss-discrepancy control during training (not just final weights) drives the performance gain.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of bilevel optimization and multi-task learning, drawing on penalty-based bilevel reformulations (Kwon et al., 2023; Shen et al., 2023) to construct a first-order algorithm for loss discrepancy control. It departs from the two dominant MTL paradigms—scalarization-based methods (LS, DWA, UW, FAMO) that are O(1) but lack explicit loss balancing, and gradient manipulation methods (MGDA, PCGrad, CAGrad, Nash-MTL, FairGrad) that balance gradients at O(K) cost—by formulating loss gap minimization as an upper-level objective over a small router network, while the lower level optimizes model parameters. The key algorithmic departure is the empirical justification for dropping the inner-loop hypergradient term, reducing the full bilevel procedure to a single-loop O(1) update, a simplification borrowed from large-scale LLM fine-tuning practice.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The O(1) time and memory complexity makes balanced multi-task learning practical for large-scale settings (40-task CelebA, 11-task QM9) where gradient manipulation methods become prohibitive, and the convergence guarantee to an ε-accurate Pareto stationary point provides theoretical grounding that most competing methods lack. A notable finding is that the dynamic training trajectory—where task weights evolve meaningfully early and then converge to near-uniform values—drives performance, rather than the final weight configuration, which challenges the assumption that the endpoint of optimization is what matters. Open questions include whether the approximation of neglecting the inner-loop gradient holds under more adversarial task configurations, and whether the approach extends to unsupervised or reinforcement-learning MTL settings as the authors note for future work.
